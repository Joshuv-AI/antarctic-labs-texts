A model-independent theory of probabilistic testing Journal: Nuclear Physics B
Weijun Chen, Yuxi Fu ✉, Huan Long, Hao Wu

Abstract
Probabilistic concurrent systems are foundational models for modern mobile computing. In this paper, a general model-independent approach to probabilistic testing is proposed. With the help of a new distribution-based semantics for probabilistic models and a probabilistic testing framework with respect to process predicates, the model-independent characterization and the external characterization for testing equivalences are studied. The latter characterization can be viewed as the generalization of the classical fair/should equivalence and may equivalence. These equivalences are shown to be congruent. A thorough comparison between these equivalences and probabilistic bisimilarities is carried out. The techniques introduced in this paper can be easily extended to other probabilistic concurrent models.

Testing equivalences

The testing framework makes use of a class of specialized testing processes called

A slightly different approach is to seek alternative characterizations without introducing external observers for testing, giving rise to the trace equivalence

Since these characterizations are more intrinsic and rely solely on the behaviors of the processes themselves, they are also referred to as the

Over the past few decades, integrating probabilistic mechanisms into traditional computational models has been a major endeavor.
The studies of probabilistic testing equivalences emerged with both theoretical and practical motivations

,

Randomness has been studied in different process models.
Some well-known models are Markov decision processes (MDP)

One would hope that the theory of probabilistic testing equivalence ought not to depend on any choice of models.
Such a model independence requirement has been the central theme of Fu et al.’s theory
[

,
12
,
20

.
The basic idea is that different interaction models may have different interaction mechanisms, while computations (internal actions) are common to all models.
If we further abstract away from the specific details of external actions, then this approach is adaptable to various models.

In this paper, we aim to extend the model-independent approach to the setting of probabilistic testing equivalences.
Although this approach abstracts away from the specific details of any particular model, it remains essential to select a concrete model for a case study.
We focus on the RCCS model, which enables direct comparison with classical testing equivalences on CCS.
We primarily focus on the may equivalence and the fair equivalence, since it is well-known that the may equivalence is compatible with the fair equivalence
[
3
,
4

[

More specifically, we provide two types of characterizations: the observer-based external characterizations and the model-independent characterizations.
These characterizations coincide and can be regarded as probabilistic extensions of classical

A comprehensive spectrum of behavioral equivalences (including testing equivalences

A more careful comparison of these equivalences, in terms of the inclusion

The interplay between nondeterminism and randomness is a central issue.
Some studies
[

resolved all nondeterministic choices probabilistically, limiting their applicability to general probabilistic concurrent systems.
The RCCS model in the paper incorporates both nondeterminism and randomness, allowing us to demonstrate a proper treatment of nondeterminism in the presence of randomness.
To help describe our general testing framework, a new form of probabilistic transitions is introduced.
Instead of using the tree-based semantic
[

25

13

Our testing equivalences are all

This is quite significant for a full-fledged interaction model.
In most previous studies, equivalence relations are defined for models that disown the concurrent composition operator or the recursion operator.

Research on probabilistic fair testing remains quite limited, whereas probabilistic may equivalence has been extensively studied.
Beyond the aforementioned differences in models and methods, regarding the may equivalence itself, we believe that our notion aligns with some existing definitions
[

For clarity, we will not include formal comparisons or proofs of equivalence with these existing definitions.
But thanks to the model-independent nature of our approach, our definitions can be readily adapted to the models studied in the literature, and the derivation of the analogous results is straightforward.

For

For any

Given a word

1.

Our semantics is more suitable for characterizing the continuous nature of probabilities.

For probabilistic testing, the tree-based method often makes too fine a distinction between nodes, where different nodes are treated as distinct, even if they are labeled with the same process.

4
A predicate-based framework for probabilistic testing

In this section, we propose a predicate-based framework for probabilistic testing.
We begin by formalizing the testing outcome of a distribution.
Recall that the classical way (in Section
2.2
) is to introduce a special action
\omega
to specify success.
Here, we take
predicates
on processes, i.e., the subsets of
\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}
, as the extension.
Given a distribution
\mu
and a predicate
\varphi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}
, we use
\mu(\varphi)=\sum_{P\in\varphi}\mu(P)
to denote the probability that the predicate
\varphi
is satisfied under the distribution
\mu
, and call it the
satisfaction probability of
\varphi
under
\mu
.
The
testing outcomes of
\mu
with respect to
\varphi
is then defined by the set
\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}:=\left\{\nu(\varphi):\mu\rightsquigarrow\nu\right\}
, that is, the collection of satisfaction probabilities of
\varphi
under the distributions reachable from
\mu
by probabilistic internal transition sequences.
We shall further abbreviate
\mathcal{O}^{\delta_{P}}_{\varphi}
to
\mathcal{O}^{P}_{\varphi}
.

4.1
The structure of testing outcomes

Classical testing equivalences are obtained by imposing varying degrees of constraints on the testing results.
Here, we show that the testing outcomes defined above are determined by their boundaries.
Therefore, it suffices only to consider the boundary points when defining probabilistic testing equivalences.
Let
O_{1},O_{2}
be two sets of real numbers, and
k\in\mathbb{R}
be a scalar.
The Minkowski sum and scalar product for sets are defined as:

O_{1}+O_{2}:=\{x+y:x\in O_{1},y\in O_{2}\},kO_{1}:=\{kx:x\in O_{1}\}
.
As we have had Lemma
2
, the intuition of the following lemma, which shows the linearity of testing outcomes, should be clear.

Lemma 4

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
be two distributions,
\varphi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}
be a predicate, and
p\in[0,1]
be a real number.
The following statements are valid.

1.

\mathcal{O}^{p\mu_{1}+(1-p)\mu_{2}}_{\varphi}=p\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{1}}_{\varphi}+(1-p)\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{2}}_{\varphi}.

2.

\partial\,\mathcal{O}^{p\mu_{1}+(1-p)\mu_{2}}_{\varphi}=p\cdot\partial\,\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{1}}_{\varphi}+(1-p)\cdot\partial\,\mathcal{O}^{\mu_{2}}_{\varphi}
, for
\partial\in\{\sup,\inf\}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
.
Lemma
4
delineates the structure of testing outcomes.
If

, then for any

, we can apply Lemma
4
(
1

px+(1-p)y\in p\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}+(1-p)\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{O}^{p\mu+(1-p)\mu}_{\varphi}=\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}.

(6)

Therefore,
\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}
is a nonempty convex subset of
[0,1]

The supremum and infimum can almost fully determine
\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}

This also explains why we need Lemma

(

In fact, when
\varphi

4.2
Approximation by degenerate sequences

Since both the external and internal characterization of probabilistic testing will be defined within this framework, we provide several general technical lemmas in this part.
Recall that a witness
\pi
of an internal transition sequence is degenerate if
\pi\in\tau^{*}
, i.e., without uncertainty.
Lemma
5
shows that any reachable satisfaction probability can be both lower and upper approximated by some probabilistic transition sequences with degenerate witnesses.

Lemma 5

Let
\varphi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}
be a predicate and
\mu,\nu\in{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
be two distributions.
If
\mu\rightsquigarrow\nu
, then there exist two distribution
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
such that

1.

for
i=1,2
,
\mu\rightsquigarrow\nu_{i}
with a degenerate witness
\pi_{i}\in\tau^{*}
, and

2.

\nu_{1}(\varphi)\leq\nu(\varphi)\leq\nu_{2}(\varphi)
.

The proof of Lemma
5
(see Appendix
A.2
) does not rely on the fact that
\varphi
is a predicate.
If we regard
\varphi
as its double dual, i.e., a function
\varphi^{*}:{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})\to[0,1]
, which maps any distribution
\mu
to
\mu(\varphi)
,
then for any distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
and
p\in[0,1]
, we have that

\varphi^{*}(p\mu_{1}+(1-p)\mu_{2})=p\mu_{1}(\varphi)+(1-p)\mu_{2}(\varphi)=p\varphi^{*}(\mu_{1})+(1-p)\varphi^{*}(\mu_{2}).

(8)

This means that
\varphi^{*}
is a linear functional, which is the only crucial property of
\varphi
used in the proof.
Therefore, if we replace
\varphi^{*}
with any other linear functional, the lemma still holds.
Lemma
4
verifies that the functional
\mu\mapsto\sup\,\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}^{\mu}
, is also linear.
Combining Lemma
4
and the proof of Lemma
5
, we get the following lemma.

Lemma 6

Let
\varphi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}
be a predicate and
\mu,\nu\in{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
be two distributions.
If
\mu\rightsquigarrow\nu
, then there exist two distributions
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
such that

1.

for
i=1,2
,
\mu\rightsquigarrow\nu_{i}
with a degenerate witness
\pi_{i}\in\tau^{*}
, and

2.

\sup\,\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}^{\nu_{1}}\leq\sup\,\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}^{\nu}\leq\sup\,\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}^{\nu_{2}}
.

A major application of Lemma
5
and Lemma
6
is that when examining the boundaries of testing outcomes, it suffices to focus on transition sequences with degenerate witnesses.

Corollary 7

Given
\mu\in{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
and
\varphi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}
, the following equalities are valid.

1.

\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\varphi}(\mu)=\sup\,\left\{\nu(\varphi):\mu\xrightarrow{\mathmakebox{\tau^{*}}}\nu\right\}
,

2.

\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\varphi}(\mu)=\inf\left\{\sup\mathcal{O}^{\nu}_{\varphi}:\mu\xrightarrow{\mathmakebox{\tau^{*}}}\nu\right\}
.

Corollary
7
provides a simplification of the definition of characteristics.
However, it is important to note that convex combinations of two degenerate sequences are not necessarily degenerate.
Therefore, the simplified form on the right cannot be viewed as the essence of the testing characteristics.

In this section, we introduce the model-independent probabilistic testing equivalences (

5.2
Probabilistic box equivalence and diamond equivalence

Observability

We now generalize the (strong) equipollence and the extensionality to the RCCS model.
We first define

\psi_{\mathcal{L}}:=\left\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}}:P\xrightarrow{\mathmakebox{\ell}}\text{ for some }\ell\in\mathcal{L}\right\}

(9)

to indicate that a process can perform an external action
and abbreviate
\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\psi_{\mathcal{L}}}
,
\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\psi_{\mathcal{L}}}
, and
\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\psi_{\mathcal{L}}}
as
\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{L}}
,
\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\mathcal{L}}
, and
\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\mathcal{L}}
, respectively.
Note that the essence of the model-independent approach lies in its disregard for the specific behavior of external actions, which is effectively captured by
\psi_{\mathcal{L}}
.
However, with the introduction of probabilities, the observability of processes should no longer be a dichotomy of observable/unobservable.
For example, consider two processes

\displaystyle P_{5}:=\tau.(0.99\tau.a\oplus 0.01\tau.\mathbf{0})+\tau.(0.01\tau.a\oplus 0.99\tau.\mathbf{0}),\text{\quad and}

(10)

\displaystyle P_{6}:=\tau.(0.49\tau.a\oplus 0.51\tau.\mathbf{0})+\tau.(0.51\tau.a\oplus 0.49\tau.\mathbf{0}),

(11)

where we might agree that
P_{5}
has a higher likelihood of being observable than
P_{6}
.
Fortunately, the testing framework introduced in Section
4
allows us to refine the definition of observability as follows.

Definition 5
(Probabilistic equipollence)

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a relation over
{D}(\mathcal{P}_{{}_{\mathsf{RCCS}}})
.
Then

1.

\mathcal{R}
is
probabilistically equipollent
if
\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{1})=\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{2})
holds whenever
\mu_{1}\,\mathcal{R}\,\mu_{2}
.

2.

\mathcal{R}
is
probabilistically strongly equipollent
if
\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{1})=\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{2})
holds whenever
\mu_{1}\,\mathcal{R}\,\mu_{2}
.

The following examples should help to understand the definition.

Example 6

We can interpret
\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu)
as the degree to which
\mu
is observable.
Accordingly, our claim that
P_{5}
is more likely to be observable than
P_{6}
is supported by the fact that
\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\mathcal{L}}(P_{5})=0.99>0.51=\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\mathcal{L}}(P_{6})
.
Similarly, since
\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\mathcal{L}}(P_{5})=0.01<0.49=\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\mathcal{L}}(P_{6})
, we may say that
P_{5}
is “more strongly observable” than
P_{6}
.
Then, a probabilistically (strongly) equipollent relation requires that any pair of relevant distributions have the same degree of being (strongly) observable.

Example 7

Let
\mathcal{R}:=\{(\delta_{P_{1}},\delta_{P_{2}})\}
, where
P_{1},P_{2}
are defined in Example
1
.
Since
\mathcal{N}(P_{1})=\mathcal{N}(P_{2})=\{a\}
, the distinguishing power of
\psi_{\mathcal{L}}
is no greater than that of
\psi_{a}
in this simple case.
According to Example
4
,
\mathcal{R}
is probabilistically equipollent but not probabilistically strongly equipollent.

From the examples above, we observe that for a distribution

Lemma 10

Example 8

Let

The following lemma demonstrates that the characterization using testing outcomes is a generalization of (strong) observability.

6.2.1
Probabilistic weak bisimilarity

Let
\mathsf{Tr}=\{(A,\alpha,\rho)\mid\text{$A\xrightarrow{\mathmakebox{\alpha}}\rho$ can be derived in the pLTS in Figure \ref{fig:pLTS1}}\}
be the set of transitions.
For a transition
\mathsf{tr}=(A,\alpha,\rho)
, we denote by
\mathsf{src}(\mathsf{tr})
the source process
A
, by
\mathsf{act}(\mathsf{tr})
the action
\alpha
, and by
\rho_{\mathsf{tr}}
the evolved distribution
\rho
.
Let
\mathsf{Tr}(\alpha)=\{\mathsf{tr}\in\mathsf{Tr}\mid\mathsf{act}(\mathsf{tr})=\alpha\}
.
An
execution fragment
of some process
A_{0}
is a finite or infinite sequence of alternating states and actions
\omega=A_{0}\alpha_{0}A_{1}\alpha_{1}A_{2}\alpha_{2}\cdots
such that
A_{i}\xrightarrow{\mathmakebox{\alpha_{i}}}\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{i}(A_{i+1})>0
.
If
\omega
is finite, we denote by
\mathsf{last}(\omega)
the last state of
\omega
.
We denote by
\mathsf{frags}^{*}(A)
and
\mathsf{frags}(A)
the set of finite and all execution fragments of
A
, respectively.
Given
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we define
\widehat{\alpha}=\alpha
if
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
, and
\widehat{\alpha}=\varepsilon
(the empty string) if
\alpha=\tau
.
The
trace
of an execution fragment
\omega
is the sub-sequence of external actions of
\omega
, i.e.,
\mathsf{trace}(\omega)=\widehat{\alpha_{0}}\widehat{\alpha_{1}}\widehat{\alpha_{2}}\cdots
.

In
[
17
]
, the notion of
scheduler
is used to resolve non-determinism.
To a process
A
, a scheduler is a function
\sigma:\mathsf{frags}^{*}(A)\to{D}(\mathsf{Tr}\cup\{\bot\})
such that for each
\omega\in\mathsf{frags}^{*}(A),\sigma(\omega)\in{D}(\{\mathsf{tr}\in\mathsf{Tr}\mid\mathsf{src}(\mathsf{tr})=\mathsf{last}(\omega)\}\cup\{\bot\})
.
We call a scheduler
\sigma
(of
A
)
Dirac
if for each
\omega\in\mathsf{frags}^{*}(A)
,
\sigma(\omega)=\delta_{\mathsf{tr}}
for some
\mathsf{tr}\in\mathsf{Tr}
or
\sigma(\omega)=\delta_{\bot}
.
We call a scheduler
\sigma
(of
A
)
finite
if there exists a finite natural number
N
such that
\sigma(\omega)=\delta_{\bot}
for all
\omega
with
|\omega|\geq N
.
A scheduler
\sigma
and a process
A
induce a probability distribution
\rho_{\sigma,A}
over finite execution fragments as follows.
The basic measurable events are the
cones
of finite execution fragments, where the cone of
\omega
is defined by
C_{\omega}=\{\omega^{\prime}\in\mathsf{frags}(A)\mid\text{$\omega$ is a prefix of $\omega^{\prime}$}\}
.
The probability
\rho_{\sigma,A}
of a cone
C_{\omega}
is defined recursively as follows:

\rho_{\sigma,A}(C_{\omega})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\omega=A$,}\\
0,&\text{if $\omega=B\neq A$},\\
\rho_{\sigma,A}(C_{\omega^{\prime}})\cdot\sum_{\mathsf{tr}\in\mathsf{Tr}(\alpha)}\sigma(\omega^{\prime})(\mathsf{tr})\cdot\rho_{\mathsf{tr}}(B),&\text{if $\omega=\omega^{\prime}\alpha B$.}\end{cases}

(25)

Finally, for any
\omega\in\mathsf{frags}^{*}(A)
,
\rho_{\sigma,A}(\omega)

is defined as
\rho_{\sigma,A}(\omega)=\rho_{\sigma,A}(C_{\omega})\cdot\sigma(\omega)(\bot)
, where
\sigma(\omega)(\bot)
is the probability of choosing no further transition (i.e., terminating) after
\omega
.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a new distribution-based semantics for the RCCS model and investigated several probabilistic testing equivalences.
We have formalized a probabilistic testing framework with respect to process predicates and introduced two types of characterizations for testing equivalences: model-independent characterizations (
=^{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{{}^{\Diamond}}
and
=^{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{{}^{\Box}}
) and observer-based external characterizations (
=^{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{\mathsf{may}}
and
=^{{}_{\mathcal{D}}}_{\mathsf{fair}}
).
These equivalences can be regarded as generalizations of their classical counterparts under the classical testing context.
We have also conducted an in-depth comparison between probabilistic weak bisimilarity, probabilistic branching bisimilarity, and our testing equivalences.
It should be emphasized that our equivalence relations are congruent.

Testing equivalences
\chi^{\mathsf{may}}_{\varphi}
and
\chi^{\mathsf{fair}}_{\varphi}
using characteristic quantities are introduced.
It is important to note that our definitions (Definition
5
and Definition
9
) require that the two related distributions to
exactly
match on these characteristics.
However, this is a rather strong requirement, especially for probabilistic models such as probabilistic Turing machines or interactive proof systems
[
28
]
, where small errors are often tolerated.
This observation suggests to investigate
weaker forms
of testing equivalence that permit small discrepancies in the characteristic values of related distributions.
For example, if two processes differ by no more than
0.001
in their probability of eventual success under all tests, we may consider them interchangeable in most practical contexts.
Exploring the properties and implications of such relaxed testing equivalences presents a promising and practical direction for future work.

Probabilistic must equivalence is not the focus of this paper, but the testing framework developed in Section
4
remains applicable to its definition and analysis.
For instance, we can define a new characteristic
\chi^{\mathsf{must}}_{\varphi}
, which may take the form of
\inf\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{\varphi}
, to provide model-independent and external characterizations of probabilistic must equivalence. Furthermore, the probabilistic transition sequence introduced in Section
3
can serve as a foundation for alternative characterizations of other probabilistic equivalences.
The linearity and continuity of testing outcomes are also notable properties, offering a natural connection to recent research on metrics
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
.
Investigating a metric-based characterization of probabilistic testing equivalences thus represents another fruitful direction for future work.

In classical testing equivalence, the may equivalence checking problem between finite-state processes can be reduced to the equivalence problem of nondeterministic automata, and is therefore in
\mathbf{PSPACE}
.
Hence, the theory of probabilistic trace equivalence is also a worthwhile research topic.
Some existing studies have already offered characterizations of probabilistic trace equivalence
[
32
,
33
]
.
An open question is how to define an appropriate distribution-based trace equivalence for our model and whether this could shed light on the development of related decision algorithms.
