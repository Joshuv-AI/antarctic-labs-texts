Medical postgraduates’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards scientific research in China: a cross-sectional study with mediation analysis
Authors: Buqing Sai, Bo Li, Xin He, Lili Yang, Jing Wu, Yuechun Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith increasing emphasis on integrating research into postgraduate medical education in China’s dual-track academic and professional systems, understanding students’ research engagement has become increasingly important. This study aims to examine the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of medical postgraduates regarding scientific research.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used a self-administered questionnaire and convenience sampling to recruit medical postgraduates from April 23 to July 18, 2024, at Kunming Medical University. The questionnaire assessed knowledge (10 items; score range 0–10), attitudes (9 items; 5-point Likert; 9–45), and practices (7 items; 5-point Likert; 7–35). Group differences were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis H test, and mediation analysis was performed using structural equation modeling with SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 26.0.ResultsAmong 1,089 respondents, 67.86% were female, 39.03% had prior publications, and 21.3% had participated in research projects. The knowledge, attitude, and practice were 7.66 ± 0.70 (possible range: 0–10), 34.96 ± 4.13 (possible range: 9–45), and 28.53 ± 3.95 (possible range: 7–35), respectively. Mediation analysis revealed that degree type had a significant direct effect on both knowledge (β = -0.091, P = 0.042) and attitude (β = -0.967, P < 0.001). Knowledge had a direct effect on attitude (β = 0.569, P = 0.001), while attitude showed a direct effect on practice (β = 0.655, P < 0.001). Additionally, income (β = 0.174, P = 0.029), prior publication (β = 0.499, P = 0.004), research participation (β = 0.418, P = 0.042), and gender (β = -1.010, P < 0.001) also directly influenced practice scores.ConclusionsMedical postgraduates showed relatively high knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive research practices, as reflected by the mean scores within the scale ranges. The findings highlight the need for tailored educational policies, including integrating research-oriented modules into curricula, structured mentorship programs, and addressing gender disparities to enhance research engagement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09160-4.Keywords: Medical postgraduates, Scientific research, Research capability, Knowledge attitude and practice, Cross-sectional study

Background

Medical master’s students are an important group in the cultivation of clinician-researchers and future academic physicians. In this study, “medical postgraduates” refers specifically to currently enrolled medical master’s students in China. Within the Chinese postgraduate medical education system, these students are generally trained in a dual-track structure comprising academic-degree programs and professional-degree programs. Academic-degree programs primarily emphasize research training and theoretical development, whereas professional-degree programs place greater emphasis on clinical competency alongside required research-related tasks [1, 2]. Despite recent governmental efforts to enhance research education and promote interdisciplinary integration, medical postgraduates face challenges such as balancing clinical duties with research training and limited access to mentorship and resources [3]. Many students report insufficient time for research due to clinical responsibilities, and mentors often lack up-to-date research experience, further hindering skill development [2]. Globally, similar challenges exist. Integrating research into medical education improves clinical decision-making and evidence-based practice, as shown in studies from the United States and Europe [4, 5]. Early exposure to research has been associated with enhanced academic productivity and skill development in these regions.

The knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) model provides a valuable framework for understanding behaviors related to research engagement, highlighting the importance of knowledge and attitudes in shaping practices [6–8]. Recent studies from China have examined research engagement mainly among undergraduate medical students and resident physicians, focusing on perceived barriers, attitudes, or general academic engagement rather than the interrelationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices within a KAP framework [9–11]. For example, recent surveys in China have investigated research perceptions and barriers among undergraduate medical students, while studies among resident physicians have emphasized limited time, insufficient guidance, and restricted research support as major obstacles to engagement. However, evidence specifically addressing medical postgraduates in China’s dual-track master’s training system remains limited, particularly with respect to KAP-based evaluation and the pathways linking these dimensions.

This study addresses this gap by evaluating the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of Chinese medical postgraduates regarding scientific research, examining factors associated with these dimensions, and exploring their research-training needs. Previous studies have mainly focused on undergraduate students, resident physicians, or general academic engagement rather than scientific-research-related KAP among medical postgraduates in China’s dual-track system [9–11]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess KAP levels and examine their interrelationships using mediation analysis in this specific population. The findings may help inform strategies for improving research education and engagement.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This cross-sectional study enrolled first-, second-, and third-year medical master’s students from academic and professional degree programs at Kunming Medical University from April 23 to July 18, 2024, using a self-administered questionnaire. In this setting, first-year students mainly complete degree coursework and clarify their research direction; second-year students are primarily engaged in laboratory- or clinically based research training, data collection, and project implementation; and third-year students mainly focus on data completion, manuscript preparation, thesis submission, and graduation requirements. Academic degree students are more research-oriented, whereas professional degree students combine clinical training with research-related tasks. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. A total of 1,287 postgraduates were invited to participate in the study, and 1,089 valid responses were collected, resulting in a response rate of 84.6%. The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and provided informed consent. The exclusion criteria were invalid questionnaires (e.g., excessive missing responses, logical inconsistencies, or abnormally short completion times identified as outliers). In addition, the questionnaire included reverse-coded attention-check (trap) items to assess response validity; questionnaires with incorrect responses to these items were excluded. The reverse-coded attention-check items were embedded in the knowledge section. The exact text of the attention-check items was: “Before conducting a study, approval from an institutional ethics committee is required.” and “Before conducting a study, approval from an institutional ethics committee is not required.” Questionnaires with incorrect responses to these attention-check items were excluded. Participants were recruited during non-busy periods to reduce their workload and improve response rates, and anonymity was ensured to encourage honest responses. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University [Approval Number: (2024) Linlun Review (162)], and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Questionnaire description

Prior to the main study, a pre-experiment (pilot study) was conducted with 50 participants to assess the questionnaire’s reliability and validity. One questionnaire with a response time of less than 90 s was excluded, resulting in 49 valid responses. The pre-experiment yielded an overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.814, indicating good internal consistency. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value of the pre-experiment was 0.896, demonstrating good sampling adequacy. Feedback from the pre-experiment was used to make minor modifications to improve question clarity and ensure content validity. The questionnaire was developed based on a comprehensive review of existing studies and expert consultation, combining both adapted and newly developed questions to ensure comprehensive coverage of key KAP dimensions [12–15]. The expert panel consisted of five postgraduate supervisors from clinical medicine, stomatology, basic medicine, rehabilitation medicine, and laboratory medicine, all of whom held senior academic and/or clinical titles and had 25–38 years of professional experience. The experts reviewed the questionnaire for relevance, clarity, and content coverage, and minor revisions were made accordingly. The final questionnaire, administered in Chinese, was developed based on literature review and expert consultation, covering four areas: demographic information, knowledge, attitudes, and practices. The demographic section included family-structure and socioeconomic indicators that may relate to access to educational resources and research opportunities in the Chinese context. “Only child” was defined as having no siblings (yes/no), reflecting the historical one-child policy and potential differences in family resource allocation. Parental education (father/mother) was recorded as the highest level completed and categorized as middle school or below, high/technical school, and associate’s degree or above. The knowledge section consisted of 10 questions, with each correct answer receiving 1 point and each incorrect answer receiving 0 points, resulting in a total score range of 0–10. The attitude section included 9 questions on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly agree (5 points) to strongly disagree (1 point), with a score range of 9–45. The practice section comprised 7 questions using a five-point Likert scale ranging from always (5 points) to never (1 point), with a total score range of 7–35.Scores above 70% of the maximum in each section were considered to indicate adequate knowledge, positive attitude, and proactive practices [14, 15].

The questionnaire was created using Questionnaire Star, which generated a QR code for the electronic survey. Participants accessed and completed the questionnaire by scanning the QR code via WeChat. To ensure the quality and completeness of the responses, each IP address was allowed only one submission, and all items were mandatory. The survey was conducted anonymously, and respondents were informed at the beginning of the questionnaire about its anonymous nature. No personal identifiers were collected; instead, responses were identified using random codes. The Questionnaire Star platform also employed encryption during data transmission and storage, and data were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Members of the research team reviewed all questionnaires for completeness, internal consistency, and response validity.

Statistical methods

Descriptive analysis of participants’ demographic data and KAP scores was conducted. Continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical data and individual question responses are expressed as frequencies (percentages).To compare differences in knowledge (K) and attitude (A) scores among various demographic groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied for three or more groups, as appropriate for non-normally distributed data. All grouping variables used for the two-group and multi-group comparisons were predefined according to the questionnaire items (e.g., gender, residence, only-child status, degree type, publication history, and research participation) rather than being generated post hoc. For these non-parametric group comparisons, both P-values and corresponding effect sizes were reported. Mediation analysis was conducted using structural equation modeling to examine the direct and indirect relationships among KAP components, while also incorporating demographic and baseline variables such as gender, income, publication history, research participation, and degree type. Given the cross-sectional design, the mediation pathways were specified as hypothesis-driven associations to quantify indirect effects within the KAP framework rather than to infer causality. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Model fit was assessed using several indices, including the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Root Mean Square Residual (RMR), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Incremental Fit Index (IFI).

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 1,089 valid questionnaires were collected, resulting in a response rate of 84.6%. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.888, and the KMO value was 0.916, indicating excellent internal consistency and sampling adequacy. The following samples were excluded: (1) 6 samples with completion times of less than 90 s; (2) 3 samples from participants under 18 years of age; and (3) 189 samples with incorrect responses to trap questions. This resulted in 1,089 valid questionnaires, yielding a validity rate of 84.6%. Among the respondents, 739 (67.86%) were females, with an average age of 26.64 ± 2.11 years. KAP dimensions and demands

The knowledge, attitude, and practice were 7.66 ± 0.70 (possible range: 0–10), 34.96 ± 4.13 (possible range: 9–45), and 28.53 ± 3.95 (possible range: 7–35), respectively. These results suggest that while medical postgraduates demonstrated adequate knowledge and positive attitudes, there remains room for improvement in actual research practice. Responses to the knowledge dimension showed that more than 96.6% of the participants were able to grasp all the knowledge items correctly, suggesting a strong theoretical foundation in research methodology (Fig. 1a). When it comes to related attitudes, 6.88% disagreed or strongly disagreed (A5), reflecting generally positive views toward research engagement (Fig. 1b). Responses to the practice dimension showed that 21.3% did not necessarily feel excited about or look forward to independently conducting research projects (P4) (Fig. 1c). Regarding the demand for research courses among medical postgraduates, 66.76% expressed a preference for hybrid learning (online + offline). The most commonly reported challenges in research design included topic selection (78.05%), research plan design (71.63%), and implementation planning (56.93%). These findings suggest a clear need for targeted training programs that focus on research topic development, methodological design, and project execution strategies. Such programs should also incorporate flexible delivery formats to accommodate students’ schedules and enhance accessibility (Supplementary Table S1).

Mediation analysis

The SEM model showed a marginal fit overall, with RMSEA and RMR within acceptable ranges, whereas CFI and IFI were below the conventional threshold for good fit (Supplementary Table S2), The mediation analysis revealed that the type of medical postgraduates’ degree had a significant direct effect on knowledge (β = -0.091, P = 0.042), indicating that professional medical postgraduates scored lower on knowledge than their academic counterparts. Knowledge (β = 0.569, P = 0.001) and the type of medical postgraduates degree (β = -0.967, P < 0.001) directly affected attitude. Attitude emerged as a key mediator, directly affecting practice scores (β = 0.655, P < 0.001). Additionally, several other factors demonstrated significant direct effects on practice, including income (β = 0.174, P = 0.029), prior publications (β = 0.499, P = 0.004), participation in research projects (β = 0.418, P = 0.042), and gender (β = -1.010, P < 0.001). Male participants showed notably higher practice scores than female participants. This pattern indicates that attitude may mediate the relationship between knowledge and practice, emphasizing the central role of fostering positive attitudes to enhance research engagement (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study found that academic medical postgraduates exhibited significantly higher KAP scores compared to their professional counterparts. One possible explanation is that academic medical postgraduates typically receive more extensive training in fundamental research methodologies, as their programs are designed to emphasize theoretical knowledge and experimental research skills. This structured approach likely provides them with more opportunities to engage in scientific research and fosters a stronger research-oriented mindset. In contrast, professional medical postgraduates focus more on clinical practice, which may limit their exposure to research activities and reduce the emphasis on cultivating research competencies. Furthermore, academic medical postgraduates are more likely to interact with research-active mentors and peers, creating an environment that encourages collaboration and knowledge sharing, which may further enhance their research engagement. These findings suggest that tailoring educational strategies to the unique needs of professional medical postgraduates is essential. For instance, incorporating research-focused modules into professional programs and offering targeted mentorship for professional medical postgraduates could help bridge the gap in KAP scores. Additionally, designing flexible training programs that balance clinical responsibilities with research activities may enable professional medical postgraduates to develop stronger research capabilities. By addressing these disparities, institutions can ensure that all medical postgraduates, regardless of their program type, are well-prepared for both academic and clinical roles [16].

Recent evidence suggests that research engagement among medical trainees in China remains constrained by limited protected time, insufficient methodological support, and variable mentorship, even when attitudes toward research are generally positive. For example, surveys among resident physicians have reported substantial barriers such as lack of time and guidance that impede translating interest into research practice [10]. Similar patterns have also been observed among undergraduate medical students, where perceived barriers and limited research opportunities contribute to lower participation despite recognition of research value [11]. Compared with these studies, our participants showed relatively high KAP scores, which may reflect differences in the target population (postgraduate trainees in a single university with structured graduate-school support), the KAP instrument and scoring thresholds used, and the timing/context of data collection [10].

Our findings both align with and differ from international studies in several aspects. Similar to studies in Western countries, we found that research experience and mentorship significantly influence research engagement and outcomes [17]. For example, studies from the United States and Europe highlight the importance of structured research training programs and early exposure to research activities in fostering academic productivity. However, the challenges observed among professional medical postgraduates in China, such as balancing clinical responsibilities with research training, reflect unique characteristics of the dual-track education system and may differ from Western educational contexts where research integration is more prominent. Furthermore, gender disparities in research practices, as identified in this study, are less commonly reported in Western studies, emphasizing the need for tailored interventions in China to address these gaps. However, the gender differences we observed in research practice scores present an interesting contrast to some international findings. Our findings about the importance of research attitudes and training echo studies from various countries [12], though the impact of the dual-track system reflects characteristics specific to the Asian medical education context.

In terms of the relationships between knowledge, attitudes, and practices, the mediation analysis demonstrated significant positive correlations between attitude and practice. This suggests that a more positive attitude is associated with higher research practice scores, consistent with prior literature indicating that motivation and positive perspectives toward research may promote engagement [18, 19]. The mediation analysis further demonstrated that while knowledge had a direct effect on attitude, its effect on practice was mediated through attitude. However, given the cross-sectional design, the mediation pathways should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than evidence of causal relationships. In addition, because the CFI and IFI values were below the conventional cutoff for good fit, the SEM findings should be interpreted with caution. This suggests that enhancing knowledge alone may be insufficient to promote active research engagement without concurrently fostering a positive attitude. While this study provides valuable cross-sectional insights into the KAP of medical postgraduates, longitudinal studies are recommended to further explore how these dimensions evolve over time. Tracking changes in knowledge, attitudes, and practices could offer a deeper understanding of the factors influencing sustained research engagement. Such research could also help evaluate the long-term effects of targeted educational interventions and identify critical periods for enhancing research competencies. These findings are consistent with research indicating that academic-oriented programs may provide more opportunities and motivation for engaging in research [20, 21].

Differences in practice scores were also significantly influenced by income and research experience, as demonstrated by mediation analysis. Participants with higher incomes showed significantly higher practice scores, which was supported by the SEM result. This may be due to the greater access to resources and time afforded to higher-income students, allowing them to engage more fully in research activities. Similarly, students who had published papers or participated in research projects demonstrated higher practice scores, with SEM results further confirming these effects. These findings align with other studies showing that prior research involvement significantly enhances future research engagement and productivity [22]. However, no significant differences were found in knowledge or attitude scores between participants with or without prior research experience, suggesting that while experience enhances practical engagement, it may not necessarily improve research knowledge or attitudes.

The overall knowledge of medical postgraduates in this study was satisfactory, with most respondents demonstrating a good understanding of fundamental research concepts such as the distinctions between basic and clinical research, research methodologies, and literature retrieval tools. However, certain areas showed room for improvement, particularly regarding the notion that basic scientific research is not concerned with practical applications, and the misconception that only randomized clinical trials qualify as clinical research. To address these knowledge gaps, targeted educational interventions should be introduced, such as integrating case studies into the curriculum that highlight the practical impact of basic research and expanding discussions on the diversity of clinical research methodologies [23, 24]. These interventions could be implemented in an iterative cycle (pilot modules → learner feedback → refinement) to better align content with trainees’ needs and available time. Online modules or workshops specifically focused on these underperforming areas could be particularly effective, especially when led by experts who can provide real-world examples [24–26].

The attitudes of students towards research were generally positive, with most students recognizing the importance of research for career development and medical education. However, a notable percentage expressed dissatisfaction with the current research resources available at their institutions, and a minority were hesitant about sacrificing personal time for research. To improve student attitudes, institutions should consider increasing the availability of research resources, such as offering more accessible research funding, mentorship programs, and dedicated time slots for research activities [12, 27, 28]. Institutions might consider implementing flexible research schedules or offering academic credit for research activities, which could help mitigate the time constraints faced by students. Specific strategies could include formalizing mentorship programs that pair students with experienced researchers, as well as offering workshops on journal clubs [29].

In addition to the KAP mediation pathways, students’ expressed needs regarding research training provide further context for interpreting the gaps in practice scores. For instance, the high proportion of students reporting difficulties in topic selection (78.05%) and research design (71.63%) may reflect underlying barriers that inhibit proactive research practice, despite generally positive attitudes. Prior studies have similarly shown that a lack of methodological guidance and challenges in topic formulation significantly hinder students’ involvement in research activities [30]. These difficulties could partially explain why practice scores remain lower relative to knowledge and attitude scores, highlighting a bottleneck in translating intention into action. Moreover, the strong preference for blended learning (66.76%) suggests that current research training models may not fully align with students’ learning styles or time constraints. Blended learning approaches have been shown to improve engagement and flexibility in medical education, particularly among learners balancing clinical duties and academic training [31]. The majority of students reported active involvement in research-related activities, including using available resources to enhance their knowledge, and expressed a commitment to engaging in research throughout their careers. However, a substantial portion of students were unsure or hesitant about independently leading research projects or making research a central part of their future work. These results suggest that while students may be interested in research, they may lack the confidence or experience to take on leadership roles. To enhance research practices, institutions should provide more opportunities for students to lead small-scale research projects under supervision, which could gradually build their leadership skills. Additionally, creating structured pathways for students to transition from team-based research to independent projects may encourage more proactive engagement. Offering incentives, such as awards for outstanding research leadership or dedicated grant opportunities for student-led projects, could also motivate greater involvement [32, 33].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of a self-administered questionnaire may introduce response bias, as participants may overestimate their knowledge, attitudes, or practices. In addition, although attention-check items were used to improve data quality, we did not perform a demographic comparison between included and excluded respondents, and selection bias cannot be completely ruled out. Additionally, potential confounding factors, such as variations in mentorship quality, academic resources between departments, and individual research opportunities, were not fully controlled for in this study. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. Finally, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other medical postgraduates in different regions or educational settings. Additionally, the voluntary nature of participation may have led to selection bias, and certain subgroups of medical postgraduates might have been underrepresented, which could affect the representativeness of the sample. Moreover, the use of convenience sampling may reduce the representativeness of the sample and further limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the convenience sampling approach and the single-center setting may reduce representativeness and limit the external validity of the findings.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, medical postgraduates reported relatively high knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive research practices toward scientific research. Degree type was associated with knowledge and attitudes, with professional-track trainees scoring lower than academic-track trainees. Structural equation modeling suggested that knowledge was associated with practice primarily through attitudes, indicating attitudes as a key mediator in the KAP pathway. Practice was also directly associated with income, prior publication, research participation, and gender. These findings highlight the need to support professional-track trainees and to strengthen attitude- and practice-oriented training opportunities alongside methodological instruction.
