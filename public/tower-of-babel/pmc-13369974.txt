Research on the failure mechanism and enhanced energy-absorbing capacity for a novel concave octagonal NPR honeycomb structure
Authors: Chengxing Yang, Yuan Zhong, Yiyang Ma, Yujia Huo, Zhao Nan, Jun Yang, Xifeng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand for lightweight and safe rail transit structures has highlighted negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) honeycombs as promising energy-absorbing architectures due to their superior failure mechanism and crashworthiness. This study introduces a novel graded concave octagonal NPR honeycomb (G-ICOH), evolved from the conventional inner concave hexagonal honeycomb (ICHH), to enhance deformation stability and energy dissipation efficiency. Specimens were fabricated from AlSi10Mg using selective laser melting followed by T6 heat treatment with residual stresses markedly reduced, confirming reliable material properties. Quasi-static compression tests and finite element simulations revealed that G-ICOH develops a distinct “X-shaped” deformation mode accompanied by a strong NPR effect. Compared to ICHH, G-ICOH demonstrates a ~ 50% reduction in initial peak stress and a smoother plateau stress response, leading to more stable energy dissipation and up to a 7.58% improvement in specific energy absorption. A parametric analysis showed that the foundation thickness-to-beam arm ratio significantly affects plateau stress and energy absorption, while the influence of the intermediate beam gradient and concave angle is comparatively minor. These results provide explicit guidelines for parameter optimization. The proposed G-ICOH structure combines NPR effect with enhanced crashworthiness, offering practical potential for buffer devices, impact protection systems, and lightweight energy-absorbing components in rail transit and broader transportation engineering applications.

Introduction

The pursuit of lightweight, high-strength, and energy-efficient structures has become a central challenge in modern transportation engineering. As rail transit systems continue to expand globally, driven by the increasing demand for sustainable, safe, and high-speed mobility, the structural requirements of railway vehicles are becoming increasingly stringent. Key performance indicators such as crashworthiness, vibration control, fatigue resistance, and energy absorption efficiency must be satisfied simultaneously while minimizing overall weight to reduce energy consumption1–4. Traditional engineering materials, such as aluminum alloys and steels, have supported decades of development in lightweight structures, but their inherent limitations in energy absorption efficiency and structural adaptability under dynamic loads have become increasingly evident. As a result, metamaterials—engineered materials with architected microstructures that exhibit unusual macroscopic properties—are emerging as transformative candidates for next-generation transportation solutions5,6.

Among these, negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) materials have garnered particular attention due to their counterintuitive mechanical response: they laterally expand under tensile loading and contract laterally under compressive loading7–9. The research on negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) materials began in the 1980s, when Lakes10 first experimentally demonstrated the existence of the NPR effect using open-cell foam materials, thereby laying the foundation for the development of this field. Unlike conventional positive Poisson’s ratio materials, NPR metamaterials offer unique advantages such as enhanced shear resistance, superior energy absorption efficiency, and more uniform stress diffusion8,11,12. These unusual characteristics arise directly from their internal geometry, enabling NPR structures to redistribute applied stresses more effectively than traditional materials. This distinct deformation mechanism is especially advantageous in safety-critical engineering applications, where energy dissipation, deformation control, and structural stability are vital13–15.

Parallel to the development of NPR metamaterials, honeycomb structures have long been recognized for their high porosity, low density, and tunable topological configurations. They are widely applied in aerospace, automotive, and rail transportation sectors for their lightweight design, impact resistance, and energy absorption capabilities16,17. Honeycombs combine low density with high specific stiffness and strength, making them especially suitable for applications where both lightweighting and crashworthiness are required18–21. When NPR concepts are introduced into honeycomb architectures, the resulting NPR honeycomb structures demonstrate a synergistic combination of two highly desirable features: the energy-absorbing advantages of honeycombs and the unconventional deformation modes of NPR materials (Fig. 1)22,23. Recent studies on modular metamaterials have further highlighted that combining NPR effects with hierarchical honeycomb architectures can provide strong auxeticity, tunable properties, and enhanced crashworthiness24. For instance, Han et al.25 proposed a partially-curved-beam hybrid auxetic (PCBHA) metamaterial that achieves near-ideal crush force efficiency while maintaining high specific energy absorption, effectively addressing the trade-off between peak stress and energy absorption capacity. In a related effort, they introduced a rotating sweep method to transform 2D truss lattices into 3D cylindrical metamaterials, achieving dramatic improvements in both stiffness and energy absorption26. These studies highlight the growing research interest in multi-objective optimization of auxetic metamaterials for impact protection applications.

Lightweight technology, in particular, has become a cornerstone of modern transportation development, driven by both global energy crises and stricter environmental regulations. The energy efficiency of rail vehicles strongly depends on the reduction of structural mass27–30. For example, decreasing vehicle mass directly lowers traction energy consumption and enables higher payload-to-mass ratios. Traditional aluminum alloys, once considered the benchmark lightweight materials, are now approaching their performance limits, particularly in terms of fatigue resistance and crashworthiness. Composite materials, though capable of reducing weight by over 30%, remain constrained by high costs and complex manufacturing processes, limiting their widespread application16,31–33. Consequently, the design of lightweight structures must achieve a delicate balance: ensuring high specific strength and stiffness while simultaneously fulfilling additional requirements such as airtightness, vibration suppression, and fatigue resistance34–36.

Energy absorption during collisions represents another crucial design criterion for rail transit vehicles. In the event of train collisions, controlled deformation is essential to dissipate kinetic energy and protect passenger compartments from catastrophic failure. However, conventional energy-absorbing structures, such as thin-walled metallic tubes, often generate high initial peak forces and undergo unpredictable deformation modes37. These drawbacks compromise safety by transmitting excessive loads to adjacent components and reduce reliability in real-world impact scenarios. Additionally, under dynamic loading conditions such as track-induced vibrations or sudden impacts, energy-absorbing components must combine high absorption efficiency with stable plateau stress responses, ensuring consistent performance across varied loading conditions12,17.

NPR honeycomb structures address many of these challenges by providing superior deformation modes and enhanced crash energy absorption. Mechanically, the NPR effect induces lateral contraction under compressive loading, producing a distinctive “V-shaped” stress distribution that improves compressive strength and load-bearing uniformity4–39. Recent work on gradient octagonal and offset hexagonal honeycombs has confirmed that introducing geometric gradation or asymmetry can substantially improve crashworthiness and energy absorption stability38. Numerical simulations and experimental studies have shown that NPR honeycombs outperform traditional honeycomb architectures in terms of impact resistance, with energy absorption capacities up to twice that of bulk materials13. Their stress-strain curves typically exhibit extended plateau regions, corresponding to more efficient energy dissipation, with efficiency improvements of approximately 20–30% compared to conventional designs4–39. In multifunctional composite designs, NPR honeycombs provide dual advantages: regions of large deformation that absorb energy and NPR regions that act as rigid supports, thereby enabling hierarchical and graded energy dissipation mechanisms40.

Furthermore, lightweighting potential is enhanced by the inherently porous topology of NPR honeycombs. Hierarchical or multi-level NPR honeycombs, often manufactured via additive manufacturing, enable structural tailoring across multiple length scales. Such designs can simultaneously reduce mass and improve energy absorption, with experimental results showing improvements of up to 40% in energy absorption performance compared to conventional honeycombs17,41. From a dynamic perspective, NPR honeycombs exhibit favorable behaviors such as necking and enhanced plateau stress under impact loading, leading to higher dynamic load-bearing capacities41. For example, optimized NPR honeycomb-based underguard components in vehicles achieved a 4.34% reduction in maximum deflection and a 20.27% decrease in absorbed kinetic energy, highlighting the engineering potential of NPR designs42.

The engineering applications of NPR materials in rail transportation extend across several domains. In lightweight car body structures, NPR materials can replace conventional aluminum alloys or carbon fiber composites in sandwich panels and frames. Metallurgically optimized NPR steels, for instance, achieve a favorable balance between strength and ductility, enabling weight reduction without compromising stability43–46. In crash safety applications, NPR honeycomb composites function as core energy-absorbing units within the train nose or coupler sections. Their orderly deformation and uniform energy dissipation reduce peak interface forces and enable more predictable structural responses than traditional energy-absorbing structures17,47–49. Similarly, in vibration and noise control, NPR materials can be employed as isolation layers or damping structures, leveraging their ability to manipulate elastic waves. This significantly reduces the transmission of wheel–rail vibrations and in-cabin noise, enhancing passenger comfort50–54. For highly loaded or impact-prone components such as bogies and chassis, re-entrant hexagonal NPR honeycomb sandwich structures mitigate local buckling and suppress baseplate deformation, leading to improved fatigue resistance and longer service life41,55–57.

Despite these promising advances, current NPR honeycomb designs—especially those based on re-entrant hexagonal configurations—still face limitations in terms of initial peak stress, plateau stress stability, and parameter optimization for rail transit applications. Traditional inner concave hexagonal honeycomb (ICHH) structures, while offering negative Poisson’s ratio effects, often suffer from sharp stress peaks at the onset of deformation and irregular plateau regions during compression. These issues limit their energy dissipation stability and overall crashworthiness. Moreover, the parametric influences of structural variables, such as foundation thickness, beam arm length, gradient, and concave angle, remain insufficiently understood, constraining rational design and optimization of NPR honeycombs for practical engineering applications58–60.

To address these gaps, this paper proposes a novel graded concave octagonal negative Poisson’s ratio honeycomb (G-ICOH), developed by extending the conventional concave hexagonal honeycomb geometry. The G-ICOH design introduces octagonal concave units and a graded configuration, enabling more ordered deformation modes and improved plateau stress responses. By integrating theoretical modeling, additive manufacturing experiments, and finite element simulations, the compressive load-bearing performance and energy absorption mechanisms of G-ICOH are systematically investigated. Furthermore, a comprehensive parametric study is performed to evaluate the influence of geometric parameters on energy absorption and plateau stress behavior.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 presents the structural design of G-ICOH, including material preparation and characterization. Section 3 details the quasi-static compression experiments and finite element modeling methods. Section 4 explores the parametric effects on compressive performance and energy absorption. Finally, the findings are summarized to provide both theoretical insights and engineering guidelines for applying NPR honeycomb structures in rail transit and other transportation engineering domains.

Design, sample preparation and material properties of negative Poisson’s ratio honeycomb structures

This section presents the design, modeling, and preparation of a novel negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) honeycomb structure. Building upon the conventional concave hexagonal configuration, a concave octagonal topology with gradient thickness variation is proposed to enhance mechanical performance. A geometric model of the new structure is established, and its relative density is analytically derived to provide a quantitative foundation for subsequent performance evaluation.

To validate the feasibility of manufacturing and ensure accurate characterization, the proposed structures were fabricated using selective laser melting (SLM) technology with AlSi10Mg alloy as the base material. In order to minimize residual stresses and optimize mechanical properties, a T6 heat treatment process was applied to the printed specimens. The tensile properties of the alloy were experimentally verified, establishing reliable input data for the following quasi-static compression experiments and finite element simulations.

This integrated approach—from structural design to material verification—lays the groundwork for systematically investigating the load-bearing capacity and energy absorption mechanisms of the proposed gradient concave octagonal honeycomb structure.

Structural design and model establishment

Negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) structures can be realized through several typical topological configurations, including concave triangular, concave hexagonal, honeycomb hexagonal, and chiral geometries (Fig. 2)61–67. Among these, extensive verification has shown that the concave hexagonal structure demonstrates superior energy absorption capacity compared with other configurations, making it a suitable foundation for further development.

Building upon this concept, a novel concave octagonal negative Poisson’s ratio honeycomb with gradient design is proposed in this study, hereafter referred to as G-ICOH (Inner Concave Octagon Honeycomb Structure with Gradient design). The G-ICOH design introduces two key modifications to the conventional concave hexagonal configuration. First, additional concave angles are incorporated along the upper and lower edges of each unit cell, transforming the geometry into an octagonal configuration. Second, variable thicknesses are assigned to both the base edges and the connecting beams within each unit cell. This gradient design not only improves structural tunability but also enhances the overall energy absorption characteristics under compressive loading.

To construct the complete honeycomb, individual G-ICOH unit cells are replicated and connected in an array, resulting in a periodic mesh model of the entire structure (Fig. 3). The gradient-thickness distribution within the concave octagonal units provides a hierarchical load transfer mechanism, enabling more controlled and stable deformation compared with traditional NPR designs. Furthermore, by extending the G-ICOH arrangement along both the x- and z-directions, a three-dimensional honeycomb grid structure can be generated, as illustrated in Fig. 4. This arrangement ensures geometric continuity and mechanical isotropy in the plane, providing a reliable basis for subsequent experimental and numerical investigations.

Sample preparation

Selective Laser Melting (SLM) is an advanced additive manufacturing technology widely used for fabricating complex metal structures with high precision and mechanical integrity66–69. The SLM process involves several key steps. First, a three-dimensional model is constructed using computer-aided design (CAD) software and then converted into layered two-dimensional cross-sectional data through slicing software. A uniform layer of metal powder is subsequently deposited onto the build platform by a recoater (Fig. 6), providing the foundation for selective melting72. A high-power laser then scans the powder bed along a predefined path, selectively melting regions according to the cross-sectional geometry of the model. To prevent oxidation during processing, the build chamber is maintained under an inert gas atmosphere, typically argon73. After each layer is consolidated, the build platform lowers by a predetermined height (generally 1.1–1.2 times the powder layer thickness), enabling deposition of a new powder layer for subsequent melting. Through repeated scanning and solidification, the structure is gradually built layer by layer. The rapid cooling rates inherent in SLM (on the order of 10³–10⁴ K/s) strongly influence the microstructure and, consequently, the mechanical properties of the printed components74–76. Compared with other metal additive manufacturing techniques, SLM offers superior resolution, mechanical performance, and structural design flexibility, making it highly suitable for fabricating lightweight architected structures such as honeycombs.

In this study, the test specimens were fabricated using an iSLM280 system. All dog-bone tensile specimens and honeycomb samples investigated in this study were physically fabricated and experimentally tested, rather than being simulation-only models. The processing parameters were optimized to ensure structural integrity and dimensional accuracy. Specifically, the infill scanning speed was set to 1600 mm/s, the contour scanning speed to 600 mm/s, and the support structure scanning speed to 2000 mm/s. Corresponding laser powers were 420 W for infill regions, 310 W for contours, and 340 W for supports. A linear infill strategy was adopted with a hatch spacing of 0.16 mm and a scanning rotation angle of 67° between layers to mitigate thermal accumulation and reduce residual stresses. The powder layer thickness was fixed at approximately 0.05 mm, and the laser spot diameter was maintained at ~ 70 μm, ensuring stable interlayer bonding and consistent forming quality. Representative samples of the baseline ICHH and the proposed G-ICOH structures are shown in Fig. 7.

Due to the high cooling rates and steep thermal gradients inherent in SLM, significant residual stresses can develop within the printed components. These stresses may deteriorate both tensile and compressive performance if not properly mitigated. To address this issue, a T6 heat treatment protocol was applied to all printed specimens, including dog-bone samples, ICHH, and G-ICOH structures, following the approach of Xu et al.77. The treatment involved solutionizing at 500 °C for 2 h, followed by water quenching (at 60–100 °C), and subsequent artificial aging at 160 °C for 6 h before air cooling. This process effectively relieved excessive internal residual stresses generated during fabrication and improved the overall mechanical stability of the samples76–81.

Material properties

The base material employed for fabricating the honeycomb energy-absorbing structures was AlSi10Mg alloy, a widely used additive manufacturing alloy known for its favorable strength-to-weight ratio and excellent processability80,85–87. To characterize its mechanical behavior, uniaxial tensile tests were performed in accordance with the Chinese standard GB/T 228.1. Dog-bone specimens were fabricated using the same SLM process and subjected to testing on a WEW-600D electro-hydraulic servo universal testing machine, as shown in Fig. 8a. The tests were conducted under displacement control at a loading rate of 0.2 kN/s.

During the experiments, the applied load was continuously recorded by an integrated data acquisition system with a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. The averaged stress-strain response of the AlSi10Mg specimens is presented in Fig. 8(b). It should be noted that, due to the absence of an extensometer, the elastic modulus cannot be accurately determined from this curve. However, the yield strength and ultimate tensile strength, which are governed by the plastic regime, are reliably captured. The alloy exhibited an initial yield strength of approximately 150 MPa, followed by mild strain hardening, and ultimately fractured at a tensile strength of 210 MPa. The corresponding mechanical parameters, including yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elastic modulus, are summarized in Table 1. The elastic modulus reported in Table 1 (72 GPa) is taken from the literature on SLM-fabricated AlSi10Mg with T6 heat treatment77.

For energy absorption applications, the mechanical response is governed by large plastic deformation rather than elastic behavior because enhancement of energy absorption is attributed to the formation of multiple plastic hinges during crushing88. Additionally, the elastic modulus of SLM + T6 heat-treated AlSi10Mg is primarily governed by the α-Al matrix, as the manufacturing and heat treatment processes do not introduce new phases with sufficient volume fraction to alter the bulk stiffness89,90. Overall, this combination of measured plastic properties and typical elastic properties from the literature provides a basis for evaluating the mechanical performance of the proposed structures in the following finite element model.

Compression test and finite element modelling of NPR honeycomb structures

This section presents the structural design, sample preparation, and material characterization of the proposed honeycomb structure. A systematic investigation is conducted on the deformation modes and energy absorption behavior of the gradient concave octagonal honeycomb (G-ICOH) through quasi-static compression experiments and finite element simulations. Based on the experimental data, a high-fidelity LS-DYNA finite element model is established, followed by a mesh sensitivity analysis to ensure computational reliability. Furthermore, key performance indicators, including force-displacement response, Poisson’s ratio, and specific energy absorption, are quantitatively compared between experimental and simulation results, thereby validating the compressive load-bearing capacity and energy absorption characteristics of the G-ICOH.

Quasi-static compression test

Quasi-static compression tests were conducted on both the ICHH and G-ICOH structures using a WEW-600D electro-hydraulic servo universal testing machine, as illustrated in Fig. 9. The testing machine provides a maximum load capacity of 600 kN and a maximum stroke of 1000 mm, enabling reliable evaluation of structural performance. During testing, each specimen was placed on the lower platen of the machine, and compression was applied in displacement-controlled mode at a constant loading rate of 5 mm/min.

A data acquisition system automatically recorded the applied load and corresponding displacement at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz, while the real-time load response was displayed on the computer interface. To prevent specimen overloading, compression was terminated once a sharp load increase was detected. The time–displacement–load data were subsequently extracted for analysis. To ensure reproducibility and minimize experimental error, each test was repeated three times, and the average values were taken as the final results.

The quasi-static compression sequences of the ICHH and G-ICOH structures are presented in Fig. 10, illustrating the deformation process prior to densification. When the axial displacement reached approximately 7.5 mm, both structures initiated notable bending deformation in the central layer, leading to an emerging X-shaped configuration. At 15 mm displacement, the central cells exhibited near-complete collapse, while the upper and lower layers simultaneously began bending. Further compression to 22.5 mm caused intensified compaction, reduced intercellular gaps, and a more pronounced X-shaped morphology. At 30 mm displacement, both structures reached a densified state, characterized by significant axial and transverse contraction. Interlocking of adjacent cells became evident, clearly demonstrating the negative Poisson’s ratio effect.

Throughout the entire process, the honeycomb structures exhibited a progressive, orderly, and controllable X-shaped deformation mode, validating the rationality of the proposed structural design and confirming its capacity for stable energy dissipation.

Finite element model

The quasi-static compression finite element model was established using the explicit finite element software LS-DYNA, as illustrated in Fig. 11(a). The G-ICOH structure was rigidly fixed to the bottom wall, while the upper moving rigid wall applied compression along the Z-direction. The geometrical dimensions of the finite element model were kept identical to those of the experimental specimens (see Table 2), ensuring consistency between experimental and numerical results. The G-ICOH structure was discretized using hexahedral solid elements. The material behavior was modeled using the MAT_PIECEWISE_LINEAR_PLASTICITY material model, which is particularly suitable for simulating the nonlinear mechanical behavior of metallic alloys under large deformations. No explicit damage or fracture criteria were included, as the structure deforms through progressive plastic buckling without material separation under quasi-static loading91,92. Strain rate effects were not considered in this model neither, as the simulation aims to replicate quasi-static conditions. The material properties used are listed in Table 3. Contact interactions were defined using the CONTACT_AUTOMATIC _SINGLE_SURFACE algorithm, with a friction coefficient of 0.293. To represent the boundary conditions of the experimental setup, two rigid walls were defined: a stationary fixed rigid wall, simulating the lower platen of the testing machine, and a moving rigid wall, representing the upper platen. To ensure that this elevated loading velocity did not introduce significant inertial effects, the ratio of internal energy to total energy (IE/(IE + KE)) was monitored throughout the simulation and remained above 95%—a criterion widely adopted and validated 94,95. To ensure that the loading velocity does not affect the quasi-static response, simulations at 1 m/s and 10 m/s were performed for the G-ICOH structure96. The corresponding stress-strain curves are shown in Fig. 11(b). The two curves are almost identical, indicating that the structural response is independent of the loading velocity. Therefore, to balance computational efficiency and accuracy, a loading velocity of 10 m/s is adopted for all subsequent simulations.

Grid validation

To assess the influence of mesh size on simulation outcomes, four mesh conditions were evaluated with element sizes of 0.5 mm, 1.0 mm, 1.5 mm, and 2.0 mm. For all cases, the model geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and loading conditions were kept strictly identical. The specific energy absorption (SEA) results obtained from the simulations under different mesh sizes were compared, with particular emphasis on accuracy and computation time.

Effect of mesh size on computing time and SEA of the G-ICOH structure under quasi-static compression. The corresponding results are presented in Fig. 12. When using relatively coarse meshes (e.g., 2.0 mm), the predicted energy absorption values deviate substantially from those obtained with finer meshes, reflecting insufficient resolution of local deformation mechanisms. As the mesh size decreases, the accuracy of the results improves, and the computed SEA values converge toward stable values. Notably, when the mesh size reaches 1.0 mm, the variation in SEA becomes negligible, demonstrating that the mesh resolution is sufficient to accurately capture the deformation and energy absorption mechanisms of the G-ICOH structure. Further refinement to 0.5 mm provides only marginal improvements in accuracy, while significantly increasing computational cost due to exponentially longer runtimes. Hence, a mesh size of 1.0 mm was selected for subsequent simulations. This choice achieves a favorable balance between computational efficiency and accuracy, ensuring that the energy absorption characteristics are reliably captured without unnecessary increases in computational expense.

Validation of the FE model

To validate the FE model, the simulation results are compared with the experimental results. The force-displacement curves and energy absorption efficiency-strain curves from the experimental tests and simulations are shown in Fig. 13, respectively. The trends of the curves from the experiment and simulation are consistent and show a good agreement, and the overall failure modes observed in the experiment are also highly consistent with those predicted by the simulation.

Table 4 summarizes the variation ranges of the performance indicators. The absolute errors of Poisson’s ratio, plateau stress, densification strain, and specific energy absorption between the experimental tests and the simulation results are 3.85%, -10.26%, 8.82%, and − 1.92%, respectively. These errors may be attributed to inherent manufacturing imperfections in the SLM process, such as micro-porosity and surface roughness, as well as idealized assumptions in the FE model regarding perfect geometry and boundary conditions [88]. Overall, all performance indicator errors are below or around 10%, indicating that the FE model can effectively simulate and validate the structural performance. The good agreement between simulation and experiment at 10 m/s further confirms that the loading velocity is appropriate for quasi-static characterization.

Parameter study

Crashworthiness performance is highly sensitive to geometric parameters for NPR honeycombs. By systematically modifying these parameters, a wide range of mechanical behaviors can be tailored. In this section, a parametric study is conducted using the validated FE model, while maintaining identical simulation conditions. The investigated parameters include beam arm length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{1},{L}_{3}$$\end{document}), base thickness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{0}$$\end{document}), middle beam gradient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{t}_{1}}{{t}_{0}}$$\end{document}), upper and lower beam gradients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{t}_{2}}{{t}_{0}}$$\end{document}), and inward angles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{1},{\theta\:}_{2}$$\end{document}). All other parameters are kept consistent with those of the G-ICOH configuration described in Sect. 4.1.

A controlled variable approach is adopted to isolate the influence of each parameter on structural performance. The corresponding design variables and parameter domains are summarized in Table 7. For reference, the baseline configuration of G-ICOH is designated as Group 0, serving as a benchmark for comparative analysis.

As shown in Figs. 20a–c, the deformation patterns under different concave angles remain generally similar, following an “X”-shaped collapse mode. Initially, before the strain reaches 0.1, structures with smaller concave angles tend to enter the densification phase more slowly. However, once the strain exceeds 0.2, their deformation behaviors converge, with the smaller-angle structures entering the overall densification stage slightly earlier. From Fig. 20d, it can be observed that for all configurations, stress increases rapidly with strain before entering a plateau stress stage, which represents the main energy absorption phase. At larger strains, stress rises sharply again as the structure approaches densification. Comparative analysis of the performance indicators (Table 17) reveals that decreasing the concave angles reduces the densification strain of the structure, while the plateau stress and specific energy absorption exhibit no consistent trend. The overlap of stress–strain and energy absorption efficiency curves across different angle conditions further demonstrates that the concave angle exerts only a minor influence on the overall energy absorption performance of the G-ICOH.

A more comprehensive investigation, including potential interactions with other parameters (e.g., beam length), is noted as a direction for future work.

Conclusions

This study proposes a novel graded concave octagonal negative Poisson’s ratio honeycomb structure (G-ICOH) fabricated via additive manufacturing for lightweight and energy-absorbing applications in rail transit. Its compressive behavior and energy absorption mechanisms were investigated through experiments and finite element simulations. The main findings are as follows: The G-ICOH was developed by introducing an indented octagonal configuration with a gradient thickness design. Compared with the traditional indented hexagonal NPR honeycomb (ICHH), G-ICOH exhibits a 7.58% improvement in specific energy absorption, reaching 3119.64 J/kg. Moreover, the initial peak stress is reduced by approximately 50%, and the plateau stress phase is extended, thereby achieving more stable and efficient energy absorption. A high-fidelity finite element model was established, demonstrating strong agreement with experimental stress–strain curves. Key performance indicators, including Poisson’s ratio, plateau stress, densification strain, and specific energy absorption, showed errors below or around 10%, confirming the accuracy and reliability of the model. Under quasi-static compression, G-ICOH exhibited an ordered “X”-shaped deformation mode accompanied by a distinct negative Poisson’s ratio effect, revealing its progressive failure mechanism. The effects of key structural parameters on energy absorption performance were investigated. Results indicate that variations in base thickness strongly affect energy absorption: thicker bases increase plateau stress and enhance energy absorption before densification. Beam arm length also influences performance, with 17 mm demonstrating superior specific energy absorption compared with 15 mm and 19 mm. In contrast, the gradient of the central beam and the concave angles have only minor influences on performance.
