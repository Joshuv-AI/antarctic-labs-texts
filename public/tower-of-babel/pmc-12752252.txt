Geographic variation in functional compounds and multi-element of Lithocarpus litseifolius: insights from chemical profiling and ecological drivers
Authors: Ping Yu, Feng Xiong, Xinwei Yan, Haodi Sun, Junhui Zhou, Ruibin Bai, Jian Yang, Lanping Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithocarpus litseifolius (Hance) Chun (LL), is a high-value edible and medicinal plant, prized for its unique flavor, health benefits and a vital role in increasing farmers’ incomes. It is extensively cultivated in multiple provinces in China, with prices and qualities variations regulated by local ecological conditions. This study investigated 63 chemical components, including 16 functional compounds and 47 multi-element, alongside 64 ecological factors to assess quality differences and regional characteristics of LL from 5 origins. LL from Yunnan province exhibited superior quality, characterized by higher flavonoid content. Furthermore, 23 key differential components were identified using VIP (Variable importance in projection) features, serving as the most important variables for discrimination and quality control. Moreover, structural equation modeling (SEM) indicated the dominantly positive effects of altitude on the major sweetness component phlorizin, whereas longitude, mean temperature of the warmest month, annual moisture index, and absolute maximum temperature had negative effects on phlorizin.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07761-7.Keywords: Lithocarpus litseifolius (Hance) Chun, Chemical components, Ecological factors, Phlorizin, Structural equation modeling

Introduction

Lithocarpus litseifolius (Hance) Chun (LL), the latest popular natural tea beverage in China, is a member of the Fagaceae family and is praised as the “Cordyceps sinensis on a tree” due to its unique trifunctional properties as a tea substitute, natural sweetener, and medicinal resource [1]. The leaves of LL have a naturally sweet flavor when brewed, producing a beverage known as sweet tea, which has historical roots dating back to the Southern Dynasty (423 AD) [2]. Modern studies have revealed that LL is rich in various functional components and nutrients, including flavonoid, triterpenoid, and essential minerals, with dihydrochalcones being the most widely used and representative components [3]. Dihydrochalcones, particularly phlorizin, serve as the principal natural sweetening agents. Notably, phlorizin can reach concentrations of up to 9.04% or even higher, and the quality of LL is currently graded as either superior or inferior based on its content. This compound not only imparts a sweetness 300 times greater than sucrose with negligible caloric impact but also demonstrates significant health-promoting effects, including hypoglycemic, antioxidant, as well as anti-inflammatory activities, making it especially important for obese and diabetic patients [4, 5]. Combining distinctive taste with health benefits properties, LL offers diverse value that enhances its appeal and commercial significance, leading to its popularity in many cities across southern China. Recognized as a novel food ingredient by the Chinese National Health Commission in 2017, LL has broad application prospects in the food, medicine, and chemical industries and has gained increasing attention in recent years for its significant role in increasing farmers’ incomes. [6]. In China, LL is cultivated across multiple provinces, including Hunan, Yunnan, Guizhou, and Jiangxi [7]. Regional differences in environmental conditions and agricultural practices lead to significant variations in LL traits, resulting in inconsistent product quality. In addition, rising market demand has caused pronounced price fluctuations and the mixing of raw material sources. Currently, there are no standardized indicators for evaluating LL quality, which hampers consistent production and management, raises consumer concerns regarding safety and authenticity, and limits market trust and sustainable cultivation [8]. Previous studies have mainly focused on the material composition, pharmacological properties, and extraction methods of LL, with few addressing comprehensive comparisons of quality differences across regions [9, 10]. Therefore, it is essential to investigate regional variations in LL chemical components, identify potential high-quality production areas, and determine key differential compounds for effective origin discrimination, informed cultivation, and quality control.

Moreover, during plant growth, local ecological conditions, including humidity, temperature, precipitation, and soil, significantly influence the synthesis of active ingredient, resulting in specific ecotypes that have evolved over time, as confirmed by numerous studies [11]. For instance, factors such as light intensity, growing medium, and temperature impact the levels of lycopene and vitamin C in tomatoes, leading to variations in the nutritional value of harvested fruits [12]. Additionally, soil environments with high salinity, as well as elevated levels of Mg, Mn, and K, enhance the monoterpene content of Citrus reticulata ‘Chachi’ by promoting the expression of salt stress-responsive genes and terpene backbone synthase [13]. Variations in plant quality typically arise from the interplay of multiple ecological drivers and their regulation across spatial and temporal scales. Therefore, conducting in-depth analyses of these simultaneous occurrences and compound impacts is crucial for understanding the ecological factors that influence the synthesis and accumulation of active ingredient. In recent years, structural equation modeling (SEM) has gained prominence as a powerful research tool, which enables researchers to interpret complex systems and quantify both direct and indirect causal relationships among multiple variables [14]. Liao et al. utilized SEM to explore the regulatory pathways impacting polymeric insoluble (PAs) in Camellia drupifera cv.‘HR’. Their findings revealed that the Moco-MCP1-GLOX1 module significantly and directly influenced PAs levels, while polyphenol oxidases (PPOs) had an indirect effect on the final PAs output through the mean degree of polymerization (mDP) [15]. Similarly, Duan et al. examined the SEM linking vegetation and engineering measures, and discovered that differences in plant functional traits on the Loess Plateau arise from varying plant responses to diverse soil properties and community structures [16]. Additionally, Djuhari et al. identified key latent variables that directly affect red chili production, including geography (specifically elevation), soil organic matter, and anthracnose disease [17]. Meanwhile, to the best of our knowledge, the mechanisms of multi-ecological factors interactions and their differential regulation of LL specific dihydrochalcones (e.g., phlorizin) remain unclear. Therefore, there is an urgent need to investigate the pathways through which ecological factors regulate quality components and assess both direct and indirect impacts, which is crucial for developing and implementing standardized cultivation and management practices to ensure high quality and uniformity of LL.

Hence, the main purposes of this study were to 1) comprehensively characterize the chemical properties, including functional compounds and multi-element, of LL from different origins and clarify the potential high-quality production areas and key differential components; 2) comprehensively analyze the ecological characteristics in various LL production areas to elucidate the differences; 3) explore the relationship between the key differential components and the ecological factors, and 4), based on part 3), to apply a structural equation modeling for the first time to explore how ecological factors regulate the synthesis and accumulation of phlorizin, the major sweetness component.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and climatic data origin

A sum of 200 batches of leaf samples and their corresponding soil samples were gathered from the 5 major production regions that producing LL (Fig. 1, Tab. S1), including Ji’an City in Jiangxi Province (JXJA, n = 58), Tongren City in Guizhou Province (GZTR, n = 34), Zhijiang Dong Autonomous County in Huaihua City, Hunan Province (HNZJ, n = 55), Xupu County in Huaihua City, Hunan Province (HNXP, n = 33), and Wenshan City in Yunnan Province (YNWS, n = 20), with all LL leaves cultivated and harvested under a unified standard. To ensure sample comparability, leaves were collected from the south-facing side of each plant, specifically within the upper to middle third of the canopy. Leaf age was standardized by touch, selecting those that had just matured from the tender stage (less than approximately three months old). At each site, the five-point sampling method was used. Soil samples were taken from the root zone with a wooden spade and gently separated by hand. All plant and soil samples were immediately labeled, kept on ice, and transported to the laboratory for further analysis. Samples were identified as Lithocarpus litseifolius (Hance) Chun (LL) by Researcher Jian Yang (National Resource Center for Chinese Materia Medica), and voucher specimens were deposited in the National Resource Center for Chinese Materia Medica, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). The voucher specimen numbers are as follows: 360800LY0829 (JXJA), 532621LY0779 (YNWS), 431228LY0155 (HNZJ), 431224LY0441 (HNXP), and 522201LY0365 (GZTR). Subsequently, the fresh samples were washed and dried in a drying oven at 50 °C for 36 h. The dried samples were then ground into fine powder with particle sizes ranging from 30 to 40 mesh, transferred to centrifuge tubes, and stored in a desiccator until further analysis. For the soil samples, the initial step involved removing sand and debris from the fresh soil. They were then dried in a cool environment, crushed, ground, and sieved through a 30-mesh screen. All samples were kept in airtight containers in a cool, dark, and dry place until analysis began.

Geographic sites of LL samples from different origins

The bioclimate factors for the 5 production areas were obtained from the Science Data Bank and extracted based on GPS locations, as detailed in Tab. S1 [18]. In total, nine climatic factors were considered: GP (growing season precipitation, mm), GDD0 (accumulated temperature of growing degree days above 0 ◦C, ◦C), GDD5 (accumulated temperature of growing degree days above 5 ◦C, ◦C), MTCO (mean temperature of the coldest month, ◦C), MTWA (mean temperature of the warmest month, ◦C), DI (annual drought index), MI (annual moisture index), Tmax (absolute maximum temperature, ◦C), and Tmin (absolute minimum temperature, ◦C). Furthermore, latitude (Lat), longitude (Lon), and altitude (ALT) of the sampling sites, were obtained using a mobile GPS device.

Soil physicochemical data origin

The soil pH (pH) was determined using the Glass electrode method (NY/T 1121.2–2006); The total nitrogen (TN) content was measured using the Kjeldahl method (HJ 717–2014); Total phosphorus (TP) was analyzed according to the method for determination of soil total phosphorus (NY/T 88–1988); Total potassium (TK) was determined following the method for determination of total potassium in soils (HJ 889–2017); The cation exchange capacity (CEC) was measured using the hexamminecobalt trichloride solution-spectrophotometric method (HJ 889–2017).

Reagents and instruments

Flavonoid reference materials, including phlorizin (Phz), trilobatin (Trb), 3-hydroxy phlorizin (Hpz_3), phloretin 2'-O-glucoside (Gpt_2), 3''-O-acetyl phloridzin (Oapz_3), 2''-O-acetyl phloridzin (Oapz_2), phloretin (Pht), guavinoside (Gua), catechin (Cac), isorhamnetin-3-O-β-D-glucoside (Ois_3), luteolin 7-glucoside (Lut), diosmetin-7-O-β-D-glucopyranoside (Dio), tiliroside (Til), astragalin (Ast), quercitrin (Que), and esculin (Esc), were purchased from Beiterenkang Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Methanol, acetonitrile, and formic acid (all LC–MS grade) were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Massachusetts, USA). Ultrapure water was purchased from Watsons in local market (Beijing, China).

Multi-element standard solutions Ⅰ (Al, As, B, Ba, Be, Bi, Cd, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ga, Li, Mg, Mn, Ni, Pb, Sb, Sn, Sr, Ti, Tl, V, Zn), multi-element standard solutions Ⅱ (La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Y), single-element standard solutions (Zr, Cs, Rb, Ge, In, Na, Ca, K, Sc, Nb, Hf, Ta, Th, Mo, Se, Sb), and internal standard solutions (Sc, Rh) were purchased from the National Centre for Analysis and Testing of Nonferrous Metals and Electronic Materials. Nitric acid, hydrofluoric acid, and hydrochloric acid (all GR grade) were obtained from ANPEL Laboratory Technologies Inc. (Shanghai, China), while ultrapure water was sourced from Watsons in the local market (Shenzhen, China).

Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (ACQUITY UPLC™ I-Class) and triple quadrupole-linear ion trap composite mass spectrometer (QTRAP 6500 system) were obtained from Waters (Massachusetts, USA) and AB SCIEX (Massachusetts, USA), respectively. ICAP-Q combined with a plasma mass spectrometer (ICP–MS) was purchased from Thermo Scientific (California, USA).

UPLC-QQQ-MS analysis of LL

Each 0.02 g sample of LL powder was extracted with 1.5 mL of 80% methanol, followed by ultrasonic treatment for 40 min (300W, 40 kHz) and centrifugation for 10 min at 13,000 rpm. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane before being injected into the liquid vial.

The liquid phase conditions were as follows: an ACQUITY UPLC™ BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) was used at 40 °C, with an injection volume of 1.0 μL and a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (B). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.0 ~ 1.0 min, 5% ~ 25% A; 1.0 ~ 3.5 min, 25% ~ 40% A; 3.5 ~ 4.5 min, 40% ~ 60% A; 4.5 ~ 5.0 min, 60% ~ 5% A; 5.0 ~ 7.0 min, 5% A.

Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed using API 6500 system (AB SCIEX, Los Angeles, CA, USA) equipped with an electrostatic ionization (ESI) source (AB SCIEX). MS analysis for flavonoid was carried out in negative ionization mode, and the operating conditions were set as follows: ion source voltage, −5500 V (ESI-); turbo spray temperature (TEM), 550 ◦C; Curtain Gas (CUR) flow, 30 L/min; Ion Source Gas (IS) flow, 50 L/min; scanning mode: Scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (Scheduled MRM). The MS parameters for flavonoid were manually optimized (Tab. S2). Data acquisition was carried out using Analyst Software 1.6.2 (AB SCIEX, Los Angeles, CA, USA), and analysis was performed using MultiQuant Software 3.0 (AB SCIEX, Los Angeles, CA, USA).

Multi-element analysis of LL

A total of 0.10 g of both the leaves and their corresponding soil powder from LL were accurately weighed and placed in a Teflon vessel. To this, 6.0 mL of 67% HNO₃ was added. The samples were then subjected to a pre-dissolution step at 120 °C for 30 min, after which they were digested using the Mars 5 microwave digestion system (CEM Corp, USA). For the leaf samples, the microwave was set to a power of 1200 W, with the temperature raised to 120 °C over 10 min and held for 2 min. It was then increased to 150 °C within 10 min and maintained for another 2 min. The temperature was subsequently raised to 180 °C over 10 min and held for 2 min before being increased to 200 °C within 10 min, where it remained stable for 20 min. In contrast, the digestion of soil samples also utilized 1200 W, but the temperature increase to 120 °C took 15 min, followed by similar steps to reach 200 °C, where it was held for 30 min. After the digestion process was complete, the final volume of each sample was adjusted to 25 mL with 67% HNO₃ and filtered through a syringe filter with a 0.45 μm pore size. Approximately 0.5 mL of the filtered solution was then transferred to a 25 mL graduated volumetric flask, thoroughly mixed, allowed to stand, and prepared for measurement.

The instrumental parameters were set as follows: high-frequency plasma power was maintained at 1550 W, with high-purity argon (99.999%) used as the carrier gas. The flow rates for the atomization gas and makeup gas were 1.05 L/min and 0.15 L/min, respectively. The sampling depth was adjusted to 8.0 mm, and the peristaltic pump operated at a rate of 0.3 r/min.

Data analysis and model evaluation

The data analysis and figure production were performed using R (version 4.4.1).

Significant differences among geographical origins were assessed using the Welch test and Games–Howell post hoc test via the ‘onewaytests’ and ‘rstatix’ packages, which are appropriate for data with unequal variances and sample sizes [19]. The coefficient of variation (CV) for ecological factors across different origins was calculated as the ratio of standard deviation to mean and visualized using ‘fmsb’ package. Principal component analysis (PCA), performed with ‘tidyverse’ and ‘factoextra’ packages, served as an exploratory tool to visualize and evaluate variations in chemical and ecological characteristics across samples from different origins. Following this, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was executed using ‘mixOmics’ package to identify key differential components that significantly differentiate origins, focusing on variables with VIP (Variable importance in projection) values exceeding 1 [20]. To ensure robust modeling despite unequal sample sizes, the optimal number of components was selected via cross-validation, and Q2 values were computed to evaluate predictive performance. To investigate the relationship between climatic factors and soil characteristics, the Mantel test and partial Mantel test were applied using ‘vegan’ package. Furthermore, the specific Spearman correlations between key differential components and ecological factors were calculated and visualized with ‘LinkET’ package. Finally, the ‘ComplexHeatmap’, ‘circlize’, and ‘paletteer’ packages were utilized for conducting Spearman correlation analysis.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to investigate the direct and indirect relationships between ecological factors and Phz, the major sweetness component of LL. Key ecological factors were initially screened using random forest (the top 20 based on importance ranking) and Spearman correlation analyses (p < 0.01, |r|> 0.4). Prior to dimensionality reduction, multicollinearity among the selected ecological variables was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and variables with VIF > 10 were excluded. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to summarize the remaining variables into orthogonal components, which were then used as independent variables in the SEM. In the initial conceptual model, we hypothesized that 1) Each type of ecological factors and multi-element in LL leaves directly influences Phz accumulation; and 2) Each type of ecological factors indirectly affect Phz accumulation by influencing multi-element in LL leaves [8, 21, 22]. We also considered the impact of climatic factors on soil factors. We first considered a full model that included all reasonable pathways, and then we iteratively refined it by eliminating non-significant pathways and adding those with significant contributions, with the aim of minimizing the difference between the observed and estimated population covariance matrices. Model estimation was achieved based on the maximum likelihood method, and the fitness statistics included p-value (Chi-square), comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). The SEM-related analysis was performed using the ‘lavaan’ R package.

Additionally, various visualizations were created using different R packages, including cluster heatmaps with ‘ComplexHeatmap’ and ‘circlize’, correlation networks with ‘igraph’, ‘ggraph’, ‘ggplot2’, and ‘tidygraph’, as well as boxplots and heatmaps using ‘ggplot2’ and ‘ComplexHeatmap’. To illustrate the sampling site locations, the packages ‘cowplot’, ‘maptools’, ‘ggplot2’, ‘ggspatial’, ‘sf’, ‘here’, and ‘tidyverse’ were employed. Furthermore, ‘raster’ and ‘rgdal’ packages were utilized to extract climatic factors from raster data for the respective sites.

Results

Statistical analysis of chemical characteristic in LL

The functional compounds features of LL from different origins

In the current study, 16 flavonoid functional compounds were examined, including Phz, Trb, Hpz_3, Oapz_3, Gua, Pht, Gpt_2, Oapz_2, Cac, Ois_3, Lut, Dio, Til, Ast, Que, and Esc. Additionally, 47 multi-element were analyzed, such as Mg, K, Ca, Mn, Fe, Zn, Mo, B, P, S, Na, Ti, V, Co, Se, Cu, Cr, Ga, Sb, As, Cd, Pb, Ag, Bi, La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Yb, Y, Tm, Li, Be, Ba, Sr, Cs, Rb, Al, and Tl. The histograms illustrating the distribution of functional compounds and multi-element in LL leaves are provided in Fig. S1and Fig. S2, respectively.

The values of the 16 functional compounds in LL varied significantly among samples from different geographic origins across five major production areas (p < 0.01). The distribution of exact data is illustrated in Fig. 2A and Tab. S3. Notably, the mean contents of Gua (208.5 ± 128.0 μg/g), Cac (590.3 ± 225.2 μg/g), Oapz_3 (2877.4 ± 899.6 μg/g), and Oapz_2 (4921.9 ± 1158.7 μg/g) were highest in samples from HNXP. In contrast, Lut, Esc, Hpz_3, and Ois_3 reached their peak levels in JXJA, with respective averages of 111.7 ± 77.15 μg/g, 49.41 ± 16.00 μg/g, 5626.3 ± 1847.4 μg/g, and 9.583 ± 6.608 μg/g. Additionally, Dio (16.35 ± 14.86 μg/g) and Pht (291.5 ± 43.4 μg/g) exhibited the highest concentrations in samples from YNWS, while the lowest levels were observed in samples from GZTR. Conversely, Gpt_2 was significantly elevated in LL from YNWS (1227.3 ± 299.3 μg/g) and GZTR (946.0 ± 175.5 μg/g). The compounds Trb, Til, Ast, and Que were more concentrated in samples from HNXP, with average contents of 20,410.7 ± 17,638.0 μg/g, 39.81 ± 15.58 μg/g, 681.2 ± 207.2 μg/g, and 430.7 ± 182.4 μg/g, respectively. Among the 16 flavonoid components analyzed, Phz showed the highest concentrations across all production areas, ranging from 77,960.0 μg/g to 135,166.6 μg/g. The average Phz values from the five major production areas were ranked as follows: YNWS > HNZJ > GZTR > HNXP > JXJA. Overall, the present study observed significant differences in the content of flavonoid functional compounds among samples from various production areas, with LL from YNWS demonstrating superior quality particularly due to its highest levels of the major sweetness component phlorizin.

The multi-element features of LL from different origins

As shown in Fig. S2, Fig. 2B and Tab. S4, multi-element features of LL among the five geographical origins were significantly different, except for Mg and Zn (p < 0.05). Notably, in terms of regional distribution, 47 multi-element of LL from eastern China (JXJA) were significantly higher than those from western China, such as YNWS and GZTR.

In this study, essential elements (EE) for plants were identified as Mg, K, Ca, Mn, Fe, Zn, Mo, B, P, and S, while beneficial elements (BE) included Na, Ti, V, Co, Se, and Ni. The contents of Ca, K, and Mg were elevated across all five production areas. Specifically, Ca levels in LL from YNWS (9591.0 ± 7645.5 mg/kg) and GZTR (7538.8 ± 3214.9 mg/kg) were significantly higher than those from other three production areas (JXJA, HNXP, and HNZJ). The highest concentrations of K (10,818.0 ± 2523.0 mg/kg) and Mg (1622.4 ± 527.5 mg/kg) were recorded in samples from JXJA. Other than this, ten elements, such as Co, Ti, V, Se, Mg, K, Mn, Fe, B, and S, showed the highest accumulation in JXJA. Conversely, YNWS accumulated the least amount of most elements, except for Zn and B, with its Mn content being only about one-seventh that of JXJA.

Totally 9 heavy metal elements (HE) were analyzed in this study. Cu content of LL belonging to 5 major production areas were the highest and in the range of 7.214 ~ 11.890 mg/kg. Additionally, Cr and Pb levels in LL from these areas were relatively high, falling between 0.700 and 3.856 mg/kg. In contrast, the contents of Ga, Sb, As, Cd, Ag and Bi were lower, ranging from 0.011 to 0.617 mg/kg. Most elements were found in higher concentrations in samples from JXJA, whereas samples from YNWS exhibited the lowest levels. The average levels of HE identified in this study were well below the limits set by Chinese Ministry of Agriculture Standards, indicating that the LL presents no risk of heavy metal exceeding and is considered a safe food ingredient [23].

A total of 14 rare earth elements (RE) were tested in this study, and all showed the highest concentrations in samples from JXJA. In the current study, except for La and Tm, the RE patterns in LL are very similar among different origins, that is, the accumulation of RE in LL from different origins could be ranked as: JXJA > HNZJ > GZTR > HNXP > YNWS. Other elements (OE) such as Li, Be, Ba, Sr, Cs, Rb, Al and Tl were also detected, as shown in Fig. 2B.

Variations in quality of LL from different origins

The PCA plot revealed differences in chemical components profiles of LL from various origins (Fig. 2C). The first two axis accounted for 43.87% of the total variation among the including 16 functional compounds and 47 multi-element. LL from YNWS and JXJA displayed greater dispersion, represented by larger ellipses, while LL from GZTR, HNXP, and HNZJ were closely clustered with smaller confidence interval ellipses, showing some overlap. This clustering may be attributed to the similar chemical characteristics of these adjacent geographic regions. Overall, the samples in the PCA score plot did not exhibit a clear separation trend. A further supervised PLS-DA was thus applied, yielding R2X = 0.676, R2Y = 0.711, and Q2 = 0.625, with permutation tests (200 iterations) showing pQ2 and pR2Y values below 0.05, indicating a robust model with good predictive ability and no overfitting. As shown in Fig. 2D, clear separation was observed among LL samples from different geographical regions, and the first two axes explained 30.80% and 5.70% of the variance, respectively. In particular, samples from YNWS and JXJA were distinctly differentiated from those of the other regions. A total of 23 key differential components selected by VIP > 1 are plotted in Fig. 2E, comprising 11 functional compounds (Phz, Hpz_3, Oapz_3, Gua, Pht, Gpt_2, Oapz_2, Cac, Lut, Ast, and Esc) and 12 multi-element (Se, K, Fe, P, S, Mo, Ca, Rb, Ba, Sr, Be, and Sb). The top 5 variables with VIP values exceeding 1.2 are ranked as follows: Oapz_2 > Oapz_3 > Rb > Phz > Gpt_2, with four of these being dihydrochalcones, highlighting their significant contribution to the model’s overall variance and discriminative power.

Statistical analysis of ecological factors in LL production areas

In the current study, 12 climatic factors were examined, including GP, GDD0, GDD5, MTCO, MTWA, DI, MI, Tmax, and Tmin, Lon, Lat, and ALT. Additionally, 52 soil factors were analyzed, which included 5 soil physicochemical properties (pH, CEC, TP, TK, TN) and 47 soil multi-element (Mg, K, Ca, Mn, Fe, Zn, Mo, B, P, S, Na, Ti, V, Co, Se, Cu, Cr, Ga, Sb, As, Cd, Pb, Ag, Bi, La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Yb, Y, Tm, Li, Be, Ba, Sr, Cs, Rb, Al, and Tl). The histograms illustrating the distribution of soil multi-element in LL production areas are provided in Fig. S3.

The climatic features of LL production areas

From a geographical distribution perspective, the study encompasses a longitudinal span exceeding 9° (Tab. S1), reflecting the widespread distribution of LL in provinces south of the Qinling Mountains in China. In contrast, the sample origins are concentrated within a narrow latitude range of 23.65°N to 27.55°N, all situated in the low-latitude region of the Northern Hemisphere. Among the climatic factors studied, altitude (ALT) exhibited the most pronounced effect, with a CV of 55.70% (Fig. 3A). The sampling sites for LL covered a broad range of ALT (Tab. S1, Fig. 3C). Specifically, YNWS recorded the highest ALT at 1312.8 m, while JXJA had the lowest at 174.4 m. In comparison, GZTR, HNZJ, and HNXP fell into the mid-ALT category, ranging from 602.5 to 836.5 m.

Furthermore, the degree of variability in MTCO was relatively high, exceeding 45%. Conversely, the variation in other factors was comparatively low. The climatic characteristics of LL production regions are highly diverse, particularly in the southwestern region of YNWS, which differs markedly from those in the eastern region, such as HNZJ. In YNWS, MTCO, Tmin, and GP were higher, while MTWA and Tmax were the lowest (Tab. S1, Fig. 3C). Conversely, JXJA exhibited the highest GDD0, GDD5, MTWA, and Tmax, alongside the lowest Tmin (Table S1, Fig. 3C). JXJA also received the highest GP but had the lowest DI. The climatic characteristics of the three main production areas in central China (GZTR, HNXP, HNZJ) are relatively similar, though there are minor differences. Among them, HNXP has lower Tmin, MTCO, Tmax, GDD0, and GDD5, but higher MI, indicating sufficient precipitation and a cooler mean annual temperature. Both HNZJ and GZTR, situated further west, exhibited moderate values across all climatic indicators. Overall, the climatic characteristics of LL origins are diverse; therefore, these climatic factors are crucial and should not be overlooked in the study of LL quality.

The soil physicochemical properties and multi-element features of LL production areas

Further, a comprehensive analysis was conducted on the planting soil of LL, encompassing a total of 47 multi-element and 5 physicochemical properties, as outlined in Fig. 3, Tab. S1, and Tab. S5. The results revealed significant differences in the content of various elements in LL cultivation soils from different origins (p < 0.01). Of these, the content of one heavy metal elements, Cd, varied significantly from 0.690 to 7.416 mg/kg, exhibiting the highest CV (136.4%) compared to other soil factors (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the coefficients of variation for Ca, Rb, Sr, and Ho were all greater than 100.00%, indicating that the content of these factors varies substantially across the different production regions.

Among the 5 production areas, Mn, B, and Zn were the top 3 of the most accumulated elements, with mean concentrations ranging from 944. 8 mg/kg (GZTR) to 6549.8 mg/kg (YNWS), 1300.5 mg/kg (YNWS) to 2801.1 mg/kg (HNZJ) and 576. 9 mg/kg (GZTR) to 1110.5 mg/kg (HNZJ) respectively, followed by V, S, Cr, Pb, As, Li, Ni and Ga (Fig. 3C, Tab. S5). Conversely, the content of other elements remained below 100 mg/kg, with Ag, Tm, Tb and Tl exhibiting the lowest content (< 1.0 mg/kg). Elements such as V, S, Cr, Cd and Ni were most abundant in YNWS. Notably, YNWS also possessed the highest concentrations of all analyzed RE except Dy. In contrast, soil samples from HNZJ and HNXP, both within the same province, exhibited lower natural abundances of RE. The JXJA production area had higher concentrations of Pb, Li, and Ga, while GZTR had elevated levels of As, Dy, and Be. Soils across all LL production areas were generally acidic, with pH values ranging from 4.97 to 5.79. Among five production areas, the soils in YNWS exhibited the highest levels of TN, TP, TK, and CEC (Fig. 3C, Tab. S1). In contrast, soils in JXJA had the lowest CEC and TN, while those in HNXP showed the lowest TP and TK levels.

Variations and correlations among ecological factors of LL production areas

The PCA results for all ecological factors, including climatic and soil characteristics, demonstrated that different LL production areas exhibit distinct ecological traits (Fig. 3D). The first two axis explained 37.14% and 19.97% of the total variance and LL from different origins were clustered to a group. As seen, samples from YNWS and JXJA could be independently clustered, setting them apart from other origins due to their unique ecological characteristics. While LL samples from HNZJ, HNXP, and GZTR were tightly clustered with notable overlap, represented by small ellipses, due to their similar ecological characteristics. These results were consistent with previous statistical analysis, further reinforcing the significant ecological differences among LL production areas.

To investigate the relationships between soil factors, climatic distance, and geographic distance, a Mantel test was conducted. The Spearman correlation coefficients for 52 soil factor dissimilarity in relation to geographic and climatic distances were 0.43 and 0.11, respectively, while the partial correlation coefficients were 0.45 and −0.19 (Fig. 3F). These results suggest a stronger overall correlation between soil characteristics and geographic distance, while the correlation with climatic distance is relatively weaker. This implies that variations in soil characteristics are primarily influenced by geographic distance, with climatic distance acting as a covariate response. Based on this, the relationships between soil factors and climatic factors were explored in detail. As shown in Fig. 3E, the soil multi-element were significantly correlated with all of the climatic factors (p < 0.01), among which the correlation coefficient ranged from 0.07 to 0.52, and the most significant correlations observed for altitude (ALT, r = 0.52), mean temperature of the warmest month (MTWA, r = 0.49), longitude (Lon, r = 0.47), followed by latitude (Lat, r = 0.41), absolute maximum temperature (Tmax, r = 0.39), and mean temperature of the coldest month (MTCO, r = 0.33). The soil physicochemical properties were significantly correlated with all of the climatic factors (p < 0.01), and the most correlated factor was altitude (ALT, r = 0.33). Additionally, significant correlations were found among climatic factors. Among them, ALT exhibited the strongest correlation with MTWA, with a coefficient of −0.962 (p < 0.001), and showed significant negative correlations with all climatic factors except the absolute minimum temperature (Tmin) and annual drought index (DI). Both Lon and Lat demonstrated strong negative correlations with DI, with coefficients of −0.83 and −0.86 (p < 0.001), respectively.

Construction of structural equation models

The hypothesized model for the data is illustrated in Fig. 5. The overall model p-value was 0.717, with fit indices including a CFI of 1.000, RMSEA of 0.000, and SRMR of 0.012, indicating an exceptional fit to the data [24, 25]. The SEM results revealed that the model accounted for 45.2% of the variance in Phz levels, demonstrating a significant correlation with these factors. Specifically, Phz levels were predominantly influenced by ENV1 (MTWA, Tmax, and MI) and ENV2 (-ALT, and Lon), with standardized direct effects of −0.644 and −0.529, respectively (p < 0.001). Thus, the variations observed in the major sweetness component, Phz, can primarily be attributed to changes in geographical location and interannual climatic factors, particularly temperature and moisture. Additionally, PE1 and SOIL1 are both RE, yet they have contrasting effects on Phz accumulation: PE1 exerts a direct positive effect (0.51), while SOIL1 has a negative effect (−0.38). The inhibitory effects of SOIL2 (-pH, TN, TK, and CEC) directly suppress Phz accumulation, with a standardized direct effect of −0.31. Moreover, all SOIL and ENV can indirectly influence Phz accumulation through PE1, and ENV also regulates soil characteristics.

Discussion

Lithocarpus litseifolius (LL), a multifunctional plant widely consumed as sweet tea in China, shows quality and price variations across regions due to ecological differences. However, lacking unified quality evaluation standards hampers its standardized production. This study decodes ecological patterns in LL’s functional compounds and multi-element profiles to provide scientific zoning and precise cultivation strategies, enabling effective quality control and enhancement.

In this study, we first conducted a comparative analysis of 63 chemical components, including 16 functional compounds and 47 multi-element, to assess quality differences in LL across regions. It is noteworthy that the content of most functional compounds in LL from the western origin YNWS was significantly higher than that of the other four origins, particularly Phz, which is the primary sweet flavor component. In contrast, LL from GZTR displayed lower levels of most compounds, despite the fact that the two origins were in neighboring provinces. This discrepancy may be attributed to various ecological factors, including topography, soil composition, and climatic conditions [26, 27]. GZTR is characterized by mountainous terrain and a fragile ecological environment, largely due to the extensive development of karst landscapes. In contrast, YNWS benefits from a subtropical monsoon climate that is warm and humid, creating favorable conditions for tea cultivation. This contrast underscores that microclimate and soil factors may outweigh geographic proximity in shaping LL quality.

JXJA samples showed almost the highest accumulation of all multi-element, while YNWS samples exhibited the lowest (except Ca, and Zn). The elevated multi-element levels in JXJA (eastern China) correlate with its alluvial soils and intensive agriculture, enhancing metal bioavailability. Interestingly, YNWS has the lowest concentration of RE, despite the soil in this production area being rich in these minerals. This discrepancy may be due to the fact that RE are primarily present in an insoluble state in the soil, resulting in low availability. Additionally, strong soil leaching leads to substantial nutrient losses, but also reduces the presence of heavy metals, minimizing the risk of heavy metal contamination in LL. LL demonstrates selective bioaccumulation, absorbing beneficial nutrients through its roots while either excluding or taking up minimal amounts of heavy metals. Moreover, RE profiles across different regions show high similarity. This similarity may be due to the comparable chemical and physical properties of REEs, which often allow them to be interchangeable [28].

Among the 23 key differential identified, the top five with VIP scores above 1.2 were primarily dihydrochalcones. This indicates that these compounds play a major role in explaining overall variance and distinguishing between different sample groups within the model. Meanwhile, the relative intensities of the 23 key differential components varied significantly among LL samples from different production areas (p < 0.01), emphasizing their potential as crucial indicators of food authenticity and quality control. Moreover, extensive studies have shown that secondary metabolite synthesis during plant growth is largely influenced by external environmental factors such as light intensity, temperature variations, soil composition, and water availability [29, 30]. The identification of specific functional compounds and multi-element in this study further emphasizes the biochemical diversity present in LL samples, while the influence of external ecological factors on the chemical characteristic of these samples still requires further exploration.

Our results reveal substantial heterogeneity in both climatic and soil conditions across the major LL production areas in China. This diversity has significant implications for understanding LL growth environments and potential influences on quality. The pronounced climatic differences are strongly linked to geography and topography. The distinct climate of YNWS, characterized by higher MTCO and Tmin but lower MTWA and Tmax, aligns with its location on the southeastern Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau. This region experiences a subtropical plateau climate, known for its small seasonal temperature amplitude and warm, humid conditions [31]. In contrast, JXJA’s climate profile—marked by the highest MTWA, Tmax, GDD0, GDD5, and GP, alongside the lowest Tmin and DI—reflects its lower altitude and flatter topography. This makes it more susceptible to monsoon influences, leading to hot, rainy summers, cold winters, and large annual temperature fluctuations. The relatively similar but subtly different climates of the central production areas (GZTR, HNXP, HNZJ) can be attributed to their shared latitudinal band but varying topography (e.g., the more complex, hilly terrain of HNXP vs. the flatter HNZJ) and longitudinal position (western sites HNZJ/GZTR vs. more eastern HNXP).

The soil factors of LL cultivation soils also varied dramatically. The exceptionally high variability in elements like Cd, Ca, Rb, Sr, and Ho underscores the profound influence of local geology. The elevated levels of V, S, Cr, Cd, Ni, and most RE in YNWS soils are likely a consequence of the region's karst landscape development, involving intense rock weathering and dissolution. Furthermore, YNWS's unique position in the geotectonic transition zone between the South China Fold System and the Yangtze Paraplatform provides a distinct geochemical background and ore-forming environment conducive to RE enrichment. Conversely, the dominance of granite and slate in the geological substrates of HNZJ and HNXP likely explains their lower REE abundances. The specific elemental signatures of JXJA (high Pb, Li, Ga) and GZTR (high As, Dy, Be) further highlight the strong local lithological control on soil chemistry. The observed soil acidity (low pH) across all sites is characteristic of southern China's tropical and subtropical regions. High temperatures and humidity drive intense weathering and leaching processes, preferentially removing base cations (e.g., Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, K⁺, Na⁺) and promoting soil acidification. The significant disparities in soil fertility indicators (TN, TP, TK, CEC) among production areas can be attributed to changes in topographical conditions and human activities [32, 33]. YNWS's higher TN, TP, TK, and CEC align with the prevalence of paddy soils and brown loams, which typically have greater organic matter content and nutrient retention capacity.

Mantel's test showed that geographic distance dominated soil variability, with climate as a covariate, suggesting that local lithology controlled soil formation processes and that matrices differences determined regional soil chemistry. Elevation had a dual role as the strongest predictor of soil elements (r = 0.52) and properties (r = 0.33), which influenced nutrient effectiveness through temperature and drainage. Strong negative correlation of latitude/longitude with DI (r < −0.83) highlights the role of coastal location in regulating moisture. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that climatic factors and rare earth elements were strongly associated with most of the key differential compounds. The chemical composition of plants is significantly influenced by local climate conditions, including altitude, temperature, and precipitation [34]. For example, high altitude can enhance flavonoid accumulation in Cyclocarya paliurus, likely due to changes in the transport and relocation of flavonoid within the leaves [35]. Additionally, climate change-induced stress events, such as heat and drought, trigger plant signaling mechanisms. These include the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide (NO), and endogenous hormones, which activate hormone-responsive factors and downstream transcription factors (TFs). This process enhances the regulation of structural genes involved in secondary metabolic pathways for phenolics, terpenoids, and alkaloids [34]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that RE can significantly influence plant growth and development by regulating resistance, physiological and biochemical responses, as well as mineral uptake and photosynthesis [36]. Applying appropriate amounts of RE and their complexes can enhance plant resilience to adversity by inducing cellular defense mechanisms and improving biomass through increased photosynthetic rates [36, 37].

ALT is the most important driver of Phz accumulation (Fig. 5), implying that the higher altitude, the higher contents of Phz. The unique climatic conditions of high-altitude tea cultivation often play a crucial role in the accumulation of flavonoid and the enhancement of antioxidant capacity, thereby affecting the flavor and quality of LL [38]. ALT gradients increase light intensity and diffuse light, particularly in the blue and violet wavelengths, which boosts plant photosynthesis and growth. For example, high-altitude environments significantly enhance the accumulation of flavonoids and polyphenols in grape leaves and skins, primarily through increased UV-B exposure. Key metabolic enzymes, including chalcone isomerase (CHI), transketolase, and 3-deoxy-D-arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate synthase (DHS), play central roles in these pathways, contributing to enhanced antioxidant capacity [39]. Similarly, studies on faba bean varieties at different altitudes have shown that critical flavonoid biosynthesis genes, such as CHI and chalcone synthase (CHS), are more highly expressed in high-altitude cultivars, with CHS exhibiting the largest differential expression [40]. Additionally, ALT serves as a photobiological signal that regulates secondary metabolism [41, 42]. The differences in longitude among various production areas are significant. YNWS, located in western China, sits on a plateau with minimal monsoon influence, resulting in relatively low temperatures. Its climate is mild and stable, with a minimum mean annual temperature of 21.85 °C and a maximum temperature of 34.70 °C, both of which are the lowest recorded values. CHS and CHI are crucial genes in Phz synthesis. Research indicates that low temperatures can upregulate their expression, enhancing both the synthesis and accumulation of Phz [43, 44]. Future research on the CHS gene family in LL is expected to clarify the molecular mechanisms driving its high flavonoid accumulation under high-altitude and low-temperature conditions. YNWS experiences low precipitation and humidity, reflected in its moisture index (MI) of 0.70. Numerous studies have demonstrated that flavonoid content significantly increases under drought stress, as seen in blueberries and citrus species [45, 46]. This aligns with our findings, which revealed a significant negative correlation between Phz levels and MI in the LL production area. Drought stress may promote the upregulation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes, leading to increased synthesis and accumulation of flavonoid.

The seemingly contradictory effects of PE1 and SOIL1 on Phz accumulation can be attributed to the dual roles of RE—promoting Phz synthesis at low concentrations and inhibiting it at high concentrations—alongside the complexity of the multi-layered regulatory networks within the soil–plant system [47]. In LL production areas, the concentration of RE in the soil is significantly higher than in the leaves. At low concentrations, these elements can act as “hormonal” signals (e.g., La3⁺ mimicking Ca2⁺ signals), promoting the expression of specific genes. However, at high concentrations, they may induce oxidative stress, which inhibits metabolic pathways. Furthermore, RE in the soil may exist in insoluble or bound forms, creating physical or chemical barriers to plant uptake, leading to lower effective concentrations within the plant [48]. This is evident in the distribution patterns of RE in the soil of YNWS compared to LL leaves. The RE that enter the plant are in bioavailable forms, filtered through selective root absorption or transport protein regulation, and can directly participate in metabolism.

Moreover, the differing distributions of RE between soils and plant tissues may be linked to specialized RE transport systems in LL that involve transport proteins and chelation processes. For example, in the xylem of Phytolacca americana L., significant heavy RE preferentially form complexes with citrate and malate, facilitating their translocation to aerial tissues [49]. Similarly, in Dicranopteris linearis, the REE transporter REE transporter NRAMP REE Transporter 1 (NREET1) selectively transports REE3⁺ ions, showing higher affinity for light RE [50]. Besides, SOIL2 is a negative driver of Phz accumulation. This suppression can be linked to nutrient allocation, where resources are primarily directed toward growth-related metabolism, such as protein and nucleic acid synthesis, rather than the production of defensive secondary metabolites like Phz, particularly during nutrient surplus periods [51]. High CEC can adsorb significant amounts of cations (such as Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, and Fe3⁺) and alter pH levels, potentially reducing the availability of these trace elements [52]. Lower pH levels can promote Phz generation, likely because LL prefers acidic soils, which support its growth and metabolism. Notably, despite the unfavorable distribution patterns of RE and soil nutrients in the YNWS production area for the synthesis of Phz, the overall effect remains positive. This is due to the dominant influence of climatic factors, which may contribute to elevated Phz levels. This underscores that the synthesis and accumulation of the major sweetness component Phz is driven by a dynamic response network composed of various ecological factors, characterized by multi-factor coupling, spatial and temporal variability, and hierarchical regulation.

Although this study considered multiple indicators, the scope of observation was still limited. Other potential factors, including genetic background, soil microbial communities, and management practices, may also influence the accumulation of plant secondary metabolites. For example, transcription factors such as HvMYB-1, HvMYB-2, and HvNAC-1 regulate structural genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis in Hordeum vulgare L., whereas the abundance of Rhodoplanes in the rhizosphere of Glycyrrhiza uralensis shows a positive correlation with the accumulation of liquiritin and glycyrrhizic acid [53, 54]. Future studies should integrate genetic, microbial, and environmental factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying secondary metabolite accumulation in plants.

Conclusions

In this study, 63 chemical components (comprising 16 functional compounds and 47 multi-element) and 64 ecological factors (comprising 12 climatic factors and 52 soil factors) were explored comparatively to determine quality differences in LL. Firstly, the variability of these variables among 5 geographical origins was described to clarify their features. PCA results suggested significant differences in both chemical and ecological characteristics among origins, with LL from YNWS demonstrating superior quality, exhibiting the highest levels of Phz and other flavonoid. According to PLS-DA, a total of 23 key differential components were identified, including Phz, Hpz_3, Oapz_3, Gua, Pht, Gpt_2, Oapz_2, Cac, Lut, Ast, Esc, Se, K, Fe, P, S, Mo, Ca, Rb, Ba, Sr, Be, and Sb (VIP > 1). These components were further examined for their correlations with ecological factors, revealing significant correlations ranging from −0.74 to 0.68. Moreover, SEM results indicated that the major sweetness component Phz was predominantly promoted by ALT, while being inhibited by Lon, MTWA, Tmax, and MI. This study provides scientifically sound zoning and precise anthropogenic cultivation control measures for effective quality control and enhancement of LL, facilitating its large-scale cultivation and applications.
