Abstract
Img2LaTeX is a practically significant task that involves converting mathematical expressions or tabular data from images into LaTeX code. In recent years, vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated strong performance across a variety of visual understanding tasks, owing to their generalization capabilities. While some studies have explored the use of VLMs for the Img2LaTeX task, their performance often falls short of expectations. Empirically, VLMs sometimes struggle with fine-grained visual elements, leading to inaccurate LaTeX predictions. To address this challenge, we propose A 2 ​ R 2 A^{2}R^{2} : A dvancing Img2LaTeX Conversion via Visual R easoning with A ttention-Guided R efinement, a framework that effectively integrates attention localization and iterative refinement within a visual reasoning framework, enabling VLMs to perform self-correction and progressively improve prediction quality. For effective evaluation, we introduce a new dataset, Img2LaTex-Hard-1K , consisting of 1,100 carefully curated and challenging examples designed to rigorously evaluate the capabilities of VLMs within this task domain. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that: (1) A 2 ​ R 2 A^{2}R^{2} significantly improves model performance across six evaluation metrics spanning both textual and visual levels, consistently outperforming other baseline methods; (2) Increasing the number of inference rounds yields notable performance gains, underscoring the potential of A 2 ​ R 2 A^{2}R^{2} in test-time scaling scenarios; (3) Ablation studies and human evaluations validate the practical effectiveness of our approach, as well as the strong synergy among its core components during inference.

Recently, vision-language models (VLMs) have exhibited strong potential in multimodal understanding, particularly in tasks involving image-text interactions

)
. One possible explanation is the direct evaluation of VLMs, which may overlook the potential benefits of leveraging their visual reasoning capabilities.

3
Img2Latex-Hard-1K

The Im2LaTeX-100k dataset introduced by
Deng et al. 2017
serves as a foundational benchmark for LaTeX recognition research. However, our preliminary evaluation reveals that state-of-the-art vision-language models (VLMs) achieve over 90% accuracy on approximately 75% of the dataset instances, indicating that a substantial portion of the data no longer offers sufficient discriminative power for rigorous model assessment. This performance saturation limits the capacity to accurately characterize model capabilities and comparative strengths in challenging scenarios.

To address this limitation and facilitate more discriminative evaluation of current VLMs, we introduce
Img2LaTex-Hard-1K
, a carefully curated subset specifically designed to stress-test contemporary models. The curation process integrates quantitative performance-based filtering with qualitative assessments of visual complexity, ensuring the selection of instances that expose fundamental weaknesses in mathematical reasoning and fine-grained visual understanding. The resulting dataset fulfills two key objectives: providing a more rigorous benchmark for method comparison and enabling the identification of specific failure modes that can inform future research directions.

3.1
Model Generation

Our filtering methodology necessitates comprehensive evaluation across diverse architectural paradigms and model scales. To this end, we select three representative open-weight VLMs that collectively span the current performance spectrum:

•

Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct

(
Bai et al. 2025
)
: Representing the
Qwen2.5
family, these models enable scale comparisons within a consistent architectural framework (7B vs. 32B parameters).

•

Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct

(
Meta AI 2024
)
: Drawn from the
Llama3.2
family, this model introduces architectural diversity and serves as a cross-family validation point.

This selection strategy ensures that our difficulty assessment captures performance variations arising from both architectural differences and parameter scaling effects, thereby mitigating potential biases toward any single model design philosophy.

We then conduct inference using each model on approximately 7,000 instances from our dataset. For every prediction, we compute three evaluation metrics:
m-ROUGE
,
BLEU-4
, and
Edit Distance
. Consequently, each instance is associated with nine evaluation scores, corresponding to the three metrics across the three models, enabling a comprehensive analysis of model behavior.

Let each instance in the dataset be denoted as
x_{i}
, where
i=1,2,\dots,N
. We evaluate each instance using three models,
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
, corresponding to the aforementioned models. For each model
M_{j}
(
j=1,2,3
), we compute the following metrics:

•

R_{ij}
: the
m-ROUGE
score for instance
x_{i}
under model
M_{j}
, normalized to the range
[0,1]
,

•

B_{ij}
: the
BLEU-4
score for instance
x_{i}
under model
M_{j}
, also in range
[0,1]
,

•

D_{ij}
: the raw
Edit Distance
for instance
x_{i}
under model
M_{j}
, requiring normalization.

To ensure metric comparability, we apply min-max normalization to the edit distance values:

\tilde{D}_{ij}=\frac{D_{ij}-\min(D)}{\max(D)-\min(D)}

where
\min(D)
and
\max(D)
denote the minimum and maximum edit distances across all instances and all models.

Next, we define a composite score
S_{ij}
for each instance-model pair as a weighted average of the three metrics, treating the normalized inverse edit distance
(1-\tilde{D}_{ij})
as a positive score:

S_{ij}=\alpha\cdot R_{ij}+\beta\cdot B_{ij}+\gamma\cdot(1-\tilde{D}_{ij})

with weights
\alpha=0.4
,
\beta=0.4
, and
\gamma=0.2
, such that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1
. This formulation yields a unified measure of model performance that balances lexical similarity and character-level accuracy.

3.2
GPT Evaluation

While textual metrics capture semantic similarity, they may overlook visual rendering similarities that influence practical usability. For instance, two LaTeX expressions might be semantically different yet produce visually similar renderings due to variations in model generation patterns. To account for this visual dimension, we employ
GPT-4o-mini
as an image-level comparator, selected for its strong visual understanding capabilities and cost-effectiveness in large-scale evaluation.

For each instance
x_{i}
, we generate three distinct LaTeX code predictions
L_{ij}
from the three models
M_{j}
(
j=1,2,3
). Each LaTeX string is compiled into an image
I_{ij}^{\text{gen}}
using rendering tools such as
pdflatex
and
imagemagick
. These generated images are then compared against the ground-truth image
I_{i}^{\text{gt}}
to assess their visual fidelity.

To evaluate reproduction accuracy, we employ the
GPT-4o-mini
model as an image comparator
(
OpenAI 2024
)
. For each pair
(I_{i}^{\text{gt}},I_{ij}^{\text{gen}})
, we prompt
GPT-4o-mini
to assign a score
G_{ij}\in[0,10]
, reflecting how faithfully the generated LaTeX output reproduces the visual content of the original image.

To align this visual fidelity score with other evaluation metrics, which lie within the range
[0,1]
, we apply a simple linear normalization:

\tilde{G}_{ij}=\frac{G_{ij}}{10}

where
\tilde{G}_{ij}\in[0,1]
denotes the normalized visual reproduction score for instance
x_{i}
under model
M_{j}
. This normalization facilitates fair integration of visual fidelity into the unified evaluation framework.

After obtaining four evaluation scores for each model’s output on each input image, we assign weights to the models based on their parameter scales and empirical capabilities. Specifically, we set the model weights as follows:

Following the metric computation and visual fidelity evaluation described above, we define the final model-specific score
S_{ij}^{\text{final}}
for instance

under model

as:

S_{ij}^{\text{final}}=S_{ij}+0.5\cdot\tilde{G}_{ij}

where

is the composite metric score and
\tilde{G}_{ij}

We rank all instances by

in descending order and select the top 1,100 examples to construct the

4
Methodology

Traditional vision-language models (VLMs) often struggle with fine-grained visual details in mathematical expressions, resulting in subtle yet critical errors in LaTeX generation. Our
A^{2}R^{2}
framework introduces an iterative visual reasoning approach: by rendering initial predictions and comparing them to input images, the model identifies and corrects its own errors through attention-guided localization and targeted refinement. The
A^{2}R^{2}
framework consists of five stages: (1) Generation, (2) Rendering, (3) Comparison, (4) Attention Localization and Feedback Verification, and (5) Refinement. We introduce each stage in detail:

4.1
Generation

In the initial stage, we employ a vision-language model
VLM
to produce the LaTeX output. Given an input image
I
and a generation prompt
P_{\text{generation}}
, the model is prompted to analyze the image content and generate the corresponding LaTeX prediction. This stage denotes as:

L=VLM(I,P_{\text{generation}})

where
L
is the LaTeX output predicted by the model.

4.2
Rendering

Once the initial LaTeX output is generated, we use tools such as
pdflatex
in conjunction with
imagemagick
to render the LaTeX code
L
into an image
I^{\prime}
. Note that for the first round of inference, the input LaTeX code comes from step (1); for later inference rounds, the input code comes from step (5).

4.3
Comparison

After rendering, this rendered image is then paired with the original input image
I
, and both are fed back into the model. The model acts as a difference evaluator, identifying discrepancies between the original and generated images. This comparison process is defined as:

D=VLM(I,I^{\prime},P_{comparison})

where
P_{\text{comparison}}
denotes the designed prompt that guides the model to identify and describe differences between
I
and
I^{\prime}
in a better structured format, which facilitates efficient extraction and analysis of the discrepancies for subsequent steps. Then,
D
denotes the feedback generated by the model, which serves as input for verification in subsequent steps.

4.4
Attention Localization and Feedback Verification

After receiving discrepancies between the original image
I
and the rendered version
I^{\prime}
, we apply an attention-based localization mechanism to identify high-attention crucial regions. These regions are presumed to correspond to semantic or structural mismatches. The goal is to crop two focused subregions both from
I
and
I^{\prime}
for refined verification.

Suppose the textual prompt fed to the model is a sequence of
\bm{\tau}=\bigl(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{n}\bigr)
containing
n
tokens that describe the mathematical content to be verified.

For each token
\tau_{i}
, we extract attention weights from a selected range of cross-attention layers, specifically from layer
l_{\text{start}}
to
l_{\text{end}}
, inclusive. Assume each of these layers contains
H_{\text{head}}
attention heads. The attention map for token
\tau_{i}
at layer
l
and head
h
is denoted as
W^{(l,h)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
.

We first average across all heads and all selected layers to compute a unified attention map for token
\tau_{i}
:

\tilde{W}_{i}=\frac{1}{(l_{\text{end}}-l_{\text{start}}+1)\cdot H_{\text{head}}}\sum_{l=l_{\text{start}}}^{l_{\text{end}}}\sum_{h=1}^{H_{\text{head}}}W^{(l,h)}_{i}

Next, we average over all
n
tokens to obtain the final attention matrix:

A=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{W}_{i}\quad and\quad A_{u,v}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{w}_{i,(u,v)},\;u=1,\dots,H,\;v=1,\dots,W

This results in
A\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
, where
H
and
W
denote the spatial dimensions (in patch units) of the image feature map and each element reflects the average attention over the selected layers, heads, and tokens, emphasizing how the model aligns the prompt with different image regions.

To prepare this matrix for contour detection, we first normalize the attention values into an 8-bit grayscale image:

A_{\text{norm}}=255\cdot\frac{A-\min(A)}{\max(A)-\min(A)}

We then threshold this normalized attention matrix using the
75^{\text{th}}
percentile to isolate the top-attention regions:

\tau=\text{Percentile}(A_{\text{norm}},75)\quad and\quad B(i,j)=\begin{cases}255&\text{if }A_{\text{norm}}(i,j)\geq\tau\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\forall(i,j)

This produces a binary map
B
in which pixels with high attention are white and others are black. Next, we extract the contours
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k}\}
from
B
using standard external contour detection and select the largest contour based on area:

\mathcal{C}=\text{Contours}(B)\quad and\quad\mathcal{C}^{*}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{C}_{i}\in\mathcal{C}}\text{Area}(\mathcal{C}_{i})

We create a binary mask
M
by filling in the region bounded by the largest contour
\mathcal{C}^{*}
. Additionally, in order to include a slightly larger region for making sure the cropped region contains all necessary context, we dilate the mask using a rectangular structuring element
K
of size
3\times 3
:

M(i,j)=\begin{cases}255&\text{if }(i,j)\in\text{Region}(\mathcal{C}^{*})\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad and\quad M_{\text{dil}}=M\oplus K

Finally, we compute the bounding box of the resulting mask to obtain the corresponding subregion
R
:

(x,y,w,h)=\text{Bounding}(M_{\text{dil}})\quad and\quad R=\{(i,j)\in I\mid x\leq j<x+w,\,\,y\leq i<y+h\}

Through this process, we obtain two subregions cropped from both the original input image and the rendered image:

I_{R}=\text{Crop}(I,R)\quad and\quad I^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}}=\text{Crop}(I^{\prime},R^{\prime})

The cropped regions
I_{R}
and
I^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}}
are then used for the self-verification to the model:

D^{\prime}=VLM(D,I_{R},I^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}},P_{\text{verification}})

This attention localization and verification step allows the model to concentrate on high-attention areas and improves its ability to discard hallucinated or incorrect feedbacks based on visual grounding.

4.5
Refinement

In the final step, we utilize the cropped regions from the original image
I_{R}
and its counterpart from the rendered image
I^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}}
, along with the previously identified correct difference description
D^{\prime}
, to instruct the vision-language model to revise the current LaTeX generation
L
.

A refinement prompt
P_{\text{refinement}}
is designed to instruct the model to revise only the part of the LaTeX code that causes the mismatch, while preserving the correct parts of
L
:

L^{\text{updated}}=VLM(L,I_{R},I^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}},D^{\prime},P_{\text{refinement}})

L^{\text{updated}}
is an updated version of the current LaTeX code
L
where the identified error has been corrected. After this refinement, the process loops back to step (2) to continue verify whether additional discrepancies exist. If so, the model repeats steps (2) to (5) iteratively:

L^{(t+1)}=\text{Refine}(L^{(t)},I,I^{\prime},P_{\text{comparison}},P_{\text{refinement}})

This self-refinement loop continues until either no new differences are detected or a predefined iteration limit
T_{\max}
is reached. The final output is:

L^{\ast}=L^{(T)},\,\,\text{where }T=T_{\max}\,\,\text{or}\,\,\text{diff}(I,I^{\prime(T)})=\emptyset

Out
A^{2}R^{2}
framework emulates a visual reasoning with iterative refinement paradigm, in which the model incrementally corrects its reasoning based on visual description, localization and verification. As a result, the model is encouraged to converge to a more faithful and accurate LaTeX representation of the input image with fewer hallucinations and improved semantic alignment.

5.3
Metrics

We evaluate the similarity between the generated LaTeX source code and the ground-truth label from both textual and visual perspectives.

•

ROUGE

(
Lin 2004
)
: A widely used metric in text generation tasks that measures the overlap of n-grams between the generated sequence and the reference.

•

BLEU-4

(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
: A precision-oriented metric evaluating n-gram overlap. We adopt the 4-gram variant with uniform weights (0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25), giving equal importance to unigrams through 4-grams.

•

Edit Distance

(
Ristad & Yianilos 1998
)
: A character-level metric that computes the minimum number of single-token insertions, deletions, and substitutions needed to convert the predicted LaTeX sequence into the ground truth.

To evaluate visual fidelity of the rendered LaTeX outputs, we use the following metrics:

•

Match
: A pixel-level metric that measures the proportion of identical pixels between the rendered prediction and the ground-truth image.

•

CW-SSIM
: A metric which assesses structural similarity in the complex wavelet domain, providing robustness to small geometric distortions.

5.4
Implementation Details

We obtain all model weights from official repositories hosted on HuggingFace. All experiments are conducted on a machine equipped with four NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs. To ensure consistency and efficiency during inference, we adopt the default settings for all inference parameters.

For attention localization, we follow methodologies established in prior work
(
Yao et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025b
)
. In the case of
Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct
, we extract attention maps from the 13th cross-attention layer, as cross-attention occurs every five layers between the 3rd and 38th layers. This particular layer has been shown to capture cross-modal interactions effectively, especially for OCR-centric tasks. For
Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct
, we compute the average attention across the central one-eighth of layers, which serves as a representative proxy for identifying salient regions relevant to the localization objective.

8
Test-Time Scaling

Our proposed framework incorporates an iterative refinement process that generates feedback over multiple rounds by identifying differences between two input images. This approach reflects the concept of test-time scaling: as the number of allowed refinement rounds increases, the model spends more time during inference but gains greater capacity to accurately identify all visual discrepancies and improve the output through successive refinements.

We evaluate the performance of two vision-language models under varying inference round limits using four evaluation metrics, with results presented in Figure
4
. The results show that as the number of inference rounds increases, the scores across all four metrics consistently improve, highlighting the benefit of multiple cycles of reflection and refinement. It is also reasonable to observe diminishing returns in performance as the number of rounds increases beyond three. Specifically, the gains from increasing the limit from three to five rounds are less pronounced than the improvements from one to two rounds. This plateau suggests that the models approach their maximum reasoning capacity on a given input, and fewer errors remain to be corrected in later rounds.

Nonetheless, the upward trend of the metric curves confirms the test-time scaling property of our approach and demonstrates its potential in leveraging iterative visual reasoning for refinement. Furthermore, these findings imply that employing a stronger base model could yield more accurate feedback, thereby enabling even greater improvements during the iterative refinement process.
