Huge primary vaginal stone in cerebral palsy patient
Authors: Linda Chamma, Ibrahim Ballout, Bachar Al Ali, Rabih Awad, Hussein Issa, Mohamad Moussa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaginal stones are rarely encountered in practice, often misdiagnosed as bladder stone on plain radiography. The clinical manifestation can be unspecific ranging from asymptomatic stones to acute onset of fever or abdominal discomfort, rendering the diagnosis challenging. They are classified into primary stones resulting from urine stasis, and more commonly secondary stones formed around a nidus.Cerebral palsy is frequently associated with urinary incontinence and leads to constant leakage of urine into the vagina. In addition, the prolonged recumbent position results in urinary stasis, thus promoting stone formation.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Endourology, Female urology, Vaginal stone

Introduction

Stones or calculus are hard deposits mainly found inside the kidneys and occasionally travel down through the urinary tract.

A rare entity often misdiagnosed as bladder stone on plain radiography are vaginal stones. The clinical manifestation can be unspecific ranging from asymptomatic stones, to acute onset of fever or abdominal discomfort, rendering the diagnosis challenging.1

Moreover, the mechanism of formation of kidney and vaginal stones are analogous.

Two subtypes of vaginal stones are identified in the literature, primary mostly resulting from urine stagnation and secondary formed in the setting of a nidus.2

The management of vaginal stones over the years was divided into two main approaches: transvaginal and transperitoneal.

We describe a case of 27-year-old woman with cerebral palsy presented with an incidental finding of a large vaginal stone managed by a safe endoscopic approach.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old female, known to have spastic cerebral palsy, bedridden, care giver dependent with history of urinary incontinence and no other comorbidities, presented to our emergency department with 3 days history of fever and chills associated with decreased oral intake, vomiting and abdominal pain. Vitals showed a febrile patient with tachycardia. On physical examination the patient looked pale, lethargic, her abdomen was distended, soft and tender, and left CVA tenderness was also noticeable. A bimanual pelvic exam was not carried on as by parents wish who insisted that the patient is not sexually active. Primary workup in the ED revealed leukocytosis WBC = 21,000/μL, high CRP of 208 mg/L, normal creatinine of 0.77 mg/dl, and a positive urine analysis.

Plain radiography was performed to rule out any obstruction and demonstrated a large pelvic calcified mass [Fig. 1]. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis showed gross calcified round structure in the pelvis occupying space between the urinary bladder and the rectum likely pushing upward the uterus occupying the vaginal space, it measures 9 × 10 cm [Fig. 2]; another obstructing stone of 1.4 cm was seen impacted in the proximal left ureter associated with hydronephrosis and perirenal fat stranding suggestive of pyelonephritis [Fig. 3].

Afterwards, gynecological exam was performed, and it confirmed the presence of the stone with no other abnormalities and no vaginal outlet obstruction.

We opted for urgent left ureteral stent insertion to decompress the kidney and patient was started on broad-spectrum antibiotics awaiting culture. Culture result returned with gram-negative bacilli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and culture targeted therapy was continued. After the infection was resolved, patient was scheduled for left flexible ureteroscopy with laser fragmentation of ureteral stone.

Six weeks later, the patient was scheduled for vaginal stone extraction using a minimal invasive technique, so endoscopic approach was planned.

Under general anesthesia, in lithotomy position, vaginoscopy was performed to assess vaginal integrity and stone position and it demonstrated normal vaginal epithelium with no obvious deformities. Then, a 28FR nephroscope was advanced into the vaginal lumen and U/S lithotripsy was initiated and the stone was fragmented into small pieces allowing their removal by forceps [Fig. 4].

Part of the stone small pieces removed by forceps.

Total operative time was 190 minutes, and no injury was noted.

Post-operative period was uneventful, and patient was discharged on antibiotics, and caregivers were advised to maintain a scheduled follow up. Stone analysis revealed a mixtures of calcium oxalate and struvite crystals.

Discussion

Vaginal stones are seldom seen, few cases are described in the literature. In resemblance to kidney stones, vaginal stones are influenced by multiple factors including diet, urine pH, urine stasis, microbiome, sex hormones and genetics.3

They are categorized into 2 types based on their pathophysiology. Primary vaginal stones are found in the setting of patients with urethrovaginal fistula, stenosis in vaginal outflow, posttraumatic or postoperative scarring, ectopic vaginal ureter or neurogenic bladder and are caused by urine stasis.4 Whereas, secondary stones are attributed to recurrent infections around a foreign body in the vagina such as mesh, sutures, intrauterine device, surgical gauze.5

Our case describes a primary vaginal stone as no nidus was identified and urine stagnation was the main contributor in its development. Cerebral palsy is often associated with urinary incontinence usually related to overactive detrusor. Urgency symptoms are the most common issues identified and leads to continuous leakage of urine into the vagina in paraplegic patients. In conjunction with extended recumbent position in our patient favoring stasis, the urinary pooling is the key for vaginal stone formation.6,7 Moreover, the large size of the stone in our case pressing the bladder thus decreasing its capacity will promote further leakage.

Notably, struvite is the most common component of vaginal stones. In contrast, calcium oxalate stones are the most common type of kidney stones, they occur when the urine becomes supersaturated with calcium and oxalic acid.8 The effect of urine pH on the formation of calcium oxalate is controversial. Most published papers concluded that the development of calcium oxalate stones is independent of the urine pH.9 The highest crystallization risk of calcium oxalate stones was perceived between pH 4.5 and 5.5, which is compatible with normal vaginal moderately acidic pH.10

Struvite is a crystal of magnesium ammonium phosphate. Struvite stones also referred as infection stones are in fact a combination of three cations (calcium, magnesium, ammonium) and one anion (phosphate). Due to the fact that pure struvite stones do not contain calcium, hence the mixture will be made of struvite, calcium phosphate with carbonate ions.11 Struvite stone formation takes place when ammonia production is increased, causing precipitation of phosphate salts. This mostly occurs in the setting of an infection with a urease producing organism such as Proteus mirabilis, klebsiella pneumoniae, ureaplasma urealyticum. Bacterial urease breaks down urea and promotes the formation of ammonia, thus rendering the vagina an alkali medium.12

In the context of mixed calcium oxalate and struvite stones, it is presumed that the leading event is calcium oxalate stone formation, serving as a nidus for a secondary infection by urease producing bacteria and struvite deposition.

The clinical picture is often unspecific ranging from subtle onset of symptoms such as suprapubic pain, fever, vomiting, decreased po intake to an incidental finding as in our case.13 In the setting of this symptomatology, diagnosis is often challenging. Therefore, we recommend that for any patient with prolonged recumbent position with signs of urinary incontinence, a gynecological exam should be carried out routinely and completed by abdominal plain radiography in case of suspicion of a vaginal stone.

In regard to the management of a vaginal stone, two main approaches were adapted. The transperitoneal approach, applied mainly in complicated cases of vesicovaginal fistulae, where the interposition of a peritoneal or omental flap between the bladder and the vagina is the mainstay of the treatment.14,15

The friable nature of struvite stones made the transvaginal extraction of a vaginal stone mostly employed. Hence, disintegration of the stone followed by extraction using ring forceps or kocher forceps, combined with episiotomy in case of a big stone was the mainstay for the management of vaginal stones through the years. However, a novel technique using a nephroscope in combination to an ultrasound device was originally used on a five years old with 2 vaginal stones, 3 × 2 cm and 2 × 1 cm by Jaspers et al.16 Subsequently, Fedrigone et all adapted the same procedure on a bigger stone (11 cm).17

Both concluded that the use of nephroscope in combination with an endoscopic ultrasound was efficient in visualization, disintegration and removal of vaginal stones. In comparison with other techniques, the latter is less invasive, safer and preserve the integrity of the genitals.

Conclusion

Lastly vaginal stones are rare. Cerebral palsy patients with prolonged recumbent position and urinary incontinence are at risk to develop them. The diagnosis is often challenging due to vague presentation and declined capacity to express in these patients. Thus, a gynecological exam must be routinely carried and completed by abdominal Xray when a vaginal stone is suspected. We highlight the use of nephroscope with an endoscopic ultrasound device in such cases as it carries out a safer, less invasive profile and more suitable in patients with special needs. Finally, as urinary incontinence is the key factor for vaginal stone formation then controlling the incontinence is a preventable measure. Treatment options include: incontinence training, medications, urotherapy, Botox injections, intermittent catheterization.
