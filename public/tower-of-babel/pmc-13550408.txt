Dynamic flavor profiles in sweet cherry during cold storage: integrated multi‑omics and volatile profiling reveals regulatory networks underlying flavor deterioration
Authors: Chen Feng, Jing Wang, Chuanbao Wu, Wei Wang, Kaichun Zhang, Xiaoming Zhang, Xuwei Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold storage induces flavor deterioration in sweet cherry, while cultivar-specific regulatory mechanisms are poorly understood. In this study, two mainstream commercial cultivars, ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’, were stored at 4 °C for 21 days. We comprehensively integrated physiological phenotyping, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and volatile compound profiling to explore their flavor deterioration patterns. Physiological results demonstrated that fruit softening and sugar-acid imbalance were typical deterioration symptoms, and ‘Tieton’ reached a 100% decay rate at the end of storage, far higher than 51.55% of ‘Rainier’. Metabolomic analysis identified 1876 metabolites, and the two cultivars showed asynchronous metabolic reprogramming. ‘Tieton’ underwent dramatic metabolic changes at the early storage stage, while obvious metabolic shifts occurred in ‘Rainier’ at the mid stage. “Starch and sucrose metabolism” and “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” were confirmed as two universally core pathways associated with flavor variation. We also detected 45 differential volatile compounds linked to fruit aroma. Furthermore, a flavor regulatory network was constructed, verifying that PavMYB3/13 as candidate positive regulators and PavC2H2 is a candidate negative regulator of flavor maintenance. This work elaborates on the genotype-specific patterns of cold-induced flavor decay and provides a theoretical foundation for developing variety-targeted postharvest preservation technologies for sweet cherries.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.), a member of the Rosaceae family, is a widely favored fruit known for its distinctive flavor, crisp and juicy texture, and abundant nutritional components, such as anthocyanins, phenolic compounds, and vitamin C1–3. The commercial value of sweet cherries is primarily influenced by their flavor quality, a complex trait shaped by the interplay of soluble solids content (sweetness), titratable acidity (acidity), flesh firmness (texture quality), and volatile aromatic compounds (aroma)4–7. However, sweet cherries are particularly vulnerable to post-harvest quality deterioration, which can result in flesh softening, flavor loss, and various physiological disorders8,9. Cold storage is the most common method for post-harvest preservation of sweet cherries, effectively reducing fruit respiration, delaying ripening and senescence, and extending shelf life10,11. Nonetheless, extended periods of cold storage can lead to flavor deterioration, with changes in sweetness, acidity, and firmness impacting consumer preferences12,13. Additionally, significant variation exists in cold tolerance and flavor deterioration patterns among different sweet cherry varieties, an important trait that has been largely overlooked in earlier mechanistic studies.

Previous studies have documented changes in flavor-related traits during cold storage of sweet cherries, including fluctuations in soluble solids content, continuous titratable acidity degradation, fruit softening, and loss of volatile compounds14,15. For instance, a cold storage investigation involving ‘Santina’ and ‘Royal Rainier’ sweet cherries found that both varieties exhibited a gradual decrease in soluble solids content (SSC) and titratable acidity (TA) over extended storage periods, with fruit firmness showing minimal change during the initial storage phase but declining significantly in later stages16. Ponce et al.17 compared changes in fruit quality between ‘Sweetheart’ and ‘Bing’ during cold storage, revealing that ‘Sweetheart’ maintained relatively stable SSC and TA levels, while ‘Bing’ displayed greater fluctuations. Further metabolomic analyses indicated that these physiological indicators were highly correlated with sugar- and acid-metabolic pathways. Wang et al.18 examined quality changes across four sweet cherry varieties during cold storage, confirming differences in initial SSC and TA levels and the rates at which they changed among varieties, with firmness consistently declining throughout storage. The study18 also identified variations in the types and levels of volatile compounds among the different varieties, with total volatile compound content showing differing degrees of change over prolonged storage periods. Although these studies have begun to explore the effects of cold storage on sweet cherry quality, most have concentrated on single varieties or single omics levels, lacking a systematic investigation into the dynamic, variety-specific changes in integrated flavor traits and the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms. Additionally, the relationships among phenotypic deterioration, metabolic reprogramming, gene expression changes, and alterations in volatile compound profiles across various varieties remain unclear, highlighting a significant knowledge gap in developing targeted preservation strategies.

With the rapid advancement of multi-omics technologies, the integrated analysis of transcriptomes, metabolomes, and volatile compounds has emerged as a powerful tool for unraveling the complex molecular mechanisms underlying post-harvest fruit quality19–21. This comprehensive approach enables the construction of more detailed regulatory networks, which are essential for revealing the mechanisms underlying flavor deterioration and identifying core regulatory factors22. While this strategy has been successfully applied to studies on the cold storage quality of apples23, nectarines24, kiwifruit25, and Hami melon26, its systematic implementation in sweet cherries remains limited. Thus, this study focuses on two prominent commercial sweet cherry cultivars, ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’, to explore their flavor deterioration patterns during cold storage. By integrating data from phenotypic profiling, transcriptomic analysis, metabolomic assessments, and volatile compound analysis, we aim to identify specific regulatory modules, including genes, metabolites, and transcription factors, as well as the molecular regulatory networks that govern flavor changes. Specifically, the novelty of this research lies in three key areas: (1) revealing the distinct temporal patterns of metabolic reprogramming specific to each cultivar, particularly between the cold-tolerant ‘Rainier’ and the cold-sensitive ‘Tieton’; (2) identifying the dynamic changes in flavor-related volatile compounds during cold storage; and (3) constructing a flavor regulatory network that identifies PavMYB3/13 as candidate positive regulators and PavC2H2 as a candidate negative regulator of flavor maintenance in sweet cherries. This research is expected to pinpoint targeted candidate factors for fruit flavor preservation and to provide valuable theoretical support for the development of variety-specific postharvest preservation technologies for sweet cherries, addressing the pressing needs of the sweet cherry industry.

Results

Dynamic changes in flavor-related phenotypes and physiological indicators of sweet cherries during cold storage

Changes observed in ‘Rainier’ (R) and ‘Tieton’ (T) under cold storage demonstrated varietal specificity (Fig. 1a). Quality deterioration began in both varieties after 4 days of cold storage; however, ‘Tieton’ exhibited a significantly higher rate of deterioration than ‘Rainier’. After 10 days of storage, the deterioration rate for ‘Rainier’ was just 2.71%, while ‘Tieton’ reached 20.30%. As storage continued to 14 and 21 days, the deterioration rates for ‘Rainier’ increased to 21.07% and 51.55%, respectively, whereas ‘Tieton’ saw rates of 49.30% and ultimately 100%. This evidence suggests that ‘Tieton’ is more sensitive to low-temperature conditions and struggles to maintain quality over extended periods in cold storage.

The soluble solids content (SSC) of both varieties demonstrated similar trends, although within different ranges of variation. Initially, SSC increased from 0 to 4 days, followed by a decline from 7 to 21 days (Fig. 1b). At 4 days of storage, ‘Rainier’ recorded an SSC of 21.91 °Brix, which was significantly higher than ‘Tieton’ (17.71 °Brix). As storage duration increased, SSC reached its minimum at 21 days for ‘Rainier’ and 14 days for ‘Tieton’, with SSC values of 13.93 °Brix and 17.57 °Brix, respectively. While ‘Tieton’ had the highest SSC at 4 days, this value was not significantly different from ‘Rainier’ at 21 days.

Titratable acidity (TA) in both varieties gradually declined, reaching its lowest levels during the final storage phase. However, ‘Rainier’ consistently exhibited higher TA levels than ‘Tieton’ (Fig. 1c). Notably, ‘Tieton’ experienced a significant reduction in TA at 2 days of storage, dropping to 0.32 g·100 g−1. As the storage period continued, ‘Tieton’ maintained a stable TA around 0.4 g·100 g−1. In contrast, the changes in ‘Rainier’ were more gradual, with a decrease from 0.80 g·100 g−1 at day 0 to 0.70 g·100 g−1.

The SSC-to-TA (SSC/TA) ratio serves as a crucial indicator for assessing fruit texture. For both varieties, the SSC/TA ratio initially increased and then decreased during storage, with no significant difference observed between the final storage stage and day 0 (Fig. 1d). At 2 d treatment, ‘Tieton’ achieved its peak SSC/TA ratio of 51.69. Although this ratio fell to 43.50 by 4 d, it remained significantly higher than ‘Rainier’ at all stages of storage. While ‘Rainier’ demonstrated the highest SSC/TA ratio at 4 d, it was still significantly lower than that of ‘Tieton’ at the same time point. These findings suggest that although both varieties experienced gradual declines in SSC and TA, the SSC/TA ratio of ‘Tieton’ consistently exceeded that of ‘Rainier’ throughout the storage period.

During cold storage, the firmness of both fruit varieties displayed a declining trend (Fig. 1e). Specifically, for ‘Tieton’, firmness decreased from 221.61 g·mm−1 to 208.39 g·mm−1, followed by a sharp decline to 140.83 g·mm−1 at 14 d. In contrast, ‘Rainier’ showed a slight increase from 81.33 g·mm−1 to 132.50 g·mm−1, but then experienced a significant drop to 67 g·mm−1. These findings suggest that fruit softening is a symptom of cold-induced deterioration.

Changes in metabolite profiles of sweet cherries during cold storage

Based on the dynamic physiological changes observed over the full 21-day storage period (Fig. 1), days 0, 4, and 10 were selected as representative time points for multi‑omics analysis. Day 0 represented the initial fresh fruit; day 4 marked early quality shifts, particularly the rapid decline in titratable acidity in ‘Tieton’; and day 10 represented the rapid deterioration inflection point, where the two cultivars began to show clear divergence in decay rates. Notably, physiological fluctuations (SSC, TA, and SSC/TA ratio) stabilized after day 10, with no significant differences observed between day 10 and day 14 for either cultivar, and fruit firmness in ‘Rainier’ also showed no significant change during this period. Therefore, these three time points were chosen to capture the early and middle stages of cold‑induced metabolic and transcriptional reprogramming.

Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated a clear separation of samples based on both storage duration and variety (Fig. 2a), indicating that differences between varieties and cold storage treatments lead to metabolic reprogramming in sweet cherries. Moreover, the observed high reproducibility within groups and significant variability between groups validate the metabolomics data. Sample correlation analysis (Fig. 2b) corroborated the PCA results. A total of 1876 metabolites were identified and categorized into 13 groups (Fig. 2c): alkaloids (9.81%), amino acids and derivatives (9.81%), flavonoids (19.51%), lignans and coumarins (5.33%), lipids (8.21%), nucleotides and derivatives (3.94%), organic acids (4.32%), phenolic acids (11.25%), quinones (0.91%), steroids (0.11%), tannins (1.28%), terpenoids (13.91%), and others (11.62%). The percentage in brackets indicates the proportion of each class among the total identified metabolites. The clustering heatmap revealed significant alterations in the metabolite profiles of the two varieties during cold storage (Fig. 2d).

To further clarify the dynamic changes in metabolite profiles during cold storage, we first conducted OPLS-DA analyses for pairwise time‑point comparisons within each cultivar. Comprehensive details on all identified DMs are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The number of DMs varied between the two sweet cherry varieties across different treatment time points (Fig. 2e, f). In the R4_vs._R0 comparison group, we identified a total of 232 DMs, including 74 upregulated and 158 downregulated metabolites. Conversely, the R10_vs._R0 comparison revealed 462 DMs, comprising 221 upregulated and 241 downregulated metabolites. Compared to R4, R10 contained an additional 238 upregulated DMs and 158 downregulated DMs. Venn analysis identified 29 core DMs, including 1 alkaloid, 3 amino acids and derivatives, 9 flavonoids, 2 lignans and coumarins, 2 lipids, 2 organic acids, 6 phenolic acids, 2 terpenoids, and 2 other compounds.

For ‘Tieton’, when compared to T0, T4 exhibited 273 DMs (175 upregulated and 98 downregulated). In the T10_vs._T0 comparison group, we identified 241 DMs, comprising 148 upregulated and 93 downregulated metabolites. Additionally, the T10_vs._T4 comparison group revealed 261 DMs, including 145 upregulated and 116 downregulated metabolites. Venn analysis identified 13 core DMs shared across ‘Tieton’, comprising 1 alkaloid, 1 flavonoid, 1 phenolic acid, 8 terpenoids, and 2 other compounds.

These findings suggest that the metabolic substances in sweet cherry fruits undergo significant changes with extended cold storage, ultimately affecting fruit quality. Moreover, the DMs between the two cultivars under the same storage conditions were identified. We discovered that the number of DMs between cultivars exceeded that within a single cultivar across different time points. Notably, ‘Tieton’ exhibited higher DM levels during early storage, while ‘Rainier’ showed higher DM levels after 10 days of storage. These results underscore the distinct differences in the metabolic profiles between the two varieties and highlight their varying responses to cold storage treatment.

The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the DMs in both cultivars were significantly associated with pathways related to flavor and secondary metabolism (Fig. 2g, h, and Supplementary Table S2). In the ‘Rainier’ cultivar, DMs were predominantly enriched in pathways such as “propanoate metabolism”, “fructose and mannose metabolism”, and “biosynthesis of flavanols”. Conversely, the ‘Tieton’ cultivar showed significant enrichment in pathways related to “valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation”, “biosynthesis of anthocyanins”, and “purine metabolism”. These differing KEGG pathways indicate that the two cultivars may engage different metabolic branches in response to cold storage, which could contribute to their distinct flavor deterioration characteristics. Furthermore, pathways that were consistently identified in the enrichment analyses of metabolomic datasets across the two cultivars, namely “starch and sucrose metabolism” and “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”, are directly associated with the accumulation of soluble solids and changes in flavor.

Transcriptomic analysis of sweet cherries during cold storage

To clarify the molecular mechanisms behind cold storage-induced changes in the fruit quality of the two sweet cherry varieties, we conducted transcriptomic analysis on cherries stored for different durations. We obtained a total of 151.73 GB of clean data, with a Q30 base percentage exceeding 97.96% (Supplementary Table S3). PCA based on RPKM values revealed well-defined separation among the samples (Fig. 3a), with three biological replicates per sample distinctly identifiable across different treatment time points. The results from the sample correlation analysis further aligned with the PCA findings (Fig. 3b). These results confirm that the sequencing quality is reliable and appropriate for the subsequent identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Transcriptomic profiling of ‘Rainier’ (R) and ‘Tieton’ (T) sweet cherries during cold storage.

a PCA score plot based on FPKM values of the transcriptome of two sweet cherry cultivars at 0, 4, and 10 days of cold storage. Each point stands for a biological replicate, with distinct colors and shapes for different cultivars and storage time points. b Sample correlation of the transcriptomic data. c Number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’ at different cold storage comparison groups. d Venn diagram of core DEGs in ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’ across all cold storage comparison groups. e KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of all DEGs in ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’ during cold storage.

To identify DEGs in sweet cherry fruits, we conducted a screening using the DESeq2 R package with criteria of |log2FoldChange | ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. The DEG statistical analysis (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table S4) revealed that 12,583 DEGs were identified across both cultivars, indicating significant cultivar-specific differences in both quantity and expression trends. Compared with the control, ‘Rainier’ exhibited 2,066 upregulated DEGs and 2140 downregulated DEGs after 4 d of storage. After 10 d of storage, the number of upregulated DEGs increased to 3333, while the number of downregulated DEGs increased to 3434 relative to the control. When comparing R10 to R4, a total of 1287 upregulated and 856 downregulated DEGs were identified.

In contrast, ‘Tieton’ exhibited 3002 upregulated DEGs and 2513 downregulated DEGs after 4 d of storage. After 10 d, it displayed 3173 upregulated and 2990 downregulated DEGs. The comparison of T10_vs._T4 revealed 1504 DEGs, consisting of 1180 upregulated and 324 downregulated genes. A Venn analysis comparing DEGs across various storage durations identified 796 and 662 core genes present in both ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’. This indicates that cold storage significantly reprograms gene expression profiles in sweet cherry fruit (Fig. 3d).

The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis results (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table S5) revealed that DEGs from both sweet cherry varieties were significantly enriched in pathways such as “biosynthesis of secondary metabolites”, “plant hormone signal transduction”, “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”, “glutathione metabolism”, “linoleic acid metabolism”, “terpenoid backbone biosynthesis”, “plant MAPK signaling pathway”, and “plant-pathogen interaction”. These findings suggest that both varieties share common physiological processes in response to low-temperature storage, which are closely linked to flavor metabolism and fruit deterioration in sweet cherries. Additionally, we identified pathways that were specifically enriched in each variety. For instance, DEGs in ‘Rainier’ showed enrichment in pathways related to “ether lipid metabolism”, “plant circadian rhythm”, and “monobactam biosynthesis”. In contrast, DEGs in ‘Tieton’ exhibited enrichment in pathways such as “brassinosteroid biosynthesis” and “cyanoamino acid metabolism”, indicating distinct physiological processes between the two varieties during cold storage.

Variety-specific dynamics of volatile compounds

While sweet cherries are not typically characterized as aromatic fruits, they do contain volatile compounds5,27,28. To explore this further, we analyzed the volatile components of two varieties across three stages. The PCA results, illustrated in Fig. 4a, b, clearly separated the samples by time point. The first principal component accounted for 23.3% and 16.9% of the variance for ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’, respectively, while the second principal component captured 58.7% and 39.2% of the variance. These findings confirm the distinct changes in the volatile compound profiles of sweet cherries during cold storage.

Each point represents a biological replicate, with different colors for different storage time points, reflecting the separation degree of volatile profiles at different storage stages. c, d Sensory classification of differential volatile compounds (DVCs) in ‘Rainier’ (c) and ‘Tieton’ (d). DVCs were classified into sensory categories based on their aroma characteristics. e, f Heatmap of the relative content changes of key DVCs in ‘Rainier’ (e) and ‘Tieton’ (f) during cold storage.

As illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1, this study identified 45 differential volatile compounds (DVCs), which can be categorized into 13 distinct groups: 4 acids, 6 alcohols, 5 aldehydes, 2 amines, 2 aromatics, 6 esters, 1 ether, 2 halogenated hydrocarbons, 2 heterocyclic compounds, 2 hydrocarbons, 5 ketones, 1 phenol, and 7 terpenoids. Based on sensory characteristics (Fig. 4c, d), the DVCs in both varieties can be organized into 10 classes, with overlapping aroma profiles that include fruity and sweet notes. Furthermore, ‘Rainier’ predominantly exhibits woody, herbal, and green aromas, while ‘Tieton’ is characterized by hyacinth, fatty, and pineapple scents. The DVCs responsible for the fruity aroma in ‘Rainier’ include 2-Hexen-1-ol, propanoate, (E)-, 4-heptanone, and butanoic acid, 3-methyl-, 2-methylbutyl ester; in contrast, ‘Tieton’ features pentanoic acid, 3-methyl-, ethyl ester, and 3-octenoic acid, ethyl ester as contributors to its fruity aroma. The sweet DVCs found in ‘Rainier’ are (E)-β-farnesene and benzaldehyde, 3-methyl-, whereas ‘Tieton’ showcases benzenemethanol, α-methyl- and benzaldehyde.

We subsequently analyzed changes in DVCs during storage for both cultivars (Fig. 4e, f, and Supplementary Table S6). The compound 1H-pyrrole, 2-methyl- was identified in both ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’, although it exhibited different trends across the two varieties. Compared to R0, this compound was upregulated 3.57-fold at R4, but no significant difference was observed at R10. In ‘Tieton’, relative to T0, the compound increased at both T4 and T10, with increases of 1.20-fold and 1.55-fold, respectively, indicating that it might be a typical volatile compound associated with cold storage in sweet cherry fruit. Unique DVCs were also detected in both cultivars during cold storage. For instance, 2-hexen-1-ol demonstrated a significant decrease compared to R0, with reductions of −2.54-fold at R4 and −2.79-fold at R10. Conversely, ‘Tieton’ showed no significant change in this compound. Additionally, 1-pentanol, 2,2-dimethyl- experienced a notable reduction in DVC in ‘Tieton’, with a decrease of 1.05 times compared to T0 in T4, while ‘Rainier’ exhibited no significant variation.

To evaluate the sensory relevance of the identified DVCs, we calculated the relative odor activity value (rOAV) for each compound based on published odor thresholds (Supplementary Table S6). Among the 45 DVCs, 12 compounds exhibited OAV ≥ 1 in at least one cultivar or storage time point, indicating their potential contribution to flavor perception. Notably, pentanoic acid, 3-methyl-, ethyl ester showed the highest rOAVs in both cultivars (6967.09-10140.30), confirming its dominant role in the fruity aroma of sweet cherries. 4-Heptanone, a characteristic fruity note compound, had rOAVs of 244.05-323.41. Furthermore, in the ‘Rainier’ cultivar, 2-hexen-1-ol had rOAVs ranging from 2.02 to 12.08, while it remained unchanged in ‘Tieton’, illustrating the distinct aroma profiles specific to each cultivar.

Construction of Fruit Quality Regulatory Network Under Cold Storage

Given the significant changes in sweet cherry fruit SSC during cold storage, we focused on sugar metabolism pathways in the metabolome and transcriptome. A total of 11 key DMs were identified, among which sucrose 6-phosphate (Suc-6P), alpha-D-glucose 1,6-bisphosphate (Glc-1,6P2), D-fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (Fru-1,6P2), and raffinose (Raf) were particularly noteworthy, as highlighted in Fig. 5a. These 11 DMs displayed distinct accumulation patterns across the two sweet cherry cultivars (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table S1). During prolonged cold storage, Suc-6P in ‘Rainier’ initially decreased by 3.49-fold, then increased by 2.96-fold. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in ‘Tieton’. Raf levels in ‘Tieton’ significantly increased after cold storage, reaching 3.25-fold and 3.13-fold higher than T0 at T4 and T10, respectively, while ‘Rainier’ exhibited no notable changes. Fru-1,6P2 displayed a continuous decline in ‘Rainier’, whereas ‘Tieton’ showed a decrease followed by an increase. Furthermore, laminarin levels increased by 1.90-fold and 1.24-fold in ‘Tieton’ after cold storage, while a decreasing trend was noted in ‘Rainier’. These differential accumulation patterns of DMs highlight, to some extent, variations in sugar metabolism processes between the two varieties following cold storage treatment.

Analysis of DMs and DEGs involved in sugar and acid-metabolic pathways of ‘Rainier’ (R) and ‘Tieton’ (T) during cold storage.

Based on transcriptome data, a total of 8 DEGs associated with sugar metabolism were identified (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table S4). These genes primarily include phosphofructokinases (PavPFKs, FUN_006074, FUN_012608, FUN_021238, FUN_031520, and FUN_039319) and raffinose synthases (PavRFSs, FUN_018648, FUN_031517, and FUN_039651) Notably, FUN_031520 exhibited a gradual decline in expression levels during cold storage in both varieties, reaching −1.04-fold and −1.34-fold relative to R0 at R4 and R10, respectively, and −1.06-fold and −3.32-fold relative to T0 at T4 and T10, respectively. FUN_039319 showed no significant change at T4 but was upregulated 2.15-fold at T10, suggesting a potential role in Fru-1,6P2 accumulation at this time point. Additionally, FUN_018648 showed a significant decrease in ‘Rainier’ with extended treatment, declining by 1.18-fold and 1.93-fold at R4 and R10, respectively.

We analyzed metabolites associated with acid metabolism in the metabolome, identified 10 key organic acids, and constructed a key acid metabolism network diagram based on KEGG metabolic pathways (Fig. 5b). In line with the TA findings, we observed significant reductions in (R, R)-tartaric acid and (S)-2-hydroxyglutarate in refrigerated ‘Rainier’ fruits. Specifically, (R, R)-tartaric acid decreased by a factor of −3.75 and −1.18 relative to R0 at stages R4 and R10, respectively, while (S)-2-hydroxyglutarate saw a reduction of 2.37-fold at both stages. Notably, these two organic acids exhibited no significant changes in ‘Tieton’ (Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, methanesulfonic acid decreased significantly, primarily in refrigerated ‘Tieton’, with a 1.20-fold reduction in T10 compared to T0. Additionally, levels of abscisate and 2-hydroxyethylphosphonate in T10 were 1.15 and 1.69 times lower, respectively, than in T0.

A total of 14 DEGs associated with acid metabolism were identified from the transcriptome data (Supplementary Table S4). This group primarily includes ATP-citrate lyases (PavACLs, FUN_001201, FUN_001295, and FUN_003864), citrate synthase (PavCS, FUN_008016), malate dehydrogenase (PavMDH, FUN_026189), aconitate hydratases (PavACOs, FUN_016735, FUN_021941, FUN_021942, FUN_038808), isocitrate dehydrogenases (PavIDHs, FUN_013532, FUN_033105, and FUN_038873), and succinate CoA ligase (PavSUCLG, FUN_020582). The expression of FUN_001201 declined gradually over extended treatment periods in both varieties, reaching its lowest level at 10 d of storage. Conversely, FUN_001295 and FUN_003864 exhibited a steady increase in expression in both varieties. While the expression of FUN_008016 gradually increased during cold storage for both cultivars, this change was not statistically significant in ‘Rainier’. However, in ‘Tieton’, it showed a significant increase compared to T0. Likewise, the expression of FUN_026189 at T4 and T10 was 1.18- and 1.21-fold higher than at T0, respectively. Furthermore, all four PavACOs showed increased expression during cold storage, although the extent of upregulation varied. These differentially expressed key enzyme genes ultimately affected changes in organic acid content during cold storage of sweet cherry fruit.

Lignin is a vital metabolite that contributes to the firmness of sweet cherry fruit. Consequently, this study concentrated on DEGs and key metabolites associated with lignin metabolism. Nine metabolites from the lignin pathway were identified in the metabolome (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table S1). Notably, L-phenylalanine exhibited significant accumulation in both ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’ fruits during the late storage period, increasing to levels 1.18-fold and 1.00-fold higher than those at 0 d, respectively. Compared with R0, L-tyrosine showed a 1.35-fold increase at R10, though no significant changes were observed during the early storage period. Additionally, 4-coumaroylshikimate was upregulated by 1.80-fold in ‘Tieton’ after 10 d of cold storage, while ‘Rainier’ showed no significant change.

Identification of DMs and DEGs in the lignin metabolic pathway of ‘Rainier’ (R) and ‘Tieton’ (T) sweet cherries during cold storage.

A total of 55 DEGs associated with the lignin metabolism pathway were identified from the transcriptomes, encompassing various key enzyme genes (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table S4). Nine DEGs showed differential expression in both varieties during cold storage, with 8 exhibiting significant upregulation and 1 significant downregulation. Specifically, 4-coumarate CoA ligase (Pav4CL, FUN_019523) was upregulated by 3.54-fold and 5.26-fold in ‘Rainier’ at 4 d and 10 d post-cold storage, respectively, and by 3.94-fold and 4.41-fold in ‘Tieton’ at the same time points. Cytochrome P450 98A2 (PavCYP98A2, FUN_007339 and FUN_007338) displayed significantly increased expression in both varieties as the duration of cold storage increased, with peak expression occurring at 10 d post-treatment. Caffeoylshikimate esterase (PavCSE, FUN_012062), which catalyzes the conversion of caffeoyl shikimic acid to caffeic acid, showed significant upregulation to high levels in both varieties after 4 d of cold storage, maintaining elevated expression thereafter. Ferulic acid 5-hydroxylase (PavF5H, FUN_038702) exhibited gradual upregulation in both varieties at 4 and 10 d post-cold storage, with increases of 2.57-fold for ‘Rainier’ and 5.08-fold for ‘Tieton’ at those respective time points. Moreover, the remaining 46 DEGs displayed differential expression at varying cold storage time points across both varieties. Collectively, these DEGs influenced post-storage fruit quality by altering metabolite levels within the lignin metabolism pathway.

Specific regulatory network governing flavor changes in sweet cherry fruits under cold storage

To further explore the complex molecular mechanisms driving physiological changes in sweet cherry fruit during cold storage, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic data using two-way orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS). Our analysis (Fig. 7a) identified 10 transcription factors (TFs) that significantly influenced the metabolome, including PavMYB3/13, PavNAC82, PavB3, PavIAA7, PavHMGB3, PavC2H2, PavPHD, PavSCL14, and PavNF-YC3 (R²X = 0.97, R²Y = 0.88). Notably, PavC2H2 displayed the highest loading scores (Loading 1 = 0.055, Loading 2 = −0.027) (Fig. 7a). The expression patterns of these transcription factors varied by cultivar: PavMYB3 and PavMYB13 showed higher expression in ‘Tieton’, which decreased progressively with extended cold storage, suggesting a potential positive regulatory role in preserving fruit quality during storage. In contrast, PavNF-YC3, PavC2H2, PavSCL14, and PavPHD were highly expressed in ‘Rainier’ during the later stages of cold storage (Fig. 7b).

We subsequently constructed a putative regulatory network for sweet cherry cold storage, utilizing FPKM values for TFs and selected key structural genes. The results indicated (Fig. 7c) that these TFs co-express with genes involved in sugar, acid, and lignin metabolism. PavC2H2 demonstrates significant correlations with 12 structural genes, comprising 4 positive and 8 negative correlations. Notably, structural genes associated with organic acid metabolism (FUN_021941, FUN_021942, FUN_034305) exhibited highly significant positive correlations with this transcription factor, showing correlation coefficients of 0.82, 0.67, and 0.70, respectively. This suggests that PavC2H2 might function as a negative regulator of sweet cherry fruit quality. In addition, PavMYB3 and PavMYB13 displayed significant correlations with 10 and 11 structural genes, respectively. Both transcription factors showed extremely significant negative correlations with three downregulated structural genes involved in acid metabolism, further indicating that they might act as positive regulators of fruit quality. Moreover, PavNF-YC3, PavSCL14, and PavPHD were significantly correlated with 13, 12, and 13 structural genes, respectively, and were positively correlated with some downregulated structural genes, suggesting these transcription factors might also exert negative regulatory functions. Overall, these findings indicate that during cold storage, the quality deterioration of sweet cherries is influenced by TFs, which ultimately regulate related structural genes and, consequently, impact fruit quality.

Discussion

Flavor deterioration during the cold storage of sweet cherries is a complex process characterized by synergistic changes in phenotypic traits, metabolism, and gene expression, with variety-specificity serving as the fundamental factor influencing post-harvest cold storage performance. This study clarified the dynamic deterioration characteristics of flavor-related phenotypes in ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’ fruits during cold storage. It demonstrated that fruit softening is closely linked to a decline in quality, which aligns with the findings of Miranda et al.16 regarding the quality deterioration of ‘Santina’ and ‘Royal Rainier’ sweet cherries during cold storage. Both studies underscore that softening is a fundamental symptom of deterioration in sweet cherries subjected to low-temperature storage. Additionally, the overall reduction in SSC and TA aligns with previous research. However, this study offers valuable insights into quality changes during the early refrigeration phase (0–4 d), thereby enhancing understanding of quality deterioration patterns in sweet cherries at low temperatures.

To gain a deeper understanding of how flavor-related phenotypic deterioration progresses over time, we investigated the physiological changes across three distinct storage stages. During the early storage phase, both cultivars showed a temporary accumulation of soluble solids, accompanied by a slight reduction in organic acids. Notably, ‘Tieton’ experienced a rapid decline in TA at this initial stage, resulting in a sharp increase in the SSC/TA ratio and creating a preliminary imbalance in the flavor profile. In contrast, ‘Rainier’ maintained a relatively stable TA and a moderate SSC/TA ratio, thus preserving a basic flavor balance. This early physiological divergence laid the foundation for subsequent differences in phenotypic deterioration between the two cultivars. In the mid-storage phase (4–10 d), soluble solids content continued to decrease in both cultivars, while fruit firmness gradually declined. For ‘Tieton’, the accelerated loss of acidity exacerbated the sugar–acid imbalance, leading to a progressively higher SSC/TA ratio. Conversely, ‘Rainier’ sustained a relatively stable acid level, showing a slower rate of decline in SSC and a more moderate increase in the SSC/TA ratio. During the late storage phase (10–21 d), ‘Tieton’ experienced a dramatic drop in firmness, reaching complete fruit deterioration by day 21. The severe sugar–acid imbalance, characterized by very low TA and ongoing SSC loss, led to complete flavor failure. In contrast, ‘Rainier’ retained significant acid content and moderate firmness, with only half of the fruits exhibiting quality deterioration by the end of the storage period.

The SSC/TA ratio is a crucial indicator of sweet cherry fruit quality29. Ponce et al.17 observed differing changes in SSC and TA between the ‘Sweetheart’ and ‘Bing’ varieties during cold storage; the former maintained both indices effectively, while the latter experienced significant fluctuations. This observation is consistent with our findings, as ‘Rainier’ demonstrated a better preservation of fruit quality during cold storage. Further insights reveal that the mechanisms driving the imbalance in the SSC/TA ratio during cold storage differ fundamentally across varieties. This imbalance stems not only from acid degradation but also from the synergistic interaction between TA loss rate and changes in SSC. Certain varieties maintain relatively stable SSC/TA ratios over extended periods, primarily due to their superior TA retention capacity and slower decline in SSC. In contrast, rapid acid loss is a primary factor contributing to accelerated flavor deterioration in other varieties. These varying patterns of phenotypic deterioration suggest the possibility of implementing targeted postharvest management strategies for different sweet cherry cultivars.

This study systematically characterized changes in the metabolic profiles of sweet cherries during cold storage. Notably, the two tested cultivars exhibited distinct patterns of metabolic reprogramming in response to low-temperature stress. The ‘Tieton’ cultivar initiated substantial differential metabolite accumulation during the early stages of cold storage, leading to the rapid generation of various metabolites shortly after refrigeration. In contrast, ‘Rainier’ showed significant metabolite rearrangements later, peaking during the mid-storage period. This divergence in the timing of metabolic remodeling aligns closely with the physiological measurements of phenotypic deterioration, in which “Tieton” showed a much faster decline in quality and a higher final decay rate than ‘Rainier’ over 21 days of cold preservation. These findings further emphasize that the timing and intensity of cold-triggered metabolic fluctuations serve as intrinsic markers of cultivar-specific storage tolerance.

The rOAV‑based analysis provides a more refined understanding of the sensory implications of volatile changes during cold storage. Compounds with rOAV ≥ 1, particularly pentanoic acid, 3-methyl-, ethyl ester, and (3E,5Z)-1,3,5-undecatriene are likely the primary contributors to the perceived aroma of sweet cherries. The significant decrease in rOAVs of key fruity volatiles during prolonged storage, especially in ‘Tieton’, correlates with the accelerated flavor deterioration observed in this cultivar. Conversely, ‘Rainier’ maintained higher rOAVs for certain sweet and fruity compounds, consistent with its better storage performance. These findings suggest that cultivar‑specific volatile dynamics, as quantified by rOAVs, can serve as potential biomarkers for flavor quality assessment during cold storage.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that ‘starch and sucrose metabolism’ and ‘phenylpropanoid biosynthesis’ were the main metabolic pathways driving flavor changes induced by cold storage in sweet cherries. This finding aligns with previous research on apples23, nectarines24, kiwifruit25, and Hami melon26, further confirming that these pathways are conserved core components of post-harvest flavor metabolism in fruits. As the primary pathway regulating soluble sugar transformation in fruits, ‘starch and sucrose metabolism’ plays a crucial role in modulating the dynamic levels of various soluble carbohydrates, ultimately shaping the sweetness profile of sweet cherry fruit. Concurrently, ‘phenylpropanoid biosynthesis’ is closely linked to the production of phenolic compounds, lignin precursors, and certain aroma precursors, collectively influencing the maintenance of fruit firmness and the development of background flavors. The consistent enhancement of these two pathways in both varieties suggests that cold stress universally triggers the remodeling of carbohydrate and phenylpropanoid metabolism in postharvest sweet cherries, providing a fundamental metabolic basis for the flavor deterioration associated with cold exposure.

Beyond conserved core pathways, this study also highlights pronounced cultivar-specific discrepancies in downstream metabolic pathway enrichment patterns, which essentially explain the divergent flavor deterioration characteristics between ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’. The differential metabolites identified in ‘Rainier’ were primarily enriched in pathways associated with ‘fructose and mannose metabolism’ and ‘flavanol biosynthesis’. The activation of the fructose and mannose metabolic cascade facilitates the stable transformation and retention of soluble sugars within fruits, effectively alleviating the sharp decline in SSC during prolonged cold storage. Furthermore, the continuous biosynthesis of flavanols also stabilizes the accumulation of secondary metabolites and delays the onset of metabolic disorders at low temperatures, thereby enhancing the capacity of ‘Rainier’ for flavor retention during refrigeration. Conversely, the differential metabolites in the cold-sensitive ‘Tieton’ were mainly enriched in pathways related to the ‘valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation’, ‘D-amino acid metabolism’, and ‘2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism’. The enhanced catabolism of branched-chain amino acids and unusual D-type amino acids may accelerate the continuous breakdown of organic acid precursors in the fruit, resulting in a rapid decrease in titratable acid content and a significant sugar-acid imbalance. Additionally, the intermediate metabolites generated from these degradation pathways are prone to converting into compounds associated with off-flavors during cold storage, thereby hastening the loss of the original fresh fruit flavor in ‘Tieton’. Collectively, the differential activation of these metabolic branches directly accounts for the differences in flavor deterioration rates between the two cultivars under identical cold storage conditions. These findings provide new mechanistic insights into genotype-dependent post-harvest metabolic differences in sweet cherries and serve as important references for future comparative research on other stone fruit germplasm resources.

Correlation analysis between key metabolites and physiological indices provided quantitative support for the metabolic‑physiological links identified in this study. Notably, methanesulfonic acid exhibited a significant positive correlation with titratable acidity (r = 0.728, p value < 0.05), confirming that this organic acid metabolite is a reliable representative of acid pool dynamics during cold storage and that organic acid degradation is a primary driver of TA decline, particularly in the cold‑sensitive ‘Tieton’ cultivar. Furthermore, L‑phenylalanine showed a significant negative correlation with fruit firmness (r = −0.649, p value < 0.01), consistent with its role as a precursor in lignin biosynthesis. In contrast, sugar‑related metabolites did not show significant linear correlations with SSC, likely because SSC is a composite index encompassing sugars, organic acids, and other soluble compounds.

In line with metabolomics findings, the number and expression trends of DEGs in the two sweet cherry cultivars varied significantly following cold storage. The ‘Tieton’ cultivar exhibited notable downregulation of DEGs early in the cold storage process, indicating a heightened sensitivity to transcriptional changes under low-temperature stress. Conversely, ‘Rainier’ demonstrated a more balanced pattern of up- and downregulation of gene expression, suggesting a more robust transcriptional regulatory system. This molecular distinction underlies the differences in cold storage tolerance between the two cultivars. Additionally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs during cold storage of sweet cherries were significantly enriched in pathways related to flavor metabolism, including ‘biosynthesis of secondary metabolites’ and ‘plant hormone signal transduction’. Furthermore, the expression patterns of core structural genes in sugar, acid, and lignin metabolic pathways directly influenced the differential accumulation of flavor-related metabolites.

Previous studies have established that the phosphofructokinase gene is associated with the accumulation of fruit sugars, and its overexpression can influence photosynthesis and carbon metabolism in fruit30. In this study, we identified PavPKF (FUN_031520) as a gene whose expression gradually decreased with prolonged cold storage in both varieties, suggesting its potential role as a key factor in SSC accumulation. The ATP-citrate lyase gene has been shown to participate in fruit citrate metabolism31. Our findings reveal that PavACL (FUN_001201) exhibited the lowest expression levels during the mid-to-late stages of cold storage, suggesting its involvement in TA changes in sweet cherries. Additionally, lignin is crucial for maintaining fruit firmness traits32,33. We discovered that genes linked to the lignin metabolic pathway played a role in sweet cherry fruit responses to cold storage, with notable changes in gene expression observed during the mid- to late-storage period. This underscores the significant influence of lignin metabolism on fruit firmness. Collectively, these results indicate that differential gene expression directly contributes to metabolic disturbances in sugars, acids, and other compounds.

Volatile compounds play a vital role in determining the flavor quality of sweet cherries. This study identified 45 DVCs, confirming that fruity and sweet notes are the primary attributes of sweet cherry flavor. While previous research has largely focused on measuring and summarizing the volatile compounds across various sweet cherry varieties34, this investigation specifically examined the patterns of volatile compound changes in two sweet cherry varieties during cold storage.

The compound 2-hexen-1-ol, known for its fruity, fresh aroma, was found to decrease with prolonged storage in the ‘Rainier’ variety, whereas it was not detected in ‘Tieton’. This difference is likely attributable to cultivar variation, as noted in past studies in which it was present in ‘Regina’ and ‘Sweetheart’ but absent in other cherry germplasm resources5,34,35. Furthermore, this study identified 1H-pyrrole, 2-methyl-, which exhibited changes in both varieties; this compound has not been previously reported in sweet cherry research. The identification of these shared and unique volatiles offers new insights into enhancing the quality of sweet cherries.

In this study, we developed a molecular regulatory network focused on TFs during the cold storage of sweet cherries. We identified key TFs, including PavMYB3, PavMYB13, and PavC2H2, and examined their interactions with key metabolic genes. MYB transcription factors play a vital role in the fruit’s response to low-temperature treatments, while C2H2 transcription factors are linked to plant reactions to cold stress. For example, the overexpression of MdMYB73 and MdMYB108L improves cold tolerance in apple callus36–38. Notably, MdMYB108L directly regulates MdCBF3 expression by binding its promoter36. Additionally, MaC2H2-like is involved in the low-temperature response of ‘Fenjiao’ banana by regulating genes involved in cell wall degradation39. While these studies have provided preliminary insights into the significant roles of MYB and C2H2, they have not explored post-harvest flavor regulation in sweet cherries or investigated their potential regulatory mechanisms during fruit cold storage.

This study demonstrates, for the first time, that PavMYB3 and PavMYB13 act as candidate positive regulators of sweet cherry flavor by maintaining stable accumulation of flavor metabolites and delaying quality deterioration. In contrast, PavC2H2 acts as a candidate negative regulator, accelerating the degradation and loss of organic acids. The differential expression patterns of these key transcription factors are crucial in explaining the variations in flavor deterioration observed among different sweet cherry cultivars. This research provides evidence of the functional roles of these transcription factors in regulating post-harvest flavor in sweet cherries, offering valuable genetic resources for future biotechnological advancements to improve fruit quality.

However, it is important to note that the current study only examines two cultivars, ‘Rainier’ and ‘Tieton’. While these two varieties represent contrasting quality types (blush-type vs. red, high SSC vs. high firmness), they do not encompass the full spectrum of genetic diversity within sweet cherries. Therefore, the generality of the identified regulatory networks should be further validated using a broader panel of newly introduced and promising cultivars. Such future studies will also help establish variety‑specific postharvest preservation strategies tailored to diverse market demands.

In summary, this study established a multi‑omics framework to elucidate the mechanisms of cultivar‑specific flavor deterioration in cold‑stored sweet cherries. Key findings include: (1) fruit softening and sugar–acid imbalance as primary quality indicators; (2) divergent metabolic reprogramming in starch/sucrose and phenylpropanoid pathways; (3) 45 differential volatile compounds characterized; and (4) a regulatory network identifying PavMYB3/13 as candidate positive and PavC2H2 as candidate negative flavor regulators. These results provide a theoretical foundation for variety‑targeted preservation strategies and molecular breeding to improve cold tolerance and flavor quality.

Methods

Fruit Materials

In this study, the sweet cherries consisted of two widely recognized commercial varieties: ‘Rainier’ (blush-type) and ‘Tieton’ (red-type). Fruits were carefully selected based on criteria of uniform ripeness, intact appearance, and absence of mechanical damage, pests, or disease infestations for low-temperature treatment at 4 ± 1 °C. The cherries were placed randomly into perforated plastic containers, each holding 500 g. Each variety was then divided into seven independent batches, with three boxes per batch. The first batch served as the 0-day untreated control sample. Subsequently, one batch was collected at each of the following time points: 2, 4, 7, 10, 14, and 21 days post-treatment. These samples were utilized for phenotypic, physiological, omics, and volatile compound analyses.

Measurement of flavor-related phenotypic traits

The identification of deteriorated fruits was primarily based on external phenotypic traits: fruits with surface browning lesions, fruit rot, dehydration shrivel, or visible fungal contamination were directly counted as deteriorated individuals when calculating the deterioration rate8,40. The deterioration rate was calculated as: Deterioration Rate (%) = (Number of deteriorated fruits/Total fruits) × 100.

The soluble solids content was measured using a refractometer (ATAGO, Japan), with results expressed in °Brix. Titratable acidity was assessed using a Titrator Excellence T5 potentiometric titrator (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Titration with 0.1 M NaOH was performed to determine the titratable acids, expressed as grams of malic acid per 100 grams of fresh weight.

Fruit firmness was measured with the FirmTech FT7 (UP GmbH, Germany), and results were expressed in g·mm−1.

Volatile component analysis

Volatile components from fruit samples were extracted using headspace solid-phase microextraction (SPME) and subsequently analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), as outlined by Feng et al.34 Initially, the fruit samples were ground to a powder in liquid nitrogen, and 0.5 g of this powder was placed into a 20-mL headspace vial. Then, 2 mL of saturated NaCl solution and 20 µL of an internal standard solution (10 μg·mL−1) were added. The internal standard, 3-hexanone-2,2,4,4-d4, was prepared from a hexane stock solution. The extraction process was conducted for 15 min using a 120 µm DVB/CWR/PDMS Arrow (Agilent) capillary column for subsequent analysis41. After extraction, the volatile compounds were identified and quantified relative to an internal standard using an Agilent 8890 GC coupled with a 7000D mass spectrometer (Agilent). The gas chromatography system was equipped with a 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm DB-5MS capillary column (5% phenyl-polymethylsiloxane). Helium served as the carrier gas, with flow rates consistent with the established reference. Mass spectrometry was conducted in electron impact (EI) ionization mode at 70 eV. Analysis and quantification were performed in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. The identification of volatile compounds was facilitated by comparing mass spectra with databases such as MWGC and NIST. The relative odor activity values (rOAVs) for the identified DVCs were calculated using published odor thresholds, as described in our previous study34. This approach helps clarify the contribution of each aroma compound to the sample’s overall aromatic profile.

Metabolomics assay

In accordance with the protocols established by Chen et al.1 and Feng et al.42, fruit samples were first ground in liquid nitrogen. A total of 100 mg of the ground samples was weighed and then combined with 1500 μL pre-chilled 70% methanol aqueous solution for metabolite extraction. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s every 30 min, completing a total of 6 cycles. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane into injection vials for subsequent analysis. Metabolite identification and quantitative analysis were carried out using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system equipped with an SB-C18 column (Agilent, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 µm) and the Metware database (MWDB)43. Instrument settings adhered to established reference parameters. Principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed utilizing R packages. Differential metabolites (DMs) were identified based on the following criteria: variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1, |log2 fold change| ≥ 1, and p value < 0.05. Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG compound database, and these annotated metabolites were subsequently mapped to the KEGG pathway database44. Correlation analysis between key metabolites and physiological indices was performed using SPSS software (v29.0).

Transcriptomics analysis

Following the kit instructions, total RNA was extracted from fruit samples using the Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology, Beijing, China). After verifying the purity and integrity of the RNA, cDNA library construction and Illumina HiSeq sequencing were carried out by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The raw sequencing data were filtered to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences, yielding clean data. HISTA2 was utilized to align the cleaned reads to the sweet cherry reference genome (Prunus avium ‘Tieton’ Genome v2.0 Assembly & Annotation)45,46, with gene expression levels quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM)47. DESeq2 was applied to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between storage periods and controls across varieties, as well as among varieties within the same storage period, using the criteria of |log2 fold change | ≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.0148,49. KEGG pathway enrichment and Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analyses were conducted using KOBAS and Gene Ontology terms50,51. Potential transcription factors (TFs) were predicted based on the PlantTFDB database52.

Correlation analysis and regulatory network construction

To identify transcription factors (TFs) that significantly influence the metabolome during cold storage, we performed two‑way orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) analysis integrating transcriptomic (FPKM values) and metabolomic (relative abundance) datasets using the OmicsPLS 2.0.2 package in R (v4.2.0). The O2PLS loading plots were visualized using the Metware Cloud Platform (https://cloud.metware.cn).

To construct the regulatory network, we first identified structural genes involved in sugar, acid, and lignin metabolism based on KEGG pathway annotations. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted on TFs and structural genes using the Metware Cloud Platform (https://cloud.metware.cn), applying screening criteria of r ≥ 0.65 and a p value < 0.05. Based on the identified significant correlations, a key regulatory network was constructed in Cytoscape (v3.9.1), featuring nodes representing DEGs and differentially regulated TFs, with edges indicating significant correlations47.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data visualization was performed using GraphPad 10.0.1. Significance analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey test for multiple comparisons. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences at the p value < 0.05 level.
