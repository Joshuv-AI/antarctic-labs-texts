Predicting blood pressure variability in hemodialysis using an explainable boosting machine model
Authors: Wun-Yi Huang, Cheng-Jui Lin, Yu-Xiang Zheng, Hung-Ting Chen, Fang-Ju Sun, Chih-Jen Wu, Kun-Pin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntradialytic hypotension and hypertension significantly increase cardiovascular risk in hemodialysis patients, highlighting the need for effective monitoring systems. By predicting systolic blood pressure (SBP) values, monitoring capabilities can be improved beyond traditional event classification. Despite this potential advantage, current predictive models report a consistent error range (11–13 mmHg) and operate as black boxes. This study aims to develop a transparent SBP prediction model and evaluate its generalizability.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 524 patients across metropolitan and suburban hospital branches (2016–19), collecting hemodialysis parameters, vital signs and predialytic measurements. The Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) was used as the primary model to predict SBP changes. Cross-branch validation was implemented to evaluate model generalizability and perform feature selection. INTRODUCTION

Intradialytic hypotension (IDH) and hypertension (IDHTN) are common complications in patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD) [1–5]. IDH/IDHTN not only compromise the quality of dialysis treatment but also increase the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality among HD patients [5–8]. Given the severity of IDH/IDHTN, developing reliable blood pressure monitoring and early warning systems has become crucial in HD care.

Our research team pioneered the first machine learning-based IDH prediction and early warning system in 2018 [9]. Subsequently, numerous studies have attempted to enhance IDH and/or IDHTN prediction accuracy using various machine learning approaches, ranging from conventional algorithms to sophisticated deep learning architectures [10–14]. However, defining IDH/IDHTN during HD sessions remains challenging, as there are no universal standards for IDH/IDHTN and patients may respond differently to similar blood pressure changes [7, 15, 16]. This heterogeneity in patient responses makes the traditional binary classification of IDH/IDHTN potentially problematic.

As research continues to address the challenges in IDH/IDHTN classification, predictive modeling of systolic blood pressure (SBP) values has emerged as an alternative approach. This approach potentially enables the establishment of personalized warning thresholds that account for individual patient differences. However, these predictive models are limited by prediction errors ranging from 11 to 13 mmHg and most operate as black boxes, lacking transparency in their decision-making processes [17–21]. Given that dramatic blood pressure changes during HD can be managed through adjustments of critical dialysis parameters such as ultrafiltration (UF) rate and dialysis time [1, 22], black-box models provide insufficient guidance for effective clinical intervention. These models cannot quantify the relationship between specific parameter adjustments and blood pressure response, limiting clinicians' ability to make evidence-based decisions during treatment.

To address the transparency requirement, our research team previously developed an interpretable blood pressure prediction model using multiple linear regression, which achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of <12.5 mmHg while providing interpretable insights into the relationships between clinical parameters and blood pressure changes [17]. However, this approach had two limitations. First, it assumed linear relationships between variables and blood pressure changes. Second, the validation was restricted to a single dialysis center, limiting generalizability to other clinical settings.

In order to overcome the limitations of previous studies, we employed the Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) algorithm [23], which can capture non-linear relationships while maintaining interpretability through its additive structure. To demonstrate generalizability beyond a single institution, we conducted cross-branch validation using data from metropolitan and suburban hospital settings with different patient populations and clinical characteristics in this current study.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants and data collection

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Mackay Memorial Hospital (18MMHIS184e). In order to evaluate the generalizability of the methods, we retrospectively collected HD data from two HD units of Mackay Memorial Hospital branches between 2016 and 2019. The Taipei Branch, located in an urban area and designated as the “metropolitan” branch, initially included 366 participants with 126 527 HD sessions. The Tamsui Branch, situated in a suburban area and designated as the “suburban” branch, initially included 210 participants with 77 967 sessions. The collected data of HD sessions comprised three main categories of parameters: dialysis parameters monitored during HD, clinical parameters measured before HD and laboratory blood tests.

Dialysis parameters and vital signs were continuously monitored throughout each HD session via a Vital Information Portal (VIP) gateway device, as described in our previous studies [17]. Vital signs monitoring included SBP, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and pulse rate. The dialysis machine parameters comprised UF rate, blood flow rate, conductivity and venous pressure. The elapsed time from the start of the HD session (Time) and time intervals between blood pressure measurements (ΔTime) were also recorded. Additionally, UF goal (target UF volume) and total UF volume were tracked during each session. These time-series values are collectively referred to as dynamic features in the following analysis.

Clinical parameters measured before each dialysis session included predialytic weight and body temperature. These parameters remained constant throughout individual HD sessions in the analysis.

Laboratory blood tests comprised 24 biochemical and hematological parameters: alkaline phosphatase, alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, carbon dioxide, calcium, creatinine, ferritin, fasting glucose, hemoglobin, hematocrit, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, serum phosphate, platelets, potassium, serum iron, sodium, total iron-binding capacity, total cholesterol, total protein, triglycerides, uric acid and white blood cells.

In the first screening step, we removed sessions with blood pressure values outside the normal measurement range (30–200 mmHg) as described in our previous study [24]. Further screening removed participants and sessions without complete laboratory test results. The final analysis included 333 participants with 71 969 sessions from the metropolitan branch and 191 participants with 52 706 sessions from the suburban branch (shown in Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study. Data screening process followed by cross-branch validation for model development and evaluation.

Data preprocessing and feature engineering

For raw data preprocessing, we addressed missing values by using the last observation carried forward method. To enhance our feature set, we generated additional features based on both current and historical values within the HD session. Specifically, for each time point, we utilized three previous values to create lagged features and first-order difference features. When historical data within the session were unavailable, we used the closest available values for the imputation of lagged features and set incalculable differences to zero. This approach was applied to all monitored parameters (vital signs and dialysis machine parameters), resulting in 65 time-series features. Combined with static clinical parameters, we had 91 initial features. Our target variable was the change in SBP between current and subsequent measurements (as illustrated in Fig. 2).

Feature engineering and the prediction task. Time-series feature construction using a historical window of three previous measurements (blue region) for predicting blood pressure change (red).

For model evaluation, we benchmarked EBM against five common machine learning approaches implemented in Python packages (version 3.13.2): Linear Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Support Vector Regressor (scikit-learn version 1.6.1 [27]) and XGBoost (xgboost version 3.0.2 [28]). Performance was assessed using multiple metrics: MAE, root mean square error (RMSE), Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). All models were initially evaluated using default hyperparameters and trained on identical feature sets. To ensure fair comparison, hyperparameter optimization was performed for all models using the Optuna framework (version 4.3.0) with Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampling [29]. We employed patient-based 5-fold cross-validation where patients were randomly assigned to folds, ensuring complete separation between training and validation sets at the patient level to prevent data leakage from multiple sessions of the same patient. The optimization process aimed to minimize MAE across validation folds. Detailed hyperparameter search spaces and optimal parameters are provided in Supplementary data, Tables S1–S6. All models were evaluated under both default and optimized hyperparameter configurations.

Experimental design

We implemented a cross-branch validation framework to evaluate model generalizability across different hospital settings. Complementary experiments were conducted by alternating metropolitan and suburban branch data as training and testing sets. In each experiment, we selected features based on their average absolute contributions in the EBM model and MAE performance from patient-based 5-fold cross-validation, and then trained the final model on the entire training dataset before evaluating its performance on the test dataset from the other branch.

RESULTS

Study population and baseline characteristics

This study included 524 patients (333 metropolitan and 191 suburban) with 124 675 HD sessions and 898 870 blood pressure measurements. Both branches showed comparable demographic characteristics in gender distribution, diabetes mellitus and hypertension prevalence. Metropolitan patients were slightly older (66±14 vs 63 ± 14 years, P = .015). Notable differences between metropolitan and suburban branches were observed in elapsed dialysis time (234.0 vs 248.9 min), UF rate (0.58 ± 0.18 vs 0.77 ± 0.28 L/h) and initial SBP (145±17 vs 151.5 mmHg), all P < .001 except for initial SBP (P = .009). Dialysate conductivity remained similar between branches (13.9 ms/cm) (shown in Table 1).

Feature selection

We systematically evaluated feature importance using EBM to optimize model performance. Starting with 91 features, we found that reducing to 16 dynamic features focusing on current measurements, historical values and dialysis parameters achieved comparable or superior performance. In internal validation using patient-based 5-fold cross-validation, the 16-feature model without static clinical parameters achieved the lowest MAE (metropolitan: 10.36 ± 0.16 mmHg; suburban: 11.30 ± 0.27 mmHg). When training on metropolitan data and testing on suburban data, the 16-feature model achieved MAE of 11.33 mmHg and RMSE of 14.93 mmHg. Similar performance was observed in reverse validation (suburban to metropolitan), with MAE of 10.57 mmHg and RMSE of 13.96 mmHg (as shown in Table 2). We tested combinations of dynamic features, predialytic weight, body temperature and laboratory tests. Removing laboratory tests and static clinical parameters (predialytic weight and body temperature) did not compromise model performance. Both metropolitan and suburban branches selected the same 16 key features. Based on these results, we selected the 16 dynamic features (Fig. 3A) as our final feature set.

To ensure fair comparison, we performed comprehensive hyperparameter optimization for all models using Optuna framework with patient-based cross-validation (detailed hyperparameter search spaces and optimized parameters in Supplementary data, Tables S1–S6).

Before optimization, we evaluated the models using internal validation (Supplementary data, Table S7, rows with minus signs) and external validation (Table 3). EBM achieved the best performance among all models in both validation directions (metropolitan-to-suburban: MAE 11.33 mmHg; suburban-to-metropolitan: MAE 10.57 mmHg). XGBoost demonstrated the closest performance to EBM, while Decision Tree consistently showed the highest prediction errors (Table 3).

After optimization, we also evaluated the models using internal validation (Supplementary Table S7, rows with plus signs) and external validation (Table 3). XGBoost achieved the best overall performance (MAE: 11.27 and 10.52 mmHg), while EBM consistently ranked second to third among all models. Most models showed performance improvements after hyperparameter tuning, with Random Forest and XGBoost demonstrating the most substantial gains (0.17–0.31 mmHg and 0.11–0.14 mmHg improvement in MAE, respectively), while Decision Tree also benefited with 0.09–0.13 mmHg improvement (Table 3).

To statistically compare the default EBM against the top-performing tuned XGBoost, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to the paired prediction errors. Although the performance difference of 0.05–0.06 mmHg was statistically significant (P < .001), the effect size was negligible (Cohen’s d = 0.002–0.007, Supplementary data, Table S8), indicating that this difference lacks practical importance. To assess EBM’s own stability to hyperparameter tuning, a similar analysis showed that the 0.01 mmHg performance improvement after tuning was also practically insignificant, with a negligible effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.002–0.004, Supplementary data, Table S8). This result demonstrates the EBM model is exceptionally stable compared with other models.

Given EBM’s competitive performance, exceptional stability, and built-in interpretability advantages, we selected the default EBM hyperparameter configuration for all subsequent analyses.

Model interpretability and case study

The additive structure of the EBM model allows us to quantify each feature’s contribution to blood pressure predictions at both the overall model level and for individual predictions. By averaging the absolute contribution values across the training dataset, we identified the most influential features with mean absolute scores expressed in mmHg units.

After feature selection as described in Table 2, both branches utilized identical 16 individual features, demonstrating consistent feature relevance across different clinical settings. The final EBM model automatically generates pairwise interaction terms based on these selected features, resulting in a complete model structure of 32 terms: 16 individual feature shape functions, 15 automatically generated pairwise interaction terms and 1 intercept. Supplementary data, Fig. S1 displays the global importance scores for all 32 EBM terms across both metropolitan and suburban training branches. Though the importance rankings showed slight variations between the two models, the 15 interaction terms contributed an average of <1 mmHg each across both branches. Furthermore, most interaction contributions were smaller than those of individual feature terms. This indicates that the primary predictive power derives from main effects rather than complex feature interactions.

Therefore, we concentrate our discussion on the main effects and present the metropolitan-trained model’s individual feature contributions in Fig. 3A. The most influential features were current SBP measurement (SBP at T0) and its first-order difference (ΔSBP at T0), with mean absolute scores of 2.84 and 2.80 mmHg, respectively. The change in total UF volume (ΔTotal UF volume at T0) ranked third in contribution (1.71 mmHg), followed by elapsed dialysis time (1.36 mmHg) and current pulse rate (1.21 mmHg). Supplementary data, Fig. S2 presents the nonlinear shape functions for these top five most influential individual features, illustrating their relationships with predicted blood pressure changes.

To demonstrate the model’s clinical utility, we present a representative case where the model achieved a session MAE of 5.11 mmHg (shown in Fig. 3B). The time-series visualization illustrates the model’s ability to accurately track SBP trends throughout the dialysis session, capturing the gradual decrease from 161 mmHg at 79 min to 145 mmHg (predicted SBP = 148.7 mmHg) at 141 min. A waterfall plot (Fig. 3C) shows how individual features contribute to this specific prediction, displaying that ΔTotal UF volume at T0 contributed –4.50 mmHg while ΔSBP at T0 contributed +3.99 mmHg to the final predicted value, with other features providing smaller adjustments.

Error analysis of EBM models

Previous blood pressure prediction studies during HD have consistently reported MAE values between 11.29 and 13.47 mmHg across various machine learning approaches [17–21], suggesting a potential ceiling for prediction accuracy in this domain. To understand the factors behind this apparent limitation, we investigated potential patterns related to these prediction limitations through patient-level analysis. Our examination revealed positive correlations (Pearson’s r: 0.64–0.73, all P < .001, shown in Fig. 4A) between patient-wise mean and standard deviation of session MAE, indicating that patients with higher average prediction errors also showed greater variation in prediction accuracy across their dialysis sessions.

(B) Correlation between blood pressure variability and model prediction errors: metropolitan model evaluated on suburban data (n = 191, r = 0.87, P = 6.8 × 10−60) and suburban model evaluated on metropolitan data (n = 333, r = 0.81, P = 1.5 × 10−79).

Given the performance variation, we hypothesized that patients with higher blood pressure variability during HD sessions might be more challenging for the model to predict accurately. When examining blood pressure variability in relation to prediction accuracy, we found that patients with larger blood pressure fluctuations during HD sessions also had higher prediction errors. This pattern was consistently observed across both branches, with strong positive correlations between patient-wise mean of session SBP standard deviation and patient-wise mean of session MAE in both metropolitan and suburban populations (Pearson’s r: 0.81–0.87, all P < .001, shown in Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed an explainable blood pressure prediction model for HD patients using cross-branch validation with retrospective data from metropolitan and suburban hospital branches. We applied EBM models to predict SBP changes during dialysis. After feature selection, our EBM model achieved competitive performance compared with five other machine learning methods, demonstrating superior accuracy and correlation. The model provided transparency through a waterfall plot for prediction reasoning, while error analysis revealed that blood pressure variability strongly correlated with prediction errors.

Advantages: performance, practicality and interpretability

Our model demonstrates advantages over previous approaches in three key areas: performance, practicality and interpretability. Regarding performance, our EBM models achieved an MAE of 10.57–11.33 mmHg in cross-branch validation. Though using different datasets, previous studies utilizing conventional machine learning techniques reported higher error ranges: Linear Regression (12.23 mmHg), LASSO (12.16 mmHg), Random Forest (12.14 mmHg), SVR (12.99 mmHg) and XGBoost (11.32–13.47 mmHg [21]). Even deep learning approaches showed slightly higher prediction errors (11.29–11.58 mmHg [18, 20]) compared with our model.

A key practical advantage is our model’s use of only 16 dynamic features during dialysis, enabling immediate application to new patients without prior records. Our analysis showed that removing static clinical parameters did not compromise model performance, indicating limited informative value of unchanging variables within the session. Furthermore, this study is pioneering in validating blood pressure prediction models across dialysis units from different hospital branches, demonstrating the approach’s robustness and potential for broader implementation. Notably, EBM’s minimal performance (0.01 mmHg) variation before and after hyperparameter optimization, combined with its consistently high ranking across performance metrics, demonstrates exceptional model stability that enables instant deployment in clinical settings without requiring time-intensive parameter tuning.

In terms of interpretability, the EBM model is conceptually similar to an additive scoring system in clinical practice, such as Early Warning Scores (EWS). However, unlike traditional scoring systems where weights are manually assigned by experts, in EBM each term’s contribution is automatically learned from data.

Among machine learning approaches, decision trees have traditionally been considered interpretable due to their explicit decision paths. However, our hyperparameter optimization revealed that achieving competitive performance required complex tree structures (optimal max depth: 15–29, max leaf nodes: 245–264, Supplementary data, Table S3), significantly reducing their interpretability advantage. Random Forests, as ensemble methods aggregating predictions from multiple decision trees (optimal number of estimators: 144–332, Supplementary data, Table S4), cannot provide instance-specific explanations due to their ensemble voting mechanism. In contrast, EBM operates with only 32 interpretable components (16 individual features + 15 pairwise interactions + 1 intercept, Supplementary data, Fig. S1), representing a more manageable structure for clinical interpretation.

Both tree-based and other black-box models require post-hoc explanation methods like LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanation [30]) or SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations [31]) to quantify feature contributions for better understanding their reasoning process. However, recent studies have shown that LIME and SHAP can sometimes produce misleading explanations [32, 33]. Without requiring additional explanation methods, the EBM model provides inherent transparency to understand its reasoning through its additive structure.

Furthermore, the transparency of EBM’s shape functions reveals deeper insights into the nature of the prediction problem. Feature importance analysis confirms that the model’s predictive power is primarily driven by main effects, with minimal contribution from interaction terms (Supplementary data, Fig. S1). This finding explains why simpler models like linear regression achieve comparable performance (Table 3), suggesting that the underlying prediction problem is predominantly linear in nature. The linear interpretation is further supported by the near-linear shape functions of key features, such as current SBP and its first-order difference. However, EBM also captured non-linear patterns that a purely linear model would miss, such as the effects of elapsed time and pulse rate (Supplementary data, Fig. S2). The ability to learn linear effects while simultaneously capturing non-linearities demonstrates the strength of EBM’s glass-box nature.

In addition to its reasoning transparency, the EBM model’s predictions are closely aligned with medical understanding of HD physiology. Non-linear shape functions are shown in Supplementary data, Fig. S2. As ΔTotal UF volume increases, the model consistently shows a negative contribution to blood pressure, reflecting the clinical observation that excessive UF leads to intradialytic hypotension [34]. Similarly, the pulse rate shape function reveals physiological insights. Most intradialytic hypotension episodes occur in the late dialysis phase [1], and a previous study showed higher heart rates during the late phase [35]. The non-linear shape function of pulse rate in our model captures this inverse relationship between pulse rate and SBP change during HD. These findings demonstrate how the model accurately captures physiological hemodynamics, and this transparency makes the model trustworthy for clinical interpretation.

Limitations

Two limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our cross-branch validation demonstrated model generalizability between metropolitan and suburban settings, external validation from different hospital systems is needed to further confirm the model’s broad applicability. Second, although our model’s error range is comparable to or better than previous approaches, these prediction errors might still be significant for patients with highly unstable blood pressure patterns (as shown in Fig. 4), suggesting that a single general model may be insufficient for these patients and highlighting the need for patient-specific solutions.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on several key areas. First, developing personalized models and/or establishing individualized blood pressure alert thresholds for patients with higher blood pressure variability. By analyzing EBM’s local feature contributions alongside patients' clinical symptoms, we could potentially identify meaningful associations between specific contribution patterns and adverse events. Second, integrating this predictive system into existing HD monitoring platforms for clinical validation and evaluation of its impact on treatment decisions.

CONCLUSION

Our EBM model achieves accurate and generalizable blood pressure prediction across different clinical settings using dynamic features monitored during HD, with interpretable clinical insights into its predictions. However, findings suggest the need for patient-specific solutions for those with unstable blood pressure patterns.
