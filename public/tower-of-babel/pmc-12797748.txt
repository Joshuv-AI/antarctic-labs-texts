Polysomnographic assessment of sleep bruxism as a potential protective factor in primary snoring
Authors: Jakub Przegrałek, Marta Waliszewska-Prosół, Dorian Nowacki, Weronika Frosztęga, Katarzyna Madziarska, Helena Martynowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Primary snoring (PS), defined as snoring in non-apneic patients, often coexists with sleep bruxism (SB), which may have a protective role. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of SB on PS in adults with attention to its potential protective effect. Data from patients who underwent polysomnography between January 2018, and June 2025 were analyzed. Patients with PS were classified using two bruxism episode index (BEI) cut-offs: SB (BEI ≥ 2; n = 156) vs. non-SB (BEI < 2; n = 68) and severe SB (BEI ≥ 4; n = 99) vs. mild/no-SB (BEI < 4; n = 125). Snore train duration did not differ between groups. In the BEI ≥ 2 group, the snore index (overall and supine) was increased in non-rapid eye movement sleep (NREM) 1 (63.40 ± 61.11 vs. 55.16 ± 67.70; p = 0.0312 and 61.67 ± 66.42 vs. 48.50 ± 63.25; p = 0.0447 respectively) and decreased in NREM 2 and NREM 3 in non-supine position (34.81 ± 70.06 vs. 64.84 ± 107.23; p = 0.0460 and 48.29 ± 124.44 vs. 82.79 ± 171.65; p = 0.0154 respectively) when compared with control. Similar results were observed for the BEI = 4 cut-off. SB did not shorten snore trains duration. These findings suggest that SB influences upper airway dynamics, but further research is needed to clarify the possible protective effects.

Introduction

Snoring is an audible phenomenon caused by airway vibration that occurs during sleep [1, 2]. Snoring trains are periods in which multiple individual, subsequent snores occur one after another [3, 4]. Narrowing of the upper respiratory tract contributes to the generation of snoring sounds [5, 6]. Thus, snoring is associated with conditions that lead to partial or total airway obturation, including obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [7] and obesity [8, 9]. However, snoring may also occur in otherwise healthy, non-apneic individuals, referred to as primary snoring (PS) [1]. PS is generally considered not to have a major impact on the physical health of affected patients [1]; yet, a growing body of evidence suggests that it may be a risk factor for cardiovascular and metabolic disorders [8–10].

Sleep bruxism (SB) is an oromotor behaviour that is characterized by repetitive masticatory muscle activity during sleep [11]. Muscle activation during a bruxism episode manifests itself in stereotypical movements such as bracing or thrusting of the mandible, or teeth grinding [11, 12]. SB is not considered a disorder; however, it may serve as a risk or protective factor for other conditions [11–13]. Its prevalence has been estimated to affect up to 21% of the global population [14].

SB is often reported to be associated with various sleep-related breathing disorders, such as snoring [15] and OSA [16, 17]. Moreover, it is suggested in the literature that SB episodes may terminate instances of disrupted breathing by mechanically reopening obturated airways [11, 13, 18]. It is hypothesized that rhythmic masticatory muscle activity during SB episodes may induce transient mandibular advancement and increase upper airways muscles tone, and thereby interrupting airway collapse [13, 18].

Although the role of SB in the mechanisms of apnea–hypopnea events has been the subject of considerable debate [18, 19], its influence on airway obstruction in non-apneic snoring has received far less attention, with only a few studies examining the association between SB and PS in adults [3, 4, 15, 20–22]. Although some evidence in the literature indicates a positive correlation between snoring and SB in non-apneic subjects [3, 20], others have reported contradicting results [21, 22]. Nonetheless, evidence is accumulating that PS and SB often co-occur [3, 22]. However, little is known about the nature of their relationship. It seems reasonable to propose a hypothesis like that concerning the influence of SB on apnea–hypopnea events, namely, that bruxism episodes may exert a protective effect on snoring episodes by interrupting snore trains and thereby shortening their duration.

Given the limited number of studies focused on this issue, the primary aim of the present study is to evaluate the impact of SB on PS in adults, with particular attention to the potential protective role of SB occurrence in relation to snoring trains duration.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted using medical data from patients who underwent polysomnography in the Sleep Laboratory at the Department and Clinic of Diabetology, Hypertension, and Internal Medicine at Wroclaw Medical University in Poland between January 2018 and June 2025. Data from all patients were collected retrospectively and anonymized for the analysis. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wroclaw Medical University (KB 67/2025). Given the retrospective nature of the analysis and the absence of identifiable personal information, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional guidelines.

Eligibility criteria

Patients were included if they underwent single-night polysomnography, had diagnosed with PS and suspected SB, and were at least 18 years old at the time of admission to the clinic. Patients with obstructive sleep apnea (apnea-hypopnea index > 5) were excluded from the study (Fig. 1). Further exclusion criteria were applied to those who were taking sedatives, neuroleptics, analgesics, antidepressants, or muscle relaxants; women during pregnancy or breastfeeding, patients with severe mental and neurological disorders or who suffer from neuropathic pain, individuals with active malignancy or inflammation, and those with decompensated cardiovascular or systemic diseases.

Characteristics of the study population

After the enrollment process, 224 individuals were included in the study. The mean age of the study participants was 35.38 ± 10.44 years, ranging from 18 to 71 years old. Women constituted 77.6% (n = 174) of the study population, while men constituted 22.3% (n = 50). One of the included patients had diabetes, and 10 subjects had hypertension. Ischemic heart disease, history of heart infarcts, or stroke were not reported among the included individuals. 39 patients were overweight, 10 had first-degree obesity, and four had second-degree obesity. Polysomnography

All polysomnographic analyses were performed using the Nox-A1 system (Nox Medical, Reykjavík, Iceland), according to American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommendations [2, 23]. Polysomnograms were scored in 30-second epochs, and automated results were manually verified by a certified clinician (HM). Cortical arousals were assessed using electroencephalography (EEG) and were defined as EEG changes lasting ≥ 3 s after at least 10 s of stable sleep and exceeding >16 Hz. Heart rate and blood oxygen saturation were measured using the NONIN WristOx2 3150 pulse oximeter (Nonin Medical Inc., Plymouth, USA). Electro-oculography (EOG) monitored eye movements, respiratory effort was measured with thoracic and abdominal inductance belts, and body position was recorded using position sensors.

Snoring was recorded using an acoustic sensor and a nasal pressure transducer. Events were defined as breathing-synchronized vibratory sounds with an audible oscillatory component, clearly distinguishable from background noise. Both inspiratory and expiratory snoring were analyzed, excluding other sounds such as coughing, grunting, or movement noise. A sequence of several consecutive snores was classified as a snoring train and was automatically recorded.

SB was evaluated using audio, video, and electromyography (EMG) recordings. The disposable Ag/AgCl surface EMG electrodes were positioned on clean and prepared skin along the muscle fiber of the masseter muscle and at the submental region to evaluate masticatory muscle tone. Active and reference electrode were spread at least 2 cm apart from each other’s. EMG events were considered SB episodes when the amplitude peak was at least twice the background amplitude. SB events were considered separate episodes if there were pauses of at least 3 s between them. According to the definitions in the AASM guidelines [2], bruxism episodes were categorized as phasic, tonic, or mixed. A tonic episode was defined as a sustained EMG activity lasting >2 s, a phasic episode as ≥ 3 bursts of 0.25–2 s with total duration >2 s, and a mixed episode as the coexistence of tonic and phasic features. Event scoring was verified using simultaneous video and audio recordings, with manual exclusion of non-bruxism events such as snoring, swallowing, or other orofacial movements. The diagnosis of SB was determined using the bruxism episode index (BEI), calculated as the number of bruxism episodes per hour of sleep. BEI values below 2 indicated insignificant SB, values between 2 and 4 denoted mild SB, and values of 4 or higher corresponded to severe SB.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed in Statistica v13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, USA). Participants were divided into two groups according to BEI values, with cut-off points being set at 2 and, in a separate analysis, at 4. Group comparisons were carried out using either the independent-samples Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on whether assumptions for parametric testing were met. Normality of distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and variance homogeneity with Levene’s test. Where distributions deviated from normality, a Box–Cox transformation was applied; if assumptions remained unmet, non-parametric tests were used. Linear relationships between continuous variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. To minimize the risk of type I error associated with multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction with Li & Ji adjustment for correlated variables was applied within physiologically related outcome families. The results remained consistent following this procedure. Additionally, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) models was performed to control for potential confounding effects of age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and total sleep time (TST). Two BEI-based grouping factors (BEI ≥ 2 and BEI ≥ 4) were tested as fixed effects for the main snoring outcomes (Snore index, number of snore trains per hour and average snore trains duration).

Results

Participants were classified according to two independent BEI cut-offs. For the first comparison, subjects were dichotomized into patients with SB (BEI ≥ 2; n = 156) and patients without SB (BEI < 2; n = 68). For the second comparison, subjects were divided into patients with severe SB (BEI ≥ 4; n = 99) and patients with no or mild SB (BEI < 4; n = 125). This type of independent stratification has been previously applied in the literature [3, 12] and is consistent with both Lavigne’s recommendations [24] and the SB severity levels outlined in the ASMM guidelines [2]. Discussion

To the best of the author’s knowledge, this study is the first to assess the potentially protective influence of SB occurrence on snore trains duration in non-apneic snoring individuals. It has been proposed in the literature that sleep SB episodes may protect against airway obstruction during apnea-hypopnea events by mechanically terminating it11,13 and inducing arousals [25]. According to this assumption, snoring, as an equivalent of mild obstruction [1, 5], should also be disrupted by the occurrence of SB. However, our observations do not confirm this phenomenon, as the duration of snore trains did not differ between the SB and non-SB groups throughout the recording and within individual sleep stages. These results suggest that SB may not be a protective factor, at least in the mild form of obturation. A possible explanation for the lack of a terminating effect of SB in the PS subjects is the absence of mechanisms thought to trigger SB episodes in response to apnea–hypopnea events, such as decreased arousal threshold [25, 26] and hypoxia [26–28]. Nevertheless, further research is required to assess the temporal relationship between snoring and SB episodes and clarify this association.

The second main observation of this study is that the impact of SB on snoring appears to be sleep-specific and position-dependent. Snoring was more pronounced in NREM 1 among patients with SB, particularly in the supine position, and the percentage of snore trains in this stage and position was also increased in the BEI ≥ 2 group. However, this effect was not evident in those with severe SB, suggesting a non-linear association between SB severity and snoring. In deeper stages of sleep (NREM 2 and NREM 3), patients with SB demonstrated lower snoring indexes in the non-supine position compared with controls. A similar pattern was observed for snore trains, which were relatively less frequent in the non-supine position during NREM 2 (in both the BEI ≥ 2 and the BEI ≥ 4 groups) and during NREM 3 (in the BEI ≥ 4 group) compared with the respective controls. Interestingly, previous studies in the non-apneic snoring patients without SB indicated a more frequent occurrence of snoring in the deeper stages of sleep, regardless of sleep position [29, 30]. In the present study, non-supine position appears to be an important factor in reducing the amount of snore trains, as over the whole recording, the SB patients had a reduced number of snore trains in the non-supine position. These observations regarding position influence on snoring are consistent with previous findings. Michalek-Zrabkowska et al. indicated that snoring, including snore trains, occurs more frequently in the supine position compared with the non-supine position in PS subjects [3]. These changes were also sleep stage dependent, as the snoring index in NREM 1 and NREM 2 differed significantly between non-supine and supine sleep positions [3]. Additionally, in the aforementioned study [3], SB events were correlated with the supine position, which is in agreement with evidence from the literature [31, 32]. The other reports comparing the effect of sleep position on snoring in non-apneic patients also showed a positive impact of the non-supine position in the PS group [29, 33]. Taken together, it could be hypothesized that the supine position aggravates both SB and snoring. However, this effect might be strictly correlated with particular sleep stages or different unidentified factors, as Li et al. results indicated that supine position is not a significant risk factor for SB occurrence in PS individuals [22].

The potential influence of sleep stage on the interaction between SB and PS remains largely unexplored. In the present study, we found that, in patients with BEI ≥ 2, the overall snore index was higher during NREM 1 and lower during NREM 3 compared to those with BEI < 2. To date, the only study directly assessing both SB and PS in different sleep stages reported that snoring increases the probability of SB episodes during rapid-eye movement (REM) sleep [21]. However, this finding contrasts with the typical distribution of events, as snoring occurs predominantly in NREM 2 and 3 and is infrequent in REM sleep stage [29, 30, 33], whereas SB episodes are most commonly observed in NREM 1 and NREM 2 stages [34]. In our study, only the snore train percentage in the supine position was increased in the REM sleep stage.

Other factors potentially associated with SB incidence in PS individuals have also been discussed in the literature. A study by Huang et al. did not identify any risk factors for SB occurrence in PS subjects; however, this analysis relied solely on questionnaires [15]. In contrast, polysomnography-based studies have indicated that age, sleep duration, and gender are significant risk factors for SB in the PS population [4, 20, 22]. Specifically, men with PS presented a higher bruxism episode index and more often had severe SB than women with PS4. Younger individuals with PS also showed a higher likelihood of coexisting SB compared with older subjects [4, 22]. The role of sleep duration remains unclear, as one study reported an association between shorter sleep duration and increased SB occurrence [7], whereas another found the opposite [20]. In this context, the supine position may aggravate the occurrence of both snoring and SB episodes, with possible additive interplay between them. However, this effect is likely influenced by other clinical factors and may be significant only in specific patient groups.

Sleep fragmentation may modulate both sleep bruxism [35] and snoring activity [36]. In our cohort, weak correlations (r < 0.3) were observed between fragmentation indices (arousal index, WASO, sleep efficiency) and BEI or certain types of bruxism episodes, while no meaningful associations were found with snoring parameters. Thus, the observed relationships are unlikely to result from generalized sleep instability, although this possibility cannot be entirely excluded. Nevertheless, given previous reports linking arousal frequency to both SB and PS, further studies explicitly designed to assess these interactions are warranted. In addition, weak negative correlations were found between mixed bruxism episodes and several snoring parameters (r < 0.3). Although of limited strength, these associations may indicate subtle interactions between mixed bruxism and upper airway stability during NREM sleep, a hypothesis that should be verified in future studies.

The main strengths of this study are the inclusion of the large study population (n = 224) and the implementation of objective, instrumental assessment of SB and PS using polysomnography. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study examining the effect of SB on PS in the context of possible protective measures, opening a novel direction that expands our understanding in a relatively unexplored area of sleep research. Despite these advantages, the study also suffers from some limitations. The main limitation lies in its retrospective design, which prevents control for certain confounding factors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption. Detailed analysis of sleep bruxism distribution across sleep stages and body positions was also not available due to the lack of sufficient data. Additionally, for organizational reasons, the patients were not given an adaptation night prior to the polysomnographic examination. This may have resulted in a first-night effect, potentially meaningfully disrupting the detection of bruxism events, especially in individuals with low rhythmic masticatory muscle activity values [37]. Furthermore, unfamiliar sleep environment may have also influenced snoring. Moreover, a female predominance and relatively young age of participants were observed in all study groups, which could have influenced the results and limited their generalizability to the general population, as age and gender are known to affect sleep parameters [4].

Conclusion

The findings of the present study imply that SB may not act as a factor meaningfully reducing snore train duration. Instead, SB tends to influence snoring in a stage- and position-dependent manner and may be associated with increased snoring in NREM 1 (particularly in the supine position) and reduced snoring indices in non-supine NREM 2 and NREM 3 sleep. SB may be related to modifications in upper airway behavior that are not necessarily protective; however, the present data are observational and insufficient to determine the causal nature or direction of this relationship. Future studies assessing the temporal relationship between snoring and SB episodes as well as studies on more diverse populations are necessary to clarify whether a protective effect exists. Furthermore, the limited and contradictory findings in the literature highlight the need for further extensive research.
