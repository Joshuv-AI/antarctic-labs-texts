An Algorithmic Approach to Inner Derivations of Low-Dimensional Zinbiel Algebras
Bouzid Mosbahi 1 , Ahmed Zahari 2

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the concept of inner derivations of low-dimensional Zinbiel algebras and investigate their properties. The primary objective of this study is to develop an algorithm to characterize the inner derivations of any n-dimensional Zinbiel algebra in matrix form. Additionally, we apply this algorithm to two, three and four-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebras, providing explicit descriptions of their inner derivations.

Introduction

Zinbiel algebras were introduced by Loday in 1995
[
1
]
while studying the cup product in Leibniz cohomology. These algebras are non-associative structures that are Koszul dual to Leibniz algebras, creating an important connection between their operads
[
2
]
. Since their discovery, Zinbiel algebras have been studied widely because of their strong links to other algebraic structures, including commutative algebras, Lie algebras, and operads. There are many theoretical studies on the derivations and inner derivations of associative, Lie, and Leibniz algebras, see
[
3
,
4
,

,

,

,
8
]
. However, few of them are in the case of Zinbiel algebras and particularly in their computation. For this reason, our interest in this paper is to study and describe the inner derivations of low-dimensional Zinbiel algebras. These algebras play an important role in understanding higher-dimensional structures and their properties. Many works have classified Zinbiel algebras of dimensions up to 4
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, which has helped reveal their structure and paved the way for studying their derivations, automorphisms, and other features.

In this paper, we focus on
inner derivations
of Zinbiel algebras, which are specific types of linear maps determined by the elements of the algebra. Our main goal is to create an algorithm to represent inner derivations in matrix form for any
n
-dimensional Zinbiel algebra. To illustrate this, We apply our algorithm to two-, three-, and four-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebras using Mathematica or Maple software, providing detailed descriptions of their inner derivations. This work contributes to a better understanding of derivations in non-associative algebras and provides a practical method for further research on Zinbiel algebras.

2.
Prelimieries

Definition 2.1
.

A
Zinbiel algebra

A
is a vector space over a field
\mathbb{C}
equipped with a bilinear map

\circ:A\times A\to A
, satisfying the identity:

\displaystyle(u\circ v)\circ w

\displaystyle=u\circ(v\circ w)+u\circ(w\circ v),\quad\forall u,v,w\in A.

In a Zinbiel algebra, we consider the
right multiplication operator

R_{u}
and the
left multiplication operator

L_{u}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle R_{u}(v)

\displaystyle=v\circ u,\quad\forall v\in A,

\displaystyle L_{u}(v)

\displaystyle=u\circ v,\quad\forall v\in A.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be two Zinbiel algebras over a field
\mathbb{C}
.
A homomorphism between
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear mapping

\displaystyle f:A_{1}\to A_{2}

such that

\displaystyle f(u\circ v)=f(u)\circ f(v),\quad\forall u,v\in A_{1},

where
\circ
denotes the Zinbiel product.

The set of all homomorphisms of
A
is denoted by
Hom(A)
.

In what follows, we define a bilinear map
[\cdot,\cdot]:A\times A\to A
by

\displaystyle[u,v]

\displaystyle=u\circ v-v\circ u,\quad\forall u,v\in A.

Definition 2.3
.

A derivation of a Zinbiel algebra
A
is a linear transformation
d:A\to A
, where

\displaystyle d(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=d(u)\circ v+u\circ d(v),\quad\forall u,v\in A.

We denote the set of all derivations of a Zinbiel algebra
A
by
\mathrm{Der}(A)
. The set
\mathrm{Der}(A)
forms an associative algebra with respect to the composition operation
\circ
, and it is a Lie algebra with respect to the bracket
[d_{1},d_{2}]=d_{1}\circ d_{2}-d_{2}\circ d_{1}
.

In the following, we provide some earlier results on the properties of derivations of Zinbiel algebras.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(A,\circ)
be a Zinbiel algebra and
d\in Hom(A)
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

d\in Der(A)
,

(ii)

[L_{u},L_{d}]=L_{d(u)}
for all
u\in A
,

(iii)

[R_{u},R_{d}]=R_{d(u)}
for all
u\in A
,

where
L_{u}(v)=u\circ v
and
R_{u}(v)=v\circ u
are the left and right multiplication operators, respectively.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii):

Assume
d\in\operatorname{Der}(A)
. Then for all
u,v\in A
:

\displaystyle d(L_{u}(v))

\displaystyle=d(u\circ v)=d(u)\circ v+u\circ d(v).

By definition,
L_{d}(v)=d(u)\circ v
. Rearranging, we obtain:

L_{d}(v)+L_{u}(d(v))=d(L_{u}(v)),

which implies
[L_{u},L_{d}](v)=L_{d(u)}(v)
.

(ii)
\implies
(iii):

From the Zinbiel identity, we know that:

\displaystyle u\circ(v\circ w)

\displaystyle=(u\circ v)\circ w+(u\circ w)\circ v.

Using this and applying the left multiplication condition from (ii), a symmetric argument establishes (iii) by showing that:

\displaystyle d(R_{u}(v))

\displaystyle=d(v\circ u)=d(v)\circ u+v\circ d(u).

Thus,
[R_{u},R_{d}](v)=R_{d(u)}(v)
.

(iii)
\implies
(i):

Assume
[R_{u},R_{d}](v)=R_{d(u)}(v)
. Then, for all
u,v\in A
:

\displaystyle R_{d}(u)(v)+R_{u}(d(v))=d(R_{u}(v)).

Substituting
R_{u}(v)=v\circ u
, we recover:

\displaystyle d(v\circ u)=d(v)\circ u+v\circ d(u),

which confirms
d\in Der(A)
.
Hence, all three conditions are equivalent.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

If
d
is a derivation of a Zinbiel algebra
A
, then
d
is also a derivation of
A
as a Lie algebra.

Proof.

Using the definition of the Lie bracket, we have:

\displaystyle d([u,v])

\displaystyle=d(u\circ v-v\circ u).

Applying the derivation property of
d
for the Zinbiel algebra, we get:

\displaystyle d(u\circ v-v\circ u)

\displaystyle=d(u\circ v)-d(v\circ u).

For the first term,

\displaystyle d(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=d(u)\circ v+u\circ d(v).

For the second term,

\displaystyle d(v\circ u)

\displaystyle=d(v)\circ u+v\circ d(u).

Substituting these into the expression for
d([x,y])
, we get:

\displaystyle d([u,v])

\displaystyle=(d(u)\circ v+u\circ d(v))-(d(v)\circ u+v\circ d(u)).

Rearranging terms,

\displaystyle d([u,v])

\displaystyle=(d(u)\circ v-v\circ d(u))+(u\circ d(v)-d(v)\circ u).

By the definition of the Lie bracket,

\displaystyle d([u,v])

\displaystyle=[d(u),v]+[u,d(v)].

Hence,
d
satisfies the derivation property for the Lie algebra
A
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be a Zinbiel algebra. The sets

\displaystyle R(A)

\displaystyle=\{R_{u}\mid u\in A\}\quad\text{and}\quad L(A)=\{L_{u}\mid u\in A\}

are subalgebras of the Zinbiel algebra
Der(A)
, the derivation algebra of
A
.

Proof.

(1)

For
R(A)
, we need to check that for any
R_{u},R_{v}\in R(A)
, their product
R_{u}R_{v}
is also an element of
R(A)
. We have:

\displaystyle R_{u}R_{v}

\displaystyle=R_{[u,v]},

where
[u,v]
is the bracket operation in the Zinbiel algebra
A
. Since
[u,v]\in A
, we can conclude that
R_{[u,v]}\in R(A)
, hence
R(A)
is closed under the Zinbiel product.

Similarly, for
L(A)
, for any
L_{u},L_{v}\in L(A)
, their product
L_{u}L_{v}
satisfies:

\displaystyle L_{u}L_{v}

\displaystyle=L_{[u,v]},

which implies that
L_{u}L_{v}\in L(A)
, so
L(A)
is also closed under the Zinbiel product.

(2)

The product in
Der(A)
satisfies the Leibniz rule for derivations, which in the context of a Zinbiel algebra also holds. Specifically, for any
u,v,w\in A
, we have:

\displaystyle[R_{u},R_{v}]

\displaystyle=R_{[u,v]}\quad\text{and}\quad[L_{u},L_{v}]=L_{[u,v]},

which ensures that both
R(A)
and
L(A)
are closed under the bracket operation and follow the associativity property.

Thus, the sets
R(A)
and
L(A)
are closed under the Zinbiel algebra product, and since both are subsets of the derivation algebra
Der(A)
, they form subalgebras of
Der(A)
.

Hence,
R(A)
and
L(A)
are subalgebras of
Der(A)
, completing the proof.

∎

3.
Inner Derivations of Zinbiel Algebras

Definition 3.1
.

Let
A
be a Zinbiel algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. For
w\in A
, define the map
ad_{w}:A\to A
by

\displaystyle ad_{w}(u)

\displaystyle=u\circ w-w\circ u,\quad\forall u\in A.

Then
ad_{w}
is called the inner derivation of
A
associated with
w
. The set of all inner derivations is denoted by
Inn(A)
.

Definition 3.2
.

The sets
Ann_{R}(A)
and
Ann_{L}(A)
of a Zinbiel algebra
A
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle Ann_{R}(A)

\displaystyle=\{u\in A\mid A\circ u=0\}

and

\displaystyle Ann_{L}(A)

\displaystyle=\{u\in A\mid u\circ A=0\},

where
A\circ u=[a,u]
for all
a\in A
(using the Zinbiel product) and
[a,u]
is the commutator of
a
and
x
. These sets are called the right and left annihilators of the Zinbiel algebra
A
, respectively.

Lemma 3.3
.

The sets
Ann_{R}(A)
and
Ann_{L}(A)
are two-sided ideals of
A
in the context of Zinbiel algebras.

Proof.

(1)

Let
u,v\in Ann_{R}(A)
. Then, we need to show that
u-v\in Ann_{R}(A)
.

Since
Ann_{R}(A)
is an additive subgroup of
A
, we have:

u\circ w=0\quad\text{and}\quad v\circ w=0\quad\text{for all }w\in A.

For any element
w\in A
, we compute:

(u-v)\circ w=u\circ w-v\circ w=0-0=0.

This shows that
u-v\in Ann_{R}(A)
. Thus,
Ann_{R}(A)
is closed under subtraction, confirming it is an additive subgroup of
A
.

(2)

Next, we show that
Ann_{L}(A)
is closed under subtraction. Let
u,v\in Ann_{L}(A)
, which means:

w\circ u=0\quad\text{and}\quad w\circ v=0\quad\text{for all }w\in A.

We want to show that
u-v\in Ann_{L}(A)
. For any
w\in A
, we have:

w\circ(u-v)=w\circ u-w\circ v=0-0=0.

Hence,
u-v\in Ann_{L}(A)
. Thus,
Ann_{L}(A)
is also an additive subgroup of
A
.

(3)

Finally, we show that
Ann_{R}(A)
and
Ann_{L}(A)
are closed under multiplication by elements of
A
from both sides.

Let
I
denote either
Ann_{R}(A)
or
Ann_{L}(A)
, and let
u\in I
and
v\in A
. If
u\in Ann_{R}(A)
, then for any
w\in A
, we have:

u\circ w=0.

Therefore,
u\circ v\in Ann_{R}(A)
. Similarly, if
u\in Ann_{L}(A)
, then for any
w\in A
, we have:

w\circ u=0.

Hence,
v\circ u\in Ann_{L}(A)
.

This shows that
I\circ A\subseteq I
and
A\circ I\subseteq I
, where
I
is either
Ann_{R}(A)
or
Ann_{L}(A)
.

Thus, both
Ann_{R}(A)
and
Ann_{L}(A)
are two-sided ideals of
A
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

The subset
\text{Inn}(A)
is an ideal of the Lie algebra
\text{Der}(A)
of a Zinbiel algebra
A
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a Zinbiel algebra. We need to show that the subset
\text{Inn}(A)
is an ideal of the Lie algebra of derivations
\text{Der}(A)
, i.e., for any
d\in\text{Der}(A)
and any
w_{1},w_{2}\in A
, we have:

(1)

[d,\text{Inn}(A)]\subseteq\text{Inn}(A)
,

(2)

[\text{Inn}(A),\text{Inn}(A)]=0
.

Let
\text{Inn}(A)
denote the set of inner derivations, where an inner derivation is of the form
\text{ad}_{w}
for some
w\in A
, defined by
\text{ad}_{w}(u)=[w,u]
for all
u\in A
.

Now, for the first part of the proof, take
d\in\text{Der}(A)
and
\text{ad}_{w}\in\text{Inn}(A)
, where
w\in A
. We want to show that:

\displaystyle[d,\text{ad}_{w}]\in\text{Inn}(A).

By the definition of the Lie bracket for derivations, we have:

\displaystyle[d,\text{ad}_{w}](u)

\displaystyle=d(\text{ad}_{w}(u))-\text{ad}_{w}(d(u))=d([w,u])-[w,d(u)].

Since
d
is a derivation, it satisfies the Leibniz rule:

\displaystyle d([w,u])=[d(w),u]+[w,d(u)].

Therefore,

\displaystyle[d,\text{ad}_{w}](u)=[d(w),u],

which means that
[d,\text{ad}_{w}]=\text{ad}_{d(w)}
, and thus
[d,\text{ad}_{w}]\in\text{Inn}(A)
.

For the second part, we want to show that
[\text{ad}_{w},\text{ad}_{w^{\prime}}]=0
for any
w,w^{\prime}\in A
. We compute:

[\text{ad}_{w},\text{ad}_{w^{\prime}}](u)=\text{ad}_{w}(\text{ad}_{w^{\prime}}(u))-\text{ad}_{w^{\prime}}(\text{ad}_{w}(u))=[w,[w^{\prime},u]]-[w^{\prime},[w,u]].

Using the Zinbiel identity, which states that:

[w,[w^{\prime},u]]=[[w,w^{\prime}],u],

we have:

[\text{ad}_{w},\text{ad}_{w^{\prime}}](u)=[[w,w^{\prime}],u]-[[w^{\prime},w],u]=0,

since
[w,w^{\prime}]=-[w^{\prime},w]
. Therefore,
[ad_{w},ad_{w^{\prime}}]=0
, which proves that
Inn(A)
is an ideal of
\text{Der}(A)
.

Thus,
Inn(A)
is an ideal of
Der(A)
, as required.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
A
be a Zinbiel algebra over a field
\mathbb{K}
. For
w\in A
, define the map
\text{ad}_{w}:A\to A
by

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u)

\displaystyle=w\circ u-u\circ w\quad\text{for all}\quad u\in A.

Then
\text{ad}_{w}
is a derivation of
A
.

Proof.

To prove that
\text{ad}_{z}
is a derivation of
A
, we need to show that it satisfies the Leibniz rule, i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=\text{ad}_{w}(u)\circ v+u\circ\text{ad}_{w}(v)\quad\text{for all}\quad u,v\in A.

Let’s compute
\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v
:

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=w\circ(u\circ v)-(u\circ v)\circ w.

Using the Zinbiel identity
(u\circ v)\circ w=u\circ(v\circ w)
, we get

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=w\circ(u\circ v)-u\circ(v\circ w).

Now, using the distributive property:

\displaystyle w(uv)

\displaystyle=(w\circ u)\circ v\quad\text{and}\quad u\circ(v\circ w)=u\circ(v\circ w).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=(w\circ u)\circ v-u\circ(v\circ w).

On the other hand, we compute
\text{ad}_{w}(u)
and
\text{ad}_{w}(v)
:

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u)

\displaystyle=w\circ u-u\circ w\quad\text{and}\quad\text{ad}_{w}(v)=w\circ v-v\circ w.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u)\circ v+u\text{ad}_{w}(v)

\displaystyle=(w\circ u-u\circ w)\circ v+u\circ(w\circ v-v\circ w).

Expanding both terms:

\displaystyle(w\circ u)\circ v-(u\circ w)\circ v+u\circ(w\circ v)-u\circ(v\circ w).

This simplifies to

\displaystyle(w\circ u)\circ v+u\circ(w\circ v)-(u\circ w)\circ v-u\circ(v\circ w),

which is exactly the expression for
\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v)
.

Hence, we have shown that

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{w}(u\circ v)

\displaystyle=\text{ad}_{w}(u)\circ v+u\circ\text{ad}_{w}(v),

proving that
\text{ad}_{w}
is a derivation of
A
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{k}
be inner derivations of a Zinbiel algebra
A
. Then the linear combination

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}d_{1}+\alpha_{2}d_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}d_{k}

is also an inner derivation of
A
.

Proof.

Let
d_{i}=\text{ad}_{w_{i}}
for each
i=1,2,\dots,k
, where
w_{i}\in A
and
\text{ad}_{w_{i}}(u)=[w_{i},u]
for all
u\in A
. Thus, the inner derivations
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{k}
are given by
d_{i}(u)=[w_{i},u]
for each
i
.

Now consider the linear combination of these derivations:

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}d_{1}+\alpha_{2}d_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}d_{k}.

For any
u\in A
, we have

\displaystyle d(u)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}d_{1}(u)+\alpha_{2}d_{2}(u)+\cdots+\alpha_{k}d_{k}(u).

Substituting the definition of each
d_{i}(u)
, we get

\displaystyle d(u)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}[w_{1},u]+\alpha_{2}[w_{2},u]+\cdots+\alpha_{k}[w_{k},u].

This can be written as

\displaystyle d(u)

\displaystyle=[\alpha_{1}w_{1}+\alpha_{2}w_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}w_{k},u].

Thus, the element
w=\alpha_{1}w_{1}+\alpha_{2}w_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}w_{k}
is in
A
, and we can express
d
as the inner derivation d(u) = [w, u].

Therefore,
d
is an inner derivation of
A
, and the proof is complete.
∎

4.
An algorithm for finding inner derivations

Let
A
be an
n
-dimensional associative algebra. Fix a basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
of
A
and a vector
w=a_{1}e_{1}+a_{2}e_{2}+\dots+a_{n}e_{n}
in
A
. Let
ad_{w}
be an inner derivation of
A
. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle ad_{w}(e_{i})

\displaystyle=e_{i}\circ w-w\circ e_{i},\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}.

Moreover,
ad_{w}
can be considered as a linear transformation of
A
, represented in a matrix form:

\displaystyle ad_{w}(e_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{ij}e_{j},\quad i=1,2,\dots,n.

This leads to:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{ij}e_{j}

\displaystyle=e_{i}\circ w-w\circ e_{i},\quad i=1,2,\dots,n.

Then, we obtain the following system of equations for the coefficients
d_{ij}
:

\displaystyle d_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{t}\gamma_{it}^{j}-\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{t}\gamma_{ti}^{j},\quad i,j=1,2,\dots,n.

Solving this system of equation, we find the inner derivation in matrix form.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A
be a two-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebra. Then it is isomorphic to one of the following pairwise nonisomorphic Zinbiel algebras
A_{2}^{1}:\;e_{1}\circ e_{1}=e_{2}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

The inner derivations of two-dimensional complex zinbiel algebras are given as follows:

Table 1: inner derivations of two-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebras

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\hline\cr\textbf{Isomorphism Class}&\textbf{Inner Derivation}&\textbf{Dimension}\\
\hline\cr A_{2}^{1}&\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)&0\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Proof.

Let
A_{2}^{1}
be a two-dimensional Zinbiel algebra. Fix a basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
A_{2}^{1}
. The multiplication in
A_{2}^{1}
is defined by
e_{1}\circ e_{1}=e_{2}
. Let
w=ae_{1}+be_{2}\in A_{2}^{1}
. Define

ad_{w}
as the inner derivation of
A_{2}^{1}
, given by

\displaystyle ad_{w}(e_{i})

\displaystyle=w\circ e_{i}-e_{i}\circ w,\quad\forall e_{i}\in A_{2}^{1},\ i=1,2.

In particular, for
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle ad_{w}(e_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}a_{ij}e_{j},

where
ad_{w}
can be represented as a linear transformation of
A_{2}^{1}
in the matrix form

\displaystyle ad_{w}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

The definition of
ad_{w}
implies

\displaystyle w\circ e_{i}-e_{i}\circ w=\sum_{j=1}^{2}a_{ij}e_{j},\quad\forall i=1,2.

Let us compute
ad_{w}
explicitly for
A_{2}^{1}
:

(1)

Since
e_{1}\circ e_{1}=e_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle w\circ e_{1}

\displaystyle=(ae_{1}+be_{2})\circ e_{1}=a(e_{1}\circ e_{1})+b(e_{2}\circ e_{1})=ae_{2},

and

\displaystyle e_{1}\circ w=e_{1}\circ(ae_{1}+be_{2})

\displaystyle=a(e_{1}\circ e_{1})+b(e_{1}\circ e_{2})=ae_{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle ad_{w}(e_{1})

\displaystyle=w\circ e_{1}-e_{1}\circ w=ae_{2}-ae_{2}=0.

(2)

For
e_{2}
, note that
e_{2}\circ e_{1}=e_{1}\circ e_{2}=0
and
e_{2}\circ e_{2}=0
, so

\displaystyle w\circ e_{2}

\displaystyle=(ae_{1}+be_{2})\circ e_{2}=0,\quad e_{2}w=e_{2}\circ(ae_{1}+be_{2})=0.

Thus,

\displaystyle ad_{w}(e_{2})=w\circ e_{2}-e_{2}\circ w=0.

From the above, the matrix representation of
ad_{w}
is

\displaystyle ad_{w}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, for
A_{2}^{1}
, the inner derivations are trivial, represented by the zero matrix:

\displaystyle ad_{e_{1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathrm{ad}_{e_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}_{w}(u)

w=a_{1}e_{1}+a_{2}e_{2}+a_{3}e_{3}+a_{4}e_{4}\in A

Corollary 4.7
.

(1)

The dimension of the inner derivations of complex two-dimensional Zinbiel algebras is zero.

(2)

The dimension of the inner derivations of complex three-dimensional Zinbiel algebras
ranges between zero and two.

(3)

The dimension of the inner derivations of complex four-dimensional Zinbiel algebras
ranges between zero and three.

5.
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the inner derivations of finite-dimensional Zinbiel algebras. The dimension of the space of inner derivations ranges between zero and two for two-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebras and between zero and three for three-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebras, respectively. The dimension of inner derivations is an important invariant in the geometric classification of algebras, and it has many applications in various scientific areas.
