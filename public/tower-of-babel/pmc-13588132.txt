Introduction

Rhinosporidiosis is an uncommon chronic granulomatous infection caused by Rhinosporidium seeberi, an organism classified within Mesomycetozoea. It occurs predominantly in tropical and subtropical regions and most commonly affects the nasal cavity and nasopharynx. Clinically, it typically presents as a unilateral, vascular, friable polypoidal mass associated with nasal obstruction and recurrent epistaxis.1,2

Exposure to stagnant or pond water has frequently been associated with rhinosporidiosis, although the exact mode of transmission remains incompletely understood.1,2 The lesion is often painless, reddish, and polypoidal in appearance; however, its clinical features may resemble those of other unilateral nasal masses. Histopathological examination remains essential for definitive diagnosis and typically demonstrates large, thick-walled sporangia containing numerous endospores.1,3

The mainstay of treatment is complete surgical excision with cauterization or electrocoagulation of the lesion base.3,4 Recurrence may occur, particularly following incomplete excision or local spillage of endospores during surgery.3,4

This case describes histopathologically confirmed right-sided nasal rhinosporidiosis in a 17-year-old male with a five-year history of progressive nasal obstruction and recurrent epistaxis. The prolonged duration of symptoms and the presence of sporangia at the resection margin highlight the importance of adequate surgical clearance and postoperative surveillance.

Case Presentation

A 17-year-old male presented with a five-year history of progressive right-sided nasal obstruction associated with recurrent episodes of epistaxis. The obstruction developed gradually and worsened over time as the nasal mass increased in size, eventually becoming visible and protruding through the right nostril. He had no history of previous nasal surgery, biopsy, or other procedures involving the lesion and no known family history of a similar condition.

The patient lived in a rural area and had regular contact with livestock, including goats. He also reported repeated swimming and playing in a nearby pond before the onset of his symptoms.

Preoperative nasal endoscopy was not performed. Baseline laboratory investigations included a complete blood count. The white blood cell count was 6.63 × 109/L, hemoglobin was 13.9 g/dL, hematocrit was 42.5%, and platelet count was 345 × 109/L. The red blood cell count was mildly elevated at 5.58 × 106/µL. The mean corpuscular volume and mean corpuscular hemoglobin were reduced at 76.2 fL and 24.9 pg, respectively. The lymphocyte percentage was mildly elevated at 49.0%, while the absolute lymphocyte count was within the laboratory reference range at 3.25 × 109/L.

Computed tomography of the paranasal sinuses demonstrated a well-defined soft-tissue lesion occupying the right nasal cavity and narrowing the right nasal passage (Figure 1). On review of the available CT images, no obvious destructive bony erosion was identified; however, subtle bony remodeling could not be reliably assessed. Intraoperatively, the lesion appeared reddish and friable within the right nasal cavity (Figure 2). The nasal mass was excised through a transnasal approach, followed by cauterization of the lesion base for hemostasis. Histopathological examination of hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections demonstrated respiratory-type epithelium overlying chronically inflamed subepithelial stroma containing numerous thick-walled sporangia filled with endospores (Figure 3). No dysplasia or malignancy was identified. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of nasal rhinosporidiosis. Sporangia were identified at the resection margin, indicating incomplete histological clearance. Postoperatively, the patient was commenced on oral dapsone 100 mg once daily, with a planned treatment duration of six months, after confirmation of normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) status and review of baseline hematological parameters. At the three-month postoperative follow-up, repeat computed tomography showed no radiological evidence of recurrent disease. Continued clinical surveillance was advised because of the risk of recurrence associated with incomplete histological clearance.

Discussion

The clinical presentation of this patient is consistent with the characteristic features of nasal rhinosporidiosis, including chronic unilateral nasal obstruction, recurrent epistaxis, and a friable polypoidal nasal mass.1,2 However, the five-year duration of symptoms in this 17-year-old patient is particularly noteworthy. Previous studies have shown that earlier intervention, particularly within 12 months of symptom onset, is associated with a lower likelihood of recurrence.4 The prolonged duration of symptoms in the present case, together with the presence of sporangia at the resection margin, emphasizes the importance of early recognition, adequate surgical clearance, and continued postoperative surveillance.

The history of repeated pond-water exposure before the onset of symptoms provides an important epidemiological clue. Exposure to stagnant or pond water has frequently been associated with rhinosporidiosis, although the precise mechanism of transmission remains incompletely understood.1,2,5 Livestock exposure alone does not establish causation and should therefore be interpreted within the broader rural environmental context. In an adolescent male presenting with unilateral nasal obstruction and recurrent epistaxis, juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma is an important differential diagnosis. Other considerations include inflammatory nasal polyps, inverted papilloma, vascular lesions such as hemangioma, other chronic granulomatous diseases, and sinonasal neoplasms. Because these conditions may have overlapping clinical appearances, histopathological examination is essential for definitive diagnosis.3,6,7

Imaging plays an important role in defining the location and extent of nasal rhinosporidiosis and assessing the involvement of adjacent structures, but it is not diagnostic. In the present case, computed tomography demonstrated a well-defined soft-tissue lesion occupying the right nasal cavity and narrowing the nasal passage. No obvious destructive bony erosion was identified on review of the available CT images, although subtle bony remodeling could not be reliably assessed. Histopathological examination therefore remained central to establishing the diagnosis. The demonstration of numerous thick-walled sporangia containing endospores within chronically inflamed stroma is characteristic of rhinosporidiosis.1,3,6 In this patient, these findings confirmed the diagnosis and excluded dysplasia and malignancy.

Complete surgical excision with cauterization or electrocoagulation of the lesion base remains the mainstay of treatment for localized nasal rhinosporidiosis.3,4 In the present patient, the mass was excised through a transnasal approach and the lesion base was cauterized for hemostasis. Treatment of the attachment site is important because residual organisms and local implantation of endospores may contribute to subsequent disease. Nevertheless, histopathological examination demonstrated sporangia at the resection margin, indicating incomplete histological clearance. This finding raises concern for residual disease and provides a strong rationale for close postoperative surveillance.

Recurrence remains an important challenge in the management of rhinosporidiosis. Factors associated with an increased risk of recurrence include more extensive or multisite disease, previous surgery, involvement of sites such as the nasal septum, middle turbinate, and nasopharynx, and a longer duration between symptom onset and treatment.4 Intraoperative spillage of endospores and incomplete removal of the lesion have also been proposed as mechanisms contributing to recurrence.3,5,8 Although lesion size may influence the technical difficulty of achieving complete excision, its independent contribution to recurrence is less clearly established.

Surgical technique may also influence outcome. One comparative study of 40 patients reported no recurrence at six months following endoscopic sinus surgery, compared with recurrence in 35% of patients treated by conventional surgical excision; however, the small sample size, nonrandomized treatment allocation, and short follow-up period limit the strength of this comparison.9 These findings nevertheless support the importance of adequate visualization of the lesion and its attachment site during surgical removal.

Dapsone has been used as adjunctive therapy following surgical excision, particularly in patients considered to be at increased risk of recurrence.3,10,11 However, evidence supporting its effectiveness remains limited, and it should not be regarded as a substitute for complete surgical excision and appropriate surveillance. In the present case, oral dapsone 100 mg once daily was commenced postoperatively for a planned duration of six months after confirmation of normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase status and review of baseline hematological parameters. At the three-month postoperative follow-up, repeat computed tomography demonstrated no radiological evidence of recurrent disease. Although this early postoperative outcome is encouraging, the short duration of follow-up and the presence of sporangia at the resection margin necessitate continued long-term surveillance. The absence of recurrence at three months cannot be attributed to dapsone alone.

Conclusion

Nasal rhinosporidiosis should be considered in patients presenting with long-standing unilateral nasal obstruction, recurrent epistaxis, and a friable nasal mass, particularly when there is a history of pond-water exposure. Histopathological examination remains essential for definitive diagnosis. Complete surgical excision with cauterization of the lesion base and close postoperative surveillance are important because of the risk of recurrence, particularly when sporangia are present at the resection margin. In this patient, no radiological evidence of recurrence was identified on CT at the three-month postoperative follow-up; however, continued long-term surveillance remains necessary because of the incomplete histological clearance.
