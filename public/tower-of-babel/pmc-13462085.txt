Scalable prediction of suicidal risk in university students: a three steps machine learning approach in university settings
Authors: Wivine Blekic, Alice Demesmaeker, Matteo Chabbert, Vanessa Tagliatti, Jenifer Denis, Fabien D’Hondt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suicide is a leading cause of death among young adults, with university students representing a particularly vulnerable subgroup. Although prevention efforts often depend on psychopathological symptom assessments, these are resource-intensive and less practical for large-scale screening. This study evaluated whether easily collectable contextual and psychosocial variables can independently predict suicidal risk in university students. Data from 470 university students were analyzed using random forest machine learning (ML) algorithm. Three models were developed: Model 1 included 53 contextual and psychosocial variables (sociodemographic, lifestyle, social support, academic stressors, and health); Model 2 relied solely on 10 psychopathological symptom scales; and Model 3 integrated all variables. Predictive performance improved progressively across the three models (AUC = 0.72, 0.77, and 0.80, respectively). Key predictors included both contextual and psychosocial variables (e.g. low social support, diminished university belonging) and psychometric evaluation of symptoms (anxiety, depression, coping strategies). Taken together, these results show that contextual and psychosocial variables provide meaningful information for identifying university students at risk for suicide, and psychometric symptom measures offer further refinement of risk detection. Findings are discussed in relation to the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide and Escape Theory. This ML approach offers a scalable framework for suicide risk screening in academic settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41809-4.

Introduction

Suicide is the second leading cause of unnatural death among adolescents and young adults aged 15 to 291, with an increasing proportion of this population being college students2. In Belgium, the age-standardized suicide death rate per 100,000 inhabitants is 1.6 times higher than the average of EU-14 countries3, with university students showing a particularly concerning upward trend4.

During adolescence, suicide thoughts rise sharply5. Beyond reflecting extreme psychological distress, they are recognized as the strongest predictors of future suicidal behavior (e.g. suicide attempts) and as major contributors to suicide itself6. Multiple psychological models have been proposed, with contemporary ideation-to-action frameworks emphasizing that the onset of suicidal thoughts and their transition to suicide attempt seems to be driven by distinct factors7. Identifying the predictors of suicidal thoughts is therefore crucial for intervening early and preventing progression toward more severe suicidal behaviors.

Suicide thoughts are especially prevalent among university students4, making them a key focus for prevention and research. Numerous studies highlighted a strong association between suicide thoughts and psychopathological symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and high stress, often assessed through psychometric measures8–11. However, the assessment of psychopathological symptoms require specialized tools and expertise, which poses challenges for large-scale or routine screening.

Beyond these psychopathological dimensions, university students face additional situational stressors that may heighten vulnerability to suicide risk. Prior research has identified a range of contextual and psychosocial factors associated with suicide risk among university students, including sociodemographic variables (e.g., familial financial situation12, being the first generation in college13, parent–student relationships14, lifestyle behaviors (e.g., physical activity patterns15, eating habits16, and sleep disturbances17, academic-related stressors (e.g., uncertainty about study choice18, financial strain, perceived social disconnection19,20, and increasing academic demands11, and general stressors (such as perceived stress21, acute life events22 or perceived burdensomeness19). At the same time, the transition to adulthood involves psychosocial changes - such as increased autonomy, relocation, and shifts in support networks - that can further incrase risk23–25.

Many of these factors can be assessed through brief self-report questions, making them both easily collectable and suitable for scalable screening approaches. However, despite growing evidence linking academic and psychosocial stressors to suicide risk, it remains unclear whether these contextual and psychosocial variables can independently influence suicide risk, beyond established psychopathological risk factors. Clarifying this relationship could inform the development of practical tools for early identification of at-risk individuals.

Addressing this question requires analytical approaches capable of handling the complexity and interplay of potential risk factors, often involving non-linear effects, for which traditional regression-based methods show limited performance26. Machine learning methods, such as random forests27,28, offer greater precision by assessing each variable’s unique contribution to risk and uncovering nuanced predictors. Recent studies have shown that machine learning techniques hold significant promise for predicting suicide risk prediction across diverse populations29–31. In addition, a review of 35 studies utilizing ML to examine suicide risk found greater prediction accuracy over the studies using traditional statistical methods, and furthermore, identified novel correlates of suicide32. This is in part due to the fact that traditional statistical approaches inherently restrict how many different factors can be simultaneously examined, and cannot capture complex multivariate patterns within data18.

The present study aims to identify whether contextual and psychosocial factors alone, assessed through simple yes/no or single-item questions, can reliably predict suicide risk among university students, and to quantify the incremental predictive value provided by multi-item psychometric scales that assess psychopathological symptoms. To address this question, we implemented a machine learning framework comparing three predictive models: (1) a model including only brief contextual and psychosocial variables that can be collected with simple yes/no questions; (2) model including only standardized multi-item psychometric symptom measures; (3) a combined model integrating both variable types. This design enables evaluation of the relative and complementary contributions of contextual and psychosocial along with psychopathological dimensions to suicide risk.

Methods

Participants and study design

Data were collected through an online questionnaire sent by email to all students currently enrolled and academic staff of the University of Mons. A total of 830 graduate, undergraduate students, academics (PhD students and professors) opened the link of the survey, 37 did not start completion, 141 dropped out at the demographic section and 182 dropped out during the questionnaires. Only participants who had available scores for suicide risk screening (the Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire – Revised; SBQ-R33) were included in the present study. See the flow chart in Figure S1. The final sample (N = 470) consisted of 293 bachelor students (63.1%), 146 master students (31.5%), and 25 doctoral researcher (5.4%). Drop-out participants did not differ from the final sample in terms of age, marital status or education level (p > 0.1 in all cases, see Table S1). The final sample included significantly more female participants (78.7%) compared to the drop-out group (71.4%, χ2 = 5.26, p = 0.02). All methods were carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local Institutional Review Board of the University of Mons approved the study (approval number: UMONS-2020.03.19-BL.MO.DJ-001). All participants signed the informed consent.

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the University of Mons (Belgium) between January 17, 2021, and February 17, 2021. During data collection, all of the students and academic staff were in a COVID-19 s lockdown.

Measures

Outcome variable

The “suicide risk” class was defined based on a cutoff score of ≥ 7 of the SBQ-R. The SBQ-R is a self-reported questionnaire that assesses the following, that is (1) history of suicidal ideation and suicide attempt (past year); (2) frequency of suicidal ideation in the past month; (3) communication of suicidal behavior (e.g. “telling someone that you were going to commit suicide, or that you might do it”); and (4) self-perceived likelihood of future suicidal behavior. The predictive model was developed to predict those who met the SBQ-R cutoff score (SBQ-R defined suicide risk, SBQR+) vs. those who did not meet this threshold (SBQR- group). The SBQ-R shows good reliability, convergent, concurrent, and structural validity33–35.

Predictors

Contextual and psychosocial variables. Fifty-three self-report items were administered, covering three main domains: sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, university-related variables, and other stressors. Details can be found in Table S2 and supplemental material.

Psychometric variables. Five standardized self-report questionnaires, validated in French, were administered to assess participants’ mental health and coping strategies: the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ36) to measure general health, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS35) to assess symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS37) to capture feelings of perceived stress. The BRIEF-Cope (an abbreviated version of the Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced) Inventory was used to assess four types of coping strategies: seeking social support, problem-solving, avoidance, and positive thinking38. A description of the items included in the subscales can be found in Table S3. The Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) was used to assess substance use39. However, due to more than 50% missing data in the questionnaire, only the alcohol subscale was retained for analysis.

Data analysis

Details of data analysis can be found the supplement material.

Data preprocessing

Categorical features were dummy coded (0 = “no” or absent, and 1 = “yes” or present) and 4 features with near zero variance were removed, resulting in 63 features: 53 situational variables and 10 psychopathological features (see Table 1 for descriptive statistics). To prevent information leakage, pre-processing steps were separately applied to the training and test set40.

Machine learning

Probability of suicide risk was predicted using a random forest algorithm. The sample was randomly split into a 75% training set and a 25% test set for internal model validation on data unseen by the model during the model-building process, stratified for the outcome variable to achieve a distribution in training and test sets matching the overall outcome distribution (see Table 2). We used 10 times 5-fold cross-validation to guard the hyperparameter tuning against overfitting. Model tuning and final model selection was based on the training set and the test set was strictly kept apart for internal model validation. Model building and evaluation were performed with R package ‘caret’ 40 in R 4.2.2 using Rstudio version 2022.12.0.

Performance metrics

Overall accuracy is the proportion of predictions that were correct; however, when an outcome is unbalanced, high accuracy can be achieved by predicting the most common outcome for all cases. To account for this, models were trained to maximize AUC40. Result reporting is based on the CHARMS checklist41 for prognostic models.

Feature importance

The importance of individual predictors in the model was ranked according to the mean information gain attributed to each predictor across the 500 trees. Information gain is an information-theoretic quantity defined as the reduction of entropy that results from splitting a parent node into two child nodes, e.g., making a different prediction of SI + for those who have 8 h sleep vs. those who do not42. To assess potential redundancy among predictors, associations among the top-ranked features were examined using Spearman correlations. As expected, distress-related measures (DASS subscales, PSS, GHQ) showed moderate to strong intercorrelations (Spearman ρ = 0.50–0.71.50.71, see Table S4), as expected for related constructs, but no evidence of extreme redundancy (all |ρ| < 0.80).

Partial dependence

Partial dependence analyses quantified how model-predicted probability of SBQ-R-defined suicide risk changes across values of a given feature, averaging over the distribution of all other features in the dataset. For continuous predictors, PDPs depict whether higher values are associated with higher or lower predicted risk; for dichotomous predictors, they compare the predicted probability between the “yes” and “no” categories.

Suicide risk prediction

Table 3 summarizes the performance metrics for each machine learning model. Model 1, based on contextual and psychosocial variables, demonstrated good predictive accuracy for suicidal risk (AUC = 0.72, 95% CI [0.63, 0.82]). Predictive performance was better with Model 2, based on psychometric variables (AUC = 0.77 [0.68, 0.85]). Partial dependance

Figures 1, 2 and 3 present partial dependence plots for the features with the highest relative importance. These plots illustrate the direction and magnitude of change in the model-predicted probability of suicide risk associated with variations in each feature, after averaging over all other predictors. Across models, even the most influential individual features were associated with relatively modest absolute changes in predicted probability, indicating that no single variable exerted a dominant marginal effect on SBQ-R-defined suicide risk. In Model 1, higher perceived social support (feeling surrounded, feeling supported by family) was associated with lower predicted probability of SBQ-R–defined suicide risk, whereas higher acute stress (being confronted with a sick loved one) was associated with higher predicted probability. In Models 2 and 3, higher depression and avoidance were associated with higher predicted probability of suicide risk, while social support (both dichotomous variable and BRIEF-COPE measure) was associated with lower predicted probability of suicide risk. We interpret these PDPs in terms of the direction and relative magnitude of changes in predicted probability, without treating any specific probability threshold as intrinsically meaningful.

Discussion

This study examined whether situational factors that are easily collectable through self-report can predict suicidal risk among university students, and to what extent their predictive power is enhanced by adding psychometric mental health measures. Using machine learning, we compared three models: one including only contextual and psychosocial variables, one including only psychometric mental health measures, and one combining both. Predictive performance improved progressively across the three models (AUC = 0.72, 0.77, and 0.80, respectively), indicating that while contextual and psychosocial indicators already capture meaningful vulnerability to SBQ-R defined suicide risk, psychometric variables provided a better predictive accuracy. Together, these findings open promising perspectives for prevention strategies based on a stepped screening approach. Easily collectable contextual and psychosocial indicators could serve as a first-line screening tool to identify students potentially at risk, who could then be referred for more comprehensive psychometric assessments. Such an approach would facilitate early detection within large populations while optimizing the use of specialized clinical resources. To support this perspective, Table 4 reports the ranked importance of all features across models, and Table S5 provides a qualitative synthesis of the most relevant variables, regrouped into conceptual domains (e.g., academic stress, social support, psychological symptoms, lifestyle) and clearly identify these that require psychometric measures. This table is intended as a practical summary of the results.

Model 1, which included only situational factors, achieved a good predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.72). The most influential features covered several domains, including lifestyle (sleep difficulties, time for meals or hobbies), social support (feeling surrounded, family support), and academic stressors (desire to stop studies, low sense of belonging at university). Mental health history (previous consultation with a professional) and stress exposure (having a sick loved one) also ranked among the top predictors.

These findings suggest that suicidal risk can be meaningfully predicted from contextual and psychosocial variables, even in the absence of explicit psychopathological assessments. Indicators of social connectedness, such as perceived support from family or peers, are particularly salient. Within the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide43, such variables reflect thwarted belongingness, a feeling of social disconnection that contributes to suicidal thoughts. Importantly, partial dependence analyses indicated that individual contextual and psychosocial factors were each associated with relatively modest marginal changes in predicted probability, rather than exerting strong standalone effects. This pattern implies that contextual and psychosocial factors exert small, cumulative effects that interact to create risk, rather than functioning as isolated triggers. The ability of Model 1 to detect this multivariate interplay underscores the advantage of machine learning methods over traditional regression, which often fail to capture such complex relationships.

Model 2, based exclusively on psychometric measures of mental health, provided stronger predictive performance (AUC = 0.77). The most influential variables were depressive symptoms, anxiety, stress, and avoidance, while adaptive coping strategies such as seeking social support, positive thinking, and problem solving were negatively associated with suicide risk.

These findings replicate earlier ML studies identifying depression as the most consistent predictor of suicidal risk31,44,45. Importantly, the strong role of avoidance aligns with the escape theory of Baumeister (1990)46, which conceptualizes suicide as a cognitive and emotional attempt to escape from self-perceived failure or unbearable affective distress. This interpretation is supported by findings from Landrault et al. (2020)47, who showed that escape motivation mediates the relationship between feelings of failure and suicidal risk among students. In our study, avoidance emerged as a stronger predictor than the desire to stop studies, suggesting that it is not academic failure per se but the drive to escape its emotional consequences that best predicts suicidal risk in this population. These findings emphasize the relevance of emotion regulation and coping strategies as proximal mechanisms linking academic stress to suicidality. They also point to the value of targeting maladaptive avoidance in student mental health interventions.

The combined model integrating both situational and psychometric variables achieved the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.80). Ten of the fifteen most important predictors were psychometric (depression, anxiety, avoidance, seeking social support), while several contextual and psychosocial indicators (such as feeling surrounded, family support, and previous consultation with a mental health professional) remained among the top contributors.

This result indicates that contextual, psychosocial and psychometric evaluation of mental health provide complementary information about suicide risk. The psychometric evaluation capture internal distress and cognitive strategies to deal with it, whereas context and psychosocial factors reflect external conditions that shape or buffer this vulnerability, such as social integration, daily routines, and academic drive. Together, these dimensions form a multilayered model of suicidal risk consistent with contemporary ideation-to-action frameworks7, which emphasize that suicidal risk results from the interaction between individual vulnerability and environmental context. Notably, while predictive accuracy was good, sensitivity remained lower than specificity, mirroring a limitation frequently reported in ML studies of suicide risk29. This imbalance suggests that the construct of “suicidal risk” encompasses heterogeneous profiles that are difficult to capture with a single model. Future work should therefore explore stratified models, focusing on more homogeneous subgroups (e.g., by gender, academic level, or coping style) to improve detection of true positives.

In summary, the patterns emerging across the three models align closely with major frameworks of suicidality. Contextual and psychosocial predictors such as low belonging and perceived burden reflect the key components of Joiner’s Interpersonal Theory of Suicide (2005), while avoidance and emotion-focused coping resonate with Baumeister’s escape theory (1990). Although this study did not aim to formally test psychological theories, they offer a coherent conceptual lens through which to understand the variables identified by our models. It is to be noted that the use of machine learning enabled the identification of less intuitive predictors that warrant further investigation. For example, students with a daily commute of 40–60 min presented lower suicide risk. Although unexpected, this finding may reflect protective factors such as living with family, and therefore benefiting from stronger support networks, or bonding with peers during shared travel. These results underscore the potential of ML to highlight proxy variables that, while not direct risk factors themselves, may capture meaningful social or contextual processes relevant for suicide risk prevention.

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations warrant consideration. Data were collected during the COVID-19 lockdown via online surveys, a context in which psychological distress rose in France from the first lockdown through summer 2021 (15-month follow-up48), potentially contributing to the high suicide risk prevalence observed here (44.5%) and limiting generalizability beyond that period. Importantly, SBQ-R ≥ 7 reflects elevated suicide risk encompassing both last year and recent components rather than current suicide behaviors alone. Therefore, the high prevalence observed here likely reflects a combination of pre-existing vulnerability and recent risk, and should not be interpreted as solely attributable to the pandemic context. This is why we included relatively stable contextual variables (e.g., commute duration), which are interpreted as proxy indicators of broader living or social contexts rather than as exposures specific to the lockdown period. Although we included indicators of social isolation (e.g., relocation to the parental home, living alone, changes in household composition), loneliness was not directly assessed, which may have attenuated the detection of social-disconnection effects. The sample was also markedly imbalanced by gender (78.7% women), a pattern frequently reported in survey research and often attributed to higher response rates among women49, which may affect representativeness. Replication with larger, more balanced, and longitudinal cohorts will be essential to validate and refine these models.

Our models showed a limitation common in ML studies applied in mental health: sensitivity was lower than specificity. ML algorithms generally identify non-risk cases more accurately than at-risk individuals29, likely due to the heterogeneity of “suicidal risk” as defined by the SBQ-R 51,52 and the context-dependent relevance of predictors53, underscoring the need for stratified rather than universal models54. Because several predictors reflect overlapping aspects of psychological distress (see correlation analyses), variable importance and partial dependence estimates should be interpreted in the context of correlated features rather than as independent effects of each variable. Some predictors are likely to index broader psychological distress rather than vulnerability uniquely associated with suicide risk. This pattern is consistent with prior ML studies showing substantial overlap between predictors of suicidal ideation and depression, with differences emerging primarily in the relative importance of shared factors rather than in outcome‑specific predictors50. The cross-sectional design also restricts prediction to concurrent suicide risk, and ML does not establish causality. Nonetheless, random forests perform reliably with small and moderately imbalanced samples55, and the number of SI cases exceeded the number of predictors, supporting model stability. Larger, longitudinal, and more balanced samples are needed to confirm these findings.

Implications for preventive strategies

The findings of this study offer two complementary avenues for suicide prevention within university settings: (1) developing scalable screening systems to detect students at risk of suicidal risk, and (2) implementing targeted interventions addressing the modifiable situational and psychological risk factors identified in this study.

Because several situational variables predicted suicidal risk with good accuracy, even without specialized psychometric measures, they could form the basis of a brief, first-line screening tool. Such easily collectable indicators (e.g., sleep difficulties, sense of belonging, family support, lifestyle habits) could be administered periodically by academic staff or counseling units to identify students who may benefit from further evaluation. Those showing elevated risk could then be referred for more comprehensive psychometric assessment and psychological support. This stepped approach would enable large-scale monitoring while optimizing the use of specialized clinical resources. A preliminary short questionnaire based on these easily collectable variables is proposed in Table S3 and will require validation in larger samples.

Conclusion

Our study contributes the understanding of suicide risk in academic populations in three ways. First, the results align with established psychological frameworks by showing that predictors linked to belongingness and avoidance play a central role in suicidal risk among university students. Second, the use of a three-model machine learning framework demonstrated the feasibility of a stratified approach to risk identification, where situational variables can serve as an accessible first-line screening tool, followed by more comprehensive psychometric assessments for students identified as potentially at risk. Finally, we were able to link these findings to prevention strategies. Taken together, these contributions illustrate how theory, advanced methodology, and practical application can be combined to inform cost-effective, real-world suicide prevention in university settings.
