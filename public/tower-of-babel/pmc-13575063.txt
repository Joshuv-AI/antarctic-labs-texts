Neural regulation of cancer: from microenvironmental hijacking to systemic circuit co-option
Authors: Zhuo Gu, Hanyue Ma, Guo-Qin Jiang, Jinrong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The nervous system is increasingly recognized as an active regulator of tumor biology rather than a passive mediator of cancer-associated symptoms. Growing evidence supports a hierarchical framework of neuro–tumor interactions encompassing chemical, structural, and electrical levels, through which bidirectional communication influences tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response. At the chemical level, neurotransmitters and neuropeptides regulate tumor cell proliferation, invasion, immune modulation, and stemness. At the structural level, neurogenesis and tumor–nerve interface coupling establish physical connectivity between nerves and cancer cells. At the electrical level, synapse-like communication, defined as functional signaling between neurons and tumor cells that resembles synaptic transmission in terms of directionality and signaling dynamics, enables direct regulation of tumor behavior by neuronal activity. Neural regulation exhibits pronounced context dependency, with both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing effects depending on tumor type, genetic background, and microenvironmental context, as exemplified in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, melanoma, and breast cancer. At the systemic level, neural circuits regulate tumor angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and extracellular matrix remodeling, while also contributing to cancer-associated pain and therapy-induced neurotoxicity. Collectively, this review proposes a hierarchical neuro–tumor interaction framework that provides an integrated conceptual basis for understanding the complexity and context dependency of neural regulation in cancer biology.

Introduction

The nervous system is increasingly recognized as an active regulator of tumor biology rather than a passive bystander responding to cancer-related symptoms [1–15]. Traditionally, studies of neuro–tumor interactions have focused primarily on perineural invasion (PNI) [16], particularly in malignancies such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and prostate cancer [5, 17–23]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that tumors and nerves engage in dynamic, bidirectional, and multilevel communication that significantly influences tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic responses [24–30].

Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), tumor cells communicate directly with nerve fibers through multiple mechanisms, including axonogenesis, neurogenesis, synapse-like signaling, and reciprocal molecular reprogramming. Cancer cells promote neural remodeling by secreting neurotrophic factors, whereas neuronal activity reciprocally regulates tumor cell behavior and stromal function [31, 32]. Increased intratumoral nerve density has been associated with aggressive phenotypes and poor clinical outcomes in multiple solid tumors, highlighting the functional significance of tumor-associated innervation [33–36]. Beyond local interactions, the central nervous system (CNS) coordinates systemic neural regulation of peripheral tumors through interconnected neuroimmune and neuroendocrine networks. The influence of the nervous system further extends to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and angiogenesis [37–41].

At the clinical level, neuro–tumor interactions influence not only cancer-associated pain but also therapeutic responses [42, 43]. Emerging evidence suggests that neural signaling represents a promising therapeutic target, while the effects of neural regulation remain highly context-dependent and may either promote or suppress tumor progression depending on the biological context [44].

In this review, we summarize current advances in (i) neuro–tumor communication patterns; (ii) CNS regulation of peripheral tumors; (iii) neural control of ECM and stromal remodeling; (iv) neuro–metabolic crosstalk; (v) neurovascular interactions; (vi) mechanisms of cancer pain; and (vii) neural-related effects of anticancer therapies. We further discuss the context-dependent nature of neural regulation, current translational challenges, and emerging therapeutic opportunities, aiming to provide an integrated framework for understanding neural regulation in cancer and its translational potential.

Modes of neuro–tumor communication: a chemical, structural, and electrophysiological integration paradigm

Neuro–tumor crosstalk can be conceptually organized into three major modes of communication: chemical signaling, structural coupling, and electrical coupling. Rather than representing an established biological sequence, this framework provides a conceptual model for understanding the diverse biophysical principles through which the nervous system interacts with malignant tissues. These three modalities represent a proposed organizational continuum, ranging from diffusible molecular exchange to contact-dependent cellular interactions and activity-dependent circuit communication, with potentially increasing spatial precision, temporal resolution, and capacity for system-level regulation (Fig. 1).

Chemical communication

Chemical communication constitutes the major mode of neuro–tumor interactions. Tumor cells actively recruit neurons and promote neural remodeling within the TME by secreting neurotrophic factors [10, 45–55], axon guidance molecules [56, 57], and inflammatory mediators [47, 58, 59]. These signals not only induce axonal sprouting and increase nerve fiber density in the tumor vicinity but also support neuronal survival, thereby establishing a highly dynamic and adaptable neuro–tumor interface. Concurrently, autonomic and sensory nerve fibers release neurotransmitters [46, 60–67] and neuropeptides [48, 68–75], which engage their respective receptors on cancer cells to regulate proliferation, invasion, stemness maintenance, and responses to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies. This bidirectional chemical dialogue not only precedes contact-dependent interactions but also may facilitate for higher-order structural and electrophysiological integration.

The role of chemical communication extends far beyond classical ligand–receptor interactions. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly exosomes, mediate the horizontal transfer of microRNAs, proteins, and metabolic enzymes, thereby establishing direct molecular connectivity between neurons and tumor cells. From a neuro-oncological perspective, vesicle-mediated transfer enables tumors not only to modulate neuronal post-translational modifications and epitranscriptomic landscapes but also to induce hyperinnervation through neural regulatory factors, thereby promoting tumor innervation and contributing to therapeutic resistance. By secreting exosomes, tumors finely tune surrounding neurons to establish a neuro–tumor interaction network that supports tumor growth and invasion. Exosomes derived from different tumor types carry a diverse repertoire of functional biomolecules, including long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs) [76], m⁶A-modified RNAs [77], enzymes [78], and axon guidance molecules [79]. Upon uptake by neurons, these cargos regulate neurite outgrowth and neuronal fate. Collectively, diverse molecular mediators derived from tumor cells, neural components, and EVs remodel the neural microenvironment and coordinate bidirectional neuro–tumor communication. Structural coupling

Structural coupling represents a higher-order mode of neuro–tumor communication, characterized by the direct transmission of information through physical cell–cell connections. Tumors can evade immune surveillance by mimicking CNS anti-inflammatory mechanisms and disrupting immune synapses [82]. Increasing evidence indicates that cancer cells are capable of forming synapse-like connections with neurons, enabling spatially confined neurotransmitter release and rapid signal transduction within the TME [83–85]. Such structures have been identified not only in gliomas, pancreatic cancer, and breast cancer, but also exhibit functional features analogous to classical neuronal synapses, including local signal amplification, efficient receptor activation, and precise temporal control of signaling [85]. For example, in glioblastoma, a subset of highly active tumor cells orchestrates network signaling through periodic Ca²⁺ oscillations. Through synapse-like connections, these “hub” cells regulate MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways in neighboring tumor cells, thereby promoting proliferation and maintaining network homeostasis.

Gap junctions further establish cytoplasmic continuity between neurons and cancer cells, permitting the direct exchange of ions and small metabolites. They have been shown to play a critical role in the propagation of cholinergic nerve signal–induced Ca²⁺ electrical signaling. Specifically, acetylcholine (ACh) released from cholinergic neurons activates nicotinic receptors on intestinal epithelial cells, triggering intracellular Ca²⁺ elevation; this signal can then spread between adjacent epithelial cells via gap junctions, thereby promoting cell differentiation, suppressing inflammatory responses, and ultimately restoring tissue homeostasis [86].

In tumors, gap junctions, often associated with tumor microtubes (TMs), interconnect astrocytoma cells into multicellular networks that enable long-range signal transmission and functional synchronization [87, 88]. Cells linked through these microtube networks rely on gap junction–mediated communication to enhance proliferation, invasion, and resistance to radiotherapy, whereas isolated cells lacking such network support are more susceptible to therapeutic interventions. Accordingly, targeting TMs and their associated gap junctions may disrupt the multicellular network of astrocytomas and reduce treatment resistance [89]. Furthermore, synapse-like structures have been identified between metastatic cancer cells and glutamatergic neurons [90]. Within these structures, tumor cells partially assume astrocyte-like functions, enabling them to access neuron-released glutamate and activate NMDAR signaling pathways. This synapse-like configuration allows cancer cells to directly exploit neuronal signals, thereby facilitating their colonization and growth in the brain and exhibiting features of neural mimicry. The functional significance of structural coupling lies in its ability to extend neural signaling from the single-cell level to coordinated multicellular interactions within the TME. Through this mechanism, neurons can regulate cancer cell proliferation, invasion, stemness maintenance, and therapeutic resistance. These findings suggest that disrupting synapse-like structures or gap junctions may represent a novel strategy for precisely targeting neuro–tumor coupling and inhibiting tumor invasion and metastasis. In summary, structural coupling not only provides spatial and functional precision for neuro–tumor communication but also translates chemical signals into network-level instructions within the local microenvironment, offering a new conceptual framework for understanding neural regulation of tumors and for developing neuro-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Electrical coupling

Electrical coupling represents the most conceptually transformative mode of neuro–tumor communication. Across multiple tumor types, malignant cells have been shown to express functional ion channels, including sodium, potassium, and calcium channels [91]. Electrophysiological analyses indicate that malignant tumor tissues exhibit significantly higher levels of electrical activity compared with benign lesions or normal tissues [92, 93]. In addition, nerve fibers within tumors may form functional connections that further contribute to increased electrical activity within the tumor bed [94]. Electrical coupling plays a central role in tumor progression, encompassing both neuron-driven electrochemical signaling and intrinsic regulation of tumor cell membrane potential. In gliomas, neurons establish electrochemical coupling networks with tumor cells through AMPA receptor–dependent synaptic transmission and non-synaptic activity-dependent potassium currents. Gap junctions facilitate the synchronized propagation of these signals, leading to membrane depolarization in glioma cells and promoting proliferation, thereby driving tumor progression [95]. In glioblastoma, OPC-like tumor cells located at the tumor–neuron interface express the voltage-gated potassium channel KV4.2, which enhances local neural network activity through extracellular K⁺ accumulation, thereby promoting functional electrophysiological integration between tumors and neural circuits [96]. Similarly, in triple-negative breast cancer, tumor cells intrinsically modulate resting membrane potential and potassium channel activity to enhance cell migration, in vivo tumor growth, and metastasis, accompanied by activation of Cadherin-11 and MAPK signaling pathways [97]. This electrophysiological coupling operates not only at the single-cell level but also establishes functional networks within the TME. Glioblastoma cells form interconnected networks that enable synchronized propagation of Ca²⁺ transients. Within these networks, a small subset of tumor cells exhibiting periodic oscillatory activity functions as hub nodes; their intrinsic Ca²⁺ oscillations precede those of neighboring cells and drive frequency-dependent activation of MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways. These findings indicate that electrical activity serves not only as a mode of signal transmission but also directly regulates downstream proliferative and survival signaling. Targeting such activity—through physical ablation or inhibition of KCa3.1 channels—can disrupt global network synchronization, suppress tumor growth, and prolong survival, highlighting electrophysiological integration as both a key mechanism of tumor progression and a potential therapeutic target [98]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that, whether driven by neural inputs or tumor-intrinsic mechanisms, ion channels and membrane potential regulation are central to electrophysiological integration in cancer progression.

The clinical significance of electrophysiological integration lies in its ability to mechanistically explain tumor sensitivity to neuronal activity and rapid adaptive responses, while also providing actionable therapeutic targets. Modulating tumor ion channel activity may disrupt electrophysiological coupling within tumors, thereby suppressing proliferation, slowing metastatic progression, and improving therapeutic responses. In summary, electrophysiological integration elevates neuro–tumor communication from static chemical signaling or structural coupling to real-time, dynamic systems-level regulation, revealing the functional nature of tumors as components of neural regulatory networks and providing a theoretical foundation for the development of novel neuro-targeted therapeutic strategies.

To integrate the above observations, we propose a unified model of neuro–tumor communication that progresses in a temporally and spatially ordered manner and consists of three interdependent stages: chemical signaling, structural coupling, and electrophysiological integration. In the initial chemical phase, tumor- and nerve-derived soluble factors, including neurotrophic factors such as NGF and GDNF, neurotransmitters, and axon guidance molecules, collectively establish a permissive microenvironment that promotes axonal remodeling, receptor priming, and enhanced neuro–tumor reciprocity. This stage serves as a prerequisite for higher-order intercellular interactions. In the intermediate structural phase, synapse-associated and adhesion-related molecules mediate the formation of synapse-like adhesive interfaces and molecular scaffolds. This structural organization facilitates spatial confinement of signaling domains, promotes receptor clustering, and enhances local signal amplification, thereby establishing a structural basis for strengthened neuro–tumor coupling. Finally, the electrophysiological integration phase represents the highest level of organization, in which ion channel activity, gap junction connectivity, and neurotransmitter-driven synaptic transmission collectively enable coordinated electrophysiological and calcium signaling synchronization between neurons and tumor cells. This stage reflects a systems-level emergent property shaped by the preceding chemical and structural phases. Importantly, these transitions remain incompletely characterized at the mechanistic level and should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating continuum rather than a fully established biological sequence. Nevertheless, this framework provides a structured basis for generating testable hypotheses, including the sequential recruitment of synaptogenic adhesion molecules and ion channel complexes during neuro–tumor integration.

Central nervous system–driven regulatory networks linking the peripheral nervous system and tumors

The CNS integrates multidimensional signals, including emotional stress and circadian rhythms, and regulates tumor biology through autonomic efferent pathways (sympathetic and parasympathetic outputs), afferent sensory neural circuits and their feedback loops, as well as neuroendocrine signaling mediated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. Collectively, these pathways modulate the TME, immune status, and cancer cell behavior. Above these regulatory systems, circadian rhythms function as a global temporal coordination mechanism, synchronizing neural, endocrine, and immune outputs and thereby imposing rhythmic regulation on tumor–host interactions. This multilayered brain–tumor regulatory network constitutes a fundamental framework of cancer neuroscience (Fig. 2).

Central regulation of tumors via sympathetic nervous system (SNS)

Sympathetic outflow, regulated by brainstem nuclei and thoracolumbar spinal neurons, controls tumor biology through NE release from postganglionic sympathetic terminals within peripheral tissues, including the TME [99]. This signaling axis exerts multilayered regulatory effects on tumor initiation and progression, immune surveillance, and metastatic dissemination [100–103].

Chronic SNS activation suppresses the abundance of tissue-resident memory CD8⁺ T cells and promotes T-cell exhaustion, thereby limiting their proliferation, cytokine production, and antitumor effector functions [60, 99, 104–106]. SNS-mediated β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) signaling sustains the survival and immunosuppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) via STAT3 phosphorylation and Fas–FasL–dependent mechanisms, and further enhances T-cell suppression through upregulation of PD-L1 and arginase I [107]. In B-cell lymphoma and melanoma models, chronic β-adrenergic signaling directly inhibits antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cells and reduces responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade, whereas β-AR antagonism restores T-cell activity and enhances immunotherapeutic efficacy without impairing innate lymphoid responses [108]. In addition, SNS activation promotes pro-tumor remodeling of the TME, including increased infiltration of CD11b⁺F4/80⁺ macrophages, M2 polarization, and induction of pro-metastatic gene programs. It also upregulates NGF, MMP2, and MMP9 via β-AR/PKA/STAT3 signaling, thereby facilitating PNI and distant metastasis. Notably, these effects are primarily mediated by locally released NE from sympathetic nerve terminals rather than systemic circulating catecholamines [81, 109]. β-AR blockade (summarized in Table 2) reverses these effects by suppressing mesenchymal programs and enhancing immune infiltration, including CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, thereby improving responses to anticancer therapies [110–112].

Central regulation of tumors via (PNS)

The PNS regulates tumor biology primarily through vagal cholinergic signaling mediated by Ach [113–117]. At the immunological level, ACh acts on immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells through muscarinic ACh receptors (mAChRs), thereby suppressing inflammatory responses and cytokine secretion, ultimately influencing tumor immune surveillance. Studies have shown that activation of mAChRs on T cells increases aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) levels in antigen-presenting cells in both human and murine colon, thereby promoting regulatory T cells (Tregs) infiltration into surrounding tissues [118]. Cholinergic signaling mediated by the vagus nerve can also promote the release of TFF2 from memory T cells in the spleen, thereby suppressing inflammation and inhibiting the initiation and progression of colorectal cancer (CRC) [119]. Evidence from pancreatic cancer models further supports this concept: the application of ACh inhibitors reduce cancer cell activity and invasiveness by targeting phosphorylated ERK (pERK) signaling within the TME, while also decreasing tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) infiltration and the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines [120].

Concurrently, vagal signaling can modulate tumor angiogenesis, thereby creating a permissive microenvironment for tumor growth and metastasis. In CRC, ACh activates muscarinic receptor subtype 3 (M3R), which in turn stimulates protein kinase C-α, leading to activation of the EGFR/ERK and PKC/p38 MAPK signaling pathways. This cascade enhances MMP1 transcription and promotes CRC invasiveness. Conversely, blockade of M3R reduces tumor invasiveness, increases T-cell infiltration, and decreases the expression of angiogenic markers such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and CD31 [121, 122]. Although the SNS and PNS are traditionally regarded as independent regulatory systems, emerging evidence indicates that they exhibit tight functional coupling under central control. In lung adenocarcinoma, vagal sensory fibers expressing Npy2r transmit tumor-derived signals from the lung to brainstem nuclei, thereby driving increased sympathetic efferent activity within the TME. In turn, this suppresses antitumor immunity via β2-adrenergic signaling in alveolar macrophages [123].

In summary, the parasympathetic–cholinergic axis exhibits pronounced cell type– and context-dependent effects within the TME. It maintains anti-inflammatory responses and immune tolerance in immune cells, yet may drive pro-invasive and pro-angiogenic signaling in tumor epithelial cells. The net biological outcome is determined by the receptor expression profile, disease stage, and the spatial remodeling of neuroimmune networks. Future studies should, within a systems-level neuroimmune framework, delineate the spatiotemporal dynamics of cholinergic signaling to define actionable therapeutic windows in cancer.

Central regulation of tumors via sensory nerves

Sensory innervation within the TME has been shown to promote tumor initiation and progression [20, 93, 124, 125]. A synapse-like structural interface between sensory nerve terminals and cancer cells has been identified, which is associated with immune responses both in the peripheral tumor niche and in draining lymph nodes [126, 127].

In melanoma models, selective inhibition or activation of sensory neuronal firing within the tumor revealed that suppression of sensory neuron activity markedly accelerates tumor growth and angiogenesis, whereas excessive activation significantly attenuates both tumor growth and vascularization [93]. Notably, the opposite effect has been observed in breast cancer [68].

Sensory nerves can also relay signals to the CNS. These afferent fibers transmit inputs derived from tumors or external stimuli to the CNS. In brain tumors, recent studies have shown that olfactory stimuli can participate in gliomagenesis via olfactory sensory neurons [128]. In PDAC, neuroinflammation and neural injury occur in both the PNS and CNS as early as the PanIN stage. Tumor-derived signals can propagate along sensory nerves to the spinal cord, while neonatal sensory nerve ablation suppresses PNI, astrocyte activation, and neural injury, ultimately improving survival [20]. Further studies have demonstrated that sensory neuron–derived CCL21 and CXCL10 promote PDAC cell migration via their receptors CCR7 and CXCR3. Pharmacological blockade of these receptors significantly attenuates hyperalgesia, reduces nerve fiber hypertrophy, and improves behavioral outcomes, without affecting T-cell or neutrophil infiltration into the tumor [129]. These apparently contradictory findings suggest that the biological effects of sensory innervation are highly context dependent rather than universally tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive. Importantly, the divergent outcomes appear to arise from differences in how neural signals are integrated within distinct tumor microenvironments, rather than from the intrinsic properties of sensory neurons themselves. In PDAC, sensory neurons establish reciprocal inflammatory communication with neoplastic cells and promote perineural invasion through chemokine-mediated neuron–tumor interactions, thereby facilitating early tumor progression [20, 129]. By contrast, increased sensory neuronal activity in melanoma enhances antitumor immune surveillance by promoting cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration while reducing immunosuppressive immune cell populations, resulting in suppression of tumor growth [93]. In breast cancer, sensory neuron-derived neuropeptides, including CGRP and substance P (SP), activate PI3K–AKT signaling, promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and reduce chemotherapy sensitivity, thereby facilitating tumor progression [48]. Collectively, these observations support a conceptual framework in which the biological consequences of neural signaling are determined by the local inflammatory milieu, immune composition, and neuron-derived molecular mediators that shape tumor-specific neuro–microenvironment interactions. This context-dependent perspective may help explain the seemingly opposing roles of sensory innervation across different tumor types and provides a rationale for developing tumor-specific neuro-targeted therapeutic strategies.

In basal cell carcinoma models, hair follicle stem cells are innervated by sensory nerves, which promote stem cell proliferation through activation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway; surgical or functional denervation suppresses tumor initiation [124]. Tumor-derived VEGF-A, PLGF-2, and VEGF-B can selectively activate VEGFR1 expressed on sensory neurons in both human cancers and murine models, thereby enhancing pain sensitivity [130].

Central regulation of tumors via HPA axis

Chronic stress induces functional and structural remodeling of neural circuits, thereby influencing systemic tumor regulation [131, 132]. The hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) serves as a central hub integrating stress signals with neuroendocrine and immune regulation. Stress activation of PVN neurons initiates the HPA axis, leading to corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) release and subsequent glucocorticoid production from the adrenal cortex [133–136]. Glucocorticoids exert their effects through glucocorticoid receptors expressed on tumor cells and immune cells [137–139]. They can induce apoptosis of immature B cells [140], suppress CD8⁺ T cells [141] and natural killer (NK) cell activity [142], thereby facilitating immune evasion and promoting tumor growth and metastasis. Elevated plasma glucocorticoid levels can further activate glucocorticoid receptors on tumor cells at distant metastatic sites, significantly enhancing metastatic colonization in breast cancer and exacerbating intratumoral heterogeneity [143]. Moreover, chronic activation of the HPA axis alters the expression profile of inflammatory mediators within the TME, promoting the secretion of pro-tumorigenic cytokines such as IL-6 [144] and TGF-β [145], thereby enhancing tumor cell invasiveness, chemoresistance, and metastatic potential. Stress-induced glucocorticoid dysregulation can lead to neuronal injury in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus, resulting in impairments in executive functions, including working memory and cognitive flexibility [146]. Cognitive decline has been reported in patients with untreated breast cancer or CRC, and is partly attributed to the stress associated with the disease [147–149]. In breast cancer patients in remission, a marked reduction in episodic autobiographical memory retrieval has been observed, accompanied by significant hippocampal atrophy [150]. Moreover, activation of the lateral hypothalamic area (LH) and its orexinergic neurons has been shown to participate in the psychological stress response by stimulating the HPA axis [151].

Circadian rhythms

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) functions as the central circadian pacemaker, coordinating peripheral metabolic, endocrine, and immune rhythms through neural and hormonal outputs [152–154]. At the molecular level, circadian disruption promotes tumor progression through oncogenic activation, metabolic reprogramming, and impaired DNA damage responses [153, 155]. Circadian clocks also regulate cell-cycle progression through rhythmic control of cyclins, WEE1, and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, linking temporal regulation to tumorigenesis [156–158]. Recent single-cell studies have revealed substantial heterogeneity among circadian neural circuits, suggesting specialized neuronal populations may differentially regulate systemic physiological processes relevant to cancer biology [159, 160].

Circadian regulation directly influences tumor growth dynamics, as demonstrated by time-of-day–dependent differences in tumor progression observed in orthotopic breast cancer and colorectal cancer models [161]. In addition to tumor cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) also display circadian oscillations in both abundance and phenotype. In murine models, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes exhibit the strongest antitumor activity at night and the weakest activity in the morning [162]. Accordingly, time-of-day–optimized administration of CAR-T cell therapy or immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) may improve therapeutic efficacy in cancer [163].

Neurovascular coupling in tumors

Neurovascular development represents a conserved program in which neural and vascular systems share common guidance mechanisms to establish spatially organized networks. In cancer, these developmental pathways are aberrantly reactivated and co-opted to support tumor vascularization and progression. Many molecules initially identified as axon guidance regulators have also been shown to regulate vascular patterning, highlighting the shared molecular basis of neurovascular organization [164]. Through modulation of angiogenesis, vascular permeability, and endothelial function, neural signals establish a dynamic neurovascular regulatory network within the TME that supports tumor growth and adaptation [165–167] (Fig. 3).

Angiogenesis

β-adrenergic signaling promotes angiogenesis through activation of the cAMP–PKA pathway and upregulation of VEGF, MMP2, and MMP9 expression [168, 169]. Sympathetic nerve–derived norepinephrine (NE) further promotes angiogenesis by inducing metabolic adaptation in endothelial cells, including enhanced glycolytic dependence and altered oxidative metabolism [170]. In breast cancer, β-adrenergic activation also enhances angiogenesis by increasing VEGF-expressing tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). β-adrenergic activation in tumor cells further contributes to angiogenic remodeling through induction of VEGF and pro-angiogenic cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-8 [102, 171].

In addition to adrenergic signaling, multiple neurotrophic factors and neurotransmitters are also involved in the regulation of angiogenesis. Overexpression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) significantly promotes endothelial cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and survival. Microarray and molecular biological studies have shown that RhoA, caspase-9, caspase-3, GAS6, and VEGF are involved in mediating BDNF/TrkB-induced angiogenic processes. Co-implantation experiments further demonstrate that endothelial cells with high BDNF expression enhance tumor angiogenesis and tumor growth [54]. Nerve growth factor (NGF) promotes pathological neovascularization in glioma through direct interaction with α9β1 integrin, with effects comparable to VEGF treatment [172]. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) also facilitates angiogenesis and breast cancer progression by upregulating VEGF expression and secretion. Its pro-angiogenic effects are thought to be mediated through actions on endothelial cells, involving activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and, in part, VEGF-dependent signaling pathways [75]. 5-HT is likewise implicated in tumor angiogenesis. In C57BL/6 mice bearing MC-38–induced tumors, 5-HT regulates angiogenesis by downregulating MMP12 expression in tumor-infiltrating macrophages and reducing angiostatin levels [173]. Activation of the glutamate receptor GRM1 triggers the AKT/mTOR/HIF-1 signaling pathway, leading to upregulation of IL-8 and VEGF expression, thereby enhancing angiogenic signaling and promoting melanoma progression [174].

Vascular permeability

Neural signaling also plays a pivotal role in regulating vascular barrier function. NMDA receptor signaling is critically involved in the regulation of blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability. NMDA receptor activation increases BBB permeability by altering endothelial transport processes and barrier integrity through intracellular signaling pathways [175]. Conversely, dopamine strengthens endothelial junctional integrity and counteracts VEGF-induced vascular permeability by modulating VEGFR signaling and endothelial junction proteins [176, 177].

In addition to pro-angiogenic signals, several neurotransmitters also exert anti-angiogenic effects. Dopamine, via DRD2, downregulates VEGFR-mediated MAPK signaling, thereby inhibiting angiogenesis in tumor endothelial cells and endothelial progenitor cells [178]. In a murine model of cholangiocarcinoma, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) suppresses VEGF-A/C expression, reduces cellular proliferation, and decreases tumor volume [179].

Beyond their roles in modulating tumor angiogenesis, neural and vascular systems can also form a structural migratory niche that facilitates distant metastasis. Breast cancer cells exploit adhesion between integrin α6 and vascular basement membrane laminin to migrate along the abluminal surface of blood vessels and invade the leptomeninges. These vessels, which connect vertebral or cranial bone marrow to the meninges, enable tumor cells to bypass the blood–brain barrier. Once within the leptomeningeal space, breast cancer cells co-localize with perivascular meningeal macrophages and induce the expression of the pro-survival neurotrophic factor glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) [180].

In summary, the neurovascular system represents an integrated signaling and structural network that coordinates vascular remodeling and tumor adaptation. Neural-derived signals regulate angiogenesis, vascular permeability, and endothelial adaptation, thereby shaping a vascular niche that supports tumor growth and microenvironmental interactions. Importantly, neural regulation is context-dependent, with different neural signals exerting distinct and sometimes opposing effects on tumor vascular remodeling. Beyond functional regulation, neurovascular interactions can also create spatial niches that facilitate tumor cell migration and metastatic dissemination. This multilayered coordination reflects the tumor-mediated co-option and reactivation of developmental programs. Future studies should therefore move beyond isolated analyses of neural or vascular components and adopt a systems-level neurovascular–TME framework to define their spatiotemporal dynamics and heterogeneity. From a therapeutic perspective, targeting either angiogenesis or neural signaling alone may have limited efficacy. In contrast, network-based combinatorial interventions, integrating neuromodulation with anti-angiogenic therapy or immunotherapy, may provide promising strategies to improve therapeutic efficacy.

Neural ECM niches as structural interfaces

The ECM is a dynamic three-dimensional macromolecular network produced by resident and stromal cells that shapes tissue architecture and signaling [181]. Beyond structural support, the ECM provides biochemical and mechanical cues that regulate cellular behavior [182, 183]. Rather than acting as a passive scaffold, the ECM serves as a biomechanical regulator and signaling hub that integrates extracellular cues to influence cell fate [181, 184–189]. In breast cancer, increased tumor stiffness is strongly associated with a higher risk of metastasis and reduced overall survival [190]. Therapeutic strategies targeting the ECM are therefore emerging as a promising frontier in cancer treatment [191–196] (Fig. 4).

Neural regulation of ECM remodeling

Growing evidence indicates a bidirectional crosstalk between the nervous system and the ECM within the TME, whereby neural signaling drives ECM remodeling, while ECM properties in turn modulate neural activity and innervation [197]. Pharmacological modulation of neural signaling can reshape the tumor ECM. For example, long-term use of benzodiazepine receptor agonists and Z-drugs (BZDRs) promotes breast cancer progression by activating the GABA-A receptor subtype GABRA3 and inducing ECM reprogramming, characterized by reduced expression of tumor-suppressive ECM components (S100B, COL6A6, and VIT) and increased expression of pro-tumor ECM molecules such as FBN3, thereby promoting cancer cell migration and invasion [198]. Engineered ECM mimetics, such as chitosan-based scaffolds, have been shown to enhance mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) adhesion, survival, and neurotrophic activity through activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway [199]. Similarly, neurotransmitters released from activated nerves can directly modulate stromal architecture and tumor cell invasive behavior. Sympathetic nerves promote Src-dependent invadopodia formation via NE– β2-AR signaling and facilitate local ECM degradation, thereby enhancing three-dimensional tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential [200]. Neurons can also remodel the ECM through direct secretion of matrix components. For example, loss of NDRG4 in neurons induces the secretion of ECM proteins such as Nidogen-1 and Fibulin-2, thereby altering ECM composition and promoting CRC cell migration [201]. Consistently, transcriptomic analyses of recurrent glioblastoma have revealed progressive enrichment of ECM-related signals derived from perivascular stromal cells, suggesting that tumor evolution is driven not only by intrinsic genetic alterations but also by dynamic remodeling of the neurovascular–ECM microenvironment [202].

CAFs as intermediaries in neuro–ECM signaling

Neural signals can regulate fibroblast activation through paracrine mechanisms, such as sympathetic nerve-derived NE–dependent activation of macrophage–fibroblast communication [203]. In addition, ECM remodeling can reciprocally influence tumor behavior. For example, ECM stiffness regulates TYK2 localization and activity, with increased stiffness causing cytoplasmic redistribution and functional impairment of TYK2, thereby promoting breast cancer progression [190]. Spatial transcriptomic studies in PDAC further demonstrate that neural invasion occurs within a specialized fibrotic niche enriched in myofibroblasts, NLRP3⁺ macrophages, and TGFBI⁺ Schwann cells. These findings indicate that neural components undergo reactive remodeling within the stiffened ECM environment, accompanied by the emergence of neurotropic tumor cell states [204].

Within the TME, CAFs are the principal effectors of ECM remodeling and serve as critical intermediaries linking neural signaling to stromal dynamics. In pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, a low tumor-to-stroma ratio is significantly associated with poorer patient prognosis [205]. In early pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia, dense newly formed nerve fibers have been observed in close proximity to myofibroblast-like CAFs (myCAFs) [61]. In non-functional pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, patients with a higher stromal fraction exhibit worse outcomes. Notably, myCAFs—characterized by FAP positivity and high α-SMA expression—are spatially closer to tumor cells [206]. These findings suggest that, in malignant tumors, CAFs, tumor cells, and newly formed nerves may exhibit coordinated spatial co-localization within the TME.

The interaction between nerves and the stromal matrix can profoundly reshape fibroblast functional states and ECM architecture. In PDAC, nociceptive neurons interact with CAFs via calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and NGF signaling, leading to suppression of IL-15 expression in CAFs. This, in turn, impairs NK cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity, thereby promoting tumor progression and cancer-associated pain [207]. Schwann cells induce an inflammatory CAF (iCAF) program through IL-1α signaling, thereby remodeling the stromal matrix and promoting a more aggressive tumor phenotype [208]. Activated Schwann cells are significantly increased in CRC and are induced by tumor cells and CAFs via c-Jun signaling. This process reprograms their secretome, including increased IL-6 production, thereby facilitating tumor growth and malignant progression [209]. The mechanical properties of the neural ECM also regulate tumor cell plasticity during PNI. High-stiffness collagen-rich epineurial regions induce transient invasive programs in tumor cells, whereas softer intraneural environments attenuate these states, suggesting that neural ECM functions as a biomechanical niche controlling tumor cell state transitions during invasion [210].

Neural signaling can further integrate ECM remodeling processes through CAF-mediated signaling circuits. When NGF levels are elevated in the TME, sensory neurons secrete the neuropeptide CGRP. CGRP activates CAFs via the RAMP1–cAMP–PKA–CREB signaling axis, thereby enhancing collagen deposition and promoting the formation of a dense stromal barrier that contributes to an immunosuppressive and immune-excluded TME [45]. Other neural-associated factors, such as SEMA3A and VGF, regulate tumor adaptation to mechanically constrained environments and promote CAF activation or metabolic coupling between stromal and tumor cells [211, 212]. Conversely, CAFs can promote tumor innervation by secreting neurotrophic factors such as NGF, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop between neural and stromal compartments. CAF-derived transcripts and non-coding RNAs (circKLHL24 [18] and PIAT [17]) further facilitate PNI through chemokine signaling and epigenetic regulation. Recent studies have also revealed extensive crosstalk among nerves, CAFs, and tumor cells. In CRC, CAFs and sympathetic nerves form a bidirectional positive feedback loop: NE stimulates CAF-derived NGF secretion, which enhances intratumoral innervation and NE accumulation. In turn, CAF-derived NGF promotes cancer cell proliferation via the PI3K/AKT pathway, while adrenergic signaling activates YAP to accelerate tumor growth [46]. In osteosarcoma, CAFs promote peritumoral nerve growth by secreting factors such as IL-6 and BDNF that enhance axonal extension. Reciprocally, nerves stimulate osteosarcoma cell proliferation and migration, establishing a reinforcing loop that drives tumor aggressiveness [47].

ECM remodeling–immune–neural crosstalk

In addition to interactions among stromal cells, ECM remodeling is closely linked to immune regulation. For example, myofibroblasts expressing Tenascin-C can stimulate sensory nerves to release CCL2, which in turn recruits macrophages along growing axons, thereby establishing a fibroblast–nerve–macrophage axis that sustains inflammatory remodeling [213]. TAMs have recently been identified as active regulators of intratumoral neurogenesis. By secreting factors such as SPP1 and activating mTORC2 signaling, TAMs directly promote neurite outgrowth and nerve infiltration [214].

Collectively, these findings support the emergence of a new conceptual framework in which neural signaling, ECM remodeling, and tumor cell plasticity form an integrated regulatory network. In cholangiocarcinoma, spatial transcriptomic analyses have revealed that PNI-associated niches are enriched with Schwann cells, M2-like macrophages, and matrix-producing CAFs, alongside activation of ECM remodeling and axonogenesis-related pathways [215]. Mechanistically, tumor-derived exosomes carrying LINC01978 can promote neurite outgrowth via GDNF signaling, which subsequently activates RET–STAT3 signaling in tumor cells to enhance malignant phenotypes. Following nerve infiltration, nerve-derived CCL2 further recruits inflammatory monocytes, which differentiate into tumor-associated macrophages and secrete cathepsin B (CTSB) to degrade the perineural ECM. Together, these processes establish a neural-coupled dual-feedback loop in which neural remodeling and ECM degradation mutually reinforce each other, thereby sustaining and amplifying PNI [76]. Overall, these studies support a model in which the neuro–ECM interface serves as a dynamic regulatory hub within the TME, coordinating stromal activation, immune cell recruitment, and tumor cell state plasticity. Accordingly, targeting this neuro–stromal signaling axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to constrain tumor invasion and metastasis.

Neural regulation of tumor metabolism

Beyond direct structural interactions between nerves and cancer cells, neural circuits can profoundly influence tumor progression by remodeling the TME and reprogramming systemic host metabolism. Increasing evidence indicates that neural inputs can establish specialized metabolic niches, thereby regulating local cellular metabolism, which may in turn be exploited by tumors. For example, NPY⁺ sympathetic axons within the perivascular niche sustain mural cell proliferation and promote thermogenic adipocyte differentiation, thereby modulating whole-body energy expenditure [216]. Disruption of this neurovascular metabolic niche leads to metabolic imbalance, highlighting the capacity of neural circuits to shape tissue metabolic states through regulation of local metabolic cell populations. Tumors are likely to co-opt this mechanism to reprogram metabolic processes within the TME (Fig. 5).

Recent studies have introduced the concept of neuro–tumor metabolic crosstalk, referring to a bidirectional metabolic coupling between neurons and tumor cells. In this process, tumors can actively reprogram neuronal metabolic circuits, thereby establishing metabolite-enriched niches [217]. For example, in breast cancer brain metastases, tumor-derived EVs carrying miR-199b-5p suppress key solute carrier transporters involved in neuron–astrocyte metabolic coupling, including EAAT2 in astrocytes, as well as SNAT2 and MCT2 in neurons. Inhibition of these transport systems disrupts metabolic exchange between neurons and glial cells, leading to the accumulation of glutamate, glutamine, and lactate in the extracellular space. These metabolites are subsequently utilized by metastatic tumor cells as metabolic substrates [218]. In addition, neural tissue itself can serve as a metabolic reservoir. In glioblastoma, tumor-associated macrophages phagocytose cholesterol-rich myelin debris and subsequently export lipids to tumor cells via the LXR–ABCA1 pathway, thereby fulfilling tumor metabolic demands [219].

Neural regulation and systemic metabolic reprogramming drive tumor progression

Tumor progression is also closely linked to neural regulation of systemic metabolism. Tumors can remotely reprogram central neural circuits through metabolites such as SAM, reshaping systemic metabolic states [220]. Peripheral sympathetic denervation prevents tumor-induced hyperglycemia [221]. Cancer-associated cachexia represents a prototypical example of this interaction. Recent studies have identified a tumor–immune–neural regulatory circuit centered on macrophage-derived growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15). Tumor-secreted colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) induces macrophages to produce GDF15, which acts on the CNS to enhance β-adrenergic signaling in both the TME and peripheral tissues. This feed-forward amplification loop ultimately drives anorexia, muscle wasting, and adipose tissue loss, indicating that tumors can exploit neural pathways to reprogram host metabolism at a systemic level [222]. Conversely, systemic metabolic states can influence tumor progression via neuroendocrine signaling. In lung cancer, lower body mass index (BMI) is associated with an increased risk of brain metastasis, a phenomenon linked to activation of the ghrelin–GHSR signaling axis [74]. Elevated ghrelin levels stimulate neuronal secretion of neuropeptide Y (NPY), which in turn acts on Y5 receptors expressed on tumor cells to drive metabolic reprogramming, thereby facilitating tumor colonization in the brain. These findings indicate that systemic metabolic cues can be transmitted through neural pathways to reshape tumor metabolism and enhance metastatic potential. Hyperglycemia activates NRG1 enhancer signaling through epigenetic remodeling in breast cancer cells. Upregulated NRG1 subsequently activates ERBB signaling and interacts with the NOTCH pathway, thereby promoting tumor growth and therapeutic resistance [223]. In parallel, in models of cancer-associated cachexia, tumor-induced systemic inflammation disrupts vagal tone and impairs the brain–liver vagal axis. This neural dysfunction leads to depletion of the key hepatic transcription factor HNF4α, resulting in profound reprogramming of hepatic protein metabolism and further amplification of systemic inflammation. The resulting metabolic imbalance ultimately drives the cachectic phenotype [224].

Neural signaling can also regulate tumor immunity through metabolic intermediates. In gastric cancer, vagus nerve–derived ACh induces upregulation of the lipase ABHD16A in tumor cells, thereby promoting the production of the lipid metabolite LysoPS. Within the TME, LysoPS activates group 3 innate lymphoid cells and, via the GPR34–AKT–STAT3 signaling pathway, stimulates their secretion of IL-22. Subsequently, IL-22 induces the unfolded protein response in tumor cells and enhances PD-L1 expression, thereby promoting immune tolerance. These findings delineate a neuro–metabolic–immune regulatory circuit in which neural signals drive tumor metabolic reprogramming to shape an immunosuppressive TME [225].

Direct structural communication between neurons and cancer cells can also support tumor metabolism. In PDAC, sensory nerve terminals form synapse-like contacts with tumor cells. Within these structures, tumor cells are enriched for the NMDA receptor subunit GRIN2D on their surface. This configuration enables cancer cells to respond to neuron-derived glutamate, thereby activating calcium signaling pathways that promote tumor growth and invasion. Concurrently, sensory innervation further increases glutamate availability, establishing a neurotrophic feed-forward loop that continuously reinforces neuron–tumor interactions [85].

Lypd6b, a neuromodulation-associated molecule, is predominantly expressed in CD8⁺ T cells within the CRC TME. Lypd6b negatively regulates T-cell metabolic reprogramming and activation, thereby suppressing antitumor immunity. Its loss enhances glycolysis and suppresses oxidative phosphorylation in a PI3K/mTOR/LDHA-dependent manner, leading to improved CD8⁺ T cells function and augmented antitumor efficacy, particularly in combination with immune checkpoint blockade therapy [226].

β2-adrenergic signaling as a central regulator of tumor immunometabolism

Sympathetic stress signaling links neural activity with cellular metabolism and tumor immune regulation through β2-AR, inducing cell type–specific metabolic reprogramming within the TME. In immunosuppressive cells, β2-AR signaling promotes metabolic adaptation of MDSCs by shifting their metabolic preference from glycolysis toward oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation. This transition enhances MDSC functional fitness and promotes the production of immunosuppressive mediators such as PGE₂, thereby strengthening their ability to suppress T-cell activation and antitumor immunity [227]. Conversely, sympathetic signaling suppresses CD8⁺ T-cell metabolic activation by reducing glucose uptake, glycolysis, and mitochondrial function, thereby impairing effector responses [228]. This ultimately contributes to increased functional exhaustion of TILs. Consistently, blockade of β2-AR signaling restores both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in T cells, increases CD28 expression, and enhances antitumor activity, underscoring the pivotal role of this pathway in immunometabolic regulation [105].

Beyond immune cells, β2-AR–mediated neural signaling also directly regulates metabolic processes in tumor cells and the tumor vasculature. In HPV-positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, β2-AR signaling enhances mitochondrial metabolic activity and increases oxidative phosphorylation, thereby supporting cellular proliferation, migration, and metastatic potential [229]. Within the vascular compartment, sympathetic signaling regulates endothelial cell metabolism via β2-AR, coordinating the balance between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to control angiogenesis. This ensures that vascular growth is matched to the metabolic demands of tumor proliferation, thereby sustaining oxygen and nutrient supply [170]. In addition, in breast cancer, sympathetic signaling can regulate tumor cell glucose metabolism through ADRB2. This effect is mediated primarily through post-transcriptional upregulation of HK2, leading to enhanced glycolytic flux and increased energy production. Conversely, blockade of ADRB2 not only suppresses tumor glucose metabolism but also reduces ¹⁸F-FDG PET imaging signals, further demonstrating the direct impact of neural signaling on tumor metabolic states [170].

Collectively, β2-adrenergic signaling integrates neural activity with metabolic and immune regulation, coordinating tumor cell metabolism, vascular remodeling, and immune suppression at the systems level. This neuro–metabolic–immune axis provides a conceptual framework for understanding stress-mediated tumor progression and supports the therapeutic potential of β-adrenergic blockade in cancer metabolism and immunotherapy.

Mitochondrial remodeling driven by neural signaling

Centered on mitochondria, recent studies have revealed that the nervous system reshapes mitochondrial function and metabolism within the TME through multiple mechanisms, thereby driving tumor progression and modulating immune states. At the organelle level, neural-associated cells can directly enhance the bioenergetic capacity of tumor cells by regulating mitochondrial abundance and mediating mitochondrial transfer. When neurons are co-cultured with cancer cells, they undergo marked metabolic reprogramming characterized by increased mitochondrial biogenesis and expansion of mitochondrial mass. A subset of these mitochondria can be transferred to adjacent cancer cells, thereby augmenting oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic adaptability in tumor cells [230]. In glioblastoma, astrocytes can transfer functional mitochondria to tumor cells via a GAP43-dependent mechanism. This process significantly enhances mitochondrial respiration and metabolic plasticity in cancer cells, while promoting cell cycle progression, self-renewal, and tumorigenesis. These findings suggest that mitochondria are not only central hubs of energy metabolism but may also serve as critical resources through which tumor cells acquire metabolic advantages [231].

Concurrently, neural signaling can influence mitochondrial homeostasis in immune cells by modulating metabolic pathways. Under conditions of chronic psychological stress, catecholamines released by the sympathetic nervous system activate ADRB2 signaling to regulate the expression of quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase in hepatocytes, thereby shifting the kynurenine pathway from NAD⁺ synthesis toward the accumulation of kynurenic acid. Given that NAD⁺ is an essential cofactor for mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and energy production, this metabolic reprogramming impairs mitochondrial function in CD8⁺ T cells, leading to defective mitochondrial respiration and disrupted energy metabolism. Consequently, antitumor immune responses are attenuated, accelerating the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma. This mechanism highlights that neural stress signaling can indirectly modulate mitochondrial function in immune cells by altering metabolic flux, thereby contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME [232].

In addition, neurotransmitters can promote the acquisition of stem-like features in tumor cells by coupling mitochondrial biogenesis with stemness programs. Within the TME, NE activates the intracellular cAMP–CRE signaling axis in cancer cells and, through the transcription factor ATF1, coordinates pluripotency-associated transcriptional networks in the nucleus with mitochondrial biogenesis processes. This regulatory program enables tumor cells to acquire a stem-like state while simultaneously enhancing mitochondrial metabolic capacity, thereby maintaining high metabolic plasticity and adapting to fluctuating metabolic stress [233].

Overall, the nervous system participates in the regulation of tumor metabolism at multiple levels, including mitochondrial transfer, mitochondrial function, and mitochondrial biogenesis. On one hand, neurons and glial cells can directly enhance the oxidative metabolic capacity of tumor cells through intercellular mitochondrial transfer. On the other hand, neurotransmitters and stress-related signals modulate cellular energy metabolism in both immune and tumor cells by regulating NAD⁺ metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis. Accordingly, mitochondria emerge as a central hub linking neural signaling, tumor metabolism, stemness maintenance, and immune regulation, providing a new conceptual framework for understanding neuro–tumor interactions and offering theoretical support for the development of novel metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Neural regulation of lipid metabolism

The nervous system plays an important role in tumor initiation and progression by regulating lipid metabolic networks and is increasingly recognized as a key exogenous driver of metabolic reprogramming in cancer. Recent studies indicate that neurotransmitters, beyond their classical role as neuronal signaling molecules, can directly participate in the metabolic regulation of tumor cells, thereby influencing tumor growth, survival, and the immune microenvironment. In PDAC, serotonin (5-HT) regulates transcriptional activity through TGM2-mediated histone serotonylation. This epigenetic modification significantly upregulates the expression of stearoyl-CoA desaturase, a key enzyme in lipid metabolism, thereby promoting the synthesis of monounsaturated fatty acids. As a result, cellular membrane lipid composition is altered and lipid biosynthetic flux is enhanced, ultimately driving tumor cell proliferation and tumorigenesis. These findings suggest that neurotransmitters can directly induce tumor lipid metabolic reprogramming through epigenetic mechanisms [234].

Beyond epigenetic regulation, neurotransmitters can also modulate tumor cell metabolism through canonical signal transduction pathways. In gastric cancer, 5-HT released by the nervous system binds to the HTR2B receptor on tumor cells, thereby activating the Fyn–PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascade. This signaling axis subsequently upregulates the expression of metabolism-related genes such as HIF1α and ABCD1, promotes the regulation of fatty acid metabolism, and reduces lipid peroxidation, thereby inhibiting the induction of ferroptosis. Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, and enhanced fatty acid metabolism together with reduced lipid peroxidation can significantly improve tumor cell survival under oxidative stress and metabolic pressure. Therefore, neurotransmitter-mediated regulation of lipid metabolism not only enhances the metabolic adaptability of tumor cells but also promotes tumor growth by suppressing ferroptosis, and is closely associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients [235].

Neuro–metabolic interactions not only influence tumor cells themselves but also play a critical role in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Within tumors, metabolic reprogramming of both cancer and stromal cells often leads to marked lipid accumulation. Excess lipids can upregulate the expression of the lipid transporter CD36 on CD8⁺ TILs, thereby enhancing their uptake of oxidized lipids. Once oxidized lipids accumulate within T cells, they induce lipid peroxidation and activate the p38 MAPK signaling pathway, which in turn suppresses cytotoxic T-cell function and promotes the development of an exhausted T-cell phenotype. This lipid metabolism–driven immunosuppressive mechanism significantly impairs the antitumor activity of CD8⁺ T cells and facilitates tumor immune evasion. Notably, inhibition of the p38 signaling pathway or enhancement of GPX4-mediated detoxification of lipid peroxides can partially restore CD8⁺ T-cell effector function, thereby improving antitumor immune responses [236].

Collectively, these studies establish the neuro–metabolic–tumor axis as a key regulatory network that tightly links neural activity, metabolic plasticity, and tumor progression. Through diverse mechanisms—including neurotransmitter signaling, metabolic substrate supply, organelle transfer, and systemic metabolic regulation—neural circuits shape a metabolic ecosystem that supports tumor growth, immune evasion, and metastasis. Therefore, targeting neural regulation of tumor metabolism may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to disrupt tumor metabolic dependencies.

Tumor progression–associated neurotoxicity and treatment-related neural injury

Tumor progression–associated neurotoxicity

Cancer-related pain is not merely a clinical symptom but also reflects the profound bidirectional interaction between tumors and the nervous system. Breast cancer–associated pain activates sensory neurons and induces the release of CGRP and SP [68]. Clinically, higher pain severity is associated with elevated serum levels of CGRP and SP, which in turn correlate with reduced patient survival. In addition, this nociceptive signaling can promote EMT in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), enhance migratory capacity, and is accompanied by activation of the Gi protein–coupled PI3K–Akt signaling pathway [68]. Concurrently, multiple tumor types can secrete granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), which act on sensory neurons to enhance neuronal excitability, CGRP release, and axonal sprouting, thereby contributing to cancer-induced bone pain [237]. Moreover, tumor-derived VEGF family ligands (including VEGF-A, PLGF-2, and VEGF-B) can act on VEGFR1 expressed on sensory neurons, promoting neural remodeling and increasing pain sensitivity, thereby driving cancer-associated pain. Genetic or pharmacological blockade of VEGFR1 alleviates neural remodeling and cancer pain. Notably, anti-angiogenic therapies targeting the VEGF/VEGFR1 axis may also provide analgesic benefits [130]. Collectively, these findings indicate that sensory neuronal activity is not only a marker of pain but is also closely linked to tumor progression.

In addition to sensory neuron sensitization, PNI of sensory nerves is also strongly associated with cancer-related pain. In pancreatic cancer, sensory neurons secrete the chemokines CCL21 and CXCL10, which, via their receptors CCR7 and CXCR3, attract pancreatic cancer cells toward sensory nerve fibers, facilitating PNI. This process leads to neural remodeling and nociceptive hypersensitivity, thereby contributing to cancer-associated pain [129]. Upregulation of the NGF/TrkA signaling pathway may further promote PNI in pancreatic cancer and play an important role in the development of cancer-related pain [238]. In cancer-induced bone pain, TRPV1 activation in spinal neurons drives Ca²⁺-dependent nociceptive signaling, whereas TRPV1 internalization suppresses neuronal activity and pain transmission [239].

More importantly, a bidirectional neuro–immune–tumor feedback network is emerging. Tumors activate nociceptive neurons in primary tumors and draining lymph nodes through the ATF4–SLIT2 axis, enhancing CGRP release and promoting an immunosuppressive lymph node microenvironment. This promotes M2 macrophage polarization and tumor growth, creating a neuro–immune circuit that couples pain signaling with immune evasion [71]. Conversely, Schwann cell TRPA1 activation induces M-CSF release, promoting resident macrophage expansion and oxidative stress that sustains cancer-associated pain. These processes further promote macrophage accumulation within nerves and amplify their pronociceptive effects [240].

Treatment-related neural injury

Cancer multimodal therapy typically involves a combination of surgical intervention, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and endocrine therapy. These approaches integrate local and systemic treatments to achieve tumor control, prolong survival, and improve patients’ quality of life [241–244]. However, these therapeutic modalities are frequently associated with a broad spectrum of neurological adverse events, including peripheral neuropathy, central neurotoxicity, cognitive impairment, and neuroimmune-related complications, which may substantially compromise functional outcomes and limit treatment tolerability (Fig. 6).

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment, commonly termed “chemotherapy brain” or “brain fog,” results from neurotoxicity, neurovascular injury, oxidative stress, and impaired neurogenesis [245–250]. Chemotherapy can induce persistent microglial activation, leading to a “tri-glial” imbalance involving the microglia–astrocyte–oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) axis. This disrupts OPC homeostasis, impairs myelination, and contributes to cognitive dysfunction. Notably, microglial depletion restores white matter integrity and cognition, highlighting inflammatory microglia as a potential therapeutic target for chemotherapy-induced neurocognitive injury [251].

Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a frequent complication of cytotoxic therapy [252]. Approximately 60% of patients develop CIPN during treatment and up to 80% after completion, with symptoms including neuropathic pain, sensory loss, and hypersensitivity [253, 254]. CIPN arises from complex interactions between chemotherapy exposure and neuronal vulnerability, involving DRG transcriptional reprogramming, ion channel dysfunction, and altered neuronal excitability [255].

Platinum-based agents: CIPN is a frequent and potentially disabling adverse event associated with oxaliplatin-based treatment for CRC [256]. Oxaliplatin-induced neuropathy shows substantial inter-individual variability, suggesting that multifactorial biological and clinical predictors are required beyond single genetic markers [257]. Oxaliplatin enhances sensory neuron excitability by altering C mechanoreceptor activity and Kv4.3 channel function, converting innocuous stimuli into pain signals [258]. Oxaliplatin-induced FGF1 activation promotes tumor progression and DRG injury, whereas FGFR inhibition may simultaneously enhance antitumor efficacy and reduce neurotoxicity [259]. In cisplatin-resistant cancers, NANOG upregulates TRPV1, triggering Ca²⁺ influx and autophagy activation, which in turn enhances epidermal growth factor (EGF) secretion and activates the EGFR–AKT signaling pathway. This process promotes chemoresistance while aggravating chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain; TRPV1 inhibition can simultaneously relieve pain and restore cisplatin sensitivity [260]. Cisplatin also induces neurocognitive toxicity by causing synaptic loss, dendritic damage, and neuronal apoptosis, including hippocampal and neural stem cell injury [261].

Microtubule inhibitors: Microtubule-targeting agents, including paclitaxel, vinorelbine, eribulin, and ixabepilone, induce CIPN primarily through axonal injury and disruption of peripheral nerve fibers. These changes are accompanied by secondary demyelination, DRG degeneration, and Schwann cell activation. In addition, neurotoxicity induced by eribulin, vinorelbine, and ixabepilone is generally reversible within 3–6 months. In contrast, paclitaxel-associated neurotoxicity is more severe, longer lasting, and associated with delayed recovery. Paclitaxel also exhibits prolonged accumulation within the DRG and sciatic nerves [262]. Paclitaxel induces EGFR-dependent upregulation of DKK1 in tumor tissues and DRG, which suppresses CD8⁺ T-cell immunity and promotes peripheral neuropathy. DKK1 blockade therefore represents a strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing neurotoxicity [263]. Taxanes also activate Schwann cells to release galectin-3, which recruits macrophages to peripheral nerves and induces neuroinflammation and mechanical allodynia, contributing to CIPN development [264].

Targeting neural signaling pathways may improve chemotherapy efficacy while limiting neurotoxicity. Netrin-1 blockade reduces PMN-MDSC-mediated cancer stem cell maintenance and enhances immunotherapy sensitivity, whereas β-adrenergic blockade improves chemotherapy responses in TNBC [64, 265]. Although biomarkers such as neurofilament light chain may facilitate CIPN monitoring, effective preventive strategies remain limited. Duloxetine remains the only treatment with relatively strong evidence for painful CIPN, although clinical benefits are modest [266–271].

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy-associated neurological injury affects both the central and peripheral nervous systems and is determined by radiation dose, exposure volume, patient age, and baseline neurological status. The underlying mechanisms involve coordinated damage to neurons, glial cells, and vascular structures, with demyelination, neuroinflammation, and microvascular injury contributing to cognitive decline and neurological dysfunction.

Radiation-induced brain injury is characterized by hippocampal neuronal damage, synaptic remodeling, and oligodendrocyte loss. Pediatric patients are particularly vulnerable due to the sensitivity of immature neural cells, in which radiation-induced apoptotic pathways contribute to long-term neurocognitive impairment [272–274]. Neuroprotective strategies, including hippocampal-avoidance whole-brain radiotherapy combined with memantine and rehabilitation-based interventions, have shown potential for preserving cognitive function [275–277].

Radiation-induced spinal cord injury involves both white matter degeneration and vascular damage, including demyelination, fibrosis, inflammation, and ischemic changes. Although conventional fractionated radiotherapy has established spinal cord tolerance limits, stereotactic radiotherapy and re-irradiation require further refinement of dose constraints to minimize neurological complications [278–280].

Radiation-induced peripheral neuropathy (RIPN) is a chronic and disabling complication. Its primary pathogenic mechanisms include nerve compression secondary to radiation-induced extensive fibrosis, direct axonal injury and demyelination, and ischemic vascular damage resulting from disruption of the capillary network [281]. The sensitivity of peripheral nerves to radiation exhibits phase-dependent characteristics. Early injury involves transient electrophysiological changes and vascular dysfunction. Late-stage injury is characterized by persistent axonal degeneration, demyelination, fibrosis, and ischemic changes. Prevention remains the cornerstone of RIPN management, requiring careful optimization of radiation dose, fractionation, irradiated volume, and patient-specific risk factors. Physical therapy can also improve limb function and reduce pain and related complications [282].

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy activates host immune responses to eliminate cancer cells and has revolutionized cancer treatment. Major approaches include immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), adoptive cell therapies such as CAR-T cells, cancer vaccines, and immunomodulatory agents. However, immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including neurological toxicities, remain important clinical challenges [283].

Neurological irAEs associated with ICIs primarily involve the CNS and PNS. ICI-associated encephalitis is the most common CNS toxicity, presenting with nonspecific symptoms such as encephalopathy, fever, seizures, and meningeal irritation, which may mimic infection or tumor progression and delay diagnosis [284]. Proposed mechanisms include antibody-mediated injury, T-cell–driven neuroinflammation, and glial damage. High-dose corticosteroids and IVIG are commonly used, with most patients improving clinically, although fatal cases have been reported [285–287]. ICI-associated transverse myelitis is rare and may reflect systemic immune activation rather than tumor-specific immunity [288]. Peripheral neurological toxicities are generally less frequent and milder than chemotherapy-induced neuropathy but may include inflammatory polyradiculoneuropathy, Guillain–Barré syndrome–like syndromes, myositis, and neuromuscular junction disorders [289–294].

CAR-T cell therapy–associated neurotoxicity, particularly immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), typically occurs within 10 days after infusion and manifests as toxic encephalopathy. Severe cases may involve seizures, cerebral edema, and coma. Mechanistically, cytokine release, especially IL-1 and IL-6 from activated monocytes, disrupts BBB integrity through endothelial activation, promoting immune cell infiltration and CNS inflammation [295–299]. CAR-T cell infiltration into the CNS and potential off-tumor immune effects may further contribute to neurological injury [300]. Elevated neurofilament light chain (NFL) levels indicate axonal injury, whereas biomarkers such as reticulocyte counts may help predict ICANS risk [301, 302].

Clinical hurdles and therapeutic strategies

Neural regulation of tumor progression has emerged as a promising therapeutic axis; however, its clinical translation is fundamentally constrained by the pronounced context-dependent and bidirectional nature of neural signaling pathways. The same neural cue may exert distinct, or even opposing, effects depending on tumor type, disease stage, cellular composition, and microenvironmental context, creating substantial challenges for conventional pharmacological targeting.

A central example is β-adrenergic signaling, which has been extensively implicated in promoting tumor proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion in malignancies such as TNBC and PDAC. In contrast, cholinergic signaling pathways, including muscarinic and nicotinic receptor axes, have been reported to exert anti-inflammatory or tissue-protective functions in certain epithelial contexts, suggesting potential tumor-suppressive roles under defined conditions. Similarly, sensory neuron–derived signaling pathways and ion channel–mediated neuroregulation may contribute to either tumor progression or stress adaptation depending on treatment stage and tissue context. Collectively, these observations indicate that neural signaling in cancer does not operate as a unidirectional regulatory system but rather as a dynamic and context-sensitive network.

From a translational perspective, we propose that effective targeting of neuro–tumor interactions require strategies that move beyond global pathway inhibition. First, spatially restricted delivery systems, such as nanoparticle-based carriers or antibody-guided targeting, may enable tumor-selective modulation while minimizing disruption of physiological neural functions. Second, tumor microenvironment–activated prodrugs, including protease-cleavable constructs, may provide contextual activation within tumor niches. Third, temporal optimization strategies, including circadian-informed dosing regimens, may help resolve time-dependent signaling conflicts between tumor-promoting and tumor-protective neural inputs. Finally, combination approaches that selectively attenuate pro-tumor neural signaling while preserving or functionally rebalancing protective pathways may offer a more sustainable therapeutic paradigm than single-axis inhibition.

Importantly, we emphasize that this functional duality should not be interpreted as a limitation of neural signaling per se, but rather as an intrinsic property of neuro–tumor systems that reflects their physiological origin and adaptive plasticity. Recognizing this duality will therefore be essential for developing rational and clinically effective neuro-targeted cancer therapies.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Although substantial progress has advanced our understanding of nerve–tumor interactions, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. First, the spatiotemporal dynamics and cell-type-specific mechanisms governing neural regulation across different stages of tumor evolution remain poorly understood. Second, the molecular determinants underlying context-dependent neural effects require further clarification to distinguish tumor-promoting from tumor-suppressive signaling. Third, the development of selective and safe neuromodulatory strategies remains a major translational challenge, particularly in minimizing therapy-induced neurotoxicity while preserving physiological neural function.

Emerging technologies provide unprecedented opportunities to address these questions. Single-cell and spatial multi-omics, advanced neural circuit mapping, intravital imaging, organoid-based co-culture systems, and AI-assisted computational modeling are expected to enable comprehensive characterization of nerve–tumor communication across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Integrating these approaches with biomarker-guided clinical studies may facilitate the development of precision neuromodulatory therapies and rational combination strategies.

Ultimately, translating mechanistic insights into clinically actionable interventions will be essential for establishing nerve–tumor crosstalk as a therapeutic target in cancer management.
