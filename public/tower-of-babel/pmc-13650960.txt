Arthroscopic Palmaris Longus Tendon Bridging for Augmented Repair of Bursal‐Sided Partial Thickness Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Guo Zheng, ZunHan Liu, JunYan Yu, MoLong Chen, YunJiao Wang, ChenKe Zhang, Lin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of bursal‐sided partial‐thickness rotator cuff tears remains controversial. Both transtendon repair preserving the residual tendon and conversion to full‐thickness repair yield satisfactory outcomes. However, treating bursal‐sided partial‐thickness rotator cuff tears with significant tendon retraction because of delamination is challenging. Direct repair of the retracted bursal layer under high tension results in a high retear rate. The purpose of this technical note is to describe an arthroscopic method using the palmaris longus tendon for augmented repair of bursal‐sided partial‐thickness rotator cuff tears, aiming to promote biological healing and reduce the retear rate.

First, the glenohumeral joint cavity was accessed, and the articular surface of the rotator cuff was found to be intact and undamaged. Subsequently, the subacromial space was entered to perform subacromioplasty, decompression, and debridement. Partial injury of the bursal‐sided rotator cuff was observed, with a rotator cuff tissue defect formed by retraction after delamination. Despite thorough release, the bursal‐sided tear could not be manipulated to cover the rotator cuff footprint using tissue forceps. An arthroscopic ruler was used through the posterior and lateral portals to measure the anteroposterior and mediolateral dimensions of the defect, respectively. A suture hook loaded with the polydioxanone suture was used to perform mattress suturing at the myotendinous junction of the residual rotator cuff tissue. The woven sutures of the graft were guided through via the polydioxanone sutures, allowing the 2 blind ends of the graft to pass through the medial stump of the rotator cuff separately. A lateral‐row anchor was used to fix the graft to the lateral aspect of the greater tuberosity. A medial‐row anchor was implanted between the graft and the posterior rotator cuff stump, followed by side‐to‐side suturing between the graft and the posterior rotator cuff stump. After tying and tightening the sutures, the sutures were further secured to the lateral aspect of the greater tuberosity using additional lateral‐row anchors, and the surgery was completed. Partial‐thickness rotator cuff tears (PTRCTs) are a common shoulder condition, presenting with pain, limited range of motion, and muscle weakness. Its prevalence in the population ranges from 13% to 40%. 1 Arthroscopic repair is frequently recommended for Ellman grade 3 PTRCTs. 2 Although the outcomes are comparable between transtendon repair that preserves the residual tissue and conversion to full‐thickness repair for bursal‐sided PTRCTs, 3 certain patients continue to encounter persistent pain and functional limitations. Transtendon repair may create uneven tension across the cuff. 4 When there is significant retraction of the bursal layer, performing a repair under high tension elevates the risk of retear. Conversion to full‐thickness repair is feasible for smaller tears. Nevertheless, in the case of larger bursal‐sided tears, conversion leads to a large or massive cuff defect that may not be suitable for direct repair, which compromises the outcomes. 5

Managing bursal‐sided PTRCTs with substantial defects is challenging. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging should assess features of delaminated tears, such as delamination type, retraction length of the bursal layer, intramuscular split length, anteroposterior tear length, and the degree of muscle fatty infiltration. 6 Diagnostic arthroscopy is essential to confirm reparability. In some cases, even after adequate mobilization of the tendon and surrounding structures, the retracted bursal layer cannot be fixed anatomically without tension when the shoulder is abducted <60°. 7 Even after footprint fixation, the poor quality and reduced volume of the residual tendon fibers contribute to a high retear rate. Superficial augmentation using the coracoacromial ligament has been reported but may not suffice for larger defects. 8 The palmaris longus tendon, because of its accessibility, serves as an excellent graft source for rotator cuff reconstruction. 9 This article details an arthroscopic technique for augmented repair of bursal‐sided PTRCTs using an autologous palmaris longus tendon graft. This technique aims to reduce repair tension, facilitate biological healing, and lower the retear rate by augmenting the repair with a palmaris longus tendon graft. SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Preoperative Preparation

The surgical technique is shown in Video 1. The procedure is performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. The affected limb is positioned in an arm limb positioner (AssistArm Limb Positioner, CONMED, Utica, NY) with 30° to 45° of abduction (Figure 2). Four standard arthroscopic portals are established (posterior, anterior, lateral, and anterolateral).

Patient positioning and portal placement. (A) Posterior view of the right shoulder. (B) Lateral view of the right shoulder. (A, anterior portal; B, lateral portal; C, anterolateral portal; D, posterior portal.)

Portal Establishment and Diagnostic Arthroscopy

To begin, a 30° arthroscope (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) from the standard posterior and anterior portals is used for routine diagnostic arthroscopy of the glenohumeral joint to evaluate the long head of the biceps tendon, subscapularis, and other intra‐articular pathologies. If a subscapularis tear is present, it is repaired first. Capsular release and/or biceps tenotomy/tenodesis are performed as needed. The arthroscope is then withdrawn and redirected into the subacromial space via the posterior portal. An anterolateral portal is established. A thorough bursectomy and subacromial decompression are performed as indicated. The rotator cuff tear is assessed (Figure 3), and extensive releases are carried out if necessary to improve reparability.

Thirty‐degree arthroscopic view showing intraoperative exploration and assessment of tear dimensions. (A) The articular side of the rotator cuff appears intact within the joint. (B) Arthroscopic measuring probe assessing the medial‐lateral retraction of the bursal‐sided tear. (C) Arthroscopic measuring probe assessing the anteroposterior length of the retracted bursal‐sided tear. (HH, humeral head; LHBT, long head of biceps tendon.)

Palmaris Longus Tendon Harvest and Graft Preparation

The ipsilateral palmaris longus tendon is harvested. The tendon is palpated at the midpalmar wrist crease. A 1‐cm transverse incision is made over the palmar wrist crease. After blunt subcutaneous dissection, a high‐strength suture is woven through the distal tendon, which is then transected. The distal end is threaded into a tendon stripper, which is advanced proximally until it detaches at the musculotendinous junction. The harvested tendon is folded to create a 9‐cm‐long graft. The ends are similarly woven with high‐strength sutures, similar to the preparation of hamstring tendons for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (Figure 4).

Palmaris longus harvest and suture preparation. (A) A 1‐cm incision over the palmar wrist crease; the distal tendon is woven and harvested using a tendon stripper. (B) Gross view of the harvested palmaris longus tendon. (C) The harvested tendon is folded to create a 9‐cm graft, and the ends are woven with sutures. (PLT, palmaris longus tendon.)

Rotator Cuff Repair and Graft Implantation

Under visualization from the lateral portal, a suture passer is introduced via the posterior portal. A polydioxanone II guide suture is passed from outside to inside through the posterior aspect of the retracted bursal cuff layer at the musculotendinous junction. The intra‐articular end of the suture is retrieved through the anterolateral portal via a cannula. Another suture passer is introduced via the anterior portal, and a polydioxanone suture is passed from outside to inside through the anterior aspect of the retracted bursal cuff layer at the musculotendinous junction. Its intra‐articular end is also retrieved through the anterolateral portal. The sutures from the graft ends are sequentially shuttled through the cuff using the previously placed polydioxanone sutures via the anterolateral portal, completing a mattress suture configuration. The graft traction sutures are then pulled out through the anterolateral portal, pulling the graft limbs beneath the retracted cuff to the lateral aspect of the greater tuberosity. The footprint area and the intended graft path over the greater tuberosity are freshened with a burr. After adjusting the tension, the graft limbs are fixed to the lateral greater tuberosity using a knotless suture anchor (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA). A 4.5‐mm medial‐row suture anchor (StarSportMed, Beijing, China) is placed at the medial edge of the graft path on the greater tuberosity to reinforce the graft fixation. Another 4.5‐mm medial‐row suture anchor (StarSportMed, Beijing, China) is placed between the graft and the residual cuff tissue to suture the posterior residual cuff tissue to the graft. Finally, the residual rotator cuff is fixed using a double‐row technique (Figure 5).

Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

A shoulder abduction brace is strictly worn for the first 6 weeks postoperatively and removed only briefly for hygiene while supporting the hand. Patients perform daily grip strength exercises using a hand dynamometer and maintain an active range of motion for the wrist and elbow. Active‐assisted and active shoulder range‐of‐motion exercises commence at 6 weeks. Resistance training for the shoulder begins at 3 months. DISCUSSION

This article describes an arthroscopic technique for augmented repair of bursal‐sided PTRCTs using an autologous ipsilateral palmaris longus tendon graft. This technique is indicated for patients with bursal‐sided PTRCTs exhibiting substantial defects, where direct repair is unlikely to achieve optimal cuff tension. It aims to reduce repair tension, promote biological healing, and lower the retear rate through palmaris longus tendon augmentation.

Historically, cadaveric studies suggested that intratendinous tears were the most common type of PTRCT. 10 However, magnetic resonance imaging‐based clinical studies have challenged this: Kong et al., 11 reviewing imaging of 81 PTRCT patients, found bursal‐sided tears to be most prevalent at 72%, suggesting that their prevalence and clinical impact are underestimated, with a tendency to progress to full‐thickness tears. Kwon et al. 12 defined delamination in PTRCTs as retraction of the articular or bursal layer with a recognizable horizontal gap between layers. In bursal‐sided delaminated tears, the separated intratendinous fibers exhibit varying degrees of retraction. Although the presence of delamination does not necessarily affect the overall outcomes of arthroscopic repair, the presence of medium‐to‐large, retracted delaminated tears may be a risk factor for poor outcomes after single‐row repair. 6 Furthermore, large retracted defects from delaminated tears may not be amenable to in situ repair or conversion to full‐thickness repair. Given these issues, previous studies have employed patches for bursal‐sided PTRCTs, proposing the concept of augmentation using bioinductive collagen implants or autologous tendons rather than relying solely on the unhealthy residual tissue. 8 , 13 , 14 , 15 These studies offer surgeons a new patch augmentation strategy. Recently, the fascia lata, plantaris, peroneus longus, and biceps tendons have been used to augment repair of articular‐sided PTRCTs, yielding satisfactory functional outcomes for some patients. 16 , 17 , 18 However, none matches the palmaris longus in terms of harvesting convenience, adaptability, and low donor site morbidity. Therefore, the autologous palmaris longus graft holds promise and research value for improving outcomes in chronic bursal‐sided PTRCTs or defects, especially compared with the more common techniques of conversion to full‐thickness repair and in situ repair. 19 Regarding biocompatibility, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons released its latest guideline on rotator cuff injury management in August 2025. A significant change from the previous version is the “strong recommendation” for using bioinductive implants/scaffolds during repair to reduce retear rates and an explicit recommendation against using porcine xenografts. 20 Compared with allografts, the palmaris longus autograft used in this technique may offer favorable biological integration because of its viable cellular components and nonimmunogenic nature, 21 , 22 which could theoretically promote healing while minimizing inflammatory reactions.

Currently, our short‐term clinical outcomes show promise for this technique in medium‐to‐large tears. However, further research and long‐term follow‐up are needed to evaluate its efficacy with expanded indications.
