Efficient Gibbs Sampling in Cox Regression Models Using Composite Partial Likelihood and Pólya-Gamma Augmentation
Shu Tamano, Yui Tomo

Abstract
The Cox regression model and its Bayesian extensions are widely used in survival analysis. However, standard Bayesian approaches require modeling of the baseline hazard, and their full conditional distributions lack closed-form expressions. Therefore, the Metropolis-Hastings sampling algorithm is typically employed, whose efficiency is highly sensitive to the choice of proposal distribution. To address these issues, we propose the GS4Cox, an efficient Gibbs sampling algorithm for the Cox regression model based on four key components: (i) general Bayesian framework, (ii) composite partial likelihood, (iii) Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme, and (iv) finite corrections. Our experiments on both synthetic and actual datasets demonstrate that the GS4Cox algorithm outperforms existing sampling methods in terms of convergence speed and sampling efficiency.



Keywords
: Composite likelihood, Cox proportional hazards model, General Bayesian inference, Markov Chain Monte Carlo, Pólya-Gamma auxiliary variables, Survival analysis

1
Introduction

In time-to-event data analysis, the Cox regression model or the Cox proportional hazards model
(
Cox, 1972
)
is commonly used in various fields, such as medical research and quality assurance.
Parameter estimation is typically performed based on the partial likelihood function, which allows estimation without specifying the baseline hazard
(
Cox, 1975
)
.
While the partial likelihood approach provides a useful framework for statistical inference on the covariate effects, it has limitations when applied to complex data structures such as hierarchical dependencies.
Moreover, it relies on the point estimates and the asymptotic distributions, which may be inadequate in the presence of high model uncertainty, such as high-dimensional settings.
Bayesian extensions can provide a more flexible modeling framework for addressing hierarchical and other complex data structures by incorporating prior knowledge or assumptions on the parameters as prior distributions
(
Ibrahim et al., 2001
;
Chen et al., 2006
)
.
These Bayesian Cox models have been studied under a variety of priors
(
Ibrahim and Chen, 1998
;
Wu et al., 2018
;
Muse et al., 2022
)
, and applied to large-scale datasets
(
Jung and Gerstung, 2023
)
.
Furthermore, a systematic review has been conducted on its use in clinical trials
(
Brard et al., 2016
)
.

Bissiri et al., 2016
proposed a framework of general Bayesian inference, which allows for using general loss functions instead of the log-likelihood functions in Bayesian update.
This framework enables Bayesian approaches to clustering and robust estimation
(
Wade, 2023
;
Rigon et al., 2023
;
Chakraborty et al., 2024
;
Hashimoto and Sugasawa, 2020
;
Yonekura and Sugasawa, 2023
)
.
The general Bayesian update also justifies the posterior distribution in the Cox regression model using the partial likelihood function instead of using full likelihood function
(
McGree et al., 2023
)
.

In the estimation process of Bayesian models, sampling algorithms such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are typically employed to draw samples from posterior distributions
(
Robert et al., 1999
)
.
The Gibbs sampling method is one of the simplest algorithms, in which each parameter is sampled conditionally on the others, allowing for efficient sampling when full conditional distributions are available in closed form
(
Geman and Geman, 1984
;
Gelfand and Smith, 1990
)
.
The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm is a more general approach that constructs a Markov chain using a proposal distribution and an acceptance criterion, enabling sampling from posterior distributions even when full conditional distributions can not be analytically obtained
(
Metropolis et al., 1953
;
Hastings, 1970
)
.
Recently,
Polson et al., 2013
proposed a Pólya-Gamma (PG) augmentation scheme for logistic and negative binomial regression models.
In this scheme, when the prior distributions are normal, the complete conditionals of the parameters can also be obtained as normal distributions.
Owing to this scheme, an efficient Gibbs sampling algorithm for logistic and negative binomial regression models can be developed.
Moreover,
Linderman et al., 2015
showed that PG augmentation scheme can be applied to the multinomial logit models with stick-breaking expression of the likelihood functions.

Neelon, 2019
proposed an efficient sampling algorithm for the zero-inflated negative binomial regression models based on the PG augmentation.
Furthermore,

Ren et al., 2024
proposed the Cox-PG algorithm, which extended the PG augmentation scheme for the Cox regression model.
Their sampling algorithm uses a monotonic spline approximation of the log-cumulative baseline hazard, PG augmentation with a negative-binomial approximation to the underlying Poisson process, and MH corrections to control the resulting bias.
The Cox-PG algorithm necessarily trades off sampling efficiency against approximation error.

In this study, we develop GS4Cox, an efficient Gibbs sampling algorithm for the Cox regression model based on four key components:
(i) generalized Bayesian framework, (ii) composite partial likelihood, (iii) Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme, and (iv) finite corrections.
First, we show that the estimating function defined as the first derivative of the log-composite partial likelihood has expectation zero under the Cox regression model, and that the estimator derived from this estimating function has weak consistency.
To maintain comparability with the estimator based on the partial likelihood, we first present the asymptotic relationship between the estimator derived from the composite partial likelihood and the traditional partial likelihood estimator, and then derive the finite-sample correction term.
Furthermore, by incorporating the composite partial likelihood and Pólya-Gamma augmentation within the general Bayesian framework, we show that the full conditional distributions of the regression parameters can be expressed in Gaussian form.
Our GS4Cox algorithm requires no explicit baseline hazard estimation, naturally handles censoring and ties, and achieves high efficiency through full Gibbs steps.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we provide a review of the Cox regression model, the general Bayesian framework, and the Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme as applied to Cox regression.
In Section
3
, we propose the statistical properties of our composite partial likelihood and present details of the proposed Gibbs sampling algorithm.
In Section
4
, we perform numerical experiments.
In Section
5
, we apply the proposed method to real-world data.
Finally, in Section
6
, we conclude the paper with a discussion.

2
Related work

In this section, we introduce the Cox regression model and its general Bayesian inference.
We then present the Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme for the logistic regression model.
Finally, we introduce a recent study by
Ren et al., 2024
, which applied the Pólya-Gamma augmentation to construct an MCMC algorithm for the Cox regression model.

2.1
Cox regression models

For each subject
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we observe
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\mathcal{D}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}=\bigl\{(T_{i},\delta_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
T_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the observed time defined by
T_{i}=\min(T_{i}^{\ast},C_{i}^{\ast})
, with
T_{i}^{\ast}
representing the event time and
C_{i}^{\ast}
representing the censoring time.
The event indicator is defined as
\delta_{i}=\boldsymbol{1}\{T_{i}^{\ast}\leq C_{i}^{\ast}\}
, and
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
is the
P
-dimensional covariate vector.
We assume non-informative censoring, meaning that
T_{i}^{\ast}
is conditionally independent of
C_{i}^{\ast}
conditioned on
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
(i.e.,
T_{i}^{\ast}\perp\!\!\perp C_{i}^{\ast}\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
).

In the Cox regression model
(
Cox, 1972
)
, the hazard function for subject
i
at time
t
is given by

h(t\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{i})=h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
is a
P
-dimensional vector of regression parameters, and
h_{0}(t)
is a baseline hazard function.
For the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the full likelihood is given as follows:

\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\{\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})h_{0}(T_{i})\bigr\}^{\delta_{i}}\exp\bigl\{-\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})H_{0}(T_{i})\bigr\},

(2)

where
H_{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h_{0}(\tau)d\tau
is the cumulative baseline hazard function.
However, direct estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is challenging due to the infinite dimensionality of
h_{0}(t)
.
To overcome this,
Cox, 1975
introduced the partial likelihood, which focuses on the relative hazard contributions of individuals at observed event times.
Let
R(t)
denote the risk set at time
t
.
Then, the conditional probability that individual
i
fails at time
t
is given by

\Pr\bigl(i\text{ fails at }t\bigm|\text{a failure occurs at }t\bigr)=\frac{h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sum_{j\in R(t)}h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})}.

The baseline hazard
h_{0}(t)
cancels out in this conditional probability.
Thus, the partial likelihood is formed by taking the product of these probabilities over all observed events:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})}\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})}\right\}^{\delta_{i}}.

2.2
General Bayesian framework in Cox regression models

In the standard Bayesian update, the posterior distribution for the parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is given by

\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}),

where
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})
denotes the prior distribution and
f(\mathcal{D}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})
denotes the likelihood contribution of the
i
th observation.

Bissiri et al., 2016
proposed a framework of general Bayesian inference, which allows for using general loss functions instead of the likelihood functions.
Namely, the generalized posterior distribution is:

\pi^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\exp\Bigl(-w{L}\left(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)\Bigr),

where
w\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a learning rate parameter and
L\left(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)
denotes some loss function.
When
w=1
and the loss function
L\left(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)
is taken as the negative log-likelihood function,
\pi^{\ast}
corresponds to the standard posterior distribution.
As
w
decreases, the influence of the prior distribution becomes stronger
(
Grünwald and Van Ommen, 2017
)
.
Thus, the learning rate adjusts the relative influence of the prior and the data on the posterior update.
A number of data-driven methods have been proposed for choosing the learning rate, such as calibrating credible intervals from the generalized posterior distribution to achieve nominal frequentist coverage probability
(
Syring and Martin, 2019
;
Tanaka, 2024
)
, employing sequential updating schemes to avoid hyper-compression (i.e., under model misspecification in a non-convex family, the Bayesian predictive mixture can lie outside the model and achieve a strictly smaller Kullback–Leibler divergence to the true data-generating distribution than any single model component)
(
Grünwald and Van Ommen, 2017
)
, using information-matching approach
(
Holmes and Walker, 2017
)
, and applying asymptotic distribution-matching approach
(
Lyddon et al., 2019
)
.

Wu and Martin, 2023
provides a comprehensive comparison of these representative methods.

By adopting the negative log-partial likelihood as the loss function, we can avoid explicit modeling of the baseline hazard function in the Cox regression model.
However, the full conditional distribution is generally not available in closed form.
Consequently, sampling from the posterior distribution is typically performed using the MH algorithm or Metropolis-within-Gibbs algorithm
(
Jalaluddin and Kosorok, 2000
;
Lambert and Eilers, 2005
;
Kosorok et al., 2024
;
Schuemie et al., 2022
;
Ren et al., 2024
)
.

2.3
Pólya-Gamma augmentation for the logistic model

Polson et al., 2013
established the following integral identity:

\frac{\exp(\eta)^{a}}{\bigl\{1+\exp(\eta)\bigr\}^{b}}=2^{-b}\exp(\kappa\eta)\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\omega\eta^{2}}{2}\right)p(\omega\mid b,0)d\omega,

where
\kappa=a-b/2
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b>0
, and
p(\omega\mid b,0)
denotes the density of a Pólya-Gamma distribution
\operatorname{PG}(b,0)
.
The general
\operatorname{PG}(b,c)
density is obtained by an exponential tilting of
\operatorname{PG}(b,0)
density:

p(\omega\mid b,c)=\frac{\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\omega c^{2}}{2}\Bigr)}{\mathbb{E}_{p(\omega\mid b,0)}\Bigl[\exp\left(-\frac{\omega c^{2}}{2}\right)\Bigr]}p(\omega\mid b,0).

Then, the density of
\omega\mid b,\eta
takes the form of

p(\omega\mid b,\eta)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\omega\eta^{2}}{2}\right)p(\omega\mid b,0)}{\int\exp\left(-\frac{\omega\eta^{2}}{2}\right)p(\omega\mid b,0)d\omega},

and this shows
\omega\mid b,\eta\sim\operatorname{PG}(b,\eta)
.

For a binary outcome
Y_{i}
, consider the logistic regression model

\Pr(Y_{i}=1)=\frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\bigl\{1+\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr\}},

with a Gaussian prior
\boldsymbol{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
.
By introducing PG auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}
and setting
\kappa_{i}=y_{i}-1/2
, the complete conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be expressed as

\begin{split}\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\omega},\{Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})&\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\prod_{i=1}^{n}f\left(Y_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}\right),\\
&\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left\{\kappa_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\frac{\omega_{i}\bigr(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr)^{2}}{2}\right\},\\
&\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})\right\},\end{split}

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\ast}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{X}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\ast}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right]
, and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})^{\top}
.
Thus, conditioned on variables from
\operatorname{PG}(1,\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})
, the complete conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a Gaussian distribution.
Moreover,
Polson et al., 2013
;
Linderman et al., 2015
showed that this augmentation scheme can be extended to the multinomial and negative-binomial (NB) distributions.

2.4
Cox-PG algorithm

Ren et al., 2024
extended the PG augmentation scheme to the Cox regression model and proposed the Cox-PG algorithm.
Their algorithm construction involves a three-stage approximation that ultimately yields a Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler.

Additive monotonic spline of the baseline log-cumulative hazard.

Let
a(t)=\log H_{0}(t)
.

Ren et al., 2024
represent
a(t)
by an additive monotonic spline

a(t)=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\xi_{d}(t)s_{d}=\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}\!(t)\boldsymbol{s},\quad\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{D})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{>0},

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)=(\xi_{1}(t),\ldots,\xi_{D}(t))^{\top}
is the fixed design vector of
I
-splines and
s_{d}>0
are slope parameters.
Introducing
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{s}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})^{\top}
and the joint covariate vector
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}=\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\!\top}(T_{i}),\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{\top}
.
Under this notation, the full likelihood in (
2
) becomes:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\exp\bigl\{\delta_{i}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\bigr\}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}\!(T_{i})\boldsymbol{s}\right\}^{\delta_{i}}\exp\bigl\{-\exp(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr\}\right]\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{s}>\mathbf{0}\}.

Slice variables for the derivative term.

The factor

\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}(T_{i})\boldsymbol{s}\right\}^{\delta_{i}}

couples every event time
T_{i}
to a different spline slope component.
To remove it,
Ren et al., 2024
introduced

\nu_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\operatorname{Unif}\left(0,\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}\!(T_{i})\boldsymbol{s}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})^{\top},

turning the above factor into the indicator
\mathbf{1}\left\{\nu_{i}\leq\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}(T_{i})\boldsymbol{s}\right\}^{\delta_{i}}
.
For each spline interval
d=1,\dots,D
, define

v_{d}:=\max\bigl\{\nu_{i}:t_{i}\in[s_{d-1},s_{d}),\delta_{i}=1\bigr\}.

Then the constraint on the spline slopes
\boldsymbol{s}
can be written as the intersection of half-spaces

\mathcal{C}_{v}:=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:v_{d}\leq s_{d}\quad(d=1,\dots,D)\bigr\},

which is imposed in the subsequent truncated-Gaussian update for
\boldsymbol{s}
.

Gamma frailties and PG augmentation.

A second layer of latent variables by placing independent Gamma frailties
z_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ga}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
on each Poisson kernel contribution.
A Mellin transform shows that the Poisson kernel
\exp\{-\lambda_{i}\}\lambda_{i}^{\delta_{i}}
becomes a NB kernel

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda_{i}z_{i})(\lambda_{i}z_{i})^{\delta_{i}}\frac{\varepsilon^{\varepsilon}}{\Gamma(\varepsilon)}z_{i}^{\varepsilon-1}\exp(-\varepsilon z_{i})dz_{i}=\frac{\Gamma(\delta_{i}+\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(\delta_{i}+1)\Gamma(\varepsilon)}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon}\right)^{\delta_{i}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon}\right)^{\varepsilon},

where
\lambda_{i}=\exp(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
The right-hand side is the NB kernel with

p_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon},\quad\Pr(Y_{i}=\delta_{i})\propto p_{i}^{\delta_{i}}(1-p_{i})^{\varepsilon}.

Then, applying the PG augmentation, we obtain

\frac{e^{\psi\delta}}{(1+e^{\psi})^{\delta+\varepsilon}}=2^{-(\delta+\varepsilon)}\exp(\kappa\psi)\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\omega\psi^{2}}{2}\right)\operatorname{PG}\bigl(\omega\mid\delta+\varepsilon,0\bigr)d\omega,\quad\kappa=\frac{\delta-\varepsilon}{2},

with
\psi=\log(\lambda_{i}/\varepsilon)=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\log\varepsilon
.
This introduces

\omega_{i}\sim\operatorname{PG}\bigl(\delta_{i}+\varepsilon,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\log\varepsilon\bigr).

Conditioned on
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\nu})
, the complete data log-posterior in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a quadratic form.
Hence, its complete conditional is a multivariate normal truncated to

\{\boldsymbol{\theta}:s_{d}>0,\mathcal{C}_{v}\}
.

Algorithmic skeleton.

A full sweep updates
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\theta})
in turn, yielding a Gibbs proposal.
Since the algorithm employs a finite-
\varepsilon
Gamma frailty-based NB approximation,
Ren et al., 2024
append an outer Metropolis–Hastings check that accepts the new state with probability

\min\bigl\{1,\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{new}}\mid\mathcal{D})/\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{old}}\mid\mathcal{D})\bigr\},

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the exact Cox partial likelihood,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{new}}
denotes the parameter vector at the current iteration, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{old}}
denotes the parameter vector from the previous iteration.
The overall sampler is therefore Metropolis-within-Gibbs.

Practical considerations.

The accuracy of the Cox-PG approximation is controlled by the number of spline segments
D
and the frailty hyperparameter
\varepsilon
, yet pushing either to the extreme (i.e., very fine partitions or a nearly degenerate frailty,
\epsilon\to 0
), lowers the MH acceptance rate and prevents mixing.
Moreover, because the baseline hazard is represented explicitly via its spline-knot heights
\boldsymbol{s}
and slice variables
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, the sampler must explore this augmented, high-dimensional space even when one’s primary interest lies only in the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Finally, promoting every distinct failure time to a spline knot can render the
I
-spline design matrix ill-conditioned in large datasets.

In summary,
Ren et al., 2024
successfully extend PG augmentation to the Cox regression model, but this gain in modeling flexibility incurs an additional approximation layer and requires an outer Metropolis–Hastings correction.

3
Proposed method

In this section, we introduce a new Gibbs sampling algorithm for the Cox regression model, called GS4Cox, composed of four key elements; (i) generalized Bayesian framework, (ii) composite partial likelihood, (iii) Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme, and (iv) finite-sample corrections for the discrepancy between the partial likelihood and the composite partial likelihood.

3.1
General Bayesian inference

In accordance with the general Bayesian framework
(
Bissiri et al., 2016
)
, Section
2.2
employs the negative log-partial likelihood as its loss function, thereby avoiding explicit specification of the baseline hazard function.
Similarly, our algorithm adopts the negative log-composite partial likelihood introduced in Section
3.2
as its loss function, thus obviating the need to identify the baseline hazard.

3.2
Composite Cox’s partial likelihood

For each subject pair
(i,j)
in
R(T_{i})
, we define a
p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
as follows:

p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\operatorname{expit}\Bigl\{\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\Bigr\}=\frac{\exp\Bigl\{\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\Bigr\}}{1+\exp\Bigl\{\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\Bigr\}},

which can be interpreted as the probability that subject
i
experiences the event before subject
j
.
Using these probabilities, we define the composite partial likelihood as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\delta_{i}},

(3)

which aligns with the definition of a composite likelihood as presented in
Lindsay, 1988
;
Varin et al., 2011
.
Correspondingly, we define the composite partial score function as

S_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}\log\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\delta_{i}\Bigl[\bigl\{1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr\}\Bigr]\bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\bigr).

(4)

The following propositions hold for the composite partial score function and the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator:

Proposition 3.1

(Zero expectation of the composite partial score function)
.

Under the data-generating process defined in Appendix
A
, the expectation of the composite partial score function is zero.
That is,

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left[S_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right]=0.

Proposition 3.2

(Weak consistency of the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator)
.

Under the regularity conditions
(C1)
–
(C6)
, the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator converges in probability to the true value
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
That is,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}\overset{P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}.

See Appendix
C.2
and
C.3
for the proof of Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
, respectively.
Proposition
3.1
shows that the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}
to the estimating equation
S_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}})=0
is asymptotically unbiased.
Proposition
3.2
presents that the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
maximizing the composite partial likelihood converges in probability to the true value
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, just as the
\boldsymbol{\beta}
maximizing the partial likelihood does.

Furthermore, from Proposition
B.2
, the Bayes estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL,Bayes}}
defined as the posterior mean

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL,Bayes}}:=\int\boldsymbol{\beta}\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\exp\Bigl(-wL_{\mathrm{CPL}}\left(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)\Bigr)d\boldsymbol{\beta},

where
L_{\mathrm{CPL}}=\log\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CPL}}
, holds the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(Asymptotic unbiasedness of the Bayes estimator under composite partial likelihood)
.

Under the regularity conditions
(B1)
–
(B4)
,
(C1)
–
(C6)
, for the data-generating process defined in Appendix
A
and assuming a correctly specified linear predictor, the Bayes estimator satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL,Bayes}}\right]-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=O\bigl(n^{-1/2}\bigr).

See Appendix
C.4
for the proof.
Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
together justify using
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL,Bayes}}
as a consistent and asymptotically unbiased estimator of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
We then develop our Gibbs sampling algorithm based on the composite partial likelihood (
3
).

Remark 3.1
.

The composite partial likelihood (
3
) is defined based on the probability that an event occurs for an individual before any other subject in the risk set at that time.
Therefore, it can be directly applied even when multiple events occur at the same time.
Similar to the partial likelihood, censoring is handled indirectly through the use of indicator functions, allowing it to be appropriately accounted for.

3.3
Posterior representation of the Cox regression model via Pólya-Gamma augmentation

Applying the Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme to (
3
) yields the following conditional composite partial likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{D})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left(\kappa_{ij}\eta_{ij}-\frac{\omega_{ij}\eta_{ij}^{2}}{2}\right)^{\delta_{i}},

where
\eta_{ij}=\bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\bigr)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\kappa_{ij}=1/2\,\,(\because a=b=1)
,
\omega_{ij}\sim\operatorname{PG}\bigl(1,(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr)
.
Defining the corresponding loss function as

\begin{split}L_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\mathcal{D};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\omega})&:=-\log\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\exp\left(\frac{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}}{2}-\frac{\omega_{ij}\bigl\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr\}^{2}}{2}\right)^{\delta_{i}}\right],\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\left(\frac{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}}{2}-\frac{\omega_{ij}\bigl\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr\}^{2}}{2}\right),\end{split}

and then we obtain the complete conditional for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
as follows:

\begin{split}&\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{D})\\
&\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\exp\bigl(-wL_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\mathcal{D};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\omega})\bigr),\\
&=\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\exp\left\{w\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\delta_{i}\left(\frac{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}}{2}-\frac{\omega_{ij}\bigl\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr\}^{2}}{2}\right)\right\}.\end{split}

(5)

Assuming a Gaussian prior
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
, (
5
) can be expressed as

\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathcal{D})\propto\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\ast}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\frac{w}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\delta_{i}\bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\bigr)\right],\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\ast}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\ast}=\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}+w\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\delta_{i}\omega_{ij}\bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\bigr)\bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\bigr)^{\top}\right]^{-1}
.
Thus, the complete conditional distributions for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be expressed in a Gaussian closed form.

3.4
Finite correction

Traditionally, inference in the Cox regression model has relied on the maximum partial-likelihood estimator.
To compare it with our composite partial likelihood estimator (Section
3.2
), we present two propositions that quantify the discrepancy between the estimators and give a first-order finite-sample correction.

Proposition 3.4
.

Under the regularity conditions
(C1)
–
(C6)
, the following relationship holds between the maximum partial likelihood estimator and the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{PL}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

See Appendix
C.5
for the proof.
Proposition
3.4
shows that the variability of the difference between the maximum partial likelihood estimator and the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator decreases as the sample size grows.
However, in finite samples, this difference remains non-negligible.
Thus, we introduce the following proposition regarding the relationship between these two estimators.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose the regularity conditions
(C1)
–
(C6)
are satisfied.
Then, we have

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{PL}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}-H_{\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}})^{-1}S_{\mathrm{PL}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}})+O_{p}(n^{-1}).

See Appendix
C.6
for the proof.
Then, we denote

H_{\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL,Bayes}})^{-1}S_{\mathrm{PL}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL,Bayes}})

(6)

as the correction term linking the two estimators.
We use this term to adjust for finite-sample discrepancies.

4
Numerical experiments

We evaluated our GS4Cox on synthetic data against several alternative methods.
Our primary comparison metrics were the effective sample size (ESS) and the effective sampling rate (ESR), defined as the effective sample size per second of runtime
(
Polson et al., 2013
;
Kass et al., 1998
)
.
The ESS is defined as:

\operatorname{ESS}:=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{M-m^{\ast}}{1+2\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\rho_{p}(l)},

where
M
is the number of samples,
m^{\ast}
is the burn-in period, and
\rho_{p}(l)
is the
l
th autocorrelation of
\beta_{p}
.
The ESS quantifies the number of independent samples that an autocorrelated Markov chain is equivalent to, thereby reflecting the true amount of independent information available for inference.
In contrast, the ESR quantifies how quickly the MCMC can produce independent draws from the posterior distribution.
Higher values of ESS and ESR indicate that the obtained samples are nearly independent, leading to greater accuracy in statistical inference and more efficient use of computational resources.

Moreover, we compared the posterior mean with the maximum partial likelihood estimates.
We then computed the autocovariance function using Fast Fourier Transform, and obtained the autocorrelation coefficient by normalizing by lag
0
autocovariance for estimating
\operatorname{ESS}
.

Synthetic datasets were generated as follows.
First,
n
independent covariate vectors were drawn from a standard normal distribution:
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
.
Using these covariates, the linear predictor was computed as
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
Survival times were then drawn from an exponential distribution with a rate parameter that depends on the linear predictor (i.e.,
T_{i}^{\ast}\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}))
).
Censoring times were generated independently from another exponential distribution (i.e.,
C_{i}^{\ast}\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)
).
The observed time for each subject
i
was defined as
T_{i}=\min(T_{i}^{\ast},C_{i}^{\ast})
, and the event indicator
\delta_{i}
was set as

\delta_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\,\,\,T^{\ast}_{i}\leq C^{\ast}_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To simulate scenarios with tied events, we modified the above procedure by rounding.
After determining the minimum observed time
\tilde{T}_{i}=\min(T_{i}^{\ast},C^{\ast}_{i})
, the observed time was rounded to the nearest multiple of a given rounding parameter
r
:

T_{i}=\operatorname{round}\left(\frac{\tilde{T}_{i}}{r}\right)\times r,

and the event indicators were computed in the same manner as before.

4.1
Baselines

We compared our GS4Cox with the MH approach within the general Bayesian framework, using a multivariate normal random-walk Metropolis–Hastings algorithm with a Hessian-based proposal covariance.
Additionally, we compared it with our method without finite-sample corrections.

Computational cost.

Our implementation achieved the asymptotic complexities derived in Appendix
D.2
.
Specifically, the per-iteration cost of GS4Cox is
O(QP^{2})
, while that of MH-Hessian sampler is
O(nP^{2})

By contrast, naive implementations would incur
O(QP^{2}+QP)
and
O(n^{2}P^{2})
costs per iteration, respectively.
Thus, our Gibbs sampler eliminated the
O(QP)
term, and the Hessian-based MH sampler achieved an
n

6
Discussion

We developed a new Gibbs sampling algorithm for the Cox regression model: GS4Cox.
For constructing the algorithm, we first establish that the composite partial likelihood constitutes a valid estimating equation in the Cox regression model.
Furthermore, we derived its asymptotic deviation from the maximum partial likelihood estimator.
Exploiting the fact that this composite partial likelihood can be expressed in a form analogous to a binomial logistic regression likelihood, we embed it within the Pólya-Gamma augmentation scheme and the general Bayesian framework to derive closed-form Gaussian complete conditional distributions for regression parameters.
We empirically demonstrated that GS4Cox achieved stationarity more rapidly and exhibits substantially lower autocorrelation than MH approaches.

In a previous study,
Ren et al., 2024
developed their sampling algorithm for the Cox regression model by approximating the log-cumulative baseline hazard with a monotonic spline and introducing Gamma frailties to approximate the full likelihood via a negative-binomial surrogate.
To correct for the bias induced by negative-binomial approximation, they incorporate MH rejection steps, resulting in a sampling algorithm that is no longer a full Gibbs sampler and potentially less efficient.
In contrast, our estimator based on the composite partial likelihood is asymptotically unbiased, and we explicitly evaluate the first-order correction needed relative to the standard partial likelihood estimator.
Incorporating this into a complete Gibbs sampler removes asymptotic bias while preserving high computational efficiency.

We observe that our proposed composite partial likelihood serves as a lower bound to the partial likelihood, as discussed in
Titsias, 2016
.
Moreover, from Proposition
3.1
and
3.2
, the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator is both unbiased and asymptotically consistent, and Theorem
3.3
shows that the Bayes estimator is also asymptotically unbiased.
Therefore, as a point estimator, the maximum composite partial likelihood estimator possesses the desired properties.
In contrast, uncertainty quantification depends on the chosen learning rate.
In regular, unimodal settings such as the Cox regression model, ensuring identical point estimates is essential, while uncertainty is governed by the chosen learning rate.

When applying the general Bayesian update, the choice of learning rate is an important issue.
Although it does not affect the posterior mean, it directly influences the scale of the posterior distribution and uncertainty estimation.
Therefore, careful tuning of the learning rate is required.
Several strategies examined in
Wu and Martin, 2023
often require substantial computational resources or yield inconsistent results in comparative evaluations.
For instance, the GPC method
(
Syring and Martin, 2019
)
used here iteratively adjusts the learning rate until bootstrap-estimated coverage matches the nominal level, while
Grünwald and Van Ommen, 2017
conducts a grid search over
(0,1]
with repeated iterations to select the optimal rate.
Both approaches are computationally intensive and susceptible to local optima traps.
Alternative schemes based on information-matching or asymptotic distributions
(
Holmes and Walker, 2017
;
Lyddon et al., 2019
)
have also shown unstable performance in comparative studies.
Developing a more efficient learning rate selection method remains an important direction for future research.

.

Appendix A
Data-generating process under the Cox proportional hazards model

Let
\{(T_{i},\delta_{i},\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent and identically distributed samples from the true data-generating distribution.
For each subject
i
, define the counting process

N_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<T_{i},\\
1,&\text{$t\geq T_{i}$ and $\delta_{i}=1$},\\
0,&\text{$t\geq T_{i}$ and $\delta_{i}=0$},\end{cases}

and the corresponding risk process

Y_{i}(t)=\boldsymbol{1}\{T_{i}\geq t\}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t^{-}}
denote the filtration up to just before time
t
.
The conditional intensity of
N_{i}(t)
is defined as

\begin{split}\lambda_{i}(t)&:=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\Pr(N_{i}(t+\Delta t)-N_{i}(t)=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t^{-}}),\end{split}

(7)

and,
dN_{i}(t)
denotes its increment over an infinitesimal time interval
[t,t+dt)
.
In the Cox regression model (
1
), (
7
) can be expressed as

\lambda_{i}(t)=Y_{i}(t)h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}).

Then, let
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\cdot]
denote expectation with respect to the above-defined data-generating distribution.

Appendix B
Asymptotic relationship between estimating equation and Bayes estimators

We assume the following conditions.

(B1)

The log-likelihood
\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D}):=\log\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})
is three times continuously differentiable in an open neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
of true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
Moreover, there exists an integrable envelop
\mathcal{M}(x)
such that
|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{k}\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})|\leq\mathcal{M}(x)
for
k=1,2,3
and every
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in neighborhood, allowing differentiation under the expectation.

(B2)

Let
u(\boldsymbol{\beta})
be the estimating function defining the
M
-estimator through
S_{n}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Set
A(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}[\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}u(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})]
,
B(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\bigl[u(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})u(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})^{\top}\bigr]
.
Both matrices are finite and non-singular.

(B3)

The Fisher information
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\partial_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})]
exists and is continuous on open neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, with
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
positive definite.

(B4)

The prior density
\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is continuous and strictly positive on open neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.

We first recall the classical result for estimators solving unbiased estimating equations.

Proposition B.1
.

(Asymptotic normality of the
M
–estimator,
van der Vaart, 1998
)
Under the regularity conditions
(B1)
and
(B2)
, the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{M}
to
S_{n}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=0
satisfies

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{M}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,A(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})^{-1}B(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})A(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})).

This proposition establishes that, under mild smoothness and non degeneracy, the
M
–estimator attains a Gaussian limit with Godambe covariance
A^{-1}BA^{-\top}
.

We now turn to the Bayesian posterior, showing it too is asymptotically Gaussian and concentrated around the same estimator.

Proposition B.2
.

(Bernstein-von Mises Theorem, (e.g.,
van der Vaart, 1998
))
Under the regularity conditions
(B1)
,
(B3)
and
(B4)
, and assuming that the model is correctly specified, the posterior distributions satisfies, in total variation,

\pi(\sqrt{n}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{M})\mid\mathcal{D}_{1:n})\overset{TV}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})^{-1}),\quad n\to\infty,

and, in particular, the posterior mean
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{Bayes}}
obeys

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{Bayes}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{M}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

This result shows that, asymptotically, the posterior concentrates around the
M
-estimator and becomes Gaussian with covariance equal to the inverse Fisher information.
Consequently, the posterior mean and the
M
-estimator coincide to first order.

Combining the Propositions
B.1
and
B.2
yields the following immediate consequence.

Corollary B.3
.

Under the regularity conditions
(B1)
–
(B4)
, both the
M
-estimator and the Bayes estimator are asymptotically normal with the same center
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
and under a correctly specified likelihood, identical covariance
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})^{-1}
, and their difference is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.
Hence, in large samples, one may use either approach interchangeably for point estimation and credible/confidence interval construction.

The corollary confirms that in regular parametric models, Bayesian credible intervals and frequentist confidence intervals coincide asymptotically, and that the two point estimates differ by a negligible
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
term.

Appendix C
Omitted proof

C.1
Regularity conditions

Throughout the proofs, we define the empirical log-partial likelihood as:

\ell_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\log\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})}\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr),

and normalized version as
\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=n^{-1}\ell_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Similarly, define empirical log-composite partial likelihood as:

\begin{split}\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})&:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\delta_{i}\log p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\\
&=\sum_{i<j}\bigl[\delta_{i}\boldsymbol{1}\{j\in R(T_{i})\}\log p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\delta_{j}\boldsymbol{1}\{i\in R(T_{j})\}\log p_{ji}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr],\\
&=\sum_{i<j}k(\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{j};\boldsymbol{\beta}).\end{split}

and its normalized form
\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Also, we define the expectation of the composite partial likelihood as:

U(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\bigl[k(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2};\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr],

and set

\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\binom{n}{2}U(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\quad\bar{\ell}_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=U(\boldsymbol{\beta}).

We then impose the following regularity conditions:

(C1)

The parameter space
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
is compact, and the true value
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
lies in its interior.

(C2)

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\left[S_{\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\right]=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[S_{\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})]\neq\boldsymbol{0}
for
\boldsymbol{\beta}\neq\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.

(C3)

The normalized log-likelihoods
\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and
\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
admit three-order continuous derivatives in a neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.

(C4)

For
k=1,2,3
,
\sup_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\left\|\nabla^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right\|=O_{p}(1)
and the same holds for
\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

(C5)

The matrix
\boldsymbol{J}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right]
is positive definite, and
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\boldsymbol{J}\right\|\to 0
in probability.

(C6)

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\left[\left\|S_{\mathrm{PL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\right\|^{2}\right]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\left[\|\varphi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta_{0}})\|^{2}\right]<\infty
where
\varphi_{i}
is defined as:

\varphi_{i}:=\delta_{i}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\left\{1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right\}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\right).

Proof of Proposition
3.1

Proof.

Under the data-generating process defined in Appendix
A
, we can express (
4
) by the Riemann–Stieltjes integral representation
(
Hildebrandt, 1938
)
as follows:

\begin{split}S_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})\setminus\{i\}}\delta_{i}\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr),\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{t\in T}Y_{j}(t)\delta_{i}(t)\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr),\\
&\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{0}^{\tau}Y_{j}(t)\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)dN_{i}(t),\end{split}

The expectation of the composite partial score function can be expressed as

(
Daley and Vere-Jones, 2003
, Lem. 7.2.V)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left[S_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right]&\to\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}Y_{j}(t)\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)dN_{i}(t)\right],\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}Y_{j}(t)\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)\lambda_{i}(t)dt\right],\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}Y_{j}(t)\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)Y_{i}(t)h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})dt\right].\end{split}

Define the integrand

A_{ij}(t):=Y_{j}(t)\Bigl\{\Bigl(1-p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\Bigr)\Bigr\}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\Bigr)Y_{i}(t)h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}).

Since
A_{ji}(t)=Y_{j}(t)Y_{i}(t)(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}-\boldsymbol{X}_{i})(1-p_{ji}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))h_{0}(t)\exp(\boldsymbol{X}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})=-A_{ij}(t)
, summation over all pairs
i\neq j
collapses:

\sum_{i\neq j}A_{ij}(t)=\sum_{\{i,j\}}\Bigl\{A_{ij}(t)+A_{ji}(t)\Bigr\}=0.

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[S_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})]=0
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2

Proof.

Under the regularity conditions
(C1)
and
(C3)
, since for each
\boldsymbol{X}=\{\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
the function
\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is continuous on
\mathcal{B}
, a maximum composite partial likelihood estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}\in\mathcal{B}
exists for every
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
.

Setting
\bar{\ell}_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=U_{n}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
, and noting that
k
is manifestly a symmetric kernel, we can regard
U_{n}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
as a
U
-statistic
(
Hoeffding, 1948
)
.
Consequently,
Serfling, 1980
implies

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}|U_{n}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-U(\boldsymbol{\beta})|\overset{P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}}{\longrightarrow}0,\quad n\to\infty,

which can equivalently be written as

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\left(\left|U_{n}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-U(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right|>\epsilon\right)=0,\quad\forall\epsilon.

Next, observe that for any
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,

\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\bigl[\log p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})-\log p_{ji}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr]=\sum_{i<j}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\|p_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr),

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
Because each Kullback-Leibler divergence equals to zero if and only if
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, it follows that

\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})>\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\quad\text{for every $\boldsymbol{\beta}\neq\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}$}.

Therefore,

\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})>\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}:|\beta-\beta_{0}|>\epsilon}\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}),

and so there exists
\delta>0
such that

\sup_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}:|\beta-\beta_{0}|>\epsilon}\bigl[\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\bigr]<-\delta.

Next, we define the set

\mathcal{C}_{n}(\delta):=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}:\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}\left|\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right|\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\right\}.

On
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\delta)
, for any
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}
with
|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|>\epsilon
, we have

\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq\frac{\delta}{2},\quad\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})<-\delta.

Adding these yields

|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|>\epsilon\Longrightarrow\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})<-\frac{\delta}{2}.

Taking contrapositives gives

\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\geq-\frac{\delta}{2}\Rightarrow|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|\leq\epsilon.

By definition,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}=\arg\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\arg\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}U_{n}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
, so equivalently,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}=\arg\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}\bigl[U_{n}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})-U(\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr].

On
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\delta)
, it follows that

\ell_{n,\mathrm{CPL}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\ell_{\mathrm{CPL}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\geq-\frac{\delta}{2},

hence
|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|\leq\epsilon
.
Thus, for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(\delta)\Rightarrow|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq\epsilon
.
Equivalently,

\mathcal{C}_{n}(\delta)\subset\bigl\{\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}:|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|\leq\epsilon\bigr\}\subset\mathcal{X}^{n}.

Taking
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}
-probabilities and using uniform convergence (
P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{n}(\delta)\bigr)\to 1
) yields

P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}\bigl(|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}|\leq\epsilon\bigr)\to 1,\quad n\to\infty,

thereby establishing
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}\overset{P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
∎

Throughout the proof, the learning rate

Next, set

By a second-order Taylor expansion of

around
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}

m_{n}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=m_{n}\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}})^{\top}\mathcal{J}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}})+o_{p}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{CPL}}\|^{2}\bigr),

Appendix D
Detailed experiments settings and results

D.1
Generalized posterior calibration algorithm

The GPC algorithm proceeds as follows.
Let
C_{\alpha}^{(w)}
denote the
w
-generated Bayes
100(1-\alpha)\%
credible region for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and let
P^{\ast}
be the true data-generating distribution.
The coverage probability function is defined as

c_{\alpha}(w\mid P^{\ast})=P^{\ast}\left\{C_{\alpha}^{(w)}(\mathcal{D})\ni\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\dagger}\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\dagger}
is the Kullback-Leibler minimizer in the model.
The goal of GPC is to choose
w
such that
c_{\alpha}(w\mid P^{\ast})=1-\alpha
.
Since
P^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\dagger}
are unknown, one substitutes the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}_{n}
and the maximum partial likelihood estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{PL}}
, yielding the surrogate target

c_{\alpha}(w\mid\mathbb{P}_{n}):=\mathbb{P}_{n}\left\{C_{\alpha}^{(w)}(\mathcal{D})\ni\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{PL}}\right\}=1-\alpha.

This is approximated via
B
bootstrap samples
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n},\dots,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{B}^{n}
:

\hat{c}_{\alpha}(w\mid\mathbb{P}_{n})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{C_{\alpha}^{(w)}\left(\tilde{D}_{b}^{n}\right)\ni\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{PL}}\right\}.

Finally,
w
is updated iteratively until the convergence criterion is met:

w_{k^{\prime}+1}=w_{k^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{m}\left\{\hat{c}_{\alpha}(w\mid\mathbb{P}_{n})-(1-\alpha)\right\},\quad k^{\prime}\geq 1.

D.2
Computing complexity

Let
e(t)
be the number of events at time
t
,
E
be the number of unique event times and
Q=\sum_{t}R(t)e(t)
be the number of all pairs.
In the pathological case in which every subject experiences an event at a distinct time we have
E=n
and
Q=n(n-1)/2=O(n^{2})
.

D.2.1
GS4Cox

Before sampling begins, we sort the event times and build each risk set via binary search in
O(n\log n)
.
We then enumerate all
Q
subject pair contrasts once, assembling the design matrix
\boldsymbol{D}=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\bigr\}_{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times P}
at a cost of
O(QP)
.

During each Gibbs iteration, computing the linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
costs
O(QP)
and sampling
\omega_{q}\sim\operatorname{PG}(1,\eta_{q})
, for
q=1,\dots,Q
costs
O(Q)
.
Constructing the weighted scatter matrix
\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{D}\odot\boldsymbol{\omega})
takes
O(QP^{2})
.
Because
P\ll Q
, the dominant per-iteration cost is
O(QP^{2})
.

If the naive alternative, the pairs were regenerated inside the loop the cost would be
O(QP)
higher each iteration, giving
O(QP^{2}+QP)
.
The pre-computation therefore removes the
QP
term.

D.2.2
MH with Hessian proposal

The routine uses an analytic score and Hessian to construct a Laplace proposal.
Computing the score and Hessian requires
O(n\log n+nP^{2})
, inverting the Hessian takes
O(P^{3})
, and evaluating the log-partial likelihood costs
2O(n\log n+nP)
.
Since
P\ll n
, the overall complexity is dominated by
O(nP^{2})
.
In a naive implementation, where one loops over each event time and rebuilds its risk set from scratch, it takes
O(n^{2}P^{2})
.
By contrast, the analytic cumulative sum trick cuts this by a factor of
n
, reducing the asymptotic complexity from
O(n^{2}P^{2})
down to
O(nP^{2})
.

D.2.3
Summary

In summary, the computational orders implemented in our experiments are as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l|c|c}\hline\cr\text{Algorithm}&\text{Optimised}&\text{Naive}\\
\hline\cr\text{GS4Cox}&O\bigl(QP^{2}\bigr)&O\bigl(QP^{2}+QP\bigr)\\
\text{MH-Hessian}&O\bigl(n\,P^{2}\bigr)&O\bigl(n^{2}P^{2}\bigr)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Additional actual data experiment results

First, Figure
7
compares the posterior distribution obtained using MH sampling with the posterior distribution obtained using our GS4Cox algorithm.
This shows that finite-sample corrections caused the samples to cluster around the maximum partial likelihood estimates.
Additionally, the variance was significantly smaller compared to MH sampling algorithm.

Next, Figure
8
compares the point estimates and 95% confidence intervals obtained from the Cox regression model with the posterior means and 95% credible intervals obtained from each sampling algorithm.
Although the width of these intervals may vary depending on the learning rate, the results confirm that the interval widths from our method were considerably narrower.
