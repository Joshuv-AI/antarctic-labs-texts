Evaluation of influenza vaccine effectiveness among older adults in Jiaxing, China, 2022–2025: A test negative design-based study
Authors: Yang Liu, Xiaofei Fu, Yuanhang Wang, Lei Jia, LiJun Xu, Rui Li, Guoying Zhu, Haoyue Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza imposes a substantial burden on older adults, underscoring the need for annual evaluation of vaccine effectiveness (VE). Using a test-negative case-control design, this study assessed VE among patients aged ≥60 y with acute respiratory symptoms in Jiaxing, China, across three influenza seasons (2022–2025). Among 3,214 participants, vaccination provided moderate overall protection against laboratory-confirmed influenza, with adjusted VE of 42.5% (95% CI: 31.2–51.9). Protection was highest in the 2024–2025 season (47.2%) and against influenza A(H1N1) (42.4%). Notably, subgroup analyses revealed significantly higher VE among females, individuals without underlying disease (49.2%), those presenting with high fever (>39°C, 44.3%), and within 90 d post-vaccination (52.3%). These findings affirm the moderate protective effect of influenza vaccines in the older individuals and strongly support the critical importance of timely annual vaccination ahead of the influenza season to mitigate risk in this vulnerable population.

Background

Annual reports indicate that approximately 3 million to 5 million severe influenza cases occur globally each year, resulting in about 700,000 deaths. Among these death cases, 80% to 95% are older individuals aged over 65 y old.1,2 During the influenza season, the risk of hospitalization, medical visits and emergency rooms due to influenza and pneumonia in this age group increases significantly.3,4 The influenza vaccine is widely recognized as the most effective means of preventing influenza virus infection and laboratory-confirmed diseases. Vaccine effectiveness (VE) estimates derived from test-negative design (TND) studies specifically measure the reduction in the risk of medically attended, laboratory-confirmed influenza among individuals presenting with influenza-like illness (ILI), rather than the absolute reduction of illness in the general population.5 Given the methodological advantages of TND in controlling for healthcare-seeking behavior bias and confounding by indication, TND has been increasingly adopted by national surveillance programs worldwide, including China, for real-world influenza VE estimation. Therefore, many countries around the world have implemented annual influenza vaccination programs to alleviate the public health burden of seasonal influenza.

China recommends that all people except infants under 6 months of age receive the influenza vaccine, but the actual coverage rate is as low as 2%,6 far below the recommended target of the World Health Organization.7 Jiaxing City is subordinate to Zhejiang Province and located in the eastern coast of China. There will be an obvious influenza epidemic season from late autumn to the next spring every year. In Jiaxing, the government provides free trivalent inactivated influenza vaccine (TIV) to the older individuals. However, VE is affected by multiple factors, including the match between vaccine strains and epidemic strains, seasonal differences, geographical location, age, and influenza subtype, etc.8–10 Despite fluctuations in VE, vaccination remains the core concept of global Influenza prevention at present. A small increase in it can significantly reduce the risk of hospitalization and functional decline in severe seasons.11 Therefore, it is very important to make a continuous annual estimation of VE. Generally, in influenza research, influenza VE assessment often adopts TND.12,13 This study aims to evaluate the real effectiveness of vaccines in three influenza seasons from 2022 to 2025, focusing on the impact of age differences, seasonal changes, vaccination time and vaccination integrity (such as annual vaccination and complete vaccination) on VE. Through a case-control study, the mechanism of the above factors on VE was analyzed, with special emphasis on the importance of vaccination for high-risk groups such as the older individuals. The results will provide a scientific basis for optimizing vaccination strategies, improving coverage, and improving public health policies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a TND to evaluate the effectiveness of seasonal influenza vaccine among older adults in Jiaxing, China, from three influenza seasons: 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025. The study was conducted across 14 sentinel hospitals participating in the local influenza surveillance network. According to the seasonal changes of influenza in Jiaxing, the influenza season is defined from October to April of the following year. In accordance with the requirements of international reporting standards, we have listed the STROBE Checklist as per STROBE (Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology; https://www.strobe-statement.org/) guidelines to facilitate a clearer research design. Please refer to Table S1 for details.

Participant identification and eligibility criteria

Participants were identified through the outpatient and emergency departments of the 14 sentinel hospitals and restricted to the defined influenza epidemic period (October to April) of each respective season. Participants were required to be permanent residents of Jiaxing City aged ≥60 y, as this group is eligible for the municipal government-subsidized free seasonal influenza vaccination program. Individuals were screened if they presented with ILI. ILI refer to one of acute onset (onset ≤7 d), fever (body temperature ≥38.0°C), and accompanied by cough or pharyngeal pain.12 If it is expected or found that it is difficult to include sufficient cases of fever with body temperature ≥38.0°C during the implementation of monitoring, the person who feels or checks to have fever (≥37.3°C), acute onset (onset ≤7 d), and accompanied by cough or pharyngeal pain can be taken as the monitoring object, and the maximum fever temperature can be recorded.

Specimen collection and laboratory testing

Nasopharyngeal swabs were collected by professionals within 7 d after the onset of the disease. The case specimens collected every day shall be registered and numbered uniformly. The specimens were immersed in 3 ml virus preservation solution in the sampling tube and labeled with relevant information. Specimens were stored at 4°C and transported to the corresponding influenza monitoring network laboratory within 48 hours, where they were tested for influenza virus using the real-time fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) method. Positive samples were further subtyped for influenza A (H1N1, H3N2) and influenza B (Victoria and Yamagata). All laboratory-confirmed influenza cases, including both influenza A and influenza B, were included in the test-positive group for the primary VE analysis. The nucleic acid is extracted using the magnetic bead method, and the universal and subtype (H1N1, H3N2, Victoria and Yamagata strain) test kits approved by the National Drug Administration are used. A 25 μL reaction system is configured, and the amplification program is 50°C reverse transcription for 30 minutes, 95°C pre-denaturation for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C denaturation for 15 seconds and 55°C annealing extension for 45 seconds. The result determination is based on the amplification curve showing a typical S-shaped pattern and a Ct value ≤38 being positive. For each batch of tests, positive control, negative control and blank control are set, and if the control is abnormal, the entire batch should be re-tested. If the same patient undergoes two or more influenza virus tests within 7 d, only the result of the first test will be retained.

Vaccination status ascertainment

Vaccination status was ascertained through linkage with the Zhejiang Provincial Influenza Vaccination Registry System using each participant’s unique national identification (ID) number (deterministic linkage). Participants without a vaccination record in the registry were classified as unvaccinated. This approach minimized misclassification bias and ensured high-quality vaccination status data for the analysis. Patients were identified as vaccinated if they had received a influenza vaccine at least 14 days prior to symptom onset in the respective season. Participants aged 60 years and older received a standard-dose TIV through the local free influenza vaccination program for older adults, and the antigen content was 15 μg for each vaccine strain.

Covariate and confounder definition

Covariates and potential confounders were collected through a standardized on-site questionnaire survey administered by uniformly trained medical staff. To ensure data quality, each staff member completed a maximum of 10 cases per day. The questionnaire was based on the national ILI information collection form and captured the following domains: (1) Demographic information: age (categorized as 60–69, 70–79, 80–89, and ≥90 years), sex, and eligibility for the local free influenza vaccination policy; (2) Clinical information: date of symptom onset, use of antiviral or other medications prior to testing, presence and severity of clinical symptoms (including fever, cough, sore throat, and highest body temperature), and history of underlying diseases; (3) Specimen information: date and time of nasopharyngeal swab collection and specimen type. Underlying diseases were defined as the presence of any of the following self-reported or medically documented conditions: cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus, renal dysfunction, neurological diseases, hematological diseases or malignant neoplasms (tumors). These variables were included as confounders in the multivariable logistic regression models to adjust for potential imbalances between vaccinated and unvaccinated groups. Detailed ICD-10 codes for ILI and underlying diseases are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Where Uα = 1.96 (corresponding to a two-sided test level α = 0.05) and Uβ = 0.84 (corresponding to 80% statistical power, β = 0.20). Based on the estimation that the protection rate of influenza vaccine Relative Risk (RR) = 0.5,15 an influenza vaccine coverage rate of p0 = 0.3, and the above parameters, the calculation indicated that at least 202 influenza virus-positive cases were required. Assuming that approximately 20% of ILI cases would test positive for influenza virus and allowing for 5% invalid questionnaires or samples, a minimum of 1,000 ILI cases needed to be enrolled per influenza season.

Statistical analysis

VE can be calculated using the following formula:VE=1−OR×100%

Odds ratio (OR) represents the ratio of the probability of vaccination between the case group and the control group. Considering the basic population characteristics and factors affecting the transmission and detection of influenza virus, the protective effect of influenza vaccine may be confused.16 The logistic regression model was adjusted for age (continuous/categorical), sex, presence of underlying chronic diseases (cardiovascular, respiratory, diabetes, etc.), and time of illness onset. We calculated VE and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) according to the or value using the formula. When the 95% confidence interval of VE does not overlap with zero, it indicates the presence of a protective effect.

VE was estimated for different influenza virus subtypes, influenza seasons (2022–2023, 2023–2023, and 2024–2025), and different age groups (people aged 60–69, 70–79, 80–89, and ≥90 y). VE was assessed according to the interval between vaccination and sample collection (0.5 to 3 months, 3 to 6 months, and older). All questionnaire items were mandatory fields in the sentinel surveillance system, resulting in complete data with no missing values for any covariate. Therefore, complete-case analysis was performed for all multivariable logistic regression models. We used Chi square test to evaluate the differences in demographic, clinical diagnosis and vaccination characteristics between positive cases and negative controls. Multivariate logistic regression model was employed to analyze the estimation of the protective effect of the influenza vaccine. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Statistical tests performed in two tailed were considered significant for P values < .05. R software version 4.3.3 was used for all analyses.

Results

Basic characteristics of the population

A total of 3,214 older adults with ILI were included in the analysis, comprising 802 (25.0%) test-positive cases and 2412 (75.0%) test-negative controls. According to the STROBE, the participant flow diagram is shown in Figure 1. The average age of the study population was 76.53 ± 7.48 y old. The distribution of participants across the three influenza seasons differed significantly between groups (χ2 = 99.5, P < .001), with a higher proportion of test-positive cases enrolled in the 2024–2025 season (49.0%) compared with the 2022–2023 (21.3%) and 2023–2024 (29.7%) seasons. Age distribution also differed markedly (χ2 = 89.1, P < .001). Test-positive cases had a higher proportion of individuals aged 60–69 y (24.8%) and a lower proportion aged 80–89 y (20.3%) than test-negative controls. The oldest subgroup (≥90 y) accounted for only 4.0% of the total sample (n = 129). No significant differences were found between the test-positive and test-negative groups regarding gender. Participants with underlying diseases were more prevalent in the test-positive group than in the test-negative group (36.5% vs. 31.2%, χ2 = 7.7, P = .005). Conversely, a higher proportion of test-negative controls had no underlying disease (68.8% vs. 63.5%) (Table 1). Among patients vaccinated against influenza during the current season, the proportion in the test-positive group was lower than that in the test-negative group (27.8% vs. 39.6%, p < .001) During two consecutive influenza seasons when receiving the influenza vaccine, the proportion in the test-positive group was lower than that in the test-negative group (14.5% vs. 24.7%, P < .001). The overall protective effect of influenza vaccines

The adjusted VE for subgroup variables (such as influenza season, age group, sex, presence of underlying diseases, and presence of high fever) were mutually adjusted for each other, while the adjusted VE for variables including current-season vaccination, vaccination for two consecutive seasons, influenza virus type, and time interval between vaccination and illness onset were further adjusted for influenza season, age group, sex, presence of underlying diseases, and presence of high fever. The results demonstrate that the adjusted VE (Figure 2), by controlling for the influence of other confounding factors, provides a more precise estimate compared to the unadjusted VE (Figure 3). Overall, the adjusted VE in the current year of influenza vaccination was 42.5% (95%CI: 31.2–51.9%), while the adjusted VE after two consecutive years of influenza vaccination was 45.0% (95%CI: 31.4–55.9%) (Figure 2).

The adjusted VE was highest during 2024–2025 season at 47.2% (95%CI: 30.5–59.9%), while the adjusted VE for 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 seasons were 28.8% (95%CI:–0.0–51.1%) and 40.6% (95%CI: 18.4–56.9%) (Figure 2).

We observed differential adjusted VE against influenza strains; adjusted VE was higher against H1N1 (42.4%; 95% CI: 27.9–54.0%) than against H3N2 (39.3%; 95% CI: 18.0–55.1%) (Figure 2). Due to the limited number of influenza B cases (n = 15, 1.9%), no statistically significant vaccine protection effect was observed for influenza B.

Whereas the adjusted VE was 36.9% (95% CI: 3.0–59.0) in the 60–69 y groups, 40.5% (95% CI: 24.5–53.1) in the 70–79 y groups, 44.2% (95% CI:17.9–62.0) in the 80–89 y groups. In the ≥90 y subgroup (n = 129), the adjusted VE was 73.2% (95% CI: 5.4–92.4). However, due to the small sample size and the extremely wide confidence interval, these results should be interpreted with caution (Figure 2).

The adjusted VE was 34.8% (95%CI: 16.6–49.0%) for male older adults and 49.8% (95%CI: 34.7–61.4%) for female older adults.

The adjusted VE was higher among older individuals without underlying medical conditions (49.2%; 95% CI: 31.6–62.4%) than among those with such conditions (39.1%; 95% CI: 23.8–51.3%) (Figure 2).

Similarly, the adjusted VE was greater among older individuals with high fever 44.3% (95%CI: 27.4–57.2%) compared to those without 41.1% (95%CI: 24.5–54.0%). A declining trend in adjusted VE was observed with increasing time since vaccination. The adjusted VE was 52.3% (95% CI: 36.1–64.3) within 90 d post-vaccination, 37.8% (95% CI: 22.7–50.0) for the interval of 90–180 d, and 6.0% (95% CI: −57.3–43.8; the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval crossed zero) beyond 180 d (Figure 2).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis, for the period when the recruitment of cases is low in the epidemic season, the ≥37.3°C is taken as a supplementary entry criterion to ensure that the part with sufficient statistical power is analyzed. Participants enrolled at this threshold accounted for only a small part of the total sample (n = 284, accounting for 8.8% of the total enrollment in the season). The results of sensitivity analysis showed that the overall adjusted VE was 37.3% (95% CI: 24.6–47.9%) (Table S3) was robust compared with 42.5% (95% CI: 31.2–51.9%) in the main analysis, supporting the validity of the main findings.

Discussion

This study indicates that influenza vaccines have a certain protective effect in all three epidemic seasons, with total VE of 28.8% during 2022–2023 season, 40.6% during 2023–2024 season, and 47.2% during 2024–2025 season for older individuals against influenza. It should be emphasized that the VE in the group without underlying diseases is higher, which does not necessarily mean that the vaccine is ineffective for people with underlying diseases. The more likely reason is that the immune response of this group is weak, or it is easier to be detected even after vaccination, thus lowering the VE value of this group. Vaccines may be more important for them to prevent severe diseases than to prevent infections, but this requires subsequent analysis of severe outcomes. This research has shown that the protection effect of influenza vaccine is better in older individuals over 60 y old, but except for those over 90 y old, this may be related to aging and immunity in the older individuals.17–19 Our findings regarding age-stratified vaccine effectiveness warrant comparison with prior literature. Lang et al.17 reported that influenza vaccines are generally effective in aging and older adults, yet effectiveness tends to decline with advancing age, which they primarily attributed to immunosenescence. In the present study, unadjusted VE estimates similarly showed a declining trend with increasing age, consistent with the immunosenescence hypothesis. However, after multivariable adjustment for season, calendar time, underlying diseases, healthcare-seeking behavior proxies, and other covariates, the adjusted VE exhibited an increasing trend across age groups (except for the small ≥90 y subgroup with wide confidence intervals). Although the point estimate of vaccine effectiveness appeared high in the ≥90 y subgroup, the small sample size (n = 129) and extremely wide 95% CI (5.4–92.4) preclude firm conclusions; this observation may partly reflect lower vaccination coverage, differences in baseline comorbidities, and selection bias toward more robust individuals within this age group, rather than a true reversal of immunosenescence. Furthermore, this study differs from that of Lang et al. in terms of the study population, vaccine types, and the inclusion of confounding factors. These differences may explain the discrepancy in the results. Future studies incorporating direct immunological markers would help further elucidate these observations. In comparison with published test-negative design studies among Chinese adults under 60 y, the adjusted VE in our older adult cohort was moderately lower.5 This age-related difference aligns with immunosenescence. Notably, while many regions in China do not yet offer free influenza vaccination to adults aged ≥60 y, our findings – derived from a local free vaccination livelihood project – demonstrate meaningful protection. These results support the expansion of free influenza vaccination programs or consideration of enhanced strategies for older adults nationwide, such as higher-antigen-dose or adjuvanted vaccines, potentially combined with optimized timing or enhanced vaccine formulations, to better protect this high-risk population.

Older individuals aged 60 and above are one of the key populations recommended for priority vaccination of influenza vaccine in the “Technical Guidelines for Influenza Vaccination in China (2023–2024)”.20 Previous studies have shown that receiving the influenza vaccine can significantly reduce the risk of influenza related hospitalization, pneumonia, and death in older individuals aged 60 and above.21,22 This also highlights the importance of administering influenza vaccines to the older individuals. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) has also listed this group as a top priority for seasonal influenza vaccination efforts.23 Therefore, we suggest strengthening the management of influenza vaccination for older individuals over 60 y old without contraindications. To improve the vaccine management effectiveness for high-risk populations, it is urgent to carry out public education activities to enhance people’s awareness of the importance of influenza vaccines. In addition, it is necessary to strengthen the training of medical staff to improve their enthusiasm and professionalism in recommending vaccines, and emphasize the necessity of vaccination in medical records to encourage patients to actively receive vaccinations during routine visits.

The observation of higher adjusted VE among participants presenting with high fever (body temperature ≥39°C) may suggest a more robust overall immune responsiveness in this subgroup. However, this remains a hypothetical explanation based solely on the clinical symptom of hyperpyrexia, without supporting data from direct immunological markers such as serum antibody titers or cellular immune responses. Therefore, the finding should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating serological or cellular immunity assessments are warranted to verify potential immunological correlates of VE.

In this study, the trivalent inactivated vaccine showed protective effects against H1N1 and H3N2 in older individuals, which is consistent with the findings of research by Domnich et al.24 and Baum et al.25 However, the protective effect of H1N1 was higher than that of H3N2, mainly due to its matching with the main virus types prevalent each year. Sentinel laboratory data from Jiaxing City indicated that influenza A viruses predominated in the 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025 influenza seasons. In this study, the protective effect of the overall vaccine in the 2022–2023 influenza season was relatively low, which was consistent with the results of studies in Europe, Italy, and the United States.26–29 In the 2023–2024 influenza season, the protective effect of influenza vaccines for the older individuals in Italy24 and the United Kingdom30 was higher than the results of this study, while the results of the United States study were lower.31 This study found that the adjusted VE among older adults (≥60 y) during the 2024–2025 season was generally higher than the contemporaneous VE estimates reported for this age group in Germany, France, and the United States.32–34 The higher vaccine effectiveness observed in the 2024–2025 season, despite a greater proportion of test-positive cases from this season, is more likely attributable to the predominance of A/H1N1 strains with superior antigenic match to the vaccine components, rather than to seasonal differences in participant enrollment. Therefore, how to deal with the high mutation rate of the virus and select the most appropriate vaccine strain is still the current challenge. In order to improve the efficacy of influenza vaccines, strategies aimed at enhancing their broad-spectrum protective effects will be crucial.

It is noted that the vaccination effectiveness was significantly higher among those who have been vaccinated with both the previous and current season’s vaccines, reaching up to 45.03%. Given the annual variation in circulating virus strains and the corresponding vaccine formulations, along with the resulting variation in serological protection, annual influenza vaccination is recommended. This study found that the adjusted VE will gradually decline over time in the current season, 52.3% w within 90 d after vaccination and 6.0% after 180 d. However, for these subgroups (including the ≥90 y age group, the >180 d post-vaccination period, and certain comorbidity strata), the wide confidence intervals reflect constrained sample sizes and should be interpreted with caution. These findings are best viewed as exploratory or hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of vaccine performance in these specific contexts. A European study on the protective effect of influenza vaccine in the 2021–2022 season showed that the vaccine efficacy against influenza A virus (H3N2 subtype) in all age groups decreased from 54% within 90 d after vaccination to 16% after 150 d.26 According to the guiding principles in China’s “Technical guidelines for seasonal influenza vaccination in China (2023–2024),” we recommend timely vaccination before local influenza activities to ensure early immune protection.20 Given the epidemiology of influenza in Jiaxing, where the epidemic season typically spans from late autumn to the following spring, vaccination should be completed between September and early October. This timing would ensure that the majority of older adults achieve optimal protection during the peak circulation period, while minimizing the proportion of individuals whose immunity has already declined by mid-to-late season. Such a targeted vaccination window aligns with current national recommendations but emphasizes the need for timely administration in regions with similar seasonal patterns. These results support strengthening public health campaigns to improve vaccination uptake in early autumn among adults aged ≥60 y.

In general, it is recommended that more places include free influenza vaccination for the older individuals over 60 y of age into practical projects for people’s livelihood, ensure its wide accessibility through policy support, and focus on high-risk groups such as the older individuals and patients with chronic diseases, so as to reduce influenza complications and mortality. Secondly, we should strengthen health education in communities and medical institutions, eliminate hesitation about vaccination, improve public awareness of influenza vaccine, and break down misunderstandings and biases. In addition, temporary vaccination sites in the community should be increased and the service time should be extended before the influenza season, so as to effectively eliminate the vaccination barriers, ensure the timely vaccination of high-risk groups, and improve the effect of vaccine protection.

Limitation

A key strength of this study is its test-negative design and large sample of older adults across multiple seasons, allowing robust subgroup and strain-specific VE estimates. However, several limitations should be noted. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as functional status, health-seeking behavior, or prior infection history may persist. Information on prior influenza infections was not available, which could have led to residual confounding if prior exposure differentially affected vaccination status or current test results. This also indicate a possible direction for improved real-world VE surveillance in China. Additionally, the number of participants aged >90 y was limited, which may explain the confidence interval included zero despite a high point estimate of VE. Finally, data on severe outcomes were not analyzed; thus, the vaccine’s effect against hospitalization or mortality remains to be evaluated.

Conclusion

Influenza vaccination provides moderate and significant protection for adults aged ≥60 y, with variation by season, subtype, time since vaccination, and recipient characteristics. To maximize public health impact, annual vaccination – ideally before influenza circulation begins – should be reinforced through systematic programmatic support, targeted education, and equitable access, especially in high-risk and older populations.
