Monoclonal antibody development against the Hepatitis C virus core protein enables rapid antigen detection
Authors: Prince Baffour Tonto, Bobby Brooke Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains a major global health burden, with limited access to affordable diagnostics impeding progress toward the World Health Organization target elimination by 2030. While HCV ribonucleic acid (RNA) testing is the gold standard, it requires costly infrastructure and technical expertise. HCV core antigen (HCVcAg) represents a reliable alternative viral analyte, but existing immunoassays remain laboratory-dependent and expensive. Here, we report the development of HCVcAg-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) optimized for rapid, instrument-free antigen detection. BALB/c mice were immunized with HCVcAg, and antigen-specific plasma cells were isolated via single cell sorting on the Beacon optofluidic platform. Paired variable heavy/light regions were recovered by RT-nested PCR, cloned into human IgG1/kappa expression vectors, transiently expressed in HEK293 cells, and the mAbs were purified. Comprehensive molecular profiling identified mAbs with high avidity, diverse germline usage, and distinct epitope recognition. Strategic pairing yielded an optimal combination (M0004–M0007) capable of detecting contrived antigen concentrations as low as 0.3125 ng/ml. In a lateral flow format, this pair achieved superior diagnostic performance: 92.0% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 95.6% accuracy, 100% positive predictive value (PPV), 91.3% negative predictive value (NPV), Cohen’s κ = 0.91, area under the curve (AUC) = 0.859, and non-significant McNemar χ². In low viremia samples, performance remained robust with 85.7% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 94.3% accuracy, 100% PPV, 91.3% NPV, κ = 0.88, 0.759 AUC, and non-significant McNemar χ². These findings establish a foundation for a clinically deployable HCVcAg rapid test with strong potential for point-of-care screening, decentralizing diagnosis, and treatment-response monitoring, particularly in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54470-8.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a blood-borne hepatotropic pathogen belonging to the order Flaviviridae1. Transmission of HCV can occur through various routes, including vertical and sexual transmission, but the most common mode is through injection drug use, needle stick injury, and contaminated blood transfusion and blood products2,3. Globally, HCV remains a major public health challenge with an estimated 1 million new infections annually and approximately 50 million individuals living with chronic HCV infection3. Countries with the highest burden of HCV include Russia, India, Pakistan, China, and the USA4. Chronic infection can lead to severe complications such as liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, resulting in approximately 242,000 deaths each year3. Chronic HCV infections can be treated with direct acting antivirals (DAAs), which typically involve an 8–12-week treatment regimen and are effective in curing more than 95% of infected individuals5–7.

To screen for HCV infection, a two-step diagnostic algorithm is used. This involves the examination of anti-HCV antibodies indicating past or active infection, followed by nucleic acid testing (NAT) for HCV ribonucleic acid (RNA) to confirm active infection and guide appropriate treatment8,9. Due to the prolonged anti-HCV seroconversion window period, typically averaging 8 to 11 weeks after exposure, patients with acute HCV are often not identified during the early stages10–12. In contrast, HCV RNA levels can be detected as early as 1 to 2 weeks after exposure, making it a more reliable marker in identifying patients with acute HCV10.

While HCV RNA testing remains the gold standard for detecting active infections, it is resource-intensive, requiring complex laboratory infrastructure, skilled personnel, and high costs, making routine and large-scale testing challenging, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICS)9. HCV core antigen (HCVcAg), typically detectable 2–3 weeks post-exposure, closely parallels HCV RNA levels, making it a promising alternative viral analyte for detecting active HCV infection11,13. HCVcAg levels correlate strongly with HCV RNA, and its diagnostic utility has been demonstrated by several studies14–16. Moreover, HCVcAg testing is less expensive and more efficient compared to NAT13.

Despite the availability of immunoassays for HCVcAg testing, these tests also require laboratory capacity, technical expertise and are relatively costly, limiting their scalability14–17. These limitations hinder progress toward the World Health Organization’s goal of eliminating HCV by 2030, which targets diagnosing 90% of infected individuals and treating 80% of them11,18. To address this gap, there is an urgent need for a cost-effective, high-performance rapid antigen test that can be used at the point of care, in decentralized settings and for monitoring treatment response, including sustained virological response (SVR) following DAA therapy. Thus, we generated and comprehensively characterized murine mAbs against HCVcAg, enabling the identification of an optimal antibody pair for the development of a high-performance rapid antigen with potential utility in point-of-care settings to detect active infection.

Results

Immunogenicity of HCVcAg in Balb/c mice

To generate HCVcAg-specific monoclonal antibodies, we first immunized BALB/c mice three times with equal amounts of HCVcAg, 14 days apart (Fig. 1A). By day 21 post-initial immunization, mice exhibited relatively low IgG responses against the HCVcAg (Fig. 1B). However, at day 35, the mice mounted a stronger IgG response against the HCVcAg, with mouse E exhibiting the strongest IgG response against HCVcAg (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the spleen from mouse E was selected for downstream HCVcAg-specific plasma cell isolation.

HCVcAg-specific plasma cells

To identify plasma cells specific to HCVcAg, we employed the Beacon™ single cell optofluidic platform (Fig. 2A), which enables automated screening, isolation and sequencing of antigen-specific B cells19. Initially, splenic plasma cells were enriched via positive selection from the harvested splenocytes. A total of 12,149 plasma cells were then loaded into the Beacon system for processing. Of the loaded cells, 1.6% exhibited specific binding to the HCVcAg. About 95% of these antigen-reactive plasma cells were successfully isolated and selected for sequencing (Fig. 2B).

Heavy and light chain variable domain sequencing, gene cloning, and transfection

Next, the variable domains of the immunoglobulin heavy and light chains of the isolated HCVcAg-specific plasma cells were amplified and sequenced (Fig. 3A and B). 59% of the isolated cells had paired heavy and light chain variable region sequences (Fig. 3A–B). Amplified heavy and light chain variable regions were cloned into mammalian expression vectors and transformed into E. coli for plasmid amplification. Purified plasmids were subsequently transfected into HEK293 cells for transient expression of recombinant mAbs.

Monoclonal antibodies-HCVcAg binding interactions

Next, we evaluated the isotypes of the generated mAbs, and their binding interactions with the HCVcAg. All 12 mAbs were confirmed to be IgG1 with kappa light chains (Supplementary Fig. 1), in contrast to the IgG2a kappa light chain HCVAg-specific mAb identified by Muerhoff et al.20. They exhibited reactivity to HCVcAg at both 0.1 and 1 ng/ml concentrations (Fig. 4B). In contrast, commercially available mAbs exhibit reactivity only down to 1 ng/ml21,22.

To further characterize antibody performance, we assessed the binding avidities of the mAbs-HCVcAg interactions. The mAbs showed varying but high HCVcAg binding avidities, that ranged from 0.01 to 8 nM (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 4C–D). Together, these data suggests that our mAbs not only recognize HCVcAg at ten-fold lower concentrations compared to those that are commercially available but also bind with higher avidity highlighting their potential as highly sensitive candidates for diagnostic applications.

Gene usage diversity and affinity maturation

The most frequently utilized immunoglobulin (Ig) heavy chain variable genes were VH1-47-3, VH1-14 and VH9-1. Notably, 50% of the mAbs were encoded by VH1-47-3, while VH1-14 and VH9-1 each encoded 16.67% of the mAbs (Supplementary Table 3; Fig. 5A and C). Analysis of the immunoglobulin light chain variable gene usage revealed Vκ/λ8–30 as the most frequently used gene, encoding 75% of the mAbs (Supplementary Table 4; Fig. 5B–C). The heavy and light chain variable regions showed high sequence similarity to their germline genes that ranged approximately from 90% to 99% (Supplementary Table 3 and 4; Fig. 5C). Additionally, mAbs encoded by the same germline gene exhibited higher sequence similarity to one another (Supplementary Fig. 2A–B).

Complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) lengths ranged from 9 to 13 amino acids for the heavy chain, with 50% of the mAbs exhibiting a CDR3 length of 12 (Supplementary Fig. 2C and 2E). Light chain CDR3 lengths ranged from 8 to 9, with 75% of mAbs having a CDR3 length of 9 (Supplementary Fig. 2D and 2F). Notably, the most frequently used genes encoded an average of heavy chain CDR3 length of 12 and a light chain CDR3 length of 8.5 (Fig. 5D).

Somatic hypermutation mutation (SHM) resulted in an average of 7 amino acid changes in the heavy chain and 4 in the light chain (Supplementary Table 3 and 4, Supplementary Fig. 3; Fig. 5D). As expected, and consistent with established B cell receptor maturation processes23, SHM rates in the heavy chain tended to be higher compared to the light chain (Supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, amino acid changes in the mAbs showed a significant negative correlation with sequence similarity to the corresponding germline genes, indicating that lower mutation levels were associated with increased germline identity (Fig. 5E–F). Together, these data demonstrate that our generated mAbs are genetically diverse, with relatively modest SHM during their maturation process.

B cell binding epitopes of HCVcAg

Developing an accurate antigen-based rapid test requires the selection of monoclonal antibodies that recognize distinct, non-competing antigenic regions24. Hence, we performed an epitope mapping analysis leveraging the full-length sequence of the HCVcAg. In silico prediction revealed key immunogenic residues, highlighted in red (Fig. 6A). We then validated these sites by an ELISA-based binding assay using overlapping peptides spanning the HCVcAg and the generated mAbs, with confirmed epitopes also highlighted in red (Fig. 6B).

Notably, mAbs M004 and M0064, both encoded by the same germline gene and exhibit a high sequence similarity with each other recognize the same epitope, WGPTDPRRRSR (Fig. 6C). Similarly, mAbs M0007 and M0012, which are also related, bound the same epitope, GGGQIV (Fig. 6C). Additionally, mAbs M0010, M0022, M0027, M0060, M0061 and M0082, all encoded by the same germline gene and highly related to one another, recognized the epitope YPWPLYGNEGL (Fig. 6C).

Given the global circulation of 7 distinct HCV genotypes, we assessed the sequence similarity of the HCVcAg used in this study relative to all genotypes. We observed high sequence similarity, ranging from 86.9% to 98%, with conserved epitopes across all the genotypes (Fig. 6D).

Together, these findings demonstrate the non-overlapping epitope recognition patterns and potential cross-reactivity of the mAbs to all the HCV genotypes, providing a rational basis for their strategic pairing in diagnostic applications.

Detection of recombinant HCVcAg by mAb pairing

The development an antigen-based rapid diagnostic test requires 2 mAbs: one immobilized at the test line and the other conjugated to a gold nanoparticle, with an anti-mouse Fc antibody embedded at the control line24,25. To identify optimal mAb pairs, all possible combinations of the 12 mAbs were assessed, resulting in 144 unique pairs arranged in 12 × 12 matrix. These pairs were evaluated for their ability to detect recombinant HCVcAg using a lateral flow chromatographic assay.

All the mAb pairs showed binding to the HCVcAg, with the strongest binding observed for the combinations M0004-M0007, M0007-M0012, M0007-M0027, and M0012-M0027 (Fig. 7A). Among these, the mAbs within each high preforming pairs were encoded by distinct germline genes and recognized non-overlapping epitopes, except for M0007-M0012, which are encoded by the same germline gene and recognize similar epitope.

Next, we evaluated the sensitivity of two high-performing pairs, M0004–M0007 and M0007–M0027, by performing limit of detection analysis using two-fold serial dilutions of recombinant HCVcAg ranging from 20 ng/ml to 0.156 ng/ml. The M0004-M0007 pair detected antigen concentrations as low as 0.3125 ng/ml (Fig. 7B), while the M0007–M0027 pair showed sensitivity down to 1.25 ng/ml (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed binding of mAbs M0004, M0007 and M0027 to HCVcAg (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that our mAb pairs effectively detect HCVcAg, with the highest sensitivity achieved by pairs targeting non-overlapping epitopes.

Performance of the HCVcAg rapid test

Finally, we validated the performance of our rapid test by utilizing serum samples from patients with acute HCV infection and healthy volunteers, whose infection status were confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a gold standard for distinguishing active from past infections. Demographic characteristics and HCV RNA levels are comprehensively described in Supplementary Table 5.

We employed the mAb pair M0004-M0007, which showed the highest sensitivity to HCVcAg. This pair successfully detected HCVcAg in 23 out of 25 PCR-confirmed HCV-positive patients, with no reactivity in serum samples from healthy controls (Fig. 8A and B). Additionally, we observed a significant positive correlation between HCVcAg signal intensities and HCV RNA levels (r (95% CI) = 0.8274 (0.6300–0.9243; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 8C).

Furthermore, the test demonstrated an overall accuracy of 95.6% in identifying patients with HCV infections and showed almost perfect agreement to the PCR results (Cohen’s kappa = 0.91). Performance of HCVcAg rapid test.

Given that existing HCVcAg tests show low sensitivities in serum samples with reduced levels of HCV viral loads13,26–28, we assessed the performance in serum samples with HCV viral loads < 1000 IU/mL. Our test yielded a sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 100%, with PPV and NPV of 100 and 91.3%, respectively. Additionally, the test demonstrated an overall accuracy of 94.3% in identifying HCV patients with viral loads < 1000 IU/mL and showed almost perfect agreement to the PCR results (Cohen’s kappa = 0.88). Also, the PLR was infinite indicating all individuals with a positive test were truly infected, while a NLR ratio of 0.143 suggests our test’s strong ability to accurately identify uninfected individuals even in low-viremia cases. The AUC was 0.759 (Fig. 8E), confirming high diagnostic accuracy. A non-significant McNemar Chi-squared test (p = 0.1573) further revealed that the sensitivity and specificity of our test is comparable to those of the PCR in low-viremia cases (Table 1). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the high diagnostic accuracy and reliability of our rapid HCVcAg test.

Discussion

In this study, we present a robust and systematic approach for generating HCVcAg-specific murine mAbs, which enabled the development of a rapid antigen test for acute HCV detection. Molecular profiling of the mAbs revealed their strong binding reactivity and avidity, along with their uniqueness, as evidenced by the diversity in germline gene usage, affinity maturation and their distinct patterns of HCVcAg epitope recognition. These distinct molecular features coupled with strategic mAb pairings enabled the identification of the most sensitive HCVcAg-recognizing pair, M0004-M0007. This pair showed superior diagnostic performance in detecting serum levels of HCVcAg, even in samples with low HCV RNAemia (< 1000 IU/mL), and showed a strong positive correlation with HCV RNA levels, underscoring the potential clinical utility of our test for point-of-care applications, decentralized screening programs, and for monitoring the efficacy of DAAs.

The discovery of HCV more than three decades ago29, followed by the FDA approval of the first generation of DAAs30, revolutionized the management of HCV infection. In 2016, the WHO launched the Global Health Sector Strategy on viral hepatitis, setting an ambitious goal to eliminate viral hepatitis, particularly hepatitis B virus (HBV) and HCV, as a public health threat by 203018. Under this strategy, elimination hinges on achieving a 90% reduction in new infections, a 65% reduction in hepatitis-related mortality, and scaling diagnosis and treatment to reach 90% and 80% of infected individuals, respectively18.

Nevertheless, these goals remain out of reach due to persistent gaps in diagnosis and treatment access, particularly in LMICs31. To address these diagnostic challenges and contribute toward the WHO’s goal of HCV eradication, we generated HCVcAg-specific mAbs and developed and validated an instrument-free rapid antigen test for the detection of acute HCV infection. Our mAbs demonstrated robust performance in detecting HCVcAg not only at 1 ng/ml but also at 0.1 ng/ml, a ten-fold improvement in sensitivity as compared to previously developed mAbs or those that are commercially available21,22,32.

Our mAbs exhibited strong but diverse binding avidities, which is rooted in the natural process of B cell development33. Sequential immunization of mice in this study allowed sufficient time for extensive affinity maturation, contributing to the enhanced binding characteristics observed for these mAbs. Consistent with this, molecular profiling of our mAbs revealed diverse germline gene origins, with sequences exhibiting modest divergence from their parental clones. This underscores the role of activation-induced cytidine deaminase, an enzyme that introduces point mutations in germline sequences during B cell affinity maturation23. Indeed, our mAbs underwent somatic hypermutation (SHM), with an average of 7 amino acid substitutions in the heavy chains and 4 in the light chains. Moreover, antibodies with distinct antigen avidities, shaped by diverse germline gene usage and SHM, often exhibit unique epitope recognition patterns34. Accordingly, we found that our mAbs also displayed distinct epitope recognition profiles, with mAbs encoded by the same germline genes recognizing similar antigenic determinants of the HCVcAg. Notably, the first 125 amino acid residues of HCVcAg are known to harbor immunodominant epitopes, and consistent with previous reports32,35–37, all 4 epitopes identified by our mAbs were mapped within this immunodominant region.

The observed differential epitope recognition patterns position these mAbs as strong candidates for rapid antigen diagnostic test development, as such tests primarily require 2 non-competing mAbs that bind to distinct antigenic sites24,38,39. Through strategic pairing of the mAbs and evaluation of HCVcAg detection performance, we identified the most sensitive pair, M0004-M0007, which was then selected to develop the HCVcAg rapid test. Upon validation, our test achieved superior diagnostic accuracy, as evidenced by enhanced performance metrics that was comparable to those of the PCR testing.

Currently, the most widely used laboratory-based diagnostic assay for HCVcAg is the ARCHITECT HCV automated antigen test, which has showed consistently high performance (e.g., > 95% sensitivity and > 98% specificity) across various sample sizes and demographics, albeit some variations13,14,26,28,40,41. For instance, Wasitthankasem et al. reported a slightly different sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV and NPV values of 99%, 100%, 99%, 100% and 97%, respectively, from our findings41. Similarly, Alonso et al., reported sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV and NPV values of 97.1%, 98.6%, 97.8%, 98.4% and 99.3%, respectively, which differed modestly from our observations26. The differences may be attributed to the larger sample size used in their validation. Nonetheless, their reported strong correlation between HCVcAg and HCV RNA is consistent with our findings. Furthermore, a meta-analysis encompassing 46 comparative studies reported pooled sensitivity, specificity, PLR and NLR values of 96%, 99%, 141 and 0.04, respectively, which differed slightly from our observations14. In contrast, Pollock et al. reported sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV values of 82.1%, 99.8%, 99.58% and 89.7%, respectively, based on a cohort of 744 samples, which were lower than those observed in our study40. Similarly, Vieira et al., reported sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV values of 88.9%, 99.1%, 97.0% and 96.4% respectively, from cohort of 145 samples, again lower than those of our findings28.

Alternatively, the laboratory-based Elecsys® HCV DUO immunoassay, which detects both anti-HCV and HCVcAg simultaneously, has shown variable sensitivities and specificities in detecting active infections compared with our test16,27,42. Notably, Inoue et al. reported sensitivity and specificity values of 55.0% and 99.2%, respectively, markedly lower than our observations27. Similarly, Kanokudom et al. reported lower sensitivity (70.6%) but comparable specificity (100%) to our test in detecting active infections16. Importantly, a key limitation of currently available HCVcAg antigen and antibody assays are their reduced sensitivity in patients with low HCV viral loads13,26,27,43. In contrast, our test achieved a sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 100% in patients with HCV RNA levels < 1000 IU/ml.

Despite the strengths of our rapid test, our study has several limitations. First, we assessed reactivity of the mAbs to only 2 HCVcAg concentrations (0.1 and 1 ng/ml), and is likely that they recognize the antigen even at a much lower concentration. Notably, Aoyagi et al. reported a detection limit of 0.06 pg/ml for their mAbs44. Secondly, we were unable to evaluate our test performance across all 7 HCV genotypes. However, the mAbs were generated against HCVcAg sequence that is highly conserved among genotypes, supporting the likelihood of broad reactivity. Moreover, a recent report attributed reduced HCVcAg detection by the ARCHITECT HCVcAg assay, particularly in genotype 3a and 6 samples to an amino acid substitution at position 49, where threonine is replaced by valine or alanine45. Importantly, none of the epitopes recognized by our mAbs include an amino acid at this position. Thirdly, we did not assess potential cross-reactivity with closely related viruses, including hepatitis A and B, other blood-borne pathogens, or potentially interfering substances. Finally, the validation cohort was relatively small, and inclusion of a larger sample size might have influenced the estimated diagnostic performance. These shortcomings warrant further multi-genotype and cross-reactivity validation studies, incorporating a larger sample size from multiple locations. Despite this, our test demonstrated superior performance for HCVcAg detection.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the generation of diverse panel of HCVcAg-specific mAbs and identification of an optimal pair (M0004-M0007) for rapid diagnostic test development. This test showed superior diagnostic performance even in low HCV viremia. While further large-scale, multi-genotype validation is warranted, these findings underscore the potential utility of our test in point-of-care applications, decentralized screening programs and monitoring of treatment response, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed to generate highly specific monoclonal antibodies against HCVcAg to aid in the development of an antigen rapid test for detecting acute HCV infection. The sample size (n = 5) was selected to ensure reproducibility of HCVcAg immunogenicity. We comprehensively characterized the mAbs by assessing their binding reactivity and avidity to the HCVcAg, along with their germline gene usage, affinity maturation and epitope recognition profiles. Afterwards, we strategically paired all the mAbs to one another and assessed the binding of each pair to the HCVcAg using the lateral flow immunochromatography assay. We then perform lower limit of detection analysis on the pairs that yielded the strongest HCVcAg binding. The most sensitive pair was selected to develop the rapid antigen test and subsequently validated using serum samples from individuals confirmed to be PCR HCV-positive and HCV-negative.

Ethics statement

All experiments using mice were performed in strict accordance with recommendations set forth in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Rutgers University (protocol approval number 202300007). This study is also reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Ethical clearance for the collection of demographic data and discarded blood samples for the human validation cohort was approved by the Rutgers Robert Wood Johnson Medical School Institutional Review Board (protocol number 2023002149); informed consent was obtained from all individuals and/or their legal guardians. The study involving human participants was conducted in accordance with the principles of Declaration of Helsinki.

Animal care, in vivo study design, and human validation cohort

Efforts were done to minimize animal suffering, including humane endpoints and appropriate monitoring. When appropriate, animal euthanasia was performed by gradual-fill carbon dioxide inhalation at 30–70% chamber volume per minute, followed by cardiac puncture, in accordance with the IACUC protocol. BALB/c mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (strain: 000651) and maintained under pathogen-free conditions in a breeding colony, with a controlled light/dark cycle at the Child Health Institute of New Jersey, part of the Rutgers Robert Wood Johnson Medical School.

The in vivo immunization experiment was designed as a donor selection and monoclonal antibody discovery platform, rather than as a comparative immunogenicity study. Accordingly, 5 BALB/c mice were initially immunized subcutaneously with 50 µg of HCVcAg (Native antigen, batch# 23052312) adjuvanted with complete Freund’s reagent (Sigma, cat# F5881). The mice received a booster of the HCVcAg, adjuvanted with incomplete Freund’s reagent (BD, cat# 263910) on days 14 and 28 post-initial immunization. One week post-second immunization, blood samples were collected from all the mice through the cheek vein. The mice were euthanized a week after the third immunization by carbon dioxide exposure and cardiac puncture for blood collection and spleen isolation. The blood samples were centrifuged at 2000 x g for 15 min, and the serum was collected and stored at - 80 °C until further analysis. The spleens were homogenized to obtain single-cell suspension, and leukocytes were enriched by lysing red blood cells using ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer (BD, cat #555899). No adjuvant-only or naïve control groups were included, as the objective was not to assess relative immunogenicity or adjuvant effects, but rather to identify a single high-responder animal with robust HCVcAg-specific humoral responses suitable for downstream plasma cell isolation.

For rapid test validation, serum samples from 46 participants were utilized. Demographic data were solicited from the participants through standardized written questionnaires. The validation cohort included subjects with laboratory-confirmed acute HCV infection and healthy volunteers that underwent NAT (Roche COBAS) for HCV RNA. Inclusion criteria required documentation of either confirmed HCV infection or negative NAT for HCV RNA.

Plasma B cell isolation and beacon sorting

Plasma B cells were isolated from splenocytes using the EasySep™ Mouse CD138 Positive Selection Kit (STEM, cat# 18957). Briefly, mouse CD138 magnetic beads was added to splenocytes, followed by positive selection to obtain CD138 + plasma cells. The isolated plasma cells were introduced into the beacon cell sorting chip by Opto-electro positioning to guide the cells into the Nanopen (channels in the chip). The cells were then cultured for 4 h, followed by the introduction of HCVcAg-coated microbeads and fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (proprietary to Sino Biological) into the beacon sorter to identify antibody-secreting plasma cells. HCVcAg-reactive plasma cells were singly sorted into a 96 well plate containing cell lysis solution.

RNA extraction, and Nested PCR

Following cell lysis, total RNA was extracted and reversed transcribed (RT) into complementary DNA according to the technical manual of reverse transcription Kit from Sino Biological (a self-use product developed by Sino Biological, not sold to the public). The heavy and light chain antibody variable regions were amplified by two rounds of Nested PCR using specific primers proprietary to Sino Biological.

Gene cloning and HEK293 cell transfection

The amplified heavy and light chain variable regions were purified and ligated into an expression vector containing the constant region and signal peptide of the immunoglobulin gene. E. coli was transformed with the ligation product in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Recombinant plasmids were isolated and analyzed by Sanger sequencing to confirm their identity to the original PCR product. The verified recombinant plasmids were then co-transfected into HEK293 cells by mixing with TF2 transfection reagent (Sino Biological, product code: STF02). The cells were cultured in serum-free CD medium (Product code: SMM 293-TI) with medium replenishment done after 1-, 3- and 5-days post-transfection. Culture supernatants were collected between 3 and 7 days post-transfection and stored in -80 °C until further analysis.

Monoclonal antibody purification and detection

The monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were purified from culture supernatants using the Protein A affinity chromatography (AC) as previously described46. Briefly, the column connected to the purification system was equilibrated with 3 column volumes (CV) of ultrapure water and then 3 CV of AC binding buffer (50mM Tris, 100mM NaCl) to achieve a stable ultraviolet (UV) baseline. The flow rate was adjusted, and cell supernatant was loaded onto the column. The column was washed with 5 CV of wash buffer (50mM Tris, 100mM NaCl) to remove unbound molecules. AC Elution buffer (100mM Glycine, pH3.5) was then added to the column to elute the antibodies, and eluent collection was based on UV peaks. The purified antibodies were mixed with sample buffer containing 2-mercaptoethanol in a 1:1 ratio and subjected to SDS-PAGE as previously described47. The resolved proteins were visualized by Coomassie Brilliant blue staining47.

Immunogenicity and reactivity of HCVcAg

Immunogenicity of HCVcAg in immunized mice and reactivity with cell supernatant and mAbs were assessed using enzyme immunoassay. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with 0.1 and 1 µg/mL of HCVcAg in 100 µL of PBS per well overnight at 4 °C. Plates were coated with only 1 µg/mL of HCVcAg for the immunogenicity assessment. The wells were blocked with 300 µL of 2% skim milk followed by 1 h incubation at room temperature. The plates were washed three times with PBS-T, followed by the addition of 100 µL/well of diluted serum (1:400) or plasma cell supernatant or 1 µg/mL of the purified antibodies as primary antibodies for 2 h. The plates were washed three times with PBS-T, followed by the addition of 100 µL/well of 500-fold diluted HRP-goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Jackson Laboratory, product# 109-036-088). The plates were washed three times with PBS-T, followed by the addition of 200 µL/well of chromogen solution for 20 min in the dark. The reaction was terminated using 50 µL of 2 M sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Biolayer interferometry

The binding avidities of the mAbs to HCVcAg were measured using the label-free analysis by BLI on an Octet-N1 (Sartorius)48. Briefly, the HCVcAg was biotinylated, followed by dilution in kinetics buffer (PBS, 0.05% BSA, 0.02% Tween20) and immobilization on the Octet SA biosensor (Sartorius). The biosensor was then immersed in the kinetics solution containing the purified mAbs to measure the degree of association. Afterwards, the biosensor was immersed in kinetics solution devoid of mAbs to measure the degree of dissociation. The association rate (ka), dissociation rate (kd), and dissociation constant (KD) were globally fit using a 1:1 binding model in Data Analysis 12.0 (Sartorius).

Monoclonal antibody isotyping

Isotyping of the purified mAbs was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BIO-RAD, lot# 164598). Briefly, the mAbs were diluted with PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin to a concentration of 1 µg/mL. 150 µL of the diluted antibodies were added to the development tubes containing lyophilized microparticles coupled with anti-lambda and anti-kappa antibodies and allowed to incubate for 30 s followed by vortexing. The isotyping strips were then immersed into the tubes for approximately 5 to 10 min.

Gene family usage and phylogenetic analysis

The gene family usage of the variable domain of the mouse IgG heavy and light chains of the mAbs was analyzed using IMGT/V-Quest, which compares the consensus nucleotide sequences against the Mus musculus immunoglobulin germline V-gene database49. This led to the identification of the complementarity-determining region and framework regions of the VH and VL sequences. Complementarity-determining region-3 amino acid sequences of the VH and VL were aligned using ClustaW. Phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor joining method, bootstrapped using 1000 replicates, with analysis conducted using the MEGA version X software50. Geneious was used to quantify the relatedness among the antibodies51.

Epitope mapping

Potential epitopes spanning HCVcAg domain were determined using Bepipred-1.0 insilico prediction tool on the Immune Epitope Database portal52. The identified epitopes were validated by binding assay using overlapping peptides (Supplementary Table 1) spanning the HCVcAg protein (BEI Resources), as previously described24. Briefly, 10 µg of each peptide was coated onto 96-well plates overnight. The plates were blocked with 200 µl/well of PBS for 1 h, followed by a 2 h incubation at 37 °C with 10 µg/ml of each of the mAbs. The plates were washed 3 times followed by the addition of 100 µl/well of goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L)-HRP (Merck Millipore, Cat# AP308P) (1:500) secondary antibody for 1 h. After washing the plates 3 times with PBS-T, 200 µl/well chromogen solution was added for 20 min in the dark. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 50 µl of 2M H2SO4 and absorbance was read at 450 nm.

Antibody conjugation to nanoparticles

The HCVcAg-specific mAbs were conjugated to gold nanoparticles (Abcam, cat# ab154873) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the mAb was first diluted to 0.2 mg/ml using the supplied dilution buffer. This diluted antibody was further diluted 4.5-fold by mixing 12 µl of mAb with 42 µl of reaction buffer. Lyophilized gold nanoparticles were then resuspended in 45 µl of the resulting antibody-buffer mixture. The antibody-nanoparticle mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of 5 µl of proprietary quencher solution to terminate the conjugation reaction. Subsequently, 100 µl of 1% Tween-20 (Catalog number P9416, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA) in PBS (MilliporeSigma, cat# P4417) and 50 µl of 50% sucrose (MilliporeSigma, cat# S0389) in 1X PBS were added to the gold conjugates prior to their use in immunochromatography.

Antibody application to nitrocellulose membranes

Nitrocellulose membrane (DCN Diagnostics) was cut into strips manually. Strips were attached to a wick with adhesive paper (DCN Diagnostics). For the control area, 0.33 µl of anti-mouse Fc antibody (MilliporeSigma, cat# M4280) at 1 mg/ml was spotted on the control line. The anti-HCVcAg capture line on the nitrocellulose was generated by pipetting 0.33 µl of mAbs at 1 mg/ml at the test area. Strips were air-dried and stored in a desiccator at room temperature before use.

Immunochromatography

Each immunochromatography strip was run in a separate microcentrifuge tube, and groups of tubes/strips were run together. The rapid test solution was a combined mixture of 50 µl recombinant HCVcAg or serum from acute HCV patients,10 µl fetal calf serum, 5 µl proprietary quencher solution (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and 15 µl conjugated gold nanoparticle mix. The run time varied between 15 and 20 min. After the strips dried, results were image captured for signal pixel intensity analysis.

Western blot

5 µg of reduced HCVcAg were loaded in 3 separate lanes and resolved by SDS-PAGE followed by blotting onto PVDF membrane. Membranes were probed with purified mAbs M0004, M0007 and M0027 (each at 1:400 dilution). Following an hour incubation, the membranes were treated with goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L)-HRP (Merck Millipore, Cat# AP308P) secondary antibody (1:500). HCVcAg detection was performed using the Clarity™ Western ECL chemiluminescent substrate (BIO-RAD), followed by image acquisition using the ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (BIO-RAD).

Image analysis

Rapid antigen tests results were analyzed using image J processing software to machine-read and quantify signal pixel intensity results. The image of the nitrocellulose membrane was captured with a mobile phone camera and analyzed using an ImageJ macro to quantify test results. ImageJ quantified the signal at the test area and blank/background area and generated a normalized intensity, calculated by dividing the maximum intensity value at the test area by the average value of a blank area on each rapid antigen test.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test53. Differences between more than 2 independent groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparison. To visualize the pairwise binding interactions between monoclonal antibodies against HCV core antigen, we generated a chord diagram using the R circlize package (4.3.2). After flowing recombinant HCVcAg through the strip, interactions were scored on a semi-quantitative scale from 0 (no band) to 3 (strong, dark test line), based on visual intensity. All pairwise combinations (n = 144) were compiled into a matrix, and intensity scores were linearly rescaled to a 0–1 range to define interaction strength for visualization. The chord diagram was constructed such that each antibody was represented as a node on the circumference. Ribbons connecting each pair indicated the direction and strength of the interaction, with a continuous blue, orange, or red ribbon used to represent weak, moderate, or strong interaction strength, respectively. Antibodies used as nanoparticle conjugates were assigned unique node colors based on internal clone ID for reference. Legends for interaction strength and nanoparticle-bound antibody identity were overlaid beneath the diagram using the grid graphics system. Pearson correlation test was used to assess the magnitude of the relationship between 2 continuous variables. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated to assess the overall agreement between the HCVcAg test and PCR43,54. Receiver operating characteristic curve was generated to determine the area under the curve (AUC) and assess the diagnostic accuracy of our HCVcAg test55,56. McNemar Chi-squared test was used to compare the sensitivity and specificity of our HCVcAg rapid test to PCR57. All statistical analyses and plots were generated by the GraphPad prism and R.
