Application of continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring in the study of spinal nerve root thermal injury in a porcine model
Authors: Chengxia Wang, Zhuofu Li, Woquan Zhong, Shuai Jiang, Weishi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA warning system to avoid potential nerve root thermal injury caused by automatic spine robot is essential. However, there is a lack of basic research to support the development of such warning system. The aims of this study are to confirm the feasibility of continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring (CIONM) in spinal nerve root monitoring and study the exposure time of spinal nerve root thermal injury at different temperature in a porcine model.MethodsThe experiment was carried out on 32 nerve roots of 4 pigs (bilateral L4-S1 nerve roots). Sham group (CIONM only), control group (Room temperature stimulation with CIONM), and 40 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, 55 °C, 60 °C, 65 °C temperature stimulation groups were conducted at random in each pig. The change of EMG was recorded continuously. The exposure time of nerve root injury at different temperatures was recorded. Histological analysis of the nerve roots was performed to confirm the injury.ResultsThe EMG of the sham, control, 40 °C and 45 °C groups were stable during the experiment, and no significant nerve root injury was observed on histological analysis. The time of 50% EMG amplitude decrease in 50℃, 55℃, 60℃ and 65℃ groups were 277.5 ± 18.2, 59.5 ± 6.2, 20.8 ± 1.0 and 16.5 ± 1.3 s, respectively. And the nerve specimens of these groups showed varying degrees of damage.ConclusionsCIONM can be used for continuous monitoring of spinal nerve roots. For 5 min, 40℃ and 45℃ did not cause significant nerve root injury. However, when the temperature reached 50℃ and above, nerve roots appeared injury.

Introduction

The field of spinal surgery has witnessed rapid advancements with the development of surgical robots, primarily semi-automated robots designed to assist in pedicle screw placement [1, 2]. These robots, while enhancing precision, are on the cusp of full autonomy, capable of executing complex procedures such as pedicle screw placement and laminectomy [3, 4]. As we approach an era of fully automated spinal robots, it is imperative to address the potential acute thermal injuries to nerve roots that may arise from robotic procedures. Current robotic systems in laboratories often employ high-speed grinding drills or ultrasonic bone scalpels [5, 6], they can generate temperatures high enough to pose a risk to adjacent nerve roots. Research has established that during osseous resection, high-speed drills can attain temperatures up to 148.2 °C, and ultrasonic bone scalpels can reach 80.3 °C, despite the use of saline irrigation [7]. This highlights the importance of incorporating thermal management into the safety protocols of robotic systems, alongside precision control. Nonetheless, research on the duration of exposure to various temperatures that leads to thermal injury of the nerve root is currently limited. Consequently, a comprehensive safety warning system that incorporates both temperature thresholds and exposure times is essential for the safe integration of such technology. In this study, we employed continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring (CIONM) [8] to investigate the temporal dynamics of thermal injury to spinal nerve roots at various temperatures, aiming to inform the design of future spinal robotic systems.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by relevant institutional Ethics Committee of Animals (approval number: A2018011). A total of 4 male pigs, weight 25–30 kg, were obtained from a local minipig breeding base for use in this study. Each pig was housed individually, with free access to food and water. And each pig was isolated for 14 days before the experiment.

Heat generating system

We designed a heat generating system to perform thermal damage operation (Fig. 1). The whole device is composed of three parts: a sterilizable aluminum cone probe, an aluminum handle with a temperature sensor, and a temperature controller. The probe, featuring a 4-millimeter-diameter tip, is the component that makes direct contact with the spinal nerve root. Integrated at the junction of the handle and the probe, a heating element and a thermometric sensor are strategically positioned. The controller is responsible for regulating the temperature of the probe. During the stimulation of the nerve root, minimal pressure is applied to ensure gentle contact. Continuous Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (CIONM) is then utilized to assess and monitor any potential injuries to the nerve roots.

Anesthesia and operation

Upon completion of the experimental procedures, the mini pig was euthanized while under anesthesia. This was accomplished by administering a lethal dose of potassium chloride intravenously at a rate of 2 mmol/kg. All anesthesia and execution procedures were carried out under the guidance of a professional animal anesthesiologist and have been reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Animals.

The mini pig was initially induced with propofol at a dosage of 2.5 mg/kg to achieve general anesthesia. Following induction, the animal was maintained under anesthesia using isoflurane inhalation at a rate of 0.41 ml/min with a fresh gas flow of 4 L/min (2% isoflurane concentration).

We monitored vital signs including heart rate, blood pressure, temperature and respiratory rate throughout the procedure. After surgical disinfection, a laminectomy was performed from the L4 to S1, and the nerve roots of L4, L5, L6 and S1 were fully exposed at least 1 cm.

Upon completion of the experimental procedures, the mini pig was euthanized while under anesthesia. This was accomplished by administering a lethal dose of potassium chloride intravenously at a rate of 2 mmol/kg. All anesthesia and euthanized procedures were carried out under the guidance of a professional animal anesthesiologist and have been reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Animals.

Neurophysiologic assessment

All procedural recordings and stimulations were executed utilizing the Cadwell Cascade intraoperative monitoring system (Cadwell Inc.). The CIONM technique was employed to examine real-time alterations in EMG signals corresponding to nerve root injury. The triggered EMG mode was engaged to provide a consistent stimulus at a rate of 1 per second with an intensity of 1 milliampere (mA) (1 Hz, 100 µs, 1 mA). An automatic periodic stimulation (APS) electrode (Medtronic Inc.) was designated for the stimulation process. Upon complete exposure of the nerve root, the APS electrode was strategically positioned at the spinal nerve root’s origin to maintain its stability (Fig. 2). Corresponding muscle groups for each nerve root were equipped with receiving electrodes to capture EMG signals: the quadriceps femoris for L4, the biceps femoris for L5, the peroneus tertius for L6, and the soleus for S1 (Fig. 2). After connecting the APS electrode to the monitoring system, baseline amplitudes of evoked responses were calibrated to serve as control data. The tEMG amplitude changes were recorded for a total of 30 min.

Study design

The study encompassed a total of four pigs, with each pig contributing eight nerve roots—specifically, the bilateral L4 through S1 nerve roots—for a cumulative total of 32 nerve roots utilized in the experiment. The assignment of these nerve roots to various study groups was randomized: one nerve root per pig was allocated to the sham group, another to the control group, and the remaining six were distributed among the experimental groups, each subjected to a different temperature—40 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, 55 °C, 60 °C, and 65 °C. In the sham group, a 30-minute CIONM session was implemented, during which only the stimulation electrode was in contact with the nerve root without any application of thermal or electrical stimulation. The control group involved the placement of the thermal probe in contact with the nerve root under CIONM surveillance, but without the application of heat. Throughout the experiment, continuous monitoring of the EMG amplitude was maintained. Should the amplitude of the triggered EMG signal decrease by a margin exceeding 50%, the application of heat must be terminated immediately. Subsequently, CIONM should be sustained for a 30-minute period to monitor and assess whether the EMG amplitude recovered, indicating the potential for reversible injury or the extent of damage sustained by the nerve root.

Histological assessment

Post-euthanasia, nerve root specimens were promptly collected and subjected to fixation using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, the specimens underwent staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as well as Luxol fast blue (LFB) to facilitate histological examination on day 1 and day 7 after the experiment. The presence of axonal necrosis, myelin digestion, endoneurial fibrosis, perineurial fibrosis, and dystrophic calcification served as the histological criteria for identifying nerve root injury. The assessment of each specimen was conducted under a light microscope by two researchers in a blinded manner to eliminate bias. In instances where discrepancies arose in the evaluation, a third researcher was engaged to mediate and reach a consensus.

Results

The EMG signals exhibited stability in both the sham and control groups over a 30-minute observation period, with no significant nerve injury detected in the corresponding histological specimens. This absence of injury was also observed in the groups subjected to temperatures of 40 °C and 45 °C. However, a notable shift was recorded once the temperature surpassed 50 °C, as evidenced by real-time EMG monitoring capturing changes in amplitude (Table 1). Specifically, the time taken for the EMG amplitude to diminish by 50% was measured at 277.5 ± 18.2 s for the 50 °C group, 59.5 ± 6.2 s for the 55 °C group, 20.8 ± 1.0 s for the 60 °C group, and 16.5 ± 1.3 s for the 65 °C group (Fig. 3). The nerve root specimens from these elevated temperature groups demonstrated varying levels of injury, corroborating the functional changes observed through EMG (Table 2) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this research, we introduced the application of continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring (CIONM) to the surveillance of spinal nerve roots, marking the first instance of its use for this purpose. We successfully developed a consistent and replicable model for the continuous EMG monitoring of spinal nerve roots. Within the sham group, four nerve roots were monitored using CIONM alone, and it was determined that the application of a 1 mA, 1 Hz stimulus did not result in any observable nerve injury. The control group was established to confirm that the physical contact of the electrode with the nerve root, without any thermal or electrical stimulation, did not lead to any discernible harm or functional impairment of the nerve root.

Employing this method, we were able to observe the temporal onset of nerve root injury at varying temperatures. Our findings indicate that exposure to 50 °C can lead to nerve injury within a 5-minute window, and as the temperature increases, the time frame for injury onset decreases. Following a recovery period of approximately 30 min, the tEMG signals, which had exhibited a decline in amplitude of over 50%, did not show signs of recovery. This persistent reduction in EMG signal amplitude probably indicates that the thermal injury to the nerves was severe enough to cause lasting damage.

The assessment of nerve injury was further validated through histological examination of tissue sections, providing a pathological confirmation of the observed electrophysiological changes. The observation that there were minimal nerve injuries on Day 1, in contrast to the nearly universal occurrence of nerve injuries in the groups exposed to 50 °C and above on Day 7, suggests that the pathological changes induced by sustained heat injury may have a chronic nature. We propose that the pathological alterations resulting from nerve heat damage are likely to be of a chronic nature. However, the effects of this damage can be rapidly detected and manifested through CIONM. This real-time monitoring provides a critical tool for immediate assessment and subsequent clinical decision-making to prevent further nerve damage. Our study did not include an assessment of hind limb strength in the experimental animals. In the clinical context, intraoperative wake-up tests and postoperative evaluation of muscle strength are pivotal diagnostic tools for confirming nerve root injury, which were not addressed in our experimental design.

It is essential to highlight that the design of our study is fundamentally a proof-of-principle investigation, which inherently limits the direct extrapolation of our findings to clinical applications. Our methodology necessitated a complete laminectomy to facilitate unobstructed spinal nerve monitoring; however, such extensive surgical intervention is not a standard procedure in spinal surgeries.

Currently, the application of CIONM is predominantly found in the context of thyroid surgery, where it is used to oversee the recurrent laryngeal nerve [8–11]. The operational mechanism involves the stimulation of the vagus nerve, which leads to the depolarization of nerve fibers. This process generates nerve impulses that travel downwards, prompting muscle movement that, in turn, produces EMG signals. These signals are then captured, amplified, and processed by receiving electrodes to create EMG waveforms. The alert threshold of a 50% decrease in amplitude is considered reliable, as evidenced by both clinical and experimental studies [9, 12]. The CIONM’s primary advantage over traditional electrophysiological monitoring techniques is its capacity for real-time surveillance, offering a more sensitive and immediate reflection of EMG changes. This has been substantiated through meta-analytical studies that have confirmed the technique’s efficacy and safety [13].

Given that the spinal nerve, much like the vagus nerve, is a peripheral nerve with motor fibers, the same principle of nerve stimulation and EMG signal generation can be applied to spinal nerve root monitoring. When a nerve root is stimulated with a current that exceeds the threshold level, it elicits a contraction in the innervated muscles, thereby generating EMG signals. This theoretical foundation underpins the rationale for utilizing CIONM in the monitoring of spinal nerve roots. In the present study, stimulation electrodes were strategically positioned at the nerve root, while recording electrodes were placed in the muscles corresponding to each nerve root to capture the EMG signals effectively.

Limited data are available regarding the specific temperature thresholds that correlate with thermal injury to spinal nerves. Konno et al. [14] have documented that a temperature of 40 °C does not inflict injury on spinal nerves, in contrast to 70 °C, which was found to completely impair nerve function within a 5-minute timeframe in a porcine model. Similarly, Tamai et al. [15] have identified a threshold of approximately 48.9 °C for nerve injury in a rabbit model. Ohyama et al. [16] observed that following bipolar cauterization near the nerve root, where temperatures averaged 60.98 °C, histological damage was evident in 47.8% of the nerves examined. Our study has delineated the critical temperature range to be approximately between 45 °C and 50 °C, beyond which spinal nerve injury becomes apparent. This finding aligns with the existing body of research. Furthermore, our investigation has provided preliminary insights into the tolerance times of nerve roots when exposed to varying temperatures. We observed that at 50 °C, continuous exposure for 4–5 min was necessary to cause nerve injury, whereas at 55 °C, this period was reduced to nearly 1 min, and at 60 °C and 65 °C, injury occurred in approximately 20 s. These findings are corroborated by Lin et al. [17], who reported damage to the recurrent laryngeal nerve after a 20-second exposure to 60 °C, a result that is congruent with our study’s outcomes. While our study proposed thresholds for the duration that nerve roots can endure at varying temperatures, the thermal stimulation applied to nerve roots in actual surgical settings is characterized by intermittent and cumulative exposure, contrasting with the fixed thermal insult employed in our experimental paradigm.

The integration of bone-powered surgical systems in spinal surgery presents a significant risk of spinal nerve thermal injury. Empirical evidence from prior research confirms that temperatures can exceed 50 °C during osseous resection, a phenomenon observed irrespective of the tool employed—be it a high-speed drill or an ultrasonic bone cutter [7, 18]. Surgeons must remain vigilant about temperature management when utilizing these instruments to avert potential safety hazards. Surgeons often manually reduce the speed of cutting to minimize heat generation, particularly when elevated temperatures are suspected.

The advent of automated spinal robots is anticipated to partially supplant human operators in performing bone-cutting procedures in the future [1, 2, 19]. Unlike manual operations, which are dependent on the operator’s experience, these robotic systems have the potential to exert more intelligent control over heat generation. Our study’s findings are expected to inform and guide future research into the development of temperature warning systems for automated spinal robots. This advancement will be instrumental in assisting surgeons in meticulously planning and executing thermally sensitive procedures in the vicinity of the nerve roots, thereby enhancing patient safety and surgical precision.

The study presents several limitations. Firstly, due to its nature as an animal experiment with a small sample size, the extrapolation of these results to human applications may be limited. This is attributable to the potential existence of subtle neural differences between porcine models and humans, which could affect the comparability of the findings. Secondly, the study did not incorporate an assessment of postoperative motor function in the lower limbs. The evaluation was confined to electrophysiological and histological assessments. Given that multiple nerve roots were examined within each pig, any functional impairment of the lower limbs post-surgery would not specifically indicate which nerve roots were compromised. Nonetheless, this approach did contribute to a reduction in the number of animals used, thereby enhancing the ethical considerations and efficiency of the experimental design. Lastly, the method of direct thermal probe contact with the spinal nerve in this study may not perfectly replicate the clinical scenario where heat is transferred from the bone surface to the spinal nerve. This discrepancy could influence the accuracy of the study’s findings in terms of real-world applicability. We believe that CIONM will represent a potent and effective method for the real-time surveillance of spinal nerve function.

Conclusion

Continuous Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (CIONM) has proven to be a viable technique for the real-time monitoring of spinal nerve roots. Our findings indicate that at temperatures of 40 °C and 45 °C, there was no significant injury observed to the nerve roots even after a 30-minute exposure period. However, once the temperature escalated to 50 °C or higher, spinal nerve roots began to exhibit signs of injury. Notably, with the rise in temperature, there was a corresponding decrease in the time required for nerve damage to manifest, signifying a direct correlation between temperature elevation and the rapidity of thermal injury to the nerve roots.
