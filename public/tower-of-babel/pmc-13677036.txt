Building climate-resilient health systems for the displaced: health information systems and the statistical invisibility of climate-related mobility in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: R Leone, Y Ouattara, L Tognaccini, W Ricciardi, MR Gualano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change is reshaping the populations health systems serve, yet health information systems (HIS) remain blind to one of the fastest-growing groups: people displaced or migrating in the context of climate change. In sub-Saharan Africa this mobility is now counted at scale, but mobility data and health data systems have developed as parallel infrastructures blind to each other's populations.ObjectiveTo examine the health of populations moving in the context of climate change in sub-Saharan Africa, and to argue that a principal obstacle to climate-resilient health policy is the failure of the health-information-systems building block to render them legible.MethodsStructured, non-exhaustive searches of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar (June–July 2026), complemented by institutional data sources (WHO, IOM, IDMC, World Bank) and humanitarian situation reporting, organised into three evidence streams: (i) health outcomes of climate-related movers in sub-Saharan Africa, (ii) health information system performance in the region, and (iii) theoretical scholarship on enumeration and legibility. Eligibility criteria, search strings and the selection process are reported in full.ResultsSub-Saharan African HIS are structurally constrained: only about one in ten deaths in the WHO African Region is recorded, DHIS2-based routine systems capture facility events rather than populations, and household surveys sample by residence. Against this baseline, climate-related mobility is linked to malnutrition, waterborne disease, psychosocial distress and unmet basic needs, with markedly gendered impacts. Yet displacement datasets rarely capture health, health surveys rarely record displacement, a dedicated migration module appeared in only 12 of 85 reviewed DHS surveys, and climate attribution ranges from 3% to 99% across contexts. Health-system responses concentrate in acute emergency delivery, leaving information systems unadapted.ConclusionsPopulations invisible to routine health information systems are excluded from anticipatory, climate-resilient health policy. Three low-cost adaptations, health modules in displacement tracking, mobility modules in health surveys, and a harmonised typology of climate-related mobility, would make them visible to health-system planning at marginal cost, and are implementable within platforms (DHIS2, DTM, DHS) already operating at national scale.Keywords: Climate change, Climate-resilient health systems, Health information systems, Internal displacement, Sub-Saharan Africa, Health system resilience

Introduction

Human mobility linked to climate change is no longer a projection. In sub-Saharan Africa, climate shocks were associated with 7.4 million new internal displacements in 2022, approaching the 9 million attributed to conflict in the same year [1]. Globally, an unprecedented 83.4 million people were living in internal displacement at the end of 2024, of which around 45.8 million were displaced by conflict and violence and the remainder substantially by disasters, with more than half of the underlying estimates derived from the International Organization for Migration's Displacement Tracking Matrix [2]. World Bank modelling projects that, absent concerted mitigation and adaptation, sub-Saharan Africa could see up to 86 million additional internal climate migrants by 2050, the largest projected total of any world region [3]. The current Horn of Africa drought illustrates the dynamic in real time: after consecutive failed rainy seasons, Somalia declared a national drought emergency in late 2025, with over 6.5 million people facing acute food insecurity and more than 1.8 million children under five projected to suffer acute malnutrition in 2026, as drought-driven displacement crosses into Ethiopia's Somali region [4].

Against this measured and rising displacement stands a specific asymmetry that motivates this review: the displaced are counted with growing precision, and it is established in aggregate that climate change harms health, yet the population at the intersection, those who move because climate change made staying untenable, remains epidemiologically invisible. This asymmetry is not merely an evidence gap; it is a health-system one. The WHO operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems requires each of the six health-system building blocks, including health information systems, to integrate climate considerations [5], and a health system whose information architecture cannot see a growing, climate-exposed population cannot be resilient to the shocks that population faces.

The argument of this review, stated in advance.

Because this review moves between three literatures that are rarely read together, health outcomes among climate-related movers, the performance of health information systems in sub-Saharan Africa, and the sociology of enumeration, it is worth stating the argument in full before developing it, so that the transition between sections is transparent rather than assumed.

The claim defended here is therefore bounded: not that health information systems are the only obstacle to climate-resilient health policy for mobile populations, but that the information-systems building block is the binding constraint on anticipatory policy, because the other building blocks cannot plan for a population they cannot enumerate. Financing, workforce and service-delivery constraints are severe and well documented in their own right.

This review's focus is the adaptation (resilience) dimension of the WHO framework rather than low-carbon transformation, and although sub-Saharan Africa is the highest-stakes setting, the argument generalises to any health system whose routine data sample by residence.

Structure

Section 2 reports the review methods, eligibility criteria and selection flow. Section 3 provides background on health information systems in sub-Saharan Africa and characterises the measurement landscape for climate-related mobility. Section 4 synthesises the available evidence on health outcomes. Section 5 develops the theoretical account of why the information-systems building block fails and defends it against four objections. Materials and methods

Review type and reporting

This is a critical, interpretive review conducted according to the principles set out by Grant and Booth [11] for the critical review type, and reported with attention to the transparency requirements of SANRA [12]. It is not a systematic review, and the reader should not treat it as one: no dual independent screening, formal risk-of-bias appraisal or quantitative synthesis was performed. Following the reviewer-relevant distinction drawn by Greenhalgh et al. [13], the intended contribution is interpretive and configurative, reading a fragmented empirical literature through a health-systems and social-science lens in order to explain the pattern of its fragmentation, rather than aggregative. What follows nonetheless reports the search and selection process in sufficient detail for it to be reproduced and audited, which the original submission did not.

Evidence streams and eligibility criteria

Three distinct evidence streams were searched, each with its own eligibility criteria, because the review's argument requires different kinds of evidence at different steps.

Stream A, Health outcomes among populations moving in the context of climate change in sub-Saharan Africa

Inclusion: empirical studies (quantitative, qualitative or mixed-methods), systematic or scoping reviews, and institutional evidence syntheses; population comprising internally displaced persons, migrants or otherwise mobile populations in sub-Saharan Africa where the reported driver of movement was wholly or partly environmental or climatic (drought, flood, cyclone, desertification, land degradation, sea-level rise, environmental resource scarcity); reporting at least one health-related outcome (morbidity, mortality, nutritional status, mental health, healthcare access or utilisation, unmet health need); published 2000–July 2026; English or French.

Exclusion: studies of mobility driven exclusively by conflict, persecution, economic opportunity or development-induced resettlement with no environmental component; studies of climate–health associations in non-mobile populations; studies outside sub-Saharan Africa unless reporting on sub-Saharan African populations elsewhere; commentary or editorial material without original data or systematic evidence synthesis; conference abstracts without full text.

Stream B, health information system performance and architecture in sub-Saharan Africa

Inclusion: empirical assessments, systematic or scoping reviews, and institutional assessments of CRVS completeness, routine health information system (RHIS/HMIS) performance and data quality, population-based health survey design and coverage, or health-facility information architecture in sub-Saharan Africa or the WHO African Region; framework documents defining HIS performance constructs; published 2005–July 2026; English or French.

Exclusion: single-facility descriptive audits without transferable findings; purely technical software documentation; studies concerning HIS in other WHO regions unless explicitly comparative with the African Region.

Stream C, theoretical and conceptual scholarship on enumeration, classification and legibility

Inclusion: peer-reviewed works and monographs in sociology of quantification, science and technology studies, or political anthropology addressing the constitutive effects of enumeration and classification on populations, with demonstrable citation impact in the health-policy or global-health literature; and works applying such scholarship to health measurement, humanitarian data or migration data specifically.

Exclusion: works engaging enumeration purely as a statistical or methodological matter without addressing its institutional or constitutive dimension.

Selection

Records retrieved by the searches above were screened on title and abstract by the first author against the criteria in Section 2.2, with uncertain cases resolved by full-text review and discussion among the author team. Full texts were retrieved for all records passing title/abstract screening and assessed against the same criteria. Reference lists of included records were chained backward and forward, and records identified by that route were screened identically.

Institutional data sources and humanitarian situation reports (IDMC, IOM DTM, WHO, World Bank, MSF) are cited throughout as data rather than as records retrieved by the literature searches, and are identified as such at the point of use; they were not subject to the eligibility criteria in Section 2.2.

Handling of theoretical sources

The theoretical apparatus applied in Section 5 is drawn from a purposively selected body of scholarship rather than an exhaustive search, and this warrants explicit justification. Three considerations governed selection. First, canonicity: works were selected that are foundational within their fields and have demonstrable uptake in health-policy and global-health scholarship, so that their application here builds on an established rather than idiosyncratic reading. Second, domain proximity: preference was given to works that themselves analyse health measurement (Bowker and Star's treatment of the International Classification of Diseases; Merry's treatment of health and human-rights indicators), reducing the interpretive distance between the source domain and the application. Third, internal contestation: works were retained that supply arguments against the position advanced here as well as for it, most importantly Scott [10], whose thesis is a critique of legibility and is therefore treated in Section 5.5 as a standing objection rather than as support.

Because these are interpretive rather than empirical sources, their contribution is assessed on argumentative merit and not on evidential weight, and Section 5 is written so that its empirical claims about health information systems stand independently of whether the reader accepts the theoretical framing.

Limitations of the method

The method has three consequential limitations, discussed further in Section 6.3. Screening was performed by a single reviewer, so selection bias cannot be excluded. Restriction to English and French under-represents Lusophone Africa in particular. And the review privileges published and institutionally reported evidence, so national or subnational datasets that exist but are not described in the accessible literature are invisible to it, an irony the analysis acknowledges rather than resolves.

Background: health information systems in sub-Saharan Africa and the measurement of mobility

Because the argument of this review turns on the properties of health information systems in the region, and because many readers of a health-policy journal will not work directly with these systems, this section provides the background necessary to evaluate the claim. Section 3.1 sets out what a health information system is expected to do within the health-systems building-blocks framework; Section 3.2 describes the three data-generating mechanisms on which sub-Saharan African HIS rest and the documented performance of each; Section 3.3 describes the parallel infrastructure that measures mobility; and Section 3.4 states the structural property that the two have in common and that the rest of the review develops.

The health-information-systems building block: what it is for

In the WHO health-systems framework, health information systems are one of six building blocks, alongside service delivery, health workforce, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership and governance [14]. The building block is defined functionally rather than technologically: a well-functioning HIS is one that ensures the production, analysis, dissemination and use of reliable and timely information on health determinants, health-system performance and health status. Three features of that definition matter for what follows. It is a system-level function, concerned with populations rather than with individual clinical records. It is explicitly for use, data that are collected but cannot inform planning do not discharge the function. And it is defined over a denominator population, since health status and system performance are quantities per population, which presupposes that the population in question can be specified.

The WHO operational framework for climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems [5] carries this building block forward into the climate context, requiring that HIS incorporate climate-informed vulnerability and adaptation assessment, integrated risk monitoring and early warning, and climate-informed health programming. The framework is explicit that resilience is a property of the system's capacity to anticipate, not only to absorb, which again presupposes a specified population at risk.

For evaluating whether a given system discharges this function, the most widely used analytic framework is PRISM (Performance of Routine Information System Management), which reframes HIS assessment around performance and its determinants and distinguishes technical, organisational and behavioural determinants of that performance [15]. PRISM's central insight, validated in a subsequent Ugandan evaluation [16] and refined in later integrative review 17., 18., is that HIS failures are rarely reducible to software or training deficits, and are typically reproduced by organisational structures and incentives. The account developed in Section 5 is consistent with PRISM and extends it: what is described there is an organisational determinant operating at the level of inter-institutional mandate rather than within a single ministry.

The three data-generating mechanisms and their documented performance

Health information in sub-Saharan Africa is generated by three mechanisms, each with a distinct architecture, coverage profile and set of limitations. A regional analysis of data sources across the 46 countries of the WHO African Region, drawing on Health Metrics Network self-assessments in 14 countries, established this tripartite structure and documented its uneven performance [19]; subsequent work has refined rather than displaced that picture.

Civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS). CRVS is the reference standard for continuous mortality measurement, registering births and deaths as they occur at a place of usual residence and, where medical certification is in place, assigning a cause of death. Its coverage in the region is severely incomplete. WHO's first global assessment of country health information systems found that in the African Region only about one death in ten is currently recorded [20], against a global figure of roughly six in ten. Country-level analyses illustrate both the depth of the deficit and its heterogeneity: completeness of death registration across African countries ranges from below 10% in some settings to above 90% in a small number of island and upper-middle-income states 21., 22.; recent multi-method estimation for Ghana yields completeness of between 28% and 56%, with little improvement over two decades [23]; and even in South Africa, where registration is comparatively strong, sustained investment was required over two decades to approach adequacy [24]. Where CRVS is incomplete, mortality and cause-of-death estimates are derived instead from surveys, censuses, sample registration and health and demographic surveillance system (HDSS) sites, with the estimation uncertainty that implies.

Facility-based routine health information systems (RHIS/HMIS). Routine reporting aggregates service events recorded at health facilities, outpatient attendances, admissions, immunisations, antenatal visits, notifiable disease cases, and transmits them upward through district and regional tiers. Since the mid-2000s this function has converged on a single platform to a remarkable degree: DHIS2, developed by the Health Information Systems Programme and originating in post-apartheid South Africa, is now used by ministries of health in more than 75 low- and middle-income countries as the government-wide system of record for health data, covering populations totalling approximately 3.2 billion people [25], and is the national HMIS across most of sub-Saharan Africa. Its adoption has produced documented improvements in reporting timeliness and completeness, Uganda's national roll-out being an early and well-characterised case [26], and its use for outbreak response and campaign monitoring is now routine.

Three properties of routine systems nonetheless constrain what they can show. First, they are facility-based: they enumerate service contacts, not populations, so people who do not attend are not counted, and utilisation cannot be distinguished from need without an external denominator. Second, those denominators are typically projected from the most recent census, so any population that moves between censuses is misallocated, a known and quantified source of error in indicator estimation across the region [27]. Third, data quality remains uneven despite platform convergence: reviews across multiple countries document persistent completeness, accuracy and consistency problems, and, more consequentially, limited use of data at the level at which they are collected 28., 29., 30., 31.. A scoping review of routine DHIS2 data use found that data frequently travel upward without being analysed or fed back downward, so that facilities generate information they do not receive back in usable form [32], a pattern consistent with the system-design barriers identified across low- and middle-income settings by Kumar et al. [33].

Population-based household surveys. The third mechanism, the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) programme, Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), and national health and living-standards surveys, supplies the nationally representative estimates that CRVS and routine systems cannot. These surveys are methodologically strong and comparably designed across countries, and they are the source of most internationally reported health indicators for the region. Their limitation for present purposes is structural rather than qualitative: sampling frames are drawn from census enumeration areas and households are sampled at a place of residence, so populations that are mobile at the time of fieldwork, that live outside conventional dwellings, or that have recently arrived are systematically under-covered. Critically, mobility is barely recorded even when mobile people are sampled: DHS identifies non-migrants principally through a “years lived in place of residence” item, and a review of 85 national DHS surveys found a dedicated module with detailed migration information present in only 12 [34]. The Living Standards Measurement Study migration module, which does record place of birth, previous residence, reasons for moving and migration frequency, demonstrates that such instruments are feasible; it is simply not standard in health surveys.

Regional data capacity in aggregate. Read together, these three mechanisms leave the region's health data architecture dependent on modelled estimates for many core quantities. A systematic assessment of data availability in international databases for 80 health-system indicators across the 47 countries of the WHO African Region found substantial gaps in coverage and temporal depth, and consequent reliance on internationally generated estimates [35]. WHO's SCORE assessment similarly found that while data availability is comparatively good for vertically funded programmes, immunisation, tuberculosis, HIV, it is markedly weaker for conditions without dedicated programme surveillance, including mental health, and that only around a third of countries assessed had strong capacity for a standards-based national digital health strategy [20].

The parallel infrastructure: measuring mobility

Alongside these health systems, and institutionally separate from them, three infrastructures dominate the measurement of climate-related mobility in the region. None feeds the health-information-systems building block described above.

The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC) produces annual country-level disaster-displacement estimates, indispensable for establishing scale, Chad alone recorded more than 1.3 million internal disaster displacements in 2024, with over 1.2 million in Nigeria and roughly 750,000 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, largely flood-driven [36], but these are stock-and-flow counts containing no individual-level health information.

The IOM Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM) operates at site and household level across the Sahel and Horn of Africa and is the single largest source of primary displacement data in the region, supplying more than half of the estimates underlying global internal displacement figures [2]. Health appears in its instruments, when at all, as infrastructure, the presence or functionality of a health facility at a site, recorded in Site Assessment and Multi-Sectoral Location Assessment rounds, rather than as the health status of displaced individuals. Neither morbidity, nor mortality, nor unmet care need is systematically enumerated.

National health data systems, as Section 3.2 established, sample by residence and rarely record displacement status even where displaced individuals are captured.

The DTM's own cause-of-displacement data reveal how radically climate attribution varies by context. In central and southern Mozambique, Round 21 mobility tracking attributes 99% of the 139,333 mapped internally displaced persons across 107 locations to climatic conditions over the 2017–2024 period [37], whereas in north-central and north-west Nigeria climate-related disasters account for only 3% of displacement causes, dwarfed by armed banditry (54%) and farmer–herder clashes (33%) [38]. Any synthesis, and any policy built on these numbers, must respect this heterogeneity, to which the analysis returns in Section 5.2.

A partial exception is emerging. The PROGRESS 2025 report, produced by IOM DTM with Georgetown University, analyses 92,549 households across internally displaced, returnee and host-community groups in East, Horn and Southern Africa, including outcomes on access to healthcare and essential services, and explicitly calls for climate-resilient durable solutions [39]. This appears to be the first population-scale dataset in the region in which displacement and healthcare access are jointly observable, an opportunity the research community has yet to exploit.

The shared structural property

The three health mechanisms and the three mobility mechanisms differ in almost every respect, mandate, funding, periodicity, unit of observation, governing institution. They share one property, and it is the pivot of this review's argument: all six enumerate populations by fixed geographical residence, and none records the transition between residences. CRVS registers events at a place of usual residence. Routine reporting attributes facility events to a catchment area. Household surveys sample from residence-based frames. IDMC counts events of leaving. DTM counts persons at arrival sites. In none of these is the pairing of where a person now is with how and why they came to be there and what their health status is recorded as a single observation.

The consequence is arithmetic rather than interpretive. A person displaced by drought is, while mobile, under-covered by every residence-based health instrument; on settling, they enter the health denominator of their destination and become indistinguishable from the resident population, because no instrument asks how they arrived. Their exposure history is erased at precisely the moment their health outcomes become observable. Section 4 shows the empirical footprint this leaves; Section 5 explains why an architecture with this property is stable.

What the evidence shows

Twenty-four records met the Stream A criteria set out in Section 2.2. What follows synthesises them, and the pattern of what they collectively do and do not cover is itself the principal finding.

Review-level evidence and its thinness

The systematic review evidence available to date associates climate-related migration with poorer mental health, food and water insecurity, lack of housing and sanitation, and marginalisation [40]. That review, however, included only twenty studies identified before 2019. A 2025 scoping review focused specifically on climate change and mental health in migrant populations located just eight original publications, all qualitative or perspective pieces, and concluded that research in the field is urgently needed [41]. The World Health Organization's global evidence review identified 95 health-system interventions for displaced and migrant populations in the context of climate change, but found them concentrated in service delivery during emergencies, healthcare provision, mental health support, immunisation, water and sanitation, rather than in structural strengthening of governance, financing, workforce or information systems [42]. That last finding is directly relevant to Section 5.3: the intervention literature mirrors the shape of the measurement infrastructure that generates it.

Drought, nutrition and communicable disease

Evidence from drought contexts is the most developed. Ethiopia's exposure to the 2015–16 El Niño drought produced high malnutrition, internal displacement and epidemics of communicable disease, straining health-system capacity and particularly maternal, newborn and child health services [43]. Humanitarian surveillance in the current Horn of Africa drought reports rising admissions of severely malnourished children in Baidoa and in Ethiopia's Afder and Shebelle zones, alongside diarrhoeal disease linked to reliance on untreated river water, while partner withdrawal due to global funding cuts reduces services precisely as demand rises [4].

Flood-related displacement and disease

Nigeria provides the region's most studied flood context. Reviews of Nigerian flood disasters report increased risk of waterborne disease outbreaks among displaced people, typhoid, cholera, leptospirosis, hepatitis A, through contamination of potable water by overwhelmed sanitation systems, with post-flood psychological distress persisting up to two years 44., 45.. In Borno State, flooding interacts with conflict to disrupt water, sanitation and agricultural systems, compounding waterborne disease and malnutrition in a double burden in which conflict itself impedes climate adaptation [46]. A mixed-methods study of the 2022 floods, which displaced more than 1.4 million people, found markedly gendered impacts: women experienced greater mortality, livelihood loss, trauma-related illness and exposure to poor post-flood sanitary conditions [47].

Migration as adaptation: heterogeneous outcomes.

Migration is often framed as adaptation, but the well-being evidence is heterogeneous. A comparative survey of ex-pastoralists in Marsabit, northern Kenya, found that those who migrated to town at the moment of abandoning pastoralism often experienced deterioration in well-being relative to those already settled, a caution against assuming migration functions as successful adaptation for the most at-risk groups [48]. Complementary work documents drought-compounded psychosocial stress and food and water insecurity among Samburu pastoralist adolescents, including stress-biomarker evidence [49], while an ethnographic study of climate distress among farmers and pastoralists in the Somali Region of Ethiopia and Kilifi County, Kenya links environmental loss, livestock death and forced settlement to forms of sorrow, worry and hopelessness that standard biomedical mental-health constructs capture poorly [50]. At the macro level, analyses of international migration from the region under climate change indicate that migration pressure interacts with underlying deficits, unemployment, weak healthcare, underinvestment, in ways that can constrain sustainable development in origin countries [51].

The shape of what is missing

Across this literature, four absences recur, and they are patterned in a way that Section 5 argues is diagnostic.

By outcome: chronic disease trajectories, maternal health continuity and healthcare access among climate-affected movers are nearly unstudied, while acute malnutrition and epidemic communicable disease are comparatively well covered.

By driver: slow-onset drivers, desertification, land degradation, sea-level rise, are far less examined than floods and drought emergencies.

By population: those experiencing compounded conflict–climate displacement, who tend to suffer the worst food insecurity [1], cannot be distinguished in most datasets.

By design: no identified study links a population-representative health survey to climate-attributed displacement status.

The first three absences share a common feature: each concerns a phenomenon that is either slow, chronic, or definitionally mixed, and therefore not capturable by an instrument that observes populations episodically and classifies movement by single cause. The fourth is the direct arithmetic consequence of the residence-based architecture set out in Section 3.4. These are not random gaps in a young literature; Section 5 argues they are the visible shape of the measurement infrastructure that produced it.

Why the information-systems building block fails: the politics of invisibility

3, 4 establish an empirical claim and an observed pattern. This section develops the explanation, and it is at this point that theoretical scholarship enters the argument. Three considerations should frame its use.

First, the empirical claim of this review does not depend on it. That sub-Saharan African health information systems enumerate by residence, that they under-cover mobile populations, and that displacement instruments do not record health, are documented properties of those systems (Section 3), demonstrable without reference to social theory.

Second, the theory is invoked for a specific and limited job: to explain why an architecture with these properties is stable, why it persists, why it has not been corrected by the accumulation of evidence, and why correcting it is a policy act rather than a technical one. A reader who rejects the framing will still have grounds to accept 3, 4, 6.

Third, each concept is defined before use, its original domain stated, and its transfer to the present domain argued rather than assumed. The relevant scholarship spans three literatures: state legibility 10., 52., the sociology of quantification and commensuration 7., 8., 53., and classification and its consequences 6., 9..

Enumeration constitutes populations

The concept. Legibility, in Scott's [10] formulation, is the property that makes a population or a landscape amenable to administrative action: standardised names, fixed addresses, cadastral maps, censuses. Scott's argument, developed through cases of state-directed agricultural and urban planning, is that modern statecraft proceeds by simplifying social complexity into categories an administration can see and act upon, and that what escapes those categories escapes governance, a claim Scott advances critically, since much of his book documents the harms of such simplification.

Hacking [52], working on the history of statistics rather than on state planning, established a complementary point: in the “avalanche of printed numbers” of 1820–1840, enumeration began generating the categories through which people are apprehended. Counting demands kinds of people to count, and many categories used to describe people originate as by-products of enumeration, from occupational classes to William Farr's nosology of permissible causes of death, direct ancestor of the WHO's international classification. Hacking adds a distinction directly relevant here: the overt aims of statistical control routinely failed, but the subversive effect, durable new categorisations of the person, remained.

Its transfer to this domain. The relevance is not analogical but direct, because the instruments Hacking analyses and the instruments described in Section 3 are the same instruments: national censuses, vital registration, cause-of-death classification. The claim is therefore that the question “how many climate migrants are there, and how healthy are they?” is malformed until some institution builds the category and the instrument that make such people countable, which is a statement about the architecture described in Section 3.4, not a metaphor.

The application. Sub-Saharan Africa's climate-affected movers currently fall between every available instrument. They are not refugees in the sense of the 1951 Convention, so they do not enter the registration systems, with their attendant health screening, that render refugees institutionally visible. They are counted as displaced by IDMC and DTM, but as flows and stocks attributed to hazard events, not as persons with bodies, morbidities and unmet needs. And they dissolve into general population denominators the moment they settle, because household health surveys sample by residence and rarely ask how, or why, a household came to reside where it does, the DHS finding reported in Section 3.2, that a dedicated migration module was present in only 12 of 85 surveys reviewed, is the measurable form of this. Each system is internally rational; jointly they produce a population that exists in mortality tables nowhere and in displacement dashboards only as an integer.

Commensuration and the attribution problem

The concept. Commensuration, in Espeland and Stevens's [7] formulation, is the transformation of qualitatively different things into a common metric. Their central claim is that this transformation is never neutral: it discards information, and the decision about what to discard is a decision, not a discovery. In later work they add that quantification's authority derives partly from its apparent capacity to close controversy [8].

Its transfer to this domain. The attribution problem running through climate mobility data is a commensuration problem in exactly this sense. When the DTM records that 99% of internally displaced persons in central and southern Mozambique were displaced by climatic conditions while climate accounts for only 3% of displacement in north-west Nigeria (Section 3.3), the divergence reflects not only different realities but different acts of classification. A household fleeing land made unworkable by drought, raided by armed groups competing over that same shrinking land, must be filed under one cause.

Why it matters for policy. The choice is consequential. Cause-of-displacement categories feed eligibility regimes, humanitarian targeting, protection mandates and prospective loss-and-damage financing, so the classificatory decision about why a person moved quietly becomes a decision about what they are owed [6].

For health research, the implication is uncomfortable but clarifying: the search for a cleanly identified population of “climate migrants” whose outcomes can be compared with others' may be chasing an artefact of classification. Bowker and Star [6] demonstrated this dynamic for health classification specifically, their analysis of the International Classification of Diseases shows a supposedly technical instrument to be a sediment of negotiated compromises in which every category valorises some point of view and silences another, and cause-of-displacement taxonomies are unlikely to be more innocent than cause-of-death taxonomies. Merry [9] adds that such instruments, once institutionalised, acquire expertise inertia and data inertia: indicators keep being built by the same experts from whatever was measured before, reshaping the phenomena they claim to capture, and working best only when paired with detailed qualitative knowledge of local contexts [53].

The methodologically honest object of study is therefore the joint distribution of environmental exposure, mobility and health, precisely what current data infrastructures, built to produce single-cause counts, cannot yield. This is the analytic basis for the second and third recommendations in Section 6.

Emergency visibility and its costs

There is one moment at which climate-affected movers become intensely visible: crisis. Flash updates, food-insecurity phase classifications and cholera line lists appear when displacement is acute, and dissolve when it is not. Section 3.2's characterisation of routine systems explains the mechanism, emergency data are generated by surge instruments layered on top of routine reporting, not by the routine systems themselves, and they are decommissioned with the emergency.

This episodic legibility has a shaping effect on response. If a population is only countable during emergencies, then interventions will be designed for emergencies, which is exactly the pattern the WHO evidence review documents, with health-system responses concentrated in acute service delivery rather than in governance, financing, workforce or information systems [42]. The empirical correspondence between the shape of the measurement infrastructure (Section 3) and the shape of the intervention literature (Section 4.1) is the strongest available evidence for the explanatory claim advanced here.

Emergency visibility also selects which harms are seen: cholera and severe acute malnutrition are countable in a crisis; interrupted hypertension treatment, lost antenatal continuity and slow-accumulating psychological injury are not, and so they vanish from the record along with the population that suffers them. This is the explanation for the outcome-level and driver-level absences catalogued in Section 4.5. What is measured episodically is governed episodically.

Invisibility is asymmetrically filled.

Finally, statistical silence is never left empty. In the absence of credible data on the health and healthcare use of climate-affected movers, the space is filled by whoever has an interest in filling it: securitised narratives of climate-driven mass migration toward Europe, humanitarian appeals calibrated to fundraising cycles, or the reassuring assumption that movers are absorbed without cost.

Quantification scholars note that numbers discipline debate precisely because they appear to close controversies 8., 53.; the corollary is that populations without numbers are argued over without discipline. Mennicken and Espeland [53] capture the resulting double movement: visibility is at once a product of power and a strategy for rectifying it. Statistical representation has repeatedly served exclusion, but the same literature documents how counted communities have mobilised their numbers to claim recognition and resources. Read in these terms, the proposal advanced here is a counter-quantification in the sense the statactivism literature gives the word: an alternative measurement designed to unsettle what existing metrics render invisible.

The data gap described here is therefore not neutral terrain. It systematically disadvantages the population it conceals, in resource allocation, in health-system planning, and in political contests over what climate mobility is and what is owed to those who move. Seen through this lens, the recommendations that follow are not technical housekeeping. To add a health module to displacement tracking, or a displacement module to a health survey, is to redraw the boundary of who exists for health policy. That is a political act, and an overdue one.

The ambivalence of visibility: objections and safeguards

Four objections to this argument deserve to be met directly.

First: the use of Scott is against the grain. Seeing Like a State is a critique of legibility, a chronicle of the harms done when states simplify societies in order to govern them, and illegibility has historically protected mobile populations from conscription, taxation and persecution [10]. The point is well taken, and it sharpens rather than defeats the argument. Legibility is ambivalent: the same visibility that exposes a population to control is the condition of its inclusion in entitlement. Pastoralists who evade enumeration escape the tax collector and the vaccination campaign alike. The claim here is not that visibility is good, but that the current configuration, visible enough to be counted as a burden, invisible as bearers of health needs, combines the costs of both conditions while securing the benefits of neither.

Second: making displaced people countable creates real risks. Biometric registration, data sharing with security and immigration authorities, and the re-identification of people fleeing violence are documented harms, not hypothetical ones. Any proposal to expand data collection on displaced populations must therefore specify its safeguards. The humanitarian sector has developed a governance architecture for exactly this problem: the Inter-Agency Standing Committee's Operational Guidance on Data Responsibility in Humanitarian Action [54], the ICRC–Brussels Privacy Hub Handbook on Data Protection in Humanitarian Action [55] and OCHA's Data Responsibility Guidelines [56] codify purpose limitation, data minimisation and do-no-harm assessment for crisis data.

Two features of that architecture matter here. The modules proposed in Section 6 would collect health status and healthcare access, not identity, and are designed for aggregate analysis. At the same time, the OCHA framework recognises that even non-personal data can be sensitive in context, a discipline the guidance rightly imposes on proposals of this kind: aggregate information about where a vulnerable group is and what conditions it suffers can itself create risk in conflict settings [56]. Safeguards must therefore extend beyond de-identification to context-specific sensitivity assessment and strict firewalls between health information systems and enforcement bodies. If those conditions cannot be met in a given context, the case for not collecting stands, but that is an argument for governed visibility, not for the present arrangement, in which invisibility to health planners coexists with visibility to border regimes.

Third: the analysis undercuts the category the field is built on. If “climate migrant” is an artefact of commensuration (Section 5.2), why build instruments around it? The objection is correct against a naive reading, and is precisely why no climate-migrant registry is proposed here. What is proposed instead is the measurement of joint distributions, environmental exposure, mobility history with multiple recordable drivers, health status, from which analytic categories can be constructed, contested and revised, rather than a single administrative label hardened into infrastructure [6]. The remedy for a bad category is not no measurement; it is measurement that keeps the category revisable.

Fourth: the account may read as functionalist, as though data infrastructures conspired to conceal a population. No conspiracy is required, and none is claimed. Each system documented in Section 3 is the rational product of its mandate: IDMC was built to advocate on displacement scale, DTM to target humanitarian operations, national health surveys to produce nationally representative estimates, CRVS to establish legal identity and vital events. The gap between them is classic institutional path dependence, nobody's mandate, therefore nobody's data, and is precisely the kind of organisational determinant that the PRISM framework identifies as the dominant source of routine information system failure [15]. But the absence of intent does not make the outcome innocent. Infrastructures distribute visibility, and with it resources, whatever their designers intended 9., 53.. Naming the pattern is the first step to assigning responsibility for it.

Discussion

Three adaptations of the health-information-systems building block

Three feasible adaptations follow from the analysis, each mapping onto the WHO framework for climate-resilient health systems [5], and each implementable within an instrument already operating at national or regional scale rather than requiring new infrastructure. Proposed adaptations, mapped to the failure addressed and responsible institutions.

First, a minimal health module in displacement tracking. A short standardised set of items, self-rated health, chronic conditions, pregnancy, disability, unmet healthcare need in the preceding three months, added to DTM household instruments would transform the evidence base at marginal cost. The PROGRESS exercise demonstrates that health-relevant outcomes can be collected at scale within existing DTM operations, having covered 92,549 households across three population groups [39]; what is missing is health status, not feasibility. This addresses the failure identified in Section 3.3: displacement instruments record health as site infrastructure rather than as individual status.

Second, mobility modules in health surveys. Demographic and Health Surveys and national health surveys should record displacement history and its drivers, with multiple drivers recordable rather than a forced single cause. Section 3.2 establishes both the need, a dedicated migration module in only 12 of 85 DHS surveys reviewed, and the precedent, since the Living Standards Measurement Study migration module already collects place of birth, previous residence, reasons for moving and migration frequency in comparable settings. This would allow, for the first time, population-representative comparison of health outcomes among climate-affected movers, other movers and non-movers: the joint distribution that Section 5.2 identifies as the honest object of study.

Third, a harmonised minimal typology of climate-related mobility, negotiated through existing African Union migration-data initiatives [1], would enable subnational ecological linkage between displacement data and health data even where individual linkage is impossible or unsafe. Given the attribution heterogeneity documented in Section 3.3 and analysed in Section 5.2, such a typology must permit multiple simultaneous drivers by design; a taxonomy that forces single-cause attribution would reproduce the problem it is meant to solve.

Feasibility, cost and immediate opportunities.

Each proposal is deliberately incremental, and their feasibility rests on platform convergence documented in Section 3.2. The routine reporting layer across most of the region now runs on a single interoperable platform, and that platform is already being extended in the required direction: the HISP Centre and network are supporting seven African countries in integrating climate and environmental data into national DHIS2 instances for climate-informed health programming and surveillance [25]. The marginal cost of adding a small number of items to instruments that already reach the relevant populations is low relative to the cost of the parallel data collection that would otherwise be required to answer the same questions.

The research community need not wait for any of this. The PROGRESS household data already permit analysis of healthcare access by displacement status in East, Horn and Southern Africa, and constitute arguably the most underexploited resource in this field.

Limitations

This is a critical review, not a systematic one. Sources were identified through structured but non-exhaustive searches, with single-reviewer screening, no formal quality appraisal and no quantitative synthesis; selection bias at screening cannot be excluded. Language restriction to English and French means that several country literatures, Lusophone Africa in particular, and to a lesser extent Francophone West Africa despite French-language inclusion, are under-represented, which is consequential given that Mozambique supplies one of the two attribution figures on which Section 5.2 turns.

The argument about data infrastructures rests on documented properties of the major international and national systems and cannot exclude the existence of local datasets outside the scope of this review; the review's own reliance on the accessible literature reproduces, at a smaller scale, the visibility asymmetry it describes. Humanitarian sources used to characterise the current Horn of Africa drought are operational rather than peer-reviewed, and the figures they report should be treated as provisional.

Finally, the theoretical claims in Section 5 rest on canonical works whose theses are widely attested but whose application to this empirical domain is novel; they should be weighed on their argumentative merits, and 3, 4, 6 are written to stand independently of them.

Conclusions

The evidence is sufficient to establish that climate-related displacement in sub-Saharan Africa carries a substantial and rising health burden, concentrated in malnutrition, communicable disease, mental ill-health and deprivation of basic needs, and that health-system responses remain reactive because the information-systems building block cannot see the affected population. It is not yet sufficient to quantify that burden, compare it across driver types, or evaluate interventions.

This review has argued that the insufficiency is structural and a matter of health-system design. Health information systems in the region rest on three mechanisms, incomplete civil registration, facility-based routine reporting on census-projected denominators, and residence-sampled household surveys, that enumerate populations by fixed residence and do not record transitions between residences. Mobility data systems, built to different mandates, count those transitions but not health. Each is rational; jointly they render climate-affected movers statistically invisible, so the health-information-systems building block is not climate-resilient, and populations invisible to routine data are excluded from anticipatory, climate-resilient health policy.

Until health is measured where mobility is measured, health systems cannot be resilient for the people climate change moves, and policy for climate migration will be made without evidence about those it most affects. The tools exist, they are already deployed at national scale, and the modifications required are small. What is missing is the decision to make them, and that decision is not technical but political.
