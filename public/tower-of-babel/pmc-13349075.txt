FOXD2-AS1/miR-185-5p/CDC42 axis in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: clinical significance and mechanistic insights
Authors: Jin Xu, Xiaojuan Hu, Zaiyuan Wang, Zhongxia Zhou, Tianxi Chen, Xudong Zhao, Yang Yang, Wei Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong non-coding RNA (lncRNA), a key molecule within gene expression regulatory network, is important in various respiratory diseases. This study assessed the expression changes and regulatory mechanism of lncRNA FOXD2-AS1 in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).Materials and methodsTwo hundred COPD patients were enrolled, comprising 100 with AECOPD and 100 with stable COPD. 16HBE cells were exposed to cigarette smoke extract (CSE) to simulate disease conditions in vitro. Relative mRNA levels were measured via RT-qPCR. Cell function, including cell proliferation, apoptosis and inflammation, was assessed. Target binding genes were predicted using online tools, with their functions annotated via GO and KEGG analysis.ResultsBoth stable COPD patients and AECOPD patients had significantly higher serum FOXD2-AS1 levels than healthy controls, with the highest values in AECOPD. It can differentiate stable COPD from healthy controls and AECOPD, and increased gradually with COPD stage progression. Silencing FOXD2-AS1 afforded protection against apoptotic and inflammatory phenotype induced by CSE in 16HBE cells. FOXD2-AS1 sponges miR-185-5p, which were downregulated in clinical serum samples and cell models. miR-185-5p downregulation reversed the effects of FOXD2-AS1 silencing on cell apoptosis and inflammation. CDC42, a downstream target of miR-185-5p, was upregulated in COPD patients and cell models, and negatively related to serum miR-185-5p levels. CDC42 reversed the role of FOXD2-AS1/ miR-185-5p in CSE-exposed 16HBE cells.ConclusionsSerum FOXD2-AS1 was markedly upregulated in COPD patients, with close correlation to disease severity. The FOXD2-AS1/miR-185-5p/CDC42 axis may contribute to COPD pathogenesis by mediating bronchial epithelial cell apoptosis and inflammation.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a clinically prevalent respiratory disorder. Its core feature is chronic airway inflammation, and it also has clear potential for preventive and therapeutic interventions [1]. For a long time, uncovering the underlying molecular and pathophysiological mechanisms involved in the occurrence and progression of COPD has been a key research focus in the field of respiratory diseases. Currently, there is a general consensus in the academic community that the typical pathological change of airway remodeling in COPD patients is closely associated with exposure to harmful particles or gases such as cigarette smoke and irritant gases [2]. These harmful factors continuously induce repeated inflammatory stimulation in the airways and lung tissues, further leading to the loss of control over the body’s abnormal inflammatory response, and ultimately promoting the process of pathological remodeling of airway structure and function [3]. Available evidence indicates that COPD represents the potential end results of a dynamic accumulation of gene-environment interactions experienced by individuals throughout their lifetimes [4]. In-depth investigation of its pathophysiology facilitates the development of improved therapeutic strategies.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) represent a category of single-stranded non-coding RNA molecules, defined by a length exceeding 200 nucleotides, that primarily partake in diverse biological processes within organisms through the regulation of gene expression. Recent research has demonstrated that lncRNAs not only fulfill critical roles in a range of biological functions but also maintain close links to the onset and progression of numerous diseases, including COPD and airway disorders [4, 5]. Of particular note, they exert significant regulatory effects on the advancement of COPD [5, 6]. Findings from existing studies support the following observations that, lnc-H19 exhibits elevated expression in the lung tissues of COPD model mice, and it contributes to the pathological progression of smoking-related COPD by targeting and modulating the miR-181/PDCD4 signaling axis [7]. LncRNA MIR155HG influences COPD advancement through the modulation of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)-driven M1/M2 macrophage polarization [8]. Furthermore, lncRNA IL6-AS1 may intensify pulmonary inflammatory responses in COPD patients by facilitating signal crosstalk between fibroblasts and macrophages [9].

FOXD2-AS1 is a widely studied lncRNA identified across diverse cancer types, with its core function centered on regulating cancer progression and drug resistance [10, 11]. Despite the lack of direct evidence regarding the role of FOXD2-AS1 in COPD, its mechanism of action in other tissues aligns closely with the core pathological processes of COPD, providing a biological rationale for its potential involvement in the disease. Research indicates that FOXD2-AS1 is highly expressed in lung cancer tissues and exerts oncogenic effects by regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is a core signaling pathway in inflammatory response during COPD [12]. This implied that FOXD2-AS1 may regulate chronic inflammation in COPD through a similar mechanism. The process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in cells can be influenced by FOXD2-AS1, which represents a key mechanism in the airway remodelling that occurs during COPD [13]. In lung cancer, FOXD2-AS1 exhibits anti-apoptotic effects, which are associated with lung tissue damage in COPD [11]. Moreover, Han et al. leveraged the GEO database to construct a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network associated with COPD. Their findings revealed FOXD2-AS1 as a key hub-lncRNA in COPD, highlighting its potential as a core biomarker for the disease [6]. Given FOXD2-AS1’s broad regulatory roles in diseases, its specific functions and underlying mechanisms in COPD have become the focus of our investigation.

Accordingly, the current research aimed to evaluate the expression of lncRNA FOXD2-AS1 in COPD patients, and also explore the its potential regulatory mechanism in bronchial epithelial cell apoptosis and inflammation, with particular emphasis on the role of the miR-185-5p/CDC42 axis. This work is intended to establish a solid experimental groundwork for deeper investigations into the pathogenic mechanisms that drive COPD progression.

Methods

Study population

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software based on a one-way ANOVA model, with an effect size of f = 0.25 (moderate effect), a significance level of α = 0.05, and statistical power of Power = 0.80. Calculations indicated a minimum sample size of 78 subjects per group, totaling approximately 234 subjects. To ensure subgroup balance and account for potential attrition, the study planned to recruit 90 healthy controls, 100 stable COPD patients, and 100 patients with acute exacerbation of COPD, resulting in a total sample size of 290 subjects.

All COPD patients were diagnosed according to the GOLD 2023 Guidelines and Clinical Standards [14]. Specific criteria are as follows: Following inhaled bronchodilator administration, pulmonary function tests confirmed a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second to forced vital capacity ratio (FEV1/FVC) of < 0.70, along with clinical symptoms such as chronic cough, sputum production, and progressive dyspnea, and excluding other diseases causing airflow limitation (e.g., bronchial asthma, pulmonary tuberculosis, interstitial lung disease, etc.). Patients in the stable phase of COPD must meet the diagnostic criteria for COPD and have experienced stable symptoms without acute exacerbations within the past 4 weeks. Patients in the AECOPD must meet the diagnostic criteria for acute exacerbation of COPD, characterized by acute worsening of respiratory symptoms requiring adjustment of the treatment regimen. The healthy control group consists of individuals with normal lung function (FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.70 and FEV1 ≥ 80% of predicted value), no history of chronic respiratory disease, and no respiratory infections within the past month.

Exclusion Criteria: All three groups of subjects excluded individuals with concomitant bronchial asthma, pulmonary tuberculosis, lung cancer, interstitial lung disease, or other pulmonary disorders; excluded those with severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, hepatic or renal insufficiency, malignant tumors, autoimmune diseases, or current use of immunosuppressive agents; excluded pregnant or lactating women, long-term smokers (control group), or subjects unable to complete the study.

Prior to the commencement of the study, all participants had read and signed written informed consent by signing the corresponding form. Additionally, the ethics committee of Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, Medical School of Nantong University reviewed and granted approval for the study protocol.

Sample collection and treatment

For patients with COPD, on the morning of the day after their admission, peripheral venous blood samples in vacuum tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulant were collected in the fasting state. At the same time, for stable COPD patients and the healthy control group, venous blood samples were collected at their outpatient follow-up visits or routine physical examinations under the same fasting standards. All the collected blood samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 15 min for separation of serum components; the serum samples were then transferred to dedicated storage containers and sealed for long-term preservation at -80 °C in a freezer, ready for subsequent experimental use.

Cell model construction

In this study, in vitro experiments were conducted using 16HBE, a human normal bronchial epithelial cell line acquired from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For cell cultivation, RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum was employed, and cells were maintained in a 37 °C constant-temperature incubator with a 5% carbon dioxide atmosphere. The establishment of the COPD model was achieved by treating 16HBE cells with cigarette smoke extract (CSE), following the protocol described in previous research [15]. Medium containing different concentrations of CSE (0%, 0.5%, 1%, 2%, and 4%) was prepared via dilution with PBS.

Cell transfection

To knockdown FOXD2-AS1 in 16HBE cells, a small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting FOXD2-AS1 was utilized. For modulating miR-185-5p expression, a miR-185-5p mimic was used to upregulate its level, while an inhibitor was employed to downregulate it, with each reagent acting respectively. All sequences, including their corresponding negative control (NC), were synthesized and supplied by RIBOBIO (Guangzhou, China). Prior to transfection, 1.0 × 10⁶ cells were seeded into 6-well plates, with 2 ml of complete medium added to each well. Once the cell density reached 80%, the specific sequences were individually transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000. The transfected cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 48 h.

RT-qPCR assay

We extracted total RNA from serum or cell samples and cells via the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit (QIAGEN, Cat No. 217204) or TRIzol reagent respectively, strictly following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. RNA purity, indicated by the A260/A280 ratio, was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 200 ng total RNA from serum and 1 µg of total RNA from cells was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the aid of reverse transcriptase using Maxima First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. K1642). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was conducted using the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. 4304886) to quantify the amplified products. GAPDH served as the endogenous control for mRNA detection, with U6 as the invariant control for miRNA data normalization [16, 17]. According to the geNorm algorithm, the average M values of GAPDH and U6 were 0.31 and 0.33, respectively, both below 0.35, indicating high expression stability. Consistent results were validated by NormFinder, with stability values of 0.36 for GAPDH and 0.41 for U6 (both < 0.5). Cross‑validation using two independent algorithms supported that GAPDH and U6 were stably expressed in serum samples from patients with COPD and suitable for the normalization. Quantitative analysis was conducted using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

CCK-8 assay

Cells that had been transfected were seeded into 96-well plates, with each well containing 1 × 10⁴ cells. At specified time intervals during the culture process, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was dispensed into each well. After a further 2-hour incubation period, the absorbance was detected to evaluate the cell proliferation potential.

Flow cytometer (FCM) assay

The annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit served to detect cell apoptosis. To put it concisely, following transfection, the cells were harvested, underwent high-speed centrifugation on ice, and were then resuspended in 100 µl of FITC-binding buffer. After that, 5 µl of ready-to-use annexin V-FITC and 5 µl of PI were introduced into the said buffer. Next, the cells were kept at 25 °C in darkness for a 30-minute incubation. The fluorescence signals of annexin V-FITC and PI were examined with a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer, and the generated data were analyzed through Kaluza analysis software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Conditioned medium was collected from cell cultures to quantify the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-8 using their respective ELISA kits. After assay completion, optical density (OD) values were read with a microplate reader. The concentrations of each inflammatory cytokine were then calculated by interpolating the OD values against their corresponding standard curves.

Target gene prediction

Potential downstream target genes of miR-185-5p were identified through three databases: miRDB (with a Target Score threshold > 80), TargetScan (applying a Total context + + score cutoff < -0.30), and ENCORI (using screening criteria of CLIP-DATA ≥ 5). To visualize genes overlapping across all three datasets, Venn diagram analyses were performed. Functional annotations of these overlapping genes, including enriched biological terms and signaling pathways, were obtained via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses.

RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

Using the Magna RIP Kit (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA), we conducted RIP assays on 16HBE cell line with specific antibodies and a normal control anti-IgG antibody. For sample preparation, 16HBE cell line was lysed in RIP lysis buffer to generate lysates, which were then incubated with magnetic beads conjugated to either human anti-Ago2 antibodies or the control anti-IgG antibodies. RNAs obtained through precipitation were subsequently subjected to RT-qPCR analysis.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (wt) and mutant (mut) sequences of CDC42/FOXD2-AS1 were cloned into pmirGLO luciferase vectors, respectively. Subsequently, these vectors were co-transfected with either the miR-185-5p mimic or inhibitor into target cells, followed by a 48-hour incubation period. Thereafter, the dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed on the cells, and the luciferase activity of the cells was measured using a GloMax fluorescence reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 software, and GraphPad Prism 9.0 was used for graph generation. Continuous variables are represented by their mean and standard deviation. The differences between the two groups were assessed using the independent samples t-test, while the multiple groups underwent one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni or Dunnett’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. Pearson’s correlation coefficient test was used to conduct the correlation analysis. Perform count and percentage analysis on categorical variables, and compare differences between groups using chi-square tests. A P-value < 0.05 was set as the criterion for determining statistical significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

In this study, the clinical characteristics of 90 healthy controls and 200 patients with COPD were compared and analyzed. Among these subjects, the COPD patients were further divided into the stable COPD group and the AECOPD group (Table 1). A comparison of baseline characteristics indicated that age, gender, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes did not differ significantly among the three groups in statistical terms (P > 0.05). Statistical analysis revealed significant reductions in FEV1, FVC, and predicted FEV1% in both COPD groups (stable and acute exacerbation) versus the healthy control group, with the lowest levels of all indicators observed in the AECOPD group (P < 0.05). What’s more, the results of GOLD staging indicated that in comparison to the stable COPD group, the AECOPD group exhibited a greater proportion of patients classified under GOLD Stage II, III, and IV, while the proportion of patients in Stage I was significantly lower (P < 0.05). Among these AECOPD patients, 58 had active infection within 24 h after admission, 27 received antibiotic therapy, and 31 were treated with systemic glucocorticoids.

Expression and diagnostic significance of lncRNA FOXD2-AS1 in COPD

RT-qPCR results showed that serum FOXD2-AS1 levels in stable COPD and AECOPD patients were significantly higher than those in healthy controls, with the highest level in the AECOPD group (Fig. 1A, P < 0.0001). Figure 1B assessed the efficacy of serum FOXD2-AS1 in distinguishing stable COPD patients from healthy controls, showing 82.0% diagnostic sensitivity, 92.2% specificity, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.911. Further analysis of its value in differentiating AECOPD from stable COPD revealed 89.0% sensitivity, 78.0% specificity, and an AUC of 0.916 (Fig. 1C). After 500 stratified bootstrap resampling for internal validation, serum FOXD2-AS1 showed a bias-corrected AUC of 0.902 (95% CI: 0.877–0.928, P < 0.001) and 0.907 (0.881–0.929, P < 0.001) respectively in differentiating control or AECOPD from stable COPD, indicating stable discriminatory ability without overfitting. Additionally, serum FOXD2-AS1 levels were compared among COPD patients with different GOLD stages. Results indicated a gradual upward trend in serum FOXD2-AS1 levels as COPD stage advanced in both stable and AECOPD group Fig. 1D-E, P < 0.05). We further conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis to investigate indicators associated with acute infections in COPD patients. Due to the high correlation between FEV1, FVC, and FEV1% (|r| > 0.7), we included only FEV1% to avoid multicollinearity issues. The analysis results indicate that FEV1% and FOXD2-AS1 were independent factors of AECOPD (P < 0.05; Table 2). Specifically, FOXD2-AS1 had an OR value of 3.220 (95% CI: 1.332–7.783), indicating its close association with AECOPD. In conclusion, FOXD2-AS1 may play an important role in the acute exacerbation of COPD.

Silencing of FOXD2-AS1 mitigated CSE-induced apoptosis and inflammation in 16HBE cells

To explore FOXD2-AS1’s functional mechanism in COPD, an in vitro smoking exposure model was established by treating 16HBE cells with CSE. Initially, 16HBE cells were exposed to varying CSE concentrations. We first assessed cell viability following CSE treatment. Results indicated that within the 0–2% CSE concentration range, cell viability remained above 60%, demonstrating that cells can maintain essential functions within this concentration interval (Fig. 2A). But FOXD2-AS1 expression rose progressively with higher CSE concentrations, with significant upregulation at 2% and 4% CSE (Fig. 2B). This indicated that the upregulation of FOXD2-AS1 represented an active cellular response to CSE-induced dysfunction, rather than a passive effect resulting from cell death. When CSE concentration reached 4%, cell viability dropped below 50%, accompanied by a modest upregulation in FOXD2-AS1 levels. This suggested that excessive cytotoxicity may have masked the true pathological regulatory effects, thus 2% CSE was selected for subsequent cell function assays. Furthermore, Fig. 2C assessed the transfection efficiency of si-FOXD2-AS1. Results demonstrated si-FOXD2-AS1 transfection notably reversed CSE-induced elevated FOXD2-AS1 expression, confirming satisfactory transfection efficiency for subsequent functional validation. Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays revealed that CSE markedly promoted apoptosis and reduced proliferation in 16HBE cells, while transfection with si-FOXD2-AS1 notably reversed these CSE-induced cellular functional impairments (Fig. 2D-E). Furthermore, FOXD2-AS1 participated in regulating CSE-triggered inflammatory responses. Following si-FOXD2-AS1 transfection, the excessive secretion of CSE-induced inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-8) was significantly attenuated (Fig. 2F). This indicates that FOXD2-AS1 plays a pro-inflammatory role in CSE-mediated cellular inflammation.

FOXD2-AS1 sponges miR-185-5p

A RIP assay was performed to confirm the coexistence of miR-185-5p and lncRNA FOXD2-AS1 in the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) (Fig. 3A). Subsequent to predicting their binding sites (Fig. 3B), a luciferase reporter assay was employed to further validate the interaction between these two nucleic acid molecules (Fig. 3C). The results showed that in cells transfected with WT-FOXD2-AS1, miR-185-5p mimic significantly inhibited luciferase activity, while miR-185-5p inhibitor markedly enhanced this activity. In contrast, the luciferase activity in cells transfected with MUT-FOXD2-AS1 was not affected by changes in miR-185-5p expression levels (Fig. 3C). Clinical data confirmed that miR-185-5p was downregulated in patients with COPD, with its lowest expression observed in those with AECOPD (Fig. 3D). Moreover, a significant negative correlation was identified between serum miR-185-5p and FOXD2-AS1 levels in COPD patients (r = -0.689, P < 0.001; Fig. 3E). Moreover, gradually downregulated miR-185-5p levels were also detected in CSE-exposed cells (Fig. 3F) The results indicated that FOXD2-AS1 and miR-185-5p may have a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory relationship in the pathological process of COPD.

FOXD2-AS1 regulates the function of miR-185-5p by targeting binding

As shown in Fig. 4A, in the CSE-treated cell model, the expression of miR-185-5p was at low expression, silencing FOXD2-AS1 could restore the level of miR-185-5p, while transfection with miR-185-5p inhibitor led to a significant decrease in its level again. Results from CCK-8 and flow cytometry assays indicated that the cytoprotective effect of FOXD2-AS1 silencing on cell proliferation and apoptosis was inverted by miR-185-5p inhibitor (Fig. 4B-C). In addition, miR-185-5p was also involved in regulating cellular inflammatory responses, the aforementioned treatment could re-induce the excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-8) in cells (Fig. 4D), suggesting that miR-185-5p plays a role in inhibiting inflammation.

CDC42 serves as a downstream target of miR-185-5p

Three online databases were utilized to identify the downstream targets of miR-185-5p, and 8 genes were obtained through intersection analysis (Fig. 5A). Among them, CDC42 plays a significant role in COPD and was selected as a candidate gene for subsequent research. RIP assay results showed that the enrichment levels of CDC42 and miR-185-5p in the Ago2 antibody group were significantly higher than those in the IgG group, implying that both bind to the Ago2 protein and have a targeted interaction (Fig. 5B). Figure 5C shows the targeted binding sequence of CDC42 and miR-185-5p. To further verify their targeted relationship, a luciferase reporter assay was conducted in this study. The results indicated that in the cell group transfected with WT-CDC42, miR-185-5p mimic could significantly inhibit the cell luciferase activity, while miR-185-5p inhibitor could significantly enhance this activity (Fig. 5D). In the cell group transfected with MUT-CDC42, the luciferase activity was not affected by the changes in miR-185-5p expression levels. In addition, the detection results of clinical COPD samples showed that CDC42 showed a marked elevation in its expression level (Fig. 5E), and its expression was significantly negatively correlated with the level of miR-185-5p in serum (r = -0.701, P < 0.001; Fig. 5F). Additionally, in cell models, CDC42 levels also increased with rising CSE concentrations (Fig. 5G). The above series of results provided additional confirmation of the direct regulatory interaction between miR-185-5p and CDC42, and also suggested that the two may play an important role in the targeted regulation during the pathological progression of COPD.

CDC42 reverses the role of FOXD2-AS1/ miR-185-5p in CSE-exposed 16HBE cells

In CSE-treated cells, concurrent inhibition of miR-185-5p following FOXD2-AS1 knockdown significantly upregulated CDC42 mRNA expression levels (Fig. 6A), however, this effect was markedly reversed upon further transfection of si-CDC42 (Fig. 6A). Experiments with CCK-8 showed that, the inhibitory effect of miR-185-5p downregulation on cell viability was reversed by silencing CDC42 (Fig. 6B). Similarly, apoptosis assays revealed that the miR-185-5p inhibitor significantly enhanced the cell apoptosis rate (P = 0.0051; Fig. 6C), whereas the apoptosis rate decreased markedly following CDC42 knockdown (P = 0.0320, Fig. 6C). ELISA results indicated that the miR-185-5p inhibitor significantly promoted the release of TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-8 (all P < 0.01); whereas knockdown of CDC42 reversed the pro-inflammatory effect of the miR-185-5p inhibitor, leading to a significant decrease in the levels of these pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6D). These data indicated that CDC42 can reverse the role of FOXD2-AS1/miR-185-5p in CSE-induced apoptosis and inflammation of 16HBE cells.

Discussion

COPD is a frequently occurring respiratory disease marked by persistent airflow limitation resulting from airway and/or alveolar abnormalities. Its pathogenesis involves multiple pathological processes, such as inflammatory response and abnormal cell apoptosis, and there are still significant deficiencies in the exploration of precise diagnostic markers and the development of effective therapeutic targets [18]. LncRNA, as a key molecule in the gene expression regulatory network, is implicated as a key regulator in the initiation and progression various respiratory diseases [19]. As a type of lncRNA, FOXD2-AS1 has been shown in previous studies to have regulatory functions in fields such as tumors and cardiovascular diseases, but its specific role and mechanism in COPD remain unclear. In this study, serum samples from COPD patients and healthy controls were first detected. The results showed that FOXD2-AS1 was highly expressed in the serum of COPD patients, which was consistent with the analysis result of the GEO dataset [6]. FOXD2-AS1 can distinguish not only between stable and healthy individuals but also between COPD patients in acute exacerbation and stable phases. Furthermore, after adjusting for clinical confounders, elevated FOXD2-AS1 levels showed independent association with AECOPD. This suggested that FOXD2-AS1 was associated with AECOPD, demonstrating its potential role in the progression of AECOPD.

From the perspective of the pathological mechanism of COPD, the key active component of cigarette smoke, CSE, is an important trigger [20]. It can induce inflammatory responses and pro-apoptotic reactions in epithelial cells, and is also the initiating factor for inflammation in lung epithelial cells and macrophages [21]. It can trigger the massive release of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby forming a vicious cycle of inflammation. To explore the function of FOXD2-AS1 in the pathological process of COPD, this study established an in vitro COPD cell model using CSE. By interfering with the expression of FOXD2-AS1, the effect on the cell damage induced by CSE was observed. The results showed that interfering with FOXD2-AS1 could inhibit the apoptosis and inflammatory response caused by CSE, suggesting that it might be a “key mediator molecule” for the activation of inflammatory pathways by CSE. Previous studies have shown that FOXD2-AS1 is abnormally expressed in osteoarthritis, Achilles tendon disorders, gastric cancer, bladder cancer and other diseases, and it affects the inflammatory response by regulating related pathways [22–25]. All of these support the conclusion of this study that FOXD2-AS1 plays a key role in the inflammatory response of COPD.

There are significant abnormal miRNA expression profiles in COPD [26]. Previous studies have shown that lncRNAs can regulate gene expression by acting as miRNA molecular sponges, thereby participating in the pathological process of the disease [27]. The results of this study suggest that FOXD2-AS1 and miR-185-5p have a ceRNA regulatory relationship in the pathological development of COPD, and miR-185-5p can reverse the effects of FOXD2-AS1 on apoptosis and inflammatory response in cells treated with CSE. Previous studies have shown that miR-185-5p is expressed at a low level in various inflammatory lung diseases and exerts protective effects by targeting pro-pathological genes. For instance, its expression is downregulated in patients with COPD and chronic obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) [28]. The umbilical cord blood exosomes (UCB-EXO) from healthy full-term pregnancies can promote neonatal pulmonary angiogenesis by upregulating the level of miR-185-5p, thereby preventing high-oxygen-induced lung injury [29]. Moreover, new evidence indicates that miR-185-5p is closely related to chronic inflammatory diseases, such as in RAW264 cells induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), miR-185-5p can exert anti-inflammatory functions [30]. In conclusion, this study presents the following conclusion: Interference with FOXD2-AS1 can affect the cell damage induced by CSE by regulating the expression of miR-185-5p and its downstream targets.

MicroRNA (miRNA) inhibits the post-transcriptional expression of target genes by binding to their 3’UTR. This study verified that the downstream target gene of miR-185-5p is CDC42, suggesting that there may be a targeted regulatory relationship between them in the pathological process of COPD. Clinical tests revealed that CDC42 was significantly upregulated in patients with COPD, which was consistent with previous literature [31]. Moreover, its expression was significantly negatively correlated with serum miR-185-5p, providing clinical evidence for the targeted regulation of these two factors. The core pathological features of COPD include apoptosis of lung epithelial cells, chronic inflammation of the airways and remodeling [32]. The upregulation of CDC42 may be a driving factor for the progression of the disease, which is consistent with the conclusions of numerous studies. As a key activating factor of inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, CDC42 is highly expressed in the peripheral blood and sputum of COPD patients. It can exacerbate pulmonary inflammation and decline in lung function by regulating surface molecules on T cells [33], promoting the activation of M1-type macrophages [30, 34], and regulating the secretion of MCP-1 by airway epithelial cells [35]. The current rescue experiments indicated that CDC42 acted as a key downstream effector and executed the observed cellular phenotypes regulated by the FOXD2‑AS1/miR‑185‑5p axis in CSE-exposed 16HBE cells.

This study has several limitations. Fasting blood samples were collected from AECOPD patients on the second day of admission, while samples from stable COPD patients and healthy controls were obtained during outpatient visits. Differences in sampling time and clinical status may alter RNA expression and introduce systematic bias. In addition, this is a retrospective observational study without prospective follow-up. Incomplete clinical data, such as missing information on treatment duration, may cause residual bias in the AECOPD group. Further large-sample prospective studies are required to validate the clinical value of FOXD2-AS1 for COPD evaluation. In addition, the interaction between CDC42 and FOXD2‑AS1/miR‑185‑5p axis was validated only in vitro. Therefore, incomplete in‑vivo verification of this ceRNA axis represents the main limitation of the present study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, serum FOXD2-AS1 was significantly upregulated in patients with COPD, exhibiting positive association with disease severity. Notably, interfering with FOXD2-AS1 expression effectively suppressed CSE-induced apoptosis and inflammatory responses in bronchial epithelial cells, which involved the participation of miR-185-5p/CDC42 axis. This study provides partial experimental evidence for the involvement of FOXD2-AS1/miR-185-5p/CDC42 axis in COPD. Nevertheless, the completeness, specificity, and physiological relevance of this mechanism require further validation through more systematic in vivo and in vitro experiments in future research.
