Continuous data assimilation applied to the Rayleigh–Bénard problem for compressible fluid flows
Eduard Feireisl, Wladimir Neves

Abstract
We apply a continuous data assimilation method to the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system governing the evolution of a compressible, rotating and thermally driven fluid. A rigorous proof of the tracking property is given in the asymptotic regime of low Mach and high Rossby and Froude numbers. Large data in the framework of weak solutions are considered.

1
Introduction

Continuous Data Assimilation is a framework to incorporate observational data continuously into
a mathematical model represented usually by an evolutionary system of partial differential equations.
The method is often based on adding
nudging
terms determined by interpolated observations to the governing system of equations.
Although the leading idea is conceptually simple and has been described in the seminal paper by Hoke and Anthes

[
16
]
as early as in 1976, the first rigorous mathematical treatment appeared much later in the pioneering work
of Azouani, Olson, and Titi
[
1
]
.

Motivated by the existing theory of determining modes and
finite dimensional attractors for the Navier–Stokes system (Bercovici et al.
[
4
]
, Constantin, Foias and Temam
[
8
]
,
Constantin et al.
[
9
]
), they proposed a nudging term based on interpolants ranging
in a suitable finite dimensional space. Applicability of the method to the real world problems arising e.g. in meteorology is, however,
limited by the lack of the required well–posedness theory for the physically relevant 3-D Navier–Stokes system.
The latter problem has been recently attacked in a series of works of Biswas et al.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
, where
a new regularity criterion for the 3-D Navier–Stokes system is proposed based solely on the observed values of the
interpolation functionals. Note, however, that such a criterion guarantees regularity of the
background solution only in the time interval when the observation
data are collected, but not after the prediction is made or computed.

1.1
Problem formulation

The real world applications arising in meteorology, astrophysics or geophysics are often represented by
the general Navier-Stokes-Fourier system describing the motion of a compressible, viscous and heat conducting fluid confined
to a physical domain and supplemented by the relevant boundary conditions. We consider a rotating fluid system
confined to a bounded domain
\Omega\subset R^{3}
,

\Omega=B(r)\times(0,1),\qquad B(r)=\left\{{x}_{h}=(x_{1},x_{2})\ \Big|\ |x_{h}|<r\right\}.

(1.1)

Here and hereafter, we write
x\in\Omega
in the form
x=({x}_{h},x_{3})
to stress the anisotropy between “horizontal” and “vertical” variables,
{x}_{h}
and
x_{3}
, respectively.
We assume the fluid domain is rotating around its vertical axis.

Let
\varrho=\varrho(t,x)
,
\vartheta=\vartheta(t,x)
, and
{\bf u}={\bf u}(t,x)
denote the mass density, the (absolute) temperature, and the velocity field,
respectively. The classical principles of conservation of mass, linear momentum, and energy written in the rotating frame give rise to the

Navier–Stokes–Fourier
(NSF) system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{x}p(\varrho,\vartheta)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\bf e}_{3}\times\varrho{\bf u}

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\varrho\Big(\nabla_{x}G+\nabla_{x}|{x}_{h}|^{2}\Big),

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta))+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}-p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}.

(1.4)

The symbols
p=p(\varrho,\vartheta)
and
e=e(\varrho,\vartheta)
denote the pressure and the internal energy, respectively, specified constitutively in Section
2
below.
The function
G

represents the gravitational potential acting in the vertical direction,

G=-x_{3}.

(1.5)

The effect of rotation is represented by the Coriolis force
{\bf e}_{3}\times\varrho{\bf u}
, where
{\bf e}_{3}=(0,0,1)
, and the centrifugal
force
\varrho\nabla_{x}|{x}_{h}|^{2}
.
The viscous stress tensor is given by
Newton’s rheological law

\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})=2\mu(\vartheta)\left(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}-\frac{1}{3}{\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}\mathbb{I}\right)+\eta(\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}\mathbb{I},

(1.6)

where
\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}+\nabla_{x}^{t}{\bf u})
is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient
\nabla_{x}{\bf u}
.
Analogously, the heat flux is given by
Fourier’s law

{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)=-\kappa(\vartheta)\nabla_{x}\vartheta.

(1.7)

The system (
1.2
)–(
1.4
) is scaled by a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
, specifically, the fluid flow is nearly
incompressible (the Mach number proportional to
\varepsilon
), stratified (the Froude number
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
), and rotating
(the Rossby number
{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
enforcing the centrifugal force proportional
\varepsilon^{-1}
), see
[
11
]

for the physical background.

We further suppose that the fluid motion is driven by thermal convection. In accordance with the scaling, we impose
the Rayleigh–Bénard type boundary condition

\vartheta|_{\partial\Omega}=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\vartheta_{B},

(1.8)

where
\overline{\vartheta}>0
is a positive constant representing the background temperature. In contrast with
\overline{\vartheta}
, the temperature
profile
\vartheta_{B}=\vartheta_{B}(x)
need not be positive as the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
vanishes in the asymptotic regime. In addition, we impose
the no–slip boundary conditions for the velocity on the lateral boundary

{\bf u}|_{\partial B(r)\times(0,1)}=0,

(1.9)

and, to avoid the friction effect of a boundary layer, the complete slip boundary conditions

{\bf u}\cdot{\bf n}|_{B(r)\times\{x_{3}=0,1\}}=0,\ [\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})\cdot{\bf n}]\times{\bf n}|_{B(r)\times\{x_{3}=0,1\}}=0

(1.10)

on the horizontal parts of the boundary.

The NSF system (
1.2
)–(
1.10
) will be termed
observed system
and its solution
observed solution
.
The time evolution of the observed system is considered on the time interval

t\in(T^{-},T^{+}),\ \mbox{where}\ T^{-}<0,\ T^{+}>0.

(1.11)

The initial state (data) of the system is
a priori
not prescribed, however, we suppose the
solution emanates from the state that is
well prepared
with respect to the adopted scaling. Specifically,
there is a reference constant density
\overline{\varrho}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\varrho(T^{-},\cdot)-\overline{\varrho}}{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim 1,\ \left\|\frac{\varrho(T^{-},\cdot)-\overline{\varrho}}{\varepsilon}-{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq d,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\vartheta(T^{-},\cdot)-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim 1,\ \left\|\frac{\vartheta(T^{-},\cdot)-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{T}_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq d,

\displaystyle\left\|{\bf u}(T^{-},\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega;R^{3})}\lesssim 1,\ \left\|{\bf u}(T^{-},\cdot)-{{\bf u}}_{0,h}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega;R^{3})}\leq d

(1.12)

for certain functions
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
,
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, and
{\bf u}_{0,h}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}

\displaystyle\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega),\ {\mathcal{T}}_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B},

\displaystyle[{{\bf u}}_{0,h},0]\equiv{{\bf u}}_{0,h}

\displaystyle\in W^{2,\infty}(B(r);R^{2}),\ {{\bf u}}_{0,h}|_{\partial B(r)}=0,\ {\rm div}_{h}{{\bf u}}_{0,h}=0,

(1.13)

and

\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\nabla_{x}{\mathcal{R}}_{0}+\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\nabla_{x}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}=\overline{\varrho}\nabla_{x}\left(G+|x_{h}|^{2}\right).

(1.14)

Remark 1.1
.

Here and throughout the paper, the symbol
a\leq b
means there exists a positive constant
C
such that
a\leq Cb
. The subscript
h

appended to a differential operator means the latter acts only in the horizontal variable
x_{h}
.

Identity (
1.14
) is nothing other than the celebrated Boussinesq relation arising from the linearization of
the momentum equation around the reference state
(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})

in the limit regime
\varepsilon\to 0
, see e.g. Zeytounian
[
17
]
.
It is worth–noting that the well–prepared velocity field
{\bf u}_{0,h}
is planar depending solely on the horizontal variable

x_{h}
as a consequence of the fast rotation, see
[
11
]
for a detailed discussion of this property that is somehow
at odds with the commonly accepted scenario advocated in applications (see e.g. Ecke and Shiskhina
[
10
]
).

1.2
Continuous data assimilation problem

The leading idea of Continuous Data Assimilation is that the data are generated by an exact solution of
(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u})
of
the observed NSF system considered on a time interval
(T^{-},T^{+})
. The data are collected and implemented
in a
synchronized system
in a time interval
[0,T)
,

T^{-}<0<T<T^{+}.

The synchronized system then generates a solution
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta},{\widetilde{\bf u}})
in
[0,T^{+})

predicting
the
expected behavior of the observed solution in the time interval
[T,T^{+})
.

The
synchronized
solution
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta},{\widetilde{\bf u}})
satisfies the NSF system of field equations
augmented by nudging terms of “friction” type active on the synchronization time interval
(0,T)
, see Section
2
.
Motivated by the seminal paper by Azouani, Olson and Titi
[
1
]
, we consider the nudging terms in the form

-\Lambda\left(I_{\delta}[{\widetilde{\bf u}}]-I_{\delta}[{\bf u}]\right)\mathds{1}_{t\in(0,T)}

acting as a friction force in the momentum equation (
1.3
), and

-\widetilde{\vartheta}\Lambda\left(I_{\delta}[\widetilde{\vartheta}]-I_{\delta}[\vartheta]\right)\mathds{1}_{t\in(0,T)}

as a heat source in the internal energy balance (
1.4
).
Here
\Lambda>0
denotes the
nudging parameter
, whereas
I_{\delta}
are the interpolants representing the available “measurements”
of the observed solution
{\bf u}
,
\vartheta
. We focus on linear interpolants
that can be represented as
projections
on a suitable finite–dimensional space approaching identity
for
\delta\to 0
.
Generalization
of our results to non–linear interpolants considered e.g. by Carlson, Larios, and Titi
[
6
]
is possible
under adequate structural restrictions.

Our main result, formulated in Section
2
, Theorem
2.4
, asserts the existence of
\Lambda>0
,

\delta>0
, and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
,
d_{0}>0
such that the synchronized solution
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta},{\widetilde{\bf u}})
is arbitrarily close to
the observed solution
(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u})
on the time interval
(T,T^{+})
as long as

0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
,
0<d<d_{0}
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first proof of convergence
of a continuous data assimilation method applied to the physically realistic 3-D compressible NSF system in the Rayleigh–Bénard
convection regime. In particular, the results is stated in the framework of weak solutions, where the desired well–posedness
remains an outstanding open problem.

Our strategy leans on several steps:

•

Using
[
11
]
we identify the asymptotic limit -
the target problem
- of the observed NSF system for
\varepsilon\to 0
in the time interval
(T^{-},T^{+})
.

•

Adopting the standard energy estimates to the target problem, we show the convergence of the continuous data assimilation method for the latter.

•

Modifying the arguments of
[
11
]
we identify the asymptotic limit of the synchronized NSF system in the time interval
(0,T^{+})
. In particular, we show solutions of the synchronized NSF system and the synchronized system
associated to the target problem are close in
[0,T^{+})
.

•

Summing up the previous results, we show proximity of the observed and synchronized solutions in the time interval
(T,T^{+})
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we collect the preliminary results concerning the theory of weak
solutions to the NSF system and state the main result. In Section
3
, using the results of
[
11
]
, we identify the asymptotic limit of solutions
to the observed system. In Section
4
, we apply the data assimilation method proposed by Azouani, Olson, Titi
[
1
]
to the target problem. Finally, in Section
5
, we perform the asymptotic limit for the synchronized NSF system.

2
Preliminaries, main results

We collect the necessary piece of information concerning the theory of weak solutions of the NSF system and state our main result.

2.1
Hypotheses imposed on constitutive relations

A suitable form of constitutive relations was introduced in
[
14
, Chapters 1,2]
and
[
15
, Chapter 1]
.
We refer the reader to
[
14
, Chapter 1]
for their physical background.

The pressure equation of state (EOS) reads

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=p_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+p_{\rm rad}(\vartheta),

where
p_{\rm m}
is the pressure of a general
monoatomic
gas,

p_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{2}{3}\varrho e_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta),

(2.1)

supplemented with the radiation pressure

p_{\rm rad}(\vartheta)=\frac{a}{3}\vartheta^{4},\qquad a>0.

The pressure and the internal energy are interrelated through Gibbs’ law

\vartheta Ds=De+pD\left(\frac{1}{\varrho}\right),

(2.2)

where
s
is the (specific) entropy. In addition, we impose the hypothesis of thermodynamic stability

\frac{\partial p(\varrho,\vartheta)}{\partial\varrho}>0,\ \frac{\partial e(\varrho,\vartheta)}{\partial\vartheta}>0.

(2.3)

The main structural hypotheses concerning EOS are formulated below:

•

Gibbs’ relation together with (
2.1
) yield the specific form of the pressure
p_{m}
,

p_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)

for a certain
P\in C^{1}[0,\infty)
.
Consequently, we get

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)+\frac{a}{3}\vartheta^{4},\quad e(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\varrho}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)+\frac{a}{\varrho}\vartheta^{4},\qquad a>0.

(2.4)

•

Hypothesis of thermodynamic stability (
2.3
)
expressed in terms of
P
yields

P\in C^{1}[0,\infty),\ P(0)=0,\ P^{\prime}(Z)>0\ \mbox{for}\ Z\geq 0,\qquad 0<\frac{\frac{5}{3}P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z}\leq c\ \mbox{ for }\ Z>0,

(2.5)

where the interpretation of the upper bound is the boundedness of the specific heat at constant volume.

•

The specific entropy takes the form

s(\varrho,\vartheta)=s_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+s_{\rm rad}(\varrho,\vartheta),\qquad s_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),\qquad s_{\rm rad}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{4a}{3}\frac{\vartheta^{3}}{\varrho},

(2.6)

where

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(Z)=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\frac{5}{3}P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z^{2}}<0.

(2.7)

•

Finally,
we impose requiring the entropy to vanish
when the absolute temperature approaches zero,

\lim_{Z\to\infty}\mathcal{S}(Z)=0.

(2.8)

In addition, we suppose

P\in C^{1}[0,\infty)\ \mbox{is such that }\ \liminf_{Z\to\infty}\frac{P(Z)}{Z}>0,

(2.9)

see
[
13
, Section 2.1.1]
for details.

Remark 2.1
.

It is worth noting that the above set of hypotheses imposed on

p_{m}
, with the exception of
(
2.8
), is compatible with the standard Boyle-Mariotte EOS

p_{m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\varrho\vartheta
.

As for the transport coefficients, we suppose they are continuously differentiable functions of the
temperature satisfying

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\mu}(1+\vartheta)

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\vartheta),\qquad|\mu^{\prime}(\vartheta)|\leq\overline{\mu},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\underline{\eta}(1+\vartheta)\leq\eta(\vartheta)\leq\overline{\eta}(1+\vartheta),

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\kappa}(1+\vartheta^{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(\vartheta)\leq\overline{\kappa}(1+\vartheta^{\beta}),\quad\mbox{ where }\ \beta>6.

(2.10)

2.2
Weak solutions to the observed system

Having collected all necessary hypotheses, we are ready to introduce the concept of
weak solution
to the observed NSF system.

Definition 2.2

(
Weak solution to the NSF system).

We say that a trio
(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u})
is a
weak solution
of the observed NSF system (
1.2
)–(
1.7
),
accompanied with the boundary conditions (
1.8
)–(
1.10
), and
the initial data

\varrho(T^{-},\cdot)=\varrho_{0},\ \vartheta(T^{-},\cdot)=\vartheta_{0},\ {\bf u}(T^{-},\cdot)={\bf u}_{0}

if the following holds:

•

The solution belongs to the regularity class

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(T^{-},T^{+};L^{\frac{5}{3}}(\Omega)),\ \varrho\geq 0\ \mbox{a.a.~in}\ (T^{-},T^{+})\times\Omega,

\displaystyle{\bf u}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(T^{-},T^{+};W^{1,2}(\Omega;R^{3})),\ {\bf u}|_{\partial B(r)}=0,\ {\bf u}\cdot{\bf n}|_{x_{3}=0,1}=0,

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\beta/2},\ \log(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(T^{-},T^{+};W^{1,2}(\Omega))\ \mbox{for some}\ \beta\geq 2,\ \vartheta>0\ \mbox{a.a.~in}\ (T^{-},T^{+})\times\Omega,

\displaystyle\vartheta

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(T^{-},T^{+};W^{1,2}(\Omega)),\ \vartheta|_{\partial\Omega}=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\vartheta_{B}.

(2.11)

•

The equation of continuity (
1.2
) is satisfied in the sense of distributions,

\displaystyle\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\varrho\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\varphi(T^{-},\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.12)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([T^{-},T^{+})\times\overline{\Omega})
.

•

The momentum equation (
1.3
) is satisfied in the sense of distributions,

\displaystyle\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u}:\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}-{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(\bf{e}_{3}\times\varrho{\bf u})\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\varrho\nabla_{x}G\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}-{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\varrho\nabla_{x}|{\bf x}_{h}|^{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}{\bf u}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}(T^{-},\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.13)

for any
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}([T^{-},T^{+})\times\overline{\Omega};R^{3})
such that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}|_{\partial B(r)}=0
,

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot{\bf n}|_{x_{3}=0,1}=0
.

•

There is a non–negative Radon measure - the entropy production rate -
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}([T^{-},T^{+}]\times\overline{\Omega})
,

\sigma\geq\frac{1}{\vartheta}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}-\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)\cdot\nabla_{x}\vartheta}{\vartheta}\right).

(2.14)

The entropy balance is satisfied in the following sense:

\displaystyle-\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)}{\vartheta}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\int_{\Omega}{\varphi\ {\rm d}\sigma(t,x)}+\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}s(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})\varphi(T^{-},\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.15)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([T^{-},T^{+})\times\Omega)
.

•

The ballistic energy inequality

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\partial_{t}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\varepsilon^{2}\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}+\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta)-\Theta\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi\Theta\ {\rm d}\sigma(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\varepsilon\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}G+{\varepsilon\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}|{x}_{h}|^{2}}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\partial_{t}\Theta+\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta+\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)}{\vartheta}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\psi(T^{-})\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\varrho_{0}|{\bf u}_{0}|^{2}+\varrho_{0}e(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})-\Theta(T^{-},\cdot)\varrho_{0}s(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.16)

holds true for any
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}[T^{-},T^{+})
,
\psi\geq 0
, and
any
smooth extension
\Theta
of the boundary temperature,

\Theta>0\ \mbox{in}\ [T^{-},T^{+}]\times\overline{\Omega},\ \Theta|_{\partial\Omega}=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\vartheta_{B}.

The concept of weak solution specified in Definition
2.2
has been introduced in
[
7
]
,
[
15
, Chapter 12]
, where
global–in–time existence is established
under the hypotheses of Section

2.3
Weak solutions to the (forced) synchronized system

In contrast with the observed NSF system, the synchronized NSF system is considered on the time interval
(0,T^{+})
. The momentum as well as the internal
energy balance are augmented by nudging forces active in the time lap
(0,T)
,
0<T<T^{+}
. Accordingly, the classical formulation of the synchronized NSF system reads:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\varrho}+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{\varrho}{\widetilde{\bf u}})

\displaystyle=0,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\varrho}{\widetilde{\bf u}})+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{\varrho}{\widetilde{\bf u}}\otimes{\widetilde{\bf u}})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{x}p(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\bf e}_{3}\times\widetilde{\varrho}{\widetilde{\bf u}}

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\widetilde{\vartheta},\mathbb{D}_{x}{\widetilde{\bf u}})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\widetilde{\varrho}\Big(\nabla_{x}G+\nabla_{x}|{x}_{h}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle-\Lambda\Big(I_{\delta}[{\widetilde{\bf u}}]-I_{\delta}[{\bf u}]\Big)\mathds{1}_{t\in[0,T]},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\varrho}e(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta}))+{\rm div}_{x}(\widetilde{\varrho}e(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta}){\widetilde{\bf u}})+{\rm div}_{x}{\bf q}(\widetilde{\vartheta},\nabla_{x}\widetilde{\vartheta})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\widetilde{\vartheta},\mathbb{D}_{x}{\widetilde{\bf u}}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\widetilde{\bf u}}-p(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta}){\rm div}_{x}{\widetilde{\bf u}}

\displaystyle-\widetilde{\vartheta}\Lambda\Big(I_{\delta}[\widetilde{\vartheta}]-I_{\delta}[\vartheta]\Big)\mathds{1}_{t\in[0,T]}.

(2.19)

The symbol
I_{\delta}
denotes the interpolation operators forcing the synchronized solution
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{\vartheta},{\widetilde{\bf u}})
to be close to the observed solution

(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u})
. Motivated by Azouani, Olson and Titi
[
1
]
, we suppose

\displaystyle I_{\delta}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)\ \mbox{is an $L^{2}-$orthogonal projection},

\displaystyle\|I_{\delta}(v)-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta\|v\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}.

(2.20)

We remark that, a non–linear variant in the spirit of Carlson, Larios, and Titi
[
6
]
is possible pending some restrictions that will become clear below.
Moreover, we do not distinguished between the scalar form of
I_{\delta}

in (
2.19
)
and its vector valued variant applied component wise to the velocity field
in (
2.18
).

The adequate boundary conditions are

\displaystyle\widetilde{\vartheta}|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\vartheta_{B},

(2.21)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\bf u}}|_{\partial B(r)\times(0,1)}

\displaystyle=0,
