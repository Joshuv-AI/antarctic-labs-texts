Simotang oral liquid alleviates functional dyspepsia-related motility dysfunction through Ca2+-dependent smooth muscle contraction and Nrf2/NF-κB modulation
Authors: Guo Mao, Chunxiang He, Jiaqin Liu, Dahua Wu, Puhua Zeng, Ye Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Functional dyspepsia (FD) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction involving heterogeneous motor, sensory, immune, and oxidative stress-related mechanisms. Simotang oral liquid (SMT), a traditional Chinese medicine formulation used for dyspeptic and gastrointestinal motility-related symptoms, has not been evaluated with an integrated clinical, functional, and molecular framework. This single-arm prospective pre-post study assessed gastric emptying by the 13C-octanoic acid breath test, gastric electrical activity by electrogastrography, postprandial tolerance by nutrient drink testing, symptom scores, serum biomarkers, and gastric antral tissue markers in patients with FD. FD-like mice were used for charcoal meal transit, smooth muscle strip tension, calcium channel blockade, and mucosal RNA sequencing. Public transcriptomic data from GSE169304 were analyzed to characterize FD-associated duodenal mucosal molecular features. SMT treatment was associated with reduced gastric emptying half-time, improved 3-cpm stability, lower gastric dysrhythmia, reduced Nepean Dyspepsia Index and Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index scores, increased motilin and gastrin levels, lower malondialdehyde, higher superoxide dismutase activity, and lower tumor necrosis factor alpha and interleukin-6 levels. Paired gastric antral biopsy analyses showed increased α-smooth muscle actin and higher phosphorylation ratios of myosin light chain kinase and myosin light chain after SMT treatment. In mice, SMT increased intestinal transit, acetylcholine-induced smooth muscle tension, Cav1.2 abundance, calmodulin fluorescence, and p-MLCK/p-MLC signaling, while nifedipine reduced the SMT-associated phosphorylation changes. Transcriptomic analyses showed enrichment of smooth muscle contraction, calcium signaling, oxidative stress response, and Nrf2-related antioxidant pathways, while protein analyses showed reduced canonical NF-κB activation markers. The data are consistent with Ca2+-dependent smooth muscle contraction as the central motor pathway associated with SMT- related functional improvement in FD, with Nrf2/NF-κB modulation as a microenvironmental regulatory component.

1. Introduction

Functional dyspepsia (FD) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction presenting with postprandial fullness, early satiety, epigastric pain, or burning, in the absence of an identifiable structural cause [1,2]. Under the Rome IV framework, FD is divided into postprandial distress syndrome (PDS), epigastric pain syndrome (EPS), and overlapping phenotypes that combine characteristics of both subtypes [2]. FD is linked to reduced quality of life and health-care use, and management is constrained by symptom heterogeneity and variable treatment response [1,3,4].

FD assessment often combines patient-reported symptoms with objective functional endpoints. Gastric emptying measured by the 13C-octanoic acid breath test is relevant for solid-phase gastric emptying assessment, while electrogastrography records gastric electrical rhythm abnormalities associated with FD [5–7]. The Nepean Dyspepsia Index (NDI) and Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI) quantify symptom burden, and nutrient drink testing assesses postprandial tolerance and maximum tolerated volume [8–10]. Gastrointestinal hormones, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress markers, including motilin (MTL), gastrin (GAS), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), provide biochemical information related to motility, inflammation, and redox status.

Duodenal mucosal inflammation, eosinophil and mast-cell enrichment, epithelial barrier changes, and altered epithelial immune responses have been reported in FD [11–13]. Nrf2-and NF-κB-related pathways are linked to oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling in mucosal tissues [14–16]. The gastric antrum is accessible during routine gastroscopy and is directly related to gastric motor assessment. Public human duodenal transcriptomic datasets can provide disease-background information on FD-associated mucosal molecular features, while gastric antral biopsy data provide tissue-level information from the clinical cohort.

Smooth muscle contraction is a motor effector process in gastrointestinal transit. Intracellular Ca2+activates calmodulin (CaM), which activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK). MLCK phosphorylates myosin light chain (MLC), and this phosphorylation pathway is required for gastrointestinal smooth muscle contraction and motility [17,18]. L-type calcium channels contribute to Ca2+ influx and excitation-contraction coupling in gastrointestinal smooth muscle [18].

Simotang oral liquid (SMT) is a traditional formula containing Aucklandia lappa Decne., Citrus aurantium L., Areca catechu L., and Lindera aggregata (Sims) Kosterm. Chemical profiling and network pharmacology studies have identified multiple SMT constituents and gastrointestinal targets [19]. Experimental work has reported SMT-associated improvement of gastric emptying, intestinal propulsion, and motilin-related changes in chronically stressed mice [20]. Evidence integrating paired clinical functional endpoints, gastric antral tissue markers, calcium channel blockade, and transcriptomic analyses remains limited.

The present study evaluated SMT-associated changes in FD using paired clinical assessment, tissue markers, FD-like mouse experiments, public human duodenal transcriptomics, and mouse mucosal RNA sequencing. The primary mechanistic focus was Ca2+-dependent smooth muscle contraction. Nrf2-and NF-κB-related markers were evaluated as inflammatory and oxidative stress-related regulatory pathways.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population and ethics statement

Twenty-five patients with FD were enrolled based on the Rome IV diagnostic criteria [2]. Eligible participants were 18–65 years old, had experienced epigastric pain, postprandial fullness, early satiety, or epigastric burning for no less than 6 months, and showed no evidence of organic gastrointestinal disease on gastroscopy. Patients were excluded if they had peptic ulcer, gastrointestinal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, severe heart, liver, or kidney dysfunction, were pregnant or breastfeeding, or had used prokinetic drugs, proton pump inhibitors, glucocorticoids, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs within 4 weeks before enrollment. All participants received SMT. For analysis, each patient’s pretreatment state was regarded as the Control/baseline condition, whereas the post- treatment state was considered the SMT/post-treatment condition. Thus, the clinical study followed a single-arm before-and-after design, without a separate placebo group or healthy control cohort.

Prior to participation, all subjects signed written informed consent forms. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the hospital’s Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. [2023] 176), and all procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines.

2.2. SMT intervention protocol

SMT oral liquid was provided by Hunan Hansen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (China), a Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant manufacturer. The formulation consisted of Aucklandia lappa Decne., Citrus aurantium L., Areca catechu L., and Lindera aggregata (Sims) Kosterm. The preparation process and quality control conformed to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia and manufacturer release standards. Previous chemical profiling of SMT identified multiple constituents and gastrointestinal disease-related targets [19]. All participants received SMT at 20 mL orally, three times daily, for 1 week.

During the intervention period, participants were instructed to maintain their habitual diet and lifestyle and to avoid any additional medications that could affect gastrointestinal motility, inflammation, or oxidative stress. Study personnel conducted regular follow-up assessments to monitor treatment adherence.

2.3. Clinical symptom assessment

Nepean Dyspepsia Index (NDI): A validated Chinese version of the NDI questionnaire was used to assess dyspepsia symptom burden before and after SMT intervention [8,21]. Participants completed the questionnaire under standardized guidance from trained research staff.

Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI): The GCSI was used to evaluate upper gastrointestinal symptom severity, including postprandial fullness, early satiety, nausea, and bloating-related symptoms [9]. Participants completed the questionnaire under supervision from study personnel.

2.4. Assessment of gastrointestinal motility

13C-Octanoic Acid Breath Test: Gastric emptying was evaluated using the 13C-octanoic acid breath test, a noninvasive approach for measuring solid-phase gastric emptying [5,6,22]. Following overnight fasting, participants consumed a standardized test meal supplemented with 13C-labeled octanoic acid (Euriso-Top, France). Breath samples were obtained at scheduled intervals and examined for 13CO2 enrichment with an infrared isotope analyzer (IRIS®, Wagner Analysen Technik, Germany). Gastric emptying half-time (T1/2) was calculated according to the standard mathematical model.

Nutrient Drink Test: Postprandial tolerance was assessed using a standardized nutrient drink test, which has been used to evaluate maximum tolerated volume and postprandial symptom generation in FD [10]. After overnight fasting, participants ingested a standardized liquid nutrient drink at a constant rate until reaching the maximum tolerated volume or until they were unable to continue due to postprandial fullness or bloating. The maximum tolerated volume was recorded as the total ingested volume. Bloating severity was assessed immediately after test termination using a standardized symptom scale, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The nutrient drink composition, ingestion procedure, stopping criteria, timing of symptom scoring, and symptom scoring scale were kept identical before and after SMT treatment.

Electrogastrography (EGG): Gastric electrical activity was recorded in fasting and postprandial states using a multichannel system (Digitrapper EGG, Medtronic, USA). The analyzed parameters included dominant frequency power at 3 cycles per minute (3 cpm), dominant frequency stability, and gastric dysrhythmia proportion. Signal analysis was performed by technicians blinded to group allocation.

2.5. Clinical sample collection

Among the 25 enrolled patients with FD, paired gastric antral mucosal biopsy specimens were randomly obtained from 6 participants during routine gastroscopic examinations before and after SMT treatment. Baseline characteristics of the total cohort, gastric biopsy subset, and non-biopsy subset are summarized in Table S1 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3605). For each tissue specimen, one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (P6148, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for subsequent histological examination, whereas the remaining portion was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at −80 °C for molecular assays. Peripheral venous blood was obtained from all 25 participants. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and then subjected to biochemical testing and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

2.6. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections with a thickness of 4 μm were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated using a graded ethanol series, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at pH 6.0. Cell samples were processed directly for the following fixation steps. Tissue sections and cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; A8020, Solarbio, China) for 30 min at room temperature to reduce nonspecific staining. The samples were subsequently incubated at 4 °C overnight with the indicated primary antibodies against α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; ab5694, Abcam, UK), CaM (ab45689, Abcam, UK), NF-κB p65 (8242, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and Nrf2 (ab62352, Abcam, UK). After repeated PBS washes the next day, appropriate Alexa Fluor–labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488: A-11008; Alexa Fluor 594: A-11012; Invitrogen, USA) were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were then counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; D1306, Invitrogen, USA). Fluorescence images were captured using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope under consistent imaging parameters, and quantitative image analysis was conducted with ImageJ software.

2.7. Western blot analysis

Total proteins were isolated from tissue or cell samples using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (78440, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Protein levels were measured with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were prepared separately using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0028, Beyotime, China). Briefly, tissues or cells were lysed and centrifuged to collect the corresponding cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions. After transfer, the membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST) supplemented with either 5% nonfat milk or 5% BSA, with BSA used for phosphorylated protein detection, to reduce nonspecific binding.

The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: MLCK (ab76092, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), phospho-MLCK (p-MLCK; ab223656, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), MLC (3672, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:1000), phospho- MLC (p-MLC; 3671, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:1000), Cav1.2 (ab84814, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), NF-κB p65 (8242, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:1000), phospho-p65 (Ser536; 3033, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:1000), inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B alpha (IκBα) (4812, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:1000), phospho-IκBα(2859, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:1000), Nrf2 (ab62352, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) (ab68477, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), NAD(P)H Quinone Dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) (ab80588, Abcam, UK; 1:1000), β-actin (4970, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:5000), and Lamin B1 (ab16048, Abcam, UK; 1:2000). After three washes with TBST for 10 min each, the membranes were exposed to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, including HRP–anti-rabbit IgG (7074, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:5000) or HRP–anti-mouse IgG (7076, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:5000). Immunoreactive bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and recorded with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band density was quantified using ImageJ software.

2.8. Animal ethics statement

All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the Regulations for the Administration of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hunan Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine Hospital (SB2026-0001). Measures were taken throughout the study to limit animal use and alleviate avoidable distress. At the end of the experiment, all animals were euthanized humanely under anesthesia.

2.9. Animals and housing conditions

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The animals were maintained in a controlled facility at 22 ± 2 °C with 50%–60% relative humidity and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Standard laboratory diet and water were provided ad libitum. Before the experiments began, all mice underwent an acclimation period of at least 7 days.

2.10. Establishment of an FD–like animal model

An FD-like mouse model was established using a chronic mild stress-based paradigm adapted from functional gastrointestinal disorder models and previous SMT-related motility work [20,23]. After acclimation, mice were subjected to one mild stress stimulus daily for 14 consecutive days. The stress paradigm consisted of light/dark cycle disruption, cage tilting, and damp bedding. For circadian disturbance, the standard 12 h light/12 h dark cycle was inverted for 24 h. During cage tilting, cages were inclined at 45°for 12 h while food and water remained freely available. For the damp bedding condition, approximately 200 mL of water was uniformly added to the bedding and maintained for 12 h. The stressors were administered in a randomized sequence, with no identical stimulus applied on two successive days. After completion of the modeling procedure, mice were randomly divided into two groups (n =6 per group): the FD model control group, which received intragastric normal saline, and the FD + SMT group, which received intragastric SMT. The SMT dose for mice was selected based on previous SMT-related gastrointestinal studies and body surface area- based dose conversion from the adult clinical regimen [20,24,25]. The adult regimen of 20 mL three times daily corresponds to 60 mL/day, or approximately 1 mL/kg/day for a 60-kg adult. Using standard Km values of 37 for humans and 3 for mice, the mouse-equivalent dose was approximately 12.3 mL/kg/day. Therefore, 10 mL/kg/day was used as a clinically relevant exploratory dose close to the converted equivalent dose. SMT was administered by gavage once daily for 14 consecutive days.

2.11. Charcoal meal transit test

Following the last treatment, mice were deprived of food for 12 h but allowed free access to water. Each mouse was then intragastrically administered 0.2 mL of charcoal meal suspension (C9157, Sigma–Aldrich, USA), which was prepared in normal saline and contained 10% activated charcoal and 5% gum arabic. Thirty minutes after gavage, the mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia. The entire small intestine, extending from the pylorus to the ileocecal junction, was quickly removed without stretching the intestinal tract.

The migration distance of the charcoal front, defined as the length from the pylorus to the leading edge of the charcoal, and the total small intestinal length were measured. The intestinal transit rate was calculated as follows: intestinal transit rate (%) =(charcoal migration distance/total small intestinal length) ×100%. For each animal, measurements were independently completed by two investigators who were blinded to the treatment groups, and both data recording and calculation were performed under blinded conditions.

2.12. Ex vivo smooth muscle strip tension assay

After the final administration, mice were euthanized, and approximately 8–10 cm of distal intestinal tissue was excised and placed in pre-cooled, continuously oxygenated Krebs solution (95% O2/5% CO2). Ileal smooth muscle strips were used to evaluate peripheral intestinal contractile responsiveness in parallel with the charcoal meal transit assay. The mesentery was removed, the lumen was rinsed, and the mucosal layer was stripped away. Smooth muscle strips measuring approximately 2 mm ×10 mm were mounted between a fixed hook and an isometric force transducer in a temperature-controlled organ bath system (ADInstruments, Australia). The strips were equilibrated in Krebs solution at 37 °C under continuous oxygenation for 60 min. The solution was replaced every 15 min. Baseline tension was adjusted to 1.0 g and stabilized. Contractile responses were induced by acetylcholine (ACh; A6625, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) at 10−6 mol/L, and peak tension and area under the curve were recorded. For L-type calcium channel blockade, selected strips were preincubated with nifedipine (N7634, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) at 10 μmol/L for 20 min before ACh stimulation. Each mouse was treated as one biological replicate, and multiple strips from the same mouse were averaged before statistical analysis.

2.13. Measurement of inflammatory cytokines and gastrointestinal hormones

Serum concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α; EK0527, Boster, China), interleukin-6 (IL-6; EK0410, Boster, China), motilin (MTL; CEA702Ge, Cloud-Clone Corp., USA), and gastrin (GAS; CEA991Hu, Cloud-Clone Corp., USA) were determined using commercial ELISA kits. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. Absorbance values were detected with a Bio-Rad microplate reader, and the concentrations of each analyte were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves.

2.14. Assessment of oxidative stress markers

Malondialdehyde (MDA) Measurement: Serum MDA levels were determined using a colorimetric assay based on the thiobarbituric acid reaction (A003-1-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) to reflect the extent of lipid peroxidation.

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Activity Assay: Serum SOD activity was measured using a colorimetric method (A001-3-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) to evaluate systemic antioxidant capacity.

2.15. Public database data acquisition and differential expression analysis

FD-related transcriptome data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, with GSE169304 selected for analysis. This dataset includes mRNA expression profiles of human duodenal mucosal tissues from 37 patients with FD and 18 healthy individuals. The dataset was used only to explore mucosal molecular characteristics associated with FD and was not applied to assess the therapeutic effects of SMT. The processed expression matrix available in the GEO series record was downloaded and analyzed using R software (version 4.2.0). Differentially expressed genes between FD and healthy control samples were identified with the limma package. When necessary, expression data were normalized before model construction, and empirical Bayes moderation was used to improve differential expression estimation. Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with an adjusted p-value (Padj) < 0.05 were selected for volcano plot generation and subsequent functional enrichment analysis.

2.16. Transcriptomic sequencing following SMT intervention

Total RNA was isolated from mouse duodenal mucosal tissues in the control and SMT-treated groups, with three samples included in each group. Tissues were pulverized in liquid nitrogen, and RNA was extracted using Thiocyanate–Phenol–Chloroform Reagent (TRIzol; 15596026, Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Only RNA samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of at least 7.0 were used for sequencing.

For library construction, 1 μg of high-quality total RNA from each sample was used. Libraries were prepared using a commercial mRNA library preparation kit (Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit, Illumina, USA), which involved poly(A) mRNA enrichment, RNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Library concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and fragment size distributions were examined with the Agilent 2100 system.

Qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a paired-end 150 bp sequencing strategy. Raw reads were first processed for quality control with FastQC software to remove adapter contamination and low-quality sequences. The resulting clean reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome GRCm39 using STAR software (v2.7.10a), and gene-level read counts were obtained with featureCounts. RNA-seq quality control and alignment statistics are presented in Table S3 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3605).

Differential gene expression was analyzed in R software (v4.2.0) with the DESeq2 package. Raw read counts were modeled using a negative binomial distribution, followed by library-size normalization and variance estimation. Gene expression profiles were compared between the FD model control group and the FD +SMT group within the DESeq2 framework. Statistical significance was evaluated using the Wald test, and multiple-testing correction was performed with the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For volcano plot visualization, differentially expressed genes were defined as those with Padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|≥1. For subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, a stricter cutoff of Padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|≥2 was adopted.

2.17. Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analyses were performed on identified DEGs. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.0), primarily using the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.0).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed for biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) terms. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was used to identify signaling pathways significantly affected by SMT treatment. Enrichment significance was assessed using the hypergeometric test, and p values were corrected for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Terms or pathways with padj < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed on the entire gene expression matrix. Genes were ranked according to log2 fold change from high to low. Enrichment analyses were conducted using gene sets from curated pathway databases. Enrichment scores were calculated from 1000 permutations, and normalized enrichment score (NES), adjusted p-value, and false discovery rate (FDR) were used to evaluate enrichment significance.

2.18. Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the clinical cohort, comparisons between Control/baseline and SMT/post-treatment conditions were performed using paired t-tests when data followed a normal distribution, whereas Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied for non-normally distributed variables. In animal experiments involving two groups, either unpaired Student’s t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used according to data normality. For experiments with three groups, including nifedipine-related analyses, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Differential expression analysis of the processed GSE169304 dataset was conducted using limma, while mouse RNA-seq data were analyzed with DESeq2. Multiple- testing correction was carried out using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 or adjusted p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism and R software were used for all statistical analyses.

3. Results

3.1. SMT improves gastrointestinal motility in patients with FD

Gastrointestinal motility was assessed in patients with FD using whole-organ functional tests and gastric antral tissue markers.

Gastric emptying function was assessed by the 13C-octanoic acid breath test. T1/2 was reduced after SMT treatment compared with the pretreatment control condition (Fig. 1A). EGG recordings showed increased 3-cpm dominant frequency stability after SMT treatment (Fig. 1B), accompanied by a lower proportion of gastric dysrhythmia (Fig. 1C). IF staining of paired gastric antral biopsy specimens showed higher α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)-positive signals after SMT treatment (Fig. 1D). Western blot analysis showed increased p-MLCK/MLCK and p-MLC/MLC ratios after SMT treatment (Fig. 1E). Baseline comparability between the gastric biopsy subset and non-biopsy subset is shown in Table S1 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3605).

These paired data show SMT-associated changes in gastric emptying, gastric electrical rhythm, and smooth muscle-related molecular markers in patients with FD.

3.2. SMT reduces symptom burden in FD and is accompanied by improvements in gastrointestinal function and hormonal profiles

Symptom severity, postprandial tolerance, bowel movement frequency, and gastrointestinal hormone levels were assessed before and after SMT treatment. NDI and GCSI scores were reduced after SMT treatment (Fig. 2A). Postprandial tolerance was assessed using the nutrient drink test. SMT treatment was associated with increased maximum tolerated volume and lower bloating score (Fig. 2B). Mean daily bowel movement frequency increased after SMT treatment (Fig. 2C). Serum motilin (MTL) and gastrin (GAS) levels were higher after SMT treatment (Fig. 2D). Paired changes in clinical, motility-related, hormonal, inflammatory, and oxidative stress-related parameters are summarized in Table S2 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3605).

These paired pre-post data show concurrent changes in symptom scores, motility-related parameters, and gastrointestinal hormone profiles after SMT treatment. These findings were interpreted as concurrent paired changes rather than formal correlation evidence. The exploratory single-arm cohort was not powered for correlation analysis between patient-reported outcomes and individual objective biomarkers.

3.3. SMT enhances smooth muscle contraction and propulsive function via Ca2+-dependent excitation–contraction coupling

FD-like mouse experiments were used to assess whole-intestine transit, ex vivo smooth muscle contractility, CaM fluorescence, L-type calcium channel-related markers, and downstream phosphorylation of MLCK and MLC.

The charcoal marker traveled a greater distance in the SMT-treated group than in the FD model control group (Fig. 3A). In the ex vivo smooth muscle strip tension assay, SMT pretreatment increased ACh-induced peak tension compared with the FD model control group (Fig. 3B). IF analysis of smooth muscle cells showed higher CaM fluorescence intensity after SMT treatment (Fig. 3C). Western blot analysis showed increased Cav1.2 abundance and increased p-MLCK/MLCK and p-MLC/MLC ratios in the SMT-treated group. Nifedipine reduced the SMT-associated phosphorylation changes (Fig. 3D).

These data were consistent with L-type calcium channel-related Ca2+signaling and CaM-MLCK-MLC phosphorylation as the main smooth muscle contraction pathway evaluated in the FD-like mouse model.

3.4. Public transcriptomic analysis identifies Immune–Inflammatory features and reduced Nrf2 pathway enrichment in FD duodenal mucosa

The public GSE169304 dataset was analyzed to characterize FD-associated human duodenal mucosal molecular features. The dataset comprised duodenal mucosal samples from 37 patients with FD and 18 healthy controls. Expression distribution analysis showed comparable normalized expression profiles across samples, without apparent outlier samples or major global shifts between groups (Fig. 4A).

Differential expression analysis was performed using limma based on the processed GSE169304 expression table. A total of 486 DEGs were identified between FD and healthy control samples under the threshold Padj <0.05, including 259 upregulated and 227 downregulated genes (Fig. 4B and C).

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed enrichment of immune response, immunoglobulin- related, oxidoreductase-related, oxidative phosphorylation, mineral absorption, and ferroptosis-related terms in FD duodenal mucosa (Fig. 4D).

KEGG enrichment included oxidative phosphorylation and ferroptosis-related pathways, consistent with metabolic stress and lipid peroxidation-related signatures in the public dataset (Fig. 4D).

GSEA showed negative enrichment of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in FD compared with healthy controls (NES =−1.737, Padj =0.023, FDR =0.001; Fig. 4E).

The public transcriptomic analysis was used as disease-background information for FD-associated mucosal inflammation and oxidative stress-related features.

3.5. SMT intervention is associated with activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and amelioration of oxidative stress

Serum MDA and SOD activity were measured before and after SMT treatment. SMT treatment was associated with lower serum MDA and higher SOD activity (Fig. 5A). IF analysis showed increased nuclear Nrf2 fluorescence after SMT treatment (Fig. 5B). Quantitative analysis showed a higher nuclear-to-cytoplasmic Nrf2 fluorescence ratio after SMT treatment (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis showed higher Nrf2, HO-1, and NQO1 protein levels after SMT treatment (Fig. 5D and E). These data show SMT-associated changes in serum oxidative stress markers and Nrf2-related antioxidant proteins in paired clinical samples (Fig. 5F).

3.6. SMT intervention is associated with suppression of NF-κB signaling and reduction of inflammatory levels

Public transcriptomic analysis identified immune- and oxidative stress-related mucosal features in FD. NF-κB pathway activity after SMT intervention was evaluated using serum cytokines, p65 localization, p65 phosphorylation, and IκBαphosphorylation in paired clinical samples.

Serum TNF-αand IL-6 levels were lower after SMT treatment (Fig. 6A). IF analysis showed lower nuclear p65 fluorescence after SMT treatment (Fig. 6B). Western blot analysis showed lower cytoplasmic p-p65 (Ser536), lower nuclear p65 abundance, and lower p-IκBα/IκBαratio after SMT treatment (Fig. 6C).

These results show reduced canonical NF-κB activation markers after SMT treatment in the paired clinical samples.

3.7. Mouse RNA-seq identifies oxidative stress- and smooth muscle function-related transcriptional programs after SMT treatment

Mouse duodenal mucosal RNA-seq was performed in FD model control and FD +SMT groups. The workflow included sample collection, RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing, read processing, differential expression analysis, and functional annotation (Fig. 7A).

Boxplot analysis showed comparable normalized signal distributions across FD model control and SMT-treated samples (Fig. 7B).

PCA showed separation between FD model control and SMT-treated samples along PC1 (Fig. 7C).

Differential expression results were displayed in a volcano plot using Padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|≥1 as the cutoff criteria. Based on this threshold, 1109 DEGs were detected in FD +SMT mice relative to FD model controls, comprising 846 upregulated and 263 downregulated genes (Fig. 7D; Table S3 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3605). For downstream GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, a more stringent threshold of Padj <0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥2 was applied, yielding 231 DEGs, including 195 upregulated and 36 downregulated genes (Table S3 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3605). GO enrichment analysis based on upregulated genes showed enrichment of “muscle system process,” “muscle contraction,” “response to oxidative stress,” “CaM binding,” “antioxidant activity,” and “Ca2+transmembrane transporter activity” (Fig. 7E).

KEGG enrichment analysis showed enrichment of pathways related to calcium signaling and smooth muscle function, including “Calcium signaling pathway,” “Oxytocin signaling pathway,” and “Vascular smooth muscle contraction” (Fig. 7F).

3.8. SMT is associated with Nrf2-mediated antioxidant signaling and calcium signaling-related transcriptional programs

RNA-seq analysis recorded RELA transcript abundance in the SMT-treated and FD model control groups (Fig. 8A). RELA mRNA abundance was not used as an indicator of NF-κB activation; NF-κB activation status was evaluated using p65 phosphorylation, nuclear p65 abundance, and IκBαphosphorylation in Fig. 6. Compared with the FD model control group, the SMT-treated group exhibited increased expression of NFE2L2 and CACNA1C (Fig. 8B and C). The expression profiles of RELA, NFE2L2, and CACNA1C across RNA-seq samples were further displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 8D).

GSEA showed positive enrichment of the WikiPathways Nrf2 pathway in the SMT-treated group (Fig. 8E; NES =3.408, Padj <0.001, FDR <0.001). The Reactome KEAP1-NFE2L2 pathway also showed positive enrichment in the SMT-treated group (Fig. 8F; NES =3.067, Padj <0.001, FDR <0.001). The “Calcium regulation in cardiac cells” gene set showed positive enrichment in the SMT-treated group (Fig. 8G; NES =2.443, Padj <0.001, FDR <0.001). This gene set was interpreted as a calcium-regulation signal rather than a cardiac-specific conclusion.

These transcriptomic data align with the protein and functional data showing Nrf2-related antioxidant changes and calcium signaling-related smooth muscle contraction changes after SMT treatment.

4. Discussion

This single-arm pre-post study evaluated SMT-associated changes in clinical symptoms, gastric emptying, gastric electrical rhythm, postprandial tolerance, serum biomarkers, gastric antral tissue markers, and FD-like mouse functional assays. SMT treatment was associated with lower NDI and GCSI scores, shorter gastric emptying T1/2, higher 3-cpm stability, lower gastric dysrhythmia, higher maximum tolerated volume, higher bowel movement frequency, higher MTL and GAS levels, lower MDA and inflammatory cytokine levels, and higher SOD activity. Animal experiments showed higher charcoal transit distance and higher ACh-induced smooth muscle peak tension after SMT treatment. These paired clinical and animal functional data were consistent with SMT-associated improvement in motility-related endpoints. Symptom changes were interpreted alongside paired objective endpoint changes rather than as formal biomarker–symptom correlation evidence. The absence of a placebo control limited causal interpretation of the clinical findings, given the placebo response reported in FD trials [26].

Previous SMT-related work reported improved gastric emptying, intestinal propulsion, and motilin- related changes in chronically stressed mice after SMT administration [20]. Chemical profiling and network pharmacology studies identified multiple SMT constituents and gastrointestinal disease- related targets, and SMT has also been studied in gastrointestinal injury and microbiota-related contexts [19,24]. The present study added paired clinical motility measures, gastric antral tissue markers, ex vivo smooth muscle contraction, nifedipine- related pathway interrogation, and mouse mucosal RNA-seq. The data were consistent with a motility-centered interpretation rather than a single-marker symptom response.

The most direct mechanistic data in this study concerned Ca2+-dependent smooth muscle contraction. Paired gastric antral biopsy samples showed higher α-SMA signal and higher p-MLCK/MLCK and p-MLC/MLC ratios after SMT treatment. FD-like mouse experiments showed higher CaM fluorescence, higher Cav1.2 abundance, and higher p-MLCK/MLCK and p-MLC/MLC ratios in the SMT-treated group. Nifedipine reduced the SMT-associated phosphorylation changes. MLCK- mediated MLC phosphorylation is required for smooth muscle contraction and gastrointestinal motility, and calcium sensitization participates in gastrointestinal smooth muscle function [17,18]. This pattern was aligned with Ca2+-CaM-MLCK-MLC signaling as the central motor pathway evaluated here.

Nrf2-and NF-κB-related results were interpreted as regulatory markers of the inflammatory and oxidative stress microenvironment. SMT treatment was associated with lower serum TNF-αand IL-6, lower nuclear p65 localization, lower p-p65, and lower p-IκBα/IκBαratio. SMT treatment was also associated with lower MDA, higher SOD activity, increased nuclear Nrf2 fluorescence, and higher Nrf2, HO-1, and NQO1 protein levels. Nrf2 signaling is linked to antioxidant defense, NF-κB signaling is linked to inflammatory activation, and crosstalk between the two pathways has been described in oxidative stress-related settings [14–16]. The present results did not separate direct SMT effects on Ca2+signaling from indirect effects related to changes in inflammatory or redox status.

Transcriptomic analyses provided pathway-level context. The public GSE169304 dataset showed immune-related enrichment and negative enrichment of the Nrf2 pathway in human FD duodenal mucosa. Published work has linked FD with duodenal low-grade inflammation, epithelial barrier changes, eosinophils, mast cells, and altered duodenal microenvironmental features [11–13]. Mouse RNA-seq after SMT treatment identified enrichment of muscle contraction, CaM binding, antioxidant activity, calcium signaling, Nrf2-related pathways, and calcium-regulation gene sets. The public human duodenal dataset, paired human gastric antral biopsy data, and mouse duodenal RNA-seq data were anatomically and biologically distinct. Their overlap was interpreted at the pathway level and not as direct human intervention transcriptomic evidence.

Autonomic regulation is relevant to FD-related motility. Vagal efferent activity contributes to gastric accommodation, gastric emptying, and gastric slow-wave regulation, and transcutaneous auricular vagal nerve stimulation has been associated with improved FD symptoms and gastric function in clinical studies [27,28]. The present study did not measure vagal tone, heart-rate variability, cholinergic neural activity, or autonomic reflexes. Autonomic regulation remains an upstream mechanism requiring direct testing in SMT- related FD models. The clinical implications of these data remain exploratory. The data place Ca2+-dependent smooth muscle contraction and Nrf2/NF-κB-related inflammatory and oxidative stress markers as candidate pathways associated with SMT treatment.

The clinical component was not designed to establish efficacy against placebo, and the molecular data did not define the active SMT constituents responsible for the observed changes. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as formulation-level effects of SMT rather than compound-specific mechanisms. The results provide a clinical and preclinical framework for subsequent controlled studies rather than a basis for definitive clinical implementation.

This study has limitations. The clinical component lacked placebo control, randomization, and long-term follow-up, and the intervention lasted 1 week. Gastric antral biopsy samples were obtained from six paired participants, and healthy control biopsy samples were not included. FD subtype analyses by PDS, EPS, and overlap presentation were not prespecified. Liver and kidney function tests were not included as safety endpoints. Animal experiments used one clinically relevant exploratory SMT dose, and dose–response relationships were not evaluated. Batch-specific LC-MS/MS quantification of SMT constituents was not performed. The study did not distinguish direct from indirect effects of SMT on Ca2+, Nrf2, and NF-κB- related markers. Cross-species and cross-tissue extrapolation remained limited.

Future work will require randomized placebo-controlled clinical designs, longer follow-up, prespecified FD subtype analyses, and standardized safety monitoring. Additional experiments should evaluate SMT dose–response relationships, batch- specific chemical characterization, calcium influx or channel activity, autonomic function, and pathway perturbation of Nrf2 and NF-κB. Human mucosal transcriptomic profiling before and after SMT treatment would help define the degree of pathway overlap between clinical samples and the current mouse RNA-seq findings.

5. Conclusion

SMT treatment was associated with improved symptom scores, gastric motility-related parameters, serum inflammatory and oxidative stress markers, and smooth muscle contraction-related molecular changes in this single-arm pre-post FD cohort and FD-like mouse model. The data were consistent with Ca2+-dependent smooth muscle contraction as the main motor pathway evaluated in this study, with Nrf2/NF-κB modulation as a regulatory component related to inflammatory and oxidative stress markers. Controlled clinical studies and targeted mechanistic experiments are required to define efficacy, active constituents, and pathway specificity.
