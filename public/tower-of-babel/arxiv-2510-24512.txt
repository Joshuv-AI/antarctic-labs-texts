Quality Coefficients for Interferometric Phase Linking
Magnus Heimpel *, Irena Hajnsek, Othmar Frey

Abstract
In multi-temporal InSAR, phase linking refers to the estimation of a single-reference interferometric phase history from the information contained in the coherence matrix of a distributed scatterer. Since the phase information in the coherence matrix is typically inconsistent, the extent to which the estimated phase history captures it must be assessed to exclude unreliable pixels from further processing. We introduce three quality criteria in the form of coefficients, for threshold-based pixel selection: a coefficient based on closure phase that quantifies the internal consistency of the phase information in the coherence matrix; a goodness-of-fit coefficient that quantifies how well a resulting phase history estimate approximates the phase information according to the characteristic optimization model of a given phase linking method; and an ambiguity coefficient that compares the goodness of fit of the original estimate with that of an orthogonal alternative. We formulate the phase linking methods and these criteria within a unified mathematical framework and discuss computational and algorithmic aspects. Unlike existing goodness-of-fit indicators, the proposed coefficients are normalized to the unit interval with explicit noise-floor correction, improving interpretability across stacks of different size. Experiments on TerraSAR-X data over Visp, Switzerland, indicate that the closure phase coefficient effectively pre-screens stable areas, the goodness-of-fit coefficient aligns with and systematically generalizes established quality indicators, and the ambiguity coefficient flags solutions that fit well but are unstable. Together, the coefficients enable systematic pixel selection and quality control in the interferometric processing of distributed scatterers. Keywords: InSAR; Phase Linking; Distributed Scatterers; Interferometric Phase Quality

Introduction

Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) allows the retrieval of surface motion based on measured phase differences between repeated SAR acquisitions. Its relevance lies in its unique ability to provide spatially dense measurements over large areas, with the potential to achieve up to millimeter precision in the line-of-sight direction under favorable conditions. The precision of the phase measurements depends strongly on the interferometric coherence between acquisitions. Decorrelation of the radar targets introduces uncertainty in the interferometric phase, which propagates to the estimated surface deformation. Point-like scatterers with high phase stability that dominate the backscatter within a resolution cell, also known as persistent scatterers (PS), serve as suitable pixels to retrieve surface motion
[

[

[
8
]
, which represent the combined return of multiple similar scatterers that are much smaller than a resolution cell, may be necessary to achieve sufficient coverage across the scene.

DS targets tend to be more prone to decorrelation, and their interferometric phases are generally inconsistent across different acquisition pairs. Therefore, strategies are needed to extract usable phase information from them. A common approach is to use phase linking methods, which estimate a single-reference interferometric phase history from the phases of all possible acquisition pairs. Over the past two decades, a variety of such techniques have been developed. In essence, they define an objective function that quantifies the loss of information between the set of all phases of interferometric pairs and a candidate phase history and solve the resulting optimization problem.

Among the most established methods are the Small Baseline Subset (SBAS) method

These methods face a common challenge: when applied with relaxed constraints, the resulting phase estimates may appear smooth yet be physically meaningless in strongly decorrelated areas. Consequently, robust criteria are needed to evaluate the reliability of phase linking outputs and to determine whether a given DS pixel should be included in subsequent processing. Previous works have introduced different quality indicators to address this issue. Ferretti et al.
[

However, these criteria are typically tied to specific methods and no unifying framework has been proposed that can generalize across the different classes of phase linking algorithms. Moreover, the scale of these indicators is not normalized and typically depends on the number of acquisitions, which complicates thresholding and comparison across datasets.

In this work, we develop such a framework that encompasses the most commonly used phase linking methods. Based on this formulation, we define several scalar quality coefficients that provide interpretable measures of reliability for DS phase estimates. Our specific contributions are:

•

A method-specific

•

A method-specific

In

The dataset exhibits typical characteristics of high-resolution InSAR time series in challenging terrain, such as temporal decorrelation and strong spatial variation in the atmospheric phase component. It serves as a representative test case for validating the plausibility and interpretability of the proposed coefficients.

4.

In Appendix

We propose a nonlinear conjugate gradient solver that is robust and applicable to all phase triangulation methods considered here, requiring no additional assumptions.

Theoretical Basis

2.1
Distributed Scatterers and the Coherence Matrix

Phase linking methods estimate single-reference interferometric phase histories based on the coherence matrix of a DS according to criteria defined by the specific method. We refer to a radar target as a distributed scatterer if it has the following properties (cf.
[
8
]
):

1.

the target spreads across a significant number of adjacent pixels with the same scattering mechanism;

2.

the backscatter of a pixel in the SLC image is the sum of the backscatter of a large number of so-called elementary scatterers that are much smaller than a resolution cell;

3.

the radar responses of the elementary scatterers are similar but independent;

4.

the phases of the elementary scatterers are uniformly distributed and independent of the amplitudes

1

A more specific condition in place of items 3 and 4 that is sometimes used states that the elementary scatterers’ responses are independent and identically zero-mean complex circular Gaussian distributed.
;

5.

the backscatter is not dominated by any small subset of elementary scatterers.

2.2
Phase Linking

In this work, we define phase linking as the estimation of a single-reference interferometric phase history from the information contained in the coherence matrix. In general, the coherence matrix
{\bf C}
contains
N(N-1)/\penalty 2
pairwise phase measurements, which are often inconsistent in the sense that closure phases exist which are not integer multiples of
2\pi
. As a result, direct and unambiguous recovery of the phase history as in
Equation

2.7
is typically not feasible without further processing.

Various strategies have been proposed to solve this problem, including SBAS
[
9
]
, PTA
[
10
]
, SqueeSAR
[
11
]
, CAESAR
[
14
]
, EMI
[
13
]
and LaMIE
[
27
]

[
15

28
]
have been developed as meta-strategies: a core phase linking method is applied to multiple subsets of the data, and the resulting estimates are subsequently combined into a global solution. Cao et al.
[
12
]
introduced a framework in which different phase linking methods correspond to different choices of weighting schemes in the optimization process. In what follows, we extend this framework and classify the methods based on their weighting schemes and optimization objectives.

Let
{\bf W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a symmetric weight matrix which encodes prior knowledge or assumptions about the reliability of specific interferometric phases, e.g., through coherence magnitude. If all entries of
{\bf W}
are non-negative, we additionally define the element-wise square root
\sqrt{\bf W}
and a
\sqrt{\bf W}
-weighted Frobenius norm

6

6

{\bf W}
are positive. Otherwise, it is a seminorm.

\prescript{\sqrt{\bf W}}{}{\lVert\,.\,\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}:\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0},\quad A\mapsto\left\lVert\sqrt{\bf W}\odot A\right\rVert_{\mathsf{F}},

(2.8)

where

denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product. For an acquisition index

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{N}|_{p}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid\angle v_{p}=0\right\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}|_{p}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\theta_{p}=0\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid\lVert v\rVert_{2}=1\;\wedge\;\angle v_{p}=0\right\}

\displaystyle\Pi^{N}|_{p}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{\theta}\in(-\pi,\pi]^{N}\mid\theta_{p}=0\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid\forall\,i:\lvert v_{i}\rvert=1\;\wedge\;\angle v_{p}=0\right\}.

2.2.1
Eigendecomposition Methods

We define the class of eigendecomposition (ED) phase linking methods as those methods that estimate the single-reference phase history with respect to the
p

\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\angle\left\{\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\left({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right)\bm{v}\right\}\right\}.

(2.10)

In other words, the resulting estimate is the element-wise phase of the eigenvector
\bm{u}
of
{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}
associated with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}

\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\angle u_{p}=0

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
is one (otherwise

(2.11)

\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\angle\left\{\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}|_{p}}\left\lVert{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}-\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\right\}.

2.2.2
Phase Triangulation Methods

We define the class of phase triangulation (PT) phase linking methods as those methods that estimate the single-reference phase history with respect to the
p
th acquisition as

\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\angle\left\{\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\left({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right)\bm{v}\right\}\right\}.

(2.13)

In contrast to ED methods, PT methods restrict the optimization to
\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}

{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}
and different optimization algorithms are required. In

(2.14)

\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\Lambda(\bm{\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}\,({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi})\,\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right\}{\mathrel{\to}^{\text{ \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{realvalpt} }}}\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)\right\}.

(2.15)

{\bf W}

Weights

{\bf W}

•

{\bf W}^{\mathrm{ML}}\coloneqq-{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\odot{\bf\Gamma}

\mathrm{M}_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\Gamma_{ij}\geq\tau\\

The primary objective and contribution of this work is to develop scalar indicators, referred to as quality coefficients, that assess the consistency of phase information within the coherence matrix, evaluate the performance of the various phase linking algorithms introduced in

2

[0,1]
and designed to be independent of the number of acquisitions

A coefficient of 1 should correspond to perfect consistency, meaning the phase linking estimates align precisely with the observed interferometric phases, while 0 should correspond to the quality that is expected when the input data is purely random noise with no temporal correlation. 3.1
Closure Phase Coefficient

As detailed in
Section

2
, if the cosine of every closure phase is one, the single-reference phase history can be obtained directly from any column of the matrix
{\bf\Phi}
. Specifically, choosing the
p
th column of
{\bf\Phi}
as the estimated phase history
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
yields:

\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}_{j})\big)=\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\phi_{ip}-\phi_{jp})\big)=\cos\left(\phi^{\Delta}_{ijp}\right).

(3.1)

Substituting this into
Equations

2.11
and
2.15
explicitly demonstrates that the optimality of this chosen phase estimate directly depends on the closure phases. The ideal scenario of maximal consistency corresponds to all closure phases being integer multiples of
2\pi
.

Motivated by this observation, we define a scalar coefficient based on the average cosine of all closure phases, quantifying the internal consistency of
{\bf\Phi}
. A higher coefficient value indicates stronger internal consistency, suggesting that subsequent phase linking algorithms will yield reliable results. We define:

{{\bf\Phi}}^{\Delta}\quad\coloneqq\quad{\binom{N}{3}}^{-1}\sum_{i<j<k}\cos\left(\phi^{\Delta}_{ijk}\right),

(3.2)

for which
{\bf\Phi}^{\Delta}\in[-1,1]
holds. Let
{\bf I}_{N}
denote the identity matrix and
\mathds{1}_{N}
the matrix with all entries equal to one. The following specific theoretical examples illustrate these bounds:

{\bf C}=\mathds{1}_{N}\;\Rightarrow\;{\bf\Phi}^{\Delta}=1,\qquad{\bf C}=\left((1+\alpha)\cdot{\bf I}_{N}-\alpha\mathds{1}_{N}\right)\;\Rightarrow\;{\bf\Phi}^{\Delta}=-1,

(3.3)

where
\alpha\in(0,(N-1)^{-1})
. The second example, being Hermitian and positive semidefinite, represents the case of a coherence matrix with maximal inconsistency between its phases, with every index triplet exhibiting a closure phase of
\pi
. However, the latter scenario is an unrealistic extreme. Consider instead a realistic worst-case scenario in which the interferometric phases between acquisitions are purely random noise with no temporal coherence. In this scenario, the phases
\phi_{ij}
can be modeled as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables, uniformly on
[-\pi,\pi]
. Under this assumption, it can be shown analytically that the expected value of
{{\bf\Phi}}^{\Delta}
is zero:

\forall\,i<j:\;\phi_{ij}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(-\pi,\pi\right)\qquad\xRightarrow{\text{\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{irwinhall}}}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bf\Phi}}^{\Delta}\right]=0.

(3.4)

Given that real SAR data typically exhibits greater consistency than pure noise, the value zero serves as a meaningful practical lower bound. We thus define the closure phase coefficient as:

\gamma_{\mathrm{CP}}\big({\bf\Phi}\big)\coloneqq\max\left({{\bf\Phi}}^{\Delta},0\right).

(3.5)

This coefficient provides a straightforward a priori indicator of phase consistency, directly computed from the coherence matrix. It enables efficient pre-selection of coherent pixels before performing computationally expensive phase linking. In applications in which the line-of-sight ground displacement is to be estimated, the closure phase coefficient can be used to set a rather strict threshold. On the other hand, when parameters that are potentially responsible for the non-zero closure phase (e.g., soil moisture) should also be estimated alongside, the closure phase criterion would be relaxed to not exclude these pixels.

3.2
Goodness-of-Fit Coefficient

As introduced in
Section

2
, phase triangulation and eigendecomposition phase linking methods can be viewed as approximations of the matrix
{\bf\Phi}
. Consequently, a natural way to evaluate the quality of a given phase linking solution is to quantify how closely it approximates the phase information, using the optimization objective. This is also referred to as a goodness-of-fit indicator. For instance, Ferretti et al. introduced a quality measure within their SqueeSAR framework
[
11
, Eq. (16)]
defined as:

\gamma_{\mathrm{PTA}}\coloneqq\frac{2}{N^{2}-N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}_{j})\big)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}^{\mathrm{EW}}_{ij}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}^{\mathrm{EW}}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}_{j})\big).

(3.6)

They employed this coefficient as a threshold criterion, selecting pixels for further processing only when the coefficient exceeded a predefined value. Cao et al.
[
12
]
noted that Ferretti et al. applied maximum-likelihood phase triangulation although the measure defined above inherently aligns more naturally with the equal-weighted phase triangulation method, as it is directly proportional to that method’s objective function (cf.
Equation

2.15
). Even and Schulz
[
8
]
further refined this measure by incorporating coherence magnitudes as weights:

\gamma_{\mathrm{PTA}w}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\Gamma_{ij}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\Gamma_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}_{j})\big).

(3.7)

Similarly, this measure aligns with coherence-weighted phase triangulation. Within their eigendecomposition-based maximum-likelihood estimator (EMI), Ansari et al. proposed the following quality measure
[
13
, Eq. (18)]
:

\lambda\coloneqq\min_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\left\{\dfrac{1}{\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\big({\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\odot{\bf\Gamma}\big)_{ij}\left\lvert v_{i}\right\rvert\left\lvert v_{j}\right\rvert\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\angle v_{i}-\angle v_{j})\big)\right\}.

(3.8)

This measure aligns with the specific objective function employed in their method (cf.
Equation

2.11
). Motivated by these observations, we introduce a generalized goodness-of-fit coefficient explicitly based on each method’s objective function. As mentioned in the beginning of this section, the coefficient should attain values between zero and one. To this end, for a given specific phase linking method and coherence matrix
{\bf C}
, we denote by

\displaystyle f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)\quad

\displaystyle\text{the achieved objective function value};

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big)\quad

\displaystyle\text{an upper bound for }f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)\text{ that is attained if and only if }\gamma_{\mathrm{CP}}\big({\bf\Phi}\big)=1;

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)\quad

\displaystyle\text{a lower bound for }f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big).

Note that the bounds may depend on the coherence matrix. Since
f_{\min}\leq f\leq f_{\max}
, a first normalization is given by

\mathcal{F}({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)\coloneqq\frac{f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)-f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)}{f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big)-f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)}\in\left[0,1\right].

(3.9)

By the definition of
f_{\mathrm{max}}
, a value of
\mathcal{F}=1
signifies the optimal scenario where the coherence matrix demonstrates maximum phase consistency, and consequently, the phase linking estimate fully captures the phase information. However, the meaning of
\mathcal{F}=0
is unclear at this point. Our objective is to establish a coefficient where a value of zero specifically corresponds to the expected outcome for input data consisting solely of random noise without any temporal coherence. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}
denote the expected value of
\mathcal{F}
when the input SAR dataset is purely Gaussian noise, modeled as all SLC pixels in
\Omega
being independent complex standard normally distributed random variables. We can then define a coefficient with the desired characteristics as:

\gamma_{\mathrm{GOF}}\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)\coloneqq\max\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}}{1-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}},\;0\right)\in\left[0,1\right].

(3.10)

It is important to note that in general this two-step normalization process cannot be simplified by directly substituting
f_{\mathrm{min}}
in
Equation

3.9
with
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}
. This is because the bounds of the objective function may depend on the coherence matrix and therefore the objectives may not be comparable prior to the first normalisation.

3.3
Ambiguity Coefficient

The goodness-of-fit coefficient from the previous section quantifies the difference between the phase information in the coherence matrix and the information in the derived phase time series. In practice, especially in areas lacking persistent scatterers, high phase inconsistencies are common. In such cases, a phase time series that only moderately approximates the coherence matrix may still be of interest, as the alternative would be to reject the pixel entirely. Therefore, a low goodness-of-fit value does not necessarily imply that the corresponding phase estimate is unusable. What becomes more relevant in these scenarios is the ambiguity of the solution: whether the estimated phase time series is stable or whether significantly different alternatives exist that fit the data equally well.

To capture this, we introduce an ambiguity coefficient that quantifies how unique the obtained solution is with respect to the objective function. This is particularly important for distinguishing cases in which a phase time series with high goodness-of-fit is in fact unreliable due to near-equivalent alternatives.

Consider the case of coherence-weighted eigendecomposition. The goodness-of-fit coefficient for this method is, up to linear scaling, the largest eigenvalue of the coherence matrix. The estimated phase time series
\bm{\hat{\theta}}
consists of the phases of the corresponding eigenvector, up to the choice of a reference acquisition. Now assume that the largest eigenvalue has multiplicity two, i.e., the second-largest eigenvalue is equal to the largest. In this case, the eigenspace corresponding to the largest eigenvalue is two-dimensional. As a result, there exist infinitely many orthogonal eigenvectors with equal goodness-of-fit. The phase estimates derived from these vectors are fundamentally different in the sense that they are orthogonal, and even though the goodness-of-fit may be relatively high, the result is therefore unreliable since the optimal solution is not determined.

We propose to quantify this ambiguity by generalizing the idea from the previous paragraph. We define the ambiguity coefficient in terms of how much worse a second, orthogonal solution performs in the same optimization framework. To this end, define
\mathcal{X}^{(1)}\coloneqq\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}
for ED methods and
\mathcal{X}^{(1)}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}
for PT methods. Then, let

\bm{\eta}^{(1)}\coloneqq\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X}^{(1)}}\left\{\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\,({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi})\,\bm{v}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\eta}^{(2)}\coloneqq\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X}^{(2)}}\left\{\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\,({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi})\,\bm{v}\right\},\;\text{with}\;\mathcal{X}^{(2)}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}^{(1)}\cap\left(\bm{\eta}^{(1)}\right)^{\perp},

(3.12)

be the primary and secondary phase linking solution, respectively. Moreover, for
i=1,2
, denote by

\bm{\hat{\theta}}^{(i)}\coloneqq\angle\bm{\eta}^{(i)},\qquad f^{(i)}\coloneqq f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}^{(i)}\big),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma^{(i)}_{\mathrm{GOF}}

(3.13)

the corresponding phase time series estimate, objective function value and goodness-of-fit coefficient, respectively.

We obtain the desired ambiguity coefficient as the relative difference

\gamma_{\mathrm{A}}\coloneqq\dfrac{\gamma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{GOF}}-\gamma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{GOF}}}{\gamma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{GOF}}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\gamma_{\mathrm{A}}\coloneqq\dfrac{f^{(1)}-f^{(2)}}{f^{(1)}},

(3.14)

with the first version yielding more interpretable results for ML methods and the second version for all other methods. In practice, the ambiguity coefficient is most easily computed for eigendecomposition methods. To obtain the primary phase linking solution, one can use standard numerical routines, such as LAPACK’s
zheevr
, that compute the largest eigenvalue and its corresponding eigenvector. These routines can also return the second largest eigenvector with only negligible additional cost in the same eigensolver pass. In contrast, for phase triangulation methods, obtaining an orthogonal solution requires solving a constrained nonlinear optimization problem (see
Appendix

C
), which is significantly more computationally expensive. In time-critical settings, one may complement the goodness-of-fit coefficient of phase triangulation methods with the ambiguity coefficient from the corresponding eigendecomposition method, especially when that method provides the initial values for the phase triangulation solver.

Processing Steps

The preprocessing of the SAR data and the DEM is performed using the GAMMA Remote Sensing software. First, a reference scene is geocoded using simulated SAR images, as described in

37

The interferograms resulting from phase linking are shown in

5
Discussion

In this study, we introduce a comprehensive mathematical framework for describing interferometric phase linking methods and evaluating the quality of the resulting phase estimates, which addresses a gap in the InSAR literature. While prior work has proposed quality metrics tied to specific algorithms (e.g., Ferretti et al.
[
11
]
, Ansari et al.
[
13
]
), this paper contributes a novel set of scalar coefficients that are applicable across a broad variety of phase linking methods. Experimental results on TerraSAR-X data over Visp, Switzerland, provide evidence of the practical utility of these coefficients.

One of the introduced quality indicators is the closure phase coefficient, an a priori indicator that measures the internal consistency of the interferometric phases. It is independent of the choice of phase linking method, as it requires only the coherence matrix. As demonstrated in the experiments, this coefficient correlates well with the a posteriori metrics, confirming its potential as an effective early-stage screening tool for distributed scatterers. It can therefore accelerate processing by skipping the computationally expensive phase linking in pixels where it falls below a threshold.

In addition, the paper introduces a method-specific goodness-of-fit coefficient that quantifies how well each phase linking strategy approximates the phase information according to its characteristic objective function. Notably, several quality indicators already established in the literature, such as the residual phase metric used in SqueeSAR
[
11
]
and the eigenstructure-based indicator of Ansari et al.
[
13
]
, can be interpreted as instances of such goodness-of-fit measures; our framework generalizes and formalizes this idea. For maximum likelihood phase linking methods, we propose two variants of the goodness-of-fit coefficient: one that follows the same structural form as those used for other weighting schemes, and one derived from the complex Wishart distribution, which aligns with the statistical assumptions of maximum likelihood estimators. In practice, the Wishart-based variant showed improved interpretability and empirical distributions that facilitate thresholding.

Moreover, we introduce an ambiguity coefficient that compares the goodness-of-fit of the obtained solution with that of an orthogonal alternative. This reveals cases in which high goodness-of-fit is misleading because the data admit two or more nearly optimal yet distinct phase linking solutions. The ambiguity coefficient therefore provides a second layer of quality control, helping distinguish genuinely reliable estimates from those that are well fit but unstable.

Importantly, the three coefficients provide a structured way to support scatterer-selection decisions in DS-InSAR, where spatial coverage must be balanced against phase quality. Rather than replacing existing indicators, the goodness-of-fit coefficient generalizes the underlying principle across a broader range of methods, and the closure phase and ambiguity coefficients contribute complementary information that goodness-of-fit alone does not capture. Additionally, each coefficient is normalized to the unit interval and, unlike existing indicators, independent of the stack size, allowing for better understanding and comparability.

The experimental results on the Visp scene support the utility of the proposed quality indicators. The closure phase coefficient was highest on man-made structures, such as buildings, roads, and the stadium field, and lowest over decorrelated vegetation, consistent with the interferograms in
Figure

4
. Similarly, the goodness-of-fit coefficients assigned high values to concrete structures and areas with short vegetation, and low values to forested areas, in line with visual inspection. The poor goodness-of-fit of the coherence-weighted eigendecomposition accords with the strong deviations of its interferogram phases from those of the other methods observed in
Figure

4
. The ambiguity coefficients then highlighted cases where the phase was poorly determined, most notably in the agricultural field near the image center. In contrast, they indicated good quality for the stadium field, where a stable scattering response is expected.

That said, there are several limitations to the current work. First, we have not explicitly developed quality coefficients for phase linking methods based on least squares such as SBAS. Second, the detection of statistically homogeneous pixels, a prerequisite for coherence matrix estimation, is not perfect. Misgrouped pixels can introduce errors in phase linking results, and the current framework does not explicitly account for or mitigate such grouping inaccuracies. Third, while the ambiguity coefficient is computationally inexpensive for eigendecomposition methods, its calculation for phase triangulation methods requires constrained nonlinear optimization. This leads to a substantial computational burden. However, if processing time is an important factor, it is an option to combine the goodness-of-fit coefficient of a phase triangulation method with the ambiguity coefficient of the corresponding eigendecomposition method, especially when the latter is employed for initial values.

6
Conclusion

We presented a mathematical framework for assessing the quality of interferometric phase linking using three normalized scalar indicators: the closure phase coefficient, method-specific goodness-of-fit coefficient, and ambiguity coefficient. Using TerraSAR-X data over Visp, Switzerland, the closure phase coefficient enabled effective early screening of stable areas, the goodness-of-fit coefficient quantified how well the estimated phase history captures the measured phase information, and the ambiguity coefficient complemented goodness-of-fit by identifying pixels whose phases remain poorly determined despite high fit. Together, these coefficients enable better-informed pixel selection and therefore have the potential to improve the accuracy of InSAR deformation analysis.

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\left\lVert{\bf M}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}-\max\left\{\bm{u}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf M}\bm{u},0\right\}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\max\left\{\bm{u}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf M}\bm{u},0\right\}^{2}\right\}=\max\left\{\argmax_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\bm{u}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf M}\bm{u}\right\},0\right\}.

(A.8)

The claim follows from the fact that
\max_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\bm{u}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf M}\bm{u}\right\}=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

In the context of
Section

2.2.2
, it holds that

\angle\left\{\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\prescript{\sqrt{\bf W}}{}{\left\lVert{\bf\Phi}-\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}\right\}=\angle\left\{\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\left({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right)\bm{v}\right\}\right\}.

(A.9)

Proof.

Due to
Equation

A.5
, it holds that

\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\prescript{\sqrt{\bf W}}{}{\left\lVert{\bf\Phi}-\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\bf W}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}-\sqrt{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\left\lVert\sqrt{\bf W}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}-2\operatorname{Re}\left(\left\langle{\sqrt{\bf W}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}},{\sqrt{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}}\right\rangle_{\mathsf{F}}\right)+\left\lVert\sqrt{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}\right\}.

(A.10)

Since
\lVert\sqrt{\bf W}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}=\lVert\sqrt{\bf W}\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}
, we have

\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\prescript{\sqrt{\bf W}}{}{\left\lVert{\bf\Phi}-\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}}=\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\langle{\sqrt{\bf W}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}},{\sqrt{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}}\right\rangle_{\mathsf{F}}=\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\langle{\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}},{{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}}\right\rangle_{\mathsf{F}}

(A.11)

and the claim follows from
Lemma

A.4
.
∎

Lemma A.7
.

In the context of
Section

2.2.2
, if
{\bf W}=-{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\odot{\bf\Gamma}
, it holds that

\angle\left\{\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\operatorname{d_{\mathrm{KL}}}\bigg(\mathcal{CN}\big(0,{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi},0\big)\,\|\,\mathcal{CN}\big(0,{\bf\Gamma}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}},0\big)\bigg)\right\}=\angle\left\{\argmax_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\left({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right)\bm{v}\right\}\right\}.

Proof.

Define

{\bf\Sigma}_{0}\coloneqq{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi},\qquad{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v})\coloneqq{\bf\Gamma}\odot\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}},\qquad{\bf D}_{\bm{v}}\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}(v).

(A.12)

Then it holds that

{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v})=D_{\bm{v}}\,{\bf\Gamma}\,D_{\bm{v}}^{\mathsf{H}}

and
\operatorname{det}{{\bf D}_{\bm{v}}}=1
, since
\forall\,i:\lvert v_{i}\rvert=1
. Therefore, we have

\det{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v})=\det{\bf\Gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v})^{-1}=D_{\bm{v}}\,{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\,D_{\bm{v}}^{\mathsf{H}}.

(A.13)

The Kullback-Leibler divergence is given by
[
31
, p. 33]
:

\operatorname{d_{\mathrm{KL}}}\left(\mathcal{CN}(0,{\bf\Sigma}_{0},0)\,\|\,\mathcal{CN}(0,{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v}),0)\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\frac{\det{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v})}{\det{\bf\Sigma}_{0}}+\operatorname{tr}\!\big({\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v})^{-1}{\bf\Sigma}_{0}\big)-N\right).

(A.14)

By
Equation

A.13
, the log-determinant term is independent of
\bm{v}
, hence

\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\operatorname{d_{\mathrm{KL}}}\left(\mathcal{CN}(0,{\bf\Sigma}_{0},0)\,\|\,\mathcal{CN}(0,{\bf\Sigma}_{1}(\bm{v}),0)\right)=\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{T}^{N}|_{p}}\operatorname{tr}\!\big(D_{\bm{v}}\,{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\,D_{\bm{v}}^{\mathsf{H}}\,({\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi})\big).

(A.15)

Since
{\bf\Gamma}
and
{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}
are real symmetric, it follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\!\big(D_{\bm{v}}\,{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\,D_{\bm{v}}^{\mathsf{H}}\,({\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi})\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\!\big((\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\odot{\bf\Gamma}^{-1})({\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi})\big)=\left\langle{\left(\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\odot{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}},{{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi}}\right\rangle_{\mathsf{F}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\odot{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}},{{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi}}\right\rangle_{\mathsf{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}},{{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\odot{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi}}\right\rangle_{\mathsf{F}}{\mathrel{\to}^{\text{ \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{realvalpt} }}}\bm{v}^{\mathsf{H}}\big({\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\odot{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi}\big)\,\bm{v},

(A.16)

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma A.8
.

The LaMIE estimator in
[
27
]
is equivalent to
Equation

2.13
with weight matrix
{\bf W}={\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Gamma}
.

Proof.

In this work, we consider estimation and regularization of the coherence matrix
{\bf C}
as prerequisites rather than as a part of the actual phase linking process. In this sense, the LaMIE is defined in
[

as

\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\angle\left\{\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\left\lVert\operatorname{diag}\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right){\bf\Gamma}\operatorname{diag}\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right)^{\mathsf{H}}-{\bf C}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}\right\}

(A.17)

It holds that

\operatorname{diag}\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right){\bf\Gamma}\operatorname{diag}\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right)^{\mathsf{H}}-{\bf C}={\bf\Gamma}\odot\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\Lambda(\bm{\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}-{\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Phi}={\bf\Gamma}\odot\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\Lambda(\bm{\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}-{\bf\Phi}\right)

\angle\left\{\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\left\lVert\operatorname{diag}\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right){\bf\Gamma}\operatorname{diag}\left(\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\right)^{\mathsf{H}}-{\bf C}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathsf{F}}\right\}=\angle\left\{\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\prescript{\sqrt{\bf W}}{}{\left\lVert{\bf\Phi}-\Lambda(\bm{\theta})\Lambda(\bm{\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}\right\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}\right\}

(A.19)

with
{\bf W}={\bf\Gamma}\odot{\bf\Gamma}
, as in
Equation

2.16
.

Lemma A.9
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\sim\mathcal{U}(-\pi,\pi)
be identical, independent and uniformly distributed random variables. Then it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\cos(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})\right]=0.

Proof.

The distribution of the sum
X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3}
is a linearly transformed Irwin-Hall distribution
[
42
, p. 269]
with density function

f_{X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3}}(x)=\begin{cases}0&x<-3\pi\quad\vee\quad x>3\pi\\
\frac{\left(x\pm 3\pi\right)^{2}}{16\pi^{3}}&x\in\left[\mp 3\pi,\mp\pi\right]\\
\frac{-(x+\sqrt{3}\pi)(x-\sqrt{3}\pi)}{8\pi^{3}}&x\in\left(-\pi,\pi\right)\end{cases}

(A.20)

and thus it follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\cos(X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3})\right]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cos(x)f_{X_{1}+X_{2}+X_{3}}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0.

(A.21)

∎

Lemma A.10
.

In the context of
Equation

2.15
, it holds that

\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)\right\}\geq 0.

(A.22)

Proof.

The entries of
{\bf W}
may be negative. However, in the context of
Equation

2.15
, negative weights can be interpreted as weights accompanied by a phase shift. More specifically, using the identity
-\cos(x)=\cos(x-\pi)
, we can define

\tilde{\phi}_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\phi_{ij},&\mathrm{W}_{ij}\geq 0\\
\phi_{ij}-\pi,&\mathrm{W}_{ij}<0\end{cases},

(A.23)

which yields

\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)=|\mathrm{W}_{ij}|\cos\big(\tilde{\phi}_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big).

(A.24)

This allows the optimization to be expressed in terms of non-negative weights while preserving the same structure. Therefore, we may proceed with the assumption that
\forall\,i,j:\mathrm{W}_{ij}\geq 0
.

We prove the statement by induction. For the initial case,
N=2
, let
\bm{\vartheta}\coloneqq(\phi_{12},0)^{\mathsf{T}}
. For this solution it holds that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\vartheta_{i}-\vartheta_{j})\big)=\mathrm{W}_{12}\cos\big(\phi_{12}-(\vartheta_{1}-\vartheta_{2})\big)=\mathrm{W}_{12}\geq 0.

(A.25)

For the induction step
N\rightarrow(N+1)
with
{\bf W}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}
, define

\zeta\coloneqq\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\omega_{N+1}\coloneqq-\left(\angle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\exp\big(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\phi_{iN+1}-\hat{\theta}_{i})\big)\right).

(A.26)

Then
\zeta
is non-negative per induction hypothesis and it follows that

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N+1}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N+1}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)\right\}

\displaystyle=\;\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N+1}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\cos\big(\phi_{iN+1}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{N+1})\big)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\;\zeta+\max_{\theta_{N+1}\in\left[-\pi,\pi\right]}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\cos\big(\phi_{iN+1}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{N+1})\big)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\;\max_{\theta_{N+1}\in\left[-\pi,\pi\right]}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\cos\big(\phi_{iN+1}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{N+1})\big)\right\}\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\cos\big(\phi_{iN+1}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\omega_{N+1})\big)

\displaystyle=\;\operatorname{Re}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\exp\big(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\phi_{iN+1}-\hat{\theta}_{i})\big)\exp\big(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega_{N+1}\big)\right)=\;\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{iN+1}\exp\big(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\phi_{iN+1}-\hat{\theta}_{i})\big)\right\rvert\geq 0.

(A.27)

∎

Appendix B
Goodness-of-Fit Coefficients

In what follows, we derive the expressions in
Tables

2
and
3.11
and the parameter values in
Table

3
. Note that, for each method, there are multiple objective functions in
Sections

2.2.1
and
2.2.2
that can be used in the context of
Equation

3.9
. Although they yield identical phase estimates under exact optimization, they induce slightly different goodness-of-fit coefficients. For concreteness, we adopt the formulation that, in our experiments, yielded the most interpretable coefficients on our test data. For phase triangulation methods with weight matrix
{\bf W}
, we have

f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}_{j})\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\left\lvert{\bf W}\right\rvert_{ij},

(B.1)

which is derived from

-1\leq\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\hat{\theta}_{j})\big)\leq 1.

(B.2)

Lemma

A.10
yields the lower bound

f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)=0.

(B.3)

For eigendecomposition methods, the optimization problem defined in
Equation

2.10
can be solved exactly by eigendecomposition of the matrix
{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}
. The value of the objective function at the global maximum is the largest eigenvalue:

f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\big)=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\left({\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}\right).

(B.4)

The upper and lower bounds must be derived in a weight-specific manner. For coherence-weighted eigendecomposition, we know from
Lemma

A.1
that
{\bf W}^{\mathrm{CW}}\odot{\bf\Phi}={\bf C}
is positive semidefinite and that all its diagonal elements are equal to one. Consequently, all eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are non-negative, and the trace of
{\bf C}
is
N
. Since the trace of a square matrix equals the sum of its eigenvalues, we obtain the upper bound:

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bf C})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=\operatorname{tr}\big({\bf C}\big)=N=f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big).

(B.5)

This bound is attained in the ideal case where
{\bf C}
has rank one, which corresponds to all eigenvalues being zero except
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
. A lower bound for
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
is the smallest eigenvalue,
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
. If this bound is attained, that is, if
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
, then all eigenvalues must be equal. From
Equation

B.5
, it follows that each eigenvalue must be equal to one in that case, leading to the lower bound
f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)=1
.

In the equal-weighted eigendecomposition case, the same lower bound
f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)=1
holds, but
{\bf W}\odot{\bf\Phi}={\bf\Phi}
is in general indefinite. Therefore, it is not clear at this point whether or not the upper bound derived in the previous case, i.e.,
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\leq N
, is still valid. In experiments, it was not violated, and
N
is still analytically the attained value when
{\bf\Phi}
has rank one. To give an upper bound that is certain, one can instead use the magnitudes of the eigenvalues, i.e.,
\textstyle\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}({\bf C})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lvert\lambda_{i}\right\rvert=f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big)
. However, computing this bound requires the full eigendecomposition and the resulting coefficient is generally smaller and much less interpretable than for
f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big)=N
.

For the maximum-likelihood eigendecomposition method, Ansari et al.
[
13
]
provided an upper bound
f_{\mathrm{max}}\big({\bf C}\big)=-1
. To get a lower bound, we use again the fact that, in the worst case, all eigenvalues are equal to each other, and consequently equal to the trace divided by
N
:

f_{\mathrm{min}}\big({\bf C}\big)=\dfrac{\operatorname{tr}\big(-{\bf\Gamma}^{-1}\odot{\bf C}\big)}{N}.

(B.6)

For all the above methods and weights the
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}

While we have already derived a goodness-of-fit coefficient for the maximum-likelihood weighted case of both phase triangulation and eigendecomposition, we present an alternative version for both methods which is based more directly on the statistical assumptions that underpin the maximum-likelihood formulation. Recall that the maximum-likelihood weights are derived from the optimization problem

\bm{\hat{\theta}}\coloneqq\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}f_{\mathcal{W}}\left({\bf C}\mid\bm{\Sigma}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right),

(B.7)

where
\bm{\Sigma}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\coloneqq\Lambda(\bm{\theta}){\bf\Gamma}\Lambda(\bm{\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}
and
f_{\mathcal{W}}
is the density function of the complex Wishart distribution
[
35
, Eq. (21)]
:

f_{\mathcal{W}}\left({\bf C}\mid\bm{\Sigma}\right)\coloneqq\dfrac{\left(M\right)^{NM}\mathrm{det}({\bf C})^{M-N}\exp\left(-\operatorname{tr}\left[M{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bf C}\right]\right)}{\pi^{\frac{N(N-1)}{2}}\mathrm{det}(\bm{\Sigma})^{M}\Pi_{j=1}^{N}\left(M-j\right)!},

(B.8)

with
M\coloneqq\left\lvert\Omega\right\rvert
denoting the number of statistically homogeneous pixels used in the estimation of
{\bf C}

[
12
, Eq. (6)]
. Interpreting
f_{\mathcal{W}}
as the objective function, we can derive an upper bound from the idealized case where
{\bf\Phi}
already has rank one, i.e.,
\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}=\bm{\Sigma}\big(\bm{\hat{\theta}})={\bf C}
, and a lower bound of zero, as
f_{\mathcal{W}}
is a probability density function:

f_{\mathrm{min}}({\bf C})=0\leq f\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\big)=f_{\mathcal{W}}\left({\bf C}\mid\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}\right)\leq f_{\mathcal{W}}\left({\bf C}\mid{\bf C}\right)=f_{\mathrm{max}}({\bf C}).

(B.9)

It follows that

\mathcal{F}\big({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\big)=\dfrac{f_{\mathcal{W}}\left({\bf C}\mid\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}\right)}{f_{\mathcal{W}}\left({\bf C}\mid{\bf C}\right)}=\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{det}({\bf C})}{\mathrm{det}\big(\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}\big)}\exp\left(N-\operatorname{tr}\left({\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{\bf C}\right)\right)\right)^{M}.

(B.10)

Observing that in practice,
\mathcal{F}
attains values very close to zero for real data, we neglect accounting for the noise value
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}
and set it to zero instead. Since the resulting coefficient depends strongly on
M
and is, as already mentioned, small in practice, we take the
M
th root to make the coefficient more interpretable and independent of the ensemble size:

\gamma_{\mathrm{GOF,W}}\coloneqq\sqrt[M]{\frac{\mathcal{F}({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}}{1-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{noise}}}}=\sqrt[M]{\mathcal{F}({\bf C},\bm{\hat{\theta}}\mkern 1.0mu\big)}=\dfrac{\mathrm{det}({\bf C})}{\mathrm{det}\big(\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}\big)}\exp\left(N-\operatorname{tr}\left({\bm{\hat{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{\bf C}\right)\right).

(B.11)

Notably, this is strongly related to the Kullback-Leibler divergence (cf.
[
18
, Eq. (25)]
):

\gamma_{\mathrm{GOF,W}}=\exp\left(-\operatorname{d_{\mathrm{KL}}}\left(\mathcal{CN}\big(0,{\bf C},0\big)\|\mathcal{CN}\big(0,\bm{\hat{\Sigma}},0\big)\right)\right).

(B.12)

Appendix C
Phase triangulation algorithms

While eigendecomposition can be approached with efficient standardized solvers that do not need initial values and are guaranteed to converge to an exact solution, phase triangulation methods need initial values and have guaranteed convergence at best locally. We therefore give special attention to algorithms and initial values in this section.

C.1
Optimization methods

As a standard solver for unconstrained nonlinear optimization problems, the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm
[

]

[

\theta_{p}^{(k+1)}\coloneqq\angle\left\{-\sum_{i\neq p}\mathrm{W}_{pi}\phi_{pi}\exp\big(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\theta_{p}^{(k)}\big)\right\}.

(C.1)

Because the original reference provides limited derivation, we briefly interpret this iteration using standard numerical optimization concepts. It can be understood as a modified Jacobi iteration (updating only the phases) for

(C.2)

Since the iteration only updates the phases, the factor
\mathrm{W}_{pp}

We seek an optimization method with minimal assumptions and a simple implementation so it can be extended to
Equation

3.12

C.2
Initial values

Phase triangulation employs iterative solvers and the choice of initial values is particularly important because the optimization algorithms used, both in the literature and in this work, are only guaranteed to converge to local maxima. However, our goal is to recover the globally optimal phase time series. Poor initialization increases the risk of convergence to a sub-optimal local maximum. Therefore, high-quality initial values are essential to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the final result.

If the corresponding algorithms are readily available, a convenient way to initialize phase triangulation is to use the result of the corresponding eigendecomposition method. Alternative initial values can be generated using simplified phase linking strategies that rely on minimal information, specifically, only
N-1
interferometric phases, which correspond to the number of degrees of freedom in the phase time series. We describe two such strategies. First, a simple approach uses only interferometric pairs between temporally adjacent acquisitions. This corresponds to a tridiagonal weight matrix,

\mathrm{W}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\left\lvert i-j\right\rvert\leq n\\
0,&\left\lvert i-j\right\rvert>n\end{cases}\;\;.

(C.4)

Applying phase triangulation with this matrix results in the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\mathrm{W}^{\mathrm{TD}}_{ij}\cos\big(\phi_{ij}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\big)\right\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Pi^{N}|_{p}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\cos\big(\phi_{i,i+1}-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{i+1})\big)\right\},

(C.5)

which can be solved directly. Given that
\hat{\theta}_{p}=0
, the remaining phases are obtained through

\hat{\theta}_{i}=\phi_{i,i+1}+\hat{\theta}_{i+1}.

(C.6)

This approach is particularly effective when the coherence is highest between adjacent acquisitions, which is often the case in repeat-pass acquisitions. Second, a more general method constructs a spanning tree
13
13

13

A concept from graph theory, a spanning tree is a connected graph using all vertices in which there are no circuits, see
[
45
]
.
over the acquisition graph using only the most coherent interferometric pairs. Let
\mathfrak{T}_{N}
denote the set of adjacency matrices of all spanning trees of order
N
. Among these, we select the one that maximizes the total coherence:

{\bf W}^{\mathrm{ST}}\coloneqq{\bf I}_{N}+\argmax_{{\bf M}\in\mathfrak{T}_{N}}{{\left\langle{{\bf M}},{{\bf\Gamma}}\right\rangle}_{\text{F}}}

(C.7)

where
{\left\langle{\;},{\;}\right\rangle}_{\text{F}}
is the Frobenius inner product, i.e., the sum of all entry-wise products. In other words, we seek a set of index pairs

\left\{(i_{k},j_{k}),\quad k=1,\dots,N-1,\quad i_{k}<j_{k}\right\}

(C.8)

such that the system

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{p}}=0,\qquad\hat{\theta}_{i_{k}}=\phi_{i_{k},j_{k}}+\hat{\theta}_{j_{k}}

(C.9)

is determined and

\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}{{\bf\Gamma}}_{i_{k}j_{k}}=\dfrac{1}{2}{\left\langle{{\bf M}},{{\bf\Gamma}}\right\rangle}_{\text{F}}

(C.10)

is maximized. Here,
{\bf M}

{\bf M}
