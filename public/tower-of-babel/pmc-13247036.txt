Design of the biosensor-dependent coupling system stabilizes the high-synthesis phenotype of cell factory
Authors: Lei Qin, Bo Yang, Rui Huang, Haocheng Zhang, Qiuyang Li, Xinyi Li, Yingqi Tan, Wentao Sun, Haiyang Jia, Bing Hu, Bo Lv, Chun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial cell factories offer a sustainable route to plant-derived natural products, but yield drift and strain degeneration persist. Growth-coupled biosynthesis can continuously enrich high producers, yet specific product-responsive biosensors remain scarce and their population-level effects are unclear. Here, we describe a rapid transcriptome-mining workflow that, as proof-of-concept, delivers yeast biosensors for glycyrrhetinic acid and medicarpin. By fine-tuning PDR5 promoter, we expand the dynamic range of the glycyrrhetinic acid sensor and wire it to an essential gene, establishing a growth-addiction circuit that increases titer by 46.8 % after subculture. Single-cell transcriptome reveals that the evolved strain population exhibits a completely different division of labor compared to the initial strain. Coupling does not eliminate phenotypic heterogeneity; instead, it amplifies a dedicated sub-population marked by discrete transcriptional signatures. Deletion of genes highly expressed in non-producing cells or enrichment of high-producing cell clusters can further boost population-level production. This study provides both a generalizable biosensor-discovery platform and single-cell-guided strategies for stabilizing and optimizing natural-product cell factories.Subject terms: Metabolic engineering, Transcriptomics, Synthetic biology

Introduction

The utilization of microbial cell factories for the synthesis of heterologous natural products confers numerous advantages in terms of sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and controllability1,2. Despite the substantial enhancement of synthetic capacity that can be achieved through classical metabolic engineering strategies, the incomplete understanding of complex intracellular networks often results in interference from unknown factors within the system, thereby hindering further breakthroughs in product yield and even leading to strain degeneration. Coupling product synthesis with microbial cell growth, termed the “metabolic addiction” strategy, has emerged as a pivotal pathway for achieving stable, efficient biosynthesis and driving synthesis-directed evolution3–10. Among these approaches, strategies leveraging biosensors that specifically respond to target products and drive the expression of essential genes for cell growth (such as antibiotic genes or nutrient-deficiency genes) represent a primary means of implementing such coupled systems9–11. Consequently, the development of efficient and specific biosensors is fundamental to the construction of growth-synthesis coupling systems. Furthermore, biosensors demonstrate broad application value in high-throughput screening, dynamic pathway regulation, and metabolic flux monitoring12,13.

Nevertheless, the identification or rational design of biosensors continues to confront substantial challenges. Biosensors with gene regulatory functions are obtained by screening naturally occurring regulatory systems, such as elements that rely on the recognition of target molecules, including transcriptional activators, repressors, or riboswitches14. Hanko et al. developed a genome-wide approach to identifying transcription factor-based biosensors, inferring transcription regulators by identifying substrate-enzyme-gene operon systems in prokaryotes15. However, such methods are constrained by intracellular endogenous metabolite-related sensing systems, whose functionality is often limited by the genetic background and regulatory environment of the host species, presenting significant species compatibility issues. To date, only a small number of biosensors targeting specific compounds have been successfully developed and applied in synthetic biology practices16–18. Consequently, there remains a critical shortage of biosensors capable of efficiently and specifically responding to target substances, necessitating the urgent establishment of a high-throughput, versatile biosensor development platform.

Furthermore, in elucidating the mechanism behind enhanced synthetic capacity in growth-synthesis coupled strains, conventional wisdom posits that this strategy confers a growth advantage to high-yielding cells by suppressing the proliferation of deceptive cells, thereby reducing metabolic phenotype heterogeneity within the population and enabling autonomous enrichment of high-yielding strains3. However, this theory remains lacking in robust evidence at the microscopic level. In recent years, single-cell transcriptomics sequencing has emerged as an effective tool for resolving gene expression heterogeneity among individual microbial cells and dynamically revealing cellular functional differentiation19–23. Analyzing expression differences in metabolic pathways and regulatory networks at single-cell resolution not only helps elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms stabilizing high-yield phenotypes within strain growth-synthesis coupling systems but also reveals finely tuned regulatory information obscured at the population level, providing a basis for discovering novel metabolic regulatory targets. Previous studies indicate that when facing environmental stress or resource limitations, microbial populations tend to differentiate into functionally specialized subpopulations: one accelerates division and proliferation to maintain population size, while the other reduces division and extends lifespan to produce specific bioactive molecules22. However, no studies have yet demonstrated whether similar division-of-labor patterns exist during microbial heterogenous product synthesis, nor how such functions are regulated and optimized.

In this study, we develop a universal workflow for the in-situ discovery of target-responsive biosensors in chassis cells. Then, we integrate this workflow into strategies for stabilizing and directing the evolution of cell factory phenotypes. To validate this strategy, we successfully identify novel biosensor elements as being capable of specifically responding to glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) and medicarpin based on the yeast transcriptome. The construction of a growth-synthesis coupling system is facilitated by the utilization of these biosensors. We demonstrate that the evolution dependent on biosensors is an effective means of suppressing the instability of high-yield traits during passage, thereby yielding engineered strains with significantly enhanced GA production. Single-cell transcriptomic sequencing reveals that high-yielding yeast populations still exhibited distinct labor division and that growth coupling only drove high yields in a subset of cells. Simultaneously, single-cell transcriptomics unveils a series of pivotal differentially expressed marker genes that had been obscured by averaged signals in bulk transcriptomics. The elimination of highly expressed marker genes in non-synthesizing populations results in a substantial enhancement of the overall GA production capacity of the microbial community. These findings provide insights and inspiration for strain stabilization, evolutionary strategy design and population structure optimization in microbial cell factories synthesizing natural products.

Results

Mining and characterization of product responsive elements

To develop biosensors for the in-situ detection of products within yeast cell factories, a rapid screening platform was established (Fig. 1a). The synthesis of glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) by Saccharomyces cerevisiae was used as an exemplary case. The presence of GA, a heterologous product in yeast (Fig. 1b), can potentially trigger a stress response within the cell and result in the induction of certain gene expressions. To identify genes that respond to GA and enable the development of a GA biosensor, we compared the transcriptomes of two yeast strains: GA180, which can synthesize GA, and SynV, the parent strain that lacks the ability to produce GA (Fig. 1c). There were 215 genes with significantly upregulated expression and 936 genes with significantly downregulated expression. Because GA is a lipophilic exogenous component, it may cause the expression of stress-related yeast drug transporters and other similar proteins. Therefore, the genes encoding transporters or hypothetical proteins with upregulated expression were selected to identify genes that respond to GA (Supplementary Table 1). The inducibility of these gene promoters is characterized using red fluorescent protein mCherry by constructing expression cassettes into GA180 and SynV strains, respectively. As a result, the PDR5 promoter (PPDR5) displays the most significant response enhancement to GA (Fig. 1d). There is a strong positive correlation between the fluorescence response of PPDR5 and the production of intracellular GA (Fig. 1e, f, and Supplementary Fig. 1a). To validate the capability and dynamic range of PPDR5 in response to GA, GA was added to control strain SynV with varying concentrations (Fig. 1g), and almost GA was detected within the cell (Supplementary Fig. 1b). When GA concentration ranges between 0 and 50 mg/L, the fluorescence intensity shows a positive correlation with the amount of added GA (Fig. 1h). When the concentration of GA exceeds 50 mg/L, the response intensity of PPDR5 becomes saturated.

Pdr5 is a member of ATP-binding cassette superfamily of transporter24. Pdr5 can transport diverse substances such as ion carriers, azoles, antibiotics, and xenobiotics25. Furthermore, PPDR5 responds to some alkaloids and flavonoids, etc9. Here, we investigated if certain metabolites and environmental variables can disrupt the response of PPDR5. Some factors can increase the response of PPDR5 (e.g., addition of urea), while some stress environments can inhibit the response (e.g., high concentrations of ethanol, high temperature, and hydrogen peroxide) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To avoid the influence of GA precursors on the response of PPDR5, the strains that produce GA precursors (compounds 2, 3, and 4) or their analogues (compounds 1 and 6) were used. The result showed that the PPDR5 biosensor does not respond to the analogues and is specific to GA in the GA biosynthesis system (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

In addition, the universality of the transcriptome-based method for rapidly identifying target product biosensors was demonstrated using the typical flavonoid medicarpin26 as another example to identify the promoters of yeast response using the same method (Fig. 1i). In the case of medicarpin, it was determined that the promoters of YOR1 and PDR16 are responsive to medicarpin (Fig. 1j). The correlation between the response signals and concentrations indicates the credibility of their responses (Fig. 1k).

Design of the GA biosensor to modify the dynamic ranges

To enhance the applicability of GA biosensor for strain screening and evolution, it is imperative to be able to adjust its dynamic range, i.e., the ratio of the maximum response signal to the basic signal. The rational modification of promoters presents an effective approach for designing transcription factor-based biosensors. Increasing the type and quantity of DNA-binding motifs for transcription activation in the promoter can recruit more transcription-activating factors and improve promoter response intensity27–29. The regulation of the PDR5 gene is controlled by transcription-activating factors Pdr1, Pdr3, Yap1, and Stb5, alongside the transcription-inhibiting factor Rdr1 (Fig. 2a)30–34. Pdr1 and Pdr3 can form both homo- and hetero-dimers, and degenerately occupy the DNA motif called pleiotropic drug resistance elements30,31. Within the promoter sequence PPDR5, these motifs are concentrated in the region between −200 and −600 bp (Fig. 2b). To determine which specific region within the promoter affects its response, promoter truncation experiments were performed. It was found that truncating the promoter from 600 to 400 bp resulted in a significant decrease in its response (Fig. 2c), suggesting that this region contains more critical motifs. The knockout of these transcription factors also confirmed that they are essential components for PPDR5 to respond to GA (Fig. 2d). Pdr1 was reported as a nuclear receptor that directly interacts with several drugs to stimulate expression of PDR534. The simulation of the complex demonstrates that there is a possibility of Pdr1 binding to GA (Supplementary Fig. 3a). This finding provides an explanation for the mechanism by which PDR5 can respond to GA.

To enhance the response of the promoter, various motifs (Pdr1/3: TCCGCGGA, Yap1: TTACTAA, Stb5: CGGAACGA) were incorporated into the interval spacer regions (−220, −407, −513 bp) to assess the response intensity and dynamic range (Supplementary Fig. 4). The type, quantity, and position of inserted motifs all have impacts on the dynamic range. On this basis, multiple combination motifs were designed, resulting in the dynamic range of most promoters being higher than that of wild-type promoters. Of all the promoters tested, PPDR5-PE23 (containing two Stb5 motifs and one Pdr1/3 motif) showed the highest dynamic range, reaching 5.16, and was deemed suitable for subsequent applications (Fig. 2e).

To further expand the dynamic range of GA biosensor, the effects of other regulatory factors were investigated. Ssz1 (also known as Pdr13) is reported to act as a positive regulator of Pdr132,33. Yeast two-hybrid validated that Ssz1 only interacts with transcription factor Pdr1 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Upon knocking out Ssz1 and Rdr1, it was observed that deletion of Ssz1 had a noteworthy effect on the dynamic range, leading to an increase to 32.2 (Fig. 2f). Therefore, we can fine-tune and coarse-tune the GA biosensor by modifying the promoter sequence and knocking down SSZ1 gene, respectively.

GA biosensor as a tool for stabilizing and evolving strain phenotype

A significant application of biosensors is in the construction of a growth-synthesis coupling system, a method which has been demonstrated to expedite synthesis performance. The engineered PPDR5 (PE23) was utilized to regulate the expression of the gene HIS3 to enhance the ability of GA synthesis. Hence, cell growth exhibiting an efficient GA synthesis ability can be exploited. Furthermore, the presence of 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT, an inhibitor of His3 protein) necessitated that yeasts produce more GA for growth maintenance (Fig. 3a)9. To verify the efficacy of this strategy, the impact of introducing PPDR5-HIS3 and 3-AT on GA synthesis was examined. After the incorporation of both PPDR5-HIS3 and 1 mM 3-AT, there was a significantly enhancement in the synthesis capacity of GA per unit cell in the media lacking histidine (Fig. 3b). The positive correlation between GA concentration and cell growth was further confirmed by supplementing exogenous GA (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results demonstrate the feasibility of the growth-production coupling strategy. Moreover, both increasing 3-AT concentration and decreasing dynamic range can enhance the constraint of biosensor on cell growth, and it is speculated that adjusting the catalytic validity of His3 will lead to an optimal value that maximizes GA production (Fig. 3c).

The next is to evolve the strain to improve the cell adaption in histidine-lacking media with or without 3-AT. We used the high GA production strain GA184, which contains the biosensing plasmid pRS415-PE23 and is named GA184-PE23, as the parent strain here. It was found that adding at least 10 mM 3-AT significantly inhibited cell growth (Supplementary Fig. 5). Therefore, 10 mM 3-AT was chosen in the initial adaptive evolution. During the evolution process, as the yeasts become adaptive, the concentration of 3-AT is progressively increased to 50 mM. The strain was also evolved in YPD media as a control. During 18 days of evolution, the growth of the evolved strains was detected, and the cells were withdrawn at 6, 12 and 18 days and inoculated into GM1 media to determine the production of GA (Fig. 3d). Consequently, the strains cultured in YPD media exhibited a dramatic reduction in triterpenoid production (Fig. 3e), indicating that the GA-producing trait is not steadily inherited as the production process of GA may exerting a burden on cell growth. The heterologous genes were almost lost as the strain continuous cultivation without the production-dependent selective pressure in YPD media (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 6), which may be due to unstable gene integration or overgrowth of the wild-type yeast. This phenomenon of strain deterioration also occurs in industrial production. In contrast, the evolution in SD-His media made the producing phenotype and genotype stable, due to the coupling of growth with biosynthesis. Furthermore, the addition of 3-AT led to an increased correlation between growth and GA production. After evolution in 3-AT for 6 days, the production of GA increased by 9.8% and the production of total triterpenoids (GA, glycyrrhetaldehyde and 11-oxo-β-amyrin) increased by 7.9% (Fig. 3e). To further reinforce the correlation between production and growth, we selected other engineered biosensors with reduced dynamic ranges (PE25 and PE31, which regulatory features are shown in Fig. 2e) to reduce the synthesis amount of histidine (Fig. 3c). As anticipated, the strains employing PE25 and PE31 exhibited substantial increases in GA production. The strains with PE31 evolved for 6 days achieved the highest production level, in which the production of GA increased by 17.5% and the production of total triterpenoids increased by 11.4% (Fig. 3e). To identify the high-production cells, single colonies were then randomly picked from the plates of the evolved strain with PE31 for 6 days (Fig. 3g). The highest production strain A8 showed a GA production of 207.3 mg/L and a total triterpenoid production of 886.5 mg/L, representing a 46.8% and 59.1% increase than the initial strain, respectively (Fig. 3h).

Reasons for high GA production of the evolutionary strain at the bulk transcriptional level

To investigate the role of the biosensor in driving the evolution, the transcriptomes of the evolved strain A8 and the control strain GA184Y (obtained from the strain GA184-PE23 evolved in YPD medium for 6 days) were analyzed. The expression levels of heterologous genes involved in synthesizing GA in the evolved strain were found to be significantly higher than the control (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), which agrees with the result of the gene loss rate (Fig. 3e). Moreover, the expression of HIS3 gene demonstrated elevated, thereby validating the efficacy of the biosensor-dependent growth-production coupling strategy. Furthermore, the INO1 gene, encoding inositol-3-phosphate synthase and involving phospholipids synthesis, was found to be significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Previous studies have shown that the expression of INO1 is regulated by the transcription factor Ino2, which promotes the expression of phospholipid synthesis genes, increases the capacity of the endoplasmic reticulum, and provides more space for the expression of P450 and terpenoid synthesis35. In this study, INO2 was also upregulated in the evolved strain. Consequently, the upregulation of INO1 and INO2 suggests that the phospholipid content and endoplasmic reticulum capacity of the evolved strain may have increased to promote the synthesis process of GA. As demonstrated in our previous research, the expression of INO2 has been shown to increase the production of glycyrrhetinic acid by 50.2%36. Concurrently, genes implicated in the competition pathway for GA synthesis, namely the ergosterol synthesis pathway, exhibited significant downregulation (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Ergosterol is an essential component for yeast cell growth. The trade-off between ergosterol synthesis and heterologous terpenoid synthesis has always been an important means of regulating high terpenoid production. Excessive inhibition of ergosterol synthesis has been demonstrated to limit cell growth and result in a reduction in target terpenoids. The identification of a suitable balance point represents a considerable challenge37,38. The high production strain A8 indeed exhibits lower biomass (Fig. 3i), which is consistent with the metabolic changes of the ergosterol synthesis pathway. It is evident that, through biosensor-dependent evolution, the biosynthesis pathway has become more adapted to the chassis metabolic network.

Single-cell RNA-seq revealed the sub-population differences of the evolved strain

The single colonies showed that there were differences in phenotype between individual cells after evolution (Fig. 3g). Previous studies have shown that there may be division of labor and cooperation within a single microbial species20–23. To investigate the potential different evolutionary directions of populations caused by different evolutionary pressures, single-cell RNA-seq was used for analysis. The single-cell transcriptome exhibited gene expression patterns analogous to those of the bulk transcriptome (Supplementary Fig. 7c), yet the former is capable of more profoundly reflecting differences between individual cells within a microbial culture.

The single-cell transcriptome data of the evolved strain A8 and the control strain GA184Y were combined for dimensionality reduction to display the differences between the two strains (Fig. 4a, b). All cells are divided into nine clusters (Fig. 4c). The evolved strain exhibited substantial separation in terms of significant main component features compared to the control strain. The 6969 cells detected in the control were predominant in clustered 3, 4, and 6, accounting for 36.9%, 23.4%, and 15.9% of the total cell count, respectively. In contrast, the 7253 cells detected in the evolved strain were primarily in cluster 0, 1, and 7, accounting for 33.9%, 32.5%, and 4.0% of the total cell count, respectively (Fig. 4d, e). Furthermore, clusters 2, 5, and 8 are shared by two strains. Furthermore, the highly variable genes and marker genes in the nine clusters were analyzed (Fig. 4f, g, and Supplementary Fig. 8). There are 2000 highly variable genes among all the cells.

In clusters 0, 1, and 7, which are predominantly comprised of evolved strain, these three clusters exhibit substantial differences in RNA feature quantity, signifying intercellular division of labor. Cluster 0 has the highest RNA features and enriched with a budding marker gene PIR1 (a marker for early G1 phase, Fig. 4e, h)39, indicating that this cluster of cells has metabolic and proliferative activity. Furthermore, genes involved in cell wall synthesis and remodeling, including EXG1, HSP150, CRH1, NCW2, PST1, and CWP2, also indicate that the cells in cluster 0 are in a state of budding (Fig. 4h, and Supplementary Fig. 8). In cluster 0, the expression levels of heterologous genes involved in product synthesis are slightly upregulated. In cluster 1, the heterologous genes involved in product synthesis, CYP72A63, CYP88D6, and βAS, exhibited significant upregulation compared to other clusters, and the cell growth-determining gene HIS3 also demonstrated significant upregulation, suggesting that cluster 1 plays the principal role in product synthesis and provides essential histidine for community growth in the evolved strain. Low RNA feature of cluster 7 indicates that cells are in a physiological resting state (e.g., the G0 phase), experiencing environmental stress, or in a senescent state. The increased expression of FIT3 and heat shock proteins (e.g., HSP12 and DDR2) also indicates cell aging and the renaturation of proteins22. The expression of the heterologous genes did not obviously increase in cluster 7, indicating that it was not the primary contributor to product synthesis.

In clusters 3, 4, and 6, which are dominated by the control strain, there was an observed downregulation of the heterologous genes involved in product synthesis. This finding is consistent with the results of the production levels, gene lost rates (Fig. 3e), and the bulk RNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 7). The upregulation of gene expression of large numbers of ribosomal subunit proteins in cluster 3 indicates that these cells are mainly responsible for protein synthesis (Fig. 4h, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Cluster 4 has a high abundance of RNA feature and many genes involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle are upregulated (e.g., MLS1, ICL1, CIT2, and SFC1), indicating that the cells are in a stage of vigorous cellular metabolism. In cluster 6, gene ADH2, ADY2, ATO3, and CTA1 were significantly upregulated. These genes have been shown to regulate the adaptation to metabolic stress such as ethanol, acetic acid, ammonia, and reactive oxygen species. It is evident that the expression of peroxisome genes in cluster 6 is elevated (CTA1 and ECI1). This finding indicates that these cells play a role in balancing cellular homeostasis. However, the expression levels of these genes are minimal in the evolved strain. The transcriptional characteristics of cluster 6 are most divergent from those of the evolved strain, indicating that the synthesis of the product does not require or is inhibited by these genes. Consequently, the knockout of these genes demonstrated that the deletion of ADY2 and CTA1 can enhance the production of GA, suggesting that these two genes may have a significant impact on GA synthesis (Fig. 4i).

Single-cell transcriptomics revealed the different division of labor between the evolved strain and the control strain at the single-cell resolution. It also provides detailed information regarding the regulation of the metabolism of a specific cell type within a population. We annotated the functional features of cell clusters based on the above analysis of the marker genes (Fig. 4j). The evolved strain exhibits high expression of synthetic pathway genes, yet it also demonstrates a pronounced division of labor mechanism: not all cells are highly productive. Instead, one group exhibits stronger cell regeneration characteristics (cluster 0), while another group displays distinct quiescent traits (cluster 7). In contrast, the control strain shows a more growth-biased transcriptional profile compared to the evolved strain.

To further verify the ability of each cluster to synthesize products, cells of strain A8 with high expression of marker genes were sorted out to detect GA production (Supplementary Fig. 9). The result shows that cells with high expression of marker genes in clusters 0, 1, 3, and 4 generally have higher than average levels of product synthesis ability, while clusters 6 and 7 have lower than average synthesis levels. It is noteworthy that cells expressing elevated levels of mitochondrial functional proteins in strain A8 also demonstrate high productivity. This suggests that, under conditions of elevated cellular activity (such as those associated with respiration and proliferation), cells may exhibit considerable potential for productivity.

Discussion

To address the issue of degeneration in cell factories, this study proposes a method for maintaining the stability of synthetic traits in yeasts. The key to this approach lies in identifying biosensors for target products. Through two examples, GA and medicarpin, we validated the universality of this method for discovering biosensors and maintaining the stability of synthetic traits. Compared to discovering new biosensors through molecular interaction mechanisms, this method is more universal and greatly shortens the mining cycle of biosensors. It will provide a rapid method for creating sensing elements for more new products. Results from transcriptomic screening of promoters responsive to target products reveal that certain yeast drug transporter genes often respond to heterologous products. Examples of such genes include PDR5 (responsive to GA), YOR1 and PDR16 (responsive to medicarpin) in this study. However, this approach may have limitations. Biosensors identified through this screening may exhibit specificity issues, potentially responding to intracellular metabolites, synthetic pathway precursors, or competing metabolites, thereby affecting their application. This necessitates validation of biosensor specificity to avoid interference factors during use. In this study, the GA biosensor demonstrated high specificity within this cell factory, exhibiting sensitivity only to certain stresses (e.g., ethanol and temperature). Therefore, temperature and high ethanol concentrations must be strictly controlled. Although previous reports indicated the capacity of Pdr5 to transport multiple drug molecules9,24,25, our prior research demonstrated Pdr5’s inefficiency in transporting GA. This indicates that regulatory molecules governing transporter gene expression do not always correspond symmetrically to the target molecules transported by the transporter. Literature indicates that Pdr1, as a homodimeric nuclear receptor, can bind multiple drug molecules to regulate the PDR5 promoter34. We have also demonstrated through molecular modeling and gene knockout that GA regulates the PDR5 promoter via Pdr1. However, more systematic regulatory mechanisms, such as how Ssz1 specifically modulates the interaction between GA and Pdr1, remain to be elucidated.

Coupling cell growth and product synthesis is a reliable approach to enhance compound production. There are two methodologies for the growth-synthesis coupling. One approach suggests that the metabolic byproducts in the synthetic pathway are essential substances for cell growth metabolism40,41, which requires that this reaction must be found in the synthetic pathway. However, this is not universally applicable. An alternative approach involves the induction of the expression of a gene that determines cell growth by responding to the target product through biosensors4,11. Consequently, the construction of such a coupling system is always feasible, as long as provided there is a suitable biosensor. Furthermore, the response intensity of biosensors has been demonstrated to affect cell growth, which in turn leads to different outcomes in the evolution. In this study, we were able to effectively increase the production of GA by substituting biosensors of different intensities. Furthermore, this coupling system has been demonstrated to effectively prevent the degeneration that is typically caused by strain subculture42, making it a universal method that can be validated. It has been shown to play an important role in the genotype and phenotype stabilization, and improve the adaptability of synthetic pathways to chassis metabolic networks.

In contrast to the previous studies on growth-synthesis coupling research4,7,10,11, this study elucidates the mechanism by which growth-synthesis coupling stabilizes and enhances product synthesis efficiency from the perspective of non-genetic cellular phenotypic differentiation. The single-cell transcriptomic landscape of the high production strain derived from coupled evolution distinctly differs from that of the control strain. Metabolic characteristics of each cluster can be inferred from the enrichment of marker genes. Contrary to previous understanding3, community differentiation persists in biosensor-dependent high production strain, indicating that growth-synthesis coupling does not entirely eliminate “deceptive cells”. Growth-synthesis coupling induces high-production metabolic profiles in the majority of cells, yet a minority remains allocated to maintaining cellular activity or repairing damage. This indicates that these few cells are necessary for coping with environmental changes or cellular aging42–44. Similarly, low-production control strain also exhibits relative differentiation between high- and low-production communities. A promising avenue for future research involves in exploring how to artificially regulate this division of labor to maximize product synthesis.

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals the different division of product synthesis yeasts. Transcriptomic analysis identified genes significantly differentially expressed in certain cell subpopulations, though these genes were obscured in bulk transcriptomic studies (Supplementary Fig. 10), highlighting the substantial advantage of single-cell RNA-seq. Significantly increasing product synthesis can be achieved by sorting cells expressing marker genes or by knocking out marker genes believed to interfere with synthesis (e.g., ADY2 and CTA1). This provides a method and workflow for identifying key regulatory target genes that are not significantly represented in bulk transcriptomic studies.

Methods

Media and strains

Escherichia coli was used for routine cloning procedures and protein purification. E. coli was cultivated at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani (LB) media containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin or kanamycin for selection. Initial strain S. cerevisiae SynV45, and glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) producing strain S. cerevisiae GA18046 and GA18447 were cultivated in YPD media (2% peptone, 1% yeast extract, 2% glucose) or GM1 media (1% corn steep liquor, 1% yeast extract, 2% glucose). The genotypes of the engineered strains were listed in Supplementary Table 248–50. Primers used in this study were listed in Supplementary Data 1.

RNA-seq

To mine the product-responsive genes, the producing strain and initial strain were cultured in YPD media for 48 h, and then the cell pellets were collected. To investigate the changes in the evolution process of the biosensor-driven strain, the evolutionary strain A8 and the control strain GA184Y (cultured in YPD medium for 6 days) were cultured in GM1 media for 120 h, and then the cell pellets were collected.

RNA was extracted by the TRIzol method. 1 μg total RNA was used for the following library preparation. The poly(A) mRNA isolation was performed using Oligo(dT) beads. The mRNA fragmentation was performed using divalent cations and high temperature. Priming was performed using Random Primers. First-strand cDNA and the second-strand cDNA were synthesized. The purified double-stranded cDNA was then treated to repair both ends and add a dA-tailing in one reaction, followed by a T-A ligation to add adapters to both ends. Size selection of Adapter-ligated DNA was then performed using DNA Clean Beads. Each sample was then amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using P5 and P7 primers, and the PCR products were validated. Then libraries with different indices were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina HiSeq instrument for sequencing using a 2×150 paired-end (PE) configuration according to manufacturer’s instructions. The raw transcriptomic data have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject accession numbers PRJNA1146062, PRJNA1415461 and PRJNA1436984. The relationship between log2FoldChange and -log10P-value was represented using a scatter diagram (volcano plot).

Strain construction

Gene parts (promoters, terminators, ORFs, and homologous arms) were amplified by PCRs using the 2×Phanta® Flash Master Mix (Vazyme, China). PCR products were separated on 1% agarose gels and purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher). Overlap extension PCR or Gibson assembly was used to assemble the gene parts into genetic circuits. Genetic circuits flanked by homologous arms were integrated into the yeast genome by homologous recombination using electro-transformation. The transformed yeasts were cultivated on YPD agar plates at 30 °C for 3 days. Positive clones were verified by diagnostic PCR.

Construction of biosensors

The transcriptomic data of the initial strain and producing strain were analyzed. Genes were selected from the upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs, log2FoldChange > 1, P-value < 0.01) as product-responsive candidates. The upstream 1000 bp sequence of the start codon was selected as the promoter region. The expression cassette was constructed with the promoters that regulate the expression of a red or green fluorescent protein gene. The expression cassettes were integrated into the initial strain and the producing strain genomes by yeast electro-transformation.

Fermentation and products determination

The yeast strains were pre-cultured in 2 mL YPD media at 30 °C, 200 rpm for 20 h. Then, the strains were inoculated into 50 mL GM1 media in flasks with an inoculated OD of 0.1. Subsequently, the strains were cultivated for 120 h for the accumulation of GA. Biomass of yeast cells were determined with a micro-spectrophotometer (Nano-300, AllSheng, China) or a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). All cultivations were performed with three independent biological replicates.

After cultivation, 2 mL of yeast cell culture was treated by a sample freezing grinder, and then extracted using ethyl acetate. For each sample, equal volumes of organic phase were collected for subsequent analysis. The resulting extracts and aqueous-phase were respectively pooled, dried, and trimethylsilylated with pyridine and N,O-Bis (trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) for analysis by GC-MS. The GC-MS analysis was performed using Agilent 8890-5977B GC/MSD Ultra (Agilent) equipped with Agilent 19091S-433. J&W HP-5ms GC Column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm, Agilent) with a carrier gas helium flow of 1.0 mL/min, using a split mode; split ratio was 5:1. The compound separation procedure was as follows: injector temperature 250 °C; column temperature start with 80 °C for 1 min, 290 °C for 38 min at a 20 °C/min increase; ion source temperature 200 °C and interface temperature set to 290 °C, mass spectra were acquired in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, using scan mode and monitoring the m/z range from 50 to 700 with a solvent delay of 15 min. GA standard (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for quantification of both GA, glycyrrhetaldehyde and 11-oxo-β-amyrin due to their structural similarity. Compound identification was performed by comparison of mass spectra with the NIST Mass Spectral Library and by comparison of retention time with authentic standards.

Measurement of cell fluorescence

Fermentation samples were centrifuged at 2348 × g for 2 min, and supernatant was disposed. 0.01 M phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (pH 7.4) was used to resuspend and wash the cells. Appropriate volume of PBS was used to resuspend cells to an OD600 range of 0.2 to 0.8. 100 μL of the samples was added in the 96-well plate and detected by the microplate reader. Cell fluorescence was determined with excitation/emission wavelengths to 580/610 nm. The fluorescence intensity of unit cell is ultimately used for evaluation.

Cell fluorescence was also determined using a flow cytometer (Bio-Rad S3eTM Cell Sorter). Cells were diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 106 cells/mL and screened through a 300-mesh cell sieve. After quality control, the filtered sample was placed on the sample stage and pushed to the run position. The gain for the FSC, SSC and FL3 channels was set to 210, 280 and 850, respectively. A total of 50,000 cells were counted per sample to determine the percentage of fluorescent cells. SynV and SynV-PTDH3-mcherry cells were used to establish the negative and positive boundaries, respectively.

Molecular docking and hydrogen bond analysis between GA and Pdr1

In this study, AlphaFold3 was employed to predict the structure of the S. cerevisiae Pdr1 homodimer, which served as the receptor for molecular docking with the GA ligand51. The Pdr1 sequence was obtained from NCBI, while the two-dimensional structure of the GA ligand was sourced from PubChem. Molecular docking, scoring, and ranking were performed using AutoDock Vina software to simulate the interaction between GA and Pdr1. During docking, Pdr1 served as the receptor and GA as the ligand. The docking region center coordinates for the design box were set to center_x = 0.561, center_y = −0.452, center_z = 0.258. The docking box dimensions were set to size_x = 71.75, size_y = 70.61, size_z = 71.75. The binding affinity and hydrogen bond interactions between Pdr1 and GA were analyzed.

Adaptive evolution of the GA producing yeast

The PPDR5-HIS3 expression cassette was constructed on the pRS415 plasmid and transformed into the high GA producing strain S. cerevisiae GA184. Positive clones were selected on the plate of SD-His-Leu media (2% glucose, 0.67% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 0.13% amino acid drop-out without histone and leucine, adjusting pH to 6.2). The engineered yeasts were cultured continuously in SD-His media (2% glucose, 0.67% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 0.13% amino acid drop-out without histone, adjusting pH to 6.2) with or without 3-AT (Sigma-Aldrich), as well as a control cultured in YPD media. Cells were transferred to the accordingly fresh media every 48 h at an initial OD600 of 0.2. Cell growth was monitored by sampling and measuring using a spectrophotometer. In the 3-AT addition group, the initial concentration of 3-AT was 10 mM, and the concentration of 3-AT was increased to 30 mM after 6 days and increased to 50 mM after 12 days. The evolutionary strains were harvested, inoculated into GM1 media and cultured for 5 days to determine triterpenoid production.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Samples of yeast cells from two biological replicates were pooled into a single trial. Yeast cells were collected by centrifuging at 3000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in PBS for counting. Cell wall was removed using 0.2 U Zymolyase (MCE) in a resuspension buffer v2.1 (MobiDrop, China). Then, cells were loaded into Microfluidic Chip A gle-cell RNA-Single-cell Kit v2.1 (MobiDrop) with MobiNova-100 (MobiDrop) to generate droplets. Each cell was involved into a droplet which contained a gel bead linked with up to millions oligos (cell unique barcode). After encapsulation, droplets suffered light cut by MobiNovaSP-100 (MobiDrop) while oligos diffuse into reaction mix. The mRNAs were captured by cell barcodes with cDNA amplification in droplets. Following reverse transcription, cDNAs with barcodes were amplified, and a library was constructed using the High Throughput Single-Cell 3′ Transcriptome Kit v2.1 (MobiDrop) and the 3′ Dual Index Kit (MobiDrop)52,53. The resulting libraries were sequenced on DNBSEQ-T7 platform with PE150 mode.

The single-cell RNA-seq raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject accession number PRJNA1415668. The raw sequencing data were analyzed by FastQC54 to check the sequencing quality. The MobiVision®-M software was used to quantify the expression levels of each gene in each cell. MobiVision®-M automatically ran following analysis: adapter removing, low quality reads filtering, barcoding, alignment, adjustment of multi-mapped reads, filtering low quality microbes, generating of report and expression matrix. Generally, following the pipeline of normal single-cell RNA-seq, the cluster analysis was performed by Seurat. The microbes with relatively low gene count or UMI count were filtered. The list of highly variable genes was selected by “FindVariableFeatures” function after normalization. Then, the expression matrix was scaled. The PCA analysis was performed based on variable genes. The cluster was separated by “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions. The results were visualized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) algorithms. “FindAllMarkers” and “FindMarkers” functions were used to analysis DEGs. The enrichment analysis started by creating customized database. The pathway or term related to each gene was annotated by eggNOG-mapper55 and eggNOG database56. Based on the annotation, the customized database was generated by R package “AnnotationForge”. The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed by R package “clusterProfiler”. The enrichment results were visualized by R package “ggplot2”. The analysis was based on the gene expression matrix with UMI count. The data was believed to follow a negative binomial distribution. The genes expressed in less than 10 microbes were removed. The genes used for analysis were identified by “FindAllMarkers” function of R package Seurat with P-value lower than 0.05.

Subgroup-representative cell sorting and fermentation

Based on single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis, distinct cellular subpopulations were identified in the evolved strain A8 and the control strain GA184Y, along with strain-specific marker genes. For each subpopulation, the top-3 marker genes with the most significant upregulation were selected based on statistical significance. The promoter region of the selected marker gene was cloned upstream of the green fluorescent protein coding sequence to construct a reporter expression cassette, which was then transformed into the evolved strain A8. After screening for positive transformants, they were cultured in GM1 fermentation medium for 5 days.

Flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting were performed using a BD FACSAria SORP flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Yeast cells were collected after cultivation, washed three times with sterile 1×PBS, and diluted to OD600 ≈ 0.5. The cell suspension was filtered through a 200-mesh sieve to remove clumps and large debris. For gating, the main yeast population was selected using FSC-A and SSC-A parameters (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Doublets and cell aggregates were excluded using FSC-A versus FSC-H, selecting the linear distribution of single cells. Green fluorescence intensity was measured using the 488 nm laser (FITC channel). The top 2% of cells with the highest fluorescence intensity were gated as the target subpopulation, and a control population was gated from cells with low fluorescence intensity and a concentrated distribution of events. For each sorted population, 20,000 cells were collected. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using FlowJo v10.9 software. All experiments were conducted in biological triplicates, and results are reported as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated.

The sorted cells were transferred to a 500 μL deep-well plate fermentation system and further cultured in GM1 medium for 5 days. Cell growth was assessed by measuring OD600 and the yields of triterpenoid products were quantitatively analyzed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were processed in Excel or GraphPad Prism. The significant comparisons of two different groups are indicated in the graphs statistical analysis was performed using two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). The graphs represented means ± SD (n = 3) unless otherwise indicated, as described in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analysis. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
