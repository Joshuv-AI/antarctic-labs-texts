Velocity Reconstruction from KSZ: Measuring f N ​ L f_{NL} with ACT and DESILS
Selim C. Hotinli, Kendrick M. Smith, Simone Ferraro

Abstract
The kinetic Sunyaev-Zel’dovich (kSZ) effect offers an indirect way to reconstruct large-scale cosmic velocities, by correlating high-resolution CMB temperature maps with galaxy surveys. In this work, we present the first three-dimensional reconstruction of the large-scale velocity field using a photometric galaxy survey, using data from the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys (DESILS) and the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) DR5. We detect an 11.7 ​ σ 11.7\sigma correlation between our velocity reconstruction and the galaxy field, using only DESILS LRGs in the northern Galactic hemisphere. We find that the overall amplitude of the kSZ-induced correlation is low relative to a halo model prediction ( b v = 0.45 − 0.05 + 0.06 b_{v}=0.45^{+0.06}_{-0.05} ), in agreement with previous results which find high feedback and smoothed gas profiles near massive galaxies. We use this measurement to place new constraints on local-type primordial non-Gaussianity (PNG), obtaining f NL = − 39 − 33 + 40 f_{\rm NL}\!=\!-39^{+40}_{-33} . This represents the most stringent f NL f_{\rm NL} constraint from kSZ velocity-based analyses to date. We validate our findings through extensive null tests, including tests for CMB foregrounds based on comparing 90 and 150 GHz CMB data.

A promising alternative is the kinetic Sunyaev-Zel’dovich (kSZ) effect, a secondary anisotropy in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) caused by the Doppler shifting of photons scattering off moving free electrons

Recently it has been shown that the kSZ effect can be used to reconstruct cosmic velocities, by cross-correlating CMB temperature maps with large-scale structure tracers, such as galaxies

II
Preliminaries

We denote 3-d comoving locations by
{\bf x}
(three-vector), and 2-d sky locations by
{\bm{\theta}}
(unit three-vector).
The line-of-sight direction (a unit three-vector oriented away from the observer) is denoted
{\bf\hat{x}}
.

We denote the comoving distance to redshift
z
by
\chi(z)
, and will sometimes implicitly change variable
\chi\leftrightarrow z
.
Our Fourier conventions are:

f({\bf x})=\int\frac{d^{3}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\tilde{f}({\bf k})e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}\hskip 42.67912pt\tilde{f}({\bf k})=\int d^{3}{\bf x}\,f({\bf x})e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}\,.

(2)

The kSZ contribution to the CMB can be written as a line-of-sight integral
Sunyaev and Zeldovich 1980
:

T_{\rm kSZ}({\bm{\theta}})=\int d\chi\,K(\chi)\,v_{r}(\chi{\bm{\theta}})\,\delta_{e}(\chi{\bm{\theta}})\,,

(3)

where
\chi
is comoving distance,
{\bm{\theta}}
is the line-of-sight direction (a unit three-vector),
\delta_{e}(\chi{\bm{\theta}})
is the electron overdensity and
v_{r}(\chi{\bm{\theta}})
is the radial velocity.
The quantity
K(z)
appearing in Eq. (
3
) is defined by

K(z)\equiv-T_{\rm CMB}\,\sigma_{T}\,n_{e0}\,x_{e}(z)\,e^{-\tau(z)}\,(1+z)^{2}\,,

(4)

where
T_{\rm CMB}
is the mean CMB temperature,
\sigma_{T}
is the Thomson cross-section,
n_{e0}
is the mean electron density today,
x_{e}(z)
is the ionization fraction at redshift
z
and
\tau(z)
is the optical depth to redshift
z
.

In an
f_{\rm NL}\neq 0
cosmology, galaxy bias acquires an extra term on large scales
Dalal et al. 2008
;
Slosar et al. 2008
:

b(k)=b_{g}+2\delta_{c}(b_{g}-1)\frac{f_{\rm NL}}{\alpha(k,z)}\hskip 42.67912pt\mbox{where }\alpha(k,z)=\frac{\delta_{m}({\bf k},z)}{\Phi({\bf k},z)}=\frac{2k^{2}T(k)D(z)}{3\Omega_{m0}H_{0}^{2}}\,.

(5)

Here,
T(k)
is the matter transfer function,
D(z)
the linear growth function,
\Omega_{m0}
is the present-day matter density,
H_{0}
is the Hubble constant, and
\delta_{c}=1.68
is the collapse threshold.
This leads to an enhancement of clustering on large scales proportional to
(f_{NL}/k^{2})
, since
\alpha(k,z)\propto k^{2}
as
k\rightarrow 0
.

III
High-level pipeline description

In this section, we describe features of our kSZ velocity reconstruction pipeline which are not specific to the datasets under consideration (ACT and DESI-LS).

III.1
The KSZ velocity reconstruction estimator
\hat{v}_{r}

The first step in our pipeline is the kSZ quadratic estimator
\hat{v}_{r}
, which reconstructs the large-scale velocity field (up to a multiplicative bias
b_{v}
). The inputs are the 3-d galaxy field
\delta_{g}({\bf x})
and the 2-d CMB
T({\bm{\theta}})
, and the output is a 3-d field
\hat{v}_{r}({\bf x})
which reconstructs the velocity field on large scales.

To motivate the definition of
\hat{v}_{r}
that we use in our pipeline, we start from the expression for
\hat{v}_{r}
in
Smith et al. 2018
, which applies to a simplified flat-sky “snapshot” geometry with no sky cut:

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf k}_{L})\propto\int_{{\bf k}_{S}+({\bf l}/\chi_{*})={\bf k}_{L}}\frac{P_{ge}(k_{S})}{P_{gg}^{\rm tot}(k_{S})}\frac{1}{C_{l}^{\rm tot}}\Big(\delta_{g}({\bf k}_{S})T_{\rm CMB}({\bf l})\Big)\,.

(6)

Here,
\chi_{*}
is comoving distance to the galaxies (assumed constant in this simplified snapshot geometry),
C_{\ell}^{\rm tot}
is the total CMB power spectrum (including foregrounds and noise),
P_{ge}(k)
is the electron-galaxy correlation, and
P_{gg}^{\rm tot}(k)
is the total galaxy power spectrum (including Poisson noise).
We have used the notation
{\bf k}_{L},{\bf k}_{S}
to emphasize that the LHS is usually evaluated at large scales
k_{L}\sim 10^{-2}
Mpc
-1
, and the integrand on the RHS peaks on small scales
k_{S}\sim 1
Mpc
-1
.

As written, Eq. (
6
) involves the small-scale galaxy field
\delta_{g}({\bf k}_{S})
, which would be memory and compute intensive to store at high resolution (
k_{S}\sim 1
Mpc
-1
).
Following
Laguë et al. 2024
, we will make the approximation
k_{S}\approx l/\chi_{*}
inside the integral.
This is an excellent approximation in the squeezed limit
k_{L}\ll k_{S}
, and allows
\hat{v}_{r}
to be written this way:

\displaystyle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf k}_{L})

\displaystyle\propto\int_{{\bf k}_{S}+({\bf l}/\chi_{*})={\bf k}_{L}}\frac{P_{ge}(l/\chi_{*})}{P_{gg}^{\rm tot}(l/\chi_{*})}\frac{1}{C_{l}^{\rm tot}}\Big(\delta_{g}({\bf k}_{S})T_{\rm CMB}({\bf l})\Big)\,.

(7)

Or, switching from Fourier space to real space:

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=\sum_{i\in\rm gal}{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i})\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i})\,,

(8)

where
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
is the angular location of the
i
-th galaxy, and the high-pass filtered CMB
{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}})
is defined by:

{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}})=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf l}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{\widetilde{T}}({\bf l})e^{i{\bf l}\cdot{\bm{\theta}}}\,,\hskip 42.67912pt{\widetilde{T}}({\bf l})\equiv\frac{P_{ge}(l/\chi_{*})}{P_{gg}(l/\chi_{*})}\frac{1}{C_{l}^{\rm tot}}T_{\rm CMB}({\bf l})\,.

(9)

The point of writing
\hat{v}_{r}
in the form (
8
) is to reduce computational requirements, by avoiding the need to store high-resolution 3-d maps such as
\delta_{g}({\bf k}_{S})
.
In code, the field
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
can be represented as either a weighted catalog (with per-galaxy weight
\smash{{\widetilde{T}}(\theta_{i})}
, or a 3-d field in a low-resolution pixelization (after “gridding” the weighted catalog).

So far, we have used a simplified snapshot geometry (following
Smith et al. 2018
).
Next, we will generalize to a lightcone geometry with a sky cut.
We will also introduce two weight functions: a CMB pixel weight function
W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})
, and a per-galaxy weight
W_{i}^{v}
.
In our ACT-DESILS pipeline,
W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})
will be used to downweight regions of high noise or large CMB foreground emission, and
W_{i}^{v}
will be used to downweight galaxies with large photo-
z
errors (see §
IV
).

First, we define the all-sky filtered CMB
{\widetilde{T}}_{lm}
by:

{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{lm}{\widetilde{T}}_{lm}Y_{lm}({\bm{\theta}})\,,\hskip 28.45274pt{\widetilde{T}}_{lm}=F_{l}\,\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})\,T_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})\,Y_{lm}^{*}({\bm{\theta}})\,,\hskip 28.45274ptF_{l}\equiv\frac{P_{ge}(l/\chi_{*})}{P_{gg}(l/\chi_{*})}\frac{b_{l}}{C_{l}^{\rm tot}}\,.

(10)

This is just the all-sky version of Eq. (
9
), with the pixel weight function
W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})
included by hand.
Then, we define
\hat{v}_{r}
by:

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=\sum_{i\in\rm gal}W_{i}^{v}\,{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i})\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i})\,,

(11)

following Eq. (
8
), with the per-galaxy weighting
W_{i}^{v}
included by hand.
In implementation, we only keep terms in the sum (
11
) where
W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})>0
.

Empirically, we find that in order to mitigate CMB foregrounds, the above construction must be modified, by subtracting the mean temperature
{\widetilde{T}}(\theta_{i})
within redshift bins of size
\Delta z\sim 0.1
.
More precisely, for each redshift bin
b
, let
{\widetilde{T}}_{b}
be the mean value of
{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i})
over all galaxies in the bin:

{\widetilde{T}}_{b}\equiv\frac{\sum_{i\in b}W_{i}^{v}{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i})}{\sum_{i\in b}W_{i}^{v}}\,.

(12)

Then, we define a “mean-subtracted” velocity reconstruction
\hat{v}_{r}({\bf x})
by:

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=\sum_{b}\sum_{i\in b}W_{i}^{v}\,\big({\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\widetilde{T}}_{b}\big)\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i})\,.

(13)

To motivate this mean-subtraction step intuitively, consider a CMB foreground which is correlated with the galaxy survey (such as tSZ, CIB, etc.)
On average, such a foreground will make an additive contribution to the value of
{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{i})
at the locations of the galaxies, in a way which is slowly varying in redshift and independent of radial velocity
v_{r}
.
If
{\hat{v}}_{r}
is defined without mean subtraction (Eq. (
11
)) then such a foreground makes a systematic contribution to
{\hat{v}}_{r}
(more precisely,
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
gets a term which is proportional to
\delta_{g}({\bf x})
).
On the other hand, if
{\hat{v}}_{r}
is defined with mean subtraction (Eq. (
13
)) then the CMB foreground cancels on average.

A subtlety: the mean subtraction step in Eq. (
13
) can potentially bias power spectrum estimates, such as the cross correlation
P_{gv_{r}}(k)
with a galaxy field.
At field level, mean subtraction is the same thing as “projecting out modes” from the 3-d field
\hat{v}_{r}({\bf x})
.
More precisely, any 3-d mode which is slowly varying in redshift, and constant in angular coordinates
{\bm{\theta}}
, is projected out.
This is very similar to the “radial integral constraint” for a galaxy field
de Mattia and Ruhlmann-Kleider 2019
, which removes modes and can bias the power spectrum.
In §
III.4
we will explain our method for removing this bias, and accounting for mode removal when assigning error bars.

The velocity reconstruction
\hat{v}_{r}({\bf x})
is a biased reconstruction of the radial velocity field, but the overall normalization is nontrivial to derive.
In Appendix
D
we show that the normalization can be written (with some approximations that are explained in the appendix) as:

\big\langle\hat{v}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle=b_{v}\,\bar{n}_{v}({\bf x})\,B({\bf x})\,v_{r}^{\rm true}({\bf x})\,,

(14)

where
\bar{n}_{v}(x)
is the 3-d galaxy number density including the per-object weighting
W_{i}^{v}
, defined formally by:

n_{v}({\bf x})\equiv\left\langle\sum_{i}W_{i}^{v}\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i})\right\rangle\,,

(15)

and the function
B({\bf x})
is defined by:

B({\bf x})\equiv W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})\,B_{0}(\chi)\,,\hskip 42.67912ptB_{0}(\chi)\equiv\frac{K(\chi)}{\chi^{2}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf L}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,b_{L}\,F_{L}\,P_{ge}^{\rm fid}(k,\chi)_{k=L/\chi}\,,

(16)

where
b_{L}
is the CMB beam, and the quantities
K(\chi)
,
F_{L}
were defined in Eqs. (
4
), (
10
).

Finally, the “kSZ velocity bias”
b_{v}
in Eq. (
14
) is an overall prefactor that is roughly the ratio between the true galaxy-electron power spectrum
P_{ge}^{\rm true}(k)
and the fiducial galaxy-electron power spectrum
P_{ge}^{\rm fid}(k)
used to define the estimator in Eq. (
10
).
The precise definition is:

b_{v}(\chi)=\frac{\int(d^{2}{\bf L}/(2\pi)^{2}))\,b_{L}F_{L}P_{ge}^{\rm true}(k,\chi)_{k=L/\chi}}{\int(d^{2}{\bf L}/(2\pi)^{2}))\,b_{L}F_{L}P_{ge}^{\rm fid}(k,\chi)_{k=L/\chi}}\,.

(17)

In our pipeline, we will treat
b_{v}
as a nuisance parameter and marginalize it (neglecting redshift dependence).
The presence of such a nuisance parameter is a general feature of kSZ velocity reconstruction
Smith et al. 2018
.

Finally, we comment on the normalization of
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
.
Our definition of
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
in Eqs. (
10
), (
11
) uses an arbitrary normalization – e.g.
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
has units Mpc
-3

\mu
K
-1
, whereas the physical radial velocity field
v_{r}({\bf x})
is dimensionless.
The function
B({\bf x})
defined in Eq. (
16
) keeps track of the normalization which relates
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
and
v_{r}({\bf x})
.
In the next section, we’ll define a power spectrum estimator
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
which is physically normalized (via the quantity
{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}
, appearing in Eq. (
19
) and defined in Appendix
D
, which contains one power of
B({\bf x})
.)

III.2
The power spectrum estimator
P_{gv}
(k)

We define the three-dimensional large-scale galaxy field
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
through the ‘
galaxies-minus-randoms
’ prescription:

\rho_{g}({\bf x})\equiv\!\!\bigg(\sum_{i\in\rm gal}\!W_{i}^{g}\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i})\bigg)\!-\!\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\bigg(\sum_{j\in\rm rand}\!\!\!W_{j}^{g}\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})\bigg)\,,

(18)

where
W_{i}^{g}
is a per-galaxy weight (e.g. FKP), and
N_{g}
and
N_{r}
are the number of galaxies in the data catalog and randoms, respectively.
Note that we distinguish notationally between the weights
W_{i}^{g}
and
W_{i}^{v}
used in the galaxy field
\rho_{g}
and velocity reconstruction
\hat{v}_{r}
(in Eqs. (
13
), (
18
)).
However, in our DESILS pipeline, we choose the weights
W_{i}^{g}=W_{i}^{v}
to be equal (Eq. (
39
)).

We define the power spectrum estimators
{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k)
and
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
by:

{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k)\equiv\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}}\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\,\rho_{g}^{*}({\bf k})\,\rho_{g}({\bf k})\,,\hskip 42.67912pt{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)\equiv\frac{3}{{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}}\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\,\rho_{g}^{*}({\bf k})\,{\hat{v}}_{1}({\bf k})\,,

(19)

where the field
{\hat{v}}_{1}({\bf k})
is defined in terms of the reconstructed radial velocity
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
as

{\hat{v}}_{1}({\bf k})\equiv\int_{{\bf x}}(i{\bf\hat{k}}_{j}\cdot{\bf\hat{x}}_{j})\,{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\,e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}\,.

(20)

The estimator
\hat{P}_{gv}(k)
defined in Eq. (
19
) is the standard “dipolar” power spectrum estimator, for cross-correlating a scalar field
\rho_{g}
with an “
\ell=1
” field
\hat{v}_{r}
, in a wide-angle geometry
Scoccimarro 2015
;
Hand et al. 2017
.

The normalizing prefactors
{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}
,
{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}
in Eq. (
19
) are defined in Appendix
A
, and are intended to approximately correct for the survey geometry.
This is an approximation, since the true effect of the survey geometry is
k
-dependent (and also slightly non-diagonal in
k
: the estimator
\smash{{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)}
receives contributions from
P_{gv}(k^{\prime})
at wavenumbers
k^{\prime}\neq k
).
Therefore, our power spectrum estimator
\smash{{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)}
is not a perfectly unbiased estimator of
P_{gv}(k)
.

This is an issue, since the main goal of this paper is to compare the values of
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
to a two-parameter model for
P_{gv}(k)
:

P_{gv}(k)=b_{v}\left(b_{g}+f_{\rm NL}\frac{2\delta_{c}(b_{g}-1)}{\alpha(k,z)}\right)\frac{faH}{k}P_{\rm lin}(k)\,.

(21)

If
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
is not strictly unbiased, then comparing to a model
P_{gv}(k)
is not straightforward.

One approach would be to modify the definition of the estimator
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
to make it strictly unbiased, by computing and deconvolving the effect of the survey geometry.
In future work, we plan to pursue this approach, which is appealing but nontrivial (e.g. in
Laguë et al. 2024
it is argued that existing approximations break down in the lowest
k
-bin of SDSS, and improved approximations are needed).

In this paper, we will use a different approach: rather than deconvolving the estimator
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
, we will convolve the model
P_{gv}(k)
with the survey geometry.
That is, we leave the estimator
\smash{{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)}
in the simple form defined by Eq. (
19
), but whenever we compare the estimator to a model, we compute the estimator response
\smash{\langle\hat{P}_{gv}(k)\rangle}
to the model
P_{gv}(k)
, in a way which precisely accounts for the survey geometry.
We’ll do this using the machinery of “surrogate fields”, which we describe next.

III.3
Surrogate fields, part 1: galaxy field

In this section and the next, we introduce
surrogate fields
, which will play a central role in our pipeline.
A “surrogate field” is a simplified random field that preserves the exact field-level covariance
\langle\delta({\bf x})\delta({\bf x}^{\prime})\rangle
of a more complicated random field (including survey geometry, light-cone evolution, and photometric redshift uncertainties).
This is in contrast to a “mock”, which is a realistic simulation that captures higher-order statistics (beyond the two-point function).
We will argue that for purposes of this paper, where analysis is limited to large scales (
k\lesssim 0.02
Mpc
-1
), it suffices to use simplified surrogate fields in place of mocks.

It is easiest to describe surrogate fields by example.
Consider the galaxy field
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
, which can be modeled on large scales as:

\rho_{g}({\bf k},z)=\bar{n}_{g}(z)\big[1+\delta_{G}({\bf k},z)\big]+(\mbox{Poisson noise})\,,

(22)

where
\delta_{G}
is the Gaussian field:

\delta_{G}({\bf k},z)\equiv\left(b_{g}(z)+f_{\rm NL}\frac{2\delta_{c}(b_{g}(z)-1)}{\alpha(k,z)}\right)\delta_{\rm lin}({\bf k},z)\,.

(23)

In this context, a “mock” is a catalog of objects such that the corresponding sum of delta functions
\sum_{i}W_{i}^{g}\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i})
is described on large scales by the model in Eq. (
22
).
A “surrogate field” can be any random field
S_{g}({\bf x})
(not necessarily a sum of delta functions) whose field-level covariance
\langle S_{g}({\bf x})S_{g}({\bf x}^{\prime})\rangle
matches the covariance
\langle\rho_{g}({\bf x})\rho_{g}({\bf x}^{\prime})\rangle
.
Note that
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
is defined with the survey mask applied, so the surrogate field must fully match the survey geometry, in order to match the field-level covariance.

We construct such a surrogate field
S_{g}({\bf x})
as follows.
Our goal is to write down the simplest possible random field that has the same covariance as
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
, assuming the clustering model (
22
).
Since this model consists of two terms (a signal term and a Poisson noise term), our surrogate field will consist of two terms:

S_{g}({\bf x})=S_{g}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})+S_{g}^{\rm noise}({\bf x})\,.

(24)

We define the signal term
S_{g}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})
as follows.
In each Monte Carlo iteration, we randomly simulate a
Gaussian
field
\delta_{G}({\bf x})
directly from its definition in Eq. (
23
).
Then, we define
S_{g}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})
by:

S_{g}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})\equiv\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{g}\,\delta_{G}({\bf x}_{j})\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})\,.

(25)

Note that we sum over randoms, not galaxies!
The purpose of the random catalog is to capture the survey geometry and redshift dependence.

Similarly, we define the noise term
S_{g}^{\rm noise}({\bf x})
by adding Gaussian white noise, whose variance is chosen to give the correct (Poisson) power spectrum
P_{gg}(k)
at high
k
:

S_{g}^{\rm noise}({\bf x})=\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{g}\,\eta_{j}\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})\,,

(26)

where
\eta_{j}
is an independent (for each
j
) Gaussian random variable with variance:

\big\langle\eta_{j}^{2}\big\rangle=\frac{N_{r}}{N_{g}}-\big\langle\delta_{G}(z_{j})^{2}\big\rangle\,.

(27)

The surrogate field
S_{g}({\bf x})
defined by Eqs. (
24
)–(
27
) has a covariance
\langle S_{g}({\bf x})S_{g}({\bf x}^{\prime})\rangle
which agrees with the model in Eqs. (
22
), (
23
).
This intuitively plausible statement is proved rigorously in Appendix
B
.

Note that the surrogate field
S_{g}({\bf x})
defined by Eqs. (
24
)–(
27
) does not really resemble a mock catalog.
The locations of the delta functions are unclustered (i.e. the sum in Eq. (
25
) runs over randoms, not galaxies), and
S_{g}({\bf x})
gets clustering power by “painting” the Gaussian field
\delta_{G}({\bf x})
onto the randoms.
The surrogate field
S_{g}({\bf x})
does not have a preferred sign (in contrast to a mock, which is a sum of delta functions).
Nevertheless, the surrogate field can be used in place of a mock, in situations where the covariance is important and higher-order correlation functions are not.
It may help to think of the surrogate field as a Monte Carlo representation of the field-level covariance, not a simulated dataset.

Why do we need surrogate fields?
In our pipeline, surrogate fields solve several problems at once:

•

Computing estimator expectation values
\langle{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k)\rangle
or
\langle{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)\rangle
, given a model for the underlying random fields (as mentioned at the end of the previous section).
Since such expectation values only depend on the field-level covariance, they can be computed by taking an average over simulated surrogate fields (which have the correct covariance by construction).

•

Estimating estimator covariance, for example
\mbox{Cov}({\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k),{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k^{\prime}))
, by taking a Monte Carlo average over surrogates.
It is not obvious
a priori
that this is a good approximation, since the power spectrum covariance includes a “disconnected” contribution from the 4PCF, which may not be accurately captured by surrogate fields.
In this paper, we limit our analysis to large scales (
k\lesssim 0.02
Mpc
-1
), where large-scale structure fields are Gaussian, and the 4PCF is expected to be small.
In the context of kSZ velocity reconstruction, it has been shown in
Giri and Smith 2020
using full
N
-body simulations that
\hat{v}_{r}
is a Gaussian field to a good approximation on such scales.

As a further check that surrogates accurately approximate power spectrum covariance on large scales, we compared
\smash{\mbox{Cov}({\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k),{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k^{\prime}))}
using surrogates and full mocks, in SDSS (not DESILS, since mocks are not available for DESILS).
We found that the surrogates accurately approximate both the diagonal and the off-diagonal covariance, for
k_{\rm max}=0.02
Mpc
-1
.
The detailed comparison is given in Appendix
C
.

•

Parameter dependence of expectation values and covariance.
As a concrete example, for the galaxy field
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
considered in this section, both the expectation value
\smash{\langle{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k)\rangle}
and the covariance
\smash{\mbox{Cov}({\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k),{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k^{\prime}))}
depend on the model parameter
f_{\rm NL}
.
In principle, this
f_{\rm NL}
dependence can be determined by running surrogate simulations with different values of
f_{\rm NL}
, where
S_{g}({\bf x})
depends on
f_{\rm NL}
via the second term in Eq. (
23
).

As an optimization, we note that
S_{g}({\bf x})
depends linearly on
f_{\rm NL}
:

S_{g}({\bf x})=S_{g}^{(0)}({\bf x})+f_{\rm NL}\,S_{g}^{(1)}({\bf x})\,.

(28)

In our pipeline, we generate the fields
S_{g}^{(0)}({\bf x})
, and
S_{g}^{(1)}({\bf x})
separately in each Monte Carlo simulation, so that the full
f_{\rm NL}
dependence of
\smash{\langle{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k)\rangle}
and the covariance
\smash{\mbox{Cov}({\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k),{\widehat{P}}_{gg}(k^{\prime}))}
can be computed, without running new simulations for each
f_{\rm NL}
value of interest.
A similar optimization is used in the next section, to compute the
b_{v}
dependence of
\hat{P}_{gv}
and its covariance.

The primary advantage of surrogate fields lies in their ease of construction compared to realistic mocks.
In particular, one challenge of working with DESILS is that the mocks are not publicly available, and making realistic DESILS mocks “from scratch” would be a daunting proposition.
On the other hand, surrogate simulations are fairly straightforward to implement, computationally inexpensive (a few CPU-minutes per simulation), and suffice for the large-scale analysis
(k\lesssim 0.02
Mpc
-1
) of this paper.

III.4
Surrogate fields, part 2: KSZ velocity reconstruction

In the previous section, we showed how to construct a surrogate field
S_{g}({\bf x})
with the same field-level covariance as the galaxy density field
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
.
Analogously, in this section we will show how to construct a surrogate field
S_{v}({\bf x})
with the same covariance as the kSZ velocity reconstruction
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
.

Our starting point is the following large-scale model for
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
, introduced previously in Eq. (
14
):

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=b_{v}\,\bar{n}_{v}({\bf x})\,B({\bf x})\,v_{r}^{\rm true}({\bf x})+(\mbox{kSZ reconstruction noise})\,.

(29)

Since this model consists of two terms (a signal term and a noise term), our surrogate field will consist of two terms:

S_{v}({\bf x})=S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})+S_{v}^{\rm noise}({\bf x})\,.

(30)

We define the signal term
S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})
as follows.
In each Monte Carlo simulation, let
v_{r}^{\rm sim}({\bf x})
be the (linear) radial velocity field.
Then we define
S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})
by as a sum over randoms (not galaxies):

S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})\equiv\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{v}B({\bf x}_{j})\,v_{r}^{\rm sim}({\bf x}_{j})\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})\,,

(31)

where
B({\bf x})
was defined in Eq. (
16
).

Next, we consider the noise term
S_{v}^{\rm noise}({\bf x})
.
By definition, kSZ reconstruction noise is the contribution to
\hat{v}_{r}
that would arise if the CMB were statistically independent of the 3-d large-scale structure.
One approach to simulating reconstruction noise would be to simulate a random CMB realization (signal + noise) in each Monte Carlo realization.
This is nontrivial since it involves modeling details such as anisotropic noise and foreground correlations between 90 and 150 GHz.
We use a simpler approach: a bootstrap procedure based on the real ACT maps.
In each Monte Carlo realization, we choose a random size-
N_{g}
subset
S
of the random catalog
\{1,\cdots,N_{r}\}
.
Then we define a bootstrap estimate of the reconstruction noise by keeping only randoms in
S
:

S_{v}^{\rm noise}({\bf x})\equiv\sum_{j\in\rm rand}M_{j}\,W_{j}^{v}\,{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{j})\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{where }M_{j}\equiv\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if $j\in S$}\\
0&\mbox{ if $j\not\in S$}\end{cases}

(32)

where
{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}}_{j})
is the high-pass-filtered ACT map (not a simulated ACT map), defined previously in Eq. (
10
).

In Appendix
D
, we show that the surrogate field
S_{v}({\bf x})
defined above correctly models the field-level covariance of
\hat{v}_{r}({\bf x})
.
In our pipeline, we will use surrogate fields to estimate
\smash{\langle{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)\rangle}
and
\smash{\mbox{Cov}({\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k),{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k^{\prime}))}
by Monte Carlo, including dependence on the parameters
(f_{\rm NL},b_{v})
.
We highlight the following aspects of our surrogate field construction:

•

The surrogate fields
S_{g}({\bf x})
and
S_{v}({\bf x})
will be correlated on large scales.
This is because the surrogate fields are constructed from Gaussian fields
\delta_{G}({\bf x})
and
v_{r}^{\rm sim}({\bf x})
, which are highly correlated since they are generated from the same realization of the linear density field.
The large-scale correlation between
S_{g}({\bf x})
and
S_{v}({\bf x})
is important when using surrogate simulations to compute
\smash{\langle{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)\rangle}
, or sample variance contributions to
\smash{\mbox{Cov}({\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k),{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k^{\prime}))}
.

•

We simulate surrogate fields
S_{v}({\bf x})
for 90 and 150 GHz CMB maps.
The “signal” components
S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})
are 100% correlated, since the radial velocity
v_{r}^{\rm sim}({\bf x})
is the same.
Less obviously, the “noise” components
S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})
are also correlated, since the underlying CMB maps
\smash{{\widetilde{T}}_{90}({\bm{\theta}}})
and
\smash{{\widetilde{T}}_{150}({\bm{\theta}})}
are correlated due to shared CMB and foregrounds.
The signal and noise correlations will be important later, when combining 90 and 150 GHz to get joint constraints.

One more detail: mode removal in surrogate fields.
Recall from §
III.1
that when we compute
{\hat{v}}_{r}
on data, we introduced a mean-subtraction step (Eq. (
11
)) in order to mitigate CMB foregrounds.
This effectively projects out modes in the 3-d field
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
.
To make our surrogate simulations consistent, we perform the same mean-subtraction step on the surrogate field
S_{v}({\bf x})
.
Then, when we use surrogate simulations to compute the mean
\smash{{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)}
and assign error bars, the surrogate sims will account for biases due to mode removal in
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
.

Similarly, our density field
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
has been constructed via a “galaxies minus randoms” procedure (Eq. (
18
)) which effectively removes modes, since the redshift distribution of the randoms is identical to the galaxies.
(This type of mode removal in a galaxy field is sometimes called the “radial integral constraint”
de Mattia and Ruhlmann-Kleider 2019
.)
To account for this mode removal in
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
, we apply the same mean-subtraction step to the surrogate field
S_{g}({\bf x})
that we do to
S_{v}({\bf x})
.

III.5
Photometric redshift errors

So far, we have assumed spectroscopic redshifts for simplicity.
Extending the velocity reconstruction technique to photometric surveys, which offer broader sky coverage but have less precise redshift information, introduces additional challenges.
While the statistical power for detecting primordial signatures like PNG increases with access to larger cosmic volumes, measurement sensitivity diminishes on radial scales comparable to or smaller than redshift errors.
In this section, we describe the pipeline changes needed for a photometric survey.

For each galaxy, let
z_{\rm obs}
,
z_{\rm true}
denote the observed (photometric) and true redshift, respectively.
Let
\sigma_{z}
denote the photo-
z
error, which we do not assume is the same for all galaxies. (In the DESILS-LRG sample,
\sigma_{z}
varies significantly between galaxies – see Fig.
3
.)

The galaxy catalog contains
(z_{\rm obs},\sigma_{z})
values for each object, but
z_{\rm true}
is unknown.
For the random catalog, we randomly assign a
(z_{\rm obs},z_{\rm true},\sigma_{z})
triple to each object.
To do this, we deconvolve the observed 2-d
(z_{\rm obs},\sigma_{z})
galaxy catalog, in order to infer the underlying 3-d
(z_{\rm obs},z_{\rm true},\sigma_{z})
distribution.

For each object in a photometric catalog, let
{\bf x}_{\rm obs}=({\bm{\theta}},z_{\rm obs})
and
{\bf x}_{\rm true}=({\bm{\theta}},z_{\rm true})
denote the comoving 3-d positions at observed/true redshifts respectively.
The fields
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
and
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
(defined previously in Eqs. (
11
), (
18
)) are defined by placing delta functions at observed (not true) locations:

\displaystyle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{i\in b}W_{i}^{v}\,\big({\widetilde{T}}(\theta_{i})-{\widetilde{T}}_{b}\big)\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm obs})\,,

\displaystyle\rho_{g}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{i\in\rm gal}\!W_{i}^{g}\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm obs})\bigg)\!-\!\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\bigg(\sum_{j\in\rm rand}\!\!\!W_{j}^{g}\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j}^{\rm obs})\bigg)\,.

(33)

We also allow the per-object weights
W_{i}^{g}
,
W_{i}^{v}
to be functions of two variables (
z_{\rm obs},\sigma_{z})
. (For a spectroscopic catalog,
W_{i}^{g}
, and
W_{i}^{v}
would be functions of
z
.)

Our procedure for simulating surrogate fields
S_{g}({\bf x})
,
S_{v}({\bf x})
in Eqs. (
25
), (
31
) is slightly modified as follows:

\displaystyle S_{g}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{g}\,\delta_{G}({\bf x}_{j}^{\rm true})\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j}^{\rm obs})\,,

(34)

\displaystyle S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{v}B({\bf x}_{j}^{\rm true})\,v_{r}({\bf x}_{j}^{\rm true})\,\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j}^{\rm obs})\,,

(35)

where
B({\bf x})
was defined in Eq. (
16
).
That is, delta functions
\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})
are placed at observed locations, whereas random fields
\delta_{g}
,
v_{r}
are evaluated at true locations.
In Appendices
B
,
D
we show that this procedure correctly models the effect of photometric redshift errors on the clustering statistics of
S_{g}({\bf x})
and
S_{v}({\bf x})
respectively.

VI
Discussion

In this paper, we have presented the first 3-d kSZ velocity reconstruction using a photometric galaxy sample, using (ACT DR5)
\times
(DESILS LRGs), restricted to the northern galactic hemisphere in order to mitigate imaging systematics.
Our analysis represents the first of its kind, leveraging the full three-dimensional galaxy density field derived from photometric redshift measurements. This approach enables novel synergies between cosmic microwave background (CMB) observations and large-scale structure surveys, facilitating unprecedented cross-correlations between disparate cosmological probes.

We find SNR
11.7\sigma
, matching other kSZ-based analyses on similar datasets
Hadzhiyska et al. 2024
.
Our constraints on
f_{\rm NL}
are the most stringent to date (
f_{\rm NL}\!=\!-39^{+40}_{-33}
) from the emerging velocity tomography program.
We also find that the overall amplitude of the galaxy-electron power spectrum is lower than a halo model prediction (
b_{v}=0.45^{+0.06}_{-0.05}
).
Our measurement of
P_{gv}(k)
passes many goodness-of-fit and null tests, including consistency (within statistical errors) between 90 GHz and 150 GHz (§
V.3
, §
V.4
).

In future work, we plan to extend this analysis in several directions as follows:

•

In this paper, we considered the galaxy-velocity power spectrum
P_{gv}(k)
, in order to report parameter constraints from kSZ alone, without including information from galaxy clustering.
Future applications of this methodology (e.g. in LSST) will combine the
P_{gg}
,
P_{gv}
, and
P_{vv}
power spectra to cancel large-scale cosmic variance, thereby enhancing statistical precision. This technique is expected to surpass the constraints on PNG obtained from
P_{gg}
alone in the near future
Münchmeyer et al. 2019
.

•

We did not make a systematic effort to construct optimal estimators or maximize SNR.
(In particular, we chose to throw out the Galactic southern hemisphere, in order to reduce imaging systematics and pass null tests.)
It would be interesting to construct optimal
(S+N)^{-1}
weighted estimators and compare, especially in the high SNR regime where sample variance cancellation is important.

•

We introduced surrogate fields as a technical tool, in order to model the effect of the survey window function on the largest scales, and to estimate error bars.
In work in progress, we are developing new tools to model the large-scale window function, and to make non-Gaussian mock catalogs in very large volumes.
In particular, mock catalogs with
f_{NL}\neq 0
will be valuable for Monte Carlo pipeline validation, and mocks with simulated CMB foregrounds will be valuable for exploring the impact of foregrounds.

•

We have neglected RSDs, since we expect their effect to be small for a photometric survey, but properly accounting for RSDs will be important for future analyses involving spectroscopic surveys (e.g. DESI).

Our measurement introduces a powerful framework for testing fundamental physics, including early-universe models and inflationary dynamics. More broadly, this work establishes a novel observational paradigm that integrates CMB secondary anisotropies with the statistical power of photometric large-scale structure surveys, paving the way for transformative advancements in precision cosmology.

Appendix A
Power spectrum normalization

The purpose of this appendix is to explain how the power spectrum normalizations
{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}
,
{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}
defined in Eq. (
19
) are computed.
We emphasize that our constraints on
f_{NL}
and
b_{v}
do not depend on the values of
{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}
,
{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}
.
Parameter constraints are obtained by comparing values of
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
on data and surrogate simulations, and are independent of the overall normalization of
{\widehat{P}}_{gv}(k)
.
The normalizations
{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}
,
{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}
are only used so that power spectrum plots (Figs
4
,
5
) will have a sensible normalization.

First, some general comments on power spectrum normalization in a finite survey volume.
Let
\phi({\bf x})
,
\phi^{\prime}({\bf x})
be random fields, and suppose we are interested in the cross power spectrum
P_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)
.
The simplest power spectrum estimator
{\widehat{P}}_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)
which makes sense in a finite survey volume is defined by:

{\widehat{P}}_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)\equiv\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\,{\widetilde{\phi}}({\bf k})^{*}{\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}({\bf k})\hskip 28.45274pt{\widetilde{\phi}}({\bf k})\equiv\int d^{3}{\bf x}\,W({\bf x})\phi({\bf x})\hskip 28.45274pt{\widetilde{\phi}}^{\prime}({\bf k})\equiv\int d^{3}{\bf x}\,W^{\prime}({\bf x})\phi^{\prime}({\bf x})

(47)

where the (non-random) weight functions
W({\bf x})
,
W^{\prime}({\bf x})
represent the survey geometry (which can be different for the two fields).

How is the estimated power spectrum
{\widehat{P}}_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)
related to the true power spectrum
P_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)
?
A short calculation shows:

\big\langle{\widehat{P}}_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)\big\rangle=\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\int\frac{d^{3}{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\big[W({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})W^{\prime}({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})^{*}\big]\,P_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k^{\prime})

(48)

For slowly varying window functions, the quantity
\big[W({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})W^{\prime}({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})^{*}\big]
will be a narrowly peaked function of its argument
({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})
, and we can make the approximation:

\big\langle{\widehat{P}}_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)\big\rangle\approx{\mathcal{N}}_{WW^{\prime}}P_{\phi\phi}(k)\hskip 42.67912pt\mbox{where }{\mathcal{N}}_{WW^{\prime}}\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}{\bf K}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,W({\bf K})\,W^{\prime}({\bf K})^{*}

(49)

Note that the exact relation (
48
) between
{\widehat{P}}_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k)
and
P_{\phi\phi^{\prime}}(k^{\prime})
involves a convolution which mixes
(k,k^{\prime})
, whereas the approximation in (
49
) treats the relation as an overall constant.
This approximation suffices for purposes of this paper, where normalization is just used for plotting.

Now we specialize this general construction to the case of galaxy and velocity fields.
Consider the fields
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
and
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
defined in Eq. (
33
).
These fields are related to the fields
\delta_{g}({\bf x})
and
v_{r}^{\rm true}({\bf x})
by:

\rho_{g}({\bf x})=W_{g}({\bf x})\delta_{g}({\bf x})\hskip 42.67912pt{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=W_{v}({\bf x})v_{r}^{\rm true}({\bf x})

(50)

where the weight functions
W_{g}
,
W_{v}
defined by this equation can be approximated (using Eq. (
14
)) as sums over the random catalog:

W_{g}({\bf x})=\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{g}\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})\hskip 42.67912ptW_{v}({\bf x})=\frac{N_{g}}{N_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W_{j}^{v}B({\bf x}_{j})\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j})

(51)

Our power spectrum normalizations
{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}
,
{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}
are obtained by specializing Eq. (
49
) above to the window functions
W_{g}
,
W_{v}
.
One more implementation detail: rather than integrating over all
{\bf K}
as in Eq. (
49
), we integrate over two
K
-shells and subtract:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{gg}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{K<K_{0}}-\int_{K_{0}<k<2^{1/3}K_{0}}\right)\frac{d^{3}{\bf K}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,W_{g}({\bf K})W_{g}({\bf K})^{*}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{gv}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{K<K_{0}}-\int_{K_{0}<k<2^{1/3}K_{0}}\right)\frac{d^{3}{\bf K}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,W_{g}({\bf K})W_{v}({\bf K})^{*}

(52)

where
K_{0}=0.1
Mpc
-1
.
We include this subtraction step in order to cancel shot noise, which arises because the window functions have been approximated by sums (
51
) over the random catalog.

Definition of galaxy field
\rho_{g}
.

The random field

Second, in each small volume

includes the angular mask.
Similarly, we create an unclustered “random” catalog by placing a random in each small volume
d^{5}{\bf y}
with probability:

\bar{N}_{r}P_{0}({\bf y})\,d^{5}{\bf y}

(59)

where
\bar{N}_{r}\gg\bar{N}_{g}

Third, define
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
by summing over all galaxies with weight

\rho_{g}({\bf x})\equiv\bigg(\sum_{i\in\rm gal}W({\bf y}_{i})\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\rm obs})\bigg)-\frac{{\bar{N}}_{g}}{{\bar{N}}_{r}}\bigg(\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W({\bf y}_{j})\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j}^{\rm obs})\bigg)

S_{g}
.

Similarly, the random field
S_{g}({\bf x})
is defined by the following simulation procedure.
First, we simulate a random realization of the Gaussian field
\delta_{G}({\bf y})

Second, in each small volume

\bar{N}_{r}P_{0}({\bf y})\,d^{5}{\bf y}

(61)

Third, we define

by “painting” the random field

onto the random catalog, and adding Poisson noise by hand:

S_{g}({\bf x})\equiv\frac{\bar{N}_{g}}{\bar{N}_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W({\bf y}_{j})\big(\delta_{G}({\bf x}_{j}^{\rm true})+\eta_{j}\big)\delta^{3}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{j}^{\rm obs})

where

\big\langle\eta_{j}\eta_{k}\big\rangle=\left(\frac{\bar{N}_{r}}{\bar{N}_{g}}-\big\langle\delta_{G}({\bf x}_{j}^{\rm true})^{2}\big\rangle\right)\delta_{jk}

Statement of theorem.

In this appendix, we will prove the following theorem:
the galaxy field
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
and the surrogate field

defined in Eqs. (
60

62

\big\langle\rho_{g}({\bf x})\rho_{g}({\bf x}^{\prime})\big\rangle=\big\langle S_{g}({\bf x})S_{g}({\bf x}^{\prime})\big\rangle

We emphasize that in Eq. (

\langle\rho({\bf x})\rho({\bf x}^{\prime})\rangle
as a function of
({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime})
with no angle-averaging.
Thus, the theorem shows that the surrogate fields fully capture effects such as survey geometry, redshift dependence, and photo-
z
errors.

B.2
Strategy of proof

We define fields
{\widetilde{\rho}}({\bf y}),{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y})
on the five-dimensional space
{\bf y}=({\bm{\theta}},z_{\rm true},z_{\rm obs},\sigma_{z})
by slightly modifying the definitions (
60
), (
62
) of
\rho_{g}({\bf x}),S_{g}({\bf x})
as follows:

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y})

\displaystyle\equiv\bigg(\sum_{i\in\rm gal}W({\bf y}_{i})\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{i})\bigg)-\frac{{\bar{N}}_{g}}{{\bar{N}}_{r}}\bigg(\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W({\bf y}_{j})\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\bigg)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\bar{N}_{g}}{\bar{N}_{r}}\sum_{j\in\rm rand}W({\bf y}_{j})\big(\delta_{G}({\bf y}_{j})+\eta_{j}\big)\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})

(65)

In §
B.3
, §
B.4
, we will show that the 5-d fields
{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y})
,
{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y})
have the same covariance:

\big\langle{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle=\big\langle{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle

(66)

This implies our main theorem (
64
), that the 3-d fields
\rho_{g}({\bf x}),S_{g}({\bf x})
have the same covariance.
This statement is not obvious, but follows from the following formal argument.

The 3-d fields
\rho_{g}({\bf x})
and
S_{g}({\bf x})
are related to the 5-d fields
{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf x})
and
{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf x})
via a formal process of “integrating out” the variables
(z_{\rm true},\sigma_{z})
:

\displaystyle\rho_{g}({\bf x})=\int d^{5}{\bf y}\,{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y})\delta^{3}\big({\bf x}-\chi(z_{\rm obs}{\bm{\theta}})\big)\hskip 42.67912ptS_{g}({\bf x})=\int d^{5}{\bf y}\,{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y})\delta^{3}\big({\bf x}-\chi(z_{\rm obs}{\bm{\theta}})\big)

(67)

where
{\bf y}=({\bm{\theta}},z_{\rm true},z_{\rm obs},\sigma_{z})
on the RHS.
Generally speaking, if two random variables
({\widetilde{\rho}}_{g},{\widetilde{S}}_{g})
have the same covariance, and the same linear operation (
67
) is applied to both, then the resulting random variables
(\rho_{g},S_{g})
also have the same covariance.
Therefore Eq. (
66
) implies our main theorem (
64
).

B.3
Computing
\langle\tilde{\rho}_{g}({\bf y})\tilde{\rho}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\rangle

In this section we compute the LHS
\langle{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\rangle
of Eq. (
66
).
We will compute the expectation value in two steps.
First, we will fix a random realization of
\delta_{G}({\bf y})
and compute the expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm pl}
over random placements of the galaxies
\{{\bf y}_{i}\}
.
Second, we will take an “outer” expectation value over random realizations of
\delta_{G}
.

We will assume
{\bar{N}}_{r}\gg{\bar{N}}_{g}
.
In this limit, the field
{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y})
can be written:

{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y})=\bigg(\sum_{i\in\rm gal}W({\bf y}_{i})\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{i})\bigg)-{\bar{N}}_{g}W({\bf y})P_{0}({\bf y})

(68)

Now we take the expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm pl}
over galaxy placements, using the placement probability (
59
).
The expectation value is the sum of “2-halo” and “1-halo” terms:

\displaystyle\big\langle{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle_{\rm pl}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{2h}({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})+\zeta_{1h}({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})

(69)

\displaystyle\zeta_{2h}({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bar{N}_{g}^{2}W({\bf y})W({\bf y}^{\prime})P_{0}({\bf y})P_{0}({\bf y}^{\prime})\Big[\big(1+\delta_{G}({\bf y})\big)\big(1+\delta_{G}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big)-1\Big]

\displaystyle\zeta_{1h}({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bar{N}_{g}W({\bf y})^{2}P_{0}({\bf y})\big(1+\delta_{G}({\bf y})\big)\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}^{\prime})

We omit details of this calculation, since it is so similar to the standard halo model.
Next, we take the expectation value of Eq. (
69
) over random realizations of
\delta_{G}

\big\langle{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle=W({\bf y})W({\bf y}^{\prime})\Big[{\bar{N}}_{g}^{2}P_{0}({\bf y})P_{0}({\bf y}^{\prime})C({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})+{\bar{N}}_{g}P_{0}({\bf y})\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}^{\prime})\Big]

(70)

where

denotes the covariance of the Gaussian random field
\delta_{G}({\bf y})
:

C({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})\equiv\big\langle\delta_{G}({\bf y})\delta_{G}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle

(71)

B.4
Computing
\langle\tilde{S}_{g}({\bf y})\tilde{S}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\rangle

In this section we compute the RHS
\langle{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\rangle
of Eq. (
66

We write
{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y})
in the form:

{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y})=\sum_{j}w_{j}\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\hskip 42.67912pt\mbox{where }w_{j}\equiv\frac{\bar{N}_{g}}{\bar{N}_{r}}\,W({\bf y}_{j})\,\big(\delta_{G}({\bf y}_{j})+\eta_{j}\big)

(72)

We will compute the expectation value
\langle{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\rangle
in two steps.
First, we will fix a realization of the random catalog
\{{\bf y}_{j}\}
, and compute the expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle_{w}
over the random variable
w_{j}
.
Second, we will take an “outer” expectation value over the random catalog
\{{\bf y}_{j}\}
.
The first step can be done as follows:

\displaystyle\big\langle{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle_{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{jk}\big\langle w_{j}w_{k}\big\rangle\,\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\delta^{5}({\bf y}^{\prime}-{\bf y}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{jk}\frac{{\bar{N}}_{g}^{2}}{{\bar{N}}_{r}^{2}}\,W({\bf y}_{j})W({\bf y}_{k})\left[C({\bf y}_{j},{\bf y}_{k})+\left(\frac{{\bar{N}}_{r}}{{\bar{N}}_{g}}-C({\bf y}_{j},{\bf y}_{j})\right)\delta_{jk}\right]\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\delta^{5}({\bf y}^{\prime}-{\bf y}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bar{N}}_{g}^{2}}{{\bar{N}}_{r}^{2}}W({\bf y})W({\bf y}^{\prime})\bigg[C({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\prime})\sum_{jk}\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\delta^{5}({\bf y}^{\prime}-{\bf y}_{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\bigg(\frac{{\bar{N}}_{r}}{{\bar{N}}_{g}}-C({\bf y},{\bf y})\bigg)\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}^{\prime})\sum_{j}\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\bigg]

(73)

where in the second line, we have computed the expectation value
\langle w_{j}w_{k}\rangle
using the definition (
72
) of
w_{j}
, and Eq. (
63
) for
\langle\eta_{j}\eta_{k}\rangle
.
The covariance

was defined in Eq. (
71
).

The next step is to take the “outer” expectation value over the random catalog
\{{\bf y}_{j}\}
.
Using Eq. (
61
) for the placement probability, one can compute the following expectation values:

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\sum_{j}\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{k})\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle={\bar{N}}_{r}P_{0}({\bf y})

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\sum_{jk}\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}_{j})\delta^{5}({\bf y}^{\prime}-{\bf y}_{k})\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle={\bar{N}}_{r}^{2}P_{0}({\bf y})P_{0}({\bf y}^{\prime})+\bar{N}_{r}P_{0}({\bf y})\delta^{5}({\bf y}-{\bf y}^{\prime})

\big\langle{\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{\rho}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle=\big\langle{\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}){\widetilde{S}}_{g}({\bf y}^{\prime})\big\rangle

Appendix D

D.1
Statement of main result

Recall the definition (

III.5
for the more general case of a photometric survey.
This follows from our main result (
80
), plus the observation that the surrogate field
S_{v}^{\rm sig}({\bf x})

D.2
Proof of main result

First, we claim that it suffices to prove the main result (
80
) in the following special case:

1.

The galaxy survey does not include an angular mask.
(However, we allow
W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})
to be arbitrary, and to have structure on small scales.)

2.

The per-galaxy weighting is given by
W_{i}^{v}=1
.

3.

The galaxy catalog is spectroscopic, i.e.
\sigma_{z}=0
.

To see this, note that if (
80
) is true without an angular mask, then it is still true after applying an angular mask on both sides (item #1).
Similarly, we can apply a per-galaxy weighting on both sides (item #2), or a photo-
z
convolution on both sides (item #3).
We will make these three assumptions in the rest of this section.

We will prove (
80
) in a “snapshot” geometry, where the universe is a 3-d periodic box at a fixed time, and the CMB is a 2-d flat-sky field along one face of the box.
The snapshot geometry is an approximation of the true “lightcone” geometry, and neglects sky curvature and redshift evolution.
However, Eq. (
80
) does not involve correlation functions on large scales (only small-scale quantities such as
P_{ge}(k_{S})
or the small-scale CMB filter
F_{L}
), so if (
80
) is true for a snapshot geometry, it must be true in a lightcone geometry as well.

Our notation for the snapshot geometry is as follows.
Slowly evolving quantities, such as
\chi(z)
or
K(z)
, are evaluated at a fixed time, and denoted
\chi_{*}
or
K_{*}
.
We denote 3-d positions by
{\bf x}=({\bf x}_{\perp},x_{\parallel})
.
We identify 2-d positions
{\bm{\theta}}
with transverse 3-d positions
{\bf x}_{\perp}=\chi_{*}{\bm{\theta}}
.

As an example of the snapshot geometry notation, the kSZ line-of-sight integral (
3
) can be written:

T_{\rm kSZ}({\bm{\theta}})=K_{*}\int dx_{\parallel}\,v_{r}({\bf x})\,\delta_{e}({\bf x})\Big|_{{\bf x}=(\chi_{*}{\bm{\theta}},x_{\parallel})}

(83)

Since the 3-d galaxy number density
n_{g}(z)
is slowly varying in
z
, we will treat it as a constant
n_{*}
in the snapshot geometry.
The velocity reconstruction
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
can be written:

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=n_{*}\,\delta_{g}({\bf x})\,{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}})\big|_{{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}}

(84)

The main result (
80
) that we want to prove can be written:

\big\langle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle=n_{*}B_{*}\,v_{r}({\bf x})\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})\big|_{{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{where }B_{*}\equiv\frac{K_{*}}{\chi_{*}^{2}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf L}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,b_{L}F_{L}P_{ge}(k)_{k=L/\chi_{*}}

(85)

In the rest of this appendix, we will derive Eq. (
85
), by direct calculation in the snapshot geometry.
It will be convenient to define real-space counterparts of the quantities
P_{ge}(k)
,
b_{L}
,
F_{L}
:

\zeta_{ge}({\bf r})\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,P_{ge}(k)e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\hskip 28.45274ptb({\bm{\Theta}})\equiv\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf L}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,b_{L}e^{i{\bf L}\cdot{\bm{\Theta}}}\hskip 28.45274ptf({\bm{\Theta}})\equiv\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf L}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,F_{L}e^{i{\bf L}\cdot{\bm{\Theta}}}

(86)

Using this notation, the filtered CMB
{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}})
defined in Eq. (
10
) can be written in terms of the observed CMB
T_{\rm obs}({\bm{\theta}})
as:

{\widetilde{T}}({\bm{\theta}})=\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}\,f({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})T_{\rm obs}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})

(87)

Similarly, the observed CMB
T_{\rm obs}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})
contains the kSZ temperature
T_{\rm kSZ}({\bm{\theta}})
, with a beam convolution:

T_{\rm obs}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\supset\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\,b({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})T_{\rm kSZ}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})

(88)

where the notation “
\supset
” means we have omitted non-kSZ terms (e.g. primary CMB, instrumental noise) on the RHS.
Combining the previous two equations with Eqs. (
83
), (
84
), we can write the following relation between
{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})
and the large-scale structure fields
v_{r},\delta_{e}
:

{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})=n_{*}K_{*}\delta_{g}({\bf x})\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\,d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\,dx^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel}\,f({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\,b({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})\,v_{r}({\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\,\delta_{e}({\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}\\
{\bf x}^{\prime\prime}=(\chi_{*}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel})\end{subarray}}

(89)

Now we can take the expectation value (in a fixed realization of
v_{r}({\bf x})
), by replacing
\delta_{g}({\bf x})\delta_{e}({\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow\zeta_{ge}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime\prime})
on the RHS (where
\zeta_{ge}
was defined in Eq. (
86
)):

\big\langle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle\supset n_{*}K_{*}\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\,d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\,dx^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel}\,f({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\,b({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})\,v_{r}({\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\,\zeta_{ge}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}\\
{\bf x}^{\prime\prime}=(\chi_{*}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel})\end{subarray}}

(90)

We make the approximation that the radial velocity field
v_{r}({\bf x}^{\prime\prime})
is slowly varying on kSZ scales (
k_{S}^{-1}\sim 1
Mpc), so that we can approximate
v_{r}({\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\approx v_{r}({\bf x})
and pull it out of the integral:

\big\langle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle=n_{*}K_{*}\,v_{r}({\bf x})\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\,d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\,dx^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel}\,f({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\,b({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})\,\zeta_{ge}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}\\
{\bf x}^{\prime\prime}=(\chi_{*}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel})\end{subarray}}

(91)

Now consider the following subexpression:

I\equiv\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\,dx^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel}\,b({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})\,\zeta_{ge}({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime\prime})\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf x}^{\prime\prime}=(\chi_{*}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime}_{\parallel})\end{subarray}}

(92)

After a short calculation using the defintions of
b(\Theta)
and
\zeta_{ge}(r)
in Eq. (
86
), the integral can be done exactly:

I=\frac{1}{\chi_{*}^{2}}\,\eta({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\big|_{{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{where }\eta({\bm{\Theta}})\equiv\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf L}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{-i{\bf L}\cdot{\bm{\Theta}}}\,b_{L}P_{ge}(k)_{k=L/\chi_{*}}

(93)

Plugging this expression for
I
back into (
91
), we get:

\big\langle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle=\frac{n_{*}K_{*}}{\chi_{*}^{2}}\,v_{r}({\bf x})\,\int d^{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\,\rho({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})\Big|_{{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{where }\rho({\bm{\Theta}})\equiv f({\bm{\Theta}})\eta({\bm{\Theta}})

(94)

The convolution kernel
\rho({\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})
is narrowly peaked, i.e. its Fourier transform
\rho({\bf L})
is approximately constant (in
L
) on large scales compared to the kSZ (
L\ll 4000
).
Since we are only interested in such large scales, we can make the approximation:

\big\langle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle\approx\frac{n_{*}K_{*}}{\chi_{*}^{2}}\rho_{0}\,v_{r}({\bf x})\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})\Big|_{{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{where }\rho_{0}\equiv\int d^{2}{\bm{\Theta}}\,\rho({\bm{\Theta}})

(95)

A short calculation, unraveling definitions of
f({\bm{\Theta}})
,
\eta({\bm{\Theta}})
and
\rho({\bm{\Theta}})
in Eqs. (
86
), (
93
), (
94
), shows that
\rho_{0}
is given by:

\rho_{0}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf L}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,b_{L}F_{L}P_{ge}(k)_{k=L/\chi_{*}}

(96)

Plugging this in to the previous equation and using the definition (
85
) of
B_{*}
, we get:

\big\langle{\hat{v}}_{r}({\bf x})\big\rangle=n_{*}B_{*}\,v_{r}({\bf x})\,W_{\rm CMB}({\bm{\theta}})\big|_{{\bm{\theta}}={\bf x}_{\perp}/\chi_{*}}

(97)

as desired (Eq. (
85
)).
