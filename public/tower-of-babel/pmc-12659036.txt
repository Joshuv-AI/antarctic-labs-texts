Inhibition of Na+,K+ -ATPase enzyme by tetrodotoxin extract in the breast tumor xenograft Balb/c nu mouse model
Authors: Ameneh Ahrari, Saber Khodabandeh, Haleh Akhavan Niaki, Hadi Zare-Zardini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe Na+, K+-ATPase (NKA) is involved in many cellular functions, and changes in its activity and expression have been linked to various diseases, including cancer. This study aims to investigate the potential anti-tumor effect of tetrodotoxin (TTX), a potent sodium channel blocker extracted from the pufferfish Chelonodon patoca, on nude mice (Balb/c nu) induced with breast cancer (4T1) to determine whether it can affect the activity of the NKA.MethodsTTX was extracted using 2% acetic acid–methanol (v/v). To determine the lethal concentration of TTX, three concentrations of the TTX extract (1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 mg/kg) were injected into transgenic mice that had already been induced by the 4T1 cell line and bearing tumors. After 7–8 weeks of monitoring tumor volume, the mice were killed, and a portion of the tumors was fixed in Bouin’s fixative for immunohistological analysis, while the rest was frozen for biochemical analysis.ResultsBiometric analysis of Balb/c nu mice indicated a notable reduction in tumor volume progression in the group subjected to TTX extract compared to the control group. Furthermore, immunohistochemical and biochemical analysis demonstrated a decrease in the intensity and activity of NKA in the TTX extract group compared to the control group.ConclusionOur results indicate that TTX extract may have anti-tumor effects by inhibiting NKA activity. Therefore, considering the presence of TTX in the pufferfish extract, TTX could be a promising therapeutic candidate for controlling metastatic breast tumor growth and metastasis through NKA inhibition.

Introduction

Tetrodotoxin (TTX) is a unique toxin found in several organisms. The toxin is primarily isolated from the ovary, liver, intestines, and skin of various species of pufferfish. TTX is a highly potent non-peptide neurotoxin that exhibits activity at very low concentrations [1–3]. TTX can potentially harm the nervous system by selectively blocking voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs). This disruption impairs the activity of neural cell membranes and prevents the transmission of action potentials [2, 4, 5]. VGSCs play a crucial role in excitable cells and are expressed in non-excitable cells such as glia, immune cells, fibroblasts, and metastatic cancer cells [6]. Over the past three decades, numerous studies have demonstrated the involvement of VGSCs in cancer progression, cellular migration, and invasion across various cancer types [7, 8]. Hence, TTX as a potent inhibitory toxin of VGSCs could act as a promising target for therapeutics. Consistent with this notion, previous reports have shown that TTX can directly impact breast cancer cell lines by inhibiting sodium channel activity and reducing invasive behavior [7, 8]. Additionally, the balance of Na+ and K+ across the cell membrane is facilitated by NKA, a sodium–potassium pump that is crucial for the function and survival of all mammalian cells [9]. Research has revealed that the role of NKA extends beyond that of an ion transporter. It also acts as a receptor, coupled with the Src family of non-receptor tyrosine kinases, forming a functional complex for signal transduction, which modulates key cellular functions [10–12]. Studies have structurally and functionally characterized various NKA inhibitors of the cardiotonic steroids family, including ouabain, digoxin, bufalin, and others [13–20]. Digoxin is a well-known cardiotonic steroid that has long been used in the treatment of cardiac arrhythmias and heart failure [21–28]. These findings highlight the importance of studying the effects of NKA inhibitors and the compounds that can impact its function, especially in cancer, including a potential indirect impact of TTX. The role of ion channels and pumps in cancer development has led to the characterization of cancer as a channelopathy [29]. In this study, we aim to investigate the effect of TTX extracted from the pufferfish Chelonodon patoca on the activity of NKA in a breast tumor xenograft model using Balb/c nu mice—which is not fully understood. By characterizing the impact of TTX extract on NKA, our goal is to evaluate its potential as a novel anti-cancer agent.

Results

TTX extraction, toxicity assessment, and effects on tumor progression in Balb/c nu mice

A total of 0.75 g and 1.5 g of TTX were extracted from the liver and ovarian tissues, respectively, of the pufferfish Chelonodon patoca, based on dry weight. Palpable tumors became visible approximately 7–10 days after injection of the 4T1 cell line. Three milligrams of the extracted toxin were initially administered to the mice, resulting in the immediate death of all inoculated animals. In contrast, when tumor-bearing mice received a lower dose of 1.5 mg of extracted TTX, all survived throughout the 7- to 8-week observation period. Tumor growth was measured twice per week for nearly 2 months. Both groups of mice (control and TTX-treated) exhibited an increase in tumor volume; however, the growth rate was significantly lower in the TTX-treated group. As shown in Fig. 1a, b, tumor volume steadily increased in control mice, whereas the TTX-treated group exhibited slower progression. Discussion

In our research, we studied the effects of TTX extract on the growth of breast tumors in Balb/c nu mice, and examined NKA to assess cellular membrane dynamics. Our findings show that administering TTX extract could effectively impact the growth rate of these tumors. Importantly, we observed a significant reduction in tumor growth rate and relative tumor volume increase after administering TTX extract to mice with tumors, compared to the control group.

These results support previous studies on TTX’s anti-tumor effects, in which researchers reported potential anti-tumor effects of TTX on female mice with Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC). These studies have revealed that treatment with either TTX or doxorubicin (DOX) led to a notable decrease in average tumor weight [30]. Another study has shown that TTX extract from the puffer fish (Arothron diadematus) increased the lifespan of mice by 46% and exhibited activity against Ehrlich ascites carcinoma cells, reducing tumor cell numbers [31]. These findings are in line with our results, collectively suggesting an antitumoral role for TTX extract.

Our histological studies revealed morphological alterations in the breast tumors of TTX-treated mice. The shift from polymorphic epithelial cells with mitotic activity to spindle-shaped cells was accompanied by an increase in apoptotic bodies. While direct assessment of cell polarity in vivo is challenging, these changes suggest treatment-induced stress and alterations in the epithelial phenotype. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that TTX extract induced apoptosis in EAC cells and increased the lifespan of mice bearing Ehrlich ascites carcinoma [31]. While the exact mechanism requires further investigation, previous studies also suggest that TTX may influence apoptosis in HeLa cells. TTX induces apoptosis, possibly via disruption of calcium homeostasis by altering intracellular ion balance, particularly Ca2⁺ levels [32].

There is significant focus on NKA mechanistically because it can have a substantial impact on cell proliferation [9]. According to scientific evidence, α1 NKA plays a significant role in regulating the proto-oncogene Src kinase. Studies have shown that this regulation is involved in controlling the Warburg effect and tumor growth [33]. Specifically, researchers have identified residue Y260 in α1 NKA as a site for Src-specific phosphorylation and binding. This phosphorylation of residue Y260 is essential for Src-mediated signal transduction in response to various stimuli, such as EGF [33]. Additionally, the NKA enzyme has been linked to the regulation of tumor growth. A decrease in α1 NKA expression has been observed in various human cancers, such as colorectal, pancreatic [34], and bladder [35], and kidney cancer [36]. Furthermore, the inhibition of NKA using ouabain in human glioblastoma cells has been shown to induce cell death with characteristics of both apoptosis and necrosis in cancer cells (50). Thus, possibly any effect on the function of the NKA for instance, partial inhibition by cardiac steroids (e.g., digoxin), either as an ion pump or as a signaling receptor through disrupting the maintenance of Na+ and K+ gradients across the cell can induce cell death (50, 51). These findings support our data regarding the inhibitory effect of the TTX extract on NKA in the breast cancer cell mouse model using BALB/c nu mice.

Therefore, based on our findings and other studies, we believe that TTX could potentially inhibit tumor growth. The significant decrease in tumor volume in the TTX treatment group suggests that the treatment may have anti-tumor effects or the ability to slow down tumor progression via NKA inhibition. However, further evaluation and analysis are necessary to fully understand the mechanisms behind this observed decrease.

Our immunohistochemical analysis, indicates that NKA is predominantly localized in tumor cells. This localization is particularly evident in the control group.

Our data also showed a significant difference in the intensity of the TTX-treated group compared to the untreated control group, suggesting the impact of the extract on NKA expression and activity.

Previous studies, such as the one conducted by Cromwell Espineda and his research group, have shown the presence and distribution of NKA subunits in bladder cancer tissues using immunohistochemical staining in bladder cancer tissues [13]. Another study using immunofluorescence and western blotting showed that NKA localized on the plasma membrane of cancerous breast cell lines, with significantly higher expression levels in breast cancer cells compared to the non-tumorigenic line. These findings align with our observations [37]. Furthermore, results showed that the morphology of the tumor epithelial cells has changed, as shown in Fig. 2. This may also align with previous research indicating that NKA dysfunction contributes to the loss of polarity, particularly in malignant growth, due to its role in maintaining tight junctions, desmosomes, and cellular architecture [38], suggesting that NKA may play a significant role in regulating cellular signaling pathways and processes such as epithelial cell morphology and signal transduction.

In our study, we also measured the activity of NKA, with a higher activity of NKA in breast cancer of Balb/c nu mice in the control group compared to the group administering the TTX extract. Several studies have reported that the activity of NKA is altered in various cancers [39–41]. For example, the expression of the ATP1A1 gene, which codes for the α1-subunit of NKA, was found to be significantly increased in the tissue of patients with breast cancer (triple negative, Her2-positive, and Luminal A and B) compared to normal breast tissues [42].

A recent study investigated the impact of chemical inhibitors ouabain and TTX (3,4,5,6-tetrahydroxyxanthone) at 10 μM on NKA activity in pII breast cancer cells. The researchers observed a significant inhibition of NKA activity, which led to reduced proliferation, motility, invasion, and membrane blebbing [37]. These findings align with our results and help explain the implications of elevated NKA activity in breast cancer. The higher NKA activity observed in our study group may be attributed to the elevated metabolic demands of cancer cells, as previously reported in studies examining ATP production and mitochondrial function [43]. This metabolic change likely results in increased NKA activity, which serves as a compensatory mechanism to maintain cellular homeostasis and support the heightened metabolic demands of the tumor. These findings support the role of NKA in shaping the functional characteristics of tumor cells, underscoring its significant contribution to cancer development—a relationship that warrants further investigation.

While our immunofluorescence results provide strong evidence for reduced NKA protein levels, quantitative validation using Western blot analysis was unfortunately beyond the scope of our available experimental resources. Such studies will be a crucial part of future validation efforts to definitively quantify NKA protein expression. In the group treated with TTX extract, no metastases or visible clusters of small or large tumors were detected in the eyes, intestines, liver, or brain. In contrast, the control group exhibited visible clusters of both small and large tumors in the eyes, lungs, brain, intestines, and subcutaneous tissue during the surgical procedure. While we observed no gross metastases in the TTX-treated group, it is important to acknowledge that microscopic metastatic dissemination might still occur and requires more sensitive detection methods. Future investigations utilizing bioluminescence imaging (IVIS) with luciferase-expressing 4T1 cells (4T1 Luc) are planned to monitor metastatic progression in real-time across different organs. Regarding safety, specific hemocompatibility tests were not performed as part of this study. However, the absence of observable systemic or organ toxicity in the treated mice suggests good clinical tolerance at the applied dose. Future comprehensive safety evaluations will include specific analyses, such as hemolysis and coagulation assays, to fully establish the hemocompatibility of the TTX extract.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated the anti-tumor effects of TTX extract on breast cancer growth using a Balb/c nu mice model. We observed a notable reduction in tumor growth rate, which correlated with decreased NKA protein expression and activity in the tumor tissue of TTX-treated groups. These results highlight the potential of TTX as a therapeutic agent against breast cancer, possibly through a mechanism involving the targeting of NKA function. The findings underscore the promise of marine toxins such as TTX in cancer treatment and open new avenues for the development of anti-cancer therapies. Further in-depth analyses are needed to elucidate the molecular pathways involved and to assess the long-term risks associated with TTX administration. Additional research is required to understand the mechanisms by which TTX extract affects breast tissue in Balb/c nu mice in vivo and to evaluate its efficacy and safety.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Thirty specimens of Chelonodon patoca pufferfish were collected from Qeshm Island in the Persian Gulf and were immediately transported on dry ice for storage at − 20 °C until experimentation.

Extraction of TTX

Toxin extracts were prepared according to the method described elsewhere [44]. Tissue samples of Chelonodon patoca, such as livers and ovaries, were dissected and cut into 30-g sections. The tissue sections were mixed with a 2% acetic acid–methanol (v/v) solution (Merck, Germany) and then extracted with 50 ml of 2% acetic acid–methanol (v/v) under reflux conditions for 10 min. The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 1500 rpm (Eppendorf, Germany), and the liquid above the sediment was filtered using a vacuum pump (Vacuubrand, Germany). The filtered liquid was evaporated under reduced pressure (Büchi, Switzerland), and the sediments were combined for a second extraction cycle, which was repeated three times. The residue was diluted with 15 ml of water, and the resulting solution was extracted three times to remove the hydrophobic components. The water phase was evaporated under reduced pressure, and the remaining residue was diluted with 1.5 ml of citrate buffer (pH 4.80) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and stored at −20 °C.

Cell culture

The 4T1 cell line, derived from mouse mammary carcinoma, was obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran’s cell bank, North Research Center. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 and 95% O2 humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The culture medium was changed every 24 h, and the cell count was determined using a hemocytometer (Neubauer, Germany).

Xenotransplantation of breast tumor cells

To establish orthotopic xenografts, 100 μl of 4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells) were injected subcutaneously into the second mammary gland of restrained female Balb/c nu mice aged 5–6 weeks. Palpable primary tumors appeared within 7–10 days. To determine the effective concentration (LC₅₀) of the TTX extract, the extract was administered intraperitoneally at three concentrations (1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 mg/kg). Following LC₅₀ determination, a working solution of the extract (1.5 mg TTX/ml saline) was prepared and injected into established tumors in Balb/c nu mice, twice weekly. Tumor growth was monitored biweekly using calipers (Mitutoyo, Japan), and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × height × width)/2 [45].

Histology and immunohistochemistry

For histological and immunohistochemical observations, tumor tissue samples were fixed in Bouin’s fixative (Merck, Germany) for 24 h, washed in running tap water overnight, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (Merck, Germany), and embedded in liquid paraffin (Merck, Germany). Serial sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Merck, Germany) for general histological evaluation.

For immunolocalization of NKA, sections were first deparaffinized, rehydrated, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Sections were then washed three times for 10 min each in PBS. A mouse monoclonal antibody (IgGα5), raised against the α-subunit of chicken NKA, was used for immunolocalization. This antibody is commercially available from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank (DSHB, University of Iowa, USA). It was applied at a dilution of 1:100 in PBS (100 μl per section), followed by a two-hour incubation at room temperature in a moist chamber [46]. After rinsing, sections were incubated for one hour in the dark with 100 μl of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, UK). Slides were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium and examined under a fluorescence microscope equipped with FITC filters (Nikon, Japan). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

NKA activity assay

NKA activity was measured in breast tumor tissue homogenates using a microplate-based colorimetric assay. Tumor samples were homogenized in ice-cold membrane isolation buffer (MIB) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C (Eppendorf, Germany). The supernatant was collected, and total protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method (Bio-Rad, USA). NKA-specific activity was calculated by measuring ATP hydrolysis in the presence and absence of ouabain (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). For each sample, 100 μl of assay buffer containing either 13.4 mM KCl (Merck, Germany) or 1.4 mM ouabain was added to initiate the reaction. After incubation, the reaction was terminated by adding ice-cold 10% trichloroacetic acid (Merck, Germany), followed by 100 μl of color reagent. After a 10-min development period, the amount of liberated inorganic phosphate (Pi) was quantified by measuring absorbance at 630 nm using a microplate reader (Biochrom, Austria). NKA activity was expressed as the ouabain-sensitive fraction of total ATPase activity.

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test in GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Basic data organization and descriptive statistics were performed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, USA). Further statistical analyses, including independent t-tests, were conducted using GraphPad Prism. When significant differences were detected (P < 0.05), Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was applied to identify pairwise group differences.
