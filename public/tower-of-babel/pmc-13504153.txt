Building OHNS Capacity in Low‐ and Middle‐Income Countries: Insights From Global Training Programs
Authors: Hudson Lin, Michelle H. Zhang, Emma Finnegan, Annabelle Fischer, Nidanur Sinanoglu, James Lawrence, Tarika Srinivasan, Fiona Kabagenyi, Mary Jue Xu, Taseer Din
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDescribe the design, goals, and perceived impacts of global otolaryngology–head and neck surgery (OHNS) training programs, with emphasis on institutional factors shaping workforce development and surgeon retention in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs).MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional qualitative study from October 2024 to October 2025 using focused, semi‐structured interviews on Zoom. Participants included program directors and faculty involved in training otolaryngologists from LMICs. Interview transcripts were analyzed using an inductive analysis approach to develop a codebook and identify emergent themes.ResultsSixteen otolaryngologists representing 15 fellowship‐level programs in seven countries were interviewed. Collectively, programs had trained 49 fellowship graduates and nine current fellows from 23 countries. Thematic analysis identified four themes: program design and operational barriers, trainee recruitment and selection, trainee support and post‐training mobility, and program impact. Directors emphasized shared goals of sustainable workforce development while describing substantial regulatory, financial, and structural constraints to recruitment and post‐training mobility. Among LMIC trainees, 75% returned to their home country, though health system limitations, economic pressures, and credentialing barriers hindered reintegration. For faculty, the inclusion of foreign trainees fostered reciprocal learning environments that influenced perspectives on training adaptability and resource‐conscious care.ConclusionUnderstanding the shared motivations and barriers shaping international OHNS training programs may inform future program design strategies that support sustainable workforce development and successful trainee reintegration within LMIC health systems.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) account for 85% of the global otolaryngology—head and neck surgery (OHNS) disease burden, including 80% of individuals with hearing loss and 82% of all head and neck cancer deaths [1, 2, 3]. Within LMICs, workforce deficits are consistently cited as a barrier to accessible OHNS care [4]. A recent global workforce assessment revealed a 57‐fold difference in OHNS provider density between high‐income countries (HICs) and LMICs, with Sub‐Saharan Africa reporting just 0.18 clinicians per 100,000 population [1]. In addition to limited or nonexistent formal training opportunities, shortages are exacerbated by physician emigration to HIC settings [5, 6, 7, 8]. With evidence across surgical fields linking increased provider density to reductions in morbidity and mortality, strengthening the OHNS workforce represents a critical target for capacity building [9, 10, 11].

The 2015 Lancet Commission on Global Surgery named increasing workforce density as a core benchmark for achieving access to safe, timely, and affordable surgical care [12]. Since then, surgical collaborations between HICs and LMICs have increasingly favored a shift from episodic, service‐delivery models toward long‐term, bidirectional partnerships focusing on local priorities [13, 14, 15]. Within OHNS, surgical missions focused on facial cleft repair [16, 17], tonsillectomies [18], and tympanomastoid operations [19] have given way to programs championing longitudinal mentorship and formalized training [20, 21, 22]. Despite this evolution, little is known about how these partnerships and fellowships are conceived.

The present study examines factors driving the migration of LMIC otolaryngologists who pursue training abroad. Through qualitative interviews with program directors (PDs) or otolaryngologists involved in international education, we sought to understand the motivations guiding program design and identify how international partnerships can better support workforce development and surgeon retention in LMICs.

Methods

Instrument Development

We developed an interview guide (Table S1) comprising open‐ended questions across five topics: program description, trainee support systems, experiences of discrimination, training materials and appropriateness, and potential solutions. The guide was designed to explore intentions behind program creation and design and investigate the role of international training partnerships in strengthening the OHNS workforce in LMICs.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited using snowball sampling from October 2024 to October 2025. Eligible participants included academic PDs or surgeons who had engaged in international teaching, with intentionally broad criteria to capture the diversity of training models. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, San Francisco (IRB #24‐41001), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted via Zoom by three trained researchers (H.L., M.H.Z., and J.L.). The interviews were audio‐ and video‐recorded, then transcribed for analysis. Demographic and program data were collected from participants and deidentified. Trainee practice locations reported by PDs were independently verified, where possible, through publicly available professional profiles, including LinkedIn and institutional websites. Coding and qualitative analysis were conducted using Dedoose (version 10.0.59; SocioCultural Research Consultants).

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using an inductive thematic approach, with no a priori hypotheses [23, 24]. The first five interviews were independently coded by team members (E.F., A.F., and N.S.). After meeting to reconcile coding discrepancies, the team developed a preliminary coding framework from emerging concepts. Discrepancies were resolved through group discussion until consensus was reached. This framework was applied to new interviews and iteratively updated to incorporate novel codes, subthemes, and themes. Thematic saturation, defined as the point at which no new codes emerged across three consecutive interviews, was reached after 16 interviews, at which point participant recruitment was halted. The finalized codebook was subsequently reapplied to all transcripts and used for final data analysis. Reflexivity practices are described in Supporting Information.

Results

Sixteen otolaryngologists were interviewed, including nine PDs, two former directors, and five otolaryngologists involved in training surgeons from LMICs (Table 1). Most participants were male (75.0%), and all currently practice in an Anglophone country. Fifteen programs (93.8%) operated at the fellowship level, with the majority focusing on pediatric otolaryngology (56.3%). Among the nine HIC‐based programs (56.3%), eight accepted a mix of domestic and international applicants, while one was explicitly designed for LMIC trainees. Among the six LMIC‐based programs (37.5%), the degree of on‐site faculty presence ranged from PDs who were fully based in the LMIC to those who provided periodic on‐site supervision supplemented by remote mentorship. In total, participants taught 87 trainees across 23 countries, including 49 fellowship graduates, nine current fellows, and additional trainees through longitudinal, residency, or virtual pathways (Figure 1). Postgraduation, 69.4% have returned or stayed in their country of origin, 8.2% are practicing in another LMIC, and 22.4% are practicing in a HIC. All trainees in LMIC‐based programs returned to a home or LMIC context, compared to 54.2% in HIC‐based programs.

Theme 1: Program Design and Operational Barriers

Subtheme 1: Program Goals—Directors Pursued Education‐Driven Capacity‐Building Efforts as an Alternative to Episodic Service Delivery

Participants cited formative experiences as international trainees themselves or exposure to global health work earlier in their careers. Surgeons with prior global surgery experience described downsides to service‐based trips, including a lack of continuity and disruption to local practice. Others emphasized a dearth of subspecialty‐trained surgeons and the urgent need for capacity‐building in LMICs. These experiences pushed surgeons to strengthen local capacity through advanced training. For many directors, the primary program objective was defined as achieving an “exponential” training effect, whereby alumni would return to home contexts and pass on learning by strengthening local programs. Other goals included building partnerships with LMIC trainees and institutions worldwide and increasing subspecialist density in areas of need.

Subtheme 2: Curriculum Design—LMIC‐Based Programs Adapted Curricula to Local Infrastructure, While HIC‐Based Programs Adhered to Local Accreditation Standards

For programs based in HICs, international trainees were incorporated into existing curricula designed around national accreditation standards, such as those set by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME). LMIC‐based programs often adapted curricula from HIC models, such as pediatric fellowships modeled after American Society of Pediatric Otolaryngology (ASPO) guidelines. Since these programs were less constrained by external accreditation, PDs described intentional efforts to modify training to local resources and infrastructure. One head and neck oncology fellowship focused its curriculum on regional flaps instead of free flaps due to patients' financial constraints. Certain pediatric otolaryngology curricula omitted complex airway surgery in areas without reliable ICU staffing or cochlear implantation where postoperative audiology services were unavailable.

Subtheme 3: Operational Barriers—Programs Faced Financial, Institutional, and Political Barriers That Threatened Sustainability

While programs set in HICs encountered hurdles in recruiting foreign trainees (see Trainee Recruitment—Recruitment Challenges), PDs who established fellowships in LMICs described substantial upfront time investments in assessing institutional readiness, building trust with local teams, designing a feasible curriculum, and selecting an inaugural trainee. Even after their establishment, some LMIC‐based programs had limited access to operating rooms as well as limited anesthesia availability and ancillary support. Patients' inability to pay for surgeries also restricted the scope of training and surgical volume. Beyond program initiation, surgeons highlighted the limited institutional and grant funding for pursuing global surgery work within OHNS. Those traveling abroad relied on nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) and private sponsors and accepted uncompensated time from home institutions. Finally, political unrest and the COVID‐19 pandemic disrupted safe international travel and temporarily halted local training altogether, demonstrating the fragility of local programs to exogenous factors.

Theme 2: Trainee Recruitment and Selection

Subtheme 1: Recruitment Strategies—Recruitment Ranged From Open Advertisements to Network‐Driven Candidate Selection

Programs in LMICs leveraged partnerships with medical technology sponsors or NGOs to identify future fellows. With established networks across various health systems, these partners possessed insight into which candidates had the requisite baseline training and supportive home context to maximize the utility of advanced training. For programs in HICs, word of mouth—such as from past trainees or institutional partnerships—also played a role in recruitment, though most relied on society websites and open advertisements. Some programs used observerships as a soft prerequisite for applicants to build familiarity with the program and demonstrate operative capability.

Subtheme 2: Selection Criteria—With Applicant Selection, Directors Prioritized Surgical Readiness and Likelihood of Return

Beyond prerequisites like English proficiency and cultural competence, programs placed heavy emphasis on prior surgical training and alignment with the program's goals. For HIC‐based programs, this entailed screening for applicants likely to return home and contribute to domestic training, whereas LMIC‐based programs prioritized identifying local champions who would eventually take over as program director for the fellowship. The most successful trainees had a solid surgical foundation and possessed both knowledge of the local infrastructure and the institutional influence to assume a program leadership role upon return. In contrast to HIC systems, where fellowship immediately follows residency, these trainees often completed residency several years earlier. Expectations regarding return were frequently communicated during the recruitment process and reinforced throughout training, often explicitly framed as an ethical obligation to avoid contributing to workforce depletion. In some cases, returning to the trainee's home country was a formal requirement for participation.

Subtheme 3: Recruitment Challenges—Regulatory, Financial, and Institutional Barriers Limited LMIC Trainee Access to HIC‐Based Programs

Visa requirements, credentialing standards, and board regulations posed significant, if not insurmountable, barriers to entry for LMIC trainees seeking training in an HIC. The high relocation expenses and cost of living in HICs deterred applicants without financial backing from home institutions or the ability to self‐fund. Institutional guidelines also often mandated the selection of domestic over international applicants. Ultimately, trainee selection reflected directors' efforts to minimize regulatory and training‐related risk and maximize the likelihood of sustainable capacity‐building through alumni.

Theme 3: Trainee Support and Post‐Training Mobility

Subtheme 1: Trainee Support—Directors Provided Logistical Support During Training and Sustained Relationships After Graduation to Maximize Long‐Term Impact

Across interviews, PDs described myriad supports to help trainees adjust to HIC environments and fully engage in training, including assistance with housing, family needs, and navigating administrative processes. To prepare trainees for a stable transition back home, directors extended teaching beyond clinical skills to include leadership and mentorship development, research literacy, and navigation of health systems. Sustaining alumni engagement was seen as integral for long‐term success, with strategies ranging from frequent WhatsApp check‐ins to the creation of regional professional societies with teaching rounds and research meetings.

Subtheme 2: Post‐Training Mobility Patterns—Post‐Training Mobility Reflected a Tension Between Trainees' Preference to Return Home and Political, Economic, and Structural Barriers to Reintegration

PDs reported that the majority of trainees from LMICs returned to practice in their country of origin (69.4%). Long‐term practice in HICs was rare, and in almost all cases, it was motivated by escaping political violence or economic strife. Retention in HICs after training was limited by structural barriers, including difficulties obtaining full licenses or credentialing processes that did not recognize foreign training. In contrast, PDs observed several personal and professional factors that motivated trainees to return home, including a strong sense of national identity, commitment to serving one's home population, and family or personal ties. Despite their motivation, participants identified several challenges to reintegration, including lower earning potential and difficulty repaying training‐related debt.

Theme 4: Program Impact

Subtheme 1: Perceived Strengths and Impact—LMIC Trainees Enhanced Learning Environments and Returned to Strengthen Local Training Infrastructure

Many PDs described international cohorts as enriching educational environments through diverse clinical perspectives. Engagement with LMIC trainees influenced how faculty approached resource use and adaptability, impacting their care delivery in underserved settings within HICs. As a result, international partnerships functioned as reciprocal learning environments rather than unidirectional transfers of expertise. For PDs at longer‐standing programs, several had already reported achieving their initial objectives (see Program Origins and Structure—Program Goals), including the rollout of trainee‐led fellowship programs and the establishment of alumni networks and professional societies.

Subtheme 2: Post‐Training Reintegration—Difficulties With Skill Transfer and Inconsistent Post‐Training Support Emerged as Consistent Gaps

PDs noted that training did not always translate seamlessly into practice in LMIC settings, due to differences in resources and care delivery models. Techniques taught during training were difficult to implement upon return, requiring substantial adaptation by trainees. Similarly, PDs in HICs described a tension between the desire to tailor training to trainees' anticipated home contexts and the need to ensure a shared baseline of clinical competence. Across interviews, they emphasized that patient safety and accreditation requirements necessitated a set of standardized competencies that all trainees were expected to achieve, regardless of eventual practice context. While contextual adaptation was considered, it was more likely to occur informally, later in training, or through postgraduate mentorship rather than through structured curricular modification. Finally, PDs acknowledged variability in post‐fellowship support. While many described maintaining informal mentorship relationships, few programs had formalized processes to support postgraduation reintegration, skill development, or longitudinal outcome tracking. Support was largely dependent on individual faculty availability rather than institutional design.

Discussion

The present study examined the scope and design of international OHNS training programs, with emphasis on their roles in supporting workforce development in LMICs. Overall, our findings reflect the field's broader shift toward sustainable, educationally focused partnerships, while identifying the regulatory and financial barriers that shape trainee recruitment and program success. Given the variability in residency‐level training, practice guidelines, and resource availability across geographical settings [1, 5, 6, 25], preparing trainees to thrive in their home environments was viewed as inherently context‐dependent. Within this landscape, we observed a dichotomy between programs based in HICs and those based in LMICs. While the former offered diverse case exposure and international prestige, financial and regulatory hurdles created a bottleneck for LMIC trainees. The advantage of widely accepted accreditation limited curricular flexibility, as PDs were unable to tailor learning to practice in lower‐resource environments. In contrast, LMIC‐based programs allowed PDs to mentor trainees within home environments and design curricula aligned with local needs. Yet this advantage exposed programs to constraints with ancillary services, patient‐level financial barriers, and political instability. Ultimately, directors were united in their goals—to promote sustainable workforce development through the exponential dissemination of skills and the development of a wide‐reaching alumni network (Figure 2).

HIC‐ and LMIC‐based training models offer complementary strengths (indicated by green plus signs) and limitations (indicated by red minus signs), each working toward achieving shared goals of sustainable capacity building.

Another significant finding was the contrast in post‐training return rates. Programs in LMICs collectively reported an 84.0% rate of return to their home country, comparable to prior estimates of surgeon migration in LMICs [8, 26]. HIC‐based programs reported a return rate of 54.2%, higher than past estimates and likely reflecting the fact that programs in this study explicitly screened for applicants with strong return intent [27]. While existing narratives on brain drain have framed the pursuit of professional development, financial remuneration, and improved quality of life as major driving factors to retention in HIC settings [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Our analysis revealed how most trainees were motivated to return home, with departures from home contexts occurring primarily when return was rendered infeasible by political or economic pressures. Prior studies have proposed models for building sustainable educational partnerships in global surgery [13, 14, 15, 32]. We extend these recommendations by highlighting additional OHNS‐specific points of intervention: PDs consistently identified two bottlenecks to fellowship recruitment: (1) strong surgical skills and (2) home environments capable of accommodating subspecialty care. Given substantial variations in surgical exposure and training length across OHNS residencies [1], expanding or standardizing residency programs in LMICs can increase the pool of fellowship‐eligible trainees and increase surgical capacity. In subspecialties that depend on multidisciplinary systems, such as head and neck oncology [33, 34, 35] and pediatric airway care [36, 37], strengthening ancillary services may enable trainees to return to environments better equipped to maximize their training [38, 39]. Other identified needs include context‐adapted curricula. In general surgery, an international Delphi study has established shared learning objectives for global surgery [40], and at the institutional level, surgeons have used case numbers and local surgical opinion to design contextually relevant curricula [41]. Formalizing analogous mechanisms within OHNS, including with accreditation frameworks, can better prepare trainees for LMIC practice [42].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. While interviewees represented several distinct institutions, all were practicing in Anglophone countries. Future studies should partner with non‐Anglophone investigators and societies to develop a more globally representative understanding of international program design. Additionally, most programs operated at the fellowship level, which may not reflect experiences in earlier stages of training. While we intentionally targeted PDs and faculty to examine institutional considerations for global training, our findings may misrepresent challenges or experiences more salient to trainees. The perspectives of LMIC trainees who sought training abroad were studied using a similar qualitative methodology and will be included in a separate forthcoming work. Finally, reliance on self‐reported, retrospective interviews may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly when participants discussed program successes or challenges. However, given the relatively small number of established programs and the absence of a one‐size‐fits‐all model for international training, our qualitative approach was well‐suited to capture the complexity and breadth of current practice and individual‐level considerations.

Future work should include trainee‐level surveys on operative case volume and scope, clinical outcomes, and post‐training practice patterns to enable more rigorous, quantitative assessment of program impact across resource‐limited settings. Studies should also compare fellowships with paired residency training to standalone fellowships, investigating whether the parallel training structure confers advantages in workforce retention and context‐appropriate skill development. Within training programs, prospective data collection on practice location, surgical scope, and teaching output at defined timepoints would enable more rigorous evaluation of workforce impact and inform future program design [43, 44].

Conclusion

This qualitative study of otolaryngologists engaged in international teaching highlights the diversity of global OHNS training programs and a shared goal toward exponential workforce growth in LMICs. While LMIC‐based programs achieved a higher rate of home‐country retention than those in HICs in this study, both modalities faced context‐dependent regulatory, economic, and political barriers. Future program development should prioritize context‐specific learning and capacity building in residency education and ancillary services to encourage durable and impactful returns to practice in LMICs.
