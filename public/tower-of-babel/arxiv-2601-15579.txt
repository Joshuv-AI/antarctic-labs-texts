Global solution curves for first order periodic problems, with applications
Philip Korman

Abstract
Using continuation methods and bifurcation theory, we study the exact multiplicity of periodic solutions, and the global solution structure, for periodic problems of first order. The results are applied to a population model with fishing, and to the existence and stability of limit cycles. We also describe in detail our numerical computations of curves of periodic solutions, and of limit cycles.



Key words: Periodic solutions, global solution curves, limit cycles, population models, numerical computations.

AMS subject classification: 34C25, 37G15, 92D25, 65P30.

Introduction

We study periodic first order equations

(1.1)

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=f(t)\,.

Here
g(t+T,u)=g(t,u)
, and
f(t+T)=f(t)
for all
t\in R
, and we are searching for
T
-periodic solutions,
u(t+T)=u(t)
for all

[
5
]
and
[

, with

and

, and

, with
\int_{0}^{T}U(t)\,dt=0
and
\xi\in R
, and show the existence of a continuous curve of solutions
(u,\mu)(\xi)
, i.e.,

, and

, under rather general assumptions on

and

. This curve exhausts the set of all periodic solutions of (

as the

. To obtain
T
-periodic solutions of

(1.2)

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=0\,,

we study the curve of
T
-periodic solutions of (
\mu\in R
)

(1.3)

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=\mu\,.

If the solution curve of (
1.3
),
\mu=\mu(\xi)
, has a root
\xi_{0}
, then the problem (
1.2
) has
T
-periodic solution of average
\xi_{0}
. We give some conditions under which

changes sign, and hence it has roots.

As a first application of the above solution curves, we re-derive two results of H. Brezis and L. Nirenberg
[
3
]
on the existence of periodic solutions of (
1.2
), providing a wider perspective on these results, and some extra information. (The paper
[
3
]
dealt with a more general setting of first order equations on a torus.) However, these results have rather stringent conditions, which tend not to hold in applications. We present further existence results for (
1.2

), and we also discuss stability properties of the corresponding
T
-periodic solutions of (
1.2
).

We apply our results to a periodic population model with fishing

(1.4)

u^{\prime}=u(a(t)-u)-\mu f(t)\,,

depending on a parameter
\mu>0
. Here
a(t)
and
f(t)
are given
T
-periodic functions, and we are looking for a
T
-periodic solution

. We study the curves of
T
-periodic solutions of (
1.4
) as the parameter
\mu
varies. It is known that
T
-period solutions of (
1.4
) lie on parabola-like curve with a turn to the left in the

plane, see
[
1
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
. If
\mu_{0}
is the turning point on the solution curve, then
\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{t}f(t)\,dt
gives the maximal sustainable level of fishing. If one changes

,
\mu_{0}
is changed too, and the natural question is: which

gives the largest maximal sustainable level of fishing. We prove that in case

is a constant, the optimal

is also a constant. For a general

, the optimal

is not necessarily a constant, as our computations show. Based on our numerical experiments, we state two open problems.

Another application is to limit cycles, that are closed solutions of autonomous systems of the type

(1.5)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}(t)=F(X,Y)

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}(t)=G(X,Y)\,.

It is well known that each limit cycle contains at least one rest point, see e.g., S.H. Strogatz
[
14
]
. Let
(x_{0},y_{0})
be a rest point of (
1.5
), so that
F(x_{0},y_{0})=G(x_{0},y_{0})=0
.
We wish to find a limit cycle, enclosing
(x_{0},y_{0})
. Setting
x=X-x_{0}
and
y=Y-y_{0}
, and introducing the polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
in the
xy
-plane, we obtain a problem of the type

(1.6)

r^{\prime}+g(\theta,r)=0\,,

with
g(\theta+2\pi,r)=g(\theta,r)
, and we are looking for a
2\pi
-periodic solution
r=r(\theta)
. The existence results of
[
3
]
relied on a priori estimates. These estimates were obtained under the following two conditions: either
g_{r}(\theta,r)>0
for all
\theta
and
r
, or the problem (
1.5
) has an ordered sub and super solution pair. Both conditions are way too stringent for limit cycles.
The term “a priori estimate” refers to estimating solutions, without knowledge of these solutions. If one attempts to get a priori estimates also without knowledge of the equation, one is led to impose very stringent conditions, as above. In our existence results we assume that a priori estimates hold, and then we derive such estimates for concrete systems by using trapping regions, or energy considerations.
Our approach to limit cycles provides a straightforward alternative to the traditional one, based on the Poincare-Bendixson theorem, making it unnecessary to search for the trapping region in each particular case. Moreover, it unifies some diverse applications, and makes it convenient to handle multiple limit cycles.

We also present a detailed description of the numerical computation of the global solution curves, and of limit cycles. In particular, we compute limit cycles for the Van der Pol and Sel’kov’s equations, and the bifurcation diagrams for periodic population models with “fishing”.

The global solution curve

We begin with some preliminary results. The following lemma is well-known as Wirtinger’s inequality. Its proof follows easily by using the complex Fourier series, and the orthogonality of the functions
\{e^{i\omega nt}\}
on the interval
(0,T)
, see e.g., P. Korman
[
8
]
.

Lemma 2.1

Assume that
f(t)
is a continuously differentiable function of period
T
, and of zero average, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{T}f(s)\,ds=0
. Then

\int_{0}^{T}{f^{\prime}}^{2}(t)\,dt\geq\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{T}f^{2}(t)\,dt\,,\;\;\mbox{with $\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}$}\,.

We shall need the following standard lemma, see e.g.,
[
11
]
,
[
8
]
.

Lemma 2.2

Consider the equation

z^{\prime}+a(t)z=0\,,

with a given continuous
T
-periodic function
a(t)
. This equation has a non-zero
T
-periodic solution if and only if
\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\,dt=0
.

The following lemma will be crucial for continuation of solutions.

Lemma 2.3

Let
a(t)
be a given continuous
T
-periodic function, and
b\in R
a constant. The differential equation

(2.1)

w^{\prime}+a(t)w=b

does not have a
T
-periodic solution,
w(t)=w(t+T)
, satisfying

(2.2)

\int_{0}^{T}w(t)\,dt=0\,,

unless
b=0
, in which case
w(t)=0
.

Proof:
With
\nu(t)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau}>0
, the solution to (
2.1
) satisfying
w(0)=w_{0}
can be written as

w(t)=\frac{1}{\nu(t)}\left(b\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)\,ds+w_{0}\right)\,.

The periodicity condition
w(T)-w(0)=0
, and the requirement (
2.2
) lead to the following two equations

\displaystyle b\int_{0}^{T}\nu(s)ds+w_{0}(1-\nu(T))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle b\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)\,ds}{\nu(t)}\;dt+w_{0}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\nu(t)}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

The determinant of this linear system for
b
and
w_{0}
is

D=\int_{0}^{T}\nu(s)\,ds\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{\nu(t)}\,dt-\int^{T}_{0}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)\,ds}{\nu(t)}\,dt+\nu(T)\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)\,ds}{\nu(t)}\,dt.

Since
\nu(t)>0
it follows that
\int_{0}^{t}\nu(s)\,ds<\int_{0}^{T}\nu(s)\,ds
for
t<T
, and then the sum of the first two terms in
D
is positive. This shows that
D>0
, and then the linear system has only the trivial solution:
b=0
and
w_{0}=0
.

\diamondsuit

We shall denote by

and

T
-periodic functions. The next lemma represents the well-known

Lemma 2.4

Consider the equation

(2.3)

y^{\prime}+a(t)y=f(t)\,,

with
a(t)
,
f(t)\in C_{T}
. If
\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\,dt\neq 0

, for any

.
In case

\int_{0}^{T}f(t)u_{0}(t)\,dt=0

(2.4)

u^{\prime}-a(t)u=0\,.

The number of
T
-periodic solutions of (

The following lemma will be used to continue solutions of fixed average.

Lemma 2.5

Let
a(t)\in C_{T}
.
For any
\xi\in R
and
e(t)\in C_{T}
, there exists a pair
(\mu^{*},z(t))
, with
\mu^{*}\in R

, solving

(2.5)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}+a(t)z=\mu^{*}+e(t)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}z(t)\,dt=\xi\,.

Proof:
Case 1.
\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\,dt\neq 0
. Consider the operator

. By Lemma

By Lemma

\int_{0}^{T}L^{-1}(1)\,dt\neq 0

to satisfy the second equation in (

Case 2.
\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\,dt=0
. This time

). Choose
\mu^{*}
so that

, where

-periodic solution of

z^{\prime}+a(t)z=0\,.

Choose the constant

\diamondsuit

Lemma 2.6

Let
u(t)\in C^{1}_{T}
be a solution of

(2.6)

u^{\prime}(t)=f(t,u)\;\;\;\;\mbox{for all $t\in R$}\,,

for some continuous

, while

is a sub (super) solution of (

v^{\prime}(t)<f(t,v)\;\;\left(v^{\prime}(t)>f(t,v)\right)\;\;\;\;\mbox{for all $t\in R$}\,.

Then

do not intersect (and hence they are strictly ordered).

Proof:
Assume that
v(t)
is a super solution, and the other case is similar. If

is a point of intersection of

and
v(t)
, then
v^{\prime}(t_{0})>u^{\prime}(t_{0})
.
If the

\diamondsuit

Any

We now consider the following problem: find

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=\mu+e(t)\,,\;\;\;\;\mbox{$u(t+T)=u(t)$}\;\mbox{ for all $t$}\,.

Theorem 2.1

Assume that
g(t,u)\in C^{0,1}(R\times R)

g(t+T,u)=g(t,u)

u
, and

. Assume that either
g(t,u)=g(u)
, or there is a constant

|g(t,u)|\leq c_{0}\,,\;\;\;\;\mbox{ for all $t\in R$, and $u\in R$}\,.

Then for any

Decompose

\xi

\mu

If

We then estimate

We now embed (
2.7

. We now continue in

\displaystyle F(u,\mu,k)\equiv u^{\prime}+kg(t,u)-\mu-e(t)=0\,,\;\;\mbox{$u(t+T)=u(t)$}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,dt=\xi^{0}\,,

\displaystyle F_{(u,\mu)}(u,\mu,k)(w,\mu^{*})=w^{\prime}+kg_{u}(t,u)w-\mu^{*}\,,

\displaystyle F(U,\mu,\xi)\equiv U^{\prime}+kg(t,\xi+U)-\mu-e(t)=0\,,\;\;\mbox{$U(t+T)=U(t)$}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}U(t)\,dt=0\,,

3
Existence of

We apply the global solution curves to give alternative proofs of two results of H. Brezis and L. Nirenberg
[
3
]
on the existence of
T
-periodic solutions of

(3.1)

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=0\,,\;u(t)=u(t+T)\;\;\mbox{ for all $t$}\,.

Theorem 3.1

Assume that
g(t,u)\in C^{0,1}(R\times R)
satisfies
g(t+T,u)=g(t,u)
for all
t
and
u
. Assume the existence of constants
\underline{M}<\overline{M}
such that

(3.2)

\int_{0}^{T}g(t,\underline{M})\,dt<0\,,\;\;\int_{0}^{T}g(t,\overline{M})\,dt>0\,.

Assume finally the existence of constants
M_{1}<M_{2}
such that for all

(3.3)

\displaystyle g(t,u)>g(t,\overline{M})\;\;\mbox{for $u>M_{2}$}

\displaystyle g(t,u)<g(t,\underline{M})\;\;\mbox{for $u<M_{1}$}\,.

Then the equation (
3.1
) has a
T
-periodic solution.

Proof:
Step 1. A priori estimate. Write
g(t,\overline{M})=\xi-v^{\prime}(t)
, with
\int_{0}^{T}v(t)\,dt=0
, and
\xi>0
by (
3.2
). Consider
u(t)-v(t)
. It satisfies

(3.4)

\left(u-v\right)^{\prime}+g(t,u)=-v^{\prime}=g(t,\overline{M})-\xi<g(t,\overline{M})\,.

Let
t_{0}
be the point of maximum of

. At
t_{0}

(3.5)

g(t_{0},u(t_{0}))<g(t_{0},\overline{M})\,,

so that

by (

u-v\leq\max\left(u-v\right)=u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})<M_{2}+\max|v|\,,

u<v+M_{2}+\max|v|\leq M_{2}+2\max|v|\,.

This gives an estimate of
u(t)
from above. To get an estimate from below, write
g(t,\underline{M})=\eta+w^{\prime}
, with
\int_{0}^{T}w(t)\,dt=0
, and
\eta<0
by (
3.2
). We have

\left(u+w\right)^{\prime}+g(t,u)=w^{\prime}=g(t,\underline{M})-\eta>g(t,\underline{M})\,.

Let
t_{1}
be the point of minimum of

. Then
g(t_{1},u(t_{1}))>g(t_{1},\underline{M})
, and

). We conclude

u+w\geq\min\left(u+w\right)=u(t_{1})+w(t_{1})>M_{1}-\max|w|\,,

u>-w+M_{1}-\max|w|\geq M_{1}-2\max|w|\,.

Step 2. Modify
g(t,u)
for
u
outside of
[\underline{M},\overline{M}]
to be a bounded function, satisfying (
3.3
), and call it
\bar{g}(t,u)
. Clearly,
\bar{g}(t,u)
satisfies (
3.2
) too. By above, any
T
-periodic solution of

u^{\prime}+\bar{g}(t,u)=0

satisfies the same a priori estimate as before, and hence it is a solution of (
3.1
). Therefore we may still work with (
3.1
), but assume that
g(t,u)
is bounded.

Step 3. Embed (
3.1
) into a family of problems

(3.6)

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=\mu\,,\;u(t)=u(t+T)\;\;\mbox{ for all $t$}\,,

depending on a parameter
\mu
. Decompose
u(t)=\xi+U(t)
, with
\xi\in R
and
\int_{0}^{T}U(t)\,dt=0
. By Theorem
2.1
we have a continuous solution curve
\mu=\mu(\xi)
for all
\xi\in(-\infty,\infty)
. Recall that
U(t)
satisfies

(3.7)

U^{\prime}+g(t,\xi+U)=\mu\,,\;\int_{0}^{T}U(t)\,dt=0\,,\;U(t)=U(t+T)\,,

and
\max|U(t)|
is bounded uniformly in
\xi
and
\mu
.

Step 4. Integrate (
3.7
):

\mu T=\int_{0}^{T}g(t,\xi+U(t))\,dt\,.

For
\xi
large,
\xi+U(t)>M_{2}
for all
t
. Using (
3.3
), and then (
3.2
)

(3.8)

\mu T>\int_{0}^{T}g(t,\overline{M})\,dt>0\,,

so that
\mu>0
. Similarly,
\mu<0
for
-\xi
large. We conclude the existence of
\xi_{0}
with
\mu(\xi_{0})=0
, which gives a solution of (
3.1
).

\diamondsuit

4
A further existence result, and applications to limit cycles

We now change the notation, and consider the existence of
2\pi
-periodic solutions of

(4.1)

\frac{dr}{d\theta}+g(\theta,r)=0\,,

where
g(\theta,r)

is
2\pi
-periodic in
\theta
. It is known that any
2\pi
-periodic solution of (
4.1
) is in fact a limit cycle, attracting the nearby solutions, see V.A. Pliss
[
12
]
. We shall assume that

g(\theta,0)=0\,,\;\;\mbox{for all $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$}\,,

which is the case in applications to limit cycles. The last assumption implies that solutions of (
4.1
) do not change sign, and we shall search for solutions with
r(\theta)>0
for all
\theta
. We now give a general result on the existence of a
2\pi
-periodic solution of (
4.1
).

Theorem 4.1

Assume that
g(\theta,0)\equiv 0
, and
g(\theta,r)\in C^{0,1}(\bar{R}_{+}\times[0,2\pi])
satisfies
g(\theta+2\pi,r)=g(\theta,r)
, for all
\theta
and
r
. Assume that there a constant
M_{0}>0
, such that any positive
2\pi
-periodic solution of (
4.1
) satisfies
0<r(\theta)<M_{0}
. Assume that for some
p\geq 1
the following limits exist, uniformly in
\theta

(4.2)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{g(\theta,r)}{r}=a(\theta)\,,\;\;\mbox{with $\int_{0}^{2\pi}a(\theta)\,d\theta>0$ $(<0)$}\,,

(4.3)

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(\theta,r)}{r^{p}}=b(\theta)\,,\;\;\mbox{with $\int_{0}^{2\pi}b(\theta)\,d\theta<0$ $(>0)$}\,.

Then the equation (
4.1
) has a
2\pi
-periodic solution
r(\theta)>0
.

Proof:
Assume that the first set of inequalities holds in (
4.2
), (
4.3
), and the other case is similar.
By modifying
g(\theta,r)
for
r>M_{0}
, and for
r<0
, we may assume that this function is bounded, for all
\theta
and
r
.
We embed (
4.1
) into a family of problems

(4.4)

r^{\prime}+g(\theta,r)=\mu\,,\;\;r(\theta+2\pi)=r(\theta)\,.

Decompose
r(\theta)=\xi+R(\theta)
, with
\int_{0}^{2\pi}R(\theta)\,d\theta=0
. By Theorem
2.1
, we have a solution curve
\mu=\mu(\xi)
, with
\xi\geq 0
, and
\mu(0)=0
. We wish to show that
\mu(\xi_{0})=0
for some
\xi_{0}>0
.

We claim that for any
\xi>0
,
r(\theta)>0
for all
\theta
. In case
\mu=0
,
r(\theta)
cannot change sign by the uniqueness theorem, and since its average is positive,
r(\theta)
is positive. In case
|\mu|>0
, we see from the equation (
4.4
) that
r^{\prime}(\theta)
has the same sign at all of its roots, which makes roots impossible. Again,
r(\theta)
is positive, since its average
\xi
is positive.

We show next that
\mu(\xi)>0
for
\xi>0
and small. Divide the equation (
4.4
) by
r(\theta)>0
, and integrate over
(0,2\pi)
. By periodicity,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}d\theta=0
, and we have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{g(\theta,r(\theta))}{r(\theta)}\,d\theta=\mu\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{r(\theta)}\,d\theta\,.

By continuity,
r(\theta)
is small when
\xi>0
and small. Then
\mu>0
, in view of the condition (
4.2
).
Similarly, dividing the equation (
4.4
) by
r^{p}(\theta)>0
, and integrating over
(0,2\pi)
, we see that
\mu(\xi)>0
for
\xi
large. By continuity of
\mu(\xi)
, we conclude that
\mu(\xi_{0})=0
at some
\xi_{0}>0
.

\diamondsuit

Example
This theorem provides the existence of positive
2\pi
-periodic solution to the equation

r^{\prime}+a(\theta)r+b(\theta)r^{p}=0\,,

with
p>1
, and
2\pi
-periodic
a(\theta)
and
b(\theta)
such that
\int_{0}^{2\pi}a(\theta)\,d\theta
and
\int_{0}^{2\pi}b(\theta)\,d\theta
have opposite signs, provided there is an a priori bound on
2\pi
-periodic solutions. An a priori bound will hold if, for example,
b(\theta)
has no roots. Both Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
do not apply in case of sign-changing
a(\theta)
and
b(\theta)
.

We now provide a version of the last result. Introduce the function (
M
is a real number)

\varphi(M)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(\theta,M)\,d\theta\,.

Then the conditions (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) can be replaced by
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
(
<0
), and
\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\varphi(M)}{M^{p}}<0
(
>0
) respectively. Indeed,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}a(\theta)\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{g(\theta,r)}{r}\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{r}(\theta,0)\,d\theta=\varphi^{\prime}(0)\,,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}b(\theta)\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g(\theta,r)}{r^{p}}\,d\theta=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\varphi(M)}{M^{p}}\,.

We now turn to the limit cycles, which are closed solutions of autonomous systems of the type

(4.5)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}(t)=F(X,Y)

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}(t)=G(X,Y)\,.

It is well known that each limit cycle contains at least one rest point, see e.g., S.H. Strogatz
[
14
]
. Let
(x_{0},y_{0})
be a rest point of (
4.5
), so that

F(x_{0},y_{0})=G(x_{0},y_{0})=0\,.

We wish to find a limit cycle, enclosing
(x_{0},y_{0})
. Setting
x=X-x_{0}
and
y=Y-y_{0}
, we obtain

(4.6)

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)=F(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})

\displaystyle y^{\prime}(t)=G(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})\,,

with the rest point at the origin
(0,0)
. We then switch to polar coordinates in the
(x,y)
plane, given by
x=r\cos\theta
and
y=r\sin\theta
. We obtain

(4.7)

\frac{dr}{dt}=\frac{xF(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})+yG(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\,,

and

\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\frac{\frac{dy}{dt}x-\frac{dx}{dt}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{xG(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})-yF(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\,.

Using that
\frac{dr}{dt}=\frac{dr}{d\theta}\frac{d\theta}{dt}
, and
defining

h(x,y)=\frac{xF(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})+yG(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})}{xG(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})-yF(x+x_{0},y+y_{0})}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\,,

and

(4.8)

g(\theta,r)=-h\left(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta\right)\,,

we obtain from (
4.7
)

\frac{dr}{d\theta}+g(\theta,r)=0\,.

This is a particular case of the equation (
4.1
),
where
g(\theta,r)
is given by (
4.8
).
Clearly,
g(\theta,0)=0
, and
g(\theta,r)
has period
2\pi
in
\theta
. We study the existence of a
2\pi
-periodic solution
r(\theta)
of that equation, which corresponds to a limit cycle enclosing
(x_{0},y_{0})
, that is a graph of a polar curve around
(x_{0},y_{0})
. Observe that the function
g(\theta,r)
is
singular
, i.e., it assumes infinite values at some finite
\theta
and
r
. Singular problems occur often in applications, see the recent book of P.J. Torres
[
15
]
for a nice review, or P. Korman
[
7
]
.

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=-x+ay+x^{2}y

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=b-ay-x^{2}y\,.

and

a^{2}+2ab^{2}+a+b^{4}-b^{2}<0\,.

\left(x+y\right)^{\prime}=b-x\,,

for

Conclusions
. Given that the autonomous system (
4.5
) has a rest point at
(x_{0},y_{0})
, we introduce polar coordinates with respect to
(x_{0},y_{0})
, and convert (
4.5
) to the periodic first order equation (
4.1
). Then we embed (
4.1
) into the family of equations (
4.4
).
Since the functions
g(\theta,r)
is often singular, we approximate the solutions of (
4.4
) by those of (
4.15
), for which we compute the global solution curve
\bar{\mu}=\bar{\mu}(\xi)
, where
\xi
is the average of the solution
r(\theta)
over
[0,2\pi]
. Each root of
\bar{\mu}(\xi)
provides us with a limit cycle of (
4.5
). This straightforward approach avoids the need to construct the trapping region for each particular system (
4.5
).
Our approach raises an interesting open question. Suppose a limit cycle of (
4.5
) encloses just one rest point. Under what condition this limit cycle
is star-shaped with respect to the rest point (so that it is a polar curve with respect to the rest point).

5
Numerical computation of solutions

To solve the periodic problem (with
g(t+T,u)=g(t,u)
, for all
t
and
u
)

(5.1)

u^{\prime}+g(t,u)=\mu+e(t)\,,\;u(t)=u(t+T)\;\;\mbox{ for all $t$}\,,

we used continuation in
\xi
. (Recall that
u(t)=\xi+U(t)
, with
\int_{0}^{T}U(t)\,dt=0
.)

Step 1
. We began by implementing the numerical solution of the following linear periodic problem: given the
T
-periodic functions
b(t)
and
f(t)
, find the
T
-periodic solution of

(5.2)

\;\;\;\;\;\;L[y]\equiv y^{\prime}(t)+b(t)y=f(t),\;\;y(t)=y(t+T)\,.

The general solution of (
5.2
) can be written as

y(t)=y_{1}(t)+cy_{2}(t)\,,

where
y_{1}(t)
is the solution of

y^{\prime}(t)+b(t)y=f(t)\,,\;\;y(0)=0\,,

y_{2}(t)
is the solution of

y^{\prime}(t)+b(t)y=0\,,\;\;y(0)=1\,,

and
c
is an arbitrary constant. We have
y(0)=c
,
y(T)=y_{1}(T)+cy_{2}(T)
, and setting
y(0)=y(T)
gives
c=-\frac{y_{1}(T)}{y_{2}(T)-1}
, so that

(5.3)

y(t)=y_{1}(t)-\frac{y_{1}(T)}{y_{2}(T)-1}y_{2}(t)

gives us the
T
-periodic solution of (
5.2
), or
L^{-1}[f(t)]
, where
L[y]
denotes the left hand side of (
5.2
), subject to the periodic boundary condition. We used
Mathematica
’s NDSolve command to compute
y_{1}(t)
and
y_{2}(t)
.

A natural question is why we did not use the explicit formula available for the equation (
5.2
). To solve the nonlinear problem (
5.1
) we shall be repeatedly solving the linear problem (
5.2
) with
b(t)
and
f(t)
given as interpolating functions (produced by
Mathematica
on the previous steps). The integrating factor
\nu(t)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}b(t)\,dt}
will require computing a definite integral for each value of
t
, and then solving of (
5.2
) will require double integration, while computation of
y_{1}(t)
and
y_{2}(t)
can be compared to a single integration. The explicit formula appears to be a useless curiosity, as far as numerical computations go.

Step 2
. Then we implemented the
“linear solver”
, i.e., the numerical solution of the following problem: given the
T
-periodic functions
b(t)
and
f(t)
, find the constant
\mu
, so that the problem

(5.4)

y^{\prime}+b(t)y=\mu+f(t),\;\int_{0}^{T}y(t)\,dt=0

has a
T
-periodic solution (of zero average), and compute that solution
y(t)
. The solution is

6
Periodic population model with fishing

We begin with the logistic population model (
u=u(t)
is the number of units of fish at time
t
)

u^{\prime}=u(a(t)-u)\,.

We assume that
a(t)
is a continuous and
T
-periodic function, satisfying
\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\,dt>0
. By direct integration, it is easy to see that there is a unique
T
-periodic solution, which attracts all other positive solutions, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, see e.g., M.N. Nkashama
[
11
]
, or P. Korman
[
8
]
(exact multiplicity results for more general polynomial models were given in P. Korman and T. Ouyang
[
9
]
). To model fishing, let
j(t)
be a positive continuous
T
-periodic function, giving the number of units of fish taken at time
t
, and let the number
b>0
give the decrease in the growth rate per one unit of fish taken, and denote
f(t)=bj(t)
. Then the model is

u^{\prime}=u(a(t)-u)-f(t)\,,\;\;\;\;u(t+T)=u(t)\;\;\mbox{for all $t$}\,.

Let us now vary the intensity of fishing, and consider

(6.1)

u^{\prime}=u(a(t)-u)-\mu f(t)\,,\;\;\;\;u(t+T)=u(t)\;\;\mbox{for all $t$}\,,

depending on a parameter
\mu>0
.

The following result is well known see
[
1
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
, and also
[
2
]
. We sketch the proof for completeness.

Proposition 1

Assume that
\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\,dt>0
, and
f(t)>0
. Then there is a critical
\mu_{0}>0
, so that the problem (
6.1
) has two exactly positive
T
-periodic solutions for
0<\mu<\mu_{0}
, exactly one positive
T
-periodic solution at
\mu=\mu_{0}
and for
\mu<0
, and no
T
-periodic solution for
\mu>\mu_{0}
. (With the bifurcation diagram as in Figure
6
. The case when
\mu<0
can be interpreted as stocking of fish). Moreover, in case
\mu>\mu_{0}
, all solutions of (
6.1
) tend to
-\infty
, in finite time.

Proof:
At
\mu=0
the equation (
6.1
) has a unique
T
-periodic solution. We now continue
T
-periodic solutions for
\mu>0
. Clearly,
T
-periodic solutions remain positive. Indeed, assuming otherwise, we can find an interval
(t_{1},t_{2})
on which
u(t)<0
that
u(t_{1})=u(t_{2})=0
. From the equation (
6.1
),
u^{\prime}(t)<0
on
(t_{1},t_{2})
, a contradiction. The continuation in
\mu
cannot proceed indefinitely, because the right hand side of (
6.1
) becomes negative. There is at most two positive
T
-periodic solutions of (
6.1
), see e.g. p. 245 in
[
8
]
.

\diamondsuit

It follows from the Proposition
1
that for
\mu>\mu_{0}
the fishing is not sustainable, while
\mu=\mu_{0}
gives the maximal sustainable level of fishing. We call
\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt

the maximal fishing take
(
\frac{\mu_{0}}{T}\int_{0}^{T}j(t)\,dt
gives the maximal possible average number of fish, caught over one period). The problem is:
find the fishing strategy
f(t)
(or
j(t)
) to maximize the maximal fishing take
\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt
. For example, assume that the growth rate
a(t)
has one point of minimum and one point of maximum, corresponding to summer and winter. What are good seasons for fishing?

In case
a(t)=a
and
f(t)=1
are constants, the
T
-periodic solutions of (
6.1
) are also constants. In that case
\mu_{0}=\frac{a^{2}}{4}
, and the maximal fishing take is then
\mu_{0}T=\frac{a^{2}}{4}T
.

Proposition 2

Assume that
a(t)=a
is constant. Then the optimal fishing strategy is for
f(t)
to be a constant too, i.e.,

(6.2)

\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt\leq\frac{a^{2}}{4}T\,.

Proof:
Assume that
u_{0}(t)
is the solution of (
6.1
) at
\mu_{0}
, i.e.,

(6.3)

u^{\prime}_{0}=au_{0}-u_{0}^{2}-\mu_{0}f(t)\,.

Since the solution curve does not continue in
\mu
to the right of
\mu_{0}
, the implicit function theorem does not apply, and so the corresponding linearized equation

w^{\prime}=(a-2u_{0})w

has non-trivial
T
-periodic solutions. By Lemma
2.2
,
\int_{0}^{T}u_{0}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}aT
. We now integrate (
6.3
), to obtain

\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}a^{2}T-\int_{0}^{T}u^{2}_{0}(t)\,dt\leq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}T-\frac{1}{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}u_{0}(t)\,dt\right)^{2}=\frac{a^{2}}{4}T\,,

concluding the proof.

\diamondsuit

The inequality (
6.2
) can be generalized to any
T
-periodic
a(t)
. Indeed writing the equation (
6.1
) as

u^{\prime}=-\left(u-\frac{1}{2}a(t)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}a^{2}(t)-\mu f(t)\,,

and integrating, we conclude that for all
\mu

(6.4)

\mu\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T}a^{2}(t)\,dt\,.

Suppose that
a(t)
is not a constant. Is it true that the optimal fishing strategy is still for
f(t)
to be a constant? Our numerical experiments indicate that the answer is no, in general.

Open Problem:
Given
T
-periodic non-constant
a(t)
, find the optimal
T
-periodic
f(t)
, maximizing the maximal fishing take
\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt

Our numerical experiments suggest the following conjecture.

Conjecture
: Decompose

, with constant
A>0

\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(t)\,dt=0

(6.6)

\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt\leq\frac{A^{2}}{4}T\,.
