On (discounted) global Eikonal equations in metric spaces
Trí Minh Lê & Sebastián Tapia-García

Abstract




Abstract.
Eikonal equations in metric spaces have strong connections with the local slope operator (or the De Giorgi slope).
In this manuscript, we explore and delve into an analogous model based on the global slope operator, expressed as
\lambda u+G[u]=\ell
, where
\lambda\geq 0
.
In strong contrast with the classical theory, the global slope operator relies neither on the local properties of the functions nor on the structure of the space, and therefore new insights are developed in order to analyze the above equation.
Under mild assumptions on the metric space
X
and the given data
\ell
, we primarily discuss:
(a)
the existence and uniqueness of (pointwise) solutions;
(b)
a viscosity perspective and the employment of Perron’s method to consider the maximal solution;
(c)
stability of the maximal solution with respect to both, the data
\ell
and the discount factor
\lambda
.
Our techniques provide a method to approximate solutions of Eikonal equations in metric spaces and a new integration formula based on the global slope of the given function.

Key words.
Global slope, Eikonal equation, Metric space, Well-posedness.

Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonempty open bounded set and let
\ell:\Omega\to(0,+\infty)
be a continuous function. The Eikonal equation reads as follows

(
\EuScript{E}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\|Du(x)\|=\ell(x),&\text{for all }x\in\Omega,\\
u=0,&\text{for all }x\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

This equation has been extensively studied in several settings (Hilbert and Banach spaces
[
13
,
14
]
) because of its many applications.
It is an important model of the larger family of the so-called Hamilton–Jacobi equations (HJEs for short), all of them having the form
H(x,u,Du)=0
.
The notion of viscosity solutions has been developed to remedy the possible lack of classical solutions. It became essential in the study of HJEs and, in particular of the Eikonal equations.

During the last decades, there has been a growing effort to generalize equation (

⁢
\EuScript
E

) to a purely metric setting motivated by applications in Optimal Transportation, Mean Field Games and Optimal Control on Topological Networks
[
1
,
3
,
8
,
10
,
28
,
41
]
.
In the literature, we can find several notions of solutions of HJEs defined on metric spaces. Some of them are based on the notion of viscosity solution.
Most notably, according to
[
34
]
, slopes– and curve–based solutions are defined and studied in
[
2
,
26
,
27
]
.
In
[
34
]
, assuming that the underlying metric space is complete and length, and under some extra continuity conditions on the data
\ell
, it is shown that these two notions coincide with Monge solutions.
Moreover, Monge solutions of metric HJEs with discontinuous Hamiltonians have been addressed in
[
7
,
23
,
35
,
37
]
.
This notion of solution does not require any test functions.
Indeed, Monge solutions for the Eikonal equation in metric spaces are pointwise solutions of what we call the local slope equation, based on the local slope operator (or De Giorgi slope).
That is, for a metric space
X
and a continuous function
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)
, we search for a function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
that pointwise satisfies

(1.1)

\begin{cases}s[u](x)=\ell(x),&\text{ for all }x\in X,\\
u=0,&\text{ for all }x\in[\ell=0],\end{cases}

where the local slope operator is defined by

\displaystyle s[u](x):=\begin{cases}\limsup_{y\to x}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))_{+}}{d(x,y)},&\text{ if $x$ is not isolated, }\\
\phantom{arisdaniilidis}0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{+}=\max\{\alpha,0\}
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The convention
s[u](x)=+\infty
if
u(x)=+\infty
is used.

The local slope has been studied in depth as an upper gradient that generalizes the norm of the derivative of a differentiable function
[
3
]
.
Another natural upper gradient is the so–called global slope. That is,

\displaystyle G[u](x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sup_{y\neq x}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))_{+}}{d(x,y)},&\text{if }u(x)<+\infty,\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Clearly,
G[u]\geq s[u]
for any function
u
, and
G[u]=s[u]
when
u
is convex.
Further information on upper gradients can be found in
[
3
]
and references therein.

The main purpose of this manuscript is to study the (discounted) global Eikonal equation, see equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) in Section
3
.
Let us focus on the global Eikonal equation without discount term:

(
\EuScript{G}_{0}
)

\begin{cases}G[u](x)=\ell(x),&~\text{for all }x\in X,\\
\inf_{x\in X}u(x)=0,&\end{cases}

where
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)
is a lower semicontinuous (lsc for short) function such that
\inf_{X}\ell=0
.
A lsc function
u:X\to[0,+\infty)
with
\inf_{X}u=0
is called a solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) if
G[u]=\ell
holds pointwise.
Note that the above equation can be a model for phenomena in which the underlying metric space has no length structure, for instance, in graphs or in fractal domains. In the Euclidean setting, an equation involving a global slope-like operator appeared in the study of Hölder infinity Laplacians
[
11
]
.

Let us discuss about the setting of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).
The assumption of lower semicontinuity of
\ell
follows from the fact that the global slope of a lsc function is lsc, Propostion
2.2
.
A natural boundary condition for the Eikonal equation is the so-called Dirichlet condition.
However, this type of condition is not enough to ensure the uniqueness of solutions for global slope equations.
Indeed, observe that the functions
f,g,h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x)=x
,
g(x)=-x
and
h(x)=\min\{x,-x\}
satisfy
G[u]=1
in
\mathbb{R}
and
u(0)=0
.
Surprisingly, in
[
16
,
31
,
42
]
, it is shown that the equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) admits at most one (bounded from below) solution.
This explains that our boundary condition reads as
\inf_{X}u=0
(i.e. we search for functions that are bounded from below).
Also, a simple consequence of Ekeland’s principle shows that the infimum of the global slope of a bounded from below function, which is defined on a complete metric space, is
0
.
Therefore, the assumption
\inf_{X}\ell=0
is a necessary condition for the existence of solutions when the underlying space is complete.

To construct a solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

), we study the asymptotic behavior of the semigroup defined by the operator

(1.2)

T_{\ell}\,u(x):=\inf\,\left\{u(y)+\ell(x)d(x,y):y\in X\right\},\text{ for all }x\in X.

In the language of discrete weak KAM theory
[
44
,
45
]
, the operator
T_{\ell}
corresponds to a (degenerated) discrete Lax–Oleinik semigroup associated with the equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).
In the mentioned theory, the term
\ell(x)d(x,y)
in the definition of
T_{\ell}
is replaced by a
continuous
cost function
c:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, suitable assumptions are made on the space
X
.
This theory has been developed as a discrete model of the weak KAM theory
[
24
]
.
We would like to emphasize that, for existence of solutions of our equation, no assumptions on the space
X
are made.

An important portion of this work is devoted to analyzing the existence of solutions, the uniqueness issue, and the stability of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

), which reads as follows:
\lambda u+G[u]=\ell
.
Concerning the existence of viscosity solutions of HJEs in the classical setting, there are two main approaches to build a solution: constructing the value function of a related control problem
[
5
]
and the Perron’s method
[
30
]
.
Note that usually, Perron’s method does not give a formula to compute the provided solution.
In the metric setting, for the local slope equation, several authors have considered a solution that is obtained through the value function of a control problem among absolutely continuous curves, addressing as well the uniqueness issue for their respective equations
[
2
,
26
,
27
]
.
In the language of determination theorems, uniqueness results have been tackled for slope–like operators, with the extra assumption that the functions are bounded from below.
Indeed, in
[
6
]
it is shown that two
\mathcal{C}^{2}
–smooth convex functions (which are bounded from below) defined on a Hilbert space coincide up to an additive constant if and only if their local slopes coincide (if and only if their global slopes coincide). This determination result was generalized threefold: from smooth convex functions to merely lsc functions, from Hilbert spaces to complete metric spaces, and from local slope to general descent modulus
[
16
,
17
,
31
,
38
,
42
]
.
As a direct consequence of
[
31
, Theorem 1]
, if the underlying space is complete, there is at most one solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).

Coming back to the classical framework of HJEs, some classical stability results are provided in
[
12
,
14
]
.
In the metric framework, we can also find stability results for metric HJEs in
[
27
,
36
]
.
In the convex case, roughly speaking, it has been shown that the difference between two functions on a bounded set is controlled by the
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
-norm of the difference of their local slopes on that set (equivalently of their global slopes)
[
15
]
.
Further, for lsc convex functions defined on finite-dimensional spaces, the stability under the
\Gamma
–convergence of the slope is established in
[
19
]
.

A third approach to solve HJEs of the form
H(x,Du)=0
is the so–called ergodic approximation method, which was introduced in
[
33
, Section 2]
to study the cell problem that arises in the periodic homogenization of HJEs.
This method considers an auxiliary equation involving a discount factor:
\lambda u+H(x,Du)=0
, where
\lambda>0
, and then studies the asymptotic behavior of the solutions as
\lambda
tends to
0^{+}
.
In the framework of weak KAM theory, the ergodic approximation method was employed in
[
20
]
where the underlying space is a smooth compact connected manifold. In the compact metric setting, a notion of discounted Lax–Oleinik semigroup was introduced in
[
21
]
, in which the discount factor
\lambda
is plugged directly into the definition of the discrete Lax–Oleinik semigroup.
Indeed, in the aforementioned work, the authors considered the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}u(x)=\inf_{y\in X}\,\lambda u(y)+c(x,y),\text{ for all }x\in X,

and proved that the
\lambda
–discrete weak KAM solutions
\{u_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in(0,1)}
(that is
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}u_{\lambda}=u_{\lambda}+\beta
, for some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
) converges to
u_{1}
as
\lambda
tends to
1^{-}
.
The above research stream was recently investigated in the context of Shapley operators in
[
9
]
.
We would like to point out that, since we apply the ergodic approximation method to the global slope equation, our
discounted operator

T_{\lambda}
does not have the shape of a discounted Lax–Oleinik semigroup (as it does for the case
\lambda=0
).
Indeed, as it is defined in (
3.1
), the natural operator associated with equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) is given by

T_{\lambda}u(x)=\inf_{y\in X}\dfrac{u(y)+\ell(x)d(x,y)}{1+\lambda d(x,y)},\text{for every }x\in X.

Moreover, for us, fix points of
T_{\lambda}
only characterize subsolutions of our equation, Proposition
3.4
.
Therefore, new insights are required to study the discounted global slope equation (

\EuScript

G

2
Preliminaries

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space (which may be complete or not).
For any
x\in X
and
\rho>0
, we denote by
B_{\rho}(x)
the open ball centered at
x
with radius
\rho
.
Given a function
u:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, the
r
–sublevel set (resp.
r
–level set) is denoted by

\displaystyle[u\leq r]:=\{x\in X:u(x)\leq r\}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ (resp. $[u=r]:=\{x\in X:u(x)=r\}$)}.

The (effective) domain of
u
is denoted by
\mathrm{dom}~u=\{x\in X:u(x)<+\infty\}
and
u
is called proper if
\mathrm{dom}~u\neq\emptyset
. We also consider the space of real-valued lsc functions on
X
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LSC}(X)=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{X}:\quad u\text{ is lsc}\right\}.

The following proposition collects some easy facts about the global slope. Its proof is left to the reader.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a function and let
x\in X
. The following assertions hold true:

•

If
G[u](x)<+\infty
, then
u
is lsc at
x
.

•

If
G[u]
is everywhere finite, then
u\in\mathrm{LSC}(X)
.

•

If
G[u]
is locally bounded, then
u
is locally Lipschitz.

Note that the function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x)=x^{3}
shows that the reciprocal of the above three implications are false in general.
In the rest of the section, several facts of the global slope are provided.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{LSC}(X)
.
Then the function
G[u]:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is lsc.

Proof.

If
u
is constant, then
G[u]\equiv 0
and there is nothing to prove.
Let us assume that
u
is not constant and so, let
x\in X
such
G[u](x)>0
(possible
+\infty
).
Let
\alpha\in(0,G[u](x))
.
Then, by definition of
G[u]
, there is
y\in X\setminus\{x\}
such that

\varphi_{y}(x):=\dfrac{u(x)-u(y)}{d(x,y)}>\alpha.

Since
d
is continuous and
u
is lsc,
\varphi_{y}
is lsc on
X\setminus\{y\}
.
Let
{\delta\in(0,d(x,y))}
be such that, for any
z\in B_{\delta}(x)
,
\varphi_{y}(z)>\alpha
.
So, for any
z\in B_{\delta}(x)
, we have that
{G[u](z)>\alpha}
.
Since
\alpha
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
G[u](x)
,
G[u]
is lsc at
x
.
Finally, since
G[u]\geq 0
, we get that
G[u]
is a lsc function.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and
u\in\mathrm{LSC}(X)
be bounded from below.
Then, one has
\inf s_{u}(x)=\inf G[u]=0
.

Proof.

It follows from a direct consequence of Ekeland’s variational principle.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space.
Let
u:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a bounded from below proper lsc function.
Then, there is a sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}G[u](z_{n})=0,~\text{and }\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G[u](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})<+\infty.

Proof.

Let
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a decreasing sequence convergent to
0
such that
\epsilon_{n}/2<\epsilon_{n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Using Ekeland’s variational principle (see, for instance
[
43
, Theorem 1.4.1]
), there exists
z_{0}\in X
such that
G[u](z_{0})\leq\epsilon_{0}<+\infty
.
We construct a sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n}
by induction such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle G[u](z_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{n},

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}G[u](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq u(z_{n})-u(z_{n+1}).

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that we have already defined
\{z_{n}\}_{n=0}^{k}
satisfying above inequalities.
Fix
\varrho\in(\epsilon_{k}/2,\epsilon_{k+1})
.
Applying Ekeland’s variational principle again, we find
z_{k+1}\in X
such that

(2.1)

u(z_{k+1})\leq u(z_{k})-\varrho d(z_{k+1},z_{k})

and

(2.2)

u(z_{k+1})<u(y)+\varrho d(z_{k+1},y),\,\text{ for all }y\neq z_{k+1}.

From (
2.2
), we get
G[u](z_{k+1})\leq\varrho\leq\epsilon_{k+1}
.
Combining (
2.1
) and the fact that
G[u](z_{k})/2\leq\epsilon_{k}/2<\varrho
, we obtain
\frac{1}{2}G[u](z_{k})d(z_{k},z_{k+1})\leq u(z_{k})-u(z_{k+1})
.
The induction step is complete.

Observe that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}G[u](z_{n})=0
.
Moreover, since
u
is bounded from below, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G[u](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})\leq 2(u(z_{1})-\inf_{X}u)<+\infty.

The proposition is proven.
∎

X

u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper function. If a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subset X
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}G[u](x_{n})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G[u](x_{n+1})d(x_{n},x_{n+1})<+\infty,

then
\liminf_{n\to\infty}u(x_{n})=\inf_{X}u
.

3
The (discounted) global slope equation

Fix
(X,d)
a metric space,
\lambda\geq 0
and let
\ell:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a lsc function such that
\inf_{X}\ell=0
. Consider the discounted version of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

):

(
\EuScript{G}_{\lambda}
)

\begin{cases}\lambda u(x)+G[u](x)=\ell(x),&~\text{for all }x\in X,\\
{}\hskip 36.98866pt\inf_{x\in X}u(x)=0.\end{cases}

In this section, we demonstrate that, under mild assumptions on the data

, the global slope equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G

) always admits a solution.
The uniqueness issue was already treated in
[
31
]
for the case when

is complete and
\lambda=0
. In

, we provide a uniqueness result when
X
is complete,
\lambda>0
and

is bounded.
Further, as a consequence of the above results, we characterize complete metric spaces in terms of the uniqueness of the mentioned equation, see Corollary
3.12
.

We are interested in pointwise solutions of equation (

\EuScript

G
λ

In Section

Moreover, in

Definition 3.1
.

A function

is said to be a subsolution of (

λ

) if
\textstyle\inf_{X}u=0
and

\displaystyle\lambda u(x)+G[u](x)\leq\ell(x),\quad\text{for all }x\in X.

Similarly, a function

is said to be a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) if it is lsc,
\textstyle\inf_{X}v=0
and

\displaystyle\lambda v(x)+G[v](x)\geq\ell(x),\quad\text{for all }x\in X.

A lsc function

is a solution of (

\EuScript

G
λ

) if it is both sub– and supersolution of (

\EuScript

G

).

Existence results

Thanks to Proposition

, if

\EuScript

) (i.e.

Assumption 3.2

We say that the function

if there is a sequence

In the following proposition, we study the existence of supersolutions of equation (

\EuScript

G

). Observe that there is always a subsolution, namely the constant function equal to

Proposition 3.3
.

The following assertions hold true:

(a)

If
\lambda>0
, then (

\EuScript

G
λ

) always admits a supersolution.

(b)

If
\ell

), then (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) admits a supersolution.

Proof.

(a)
Since
\ell
is a lsc function with
\textstyle\inf_{X}\ell=0
, the function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by

is a supersolution of (

\EuScript

G
λ

).

(b)
We split the argument into two cases. Assume that the set
[\ell=0]
is nonempty (and therefore (
3.2
) is trivially satisfied).
Then, it follows that the function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by

u(x):=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{ for all }x\in[\ell=0]\\
+\infty&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).
Assume now that
[\ell=0]=\emptyset
and consider
\{\bar{x}_{n}\}_{n}\subset X
be a sequence given by
(H_{0})
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\{\bar{x}_{n}\}_{n}
is injective.
Also, since
\ell
is lsc and
\ell(x)>0
for all
x\in X
, the sequence
\{\bar{x}_{n}\}_{n}
does not have accumulation points.
Consider the function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

u(x):=\begin{cases}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\ell(\bar{x}_{n})d(\bar{x}_{n},\bar{x}_{n+1}),&~\text{if }x=\bar{x}_{k}\\
+\infty,&~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We show that

is lsc,
\textstyle\inf_{X}u=0
and
G[u]\geq\ell
in
X
.
Indeed, since
\{\bar{x}_{n}\}_{n}
has no accumulation points,
\textup{dom}\,u
is a discrete set and therefore

is lsc.
It is clear that
u\geq 0
and that, since the series given by assumption (
3.2
) is convergent,
\lim_{n\to\infty}u(\bar{x}_{n})=0
.
It only rests to compute

.
Let
x\in X
.
If

, then

. Hence
G[u](x)=+\infty
.
If
x=\bar{x}_{k}
, for some
k\in K
, we have

G[u](\bar{x}_{k})\geq\dfrac{u(\bar{x}_{k})-u(\bar{x}_{k+1})}{d(\bar{x}_{k},\bar{x}_{k+1})}=\ell(\bar{x}_{k}).

So,
G[u]\geq\ell
on
X
.
∎

Let
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a function such that
\lambda u(x)+G[u](x)\leq\ell(x)
for some

. Then, we have that

\displaystyle u(x)\leq\dfrac{u(y)+\ell(x)d(x,y)}{1+\lambda d(x,y)},\quad\text{ for all }y\in X.

Motivated by above inequality, we formally define the operator
{T_{\lambda,\ell}:[-\infty,+\infty]^{X}\to[-\infty,+\infty]^{X}}
by

(3.1)

T_{\lambda,\ell}u(x):=\inf\left\{\dfrac{u(y)+\ell(x)d(x,y)}{1+\lambda d(x,y)}:~y\in X\right\}.

If there is no risk of confusion, we may drop the subindexes
\lambda
and
\ell
and just write
T:=T_{\lambda,\ell}
.

Observe that, for any function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we have that
u\geq Tu
.
Also, it easily follows that, if
\textstyle\inf_{X}u=0
, then
\textstyle\inf_{X}Tu=0
.
That is,
T:[0,+\infty]^{X}\to[0,+\infty]^{X}
.
Albeit simple, let us proceed with the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a function.
Then,
Tu=u
if and only if
\lambda u(x)+G[u](x)\leq\ell(x)
for all
x\in X
.
Moreover,
u
is a subsolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) if and only if
Tu=u
and
\inf_{X}u=0
.

Proof.

The first equivalence immediately follows from the definition of
~T
.
The second equivalence is a consequence of the first part of the proposition and the definition of subsolutions.
∎

The next proposition shows that
T
can be used to define a projection onto the set of subsolutions of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
u_{0}:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a non-negative function.
Define
u_{n}:=Tu_{n-1}
for
n\geq 1
.
Then, the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to a function
v:X\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
Tv=v
.

Proof.

Since
u_{0}\geq 0
, we have that
T^{n-1}u_{0}\geq T^{n}u_{0}\geq 0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Hence,
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to some function
v
. To show that
Tv=v
, it suffices to check that
v\leq Tv
.
Reasoning towards a contradiction, we assume that there is
x\in X
such that
Tv(x)<v(x)
. Therefore, there is
y\in X
such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{v(y)+\ell(x)d(x,y)}{1+\lambda d(x,y)}<v(x)

By definition of
v
, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{u_{n}(y)+\ell(x)d(x,y)}{1+\lambda d(x,y)}<v(x)\leq u_{n}(x).

The above inequality yields that
v(x)\leq u_{n+1}(x)<v(x)
, which is a contradiction. The proof is complete.
∎

We denote by
T^{\infty}:[0,+\infty]^{X}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]^{X}
the operator defined by

(3.2)

\displaystyle T^{\infty}u(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{n}u(x).

Observe that, thanks to Proposition
3.5
, the operator
T^{\infty}
is well defined.
Moreover, if
u\in[0,+\infty]^{X}
is proper, then
Tu(X)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Also, a direct computation gives us that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle T^{n}u(x)=\inf\left\{\dfrac{u(x_{n})}{\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\lambda d(x_{k},x_{k+1})\right)}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\dfrac{\ell(x_{j})d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}{\prod_{k=0}^{j}\left(1+\lambda d(x_{k},x_{k+1})\right)}\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all finite sequences
\{x_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}\subset X
with
x_{0}=x
. The main result of this section reads as follows.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose that
u
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).
Then, the function
T^{\infty}u
is a solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).

Proof.

For the sake of brevity, we denote by
v:X\to\mathbb{R}
the function defined by

v(x):=T^{\infty}u(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{n}u(x).

Observe that
v\geq 0
and that
v(X)\subset\mathbb{R}
(because
u
is proper).

Claim 1
:
v
is a subsolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

)
.

Indeed, by Proposition
3.5
we have that
Tv=v
, and since
\inf_{X}u=0
, we have that
\inf_{X}v=0
. Therefore,
Proposition

3.4
implies that
v
is a subsolution.

Claim 2
:
v
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

)
.

Since
v
is a subsolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

), it follows that
v
is lsc and
\inf_{X}v=0
. So, we only need to show that
\lambda v+G[v]\geq\ell
.
Fix
x\in X
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider
\{x_{i}^{n}\}_{i=0}^{n}\in X
given by (
3.3
) such that
x_{0}^{n}=x
and

(3.4)

\displaystyle T^{n}u(x)+\frac{1}{n}\geq\dfrac{u(x_{n}^{n})}{\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\lambda d(x_{k}^{n},x_{k+1}^{n})\right)}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\dfrac{\ell(x_{j}^{n})d(x_{j}^{n},x_{j+1}^{n})}{\prod_{k=0}^{j}\left(1+\lambda d(x_{k}^{n},x_{k+1}^{n})\right)}.

For the sake of brevity, we shall write

\displaystyle A_{j}^{n}:=\dfrac{1}{\prod_{k=0}^{j}\left(1+\lambda d(x_{k}^{n},x_{k+1}^{n})\right)},\text{ for every }j<n.

Note that the finite sequence
\{x_{i}^{n}\}_{i=0}^{n}
depends on
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
To prove that
v
is a supersolution, we split the analysis into two cases.

Case 1:

v(x)=u(x)
.
Since
v\leq u
, we immediately get that
G[v](x)\geq G[u](x)
.
Thus,

\lambda v(x)+G[v](x)\geq\lambda u(x)+G[u](x)\geq\ell(x).

Case 2:

v(x)<u(x)
.
We again split the analysis into two cases.
To do this, let us set, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

{\epsilon_{n}=\max\left\{d(x,x_{i}^{n}):~i=1,...,n\right\}}.

Case 2.1:

\textstyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
.
Up to a subsequence, we assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
.
Notice that
\{x_{n}^{n}\}_{n}
converges to
x
.
Let us assume that
\lambda=0
.
Then,
A_{j}^{n}=1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j<n
.
This leads to

v(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{n}u(x)\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}u(x_{n}^{n})-\dfrac{1}{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\ell(x_{j}^{n})d(x_{j}^{n},x_{j+1}^{n})\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}u(x_{n}^{n})\geq u(x),

where the last inequality follows because
u
is lsc.
Therefore,
v(x)=u(x)
, which contradicts the assumption of Case
2
.
Now, assume that
\lambda>0
.
Observe that, thanks to a telescopic sum, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A_{j}^{n}d(x_{j}^{n},x_{j+1}^{n})=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\left(1-A_{n-1}^{n}\right).

Let
\rho>0
.
Recalling that
\ell
is lsc, consider
\delta>0
such that
\ell(y)\geq\ell(x)-\rho
for all
y\in B_{\delta}(x)
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be large enough such that
\epsilon_{n}<\delta
.
Then,

\displaystyle T^{n}u(x)+\dfrac{1}{n}

\displaystyle\geq A_{n-1}^{n}u(x_{n}^{n})+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\ell(x_{j}^{n})A_{j}^{n}d(x_{j}^{n},x_{j+1}^{n})

\displaystyle\geq A_{n-1}^{n}u(x_{n}^{n})+\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}\left(1-A_{n-1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}+A_{n-1}^{n}\dfrac{\lambda u(x_{n}^{n})-\ell(x)+\rho}{\lambda},

where in the second line we have used (
3.5
).
We define two sets of indexes

\displaystyle J_{1}:=\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda u(x_{n}^{n})\geq\ell(x)-\rho\right\}\text{ and }J_{2}:=\mathbb{N}\setminus J_{1}.

Since
J_{1}\cup J_{2}=\mathbb{N}
, at least one of these sets has countably infinitely many elements. If
J_{2}
has countably infinitely many elements, using the fact that
A_{n-1}^{n}\leq 1
and
\lambda u(x_{n}^{n})<\ell(x)-\rho
for every
n\in J_{2}
, we have

(3.6)

T^{n}u(x)+\dfrac{1}{n}\geq\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}+\dfrac{\lambda u(x_{n}^{n})-\ell(x)+\rho}{\lambda},\text{ for all }n\in J_{2}.

Therefore, taking limit inferior as
n\rightarrow\infty
and using the lower semicontinuity of
u
, we get

v(x)\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}u(x_{n}^{n})\geq u(x),

which contradicts the assumption of Case 2. Hence, the set
J_{2}
has finitely many elements and so
J_{1}
has infinitely many elements.
We then directly get

\displaystyle T^{n}u(x)+\dfrac{1}{n}\geq\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda},\text{ for all }n\in J_{1}.

Sending
n
to infinity and then
\rho
to
0
, we obtain that
\lambda v(x)\geq\ell(x)
.
Since
G[v](x)\geq 0
, it follows that
v
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).

Case 2.2:

\textstyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\epsilon_{n}>0
.
Let
r>0
be such that
\textstyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}>r
.
Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be large enough such that
\epsilon_{n}>r
for all
n\geq N
.
Fix
n\geq N
.
Thanks to the lower semicontinuity of
\ell
, let
\rho>0
and
{\delta\in(0,r)}
be such that
\ell(y)>\ell(x)-\rho
for every
y\in B_{\delta}(x)
.
Consider now

m_{n}:=\inf\left\{m\in\{1,..,n\}:~d(x,x_{m}^{n})>\delta\right\}.

Note that, by the definition of
\delta
, we have that
1<m_{n}\leq n
.
Fix
\sigma>0
.
Followed by (
3.4
), for
n
large enough we have

\displaystyle v(x)+\sigma\geq

\displaystyle~T^{n}u(x)+\frac{1}{n}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\left(A_{n-1}^{n}u(x_{n}^{n})+\sum_{j=m_{n}}^{n-1}\ell(x_{j}^{n})A_{j}^{n}d(x_{j}^{n},x_{j+1}^{n})\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}-1}\ell(x_{j}^{n})A_{j}^{n}d(x_{j}^{n},x_{j+1}^{n}).

Let us assume that
\lambda=0
. Thus,
A_{j}^{n}=1
for all
j<n
and therefore, combining inequality (
3.7
), triangle inequality and the definition of the operator
T
, we deduce

\displaystyle v(x)+\sigma\geq T^{n-m_{n}}u(x_{m_{n}})+(\ell(x)-\rho)d(x,x_{m_{n}})\geq v(x_{m_{n}})+(\ell(x)-\rho)d(x,x_{m_{n}}).

So, rearranging the terms of the above inequality and recalling that
d(x,x_{m_{n}})\geq\delta
, we get

\displaystyle G[v](x)+\dfrac{\sigma}{\delta}\geq\dfrac{v(x)-v(x_{m_{n}})}{d(x,x_{m_{n}})}+\dfrac{\sigma}{d(x,x_{m_{n}})}\geq\ell(x)-\rho.

So, after sending
\sigma
to
0
and then sending
\rho
to
0
, we conclude that
G[v](x)\geq\ell(x)
.
This shows that
v
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).

Assume now that
\lambda>0
. From (
3.5
) and (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle v(x)+\sigma\geq

\displaystyle~A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}T^{n-m_{n}}u(x_{m_{n}})+\frac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}\left(1-A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}\right)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}v(x_{m_{n}})+\frac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}\left(1-A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}\right).

Since
A^{n}_{m_{n}-1}<1
, above inequality leads to

(3.9)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lambda}{1-A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}}(v(x)-v(x_{m_{n}}))\geq\ell(x)-\rho-\lambda v(x_{m_{n}})-\dfrac{\lambda\sigma}{1-A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}}.

Observe that

A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}\leq\dfrac{1}{1+\lambda d(x,x_{m_{n}}^{n})}.

Hence, we have the estimates

(3.10)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\lambda}(1-A_{m_{n}-1}^{n})\geq\dfrac{d(x,x^{n}_{m_{n}})}{1+\lambda d(x,x^{n}_{m_{n}})},\quad\text{and so }\quad\dfrac{\lambda}{1-A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}}\leq\lambda+\dfrac{1}{\delta}.

We need to divide again the argument into two cases.

Case 2.2.1:

v(x)<v(x_{m_{n}}^{n})
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
v(x)<v(x_{m_{n}}^{n})
.
Then, since
A_{m_{n}-1}^{n}<1
, inequality (
3.8
) becomes

\displaystyle v(x)+\dfrac{\sigma}{1-A^{n}_{m_{n}-1}}\geq\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}.

Plugging in the inequality (
3.10
), we obtain

(3.11)

\displaystyle~v(x)+\sigma\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\lambda\delta}\right)\geq\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}.

Noting that we can set
n
as large as we want,
inequality (
3.11
) is valid for all
\sigma>0
. After sending
\sigma
to
0
, we have

(3.12)

v(x)\geq\dfrac{\ell(x)-\rho}{\lambda}.

Case 2.2.2:

v(x)<v(x_{m_{n}}^{n})
for finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
So, we can (and shall) assume that
n
is large enough such that
v(x)\geq v(x_{m_{n}}^{n})
.
Using inequalities (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we deduce that

(3.13)

\left(\lambda+\dfrac{1}{d(x,x^{n}_{m_{n}})}\right)\left(v(x)-v(x^{n}_{m_{n}})\right)\geq\ell(x)-\rho-\lambda v(x^{n}_{m_{n}})-\sigma\left(\lambda+\dfrac{1}{\delta}\right).

Rearranging terms in the inequality (
3.13
), we finally obtain

(3.14)

\lambda v(x)+G[v](x)\geq\ell(x)-\rho-\sigma\left(\lambda+\dfrac{1}{\delta}\right).

Sending
\sigma
to
0
, we conclude that

(3.15)

\lambda v(x)+G[v](x)\geq\ell(x)-\rho.

Combining Case 2.2.1 and Case 2.2.2, that is, (
3.12
) and (
3.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda v(x)+G[v](x)\geq\ell(x)-\rho,

independently of the behavior of the sequence
\{v(x^{n}_{m_{n}})\}_{n}
. Therefore, we can send
\rho
to
0
and obtain that
v
is a super solution.
The proof of Theorem
3.6
is complete.
∎

Proposition 3.9

In the classical theory of viscosity solutions, one can take benefit from the smoothness of test functions to study first order HJEs. Proceeding with a similar idea, noting that we are considering equations of the form

\EuScript

G
λ

Recall that

5.1
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
–stability with respect to the potential

In this subsection, we discuss the stability of the Perron solution of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) in terms of the data
\ell
.
Albeit simple, the following example shows that we cannot expect stability in full generality.

Example 5.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\ell_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
given by
\ell_{n}(n^{2})=0
and
\ell_{n}(x)=n^{-1}
for all
x\neq n^{2}
.
Clearly,
\{\ell_{n}\}_{n}
converges uniformly to
\ell_{\infty}\equiv 0
.
Also, the solution
u_{n}
of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) associated with
\ell_{n}
is given by
u_{n}(x)=n^{-1}|x-n^{2}|
, and
u_{\infty}\equiv 0
.
However,
u_{n}(0)=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so
\{u_{n}\}
does not converges pointwise to
u_{\infty}
.
Moreover,
u_{n}
converges (uniformly in bounded sets) to the constant function equal to
+\infty
.

The above example shows that we need to assume the boundedness of
X
.
Let
\ell
,
\ell_{n}\in\mathrm{LSC}(X)
be such that
\inf_{X}\ell=\inf_{X}\ell_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

be the respective Perron solutions of
(

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

)
associated with
\ell
and
\ell_{n}
.
We prove that if
X
is bounded and
\{\ell_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
\ell
uniformly, then the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
u
uniformly.
Let us start with the following proposition.
The diameter of
X
is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(X):=\sup\{d(x,y):~x,y\in X\}.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
X
be a bounded metric space and let
\lambda\geq 0
.
Let
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)
be a lsc function such that
\textstyle\inf_{X}\ell=0
.
Then, the function
v(x):=\ell(x)\textup{diam}(X)
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) associated with
\ell
.
Moreover,
T^{\infty}_{\lambda}v
is the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).

Proof.

Since
\inf_{X}\ell=0
, it follows that
\inf_{X}v=0
.
So, for any
x\in X
, direct computations lead to

(5.1)

\displaystyle G[v](x)\geq\inf_{y\neq x}\dfrac{\ell(x)\textup{diam}(X)}{d(x,y)}\geq\ell(x).

Thus,
v
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).
Let
w:X\to\mathbb{R}
be any subsolution of
(

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

)
. Then,
\inf_{X}w(x)=0
and
G[w](x)\leq\ell(x)-\lambda w(x)\leq\ell(x)
.
Therefore, reasoning as in (

) we deduce that
{w(x)\leq\ell(x)\textup{diam}(X)}
.
Finally, thanks to Theorem

,
T^{\infty}v
is the Perron solution of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

).
∎

Let us proceed with the following lemma, which is needed to prove the stability of the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) in terms of
\ell
.
Observe that if
X
is a bounded metric space, then any lsc function

, satisfies (

).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X
be a bounded metric space and let
\lambda\geq 0
.
Let
\ell,{k}:X\to[0,+\infty)
be lsc functions such that
\inf_{X}\ell=\inf_{X}{k}=0
.
Let
u,v:X\to\mathbb{R}
be the Perron solutions of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

) associated with
\ell
and
{k}
respectively.
Then, for any
\rho>0
we have that

v(x)-u(x)\leq(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X)+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)}{\rho},\quad\text{ for all }x\in X.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\rho>0
.
Thanks to Proposition
5.2
,
u
and
v
are bounded from above by
\textup{diam}(X)\ell
and
\textup{diam}(X){k}

Assume that

.
Then,

\displaystyle v(x)\leq{k}(x)\textup{diam}(X)\leq(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X).

From the above inequality, we get

(5.2)

\displaystyle v(x)-u(x)\leq(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X).

Now, assume that
\ell(x)>\rho

Let

Let

be the largest interval of integers containing

\ell(x_{i})\geq\rho

Then, if

(5.3)

\displaystyle\dfrac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{\rho}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{\max(I)}\frac{d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}.

(5.4)

\displaystyle\dfrac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{\rho}\geq\frac{\textup{diam}(X)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}.

Case 1
:
\max(I)<n
.
Then
\ell(x_{\max(I)+1})<\rho

{k}(x_{\max(I)+1})<\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}

Using (

, we compute

\displaystyle u(x)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{\max(I)}\frac{\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{\max(I)}\frac{{k}(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}+\dfrac{{k}(x_{\max(I)+1})\textup{diam}(X)}{\prod_{j=0}^{\max(I)}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X)}{\prod_{j=0}^{\max(I)}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}-\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\max(I)}\frac{d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1})}

\displaystyle\geq v(x)-(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X)-\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{\rho}.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle v(x)-u(x)\leq\varepsilon+(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X)+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{\rho}.

Case 2
:
\max(I)=n
. We can proceed as above but using (

) instead of (

) and obtain

v(x)-u(x)\leq\varepsilon+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{\rho}.

Combining the inequalities (

), (

) and (

:

v(x)-u(x)\leq\varepsilon+(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X)+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)+\varepsilon}{\rho},

independently of the behavior of the sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n}
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we finally get

v(x)-u(x)\leq(\rho+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty})\textup{diam}(X)+\|{k}-\ell\|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)}{\rho}.

∎

Now, we are able to prove the

–stability of the Perron solution of equation (

Theorem 5.4

Let
X
be a bounded metric space and let
\lambda\geq 0
.
Let

Further, assume that

the Perron solutions of (

⁢
\EuScript

G

Since

Remark 5.5
.

A careful inspection of the proof of Lemma

shows that the norm

can be replaced by its asymmetric version

This is related to the unilateral results obtained in

In the context of

\displaystyle v(x)-u(x)\leq\left(\rho+\|{k}-\ell|_{\infty}\right)\text{diam}(X)+\|{k}-\ell|_{\infty}\dfrac{u(x)}{\rho},\text{ for all }x\in X.

Consequently, we get

\displaystyle\|v-u|_{\infty}\leq\left(\rho+\|{k}-\ell|_{\infty}\right)\text{diam}(X)+\|{k}-\ell|_{\infty}\dfrac{\|\ell\|_{\infty}\mathrm{diam}(X)}{\rho},\text{ for all }\rho>0.

For the sake of simplicity, let us assume that
\alpha=1
. We first prove the case
\lambda\to 1^{-}
.
Thanks to Proposition
5.7
(a), we know that the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda<1}
is decreasing (as
\lambda
tends to
1
) and bounded from below by
u_{1}
.
Therefore, there is a function
v:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u_{\lambda}
converges to
v
pointwise as
\lambda
tends to
1^{-}
.
Since
X
is compact and
u_{\lambda}
is continuous, Dini’s theorem implies that this convergence is uniform.
Due to Proposition
5.7
(c),
u_{\lambda}
is the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) associated with
\ell_{\lambda}=\ell-\lambda u_{\lambda}
.
From here, it readily follows that
\ell_{\lambda}
converges to
\widetilde{\ell}:=\ell-v
uniformly, and therefore, thanks to Theorem
5.4
,
u_{\lambda}
converges uniformly to a function
w
which is the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) associated with
\widetilde{\ell}
.
By uniqueness of the pointwise convergence, we deduce that
v=w
, and therefore
v
satisfies
v+G[v]=\ell
.
The argument for the limit of
u_{\lambda}
as
\lambda
tends to
1^{+}
is analogous.
Indeed, the only difference is that the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>1}
is increasing (as
\lambda
goes to
1
) and bounded from above by
u_{1}
.

∎

To finish this section, we present an ergodic result that does not require any assumption on
X
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)
be a lsc function such that
\inf_{X}\ell=0
.
For any
\lambda>0
, denote by
u_{\lambda}
the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
λ

). Then, for any
\alpha>0
,
u_{\lambda}
converges to
u_{\alpha}
pointwise as
\lambda
tends to
\alpha^{-}
.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity let us fix
\alpha=1
.
Thanks to Proposition
5.7
(a), the family of functions
\{u_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda<1}
is decreasing as
\lambda
tends to
1^{-}
and is bounded from below by
u_{1}
.
Therefore,
u_{\lambda}
converges pointwise to a function
v:X\to\mathbb{R}
, as
\lambda
tends to
1^{-}
, and this limit satisfies
v\geq u_{1}
.
So, it suffices to check that
v\leq u_{1}
.
Let
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Thanks to Corollary
4.7
and Theorem
4.5
, we deduce that
u_{\lambda}=T^{\infty}_{\lambda}(2\ell)
, for all
\lambda\geq 1/2
.
Fix
\lambda>1/2
.
Followed by the definition of
T^{\infty}
(
3.2
), there is a finite sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subset X
, with
x_{0}=x
such that

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)+\varepsilon\geq\dfrac{2\ell(x_{k})}{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1+d(x_{j},x_{j+1}))}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\dfrac{\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}(1+d(x_{j},x_{j+1}))}.

Observe that the right hand side of the following inequality

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x)\leq T^{k}_{\lambda}(2\ell)(x)\leq\dfrac{2\ell(x_{k})}{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1}))}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\dfrac{\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{i}(1+\lambda d(x_{j},x_{j+1}))},

is continuous with respect to

. Therefore, we deduce

v(x)=\lim_{\lambda\to 1^{-}}u_{\lambda}(x)\leq u_{1}(x)+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we get that
v(x)\leq u_{1}(x)
.
The proof is now complete.
∎

6.1
An approximation scheme

We have already shown that only mild conditions on the data

\EuScript

However, this is not the case if we replace the global slope operator

Indeed, the classical Eikonal equation is considered whenever

\textstyle\mathbb{R}^{d}

To fix ideas, let

Under these assumptions, it is standard that the local slope equation

\begin{cases}s[V](x)=\ell(x),&~\text{for all }x\in X,\\
V(x)=0,&~\text{for all }x\in[\ell=0],\\

admits a solution

V(x):=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\ell(\gamma(t))dt\right\},~\text{for all }x\in X.

where the infimum is taken over all the

-Lipschitz curves starting at

(i.e.
\gamma(0)=x
) and landing on

(i.e.

) (see
[

A complete discussion about the uniqueness issue of equation (

\EuScript

[
16
,
17
]
.
In the forthcoming analysis, we use the following result that summarizes
[
27

and
[
18

.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
X
be a complete length space.
Let
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)
be a continuous function such that
[\ell=0]
is nonempty.
Then, the function defined by (
6.1
) is a solution of equation (

⁢
\EuScript
L

). Moreover, if
X
is compact, equation (

⁢
\EuScript
L

) admits only one solution.

Let us now define the
R
-semiglobal slopes
G_{R}
(with
R>0
), which formally interpolate the local slope and the global slope.
The semiglobal slopes were introduced in
[
17
, Proposition 3.7]
as examples of what the authors called abstract descent modulus.
For a fixed
R>0
and
u:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the
R
-
semiglobal slope
of
u
at

is defined by

(6.2)

G_{R}[u](x):=\begin{cases}\sup_{y\in B_{R}(x)\setminus\{x\}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))_{+}}{d(x,y)},&\text{ if }B_{R}(x)\setminus\{x\}\neq\emptyset,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Recall that
B_{R}(x)
denotes the open ball of center
x
and radius
R
. Note that, for any
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
and any
x\in X
the following limits hold

G[u](x)=\lim_{R\to\infty}G_{R}[u](x),~\text{and }s[u](x)=\lim_{R\to 0}G_{R}[u](x).

Assume now that
[\ell=0]\neq\emptyset
.
Inspired by the
R
-semiglobal slope and the solution of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

), let us formally define the function
v_{R}:X\to\mathbb{R}
by

(6.3)

\displaystyle v_{R}(x)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1}):x_{0}=x,x_{k}\in[\ell=0],\text{ and }d(x_{i},x_{i+1})<R~\text{ for }i\in\{0,...,k\}\right\}.

The above functions can be compared to the ones used in
[
22
, Section 1.1]
in which the function
\ell
is replaced by the so-called minimal weak upper gradients. The following lemma implies that, if
X
is connected, then

is well defined.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that
X
is connected,
R>0
and
Y\subset X
is a nonempty set.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of points
x\in X
such that there exists a finite sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subset X
satisfying

(6.4)

x_{0}=x,\quad x_{k}\in Y\quad\text{and}\quad d(x_{i},x_{i+1})<R\text{ for every $i\in\{0,\cdots,k-1\}$ }.

Then,
\mathcal{A}=X
.

Proof.

To check that
\mathcal{A}
is open, note that
B_{R}(x)\subset\mathcal{A}
for every
x\in\mathcal{A}
.
This also shows that
\mathcal{A}
is closed.
Now, since
X
is connected and
Y\subset\mathcal{A}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{A}=X
.
∎

The functions
v_{R}
enjoy the following property.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
X
be a connected metric space,
R>0
and
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)
be a lsc function such that
[\ell=0]
is nonempty.
Then,
G_{R}[v_{R}]\leq\ell
.

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
6.2
, the function
v_{R}
is well defined.
Let
x\in X
and
y\in B(x,R)\setminus\{x\}
. Observe that immediately from the definition of
v(x)
we have

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1}):x_{0}=x,x_{k}\in[\ell=0],\text{ and }d(x_{i},x_{i+1})<R~\text{ for }i\in\{0,...,k\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\ell(x)d(x,y)+v(y).

Since the above inequality holds true for any
y\in B(x,R)\setminus\{x\}
, we deduce that
G_{R}[v_{R}](x)\leq\ell(x)
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The functions

may have their own interest, but their study is out of the scope of this paper.
For instance, a natural question arises: under which metric conditions of

(if any) does the function
v_{R}
satisfy
{G_{R}[v_{R}]=\ell}
?

In what follows, we are interested in the following natural question

What are the asymptotic behaviors of the family

as

tends to
+\infty
and tends to

?

In Theorem
6.5
we provide necessary conditions on the metric space

to obtain the convergence of the family

to the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) and to the function defined by (

) (the solution of (

\EuScript

)) as

and to

respectively.
Recall that if
[\ell=0]
is nonempty, then the assumption (

) is trivially satisfied.

Theorem 6.5
.

Assume that
\ell:X\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
is a lsc function such that
[\ell=0]\neq\emptyset
. For each
{R>0}
, we denote by
v_{R}
the function defined by (
6.3
).
Denote by
u_{0}:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
V:X\to\mathbb{R}
the Perron solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) and the solution of (

⁢
\EuScript
L

) given by (
6.1
) both associated with
\ell
.
Then, the following statements hold true.

(i)

If
X
is connected, then
v_{R}
converges pointwise to
u_{0}
and
R
tends to
+\infty
.

(ii)

If
X
is a complete length space and
\ell
is continuous, then
\liminf_{R\to 0^{+}}v_{R}\leq V
pointwise.

(iii)

If
X
is a compact length space (and therefore geodesic) and
\ell
is continuous, then
v_{R}
converges uniformly to
V
as
R
tends to
0
.

Proof.

First, observe that by the definition of infimum, for any
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
, we have that
{v_{R_{1}}\geq v_{R_{2}}}
.
So, the following limits are well defined:

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{R\to\infty}v_{R}(x)=\inf\left\{v_{R}(x):~R>0\right\},

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{R\to 0}v_{R}(x)=\sup\left\{v_{R}(x):~R>0\right\},\quad\text{ for every }x\in X.

(i).

Let us recall that the function
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by
u=0
on
S
and
u=+\infty
on
X\setminus S
is a supersolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

). Moreover, since
u
is larger than any subsolution, Theorem
4.5
implies that
T^{\infty}u=u_{0}
.
So, a direct computation gives that, for any
x\in X

u_{0}(x)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1}):~k\in\mathbb{N},\,\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subset X,~x_{0}=x,~x_{k}\in[\ell=0]\right\}.

Then, it readily follows that
u_{0}(x)\leq v_{R}(x)
for all
x\in X
and
R>0
.
Hence, it holds
u_{0}\leq v_{\infty}
.

We show that
v_{\infty}\leq u_{0}
. To do this, we check that
v_{\infty}
is a subsolution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).
Reasoning towards a contradiction, assume that there exist
\epsilon>0
and
\bar{x}\in X
such that
G[v_{\infty}](\bar{x})>\ell(\bar{x})+\epsilon
.
By definition of the global slope, there is
y\in X\setminus\{\bar{x}\}
such that

(6.5)

v_{\infty}(\bar{x})>v_{\infty}(y)+(\ell(\bar{x})+\epsilon)d(\bar{x},y).

Noticing that
v_{R}(y)\xrightarrow{R\rightarrow\infty}v_{\infty}(y)
, we fix
R>0
such that

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(y)+\epsilon d(\bar{x},y)>v_{R}(y)\text{ and }R\geq d(\bar{x},y).

Then, from inequality (
6.5
), we get
v_{R}(\bar{x})\geq v_{\infty}(\bar{x})>v_{R}(y)+\ell(\bar{x})d(\bar{x},y).

Consequently,
G_{R}[v_{R}](\bar{x})>\ell(\bar{x})
, which contradicts Proposition
6.3
.

(ii).

Fix
x\in X
and
R>0
. Let
\epsilon>0
and fix a 1–Lipschitz curve
\gamma_{\epsilon}:[0,T]\to X
such that

(6.6)

V(x)\geq\int_{0}^{T}\ell(\gamma_{\epsilon}(t))dt-\epsilon,

\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(T)\in[\ell=0]
.
Since
\ell
is continuous,
\ell\circ\gamma_{\epsilon}
is Riemann integrable.
Hence, there exists a partition
P:=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}=T\}
of
[0,T]
such that
|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|<R
for all
i\in\{0,\cdots,k-1\}
and

(6.7)

\int_{0}^{T}\ell(\gamma_{\epsilon}(t))dt\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ell(\gamma_{\epsilon}(t_{i}))|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|-\epsilon.

Set
x_{i}=\gamma_{\epsilon}(t_{i})
for
i\in\{0,\cdots,k\}
. Since
\gamma_{\epsilon}
is 1–Lipschitz, one gets

(6.8)

d(x_{i+1},x_{i})=d(\gamma_{\epsilon}(t_{i+1}),\gamma_{\epsilon}(t_{i}))\leq|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|<R.

Combining the inequalities (
6.6
), (
6.7
) and (
6.8
) and using the definition of
v_{R}
, we obtain

(6.9)

V(x)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i+1},x_{i})-2\epsilon\geq v_{R}(x)-2\epsilon.

Sending
\epsilon
to
0
, we get that
V(x)\geq v_{R}(x)
.
Since
R>0
is arbitrary, we conclude

(6.10)

V(x)\geq\lim_{R\rightarrow 0}v_{R}(x)=\sup_{R>0}v_{R}(x)=v_{0}(x).

So,
V\geq v_{0}
.

(iii).
Thanks to part
(ii)
, it remains to show that
V\leq v_{0}
. Let
x\in X
. If
\ell(x)=0
, then
v_{R}(x)=0
for any
R>0
. So
v_{0}(x)=0=V(x)
.
Assume that
\ell(x)>0
.
Let
\rho>0
be such that
\textup{dist}(x,[\ell=0])>\rho
. Consider the open set

\Omega_{\rho}:=\bigcup_{y\in[\ell=0]}B_{\rho}(y).

Note that
x\notin\Omega_{\rho}
.
By compactness of
X
and continuity of
\ell
, we know

L_{\rho}:=\inf\left\{\ell(y):~y\in X\setminus\Omega_{\rho}\right\}>0.

Fix
R>0
. Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subset X
be such that
x_{0}=x
,
x_{k}\in[\ell=0]
,
d(x_{i},x_{i+1})<R
for all
i
, and

(6.11)

\displaystyle v_{R}(x)+1\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\ell(x_{i})d(x_{i},x_{i+1}).

Define

(6.12)

\displaystyle I:=\{i\in\{0,...,k\}:~x_{i}\in X\setminus\Omega_{\rho}\}.

It follows that
0\in I
and
k\notin I
.
Thanks to inequality (
6.11
) and the definition of
L_{\rho}
, we get

(6.13)

\displaystyle\dfrac{V(x)+1}{L_{\rho}}\geq\sum_{i\in I}d(x_{i},x_{i+1}).

Observe that above estimate is independent of
R
.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{x_{i}^{n}\}_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\subset X
be such that
x_{1}^{n}=0
,
x_{k_{n}}^{n}\in[\ell=0]
,
d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})<1/n
for all
i
and

(6.14)

\displaystyle v_{\frac{1}{n}}(x)+\dfrac{1}{n}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}-1}\ell(x_{i}^{n})d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n}).

Let
I_{n}\subset\{0,...,k_{n}\}
be the set defined as (
6.12
) but using the sequence
\{x_{i}^{n}\}_{i=0}^{k_{n}}
.
Set
I_{n}^{0}
as the largest interval of integers contained in
I_{n}
such that
0\in I_{n}^{0}
.
Also, define

a_{n}:=\max I_{n}^{0}+1\in I_{n}\setminus I_{n}^{0}.

By definition,
x_{a_{n}}\in\Omega_{\rho}
.
By compactness of
X
and continuity of
\ell
, we have

C:=\max\{\ell(x):x\in\overline{\Omega}_{\rho}\}<+\infty.

Since
[\ell=0]
is compact, there is
y_{n}\in[\ell=0]
such that

\textup{dist}(x_{a_{n}},[\ell=0])=d(x_{a_{n}},y_{n})<\rho.

Denote by
\gamma_{a_{n}}^{n}
the geodesic (of speed
1
) joining
x_{a_{n}}
and
y_{n}
.
Then,

(6.15)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{d(x_{a_{n}},y_{n})}\ell(\gamma_{a_{n}}^{n}(t))dt\leq Cd(x_{a_{n}},y_{n})\leq C\rho.

For any
i\in I_{n}^{0}
, let
\gamma_{i}^{n}:[0,d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})]\rightarrow X
be a geodesic (of speed
1
) joining
x_{i}^{n}
and
x_{i+1}^{n}
.
Also, we denote by
\gamma^{n}:[0,T_{n}]\to X
the curve obtained by concatenating the curves
\{\gamma_{i}^{n}\}_{i=0}^{a_{n}}
.
Observe that

\textup{Lip}(\gamma^{n})\leq 1,\quad\gamma^{n}(0)=x\quad~\text{and }\quad\gamma^{n}(T_{n})=y_{n}\in[\ell=0],

where
T_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{a_{n}-1}d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})+d(x_{a_{n}},y_{n})
.
Denote by
\omega:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty]
the modulus of continuity of
\ell
, i.e.
\omega
is the increasing function defined by

\omega(r):=\sup\left\{|\ell(z_{1})-\ell(z_{2})|:~z_{1},z_{2}\in X,~d(z_{1},z_{2})\leq r\right\}.

Since
X
is compact and
\ell
is continuous,
\omega(r)<\infty
for all
r\geq 0
and
\textstyle\lim_{r\to 0}\omega(r)=0
.
So, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in I_{n}^{0}
we have

(6.16)

\begin{split}&~\left|\ell(x_{i}^{n})d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})-\int_{0}^{d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})}\ell(\gamma_{i}^{n}(t))dt\right|\\
\leq&~\int_{0}^{d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})}|\ell(\gamma_{i}^{n}(t))-\ell(x_{i}^{n})|dt\leq\omega\left(n^{-1}\right)d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n}).\end{split}

Finally, putting all together we get that

\displaystyle v_{\frac{1}{n}}(x)+\dfrac{1}{n}+C\rho

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in I_{n}^{0}}\ell(x_{i}^{n})d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})+\int_{0}^{d(x_{a_{n}},y_{n})}\ell(\gamma_{a_{n}}^{n}(t))dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\ell(\gamma(t))dt+\sum_{i\in I_{n}^{0}}\left(\ell(x_{i}^{n})d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})-\int_{0}^{d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})}\ell(\gamma_{i}^{n}(t))dt\right)

\displaystyle\geq V(x)-\omega(n^{-1})\sum_{i\in I_{n}^{0}}d(x_{i}^{n},x_{i+1}^{n})

\displaystyle\geq V(x)-\omega(n^{-1})\dfrac{V(x)+1}{L_{\rho}},

where in the first line we use (
6.14
) and (
6.15
), in the third one (
6.1
) and (
6.16
) and in the last line (
6.13
).
Sending
n
to
+\infty
, we get

v_{0}(x)+C\rho\geq V(x).

Finally, sending
\rho
to
0
we obtain
v_{0}(x)\geq V(x)
.
The proof is now complete.
∎

Remark 6.6

We denote again by

Example 6.7
.

There exist a complete bounded geodesic metric space

and continuous bounded function
{\ell:X\to[0,+\infty)}
, with

, such that

is not a solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

).

Let
X
be the set defined by

X:=\{0\}\cup\{1\}\cup\{(x,n):~x\in(0,1),~n\in\mathbb{N}\}

and let

be the function defined by

d(z_{1},z_{2})=\begin{cases}0&~\textup{if }z_{1}=z_{2},\\
1&~\text{if }z_{1}=0~\text{and }z_{2}=1,\\
x&~\text{if }z_{1}=0~\text{and }z_{2}=(x,n),\\
|x-y|&~\text{if }z_{1}=(x,n)~\text{and }z_{2}=(y,n),\\
1-x&~\text{if }z_{1}=1~\text{and }z_{2}=(x,n),\\
\min\{x+y,2-x-y\}&~\textup{if }z_{1}=(x,n),~z_{2}=(y,m)~\text{and }n\neq m.\\
\end{cases}

All the unwritten cases are defined by symmetry.
Observe that
(X,d)
is a metric space.
For any

, let us denote by
S_{n}
the segment

S_{n}:=\{0\}\cup\{(x,n):x\in(0,1)\}\cup\{1\}.

The metric space
X
can be regarded as countably many segments joining
0
and
1
.
Let us collect some simple facts of

\textup{diam}(X)=1
, complete and not compact (the sequence

has no accumulation points).
Moreover,

is a geodesic space.

Let us fix a continuous function

with the following properties:

(P1)

\ell(0)=0
,
\ell(1)=1
and
\ell((x,n))=x,~\text{for all }x\in\left(0,\dfrac{1}{3}\right)\cup\left(\dfrac{2}{3},1\right)\,\text{and all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(P2)

\ell((x,n))\geq x
for all

and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(P3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\ell((x,n))dx=1,~\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(P4)

The set
P_{n}:=\{z\in S_{n}:~z=(x,n)~\text{and }\ell(z)=x\}
is a
\frac{1}{n}
-net of
S_{n}
.

We can also choose
\ell
bounded, for instance, by

.
Also, due to (P3) and (P4),

cannot be uniformly continuous.
In what follows, we present a simple consequence of
[

, Theorem 3.6]
.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
X
and

be the metric space and function defined above. Then the problem (

L

) has a unique non negative solution, namely

defined by (

Proof.

By Theorem

,
V

) associated with

.
Let
W
be any other positive solution.
That is,
s[V]=s[W]=\ell
on

and

.
Let us recall the notion of asymptotically critical sequence defined in

: a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subset X
it is called

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s[V](x_{n})d(x_{n},x_{n+1})<+\infty.

.
Let

Now, as a direct consequence of

.

6.2
Integration formula

In this subsection, we explore the case in which the lsc function
\ell
admits the value
+\infty
.
The main idea is to apply our previous results to the subset
X_{f}=[\ell<+\infty]\subset X
and then to consider its lsc envelope.
As a byproduct of our result, we provide an integration formula for lsc bounded from below functions defined on a complete metric space in terms of their global slopes.
Some further consequences of our integration formula are also established at the end of the subsection.

To fix notation, for a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we denote by
\mathrm{lsc}(f):X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
its lsc envelope, that is,

\mathrm{lsc}(f)(x):=\liminf_{y\to x}f(y),\text{ for all }x\in X.

Observe that if
f
is lsc on
\mathrm{dom}~f
, then
f=\mathrm{lsc}(f)
on
\mathrm{dom}~f
.

In the language of the Global slope equation, we have the following existence result.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
\ell:X\to[0,+\infty]
be a lsc function satisfying assumption (
3.2
).
Then, the equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) admits a lsc solution.

Proof.

Since
\ell
satisfies (
3.2
),
X_{f}:=[\ell<+\infty]
is a nonempty set.
Consider the function
{u_{f}:X\to\mathbb{R}}
defined by

u_{f}(x):=\inf\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ell(x_{n})d(x_{n},x_{n+n}):~\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subset X,~x_{0}=0,\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell(x_{n})=0\right\},~\text{for all }x\in X.

Observe that
u_{f}(x)<+\infty
if and only if
x\in X_{f}
.
Moreover,
u_{f}|_{X_{f}}
is the Perron solution of equation (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

) on
X_{f}
associated with
\ell|_{X_{f}}
.
Let
u:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be the lsc envelope of
u_{f}
, i.e.
u=\mathrm{lsc}(u_{f})
.

Claim:

u
is a solution of (

⁢
\EuScript

G
0

).

Let
x\in X
.
If
x\in X_{f}
, since
u(x)=\mathrm{lsc}(u_{f})(x)
, it readily follows that

G[u](x)=\sup_{y\in X\setminus\{x\}}\dfrac{\max\{u(x)-u(y),0\}}{d(x,y)}=\sup_{y\in X_{f}\setminus\{x\}}\dfrac{\max\{u(x)-u(y),0\}}{d(x,y)}=G_{X_{f}}[u_{f}|_{X_{f}}](x)=\ell(x).

If
x\in X\setminus\overline{X_{f}}
, it follows that
u=+\infty
. Thus,
G[u](x)=+\infty
.
So, we only need to analyse the case when
x\in\overline{X_{f}}\setminus X_{f}
.

Case 1
:
u(x)=+\infty
.
Then,
G[u](x)=+\infty
.
Since
x\in\overline{X_{f}}\setminus X_{f}
, we also have that
\ell(x)=+\infty
.

Case 2
:
u(x)<+\infty
.
We need to show that
G[u](x)=+\infty
.
Fix
R>0
.
Since
\ell
is lsc, there is
\delta>0
such that
\ell(y)>R
for all
y\in B(x,\delta)
.
Let
\rho>0
.
By the definition of
u_{f}
, for any
y\in B(x,\delta/2)\cap X_{f}
, there is a sequence
\{x^{y}_{n}\}_{n}\subset X
such that
x^{y}_{0}=y
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell(x^{y}_{n})=0
and

u_{f}(y)+\rho\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ell(x^{y}_{n})d(x^{y}_{n},x^{y}_{n+1}).

Set
N=N(y):=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:~x^{y}_{n}\notin B(x,\delta)\}
.
Then, we have

u_{f}(y)+\rho\geq\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ell(x^{y}_{n})d(x^{y}_{n},x^{y}_{n+1})+u_{f}(x^{y}_{N})\geq Rd(y,x^{y}_{N})+u_{f}(x^{y}_{N}),

which leads to

\dfrac{u_{f}(y)-u_{f}(x^{y}_{N})}{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}\geq R-\dfrac{\rho}{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
y\in B(x,\delta/2)
be such that
|u(x)-u(y)|<\varepsilon
.
Recalling that
u_{f}=u
on
X_{f}
, we get that

(6.17)

\begin{split}\dfrac{u(x)-u(x^{y}_{N})}{d(x,x^{y}_{N})}&=\dfrac{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}{d(x,x^{y}_{N})}\left(\dfrac{u(x)-u(y)+u(y)-u(x^{y}_{N})}{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}\right)\\
&\geq\dfrac{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}{d(x,x^{y}_{N})}\left(\dfrac{-\varepsilon}{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}+R-\dfrac{\rho}{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}\right).\end{split}

Notice that

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to x}\dfrac{d(y,x^{y}_{N})}{d(x,x^{y}_{N})}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in X_{f}\\
y\to x\end{subarray}}d(y,x^{y}_{N})\geq\delta.

From inequality (
6.17
), after taking limit inferior as
y\in X_{f}
tends to
x
, we obtain

G[u](x)\geq\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathrm{dom}~\ell\\
y\to x\end{subarray}}\dfrac{u(x)-u(x^{y}_{N})}{d(x,x^{y}_{N})}\geq R-\dfrac{\varepsilon+\rho}{\delta}.

Letting
\varepsilon
tend to
0
and then
\rho
tend to
0
, we deduce that
G[u](x)\geq R
.
Since the above conclusion holds for any
R>0
, we obtain that
G[u](x)=+\infty
.
The proof is complete.
∎

In the following theorem, we provide an integration formula for proper lsc bounded by below functions defined on a complete metric space via their global slopes.
For the sake of convenience, for any fixed proper lsc function
{f:X\to\mathbb{R}}
, we denote

(6.18)

\mathcal{I}[f](x):=\inf\left\{\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}G[f](x_{n})d(x_{n},x_{n+1}):\{x_{n}\}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}(x)\right\},\text{for every }x\in X,

where
\mathcal{K}(x):=\left\{\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subset X:x_{0}=x\text{ and }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}G[f](x_{n})=0\right\}
. Observe that if
f
is bounded from below, then
\mathcal{I}[f](x)<+\infty
iff
G[f](x)<+\infty
.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper lsc function with
\inf_{X}f=0
. Then, one has

\displaystyle f=\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])\text{ in }X.

Proof.

Thanks to Remark
2.5
(ii), we note that
\mathrm{dom}~f\subset\overline{\mathrm{dom}}~G[f]
. First, we prove that
f\leq\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])
in
X
. Denote
D:=\mathrm{dom}~G[f]
. We recall that
\mathcal{I}[f]\big|_{D}
is the Perron solution of the equation

(6.19)

\begin{cases}G_{D}[u](x)=G[f](x),\,x\in D,\\
\inf_{D}u=0,\end{cases}

where
G_{D}
is the global slope operator for functions defined on
D
.
Notice that
\inf_{X}f=\inf_{D}f=0
.

Claim
:
G_{D}[f\big|_{D}](x)=G[f](x)
for every
x\in D
.

For the sake of brevity, we write
G_{D}[f]
instead of
G_{D}[f\big|_{D}]
.
Reasoning towards a contradiction, assume that there is
\bar{x}\in D
such that
G_{D}[f](\bar{x})\neq G[f](\bar{x})
. Therefore,
G_{D}[f](\bar{x})<G[f](\bar{x})
.
Using the definition of global slope, there exists
\bar{y}\in X\setminus D
such that

\displaystyle\dfrac{f(\bar{x})-f(\bar{y})}{d(\bar{x},\bar{y})}>G_{D}[f](\bar{x}),

which leads to

(6.20)

f(\bar{y})<\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\to\bar{y}\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}f(y).

This contradicts Remark
2.5
(ii), which asserts that there is always a sequence
\{y_{n}\}_{n}\subset D
such that
{y_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}y}
and
\textstyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f(y_{n})\leq f(y)
. This finishes the proof of the claim.

So, the function
f
satisfies

(6.21)

\begin{cases}G_{D}[f](x)=G[f](x),\,x\in D,\\
\inf_{D}f=0.\end{cases}

Since
\mathcal{I}[f]\big|_{D}
is the Perron solution of (
6.19
), it follows that
f\leq\mathcal{I}[f]
on
D
. Further, since
f
is lsc and
f=\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])=\infty
on
X\setminus\overline{D}
, we obtain
f\leq\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])
in
X
.

It remains to show that
\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])\leq f
. Fix
\sigma\in(0,1)
and
x\in D
. Applying Remark
2.5
(i) to the function
f
at
x
and
\sigma
, there exists a sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n}
starting at
x
that satisfies

(6.22)

\lim_{n\to\infty}G[f](z_{n})=0,\quad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G[f](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})<+\infty,

and

(6.23)

(1-\sigma)G[f](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})\leq f(z_{n})-f(z_{n+1})\text{ for every }n\geq 0.

Moreover, up to a subsequence, we can and shall assume that
\{z_{n}\}_{n}
satisfies (
6.22
)–(
6.23
) and
\{G[f](z_{n})\}_{n}
is decreasing.
Indeed, we can construct such a subsequence as we did in the proof of
Proposition

3.9
.

Applying
Proposition

2.6
, one has

\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(z_{n})=\inf_{X}f=0.

By the definition of
\mathcal{I}[f]
, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we directly obtain

(6.24)

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}[f](x)\leq&\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}G[f](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})\\
\leq&~\dfrac{1}{1-\sigma}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(f(z_{n})-f(z_{n+1}))+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}G[f](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1})\\
\leq&~\dfrac{1}{1-\sigma}(f(x)-f(x_{N}))+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}G[f](z_{n})d(z_{n},z_{n+1}).\end{split}

Taking first the limit inferior as
N
tends to
+\infty
and then sending
\sigma
to
0
, we infer that
\mathcal{I}[f](x)\leq f(x)
.
Hence, we get
\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])\leq f
in
D
.

Fix
x\in\mathrm{dom}~f\setminus D
, by Remark
2.5
(i), there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subset D
, convergent to
x
, such that
\textstyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})\leq f(x)
.
Using the fact that
\mathcal{I}[f]\leq f
in
D
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])(x)=\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\to x\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}\mathcal{I}[f](y)\leq\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\to x\\
y\in D\end{subarray}}f(y)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})\leq f(x).

So
\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])\leq f
in
\mathrm{dom}~f
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.11
.

Up to the best of our knowledge,
Theorem

6.10
can be applied to lsc convex functions which are bounded from below to obtain an integration formula using less information than the one given by Rockafellar in
[
39
, Theorem 1]
.
Indeed, let
X
be a Banach space and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lsc convex function which is bounded from below.
Then
Theorem

6.10
implies that
f=\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])+c
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
On the other hand, if
\partial f
denotes the convex subdifferential of
f
, since
f
is convex, it is known that
G[f](x)=\inf\{\|x^{*}\|:~x^{*}\in\partial f(x)\}
when
\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset
and
+\infty
otherwise, see
[
4
, Proposition 1.4.4]
.

Also, we immediately recover the determination result due to Rockafellar for convex functions which are bounded from below.

Corollary 6.12

(Rockafellar’s determination result)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be convex proper lsc bounded from below functions.
If
\partial f(x)=\partial g(x)
for every
x\in X
, then
f=g+c
in
X
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Indeed, since
f
and
g
are lsc convex functions and
\partial f=\partial g
, we deduce that
G[f]=G[g]
.

Theorem

6.10
readily implies that there are
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f+c_{1}=\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[f])=\mathrm{lsc}(\mathcal{I}[g])=g+c_{2}.

∎

Even more, we also recover the following result from
[
31
]
.

Corollary 6.13

(i)
Thanks to

, to realize that

(ii)
It readily follows from

.
