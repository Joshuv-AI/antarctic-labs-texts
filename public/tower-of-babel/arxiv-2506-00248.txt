A higher dimensional generalization of the Kitaev spin liquid Preprint: APS/123-QED
Po-Jui,Chen, Piers Coleman

Abstract
We construct an exactly solvable model of a four-dimensional Kitaev spin liquid. The lattice structure is orthorhombic and each unit-cell contains six sublattice degrees of freedom. We demonstrate that the Fermi surface of the model is made up of two-dimensional surfaces. Additionally, we evaluate the energy cost of creating visons using scattering theory. The positive bond-flip energy suggests that the system’s ground state is flux-free, similar to the two-dimensional Kitaev honeycomb model. Our model sheds light on the realization of high-dimensional fractionalization.



I
Introduction

The quantum spin liquid is an exotic phase of matter which lacks long-range magnetic order and does not exhibit any broken symmetry. Instead, the ground state of a spin liquid is characterized by its long-range quantum entanglement
Anderson 1987
;
Anderson 1973
;
Savary and Balents 2016
;
Balents 2010
;
Wen et al. 2019
. Another useful way to understand quantum spin liquids is in terms of its elementary excitations. The low-energy effective field theory for quantum spin liquids often involves fractionalized spin excitations moving under an emergent gauge field. Here fractionalization refers to the phenomenon whereby elementary excitations carry fractional quantum numbers: typical examples include neutral spin 1/2 spinons, and spinless visons, which describe excitations of the gauge fields. Although quantum spin liquids provide a new venue for physicists to explore quantum materials and solve fundamental problems in strong correlation physics, it is a challenge to discover exactly solvable models that can be used as platforms for studying quantum spin liquids.

One of the great innovations in this respect, is Kitaev’s exactly solvable honeycomb model
Kitaev 2006
which consists of bond-dependent Ising interactions between spin-1/2 local moments. Kitaev’s decomposition reveals the underlying physics of spins, fractionalized into Majorana fermions moving through a static
Z_{2}
gauge field. The defects of the
Z_{2}
gauge field are plaquette fluxes, called visons. Kitaev’s model demonstrated that the transition between gapless
and gapped topological phases is driven by tuning the strengths of the spin coupling constants. The gapped phases of the Kitaev honeycomb model support the existence of non-abelian anyons of possible interest to topological quantum computation
Lahtinen and Pachos 2017
;
Nayak et al. 2008
. Since the original work by Kitaev, many exactly solvable three-dimensional variants of the original model have been studied
Mandal and Surendran 2009
;
Hermanns and Trebst 2014
;
Hermanns et al. 2015
;
O’Brien et al. 2016
;
Yamada et al. 2017
;
Eschmann et al. 2020
;
Jahromi et al. 2021
, classified through their topology and the presence of gapless Dirac cones, or Fermi surfaces in the excitation spectrum
O’Brien et al. 2016
;
Yamada 2021
. There are also several theoretical proposals which extend the Kitaev model by introducing an additional orbital degree of freedom
Coleman et al. 2022
;
Tsvelik and Coleman 2022
;
Panigrahi et al. 2024
.

On the experimental side, several experiments have sought the realization of the Kitaev spin liquid in materials with strong spin-orbit coupling
Do et al. 2017
;
Mehlawat et al. 2017
;
Winter et al. 2017
;
Hermanns et al. 2015
.
\alpha
-RuCl
3

Do et al. 2017
;
Takayama et al. 2015
are candidates for Kitaev materials, in which the interplay between strong correlation in
d
electrons and spin-orbit coupling gives rise to the anisotropic Ising interaction. In these Kitaev materials, inelastic neutron scattering
Banerjee et al. 2017
and Raman scattering
Glamazda et al. 2016
;
Sandilands et al. 2015
;
Panigrahi et al. 2024
have revealed tentative signatures of spin fractionalization in these materials.

In an effort to gain further insight into Kitaev spin liquids, here we introduce a four-dimensional realization of the Kitaev spin model, which we call the hyper-hexagonal lattice. This lattice is a natural generalization of the 2D and 3D lattices as shown in Fig.
1
. The basic idea is to form a chain of alternating xx and yy bonds, which we then wrap around a
d=2(D-1)
-sided helix to form a structure with d atoms per unit cell, as shown in Fig.
1
. We then add zz-bonds as cross-links between the helices. Thus, the 2D honeycomb model consists of zig-zag chains, connected by zz cross-links. The 3D hyperoctagonal lattice contains a four-sided helix with four atoms per unit cell, while the 4D hyper-hexagonal lattice consists of a six-sided helix with a hexagonal cross-section. To visualize this helix, we project it into the 3-dimensional hyperplane perpendicular to the
\hat{n}=(1,-1,1,0)
direction: viewed along the axis of the helix, it then forms a hexagonal structure in which the xx-yy chain wraps around the hexagon as one proceeds along the fourth w-axis, (0,0,0,1) as shown in Fig.
1
.

With this lattice geometry, we can perform a fermionization in four dimensions that is formally identical to those in two and three dimensions. The fermionized model describes a free majorana fermion moving on a four-dimensional lattice with six sublattices in a unit cell. Its exact solvability enables us to investigate its energy spectrum, ground state, and elementary excitations.

The remaining discussion is organized as follows. Section
II

III

IV

The model and the fermionizations

We begin by introducing the detailed helical structure of our four-dimensional spin liquid, called the hyper-hexagonal lattice. We consider an orthorhombic lattice with six lattice points in one unit cell. The projected lattice structure is shown in Fig.

II.1
Majorana representation

Here we briefly review the application of Kitaev’s method to the hyper-hexagonal structure. The central idea of Kitaev’s trick is to fractionalize spins into Majorana fermions, according to
\sigma^{\alpha}_{i}=2ic_{j}b^{\alpha}_{j}
, where
c_{j},b_{j}^{\alpha}
are Majorana operators whose particles are their own anti-particle
c_{j}=c_{j}^{\dagger},b^{\alpha}_{j}=b_{j}^{\alpha\dagger}
. These operators also obey the commutation relations
\{c_{i},c_{j}\}=\delta_{ij},\{b^{\alpha}_{i},b^{\beta}_{j}\}=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\delta_{i,j},\{c_{i},b^{\alpha}_{j}\}=0
. One should notice that when spin operators are replaced as a product of Majorana operators, the local Hilbert space of the system is expanded, necessitating a projection into the physical subspace through the constrain
D_{j}=-4ic_{j}b^{x}_{j}b^{y}_{j}b^{z}_{j}=1
. After this substitution, the resulting Hamiltonian reads

H_{K}=\sum J_{\alpha_{ij}}u_{ij}c_{i}c_{j},

(2)

where
u_{ij}=ib^{\alpha_{ij}}_{i}b^{\alpha_{ij}}_{j}
satisfies the unitary condition
u_{ij}^{2}=1
. If we exchange the index
i,j
, the operator picks up a minus sign
u_{ij}=-u_{ji}
. This factor characterizes the emergent
Z_{2}
gauge symmetry. It is easy to verify the gauge invariance of the model by performing the gauge transformation

c_{i}\rightarrow Z_{i}c_{i},u_{ij}\rightarrow Z_{i}u_{ij}Z_{j}
.

Another important aspect of this model is the conserved quantities. Similar to the two-dimensional honeycomb model, we can find the fundamental loop operators that commute with the Hamiltonian. In our model, the length of loops is 12 or 14, as shown in FIG.
2
.The corresponding loop operators are defined as products of Pauli matrices along the selected path

\begin{split}W_{p1}&=(\prod_{i=2-6}\sigma^{z}_{i})\sigma^{x}_{7}\sigma^{y}_{8}(\prod_{j=9-13}\sigma^{z}_{j})\sigma^{x}_{14},\\
W_{p2}&=\sigma^{y}_{1}(\prod_{i=1-3}\sigma^{y}_{i})\sigma^{z}_{4}(\prod_{j=5-6}\sigma^{x}_{j})\sigma^{y}_{8}(\prod_{j=9-11}\sigma^{z}_{j})\sigma^{x}_{12}.\end{split}

(3)

Both operators are constants of motion since
[W_{p},H]=0
. In terms of the Majorana language, they can be rewritten as
W_{p}=\prod_{<ij>\in loop}u_{ij}
. If we take the square of the loop operators, it will be unity. Thus, we know that the operator carries eigenvalues
\pm 1
, leading to
Z_{2}
flux. In what follows, we provide the guiding rule of gauge choice on bonds
u_{ij}
. Our construction relies on the definition of emitter and absorber (see Fig.
2
). For our fundamental loops, we specify a flux-free configuration(all
W_{p}
s are 1) by assigning an alternative emitter-absorber arrangement: i.e. sublattices with odd number labeling are emitters while sublattices with even number labeling are absorbers. With this pattern, we can further define the ordered bond variable

u_{\langle ij\rangle}=\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{ij},\text{if $i$ is emitter, $j$ is absorber}\\
u_{ji},\text{if $i$ is absorber, $j$ is emitter}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

With this definition, we can express the loop operators as
\prod_{\langle ij\rangle\in loop}u_{\langle ij\rangle}
the gauge choice for the flux-free sector is
u_{\langle ij\rangle}=1
for all
\langle ij\rangle
pairs. One should notice that there are lots of bond configurations encoding the flux-free state: i.e., if we exchange the assignment of emitter and absorber, the resulting eigenvalue of
W_{p}
is still
1
.

III
Ground state and spectrum

Having explained the gauge choice, we are now in a position to rewrite the ground-state Hamiltonian in momentum space. We first perform the Fourier transform,
c_{\alpha,i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R_{i}}}c_{\alpha,\bm{k}}
, where
\alpha,i,\bm{k}
denote sublattices, site indices, and momenta respectively. After some algebra, one can obtain a six-by-six matrix written in Majorana basis, as shown below (assuming a flux-free configuration).

\begin{split}H(\bm{k})&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\textbf{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha,\bm{k}}h_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{k})c_{\beta,\textbf{k}}\\
h(\textbf{k})&=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}0&J_{x}i&0&-J_{z}ie^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{1}}}&0&-J_{y}ie^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{4}}}\\
-J_{x}i&0&-J_{y}i&0&J_{z}ie^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{2}}}&0\\
0&J_{y}i&0&J_{x}i&0&J_{z}ie^{i\bm{k}k\cdot\bm{a_{3}}}\\
J_{z}ie^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{1}}}&0&-J_{x}i&0&-J_{y}i&0\\
0&-J_{z}ie^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{2}}}&0&J_{y}i&0&J_{x}i\\
J_{y}ie^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{4}}}&0&-J_{z}ie^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{3}}}&0&-J_{x}i&0\end{array}\right)\end{split}

(5)

Notice that in the above matrix representation, we have chosen the convention that only inter-unit-cell hopping contributes to the Fourier phase. Subsequently, we compute the energy spectrum with isotropic coupling constants(
J_{x}=J_{y}=J_{z}
) via the eigenvalue equation
Det(E-H(\bm{k}))=0
. Here we use the notation
k_{1}=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{1}},k_{2}=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{2}},k_{3}=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{3}},k_{4}=\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a_{4}}

Having constructed an exactly solvable model and diagonalized it numerically, it is natural to explore the ground state wavefunction and energy. Notably, our fermionized model shares the mathematical similarity with a free fermion model. Therefore, we can analyze the ground state properties using the same approach as that for a free fermion model. Notice that the term ”Ground state” here refer to the lowest energy eigenstate with a fixed gauge configuration. The ground wave function can be constructed by creating all energy eigenstates below the Fermi surface. In our case, the doping is half-filling, meaning the Fermi energy is zero. The ground state wave function can be expressed as the creation of all negative energy states. That is
|GS\rangle=\prod_{E_{m,\bm{k}}<0}d^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},m}|0\rangle
, where
d^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},m}
is the creation operator of the electron eigenstate with momentum
\bm{k}
and negative eigenenergy
E_{m,\bm{k}}
with band index
m
. The corresponding ground state energy is nothing more than the summation of all negative eigenenergies
E_{tot}=\sum_{E_{k}<0}E_{\alpha,\bm{k}}
. Evaluating the ground state energy numerically, we obtain
E_{G}=-4.58(K)
.

We also identify the phase transition between the gapless phase and the gapped phase by tuning the anisotropy between the three coupling constants
J_{x},J_{y},J_{z}
(see FIG.
5
). In contrast to the two-dimensional honeycomb lattice, where the equation for the gapless condition can be handled analytically, the parameter region that corresponds to gapped phases in our 4D model can only be numerically determined. From the phase diagram, it is obvious that if the three coupling constants largely deviate from each other, the system is gapped, much similar to the case in the Kitaev honeycomb model
Kitaev 2006
. This result also suggests the possibility of realizing the topological order and non-abelian anyon in our model.

IV
Stability against vison creation

Although the energy spectrum and energy of the flux-free state are obtained in the previous section, one should notice that all the theoretical calculation done so far is based on the flux-free assumption. Central to our work is the assumption that the zero-flux state is energetically favorable. Although the Lieb theorem states that the ground state is flux-free for a two-dimensional system, the theorem only applies to two dimensions. To confirm that the vortex-free configuration is the lowest energy state in our model, we need to confirm that the energy of single bond-flip is positive, allowing stability against
gauge fluctuations.

To carry out this calculation, we follow the methodology of
Panigrahi et al. 2023
, evaluating the energy cost of a bond-flip in terms of the Majorana scattering phase shift.
For simplicity, we consider the isotropic case(
J_{x}=J_{y}=J_{z}=J
). The formalism is summarized in the Appendix
D
. Here we only mention the crucial results. The presence of the bond flipping potential causes the phase shift and the energy change due to the vison creation is given by the energy integration of the phase shift
\Delta F=\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\frac{\delta(E)}{\pi}
, where
\delta(E)
is the scattering phase shift. The FIG.
6
shows our numerical calculation of the
\delta(E)
. After numerical integration, we find that the change of energy due to the bond flip is positive, given by
0.0117(J)
confirming that the flux-free state is stable against gauge fluctuations.

V
Discussion and summary

V.1
Jordan-Wigner transformation

We can also solve our model by using the Jordan-Wigner transformation
Jordan and Wigner 1928
. This approach can be applied to the Kitaev model and its variant, as discussed in Ref.
Jin et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2007
;
Chen and Nussinov 2008
. Generally, the Jordan-Wigner transformation does not work very well in a high-dimensional model since the resulting Hamiltonian usually brings a long non-local string, making the model computationally difficult. Fortunately, the Kitaev-type model can get rid of this issue since
zz
bonds extend in three dimensions and do not lead to phase strings along the three directions. The Jordan-Wigner transform for spin operators can be expressed as

\begin{split}S^{+}_{i}&=c^{\dagger}_{i}P_{i}\\
S^{-}_{i}&=c_{i}P_{i}\\
S^{z}_{i}&=c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i}-1,\end{split}

(6)

where
f
denotes spinless fermion operators.
P_{i}
is the string operators running according to a particular order from the first site to the site with index
i
. The order is defined as follows: Consider a finite system(with
N\times N\times N\times N
unit cells) with a Hyper-hexagonal structure and describe the lattice point with site index
i
via a vector
\bm{R_{i}}=a\bm{a_{1}}+b\bm{a_{2}}+c\bm{a_{3}}+d\bm{a_{4}}+\sum_{i\leq e}\bm{\delta_{i}}
. The string runs toward the spiral direction
(\bm{a_{4}})
first until hitting the end, and extends to
\bm{a_{1}},\bm{a_{2}},\bm{a_{3}}
directions. Based on this ordering convention, the site index can be defined as
i=cN^{3}+bN^{2}+aN+d
. Subsequently, the string can be defined as
P_{i}=e^{i\Phi},\Phi=\pi\sum_{j<i}n_{j}
. With a well-defined ordering for string operators, we can perform the Jordan-Wigner transform first and express the complex fermion operators as majorana operators. The final Hamiltonian turns out to be

\begin{split}H&=\sum_{<ij>\in x-bond}J_{x}c_{A,i}c_{A,j}+\sum_{<ij>\in y-bond}J_{y}c_{A,i}c_{A,j}+\\
&\sum_{<ij>\in z-bond}J_{z}D_{ij}c_{A,i}c_{A,j},\end{split}

(7)

where
c_{\alpha,i},\alpha=A,B
are Majorana operators. The operator
D_{ij}=\chi_{B,i}\chi_{B,j}
has an eigenvalue
\pm 1
. Since each
D_{ij}
lives on a
z
bond and does not overlap with each other, this operator commutes with the Hamiltonian and becomes a constant of motion. With a fixed gauge structure, we are able to solve the Hamiltonian exactly. The above treatment is a demonstration of extending the Jordan-Wigner transformation to an even higher dimension.

V.2
Arbitrary dimension

We have demonstrated how to construct a four-dimensional Kitaev spin liquid. A natural question is whether we can find a general principle for constructing exactly solvable Kitaev spin liquids for arbitrary dimensions. One important feature of Kitaev-type models is that each site in the lattice has only three bond directions, corresponding to the three quantization axes
x,y
and
z
. For a D-dimensional lattice structure, let us assume n atoms per unit cell. For each unit cell, one needs
2D
legs to connect to another unit cell. Inside a unit cell the atoms form a one-dimensional chain with
n-1
internal bonds which provide
2(n-1)
legs to the atoms. Thus all together the
n
atoms have
2D+2(n-1)
bonds. Since each atom must have three legs, this implies
2D+2(n-1)=3n
, so that
n=2D-2
corresponding to
n=2,4
and
6
for the honeycomb, hyper-octagon, and hyper-hexagon, respectively. This reasoning allows us to construct Kitaev spin liquids in
arbitrary dimensions
with a lattice structure equipped with bond-dependent Ising interaction and spiral geometry. These observations may be of future mathematical interest.

To conclude, we have proposed an exactly solvable 4-dimensional Kitaev model. The key ingredient is the spiral structure which allows a well-defined Jordan-Wigner string. Our model calculation demonstrates that the 4D Kitaev spin model possesses a Majorana Fermi surface. Furthermore, we utilize scattering theory to compute the energy cost to create a vison. The positivity of the energy cost indicates that the ground state of the model is characterized by the flux-free configuration. Our results offer a possibility of extending the Jordan-Wigner transform and fractionalization to a high-dimensional scenario.

Appendix C
Calculation of the density of states

Here we calculate the energy dependence of the density of states, defined as

\begin{split}D(E)&=\int d^{4}k\delta(E-\frac{J}{4}\sqrt{2(k_{1}-k_{4})^{2}+12(k_{2}-k_{4})^{2}k_{3}^{2}})\\
&=\int d^{4}k\delta(E-\frac{J}{4}\sqrt{2k_{1}^{2}+12k_{2}^{2}k_{3}^{2}}).\end{split}

(C1)

Here we redefine the integral variables
k_{1}-k_{4}\rightarrow k_{1},k_{2}-k_{4}\rightarrow k_{2}
and properly rescale the variables so that we can obtain a simplier form of
D(E)
.

To evaluate Eq.
C1
, we first introduce a coordinate transformation as follows,

\begin{split}k_{1}&=r\cos(\theta),\\
k_{2}&=\sqrt{r}\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi),\\
k_{3}&=\sqrt{r}\sin(\theta)\sin(\phi).\end{split}

(C2)

Since there is a volume element in the integral, we need to compute the Jacobian matrix, defined as

\begin{split}J(r,\theta,\phi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta&0\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\sin\theta\sin\phi&\sqrt{r}\cos\theta\sin\phi&\sqrt{r}\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\sin\theta\cos\phi&\sqrt{r}\cos\theta\cos\phi&-\sqrt{r}\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
\end{array}\right).\end{split}

(C3)

The determinant
\det(J(r,\theta,\phi))=-r(\cos^{2}\theta\sin\theta+\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta)
. After performing the radial integral, we obtain the following expression

\begin{split}D(E)&\sim E\alpha\\
\alpha&=\int\int d\theta d\phi\frac{\cos^{2}\theta\sin\theta+\frac{1}{2}\sin^{3}\theta}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi}}.\end{split}

(C4)

The analytical calculation indicates that the density of state
D(E)
linearly depends on energy.

Appendix D
Scattering theory

As mentioned in the main text, the flux-free state is not necessarily the ground state in our model since the validity of Lieb theorem
Lieb 1994
is not guaranteed in a higher-dimensional system. To examine whether the flux-free state is the ground state, we consider a scenario where the visions are created via flipping a gauge-embedded bond. We then adapt the scattering theoretic description, treating the bond flip as a scattering potential. Subsequently, we utilize the Green function approach
Panigrahi et al. 2023
;
Panigrahi et al. 2024
to evaluate the change of free energy due to flipping a bond on the lattice. Let us consider the case of flipping a single bond
u_{ij}
for a
xx
or a
yy
bond. We then rewrite the Hamiltonian as a flux-free part and bond-flip part,
H=H_{0}+V
, where

\begin{split}H_{0}&=\sum_{i,j}iJ_{\alpha_{ij}}u_{\langle ij\rangle}c_{i}c_{j},\text{with all $u_{\langle ij\rangle}=1$},\\
V&=-2J_{\alpha_{ij}}ic_{i}c_{j}.\end{split}

(D1)

To calculate the vison gap, we first write down the free energy as

\begin{split}F=\frac{-1}{2\beta}\sum_{\omega,k}tr(\ln(-G^{-1})),\end{split}

(D2)

where
\beta=1/T
. The factor
\frac{1}{2}
on the right-hand side prevents the double counting of modes in the Brillouin zone. The trace denotes the summation over the sublattice degree of freedom.
G
is the Green function, which can be expressed as
G=(G_{0}^{-1}-V)^{-1}
.
G_{0}
is a flux-free Green function given by

\begin{split}G_{0}(i\omega,\bm{k})=\frac{1}{i\omega-H_{0}(\bm{k})}=\sum_{n}\frac{|\psi_{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}|}{i\omega-\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})}.\end{split}

(D3)

\epsilon_{n}(k)
represents the eigen-energy of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}(k)
and
|\Psi_{n}\rangle
is the corresponding eigenstate. Separating zero-flux free energy and change of free energy due to scattering potential, it yields

\begin{split}\Delta F=-\frac{-1}{2\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}tr(1-\hat{V}\sum_{k}G_{0}(i\omega,\bm{k})).\end{split}

(D4)

For our convenience, one can define the local Green function

\begin{split}g(i\omega_{n})=\sum_{k}G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\bm{k}).\end{split}

(D5)

Thus the change of free energy becomes

\begin{split}\Delta F=\frac{-1}{2\beta}\sum_{i\omega}tr(1-\hat{V}g(i\omega_{n})).\end{split}

(D6)

Additionally, the bond-flipping potential can be expressed in matrix form

\begin{split}\hat{V}_{m}=VO_{m}\oplus\sigma_{2}\oplus O_{4-m},\end{split}

(D7)

where V is the coupling constant and
\sigma_{2}
is the Pauli-y matrix.
O_{m}
is a
m
by
m
null matrix, and the index
m
denotes different types of bond flipping. The final expression of the free energy is

\begin{split}\Delta F=\frac{-1}{2\beta}\sum_{i\omega}tr(\ln(1-\hat{V}O_{m}\oplus\sigma_{2}\oplus O_{4-m}g(i\omega_{n}))).\end{split}

(D8)

The trace of the logarithm function can be further simplified as the logarithm of the determinant of the matrix. It yields

\begin{split}\Delta F=\frac{-1}{2\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n},\alpha}\ln\lambda_{\alpha}(i\omega_{n}),\end{split}

(D9)

where
\lambda_{\alpha}
is an eigenvalue of
1-\hat{V}O_{m}\oplus\sigma_{2}\oplus O_{4-m}g(i\omega_{n})
. We can further evaluate the Matsubara summation by converting the summation into a real energy integral, see FIG.
8
.

\begin{split}\Delta F&=-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}f(\omega)Im\ln\lambda_{\alpha}(\omega)\\
&=-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}f(\omega)\delta(\omega).\end{split}

(D10)

Since the phase shift is odd in frequency (
\delta(-\omega)=-\delta(\omega)

\Delta F=\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{d\omega}{\pi}\delta(\omega).

(D11)

The remaining part of the calculation is performed numerically. Performing the numerical integration, one can obtain the single bond flip energy is around 0.0117(J). The energy cost to create visons is positive, indicating that the flux-free state has the lowest energy among all possible gauge configurations. Another issue is the energy cost to flip a
zz

zz
bonds belong to different unit cells, according to our definition. A straightforward symmetry argument tells us the energy cost to flip a
zz

xx,yy
