Selecting for empathy? A cross-sectional study on empathy and empathic care intentions in newly enrolled dentistry, medicine, nursing, and pharmacy students in Singapore to inform admissions and training
Authors: Andre Matthias Müller, Claire Gek Ling Tan, Siew Tiang Lydia Lau, Paul John Gallagher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmpathy is a key attribute of healthcare professionals. While it can be nurtured, predispositions toward empathy predict success in healthcare training and practice. We examined empathy and empathic care intentions among newly enrolled healthcare students from Dentistry, Medicine, Nursing, and Pharmacy in Singapore to inform admissions and curriculum planning.MethodsNewly enrolled students from the four disciplines at the National University of Singapore were invited to participate. Empathy was measured using the Jefferson Scale of Empathy-Health Professions Student version (JSE-HPS), while empathic care intentions were assessed with a custom-designed seven-item instrument. Parametric or non-parametric statistical tests were used to compare scores between disciplines and across demographic categories. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 646 students participated, with a 37% response rate. The median empathy score was 109 (IQR: 102–116) out of 140. Medicine students had significantly higher empathy scores than students from other disciplines, and Dentistry students scored significantly higher than Nursing students. Similar patterns were observed for empathic care intentions, with Medicine and Dentistry students scoring significantly higher than Nursing and Pharmacy counterparts.ConclusionsNewly enrolled dentistry and medical students had higher empathy and empathic care intentions scores that their counterparts studying Nursing and Pharmacy. Our findings emphasize the importance of integrating valid empathy assessments into the admissions process to identify students who are well-suited for healthcare training and practice. Simultaneously, curricula should be designed to purposefully nurture empathy throughout the training period. This is especially crucial for disciplines where more stringent admissions procedures are not warranted.

Background

Healthcare professionals are not only expected to have sound clinical knowledge and skills but also possess adequate socio-psychological competencies [1]. While the rationale for clinical skills is apparent, the success of healthcare often hinges on how that care is delivered. Empathy, in particular, has gained prominence as a key professionalism competency in the context of patient care [2–4]. Defined as “…a predominantly cognitive attribute that involves an understanding of experiences, concerns, and perspectives of the patient, combined with a capacity to communicate this understanding, and an intention to help” [5], empathy is a central attribute of healthcare professionals.

Empathy of healthcare professionals has consistently been linked to immediate benefits in patient health and well-being during clinical encounters [5, 6] and to increased trust, which frequently translates into better treatment adherences as well as outcomes [7–9]. Moreover, more empathic healthcare professionals experience greater job fulfilment and reduced stress [10–12], which is crucial from a health systems perspective.

The critical importance of nurturing empathy during healthcare training is widely recognized, with many curricula integrating various opportunities to foster it [12–14]. However, it is also important to examine empathy of students entering healthcare training. Firstly, pre-training empathy and related personality qualities such as sensitivity and interpersonal skills are reliable predictors of success in healthcare education [15, 16], particularly in the development of competencies that involve patient contact [17]. Relatedly, healthcare students with higher empathy levels at baseline are likely to be more resilient against empathy decline [18], a phenomenon that has been reported during clinical years [5, 19–22]. This increases the likelihood that such students will remain empathic in future practice [23]. Finally, students with more empathic predispositions are more likely to engage with and benefit from empathy training due to their strong orientation toward care, allowing them to further enhance their empathic abilities which is beneficial for patients and professionals alike [17]. Taken together, enrolling healthcare profession students with an empathic orientation would be desirable in many respects [24].

Considering the above and from an admissions perspective, assessing empathy at entry provides valuable information on current selection procedures. Appropriate levels of empathy among newly enrolled students may indicate that these processes effectively identify individuals with a natural inclination to care for others and strong potential for success in training and practice. Conversely, lower levels could signal a need to adjust admissions procedures or enhance empathy training during the program [25].

The current study aims to examine empathy and empathic care intentions among newly enrolled students from four healthcare disciplines—Dentistry, Medicine, Nursing, and Pharmacy—in Singapore. While empathy has been extensively studied among healthcare students in some regions, such research remains scarce in Asia [12, 26]. Notably, to the best our knowledge there is no research examining empathy among newly enrolled students across four main healthcare disciplines in Asia. By addressing this gap, this study seeks to enhance understanding of empathy in healthcare students while informing admissions processes and curriculum planning.

Methods

Study design

We employed a cross-sectional design to examine empathy and empathic care intentions among newly enrolled students from four healthcare disciplines—Dentistry, Medicine, Nursing, and Pharmacy—at the National University of Singapore. The data presented in this manuscript serves as baseline data for an ongoing mixed-methods longitudinal intervention study on empathy in healthcare students. This study has been approved by the Departmental Ethics Review Committee of the Saw Swee Hock School of Public Health, National University of Singapore (Ethics ID: SSHSPH-214). Detailed information on the study’s materials and methods is available in the published protocol [27]. The STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies informed the reporting of the current study [28].

Participants

All first-year students enrolled in the undergraduate programs of Dentistry, Medicine, Nursing, and Pharmacy at the National University of Singapore during the academic years 2023/24 and 2024/25 were invited to participate in the study. Invitations were disseminated via email, and in-class announcements were conducted by a staff member who is neither involved in the study nor in teaching the students. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Data collection

Data were collected between mid-July (before the academic year) and mid-August (week two of the academic year) via a self-administered online survey hosted on the Qualtrics platform. The survey consisted of 32 items, including demographic questions and instruments to measure empathy and empathic care intentions.

Demographic variables

Participants provided information on their study discipline, age, sex (male, female, other), ethnicity (Chinese, Indian, Malay, Other), and housing type (private or large public housing apartment, medium-size public housing apartment, small-size public housing apartment). Household income information was not collected due to its sensitivity in Singapore, with prior research reporting significant missing data when asking participants about income [29]. Instead, we collected information on housing type, a strong correlate of income, and, hence, a suitable proxy for socio-economic status [30].

Empathy

Empathy was assessed using the Jefferson Scale of Empathy, Healthcare Professions Student version (JSE-HPS), a widely used and psychometrically robust instrument in healthcare education research [5, 26]. The JSE-HPS consists of 20 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (from strongly disagree to strongly agree), focusing on cognitive dimensions of empathy. An example item reads ‘Patients feel better when their health care providers understand their feelings’. Total scores range from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating greater empathy.

Empathic care intentions

Empathic care intentions were measured using a custom-designed 7-item instrument. While the JSE-HPS assesses attitudes and beliefs related to patient-centred empathy, intentions are a more direct predictor of future behavior [31]. In the absence of an appropriate tool, items were adapted from the psychometrically sound Reynolds Empathy Scale [32] and the CARE measure [33] to align with the study objectives. Participants responded to items beginning with the stem “When I work as a healthcare professional, I will/want to.“, which included empathy-related behaviors of understanding patients, communicating understanding, and acting to help [5]. Responses were scored on a 7-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 7 to 49, with higher scores indicating stronger intentions to provide empathic care in the future.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using STATA version 18.5 SE (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). We first compared demographic characteristics between students recruited in AY2023/24 and AY 2024/25 to examine recruitment bias. There were no statistically significant differences (discipline: p = 0.939; age: p = 0.536; sex: p = 0.219; ethnicity: p = 0.310; housing type: p = 0.909), indicating no such bias. Second, we compared the distributions of empathy and empathic care intentions between students recruited in AY 2023/24 and 2024/25 using Mann-Whitney U tests. Results were non-significant (p = 0.2214 and p = 0.1017, respectively), indicating the data could be combined. Third, we examined whether our combined sample was representative of the overall cohort in terms of proportions by discipline and sex using Chi-Square tests. The combined sample was not representative of the overall student cohort in terms of proportions by discipline (p < 0.001) with Nursing and Pharmacy students overrepresented and Dentistry and Medicine students underrepresented. In contrast, the sex distribution in our sample aligned with that of the overall cohort (p = 0.539).

Depending on the variable distribution, we conducted appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests. Differences in empathy and empathic care intentions scores across demographic characteristics were examined. Pair-wise comparisons were performed to identify specific group differences.

Results

Study sample

Of the 1,744 eligible freshmen students invited to participate, 781 (Wave 1: 308; Wave 2: 467) consented to join the study. A total of 646 and 632 students provided data for empathy and empathic care intentions, respectively. With this, we observed response rates of 29.6% for the 2023/24 cohort and 44.4% for the 2024/25 cohort, with an overall response rate of 37.0%. See Fig. 1 for the participant flow.

Age of students varied by discipline – Nursing students (22.8, SD = 7.1) were significantly older than their peers (19.5, SD = 1.2), p < 0.001, reflecting the inclusion of mature students in this group (mainly career converters or diploma upgraders). Empathy scores

The median empathy score, as measured by the JSE-HPS, for all 646 participants was 109 (IQR: 102–116). Statistically significant differences in the distribution of empathy scores were observed across disciplines (p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons revealed that Medicine students scored significantly higher than students from all other disciplines (p < 0.001 for comparisons with Nursing and Pharmacy students, p = 0.014 for the comparison with Dentistry students). Dentistry students had significantly higher empathy scores than Nursing students (p = 0.026). See Fig. 2 for the median empathy scores by discipline.

Median empathy scores and interquartile ranges as measured by the JSE-HPS by discipline

While the median empathy score for female students (109, IQR: 102–117) was slightly higher than that of male students (108, IQR: 101–116), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.228). This trend between sexes was consistent within each discipline (p > 0.05). Similarly, no significant differences in empathy scores were found across ethnic groups (p = 0.670).

However, students residing in private properties or larger public housing apartments (110, IQR: 103–117) reported significantly higher empathy scores compared to those living in more affordable housing (107, IQR: 101–115; p = 0.003). This difference was not observed within individual disciplines (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Empathic care intentions

The median empathic care intentions score was 42 (IQR: 39–46). Statistically significant differences in the distribution of empathic care intentions scores were observed across disciplines (p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons indicated that Dentistry and Medicine students had significantly higher scores compared to Nursing and Pharmacy students (p = 0.006 for the comparison between Dentistry and Nursing students, p < 0.005 for the comparison between Dentistry and Pharmacy students, p < 0.001 for comparisons between Medicine and Nursing as well as Pharmacy students). Across the overall sample, female students reported significantly higher empathic care intentions scores (43, IQR: 40–46) than male students (42, IQR: 38–45), p = 0.005). This difference was also significant within the Nursing (p = 0.018) and Medicine disciplines p = 0.001). No significant differences in empathic care intentions scores were observed across ethnic groups (p = 0.088).

As with empathy scores, students residing in private properties or larger public housing apartments scored significantly higher on empathic care intentions (43, IQR: 40–46) compared to those living in more affordable housing (42, IQR: 38–44; p < 0.001). This trend was also evident within the Nursing cohort (p = 0.049).

Discussion

Research on empathy in healthcare education has predominantly focused on contexts in North America and Europe, with limited representation from Asia [12, 26]. Additionally, few studies have compared empathy and empathic care intentions among newly enrolled healthcare students across multiple disciplines. This gap limits insights into the success of admissions processes and potential training needs related to empathy. To address these gaps and inform both admissions practices and curriculum planning, we conducted the present study, examining empathy and empathic care intentions among nearly 650 newly matriculated students from Dentistry, Medicine, Nursing, and Pharmacy programs in Singapore.

Overall empathy and empathic care intentions

The median empathy score across all participants, as measured by the psychometrically robust JSE-HPS [5], was 109 out of a maximum of 140. Our custom-designed instrument assessing intentions to provide empathic care in future practice yielded a median score of 42 out of 49. Interpreting these findings is challenging due to the absence of population-specific normative data that would indicate desirable levels of empathy and care intentions at the start of tertiary healthcare training across disciplines [5]. Normative data published internationally have focused exclusively on Medicine students [34–36].

Differences in empathy and empathic care intentions between disciplines

Newly matriculated Medicine students in our study had the highest empathy and empathic care intention scores, significantly surpassing those of Nursing and Pharmacy students. This pattern contrasts with previous research [37]. Moreover, the empathy scores of our Medicine students exceeded those reported in earlier studies from Singapore [38] and Japan [36]. Additionally, their scores are on par with scores from newly enrolled Medicine students in the United States [34, 39] and Spain [35].

When benchmarked against published normative data from the United States [34] and Japan [36], our Medicine students’ empathy scores fall within the 53rd to 71st percentile and 63rd to 77th percentile, respectively. This is encouraging as it may indicate that our Medicine students enter tertiary education with personality dispositions that are well-suited for healthcare training and practice.

Newly enrolled Dentistry students in our study demonstrated significantly higher empathy scores (median 113) than Nursing students (median 107). They also reported significantly higher empathic care intention scores (median 44) compared to both Nursing (median 42) and Pharmacy students (median 42). Given the limited literature on empathy in newly matriculated Dentistry students, especially within a comparative context [37, 40], these findings are rather novel. Our results suggest that Dentistry students in our setting may possess a desirable degree of people-orientation upon entry into their professional training. However, this interpretation should be considered cautiously due to the relatively small number of Dentistry students enrolled in our study.

The lower empathy and empathic care intentions scores observed among newly enrolled Nursing students in our study were unexpected. Usually, Nursing students exhibit higher empathy than students from other disciplines due to their altruistic nature and a strong desire to build supportive relationships with patients—key drivers for their career choice [41, 42]. For example, prior studies from the United Kingdom and Italy found that newly matriculated Nursing students reported significantly higher empathy scores compared to students from other healthcare disciplines [37, 43].

One possible explanation for our finding is the inclusion of mature students in the Nursing program at the National University of Singapore. These students often have some experience in the healthcare fields; this is especially so for those who have obtained a nursing diploma previously and are now pursuing a degree. Previous work suggests that such experience can lead to cynicism, affecting empathy negatively [5]. In our sample, about 18% of Nursing students were mature students, whose empathic intentions scores were significantly lower than that of their younger counterparts (p < 0.001), lending some support for this interpretation. However, our finding still necessitates reflection. Since nurses typically provide the most direct and continuous care to patients [23], lower baseline empathy levels warrant attention from an admissions and curriculum perspective.

The comparatively lower empathy and empathic care intention scores among newly enrolled Pharmacy students in our study were not entirely unexpected. Prior research has reported similar findings [37, 44]. Traditionally, Pharmacy has been a product-focused discipline, with professional responsibilities often centred around medication management rather than direct patient care. Consequently, it is plausible that the discipline attracts students with lower empathic predispositions. This notion aligns with research showing that freshmen Medicine students interested in procedure-oriented specialties such as radiology and pathology report lower empathy scores [5, 45]. Despite this, it is important to note that the pharmacist profession is changing with many future pharmacists expected to care for patients in more personalised ways [46, 47]. This is also true in Singapore where healthcare is increasingly delivered in the community, and the community pharmacist is a common encounter [48]. Given this transformation, enrolling students with empathic orientations and developing empathy through the curriculum are critical.

Differences in empathy and empathic care intentions by sex and housing type

Female students in our study had higher empathy and empathic care intentions scores than male students. However, this difference was only significant for the latter, and the small magnitude suggests that it is less meaningful. This finding contrasts with most international studies, which have found significantly higher empathy scores among female healthcare profession students [5, 43, 45]. This is often explained by suggesting that women in general have more empathy due to genetic and evolutionary pre-dispositions which are reinforced by existing social as well as cultural norms and expectations [5]. However, our findings are not entirely anomalous. A recent study on freshmen Dentistry students also reported no significant sex-based differences in empathy [40].

Our finding that students who reside in housing that indicates higher socio-economic status are significantly more empathic than students who reside in more affordable housing is intriguing as the relationship between socio-economic status and empathy is less researched. However, the magnitude of the difference was small prompting us to not speculate about this finding.

Implications of findings for admissions and training

Our findings, alongside prior research, reveal considerable inter-disciplinary variation in empathy and empathic care intentions among newly enrolled healthcare students. This is of note, as empathic predispositions have been shown to be solid predictors of success in healthcare training, clinical competency, and quality of care post-graduation [17, 25, 45]. For institutions offering resource-intensive healthcare degrees, ensuring that students possess adequate levels of empathy upon admission may be an important consideration.

At the National University of Singapore, Dentistry and Medicine students undergo rigorous admissions processes such as interviews and role-playing activities designed to evaluate pertinent attributes and communication skills, which can reflect empathic predispositions. Our finding that newly enrolled Dentistry and Medicine students displayed comparatively higher empathy and empathic care intentions suggests that existing admission processes may already select candidates with desirable attributes. Nevertheless, incorporating a validated instrument such as the JSE-HPS could enhance accuracy in assessing empathy [17, 25]. In practice, the JSE-HPS could serve as a supplementary screening tool alongside existing measures. Discipline-specific cut-offs could be established to inform admission decisions or identify otherwise suitable candidates who may benefit from more empathy-focused training.

Admissions procedures of Nursing and Pharmacy students at the National University of Singapore involve few assessments of personality characteristics. For Nursing, upon meeting general admission criteria, about one third of candidates will be called for an interview conducted by faculty and practitioners. For Pharmacy, and in addition to assessing academic scores, aptitude-based assessments are conducted for a considerable number of applicants. This involves written responses to questions incorporated into the online application form.

Considering the above, students with limited inclinations toward caring empathically for others may enter these programs, as reflected in our findings of comparatively lower empathy and empathic care intentions among these students. Efforts are under way to incorporate personality assessments, including empathy, into admissions procedures for prospective nurses [49, 50]. However, the implementation of more stringent admissions procedures may shrink the pool of nurses and pharmacists available in the future. This needs to be considered carefully in the context of a shortage of these healthcare workers globally [51] and in Singapore [52].

Targeted empathy development opportunities for Nursing and Pharmacy students may be a more feasible way forward. Implementing these early in the curriculum could help prevent declines in empathy over time. Pharmacy students may benefit from an emphasis on the expanding role of empathy and patient-centred care within their profession, countering the traditional perception of pharmacists as merely medication dispensers [47].

Strengths and limitations

Several strengths of the current study deserve mention. First, collecting empathy data from newly enrolled healthcare students across four disciplines enabled meaningful between-discipline comparisons. With this, we also present the first data set on empathy among newly enrolled Dentistry, Nursing, and Pharmacy students in Singapore. Data collection right at the start of training made inferences about student selection and training needs possible. This is especially important from a policy perspective. Second, the use of a psychometrically robust instrument to measure empathy increases the confidence in our findings. Third, although not based on a validated tool, our assessment of empathic care intentions provided a more immediate indicator of how empathy might translate into care behaviors post-graduation.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, Dentistry and Medicine students were underrepresented in our sample, particularly Dentistry, with only about 17% of the cohort participating. This has likely reduced statistical power when comparisons were conducted involving students from these disciplines. However, statistically significant differences were still detected which gives us confidence in the robust nature of our findings.

Second, volunteer bias may have influenced the results, as students more interested in the study and in person-centred care may have been more likely to participate [8]. This bias could be particularly relevant for Dentistry and Medicine students, where those with a stronger inclination toward empathy and patient-centred care may have been overrepresented, potentially inflating the empathy scores compared to a more representative sample. However, the large sample (37% of the overall cohort) and the fact the distribution by sex was representative of the overall cohort, indicates that this bias did not exert undue influence, at least not for Medicine, Nursing, and Pharmacy students which we recruited in good numbers.

Third, social-desirability bias, where students might provide socially acceptable responses, could also have affected our results. However, constructing entirely neutral survey items when assessing personality traits is inherently challenging. To mitigate this bias, recruitment materials assured students of survey anonymity, emphasized that responses would not influence academic progress, and framed the survey as an opportunity to benefit future cohorts and society. Notably, previous research has shown that social-desirability bias did not significantly impact empathy assessments [8].

Conclusion

Institutions offering costly healthcare training carry a significant social responsibility to enrol students likely to succeed both in training and in practice [53]. More importantly, they must be confident that the graduates they produce deliver high quality care that goes beyond diagnosis and treatment. Empathy, as a personality characteristic, is a good predictor of competence and provision of quality care. Therefore, selecting students with empathic predispositions is a sound approach. Incorporating empathy assessments, ideally using validated tools such as the JSE-HPS, into admissions procedures could help ensure that those admitted to healthcare professional programs are well-suited for the demands of person-centred care.

Tightened admissions criteria might be feasible for Dentistry and Medicine as these disciplines are commonly oversubscribed, affording admissions committees the luxury of selecting only the most suitable candidates. In contrast, the current and anticipated future shortage of Nurses and Pharmacists is a likely disincentive to further tighten admissions criteria by incorporating empathy assessments in these disciplines. Consequently, it is crucial to provide educational opportunities to foster empathy across all healthcare disciplines. Various pedagogical tools, including perspective-taking exercises, communication training, and exposure to the arts [5], have been used successfully to achieve this, with some showing promise in the Singapore context [54]. Building on these efforts, a novel core curriculum that explicitly and implicitly targets empathy through sound pedagogical strategies is currently being evaluated [27, 55].
