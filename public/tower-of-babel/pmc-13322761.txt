Urinary indoxyl sulfate in children: age-related decline and its association with Bacteroidota in the gut microbiota
Authors: Yuko AKAGAWA, Shohei AKAGAWA, Shoji TSUJI, Tomoki KITAWAKI, Ayaka ITO, Mamoru NAKANISHI, Takahiko OKADA, Sohsaku YAMANOUCHI, Kazunari KANEKO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary indoxyl sulfate (IS), a gut microbiota-derived uremic toxin, is associated with the adult gut microbiota and is elevated in children with autism spectrum disorder. However, its age-related trajectory and association with microbiota in healthy children remain uncharacterized. Therefore, we aimed to elucidate age-related changes in urinary IS in healthy children and to investigate the potential utility of urinary IS as a surrogate marker for their gut microbiota. We measured urinary IS/creatinine (Cr) ratios and analyzed the fecal gut microbiota composition (16S rRNA gene sequencing) in 102 healthy Japanese children aged 5 months to 12 years. Associations were assessed using Spearman’s correlation. An empirical age-adjustment model (inverse proportional) was developed to derive an age-adjusted IS/Cr ratio (actual/predicted), thereby removing the strong age dependence before re-evaluating correlations with gut microbiota. The unadjusted IS/Cr ratio showed a strong negative correlation with age (rs=−0.47, p<0.001) and declined markedly during early childhood, stabilizing at approximately 5 years. Weak correlations with microbiota metrics were observed before adjustment; however, the age-adjusted IS/Cr ratio showed no correlation with age (rs=−0.03, p=0.768). Notably, age adjustment revealed significant positive correlations between the IS/Cr ratio and the phylum Bacteroidota (rs=0.26, p=0.008) and strengthened the correlation with the genus Bacteroides (rs=0.31, p=0.002), associations that were not apparent before adjustment. Correcting for this age-dependent variation is crucial, after which the IS/Cr ratio can serve as a potential non-invasive surrogate marker reflecting the abundance of Bacteroidota, particularly the genus Bacteroides, in the pediatric gut microbiota.

INTRODUCTION

Indoxyl sulfate (IS), a uremic toxin, is an end-metabolite derived from tryptophan metabolism in humans. Dietary L-tryptophan, the substrate, is converted to indole by specific gut bacteria that express the enzyme tryptophanase. Subsequently, indole is absorbed and undergoes sulfation in the liver to form IS. In individuals with normal kidney function, IS is efficiently excreted via urine [1, 2]. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in this pathway, as evidenced by the absence of IS production in germ-free mice administered L-tryptophan [3]. Furthermore, studies have shown that bacteria belonging to the genus Bacteroides produce tryptophanase in gnotobiotic mice [4].

Consequently, urinary IS has emerged as a potential non-invasive surrogate marker for assessing gut microbiota. Reportedly, IS exerts various toxic effects, including neurotoxicity. Clinical interest has increased following recent reports, including meta-analyses of multiple studies, indicating elevated IS levels in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [5] and suggesting a role for IS in the gut-brain axis [6]. Amid the growing significance of this marker, studies in adults have shown correlations between urinary IS and both gut microbiota diversity (Shannon index) [7] and the relative abundance of bacteria belonging to the class Clostridia [7, 8].

Although elevated IS levels have been reported in specific pediatric conditions, such as ASD, the fundamental aspects in healthy children, namely, the developmental trajectory of urinary IS and its association with specific gut bacteria, remain largely uncharacterized. Therefore, we aimed to elucidate age-related changes in urinary IS within a cohort of healthy children and to investigate the potential utility of urinary IS as a surrogate marker for their gut microbiota.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kansai Medical University (Approval No. 2021206). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants, and child assent was obtained where appropriate.

Participants and sample collection

We enrolled 102 healthy children aged 5 months to 12 years. Participants were recruited through outreach at kindergartens and elementary schools near the study institution. Children whose guardians provided informed consent were enrolled in this study. We excluded children requiring regular hospital visits for chronic conditions, those receiving daily medication, or those with a history of antibiotic use within the month before sample collection.

Midstream urine (approximately 10 mL) was collected as a spot urine sample, defined as a sample from a single, random daytime collection. Stool (approximately 0.5 g) samples were collected from each participant on the same day. Urine samples were collected in sterile containers, immediately frozen at −20°C, and analyzed for urinary IS and Cr concentrations within 3 days. Stool samples were collected using a stool sampling kit (TechnoSuruga Laboratory, Shizuoka, Japan), which contained guanidine thiocyanate, allowing the sample to be stored at room temperature. DNA was extracted within 4 weeks using a QIAmp DNA Micro Kit (QIAGEN N.V., Hilden, Germany). The extracted DNA was stored at −80°C until further analysis.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing

Sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene was performed by Macrogen Japan Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). Sequencing libraries targeting the V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were constructed following the Illumina 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library preparation protocol.

In the initial amplification step (first polymerase chain reaction [PCR]), 2 ng of genomic DNA was used as the template. This reaction mixture contained 5× reaction buffer, 1 mM deoxynucleotide triphosphate mix, and 500 nM each of the universal forward and reverse primers (V3-F: 5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTAT AAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ and V4-R: 5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′), which included Illumina adapter overhang sequences, and Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Thermal cycling conditions involved an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles at 95°C for 30 sec, 55°C for 30 sec, and 72°C for 30 sec, and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The resulting amplicons were purified using AMPure beads (Agencourt Biosciences, Beverly, MA, USA).

Subsequently, a second PCR was performed to attach the dual indices, employing Nextera XT indexed primers. In this reaction, 2 µL of the purified product from the first PCR was used as a template. Both PCRs had identical thermal cycling profiles; however, the second PCR was reduced to 10 cycles. Following amplification, the final PCR products were purified with AMPure beads and quantified via quantitative PCR using the KAPA Library Quantification kits for Illumina. The quality of the libraries was verified using TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany).

Sequence processing and analysis

Bioinformatics processing of the sequence reads was performed using the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology version 2 pipeline (version 2021.12) [9]. During the denoising step with DADA2, chimeric sequences and reads with low-quality scores (<20) were identified and removed. High-quality reads were subsequently clustered into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [10]. Taxonomic assignment of the identified ASVs was performed by aligning them against the Greengenes reference database. Features classified as host-derived DNA, such as mitochondrial or chloroplast sequences, were excluded from further analysis.

Measurement of urinary indoxyl sulfate and creatinine

Urinary IS concentrations were quantified based on their autofluorescence properties, using a method described by Nakanishi et al. [11]. A standard curve was prepared using indoxyl sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in a buffer solution consisting of 40 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) and 80% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), adjusted to pH 7.0. For the assay, 250 µL of 80% DMSO/HEPES buffer was added to each well of a 96-well fluorescence microplate. Subsequently, 2 µL of either the standard solution or the neat urine sample was added to the wells. The plate was mixed for 2 min using the shaker function of a SpectraMax® iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, LLC, San Jose, CA, USA). Immediately after mixing, the fluorescence intensity was measured on the same instrument at 280/400 nm (excitation 280 nm/emission 400 nm).

Urinary Cr concentrations were measured using a colorimetric Jaffe-based method with a commercial kit (LabAssay™ Creatinine, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Cat No. 291-93901). Urine samples were first diluted 50-fold with the Deproteinizing Reagent included in the kit. A 100 µL aliquot of the diluted urine or standard solution was added to a transparent 96-well microplate. The reaction was initiated by adding 100 µL of a reaction reagent (prepared by mixing equal volumes of the Picric Acid Reagent and the 0.75 mol/L Sodium Hydroxide Solution) to each well. The plate was mixed for 1 min on the SpectraMax® iD3 reader. After incubating at room temperature for 20 min, absorbance was measured at 520 nm with the same instrument.

The concentrations of IS and Cr were determined from their respective standards using the SoftMax® Pro Software (Molecular Devices, LLC). To account for individual variations in urinary concentration among the spot urine samples, the final IS concentration was normalized to the Cr concentration and expressed as a ratio (e.g., μM/gCr) for all subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rs) was used to assess correlations between the urinary IS/Cr ratio and study variables. The focus of the primary analyses was on alpha diversity metrics (Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices), the relative abundances of the four major phyla (Actinomycetota [formerly Actinobacteria], Bacillota [formerly Firmicutes], Bacteroidota [formerly Bacteroidetes], and Pseudomonadota [formerly Proteobacteria]), and key phylum ratios (such as Bacteroidota/Bacillota). Based on previous reports on tryptophanase production [4, 12], the relative abundances of key indole-producing genera (Bacteroides, Clostridium, Enterococcus, and Escherichia/Shigella) were also evaluated for correlation in a hypothesis-driven manner.

To account for age, the relationship between the IS/Cr ratio and age (in months) was visualized using a scatter plot. Based on the observed distribution, an empirical inverse proportional model was applied: Y = (1600 × 5) / (Xage + 5) + 200, where Y represents the predicted IS/Cr ratio and Xage represents age in months. Subsequently, an ‘age-adjusted IS/Cr ratio’ was calculated for each participant by dividing their actual measured IS/Cr ratio by the predicted value derived from this model (actual/predicted).

Correlations between the age-adjusted IS/Cr ratio and the aforementioned gut microbiota data were re-evaluated using rs. All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP Pro 16 software (SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to report the characterization of the age-related trajectory of urinary IS levels in children. The findings show that, after age adjustment, the urinary IS level may serve as a surrogate marker for gut microbiota composition, particularly reflecting the abundance of the phylum Bacteroidota.

A significant finding of this study was the strong inverse correlation observed between the IS/Cr ratio and age. Notably, the relative abundance of the genus Bacteroides showed no significant correlation with age (rs=0.03, p=0.773). This lack of an age-dependent trend suggests that the high IS levels observed in early childhood are not attributed to changes in the population size of this genus. Instead, this phenomenon likely reflects the general physiological trend of higher amino acid requirements and consequently higher tryptophan intake per kilogram of body weight during early infancy to support rapid growth. Consistent with this hypothesis, the estimated average requirement for tryptophan established by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare highlights a marked age-dependent decrease per kilogram of body weight, 9.5 mg/day for 6-month-old infants versus 4.0 mg/day for adults [13].

Infancy is characterized by a high intake of tryptophan-rich foods, such as breast milk (particularly colostrum) and infant formula. This intake pattern likely led to the highest abundance of tryptophan in the gut lumen, where it is available as a substrate for bacterial indole production. These conditions likely boost indole production (the precursor to IS), resulting in the elevated urinary IS levels observed in our cohort during early childhood. Additionally, although urinary Cr excretion physiologically increases with age, in parallel with muscle mass development, the observed negative correlation between the IS/Cr ratio and age suggests that the decline in this ratio is not solely dependent on the increase in Cr excretion.

Another key finding pertains to the correlations observed after adjusting for age. Of the four major phyla, only Bacteroidota exhibited a specific positive correlation with the age-adjusted urinary IS level. Furthermore, evaluation of the major indole-producing genera against prior hypotheses revealed that only Bacteroides showed a significant positive association. Additionally, as shown in our results, Bacteroides was the most abundant among the known or potential indole-producing genera in this cohort. This observation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating the presence of tryptophanase within the genus Bacteroides [4].

Our results provide the first indication that among the multiple known indole-producing bacterial genera, Bacteroides may primarily contribute to physiological variations in urinary IS levels in healthy children. However, the observed correlation with Bacteroides differs from the findings of several previous studies in humans.

Weber et al. [8] reported that among adult patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HCT), higher urinary IS levels were associated with bacteria belonging to the class Clostridia, specifically the families Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae. Similarly, Farowski et al. [7] concluded that urinary IS serves as a predictive marker of the relative abundance of Clostridia in hospitalized adult patients receiving antibiotic treatment following non-allo-HCT.

We attribute this discrepancy to fundamental differences between the study populations. The cohorts in previous studies comprised hospitalized adults undergoing intensive interventions, such as stem cell transplantation (allogeneic or autologous) and antibiotic therapy, which are conditions that are likely associated with significant dysbiosis. In contrast, our participants were healthy children with no recent history of antibiotic use. Therefore, our finding of a specific correlation with Bacteroides does not necessarily contradict those of previous reports. Rather, it reveals the primary dynamics of IS production under physiological conditions, specifically in healthy children without overt dysbiosis.

Furthermore, the stratified analysis at 5 years of age clarified the characteristics of this surrogate marker. The significant correlation observed in children under 5 years of age indicates that the adjustment model effectively accounts for the physiological variations during this dynamic developmental phase. In contrast, the lack of correlation in children aged 5 years and older is statistically expected; as the unadjusted IS/Cr ratio reaches a plateau, the variance of the data narrows, making detection of significant correlations challenging. Biologically, this likely reflects the structural maturation of the gut microbiota during early childhood. Although these results suggest that the IS/Cr ratio is particularly sensitive as a surrogate marker in early childhood, we applied the uniform adjustment model across all samples to delineate the continuous trajectory of IS production from early infancy to the stabilized phase within a single cohort.

Our findings have clinical significance. Recently, metabolites derived from the gut microbiota, including urinary IS, have garnered attention for their potential involvement in the pathophysiology of the gut-brain axis, particularly in neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD. Findings from a recent systematic review showed that across all six studies examined, children with ASD exhibited significantly higher urinary IS levels than neurotypical controls [5].

Reports on gut microbiota composition in children with ASD, particularly concerning the genus Bacteroides, have yielded inconsistent results. Nevertheless, in their comprehensive meta-analysis [14] including 18 studies involving 493 children with ASD, Iglesias-Vázquez et al. concluded that Bacteroides were more abundant in the ASD group (9.04%) than in neurotypical controls (4.69%), with a large effect size. Given this, the consistently observed elevation of urinary IS levels in children with ASD could plausibly be attributed to the increased abundance of Bacteroides reported in the meta-analysis. Our finding of a specific positive correlation between urinary IS levels and Bacteroides in healthy children supports this hypothesis.

This study had some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the assessment of longitudinal changes within individuals. Second, dietary intake was not assessed, thus preventing the direct evaluation of actual tryptophan intake. Furthermore, although the study included a wide age range (5 months to 12 years), potential confounding factors that strongly influence the gut microbiota, particularly during infancy, such as feeding method (breast milk vs. formula) and mode of delivery (vaginal vs. cesarean section), were not evaluated. Third, the use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing limits the assessment of functional aspects, such as the presence or absence of tryptophanase-encoding genes, which could be addressed in future studies using shotgun metagenomics or targeted qPCR. Fourth, the age-adjustment model was empirically derived from this specific cohort and requires validation in an independent population. Fifth, although the participants were physically healthy, formal psychiatric or neurodevelopmental evaluations were not conducted. Therefore, the presence of individuals with undiagnosed disorders cannot be excluded, and future longitudinal studies are required to provide further insights. Finally, the study included only 102 Japanese children residing in the Kansai region. Hence, the generalizability of our findings, including the proposed age-adjustment model and the specific correlation with Bacteroides, to pediatric populations in other regions, such as Western countries, remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Nevertheless, this study is the first to report the systematic delineation of the age-related dynamics of urinary IS production and the specific association of urinary IS with major bacterial contributors, namely the phylum Bacteroidota and the genus Bacteroides, within a cohort of healthy children. Establishing this physiological baseline, encompassing microbial sources and age-dependent variation in IS, provides crucial foundational knowledge. This knowledge is indispensable for accurately evaluating and interpreting the clinical significance of elevated IS levels reported in neurodevelopmental disorders, such as ASD.

Urinary IS/Cr ratios in the children declined with age and tended to stabilize at 5 years in this cohort. After correcting for this age-related variation, the urinary IS/Cr ratio emerged as a potential non-invasive surrogate marker reflecting the abundance of the phylum Bacteroidota and particularly the genus Bacteroides in the pediatric gut microbiota.
