Early Stopping for Regression Trees
Ratmir Miftachov, Markus Reiß

Abstract
We develop early stopping rules for growing regression tree estimators. The fully data-driven stopping rule is based on monitoring the global residual norm. The best-first search and the breadth-first search algorithms together with linear interpolation give rise to generalized projection or regularization flows. A general theory of early stopping is established. Oracle inequalities for the early-stopped regression tree are derived without any smoothness assumption on the regression function, assuming the original CART splitting rule, yet with a much broader scope. The remainder terms are of smaller order than the best achievable rates for Lipschitz functions in dimension d ⩾ 2 d\geqslant 2 . In real and synthetic data the early stopping regression tree estimators attain the statistical performance of cost-complexity pruning while significantly reducing computational costs.

Introduction

Classification and regression trees (CART) form a class of very popular statistical learning algorithms. Initially introduced by
Breiman et al. 1984
, CART serve as the foundation for more advanced machine learning techniques, including bagged decision trees
(
Breiman 1996
)
, random forests
(
Breiman 2001
)
, and, notably, boosted decision trees like AdaBoost
(
Freund and Schapire 1997
)
, XGBoost
(
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
, CatBoost
(
Dorogush, Ershov and Gulin 2018
)
, and LightGBM
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
. Remarkably, many of the winning solutions for tabular data competitions on Kaggle.com rely on advanced variations of Breiman’s decision tree algorithm. Because of their clear interpretability and their stability, practitioners still often prefer the basic CART methods to these more complex algorithms.

We focus on regression trees, which are nonparametric estimators constructed by an iterative and greedy data-fitting algorithm. Typically, iterative estimators require a data-adaptive choice of the iteration number to efficiently prevent from over- and underfitting. The current folklore in practice for decision trees is
post-pruning
, as initially proposed by
Breiman et al. 1984
. This bottom-up method involves growing the tree to its full depth, which interpolates the data, and subsequently cutting it back iteratively to an optimal subtree using cross-validation. Oracle-type inequalities on the pruned tree have been established by
Gey and Nedelec 2005
, and a general overview is given in
Mingers 1989
. In recent work on asymptotic properties, trees are generally grown to their maximum possible depth
(
Biau 2012
;
Scornet, Biau and Vert 2015
)
. Alternatively, the depth is controlled indirectly by limiting the number of observations in the terminal nodes
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
or growing the tree until a prespecified depth is reached
(
Klusowski and Tian 2024
)
. These techniques are commonly referred to as
pre-pruning
in the applied literature.

The current state of the art on tuning the decision tree depth, the post-pruning, is computationally very costly. We consider a fundamentally different approach in which the decision tree is grown iteratively and stopped early using an entirely data-driven stopping rule, usually far before the tree is fully grown. Unlike previous pre-pruning (or early stopping) methods, our stopping rule monitors the global residual norm, as for the discrepancy principle for inverse problems
(
Engl, Hanke and Neubauer 1996
)
and its statistical applications for linear estimators
(
Blanchard and Mathé 2012
)
.

Let us list our main contributions:

•

We interpret the regression tree iterates as a special instance of piecewise constant estimators on a sequence of refining partitions. Using linear interpolation between consecutive iterates, we put them in the framework of
generalized projection flows
. For this general concept, which also encompasses other popular smoothing estimators like ridge regression or gradient flow, we build a general theory of early stopping based on the residual norm. We derive an
\omega
-wise, that is non-probabilistic, error decomposition and an oracle inequality, comparing to the theoretically best estimator along the flow.

•

We specify the error terms for the breadth-first and best-first regression tree building, which refine partitions globally and locally, respectively. Given the globally defined residual norm, they give rise to the global and semi-global early stopping methods for trees. A main mathematical challenge is the bound for the cross term for the data-driven splitting in CART. The final oracle inequalities show that the error due to data-driven early stopping is typically smaller than the oracle error so that the early stopping estimator becomes statistically adaptive.

•

Based on a nearest-neighbour estimate of the noise level, we provide ready-to-use algorithms and apply them to standard data sets and in simulations. We implement our stopping algorithm in a Python library, see
Ziebell et al. 2025
. In practice, early stopping reduces the computational run time by a factor of 10 to 20 while being on par with the performance of the state-of-the-art pruning methods. The early stopped regression tree usually comes with a small number of nodes, thus leading to a sparse representation and good interpretability.

This work bridges the decision tree and random forest literature with the early stopping literature based on the discrepancy principle.
Recent advances in the iterative early stopping literature have evolved from statistical inverse problems such as
Blanchard, Hoffmann and Reiß 2018a
;
Blanchard, Hoffmann and Reiß 2018b
for linear estimators and
Hucker and Reiß 2024
for conjugate gradients.
Stankewitz 2024
has successfully applied the early stopping methodology for
L^{2}
-boosting in the setting of high-dimensional linear models. Our general framework unifies arguments from these papers and opens up an even wider scope of applications. Conceptually, our projection flow approach is inspired by the general theory for histogram regression estimators of
Nobel 1996
.

Further work on early stopping in nonparametric regression includes
Celisse and Wahl 2021
, which investigated kernelized spectral filter learning methods. Early stopping bounds for nonparametric regression with reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces have been studied in
Raskutti, Wainwright and Yu 2014
.

Consistency for the random forest under Breiman’s greedy splitting is proven by
Scornet, Biau and Vert 2015
under the additive model assumption; yet, convergence rates are not provided.
Chi et al. 2022
prove consistency for the original Breiman algorithm under further assumptions in a high-dimensional setting with a polynomial convergence rate in
n
. Subsequently, under the assumption of an additive model,
Klusowski and Tian 2024
achieve a logarithmic rate for the regression tree. They control the tree depth using a depth parameter, but do not provide data-driven guidelines for choosing the tree depth.

The literature has tried to reduce the splitting complexity by working with simplified versions of the regression tree.
For the purely uniform random forest,
Genuer 2012
provides a convergence rate of
n^{-2/3}
under Lipschitz conditions in dimension one. Further convergence rates are given in
Biau 2012
and
Arlot and Genuer 2014
for the centered forest under Hölder class assumptions for
d\geq 1
. Subsequently,
Klusowski 2021
improves the rate of
Biau 2012
for the centered forest, yet not attaining the minimax optimal rate for Lipschitz functions and
d>1
. Further work on the Mondrian forest achieves minimax optimality under appropriate tuning parameter selection as shown in
Mourtada, Gaïffas and Scornet 2020
and
Cattaneo, Klusowski and Underwood 2023
. Here, we show that the dominant term in our oracle inequality for the data-independent case is negligible with respect to these rates. In further research, the
\alpha
-fraction constraint was introduced in
Wager and Walther 2015
and utilized in consistency results of recent work in
Athey, Tibshirani and Wager 2019
,
Friedberg et al. 2020
and
Wager and Athey 2018
in ’honest trees’.
Cattaneo, Chandak and Klusowski 2024
develop a theory for oblique decision trees, where the splits are not axis-aligned.
Cattaneo, Klusowski and Underwood 2023
show that the Mondrian random forest is minimax optimal. An extensive overview of the convergence rates for various random forest versions is given in
Zhang, Ji and Bradic 2024
.

We proceed as follows. In Section
2
, we define the statistical setting and introduce the regression tree algorithm. Section
3
provides the theory in a unifying framework, beginning with refined orthogonal projections and proceeding to the generalized projection flows in Section
3.2
. First, Section
4
provides a general error decomposition and oracle inequality. Then, specifying to global and semi-global early stopping for regression trees, our main results, oracle inequalities under the original CART algorithm, are established in Section
4.4
.
In Section
5
, we elaborate on the implementation of our algorithms and apply them to standard datasets. In Section
6
, we compare the early-stopped estimator with post-pruning in simulations. Section
7
concludes the paper with a short discussion. Some further proofs and simulation results are delegated to the
Appendix
.

2
Regression trees

2.1
Regression model

Let the
d
-dimensional covariates be denoted as
X_{i}=(X_{i,1},\ldots,X_{i,d})^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with real-valued response variables
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We assume
(X_{i},Y_{i})\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}
for some distribution
\mathbb{P}
.
Consider the nonparametric regression setting

\displaystyle Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i

\displaystyle=1,\ldots,n,\text{ with }\E[\varepsilon_{i}\,|\,X_{i}]=0,\,\Var(\varepsilon_{i}\,|\,X_{i})=\sigma^{2}.

(2.1)

The target of estimation is the regression function
f(x)=\E[Y|X=x]
. We assume throughout that the design points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are a.s. distinct.
For functions
g:\R^{d}\to\R
we consider their empirical norm

\lVert g\rVert_{n}:=\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(X_{i})^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

the corresponding scalar product
\langle{\scriptstyle\bullet},{\scriptstyle\bullet}\rangle_{n}
and the associated
n
-dimensional function space
L_{n}^{2}
, obtained by identifying
g_{1},g_{2}:\R^{d}\to\R
with
\lVert g_{1}-g_{2}\rVert_{n}=0
, that is coinciding on the design. The vectors
(Y_{i})
,
(\varepsilon_{i})
can be lifted from
\R^{n}
to
L_{n}^{2}
via
Y(X_{i})=Y_{i}
,
\varepsilon(X_{i})=\varepsilon_{i}
and, similarly,
g\in L^{2}_{n}
is encoded by
(g(X_{i}))_{i=1,\ldots,n}\in\R^{n}
. In this sense,
L_{n}^{2}
is identified with
\R^{n}
.

2.2
Regression tree algorithm

We follow the classical algorithm introduced by
Breiman et al. 1984
, a greedy tree-growing procedure where the nodes identify subsets
A
of
\R^{d}
.
The CART algorithm starts with a parent node
A=\mathcal{X}
, recursively partitioned into non-overlapping
d
-dimensional (hyper-)rectangles. The partitioning begins by selecting a coordinate
x_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, and a threshold
c\in\R
, dividing
A=\mathcal{X}
into the (in coordinate
j
) left child node
A_{L}=\{x\in A:x_{j}<c\}
and the right child node
A_{R}=\{x\in A:x_{j}\geq c\}
. Here, all sets in a partition are required to contain at least one design point
X_{i}
, sets containing only one
X_{i}
are no longer split. The child nodes
A_{L},A_{R}
then serve as parent nodes for subsequent iterations, continuing the recursive partitioning process. At each instance, the
terminal nodes
(nodes without children) define a
partition
of
\R^{d}
, which is refined as the tree grows.

The CART algorithm is characterized by a data-driven splitting rule. Consider a generic parent node
A
and let
n_{A}
be its local sample size, that is the number of covariates
X_{i}
with
X_{i}\in A
. We define the splitting criterion or impurity gain as

\displaystyle L_{A}(j,c)=R^{2}(A)-\frac{n_{A_{L}}}{n_{A}}R^{2}(A_{L})-\frac{n_{A_{R}}}{n_{A}}R^{2}(A_{R}),

where the impurity for regression is set as the average squared residuals within a node (also called
impurity
)

\displaystyle R^{2}(A)=\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i:X_{i}\in A}\left(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}_{A}\right)^{2},

with
\bar{Y}_{A}=\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i:X_{i}\in A}Y_{i}
being the node average. The maximum impurity gain and the corresponding splitting rule are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{IG}(A)=\max_{(j,c)}L_{A}(j,c),\quad(j^{*},c^{*})\in\underset{(j,c)}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ L_{A}(j,c),

(2.2)

respectively. The regression tree is thus based on a greedy minimization of the training error in each split. Other splitting variants include splitting each node at the empirical median
(
Duroux and Scornet 2018
)
or a randomly chosen threshold
(
Genuer 2012
)
, among others.

Often, a stopping rule for the tree depth is applied to find a balance between underfitting and overfitting of the piecewise constant estimator based on the partition defined by the terminal nodes.
Common approaches that are referred to as pre-pruning (or early stopping) in the applied literature include growing the tree until it reaches a pre-specified depth
(
Klusowski 2021
)
or stopping the splitting process at a node when it contains a single observation, whichever occurs first (see, e.g.,
Klusowski and Tian 2024
). Another indirect way to control tree depth is by limiting the maximum number of observations allowed in the terminal nodes
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
. In contrast to these strategies,
Breiman et al. 1984
introduced cost-complexity post-pruning, a popular bottom-up approach that we use as a benchmark in our empirical analysis (see Section
5.2
). Note that also standard sequential model selection procedures like Lepski’s method start in the low bias situation and then gradually increase the bias, see e.g.
Lepski, Mammen and Spokoiny 1997
, and thus require a bottom-up approach in our tree framework.
In this work, we introduce early stopping based on the discrepancy principle, which fundamentally differs from the previous stopping methods. It is a fully data-adaptive top-down approach without relying on cross-validation.

There are at three common sequential ordering mechanisms to build a decision tree. This defines an order to which we apply the residual-based early stopping rule. Initially, the original CART algorithm is based on a depth-first search principle to grow the tree. However, this approach is statistically unsuitable for implementing our stopping condition, as Breiman’s tree grows each branch to a pre-specified depth. In contrast, we propose combining two distinct ordering mechanisms, the breadth-first search and the best-first search, with our data-driven stopping rule. We call these approaches
global
early stopping and
semi-global
early stopping, respectively.
In the global stopping procedure, all nodes at a given generation (i.e., level or depth) are split simultaneously before progressing to the next generation. On the other hand, the semi-global procedure splits one node at a time, selecting the next node to split based on the largest impurity gain,
\mathbb{IG}(A)
, across all partitions of the tree. Both stopping methods are illustrated in Figure
2.1
. The following chapter presents a unified mathematical framework for these two proposed stopping variants.

Fig 2.1:
Exemplary semi-global (left) and global (right) tree structure after three splitting iterations/generations. The global stopped tree relies on breadth-first search, whereas the semi-global stopped tree is based on best-first search. The generation/iteration is denoted by
g
, and the number of terminal nodes is denoted by
k
.

A unifying framework

3.1
Refining orthogonal projections

Let us consider general partitions
P=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\}
of
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\R^{d}
such that
\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}
, the
A_{k}
are pairwise disjoint and contain each at least one design point
X_{i}
, which requires
m\leqslant n
. Such a partition
P
generates the
m
-dimensional subspace

V_{P}=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\R\,|\,f|_{A_{k}}=\text{constant for all }k=1,\ldots,m\}\subseteq L_{n}^{2}

and the orthogonal projection
\Pi_{P}:L^{2}_{n}\to V_{P}
onto this subspace. Thus,

\Pi_{P}f(x)=\bar{f}_{A_{k}}:=\frac{1}{n_{A_{k}}}\sum_{i:X_{i}\in A_{k}}f(X_{i})\text{ for }x\in A_{k}

yields the average of
f(X_{i})
for the
X_{i}
in the set
A_{k}
containing
x
. As an orthogonal projection
\Pi_{P}
is selfadjoint, positive semi-definite and satisfies
\Pi_{P}^{2}=\Pi_{P}
,
\lVert\Pi_{P}\rVert=1
and
\trace(\Pi_{P})=m
. In principle,
f|_{A_{k}}
need not be a constant function for
k=1,\ldots,m
. We could also project, for example, on a linear function within the node as in the local linear forest
(
Friedberg et al. 2020
)
.

Now fix some integer dimensions
0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{G}=n
and
suppose that the sequence
P_{k}=\{A_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,A_{k}^{(k)}\}
,
k\in\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{G}\}
, of partitions is refining in the sense that each
A_{i}^{(k_{g+1})}
is contained in some
A_{j}^{(k_{g})}
, where
g=0,\ldots,G-1
. Write
V_{{k_{g}}}=V_{P_{k_{g}}}
and
\Pi_{{k_{g}}}=\Pi_{P_{k_{g}}}
for short and set
k_{0}=0
,
V_{k_{0}}=\{0\}
,
\Pi_{k_{0}}=0
. Then we have the inclusion
V_{{k_{g}}}\subseteq V_{{k_{g+1}}}
and thus
\Pi_{{k_{g}}}\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}=\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}\Pi_{{k_{g}}}=\Pi_{{k_{g}}}
for the projections. Moreover,
\Pi_{{k_{g}}}\preceq\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}
holds, in the sense that
\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}-\Pi_{{k_{g}}}
is positive semi-definite, because

\langle(\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}-\Pi_{{k_{g}}})f,f\rangle_{n}=\langle(\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}-\Pi_{{k_{g}}})^{2}f,f\rangle_{n}=\lVert(\Pi_{{k_{g+1}}}-\Pi_{{k_{g}}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}\geqslant 0.

Definition 3.1
.

For the orthogonal projections
\Pi_{k_{g}}
,

, consider the corresponding estimators and residuals (or in-sample training errors)

\hat{F}_{k_{g}}=\Pi_{k_{g}}Y,\quad R_{k_{g}}^{2}=\lVert Y-\hat{F}_{k_{g}}\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g}})Y\rVert_{n}^{2}.

Our non-interpolated stopping rule is given by

\hat{g}=\inf\{g\in\{0,\ldots,G\}\,|\,R_{k_{g}}^{2}\leq\kappa\}

for some threshold value

, and

.

3.2
Generalized projection flows

We generalize sequentially refining projections to a continuously parametrized projection-type family. The continuous parameter allows to balance between over- and underfitting more granularly for regression trees and avoids additional discretization errors in the analysis. This generalization is especially helpful for the global stopping rule where the partition size
k_{g}
grows geometrically with the generation
g
. Thus, the estimators’ performance from generations
g
to
g+1
might drastically change from underfitting to overfitting. This phenomenon also occurs for early stopping in the conjugate gradient algorithm
(
Hucker and Reiß 2024
)
and is referred to as
overshooting
.

Definition 3.2
.

A family of selfadjoint, positive semi-definite operators
\Pi_{t}:L_{n}^{2}\to L_{n}^{2}
,
t\in[0,n]
, is called a
generalized projection flow
or
regularization flow
if for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant n

\Pi_{s}\Pi_{t}=\Pi_{t}\Pi_{s},\;\Pi_{s}\preceq\Pi_{t},\;\lVert\Pi_{t}\rVert\leqslant 1,\;\trace(\Pi_{t})=t.

Here
\Pi_{s}\preceq\Pi_{t}
means that
\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s}
is positive semi-definite.

Note that
\Pi_{0}
is necessarily zero and
\Pi_{n}
is the identity on
L^{2}_{n}
due to
\trace(\Pi_{0})=0
,
\trace(\Pi_{n})=\dim(L_{n}^{2})
and
\lVert\Pi_{n}\rVert\leqslant 1
. Moreover,
\trace(\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s})\to 0
as
t\downarrow s
, together with
\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s}\succeq 0
, implies that
t\mapsto\Pi_{t}
is continuous in nuclear and thus in operator norm. To fully reflect the original CART algorithm, we explicitly allow
(\Pi_{t})
to depend on the data. This concept unifies the interpolated regression tree methods of this paper.

3.3 Example
.

For
k_{g}\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
and generations
g=0,\ldots,G
let orthogonal projections
\Pi_{k_{g}}
on nested
k_{g}
-dimensional subspaces be given. Assume
k_{0}=0
and
k_{G}=n
.
For
t\in[0,n)
we define the linearly interpolated projections

\Pi_{t}=(1-\alpha)\Pi_{k_{g(t)}}+\alpha\Pi_{k_{g(t)+1}},\text{ where }g(t)=\max\{g\,|\,k_{g}\leqslant t\},\,\alpha=\tfrac{t-k_{g(t)}}{k_{g(t)+1}-k_{g(t)}}\in[0,1).

Then
(\Pi_{t})_{t\in[0,n]}
forms a generalized projection flow.

For semi-global early stopping we have
G=n
and
k_{g}=g\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
. Then
\Pi_{t}=(1-\alpha)\Pi_{\lfloor t\rfloor}+\alpha\Pi_{\lfloor t\rfloor+1}
holds with
\alpha=t-\lfloor t\rfloor
,
t\in[0,n]
.
For global early stopping, we have
k_{0}=0
,
k_{1}=1
and then every generation at most doubles the dimension of the projection space so that
k_{g}<k_{g+1}\leqslant 2k_{g}
holds for
g\geqslant 1
. A priori, nothing more is known about the generalized projection flow
(\Pi_{t})_{t\in[0,n]}
obtained in this case. From a computational perspective, the interpolation comes at no additional cost since the required quantities are calculated in either case.

Although we focus on regression tree methods, the early stopping theory developed here applies in much more generality.
Let us discuss some prominent regularization methods in the scope of our framework.

3.4 Example
.

Consider the high-dimensional linear model where
f(x)=\langle\beta,x\rangle
for an unknown parameter
\beta\in\R^{d}
. Then the ridge estimator of
F=(f(X_{i}))_{i=1,\ldots,n}\in\R^{n}
is given by
\hat{F}_{\lambda}=S_{\lambda}Y
with the smoother matrix
S_{\lambda}=X(X^{\top}X+\lambda\Id)^{-1}X^{\top}
in terms of the design matrix
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{\top}\in\R^{n\times d}
and the ridge parameter
\lambda\geqslant 0
. Its effective dimension (or effective degrees of freedom) is given by
t(\lambda)=\trace(S_{\lambda})
. Setting
\Pi_{t}=S_{\lambda(t)}
for the inverse function
\lambda(t)
of
t(\lambda)
and
\Pi_{0}=S_{\infty}=0
we obtain a regularization flow. Formally, we need
\rank(X)=n
to ensure
\trace(S_{0})=n
, but otherwise, we can work on the lower-dimensional range of
X
instead of the full
\R^{n}
.

This approach also applies to other penalized least squares approaches; see Section 5.4.1 in
Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman 2017
for a general discussion and an application to smoothing splines. The aim of early stopping in this context is to choose the penalization parameter
\lambda
sequentially, starting from large values of
\lambda
and decreasing it gradually. Let us stress at this point that the intrinsic parametrization of
(\Pi_{t})
by
\trace(\Pi_{t})
is only for mathematical convenience.

3.5 Example
.

Gradient descent methods fit the paradigm of early stopping even better in view of their implicit regularization when stopping the iterations early. The standard gradient descent flow (sometimes called Showalter method) for the linear model is obtained by setting
\hat{\beta}_{0}=0
,
\frac{d}{ds}\hat{\beta}_{s}=-\nabla_{\beta}(\frac{1}{2}\lVert Y-X\beta_{s}\rVert^{2})
for
s\geqslant 0
. This yields
\hat{F}_{s}=X\hat{\beta}_{s}=G_{s}Y
with
G_{s}=X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}(\Id-e^{-sX^{\top}X})X^{\top}
. Introducing the effective dimension parametrization
t(s)=\trace(G_{s})=\trace(\Id-e^{-sX^{\top}X})
, we obtain the regularization flow
\Pi_{t}=G_{s(t)}
in terms of the inverse parametrization
s(t)
. Similarly, linear interpolation of discrete gradient descent steps gives rise to a regularization flow, compare to the Landweber method in
Blanchard, Hoffmann and Reiß 2018b
.

Often,
(\Pi_{t})
is also data-dependent, resulting in nonlinear methods. We refer to
Stankewitz 2024
for
L^{2}
-boosting and to
Hucker and Reiß 2024
for conjugate gradient flows.

In the spirit of the data-dependent histogram regression estimators in
Nobel 1996
, any generalized projection flow defines a flow of estimators to which we can apply the early stopping methodology.

Definition 3.6
.

For a generalized projection flow
(\Pi_{t})
, define the corresponding estimators and global residuals (or in-sample training errors)

\hat{F}_{t}=\Pi_{t}Y,\quad R_{t}^{2}=\lVert Y-\hat{F}_{t}\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})Y\rVert_{n}^{2}.

Lemma 3.7
.

The squared loss of
\hat{F}_{t}
decomposes in an approximation error, a stochastic error, and a cross term as

\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}-2\langle\Pi_{t}\varepsilon,(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rangle_{n}.

It satisfies the bound

\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}\leqslant\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}\Big)^{2}\leqslant 2\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+2\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}.

The approximation error
\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}
decreases continuously from
\lVert f\rVert_{n}^{2}
at
t=0
to zero at
t=n
. The stochastic error
\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}
increases continuously from zero at
t=0
to
\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}
at
t=n
.

By insertion of
Y=f+\varepsilon
we obtain

\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert\Pi_{t}(Y-f)-(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}-2\langle\Pi_{t}\varepsilon,(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rangle_{n}.

This is the claimed identity, and the upper bound follows by triangle inequality along the same lines.

The values of approximation and stochastic error at
t\in\{0,n\}
are obtained from
\Pi_{0}=0
and
\Pi_{n}=\Id
, respectively, while their continuity in
t
is a consequence of the continuity of
t\mapsto\Pi_{t}
. The monotonicity of
\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}
follows from
\Pi_{s}\preceq\Pi_{t}
and their commutativity via

\lVert\Pi_{s}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}=\langle\Pi_{s}(\Pi_{s}^{1/2}\varepsilon),\Pi_{s}^{1/2}\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\leqslant\langle\Pi_{t}(\Pi_{s}^{1/2}\varepsilon),\Pi_{s}^{1/2}\varepsilon\rangle_{n}=\langle\Pi_{s}(\Pi_{t}^{1/2}\varepsilon),\Pi_{t}^{1/2}\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\leqslant\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}.

The monotonicity argument for
\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}
is completely analogous.
∎

3.8 Remark
.

For
\Pi_{t}=(1-\alpha)\Pi_{k_{g(t)}}+\alpha\Pi_{k_{g(t)+1}}
as in Example
3.3
the cross term satisfies

\langle\Pi_{t}\varepsilon,(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rangle_{n}=\alpha(1-\alpha)\langle(\Pi_{k_{g(t)+1}}-\Pi_{k_{g(t)}})\varepsilon,f\rangle_{n},

which follows by simple algebra, see (
4.6
) below. In this case, it thus represents an interpolation error between projections, which for specific choices of
f
and
\varepsilon
might be relatively large (even
\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}=0
is possible for particular choices of
f,\varepsilon\not=0
). For deterministic (or independent)
\Pi_{t}
, however, its expectation is zero. Its standard deviation
\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\lVert(\Pi_{k_{g(t)+1}}-\Pi_{k_{g(t)}})f\rVert_{n}
is usually small, at least in the semi-global setting with
k_{g(t)+1}-k_{g(t)}=1
.

The following result is fundamental to base the early-stopping criterion on the residual.

Lemma 3.9
.

The residuals
R_{t}^{2}
are continuous and non-increasing in
t
from
R_{0}^{2}=\lVert Y\rVert_{n}^{2}
to
R_{n}^{2}=0
.
For
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant n
we have

\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-\hat{F}_{s}\rVert_{n}^{2}\leqslant R_{s}^{2}-R_{t}^{2}.

We calculate

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-\hat{F}_{s}\rVert_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{s})Y-(\Id-\Pi_{t})Y\rVert_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=R_{s}^{2}+R_{t}^{2}-2\langle(\Id-\Pi_{s})Y,(\Id-\Pi_{t})Y\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=R_{s}^{2}-R_{t}^{2}-2\langle(\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s})Y,(\Id-\Pi_{t})Y\rangle_{n}.

Since
\Pi_{t}
and
\Pi_{s}
commute and
\Pi_{s}\preceq\Pi_{t}
holds, the operators
\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s}
,
\Id-\Pi_{t}
are commuting, positive semi-definite and we obtain

\langle(\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s})Y,(\Id-\Pi_{t})Y\rangle_{n}=\langle(\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{s})((\Id-\Pi_{t})^{1/2}Y),(\Id-\Pi_{t})^{1/2}Y\rangle_{n}\geqslant 0.

Hence,
\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-\hat{F}_{s}\rVert_{n}^{2}\leqslant R_{s}^{2}-R_{t}^{2}
follows, in particular
R_{s}^{2}-R_{t}^{2}\geqslant 0
. The continuity of
t\mapsto R_{t}^{2}
follows from the continuity of
t\mapsto\Pi_{t}Y
.
∎

3.10 Remark
.

Note that for orthogonal projections
\Pi_{s}
,
\Pi_{t}
with
\Pi_{s}\preceq\Pi_{t}
we have
\Pi_{s}\Pi_{t}=\Pi_{t}\Pi_{s}=\Pi_{s}
, which implies
\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-\hat{F}_{s}\rVert_{n}^{2}=R_{s}^{2}-R_{t}^{2}
exactly.

4
Oracle inequalities

4.1
Error decomposition under early stopping

Definition 4.1
.

We introduce the
\omega
-wise
balanced oracle
(depending on
f
and
\varepsilon
)

\tau_{b}=\inf\{t\in[0,n]\,|\,\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}\leqslant\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}\}

and for
\kappa\geqslant 0
the data-dependent
early-stopping rule

\tau=\inf\{t\in[0,n]\,|\,R_{t}^{2}\leqslant\kappa\}.

By continuity and monotonicity of approximation and stochastic error, we see that
\tau_{b}
exists in
[0,n]
and the errors are balanced:
\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}=\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}
. Equally,
\tau\in[0,n]
exists and
R_{\tau}^{2}=\kappa
holds, provided
\kappa\leqslant R_{0}^{2}=\lVert Y\rVert_{n}^{2}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

At the balanced oracle, the loss satisfies the oracle-type property

\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau_{b}}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}\leqslant 2\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+2\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\leqslant 4\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big).

By the monotonicity properties of the error terms we have
\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}\geqslant\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}
for
t\leqslant\tau_{b}
and
\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}\geqslant\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}
for
t\geqslant\tau_{b}
. Consequently, for any
t\in[0,n]

\displaystyle\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\max\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2},\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2},\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)

holds. By Lemma
3.7
, the last line is larger than
\frac{1}{4}\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau_{b}}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}
, and the result follows by taking the infimum over all
t\in[0,n]
.
∎

We provide the main
\omega
-wise error decomposition for early stopping when compared to the balanced oracle as a benchmark.

Proposition 4.3
.

The distance between the early stopping estimator and the balanced oracle estimator is bounded as

\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau}-\hat{F}_{\tau_{b}}\rVert_{n}^{2}\leqslant\underbrace{\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert}_{\text{early stopping error}}+\underbrace{2\langle(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}}_{\text{interpolation error}}+\underbrace{2\lvert\langle(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\rvert}_{\text{cross term}}.

4.4 Remark
.

Ideally, the threshold
\kappa
in early stopping should equal the squared empirical norm of the noise
\varepsilon
. This is not accessible for the statistician and yields the main general error for the estimator obtained by early stopping. If we are to choose a deterministic threshold value
\kappa
in early stopping, then the natural value is
\kappa=\E[\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}
. More generally, we have

\E[\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert]\leqslant\E[\lvert\kappa-\sigma^{2}\rvert]+\E[\lvert\sigma^{2}-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert^{2}]^{1/2}\leqslant\E[\lvert\kappa-\sigma^{2}\rvert]+n^{-1/2}\Var(\varepsilon_{1}^{2})^{1/2}

and for
\kappa=\sigma^{2}
the early stopping error will still be of order
n^{-1/2}
, which represents an intrinsic information loss due to early stopping, compare the lower bound in
Blanchard, Hoffmann and Reiß 2018a
.

The interpolation error is due to the fact that
\Pi_{\tau_{b}}
is usually not a projection, in which case it vanishes. It must be analyzed for the concrete flow
(\Pi_{t})
under consideration, which is done for the semi-global and global regression tree algorithms in Lemma
4.7
below. The cross-term is challenging to control because both arguments of the scalar product are random with a complex dependency structure. Section
4.3
below is devoted to controlling this term.

For
\kappa\in[0,\lVert Y\rVert_{n}^{2}]
we have
R_{\tau}^{2}=\kappa
and we obtain by Lemma
3.9

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau}-\hat{F}_{\tau_{b}}\rVert_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lvert R_{\tau}^{2}-R_{\tau_{b}}^{2}\rvert

\displaystyle={\Bigl\lvert\kappa-\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})(f+\varepsilon)\rVert_{n}^{2}\Bigr\rvert}

\displaystyle={\Bigl\lvert\kappa-\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}-\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}-2\langle(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f,(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\Bigr\rvert}

\displaystyle={\Bigl\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}+2\langle(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}-2\langle(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\Bigr\rvert},

where
\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}
for the balanced oracle entered in the last line. For
\kappa>\lVert Y\rVert_{n}^{2}
we stop at
\tau=0
such that

0\leqslant R_{\tau}^{2}-R_{\tau_{b}}^{2}=\lVert Y\rVert_{n}^{2}-R_{\tau_{b}}^{2}<\kappa-R_{\tau_{b}}^{2}

holds. Hence, in this case, the first identity in the equation array above becomes an inequality, and the same upper bound holds. It remains to apply the triangle inequality.
∎

4.2
General oracle inequalities

We directly obtain a first oracle-type inequality in expectation under subgaussian noise. While the results could also be achieved with high probability, doing so would lead to more involved formulations.

Theorem 4.5
.

If the noise vector
\varepsilon
is
\bar{\sigma}
-subgaussian in the sense that
\E[\exp(\langle\lambda,\varepsilon\rangle_{n})]\leqslant\exp(\lVert\lambda\rVert_{n}^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}/(2n))
for all
\lambda\in\R^{n}
, then the interpolation error satisfies

\displaystyle 2\E\big[\langle(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant 16\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log(n)n^{-1}\E[\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})].

(4.1)

For the risk we
obtain the early-stopping oracle-type inequality

\displaystyle\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leqslant 9\E\Big[\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)\Big]+2\E[\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert]

\displaystyle\quad+32\bar{\sigma}^{2}\frac{\log(2n)}{n}\E[\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})]+4\E\big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\big].

(4.2)

Here and in the sequel, we apply the convention

\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}}:=0\text{ in case }(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f=0.

(4.3)

4.6 Remark
.

The interpolation error bound (
4.1
) depends on the underlying generalized projection flow. For semi-global early stopping, it will be negligible, but for global early stopping, it matters.
The numerical constants in (
4.2
) are explicit but could be optimized, depending on the size of the different terms. This also applies to all bounds obtained in the sequel.

Since all
\Pi_{t}
commute, there is one orthogonal matrix
O
, only depending on the projection flow, such that

D_{t}:=O\Pi_{t}O^{\top}\text{ is diagonal for all $t$.}

(4.4)

For the interpolation term, we introduce
\tilde{\varepsilon}=O\varepsilon
and obtain by diagonalization

\displaystyle\langle(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=\langle(D_{\tau_{b}}-D_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\tilde{\varepsilon},\tilde{\varepsilon}\rangle_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(D_{\tau_{b}}-D_{\tau_{b}}^{2})_{ii}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\trace(D_{\tau_{b}}-D_{\tau_{b}}^{2})n^{-1}\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}.

The Legendre transformation of
g(v)=e^{v}
is
g^{\ast}(u)=u\log(u)-u
, that is
uv\leqslant e^{v}+u\log(u)-u
holds for
u,v\geqslant 0
. For nonnegative random variables
U,V
with
\E[U\log(U)]<\infty
and
\E[e^{\alpha V}]<\infty
for some
\alpha>0
we insert
u=\frac{U}{\E[U]}
,
v=\alpha V-\log(\E[e^{\alpha V}])
and take expectations to infer

\alpha\E[UV]\leqslant\log(\E[e^{\alpha V}])\E[U]+\E[U\log(U)]-\log(\E[U])\E[U].

By the
\bar{\sigma}
-subgaussianity of
\varepsilon
also
\tilde{\varepsilon}=O\varepsilon
is
\bar{\sigma}
-subgaussian and we deduce that

\E\Big[\exp\Big(\alpha\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}/\bar{\sigma}^{2}\Big)\Big]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\big[\exp\big(\alpha\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}/\bar{\sigma}^{2}\big)\big]\leqslant 2n\text{ for }\alpha=1/8,

by
Boucheron, Lugosi and Massart 2013
, Eq. (2.4), and thus

\tfrac{1}{8}\E\Big[U\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{-2}\Big]\leqslant\log(2n)\E[U]+\E[U\log(U)]-\log(\E[U])\E[U].

For
U=\trace(D_{\tau_{b}}-D_{\tau_{b}}^{2})=\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})
, we always have
U\log(U)\leqslant\log(n)U
whence

\displaystyle\E[\langle(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\Big[n^{-1}\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant 16\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log(2n)n^{-1}\E[\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})]

and we arrive at (
4.1
).

To establish (
4.2
), we bound for any
\delta>0
, using
2AB\leqslant\delta A^{2}+\delta^{-1}B^{2}
,

2\E\big[\lvert\langle(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\rvert\big]\leqslant\delta\E\big[\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}\big]+\delta^{-1}\E\big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\big],

which holds in particular also under convention (
4.3
).
Therefore we find by triangle inequality and Lemma
4.2

\displaystyle\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau_{b}}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}]+2\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau}-\hat{F}_{\tau_{b}}\rVert_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leqslant(4+2\delta)\E[\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}]+4\E[\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}]+2\E[\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert]

\displaystyle\quad+32\bar{\sigma}^{2}\frac{\log(2n)}{n}\E[\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})]+2\delta^{-1}\E\big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\big].

\displaystyle\leqslant(8+2\delta)\E\Big[\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)\Big]+2\E[\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert]

\displaystyle\quad+32\bar{\sigma}^{2}\frac{\log(2n)}{n}\E[\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})]+2\delta^{-1}\E\big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\big].

Inequality (
4.2
) follows with the choice
\delta=1/2
.
∎

Next, we provide bounds for the interpolation error.

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider the generalized projection flow
(\Pi_{t})
from Example
3.3
for semi-global and global early stopping. Then the interpolation error satisfies

\displaystyle 32\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log(2n)n^{-1}\E[\trace(\Pi_{\tau_{b}}-\Pi_{\tau_{b}}^{2})]\leqslant\begin{cases}8\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log(2n)n^{-1},&\text{ semi-global,}\\
8\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log(2n)n^{-1}\E[\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\rVert_{HS}^{2}\vee 1],&\text{ global.}\end{cases}

4.8 Remark
.

Naturally, the global bound is larger than the semi-global.
The global bound is formulated in terms of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\rVert_{HS}
because for independent
\Pi_{t}
, the stochastic error satisfies

\E[\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}n^{-1}\E[\lVert\Pi_{t}\rVert_{HS}^{2}]\leqslant\frac{\sigma^{2}t}{n},

using
\lVert\Pi_{t}\rVert_{HS}^{2}\leqslant\trace(\Pi_{t})=t
. Standard entropy arguments, compare Remark 2 of
Klusowski and Tian 2024
and the proof of their Theorem 4.3, yield the following order for the dependent CART case and random
\tau_{b}

\E[\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}]\lesssim\frac{\sigma^{2}\E[\tau_{b}]\log^{2}(n)\log(dn)}{n},

(4.5)

where the factor
\log^{2}(n)
takes care of the possibly unbounded support of
\varepsilon_{i}
.
This upper bound for the stochastic error at the balanced oracle has a larger order than the bounds of Lemma
4.7
, suggesting that the interpolation error is asymptotically negligible.

In the semi-global setting of Example
3.3
, it suffices to note that

\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{t}^{2}=((1-\alpha)\Pi_{k}+\alpha\Pi_{k+1})-((1-\alpha^{2})\Pi_{k}+\alpha^{2}\Pi_{k+1})=\alpha(1-\alpha)(\Pi_{k+1}-\Pi_{k}),

(4.6)

implying
\sup_{t}\trace(\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{t}^{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
.

In the global case, we have

\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{t}^{2}=\alpha(1-\alpha)(\Pi_{k_{g(t)+1}}-\Pi_{k_{g(t)}})
and
k_{g(t)+1}\leqslant 2k_{g(t)}\vee 1
.
This gives

\trace(\Pi_{t}-\Pi_{t}^{2})=\alpha(1-\alpha)(k_{g(t)+1}-k_{g(t)})\leqslant(k_{g(t)}\vee 1)/4\leqslant(\trace(\Pi_{t}^{2})\vee 1)/4.

With
\lVert\Pi_{t}\rVert_{HS}^{2}=\trace(\Pi_{t}^{2})
the result follows.
∎

4.3
Bounding the cross term

The cross-term is much harder to control. First, we derive a bound in the advantageous setting where the generalized projection flow is independent of
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
, e.g. obtained from an independent sample.
This setting is similar to the centered forest initially introduced in a technical report of
Breiman 2004
, which simplifies the random forest. Further theoretical results under this assumption are developed by
Biau 2012
and
Klusowski 2021
. An analysis of early stopping for deterministic (or independent) projections is conducted in
Blanchard, Hoffmann and Reiß 2018a
but with respect to a deterministic oracle, which simplifies the treatment of the cross term. Here, the independent case serves mainly as a benchmark result.

Proposition 4.9
.

If the generalized projection flow
(\Pi_{t})
is independent of the observations
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
and
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
conditional on
X_{i}
, then

4\E\big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\big]\leqslant 16\frac{\sigma^{2}\log(\sqrt{2}n)}{n},

where convention (
4.3
) is in force.

See Appendix
Proof of Proposition
. ∎

4.10 Remark
.

As the proof reveals, we can generalize the preceding result to subgaussian noise vectors
\varepsilon
for which
\E[(O\varepsilon)_{k}(O\varepsilon)_{l}\,|\,(X_{i},(O\varepsilon)_{i}^{2})_{i=1,\ldots,n}]=0
holds for
k\not=l
and the orthogonal transformation
O
diagonalizing the flow
(\Pi_{t})
. If we require this property for all orthogonal transformations, however, it is not clear whether any interesting class of distributions beyond the Gaussian qualifies.

Now, we allow the projections
(\Pi_{t})
to be data-driven and to depend arbitrarily on the data
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
. This aligns with the standard CART splitting criterion, analyzed theoretically in
Scornet, Biau and Vert 2015
,
Chi et al. 2022
and
Klusowski and Tian 2024
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose that
\varepsilon
is
\bar{\sigma}
-subgaussian and that
the data-driven generalized projection flow
(\Pi_{t})
is generated by semi-global or global early stopping. Then the cross term satisfies (noting convention (
4.3
))

4\E\big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\big]\leqslant 16\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}\E[\tau_{b}+1]\log(dn)}{n}.

See Appendix
Proof of Proposition
. ∎

The proof relies on bounding the number of all possible projections after
k_{g}
splits, which is also the complexity measure underlying the results by
Nobel 1996
;
Klusowski and Tian 2024
and Theorem 1 of
Scornet, Biau and Vert 2015
. For the original data-driven splitting in CART, as described in Section
2.2
, there are at most
d(n-1)
different options for splitting in all
d
coordinate directions and at all interstices between the
n
data points. Thus,
(dn)^{k_{g}}
bounds the number of possible projections, which leads to the entropy-type bound
\E[\log((dn)^{\tau_{b}+1})]=\E[\tau_{b}+1]\log(dn)
in the proof.

Better bounds can be reached if we can restrict the number of possible partitions along the tree growth with high probability. For example, for additive regression functions in
s
covariates with
s
much smaller than
d
, Proposition 1 of
Scornet, Biau and Vert 2015
shows that asymptotically only splits in the
s
relevant coordinate directions occur. If this can be ensured, then the bound
\E[\tau_{b}+1]\log(sn)
in terms of the intrinsic dimension
s
applies.

The maximal deviation inequality in our argument does not profit from any correlation between
\langle(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}
for different projections
\Pi
. The problem is that for
\Pi,\Pi^{\prime}
, where
\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f-(\Id-\Pi^{\prime})^{2}f\rVert_{n}
is small, the intrinsic normalised distance
\lVert\frac{(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f\rVert_{n}}-\frac{(\Id-\Pi^{\prime})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi^{\prime})^{2}f\rVert_{n}}\rVert_{n}
might still be large because the approximation-type error
\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f\rVert_{n}
in the denominator is typically small.

Only in the case
d=1
, a partial summation (or integration by parts) argument for bounded variation functions
f
yields a useful cross-term bound of order
\bar{\sigma}^{2}n^{-1/2}
. This improves the bound of Proposition

for all
\tau_{b}\geqslant n^{1/2}
and is not larger than the typical order of the early stopping error. Unfortunately, the novel approach by
Klusowski and Tian 2024
for analyzing the impurity gain in additive models of bounded variation seems to give only suboptimal bounds for our cross-term. So, improvements in the case
d\geqslant 2
of interest remain open.

4.4
Main results for semi-global and global early stopping

We obtain the major theoretical results concerning early stopping for regression trees.

Theorem 4.12
(Semi-global early stopping, independent splitting).

If the projection flow
(\Pi_{t})
for semi-global early stopping is independent of
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
and
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
conditional on

, then
the risk of semi-global early stopping satisfies the oracle inequality

\displaystyle\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{\tau}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leqslant 9\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}]+2\E[\lvert\kappa-\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\rvert]+48\frac{\sigma^{2}\log(2n)}{n}.

4.13 Remark
.

As discussed in Remark

or larger and thus clearly dominates the last term in this bound. Typically, the oracle error will have a larger order than

is the best achievable minimax rate for estimating Lipschitz-continuous

and noting

already for

Usual convergence rate results for regression trees are even much slower than the nonparametric minimax rates and utilize modified splitting techniques; see, e.g.,
Genuer 2012

Biau 2012

Klusowski 2021
and the overview in
Zhang, Ji and Bradic 2024

Insert into the oracle inequality of Theorem

the semi-global interpolation error bound of Lemma

and the cross-term bound of Proposition

\displaystyle\E\Big[\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\Big(\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\E\Big[\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\inf_{t\in[0,n]}\E[\lVert\hat{F}_{t}-f\rVert_{n}^{2}],

by the independence of
\Pi_{t}
from noise and design.

\displaystyle\quad+8\frac{\sigma^{2}\log(2n)}{n}\big(\E[\lVert\Pi_{\tau_{b}}\rVert_{HS}^{2}\vee 1]+2\big).

4.18 Remark

We do not consider the out-of-sample prediction error here. The transfer of bounds from the empirical to the population prediction error depends on the regression tree algorithms and often requires some additional assumptions. General results are obtained by

5
Implementation and application to real data

In this section, we discuss the nearest neighbour estimator for
\sigma^{2}
, cost-complexity post-pruning and an improved two-step procedure. Then empirical results for standard data sets are presented and compared.

5.1
Noise level estimation

We want to choose the threshold
\kappa
close to the noise level
\lVert\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}
or its expectation
\sigma^{2}
.
To this end, nearest neighbour estimators of
\sigma^{2}
are simple and suitable for random design in higher dimensions. Under Lipschitz smoothness of the regression function
f
, they can achieve the rate
n^{-1/2}\vee n^{-2/d}

(
Devroye et al. 2013
)
, which is of smaller order than the minimax estimation rate
n^{-2/(2+d)}
for
f
.
Our implementation makes use of the nearest-neighbour estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}Y_{i}Y_{nn(i)},

(5.1)

proposed by
Devroye et al. 2018
and building up on results of
Biau and Devroye 2015
, where
X_{nn(i)}
is the nearest neighbour of
X_{i}
with respect to the Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Y_{nn(i)}
is the corresponding response.

In practice, the estimator
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
is biased upwards, which leads our stopping algorithms to terminate slightly earlier than intended. We can understand this in the case
\{X_{nn(i)}\rvert i=1,\ldots,n\}=\{X_{j}\rvert j=1,\ldots,n\}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}-\frac{2}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}Y_{i}Y_{nn(i)}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{nn(i)}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\E[\hat{\sigma}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E[(f(X_{i})-f(X_{nn(i)}))^{2}]\geqslant\sigma^{2}.

Here, we do not pursue further improvements of
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
, which nevertheless would have the potential to generate somewhat better results for early stopping in practice.

5.2
Cost-complexity pruning

The state-of-the-art approach for determining the structure of a regression tree is cost-complexity pruning with weakest-link pruning (also called post-pruning), as introduced by
Breiman et al. 1984
and discussed in
Zhou and Mentch 2023
. Oracle inequalities in a similar spirit to our work are developed by
Gey and Nedelec 2005
.

Let
T\preceq T_{n}
mean that
T
is a subtree of the fully grown tree
T_{n}
, which has
n
terminal nodes. The pruned subtree shares the same root node as the fully grown tree.
By Theorem 10.9 of
Breiman et al. 1984
, there exists a unique subtree that minimizes the risk function for a given cost-complexity hyperparameter
\lambda

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}=\argmin_{T\preceq T_{n}}\{R^{2}(T)+\lambda|T|\},

(5.2)

where
R^{2}(T)
denotes the residual norm of subtree
T
. After growing the tree to its full depth, the weakest-link pruning searches iteratively for the internal node to collapse, which increases
R^{2}(T)
the least. This procedure is repeated iteratively until only the root node is left. As a result, a sequence of increasing hyperparameters is obtained, denoted by
\Lambda_{\text{prun}}
.
This sequence of hyperparameters corresponds to a sequence of pruned subtrees with decreasing complexity, such that
\lambda=0
gives
T_{n}
, and a sufficiently large hyperparameter results in the root node. Each hyperparameter can be substituted into Equation
5.2
, giving a unique pruned regression tree.

In our simulation and empirical applications, the optimal hyperparameter
\lambda_{\text{opt}}\in\Lambda_{\text{prun}}
is selected using 5-fold cross-validation. The pruned regression tree estimator is then denoted as
\hat{F}_{\text{prun}}=\hat{F}(\lambda_{\text{opt}})
, where the simplified notation
\hat{F}_{\text{prun}}
is used subsequently. The oracle is determined by selecting the pruned subtree that minimizes the error on a test set, denoted by subscript
n^{\prime}
. We use the relative efficiency as a performance measure, which quantifies the performance of the pruned estimator relative to its oracle, defined as
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{prun}}\|\hat{F}({\lambda})-f\|_{n^{\prime}}/\|\hat{F}_{\text{prun}}-f\|_{n^{\prime}}
.

5.3
A two-step procedure

We propose a two-step procedure by combining the advantages of a first step, a top-down early-stopping approach, and a second step, a bottom-up pruning approach. The procedure inherits the computational advantage of the early stopping rule in the first step, while the bottom-up nature of the second pruning step allows more flexibility in building the final regression tree by using less biased data. In simple truncated series estimation, a two-step approach combining early stopping with AIC-like model selection was analyzed in
Blanchard, Hoffmann and Reiß 2018a
. A similar approach for boosting has been taken by
Stankewitz 2024
, and in both cases, the practical performance is convincing.

The procedure is described in Algorithm
5.1
. We consider the global early stopping at generation
\hat{g}
, according to Definition
3.1
with threshold
\kappa
. To further reduce the risk of potential underfitting, we consider the tree obtained at generation
\hat{g}+1
as a starting point for cost-complexity pruning. This is similar to
Stankewitz 2024
, where the threshold is reduced to
0.9\kappa
. The sequence of pruning parameters
\Lambda_{\text{2step}}
is different since we do not use the fully grown tree
T_{n}
, as in the previous section. Further, the number of potential hyperparameters to consider is substantially smaller after stopping early in the first step, such that we do not need to consider the full path of pruning hyperparameters as in Section
5.2
.

The optimal tuning parameter
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{opt}}\in\Lambda_{\text{2step}}
is determined by 5-fold cross-validation. Note that both hyperparameter choices
\lambda_{\text{opt}}
(pruning) and
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{opt}}
(two-step) are not guaranteed to be equal. Our empirical results indicate, however, that the two-step estimator is often quite similar to the pruned estimator from Section
5.2
. The analysis of this two-step procedure is challenging because of the intricate dependency between the first and second-stage results. We leave the theory for future research. Similar to the pruned estimator, we define the relative efficiency of the two-step estimator as

\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{2step}}\|\hat{F}(\lambda)-f\|_{n^{\prime}}/\|\hat{F}_{\text{2step}}-f\|_{n^{\prime}}

, denoted as
\Lambda_{2step}

Determine the optimal hyperparameter
\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{opt}}\in\Lambda_{2step}

7
Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel, data-driven stopping rule for regression trees based on the discrepancy principle, offering a computationally efficient alternative to existing post-pruning and pre-pruning methods. Clear theoretical guarantees in terms of oracle inequalities are provided to understand how far early stopping is away from the oracle estimator along the tree growth. The generalized projection flow framework further unifies and widens the scope of the proposed early stopping methodology. The oracle errors along breadth-first (global), best-first (semi-global) and pruning-type tree building are still the subject of intense current research, depending on specific splitting criteria and function classes. Also given our comparison of the oracle errors among the different methods, progress in the error analysis is highly desirable and would immediately give rise to complete risk bounds for early stopping. First empirical results are quite convincing, and given the simplicity of the early stopping methods, wider practical experience is clearly within reach.

Proof of Proposition
4.9

By definition,
\tau_{b}
only depends on
(\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2})_{t\in[0,n]}
. In view of the diagonalisation
\Pi_{t}=O^{\top}D_{t}O
from (
4.4
),
\lVert\Pi_{t}\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert D_{t}O\varepsilon\rVert_{n}^{2}
only depends on
(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
with
\tilde{\varepsilon}:=O\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}\Id)
and on the design
(X_{i})
, treating
\Pi_{t}
and thus
D_{t},O
as fixed by independence. This shows for the conditional expectation

\displaystyle\E\big[\langle(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\,

\displaystyle\big|\,(X_{i},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2})_{i=1,\ldots,n}\big]=\E\big[\langle(\Id-D_{\tau_{b}})^{2}Of,\tilde{\varepsilon}\rangle_{n}^{2}\,\big|\,(X_{i},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2})_{i=1,\ldots,n}\big]

\displaystyle=\E\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}((\Id-D_{\tau_{b}})^{2}Of)(X_{i})\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\Big)^{2}\,\Big|\,(X_{i},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2})_{i=1,\ldots,n}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}((\Id-D_{\tau_{b}})^{2}Of)(X_{i})^{2}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}.

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert(\Id-D_{\tau_{b}})^{2}Of\rVert_{n}^{2}n^{-1}\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2},

where we used
\E[\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\,|\,(X_{i},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2})_{i=1,\ldots,n}]=0
for
i\not=j
due to
\tilde{\varepsilon}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}\Id)
conditional on the design. Noting
\lVert(\Id-D_{\tau_{b}})^{2}Of\rVert_{n}^{2}=\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f\rVert_{n}^{2}
, this shows for the full expectation

\E\Big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\Big]\leqslant n^{-1}\E\Big[\tfrac{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f\rVert_{n}^{2}}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}}\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}\Big]\leqslant n^{-1}\E\Big[\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}\Big].

By a standard Gaussian maximal inequality
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
, Corollary 1.3, we have
\E[\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}]\leqslant 4\sigma^{2}\log(\sqrt{2}n)
and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.11

The proof relies on a deviation inequality for maxima of normalized quadratic functionals and a complexity bound for the number of possible projections until time
t
. This is inspired by
Hucker and Reiß 2024
, but the argument differs.

By the linear interpolation property
\Pi_{\tau_{b}}=\alpha\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}}+(1-\alpha)\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}}
holds, where
\tau_{b}=(1-\alpha)k_{g(\tau_{b})}+\alpha k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
, see Example
3.3
. Simple algebra shows

(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}=(1-\alpha)^{2}(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})+(1-(1-\alpha)^{2})(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}}).

This yields

\displaystyle\lvert\langle(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\alpha)^{2}\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f\rVert_{n}{\Bigl\lvert\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\Bigr\rvert}

\displaystyle\quad+(1-(1-\alpha)^{2})\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f\rVert_{n}{\Bigl\lvert\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}\Bigr\rvert}

as well as

\displaystyle\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{t})f\rVert_{n}

\displaystyle=\langle(\Id-\Pi_{t})^{2}f,f\rangle_{n}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Big((1-\alpha)^{2}\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}+(1-(1-\alpha)^{2})\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f\rVert_{n}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\alpha)^{2}\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f\rVert_{n}+(1-(1-\alpha)^{2})\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f\rVert_{n},

where Jensen’s inequality was used in the last step. We deduce

\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\leqslant\max\Big(\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2},\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\Big).

Since
\Pi_{\tau_{b}}
and
\varepsilon
have an almost arbitrary dependence structure, we bound the expectation by the maximum over all possible splits leading to
\Pi_{\tau_{b}}
:

\E\Big[\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})^{2}f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi_{\tau_{b}})f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\Big]\leqslant\E\Big[\max_{\trace(\Pi)\in\{k_{g(\tau_{b})},k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}\}}\langle\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi)f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi)f\rVert_{n}},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}\Big],

where the maximum is taken over all deterministic projections
\Pi
reachable by data-driven
k_{g(\tau_{b})}
-fold or
k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}
-fold global and semi-global splitting, respectively.

We must bound the expectation of this maximum. Since
v:=\tfrac{(\Id-\Pi)f}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi)f\rVert_{n}}
is a unit vector, we get without the maximum
\E[\langle v,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}n^{-1}
. For
M_{g}:=\#\{\Pi:\trace(\Pi)=k_{g}\}
, the cardinality of the maximisation set at a deterministic generation
g
, we consider

\max_{m=1,\ldots,M_{g}}\langle v_{m},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}
generally for any deterministic unit vectors
v_{m}
.
By
\bar{\sigma}
-subgaussianity we have

\PP\Big(\max_{m=1,\ldots,M_{g}}\langle v_{m},\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}>\tfrac{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{n}(\log(M_{g})+u)\Big)\leqslant e^{-u},\quad u\geqslant 0.

Let us determine an upper bound for
\log(M_{g})
. Projections
\Pi
with
\trace(\Pi)=k_{g}
are obtained after
k_{g}
splits.
At each split, we have at most
d(n-1)
different thresholds for splitting in each of the
d
directions and at all interstices between the
n
data points. Therefore
M_{g}\leqslant(dn)^{k_{g}}
,
\log(M_{g})\leqslant k_{g}\log(dn)
holds, which does not seem a too rough upper bound at the logarithmic level. We conclude

\PP\Big(\max_{\trace(\Pi)=k_{g}}\frac{n\langle(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}}{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f\rVert_{n}^{2}}-k_{g}\log(dn)>u\Big)\leqslant e^{-u},\quad u\geqslant 0.

Bonferroni’s inequality and
\sum_{g=0}^{G-1}e^{-(u+\log(G))}=e^{-u}
yield

\PP\Big(\max_{g=0,\ldots,G-1}\Big(\max_{\trace(\Pi)=k_{g}}\frac{n\langle(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}}{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f\rVert_{n}^{2}}-k_{g}\log(dn)\Big)-\log(G)>u\Big)\leqslant e^{-u}.

Integrating this bound over
u\in[0,\infty)
gives

\E\Big[\max_{g=0,\ldots,G}\max_{\trace(\Pi)=k_{g}}\Big(\frac{n\langle(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}}{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f\rVert_{n}^{2}}-k_{g}\log(dn)\Big)\Big]\leqslant\log(G)+1=\log(eG),

noting
(\Id-\Pi)f=0
for
\trace(\Pi)=k_{G}=n
, i.e.
\Pi=\Id
, such that the maximum trivially extends to
g=G
.
The uniformity over
g
then shows

\E\Big[\max_{\trace(\Pi)\in\{k_{g(\tau_{b})},k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}\}}\frac{\langle(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f,\varepsilon\rangle_{n}^{2}}{\lVert(\Id-\Pi)^{2}f\rVert_{n}^{2}}\Big]\leqslant\frac{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{n}\Big(\E[k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}]\log(dn)+\log(eG)\Big).

For both semi-global and global early stopping, we have
G\leqslant n
and
k_{g(\tau_{b})+1}\leqslant 2\tau_{b}\vee 1
such that
the result follows by simplifying the numerical constants.
∎
