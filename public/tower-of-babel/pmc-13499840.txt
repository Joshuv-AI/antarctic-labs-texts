A Real-World Target Trial Emulation of Eteplirsen, Casimersen, and Golodirsen to Evaluate Survival Among Patients with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy
Authors: Sai Dharmarajan, Shannon Grabich, Richard Baxter, Aalok Nadkar, Carol Schermer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionReal-world data are needed to understand the treatment effect of eteplirsen, casimersen, and golodirsen (phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers [PMOs]) on all-cause mortality in patients with DMD.MethodsThe study objectives were to describe the unadjusted time to death from treatment start and from birth, assess PMO treatment’s impact on survival, and describe the frequency of comorbidities prior to death. Sequential target trial emulation with inverse probability of treatment and censoring weighting were used to compare survival between patients who received glucocorticoids (GCs) and eteplirsen, casimersen, or golodirsen (PMO + GCs) or GCs alone (GCs-only). Inovalon closed claims data (January 2016–September 2024) were tokenized and linked with Datavant mortality data.ResultsIn the PMO + GCs group, 208/372 (56%) received eteplirsen, 123/372 (33%) casimersen, and 41/372 (11%) golodirsen. Twelve of 372 patients (3%) died after treatment initiation with PMO + GCs versus 114/2656 (4%) for GCs-only without adjustment for confounding or length of follow-up. In the adjusted target trial emulation analysis, 112 patients initiated PMOs and 2916 initiated GCs-only treatment in the first trial; median follow-up time was 4.6 and 4.8 years, respectively. In sequential trials, 260/2916 patients originally in the GCs-only group initiated treatment with PMOs. While the mortality rate in both groups was low, data from 48 sequentially emulated trials estimated PMO + GCs reduced mortality risk versus GCs-only (hazard ratio 0.303; 95% confidence interval 0.119–0.769; P = 0.012). In patients from either group with claims in the 3 months preceding death (n = 84), more than 75% had circulatory/respiratory claims.ConclusionThe relative risk reduction in mortality suggests a promising treatment effect of PMO + GCs versus GCs-only, which should be confirmed in future studies with longer follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03609-0.Keywords: Casimersen, Causal inference, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Eteplirsen, Golodirsen, Phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer, Real-world data, Survival, Target trial emulation

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Exon-skipping phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs) are approved therapies for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) in the USA, but their long-term impact on patient survival in a real-world setting requires further evaluation.This real-world study used causal inference methods to compare all-cause mortality between insured US patients with DMD treated in clinical practice with the PMOs eteplirsen, casimersen, or golodirsen paired with glucocorticoids (GCs) or with GCs alone. What was learned from the study? Target trial emulation indicated that the mortality rate in patients with DMD being treated with PMOs was lower than in patients receiving GCs alone during the observation period.This is the first study evaluating mortality in patients treated with eteplirsen, casimersen, or golodirsen using a robust confounding control with findings that corroborate previously published results on patients treated with eteplirsen.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a rare, fatal, X-linked degenerative neuromuscular disease with an estimated pooled global prevalence of 7.1 cases per 100,000 male individuals [1]. Pathogenic variants of the DMD gene disrupt the production of functional dystrophin protein, resulting in progressive damage and degeneration of muscle fibers [2]. Although muscle damage in DMD is present at birth, patients with DMD typically begin to show clinical signs of progressive muscle weakening during childhood [3]. Loss of ambulation typically occurs in early- to mid-adolescence [3–5], followed by decline of respiratory function and cardiomyopathy in mid- to late-adolescence [5–7], and ultimately, premature death as the patients reach their late twenties [6, 8–10].

Life expectancy for patients with DMD has increased over time, attributed at least partially to the broad use of glucocorticoids (GCs) [6, 8]. GCs have been shown to delay loss of ambulation, preserve cardiac and pulmonary function, and improve survival in patients with DMD [5, 9, 11–13]. However, GC use is often associated with serious long-term adverse events, such as decreased height, increased weight, and potentially increased number of bone fractures [13, 14].

Phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomers (PMOs) are designed to address the underlying cause of DMD pathology by skipping target exons, restoring the reading frame, and resulting in a truncated dystrophin protein that retains key functional domains [15, 16]. To date, four PMOs have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of DMD for patients with confirmed mutations amenable to skipping exon 51 (eteplirsen, approx. 14% of DMD population; approved in 2016), exon 53 (golodirsen and viltolarsen; approved in 2019 and 2020, respectively, approx. 8% of DMD population), and exon 45 (casimersen, approx. 8% of DMD population; approved in 2021) [17]. PMOs have a favorable safety profile and slow disease progression, including delaying deterioration of ambulatory, cardiac, and respiratory function compared with external controls [18–23].

In addition to assessing clinical trial data, there is a need to evaluate the impact of PMOs on patient survival in a larger patient population and one outside of the clinical trial setting. Real-world data (RWD) can complement trial evidence by providing insights from larger cohorts in routine settings, with added strengths of generalizability and long-term observation periods [24, 25].

To address this gap, this study sought to evaluate the effect of treatment with eteplirsen, casimersen, and golodirsen on survival of patients with DMD who received care in a real-world setting. Given the relatively recent approval of PMOs and the rarity of DMD, a limited number of mortality events would be expected to be observed among the patients receiving PMO treatment to date. In order to maintain statistical rigor, causal inference methods that can minimize bias were employed to assess survival.

Methods

Data Source and Tokenization

Retrospective claims data from insured patients in the USA were obtained from the Inovalon closed claims database (January 2016–September 2024), which contains insurance claims from all 50 US states, Washington DC, and Puerto Rico, including patients with Medicaid, Medicare, and commercial insurance coverage. Survival status was ascertained using the Datavant mortality file (January 2019–October 2024), a comprehensive database constructed using a validated matching process to integrate obituary data with the Social Security Death Master File [26, 27].

A multistep process was used to translate patients’ identities to anonymous transit tokens for each patient in both databases, enabling linkage of patient data across the two sources while maintaining anonymity (see Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Material for details). Datavant’s Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant de-identification technology converts personally identifiable information into encrypted, irreversible site-specific tokens, allowing for accurate record linkage without the exchange of direct identifiers [27]. Linkage was performed in the Real World Evidence Data Platform Environment. The linked database went through a certification process by third-party statisticians to confirm de-identification per the HIPAA Expert Determination method (Title 45 §164.514(b) (1)) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule.

Study Population

Patients had to be of male sex, successfully linked across databases, and either meet DMD diagnostic criteria from a validated claims-based algorithm or have a record of receiving DMD-specific treatment [28]. The DMD identification algorithm included requirements, such as first claim with DMD or Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis code at age ≤ 40 and record of ≥ 2 claims with DMD/BMD ICD-10 diagnosis code (Fig. S2). Further enrollment criteria included evidence of treatment with oral or intravenous GCs (betamethasone, budesonide, cortisone, deflazacort, dexamethasone, hydrocortisone, methylprednisolone, prednisolone, prednisone, or triamcinolone) and a valid index date (T0; time zero [defined as the date of first observed continuous GC use ≥ 30 days with a ≤ 30-day allowable gap, or incident PMO]) (Fig. 1). Patients with evidence of treatment with DMD gene therapy or viltolarsen prior to or on T0 were excluded from the main analysis. In order to capture data from all commercially available PMOs, a supplemental analysis, including viltolarsen in the PMO + GCs group was conducted using the same sequential target trial emulation (TTE) methodology described below.

Patients were grouped into the PMO + GCs or GCs-only group based on whether they were treated with PMOs during the observation period. For the unadjusted analysis, patients who were treated with PMOs at any point in time were part of the PMO + GCs group. This analysis compared the unadjusted mortality rate in patients treated with PMOs at any point to those who only received GCs during the observation period without adjusting for the duration of GCs before initiating PMO therapy or other key baseline variables. An analysis such as this is susceptible to biases such as confounding and immortal time bias [29]. To account for these biases, a sequential TTE design was used, where patients were initially allocated to the PMO + GCs and GCs-only groups based on the criteria they met at T0 [30]. Then, during the observation period, if a patient who was initially in the GCs-only group initiated PMO treatment, their membership was changed to the PMO + GCs group from that point on (Fig. S3). Thus, patients treated with PMO contributed event time to the PMO + GCs group only after they had initiated PMOs, effectively mitigating immortal time bias. Further, adjustment for baseline risk factors, as described below, controlled for confounding.

Study Objectives

The study objectives were to describe the unadjusted time to death from treatment start and from birth in the PMO + GCs and GCs-only groups, as well as to assess the effect of eteplirsen, casimersen, and golodirsen on survival by comparing the PMO + GCs and GCs-only groups using TTE. Additionally, in order to capture the progression of their DMD, comorbidities occurring in the 3-month period preceding death among patients who died were described. Per protocol, objectives were to be evaluated for each PMO (stratified analysis) if the number of deaths exceeded five for each therapy.

This study was performed in accordance with ethical principles consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice. Institutional review board (IRB) exemption was sought from Advarra IRB and received 13 November 2023. Advarra IRB is registered with the Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP) and FDA under IRB#00000971. Data were de-identified and compliant with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations thus meeting determination for exemption.

Sequential TTE Design

To enable causal inference, a TTE framework was used to compare the effectiveness of treatment with PMO + GCs versus GCs-only on survival [31]. After defining each patient’s T0 as their earliest qualifying index date, a sequential trial approach was implemented. This approach enabled inclusion of patients who initiated PMO treatment after their initial entry into the GCs-only group while avoiding immortal time bias [32]. The sequential trial approach emulates a series or sequence of auxiliary trials. The first trial compared all patients who initiated GCs-only at their T0 to all patients who had initiated PMO at T0. After T0, an auxiliary trial was emulated each month in which at least one patient from the GCs-only group initiated PMO therapy (Fig. S3). These patients were censored from the GCs-only group in the original trial and entered the PMO + GCs group in this new auxiliary trial, where they were compared with all the patients who remained in GCs-only arm. In this study design, patients in the GCs-only group could contribute to multiple auxiliary trials, but patients in the PMO + GCs group only contributed to one trial after PMO initiation. Follow-up for all patients in each trial continued until death or censoring. Treatment assignment followed an intention-to-treat principle, where patients remained in their assigned group (PMO + GCs or GCs-only) for analysis regardless of subsequent treatment discontinuation or non-adherence. Censorship was defined as the first of end of continuous enrollment, end of observation period, presence of viltolarsen, or presence of DMD gene therapy.

This framework ensured a comparison of patients initiating PMOs with a concurrent control group of patients treated with GCs-only with a similar duration of GC use.

Statistical Analyses

Prior to any comparison or adjustment, the unadjusted number of deaths and times to death among patients who received PMO + GCs at any point during the observation time and patients who received GCs only were described using descriptive statistics and Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves. The sequential TTE analysis was adjusted for confounding using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to balance the PMO + GCs and GCs-only groups across key baseline prognostic covariates. Covariates included age, birth year, GC treatment duration, prior 12-month cardiac medication use, ventilation use, comorbidity burden (defined by pediatric complex chronic conditions V3 [PCCC] and pediatric comorbidity index), cardiovascular disease, kidney conditions, diabetes mellitus, and transplant. Adjustment for informative censoring and correlation because of repeated participation across trials was carried out using inverse probability of censoring weights and use of robust standard errors. The final weight applied was a product of the inverse probability of treatment weighting and inverse probability of censoring weights for each patient-trial unit in the sequential TTE analysis to create a pseudo-population in which treatment assignment and censoring were independent of measured covariates [33]. Covariate balance after weighting was checked using standardized mean differences (SMDs). Data from multiple sequentially emulated trials were then pooled and summarized using weighted KM curves. The survival hazard ratio (HR) for the effectiveness of PMO + GCs versus GCs-only treatment was estimated using a weighted pooled logistic regression with robust standard errors clustered by patient to account for repeated participation across trials. No imputation of missing data was performed.

Results

Study Population

A total of 3028 unique patients met the enrollment criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). At T0, there were 112 patients in the PMO + GCs group and 2916 in the GCs-only group (Table 1). Patients across both groups had similar age at T0, at first treatment with GCs, and at first record of ICD-10 G71.01 diagnosis (Table 1). The median follow-up time was 4.8 and 4.6 years for patients starting in the GCs-only group and in the PMO + GCs group, respectively (Table 1). Across the full study timeframe, 372 patients were treated with PMO + GCs, including 260 patients who started in the GCs-only group at T0 and moved to the PMO + GCs group after initiating PMO therapy in subsequent auxiliary trials (Table 2). The most commonly used PMO was eteplirsen (n = 208, 56%), followed by casimersen (n = 123, 33%), and golodirsen (n = 41, 11%; Table 2). A supplemental analysis incorporated six patients treated with viltolarsen to the PMO + GCs group; baseline characteristics for both cohorts were similar to the main analysis.

Unadjusted Time to Death

In the analysis unadjusted for confounding or length of follow-up, a total of 12/372 (3%) patients who received a PMO at any point in the study and 114/2656 (4%) patients treated with GCs only died (Table 2). While a prespecified objective was to describe the unadjusted time to death, summary statistics such as median time to death from treatment start and from birth could not be calculated due to an insufficient number of events and accumulated time (Fig. S4).

Sequential TTE

Treatment arms for the sequential TTE analysis were well balanced for key covariates after weighting. Most SMDs were less than or equal to 0.1, excluding Renal PCCC with an SMD of 0.11. (Fig. S5). Data from the first 48 emulated trials were pooled to enable survival HR calculation, which yielded 0.303 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.119–0.769; P = 0.012) (Fig. 2). HRs of survival calculated for 54 (HR 0.373; 95% CI 0.167–0.834; P = 0.016), 60 (HR 0.397; 95% CI 0.173–0.909; P = 0.029), and 66 (HR 0.477; 95% CI 0.194–1.169; P = 0.105) pooled trials showed a decreased effect on survival as more trials were added to the analysis (Fig. 2). The HR calculations indicated that the relative mortality risk for the PMO + GCs group was 69.7%, 62.7%, 60.3%, and 52.3% lower than in the GCs-only group in calculations with 48, 54, 60, and 66 pooled trials, respectively. Pooling of trials was stopped at 66 trials, at which point 94% of patients who received PMOs had been included. A supplemental analysis including six additional patients who were treated with viltolarsen in the PMO + GCs group was conducted using the same methods and produced similar results for 48 pooled trials: HR 0.310 (95% CI 0.122–0.788, P = 0.014).

Comorbidities Experienced by Patients Who Died

Of the 126 patients who died during the observation period, 84 had claims data available to assess comorbidities during the 3 months preceding death. Most of these patients had comorbidity claims regarding the circulatory (71/84 [85%]), respiratory (64/84 [76%]), endocrine (56/84 [67%]), musculoskeletal (51/84 [61%]), and digestive systems (48/84 [57%]) in the 3 months preceding death (Table 3). The five most common ICD-10 codes observed were calcification of the lungs (39/84 [46%]), obstructive sleep apnea (37/84 [44%]), cardiac arrest of cause unspecified (35/84 [42%]), rapid heartbeat (34/84 [41%]), and acute respiratory failure with hypoxia (30/84 [36%]).

Discussion

This study reports all-cause survival from a large US claims cohort using sequential TTE designed for evaluation of causal treatment effects. Data tokenization and causal inference methods enabled an RWD analysis showing that treatment with eteplirsen, casimersen, or golodirsen in combination with GCs may prolong survival compared with treatment with GCs-only.

The results of this study are comparable to previously published literature that demonstrated a 35% reduction in mortality in patients treated with eteplirsen compared with age-matched controls using a Cox proportional hazards model [34]. The present study further expands on previous findings by controlling for confounding factors such as age, ventilation use, and comorbidity burden. Furthermore, this study required all patients to be treated with GCs, providing a clinically relevant comparison given the effect of GCs on prolonging survival in patients with DMD [6, 8]. Most of the patients in the PMO + GCs group in this study were treated with eteplirsen. This was expected because DMD pathogenic variants amenable to exon 51 skipping are more common than those amenable to exon 53 or 45 skipping, and because eteplirsen has been commercially available for longer than golodirsen and casimersen [17]. Additionally, over 75% of patients who died had circulatory or respiratory claims prior to death, which is consistent with prior evidence showing most patients with DMD succumb to cardiac or respiratory failure in their 20s [10].

Studies with RWD can be an important complement to clinical trial data. Clinical trials in rare, slowly progressive diseases tend to have insufficient duration to examine survival [35]. RWD studies should be considered to be hypothesis-generating and be used to support findings from clinical trials, as they have inherent challenges such as selection and immortal time bias, which can compromise the validity of causal conclusions [29, 36]. Robust methodologies such as TTE and inverse probability weighting can be employed to mitigate these biases and enable causal inference by mimicking randomized controlled trials [33, 36, 37]. In sequential trial methods, auxiliary trials initiated later in the study tend to have smaller samples sizes, making it more difficult to balance key confounders. Lack of balance in key covariates, lower sample sizes, and the inherently shorter observation periods of later trials make the estimates of the survival benefit potentially less reliable and less statistically efficient. The reduction in efficiency is evident from the increasing width of the confidence intervals for the HR estimates as more trials are included (Fig. 2). Including later trials in the HR calculation seems to increase noise, making the estimates less precise. With shorter follow-up periods and fewer events, these later trials provide less opportunity to observe a difference between groups, thereby attenuating the treatment effect. In our study, the observed survival effect in 48 pooled sequential trials was supported by reporting results from a range of pooled trials, showing a consistent treatment effect even as its magnitude naturally attenuated and precision decreased with inclusion of later trials.

This study’s findings should be interpreted considering certain limitations. The data in administrative claims databases are collected for billing purposes; therefore, the study only included data from insured patients. Inovalon® is a health plan claims database comprising fully adjudicated medical and pharmacy claims, with claim transactions data covering more than half of the total US insured population across Medicaid, Medicare (both public insurance plans), and commercial coverage and more than one million providers. In a feasibility assessment, an expected approximately 50/50 public/private distribution was observed. Given feasibility and patient counts compared with commercial data knowledge, the Inovalon data provide insights into PMO use for patients with DMD within the USA, although it may not be generalizable outside the USA. The strengths and limitations of the use of open versus closed claims data to examine health outcomes have been well described in the published literature [38]. Inovalon data are a closed claims dataset, which allow continuous follow-up in a specific payer setting, thus accurately capturing changes in medication, comorbidities, or healthcare-related information. However, closed claims data may be underreported in cases of dual insurance and patients could possibly be lost to follow-up if they change from private to commercial or government/public payer, as continuous payer enrollment may not be captured in a closed claims data frame.

As a result of its X-linked nature, it is very rare (< 1 per million) for girls to have DMD, although instances can occur due to Turner syndrome, translocations including the DMD gene, or biallelic pathogenic variants on the DMD gene [1, 2]. Some carriers of a DMD pathogenic variant can present with skeletal muscle symptoms or cardiomyopathy, potentially caused by skewed X-inactivation [39, 40]. In line with the low prevalence of DMD in girls and with the clinical trial experience to date for the PMOs approved for the treatment of DMD [41–44], the validated claims data methodology for identifying patients with DMD used in this analysis included only male patients. Future claims-based studies should consider developing algorithms that do not exclude female patients.

In this analysis, the median follow-up was 4.6–4.8 years, and there were 126 deaths observed, with only 12 deaths in patients treated with PMOs, which limited the statistical power. In addition to the relatively short follow-up time for measuring survival, the median age of patients enrolled in the study at T0 was 10–11 years, meaning most patients were still well below the age of expected mortality for this population [8]. In insured patients treated with PMOs, more events and follow-up time would be needed to calculate the median time to mortality events. Although the integrated Death File closely matches the number of US deaths from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Death Index, which is considered the “gold standard” for mortality data, information on cause of death is not included based on privacy protection [45]. While the possible underlying comorbidities prior to death may be present in Inovalon claims data, the immediate cause of death could not be assessed in this study. An additional limitation was the absence of information in the database on socioeconomic status and the type of facility where patients were treated (e.g., community versus academic centers, or whether the facility included a specialized neuromuscular department), meaning that it was not possible to ascertain whether these factors differed by treatment group or if they had an impact on outcomes.

Given that detailed measures of disease severity (such as functional scores) are not available in administrative claims, balancing disease severity across two arms is a challenge to RWD studies using these claims as the data source. To control for disease severity, IPTW was used to balance covariates known to be either predictive of disease progression (e.g., age, duration of GC treatment) or to develop over disease progression (e.g., ventilation support, cardiovascular disease) [46]. Furthermore, since the majority of DMD-related deaths are driven by cardiopulmonary causes [10, 47], balancing the treatment arms with respect to these variables was essential to ensure comparable baseline mortality risk. Of the 84 patients who died and had claims in the 3 months preceding death, 85% had documented cardiovascular comorbidities and 76% had respiratory comorbidities, suggesting that choosing use of cardiac medication and ventilation support as proxies for disease severity was appropriate. Calcification of the lung was the most commonly observed comorbidity, and may have been caused by infections, which have been documented to be both prevalent in patients with DMD and associated with causing lung calcification [48, 49].

Additionally, the first observed GC claim in the eligible data may not represent initial treatment onset, and gaps in treatment may be present; therefore, GC treatment duration may be underreported in some patients. To account for this, the analysis used an intention-to-treat framework, where GCs-only patients were analyzed in their initial treatment cohort for the entire observational period or until they initiated PMOs, regardless of discontinuation. Similarly, patients in the PMO + GCs group were analyzed in this cohort from the initiation of PMOs until the end of the observational period. The assumption that patients remained in their initial treatment cohort is supported by current DMD treatment guidelines, which recommend continuous GC use [50], and by real-world evidence demonstrating a low number of patients who stopped therapy among those who initiate PMOs [51, 52].

While PMO eligibility restricted the treatment group to specific genotypes, the lack of genetic data in claims records likely meant the GCs-only group comprised a heterogeneous mix of pathogenic variants. Given that over 80% of patients treated with PMO + GCs had pathogenic variants amenable to exon 51 or 53 skipping (which are typically described as severe phenotypes [53–57]), this comparison suggests that a bias due to patient genotype might work against PMO treatment. In addition, because claims data cannot distinguish between DMD and BMD due to their shared ICD-10 code (G71.01), the screening algorithm may have inadvertently included patients with BMD in the GCs-only group, as previously reported [28]. However, because treatment with GCs was a screening criterion for this study and chronic GC use is less common among patients with BMD, the proportion of patients in this analysis with BMD is likely low [58]. This inclusion would also most likely decrease the observed treatment effect, as BMD has a slower, more variable progression of symptoms compared with DMD resulting in a longer life expectancy [59].

Conclusion

This is the first study to estimate the effect of treatment with the PMOs eteplirsen, casimersen, or golodirsen on survival using sequential target trial emulation causal methods and data tokenization, a robust design for controlling confounding because of GC use and prognostic factors. Among patients treated with eteplirsen, golodirsen, or casimersen, the results suggest a survival benefit during the observation period compared with patients treated with GCs, which is consistent with prior findings [34]. Future research with longer observational periods will be essential to calculate the median time to mortality events and further evaluate the effect of PMOs on survival in patients with DMD.
