Research progress of DNA damage repair (DDR) and DDR inhibitors in tumor immunotherapy
Authors: Qi Liu, Chunmei Zhang, Yixuan Gao, Dongmei Feng, Duo Deng, Yun Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human cells possess a complex network of regulatory systems——the DNA damage repair pathway (DDR), to deal with various DNA damage to prevent the inheritance of deleterious mutations. DDR perform its functions via cell cycle checkpoint activation. Mutations in the DDR gene are also the basis for tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic effect to treatments that cause DNA damage. Consequently, DDR has received high attention for its role in the therapy against cancer, and the related experiments have been carried out to further explore. Up to now, PARP inhibitors such as Olaparib, Rucaparib, Niraparib, Talazoparib, etc. have been approved for marketing and clinical use, other DDR inhibitors (DDRi) such as ATMi/ATRi/DNA-PKi, Chk1/2i, and WEE1i have partially entered clinical trials. Synthetic lethality has led to the exploration of combination of DDRi with other anti-tumor therapies, in particular, the combination of DDRi with immunotherapies such as chemoradiotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has achieved potential efficacy. This article reviewed the DDR inhibitors that are currently in development or in clinical therapy, analyzed the limitations and future development prospects, and stated the mechanisms and clinical trials in antitumor and immunotherapy, to provide ideas for the subsequent development of DDRi and enhance the efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy.

Background

Maintaining the integrity and stability of genes is crucial for cell survival and stability. Since genes are exposed to various endogenous factors (such as aldehydes, reactive oxygen species, and other cellular metabolic products) and exogenous factors (such as chemotherapeutic drugs, ionizing radiation, ultraviolet light, etc.), cells may suffer DNA damage such as nucleotide alterations (deletions, insertions, and substitutions), DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), and DNA single-strand breaks (SSBs). To counter these threats, the DNA damage response (DDR) plays an extremely important role. DDR maintains genetic integrity by recognizing DNA damage sites and activating complex signaling networks to repair the DNA damage. Different types of DNA damage caused by different factors have distinct repair mechanisms. These damage repair pathways include base excision repair (BER); nucleotide excision repair (NER); mismatch repair (MMR); translesion DNA synthesis (TLS); non-homologous end joining (NHEJ); and homologous recombination (HR).

Following chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgical treatment and targeted therapy as the main methods of anti-tumor therapy, Science announced in 2013 that immunotherapy for cancer had become the “breakthrough of the year“ [1]. Tumor immunotherapy refers to strategies that enhance the immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells. This is achieved by activating anti-tumor immunity, releasing cytokines, and infiltrating immune cells into the tumor microenvironment (TME) to exert anti-tumor effects [2]. Innate immunity does not express specific antigen-recognition receptors. However, it can recognize and bind to pathogens, their products, or cell surface-associated ligands — such as those derived from apoptotic or deformed cells in vivo — via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). It then secretes innate immune molecules (e.g., complement and cytokines), mediates non-specific anti-infection, anti-tumor, and immunomodulatory effects, and participates in the initiation and execution of the entire adaptive immune response. The adaptive immune system plays a more prominent role in anti-tumor immunity, as it can recognize tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) expressed on the cell surface. While the immune system exerts anti-tumor functions, tumor cells can also negatively regulate the host’s immune response through multiple pathways to escape immune surveillance.

Currently, encouraging results have been achieved with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs); however, not all tumors derive significant benefits from immunotherapy. First, ICIs are only effective in tumors with high-density immune infiltration, but nearly ineffective in “cold” tumors — which are characterized by limited immune cell infiltration. Second, due to tumor heterogeneity and individual differences, patients with the same type of cancer may exhibit varying responses to ICIs. Therefore, for “cold” tumors that are refractory to ICIs, combining ICIs with other anti-tumor therapies (e.g., tumor vaccines or DDR inhibitors) is a promising strategy. This combination can enhance the exposure of TAAs or tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) and increase tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in “cold” tumors, thereby strengthening the immune system’s attack on tumors through enhanced immunological mechanisms [3].

The generation of genomic mutations is significantly influenced by the fidelity of the DNA repair process [4]. DDR defects or DDR inhibitors exacerbate DNA damage and genomic instability. This, in turn, increases microsatellite instability (MSI), tumor mutation burden (TMB), and neoantigen load (NAL), thereby impacting the immune response of tumor cells [5–8], which in turn affects the immune response of tumor cells.

Because some cancers have unique genetic vulnerabilities, we can leverage this feature to apply synthetic lethality in clinical treatments. For example, PARP inhibitors (PARPi) can specifically target tumors defective in DDR factors — such as BRCA1 or BRCA2, which are involved in homologous recombination (HR) [9, 10] — or other HR-deficient tumors. In addition, through a mechanism independent of HR deficiency, certain tumors with wild-type BRCA1/BRCA2 genes may also exhibit responsiveness to PARP inhibition [11]. Notably, targets for DDR in cancer are not limited to PARPi. Recently, the development of small-molecule inhibitors targeting other potential DDR pathways has been actively pursued; some have even found limited applications in clinical practice, while others — newly developed DDR inhibitors (DDRi), such as ATMi/ATRi/DNAPKi, WEE1i, CHK1i, and POLEi/POLDi — are undergoing clinical trials. Prior research has demonstrated that combining DDRi with ICIs can not only enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs and extend patient survival but also improve overall patient prognosis [12]. Therefore, the significant role played by DDR in tumor immunotherapy should not be underestimated.

The exploration of DDR small-molecule inhibitors and novel DDR-targeted agents has become an urgent development focus in tumor immunotherapy. This review elaborates on the major DNA damage checkpoints, repair mechanisms, and DDR inhibitors currently undergoing clinical trials, while exploring the anti-tumor mechanisms and limitations of these inhibitors. Additionally, we outline the mechanisms of resistance to DDR inhibitors and propose potential solutions.4.

Research on DNA damage repair and DNA damage mediated antitumor Immune-Related pathways

Cells with defective DDR systems have been confirmed to be more sensitive to DNA-damaging agents [13]. Specifically, DDR pathways vary significantly depending on the type of DNA damage [14]. In MMR, the detection of mismatches and insertion/deletion loops triggers SSBs, which are then repaired by nucleases, polymerases, and ligases. In BER, damaged bases are typically recognized by DNA glycosylases, which mediate base excision; nucleases, polymerases, and ligases then complete the repair. This process overlaps with single-strand break repair (SSBR). NER primarily targets bulky, helix-distorting lesions, and its regulation is mainly mediated by post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins. Eukaryotes have two NER sub-pathways: transcription-coupled NER (TC-NER), which removes DNA lesions from the transcribed strand of active genes; and global-genome NER (GG-NER), which removes damage in non-transcribed chromatin. DSBs are primarily repaired via two pathways: HR and NHEJ. Among these, HR is the preferred pathway, as it relies on homologous templates and operates mainly in the S/G2 phases of the cell cycle [15–17].

HR is a relatively complex but highly accurate repair mechanism that relies on the use of a homologous template strand to synthesize new DNA. It mainly occurs during the S phase (DNA synthesis phase) and G2 phase (post-DNA synthesis phase) of the cell cycle. NHEJ is a repair process that directly joins the ends of DSBs via DNA ligase, independent of homologous DNA sequences. The Ku protein (Ku70/Ku80) complex recognizes and binds to the ends of DSBs. The Ku-DNA complex then recruits the DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs), activates its kinase activity, and phosphorylates itself to initiate the NHEJ pathway. Subsequently, it further recruits the recombinase Artemis to bind to the DNA ends, and then assembles the XRCC4-DNA ligase 4-XLF complex to facilitate the ligation of the DNA ends [19, 20].

In addition, there is a less well-defined Ku-independent NHEJ pathway known as microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) or alternative non-homologous end joining (AEJ). However, this repair method tends to cause direct deletions of nucleotide sequences.

In general, although both NHEJ and MMEJ are error-prone, they can operate at any stage of the cell cycle. In contrast, HR is typically restricted to the S phase and G2 phase, as it relies on sister chromatid sequences as templates to mediate highly accurate repair.

There is already substantial evidence indicating that DNA damage is closely related to anti-tumor immunity [21]. DNA damage can be caused by endogenous or exogenous gene-damaging factors. One potential outcome of damaged DNA that has not been properly repaired surviving through adaptation at cell cycle checkpoints is the formation of micronuclei [22]. The characteristic of tumor cells is genetic instability, and genetic instability can promote the formation of micronuclei. Micronuclei are formed when a damaged DNA fragment from the nucleus recruits nuclear membrane components during mitosis, but the exact process is not clear. This membrane structure is fragile and easily ruptures during interphase, exposing the DNA to the cytoplasm [23, 24]. DNA enclosed in micronuclei can bind to cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase (cGAS), causing a conformational change in cGAS that enables it to use ATP and GTP as substrates to cyclicguanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP). cGAMP binds to the endoplasmic reticulum-anchored stimulator of interferon genes (STING), translocates together to the Golgi apparatus, and there recruits the heterodimeric IKKα/β kinases and TANK-binding kinase 1 to phosphorylate interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and the inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), respectively. This subsequently releases NF-κB. Once NF-κB and IRF3 are activated, they translocate to the nucleus to mediate the expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interferons (IFNs). IFNs are multifunctional cytokines with antiviral, antitumor, and immunomodulatory properties, serving as central coordinators of immune responses. IFNs are mainly classified into types I, II, and III. Type I interferons are not only antiviral cytokines but also inducers of tumor cell apoptosis and anti-angiogenesis, playing various important roles in the immune system. Type II interferon is a cytokine that possesses both antitumor and pro-tumor activities and can act as a link in immune therapy responses, especially IFN-γ. The secreted IFN-γ bind to their heterodimeric receptor IFNAR1/2, activating Janus kinase 1 (JAK1). JAK1 phosphorylates the signal transducer and activator of the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) to induce the expression of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) [21, 25].

IFN provides a link between innate immune responses and adaptive immune responses against tumors. IFN-γ affects different immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. IFN-γ promotes the activity of Th1 cells, Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and macrophages, enhancing antigen presentation and playing a role in anti-tumor immunity, and activates macrophages, giving them a pro-inflammatory and tumoricidal phenotype. IFN-γ also upregulates surface major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II) on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), facilitating the specific activation of CD4+T cells, and inhibits the differentiation and function of regulatory T (Treg) cells, Th2, and Th17 cells. IFN-γ suppresses IL-4 production by inhibiting Th2 cell differentiation [25, 26]. In addition, IFN-γ promotes tumor cell apoptosis by upregulating the expression of caspase-1, −3, and − 8, enhancing the secretion of FAS and its ligand as well as TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand. IFN-γ can also inhibit angiogenesis, impair the proliferation and survival of endothelial cells, and induce ischemia in the tumor stroma. IFN-γ promotes the recruitment of immune cells to the TME and restricts tumor growth by transcriptionally regulating CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 and their homologous receptor CXCR3 on T cells, NK cells, monocytes, DCs, and cancer cells [27] (Fig.1).

Research progress in tumor immunotherapy

Cancer, as a globally incurable disease, still relied on surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy well into the 21 st century. However, the high metastatic potential of many cancers makes surgical resection infeasible in numerous cases; furthermore, radical organ removal can directly impair patients’ basic physiological functions. Notably, early chemotherapy—including platinum compounds and antimetabolites—exerts its effects by directly targeting DNA strands, effectively blocking DNA replication and inducing significant DNA damage. Subsequent generations of chemotherapeutic drugs, such as camptothecin, vincristine, and paclitaxel, primarily target and inhibit enzymes involved in DNA replication and mitotic processes. While chemotherapy yields certain therapeutic benefits, it also causes irreversible damage to the immune system and normal cells. In contrast, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)—a therapeutic approach that uses ICIs to target regulatory pathways in T cells, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity—has demonstrated higher survival rates than traditional cancer therapies and thus provides a powerful weapon against tumors [28].

As the pioneering cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated molecule-4 (CTLA-4) inhibitor, ipilimumab has demonstrated a significant survival advantage in metastatic melanoma patients receiving this immunotherapeutic agent. In 2010, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved ipilimumab for the treatment of advanced-stage melanoma [29]. Programmed cell death receptor ligand-1 (PD-L1) is expressed in 50% or more of tumor cells in cancer patients. Compared with platinum-containing chemotherapy, pembrolizumab—a PD-L1 inhibitor—administered as monotherapy, shows significant advantages in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Data published by the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) demonstrated that pembrolizumab monotherapy achieved a median OS of 26.3 months, with a 5-year OS rate of 31.9%—markedly superior to the outcomes observed in the chemotherapy group, where OS nearly doubled. In melanoma patients, a study confirmed that a higher number of PD-L1-positive cells is associated with a more favorable response to checkpoint blockade therapy [30]. Given the significant anti-cancer efficacy of ICIs, their role in anti-tumor immunity has been increasingly emphasized.

The cellular basis of immunotherapy lies in immune cells. In tumors with high-density immune cell infiltration—such as glioblastoma—tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) can account for over 40% of the cells [31]. Strategies targeting these tumors are designed to achieve more effective anti-tumor immunity by enhancing and activating TILs in the cold tumor TME [32]. Understanding the infiltration pattern of immune cells within the TME is crucial for improving response rates and developing novel strategies in cancer immunotherapy, especially since CTLs (CD8 + TILs) are positively linked to survival in multiple cancer types. PD-L1-positive tumor cells can mediate T cell apoptosis and the expression of interleukin-10 (IL-10) [33]. Additionally, increased CD8 + T cell density is positively correlated with a higher percentage of PD-L1 expression in tumors and stroma. In primary cutaneous Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC), PD-L1 expression in tumor cells is associated with intratumoral CD8 + T cell infiltration, which in turn influences adaptive anti-tumor immunity [34]. These findings highlight the critical importance of intervening in T cell dysfunction for cancer therapy.

More and more research has indicated that B cells, as an important component of the TME, also possess anti-tumor immune functions, which may improve patient prognosis. CD20 + tumor-infiltrating B cells (TIBs) are associated with favorable prognosis in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and ovarian cancer—possibly because they act as antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to enhance the cytolytic T cell response. Specifically, cytolytic T lymphocytes directly kill and lyse target cells by directly contacting them [35, 36]. Notably, according to Cabrita et al., the coexistence of CD20 + B cells and CD8 + T cells within tumors not only promotes the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures in the TME but also correlates with improved survival rates in patients with metastatic melanoma, thus serving as a predictive factor for the clinical outcomes of ICIs [37]. Hollern et al. demonstrated that ICIs can induce the activation of both follicular helper T cells and B cells; the latter enhances anti-tumor immune responses by secreting antibodies and presenting antigens to activate T cells [38]. These studies highlight the critical role of B cells in activating T cells and mediating anti-tumor immunity.

Given that the importance of the TME in tumor progression has been confirmed, anti-tumor therapies designed to better adapt to ICIs by modulating the TME have gradually entered clinical trials. These therapies aim to exert anti-tumor effects when combined with ICIs; among these approaches, successful trials of ICIs combined with DDRi have been shown to improve anti-tumor immunotherapy and enhance patient survival rates. This has alleviated the limitations of ICI monotherapy, making the exploration of various small-molecule DDR inhibitors a crucial research direction in cancer treatment.

Mechanism and clinical Trials/Application of DDR inhibitors in tumors

Research has shown that human cells experience approximately 70,000 DNA damage events daily [39]. Most of these damages are SSBs, accounting for roughly 75% [40]; importantly, the accumulation of SSBs may further convert them into DSBs. Through a series of coordinated processes, DDR systems not only respond to DNA damage but also maintain genomic stability. Among these systems, several key DDR proteins—including PARP, ATM/ATR, and DNA-PK—act as crucial factors in transducing DNA damage signals and mediating subsequent repair processes.

Defects in DNA damage repair drive cancer development by promoting DNA mutations, but they also expose cancer-specific vulnerabilities that enable treatment via DDR-specific targets. For instance, PARPi have obtained regulatory approval as a therapeutic option for ovarian cancer, marking a major advancement in targeted cancer therapy [41]. This progress has significantly accelerated the development of targeted inhibitors against various DDR-associated kinases, such as ATR, ATM, CHEK1, CHEK2, DNA-PK, and WEE1—with several of these inhibitors already advancing into clinical trials.

Mechanism and clinical application of PARP inhibitors in tumor immunity

Mechanism and clinical trials of PARP inhibitors

In addition to inhibiting PARP expression, PARP inhibitors can also bind to the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) binding pocket of PARP1 (and/or PARP2). This binding induces conformational isomerism, which stabilizes the otherwise reversible dissociation of DNA-PARP complexes—while keeping PARP bound to DNA. This phenomenon is referred to as the “trapping” of DNA-PARP complexes, thereby ensuring their prolonged stability and persistence. In turn, this prevents subsequent DNA repair from occurring. Olaparib was initially identified as a targeted treatment for HR-deficient tumors and has since received FDA approval for treating cancers associated with germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, marking a significant advancement in precision oncology [9, 10, 42, 43]. Moreover, PARPi is now becoming an emerging therapy for acute leukemia with specific genetic background, for example, they are effective against IDH1/2- or AML1-ETO-mutant AML cells. Nonetheless, PARPi appear to have limited activity as single agents in treatment of relapsed or refractory AML cells. Therefore, combination therapy has emerged and is anticipated to expand the efficacy of PARPi against AML. Indeed, several studies have tested the efficacy of combination therapy with PARPi and different small molecules, such as antibody-drug conjugates, FLT3 inhibitor, DNMTi, and HDACi, in AML cells [44].

The mechanism and clinical application of PARP inhibitors affecting the efficacy of ICIs

The principle of PARPi combined with ICIs mainly involves increased immunogenicity, elevated TMB, the production of neoantigens, enhanced antigen presentation, greater infiltration of TILs, upregulated PD-L1 expression, as well as the modulation of other molecules involved in TME composition and the reprogramming of immune cells. Mechanistically, PARP inhibitors enhance and prolong the durability of the response to ICIs by modulating the regulatory dynamics of the entire TME. For instance, Olaparib has been shown to enhance the cytotoxic activity of NK cells [70]. Additionally, by targeting the PARP1/HIF1-α/VEGF signaling pathway, PARP1 inhibitors can inhibit both angiogenesis and the migration of tumor cells [71]. Given the multifaceted impacts of PARPi on TME and tumor cell biology, their combination with ICIs holds great therapeutic prospects.

PARPi enhances anti-PD-L1 therapy-induced anti-tumor immunity [72–75]. The results of BRCA-deficient Kras ovarian cancer model [76] showed that anti-PD-L1 was treated in combination with the PARP inhibitors Olaparib, Talazoparib and Rucaparib, respectively, and significantly improved anti-tumor activity. Following treatment with Olaparib and durvalumab exhibiting a good prognosis and survival rate [77]. Niraparib in combination with pembrolizumab has been found in the treatment of TNBC and ovarian cancer, and its effectiveness in anti-tumor activity has also been demonstrated in phase I and II trials [78]. In a BRCA-/- mouse model, the PARP inhibitor Veliparib has been documented to not only augment the therapeutic efficacy of CTLA-4 blockade but also facilitate tumor eradication and promote long-term survival [79]. Yap and colleagues’ recent work reports the results of a large, phase 1b and 2 basket, nonrandomized trial exploring the combination of avelumab (PD-L1) and Talazoparib in solid tumors. This study highlighted BRCA1 and BRCA2’ s central role as biomarkers for response to PARPi, and also other alternations known to confer PARPi sensitivity such as ATM, RAD51B/C, CHK2, and PALB2, detailed molecular subtyping in this large cohort with PD-L1 expression, TMB status, CD8+count, and DDR status should also be noticed [80]. Yu Chen et al. found that PARP inhibitor and immune checkpoint inhibitor have synergism efficacy in gallbladder cancer [81].

However, PARP inhibitors in combination with ICIs are not always significantly effective. For example, in gastric and pancreatic cancers, there are few studies on the combination of PARPis and ICIs, and most studies have not a significant improvement in efficacy. The phase II MEDIOLA trial, focusing on recurrent gastric cancer patients treated with Olaparib and Durvalumab, reported an ORR of only 10% and a 12-week DCR of approximately 26%, which seems to falling short of anticipated efficacy [82]. Similarly, NCT02660034 revealed that the combination of Pamiparib and Tislelizumab in treating advanced/metastatic gastric or gastroesophageal junction carcinoma(GEJC) yielded an ORR of only 10% and a median PFS of just 2.1 months [83]. For advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, the effectiveness of PARPis combined with ICIs also appears limited. Results from two phase I or II studies indicated an ORR of less than 10% and a median PFS of only 1.9 months, suggesting suboptimal efficacy seemingly [83, 84]. This may be related to adverse reactions caused by combination therapy such as nausea, vomiting, anemia, hypothyroidism, rash, and immune-related pneumitis, but most of them can be tolerated after symptomatic treatment. These adverse events can be managed mainly by adjusting the dose, stopping the drug or symptomatic treatment [85].

In light of the aforementioned encouraging data from preclinical studies, a series of clinical trials are progressively being initiated for investigation.

Adverse reactions and counteracting mechanisms of PARP inhibitors

Adverse events associated with PARP inhibitors are mostly “class effects”—meaning that different PARP inhibitors share similar categories of adverse events, though differences exist in the incidence, grade, and other characteristics of these events across individual agents. Furthermore, the combination of PARP inhibitors with other drugs has also been associated with additional adverse reactions.

PROpel is a global, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, phase III study evaluating the efficacy and safety of Olaparib in combination with abiraterone (Olaparib arm, n = 399) as first-line treatment for patients with mCRPC [86].

At the data cutoff, the median treatment duration with Olaparib was 17.5 months. In patients with prostate cancer, the most common hematologic adverse events in the Olaparib group was anemia (46.0%); notably, anemia (15.1%) was also the most common ≥ Grade 3 adverse event. Non-hematological adverse events included gastrointestinal adverse events (nausea [28.1%], diarrhea [17.3%], constipation [17.3%], decreased appetite [14.6%], and vomiting [13.1%]) and fatigue/asthenia [37.2%]. In addition, the incidence of pulmonary embolism in the Olaparib group was 6.5% (including 1 fatal event), and the discontinuation rate due to this event was 2.0%; the incidence of deep vein thrombosis was 1.8% [86]. Some ovarian cancer patients also experience gastrointestinal, neurological, and respiratory toxicities, accompanied by severe side effects such as bone marrow suppression and secondary malignancies. For Grade 1–2 toxicities, monitoring is generally required—treatment may either be continued or paused for up to 28 days (after which treatment may resume). However, Grade 3 or higher toxicities require careful evaluation; if necessary, dose reduction or treatment discontinuation should be considered [87]. Olaparib-related events typically occur within the first 3 months of treatment initiation and resolve within 6 months. Most adverse events can be managed with supportive therapy, Olaparib dose reduction, and temporary treatment interruption—thereby reducing the need for permanent treatment discontinuation. Reportedly, 13.8% of patients in the Olaparib group discontinued treatment permanently, 20.1% had their dose reduced, and 44.7% experienced treatment interruptions due to adverse events [86].

MAGNITUDE is a phase III, randomized, double-blind study evaluating the efficacy and safety of Niraparib in combination with Abiraterone and Prednisone (the niraparib group) in two patient cohorts: those with homologous recombination repair gene mutations (HRRm; HRR + cohort, n = 423) and those without HRRm (HRR − cohort, n = 247) [88].

In the HRR + cohort, the median duration of the specified treatment was 13.8 months in the niraparib group (n = 212). The incidence of ≥ Grade 3 adverse events in the niraparib group was 67.0%, with the most common Grade 3 adverse events being anemia (28.3%) and hypertension (14.6%). Other notable Grade 3 or 4 adverse events included thrombocytopenia (6.6%) and neutropenia (6.6%). The incidence of serious adverse events in the niraparib group was 35.8%. During the study treatment period, a total of 19 patients in this group died from adverse events, with infection as the primary cause of death. Notably, the relapse-free survival (rPFS) benefit in niraparib-treated patients who had treatment interruptions or dose reductions was comparable to that observed in the overall HRR cohort. This suggests that dose interruptions or reductions can be used to manage adverse events in patients without compromising treatment efficacy [88].

TALAPRO-2 is a phase III, international, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study evaluating the safety and patient-reported outcomes of Talazoparib in combination with Enzalutamide versus Placebo plus Enzalutamide in patients with mCRPC. The safety analysis population in Cohort 2 included 198 patients with homologous recombination repair (HRR) defects. The safety analysis included the type, severity, timing, and relationship to study treatment of the most common treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). In Cohort 2, the median follow-up was 15 months in the Talazoparib combination group, and 66.2% of patients reported Grade 3 or 4 TEAEs. The most common non-hematologic TEAEs (≥ 20% incidence) were fatigue (33.3%). Hematologic TEAEs typically occurred within the first 4 months of treatment, with the most common Grade 3 or 4 hematologic TEAEs being anemia (40.9%), neutropenia (18.7%), and thrombocytopenia (7.1%). From the first dose of Talazoparib, the median time to first occurrence of Grade 3 or 4 TEAEs and the median duration of these TEAEs were as follows: for anemia, 3.3 months and 17 days, respectively; for neutropenia, 2.3 months and 14 days, respectively; for thrombocytopenia, 1.5 months and 15 days, respectively [89].

Hematologic adverse events of PARP inhibitors include anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, and others. These events are more common within the first 3 months of treatment and gradually resolve over time; in some cases, they may require treatment interruption or dose reduction of the PARP inhibitor. For PARP inhibitor treatment, blood counts should be monitored weekly during the first month, monthly during the first year, and regularly thereafter. If PARP inhibitor treatment is interrupted due to Grade 3 or 4 hematologic adverse events, consider monitoring the complete blood count (CBC) weekly after resuming treatment until blood counts return to normal levels. Non-hematologic adverse events of PARP inhibitors include gastrointestinal toxicity, neurologic toxicity, cardiovascular toxicity, and others. These events should generally be monitored within the first 4–8 weeks of initiating therapy. For the grading of both hematologic and non-hematologic adverse events, please refer to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Version 4.03 [90].

Mechanism and clinical trials of ATM inhibitors in tumors

The important role of ATM in tumors

Cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents induce DNA damage in cancer cells, and cancer cells employ a variety of mechanisms to repair these lesions and maintain cell survival [91, 92]. Currently, a number of transcription factors have been identified to facilitate the activation of ATM in cancer cells during their response to chemotherapeutic agents, thereby reducing the efficacy of chemotherapy [93]. To improve patient response to anticancer therapy, inhibition of ATM activity is critical.

Patients with hereditary homozygous or pathogenic compound heterozygous ATM mutations develop ataxia telangiectasia (A-T) and are at further risk of developing cancer—particularly common hematologic cancers. Approximately 45% of cases of mantle cell lymphoma and T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia carry ATM mutations (which lead to reduced ATM expression). Individuals with pathogenic ATM mutant alleles have a significantly higher risk of developing cancer: ATM mutations are present in 10% to 20% of colorectal, gastric, and lung adenocarcinomas. Additionally, the frequency of ATM mutations in cancers such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and breast cancer is reported to be less than 10% [94]. Despite the relatively high frequency of ATM mutations in cancer suggested by these data, a significant limitation lies in the small sample size of these studies. Additionally, ATM deletion is even more common in cancers than mutations [95–98].

The principal mechanism underlying ATM deficiency involves the hypermethylation occurring within the promoter region of the ATM gene [99]. The hypermethylation phenomenon observed in the promoter region of the ATM gene has been extensively documented across various malignancies [100–103]. As noted above, the use of ATM inhibitors (ATMi) has received widespread attention in the medical community, and several targeted ATMi have been studied [94, 104, 105].

Clinical trials with ATM inhibitors

ATM inhibitors currently in development include XRD0394, AZD1390, WSD0682, and ZN-B-2262. Another ATM inhibitor, KU-55,933, failed to enter clinical trials due to its hydrophobicity and high toxicity to healthy cells. AZ31 exerts non-tumor-specific systemic effects and blocks the hERG ion channel, thus precluding its clinical application. AZD0156 exhibits extremely high ATM selectivity, high solubility, and good bioavailability; however, its clinical trial was terminated due to hematologic toxicity. M3541’s clinical development was terminated due to multiple adverse reactions in patients, limited drug absorption, and a lack of evidence for significant tumor volume reduction. The clinical advancement of ATM inhibitors remains in the relatively nascent stages of development. Clinical trials with ATM inhibitors

Adverse reactions and counteracting mechanisms of ATM inhibitors

ATM inhibitors have limited clinical efficacy as single agents in the treatment of cancer, with their true potential residing in combination therapies. The critical role of ATM DSB repair makes it an ideal target to sensitize cancer cells to radiotherapy, although its efficacy is heavily influenced by the genetic and mutational landscape of the cancer. Multiple studies shown that the p53 status has a significant impact on the therapeutic efficacy of ATM inhibitors, with p53-deficient cells being highly sensitive to ATM inhibition [111–113]. Despite the pleiotropy of ATM inhibition has been demonstrated in preclinical studies and promising results have been achieved in multiple clinical trials, several issues still limit its application, for example, its high toxicity and various adverse reactions. KU − 60,019 High-dose combination irradiation can cause neurologic symptoms [114], M3541 clinical trial was terminated due to emergent adverse reactions [114], AZD1390 can cause slight lesions in normal brain tissue [115], M4076 can cause anemia and rash at high dose [116], and AZD156 was discontinued due to hematologic toxicity [117]. In addition, there is insufficient understanding of the off-target effects and potential long-term consequences of these drugs, and is also a lack of long-term studies on ATM inhibition. Therefore, patients in clinical trials of ATM inhibitors should monitor complete blood count and liver and kidney function regularly, and reduce the dosage or discontinue the drug immediately in the event of the above hematologic and gastrointestinal adverse reactions. Even so, ATM inhibition has potential to sensitize resistant cancers to therapy and combined treatment with tumor-specific characteristics is a feasible option for some cancer patients. Overall, ATM inhibitors provide promising and unique strategies for cancer therapy, with potential to play a significant role in personalized medicine.

Mechanism and clinical trials of ATR inhibitors in tumor immunity

The important role of ATR in tumors

ATR kinases guarantee the punctual and precise execution of DNA replication by regulating initiation activation and bifurcation processes in the S phase, in which way tumor cells process DNA replication and cell division and reproduction. Therefore, a variety of potent and specific ATR inhibitors (ATRi) are being developed, and ATR has become a very attractive target for cancer treatment [118–120]. ATRi increases replication fork delay, promotes chromosomal breakage, and leads to cytotoxicity in tumor. Tumors cells carrying ATM mutations have also been found to be susceptible to ATR inhibitors. ATRi enhances tumor sensitivity to ionizing radiation, and the combination of ATRi has been shown to be effective in TNBC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [121]. Interestingly, recent studies have shown that ATRi enhances the radiation-induced interferon signaling pathway, suggesting that ATR inhibitors have potential promise in combination with immunotherapy. This phenomenon has been demonstrated in a mouse model of CRPC, where ATRi mediates cGAS-STING pathway priming, IFN-β secretion, and apoptosis. ATRi in combination with immunotherapy targeting the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 shows synergistic antitumor activity.

Existing anti-cancer drugs that exert anti-tumor effects through the disruption of DNA replication or the induction of DNA damage have been demonstrated in several studies to produce synergistic effects when combined with ATR inhibitors [119, 122–126]. In tumors harboring BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations, ATR inhibitors have been demonstrated to exhibit synergistic effects when combined with PARP inhibitors [127]. The synergistic effect between the two agents still need to be further confirmed.

Among the most promising clinical applications of ATR inhibitors lies their therapeutic potential for addressing tumors resistant to PARP inhibitors, as BRCA1-depleted cancer cells may circumvent the toxicity induced by PARP inhibitors through transfection into BRCA1-independent RAD51 at DSBs. In vitro experiments demonstrate that ATR inhibitors effectively block this BRCA1-independent function, thereby restoring tumor cell sensitivity to PARP inhibition [128]. In addition, Inactivation of the helicase SLFN11 led to PARP inhibitor resistance, which was effectively reversed by ATR inhibitor in HR-normal and HR-deficient cancer cells [129].

Currently, at least five ATRis are showing efficacy in ongoing randomized clinical studies: Berzosertib (M6620/VE82), Ceralasertib (AZD6738), elimusertib (BAY1895344), M177, and Camonsertib (RP-3500). Clinical trials with ATR inhibitors

Adverse reactions and development prospects of ATR inhibitors

The ATRi combination strategy has shown prospects so far, and the combination of ATRi with ICIs is also a hot spot in current research, which may overcome the problem of resistance to ICIs. Nevertheless, the major challenge of clinical development of the above-mentioned drugs is still adverse reactions, especially myelosuppression [152]. To address this issue, hotspot of current clinical research is to explore alternative administration regimens and orders of drug combinations. In conclusion, there is no ATRi approved for the market worldwide at present, but according to the currently disclosed clinical data, the safety of ATRi is controllable, and it has shown good antitumor activity in solid tumors, with broad prospects for the future. Therefore, it is urgently needed to carry out more large-sample clinical studies to bring ATRi into the standard treatment scheme for tumors, so as to benefits to patients.

Mechanism of DNA-PK inhibitors in tumor immunity

The holoenzyme DNA-PK, which encompasses the Ku70 and Ku80 proteins, has DNA-PKcs as its catalytic subunit. DNA-PK responds to DSBs and participates in DDR through NHEJ, in which DNA-PKcs could associate at the site of DNA breakage by binding the Ku complex [157]. Therefore, DNA-PKcs-deficient cells are sensitive to DSB inducers, which makes DNA-PKcs inhibitors effective [158–161]. Similar to ATM, DNA-PKcs inhibition combined with radiotherapy is a promising anticancer strategy [161, 162].

Research indicates that radiotherapy and the high expression of DNA-PKcs play a synergistic role in overcoming drug resistance in tumor cells to achieve better therapeutic effects. For example, the high expression of DNA-PKcs in CD133-positive MG-63 (osteosarcoma) cells not only enhances cisplatin resistance in the cells, but also mediates this cisplatin resistance through DNA damage repair and regulation of P-gp expression via the Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway. Therefore, DNA-PK is considered a suitable target for anti-cancer therapy [163–165]. Most importantly, according to related studies, ATM and DNA-PKcs may exhibit a synthetic lethal effect [166, 167].

Adverse reactions and development prospects of DNA-PK inhibitors

Although significant clinical progress has been achieved, substantial challenges still persist in the advancement of therapeutic DNA-PK inhibitors. DNA-PK belongs to the PIKK family and shares a high degree of sequence homology with other kinases, thus making the development of highly selective DNA-PK inhibitors challenging. Further research efforts should focus on improving the affinity of inhibitors for DNA-PK and ensuring their selectivity relative to other similar kinases to reduce potential side effects. It is also crucial to identify a drug combination strategy to address potential drug resistance to DNA-PKi in the future.

Mechanism and clinical trials of CHK1 and WEE1 inhibitors in tumor immunity

Mechanism and clinical trials of CHK1 and WEE1 inhibitors

Due to their important role in DDR, CHK1 and WEE1 are considered small-molecule anticancer drug targets. It should be noted that most CHKi/WEE1i drugs are kinase inhibitors, which render cancer cells more sensitive to chemotherapy-induced DNA damage via their synthetic lethality or abrogate the G2/M DNA damage checkpoint [168, 169]. AZD-1775, for example, sensitizes P53-deficient tumors to radiation- or chemotherapy-induced DNA damage.

Current clinical research on CHK1 inhibitors focuses on three core drugs (PEP07, Prexasertib, and BBI-355), which are generally in the transitional stage from early exploration to mid-term verification. Their indicationscover both hematological tumors and solid tumors, with research designs centered on “safety evaluation” and “exploration of combination therapies”. Studies on CHK1 inhibitors have initially identified safe dose windows in some tumors; the next step requires more Phase II trials to verify the efficacy of combination therapies (e.g., with gemcitabine), especially their therapeutic potential for specific molecular subtypes of solid tumors.

Up to now, several published studies have demonstrated the efficacy of CHK1 inhibitor combination therapy. For instance, Booth L et al. [170] found that the combination of the CHK1 inhibitor SRA737 with the PARP1 inhibitors olaparib and niraparib can induce cancer cell death. Additionally, Xiao Y et al. [171] reported that the CHK1 inhibitor GNE-783 can enhance the activity of antimetabolite chemotherapeutic agents such as gemcitabine. Latest studies have shown that the combination of the CHK1 inhibitor SRA737 and the WEE1 inhibitor AZD1775 has potential therapeutic effects on CRPC [172].

Current clinical research on WEE1 inhibitors centers on five core drugs (Adavosertib, Azenosertib, APR-1051, WJB001, and WEE1-IN-5/DEBIO 0123), and is generally in a phase where “early-stage dose exploration and mid-stage efficacy verification proceed in parallel”. Their indications are highly focused on refractory solid tumors, with research designs taking “synergistic effect through combination therapy” and “precision matching of specific molecular subtypes” as key directions.

Unlike CHK1 inhibitors, WEE1 inhibitors have a higher proportion of “combination therapy” in their clinical trials. Existing studies have shown that MK-1775 and vitamin K2 (VK2) involves targeting the cell cycle regulatory protein PKMYT1 and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). This mechanism significantly inhibits the proliferation of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells and induces caspase 3/7-mediated apoptosis, thereby providing a new strategy to overcome tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance [173]. For example, they are used in combination with common chemotherapeutic drugs such as carboplatin, gemcitabine, and etoposide. Through the synergistic effect of “WEE1 inhibiting DNA repair + chemotherapy directly damaging DNA”, they enhance chemotherapy sensitivity, especially for platinum-resistant tumors. In addition, Azenosertib is used in combination with trastuzumab deruxtecan for gastric cancer patients with HER2 positivity and Cyclin E amplification, achieving dual precision targeting of “HER2 targeting + Cyclin E-related DNA repair defects”.

Overall, research on WEE1 inhibitors has formed a clear path of “focusing on refractory solid tumors, taking combination therapy as the core, and integrating molecular marker screening”. Particularly, progress in the fields of breast cancer and gynecological tumors is expected to provide new treatment options for clinical practice. The next step requires more Phase II trial data to verify the long-term efficacy and safety of combination regimens, while expanding the application scope of molecular markers.

Adverse reactions and development prospects of CHK1/WEE1 inhibitors

Prexasertib (LY2606368), a second-generation ATP-competitive CHK1 inhibitor, has shown potent antitumor activity in clinical trials. However, it exhibits dose-limiting hematologic toxicity, necessitating the exploration of low-dose optimization and rational combination strategies. Meanwhile, CHK1 exerts dual immunomodulatory effects in regulating the tumor microenvironment, and its relationship with antitumor immunity is complex. Notably, the sensors of immunostimulatory agents activated by CHK1 inhibitors and their dose-response relationships remain unclear.

Although Wee1 kinase activity plays a crucial role in tumor cell signaling, most reported Wee1 kinase inhibitors have failed to enter clinical development to date. Even those that have entered the clinic, such as adavosertib, have raised safety concerns due to their low selectivity and associated adverse reactions. Adavosertib monotherapy exhibits a certain degree of cytotoxicity, leading to grade 3 or higher adverse events (AEs). ZN-c3 demonstrates higher selectivity than adavosertib; however, limited clinical data have been disclosed thus far, and its efficacy and potential toxicity remain unclear.

In clinical trials, Wee1 inhibitors are often combined with other anticancer drugs; however, further research is required to determine the off-target effects and clinical efficacy of these combination regimens. A key challenge currently is how to maintain the intrinsic efficacy of the inhibitors while balancing Wee1 kinase inhibitors with other drug therapies to maximize patient benefits. Therefore, identifying highly selective and low-toxicity Wee1 inhibitors, as well as exploring optimal combination approaches and dosages with other drugs, remains the focus of future research.

Mechanism and clinical trials of POLE/POLD inhibitors in tumor immunity

Research progress on the correlation between POLE/POLD mutation and TMB and MSI

POLE mutations and MSI have been identified as two key indicators for molecular subtyping, reflecting their critical role in classifying tumor profiles. In studies of endometrial cancer (EC), POLE mutations and MSI have been divided into ultra-high mutant phenotypes and strong mutant phenotypes, respectively, serving as two independent indicators. In colorectal cancer (CRC), Carethers et al. categorized POLE-mutant tumors and MSI tumors under the umbrella of strongly mutant phenotypes, highlighting their distinct genetic profiles [192]. Church et al. observed that MSI and POLE mutations tend to be mutually exclusive, rarely co-occurring in the same tumor sample. The incidence of MSI in EC was as low as 18.5%, whereas all ECs with POLE mutations were Microsatellite Instability-Stable (MSS) [193]. A study involving 544 EC patients with POLE mutations revealed that MSS and MSI were present in roughly equal proportions, with the most frequent subtype being MSI lacking MLH1 methylation. This finding suggests that POLE mutations could aid in the diagnosis of Lynch syndrome cases [194]. Proteomic analyses demonstrated that microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) colon cancer is predominantly associated with the enrichment of mismatch repair pathways, with POLE mutations also making a notable contribution [195].

Tumors harboring both POLE mutations and MSI-H have become a research focus, particularly regarding the interplay between POLE mutations and deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) mechanisms. Notably, methylation of the MLH1 promoter is the primary driving factor for most MSI cases, a phenomenon that underscores the complexity of these molecular pathways. If dMMR occurs before POLE mutation, the proportion of MLH1 methylation tends to be similar among MSI patients, regardless of whether they have wild-type POLE or POLE mutations. However, patients with wild-type POLE account for a higher proportion of MLH1-silenced cases, whereas those with POLE mutations represent a lower percentage of MLH1-silenced cases—this provides contradictory evidence against the “MSI-first” model [196]. Moreover, PTEN mutations are thought to play an early role in EC development. When analyzing EC-associated pathogenic PTEN mutations, researchers observed that these mutations were significantly more frequent in POLE-mutant tumors, whether the tumors had proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) or dMMR [197]. Additionally, since dMMR is not as sensitive as other mutagenic processes, it might be mistakenly classified as MSS in the early stages of analysis. Pathogenic somatic POLE mutations are regarded as early, even initial, mutational events, and their associated mutational phenotype shapes distinct oncogenic pathways. Repeated evaluation of microsatellite status in advanced disease stages may yield results differing from initial findings, potentially providing new insights for clinical strategies. Hwang et al. noted that deletions in MSH6 and MSH2 tend to occur before POLE mutations, suggesting a sequential pattern of genetic alterations. These authors argue that POLE mutations likely represent secondary alterations following dMMR, and their impact on POLE function appears to drive a substantial increase in tumor mutational burden (TMB) in tumors with both dMMR and POLE mutations [198].

In summary, existing evidence suggests that in tumors with both POLE mutations and MSI-H, POLE mutations likely act as a driver of dMMR, which in turn contributes to the development of MSI-H.

Mechanism and clinical trials of POLE/POLD inhibitors in tumor immunity

TMB is regarded as a pivotal molecular biomarker associated with immunotherapy response, primarily due to its role in enhancing tumor immunogenicity and augmenting T cell activity. However, current research investigating the correlation between POLD1 and ICI efficacy remains limited, with most studies predominantly focusing on POLE mutations. Pathogenic POLE mutations exhibit a strong association with elevated TMB.

Mo et al. conducted an in-depth analysis of the correlation between POLE exonuclease domain mutations (POLE-EDMs) and the expression levels of immune-related genes using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [199, 200]. Their findings revealed that POLE-EDMs were significantly positively correlated with the infiltration of CTLs, alongside a marked upregulation of cytotoxic T cell effector markers. Further investigations showed that the concentrations of CD8+ CTLs, CD45RO+ memory immune cells (MICs), and CD8+ CD45RO+ MICs in POLE-EDMs were significantly higher than those in POLE non-exonuclease mutants. Additionally, when analyzed in conjunction with MSI status, the levels of these immune cells (CD8+ CTLs, CD45RO+ MICs, and CD8+ CD45RO+ MICs) were lower in MSI-H tumors than in POLE-EDM tumors but higher than those in MSS tumors.

This indicates that POLE-EDM tumors exhibit robust TILs activity and enhanced anti-tumor immunity, irrespective of their MSI status [194].

To investigate the impact of POLE/POLD1 mutations on immunotherapy efficacy, a comprehensive analysis was conducted, focusing on their potential role in modulating therapeutic outcomes. Research has shown that immune checkpoint-associated proteins (e.g., CTLA-4) are highly expressed in POLE-mutated EC, alongside increased TIL infiltration. This is likely because POLE mutations drive tumor hypermutation, which in turn enhances neoantigen expression and potentially amplifies immunogenic responses [201, 202].

POLE-EDMs exert a favorable effect on prognostic outcomes. Research has demonstrated that only a small subset of somatic mutations in tumor cells are classified as driver mutations, which lead to cancer cells being unable to undergo normal differentiation—ultimately transforming them into malignantly proliferative entities characterized by unchecked growth and division. Moreover, most mutations are passenger mutations (functionally neutral, not subject to selection, and not involved in carcinogenesis, acting like “passengers”).

Recent studies have shown that the accumulation of passenger mutations can disrupt tumor cells, inhibit their proliferation, and potentially induce cell death through various mechanisms, including the synthesis of toxic proteins, the stimulation of immune responses, or the impairment of tumor cell function. In POLE-mutated tumors, the loss of proofreading activity results in the extensive generation of base errors during DNA replication, thereby contributing to genomic instability and increased mutational burden. The gradual accumulation of passenger mutations in cells may either slow the proliferation of POLE-mutated tumor cells or induce their apoptosis—this may serve as a key factor underlying the favorable prognostic outcomes observed in POLE-mutated neoplasms.

The prognosis of patients with POLE mutations has been further investigated in several studies involving the use of ICIs. Studies by Min et al. indicate that levels of infiltrating T cells were elevated in POLE-mutated NSCLC, and these patients had a favorable prognosis [203]. The KEYNOTE-028 study reported a partial response in a patient with POLE-mutated advanced EC who received pembrolizumab for 8 weeks and maintained a response thereafter [204]. Although POLE mutations outside the exonuclease domain do not induce a high mutational burden, they may enhance immunogenicity through a mechanism independent of neoantigen presentation.

Conclusion

With the continuous development of immunotherapy in recent years, it has become a major hotspot in medical research. However, while initial results of anti-tumor immune efficacy have been observed, accompanying drawbacks have also emerged: most immunotherapies show excellent efficacy initially, but drug resistance subsequently develops, which significantly reduces treatment efficacy for patients. At this point, DDR and its role in promoting tumor cell progression have attracted widespread attention, and the development of DDR inhibitors has given people hope of inhibiting tumor proliferation. Moreover, combining DDRi with other DDRi or immunotherapies such as ICIs can enhance anti-tumor responses and reduce the occurrence of drug resistance. Even more promisingly, POLE/POLD1 mutations have been found to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy, and the benefits of combination immunotherapy have become more apparent.

After years of intensive research, a variety of inhibitors targeting DDR pathways have been successfully developed worldwide. Furthermore, DDR inhibitors can be used in combination with standard treatment approaches—for example, PARP inhibitors are used to enhance the efficacy of platinum-based drugs, and other studies are exploring the combination of DDR inhibitors with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Precision targeted therapy, which has the potential to gradually replace traditional chemotherapy and reduce severe adverse effects, is expected to improve both the survival time and quality of life of patients with advanced cancer in the future.

DDRi have gradually emerged as promising therapeutic agents in combination with anti-tumor immunotherapeutics, which effectively enhances anti-tumor immune responses. The exploration of the roles of various key DDR proteins and the development of related inhibitors are becoming increasingly important. Although the combination of PARPi and ICIs has yielded positive results, other DDR inhibitors still require further research in the context of combination therapy, for example, certain severe adverse reactions. Further clinical trials on DDRi combination therapies will expand the scope of applicable combination therapies and improve patient prognosis.
