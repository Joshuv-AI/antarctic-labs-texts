The application of drug delivery systems based on targeted protein degradation chimeras in disease treatment strategies
Authors: Weiqi Lin, Enqi Qiao, Zhongkai Wang, Chunqing Tang, Chenghui Li, Jian Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the technology of targeted protein degradation (TPD) has developed rapidly. Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have achieved remarkable results in the specific clearance of target proteins through the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Meanwhile, novel strategies such as Molecular Glue, autophagosome tethering compounds (ATTEC), lysosome targeting chimeras (LYTAC) and autophagy targeting chimeras (AUTOTAC) have successively emerged. These approaches, through their distinct mechanisms, collectively expand the targeting dimensions of protein degradation. Unlike the occupancy-driven mechanism of traditional small-molecule inhibitors, TPD is event-driven. It can target “undruggable” proteins that are difficult to intervene with traditional small-molecule drugs. TPD can efficiently degrade target proteins at low doses, significantly reduce off-target toxicity and overcome drug resistance while enhancing the selectivity and specificity of drugs. However, most TPD face challenges such as poor solubility, low permeability, low bioavailability, poor tissue selectivity and the Hook effect, which prevent them from entering clinical trials in humans. Drug delivery systems (DDS) offer new insights into addressing these issues by improving the physicochemical properties of TPD, optimizing their pharmacokinetic characteristics and acting as transport carriers to increase tissue specificity, thereby enhancing the targeting of treatment. This review systematically summarizes the application progress of TPD technology in combination with DDS in neurodegenerative diseases, oncological diseases and vascular inflammatory diseases, and prospects for its future development.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Targeted protein degradation, Drug delivery systems, Inflammation, Cancer, Neurodegenerative disease

Introduction

As the core components of cellular functions, proteins play an extremely important role in basic research and therapeutic applications [1]. However, traditional protein regulation methods have many limitations. For example, genetic manipulation is difficult to widely apply to the precise regulation of proteins due to technical complexity and ethical restrictions. Meanwhile, pharmacological methods based on inhibitors are unable to effectively target many proteins due to the scarcity of specific antagonists. These proteins are clinically referred to as “undruggable” proteins [2]. More than 80% of pathogenic proteins are “undruggable” proteins. The common feature of “undruggable” proteins is that their surfaces are relatively flat, lacking pockets that traditional small-molecule drugs can bind to. Moreover, their functions involve complex protein-protein interactions (PPIs) [3], it is difficult to effectively intervene with small-molecule drugs [4].

In recent times, the continuous evolution of emerging targeted protein degradation (TPDs), such as proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and lysosome targeting chimeras (LYTACs), has offered fresh perspectives for new drug research and development [5, 6]. TPD differs significantly from traditional small-molecule inhibitors, as they eliminate target proteins rather than modulate their functions [7, 8]. One end of the PROTAC recruits and binds to the protein of interest (POI), while the other end recruits and binds to the ubiquitin ligase (E3). By forming an E3-PROTAC-POI ternary complex, it induces the polyubiquitination of the POI, which is subsequently degraded by the 26 S proteasome [9]. PROTAC relies on proteasomal degradation of the POI, while the emergence of LYTAC and other TPD technologies that depend on lysosomal degradation of the POI has significantly expanded the scope of TPD applications(Fig. 1A) [10]. Lysosomes are the primary degradative compartments of the cell, capable of degrading materials through three distinct mechanisms: endocytosis, phagocytosis, and autophagy (Fig. 1B). LYTAC recruits the POI at one end and the lysosome targeting receptor (LTR) at the other, inducing the formation of a ternary complex of LTR-LYTAC-POI that is internalized into lysosomes, thereby achieving efficient degradation of the POI [5].

Protein degradation pathways dependent on proteasomes and lysosomes. However, the majority of TPDs currently remain in the preclinical research stage, facing challenges in transitioning from the laboratory to clinical application [11]. The main obstacles include poor solubility, low cellular permeability, insufficient bioavailability, nonspecific distribution and unpredictable Hook effects [12]. These unfavorable physicochemical properties and pharmacokinetic characteristics severely restrict the clinical application of TPD. To break through these bottlenecks, researchers have begun to explore the combination of TPD with advanced drug delivery systems, (DDS) to optimize their in vivo performance [13].

As an interdisciplinary cutting-edge field, DDS relies on the precise design of nanocarriers to achieve spatiotemporally controlled drug delivery [14]. Passive targeting prepared via self-assembly, emulsification and other methods achieve selective enrichment of nanocarriers such as vesicles, liposomes, inorganic nanoparticles, MOFs and exosomes at lesion sites through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of tumor vasculature, without ligand modification(Fig. 2). Bioconjugation-based active targeting systems can not only enhance delivery efficiency by leveraging the specific recognition between antibodies, peptide ligands and cell surface receptors, but also enable prodrugs to be precisely delivered to lesion sites and released on demand via conjugation with environmentally responsive chemical bonds [15]. More notably, emerging bispecific antibodies have evolved into a novel type of combined targeting strategy through their synergistic blocking mechanism. By enhancing the spatiotemporally precise delivery of degraders, these approaches lay the technological foundation for a paradigm shift in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, tumors, and vascular inflammatory diseases (Table 1).

TPD Dependent on ubiquitination

PROTACs primarily degrade intracellular proteins, as well as soluble, short-lived, and misfolded monomeric proteins. PROTACs are heterobifunctional small-molecule degraders composed of two ligands connected by a linker (Fig. 3A). One ligand recruits and binds to the POI, while the other ligand recruits and binds to an E3 ubiquitin ligase. The formation of the E3-PROTAC-POI ternary complex induces polyubiquitination of the POI, which is subsequently degraded by the 26 S proteasome [39]. This unique “event-driven” mechanism enables it to target “undruggable” proteins that are difficult for traditional small-molecule drugs to intervene in, thereby breaking through the bottleneck of traditional drug development. Another proteasome-dependent TPD technology is Molecular Glues (Fig. 3C) [24]. Molecular glue primarily induces PPI between E3 and POI, forming a ternary complex, thereby ubiquitinating POI and leading to its degradation by the proteasome. Compared to PROTAC, Molecular glue has a smaller molecular weight, simpler chemical structure, less spatial interference and better draggability. However, Molecular glue cannot be obtained through large-scale screening of its components like PROTAC, and its design is more challenging than that of PROTAC. Double-mechanism degraders are a special class of molecular entities (Fig. 3B) [25]. Its core feature lies in the dual mode of action of a single molecule as both a PROTAC and a Molecular glue, thereby inducing the degradation of two target proteins. Such compounds demonstrate potential value in enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

E3 ubiquitin ligase

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) generally relies on the coordinated activity of three distinct enzymes, namely ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2) and E3 [40]. To date, two E1s have been reported in the human proteome, alongside roughly 40 E2s. There are also approximately 600 E3s—of which 40% are thought to be involved in the UPS. Based on their ubiquitin transfer mechanisms, E3 can be broadly classified into three categories: RING-type, HECT-type and RBR-type (Table 3). Although there are numerous types, only a small fraction are widely utilized in PROTAC design [41]. Common E3s such as cereblon (CRBN) and Von Hoppel-Lindau (VHL) are widely expressed in various tissues, providing a design basis for protein degradation agents targeting various tissues [42]. The current exploration of new E3 ligases is increasingly guided by computational predictions of their interface interactions with substrates, thus enabling the de novo design of degraders for “undruggable” targets [43].

TPD technology relying on lysosomes

TPD, which depends on lysosomes, primarily degrades membrane proteins, extracellular proteins, long-lived proteins, protein aggregates and damaged organelles, greatly expanding the range of substrates. Lysosomes are the main degradation compartments in cells, degrading substances through three different mechanisms: endocytosis, phagocytosis and autophagy [64–67]. LYTACs that depend on the endocytosis-lysosomal pathway are mainly composed of three key components: an oligosaccharide motif that targets the LTR on the cell surface, a ligand specific to the POI and a linker segment that links these two moieties. The action mechanism of LYTACs involves the following sequential steps: first, the ligand moiety targeting the POI associates with the extracellular domain of the POI, whereas the oligosaccharide motif binds to the cell surface-localized LTR, thereby forming a ternary complex designated as “LTR-LYTAC-target protein”; Subsequently, this complex undergoes internalization into the cell through clathrin-mediated endocytosis and is delivered to early endosomes via vesicular trafficking; as the endosomes undergo acidification, the LTR within the ternary complex dissociates from the complex and recycles back to the cell membrane or Golgi apparatus, while the LYTAC-POI complex is subsequently delivered to lysosomes for degradation [5].

The autophagy-lysosome pathway, as an important degradation pathway within cells, provides the basis for technologies such as autophagy-targeted chimeras (AUTAC), autophagy-trapping compounds (ATTEC) and autophagy-targeted chimeras (AUTOTAC) (Fig. 4) [29–31]. AUTAC exerts its function through a bifunctional molecule, with one end specifically recognizing the POI via a ligand and the other end connected to a guanine derivative as an autophagy tag, mimicking S-guanosine modification to induce K63-linked polyubiquitination of the POI. p62 possesses a ubiquitin-associated domain (UBA), which recognizes the K63 ubiquitin chain of the POI. Subsequently, through its PB1 domain, it interacts with the LC3 protein on the autophagosome membrane, mediating the encapsulation of the target protein by the autophagosome. After fusion with the lysosome, the target protein is completely degraded under the action of lysosomal acidic hydrolases. ATTEC technology bypasses the traditional ubiquitination modification step, directly bridging the target protein with the autophagy-related protein LC3. AUTOTAC is an optimized version of AUTAC, directly activating the autophagy receptor p62 without relying on ubiquitin tags. Unlike AUTAC, which depends on a single p62 pathway, AUTOTAC can utilize multiple autophagy receptors to form a broader autophagy degradation network within cells [31].

Lysosome-dependent protein targeted degradation technology. A) Various protein targeted degradation chimeras dependent on different lysosomal pathways, such as endocytosis, phagocytosis, and autophagy. B) Specific degradation pathways of different protein targeted degradation chimeras

GlueTAC efficiently degrades membrane proteins via three key synergistic components: Gluebody, cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) and lysosomal sorting sequence (LSS). The Gluebody—an engineered nanobody with unnatural amino acids—forms covalent bonds with target membrane proteins through proximity-induced cross-linking, stabilizing the GlueTAC-target complex during endocytosis. The arginine-rich CPP aids clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the entire complex into cells, boosting transmembrane delivery. Additionally, the LSS’s NPXY motif interacts with clathrin adaptors, directing endosomes to lysosomes for degradation. Concurrently, V-ATPase on lysosomal membranes maintains an acidic environment, activating lysosomal acid hydrolases that break down target proteins into small molecules like amino acids and nucleotides. The Gluebody’s covalent binding prevents complex dissociation during endocytosis, ensuring degradation efficiency. CPP-LSS synergistically enhances endocytosis and lysosomal targeting efficiency, overcoming the limitations of the proteasome pathway for membrane protein degradation and establishing a universal membrane protein degradation strategy independent of cell surface receptor expression [33].

Degraders based on molecular chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA-based degraders) emulate the intracellular CMA pathway, thereby effecting the selective degradation of POIs [32]. CMA-based degraders comprise a ligand directed against the target protein, a KFERQ motif and CPP, functioning essentially as a peptide. Following cellular entry, the CMA-based degrader binds specifically to the POI through its targeting ligand, whereas the KFERQ motif is recognized by the molecular chaperone HSC70, leading to the formation of a ternary complex. HSC70’s ATPase activity mediates the unfolding of the substrate protein, allowing it to gain transmembrane transport capacity. Subsequently, the complex associates with the LAMP-2 A receptor on the lysosomal membrane, inducing the polymerization of LAMP-2 A monomers into channels measuring roughly 2–3 nm in diameter. The unfolded target protein translocates into the lysosomal lumen via this channel; thereafter, HSC70 dissociates from LAMP-2 A and recycles to the cytoplasm for involvement in the subsequent cycle [68].

Lysosome targeting receptors

The efficient degradation function of lysosomes depends on substrate-specific recognition and directed transport mediated by lysosomal targeting receptors (LTRs) [69], these receptors precisely guide substrates into lysosomal degradation compartments via different pathways by binding to specific molecular tags (Fig. 5). The cation-independent mannose-6-phosphate receptor (CI-M6PR) and its homologous molecule, the insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor (IGF2R), constitute the core regulatory system for lysosomal enzyme sorting. Both of which recognize the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) group on newly synthesized lysosomal hydrolases and, through receptor-mediated endocytosis (RME), target and transport them from the Golgi apparatus or cell surface to lysosomes, playing a crucial role in lysosomal biogenesis [70]; It is worth noting that the insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor (IGF2R) also binds to insulin-like growth factor 2 and other ligands, mediating their lysosomal degradation to regulate cell growth signaling pathways. Additionally, the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) is highly expressed on the surface of hepatocytes, capturing the terminal galactose or N-acetylgalactosamine residues exposed on glycoproteins to drive RME activity, thereby achieving efficient clearance of ASGPR from the bloodstream. This pathway constitutes the core processing mechanism for metabolic waste in the liver [71]. In terms of immune defense and cellular homeostasis maintenance, scavenger receptor class A (SR-A) acts as a pattern recognition receptor that can widely bind to disease-related molecules such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) and bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS). These molecules are delivered to lysosomes for degradation via endocytosis or phagocytosis. This process plays a dual role in the formation of atherosclerotic plaques and defense against pathogens [72]; Lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 A (LAMP-2 A) acts as a receptor specific to the CMA pathway, recognizing the KFERQ-like motif of cytoplasmic proteins and mediating their transmembrane translocation, thereby achieving selective clearance of misfolded proteins [73]. At the same time, some nutrient transport proteins also have lysosomal targeting functions: glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) not only regulates sugar uptake, but its lysosomal localization also participates in maintaining the metabolic microenvironment of the lumen [74]; Folic acid receptor α (FRα) binds folate derivatives with high affinity and delivers bioactive molecules via the endocytosis-lysosome pathway [75]; The transferrin receptor (TFR) mediates the internalization of iron-carrying transferrin and releases iron ions in the acidic environment of lysosomes to maintain metal homeostasis [35]. These receptors collectively construct a multidimensional regulatory network for lysosome-targeted degradation through differentiated ligand recognition patterns and subcellular localization.

Development of emerging TPD modes

Classical TPD technologies primarily rely on two native intracellular degradation systems: the ubiquitin–proteasome system and the lysosomal degradation pathway. Although these strategies have fully validated the therapeutic potential of TPD, they still face numerous challenges, including dependence on specific E3 ligases or lysosomal receptors, limited degradation efficiency for certain target types, and potential off-target toxicity. To overcome these bottlenecks, researchers are actively exploring a series of more innovative TPD modes. These emerging technologies aim to expand the scope of degradable targets, achieve precise spatiotemporal regulation, and revolutionize the degrader design paradigm with the aid of artificial intelligence, injecting new vitality into the field of TPD. This section will focus on these emerging TPD technologies that transcend the classical paradigm, which collectively drive the field of TPD toward a broader and more precise future.

RNA-targeted degraders

RNA-targeted degraders represent a family of TPD technologies that target RNA as their substrate. By specifically recognizing and inducing the degradation of pathogenic RNAs, they indirectly regulate downstream protein expression, effectively covering the “undruggable” RNA targets that are inaccessible to traditional small-molecule inhibitors. To date, several representative platforms have been developed, including ribonuclease-targeting chimeras (RIBOTACs), iRIBOTAC, LipoSM-RiboTAC, and Aptamer-RIBOTAC [76–79]. As a classic representative in this field, RIBOTACs are bifunctional chimeric molecules, featuring a modular structure of “RNA-specific ligand–ribonuclease-recruiting domain”. By recognizing the unique secondary structure of pathogenic RNAs, they precisely recruit endogenous ribonucleases (such as RNase H and RNase L) and trigger enzymatic degradation reactions [76].

Deubiquitinase-targeting chimeras

Deubiquitinase-targeting chimeras (DUBTACs) target deubiquitinases (DUBs) as their core functional entities, featuring a bifunctional design of “DUB-specific ligand–E3 ubiquitin ligase-recruiting domain”. This technology induces the formation of a ternary complex between DUBs and E3 ubiquitin ligases, promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of DUBs themselves, thereby relieving their stabilizing effect on downstream target proteins. Notably, DUBTACs have for the first time incorporated the DUB family into the TPD target repertoire. By regulating the “deubiquitination–ubiquitination balance” of the UPS, they achieve indirect regulation of multiple downstream pathogenic proteins, ultimately yielding a “one drug, multiple targets” therapeutic effect [80].

Nuclear receptor pathway

The core innovation of TPD targeting the nuclear receptor pathway lies in the integration of a trifunctional structure comprising an “MDM2-binding domain–CRBN ligand–nuclear receptor ligand unit”, which combines the direct degradation activity of classical PROTACs with nuclear receptor-mediated transcriptional regulatory functions [81]. Its mechanism of action exhibits a dual synergistic effect: on the one hand, it mediates the binding of MDM2 to CRBN through the classical PROTAC mode, initiating ubiquitination and degradation to release the tumor suppressor protein p53; on the other hand, the nuclear receptor ligand unit binds to RARα/PPARγ highly expressed in cancer cells, activating transcriptional regulatory programs and upregulating the expression level of E3 ubiquitin ligases, thereby forming a “degradation efficiency amplification loop”. Studies have shown that MD-224 efficiently induces MDM2 degradation in human leukemia cells at a concentration of less than 1 nM, demonstrating excellent in vivo efficacy. In addition, the tissue-specific expression characteristics of nuclear receptors—high expression in cancer cells and low expression in normal tissues—significantly reduce off-target toxicity and improve therapeutic safety.

Environment-responsive TPD

Environment-responsive TPD technology relies on bioconjugation strategies. By incorporating environment-sensitive moieties—such as photo-responsive groups [82] and hypoxia-sensitive groups—into the structure of degrader molecules, prodrug-type TPD molecules (Pro-PROTACs) are constructed to achieve spatiotemporally precise regulation of degradation function [83]. Its core design principle is to maintain biological inertness under normal physiological conditions and activate degradation activity only in specific microenvironments such as hypoxia, acidity, and high GSH levels, or under exogenous stimuli including light and radiation (Table 3). This strategy has a broad scope of application and can significantly reduce the systemic off-target toxicity of conventional TPD molecules. The caging strategy represents the core approach to realizing such technologies, with the photo-responsive sr-PROTAC as a typical representative [84]. It employs a photosensitive group to cage the key active sites of PROTAC molecules, restoring degradation activity only in regions illuminated by light of a specific wavelength, thereby effectively enhancing the spatial specificity of therapy.

Combination strategies of DDS and TPD

Although TPD technologies have conceptually broken through the limitations of traditional occupancy-driven inhibitors, their clinical translation is facing severe challenges arising from the intrinsic molecular properties of TPD agents. Most TPD molecules possess high molecular weights and pronounced hydrophobicity, which directly lead to issues such as poor water solubility, low cell membrane permeability, and unsatisfactory oral bioavailability. In addition, their relatively large molecular size renders them unable to effectively penetrate the blood–brain barrier or infiltrate deep into solid tumors following systemic administration. More complexly, the “event-driven” catalytic mechanism of TPD may result in the “Hook effect” at high doses, causing degradation efficiency to decline rather than increase; their non-specific distribution may trigger off-target toxicity and limit the therapeutic window due to insufficient accumulation at lesion sites. Against this background, the integration of TPD technologies with advanced DDS is no longer a simple auxiliary approach but a key enabling strategy to overcome the aforementioned bottlenecks and unlock their full therapeutic potential. Through precise material design and functional modification, DDS can reshape the in vivo fate of TPD agents from three dimensions: spatial distribution, temporal regulation, and dose optimization. Its core objectives are to achieve three critical functions: structural protection in circulation, specific targeted enrichment at lesion sites, and on-demand controlled release at the site of action. Non-covalent binding

Physical encapsulation is the most fundamental strategy, whose core lies in encapsulating highly hydrophobic TPD molecules into the hydrophobic cavities of various nanocarriers through non-covalent interactions such as van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, and electrostatic interactions. Common delivery systems include liposomes [92], MOFs [20], and vesicles [21]. This strategy can effectively improve the water solubility and stability of TPD molecules and enable their enrichment in tumor tissues via the natural EPR effect of nanocarriers. However, it faces challenges such as limited drug loading and burst release. To overcome these limitations, host–guest assembly strategies have emerged [69]. This strategy utilizes highly specific “host–guest” molecular pairs such as cyclodextrin–adamantane to precisely anchor TPD molecules in delivery systems via reversible non-covalent bonds. This supramolecular mode not only significantly improves drug loading efficiency, but the dynamic and reversible binding nature also endows the system with the ability to terminate degradation on demand.

Chemical conjugation and in-situ construction

Chemical conjugation is a classic paradigm for constructing targeted prodrugs. On one hand, environment-responsive chemical bonds can be directly introduced into TPD molecules to enable the activation and regulation of prodrugs. Responsive bonds such as acid-sensitive hydrazone bonds, enzyme-sensitive peptide bonds, and reduction-sensitive disulfide bonds can cleave under conditions like tumor acidity, cathepsin cleavage, or high intracellular glutathione, thereby activating TPD degradation activity and effectively reducing off-target toxicity. On the other hand, TPD molecules can also be covalently conjugated to targeting carriers such as antibodies, peptides, and aptamers to achieve the synergistic effect of targeted delivery and activation.

On the basis of traditional chemical conjugation, the more advanced template-guided in-situ construction strategy upgrades the synthetic process from pre‑setting in vitro to efficient assembly guided by templates in vivo. This strategy encompasses two sophisticated implementation pathways:

First, the template synthesis strategy. Its core lies in designing the nanocarrier itself as a prefabricated “reaction template”: one component of the TPD molecule, such as an E3 ligase ligand, is first covalently anchored onto the nanocarrier surface. After the carrier is delivered to the target site, another component of the TPD molecule, such as a target protein ligand, is introduced or activated. The latter undergoes a specific chemical coupling reaction to in-situ assemble into a structurally intact, highly active TPD molecule. Second, in-situ synthesis initiated entirely in vivo. Distinct from template synthesis, no components are pre‑immobilized; instead, two inactive precursor reaction modules are delivered simultaneously. Upon reaching the target site, they are converted into active moieties by the local microenvironment and covalently coupled into functional TPD via bioorthogonal reactions [93].

Self-assembled nanoparticles

The self-assembly strategy abandons exogenous carriers and pioneers a “drug-carrier integrated” design paradigm. In this strategy, TPD molecules are rationally designed as amphiphilic building blocks, enabling them to spontaneously assemble into well-structured nanoparticles in aqueous solutions via intermolecular forces. Based on the differences in assembly modes of TPD molecules, such self-assembled nanoparticle TPD systems are mainly divided into two categories: the first is self-assembly mediated by TPD molecules themselves, that is, through amphiphilic modification of TPD molecules, they can spontaneously form nanoparticle structures relying solely on synergistic intermolecular forces without the aid of other carrier components, achieving self-loading and delivery [94]; The second is self-assembly mediated by the conjugation of TPD with self-assembling units. In this approach, TPD molecules are covalently linked to molecular units with self-assembly capability. These self-assembling units first spontaneously form a nanocarrier scaffold in aqueous solutions via intermolecular interactions, and the TPD molecules attached to them are synchronously loaded into the nanoparticle. Leveraging the excellent biocompatibility and targeting potential of the self-assembling units, efficient delivery and in vivo activity regulation of TPD are achieved.

Biosynthesis

The biosynthesis strategy employs the most fundamental intervention approach: instead of directly delivering TPD molecules, it delivers genetic information. This strategy uses DDS to deliver mRNA or DNA encoding TPD functional macromolecules into the body. Subsequently, host cells utilize their own gene expression systems to continuously produce and secrete these degraders. This strategy greatly expands the target scope of TPD and offers the potential for long‑acting therapy, representing a cutting‑edge technology for the future.

The “ClickRNA‑PROTAC” technology developed by Academician Li’s team ingeniously integrates the advantages of mRNA transfection and click chemistry. Through mRNA transfection, E3 ubiquitin ligase fusion proteins bearing a covalent tag are expressed intracellularly. Then, a ligand modified with dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) specifically binds to the covalent tag of the fusion protein, completing the functionalization of the fusion protein. Finally, an azide‑conjugated POI ligand is efficiently linked to the functionalized fusion protein to form a complete functional degradation complex, which in turn induces POI ubiquitination and degradation by the cellular proteasome [53].

The synergy between DDS and TPD has achieved a critical leap from simple “transport-unload” to in-depth “integrated” design. Hierarchically progressive strategies such as physical encapsulation, chemical covalent conjugation, and in-situ self-assembly have precisely addressed core bottlenecks of TPD including poor water solubility and insufficient targeting. Meanwhile, cutting-edge directions like biosynthesis have further expanded its technological boundaries. In the future, with the deep integration of carrier material engineering, bioorthogonal chemistry, and gene-editing technologies, it is expected to further enhance the in vivo safety, targeting precision, and therapeutic long-acting nature of TPD, accelerating the translation of this highly promising therapeutic technology from basic research to clinical application.

Currently, TPD technologies have evolved from the classic strategies initially relying on the ubiquitin–proteasome system and lysosomal pathways to a variety of emerging paradigms, including RNA-targeted degraders, DUBTACs, and environment-responsive TPD. Driven by artificial intelligence, TPD has further undergone a paradigm upgrade from empirical exploration to rational design, significantly expanding target coverage and degradation efficiency. However, both classic and emerging TPD strategies still face core challenges such as poor water solubility, insufficient tissue targeting, and low in vivo delivery efficiency. These bottlenecks impose higher demands on the precision and intelligence of delivery systems. The integration of TPD with DDS is expected to markedly improve the targeted delivery efficiency of degraders, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing adverse reactions. This review systematically discusses the research progress and application potential of TPD–DDS combination strategies in neurodegenerative diseases, tumors, and vascular inflammatory diseases. This synergistic approach not only holds promise for overcoming the delivery barriers of current TPD technologies but also provides new ideas and directions for the treatment of related major diseases.

Application of TPD combined with DDS in diseases

Neurodegenerative diseases

With the accelerated aging of the global population, the incidence and mortality of neurodegenerative disorders including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are exhibiting a marked upward trend. AD is defined by progressive cognitive decline, and its pathological mechanisms include the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques within the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, the abnormal phosphorylation of tau protein and chronic neuroinflammation induced by the overactivation of microglia [95]. The core pathology of PD is the degeneration and loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and the formation of α-synuclein Lewy bodies [96], directly leading to core motor disorders such as bradykinesia, resting tremor and muscle rigidity. ALS primarily manifests as progressive muscle weakness and atrophy, with the pathological essence being degenerative changes in both upper and lower motor neurons [97]. TPD technology, characterized by high specificity, can recognize and degrade pathogenic proteins such as amyloid beta (Aβ), tau protein and α-synuclein, thereby intercepting disease progression at the pathological source. Additionally, DDS enable effective crossing of the blood-brain barrier to facilitate precise targeted drug delivery. The synergistic combination of these two technologies generates a synergistic outcome: TPD selectively eliminates abnormal proteins to prevent interference with normal cellular functions, while DDS boosts drug accumulation at lesion sites to reduce systemic toxicity. Such technological integration—by reversing abnormal protein aggregation, preserving neuronal integrity and slowing disease progression—offers promise for addressing the limitations of existing treatments.

Alzheimer’s disease

AD is characterized by progressive cognitive decline and memory impairment. Pathologically, it is marked by abnormal Aβ deposition, hyperphosphorylated tau protein aggregation and neuroinflammation. More importantly, existing clinical drugs are unable to penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to achieve efficient delivery to the brain and lack the ability to specifically clear pathogenic proteins, resulting in a gradual decline in clinical benefits as the disease progresses [101]. Therefore, developing novel therapeutic strategies that can target and degrade Aβ, regulate neuroinflammation and overcome BBB limitations has become a core challenge in the field of AD treatment.

A newly emerging hypothesis suggests that BBB dysfunction is closely related to the pathogenesis of AD [102]. Pathologically elevated RAGE expression in the BBB can bind to Aβ in the peripheral circulatory system, promoting Aβ influx and exacerbating Aβ accumulation in the brain. Therefore, targeting RAGE may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for protecting the BBB and preventing AD progression. Based on this, Professor Gao’s team developed an endocytosis-activated lysosomal shuttle nanocomposite (endoTAC). The PLGA5000-PEG2000-MAL copolymer self-assembles into nanoparticles with exposed maleimide (MAL) groups on their surfaces (Fig. 7a). Subsequently, the thiolated RAP peptide covalently couples with the MAL groups on the nanoparticle surface via a Michael addition reaction, forming stable sulfide bonds. ultimately constructing an endoTAC nanomedicine system targeting the RAGE receptor. This system induces endocytosis through multivalent ligand-receptor interactions, enhancing RAGE degradation within lysosomes. Finally, simvastatin (SV) is loaded onto the system, forming SV@endoTAC. In vivo studies demonstrated that SV@endoTAC-treated FAD4 transgenic mice displayed greater nanoparticle accumulation in the brain relative to the non-targeted group—with notable enrichment in the hippocampus and cortex. Specifically, Aβ deposition was diminished, while levels of ROS and neuroinflammation were reduced; neuronal count and morphology were restored, and BDNF expression was elevated. Behaviorally, the escape latency in the Morris water maze was shortened by roughly 45%, and the self-alternation rate in the Y maze was enhanced to match wild-type levels. Concurrently, the BBB was repaired, Evans blue leakage was reduced, and RAGE was downregulated; meanwhile, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) were upregulated. In vitro experiments further revealed that SV@endoTAC markedly upregulates LRP1 expression, repairs the tight junction protein ZO-1, reduces Aβ uptake by approximately 22%, decreases ROS levels by 31% and suppresses p65 expression in the NF-κB pathway. From precise molecular-level regulation to systematic repair of the pathological microenvironment and significant improvement in cognitive function, endoTAC offers a new paradigm for AD therapy through an integrated strategy [98].

Addressing the key challenge of clearing extracellular Aβ fibrils in AD, Xiaogang Qu developed a biorthogonal activation-mediated lysosome-targeted chimera (cli-LYTACs) that combines BBB permeability with Aβ aggregate degradation capacity. cli-LYTACs are bifunctional chimeras generated in situ at the lesion site through a biorthogonal reaction catalyzed by Cu²⁺, combining alk-PiB and azi-Man (Fig. 7b). They mediate lysosomal degradation of Aβ aggregates via the CD206 receptor. KPLYs are poly dopamine nanoparticles surface-modified with KD8 peptides, capable of penetrating the BBB and releasing precursor drugs in response to ROS. In vitro experiment, cli-LYTACs promote Aβ endocytosis. When used alone, intracellular Aβ abundance was 1.9-fold higher than in the Aβ-Cu-treated group. Confocal microscopy revealed Aβ relocalization from the cell membrane to intracellular lysosomes, indicating degradation via the CD206-mediated endocytosis-lysosomal pathway. These results suggest that KPLYs are not only an effective combination therapy but also provide new insights into the application of LYTAC in AD treatment [99].

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

ALS is a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disease that primarily affects motor neurons in the spinal cord, brainstem, and cerebral cortex [103]. Research indicates that mutations or abnormal aggregation of the FUS protein are central pathogenic factors in ALS. However, as an intracellular pathogenic protein, FUS is difficult to degrade precisely using antibodies or small molecule inhibitors [104].

To address the above challenges, Xie’s team constructed the DNA nanoblume-oligo-PROTAC system. The DNA nanoblooms were prepared using rolling circle amplification (RCA) technology and surface-modified with hundreds of TfR aptamers (Fig. 7c). They utilize the high expression of TfR in brain microvascular endothelial cells to mediate endocytosis and cross BBB. Their multivalent binding sites can stably load Oligo-PROTAC, with in vitro experiments showing a loading efficiency as high as 79.4%. Oligo-PROTAC is prepared by click chemistry, conjugating RNA oligonucleotides with thalidomide. One end specifically binds to the RGG2 domain of FUS, while the other end recruits CRBN. Through the “bridging effect,” it induces FUS ubiquitination and subsequent degradation by the proteasome. In vivo experiments showed that after a single intravenous injection, the system can actively target the brain via TfR-mediated transport, penetrate the blood-brain barrier within 2 h, and accumulate in brain tissue. It achieves an 80% degradation efficiency for FUS and its mutants, with degradation effects lasting up to 2 weeks. In vivo experiment, FRONTACFUS significantly reduced FUS aggregates in the brain without causing noticeable toxicity or inflammatory reactions. This strategy not only overcomes the dual challenges of “intracellular proteins being undruggable” and “inefficient brain delivery,” providing a precise intervention pathway for ALS, but its modular design can also be adapted for the treatment of other RNA-binding protein-related neurodegenerative diseases by replacing the Oligo-PROTAC sequence to target tau protein and so on, establishing a new paradigm for the synergistic translation of nucleic acid-based PROTACs and brain-targeted carriers [100].

Oncological diseases

Malignant tumors pose a major challenge to global public health. The core of the diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma lies in the inherent contradiction between the complex biological characteristics of tumors and existing treatment methods. Currently, the pan-toxicity of traditional chemotherapy, the problem of drug resistance in molecular targeted therapy, the low response rate of immunotherapy, and the clinical bottleneck in the intervention of metastatic lesions collectively constitute the core barriers to improving therapeutic efficacy [105]. Unlike traditional small molecule inhibitors, PROTACs do not require continuous blocking of protein activity, but instead induce target protein degradation [106], By fundamentally reducing their numbers, this unique mechanism offers the possibility of targeted intervention for “undruggable” proteins.

However, TPD technologies still confront multiple formidable challenges in clinical cancer treatment. These challenges primarily arise from the intrinsic physicochemical properties of TPD molecules: their large molecular weight confers poor aqueous solubility and low cell membrane permeability, thereby restricting drug absorption and systemic distribution; unfavorable pharmacokinetic profiles, including low oral bioavailability and rapid systemic clearance, impede the maintenance of effective drug concentrations within tumor tissues. Furthermore, non-specific distribution following systemic administration may elicit off-target toxicity, while the complex tumor microenvironment—characterized by high interstitial fluid pressure and dense extracellular matrix—further impedes deep tumor penetration. More intractably, the “Hook effect” observed at high doses paradoxically compromises degradation efficiency. To surmount these bottlenecks, the integration of DDS has emerged as a pivotal enabling strategy. By encapsulating TPD molecules within nanocarriers such as liposomes, DDS markedly enhances their solubility and stability while protecting them from premature degradation. Additionally, smart stimuli-responsive DDS can exploit the unique biochemical and biophysical cues of the tumor microenvironment to achieve spatiotemporally controlled drug release, thereby further elevating therapeutic precision. The combined strategy of TPD and DDS, with its innovative mechanism and functional synergy, has become a potential direction for breaking through the above-mentioned treatment bottlenecks (Table 4). This synergy is reflected in multiple aspects: DDS overcomes TPD’s delivery bottlenecks to achieve precise enrichment in the tumor microenvironment, while TPD’s targeted degradation mechanism efficiently eliminates pathogenic proteins at the target, forming a closed-loop synergy of “precision delivery + radical clearance”; DDS optimizes TPD’s pharmacokinetics via immune evasion and controlled release, prolonging its tumor retention and half-life to provide a sustained window for catalytic degradation, and TPD remodels the tumor immune microenvironment post-degradation, with long-retained TPD continuously driving immune activation to realize “long-acting degradation + immune amplification”; additionally, DDS co-delivers TPD and chemotherapeutics—TPD degrades oncoproteins to reverse drug resistance and inhibit proliferation, while chemotherapeutics directly kill tumor cells, forming a complementary synergy of “protein degradation + chemical cytotoxicity” that enhances efficacy, reduces single-agent dosage and mitigates toxicity. In summary, the integration of TPD and DDS addresses core challenges in TPD’s clinical translation through multi-dimensional synergy, showing broad prospects in cancer therapy.

PROTACs mediate the degradation of target proteins via the ubiquitin-proteasome system and exhibit unique advantages for cancer therapy. Nevertheless, their restricted in vivo efficacy and pharmacokinetic drawbacks have impeded their clinical translation. Degrader-antibody conjugates (DACs) function as a novel targeted delivery platform, which are constructed by covalently conjugating PROTACs to monoclonal antibodies through a linker. They leverage the tumor-targeting properties of antibodies to achieve precise delivery of PROTAC [110]. Compared to traditional antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) [111, 112], The payload of the DAC is a PROTAC rather than a cytotoxic small molecule. What’s more, its mechanism of action involves catalyzing the degradation of oncogenic proteins rather than directly killing cells. It combines targeted enrichment with sustained degradation properties, significantly reducing off-target toxicity and improving pharmacokinetic parameters. Additionally, DAC extends its half-life in vivo through the antibody Fc fragment and can enhance immunogenicity by modulating the tumor microenvironment, demonstrating a distinct multi-mechanism of action compared to ADCs.

Receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) functions as a tumor-associated antigen that exhibits high expression levels in diverse hematological malignancies and solid tumors, yet shows low expression in normal adult tissues. The ROR1 antigen-antibody complex undergoes rapid internalization into tumor cells, thus rendering ROR1 an optimal candidate for tumor-targeted delivery [113]. Based on this, Zhang’s team constructed a ROR1-targeted DAC (ROR1 DAC). They developed a new kind of DAC by conjugating a PROTAC that degrades BRD4 with an ROR1 antibody (Fig. 8b). In vivo experiments demonstrated that ROR1 DAC exhibited superior pharmacokinetic properties, with a half-life extended to 4.96 days compared to free MZ1. In vivo experiment, a 15 mg/kg intravenous injection reduced tumor volume by 58%, with antitumor efficacy significantly superior to MZ1 monotherapy. When combined with αmPD-1 mAb, it significantly increased the proportion of CD8⁺ T cells infiltrating solid tumors. RNA sequencing revealed upregulation of Th1-biased cytokine mRNA levels in the tumor microenvironment, markedly enhancing antitumor immune responses. In vitro experiments showed that ROR1 DAC exhibited significant dose-dependent activity after binding to antigens and internalization, with a BRD4 degradation rate approaching 90% at 10 nM. It also induced an increase in the G2/M phase proportion of the cell cycle and elevated the combined early and late apoptosis rate to 17.2%, demonstrating potent degradation activity and cytotoxicity [108].

The nucleolin-targeting aptamer AS1411 (AS), cytotoxic fluorouracil (Fdu) and nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) PROTAC are conjugated via a tumor microenvironment-responsive linker (Fig. 8c). In the tumor microenvironment, they are sequentially activated by GSH and DNase II, releasing Fdu and PROTAC to exert synergistic antitumor effects. Its degradation activity against NAMPT is significantly superior to that of free NP, and its cytotoxicity is significantly stronger than that of FU, NP, and their 2:1 combination. In vivo experiments showed that AS-2 F-NP significantly enhanced tumor targeting, achieving a tumor growth inhibition rate (TGI) exceeding 92% within 19 days, far surpassing the 78.8% of the 2FU + NP combination. Additionally, no significant weight loss or organ damage was observed, with toxicity significantly reduced. This “precision targeting + stimulus-responsive release” design significantly improved the uptake rate and retention time of PROTAC in tumor tissues, achieving a dual breakthrough of “targeted delivery enhancement + synergistic mechanism sensitization,” providing new strategies and insights for tumor therapy [109].

Vascular inflammatory diseases

Vascular inflammatory diseases are a major cause of cardiovascular events. Their incidence rate has been rising annually due to population aging and the high prevalence of metabolic syndrome. These diseases are characterized by chronic inflammation of the vascular wall, involving complex pathophysiological processes such as endothelial dysfunction, lipid deposition, and immune cell infiltration, posing a serious threat to human health [137]. Current conventional treatment options primarily include anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, and vascular intervention procedures; however, these approaches have significant limitations. TPD technology achieves precise inhibition of inflammatory cascades by specifically degrading inflammation-related pathogenic proteins, such as NF-κB and IL-6 receptors, while DDS enables targeted accumulation and controlled release of drugs within diseased vessels. The combination of the two can exert a synergistic effect, reducing off-target effects and increasing local treatment concentrations, while also inhibiting inflammation progression at its source by degrading key pathogenic proteins (Fig. 9). This offers a novel strategy for reversing vascular lesions and lowering recurrence rates, and holds the potential to markedly enhance clinical outcomes in patients with vascular inflammatory diseases.

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis (AS) is the main pathological basis of cardiovascular disease and causes a heavy health burden worldwide [141]. The pathological process of AS is complex and characteristic. AS starts with vascular endothelial cell damage. Endothelial dysfunction causes barrier damage, enabling blood low-density lipoprotein (LDL) to infiltrate the subendothelium and oxidize into toxic oxidized LDL (ox-LDL); ox-LDL then chemotaxes monocytes into the endothelium, where they differentiate into macrophages [142]. Macrophages take in a bunch of ox-LDL through scavenger receptors, turning into foam cells packed with lipids, which is a typical early sign. As the disease progresses, smooth muscle cells move from the middle layer of blood vessels to the inner layer and multiply. Current clinical treatment guidelines recommend statins for primary and secondary prevention of AS. However, statins have numerous and severe side effects, with the most common adverse reactions being muscle symptoms such as myalgia and muscle weakness. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need for safer and more effective drug treatments for AS.

Based on the above issues, Xuesong Li and Hongshan Chen designed PROTAC-PEM conjugated nanoparticles to target and degrade METTL4, effectively slowing the progression of AS. Researchers first produced the METTL4 degradation product-dMETTL4, then encapsulated dMETTL4 within PLGA nanoparticles coated with M2 macrophage membranes, targeting M1 macrophages in plaques via the CD206 receptor on the membrane surface (Fig. 9a). In vitro studies revealed that engineered M2 macrophage membranes encapsulating PROTAC-PEM specifically degraded METTL4 in macrophages, effectively reducing MT-ATP6 DNA adenine 6th position methylation modification, increasing MT-ATP6 expression, improving mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, reducing lipid-induced mtDNA release and inflammation levels, thereby reducing plaque area. These data indicate that PROTAC-PEM exerts its therapeutic effects via the proteasome pathway in vitro. To validate its efficacy in vivo, different doses of PROTAC-PEM were administered to AS mice. The results showed that it significantly reduced aortic plaque deposition, macrophage content in plaques, and inflammatory factor expression. Additionally, mtDNA 6 mA levels were reduced, MTATP6 expression levels increased, mitochondrial ROS decreased, cytoplasmic mtDNA decreased, and IL-1β levels in plaques significantly decreased. These results indicate that the authors have successfully demonstrated for the first time that engineered M2 macrophage membranes encapsulating PROTAC-PEM can be applied to AS treatment by targeting the degradation of METTL4 in macrophages [138].

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is an important subtype of blinding retinal diseases and its neovascular form (n-AMD) is closely associated with a chronic inflammatory microenvironment. Currently, first-line clinical treatment for n-AMD primarily relies on intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF monoclonal antibodies and fusion protein-based drugs. While these therapies can effectively improve patients’ vision in the short term, they still have numerous limitations. For example, long-term inhibition of VEGF may lead to adverse outcomes such as retinal ischemia and compensatory proliferation of neovascularization. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel treatment strategies to overcome the shortcomings of existing therapies and achieve more efficient and sustained therapeutic effects [143]. In this regard, the development of a novel DDS that combines TPD technology and gene regulation functions, through multi-mechanism synergistic intervention in inflammation-driven angiogenesis networks, has become a key strategy for breaking through the treatment dilemma of n-AMD.

To address the aforementioned clinical challenges, Professor Zhang integrated LYTAC with gene silencing functionality to develop a nucleic acid-based LYTAC Plus hydrogel platform, which innovatively enables spatiotemporally controlled intervention of pathogenic proteins. The hydrogel uses a DNA four-arm connector as its scaffold and employs the principle of base complementary pairing to cleverly cross-link the LYTAC unit targeting VEGFR-2 with the siRNA targeting ANG-2, forming a three-dimensional network structure (Fig. 9b). Additionally, after injection into the vitreous cavity, the hydrogel gradually disassembles into nanoscale complexes under physiological conditions, significantly enhancing uptake efficiency by retinal pigment epithelial cells and vascular endothelial cells. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the system can sustainably reduce VEGFR-2 protein levels by 75% within 72 h while inhibiting ANG-2 mRNA expression with over 80% silencing efficiency. In vivo experiment, the hydrogel’s local sustained-release properties extended the drug’s action time to three weeks, reducing the dosing frequency by 50% compared to traditional antibody therapy. Through dual-target inhibition, the hydrogel reduced CNV area by 48% and significantly alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration between retinal layers. As such, this nucleic acid self-assembly-based delivery system, with its excellent biocompatibility, high programmability, and synergistic therapeutic advantages, offers a highly promising innovative paradigm for precision medicine in inflammation-related vascular diseases [22].

Liver fibrosis

Chronic liver fibrosis, as a critical pathological stage in the progression of end-stage liver disease, exhibits a distinct inflammatory-oxidative stress-fibrosis cascade mechanism. Within its inflammatory microenvironment, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) undergo a phenotypic transformation from a quiescent state to a myofibroblastic phenotype, inducing the secretion of extracellular matrix (ECM) components. Concurrently, the abnormal expression of chemokines in the inflammatory microenvironment further recruits inflammatory cell infiltration, accelerating ECM deposition and the remodeling of hepatic lobule structure, ultimately progressing to cirrhosis [144]. Current clinical strategies for treating liver fibrosis still face significant limitations. For example, anti-inflammatory drugs such as glucocorticoids may rapidly alleviate inflammatory responses by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, but their immunosuppressive effects can easily lead to serious adverse reactions such as opportunistic infections and osteoporosis. More seriously, existing therapies generally lack the ability to synergistically intervene in the processes of inflammation and long-term treatment can easily lead to drug resistance, resulting in a gradual decline in disease control [145]. Therefore, developing innovative therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target inflammation regulation and fibrosis reversal is of significant clinical importance.

To overcome the limitations of traditional treatment paradigms, Professor Ren developed a novel NanoPROTAC delivery platform based on the ROS response mechanism. The nanoparticle uses boronic acid-modified ARV771 as its functional core, which self-assembles with poloxamer 188 to form a stable BT-NanoPROTAC delivery system (Fig. 9c). After the nanoparticles accumulate at liver fibrosis lesions via EPR effect, locally high concentrations of ROS can specifically oxidize the boric acid groups, converting them into phenolic groups, thereby triggering the controlled release of ARV771. In normal tissues, where ROS levels are low, the system remains inert. Upon release, ARV771 simultaneously binds to BRD4 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase CRBN, inducing BRD4 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation by the proteasome. This NanoPROTACs system demonstrates multiple synergistic advantages: First, ROS responsiveness confers high lesion-specific targeting, with a half-maximal effective concentration (DC50) for BRD4 degradation as low as 73.51 nM, significantly outperforming the traditional small-molecule inhibitor JQ1; Second, it inhibits Smad3 acetylation while downregulating the expression of fibrosis markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and type I collagen α1 chain (CoL1A1), achieving dual inhibition of inflammation regulation and ECM deposition; Third, the nanoparticles exhibit excellent pharmacokinetic properties in vivo, remaining stable in serum for over 12 h after intravenous injection. A single dose can significantly reduce serum alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) levels within three weeks, reduce collagen deposition area by 48%, and no obvious systemic toxic reactions were observed. In summary, ROS-activated BT-NanoPROTAC employs a triple strategy of “lesion microenvironment triggering-precision protein degradation-multi-target synergistic intervention” to provide a new treatment approach for liver fibrosis that combines safety and translational potential, and offers a modular platform for precision treatment of fibrosis in other organs [139].

Infectious inflammation

Inflammatory diseases caused by viral infections are a major challenge to global public health. Their pathological basis lies in the host immune system’s excessive response to viral invasion. The abnormal release of excessive amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines forms a “cytokine storm,” which in turn triggers systemic inflammatory responses and tissue damage [146]. Current clinical treatment strategies for such diseases primarily encompass antiviral drug interventions, immunomodulation, and supportive therapies. While drugs that directly inhibit viral replication can to some extent block viral proliferation, the issue of drug resistance significantly limits their long-term efficacy; Immune modulation drugs can alleviate inflammatory damage by suppressing an overactivated immune system, but long-term use may lead to adverse reactions such as metabolic disorders; Supportive therapies such as mechanical ventilation and fluid resuscitation cannot fundamentally block the inflammatory cascade induced by the virus. TPD technology exhibits unique therapeutic potential: by precisely degrading key proteins essential for pathogen survival or overactivated inflammatory signaling proteins in host cells, it achieves the fundamental elimination of infectious agents and the curbing of uncontrolled inflammatory cascades at the molecular level. In recent years, TPD strategies have expanded from traditional therapeutic fields to the development of innovative preventive vaccines, forming a novel antiviral paradigm of integrated “prevention and treatment”. At the preventive level, attenuated influenza vaccines based on proteolysis-targeting mechanisms show great promise [147, 148]. At the therapeutic level, remarkable progress has also been made. By employing multi-targeted PROTAC technology to comprehensively target influenza A virus, it can act on multiple viral components simultaneously, thereby raising the barrier to viral escape and enhancing therapeutic efficacy [149]. Another innovative strategy utilizes small molecules and viral RNA mimetics to generate host-directed and virus-specific antiviral pre-PROTACs, which conditionally assemble or activate PROTAC molecules within infected cells. This achieves the clearance of viral reservoirs while preserving uninfected cells intact, attaining unprecedented specificity [150].

However, the direct application of this technology faces a critical biological barrier: the natural biological barriers possessed by pathogens themselves, such as the tough cell walls of bacteria or the dense capsid structures of viruses, effectively block the penetration and intracellular delivery of TPD small molecules, greatly limiting their efficacy. To break through this barrier, the nanoscale engineering design of DDS provides a key solution. By encapsulating TPD molecules in functionalized carriers and leveraging targeting ligands modified on the carrier surface, DDS enables active recognition and anchoring of infectious agents or infected cells, mediating the effective crossing of TPD across biological barriers and precise delivery to the lesion compartments.

Liu’ team has developed C-PROTAC, which offers a breakthrough strategy to address the aforementioned challenges. One end features the DNA aptamer Z3;the middle connecting arm incorporates an N-acyl-N-alkyl sulfonamide (NASA) group, which forms a covalent bond with ZBP1, addressing the issue that traditional PROTACs rely on hydrogen bonds, which are susceptible to changes in target conformation; the other end is a hydroxyproline derivative of the VHL ligand, forming the C-PROTAC (Fig. 9d). Using a liposomal delivery system, the final product NLP@C-PROTAC was obtained. In vivo experimental results showed that the survival rate of mice in the virus-infected group was only 20%, while the survival rate in the NLP@C-PROTAC-treated group increased to 80%, with lung wet-to-dry weight ratio and lung index approaching normal levels. Histological analysis revealed that NLP@C-PROTAC reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in lung tissue and repaired alveolar structure, as confirmed by HE staining. TUNEL staining demonstrated its ability to inhibit virus-induced apoptosis and qRT-PCR indicated a significant reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in lung tissue. Vitro immunofluorescence experiments showed that viral infection significantly upregulates ZBP1 expression, while C-PROTAC treatment significantly reduces ZBP1 levels. By combining the high specificity and covalent binding stability of DNA aptamers with the targeted delivery advantages of liposomal delivery systems, NLP@C-PROTAC offers an innovative strategy for antiviral and anti-inflammatory therapy that combines targeting and safety [140].

Translation Challenges

Despite the tremendous application potential demonstrated by the integration of TPD and DDS in preclinical studies, their clinical translation remains constrained by profound barriers arising from the complexity of molecular mechanisms and biological systems. Beyond the inherent challenges of the Hook effect and conventional pharmacokinetic optimization, critical hurdles—including the limited repertoire of E3 ligases, spatiotemporal dyssynergy in “delivery–release–degradation”, multidrug resistance mediated by tumor heterogeneity, and the translational disconnect between computer-aided design models and in vivo efficacy—all urgently require systematic research to overcome (Fig. 10).

Transformation and Challenges

Limitations of the E3 ligase repertoire and heterogeneity in tissue expression

Current PROTAC development strategies are constrained by the limited E3 ligase repertoire, with most TPD studies relying on a small number of members such as CRBN and VHL. This dependence poses dual risks: first, the expression profile of E3 ligases exhibits significant tissue and pathological specificity—their low expression or absence in target tissues directly impairs degradation efficiency, while constitutive expression in essential organs may mediate off-target toxicity. Second, tumor cells can downregulate E3 ligase expression as an evolutionary drug resistance mechanism, thereby developing tolerance to degraders. Therefore, expanding the E3 ligase toolkit available for targeted degradation—especially identifying E3 members selectively highly expressed in specific disease contexts and developing their corresponding high-affinity ligands—is crucial for improving the therapeutic window and overcoming drug resistance.

The spatiotemporal coupling bottleneck of “Delivery–Release–Deradation”

The entire “delivery–release–degradation” cascade of TPD technology must achieve precise synergy at the cellular level, yet it currently faces three core bottlenecks: low endosomal escape efficiency leads to over 80% of drug carriers being degraded by lysosomes before reaching their target sites, failing to effectively degrade the protein of interest (POI); immunogenicity challenges render exogenous carriers susceptible to recognition and clearance by the immune system, significantly shortening blood circulation time and even triggering cytokine storms, which restricts the safe therapeutic window; and mismatched release kinetics relative to target protein turnover result in a large proportion of active components being retained within carriers as inactive “silent payloads”. Therefore, next-generation delivery systems must address these challenges in an integrated manner: protonatable polymers can enhance endosomal escape through the “proton sponge effect”; multivalent modification of nanoparticles enables simultaneous binding to multiple target receptors on the cell surface to improve POI binding and degradation efficiency; PEGylation can be employed to achieve immune stealth and evade immune clearance; and environmentally responsive linkages allow precise regulation of release kinetics to match drug release rates with the turnover cycle of the target protein, ultimately transforming the mutually restrictive triangular relationship among delivery efficiency, immune tolerance, and pharmacodynamics into a unified system of synergistic enhancement.

Tumor heterogeneity and multidimensional drug resistance mechanisms

Drug resistance represents a core challenge in targeted therapy, with its mechanisms exhibiting high complexity in the context of tumor heterogeneity. Beyond the downregulation of E3 ligase expression, tumor cells can evade degradation through a variety of adaptive alterations, including structural mutations in the target protein that impair degrader binding, changes in the expression or activity of proteasomal subunits, overexpression of non-functional “decoy” proteins that competitively occupy the degradation machinery, and activation of bypass signaling pathways to compensate for the loss of target protein function. Furthermore, repeated administration of nanocarrier-based therapeutics may induce the “accelerated blood clearance” phenomenon, leading to rapid recognition and clearance of carriers by the immune system, altering their pharmacokinetic profiles and reducing targeting efficiency. Overcoming drug resistance requires starting from the source of drug design—for instance, developing bispecific degraders capable of co-degrading multiple pathogenic proteins, or allosteric degraders that can accommodate target mutations—and formulating rational combination therapeutic strategies with drugs acting through other mechanisms.

Computationally empowered degrader design

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and computational methods has driven TPD from a traditional experience-driven paradigm to a new rational design model characterized by “predictability, programmability, and optimizability.” Currently, computational simulation-based prediction of E3 ligase–substrate interface interactions has become a key basis for the rational design of degraders. Since 2022, such computation-driven strategies have gradually emerged as the mainstream approach for the development of novel TPD agents, facilitating the research and development of next-generation TPD molecules with high selectivity.

Nevertheless, existing computational models still exhibit significant limitations: on the one hand, the effectiveness of these models is highly dependent on the scope of training data. Most models are solely built on in vitro binding and cellular activity data, with insufficient integration of in vivo delivery efficiency and pharmacokinetic parameters, such as endosomal escape efficiency, tissue distribution characteristics, and immune clearance rate. This deficiency leads to the failure of numerous computationally optimized high-activity molecules in in vivo experiments, forming a translational gap characterized by “high computational activity but low in vivo efficacy.” On the other hand, the length of the linker in TPD is crucial for TPD efficacy, yet the accurate prediction of its conformational optimization and length matching remains a weak link in current computational models, and effective, systematic rational design has not yet been achieved—this also constitutes a major challenge for the translation of TPD from computational design to clinical application. To realize predictable design “from computation to in vivo application,” the core lies in constructing a multi-level data closed loop covering “molecule–cell–tissue–living organism”.

Conclusion

Looking ahead, the development of TPD technology will place greater emphasis on the in-depth integration of degradation mechanisms and delivery systems, while integrating the rational design capabilities driven by computational biology and AI. To address translational challenges such as limited E3 ligase selectivity and low endosomal escape efficiency, smart responsive nano-degradation platforms have become a current research focus. Within this framework, PROTAC technology is no longer confined to the traditional chemical structure of “bifunctional molecule + flexible linker”; instead, it is gradually evolving toward a novel model of “modifying target protein ligands on the surface of nanocarriers.” This forms design strategies where “nanoparticle-targeting moiety” binds directly or self-assembles into nanoparticles via modified peptides, leveraging the high permeability of nanocarriers to achieve efficient accumulation at tumor sites and improve drug utilization. Meanwhile, emerging gene delivery systems represented by adeno-associated virus (AAV) have gradually become a potential exploration direction in the TPD field. By delivering genes encoding degradation elements to target cells, combined with in situ synthesis and bioorthogonal technologies, it is theoretically feasible to achieve sustained and precise regulation of protein degradation function at lesion sites, thereby breaking through the bottlenecks of traditional delivery methods.

In disease applications, the combined strategy of TPD and DDS exhibits significant synergistic advantages and field-specific differences. In the direction of cancer therapy, relying on the active targeting or EPR effect of nanocarriers, efficient delivery of TPD molecules can be achieved; furthermore, co-delivery of immune checkpoint inhibitors can form a synergistic mechanism of “protein degradation + immune activation,” significantly improving anti-tumor efficacy. In the fields of neurodegenerative diseases and vascular inflammation, this technology is still in the early exploration stage, and breakthroughs in two aspects are urgently needed: first, the development of smart nanocarriers capable of crossing complex biological barriers such as the BBB; second, the design of TPD molecules targeting disease-specific targets and optimization of their retention time in diseased tissues. The key future development direction lies in constructing an “all-in-one” integrated nano-degradation platform, which integrates targeting ligands, stimulus-responsive modules, protein degradation elements, and tissue repair factors into nanocarriers to achieve systematic regulation from clearing pathogenic proteins at the molecular level to reconstructing functions at the tissue level. In this process, computational simulation and AI will continue to play a core role, supporting the rational design of degraders, intelligent optimization of delivery systems, and systematic construction of personalized therapeutic strategies, collectively advancing the clinical translation of TPD technology.
