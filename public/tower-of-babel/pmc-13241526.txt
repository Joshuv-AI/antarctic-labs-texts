Asymmetric alloying for heterogeneous metal-ion clusters of chiral-at-carbon CAuI4AgI6 polyhedra exhibiting red to near-infrared photoluminescence
Authors: Xiao-Li Pei, Pei Zhao, Wen-Ting Liu, Hitoshi Ube, Zhen Lei, Masahiro Ehara, Mitsuhiko Shionoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWell-defined subnano-sized metal clusters are highly susceptible to changes in the type, number and arrangement of the metals. Precise alloying and stereocontrol of metal clusters at the atomic level are therefore expected to elucidate unexplored structures and functions. Here, we report an asymmetric alloying method by adding silver trifluoroacetate to a highly symmetric C-centered hexagold(I) cluster protected by triphenylphosphine, and provide detailed descriptions of the structure and propertied of the resulting chiral-at-carbon hexasilver(I)-alloyed tetragoldmethane, CAuI4AgI6, in a bicapped square antiprism. Theoretical calculations reveal the bonding natures of C–AuI bonds and C···AgI interactions. By using homochiral carboxylate ligands, asymmetric induction to (R,Ʌ)- or (S,∆)-CAuI4AgI6 species is quantitatively achieved, each of which exhibits red to near-infrared phosphorescence and distinct chiroptical activity. This asymmetric silver(I)-alloying involves etching of gold(I) ions from the hexagold(I) cluster, which will further lead to the development of heterometal ion clusters as chiral luminescent materials.Subject terms: Organic-inorganic nanostructures, Synthetic chemistry methodology, Ligands

Introduction

In the synthetic chemistry of metal cluster molecules, the development of atomic-level molecular editing methods is an important challenge, because well-defined subnano-sized metal clusters are highly susceptible to changes in the type, number and arrangement of the constituents. Alloying metal clusters is a powerful approach to expand their structural and functional diversity1–7, leading to structure-specific properties5,6, near-unity near-infrared (NIR) photoluminescent quantum yields5, and unique catalytic reactivities6 and electronic structures7. To clarify the structure-reactivity correlations, site-specific alloying with atomic precision is required. The arrangement of two or more metal atoms, and the variations in the bonding and interactions between the atoms involved, have a significant impact on the symmetry of their overall structure and, therefore, their electronic structure. Currently, a particularly important challenge is to develop an asymmetric alloying method that generates inherent metal core chirality by selectively placing heterometal atoms at non-equivalent sites within a metal cluster to desymmetrize the cluster, and to explore the chiroptical properties and functions of the resulting homochiral clusters. In other words, in addition to the direct induction of chirality using chiral ligands8–10, the development of highly selective asymmetric synthesis methods for heterometal clusters through facile alloying processes is expected to make great progress in providing a source of chirality for chiroptical materials.

Meanwhile, the origin of the intrinsic chirality of ligand-protected gold clusters (including Au0 and AuI clusters) has been elucidated by detailed X-ray crystallography11–16. In particular, the metal core-induced chirality12,13, the ligand-Au interface14, and the ligand arrangement15 are the main factors that explain their intrinsic chirality. In contrast, alloying holds great potential for fundamentally altering the properties of metal cores, including the introduction of intrinsic chirality, and the synthesis of optically pure chiral heterometal clusters can play an important role in the development of metal cluster chemistry. For example, alloys containing Au and Ag, which have similar atomic radii and electronic structures, often exhibit disordered structures but exhibit unique properties resulting from subtle variations in intermetallic bonding and interactions3. Indeed, sequence-specific heterometal clusters are an active area of research. In particular, the site-selective binding of shell ligands to heterometal clusters16–19 contributes significantly to the synergistic function around the active center. Similarly, the program of heterometallic disorder also arises in metal carbide clusters20,21. Therefore, creating asymmetry in the structure by varying the bonding and interactions between the metal atoms and the surface ligands and the central coordinating atom makes it possible to introduce functional centers at specific positions of the heterometallic atoms within the structures.

We chose a ligand-protected hexagold(I) (CAuI6) cluster with a well-defined octahedral structure and a carbon (C)-center22 as a candidate cluster for exploring asymmetric alloying. The structure of this type of cluster has a backbone formed by hypercoordinate bonds between a central carbon atom and six gold(I) atoms22,23 and is stabilized by aurophilic interactions24,25 between the gold(I) atoms. However, the details of the bonding mode remain controversial. In fact, although the C-centered CAuI6 cluster has been reported to exhibit high stability26,27, we recently synthesized a CAuI5 cluster by single-atom etching of the CAuI6 cluster, and successfully demonstrated the reactivity of the carbon-centered gold(I) cluster28. We also discovered that the highly symmetric (Oh) and stable CAuI6 structure exhibits intrinsic chirality due to the twisting of the metal cluster portion29. This clearly demonstrates that C-centered gold(I) clusters can serve as potential chiral frameworks induced by chiral ligands.

This study demonstrated that the addition of silver trifluoroacetate to a PPh3-protected (PPh3 = triphenylphosphine) CAuI6 cluster affords a heterometallic CAuI4AgI6 cluster (Fig. 1) with carbon-centered chirality, accompanied by etching of two gold(I) atoms, as shown in the formula below. Furthermore, the use of homochiral carboxylate ligands enables highly diastereoselective asymmetric induction of the carbon-centered chirality.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis revealed that the tetragoldmethane CAuI4 core forms a stable bicapped square antiprismatic structure with six silver(I) ions, in which the C-center forms four sigma-covalent C–AuI bonds with the gold ions and weakly interacts with the silver(I) ions. Computational analyses further elucidated its binding properties, revealing that the asymmetric arrangement and directionally selective twisting of the metal ions endow the bicapped square antiphrastic geometry with unprecedented chirality. The asymmetric synthesis of (R,Ʌ)- and (S,∆)-CAuI4AgI6 clusters was quantitatively achieved by partial ligand exchange of TFA− (TFA = CF3CO2) in cluster 1 with homochiral α-methoxy-α-(trifluoromethyl)phenylacetic acid (Mosher’s acid, (R)-MTPA and (S)-MTPA, respectively). These compounds exhibited red-shifted signals in their absorption and PL spectra. In particular, the PL of (R,Ʌ)-/(S,∆)-CAuI4AgI6 in the solid state was significantly red-shifted to the near-infrared (NIR) region and showed a fairly high quantum yield of 34%. Their chiroptical resonances were elucidated by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) measurements, and theoretical calculations. This study presents an asymmetric alloying method involving atomic-level metal etching for metal ion clusters with carbon core and shell ligands, which will provide the principles for constructing subnano hetero-metal ion clusters with well-defined structures by utilizing carbon-centered chirality, the coordination bonds and interactions between the central carbon and the metals, and the metal-metal interactions.

Results

Synthesis of CAuI4AgI6 by etching CAuI6 with CF3CO2Ag

When a methanol solution of CF3CO2Ag was added to the CAuI6 cluster22 [(C)(AuI-PPh3)6](BF4)2 (3) in dichloromethane, the colorless solution immediately turned orange-red. The reaction mixture was monitored by UV-vis spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1), which showed that the absorption band at 375 nm was replaced by those at 400 nm and 431 nm, and obvious isosbestic points appeared at 360 and 285 nm. Finally, crystallization afforded orange-red blocky crystals of the CAuI4AgI6 cluster, (C)(AuI-PPh3)4AgI6(CF3CO2)6 (1), in 70% yield (based on the central carbon). Notably, the absorption bands of the reaction mixture after 2 h coincided with those of 1 redissolved in dichloromethane (Supplementary Fig. 1). Cluster 1 was further characterized by ESI-MS spectrometry, NMR spectroscopy, and elemental analysis (Supplementary Figs. 2–7). In the ESI-MS spectrum, a fragment of 1 was identified at m/z 3060.5842, which corresponds to [(C)(AuI-PPh3)4AgI6(CF3CO2)5]+ (calcd. 3060.5868), resulting in the dissociation of one CF3CO2 anion. Other fragment species of 1 obtained by removing CF3CO2Ag under ESI-MS conditions were also observed, such as [(C)(AuI-PPh3)4AgI5(CF3CO2)4]+ at m/z 2840.6988 (calcd. 2840.6971). The 31P NMR chemical shift of cluster 1 in d2-dichloromethane (40.4 ppm) was significantly downfield shifted compared with 27.9 ppm for 3 (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the 1H NMR spectrum, the signals assigned to the phenyl group of PPh3 were broad (7.44–7.32 ppm, Supplementary Fig. 4). The signal patterns in the NMR spectra suggest that the environments of the phosphine ligands are identical, which is also explained by the pseudo-D2 symmetry of the structures. Notably, cluster 1 can also be obtained from the reaction of pentanuclear analogue28 [(C)(AuI-PPh3)5](BF4) with CF3CO2Ag. This etching method was carried out with [(C)(AuI-P(4-OMeC6H4)3)6](BF4)2 (P(4-OMeC6H4)3 = tris(4-methoxyphenyl)phosphine) to afford the tris(4-methoxyphenyl)phosphine-protected CAuI4AgI6 analogue (C)(AuI-P(4-OMeC6H4)3)4AgI6(CF3CO2)6 (1OMe) (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, as control experiments, the N- and P-centered pentagold(I) cluster counterparts30,31 were etched with CF3CO2Ag resulted in the decomposition of the clusters, suggesting that the C-center plays an important role in ensuring stability.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analysis and computational bonding analyses

In the SCXRD structure of cluster 1 (Fig. 2a), the CAuI4AgI6 core has a bicapped square antiprism structure, with four gold(I) ions coordinated by four PPh3 ligands and six silver(I) ions bridged by six CF3CO2− ions to form a neutral cluster molecule. Within the CAuI4AgI6 core, the following metal-metal interactions were found: AuI⋅⋅⋅AuI (2.8606(2)–3.2842(2) Å), AgI⋅⋅⋅AgI (3.0267(4)–3.3576(4) Å), and AuI⋅⋅⋅AgI (2.7683(4)–3.0914(4) Å) (Supplementary Table 2). The interstitial carbon atom is located approximately at the actual geometric center with a displacement of only 0.154(2) Å, suggesting that the radial bonding mode of the C-center is maintained. Furthermore, the structure of 1 can be viewed as a combination of two neutral fragments: a tetrahedral CAuI4 cluster coordinated by PPh3 and a twisted boat ring consisting of (CF3CO2Ag)6 (Supplementary Fig. 9). It is noteworthy that the four C–AuI bonds vary from 2.056(4) to 2.080(4) Å, slightly shorter than those in cluster 3, and the distances between the C-center and the four AgI ions at the vertices of the square antiprism (2.559(4)–2.897(4) Å) are shorter than the sum of the van der Waals radii32 for carbon and silver atoms (3.42 Å). In contrast to previous studies in which silver(I) ions were attached to the surface of pyridinylphosphine-supported CAuI6 clusters26,27, the unique noncovalent interactions between the silver(I) ions and the C-center in 1 revealed the reactivity of silver(I) ions penetrating into the gold(I) layer via alloying.

Next, we performed a theoretical analysis of the structure of cluster 1 to clarify its bonding properties (Table 1). The optimized structure of cluster 1 was found to be consistent with the experimental results in terms of geometry and bond length (Supplementary Fig. 10, Supplementary Tables 3, 4). The Wiberg bond orders (WBOs) of the C–Au bonds are in the range from 0.53 to 0.57, suggesting a single bond. The WBOs of the metallophilic interactions are Au···Au (0.17–0.18), Au···Ag (0.10–0.13), and Ag···Ag ( ≈ 0.06). In particular, the calculated C···Ag distances show a similar trend to the experimental results, with the distances between the C-center and the four AgI ions at the vertices of the square antiprism (2.96–3.22 Å) being shorter than those between the C-center and the two capped silver(I) ions (3.46 Å). Although the WBO of C···Ag, which is longer in distance, is small (0.06–0.08), it is comparable to the WBO of Ag···Ag (0.06), which clearly indicates weak interactions between the central carbon and the four silver(I) ions. Their delocalization indexes (DI) also showed a trend that fully supported the WBO data (Supplementary Table 4).

To gain a deeper understanding of the bonding properties, we identified bonding critical points (BCPs) along the C–Au, Au···Ag, Ag···Ag, Au–P, and Ag···O bonding paths by quantum theory of atoms (QTAIM) analysis of the molecules (Supplementary Fig. 11). The BCP parameters confirm that the C–Au and Au–P bonds are single bonds with strong covalent properties (Supplementary Table 5). The interaction region indicator (IRI) also indicates that the C–Au bond is a chemical bond, and there is a weak interaction between Au···Ag and Ag···Ag (Supplementary Fig. 12). The electron localization function (ELF) also clearly reveals that electrons are localized in the region between C and Au. whereas the degree of electron localization is much lower in the regions between C and Ag and between Au and Ag (Supplementary Fig. 13). Natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis of cluster 1 identified four C–Au bonds, with a C atom contribution of approximately 73% and an Au atom contribution of approximately 26% (Supplementary Fig. 14). Four single bonds with an occupancy of approximately 1.7 are formed between sp3 hybridized C atom and Au s orbitals, which can be regarded as two-center, two-electron (2c–2e) sigma bonds. Adaptive natural density partitioning (AdNDP) analysis also identified four C–Au bonds with an occupancy of approximately 1.99 (Supplementary Fig. 15).

Energy decomposition analysis (EDA) combined with natural orbitals for chemical valence (NOCV)33,34 was applied to elucidate the nature of the bonding between the C-center and the surrounding atoms. The C-center exhibits a negative charge of −1.396 e (Supplementary Table 6), and the most appropriate representation of its bonding situation is revealed to be C(2s22px12py12pz1)− (quartet) and [(AuPPh3)4(CF3CO2Ag)6]+ (quartet) (Supplementary Table 7). The interaction energy between the C-center and (AuI-PPh3)4(CF3CO2Ag)6 is −332.8 kcal mol−1, which is mainly composed of two attractive terms, the Coulomb term ΔEelstat and the orbital interaction term ΔEorb, which are counterbalanced by the Pauli repulsion (Supplementary Table 7). The ΔEorb term accounts for 35.1% ( −424.3 kcal mol−1) of the total attractive interactions. The maximum value of the orbital interaction, ΔEorb1, is −206.8 kcal mol−1, which characterizes the sigma bonds between the C-center and the four Au atoms and is shown in the associated deformation density (Fig. 2b). Next, the interactions between the two fragments, (C)(AuI-PPh3)4 and (CF3CO2Ag)6, was clearly represented by evaluating the interactions of the six silver(I) ions and the CAu4 kernel using EDA-NOCV analysis. The interaction energy is −414.4 kcal mol−1, and the orbital interactions account for 31.6% ( −207.0 kcal mol−1) of the total attractive interactions (Supplementary Table 8). The breakdown of the orbital term shows that the maximum orbital interaction, ΔEorb1, is only −32.1 kcal mol−1, and a charge reduction is observed in CAuI4, while a charge accumulation is observed on the two equatorial silver(I) atoms and in the region between them (Supplementary Fig. 17). The dispersion energy, ΔEdisp (−160.9 kcal mol−1), was confirmed to be a major contribution from the interaction between (C)(AuI-PPh3)4 and (CF3CO2Ag)6. This value is significantly larger than the dispersion energy between C and (AuI-PPh3)4(CF3CO2Ag)6 ( −9.5 kcal mol−1). This finding confirms that the weak interactions between Ag ions and the CAu4 kernel play an important role in stabilizing the overall structure. Notably, this bonding hierarchy, including the strong C–AuI σ bonds and the much weaker C···AgI interactions, provides stereoelectronic effects for the atomically stable and asymmetric arrangement of AgI ions around the CAuI4 kernel, in stark contrast to the nearly indistinguishable Au–Ag distributions typically observed in mixed Au/Ag alloy clusters and disordered heterometallic carbide clusters20,21.

Intrinsic chirality analysis and asymmetric synthesis

The original bicapped square antiprism (Johnson solid No. 17) has D4d symmetry, with the plane of symmetry (σd) bisecting the squares and passing through the capping vertices (Fig. 3a). In cluster 1, four gold(I) ions and six silver(I) ions are asymmetrically arranged at the vertices, and the symmetry is broken by lowering to pseudo-D2, forming an enantiopure core. Here, we defined the metal atoms of AuI4AgI4 in the core arranged anti-clockwise around the square antiprism as Ʌ-CAuI4AgI6, and the metal atoms in the core arranged clockwise around the square antiprism as ∆-CAuI4AgI6 (Fig. 3b). Geometrical twists are observed in the constituent CAuI4 and AgI6 moieties, as well as in the overall core CAuI4AgI6: for example, the Au-C-Au angles of the CAuI4 tetrahedron are twisted between 87.16(16)° and 136.8(2)°, as opposed to the ideal tetrahedron (109.5°). The AgI6 ring adopts a twist-boat conformation similar to that of cyclohexane (Fig. 3c). The overall geometry of the CAuI4AgI6 core is directionally twisted, with dihedral angles α1 = 37°, β1 = 57°, α2 = 35° and β2 = 51°. This contrasts with the perfect bicapped square antiprismatic shape shown in Fig. 3d, with a dihedral angle θ = 45°. Thus, the asymmetric arrangement of metal ions and the chiral twist geometry contribute to the intrinsic chirality of the asymmetric unit of cluster 1. Furthermore, continuous chirality measure (CCM)35 determined the chirality values of CAuI4 (1.17), the twist-boat-shaped AgI6 (7.66), and the CAuI4AgI6 core (18.22), indicating chirality amplification from the CAuI4 unit to the CAuI4AgI6 core.

Partial bridging ligand exchange with TFA− of cluster 1 with (R)-MTPA or (S)-MTPA quantitatively afforded the enantiopure chiral clusters (C)(AuI-PPh3)4AgI6(CF3CO2)2((R)-MTPA−)4 ((R,Ʌ)-2) and (C)(AuI-PPh3)4AgI6(CF3CO2)2((S)-MTPA−)4 ((S,∆)-2), respectively (Fig. 3e, see Methods for synthetic details). Detailed characterization of (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 was performed by elemental analysis, NMR spectroscopy, and ESI-MS spectrometry (Supplementary Figs. 20–22). Notably, the fragment species of (R,Ʌ)-2 are similar to those of 1 under mass spectrometry conditions, and the CAuI4AgI6 species was detected at m/z 3540.8305 ([(C)(AuI-PPh3)4AgI6(CF3CO2)((R)-MTPA−)4]+, calcd. 3540.8181). The solubility of the deprotonated Mosher’s acid bound cluster molecules ((R)-MTPA− for (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S)-MTPA− for (S,∆)-2) in dichloromethane, acetone, and diethyl ether was significantly improved, and crystals suitable for SCXRD were obtained by slow evaporation. The SCXRD structures of (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 (Fig. 3e) show that they are in the Sohncke space group P21, with low Flack parameters 0.015(8) and 0.035(7), respectively, indicating homochiral packing characteristics. Both (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 have similar overall structures to 1. For example, in (R,Ʌ)-2, only four CF3CO2 anions are replaced by deprotonated (R)-MTPA, while two vertices of the CF3CO2 unit are retained. The Ʌ-CAuI4AgI6 core in (R,Ʌ)-2 and the ∆-CAuI4AgI6 core of (S,∆)-2 were nearly identical to those in 1, except for slight changes in bond lengths (Supplementary Table 2). The CCM35 values of the CAuI4AgI6 cores in (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 (Supplementary Table 10) were similar to those in 1, indicating that these cores possess intrinsic chirality. Furthermore, π···π interactions were observed between adjacent PPh3 groups in (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 (Supplementary Fig. 23). This is due to the repulsion caused by the enhanced steric hindrance of (R)-MTPA. Notably, (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 each assembled into a helical packing structure opposite to each other (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Optical absorption profiles and theoretical calculations

The UV-vis absorption spectra of CAuI4AgI6 clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 were significantly shifted to longer wavelengths compared to the original CAuI6 cluster 3 (Supplementary Fig. 25). The absorption spectra of 1 showed two low-energy absorption bands at 395 nm and 431 nm. The UV-vis spectral traces of cluster 1 at various concentrations showed that the relationship between concentration and absorbance at 395 nm obeyed the Beer-Lambert law, confirming that no dissociation or association of the cluster molecules occurred within the measured concentration range. Similarly, the absorption spectrum of (R,Ʌ)-2 shows absorption bands at 419 nm and 445 nm, and a minor band at 512 nm. The absorption wavelength of (R,Ʌ)-2 was slightly shifted to longer wavelengths compared to 1, indicating the ligand effect of (R)-MTPA, which has a higher electron-donating property than TFA. A linear relationship was also observed between the concentration of (R,Ʌ)-2 and the absorbance at 445 nm. The results showed that both clusters exhibit high stability, even in dilute solutions, and remain stable in the solid state for up to 1 year.

We next investigated the electronic structures of these compounds using time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) (Supplementary Figs. 26–28). Clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 exhibited similar atomic contributions to the frontier molecular orbitals: the C-center had comparable contributions to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) (23.6% in 1 and 23.1% in (R,Ʌ)-2), whereas the silver(I) ions mainly contributed to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) with only a small contribution to the HOMO. The simulated absorption spectra of clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 closely reproduced the experimental results (Supplementary Fig. 26). The first absorption band calculated at 412 nm for cluster 1 is mainly attributed to the HOMO–1 to LUMO transition, suggesting a metal-cluster-centered local excitation, accompanied by intracluster charge redistribution into the CAuI4AgI6 core (Supplementary Fig. 27, Supplementary Tables 11, 13). The second absorption band, with peaks at 367, 360, and 355 nm, is primarily due to three transitions HOMO → LUMO + 1, HOMO → LUMO + 2, and HOMO–1 → LUMO + 2, respectively, indicating a similar metal ion-coupled charge transfer. The simulated spectrum of (R,Ʌ)-2 shows similar characteristics to 1, reproducing a prominent, red-shifted, small absorption band in the lowest energy region.

Chirality, photoluminescence, and circularly polarized luminescence properties

The CD spectra in Fig. 4b show that the chiroptical signals of (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 are perfectly mirror images in the range of 250 to 600 nm. Compared with the enantiopure acids, which showed chiroptical signals in the range of 230–270 nm (Supplementary Fig. 29), the chiroptical spectra of (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 in dichloromethane exhibited distinct features in the range of 300–600 nm (Fig. 4a). For (R,Ʌ)-2, a significant Cotton effect (peaks I–V) was observed at 320( + ), 350( − ), 419( + ), 445(+), and 512(−) nm, with the strongest CD signal at 320 nm. Of particular note is that the CD signal at 320 nm for (R,Ʌ)-2 gradually decreased with decreasing concentration, demonstrating a good linear relationship (Fig. 4c, d). Although CD spectra of the solid samples measured using KBr tablets did not yield clear signals, the CD signals of the samples immobilized in polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) films were similar to those in solution (Supplementary Fig. 30). The CD spectra of (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 were theoretically reproduced, and the Cotton effect agreed with the experimental results (Fig. 4e). For example, the calculated peaks i–v of (R,Ʌ)-2 coincide with the experimental peaks I–V. The peaks i and ii are attributed to the transitions from the HOMO and HOMO–1 to the LUMO, and the peak iii is attributed to the transitions from the HOMO to the LUMO + 1 and LUMO + 2. These peaks i–iii in the low-energy region indicate metal-cluster-centered local excitation accompanied by intracluster charge redistribution within the CAuI4AgI6 core, including the contribution of the carbon center. The peaks in the high-energy region, such as iv (HOMO–3 → LUMO transition) and v (HOMO–7 → LUMO transition), mainly indicate the ligand-to-metal charge transfer (Supplementary Tables 14, 15). These results revealed the origin of the intrinsic chirality, which is mainly provided by the chiral CAuI4AgI6 core. Such chiroptical responses of the chiral CAuI4AgI6 system, in contrast to conventional chiral ligand-protected Au-Ag alloy nanoclusters, where the chiroptical responses are reduced due to the statistical distribution of Ag heteroatoms in the nanoclusters36.

Theoretical calculations and photophysical spectra.

Figure 4f shows that the PLs of CAuI4AgI6 clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 are red-shifted to the red and NIR regions compared to the green PL of CAuI6 cluster 326,28. Specifically, the solid-state of 1 exhibits red PL (λemmax = 683 nm) at room temperature upon excitation at 374 nm. The solid-state of (R,Ʌ)-2 exhibits PL (λemmax = 721 nm) upon excitation at 468 nm, which is red-shifted by up to 196 nm compared to 3 (λemmax = 525 nm) and significantly shifted into the NIR-I region5. Under non-degassed conditions at 293 K, cluster 1 showed no detectable PL in dichloromethane, and (R,Λ)-2 also showed only weak emission (λemmax = 720 nm) in dichloromethane upon excitation at 450 nm (Supplementary Fig. 32), which is likely due to the nonradiative deactivation promoted by the free movement of the ligands in solution. Of note is that while the HOMO and LUMO are primarily due to the metals, the ligand contributions to the excited states are also observed in both cluster 1 and (R,Λ)-2 (Supplementary Tables 13, 14). This means that the excitation states are delocalized across the clusters and dissipate into the solution via the ligands17. Moreover, the PL excitation profile (λexcmax = 450 nm) and emission profile (λemmax = 718 nm) of cluster (R,Ʌ)-2 in dichloromethane showed a linear increase in intensity with increasing concentration (Supplementary Fig. 32C–F), indicating that neither aggregation nor dissociation occurred within the concentration range investigated, consistent with the absorption results. In a mixed solvent of CH2Cl2/Et2O (v/v = 1:9), clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 exhibited significant red PL at λemmax = 660 nm and 708 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 33), with a slightly blue-shifted emission maximum compared to that in the solid state. Increasing the Et2O content in this mixed solvent (Supplementary Fig. 33) resulted in a significant emission enhancement and a slightly blue-shifted PL emission maximum of (R,Ʌ)-2, suggesting aggregation-induced emission enhancement. When clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 were dispersed in PMMA films (Supplementary Fig. 34), they exhibited more blue-shifted PL emissions at 636 nm and 690 nm, respectively. These results suggest that the CAuI4AgI6 system is sensitive to different aggregation states in PMMA films, solutions, and crystals. Furthermore, the absolute PL quantum yields (PLQYs) of 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 in the solid state were 0.39 and 0.34, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 35). The PLs of clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2 were weak in solution (Supplementary Table 16). The PLQY of 1 was 0.016 in CH2Cl2/Et2O (v/v = 1:9), while the PLQY of (R,Ʌ)-2 was 0.005 in CH2Cl2 and 0.018 in CH2Cl2/Et2O (v/v = 1:9). On the other hand, the PL of cluster 1 was weak in PMMA (PLQY = 0.034), whereas the PLQY of (R,Ʌ)-2 in PMMA was significantly improved to 0.45 in PMMA. Microsecond-level lifetimes were observed for clusters 1 and (R,Ʌ)-2, particularly in the solid state, where 1 (5.30 µs) and (R,Ʌ)-2 (5.51 µs) were significantly longer than cluster 3 (2.64 µs)28, indicating that silver alloying stabilized the triplet-state and enhanced phosphorescence properties. Overall, these results suggest that the luminescence processes of the CAuI4AgI6 clusters are dominated by electron redistribution within the cluster, resulting in emission from a low-energy cluster-centered triplet state with nonnegligible ligand contribution. This luminescence property contrasts with the metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT)-dominated luminescence observed for the CAuI6 clusters37.

To reveal the structure-specific chiroptical properties and PL in the red-to-near-infrared region of these clusters, we further investigated their circularly polarized luminescence (CPL). The luminescence dissymmetry factor is quantified as glum (glum = 2(IL − IR)/(IL + IR), where IL and IR represent the intensities of left- and right-handed CPLs, respectively, and are used to evaluate the degree of CPL intensity. Figure 4g shows that (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 exhibit mirror-image profiles between 550 and 850 nm, which are consistent with the corresponding PL spectra. These CPL spectra show a single band with a peak around 720 nm, indicating CPL activity in the near-infrared region. The glum factors of the two enantiomers are ±1.4 × 10−3 in the range of 600 to 850 nm (Supplementary Fig. 36). Specifically, (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2 show negative and positive signals, respectively, consistent with the CD bands in the low-energy region between 470 and 600 nm, suggesting that chirality and optical activity are maintained in the absorption-to-emission transition upon interacting with circularly polarized light.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a method for asymmetric alloying by adding silver(I) ions starting from C-centered hexagold(I) clusters. Specifically, a chemically stable chiral cluster with a chiral-at-carbon hexasilver(I)-alloyed tetragoldmethane, CAuI4AgI6, was synthesized by adding silver trifluoroacetate to a C-centered hexagold(I) cluster protected with highly symmetric triphenylphosphine ligand. Single-crystal X-ray structure analysis confirmed the chiral arrangement of gold(I) and silver(I) atoms around the central carbon, the etching of the gold(I) atoms, and the chiral twisted structure of a bicapped square antiprism. Asymmetric synthesis of (R,Ʌ)- and (S,∆)-CAuI4AgI6 species was quantitatively achieved by highly efficient partial bridging ligand exchange using the homochiral carboxylate ligands (R)-MTPA– and (S)-MTPA–, respectively. Furthermore, their absorption and emission spectral measurements revealed a significant red-shifted PL to the NIR region. The chiroptical properties and NIR-PL of the optically active CAuI4AgI6 clusters enabled the generation of CPL. Elucidating and controlling this relationship between PL and CPL remains an important future challenge. Theoretical calculations shed light on the bonding and interactions between the carbon center and the gold(I)/silver(I) metal ions, and the origin of their unique electronic structure and chirality. Overall, this study demonstrates the feasibility of asymmetric alloying in atomically precisely editable Au-Ag alloy clusters with diverse photophysical properties, highlighting their potential for emerging chiroptical materials, CPL-active emitters, and enantioselective optoelectronic applications.

Methods

Handling

All the syntheses were conducted under air unless otherwise mentioned.

NMR spectra

31P, 1H, 13C and 2D NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III-500 spectrometer and a JNM-ECZ400S spectrometer. When CD2Cl2 was used, the residual solvent signals were used to calibrate the 1H (5.32 ppm) and 13C NMR (53.84 ppm) measurements.

ESI-MS analysis

ESI-MS data were recorded on a JMS-T100LP mass spectrometer under the following experimental conditions (ESI source; positive mode; needle voltage: 2500 V; desolvation temperature: 150 °C; orifice temperature: 80 °C; orifice 1: 45 V; ring lens: 15 V; orifice 2: 5 V; ion guide RF: 2500 V; range: m/z 1000–4000; calibration: TFANa) unless otherwise noted.

Elemental analysis

Elemental analyses (C, N, H) were conducted in the microanalytical laboratory, Department of Chemistry, School of Science, the University of Tokyo, using a Vario MICRO Cube elemental analyzer with MgO added.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction crystallography

X-ray crystallographic analysis was performed using a Rigaku XtaLAB PRO MM007DW PILATUS diffractometer with MoKα and CuKα radiation (93 K), and the obtained data were calculated using the Crystal Structure crystallographic software package. The refinement was performed using an OLEX2 software38 with SHELXT39. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically, except for the central carbon atoms in (R,Ʌ)-2 and (S,∆)-2. Restraints were used for those disordered atoms that were caused by their high thermal motion and thus formed an averaged structure of various geometries and orientations. All hydrogen atoms were geometrically placed and refined using the riding model. The distance between the real geometrical center of the bicapped square antiprism of Au4Ag6 core in 1 and the carbon center was calculated by the SHELXL matrix in the OLEX2 software.

Photophysical analysis

UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded on a JASCO V-770 UV-vis spectrophotometer. The emission and excitation spectra were measured using a Jasco FP-8500 fluorometer. Absolute quantum yields in the solid state were measured using Quantaurus-QY (Hamamatsu C9920-02G). Lifetime measurements were made using a Hamamatsu Photonics C11367-02. CD spectra were measured on a JASCO J-820 circular dichroism spectrometer. The experimental conditions for CD analysis were a bandwidth of 1 nm, a response of 0.5 s, a data acquisition interval of 0.5 nm, and a scanning rate of 100 nm min–1. Circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) spectra were obtained using a JASCO CPL-300 spectrophotometer.

Computational methods

Density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations were performed for the model CAuI4AgI6 clusters using the B3LYP functional40. Relativistic effective core potential LANL2DZ41 was used for Au and Ag atoms, and the basis sets of other atoms were 6–31 G*42. For simulating absorption and electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectra, 200 excited states were solved to cover the spectrum in the energy range up to about 220 nm. TDDFT calculation was conducted, including the solvent effects of dichloromethane with the polarizable continuum model (PCM) and the non-equilibrium linear response scheme43. All calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 suite of programs44.

Wiberg bond order and natural bond orbital were calculated at the level of B3LYP/6-31 G* ~ LANL2DZ using NBO 3.1 as implemented in Gaussian 16. Orbital composition analysis with Mulliken partition was conducted with the Multiwfn program45. Additionally, Atom-in-Molecule (AIM) analysis and calculation of the delocalization index (DI) based on AIM basins were performed using the Multiwfn program46. Adaptive Natural Density Partitioning (AdNDP) analysis was performed by AdNDP 2.047,48 based on the calculations at the level of B3LYP/6-31 G* ~ LANL2DZ.

The energy decomposition analysis (EDA) developed by Ziegler and Rauk49 was applied to study the nature of chemical bonds. The EDA is concerned with the instantaneous interaction energy ΔEint of a bond A–B between the fragments A and B in the specific electronic reference state at the frozen geometry of the molecule AB50. The interaction energy ΔEint is subdivided into three main components, as indicated by the following equation. In the present calculations, the dispersion correction ΔEdisp term was also computed.

The term ΔEelstat represents the quasiclassical electrostatic interaction between the unperturbed charge distributions of the prepared atoms and is typically associated with an attractive force. The Pauli repulsion ΔEPauli is the energy change associated with the transformation from the superposition of the unperturbed electron densities ρA + ρB of the isolated fragments to the wavefunction Ψ0=NÂ [ΨAΨB], which properly obeys the Pauli principle through explicit antisymmetrization (Â operator) and renormalization (N = constant) of the product wavefunction. The ΔEPauli term represents the destabilizing interactions between electrons of the same spin on either fragment. The orbital interaction, ΔEorb, accounts for the charge transfer, polarization effects, and electron-pair bonding.
