Durable responses and favorable cost-effectiveness of rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy for primary cold agglutinin disease
Authors: Kenichi Ito, Tomoko Kitagawa, Saya Motohashi, Kazuhiko Hirano, Naohiro Sekiguchi
Published: 2025

Primary cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is defined as chronic complement-mediated hemolysis associated with a small clonal B-cell population, in the absence of clinical or molecular evidence of Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM) (including mutations in MYD88 L265P/CXCR4) [1]. Randen et al. were the first to report that all cases of CAD harbored a monoclonal B-cell population with an immunoglobulin (IG) gene repertoire, predominantly IGHV4-34, thereby providing molecular evidence distinguishing CAD from other lymphoproliferative disorders [2]. Subsequent studies corroborated this finding [3, 4], and CAD is now recognized as a distinct clinicopathological entity in the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [1].

More than 90% of patients with CAD present with serum monoclonal immunoglobulin M (IgM) paraproteins [5]. Bone marrow infiltration typically appears as nodular aggregates of small B-lymphocytes, with plasma cells predominantly located outside the nodules. Molecularly and biologically, CAD is characterized by a monoclonal B-cell population, mainly utilizing IGHV4-34, and is uniformly negative for MYD88 mutations [2]. Differentiating CAD from cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS) secondary to malignancy or infection is essential, particularly in cases of lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL)/WM associated with CAS, as the MYD88 mutational status serves as a valuable discriminator marker [2].

CAD treatment strategies are broadly divided into therapies targeting B-cell clones and those that inhibit complement activation; however, no uniform treatment guidelines are currently available. The former includes rituximab, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, as well as proteasome inhibitors, anti-CD38 antibodies, and Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors [6–9]. The latter primarily involves sutimlimab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting C1s [10]. Although sutimlimab is effective, it requires continuous administration and is expensive. Conversely, rituximab-based chemotherapy represents a fixed-duration treatment that elicits durable responses [11]. However, previous studies describing primary CAD did not confirm the MYD88 status [6–10]; thus, they may have included a mixture of CAD and CAS [6–10], limiting the relevance of their conclusions to strictly defined primary CAD.

We retrospectively analyzed patients with CAD diagnosed at our institution, confirmed their MYD88 L265P mutation status using digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR), and characterized their clinical features and treatment courses.

Moreover, we retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients with CAD who were managed at the National Hospital Organization Disaster Medical Center between April 2010 and March 2024. CAS cases secondary to malignancy or infection were excluded based on clinical, laboratory, pathological, and MYD88 mutation analyses. Primary CAD was diagnosed according to the revised fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Hematolymphoid Tumors: Lymphoid Neoplasms [1].

Clinical data extracted from medical records included age, sex, hemoglobin (Hb) level, serum total bilirubin concentration, serum IgM concentration, cold agglutinin titer, presence of splenomegaly, bone marrow chromosomal abnormalities, gene mutation status, percentage of lymphocytes and plasma cells in the bone marrow, frequency of red blood cell transfusions, response to initial therapy, time to response, duration of response, and adverse events related to therapy.Bone marrow specimens were reviewed to confirm the diagnosis of primary CAD and to exclude other lymphoproliferative disorders, including LPL/WM, multiple myeloma, and marginal zone lymphoma.

Chromosome banding analysis was performed using bone marrow aspirates obtained during the initial diagnosis of CAD.

Mutation analysis was performed using ddPCR on a QX200 AutoDG Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad). Additionally, ddPCR mutation detection assays (Bio-Rad) were employed to identify MYD88 L265P and CXCR4 mutations (T318 frameshift, S338 nonsense, and S338 frameshift). This methodology has been described previously [12].

Primary therapy was selected by the treating physician in accordance with prevailing clinical recommendations.

Treatment responses were evaluated according to the criteria reported in clinical trials of ibrutinib [8] and daratumumab [9] for CAD/CAS. Complete response (CR) was defined as an Hb level > 12 g/dL, absence of clinical symptoms of hemolysis, no requirement for red blood cell transfusion, and normalization of bilirubin and lactate dehydrogenase levels. A partial response (PR) was defined as Hb levels of 10–12 g/dL or an increase in Hb of ≥ 2 g/dL from baseline, in the absence of recent transfusions, accompanied by improvements in hemolytic markers without full normalization. No response was defined as an Hb level of < 10 g/dL, persistent hemolytic symptoms, or transfusion dependence. The overall response rate (ORR) was calculated as the percentage of patients who achieved CR or PR.

Serum IgM levels and cold agglutinin titers were recorded before treatment (“pre”) and at their lowest levels after treatment (“post”). The IgM reduction rate was calculated as (pre-treatment value − post-treatment value)/pre-treatment value. Patients were followed up from the date of CAD diagnosis until their last available clinical visit.

Seven patients with primary CAD were included in the study. Most patients presented with anemia and hyperbilirubinemia, while one exhibited an abnormal karyotype. Treatment-related grade 3 neutropenia occurred in four of the five treated patients (80%), and grade 2 infections were observed in two of the five (40%) patients. Additionally, one patient developed gastric cancer 2 years after therapy.

CAD is a distinct clonal B-cell disorder; however, its differentiation from LPL/WM requires morphological and molecular evaluation. Although Randen et al. described the characteristic bone marrow features of CAD, LPL/WM frequently demonstrate diffuse or paratrabecular infiltration, whereas CAD more often exhibits a nodular pattern [2]. As morphological analysis alone is insufficient, molecular studies may be required. Investigations of the IG gene repertoire in CAD have demonstrated a highly restricted pattern. Małecka et al. reported that approximately 80% of the cases utilized the IGHV4-34 gene segment [4]. Conversely, Randen et al. demonstrated that the MYD88 L265P mutation, which is present in the majority of LPL/WM cases [14], is consistently absent in primary CAD [2]. Therefore, assessment of IG usage and MYD88 mutational status is crucial for establishing a diagnosis of primary CAD. Although IG repertoire analysis was not performed in the present study, we evaluated MYD88 L265P using a highly sensitive ddPCR assay and confirmed that all cases were negative, thereby supporting a precise diagnosis of CAD.

Berentsen et al. conducted a prospective, uncontrolled, multicenter study involving 45 patients with CAD without overt lymphoma, in whom MYD88 mutational status was not assessed. Patients were treated with BR, resulting in an ORR of 71% (CR 40%, PR 31%), and a median time to response of 1.9 months [6]. Although our study was limited by a small sample size and less stringent response criteria, all treated patients attained a hematological response: four achieved CR with reduced-dose R-CHOP and one achieved PR with BR, with a median response duration of 90 months. These findings suggest that rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy can provide durable disease control in selected patients with CAD.

Sutimlimab, a recently approved C1s complement inhibitor, has demonstrated rapid efficacy in the treatment of CAD. In a phase 3 randomized, placebo-controlled trial, 72.7% of patients (16/22) achieved a response, with most responses occurring within 3 weeks [10]. However, a cost-effectiveness analysis using a Markov model based on transfusion-dependent patients with CAD reported an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $2,340,000 per QALY, which is well above the commonly accepted U.S. willingness-to-pay threshold of $150,000 per QALY. In the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, standard therapies, including chemoimmunotherapy, were more cost-effective than sutimilmab in all 10,000 simulations (100%) [11]. Therefore, although sutimlimab provides clinical benefits, the treatment’s limited cost-effectiveness reflects its extremely high price and the need for continuous administration. In contrast, the fixed-duration rituximab-based regimen employed in the present study achieved durable responses with a more favorable cost-effectiveness.

This retrospective single-center series, with a small sample size and no control group, limits generalizability, and the response assessment relied on routine clinical data. In addition, IG repertoire analysis was not performed.

In conclusion, rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy elicited durable responses with an acceptable safety profile in patients with CAD, highlighting the potential of fixed-duration therapy as a feasible and economically favorable option.
