A 72-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 2-week history of hypotension, postural dizziness, and muscle weakness. He had a medical history of HIV, lipodystrophy, peripheral neuropathy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), recent stroke, recurrent venous thromboembolism, hypertension, and urinary retention, for which he was taking an extensive pharmacologic regimen (Table 1).

The patient’s current antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimen was initiated in 2018. In 2020, he received a diagnosis of COPD and was treated with inhaled fluticasone. Over the next 4 years, he had increasing episodes of fatigue, postural dizziness, and proximal muscle weakness. After an ischemic stroke in December 2023, his antihypertensive regimen was increased from single-therapy perindopril to triple therapy of ramipril, amlodipine, and chlorthalidone. Terazosin was also started for incidental urinary retention. He developed worsening fatigue, postural dizziness, and increasing weakness, without any new neurologic deficits. He returned to hospital in April 2024 after a home blood pressure (BP) measurement of 68/46 mm Hg.

In the emergency department, the patient received a 500 mL normal saline bolus for presumed hypovolemia. His BP improved from 80/51 mm Hg to 112/61 mm Hg. He could not provide much information at that time, but collateral history confirmed his recent low oral intake secondary to weakness. Physical examination showed central adiposity, extremity wasting, thin skin, bruising, and a dorsocervical hump, attributed to his history of HIV-related lipodystrophy. His remaining vital signs, physical examination, and investigations — including a metabolic panel and infectious diseases workup — were unremarkable. His CD4+ count was 0.17 × 109/L from a baseline of approximately 0.40 × 109/L, and HIV RNA had increased to 99 copies per millilitre from previously undetectable levels.

We initially attributed the patient’s hypotension to low oral intake and the recently increased regimen of antihypertensive therapy. We held ramipril, amlodipine, chlorthalidone, and terazosin on admission, and his BP improved. However, after the reinitiation of low-dose ramipril, he developed profound orthostasis with a lying BP of 145/79 mm Hg, dropping to 73/41 mm Hg on 1 minute of standing. A workup for his orthostasis showed a normal B12 level, thyrotropin, and serum protein electrophoresis, and infectious or metabolic causes had been previously ruled out. We suspected adrenal insufficiency when blood work showed an 8:00 am serum cortisol level of less than 28 (normal 101–536) nmol/L and corticotropin level of 4.1 (normal < 10.2) pmol/L; bilateral adrenal atrophy was also evident on computed tomography scan, compared with scans from 3 years earlier. At this point, the patient clarified that he had actually stopped taking all of his medications 2 weeks before admission.

We made a diagnosis of iatrogenic adrenal insufficiency secondary to a drug–drug interaction, with the cobicistat potentiating the systemic effects of inhaled fluticasone. The patient’s abrupt medication cessation 2 weeks before admission resulted in acute glucocorticoid withdrawal and the unmasking of his adrenal insufficiency. We treated him with immediate steroid replacement (hydrocortisone 20 mg by mouth every morning, hydrocortisone 10 mg by mouth at bedtime, fludrocortisone 0.05 mg by mouth daily), antihypertensive discontinuation, and adjustment of COPD management, which resulted in normal BP and improved functional status. Modification of the patient’s ART — including initiation of dolutegravir and fostemsavir, discontinuation of elvitegravir–cobicistat–emtricitabine–tenofovir alafenamide and darunavir, and a dose increase of maraviroc — occurred after discharge, during outpatient follow-up to treat his HIV. Four months after discharge, his laboratory values improved (corticotropin level of 1.2 pmol/L, cortisol of 41 nmol/L), he was clinically well, and a steroid taper was continuing.

Discussion

Orthostatic hypotension is defined as a decrease in greater than 20 mm Hg systolic or greater than 10 mm Hg diastolic BP within 3 minutes of standing from the supine position. This drop results from inadequate compensation by the baroreceptor reflex, which normally adjusts cardiovascular responses to maintain stable BP during positional changes. Although the differential diagnosis for orthostatic hypotension in the general population is broad, there are additional considerations for patients living with HIV (Table 2).

The presence of orthostatic hypotension is a key differentiating feature between steroid-induced adrenal insufficiency and HIV-related lipodystrophy (a syndrome of altered body fat distribution).1 People with steroid-induced adrenal insufficiency may have Cushingoid features from iatrogenic steroid administration. Cushingoid presentations and HIV-related lipodystrophy both exhibit increased abdominal girth, muscle wasting, and a dorsocervical hump, as seen with our patient.1,2 Facial changes may be subtly different, with moon facies and plethora seen in Cushing syndrome, and facial lipoatrophy in lipodystrophy.2 Our patient lacked any facial changes and the abdominal striae often associated with Cushing syndrome.2 He did have easy bruising — more commonly seen in Cushing syndrome than lipodystrophy — which we initially attributed to his anticoagulants.

Antiretroviral therapy regimens commonly contain pharmacokinetic enhancers designed to boost plasma concentrations of pharmacologically active ARTs, with the 2 most common agents being ritonavir and cobicistat. Cobicistat was introduced in 2012 as an alternative to ritonavir, with a narrower cytochrome P450 (CYP)–isozyme interaction profile.3 However, given its strong CYP3A4- and P-glycoprotein-inhibiting properties, clinicians should still consider a wide variety of potential drug interactions.3

Fluticasone is metabolized by hepatic CYP3A4. Strong inhibitors of CYP3A4 have been shown to increase systemic exposure to inhaled fluticasone, resulting in decreased serum cortisol levels.3 This sustained increase in glucocorticoid exposure leads to the well-known adverse effects of long-term steroid use, including hypertension, osteoporosis, and decreased CD4+ count, in addition to Cushing syndrome and adrenal insufficiency. Several case series and retrospective studies describe adrenal suppression and iatrogenic Cushing syndrome resulting from the combined use of inhaled fluticasone and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, with most studies involving ritonavir.4,5 Although cobicistat is an equally strong inhibitor of CYP3A4, case reports describing the clinical effects of combined use with inhaled corticosteroids are less common.6–9

Although inhaled routes of corticosteroid administration are generally perceived as exerting localized effects, even these “nonsystemic” formulations can produce systemic effects in patients taking strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. Among the available inhaled corticosteroids, fluticasone shows the highest degree of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal suppression and is most frequently implicated in case reports.5,10 However, almost all inhaled corticosteroids are metabolized via CYP3A4 and can be affected by this interaction. One exception to this is beclomethasone, which has been shown to undergo clinically insignificant pharmacokinetic changes when combined with ritonavir.11 This is likely due to a combination of alternative metabolic pathways, low receptor binding affinity, and a short elimination half-life.11

In our patient, de-escalation of COPD management was clinically feasible. However, for patients on cobicistat where COPD de-escalation is not feasible, consideration should be given to switching fluticasone with beclomethasone to reduce the risk of adrenal suppression. Alternatively, barring no substantial history of resistance or adverse effects, ART modification to a regimen without inhaled corticosteroid interactions (e.g., dolutegravir) can be considered.

As the life expectancy of people with HIV increases owing to advancements in ART, patients are at increased risk of age-related comorbidities and polypharmacy, necessitating heightened vigilance for ART-related drug interactions. In light of these complex interactions, consultation with the patient’s pharmacist or HIV specialist is recommended to ensure safe and effective management. In addition, drug interaction checking and avoiding alert fatigue is of utmost importance in patients on ART.12
