Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning for Zonal Ancillary Market Coupling
Francesco Morri, Hélène Le Cadre, Pierre Gruet, Luce Brotcorne

Abstract
We characterize zonal ancillary market coupling relying on noncooperative game theory. To that purpose, we formulate the ancillary market as a multi-leader single follower bilevel problem, that we subsequently cast as a generalized Nash game with side constraints and nonconvex feasibility sets. We determine conditions for equilibrium existence and show that the game has a generalized potential game structure. To compute market equilibrium, we rely on two exact approaches: an integrated optimization approach and Gauss-Seidel best-response, that we compare against multi-agent deep reinforcement learning. On real data from Germany and Austria, simulations indicate that multi-agent deep reinforcement learning achieves the smallest convergence rate but requires pretraining, while best-response is the slowest. On the economics side, multi-agent deep reinforcement learning results in smaller market costs compared to the exact methods, but at the cost of higher variability in the profit allocation among stakeholders. Further, stronger coupling between zones tends to reduce costs for larger zones.



I
Introduction

A characteristic feature of electricity is the impossibility to store it in large proportions. Hence, it must be produced exactly when it is consumed, which raises a need for a real-time monitoring of the balance between production and consumption to guarantee the stability of the power grid. When imbalances occur, mechanisms are automatically triggered to restore the nominal frequency of the electric system: a change in power production is made by some production units, which are then said to provide
ancillary services
, in exchange of a monetary contribution.
In Western Europe, many market zones are progressively switching from an automatic and mandatory involvement of qualified production units with an amount that is fixed by the system operator to a market mechanism based on daily auctions in which the market operator publishes a demand and then the producers submit offer curves that are aggregated until they meet the demand. From the point of view of a market actor, submitting a bid in such an auction leaves some space for strategic bidding, which requires some information about the market participants. This knowledge, together with the design of a relevant bidding strategy, should enable market actors to engage their production assets in the best way in ancillary services markets, which will then give them a fair idea of the revenue they can expect.
Although these ancillary services markets exist in many countries, the bidding zones can be somehow associated: for instance the Austrian and German areas can share their bids, subject to some constraints and to the availability of the transmission network across both countries. Market participants might be willing to investigate the impact of this shared market structure and to see if it stabilizes their revenues over time.
The main challenges to address are the characterization of the market equilibria and the understanding of their evolution when market zones get interconnected: from the perspective of the market operator, it is important to gain insight into the behaviors of the stakeholders and to gauge the opportunity to enforce market integration over multiple countries if this can, e.g., mitigate price peak events in one country thanks to interconnection.
In this article, we use noncooperative game theory to model ancillary services markets. Market equilibria are computed relying on reformulations of the game and multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (MARL). Our flexible framework allows us to assess the consequences of the interconnection of market zones by dynamically changing the coupling factors. The approach can be generalized to any number of zones.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

we formulate a multi-zone ancillary market model as a bilevel problem, that we subsequently reformulate as a generalized Nash game with generalized potential structure. We obtain theoretical guarantees for the existence of market equilibria;

•

we propose three algorithmic solutions for the market model: two exact approaches to compute an equilibrium, integrated optimization and Gauss-Seidel best-response, and a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (MARL) simulation. The latter allows us to run a decentralized simulation of the energy market with strategic learning and non-strategic agents;

•

we compare the three algorithm performance on real data from the Germany-Austria market, computing social cost and fairness indicators. Finally, we test multiple coupling settings for the market, observing a decrease in the market operators’ costs with higher couplings.

This paper is organized as follows: in Section
I-A
, we introduce the relevant literature; in Section
II
, we formulate the ancillary service market as a noncooperative game. In Section
III
, we derive conditions for existence of market equilibria, that can be subsequently computed relying on the algorithms introduced in Section
IV
. Finally, in Section
V
, we implement the algorithms on real data from Austria and Germany, that we compare numerically using performance measures and economic metrics, concluding in Section
VI
.

II
An Adversarial Game Formulation

The ancillary services market can be formulated as a multi-leader single follower bilevel optimization problem. At the upper level, producers (leaders) bid strategically to maximize their revenues while anticipating the market clearing. At the lower level, the market operator (follower) determines the fractions of the bids that are accepted in order to minimize the total cost it has to pay to the producers to meet the zonal demands, while satisfying zonal, interzone and bid-related constraints. Because of the opposite objectives of the producers and market operator, the problem can be interpreted as an adversarial game, also called a zero sum game.

IV-B

Multi Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning

The core idea of reinforcement learning is to have an agent learning directly from exposure to an unknown environment. It is formally modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
\{S,A,r\}
where
S
is the state space,
A
the action space and
r:S\times A\to\mathbb{R}
the reward function. We define the discounted return over a trajectory of length
L
as
R(s,a):=\sum_{t=0}^{t=L-1}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})
, where
0<\gamma<1
is the discount factor. An agent interacts with the environment sampling action from its policy
a\sim\mu(s)
, and it aims at maximizing the reward, so the goal is to find the policy that maximizes the discounted return over an infinite horizon. In particular, defining the
Q
-function as:

Q_{\mu}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[R(s,a)|\mu,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a],

we want to solve the following optimization to obtain the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
:

Q^{*}(s,a)=Q_{\mu^{*}}(s,a)=\max_{\mu}Q_{\mu}(s,a).

Generally, we do not have access to analytical form for neither
Q
nor
\mu
, making the optimization extremely complex to solve. RL algorithms, and in particular
deep
RL (DRL) algorithms, try to solve this problem learning a policy that approximates the optimal one. DRL employs neural networks to model both the policy
\mu
and the
Q
-function, so that the resulting algorithm is model free and can be used for continuous or discrete states and actions. We will not explore in depth here the differences between DRL algorithms, but we will provide a quick overview of the one we use.

We implemented the multi-agent deep reinforcement learning algorithm described in
[
20
]
: this is called an
actor-critic
algorithm, where an actor learns the policy and a critic the
Q
-function. The actor and critic are neural networks parameterized by
\theta
and
\omega
respectively, which are trained over specific losses. In particular the loss function for the critic is based on the Bellman’s equation:

\mathcal{L}_{Q}=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[(Q_{\omega}(s,a)-(r+\beta Q_{\omega}(s^{\prime},\mu_{\theta}(s^{\prime}))))^{2}\right],

where
\mathcal{D}
is a buffer where we save previously encountered states, the actions that the agent used and the reward it obtained.
The actor instead minimizes the following loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}=-\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[Q_{\omega}(s,\mu_{\theta}(s))\right]

The intuition behind this algorithm is that the critic learns to map future rewards to specific states and actions (which is the
Q
-function), while the actor tries to maximize the
Q
-function itself (represented by the critic output). What we presented here is the single agent implementation, in our multi-agent settings we use a modified version, where each agent has its own actor and critic, but the critics receive as input the actions
of all the agents
. This was introduced in
[
20
]
as a way of stabilizing the learning and improving the results. In particular, given the zonal nature of our environment, we share the actions between the agents of the same zone, but not between all agents. In Alg.

MARL Solution and Classical Solutions

We first discuss the theoretical difference between the integrated solution and the Best-Response solution. Starting from the potential problem, we consider the role of the stationarity condition coming from Eq. (
10
), which for player
n
, bid
k
and zone
z
is the following:

\begin{split}&\nabla_{x_{n,k}^{z}}L(x;\lambda,\mu,\sigma,\delta)=\Delta_{n,k}\pi_{n,k}-\lambda_{z}\Delta_{n,k}+\mu_{n,k}+\\
&+(1-\mathbb{I}_{z=z(n)})\sigma_{z(n)}\Delta_{n,k}-\mathbb{I}_{z=z(n)}\delta_{z(n)}\Delta_{n,k}.\end{split}

(17)

Notice that every
\mu
must be always
0
, since every other term is multiplied by
\Delta
and can be grouped. Furthermore,
\delta
will be
0
for most realistic cases, since the core portion is usually a small fraction of the total demand, meaning that the constraint is always saturated.
\sigma
on the other hand may be different from
0
, which means that
\lambda
must be different from
0
too, given the signs in the equation. Lastly, we highlight that
\pi
is indexed by agent and bid, while
\lambda
by zone, which means that, in most cases, the prices have to be equal to
\lambda
, virtually creating a pay-as-clear market.
On the other hand, when using the Best Response algorithm, every agent computes its own
\lambda
, meaning that they are not forced to bid at the same price, and they can actually bid at very high prices with no repercussions.
This constraint creates two very different markets, providing two different solutions to analyze.

Regarding the solution obtained from the MARL algorithm, we have less theoretical guarantees, but we can consider it as a more realistic simulation of the market, since every producer has access only to its own information and its only aim is to increase its profit.

We start by analyzing the average cost to run the market. This value is computed by summing the average profits of each producer over the full data available. In particular, for the MARL producers, we consider the cost for the final episode of their training, where we assume they reached stable bidding strategies. The results are reported in Fig.

We can immediately see how the two exact algorithms produce higher costs than the learning-based counterpart, which can be explained by the form of the constraints, as detailed above, and by the lack of actual competition. In the integrated solution, the problem is solved by an exogenous external coordinator, so by definition there will be no competition; while for the best response, each producer is free to update its decision variables uncoupled from the others. We can see the average cost for the MARL simulation with only 4 learning producers is very low, but this is also affected by the presence of static producers that always bid at marginal cost, driving the electricity price down.

V-C

Effects of Coupling Between Zones

We now analyze how the two zones influence each other in our model. To do so we simulate the market with a range of different values for the export limit
E_{z}
in Eq. (
5
). We write the export limit for Germany (Austria) as a fraction of the maximal demand of Austria (Germany):
E_{G}=c_{G}\cdot\max{D_{A}}
, and we explore the ranges
c_{G}\in[0,2]
and
c_{A}\in[0,0.2]
, so we go from completely uncoupled zone to a situation where the larger zone (Germany) has an export limit which is double the total demand of the smaller zone (Austria).

To quickly evaluate all of these values we use the MARL simulations, in both cases (4 and 8 RL agents). We report in Fig.
4
the average cost to run the market, computed in the same way as for Fig.
3
, for the range of constraints values. In particular, we trained the agents for the two cases with
c_{G}=0.4,c_{A}=0.04
, and used them on all other values. When analyzing these results, the actual costs are not particularly relevant, as they depend on the demand and other external factors, while the trend of increase/decrease is what we have to interpret.

We can see that in the case of
4
RL agents, the average cost is not influenced by the export constraint: this is likely due to the fact that the presence of static agents, always bidding at marginal price, already drives the cost down, and it is not possible to decrease it further. The case with
8
RL agents is much more interesting: we observe that the cost for Austria is rather stable, while the one for Germany is basically linearly decreasing. We can interpret this behavior considering the differences between the producers in Austria and Germany: in the smaller zone we have less producers, with lower capacities and marginal prices, while in the larger Germany the producers have higher marginal prices. Once the Austrian producers can enter the German market with their leftover capacity, they can bid the same way they would bid for their local market, which in turns, forces the German producers to bid lower, in order to be more competitive. Furthermore, since it is the market that decides how to divide each bid between zone, a producer can not create a priori a specific bid for a specific zone, each bid needs to be
general
enough to be competitive on every zone available.

We conclude by mentioning
[
23
]
, where the authors simulated a more economically sophisticated model of the European electricity market, to test what the addition of another smaller zone would do to the costs in the French zone. Their results show the same trend we observe here, with the smaller zone obtaining similar cost, while the larger one decreases its own. Our main methodological advantage, using a MARL based simulation, is that we can quickly test a range of different settings, not just a single scenario, providing a less detailed but broader view of the effect of different market designs. Further, using pre-trained agents, we are testing how a bidding strategy developed for a certain market design translates to a different one, when there is no time to adapt. This approach is of particular interest for situations where the coupling may change due to grid failures, with no warning.

VI
Conclusions

In this work we presented a model for multi-zonal ancillary markets based on a bilevel formulation. Each electricity producer is represented by an optimization problem with local and coupling constraints, with the objective of maximizing its profit, while the market operator objective is to minimize the total cost, so we create an adversarial formulation. We then reformulated the bilevel problem as a single level optimization, from the perspective of each producer, using the KKT reformulation of the market optimization. We interpret the ensemble of single level problems of each producer as a generalized Nash game. The game we obtain has side constraints, trilinear objective functions and nonconvex constraints, making it particularly challenging to solve. We analyze this game considering a relaxed version, and we obtain existence results for the Nash equilibrium that we relate to the initial generalized Nash game. We conclude our theoretical analysis showing that the generalized Nash game has a generalized potential structure.

We then consider different algorithms specifically tailored to our problem: as baselines, we rely on an integrated optimization reformulation relying on the potential structure of the game, and Gauss-Seidel Best Response to solve the generalized Nash game. Further, we implement a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning algorithm to simulate the market in a more realistic, distributed way.

We conclude validating the three algorithmic solutions on real data from the ancillary market between Germany and Austria. Our results showcase that computationally, the MARL simulation is the fastest to evaluate the data, but the agents have to be trained once before, while the best response approach is the slowest. The two classical algorithms also produce solutions with market costs much higher than the multi-agent counterpart, but the latter produces a more unfair split of the profits between the players. Finally, we test different forms of coupling between the zones, showing that a stronger coupling could lead to a decrease in cost for the larger zone, a result which is validated by other analysis present in the literature
[
23
]
.

Appendix E
MADDPG Implementation Details

There are three main problems related to the use of RL algorithms: defining the input state and the output action, mapping the action to satisfy the constraints and deciding the reward function.

State and Action

We choose the following forms for the input state and output action, for each producer:

•

state:

s^{t}_{z}=[p^{t-1},d^{t-1},w^{t-1},\sigma^{t-1}_{z}]
where
p^{t-1}
are the previous day clearing prices,
d^{t-1}
is the day,
w^{t-1}
is the day of the week and
\sigma_{z}^{t-1}
is a random signal which is zone-specific.

•

action:

a^{t}_{i}=\left[\left[\pi_{n}^{t,0},\dots,\pi_{n}^{t,K_{n}}\right],\left[\Delta_{n}^{t,0},\dots,\Delta_{i}^{t,K_{n}}\right]\right]
, where
K_{n}
is the number of bids each producer can submit.

Action Mapping

In our market model we have the following features:

•

Capacity Output:
we want each capacity bid to be at least 5 MW and less than the maximum capacity, with also the sum being less or equal than the total capacity of the producer.

•

Price Output:
each price bid must be larger than the marginal price
\pi^{min}_{n}
and smaller than the maximum bid
\pi^{max}_{n}

We tackle the two outputs separately, building a module for the capacity and one for the price. We use the following mapping:

•

Capacity Module:
The output of the capacity module is a vector of length equal to the length of the bid sequence, which is passed through the soft-max function, representing the percentage of capacity to offer at each step. The benefit of this mapping is that is rather simple, with the drawback of forcing each producer to bid its whole capacity at each bid.

•

Price Module:
The price module produces two values
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, which are used to obtain the lowest and highest price of the bidding sequence as:
\pi_{0}=\min(\pi^{max},\pi^{min}+\min(\alpha,\beta))
and
\pi_{K}=\min(\pi^{max},\pi^{min}+\max(\alpha,\beta))
. We then compute the other prices interpolating between
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{K}
.

Reward Design

The most direct approach for the reward function in the market environment would be to use the profit, but it does not encode important information: a producer may place a non-optimal bid, but if the demand is high enough, it will be accepted anyway; further the zones have different demands, so a producer in a certain zone may have a smaller profit just because the demand is lower.
What we propose is a reward formed by two terms: a normalized zone profit and a penalty for bid prices that are higher than the clearing price, weighted by the hyperparameter
\beta
:

r^{t}_{n}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left(\frac{p_{n,z}^{t}}{d^{t}_{z}/|\mathcal{N}(z)|}-\beta\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\mathbb{I}[\lambda_{z}^{t}>\pi_{n}^{min}]\cdot(\pi_{n}^{t,k}-\lambda_{z}^{t})\right)

(20)
