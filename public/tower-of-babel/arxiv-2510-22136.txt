Two dimensional anisotropic mean curvature flow with contact angle condition
Can Cui, Nung Kwan Yip Department of Mathematics, Purdue University, West Lafayette, 47907 cui147@purdue.edu, yipn@purdue.edu

Abstract
In this paper, we study surfaces which evolve by anisotropic mean curvature flow with contact angle boundary condition over a strictly convex domain in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . We establish a prior gradient estimate for smooth solutions to this boundary value problem. The same approach can also handle Dirichlet boundary condition in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . For both problems, we prove that the solutions converge to one that is translation invariant in time. Keywords : Anisotropic mean curvature flow, contact angle, gradient estimate, asymptotic behavior.



1
Introduction

The evolution of an
n
-dimensional hypersurface
M(t)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\ t\geq 0
by its mean curvature is given by the equation

\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}=\vec{H}(x,t)

(1.1)

where
x\in M(t)
, and
\vec{H}(x,t)
is the mean curvature vector at
(x,t)
.
Equation (
1.1
) is also the gradient flow of the surface area. In this sense,
the mean curvature vector is the first variation of the surface area functional.
This will be made more precise in the anisotropic case.
Mean curvature flow can be applied in various fields. It is considered in modeling the behavior of the interfaces in materials and the formation of microstructures in materials science, see
[
17
]
and
[
20
]
. It can also help enhancing features and removing noise from images in image processing and smoothing and simplifying complex meshes in computer graphics
[
3
]
and
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we will concentrate on the case
M(t)
is written as the graph of a function
u(\cdot,t)
over the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-plane, i.e.

M(t)=\{(x,u(x,t))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\}
. In this case,
the unit upward normal for the graph of
u
and its scalar mean curvature
H
are given by

\nu=\frac{(-Du,1)}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}}}{1+|Du|^{2}}\right)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}.

(1.2)

where we use the Einstein convention of summing over repeated indices

1\leq i,j\leq n
.
As the normal velocity of the graph is given by
\frac{u_{t}}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}
, the evolution equation (
1.1
) can be written in the following (non-parametric) form:

u_{t}=\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}}}{1+|Du|^{2}}\right)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}.

(1.3)

In
[
10
]
, Ecker and Huisken proved that under Lipschitz initial data with linear growth, equation (
1.3
) has a smooth solution for all times. In
[
11
]
, they relaxed the initial data to be just locally Lipschitz continuous. In
[
15
]
, Huisken considered (
1.3
) over a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with prescribed contact angle condition at the boundary:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllcl}u_{t}&=&\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{u_{x_{i}}u_{x_{j}}}{1+|Du|^{2}}\right)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}&\text{in}&\ Q_{T}=\Omega\times[0,T],\\
D_{N}u&=&-\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}\cos\theta&\text{on}&\ \Gamma_{T}=\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
u(\cdot,0)&=&u_{0}(\cdot)&\text{in}&\ \ \Omega_{0}=\Omega\times\{0\},\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

where the contact angle
\theta
is described as the angle between the unit upward normal
\nu
to the graph of
u
and the unit inner normal
N
to
\partial\Omega
in
the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-plane, or equivalently the angle between the tangent plane of
u
and the vertical direction – see Figure 1, left. In
[
15
]
, Huisken only considered the case
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, which reduces (
1.4
) to a homogeneous Neumann problem. He proved that the solution to (
1.4
) asymptotically converges to a constant function. In
[
2
]
, Altschuler and Wu considered (
1.4
) with
n=1
and a constant
\theta
between
0
and
\pi
. They proved that the solution converges to a translating solution. This result also works for a class of quasilinear equations. Later, in
[
1
]
, they proved the same convergence result over a strictly convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
under the assumption that the derivative of
\theta
along the boundary is bounded by the curvature of
\partial\Omega
. In physical settings,
\theta
is the angle between the liquid (graph of
u
) and solid phases at their interface (see Figure 1, right) and hence describes the scenario that a liquid intersects with a solid phase – see
[
12
]
for physical background.

Figure 1:
Left: contact angle
\theta
, Right: liquid contact with solid.

In this paper, we are interested in extending (
1.4
) to the anisotropic case. Compared to the isotropic equation, anisotropic mean curvature flow has several applications where directional dependence plays a crucial role. In crystalline materials, the energy associated with the surface often varies by direction due to atomic arrangement. Anisotropic mean curvature flow can model shape evolution of crystal surfaces more accurately than isotropic mean curvature flow by accounting for this directional dependency. Isotropic flow would tend to smooth surfaces uniformly, but anisotropic flow can capture faceted structures where certain directions are favored, leading to realistic simulations of crystal shapes with sharp edges and specific orientations. We refer to
[
19
,
20
]
for further information on
the use of mean curvature flows in the context of materials science.

Now we introduce the concept of anisotropy. Consider a positive function
F
defined on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}

which is extended to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by homogeneity of degree one:

F(\lambda\hat{p})=\lambda F(\hat{p}),\quad\text{for any $\lambda>0$ and $\hat{p}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}$}.

Then the anisotropic analogue of equation (
1.3
) is given by

u_{t}=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}.

(1.5)

(Note that in the above,
D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F
refers to the second derivatives of
F
with respect to the first
n
variables with
1\leq i,j\leq n
.)
In order for the above equation to be parabolic,
F
is required to be convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
We remark that equation (
1.3
) can in fact be put into the same form as (
1.5
) if
F
is
simply given by
\overline{F}(p)=|p|
. Similar to the isotropic mean curvature flow (
1.3
), (
1.5
) arises as a gradient flow for the following surface energy functional:

E(u)=\int F(-\nu)\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}dx\,\,\left(=\int F(Du,-1)dx\right).

(1.6)

With this, we compute the variation of
E
with respect to
u
:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}E(u+s\varphi)\right|_{s=0}=\left.\frac{d}{ds}\int F(Du+sD\varphi,-1)dx\right|_{s=0}=\int-D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\varphi\,dx.

From the above, we define the first variation of
E
as

\partial E=D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}

which is naturally called the anisotropic mean curvature.
In fact, in this paper, we can consider the following more general version of (
1.5
)

u_{t}=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}\,G(-\nu)D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}},

(1.7)

by incorporating a positive mobility function
G(\cdot):\mathbb{S}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. (We also extend
G
to be a homogeneous degree one function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
:
G(\lambda\hat{p})=\lambda G(\hat{p})

for any
\lambda\geq 0
and
\hat{p}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
.)

Here we mentioned some relevant results for anisotropic mean curvature flows. Given a convex, homogeneous function
F
of degree one, in
[
4
]
, Andrews proved that the solution to the volume-preserving anisotropic mean curvature flow stays smooth and convex for all times, and converges to the Wulff shape corresponding to
F
as
t\longrightarrow\infty
. Furthermore, in
[
6
]
, under some assumption guaranteeing that
F
does not deviate too much from the isotropic function
\overline{F}
, Clutterbuck proved an interior gradient estimate. Recently, in
[
5
]
, Cesaroni, Kröner and Novaga construct soliton solutions as an entire graph assuming cylindrical anisotropies and mobilities – see equation (4.1) in their work.

Following the works
[
15
]
,
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
, in this paper, we study the contact angle boundary value problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllcl}u_{t}&=&G(Du,-1)\ D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}&\text{in}&\ Q_{T}=\Omega\times[0,T],\\
D_{N}u&=&-\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}\cos\theta&\text{on}&\ \Gamma_{T}=\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
u(\cdot,0)&=&u_{0}(\cdot)&\text{in}&\ \ \Omega_{0}=\Omega\times\{0\},\end{array}\right.

(1.8)

where
\Omega
is a bounded and strictly convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Hence we generalize the result in
[
1
]
to (
1.8
). In Section 2, we will present proper conditions on the anisotropic function
F
and its derivatives to guarantee that it does not deviate too much from the isotropic function
|p|
.

We introduce our main results in Section 3. We establish a priori gradient estimate to the solution to (
1.8
), which implies uniform ellipticity of the operator. Existence and uniqueness of the solution then follow from the standard theory of quasilinear parabolic equations. The long time behavior of the solution to (
1.8
) is related to the solution of the following elliptic problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllcl}\lambda&=&G(Dw,-1)\ D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Dw,-1)w_{x_{i}x_{j}}&\text{in}&\ \Omega,\\
D_{N}w&=&-\sqrt{1+|Dw|^{2}}\cos\theta&\text{on}&\ \partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

where the solution is the pair
(u,\lambda)
with a constant
\lambda
to be determined.
It turns out that the techniques to establish gradient estimate to (
1.8
) is also applicable to (
1.9
). This gradient estimate then implies the existence of
\lambda
. Uniqueness of
\lambda
is derived from the boundary condition. As a result, the function
w
leads to the translating solution
\tilde{w}(x,t)=w(x)+\lambda t
to (
1.8
) with speed
\lambda
. Finally, an application of the strong maximum principle implies that solutions to (
1.8
) converges to
\tilde{w}
(up to a constant).

Unfortunately this technique does not apply when
n>2
. This is due to the presence of mixed second order tangential derivatives along the boundary in higher dimensions, as we will remark in Section 3. Nevertheless, it can be applied to Dirichlet boundary problem in arbitrary dimension
n
. In this case, as shown in Section 4, the convexity condition of the domain boundary can be reduced to weighted mean convexity which is related to the anisotropic function.
This condition, regarded as a replacement of the boundary curvature condition considered in
[
18
, Section 9]
and
[
14
, Section 14.3]
, is compatible with the results from the theory of quasilinear parabolic equations. Similar to Section 3,
we also prove results for the corresponding elliptic version.

In recent years, the results in
[
1
]
about isotropic mean curvature flows were generalized to arbitrary dimension
n
– see
[
16
]
for Neumann boundary condition and
[
13
]
for contact angle condition. In an upcoming work
[
7
]
, we are also able to
modify their techniques to the anisotropic case.

Preliminary Information

Here we provide some preliminary computation useful for the rest of this paper.

First we give formulas for the derivatives of the function
|p|
and our anisotropic function
F
which is assumed to be convex and homogeneous of degree one. Note that

D_{p_{i}}|p|=\frac{p_{i}}{|p|},\quad\text{and}\quad D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}|p|=\frac{1}{|p|}(\delta_{ij}-\frac{p_{i}p_{j}}{|p|^{2}}).

(2.1)

Upon introducing
\xi=\xi(p):=\frac{p}{|p|}
, we have

\displaystyle F(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|p|F(\xi),

(2.2)

\displaystyle D_{p_{i}}F(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{|p|}F(\xi)+|p|D_{p_{i}}F(\xi),

(2.3)

\displaystyle D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|p|}(\delta_{ij}-\frac{p_{i}p_{j}}{|p|^{2}})F(\xi)+\frac{p_{i}}{|p|}D_{p_{j}}F(\xi)+\frac{p_{j}}{|p|}D_{p_{i}}F(\xi)+|p|D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(\xi).

(2.4)

Note that in the above, the indices
i,j
range from
1
to
n+1
.

The following assumptions are given to quantify the deviations of the mobility and anisotropic functions

G
and
F
from their isotropic versions,
\overline{G}(\xi)\equiv 1
and
\overline{F}(\xi)\equiv 1
:

1.

For
G(\xi)
, there exist positive constants
g_{0}
and
G_{0}
, such that

g_{0}\leq G(\xi)\leq G_{0}.

(2.5)

2.

For
F(\xi)
, there exist positive constants
m_{0},\ M_{0},\ m_{1},\ m_{2}
, such that for any
1\leq i,j\leq n

\begin{split}&m_{0}\leq F(\xi)\leq M_{0},\\
&|D_{p_{i}}F(\xi)|<\frac{m_{1}}{|p|},\\
&\left\|\left[D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(\xi)\right]\right\|<\frac{m_{2}}{|p|^{2}},\end{split}

(2.6)

where in the above,
\|M\|=\sqrt{\text{tr}(M^{T}M)}
refers to the Frobenious norm of a matrix.
In Sections 3 and 4, we will give further conditions on these constants.
As a reference, note that in the case of isotropic function
\overline{F}(p)=|p|
, we have
m_{0}=M_{0}=1
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=0
.

For convenience, we introduce the following notations which are used frequently later:

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}},

\displaystyle A\,(=A(Du))=\big(a^{ij}\big),\,\,\,a^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(Du,-1)\ D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1),

\displaystyle\triangle_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{ij}D_{x_{i}}D_{x_{j}}.

Note that as

Then for any vector fields

\displaystyle D_{V}W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{j}D_{j}W^{i}e_{i},

\displaystyle D_{V}D_{W}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{i}W^{j}D^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}u+\langle D_{V}W,Du\rangle.

We further introduce the following notations.

\displaystyle\langle V,W\rangle_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{ij}V^{i}W^{j};\quad\langle V,W\rangle_{\delta}=\delta^{ij}V^{i}W^{j}=V^{i}W^{i},

and for matrices

\displaystyle\langle P,Q\rangle_{A,\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{ij}\delta^{kl}p_{ik}q_{jl},

where
\delta^{kl}

In order to prove our gradient estimate, we need to differentiate along the boundary
\partial\Omega

To do this, we will extend using parallel transport of the unit tangent

\partial\Omega

\partial\Omega
(along the normal direction). This leads to the following formula.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
k
be the curvature of
\partial\Omega
. Then on
\partial\Omega
, we have:

1.

For
f\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
, the mixed derivatives of

D_{N}D_{T}f=D_{T}D_{N}f+kD_{T}f.

2.

D_{T}T=kN;\ D_{T}N=-kT;\ D_{N}T=D_{N}N=0
.

3.

Using the boundary condition in (
1.8
), we have

|D_{N}u|^{2}=\cot^{2}\theta(1+|D_{T}u|^{2}),

|D_{T}u|^{2}=\sin^{2}\theta(1+|Du|^{2})-1.

4.

For the second derivations, we have

\displaystyle D_{N}u_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cos{\theta}\frac{Du\cdot Du_{t}}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}},

\displaystyle D_{T}D_{N}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}(D_{T}\theta)v-\cos{\theta}(D_{T}v),

\displaystyle D_{N}D_{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}(D_{T}\theta)v-\cos{\theta}(D_{T}v)+kD_{T}u,

\displaystyle D_{T}D_{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin{\theta}\cos{\theta}(D_{T}\theta)v^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\ vD_{T}v}{D_{T}u}.

From item

all the second order derivatives of

are given except

,
which can only be found using the governing equation (

). This is a key step in deriving our gradient estimate.

3
Main Results with Contact Angle Boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

In this section, we prove the crucial gradient estimate for (
1.8
).
This will imply the long time existence of solution.

First we state the main assumptions for our results.

A1.

The domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is assumed to be bounded, of class

C^{3}
, and uniformly convex in the sense that there are positive constants
k_{0},k_{1}

such that
k_{0}\leq k(x)\leq k_{1}
for any
x\in\partial\Omega
.

A2.

For the boundary contact angle
\theta
, there are positive constant

\delta_{0},\theta_{0}
such that

|D_{T}\theta(x)|\leq k(x)-\delta_{0}\quad\text{for any $x\in\partial\Omega$, and}\quad\theta_{0}\leq\theta\leq\pi-\theta_{0}.

A3.

For the anisotropic function
F
, the constants
m_{0}
and
m_{2}

from (
2.6
) satisfies
m_{2}<m_{0}
.

A4.

The initial condition
u_{0}
is assumed to be
C^{3}(\overline{\Omega})
and satisfies
D_{N}u_{0}=-\sqrt{1+|Du_{0}|^{2}}\cos\theta
on
\partial\Omega
.

We will assume A1–A4 for the rest of this Section.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a positive constant
C_{1}
, independent of time such that for any solution
u
to (
1.8
), we have,

\sup_{Q_{T}}|Du|\leq C_{1}.

(3.1)

Before proving the above theorem, similar to
[
1
]
,
we first establish an a priori bound on the time derivative of
u
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The following holds for
u_{t}

\sup_{Q_{T}}|u_{t}|=\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|u_{t}|=:C_{2}.

Proof.

From (
1.8
) we compute

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|u_{t}|^{2}&=\sum_{i,j}(|u_{t}|^{2})_{x_{i}x_{j}}-2\sum_{i,j}a^{ij}u_{t,x_{i}}u_{t,x_{j}}+\sum_{i,j,k}u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\frac{\partial a^{ij}}{\partial p^{k}}(|u_{t}|^{2})_{x_{k}}\\
&=\triangle_{A}|u_{t}|^{2}-2\langle Du_{t},Du_{t}\rangle_{A}+u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\langle D_{p}a^{ij},D|u_{t}|^{2}\rangle_{\delta}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Hence, for the elliptic operator
L(\varphi):=-\triangle_{A}\varphi-u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\langle D_{p}a^{ij},D\varphi\rangle_{\delta}
,
we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|u_{t}|^{2}+L|u_{t}|^{2}=-2\langle Du_{t},Du_{t}\rangle_{A}\leq 0.

(3.3)

Thus the weak maximum principle leads to

\sup_{Q_{T}}|u_{t}|^{2}=\sup_{\Gamma_{T}\cup\Omega_{0}}|u_{t}|^{2}
.

Suppose the maximum of
|u_{t}|
occurs at
(x_{0},\tau)\in\Gamma_{T}
.
Then
D_{T}u_{t}(x_{0},\tau)=0
. Applying the boundary condition in (
1.8
) at this point, we have

D_{N}u_{t}=-\cos{\theta}\ \frac{D_{N}u\ D_{N}u_{t}+D_{T}u\ D_{T}u_{t}}{v}=-\cos{\theta}\ \frac{D_{N}u\ D_{N}u_{t}}{v}=\cos^{2}{\theta}D_{N}u_{t}

so that
D_{N}u_{t}=0
. Now, if
L(|u_{t}|^{2})\leq 0
, then Hopf Lemma gives
D_{N}u_{t}<0

which is clearly a contradiction. Hence
L(|u_{t}|^{2})
must be strictly
positive. Then (
3.3
) implies that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|u_{t}|^{2}<0
so that
(x_{0},\tau)
cannot be a
maximum point of
u_{t}
. Thus we conclude that

\sup_{Q_{T}}|u_{t}|=\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|u_{t}|
.
∎

Next, we prove that the maximum of
|Du|
occurs on the parabolic boundary,
\Gamma_{T}\cup\Omega_{0}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

\sup_{Q_{T}}|Du|^{2}=\sup_{\Gamma_{T}\cup\Omega_{0}}|Du|^{2}
.

Proof.

Similar to the calculation of above Lemma, we have from (
1.8
) that,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|Du|^{2}&=\sum_{i,j}a^{ij}(|Du|^{2})_{x_{i}x_{j}}-2\sum_{i,j}a^{ij}\sum_{k}u_{x_{k}x_{i}}u_{x_{k}x_{j}}+\sum_{i,j,l}u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\frac{\partial a^{ij}}{\partial p^{l}}(|Du|^{2})_{x_{l}}\\
&=\triangle_{A}|Du|^{2}-2\langle D^{2}u,D^{2}u\rangle_{A,\delta}+u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\langle D_{p}a^{ij},D|Du|^{2}\rangle_{\delta}.\end{split}

(3.4)

Hence, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|Du|^{2}+L|Du|^{2}=-2\langle D^{2}u,D^{2}u\rangle_{A,\delta}\leq 0.

By the weak maximum principle again, we obtain
\sup_{Q_{T}}|Du|^{2}=\sup_{\Gamma_{T}\cup\Omega_{0}}|Du|^{2}
.
∎

Now we provide the proof of the gradient estimate (
3.1
).
From the previous Lemma, we
only need to estimate the gradient on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. To do this,
we decompose the equation in (
1.8
) along the tangential and normal directions. Using the formula from Lemma

, the second normal derivative

can be computed.

Proof of Theorem

3.1
.
Note that we just need to consider the case that the maximum of
|Du|^{2}
occurs at
(x_{0},\tau)\in\Gamma_{T}
. Suppose
|D_{T}u|(x_{0},\tau)<1

|Du|^{2}(x_{0},\tau)=\left.\frac{|D_{T}u|^{2}+1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\right|_{x_{0}}-1<\frac{2}{\sin^{2}\theta_{0}}-1,

(3.5)

where in the last step, we have used assumption A2 about
\theta
.
Hence the gradient bound holds in this case. Therefore without loss of generality,
we can assume that
|D_{T}u|(x_{0},\tau)\geq 1
.

At any maximum point
(x_{0},\tau)
of
|Du|^{2}
, we have

D_{N}|Du|^{2}(x_{0},\tau)\leq 0,

(3.6)

D_{T}|Du|^{2}(x_{0},\tau)=0.

(3.7)

The latter in the above implies
D_{T}v(x_{0},\tau)=0
. From Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle D_{T}D_{N}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}(D_{T}\theta)v,

(3.8)

\displaystyle D_{N}D_{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}(D_{T}\theta)v+kD_{T}u,

(3.9)

\displaystyle D_{T}D_{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin{\theta}\cos{\theta}(D_{T}\theta)v^{2}}{D_{T}u}.

(3.10)

We can then rewrite (
3.6
) as

D_{N}u\ D_{N}D_{N}u(x_{0},\tau)+D_{T}u\ D_{N}D_{T}u(x_{0},\tau)\leq 0.

(3.11)

Consider the orthonormal matrix
O=(T,N)
. In order to obtain
D_{N}D_{N}u
,
we will rewrite (
1.8
) by decomposing the elliptic operator into its tangential and normal components:

\tilde{b}^{i}=(u^{2})_{x_{k}x_{l}}\int^{1}_{0}a^{kl}_{p_{i}}(\eta Du^{1}+(1-\eta)Du^{2})d\eta
and
\tilde{c}^{ij}=\int_{0}^{1}D_{p_{j}}h^{i}(\eta Du^{1}+(1-\eta)Du^{2})d\eta
(define
h^{i}(p)=\frac{p^{i}}{\sqrt{1+|p|^{2}}}
and hence
[\tilde{c}^{ij}]
is a positive definite matrix). The strong maximum principle implies that the oscillation of
\tilde{u}

\text{osc}(\tilde{u})(t)=\max_{\bar{\Omega}}\tilde{u}(x,t)-\min_{\bar{\Omega}}\tilde{u}(x,t),

is strictly decreasing in
t
. The result follows if

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\text{osc}(\tilde{u})(t)=0.

Suppose otherwise that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\text{osc}(u)(t)=\delta>0
. Then for some sequence
t_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
, we define

u^{1,n}(x,t):=u^{1}(x,t+t_{n})-\lambda t_{n},

and

u^{2,n}(x,t):=u^{2}(x,t+t_{n})-\lambda t_{n}.

Notice that by the last Corollary, we have
|u^{i,n}-\lambda t|\leq C
.
Then there exists a subsequence of
u^{1,n}
and
u^{2,n}
converging locally uniformly to
u^{1,\ast}
and
u^{2,\ast}
respectively. Let
\tilde{u}^{\ast}=u^{1,\ast}-u^{2,\ast}
. Then we have

\text{osc}(\tilde{u}^{\ast})(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{osc}(\tilde{u}^{\ast})(t+t_{n})=\delta.

Notice that
\tilde{u}^{\ast}
satisfies a parabolic equation similar to (
3.32
). By the strong maximum principle and Hopf lemma,
\tilde{u}^{\ast}
is a constant contradicting the fact that
\text{osc}(\tilde{u}^{\ast})(t)=\delta
.
∎

4
Dirichlet Problem in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

We found that the method in the proof of Theorem
3.1
can be applied to obtain a gradient bound on the solution to the following Dirichlet boundary value problem in general dimensions.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllcl}u_{t}&=&G(Du,-1)\ D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}&\text{in}&\ Q_{T},\\
u&=&f(\cdot,t)&\text{on}&\ \Gamma_{T}\\
u(\cdot,0)&=&u_{0}(\cdot)&\text{in}&\ \Omega_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

In order to utilize some existing results, we find it convenient to assume that

f
is of class
C^{3}
. The precise condition on
f
will be stated just before the main theorem below.

Before proceeding, we need to generalize the geometric formulas from Section 2 to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
x\in\partial\Omega
. Choose an orthonormal basis
T_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n-1
, such that each
T_{i}
is along a principal direction of
\partial\Omega
with principal
curvature
k_{i}
. As before, let also
N
be the inward normal to
\partial\Omega
.
Upon extending
T_{i}
and
N
to a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
by means of parallel transport along the normal direction of
\partial\Omega
, we have the following formula.

1.

D_{T_{i}}T_{i}=k_{i}N
and
D_{T_{j}}T_{i}=0
for
i\neq j
.

2.

D_{T_{i}}N=-k_{i}T_{i}
.

3.

For
D_{N}T_{i}
, by considering the geodesic in the direction
T_{i}
, we have a similar formula as in two dimensions,

D_{N}D_{T_{i}}f=k_{i}D_{T_{i}}f+D_{T_{i}}D_{N}f.

(4.2)

which implies
D_{N}T_{i}=0
.

4.

The fact
D_{N}N=0
shows by differentiating
|N|^{2}=1
and using
N\cdot T_{i}=0
.

Now consider the boundary value problem (
4.1
). We assume there is a constant
M
such that the boundary
function
f
is
C^{3}
satisfying the following,

|f_{t}|,\ |D_{T_{i}}f|,\ |D_{T_{i}T_{j}}f|\leq M.

(4.3)

At each point on the boundary
\Gamma_{T}
, we order the principal curvatures
k_{i}
such that for some
\alpha
,
1\leq\alpha\leq n-1
, we have

k_{1}\geq\cdots\geq k_{\alpha}\geq 0>k_{\alpha+1}\geq\cdots k_{n-1}.

(4.4)

Let
\max_{i}|k_{i}|<\infty
. Then we state the corresponding gradient estimate for the solution
u
of
(
4.1
) in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
M_{0},\ m_{0},\ m_{1},\ m_{2},\ g_{0},\ G_{0}
be given in (
2.5
) and (
2.6
). Consider the following two constants

\begin{split}\gamma_{1}&:=g_{0}\left(\frac{2}{2+M^{2}}m_{0}-\sqrt{2}Mm_{1}-m_{2}\right),\\
\gamma_{2}&:=G_{0}\left(M_{0}+\sqrt{2}Mm_{1}+m_{2}\right).\end{split}

(4.5)

Assume that they satisfy the following conditions:

\gamma_{1}>0,\quad\gamma_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}k_{i}+\gamma_{2}\sum_{i=\alpha+1}^{n-1}k_{i}>0.

(4.6)

Then there exists a constant
C=C(u_{0},M,\partial\Omega,F,n)
such that

\sup_{Q_{T}}|Du|\leq C.

Here we make some remarks about the assumptions and statement.

1.

The condition
\gamma_{1}>0
holds for small enough
m_{1},m_{2}
together
with small
M
. The former means that
F
is close to
the isotropic function
\overline{F}
while the latter indicates that
the Dirichlet boundary condition
f
cannot change too quickly along the boundary.

2.

Since
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}
, the second condition in (
4.6
) holds when

\partial\Omega
is convex, i.e.,
\alpha=n-1
or if
\left|\sum_{i=\alpha+1}^{n-1}k_{i}\right|
is small.

3.

Similarly to the strategy in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we apply maximum principle to obtain a gradient bound on the boundary. In the current Dirichlet case, all we need is an upper bound for
D_{N}u
as all the tangential derivatives of
u
are given
by the boundary function
f
.

Proof.

Notice that Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
also hold in general dimensions, i.e. for some positive constant
C_{3}=C_{3}(u_{0},M)
, we have

\sup_{Q_{T}}|u_{t}|\leq C_{3},\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\sup_{Q_{T}}|Du|^{2}=\sup_{\Gamma_{T}\cup\Omega_{0}}|Du|^{2}.

(4.7)

Now suppose the maximum of
|Du|
occurs at
(x_{0},\tau)\in\Gamma_{T}
. Note that on
\partial\Omega
,

|Du|^{2}=\sum_{k}|D_{T_{k}}u|^{2}+|D_{N}u|^{2}\,\,\,\text{and}\ \ \ D_{T_{k}}u=D_{T_{k}}f.

Hence, we only need to estimate
|D_{N}u|
at
(x_{0},\tau)
. Assume
|D_{N}u|\geq 1
. At this point, by maximum principle, we have

\displaystyle D_{N}|Du|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0,

(4.8)

\displaystyle D_{T_{i}}|Du|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\ 1\leq i\leq n-1.

(4.9)

The last equation becomes

D_{T_{i}}\left(|D_{N}u|^{2}+\sum_{k}|D_{T_{k}}u|^{2}\right)=0,

D_{N}u\ D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u+\sum_{k}D_{T_{k}}u\ D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{k}}u=0,

D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u=-\frac{\sum_{k}D_{T_{k}}u\ D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{k}}u}{D_{N}u}.

(4.10)

Since
\{T_{1}\ \cdots\ T_{n-1}\ N\}
is an orthonormal basis, similar to (
3.12
), we have

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}a^{T_{i}T_{j}}D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{j}}u-\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}D_{N}u+\sum_{i}a^{T_{i}N}D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u+\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}N}D_{T_{i}}u

(4.11)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}a^{NT_{i}}D_{N}D_{T_{i}}u+a^{NN}D_{N}D_{N}u,

where we similarly define the following quantities:

\begin{split}&a^{T_{i}T_{j}}:=\left(T_{i}^{t}A\ T_{j}\right),\quad a^{T_{i}N}:=\left(T_{i}^{t}A\ N\right),\\
&a^{NT_{i}}:=\left(N^{t}A\ T_{i}\right),\quad a^{NN}:=\left(N^{t}A\ N\right).\end{split}

(4.12)

Using the relation
D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u=k_{i}D_{T_{i}}u+D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u
and (
4.11
), we can express
D_{N}D_{N}u
as

\begin{split}a^{NN}D_{N}D_{N}u\ =\ &u_{t}-\sum_{i,j}a^{T_{i}T_{j}}D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{j}}u+\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}D_{N}u\\
&-2\sum_{i}a^{T_{i}N}D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u-2\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}N}D_{T_{i}}u.\end{split}

(4.13)

Upon expanding (
4.8
), we get
D_{N}u\ D_{N}D_{N}u+\sum_{k}D_{T_{k}}u\ D_{N}D_{T_{k}}u\leq 0
. Using the above expression for
D_{N}D_{N}u
, we obtain,

D_{N}u\Big(u_{t}-\sum_{i,j}a^{T_{i}T_{j}}D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{j}}u+\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}D_{N}u-2\sum_{i}a^{T_{i}N}D_{T_{i}}D_{N}u-2\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}N}D_{T_{i}}u\Big)\\
+a^{NN}\sum_{l}D_{T_{l}}u(D_{T_{l}}D_{N}u+k_{l}D_{T_{l}}u)\leq 0.

Dividing the above by
|D_{N}u|
, we obtain,

\begin{split}\left(\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}\right)|D_{N}u|\ \leq\ &|u_{t}|+\sum_{i,j}|a^{T_{i}T_{j}}||D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{j}}u|\\
&+2\sum_{i}|a^{T_{i}N}|\frac{\sum_{l}|D_{T_{l}}u||D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{l}}u|}{|D_{N}u|}+2\sum_{i}|k_{i}||a^{T_{i}N}||D_{T_{i}}u|\\
&+\sum_{i,l}|a^{NN}|\frac{|D_{T_{l}}u||D_{T_{i}}u||D_{T_{l}}D_{T_{i}}u|}{|D_{N}u|^{2}}+\sum_{l}|a^{NN}||k_{l}|\frac{|D_{T_{l}}u|^{2}}{|D_{N}u|}\end{split}

(4.14)

Note that on
\partial\Omega
, we have

|u_{t}|=|f_{t}|,\ |D_{T_{i}}u|=|D_{T_{i}}f|,\ |D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{j}}u|=|D_{T_{i}}D_{T_{j}}f|

which according to (
4.3
) are all bounded by the constant
M
.

Next, without loss of generality, we assume
|D_{N}u|>\epsilon>0
for some constant

\epsilon
which can be chosen as any positive number. Now we estimate the terms in
(
4.12
). The crucial estimate is the lower bound for
a^{T_{i}T_{i}}
:

\begin{split}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}&=G(\xi)\Bigg(\left(1-\frac{|D_{T_{i}}u|^{2}}{1+|Du|^{2}}\right)F(\xi)+2D_{T_{i}}uD^{T_{i}}_{p}F(\xi)+v^{2}D^{T_{i}}_{p}D^{T_{i}}_{p}F(\xi)\Bigg)\\
&\geq G(\xi)\Bigg(\left(1-\frac{M^{2}}{1+M^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}\right)F(\xi)-\frac{2Mm_{1}}{v}-m_{2}\Bigg)\\
&\geq g_{0}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon^{2}}{1+M^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}m_{0}-\frac{2Mm_{1}}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}}-m_{2}\right).\end{split}

(4.15)

By homogeneity for
F
and
G
, upper bounds for
a^{T_{i}T_{j}}
and the other terms are simple to obtain.

|a^{T_{i}T_{i}}|\leq G(\xi)\left(F(\xi)+\frac{2Mm_{1}}{v}+m_{2}\right)\leq G_{0}\left(M_{0}+\frac{2Mm_{1}}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}}+m_{2}\right).

(4.16)

For
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle|a^{T_{i}T_{j}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\xi)\Bigg|\left(-\frac{D_{T_{i}}u\ D_{T_{j}}u}{1+|Du|^{2}}F(\xi)\right)+D_{T_{i}}u\ D^{T_{j}}_{p}F(\xi)+D_{T_{j}}u\ D^{T_{i}}_{p}F(\xi)+v^{2}D^{T_{i}}_{p}D^{T_{j}}_{p}F(\xi)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G(\xi)\left(\frac{M^{2}}{1+M^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}F(\xi)+\frac{2Mm_{1}}{v}+m_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G_{0}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{1+M^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}M_{0}+\frac{2Mm_{1}}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}}+m_{2}\right),

\displaystyle|a^{T_{i}N}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\xi)\Bigg|\left(-\frac{D_{T_{i}}u\ D_{N}u}{1+|Du|^{2}}F(\xi)\right)+D_{T_{i}}u\ D^{N}_{p}F(\xi)+D_{N}u\ D^{T_{i}}_{p}F(\xi)+v^{2}D^{T_{i}}_{p}D^{N}_{p}F(\xi)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G(\xi)\left(\frac{1}{2}F(\xi)+\frac{Mm_{1}}{v}+m_{1}+m_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G_{0}\left(\frac{1}{2}M_{0}+(\frac{M}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon^{2}}}+1)m_{1}+m_{2}\right),

\displaystyle|a^{NN}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(\xi)\left|\left(1-\frac{|D_{N}u|^{2}}{1+|Du|^{2}}\right)F(\xi)+2D_{N}u\ D^{N}_{p}F(\xi)+v^{2}D^{N}_{p}D^{N}_{p}F(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G(\xi)\left(\left(1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{1+\epsilon^{2}+(n-1)M^{2}}\right)F(\xi)+2m_{1}+m_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G_{0}\left(\frac{1+(n-1)M^{2}}{1+\epsilon^{2}+(n-1)M^{2}}M_{0}+2m_{1}+m_{2}\right).

Hence the right hand side of (
4.14
) is bounded by some constant
C(M,F,\epsilon,n)
.

Now we simply take
\epsilon=1
. Then the right hand side of (
4.14
) can be bounded by the boundary function
f
. Hence, we have the following estimate,

\left(\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}\right)|D_{N}u|\leq C(M,K,F,n).

(4.17)

Then the lower and upper bounds (
4.15
), (
4.16
)
for
a^{T_{i}T_{i}}
become

\displaystyle a^{T_{i}T_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle g_{0}\left(\frac{2}{2+M^{2}}m_{0}-\sqrt{2}Mm_{1}-m_{2}\right)=:\gamma_{1},

(4.18)

\displaystyle|a^{T_{i}T_{i}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle G_{0}\left(M_{0}+\sqrt{2}Mm_{1}+m_{2}\right)=:\gamma_{2}.

(4.19)

From assumption (
4.6
), we have that

\sum_{i}k_{i}a^{T_{i}T_{i}}
is bounded from below by a positive number that depends only
on the functions
F
and
G
and the boundary
\partial\Omega
but not on the solution

u
.
We can then conclude that

|D_{N}u|\leq C(M,\partial\Omega,G,F,n).

finishing the proof of the gradient estimate.
∎

Next we consider the following elliptic problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllcl}\lambda&=&G(Dw,-1)\ D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Dw,-1)w_{x_{i}x_{j}}&\text{in}&\ \Omega,\\
w&=&g&\text{on}&\ \partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(4.20)

for some constant
\lambda
.
Now this boundary function
g
is independent of time, and satisfies

|D_{T_{i}}g|,\ |D_{T_{i}T_{j}}g|\leq M,

(4.21)

for some constant
M
. For the existence of solution of the above equation, we can in fact invoke the Fundamental Existence Theorem in
[
18
, Chapter 1, Section 1]
. This is applicable in our present case because
similar to the proof in Theorem
4.1
, by substituting
u_{t}
with
\lambda
, we do have the gradient estimate for (
4.20
), assuming the existence of a solution.
Note that the solution

w
exists for any finite value of
\lambda
. This is in contrast to the contact angle case in which
\lambda
is uniquely determined by the function
\theta
appearing in the boundary condition (
1.4
).

Similar to the contact angle boundary condition, the function
w
suggests a translating solution
\tilde{w}(x,t)=w(x)+\lambda t
to the parabolic problem
(
4.1
) with Dirichlet boundary function
f
and initial condition
u_{0}(x)
replaced by
g(x)+\lambda t
and
u_{0}(x)=w(x)
, respectively.
With this, the next step is to study the asymptotic behavior of a solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllcl}u_{t}&=&G(Du,-1)\ D^{2}_{p_{i}p_{j}}F(Du,-1)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}&\text{in}&\ Q_{T},\\
u&=&g(\cdot)+\lambda t&\text{on}&\ \Gamma_{T},\\
u(\cdot,0)&=&u_{0}(\cdot)&\text{in}&\ \Omega_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(4.22)

With the gradient estimate in Theorem
4.1
, we can have the following simple but useful result.

Corollary 4.2
.

For a solution
u=u(x,t)
of (
4.20
), there exists a constant
C
such that

|u(x,t)-\lambda t|\leq C.

(4.23)

Proof.

This is simply due to the fact that
u(x,t)-\lambda t=g
on the boundary and the gradient estimate obtained in Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Then we can follow the idea of
[
9
, Theorem 4.6]
to obtain the following
convergence result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
u(x,t)
be a classical solution to (
4.22
). Then there exists a constant
C
such that
u(x,t)
will converges to
w(x)+\lambda t+C
as
t\longrightarrow\infty
. The convergence is uniform in
x\in\Omega
.

Just a word about the idea of proof. At time
t=0
, we can choose
C_{1}<C_{2}
such that

w(x)+C_{1}\leq u(x,0)\leq w(x)+C_{2}
for all
x\in\Omega
. As both
w(x)+\lambda t
and
u(x,t)
are solutions of (
4.22
), we have

w(x)+\lambda t+C_{1}\leq u(x,t)\leq w(x)+\lambda t+C_{2}
for all
t>0
.
Then by strong maximum principle, we can choose a
C_{1}^{\prime}
and
C_{2}^{\prime}
satisfying

C_{1}<C_{1}^{\prime}<C_{2}^{\prime}<C_{2}
such that

w(x)+\lambda t+C_{1}^{\prime}\leq u(x,t)\leq w(x)+\lambda t+C_{2}^{\prime}
for
t>t^{\prime}

where
t^{\prime}>0
. Iterating this procedure we can in fact keep increasing
C_{1}^{\prime}
and decreasing
C_{2}^{\prime}
such that they converge to a single constant. With more careful estimation, we can in fact show that the convergence is exponential in time, as in

[
9
, Theorem 4.6]
.
