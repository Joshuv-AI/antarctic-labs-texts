Thyroid hormone augmentation for bipolar disorder: a neurobiological and clinical narrative review
Authors: Muhammad Ibrahim, Umair F Bhutto, Ramy Yassa, Rif S El-Mallakh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBipolar disorder (BD) is a severe, chronic psychiatric illness defined by profound and recurrent fluctuations in mood, energy, and function. Treatment is generally limited by both the efficacy and safety of available agents. Thyroid augmentation has been found to be useful to treat depression and rapid cycling in BD. Herein, we provide a narrative review of adjunctive use of thyroid hormone in BD with a specific focus on its potential mechanism of action.DiscussionIon dysregulation with mood-state-related reduced sodium pump activity and increased intracellular sodium and calcium are among the most reproduced biologic findings in manic and depressed bipolar patients. Thyroid hormone directly increases sodium pump expression and activity, and indirectly improves energy utilization in bipolar patients. Across the reviewed literature, approximately 300–350 patients received supraphysiologic doses of thyroid hormone (T3 or T4), with the majority comprising individuals with BD and yielding generally very positive outcomes and minimal harm. Thyroid hormone augmentation can improve depression and reduce rapid cycling, and while never studied to treat mania, it does not increase the risk of manic episodes in all of the studies. Inconsistencies in data are related to different dosing regimens in different studies.ConclusionThyroid hormone appears to directly correct known pathophysiologic abnormalities in patients with bipolar disorder. While additional studies are needed to confirm these findings, sufficient data are available to warrant the use of supertherapeutic thyroid hormone augmentation in selected patients.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Thyroid hormone augmentation, Levothyroxine, Rapid-cycling bipolar disorder, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, Ion dysregulation

Introduction

The challenge of treatment-resistant and rapid-cycling bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe, chronic psychiatric illness defined by profound and recurrent fluctuations in mood, energy, and function, ranging from episodes of debilitating depression to elevated mania (Lichtstein et al. 2018). It affects approximately 5 million people in the United States (Blanco et al. 2017) and approximately 40 million individuals worldwide (World Health Organization, 2025). Depression is the most common initial presentation, and almost 75% of the time a patient is symptomatic consists of depressive symptoms (Judd et al. 2003). On average, bipolar patients die 7–13 years earlier than the rest of the population (Laursen 2011; Chan et al. 2021). It is estimated that 15–20% of bipolar individuals die by suicide (Nierenberg et al. 2023). Despite a range of available psychotropic medications, a significant subset of patients fails to achieve satisfactory or sustained remission (Lichtstein et al. 2018). This challenge is most pronounced in treatment-resistant bipolar disorder (TR-BD) and rapid-cycling (RC-BD), a specifier for individuals who experience four or more mood episodes within a single year. RC-BD, in particular, is often refractory to conventional therapeutic interventions and is associated with substantial long-term disability, representing a critical unmet need in psychiatric care (Zamar et al. 2022).

The thyroid-mood axis in mood disorders

The association between thyroid gland function and psychiatric well-being is a cornerstone of psychosomatic medicine (Stanić et al. 2022), with clinical reports dating to the 1950s documenting the high prevalence of depressive symptoms in patients with hypothyroidism (Seshadri et al. 2022). Since the discovery of triiodothyronine (LT3) and levothyroxine (LT4), their effects in the context of affective disorders have been studied repeatedly, even in cases with no metabolic abnormality in thyroid function per se (Bauer et al. 2008). These observations led to investigations into the antidepressant properties of thyroid hormones (TH), establishing the use of liothyronine (T3) as an effective augmentation strategy for treatment-resistant major depressive disorder (MDD) (Seshadri et al. 2022). The large-scale Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR*D) study provided strong support for this practice, demonstrating that T3 augmentation was comparable in efficacy to lithium augmentation for refractory unipolar depression, yet was significantly better tolerated (Seshadri et al. 2022).

The exploration of TH augmentation specifically for bipolar disorder was initiated by early case series reporting remissions in RC-BD courses (Stancer and Persad 1982). In this context, TH supplementation is not conceptualized as mere hormone replacement for a peripheral deficiency; instead, it is employed as a primary pharmacological intervention, often requiring supraphysiologic doses to target a core neurobiological deficit hypothesized to underlie the disorder (Seshadri et al. 2022). In bipolar patients, the potential use of TH augmentation is even more pronounced.

A cross-sectional study concluded that BD individuals have a 2.55-fold higher odds of having thyroid dysfunction compared to the general population (Krishna et al. 2013). A prospective study by Bauer et al. (1990) showed that the prevalence of thyroid dysfunction in bipolar individuals is not entirely explained by the use of lithium, particularly for the rapid cycling (RC-BD) type. Furthermore, a study showed that the prevalence of thyroid-peroxidase antibodies (TPO-ABs) in bipolar patients was 28% while that in the population and psychiatrically healthy individuals was 3–18%, and not associated with lithium treatment (Kupka et al. 2002). However, 17% of bipolar patients experienced thyroid failure, higher in women and in those on lithium treatment regimens (Kupka et al. 2002). Another study shows that in a 43 patient cohort, elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone levels (TSH) were seen in 92% of RC-BD patients as compared to 32% in the non-rapid cycling BD group (Cowdry et al. 1983).

The thyroid-brain axis: a review of cellular and molecular mechanisms

The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Thyroid (HPT) axis and tsh signaling

Thyroid hormone synthesis is led by the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis. The hypothalamus releases thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). TSH, in turn, acts on the thyroid gland to stimulate the synthesis and release of thyroid hormones, primarily thyroxine (T4) and, to a lesser extent, triiodothyronine (T3) (Rosenthal et al. 2011).

At the cellular level, TSH exerts its effects by binding to the TSH receptor, a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) on the surface of thyroid follicular cells (Allgeier et al. 1994). This binding event activates an associated Gs (stimulatory) protein, which in turn activates the enzyme adenylyl cyclase. Adenylyl cyclase catalyzes the conversion of intracellular adenosine 5’-triphosphate (ATP) into cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) (Allgeier et al. 1994). This cAMP molecule functions as a critical second messenger, activating Protein Kinase A (PKA). PKA then phosphorylates a cascade of downstream intracellular targets, including the sodium-iodide symporter, to drive the synthesis and release of T4 and T3 (National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2018; National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2022). Interestingly, the adenylyl cyclase enzyme is inhibited by both lithium and carbamazepine (Mann et al. 2009).

Thyroid hormone (T3/T4) action at the cellular level

Once released into the circulation, T4 functions primarily as a “prohormone” with a long half-life and relatively little intrinsic activity. It is converted in peripheral tissues and, crucially, within the brain, by deiodinase enzymes into the highly biologically active form, T3 (Rosenthal et al. 2011). T3 exerts its profound physiological effects through two primary pathways: genomic and non-genomic.

Genomic Pathways: The classic mechanism of T3 action involves its function as a ligand-activated transcription factor. T3 enters the cell, traverses the cytoplasm, and enters the nucleus, where it binds to nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TRs), such as TR-alpha and TR-beta. This T3-TR complex then binds to specific DNA sequences known as Thyroid Hormone Response Elements (TREs), which are located in the promoter regions of target genes. This binding event modulates the transcription of these genes, effectively “turning on” or “turning off” the synthesis of specific proteins (Cheng et al. 2010).

Non-Genomic Pathways: Emerging evidence also points to rapid, non-genomic effects. T3 and T4 can bind to receptors on the cell membrane, such as integrin-alpha-v-beta-3, or in the cytoplasm to activate intracellular signaling cascades like the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways, which can rapidly alter cellular function without requiring new protein synthesis (Axelband et al. 2011; Bergh et al. 2005).

The critical link to bipolar pathophysiology: thyroid hormone and the Na+,K+-ATPase

One of the most critical proteins synthesized as a direct result of T3’s genomic action, its role as a transcription factor, is the Na+,K+-ATPase (the sodium and potassium-activated adenosine triphosphatase or sodium pump) (Ismail-Beigi 1992). Thyroid hormone is known to increase the gene expression of the Na+,K+-ATPase, leading to an increase in the total number of functional pump units embedded in the cell membrane (National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2022). This upregulation of the sodium pump is a primary mechanism behind thyroid hormone’s thermogenic and metabolic effects, as the pump is a major consumer of cellular ATP (National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2022) and accounts for some 50% of energy consumed by the brain (Meyer et al. 2022). Furthermore, T3 may also exert a non-genomic effect, stimulating the rapid translocation of pre-existing, intracellular pump units to the plasma membrane. For example, an animal study by Lei et al. found that T3 causes increased sodium pump activity while also increasing cell surface expression of Na+,K+-ATPase alpha1- or beta1-subunit proteins by 1.7- and 2-fold, respectively (Lei et al. 2003). On the other hand, thyroid hormone deficiency has been shown to reduce the expression of Na+,K+-ATPase in the mouse hippocampus and rat synaptosomes (Sundaram et al. 2022).

This creates a direct, mechanistic link: since, as the following section details, the pathophysiology of BD probably involves a functional deficiency of Na+.K+-ATPase activity, then TH augmentation can be considered a targeted, rational, and potentially disease-modifying therapy aimed at upregulating the synthesis and function of the very protein believed to be dysfunctional.

Bipolar disorder as a state of impaired neuronal ion homeostasis

Cells of all tissue lineages require to maintain a sodium electrochemical gradient of low intracellular and high extracellular sodium concentrations. This gradient is especially important in tissues where it is used to maintain delicate cellular excitability and action potentials. Cation dysregulation, specifically of sodium and calcium, with secondary dysregulation of neuronal excitability and ion gradients, is a highly reproducible pathological abnormality in ill bipolar individuals (El-Mallakh 2004; Lichtstein et al. 2018). Tissue culture studies with neurons, leukocytes, erythrocytes, platelets and lymphoblasts have demonstrated that cells obtained from patients with bipolar disorder have an inherent incapacity to normalize their intracellular sodium levels compared to cells from psychiatrically healthy individuals (Dubovsky et al. 1992; Naylor et al. 1970; El-Mallakh et al. 1996; Li and El-Mallakh 2004; Gao et al. 2019, 2022a, b). Induced pluripotent stem cell studies of BD have been concluding that altered sodium and calcium currents are one of the main drivers of the impaired excitability of neurons in BD (Stern et al. 2020; Hewitt et al. 2023). The increase of intracellular sodium levels, and the subsequent depolarization of neurons, can explain nearly all symptoms of mania and depression (El-Mallakh 2004; 2016; El-Mallakh and Wyatt 1995; McCarren and Alger, 1989). Additionally, the processes known to increase the likelihood of a manic episode, like sleep deprivation, are associated with an increased need to reduce intracellular sodium (Chang et al. 2010; El-Mallakh et al. 2022). Lithium and mood-stabilizing anticonvulsants reduce intracellular sodium in an activity-dependent manner (El-Mallakh and Huff 2001). Similarly, blockade of dopamine by antipsychotic medications results in increased sodium pump activity that can also normalize intracellular sodium (Roberts et al. 2010).

The central hypothesis regarding thyroid hormone action in bipolar disorder revolves around its potential to prevent intracellular sodium buildup in neurons. The Na+,K+-ATPase, or sodium pump, is the primary enzyme responsible for maintaining the neuronal resting membrane potential. It actively transports three Na+ ions out of the cell in exchange for two K+ ions in, a process fueled by the hydrolysis of one ATP molecule (de Lores Arnaiz and Ordieres 2014). This pump is one of the most important mechanisms for preventing the excessive intracellular sodium buildup implicated in BD (Lichtstein et al. 2018).

A large body of evidence supports the pump’s dysfunction in the disorder. In people with bipolar I disorder, reduced sodium pump activity and consequent increases of intracellular sodium and calcium are the most reproduced mood-state-related biological abnormalities (Whalley et al. 1980; Dagher et al. 1984; El-Mallakh et al. 2016; Lichtstein et al. 2018). While the majority of this data is in peripheral tissues, like red blood cells (Looney and El-Mallkah, 1997), it has also been demonstrated in neuronal cells derived from subjects with bipolar disorder (Mertens et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2022a, b). Manic and depressed bipolar patients demonstrate both global and focal reductions in brain glucose metabolism, which could be used as a reflection of brain sodium pump activity (Baxter et al. 1985; Hosokawa et al. 2009), and reduced sodium pump expression has been demonstrated in post-mortem brains of individuals with bipolar illness (Rose et al. 1998). Furthermore, animal model testing of the causal link that sodium pump inhibition can induce a manic-like state has been demonstrated both after non-selective inhibition of the Na+,K+-ATPase with intracerebroventricular injection of ouabain in rats (el-Mallakh et al. 1995; Mack et al. 2019) or in an animal knockout model in which the neuron-specific alpha3 sodium pump isoform is knocked out (genetically removed) (Mack et al. 2019).

These results are also consistent with a unified theory for mood stabilizers in which all effective antimanic or mood stabilizing agents reduce intracellular sodium either directly through inhibition of voltage-dependent sodium channels (for anti-seizure agents) or displacement of sodium by lithium (Huff and El-Mallakh, 2001) or indirectly by activation of the sodium pump by antipsychotic agents (Roberts et al. 2010). Thyroid hormone genomically upregulates the production of new Na+,K+-ATPase pumps while also enhancing its activity non-genomically (National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2022). All classes of effective drugs, therefore, converge on the same final common pathway: correcting the intracellular sodium imbalance.

Furthermore, thyroid hormone greatly influences cellular energy metabolism. Recent studies indicate that organoids derived from bipolar disorder (BD) patients show marked mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced basal respiration and lower ATP generation (El Soufi El Sabbagh et al. 2025). This observation is particularly important because the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is among the most energy-intensive enzymes in the cell; therefore, diminished ATP availability would be expected to impair its activity (Herman et al. 2007).Patients with bipolar disorder who have abnormalities in their energy metabolism (e.g., type II diabetes) may have a more severe clinical psychiatric presentation than those with normal energy metabolism (Steardo et al. 2019). So, one would expect that improvement of energy metabolism would improve clinical outcomes in BD individuals.

Clinical evidence for thyroid hormone augmentation in bipolar disorder

Early open-label and retrospective data (LT4 and T3)

Initial support for high-dose TH augmentation came from open-label and retrospective studies targeting the most treatment-resistant patients. A key prospective, open-label study by Bauer et al. (2002a) based in Germany investigated adjunctive supraphysiologic L-thyroxine T4 (LT4) in 21 patients (16 women, 5 men; intention-to-treat analysis) with prophylaxis-resistant affective disorders, including 13 with BD (62% women). Over a mean treatment period of 51.4 months with a mean LT4 dose of 378.6 ± 90.2 mcg/day, patients showed significant improvement. Using a “mirror-image” method comparing the period before and during treatment, the total number of affective recurrences significantly declined from 8.6 to 2.8 (P = 0.004), and the Morbidity Index (a measure of time spent ill) was reduced from 0.71 to 0.28 (P < 0.001). Reductions in morbidity and relapse frequency were observed across all diagnostic groups; however, these findings were generally not statistically significant because of small sub-sample sizes. The only significant result found during subgroup analysis was for the Morbidity Index within the bipolar population (p = 0.021). On the Clinical Global Impression for Bipolar Disorder (CGI-BP) scale, 71.4% of patients (15 of 21) were rated as “much” or “very much” improved. Only one patient showed significant side effects involving worsening of pre-existing tremor (Bauer et al. 2002a).

Another open-label study by Bauer and Whybrow (1990) based in the US, comprising 11 patients (91% women) with RC-BD (refractory to treatment) treated with high-dose LT4 (150–400 mcg/day), showed a significant improvement in HDRS (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) and YMRS (Young Mania Rating Scale), especially in 10 of 11 patients’ depressive symptoms; with mania improvement in 5 of 7 patients symptomatic with mania at baseline. Three patients relapsed into depression or cycling. They found that supraphysiological doses of free thyroxine were needed to manifest improvement. Side effects were minimal, with exacerbation of tremor being the common one. However, no signs of hypermetabolism were seen. The minimum duration of treatment was 60 days (Bauer and Whybrow 1990).

In Germany in 1998, Bauer et al. also conducted an open-label trial of supraphysiologic doses of LT4 (titrated to mean dose of 482 ± 72 mcg/day), comparing it to conventional antidepressant therapy for 12 individuals with severe, treatment-resistant bipolar depression (refractory to lithium, carbamazepine or neuroleptics). At the end of the 8-week duration, 5 out of the 12 (42%) patients showed a reduction in HDRS of 50% or greater, with one patient experiencing mild improvement and 6 showing none. Sweating was the most common side effect with no thyrotoxicosis seen.

In a large retrospective chart review, Kelly and Lieberman (2009) examined T3 augmentation in 159 patients (N = 159) with treatment-resistant depression in the context of BD-II or BD-NOS. Patients were followed for a mean of 20.3 months and outcomes were measured with the Clinical Global Impressions Scale Improvement (CGI-I). With an average T3 dose of 90.4 mcg/day, 84% of BD-II patients experienced improvement, and 32% achieved full remission, with similar outcomes in those with BD-NOS, 85% experienced improvement with 38% achieving remission. A critical finding for BD management was that no patients experienced a switch into hypomania, a common risk with other antidepressant augmentation strategies, and only 16 out of the 159 (10%) patients discontinued due to side effects, with tremor being the most common and 3 patients experiencing osteoporosis (not assessed systematically).

The first comparative double-blind, placebo-controlled trial using LT4 and T3 as augmentation therapy for RC-BD was conducted in the US by Walshaw et al. (2018). It involved 32 treatment-resistant (refractory to lithium) RC-BD being randomized into treatment arms of LT4, T3, or placebo, followed for 4 months or more. LT4 dosage was titrated to a free T4 index of 4.5–7.5 or TSH < 0.1 mIU/mL, with T3 dosage titrated to a T3 resin uptake of 0.65–1.36 units (note that this is not how T3 resin uptake is measured, so it is not clear to what level T3 was titrated). The LT4 gained time spent euthymic versus placebo (+ 33.1% vs. − 6.5%, P = 0.033) and reduced time spent in a mixed state versus placebo (− 13.3% vs. + 9.3%, P = 0.045). T3 was not significantly different from placebo or LT4. Side effects of mild tremors were seen in 10 patients, diarrhea in 8, hot flashes or sweating in 5, tachycardia in 4, and dizziness in 3 in the active LT4/T3 groups. The difficulty in understanding the dose titration used makes it difficult to interpret the outcome of the T3 group.

A multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT by Stamm et al. (2014) randomized 62 patients (N = 62) with bipolar depression to receive adjunctive fixed-dose LT4 (300 mcg/d) or placebo for 6 weeks. The study failed to detect a statistically significant difference between groups on the primary outcome (change in HDRS) for the total sample (a 2.7-point difference, P = 0.198). This was largely attributed to an unusually high placebo response rate. This is despite the observation that the groups did separate (P = 0.046) at week 4. A secondary analysis of the 32 females found that the LT4 group experienced a -42.4% reduction in HDRS scores, compared to only − 16.6% in the placebo group (P = 0.018). Higher baseline TSH levels predicted a better response in women (P = 0.05).

This gender-specific finding is supported by a Bauer et al. (2005) open-label Positron Emission Tomography (PET) study, which examined 10 euthyroid depressed women with BD, and 10 age-matched healthy women without LT4 treatment. After 7 weeks of adjunctive LT4 (mean dose 320 mcg/day), 7 of 10 (70%) patients achieved remission, with the remaining 3 showing partial response. The PET scans provided objective neurobiological support, showing that LT4 treatment significantly decreased relative glucose metabolism in anterior limbic regions, including the subgenual cingulate cortex, thalamus, amygdala, and hippocampus. Critically, the degree of metabolic decrease in the left thalamus, left amygdala, left hippocampus, and left ventral striatum was significantly correlated with the degree of reduction in depression scores. It is important to understand that the normalization calculations utilized in this analysis removed the ability to examine global changes, thus, if thyroid treatment increased overall glucose utilization, changes could still be seen as “reductions” despite the fact that they actually represent smaller increases.

An extension of this study was performed in a 6-week, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled study analyzing the efficacy of LT4 (300 mcg/day) in bipolar depression individuals who had failed to respond to a course of mood stabilizer and/or antidepressant medication (Bauer et al. 2016). Cerebral glucose metabolism was analyzed with the help of PET and fluorodeoxyglucose (F-18). The Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression was also assessed between the two groups at the 6-week endpoint. At endpoint (week 6), the mean HamD score showed a group difference of 3.7 points in favor of L-T4. Activity in left thalamus, left dorsal striatum, and the subgenual cingulate was decreased and, again, in correlation with significant decline in depression symptoms (p < 0.05).

A 96.4% remission rate was obtained in a 2022 cohort study of 55 patients with severe, rapid-cycling Bipolar Spectrum Disorders (BSD) who were recruited for a combined protocol of High-Dose Levothyroxine (HDT) plus repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS) (Zamar et al. 2022). Fifty-three of the 55 patients (96.4%) achieved remission which was sustained for a mean of 2.0 years. The average LT4 dose was 303.7 mcg/d. Outcome was measured with the Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS) with a very large effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.61, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.81–2.83, P < 0.001). The only reported side effect was one patient with reversible sensation of feeling hot with palpitations disappeared when LT4 was re-titrated after discontinuation. There was no mention of any switch to hypomania or mania (Zamar et al. 2022). This study also introduced a potential biomarker. Genetic testing revealed that 52 of the 55 patients (94.3%) had mutations in the deiodinase genes (DiO1/DiO2) or the SLCO1C1 gene, which codes for a TH transporter protein in the brain.

There seems to be a sex effect to thyroid augmentation. One of the multisite RCTs found evidence of efficacy only in women (Stamm et al. 2014). This aligns with a PET study, which demonstrated a neurobiological effect in an all-female cohort (Bauer et al. 2005). But there is a sex difference in thyroid replacement. Women generally are more likely to experience thyroid disease which requires replacement, and will generally require higher doses than men to achieve the same level of TSH (Jonklaas 2010; Devdhar et al. 2011). Thus, the differences in response rates between men and women may be related to the dosing strategy utilized, and dosing strategies are where future work needs to focus.

In addition to benefits of using TH augmentation, one must remember that most medications for bipolar disorder are associated with some form of thyroid dysfunction. Lithium has a well-known effect in which it can induce goiter or hypothyroidism in up to 40% of long-term users (Lazarus 2009; Joseph et al. 2023). Its mechanism of action is a direct molecular interference with the HPT axis. Lithium is known to inhibit the enzyme adenylyl cyclase (Risby et al. 1991), which directly blunts the TSH-cAMP signaling cascade (Mori et al. 1989). Lithium also directly inhibits the coupling of iodotyrosines and the physical release of T4 and T3 from the thyroid gland (Lazarus 1998). Valproate may be associated with thyroid dysfunction, although the evidence is not as strong as that for lithium. Several cohort studies have shown subclinical hypothyroidism (increased TSH with decreased free T4) in about 15–20% of patients being treated with valproate (Alhyan et al. 2023). Similarly, antipsychotics have been associated with creating subclinical thyroid dysfunction (Vedal et al. 2018). This may occur through dopamine receptor antagonism, which can cause an increase in dopamine’s effect on the pituitary system and lower thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) secretion, disrupting TSH regulation or even by promoting the increase of anti-thyroid peroxidase autoantibodies (Li et al. 2019). This should reduce the threshold of clinicians moving to use TH in their bipolar patients.

The primary clinical barrier to adopting this strategy is the fear of inducing iatrogenic thyrotoxicosis (Blick et al. 2026). Controlled studies generally report a lack of typical thyrotoxic adverse effects with the heart, bone, or even anxiety (Kelly 2014, 2015; Kelly et al. 2016; Pilhatsch et al. 2018; Seshadri et al. 2022). Two cross-sectional studies involving 26 pre-menopausal and postmenopausal women and men with mood disorders treated with supraphysiologic LT4 for 12 months or longer, showed no significant loss of bone mineral density tested with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) (Gyulai et al. 1997, 2001). Interestingly, Bauer et al. (2002b) concluded in an open-label study that healthy patients showed a significantly negative response to thyroid hormone supplementation, with regard to side effects and discontinuation, as compared to those with refractory mood disorders, with a discontinuation rate of 38% in healthy controls as compared to 0% in patients, and more severe side effects.

Supraphysiologic dosing and monitoring: “Cellular Hypothyroidism”

Rationale for supraphysiologic dosing

The consistent finding that supraphysiologic, rather than replacement, doses are required for a therapeutic effect in BD has led to the “cellular hypothyroidism” or “thyroid resistance” hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that the brain in BD may be in a hypothyroid state even when peripheral thyroid tests (TSH, T4) are normal, meaning that thyroid hormone levels in the blood in bipolar patients are not a true representation of actual thyroid functioning (Kelly 2016).

The proposed mechanism is rooted in the bioenergetics of the disorder. BD has been increasingly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in inadequate cellular levels of ATP (Kelly 2016; Jurado-Flores et al. 2022). The transport of T4 and T3 across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and into neurons is not a passive process; it is an energy-intensive, ATP-dependent process mediated by specialized transporters (like SLCO1C1, the gene mentioned by Zamar et al. 2022) (Jurado-Flores et al. 2022). Furthermore, electrical function of the brain is dependent on the activity of the Na+,K+-ATPase in the neuronal membranes, and optimal expression and function of that pump requires TH (El-Mallakh and Wyatt, 1995). These abnormalities lead to a failure of TH to enter the central nervous system efficiently, leading to a state of central/cellular hypothyroidism (Kelly 2016; Jurado-Flores et al. 2022). This state exists despite normal or even high-normal peripheral serum T4 and TSH levels. The therapeutic rationale for supraphysiologic doses (e.g., 300–600 mcg/day) is, therefore, to create an enormous concentration gradient of T4 in the blood, overwhelming the compromised ATP-dependent system and “forcing” the hormone into the brain via passive diffusion (Kelly 2016).

T3 (Liothyronine) vs. T4 (Levothyroxine)

The choice between T3 (liothyronine) and T4 (levothyroxine) is dictated by their distinct pharmacological profiles and the clinical goal. T4 is the prohormone with a long half-life of approximately 7 days (Lipp 2021). This profile provides stable, consistent serum levels, making it the ideal choice for chronic, long-term augmentation, as seen in the maintenance studies by Bauer et al. (2002a); Stamm et al. (2014). T3 is the active hormone, characterized by a rapid onset and a short half-life of about 1 day. This leads to peaks and troughs in serum levels (Jonklaas et al. 2015). While less suitable for stable, chronic maintenance, its rapid and potent effect makes it the preferred agent for acute augmentation to “accelerate” or “hasten” the onset of an antidepressant response. It is often used to “break” a severe, refractory depressive episode in an outpatient or inpatient setting (Iosifescu et al. 2005). In one of the studies we mentioned, while the T3 group showed a similar pattern of improvement to the LT4 group, the results for T3 did not reach statistical significance over placebo unlike LT4 (Walshaw et al. 2018). The difficulty in understanding the dose titration used in the study makes it difficult to interpret the outcome of the T3 group, as well as how, at least, theoretically, T3 would be better suited for acute use, compared to T4 for which there is more data as well. Furthermore, it must be pointed out that some drugs, such as propranolol, interfere with thyroid hormone pathways. Propranolol inhibits the conversion of T4 to T3 (Wiersinga 1991), as evidence by reduction in serum T3 with no significant changes in serum T4 (Kallner et al. 1978). Hence, it is prudent to notice this effect if propranolol is being used in a patient.

Dosage and monitoring

Evidence-based dosing for thyroid hormone augmentation in bipolar disorder—particularly for treatment-resistant or rapid-cycling cases—emphasizes supraphysiological levels. For Levothyroxine (T4), clinical trials suggest effective ranges between 150 mcg/day and nearly 500 mcg/day (Bauer and Whybrow 1990; Bauer et al. 1998), with several key studies settling on a mean dose of approximately 300–380 mcg/day to achieve remission (Bauer et al. 2002a; Stamm et al. 2014; Zamar et al. 2022). While Liothyronine (T3) is less frequently the primary focus in high-dose RCTs, retrospective data indicates an average effective dose of approximately 90 mcg/day (Kelly and Lieberman 2009). These aggressive strategies appear especially potent in women (Bauer and Whybrow 1990; Stamm et al. 2014) and maintain a low risk of inducing manic or hypomanic switches compared to traditional antidepressant augmentations (Kelly and Lieberman 2009; Zamar et al. 2022).

Because current research is still in the early stages and there is a clear need for more detailed, large-scale RCTs, a cautious approach to initiation is warranted. It may be wise to start with a conservative dose, such as 25–50 mcg, using TSH monitoring, clinical improvement, and the emergence of side effects to guide the titration regimen (Rosenthal et al. 2011; Bauer et al. 2002a; Walshaw et al. 2018). While literature targets often involve a fully suppressed TSH (< 0.1 mIU/mL) or a free T4 index between 4.5 and 7.5 (Walshaw et al. 2018), this incremental strategy allows for better management of common side effects like tremors, sweating, gastrointestinal effects and palpitations (Bauer et al. 2002a; Zamar et al. 2022). Despite the ultimate aim of supraphysiological levels, research indicates a lack of overt thyrotoxicosis in these populations (Bauer and Whybrow 1990; Bauer et al. 1998). Furthermore, as discussed previously, women will generally require higher doses than men to achieve the same level of TSH, especially in the presence of thyroid disease (Jonklaas 2010; Devdhar et al. 2011). Thus, the differences in response rates between men and women may be related to the dosing strategy utilized, and dosing strategies are where future work needs to focus.

Conclusion

The use of thyroid hormone augmentation in bipolar disorder, particularly with supraphysiologic doses, is supported by a multifaceted and compelling rationale that integrates neurobiology, pharmacology, and clinical evidence. While broad systematic reviews conclude the evidence is “mixed” (Seshadri et al. 2022), a granular analysis of key RCTs and cohort studies reveals a consistent, specific, and positive signal. Supraphysiologic LT4 appears particularly effective in women with prophylaxis-resistant or rapid-cycling BD. Dosage is likely to be variable and may depend on specific individual parameters. Nonetheless, data from the reviewed studies suggest it is well-tolerated and that severe risks, such as atrial fibrillation and bone demineralization, do not occur in most patients. Finally, the strategy is mechanistically sound. At a cellular level, T3 acts as a transcription factor to upregulate the synthesis of the Na+/K+-ATPase, while also enhancing its activity, directly addressing the impaired neuronal sodium homeostasis.

It is clear that there are a limited number of studies available to make supraphysiologic thyroid administration a near first-line treatment, as well as the heterogeneity of the studies performed (Seshadri et al. 2022). But there is overwhelming evidence that supraphysiologic thyroid augmentation is beneficial, reduces cycling, improves depression, and does not increase the risk of mania, and may even reduce it. To move this intervention from a later-line, specialized strategy to a validated, first-line approach, future research must evolve beyond generic calls for “more RCTs” (Seshadri et al. 2022). The existing evidence provides clear direction for the design of next-generation trials. Future studies must be adequately powered, evaluate longer-term follow-up, and set dosing parameters. Importantly, additional work is needed to test the hypothesis generated by the Zamar et al. (2022) cohort regarding the role of polymorphisms of the DIO1, DIO2, and SLCO1C1 genes.
