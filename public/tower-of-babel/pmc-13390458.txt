Shared pathophysiological mechanisms between diabetes and calcific aortic valve disease: an immunometabolic perspective
Authors: Zhang Yue, Qiuchen SiTu, Jin-hui Bian, Hong-xiang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review synthesizes the immunometabolic mechanisms linking diabetes mellitus to accelerated calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) and evaluates their therapeutic implications. Despite the absence of disease-modifying pharmacotherapy for CAVD, diabetes consistently increases incident aortic stenosis risk (HR 1.3–1.7), calcification burden, and disease severity, independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Epidemiological, imaging, and histological evidence demonstrate that dysglycemia promotes earlier onset, denser valvular calcium deposits, and worse post-intervention outcomes. We delineate seven interconnected and partially overlapping pathways through which diabetes remodels the aortic valve: hyperglycemia-driven valvular interstitial cell (VIC) phenotypic switching and insulin resistance; advanced glycation end-product–RAGE signaling; oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction; macrophage NLRP3–IL-1β inflammasome activation; endothelial barrier compromise; BMP–Runx2–Wnt–Notch osteogenic reprogramming; and CKD–mineralocorticoid receptor cross-talk. These processes convert metabolic stress into sustained valvular inflammation, matrix remodeling, and ectopic ossification. Mechanistic and observational data provide a rationale for evaluating metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, IL-1β/NLRP3 inhibitors, and Lp(a)-lowering strategies as candidate approaches to modulate calcification progression. Integrated metabolic–inflammatory–imaging biomarkers offer potential for risk stratification and trial enrichment. This immunometabolic framework identifies actionable nodes and underscores the urgent need for dedicated, valve-focused randomized trials in diabetic CAVD to translate mechanistic insights into clinical benefit.Graphical abstract Keywords: Calcific aortic valve disease, Aortic stenosis, Diabetes mellitus, Immunometabolism, Inflammation, Valvular interstitial cells, Cytokines

Introduction: why an immunometabolic lens is needed for diabetes-associated CAVD

Unmet clinical needs in CAVD/AS

Aortic stenosis (AS) usually evolves over years and, once established, progresses in a largely unidirectional manner. At present, no pharmacotherapy has convincingly delayed disease progression; many patients ultimately require surgical or transcatheter valve replacement [1]. Lipid-lowering with statins did not slow AS progression in large trials, supporting the view that calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) follows a biology that cannot be treated as a simple extension of atherosclerosis [2].

Diabetes is consistently linked to higher AS incidence and faster progression [3–5]. Population data suggest an approximately 1.3–1.6-fold higher AS prevalence in people with diabetes than in those without diabetes [6–9]. Conversely, 30–40% of patients with AS have diabetes, and diabetes correlates with greater disease severity, higher calcification burden, and worse prognosis independent of other factors [10–13]. These patterns are compatible with diabetes acting as a catalyst that converts metabolic stress into sustained inflammatory and osteogenic signaling within the valve. Diabetes also worsens outcomes in other valvular conditions; for example, in infective endocarditis it is associated with more extensive structural destruction [14]. Together, these observations argue for a mechanistic framework that links dysmetabolism to valve inflammation and calcification, with the aim of identifying targets that could slow CAVD progression.

Diabetes as a catalyst: immunometabolic dysfunction as a driver of CAVD

Evidence increasingly supports a coupled process in which chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic stress in diabetes reshape immune behavior and local metabolism in the valvular niche. Hyperglycemia promotes phenotypic switching in valvular interstitial cells (VICs) and may alter intracellular energy-sensing and stress-response pathways, thereby increasing the release of mediators that favor calcification [15–18]. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) accumulate and activate inflammatory pathways through the AGE–RAGE axis, linking metabolic stress to inflammatory and pro-osteogenic signaling [15, 19, 20]. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial impairment further facilitate formation of calcified nodules and matrix remodeling by lowering metabolic resilience and amplifying pro-inflammatory and pro-calcific signaling [15, 21–24]. In combination, these signals can move the valve from early fibrotic remodeling toward established calcific sclerosis.

We propose a conceptual model in which diabetes may promote immunometabolic reprogramming, amplify inflammatory and oxidative signaling, and facilitate osteogenic conversion within the aortic valve (Fig. 1). More specifically, this framework proposes that diabetic stress may bias the intracellular state of VICs toward impaired energy sensing, oxidative stress susceptibility, and heightened inflammatory responsiveness, thereby increasing their propensity for osteogenic transition [15–19, 21, 22]. This framework is intended to organize the available evidence rather than to imply that all pathways are equally established or uniformly causal. Importantly, the evidence supporting individual immunometabolic pathways is not uniform. While epidemiological, imaging, and tissue-based studies support an association between diabetes and a more calcific aortic valve phenotype, much of the mechanistic detail remains derived from animal models, ex vivo tissue analyses, or in vitro valvular cell systems. Accordingly, the pathways discussed in this review should not be interpreted as having equal causal weight; rather, they represent a spectrum ranging from relatively well-supported biological contributors to still-evolving mechanistic hypotheses. On this basis, this review synthesizes recent mechanistic findings and evaluates pharmacological opportunities in diabetes-associated valvular calcification, emphasizing immunometabolic nodes that may be actionable.

Literature search and article selection

This narrative review was based on a structured literature search performed primarily in PubMed. We first identified publications related to calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) and diabetes mellitus using combinations of these core terms and then expanded the search by following key mechanistic and translational themes emerging from the retrieved literature, including endothelial dysfunction, AGE–RAGE signaling, oxidative stress, inflammasome activation, osteogenic transition, CKD-related mineral dysregulation, microRNA/epigenetic regulation, biomarkers, and valve-directed therapeutic strategies. Articles were selected on the basis of their relevance to the central topic of diabetes-associated CAVD, with priority given to human studies, valve-focused mechanistic studies, and representative translational reports. Additional references were identified through citation tracking of key original studies and recent review articles. Because the available literature is heterogeneous in design, tissue source, and endpoint definition, this review was intended as a structured narrative synthesis rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis.

Evidence map and clinical landscape: association strength and evidence distribution

Throughout this review, evidence is interpreted according to study context. Human cohort, imaging, and tissue studies are prioritized for clinical relevance, whereas animal and cell-culture studies are used primarily to support mechanistic plausibility. This distinction is important because association with calcific aortic valve disease does not necessarily establish causality, and experimentally tractable pathways may not exert equivalent effects in the human valvular microenvironment.

Because the available literature is highly heterogeneous in study design, endpoint definition, and experimental context, a formal quantitative synthesis was not methodologically appropriate. We therefore adopted a structured semi-quantitative evidence-ranking framework (Table 1) to distinguish pathways with relatively strong support from those that remain more preliminary or hypothesis-generating. Where specific quantitative estimates are reported below, they should be interpreted as study-specific observations rather than fixed physiological constants. In CAVD, the apparent magnitude of an effect may vary substantially according to imaging modality, cohort composition, disease stage, tissue source, and endpoint definition.

Dysglycemia and risk of incident AVC/AS

Epidemiology supports diabetes as a major risk factor for CAVD, and risk appears to rise across the spectrum from early dysglycemia to overt diabetes. A prospective analysis from UK Biobank reported that impaired glucose tolerance was associated with approximately 21% higher AS risk compared with normoglycemia, whereas diagnosed diabetes was associated with approximately 66% higher risk; importantly, the association persisted after accounting for common AS susceptibility genes [6]. A nationwide cohort similarly reported that fasting glucose of 6.1–6.9 mmol/L already corresponded to a higher incidence of AS [7, 9].

In Denmark, diabetes was associated with approximately 71% higher risk of AS-related hospitalization or death (HR 1.71) and an odds ratio of approximately 1.67 for CT-detected valvular calcification [8]. Multicenter imaging studies also indicate that glycemic status relates to subclinical aortic valve calcification in the general population: among newly diagnosed diabetes, CT-detected calcification prevalence reached approximately 14%, higher than in non-diabetes (men 8% vs. 5%; women 9% vs. 5%; both p < 0.01) [25]. Overall, these data suggest that dysglycemia and diabetes increase CAVD risk beyond age and other shared correlates.

Diabetes effects on valvular phenotype and disease severity

Diabetes is not only associated with incidence; it is also linked to a more calcified and clinically complex phenotype. Histology and imaging suggest denser, larger calcific deposits in valves from patients with diabetes and AS. In one micro-CT analysis of explanted valves, hyperglycemia was associated with greater calcified nodule burden and trabecular thickness than normoglycemia, with the reported differences reaching approximately 128% and 50%, respectively [26]. However, these values should be interpreted as study-specific estimates from a small explant cohort rather than as fixed effect sizes, because the measured magnitude of calcific burden is likely to depend on tissue selection, disease stage, and the imaging or morphometric method used.

Diabetes may also alter hemodynamic presentation. Because left ventricular dysfunction is more frequent in diabetes, patients can show lower peak transvalvular gradients than non-diabetic patients at similar aortic valve area (AVA) [27–32]. This increases the likelihood of low-flow, low-gradient AS and can lead to underestimation of severity if flow and ejection fraction are not integrated [27]. Mechanistically, inflammation may be more tightly coupled to stenosis in diabetes: intravalvular NF-κB expression has been reported to correlate with smaller AVA and higher gradients only in diabetic AS patients [12, 15]. Taken together, diabetes appears to worsen disease at microstructural and functional levels.

Surgical/interventional outcomes: SAVR vs. TAVR and the role of glycemic variability

Historically, diabetes was viewed as a risk factor for higher perioperative morbidity and mortality after surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). With expansion of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), outcome differences between diabetic and non-diabetic patients appear smaller [33–35]. In a cohort of > 11,000 TAVR procedures, diabetes was associated with similar 30-day and 1-year mortality after matching (both approximately 4.5% and approximately 17%, respectively; not statistically different) [10, 36–40]. In contrast, diabetes remains associated with somewhat worse short-term outcomes after SAVR in some reports (e.g., adjusted HR of 1.13 for 30-day mortality) [33–35]. Interpretation of these studies requires caution, because TAVR and SAVR cohorts differ substantially across reports in diabetes definition, HbA1c distribution, age, comorbidity burden, renal function, baseline surgical risk, and procedural era.

Glycemic control and glucose dynamics are clinically relevant. In one TAVR cohort, baseline HbA1c ≥ 6.5% was associated with higher long-term mortality risk than better glycaemic control (adjusted HR 10.78) [41]; however, this large effect size should be interpreted cautiously as a study-specific finding from a single cohort, because the observed magnitude may have been influenced by sample size, cohort composition, residual confounding, and the absence of clear replication across independent studies. Stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and glycemic variability (GV) have also been linked to 30-day and 1-year mortality after TAVR [42, 43], supporting careful perioperative management and attention to long-term variability. Relief of outflow obstruction may improve glycometabolic status; in one comparison, HbA1c decreased more after TAVR than after SAVR (TAVR 8.31%→7.86%, p < 0.001; SAVR 8.33%→8.15%, p = 0.046) [25, 33]. However, this observation should be interpreted cautiously, because differences in peri-procedural metabolic management, post-procedural recovery trajectory, baseline disease severity, and procedure-selection factors may have contributed to the apparent between-group difference. A meta-analysis suggested diabetes may modestly increase 1-year mortality after TAVR, although the difference did not reach statistical significance [13, 44–46]. Overall, current procedural outcome data are informative but heterogeneous, and should be interpreted as context-dependent observational evidence rather than as a uniform estimate of diabetes-related excess risk across all TAVR and SAVR settings.

Primer: valvular immune microenvironment and calcification biology

The VEC–VIC–ECM triad and mechanical context

The normal aortic valve contains a trilaminar extracellular matrix (ECM) covered by valvular endothelial cells (VECs): a collagen-rich fibrosa, a proteoglycan-rich spongiosa, and an elastin-rich ventricularis. Valvular interstitial cells (VICs) reside throughout the matrix and maintain mechanical competence by regulating ECM turnover. Under injury or abnormal loading, VECs can become more permeable and pro-inflammatory, allowing entry of circulating mediators and immune cells. Mechanical stress together with inflammatory cues can drive VIC activation toward myofibroblast-like and osteoblast-like states, increasing ECM production and release of calcifying matrix vesicles. The valve experiences cyclic loading and high shear stress, which predisposes particular regions—especially the base and outflow side—to focal injury and lesion initiation.

CAVD as an active continuum: inflammation, fibrosis, and osteogenesis

Aortic valve calcification is now widely regarded as an active process that can begin with remodeling and fibrosis and later progress to ossification [1, 2, 47, 48]. We summarized key evidence from existing clinical studies regarding the associations between dysglycemia and the incidence, progression, and prognosis of CAVD/AS, as detailed in Table 2. While early drivers overlap with atherosclerosis (lipid deposition, macrophage infiltration, foam-cell formation), the dominant endpoint is osteogenic remodeling rather than plaque rupture. Early lesions show ECM reorganization and limited inflammation; later, calcifying vesicles produced by VICs accumulate into nodules, and advanced lesions can include osteoid- and bone-like structures. Across stages, chronic inflammation and remodeling promote continued progression, supporting therapeutic framing of CAVD as a chronic inflammatory–remodeling disorder rather than passive degeneration.

Immune entry into the valve and pro-calcific signaling

A key step in CAVD is loss of relative immune restriction, enabling infiltration of valvular tissue. Endothelial injury promotes monocyte adhesion and migration into the interstitium. In diabetes, glucotoxicity and inflammatory mediators may compromise VEC junctions earlier, increasing permeability. Within the valve, macrophages ingest modified lipids and debris, adopt foam-cell features, and secrete chemokines that recruit additional immune cells, including T cells and mast cells. Cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α induce VIC expression of matrix-remodeling enzymes (e.g., MMPs) and osteogenic proteins, accelerating calcification. B cells may contribute via antigen presentation and antibody responses to modified proteins, reinforcing a chronic inflammatory milieu. Over time, sustained immune signaling promotes irreversible calcific remodeling.

How diabetes may remodel the aortic valve: seven actionable immunometabolic pathways

The seven pathways discussed below are not supported by identical levels of evidence; rather, they represent partially overlapping processes through which diabetic stress may reprogram VIC metabolic state and thereby influence inflammatory output, matrix remodeling, and osteogenic transition. The strongest support for these pathways comes from different combinations of epidemiological, tissue-based, ex vivo, and cell-based studies (Table 1) [12, 15–19, 21, 22, 49–51]. To facilitate prioritization, Table 1 ranks the reviewed pathways according to the current strength of evidence, rather than implying an absolute quantitative hierarchy of pathogenic contribution.

To improve clarity, the following mechanistic sections are organized according to the level of evidence, beginning with human clinical and tissue observations, followed by animal or ex vivo findings, and finally cell-based mechanistic studies.

Hyperglycemia/insulin resistance: metabolic shifts and VIC phenotypic drift

Human evidence. Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance are central metabolic disturbances in diabetes and are increasingly linked to aortic valve degeneration. Valvular tissue in diabetes shows impaired insulin signaling: chronic hyperinsulinemia can blunt AKT phosphorylation, consistent with local insulin resistance within the valve [16]. These observations support the concept that metabolic dysregulation is not merely systemic but may also be expressed locally within valvular tissue.

Cell culture and mechanistic evidence. Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance alter VIC metabolism and gene expression, suggesting that diabetic stress may influence valve calcification not only through systemic exposure but also through intracellular metabolic reprogramming within valve-resident cells (Fig. 2) [15–17]. High glucose increases pro-inflammatory and osteogenesis-related transcripts in VICs [15]. Metabolic sensors such as AMPK are suppressed under high-glucose conditions, shifting VICs away from adaptive energy homeostasis toward a secretory program enriched for inflammatory and pro-calcific mediators [17]. Activation of AMPK can counteract some of these changes. For example, metformin reduces calcium deposition in VICs, lowers osteogenic gene expression, and improves markers of matrix homeostasis while increasing AMPK phosphorylation and supporting VIC survival/proliferation [17]. These findings suggest that the diabetic milieu may alter VIC fate not only by exposing the cells to inflammatory mediators, but by reprogramming intracellular energy-sensing and stress-response pathways that determine how VICs interpret and amplify those cues [15–17, 52, 53]. A metabolically dysregulated VIC may therefore become more permissive to inflammatory signaling and more prone to osteogenic transition, rather than these processes occurring as fully independent events [15, 17]. Taken together, these findings support the idea that altered energy sensing and insulin-related signaling can promote VIC phenotypic drift toward a pro-inflammatory and pro-calcific state.

Focused mechanistic model of valve-specific biomechanical–metabolic coupling in diabetic CAVD

Notably, the direct effect of hyperglycemia on VIC calcification is not uniform across experimental systems. Although several studies report that high glucose enhances pro-inflammatory and osteogenic signaling in VICs, at least one recent in vitro study found that hyperglycemia-simulating conditions attenuated experimentally induced calcification in cultured human aortic VICs [54]. This apparent discrepancy suggests that glucose exposure alone may be insufficient to reproduce the full diabetic valvular phenotype. Differences in exposure duration, glucose concentration, osteogenic medium composition, donor cell source, and the absence of mechanical loading, endothelial interaction, and immune-cell crosstalk may all influence whether hyperglycemia promotes or attenuates calcific responses. Accordingly, isolated high-glucose results should be interpreted as context-dependent model outputs rather than direct refutations of the broader immunometabolic framework.

Overall appraisal and therapeutic implications. These findings support targeting energy-sensing pathways, such as AMPK activation and PPARδ/γ modulation, to slow diabetes-associated VIC remodeling. However, the extent to which hyperglycemia per se acts as a direct valvular calcification driver in humans remains uncertain, because clinical studies are largely associative and experimental systems often simplify the combined effects of glucose exposure, mechanical stress, dyslipidemia, and immune-cell crosstalk. The conflicting result reported in ref [54]. further underscores that diabetic valve remodeling is likely context-dependent and may require multi-factor interaction rather than glucose exposure alone.

AGE–RAGE axis: converting glycation stress into inflammatory and osteogenic programs

Human evidence. AGE accumulation is a defining feature of diabetes, and the AGE–RAGE axis may connect metabolic stress to valvular inflammation and calcification. In valves from AS patients with diabetes, AGE content is markedly higher than in non-diabetic counterparts (~ sixfold), and RAGE expression is increased by approximately 80% [19]. Circulating AGE and soluble RAGE are also elevated and track with HbA1c. In addition, Kopytek et al. reported increased intravalvular NF-κB and BMP-2 in diabetic AS; notably, only in the diabetic group did NF-κB correlate with HbA1c [15]. Together, these human data support the clinical relevance of glycation-related stress in the diabetic valvular microenvironment.

Cell culture evidence. Mechanistically, AGE–RAGE engagement activates NF-κB and promotes secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α by macrophages and VICs, while inducing osteogenic markers such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and BMP-2 [15, 19, 20]. In support of this mechanism, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) or NF-κB inhibition can block high glucose–induced NF-κB/BMP-2 upregulation [15], consistent with oxidative and inflammatory intermediates linking glycation stress to pro-calcific signaling.

Overall appraisal and therapeutic implications. Therapeutically, approaches that reduce AGE formation or interfere with RAGE/NF-κB signaling could attenuate the link between glycation stress and calcification. Among the pathways discussed in this review, the AGE–RAGE axis has relatively strong biological plausibility in diabetes; nevertheless, current human evidence remains dominated by tissue correlation and cross-sectional observations, and direct proof that selective interruption of AGE–RAGE signaling slows valve calcification in patients is still lacking.

Oxidative stress and mitochondrial injury: early microcalcification triggers and inflammatory amplification

Human evidence. Oxidative stress can both initiate microcalcification and reinforce inflammatory signaling, including NF-κB activation. In diabetic AS, circulating oxidative markers such as myeloperoxidase (MPO) and oxidized phospholipids (oxPL) are elevated and inversely correlated with valve area [23, 24]. When diabetes coexists with AS, antioxidant reserves appear reduced: plasma free thiols are lower than in non-diabetic AS, suggesting diminished reductive capacity [22]. Beyond direct oxidative stress, dyslipidemia further exacerbates the pro-oxidant and inflammatory milieu in CAVD. Diabetes often features hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL, and qualitative LDL abnormalities that may increase lipid deposition in the valvular matrix and intensify inflammation. Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a major genetic risk factor for AS and carries oxidized phospholipids that accumulate in the valve and drive macrophage inflammation [55]. Diabetes may not directly raise Lp(a), but the associated inflammatory context could amplify Lp(a)–oxPL signaling. One interpretation of negative statin trials in AS is that LDL reduction does not address dominant drivers such as Lp(a) and inflammation in many patients [2, 56].

Cell culture and mechanistic evidence. High glucose–induced ROS can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling [15], forming a reinforcing inflammatory loop. Mitochondrial dysfunction is another potential contributor. Transcriptomic work has reported downregulation of genes related to mitochondrial respiration and calcium handling, together with alterations in mitochondria–ER contacts in myocardial tissue from diabetic AS patients [21]. These signatures are compatible with impaired energy metabolism and weaker ROS buffering, which could accelerate VIC calcification, although the mechanistic link from myocardial metabolic injury to valvular calcification remains indirect.

Overall appraisal and therapeutic implications. Collectively, these findings suggest that oxidative stress is both a trigger and amplifier of calcific remodeling in diabetes-associated valve disease, while mitochondrial dysfunction may further weaken metabolic resilience. These observations motivate antioxidant and mitochondrial-targeted strategies, such as Nrf2 activation and mitochondria-directed antioxidants, although effective delivery to the avascular valve remains a practical constraint. In parallel, Lp(a)-lowering therapies, including Apo(a) antisense or siRNA approaches, merit evaluation in CAVD, particularly in patients with elevated Lp(a) and diabetes [55]. PCSK9 inhibitors provide moderate Lp(a) lowering and strong LDL-C reduction and may benefit selected subgroups. Overall, these data point toward stratified lipid and redox-targeted strategies rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

Rather than representing a separate layer of pathology, oxidative and mitochondrial stress may be one of the principal mechanisms through which diabetic metabolic reprogramming is translated into inflammatory activation in the valve [15, 21–24].

In this view, ROS excess, impaired mitochondrial resilience, and altered energy handling are not merely metabolic abnormalities; they are candidate determinants of how strongly VICs and infiltrating immune cells engage NF-κB- and inflammasome-related programs [15, 21–24, 57].

Inflammation and immunity: macrophage polarization and the NLRP3–IL-1β loop

Human evidence. Inflammation is integral to CAVD, but the tissue source supporting each inflammatory signal should be distinguished carefully. Macrophages are central to calcific remodeling and, in a diabetic milieu, may adopt a more pro-inflammatory profile. In myocardial biopsies from AS patients, Herwig et al. reported increased inflammatory mediators (HMGB1, calprotectin), increased TLR2/4 signaling, and stronger NLRP3-associated cytokine release, including IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18, in the diabetic group [57]. These findings support enhanced inflammatory activation in the myocardial milieu of diabetic AS, but they do not by themselves establish equivalent inflammasome activation within valvular tissue. More direct valve-related support comes from diabetic valve studies showing increased intravalvular NF-κB signaling and stronger associations between inflammatory activation and stenosis severity [12, 15]. Consistent systemic signals are also seen in diabetic AS, including higher soluble IL-1 receptor 1/2 and increased endothelial/adhesion markers such as E-selectin, integrin β2, and ICAM-2 [3].

Cell culture and mechanistic evidence. IL-1β can act on VICs to induce chemokines and strengthen immune recruitment, thereby sustaining a positive-feedback loop. T and B cells may further contribute; diabetes may favor Th17-related inflammation with higher IL-17, which could modulate osteogenic and osteoclast-linked pathways. Together, these observations make the NLRP3/IL-1β axis an attractive candidate for intervention, including IL-1β blockade with canakinumab and colchicine or other NLRP3-related strategies, although valve-focused trial data remain limited.

Overall appraisal. Inflammatory activation is consistently observed in CAVD, but its compartment-specific contribution remains incompletely resolved. Myocardial biopsy studies indicate that diabetes intensifies AS-associated cardiac inflammation, whereas direct evidence for valve-specific inflammasome activation remains more limited and is supported primarily by tissue-correlation and cell-based studies. This distinction should be kept in mind when extrapolating from broader cardiac inflammatory biology to human valvular disease.

Endothelial dysfunction and mechanical imbalance: barrier loss, inflammatory entry, and stress signaling

Human and valve-specific evidence. The valvular endothelium is not merely a passive barrier regulating immune access, but an active and mechanosensitive participant in calcific remodeling. Diabetes-associated endothelial dysfunction may weaken this barrier through oxidative and inflammatory activation, reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, and increased permeability. Consistent with this concept, endothelial activation markers such as E-selectin and ICAM-2 are upregulated in diabetic AS [3], facilitating leukocyte migration and local inflammation. In addition, valve-specific studies indicate that valvular endothelial cells (VECs) can undergo endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), thereby contributing directly to the pool of pro-calcific mesenchymal-like cells rather than acting only through secondary fibrosis-related effects. In human valvular endothelial cells, reduced integrin-linked kinase (ILK) expression has been linked to endothelial dysfunction, osteogenic transition, increased RUNX2 expression, and downstream calcific nodule formation, supporting a protective role of ILK in maintaining valvular endothelial homeostasis [58]. More recent valve-specific work further showed that pro-calcific stressors such as indoxyl sulfate can induce EndMT and calcification in human VECs in an ILK-dependent manner [59].

Mechanistic and valve-focused evidence. The diabetic milieu may amplify this process through both biochemical and biomechanical stress. AGEs–RAGE signaling has been shown to induce valvular EndMT in early CAVD through TGF-β1 and BMPR2-related signaling, providing a direct bridge between diabetic glycation stress and endothelial contribution to calcific remodeling [20]. Oxidative stress further impairs VEC homeostasis, disrupts NO-related protective signaling, and facilitates inflammatory and osteogenic coupling between endothelial and interstitial cells. Valve-focused reviews emphasize that VEC dysfunction is tightly linked to oxidative stress, EndMT, and pathological VIC activation in CAVD [60]. Mechanical loading appears to be an important modifier of these responses. In bioreactor studies, dynamic flow induced more fibrosis than static conditions [16, 53], and high glucose/high insulin produced stronger remodeling primarily when mechanical stress was present, consistent with a “metabolic + mechanical” interaction. These observations are compatible with the broader concept that disturbed or oscillatory shear stress may favor endothelial dysfunction and mesenchymal transition within the valve microenvironment. This interpretation is also consistent with valve-focused mechanobiology reviews emphasizing that abnormal shear stress and inflammatory signaling form a self-reinforcing loop in CAVD progression [61].

Overall appraisal and therapeutic implications. Taken together, diabetes-related endothelial dysfunction likely facilitates inflammatory entry, promotes EndMT, and sensitizes the valve to mechanically induced injury, rather than acting as an isolated pathogenic pathway. Compared with the original version of this review, we now interpret endothelial dysfunction as a valve-specific and biologically active contributor to calcific remodeling, supported not only by systemic endothelial markers but also by direct studies in human VECs and valve-focused experimental models. This updated interpretation is also aligned with recent EndMT-focused CAVD reviews that frame endothelial transition as a direct contributor to inflammation, fibrosis, and osteogenic remodeling rather than as a secondary bystander process [62]. These observations suggest that strategies aimed at preserving endothelial homeostasis, including support of the NO–sGC–cGMP axis, reduction of oxidative stress, and tighter control of blood pressure and aortic stiffness, may be important for slowing disease progression in diabetes.

Osteogenic/chondrogenic signaling networks: BMP–Runx2–Wnt–Notch cross-talk

Human evidence. Valvular calcification reflects ectopic osteogenic programming driven by developmental pathways, and diabetes may shift these networks via metabolic and inflammatory stress. In diabetic AS valves, BMP-2 is increased [15]. These findings support the presence of a more pronounced osteogenic program in the diabetic valvular environment and suggest that diabetes may favor tissue remodeling toward calcification.

Ex vivo evidence. In ex vivo culture, high insulin plus high glucose altered VIC expression of cartilage-like matrix components, including hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, suggesting a chondrogenic tendency [16]. These observations support the concept that diabetic metabolic conditions may promote not only osteogenic but also cartilage-like matrix remodeling in valvular tissue.

Cell culture and mechanistic evidence. Runx2 promotes osteoblast-like differentiation and induces bone-matrix proteins such as collagen X and osteocalcin. Wnt/β-catenin is another major pro-osteogenic pathway, and oxidative stress and inflammation in diabetes may facilitate β-catenin nuclear translocation, potentially via reduced antagonism such as sclerostin, thereby accelerating osteogenic programs. Notch signaling tends to restrain osteogenesis in the valve, and available experimental observations suggest that diabetic stress may weaken this protective axis [47, 48, 63]. However, the upstream diabetes-specific regulatory mechanisms governing Wnt–Notch imbalance remain insufficiently defined in valvular tissue. In particular, current valve-focused evidence does not directly demonstrate hyperglycemia-induced DNA methylation at the Notch1 promoter, nor has a reproducible diabetes-specific microRNA signature in VICs been established that selectively targets the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in the context of CAVD. We therefore interpret these epigenetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms as plausible but currently unconfirmed layers of regulation, rather than established diabetes-specific drivers. Because these pathways interact extensively, diabetes likely produces a net shift toward osteogenic remodeling; however, the most convincing current evidence still supports pathway-level dysregulation rather than a fully mapped upstream regulatory program. Although BMP or β-catenin inhibition is mechanistically appealing, systemic safety concerns argue for targeted delivery approaches if these nodes are pursued clinically [47].

Overall appraisal. Osteogenic signaling likely represents a final common pathway of advanced calcific remodeling; however, upregulation of BMP-, Runx2-, Wnt-, or Notch-related markers in excised valves does not by itself establish which upstream regulatory events are diabetes-specific, nor does it yet resolve whether epigenetic or microRNA-mediated mechanisms are causal initiators or secondary amplifiers of disease progression.

The kidney bridge (CKD–MBD): phosphate–calcium balance, FGF23/Klotho, and MR signaling

Human evidence. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) often coexists with diabetes and independently increases CAVD risk. CKD-associated mineral and bone disorder (MBD), including hyperphosphatemia, vitamin D deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism, elevated FGF23, and Klotho deficiency, promotes soft-tissue calcification. Diabetes and CKD can therefore compound risk. In AS cohorts, worse renal function correlates with stronger oxidative/antioxidant imbalance [22], and moderate-to-severe CKD in diabetic AS is linked to faster calcification progression and poorer outcomes [64]. These observations support the concept that the diabetic kidney–valve axis may intensify calcific remodeling through both mineral dysregulation and oxidative stress. However, because the aortic valve is an avascular fibrous structure rather than a vessel wall, direct translation of phosphate/FGF23/Klotho mechanisms from vascular calcification to valvular calcification remains incompletely validated and should currently be interpreted with caution.

Valve-focused and mechanistic evidence. More direct valve-relevant support is now available for uremic stress. In human valvular endothelial cells, indoxyl sulfate has been shown to induce endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and calcific changes in an integrin-linked kinase-dependent manner, providing a mechanistically concrete bridge between uremic conditions and valvular calcification [59]. In parallel, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation is common to diabetes and CKD. Proteomics work identified chemerin as elevated specifically in valves from diabetic AS patients and linked it to aldosterone/mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) signaling [65]. Aldosterone increased chemerin and multiple inflammatory/osteogenic markers in VICs derived from diabetic patients, whereas MR antagonism blocked these effects [65]. Taken together, these findings suggest that CKD-related stress may act on the valve through both uremic endothelial injury and MR-linked inflammatory/osteogenic signaling, with the chemerin/aldosterone/MR axis currently representing the most direct valve-specific mechanistic support in this subsection.

Overall appraisal and therapeutic implications. Overall, CKD–MBD should be viewed as an important bridge between systemic metabolic disease and accelerated valvular calcification, although separating the independent effect of diabetes from that of CKD remains challenging in clinical cohorts. At present, phosphate dysregulation, FGF23, and Klotho deficiency are best regarded as biologically plausible contributors and comorbidity-associated amplifiers rather than fully validated valve-specific drivers. By contrast, uremic toxin–induced valvular endothelial injury and the chemerin/aldosterone/MR pathway provide more direct mechanistic links to valve remodeling. Accordingly, CKD–MBD management, including phosphate control and correction of vitamin D/PTH abnormalities, together with consideration of MR antagonists, may be particularly relevant in this high-risk setting. SGLT2 inhibitors may further contribute indirectly through renoprotection and reductions in systemic inflammation and oxidative stress.

Throughout this section, valve-focused preclinical findings, observational clinical signals, and hypothesis-driven translational proposals are distinguished explicitly, and single-study observational associations are interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Because the human aortic valve is relatively avascular and progressively thickened by fibrosis and calcification, mechanistic plausibility alone is not sufficient for translation; drug penetration, local tissue exposure, and stage-specific delivery feasibility must also be considered when interpreting repurposing strategies [1, 2, 47].

Pharmacological targeting of key immunometabolic nodes in diabetic CAVD

Extra-glycemic valvular effects of glucose-lowering agents

Some glucose-lowering drugs may influence calcification biology beyond glycemic control. Metformin reduced calcium deposition and early osteogenic markers in an ex vivo sheep valve model, associated with AMPK activation [17]. Observationally, metformin use in diabetes with AS has been linked to lower systemic oxidative stress [18, 24].

SGLT2 inhibitors are of interest in CAVD primarily because of their plausible effects on oxidative stress, energy sensing, and inflammatory signaling. In an aortic valve injury model, canagliflozin reduced calcification burden, plausibly through AMPK/Nrf2/HO-1 activation and reduced ROS [66]. In vitro, canagliflozin inhibited calcific nodule formation in VICs, and an Nrf2 inhibitor partially attenuated this effect, supporting Nrf2 involvement [66]. By contrast, the available clinical data in diabetic AS/TAVR populations should be interpreted more cautiously. Registry analyses showing improved LV systolic function, fewer heart-failure hospitalizations, and lower MACE with SGLT2i use [66–68], as well as lower AKI incidence in high-risk TAVR patients with CKD [69], are clinically encouraging but more likely reflect cardiorenal and heart-failure benefits than a proven valve-specific disease-modifying effect. Because these clinical observations derive primarily from single-registry or non-randomized cohort analyses, they remain susceptible to residual confounding, treatment-selection bias, and baseline between-group differences, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. For GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), direct CAVD evidence remains limited. Their effects on weight and systemic inflammation could plausibly benefit valvular remodeling indirectly, but whether such systemic actions can generate sufficient drug exposure within an avascular, fibrotic, and calcified leaflet remains uncertain, and this translational gap should be acknowledged explicitly [1, 2]. Overall, mechanistic and observational signals are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and randomized studies with valve-specific imaging or progression endpoints are needed before any disease-modifying role in CAVD can be inferred [2].

Anti-inflammatory/immunomodulatory strategies

Given the role of chronic inflammation, targeting IL-1β/IL-6-related pathways is of interest in diabetes-associated CAVD. However, the translational logic in aortic valve disease differs from that in atherosclerosis and requires more careful framing. Although CAVD and atherosclerosis share early features such as endothelial injury, lipid retention, macrophage infiltration, and NF-κB-linked inflammatory activation, their dominant pathological endpoints diverge substantially [1, 2]. In atherosclerosis, inflammation contributes importantly to plaque destabilization and rupture-prone biology, whereas in CAVD the downstream consequence is more often progressive fibrosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and osteogenic/chondrogenic reprogramming of valvular interstitial cells, ultimately leading to leaflet stiffening and calcific obstruction rather than acute plaque rupture [1, 2, 47].

This distinction is important for interpreting why anti-lipid and anti-inflammatory strategies may not translate directly from vascular disease to valve disease. The failure of statins to slow AS progression in major trials does not imply that inflammation is irrelevant in CAVD; rather, it suggests that once the disease is established, lowering LDL alone may be insufficient to reverse a valve-specific inflammatory–osteogenic program that has already become self-sustaining [2]. In diabetic CAVD, this issue may be even more pronounced because inflammatory activation is coupled to hyperglycaemia, AGE–RAGE signaling, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and VIC osteogenic conversion, all of which reinforce each other [2, 3, 47, 55, 57]. In addition, the aortic valve is exposed to a unique biomechanical environment characterized by repetitive flexion, shear heterogeneity, pressure loading, and matrix-constrained cellular responses. These biomechanical cues interact with diabetic metabolic stress rather than merely coexisting with it, thereby amplifying VIC phenotypic drift and favoring a fibro-calcific endpoint that is less reversible than lipid-rich vascular lesions. Accordingly, inflammatory signaling in CAVD should be viewed less as an isolated target and more as part of a broader immunometabolic remodeling network.

Within this framework, IL-1β blockade and IL-6 inhibition remain biologically plausible strategies. IL-1β blockade reduced cardiovascular events in atherosclerosis and may be relevant to CAVD because IL-1β and IL-6 lie upstream of VIC activation, matrix remodeling, and inflammatory amplification [47, 57]. Likewise, NLRP3-targeted strategies, including colchicine and experimental NLRP3 inhibitors, may be of interest because diabetes-associated AS is characterized by stronger inflammasome-linked inflammatory signaling [57]. However, the current evidence base remains indirect. Much of the rationale is extrapolated from inflammatory cardiovascular disease or from mechanistic studies rather than from dedicated valve-focused intervention trials [2, 47].

Several additional factors may also limit efficacy. The aortic valve is relatively avascular, which may reduce tissue exposure to circulating agents. This limitation may be particularly relevant for large-molecule biologics, for which diffusion into a thickened and densely remodeled leaflet is likely to be more constrained than systemic pharmacology alone would imply [1, 2]. Moreover, late-stage CAVD may be less responsive to anti-inflammatory monotherapy because the predominant biology has already shifted toward fibrosis and osteogenic calcification rather than a reversible inflammatory phenotype [1, 2]. Therefore, parallels with atherosclerosis should be interpreted as mechanistic context rather than therapeutic equivalence. Systemic immunosuppression also carries infection risk, and diabetic AS may combine chronic inflammation with impaired immune responsiveness, as reflected by higher postoperative inflammatory complications in some settings [70, 71]. For these reasons, anti-inflammatory/immunomodulatory therapy in CAVD is more likely to be effective in carefully selected patients, at earlier disease stages, and ideally in studies using valve-specific imaging or progression endpoints rather than broad cardiovascular outcomes alone. If such approaches are to become clinically realistic, future work may also need to consider local or valve-targeted delivery concepts, rather than assuming that systemic exposure will necessarily translate into effective leaflet-level drug concentrations.

Pharmacology of antioxidant and stress-response pathways

Enhancing antioxidant defenses is attractive because oxidative stress is repeatedly implicated in diabetic CAVD. Nrf2 activation is a central node; Nrf2 agonists (e.g., bardoxolone; kaempferol) induce antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and glutathione-related pathways. The canagliflozin data provide a concrete example of anti-calcific effects consistent with Nrf2 activation [66]. NAC can inhibit high glucose–induced NF-κB and BMP-2 upregulation in vitro [15, 24].

Single-agent antioxidant supplementation often shows limited efficacy in vivo, motivating mitochondria-targeted approaches (e.g., MitoQ) or SOD-mimetics to preserve VIC mitochondrial function. Restoration of NO–sGC–cGMP signaling has also been suggested by myocardial findings in diabetic AS [29–31, 57]. In practice, antioxidant strategies may be most effective early and may need to be evaluated in combination regimens.

Anti-osteogenic pathways and epigenetic regulation

Direct inhibition of osteogenic pathways (BMP, Wnt/β-catenin) can reduce calcification in models but is constrained by systemic toxicity and broad physiological roles. Notch activation is mechanistically protective but difficult to target pharmacologically; gene-therapy approaches remain experimental.

Epigenetic and microRNA-based regulation remains an emerging but increasingly relevant therapeutic area in CAVD. Importantly, valve-specific evidence is available beyond general mechanistic inference. For example, miR-30b has been characterized in the human valvular context as an anti-calcific regulator that directly targets RUNX2-related osteogenic signaling and attenuates valvular interstitial cell calcification [72]. In addition, miR-204 is reduced in diseased human aortic valve tissue and in AVICs from diseased valves, and its deficiency is associated with increased osteogenic activity, whereas restoration of miR-204 suppresses ALP expression and calcium deposition [73]. Likewise, miR-449c-5p is downregulated in calcified human aortic valves, and its overexpression inhibits VIC osteogenic differentiation through a Smad4-dependent mechanism, including suppression of RUNX2 expression and ALP activity [74]. These findings support the concept that specific miRNA networks can directly restrain VIC osteogenic transition and may represent both therapeutic targets and mechanistic biomarkers. Beyond intracellular regulation, circulating and extracellular vesicle-associated miRNA signatures have also been proposed as early biomarkers of CAVD activity, with potential future value for risk stratification, disease monitoring, and RNA-based delivery strategies, although prospective validation remains limited [75, 76]. More broadly, multi-omics analyses further suggest that DNA methylation and other epigenetic regulatory patterns contribute to valvular homeostasis and disease-associated remodeling, supporting continued exploration of epigenetic therapeutics in CAVD [63].

Progress may depend not only on reducing off-target effects, but also on overcoming the anatomical constraint of an avascular and progressively calcified leaflet; accordingly, local or valve-targeted delivery strategies may be necessary if anti-osteogenic or epigenetic therapies are to achieve meaningful tissue exposure in CAVD [1, 2, 47].

CKD–MBD and MR axis: integrating comorbidity management

In diabetes, CKD and RAAS/MR signaling should be treated as part of the therapeutic landscape for CAVD. In moderate-to-severe CKD, managing phosphate balance and correcting vitamin D/PTH abnormalities are essential and may reduce calcification propensity. For aldosterone/MR-driven pathways, non-steroidal MR antagonists (e.g., finerenone) offer a safer profile than older agents and reduce cardiovascular events in diabetic kidney disease trials, providing a rationale for testing valvular effects. While MR antagonists have not been evaluated in dedicated CAVD trials, the chemerin mechanism supports a plausible direct role [65]. SGLT2 inhibitors may further reduce systemic drivers through renoprotection and improvements in inflammation/oxidative stress [64, 68].

Lipids and Lp(a): interpreting statin failures and future directions

Statins did not slow AS progression in multiple trials, reinforcing the view that established CAVD cannot be treated as a simple extension of atherosclerosis and that LDL lowering alone is unlikely to reverse a valve-specific inflammatory–osteogenic remodeling program [2, 56]. Nonetheless, early intensive lipid control may still reduce initiation risk in high-risk metabolic settings. In addition, Lp(a)-directed therapies are particularly relevant because statins do not lower Lp(a) meaningfully. Apo(a) antisense/siRNA approaches that reduce Lp(a) by > 80% should be evaluated in AS populations enriched for high Lp(a), including diabetic subgroups [55]. PCSK9 inhibitors may help selected patients via LDL-C reduction and modest Lp(a) lowering.

Biomarkers and risk stratification: making mechanisms measurable

From a clinical perspective, the biomarkers discussed below can be interpreted according to their current utility for routine risk assessment, their potential value for patient stratification or trial enrichment, and their present limitation to research settings.

Metabolic and insulin-resistance indices

Metabolic markers (fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin) are associated with incident AS, and insulin resistance may carry risk even before diabetes diagnosis. Among these markers, HbA1c and fasting glucose are currently the most clinically applicable because they are standardized, widely available, and already integrated into routine diabetes care. In > 10,000 Finnish men, higher fasting/postprandial insulin and C-peptide predicted higher AS risk (HR ~ 1.4), while a higher insulin sensitivity index (Matsuda index) was protective (HR 0.68); associations persisted after excluding known diabetes [77]. Composite indices such as the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index may also be practical for risk stratification [78].

In established AS, HbA1c can inform prognosis. In mild-to-moderate AS, each 1% rise in baseline HbA1c increased the likelihood of faster progression, and HbA1c ≥ 7.0% was associated with approximately 1.5-fold higher risk of stenosis progression [11, 79]. Tracking metabolic indices longitudinally may help identify rapid progressors. By contrast, fasting insulin, C-peptide, and composite insulin-resistance indices such as the TyG or Matsuda index are biologically informative and may improve risk phenotyping, but at present they are better suited to translational stratification than to routine valve-specific clinical decision-making.

Inflammation, oxidative stress, and AGE-related markers

Circulating markers may reflect valvular inflammatory–calcific activity. However, most of these biomarkers currently provide greater mechanistic than immediate clinical value, because assay standardization, valve specificity, and longitudinal validation remain limited. While hsCRP lacks specificity, IL-6 and galectin-3 may be elevated in severe AS, especially with diabetes. Oxidative stress markers such as MPO and oxLDL correlate with calcification burden: MPO and oxPL were reported to be 1.3–2.5-fold higher in diabetic AS than non-diabetic AS and inversely correlated with valve area [23, 24]. These markers may help identify a high-inflammatory/high-oxidative phenotype, but they are not yet sufficiently standardized for routine valve-focused clinical use [78].

AGE–RAGE biomarkers are also informative. In diabetic AS, intravalvular AGE content was inversely correlated with AVA (r = − 0.68), and in poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c > 7%) valvular AGE correlated with higher gradients [19]. Multi-omics studies propose additional candidates such as MFAP5, which is upregulated in diabetic AS valves and detectable in plasma [80]. A combined panel integrating metabolic, inflammatory, oxidative, and AGE-related markers may better capture disease activity. AGE-related markers and emerging candidates such as MFAP5 are promising for mechanistic phenotyping and trial enrichment, but they should currently be regarded as exploratory rather than practice-defining biomarkers.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are an emerging biomarker class that may help bridge molecular activity and structural disease in CAVD. Recent work suggests that EV cargoes derived from valvular and circulating sources can contain osteogenic proteins, inflammatory microRNAs, and lipid mediators that reflect active fibro-calcific remodeling before overt macroscopic calcification is detectable. In addition, human and translational studies indicate that EV-associated miRNA signatures from aortic stenosis/CAVD patients can modulate VIC osteogenic programs, including BMP4-, RUNX2-, osteocalcin-, and osteopontin-related pathways, supporting the view that EVs may function not only as passive markers but also as active propagators of calcific signaling. At present, however, EV-based biomarkers remain investigational because assay standardization, source attribution, and prospective longitudinal validation are still limited [76, 81].

Integrating imaging with biomarkers

CT-derived aortic valve calcium scores quantify calcification burden and relate to severity and prognosis. Among the biomarkers discussed in this review, CT-based valve calcification burden is one of the most clinically actionable at present because it directly reflects structural disease burden and can be integrated into existing imaging workflows. CT-detected aortic valve calcification is more common in prediabetes/diabetes than normoglycemia in mid-life cohorts (men 8% vs. 5%; women 9% vs. 5%) [25]. PET-CT with 18 F-NaF can detect active microcalcification and may serve as a pharmacodynamic endpoint. By contrast, 18 F-NaF PET-CT is currently better viewed as an investigational or trial-oriented tool for detecting active calcification biology rather than a routine biomarker for everyday clinical care. Combining imaging with biomarker profiles may improve risk prediction and trial enrichment [78].

Overall, the most practical near-term strategy is likely to combine routine metabolic markers such as HbA1c with structural imaging of valve calcification, while reserving inflammatory, oxidative, and omics-derived biomarkers for advanced phenotyping or research use.

Controversies and gaps

A major interpretive challenge in diabetes-associated CAVD is that the field contains an uneven mixture of epidemiological association, tissue-level observation, and experimentally induced mechanistic findings. Accordingly, several pathways discussed earlier in this review should be interpreted as biologically credible but not yet clinically validated drivers. Particular caution is warranted when assigning causal hierarchy, because diabetes commonly coexists with chronic kidney disease, hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia, and because many experimental systems do not fully reproduce the hemodynamic, extracellular-matrix, and immune-cell complexity of the human aortic valve.

Drivers versus markers: the comorbidity question (especially CKD)

Because diabetes co-occurs with hypertension, obesity, and CKD, residual confounding is a valid concern. However, multiple cohorts report diabetes as an independent predictor of AS incidence and progression after adjustment for blood pressure, lipids, BMI, and related factors [4–7, 46, 82]. A reasonable synthesis is that diabetes and its comorbidities contribute actively, but with variable effect sizes across individuals. Causal modeling linked to mechanistic studies is needed to prioritize targets.

Limits of model extrapolation: why context matters

Some in vitro studies report attenuated calcification under high-glucose conditions [54], which at face value appears to conflict with the broader literature linking diabetes to pro-calcific valvular remodeling. Rather than invalidating the proposed mechanisms, this discrepancy highlights an important limitation of reductionist models. Diabetes-associated valve disease likely emerges from the combined effects of hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, endothelial dysfunction, and abnormal mechanical loading. In simplified culture systems, altering glucose concentration alone may produce responses that depend heavily on exposure duration, osteogenic induction conditions, donor cell phenotype, and the absence of haemodynamic and immune context. These findings therefore argue for caution in extrapolating single-factor in vitro results and support the use of more physiologically integrated platforms, including co-culture systems, flow-conditioned models, and large-animal metabolic disease models [47, 53, 54].

The major gap: valve-specific trials and endpoints

Despite rapid mechanistic progress, dedicated randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for diabetic CAVD are scarce [1, 68]. Hard endpoints occur late, driving large sample sizes and long follow-up. Stratified enrollment and intermediate endpoints (CT calcium progression; biomarker trajectories) may allow smaller, faster phase II signals and better inform phase III endpoint trials.

Beyond the aortic valve: diabetes, multivalvular calcification, and the clinical uniqueness of CAVD

Diabetes is unlikely to affect only the aortic valve. Population-based data suggest that diabetes and elevated plasma glucose are associated not only with aortic valve calcification and aortic stenosis, but also with mitral valve calcification, supporting the concept that diabetic metabolic stress may predispose to a broader valvular calcific phenotype [8]. In addition, studies in diabetic cohorts have shown that the coexistence of calcification at multiple valvular sites, including aortic valve calcification and mitral annular calcification (MAC), is associated with worse prognosis, suggesting that multivalvular calcification may reflect a more advanced systemic fibro-calcific milieu [83]. More broadly, diabetes has been linked to left-sided degenerative valvular heart disease beyond isolated AS, reinforcing the view that dysglycaemia can contribute to valvular degeneration at multiple anatomical levels [84].

Among non-aortic valvular entities, MAC is particularly relevant in the context of diabetes. Unlike leaflet-dominant calcific aortic valve disease, MAC develops within the fibrous annular structure of the mitral apparatus, where mechanical stress, age-related degeneration, renal dysfunction, and metabolic disturbance frequently coexist. This makes MAC a useful comparator for understanding which aspects of diabetic valvular pathology may be shared across valves—such as oxidative stress, inflammation, matrix remodeling, and calcific tendency—and which may be more specific to the aortic valve [8, 83, 84]. In this sense, diabetes may promote a general calcific susceptibility, while the final disease phenotype remains shaped by local tissue composition, haemodynamic loading, and valve-specific biology.

By contrast, the aortic valve appears to be uniquely vulnerable to diabetes-associated immunometabolic remodeling because it is exposed to repetitive high-pressure mechanical stress, has a well-characterized VIC-driven osteogenic program, and progresses toward leaflet stiffening and outflow obstruction rather than annular calcification alone [1, 2, 16, 50, 51]. Thus, adding other valvular phenotypes to the discussion does not weaken the focus on CAVD; rather, it helps define the relative uniqueness of diabetes-associated aortic valve disease. At present, however, mechanistic data for pulmonic and tricuspid valve disease in diabetes remain sparse, and the strongest non-aortic comparative signal currently relates to MAC and broader left-sided degenerative valvular calcification [8, 84].

Calcification pattern matters: tricuspid, bicuspid, and rheumatic aortic valve disease are not equivalent

Even within aortic valve disease itself, the spatial pattern of calcification varies across valve phenotypes, which is relevant when interpreting the specificity of diabetes-associated mechanisms. In degenerative tricuspid aortic valve stenosis, calcification tends to be concentrated around the commissures and annular/hinge regions, with the non-coronary cusp often showing the greatest calcium burden, a pattern thought to reflect the local distribution of mechanical stress and leaflet loading. By contrast, in bicuspid aortic valve disease, calcification is more prominently distributed along the free leaflet edge and leaflet body, consistent with altered cusp geometry and eccentric flow-related stress rather than predominantly annular involvement. These imaging-based observations reinforce the concept that the anatomical substrate strongly shapes where calcific remodeling develops within the valve [85, 86].

This distinction is clinically relevant for the present review. The immunometabolic mechanisms discussed here are primarily intended to explain diabetes-associated degenerative CAVD, which most commonly develops in the setting of tricuspid fibro-calcific valve disease. In contrast, rheumatic aortic valve stenosis is characterized more by leaflet thickening, commissural fusion, and cusp-level deformity, with calcification occurring on a different structural background from degenerative CAVD. Therefore, although inflammatory and metabolic stress may still modulate rheumatic or bicuspid valve disease, the spatial pattern of calcification and the dominant pathological substrate are not identical. Recognizing these phenotype-specific differences helps delineate where diabetes-related immunometabolic mechanisms are likely to be most applicable and where extrapolation should be made more cautiously [85, 86].

An important unresolved question is whether diabetic VICs undergo a defined intracellular metabolic rewiring program comparable to that described in classical immunometabolism, such as altered glycolytic flux, fatty acid oxidation, or accumulation of TCA-cycle intermediates with signaling or epigenetic effects. At present, direct valve-specific evidence for such mechanisms remains limited. Although existing studies support roles for impaired AMPK signaling, oxidative stress, AGE–RAGE activation, and mitochondrial dysfunction in shaping VIC behavior [15, 17, 19, 21–24], it is still unclear whether metabolites such as succinate or other TCA-cycle intermediates act as bona fide regulators of inflammatory fate or osteogenic programming in VICs. Accordingly, these processes should be regarded as important hypotheses for future study rather than established drivers of diabetes-associated CAVD.

Future directions: executable research and trial roadmaps

Single-cell and spatial omics

Single-cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics can define cell states and intercellular signaling in diabetic vs. non-diabetic AS valves, localize immune–calcification interactions, and prioritize druggable ligand–receptor axes. Proteomic studies have already highlighted candidate biomarkers such as chemerin and other emerging circulating protein signals [49, 63, 65, 87]; higher-resolution mapping could help identify their cellular sources, downstream targets, and therapeutic relevance.

Translational platforms

Improved experimental systems are needed: human valvular organoids, co-culture systems with pulsatile flow, large-animal metabolic syndrome models (e.g., pigs), and perfused human explant platforms for candidate drug screening [47, 53, 87]. “Valve-on-a-chip” concepts may eventually enable multi-factor testing under controlled mechanical and metabolic conditions.

Trial design recommendations for diabetes + CAVD

Candidate trial elements include: enrolling type 2 diabetes with mild-to-moderate AS (AVA 1.5–2.5 cm²) or high CT calcium burden; comparing metabolically active agents with proven cardiovascular benefit (e.g., SGLT2i; potentially GLP-1RA) and inflammatory comparators (e.g., colchicine or low-dose IL-1β inhibition) on top of standard care; using CT calcium progression over 18–24 months as a primary endpoint with secondary echocardiographic, biomarker, and functional outcomes; stratifying by renal function, Lp(a), and glycemic control/variability (HbA1c, SHR) [42, 43, 68]. Such studies could generate phase IIb signals to justify larger endpoint trials.

Conclusion

Diabetes promotes aortic valve inflammation and osteogenic remodeling through coupled immunometabolic mechanisms, contributing to earlier onset and faster progression of CAVD [1, 2, 4, 51]. This review outlines key pathways and druggable nodes that may support mechanism-guided therapeutic development. The principal bottleneck remains the lack of valve-focused RCTs and validated surrogate endpoints in diabetic subgroups. Progress will likely depend on stratified trial designs, improved translational models, and integration of imaging with biomarker-based monitoring, with the aim of moving beyond “no medical therapy” toward targeted intervention for high-risk patients.
