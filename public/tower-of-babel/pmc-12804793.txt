Dynamic response analysis of semi-rigid asphalt pavement under combined low-temperature and heavy-load conditions
Authors: Jinlei Gai, Xujiao Yang, Qian Yu, Yi Xie, Xinliang Zheng, Xiaomiao Yu, Yunlong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In cold regions, asphalt pavements undergo severe deterioration driven by the synergistic effects of thermal contraction and dynamic heavy loading, resulting in premature failures such as thermal cracking and rutting. This study presents a comprehensive thermo-mechanical coupling analysis to address this critical issue. A sophisticated seven-degree-of-freedom (7-DOF) vehicle model was developed to simulate realistic dynamic loads, with load magnitudes quantified via MATLAB simulations. A three-dimensional (3D) finite element (FE) model was established, incorporating temperature-dependent material properties and transient heat transfer analysis to replicate a typical winter cooling event in Jilin Province, China. The results quantified key responses: a maximum surface thermal stress of 8.01 MPa, a vertical compressive stress of 0.86 MPa beneath the wheel load, and a detrimental tensile strain of 90.16 με at the asphalt layer base. Shear stress concentrations of up to 0.16 MPa were observed, accelerating shear-induced damage. A critical finding was the stress reversal (compression to tension) at the bottom of asphalt layers under moving load, a phenomenon crucial for fatigue assessment but often overlooked in static analysis. The study conclusively shows that low temperatures exacerbate stress accumulation and fatigue damage under heavy traffic, providing critical insights for designing durable pavement structures in cold regions.

Introduction

The growing intensity and frequency of heavy-duty transportation, coupled with widespread overloading, pose unprecedented challenges to the durability of highway infrastructure—especially in northern China. These regions experience harsh winter conditions with sharp temperature drops, which induce significant thermal stress in asphalt pavements. The consequent embrittlement of asphalt binders significantly reduces their strain tolerance, accelerating thermal cracking and structural degradation1,2.

Extensive research has focused on understanding pavement performance under diverse operational conditions. Ahmed et al.3 characterized heavy-traffic axle load spectra and developed on-board monitoring systems to capture real vehicle-pavement interactions. Prastyanto et al.4 studied overloaded trucks in Indonesia, establishing empirical correlations between vehicle weight, axle distribution, and tire contact pressure. To advance mechanical response prediction, Liu et al.5 used a semi-analytical finite element method (SAFEM) to assess stress–strain behavior and fatigue life under heavy traffic. Guo et al.6 integrated vibration testing with Bayesian inference to model dynamic responses in composite pavements, whereas García et al.7 identified rutting and deformation patterns in concrete pavements under heavy vehicles via full-scale experiments.

Parallel work has rigorously examined vehicle–pavement interaction dynamics. Miguel et al.8 proposed robust design optimization of multiple tuned mass dampers (MTMDs) to mitigate vehicle–bridge vibrations, using stochastic methods and metaheuristic algorithms. Ma et al.9 developed a coupled tire–vehicle dynamics model with 9 degrees of freedom (9-DOF), validated via stability measurements. Al Mansour et al.10 applied equivalent linearization and Monte Carlo simulation to optimize semi-active suspension control, while Krishnanunni et al.11 proposed an efficient decoupling technique for vehicle–pavement systems using combined numerical methods.

For low-temperature performance, Behnia et al.12 pioneered acoustic emission (AE) techniques to determine the embrittlement temperature and self-healing properties of asphalt binders under thermal cycling. Moradi et al.13 integrated mechanical testing with surface free energy (SFE) measurements to quantify aging-induced reductions in fracture energy and crack resistance, highlighting the potential of polymer modifiers like ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE). Gong et al.14 used a bending beam rheometer (BBR) to investigate the low-temperature behavior of asphalt fine aggregate matrix (FAM) and hot-mix asphalt (HMA), identifying optimal compaction as critical for durability and noting FAM’s increased sensitivity to moisture damage and UV aging. Li et al.15 combined ductility tests, BBR measurements, and compositional analysis to evaluate SBS-modified binders under thermo-oxidative and UV conditions, revealing a significant decline in low-temperature performance early in the aging process—especially under elevated temperatures and high light intensity—while confirming the effectiveness of SBS modification. Zhang et al.16 further investigated the physical hardening of reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP)-modified binders using extended BBR tests and dynamic mechanical analysis, showing that RAP accelerates hardening and increases thermal stress—effects that intensify with aging time and RAP content—though low-wax base binders can act as recycling agents to allow higher RAP proportions. Behnia et al.17 also showed that fiber reinforcement significantly improves fracture energy and tensile strength in asphalt mixtures under low-temperature conditions.

Recent studies have further advanced the understanding of fracture mechanisms and thermo-mechanical behavior in asphalt materials. Investigations into the pre- and post-cracking behavior of modified asphalt mixtures under modes I and III at low and intermediate temperatures have highlighted the effectiveness of fiber and chemical additives in enhancing fracture energy and resistance to elastic deformation18. Similarly, research on the utilization of recycled materials like pyrolysis carbon black (PCB) and pyrolysis hydrocarbon (PHC) has demonstrated significant improvements in the low-temperature fracture toughness and energy of asphalt mixtures, even under freeze–thaw damage cycles19. The fracture behavior of amorphous poly alpha olefin (APAO)-modified hot mix asphalt under both constant and variable temperature conditions has been quantified, revealing enhanced fracture toughness and energy in modes I and II20. Furthermore, studies on nanomaterial-modified asphalt, such as nano reduced graphene oxide (RGO), have shown improved long-term fracture resistance against aging and freeze–thaw damage21. Beyond asphalt materials, research on geopolymer composites has demonstrated that the synergistic use of nano-silica (SiO₂) and steel fibers can effectively optimize the pore structure and interface bonding, thereby maintaining high mechanical strength and microstructural integrity under severe freeze–thaw cycles, offering valuable insights for developing durable composite materials in cold regions22. Complementing these material-focused studies, the critical influence of temperature distribution patterns (uniform, linear, harmonic) on the dynamic response of asphalt pavements under moving loads has been established, underscoring the necessity of incorporating realistic thermal boundary conditions in pavement analysis23. Collectively, these studies provide valuable insights into material improvement strategies and the importance of thermo-mechanical coupling.

While these studies have advanced understanding of thermal and mechanical responses in isolation24,25, a critical gap remains in the integrated analysis of coupled thermo-mechanical loading conditions—especially under the synergistic effects of extreme low temperatures and dynamic heavy loading, which are common in cold regions with heavy freight traffic. Current pavement design specifications—largely based on static loading assumptions—do not capture the complex thermo-mechanical coupling under real traffic and environmental conditions. This study therefore develops an integrated simulation approach to elucidate the dynamic response of semi-rigid asphalt pavements under combined thermal and heavy mechanical loading, addressing this research gap.

The novelty of this study lies in three key methodological and analytical aspects:The development of a coupled vehicle–pavement–temperature interaction model that simultaneously accounts for transient thermal fields and dynamic moving loads;A comprehensive quantification of key pavement responses (stresses, strains, displacements) under the synergistic effect of extreme low temperature and heavy traffic;The identification of critical failure mechanisms, such as stress reversal at the asphalt layer bottom, shear concentration in the intermediate layer, and tensile vulnerability at the base bottom, which are often overlooked in conventional design methods.

The specific objectives are: (1) develop a coupled vehicle–pavement interaction model to simulate realistic dynamic wheel loads; (2) establish a 3D FE model that incorporates transient thermal fields and moving load effects; and (3) identify key pavement responses—stresses, strains, and displacements—that drive failure mechanisms, thereby informing the design of more resilient pavement systems in cold climates.

Pavement structure and temperature-dependent material properties

The pavement structure was modeled after the Hunwu Expressway (Jilin Province, China), consisting of seven layers (top to bottom): 4 cm stone mastic asphalt (SMA-13), 6 cm dense-graded asphalt concrete (AC-20), 10 cm asphalt-treated base (ATB-25), 36 cm cement-stabilized gravel base, 20 cm graded gravel subbase, 20 cm graded sand cushion, and a soil subgrade. All layers were assumed to be continuous with full interfacial bonding.

Notably, material properties—particularly for asphalt layers—were defined as temperature-dependent based on comprehensive laboratory testing and literature review36–38. Finite element modeling and analysis procedure

A 3D FE model (16.0 m × 6.0 m × 5.0 m) was constructed in ABAQUS to represent the multi-layered pavement structure (Fig. 3). A mesh sensitivity analysis was performed using multiple mesh densities until the change in maximum vertical displacement was < 2%, ensuring mesh independence. The final mesh used approximately 85,000 swept hexahedral elements (C3D8T—8-node thermally coupled brick, trilinear displacement and temperature), with higher density in the wheel path (element size ~ 20 mm) to capture high stress gradients (Fig. 4). All layers were modeled with full interfacial bonding (“TIE” constraint in ABAQUS). Boundary conditions were set as follows: the bottom surface was fully fixed (ENCASTRE), and the four lateral surfaces were assigned symmetric constraints (XSYMM, ZSYMM) to simulate pavement continuity.Fig. 33D FE model of semi-rigid asphalt pavement under low-temperature and heavy-load conditions.Fig. 4Meshing of the FE model (high density in the wheel path).

A transient heat transfer analysis was conducted over 12 h to simulate an overnight winter cooling event. The pavement surface temperature was prescribed to decrease from an initial -13 °C to -28 °C, based on meteorological data from Jilin. The thermal boundary conditions accounted for heat convection with the ambient air and radiation cooling to the sky, while solar radiation was neglected for this nighttime scenario. Results and discussion

Temperature field and thermal stress analysis

The transient heat transfer analysis captured the temperature distribution across pavement depths over time (Fig. 5). As expected, the surface layer exhibited the largest temperature change, with a pronounced time lag in deeper layers due to material thermal inertia.Fig. 5Temperature distribution of each structural layer of asphalt pavement.

Temperature distribution of each structural layer of asphalt pavement.

This cooling process induced significant thermal stress. At the end of the 12-h cooling period, the maximum tensile stress (8.01 MPa) was recorded at the pavement surface (Fig. 6). This value exceeds the typical low-temperature tensile strength of conventional asphalt mixtures (usually 5–7 MPa)46, indicating a high risk of thermal cracking. The predicted maximum thermal stress aligns well with field observations from similar climatic regions47,48, where thermal cracking is prevalent. The gradient of thermal stress with depth further explains the mechanism of top-down cracking initiation, which is commonly observed in cold regions. Thermal stress decreased with depth: 7.43 MPa at the bottom of the intermediate asphalt layer and 6.61 MPa at the bottom of the bottom asphalt layer. This vertical stress gradient is a key driver of top-down cracking.Fig. 6Variation of maximum thermal stress with pavement depth at the end of the cooling period (t = 12 h).

Variation of maximum thermal stress with pavement depth at the end of the cooling period (t = 12 h).

Mechanical stress response under moving load

Vertical stress

The maximum vertical compressive stress (0.86 MPa) occurred at the surface layer’s top, directly beneath the load (Point A, Fig. 7). Stress dissipated rapidly with depth. A critical observation was the stress reversal (from compression to tension) at the bottom of asphalt layers as the load approached and passed (Fig. 8). This cyclic stress state accelerates fatigue damage—exacerbated by the reduced stress relaxation capacity of cold asphalt. This finding highlights the limitations of static analysis and the necessity of dynamic simulation for accurate fatigue life prediction.Fig. 7Trends in vertical stresses.Fig. 8Trend of lateral variation of compressive stress in asphalt pavement.

Trends in vertical stresses.

Trend of lateral variation of compressive stress in asphalt pavement.

To better understand the mechanical response at the load interface, the contact stress distribution in the wheel-pavement interaction zone was analyzed in detail. Figure 7 clearly illustrates the stress concentration beneath the wheel load, with the maximum compressive stress of 0.86 MPa occurring directly under the load center. The contour plot reveals a characteristic stress distribution pattern that extends laterally and diminishes with depth, creating a “stress bulb” effect typical of layered elastic systems. At the edges of the contact area, stress singularities were observed due to the abrupt transition from loaded to unloaded regions, with the left edge reaching 1.02 MPa and the right edge 0.51 MPa in the surface layer. This non-uniform stress distribution at the load interface significantly contributes to localized damage initiation and propagation, particularly under repeated heavy loading conditions.

Horizontal stress

The analysis revealed a complex triaxial stress state (Fig. 9). Asphalt layers experienced horizontal compression near the surface, but a tensile stress of 0.12 MPa was detected at the bottom of the semi-rigid base. This is critical as cement-stabilized materials have low tensile strength (typically 0.4–0.8 MPa)49, making this interface a primary initiation site for bottom-up fatigue cracking (which can propagate to the surface). Comparison with experimental data from accelerated pavement testing showed good agreement in the identified critical tensile zones50. This result identifies a key weakness in conventional semi-rigid base structures under combined thermo-mechanical loading, suggesting design improvements should prioritize enhancing the base’s bottom tensile resistance.Fig. 9Horizontal stress variation with pavement depth.

Horizontal stress variation with pavement depth.

Shear stress

The maximum shear stress of 0.16 MPa was found in the middle asphalt layer (Fig. 10). The distribution on the horizontal plane showed an alternating pattern along the direction of travel. This alternating shear stress, combined with low-temperature-induced brittleness, accelerates shear-driven failures such as rutting and pushing. The concentration of shear stress in the middle layer provides a mechanistic explanation for the observed phenomenon of middle-layer shear failure in rutting51, guiding future material design to prioritize shear resistance in intermediate asphalt layers. The shear stress distribution contours (Fig. 10) provide further insight into the interfacial mechanical response, particularly in the critical XZ plane where shear-driven damage mechanisms operate. The alternating positive and negative shear stress pattern along the direction of travel creates a reversing shear action that progressively weakens the material structure. The concentration of maximum shear stress (0.16 MPa) in the middle asphalt layer, rather than at the surface, indicates that shear failure initiates subsurface and propagates upward, explaining the common observation of middle-layer shear flow in rutting failure.Fig. 10Peak shear stress (XZ plane) values at different depths within the pavement structure.

Strain and displacement response

Vertical strain

Vertical strain at the bottom of asphalt layers alternated between tension and compression as the wheel load passed (Table 4). Under low temperatures—where asphalt’s stress relaxation capacity is compromised—this cyclic loading drastically shortens pavement fatigue life. Vertical displacement

The load induced a basin-shaped deformation, with a maximum surface displacement of 0.596 mm (Fig. 12, Table 5). Displacement decreased non-linearly with depth, highlighting the semi-rigid base’s role in load distribution. However, low temperatures inhibit asphalt’s viscoelastic recovery, leading to permanent deformation (rutting) accumulation under repeated heavy loading. The small elastic displacement observed does not preclude long-term rutting, as plastic strain accumulation under repeated loading is the primary rutting mechanism. Engineering implications and design recommendations

The findings of this study provide several actionable insights for the design and maintenance of asphalt pavements in cold regions subjected to heavy traffic. Based on the identified critical stress and strain states:Surface layers should incorporate modified asphalt binders (SBS-modified) or fiber reinforcement to enhance tensile resistance and mitigate thermal cracking, particularly given the high surface thermal stress (8.01 MPa) identified in the analysis.Intermediate asphalt layers require high shear resistance to prevent middle-layer shear flow, which can be achieved through optimized aggregate gradation and polymer modification, addressing the maximum shear stress (0.16 MPa) concentration in this layer.Semi-rigid base layers should be designed to withstand tensile stress at the bottom, potentially by using lean concrete or fiber-reinforced cement-stabilized materials, given the identified tensile vulnerability (0.12 MPa) at the base bottom.Pavement design specifications should incorporate thermo-mechanical coupling effects and dynamic load simulations to more accurately predict fatigue and thermal cracking life, moving beyond traditional static analysis methods.

Conclusion

This study employed a sophisticated thermo-mechanical FE framework to quantitatively investigate the coupled effects of low temperature and heavy dynamic loads on semi-rigid asphalt pavement, revealing several unique insights beyond common knowledge:The dynamic load from heavy vehicles increases significantly with speed, generating complex stress states that far exceed those predicted by static load models. Furthermore, the simulation captured the critical stress reversal (compression to tension) at the bottom of asphalt layers under a moving load, a phenomenon largely overlooked in static analysis but crucial for fatigue damage assessment.Low-temperature conditioning induces substantial thermal tensile stress (up to 8.01 MPa) at the pavement surface, which exceeds the typical tensile strength of conventional asphalt mixtures and is the primary initiator of transverse cracking. More importantly, the thermal stress gradient through the pavement depth was quantified, providing a clear mechanism for top-down cracking initiation.The moving load creates a critical triaxial stress state. The bottom of the asphalt layers is susceptible to tensile strain-induced fatigue, while the bottom of the semi-rigid base is vulnerable to tensile stress, identifying these as two key locations for damage initiation. The identification of the base layer as a critical tension zone under combined loading is a significant finding, suggesting that base material selection is as important as surface material for overall pavement durability in cold regions.The semi-rigid base is effective in reducing vertical deformation but is itself prone to fatigue cracking under repeated tensile stress. The middle asphalt layer was identified as the critical zone for shear stress concentration (0.16 MPa), directly linking simulation results to the common failure mode of middle-layer shear flow in rutting.

The finite element model assumed perfect bonding between layers and idealized material behavior, which may not fully capture the progressive damage and interface degradation under real environmental and loading conditions. Additionally, the vehicle-pavement interaction was simulated using a prescribed road profile without considering real-time feedback between the vehicle dynamics and pavement response. Most notably, the viscoelastic behavior of asphalt layers was not explicitly modeled, which could affect the accuracy of long-term deformation predictions and the relaxation of thermal stresses, as the time-dependent characteristics of asphalt are crucial under cyclic loading and varying temperatures.
