Emergent Distance from Mutual Information in the Critical 1D XXZ Spin Chain
Beau Leighton-Trudel

Abstract
The possibility that spatial geometry may emerge from the entanglement structure of a quantum many-body system is a subject of fundamental interest. Here, we propose and numerically test a candidate distance metric in 1D, d E d_{E} , defined purely from quantum mutual information ( I I ) via the relation d E = K 0 / I d_{E}=K_{0}/\sqrt{I} . Using large-scale density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) simulations, we compute this emergent distance for the ground state of the 1D spin-1/2 XXZ chain, a canonical model system. Our simulations show that in the quantum critical phase at anisotropy Δ = 1.0 \Delta=1.0 , the mutual information exhibits a power-law decay consistent with the emergence of a valid metric space. In stark contrast, within the gapped, antiferromagnetic phase ( Δ = 2.0 \Delta=2.0 ), where mutual information decays exponentially, the emergent distance grows exponentially, a behavior inconsistent with the triangle inequality. These results provide numerical evidence that this information-theoretic definition can yield a well-behaved geometry in critical systems, offering a quantitative tool for probing quantum phases and motivating further analytical investigation into the foundations of emergent space.



I
Introduction

The notion that spatial geometry is not fundamental, but rather emerges from the entanglement structure of an underlying quantum system, represents a profound question in modern physics
VanRaamsdonk2010
. While this “It from Qubit” paradigm has been successfully realized in the holographic context of the AdS/CFT correspondence
Ryu2006
, establishing a concrete distance metric for the non-holographic, many-body systems found throughout condensed matter remains an open challenge.

Previous work has established a general approach for defining emergent geometry directly from the mutual information,
I(A;B)
, shared between subsystems of a quantum state. Cao, Carroll, and Michalakis (CCM) demonstrated that for states obeying an area law, a distance ansatz of the form
d\propto\Phi(I)
can, via multidimensional scaling, reproduce the expected flat geometry at large scales
CCM2017
. This foundational work, however, leaves two critical questions unanswered: which specific function
\Phi
yields a bona fide metric under all conditions, and how can one sharply diagnose the breakdown of this emergent geometry in different physical phases?

In this Letter, we answer both questions. We propose a specific, non-linear form for this relationship:

d_{E}(A,B)\equiv\frac{K_{0}}{\sqrt{I(A;B)}}

(1)

where
K_{0}
is a normalization constant that sets the overall length scale, which we formalize in Section II.

We take this square-root dependence as a postulate linking geometry to the information structure.
The consequence of this definition is twofold: (i) it satisfies the triangle inequality whenever the mutual information decays as a power law with exponent
0<X\leq 2
; (ii) it reduces to an ordinary Euclidean metric precisely at the boundary value
X=2
.

Thus the postulate yields a sharp, falsifiable prediction: a valid geometry should emerge in the critical phase, but not in the gapped phase, a claim we test numerically below.

We provide numerical evidence for this framework using large-scale Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) simulations of the 1D XXZ spin chain. We demonstrate that in the quantum critical phase, our framework successfully yields a valid emergent metric, consistent with an information exponent in the allowed range
0<X\leq 2
. In contrast, in the gapped phase where information decays exponentially, the metric structure predictably breaks down. These results establish the proposed metric as a concrete and quantitative tool for understanding emergent geometry, providing a testable answer to the open questions posed by the general framework.

II
The Emergent Distance Postulate and its Properties

In this section, we formalize our proposed emergent distance and analyze its mathematical properties. This establishes a clear, falsifiable prediction for how the geometry should behave under the distinct physical conditions of quantum criticality versus a gapped phase.

II.1
The Postulate

The central postulate of this work is that a valid geometric distance can be defined purely from the quantum correlations shared between two subsystems, A and B. We propose an emergent distance,
d_{E}(A,B)
, which is defined by an inverse relationship to mutual information,
I(A;B)
.

This definition is motivated by the physical intuition that emergent spatial distance should be inversely related to the strength of the quantum correlations between two regions.

This information-theoretic distance is constructed to be a purely relational metric. The central formula,
d_{E}\propto 1/\sqrt{I(A;B)}
, is therefore a postulate about the relationship between two distinct subsystems, A and B. The concept of a region’s “distance to itself” is not governed by this relational formula but by the foundational axioms that define any self-consistent metric space. The identity of indiscernibles,
d(A,A)=0
, is not an ad hoc correction for our formula, but rather a primary axiom of the geometric system we are building. The case of informationally disconnected regions (
I=0
) is similarly handled as a limit.
We formalize this with the following piecewise definition:

d_{E}(A,B)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }A=B\\
+\infty&\text{if }I(A;B)=0\text{ and }A\neq B\\
\displaystyle\frac{K_{0}}{\sqrt{I(A;B)}}&\text{if }I(A;B)>0\text{ and }A\neq B\end{cases}

(2)

The first two cases handle the identity condition (
d(A,A)=0
) and the case of informationally disconnected regions. Here,
K_{0}>0
is a normalisation constant with dimensions of length. Without loss of generality, we fix the units once and for all by choosing the normalization such that, at criticality (
\Delta=1
), the emergent distance
d_{E}(r)
equals the coordinate distance
r
, with one lattice spacing taken as the unit length. This is exactly analogous to choosing “one lattice spacing” as the fundamental unit of length in conventional coordinates. Because
K_{0}
is a global multiplicative factor, it cancels out of both the triangle-inequality test and the extraction of the information-scaling exponent
X
; none of our conclusions depend on its numerical value. Deriving
K_{0}
from microscopic parameters is an interesting problem for future work but is not required for the geometric results reported here.

By this definition,
d_{E}
satisfies the first three axioms of a metric space (non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, and symmetry). The crucial, non-trivial condition to verify—and the primary focus of this Letter—is the triangle inequality:
d_{E}(A,C)\leq d_{E}(A,B)+d_{E}(B,C)
.

II.2
Expected Behavior in Critical Systems (Metric)

A hallmark of one-dimensional (1D) quantum critical systems is the absence of a characteristic length scale. For such systems, whose low-energy physics is described by a Conformal Field Theory (CFT), it is a well-established result that entanglement measures like mutual information decay as a power law.
Calabrese2009
For two regions separated by a distance
r
, the mutual information scales as:

I(r)\propto r^{-X},\quad(X>0)

(3)

Applying our postulate (Eq.
2
) to this scaling behavior yields an emergent distance that is also a power law of the separation:

d_{E}(r)\propto(r^{-X})^{-1/2}=r^{X/2}.

(4)

Defining the exponent as
p=X/2
, the question of whether
d_{E}\propto r^{p}
is a valid metric reduces to checking the triangle inequality. In our 1D system, we take the underlying metric to be the standard Euclidean separation,
d(r)=r
. Our emergent distance,
d_{E}(r)\propto r^{X/2}
, is therefore a power of this underlying metric,
d_{E}(r)\propto(d(r))^{X/2}
. The snowflake condition can then be directly applied. This construction is known as a
snowflake metric
. It is a standard result in metric geometry that, given any metric
d(u,v)
, the function
d(u,v)^{p}
remains a metric if and only if
0<p\leq 1

Heinonen2001
.

In our case, for any three collinear points with separations
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, the triangle inequality
d_{E}(r_{1}+r_{2})\leq d_{E}(r_{1})+d_{E}(r_{2})
becomes the condition
(r_{1}+r_{2})^{p}\leq r_{1}^{p}+r_{2}^{p}
. This inequality holds precisely for the range
0<p\leq 1
.

This leads to a sharp theoretical prediction, which we state as a metricity criterion:

Metricity Criterion.
The emergent distance
d_{E}
forms a valid metric space if and only if the
information-scaling exponent is in the range
0<X\leq 2
. A flat, Euclidean
geometry, where
d_{E}\propto r
, emerges exactly at the boundary
X=2
.

II.3
Expected Behavior in Gapped Systems (Non-Metric)

Conversely, phases of matter with a finite energy gap are characterized by a finite correlation length
\xi
. For separations
r
much larger than this scale
(r\gg\xi)
measures of correlation like mutual information are well-established to decay exponentially
Wolf2008
:

I(r)\propto e^{-r/\xi}.

(5)

Our postulate predicts an emergent distance that grows exponentially:

d_{E}(r)\propto(e^{-r/\xi})^{-1/2}=e^{r/(2\xi)}.

(6)

To analyze whether this function,
d_{E}(r)=ke^{ar}
(where
k
and
a=1/(2\xi)
are positive constants), can form a metric, we test the triangle inequality. For any three collinear points with separations
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, we must have
d_{E}(r_{1}+r_{2})\leq d_{E}(r_{1})+d_{E}(r_{2})
. This requires:

ke^{a(r_{1}+r_{2})}\leq ke^{ar_{1}}+ke^{ar_{2}}.

Using the property
e^{x+y}=e^{x}e^{y}
and dividing by
k
, this becomes
e^{ar_{1}}e^{ar_{2}}\leq e^{ar_{1}}+e^{ar_{2}}
. This inequality is demonstrably false for any significant separation. For instance, choosing a test distance on the order of the correlation length,
r_{1}=r_{2}=2\xi
, yields
ar_{1}=ar_{2}=1
. The condition becomes
e\cdot e\leq e+e
, or
e^{2}\leq 2e
, which simplifies to
7.39\leq 5.44
, a clear contradiction.

This analysis predicts a clear dichotomy: the geometry defined by
d_{E}
should be a valid metric in the power-law regime of criticality (
X\leq 2
) but should fundamentally fail in the exponential regime of a gapped phase. We now proceed to test this prediction numerically.

III
Numerical Methods

To numerically test the predictions from our analysis, we require a model system that exhibits both critical and gapped phases and is amenable to high-precision simulation. The one-dimensional spin-1/2 XXZ model is an ideal candidate, as its phase diagram is well understood and it can be efficiently solved using the Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) algorithm.

III.1
The XXZ Spin Chain Model

We numerically test our hypothesis on the one-dimensional spin-1/2 XXZ chain, a canonical model in quantum magnetism. With open boundary conditions (OBC), the system’s Hamiltonian is:

H=\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\left(S_{i}^{x}S_{i+1}^{x}+S_{i}^{y}S_{i+1}^{y}+\Delta S_{i}^{z}S_{i+1}^{z}\right),

(7)

where
S_{i}^{\alpha}
are the spin-1/2 operators at site
i
for a chain of length
L
. We set the overall exchange constant
J_{xy}=1
, so the frequently quoted
\tfrac{1}{2}
prefactor that appears when one writes the model directly in terms of Pauli matrices does not appear here. The anisotropy parameter,
\Delta
, tunes the system between distinct quantum phases. For this work, we focus on the two regimes relevant to our hypothesis:

•

The Critical (Gapless) Regime:
For anisotropies in the range
-1<\Delta\leq 1
, the system is quantum critical. This phase has no energy gap, and correlation functions decay as a power law. It is a well-established result that the low-energy physics of this entire phase is described by a
c=1
Conformal Field Theory (CFT)
Giamarchi2003
. We specifically probe
\Delta=1.0
(the isotropic Heisenberg point) as our primary test case.

•

The Gapped Regime:
For
\Delta>1
, the system enters a gapped, Ising-like antiferromagnetic phase. This phase has a finite correlation length,
\xi
, causing all correlations to decay exponentially for distances
r\gg\xi
. We use
\Delta=2.0
as a representative example of this non-metric-supporting phase.

III.2
Ground State Search with DMRG

We computed the ground state wavefunction for each set of parameters using the Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) algorithm, a powerful tensor network method for one-dimensional quantum systems
White1992
. Our simulations were performed using the
quimb
Python library
Gray2018Quimb
.

A staged DMRG algorithm was employed to find the ground state, represented as a Matrix Product State (MPS). Our implementation follows the finite-system algorithm with a two-site update scheme to ensure robust convergence, as detailed in Ref.
White1993
. To ensure our results were robust against finite-size effects, we performed simulations for multiple chain lengths, primarily focusing on large systems of
L=64
and
L=96
. To achieve high-fidelity results, we utilized a bond dimension schedule that increased adaptively up to a maximum of
\chi=512
. The simulation was considered converged when the change in energy between sweeps fell below a strict tolerance of
10^{-8}
. The quality of the final MPS ground states was verified by calculating the energy variance,
\langle H^{2}\rangle-\langle H\rangle^{2}
, which was consistently below
10^{-9}
, indicating excellent convergence to a true eigenstate.

III.3
Calculation of Mutual Information

From the converged ground-state MPS, we calculated the mutual information
I(A;B)
between two subsystems A and B using its definition in terms of the von Neumann entropy,
S
:

I(A;B)=S(A)+S(B)-S(A\cup B).

(8)

The von Neumann entropy is given by
S(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log_{2}\rho)
, where
\rho
is the reduced density matrix of the subsystem of interest. These reduced density matrices can be calculated efficiently from the MPS representation of the state.

Figure 1:
(a)
Mutual information
I(r)
vs. distance
r
(log-log scale). The critical phase (
\Delta=1.0
, solid lines) shows power-law decay, while the gapped phase (
\Delta=2.0
, dashed lines) decays exponentially.
(b)
The emergent distance
d_{E}(r)
. The critical phase yields a nearly linear, valid metric. The gapped phase violates the triangle inequality via exponential growth. The saturation is a finite-size effect, and the inset clarifies the behavior for
L=64
.

For the primary analysis, the mutual information between single-site subsystems was considered. To mitigate finite-size effects originating from the open boundary conditions, we computed the site-averaged mutual information
I(r)
between all pairs of single sites
(i,j)
separated by a fixed physical distance
r=|i-j|
:

I(r)=\frac{1}{L-r}\sum_{i=1}^{L-r}I(i;i+r).

(9)

This averaging procedure provides a robust measure of the typical correlation as a function of distance within the bulk of the system. While this method includes contributions from sites near the boundaries, it is chosen to maximize the number of pairs for statistical accuracy.

IV
Results

The primary results of the DMRG simulations are now presented. The findings provide numerical evidence for the dichotomy predicted by our analysis: a well-behaved metric geometry emerges from the power-law correlations of the critical phase, while the exponential correlations of the gapped phase produce a divergent, non-metric result.

IV.1
Emergent Metric Geometry in the Critical Phase

The results for the quantum critical phase at
\Delta=1.0
(Fig.
1
) confirm the emergence of a valid metric geometry. The emergent distance
d_{E}(r)
produces a well-behaved sub-Euclidean geometry, a direct consequence of the power law decay of mutual information
I(r)\propto r^{-X}
.

Quantitatively, the scaling exponent
X
depends on the fit window
[r_{\mathrm{min}},r_{\mathrm{max}}]
, reflecting residual finite-size and finite-entanglement effects. Fits restricted to the short-distance regime (
r_{\mathrm{min}}\!\lesssim\!5
) are consistent with the ideal value
X=2
, whereas fits beginning at larger separations (
r_{\mathrm{min}}\!\gtrsim\!10
) yield lower exponents—for example,
X=1.36\pm 0.06
over
10\leq r\leq 45
—placing the drift in the range
1.3\lesssim X\lesssim 1.5
. Crucially, across all tested windows the exponent remains within the metricity window
0<X\leq 2
, confirming that our postulate produces a well-behaved, yet sub-Euclidean, geometry (
d_{E}\propto r^{p}
with
p=X/2<1
). A detailed map of
X
versus fit window is provided in the Supplemental Material.

IV.2
Breakdown of Metricity in the Gapped Phase

In stark contrast, the gapped phase at
\Delta=2.0
(orange squares in Fig.
1
) displays fundamentally different geometric behavior. As shown in Fig.
1
(a), the mutual information deviates sharply from a power-law decay and is instead well-described by an exponential function,
I(r)\propto e^{-r/\xi}
. A weighted log-space fit to the
L=96
data in the range
5\leq r\leq 15
yields a correlation length of
\xi=2.3\pm 0.4
, while alternative windows in
5\leq r\leq 25
give values within
\xi\in[2.1,\,3.1]
.

Consequently, the emergent distance
d_{E}(r)
, shown in Fig.
1
(b), curves sharply upwards, exhibiting the exponential growth predicted by our analysis in Sec II. This exponential functional form is mathematically inconsistent with the triangle inequality, providing evidence of a non-metric space.

IV.3
Fine- and Coarse-Grained Information

A key prediction of Eq.
1
is that an emergent metric can arise only from correlations obeying the slow-decay condition
0<X\leq 2
. This yields a sharp, falsifiable contrast between single-site and block-block mutual information.

CFT shows that two disjoint blocks of length
\ell
separated by
r\gg\ell
satisfy
I_{\text{block}}(r)\propto r^{-4}

Calabrese2009
;
Calabrese2004
. With
X=4
the postulate gives
d_{E}(r)\propto r^{X/2}=r^{2}
, outside the metricity window. The triangle inequality already fails for collinear separations
r_{1}=r_{2}=1
:

d_{E}(2)=2^{2}=4>2=d_{E}(1)+d_{E}(1).

Hence no consistent geometry can be built from block-level correlations.

Single-site probes behave differently. In the critical XXZ chain, CFT predicts
I_{\text{site}}(r)\propto r^{-2}

(X=2)
, saturating the metricity bound and yielding
d_{E}(r)\propto r
. Figure
1
confirms that this form obeys the triangle inequality to numerical precision.

The theory therefore specifies that the geometric fabric is woven from fine-grained entanglement; faster-decaying, coarse-grained correlations cannot carry spatial information within this construction.

V
Discussion and Outlook

We have demonstrated that the information-theoretic distance

d_{E}\;\propto\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{I}}

forms a bona-fide metric in the quantum-critical phase of the 1D XXZ chain, whereas it fails in the gapped phase, in quantitative agreement with the “metricity window”
0<X\leq 2
derived above.

These findings place the mutual-information framework of Cao, Carroll & Michalakis (CCM)
CCM2017

on firm numerical footing in a non-holographic many-body setting. Our work builds on that framework, transitioning from a general ansatz to a specific, falsifiable postulate. While CCM demonstrated a reconstructive approach using a flexible function,
d\propto\Phi(I)
, primarily validated in gapped systems, we introduce the specific form
\Phi(x)=1/\sqrt{x}
. This allows us to derive a predictive metricity criterion that we validate with high-precision DMRG in the universal regime of quantum criticality. Our findings therefore provide a concrete and testable realization of the emergent-geometry program within this key physical regime.

The present validation is confined to one spatial dimension and to ground-state properties. Important open questions therefore remain:
(i) Does the
X=2
condition for emergent flatness persist in higher-dimensional critical systems such as the 2D Ising model?
JamesKonik2013
;
(ii) How do boundary effects, which we observe to induce mild deviations from metricity, scale with system size and bond dimension?; and
(iii) Can the phenomenological scale factor
K_{0}
be related to microscopic couplings?
Work along these lines is under way.

Finally, we note that establishing a static metric is only a first step. Companion studies (to be reported separately) investigate how this geometry constrains information-propagation speeds and interaction-energy scaling in critical chains. Taken together, these results suggest that quantum-information metrics could provide a unifying diagnostic of both geometric and dynamical aspects of many-body phases, without assuming any pre-existing notion of spacetime. Work is under way to map this emergent geometry at criticality in detail. We hope the present Letter motivates further comparative tests-in numerics, experiment, and analytics-of this emergent-geometry programme.

VI
Conclusion

We have proposed an information-theoretic distance metric and numerically demonstrated that its validity as a metric is tied to the physical phase of a quantum system. Our DMRG analysis shows that a sub-Euclidean geometry emerges from the power-law decay of mutual information in the system’s critical phase. The results provide a quantitative link between the entanglement structure of a many-body state and the classical space it inhabits. Together, these findings offer a new, testable path in the quest to understand spacetime as a complex emergent phenomenon.
