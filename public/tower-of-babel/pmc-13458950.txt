The pivotal role of the food service industry in alleviating urban segregation and boosting city vitality
Authors: Mingxing Chen, Yue Xian, Junfan Lin, Yuanxuan Yang, Alexis Comber, Liangkan Chen, Wenming Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban segregation challenges the sustainable and inclusive development of cities, yet little is known about how to address it effectively. This study examines how restaurants in Chengdu, China, a city renowned for its vibrant food culture, shape social interactions in daily life, aiming to uncover ways to promote greater social mixing. Drawing on 1.52 million cell phone records and information on 5,347 restaurants from Dianping.com, social isolation indices were measured across residential contexts and dining-out activities. The results show that the social isolation index during dining-out activities is, on average, 47.3% lower than in residential settings. Community restaurants with dense service coverage within 5.76 km demonstrate substantial potential to reduce social isolation. However, restaurant visits are highly unevenly distributed and concentrated in a limited number of neighborhoods in central and newly developed districts. Regression analyses reveal that restaurant-level social segregation is strongly negatively associated with customer flows, while service radius has a positive effect, in contrast, price level and cuisine type show no significant impact. These findings from Chengdu, China, provide a motivation and an analytical framework for future research in other contexts to examine how the food service industry may relate to social integration, including through factors such as affordability and transport accessibility.

Using 1.52 million mobility records and data on 5,347 restaurants in Chengdu, China, this study demonstrates that dining-out substantially reduces individuals’ experienced segregation compared with residential settings in the case study city. Community restaurants, particularly those serving diverse socioeconomic groups within an accessible service radius, show strong potential to foster social mixing. Findings from Chengdu, China, provide a motivation and an analytical framework for future studies in other contexts to examine whether and how food consumption activities contribute to social mixing.

Introduction

As articulated in the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, mitigating social segregation and fostering inclusive environments are essential for achieving sustainable communities. Social segregation is typically defined as the objective absence—or severe limitation—of social ties with others (1). Prior research has demonstrated that the demographic composition of residential neighborhoods reflects the increasing severity of social segregation in major metropolitan areas (2). Beyond its structural implications, social segregation exacerbates inequalities in domains, such as gender, education, and healthcare (3–5), and contributes to the fragmentation of urban societies (6). Outdoor social activities have increasingly become an integral part of urban residents’ daily lives (7). Social segregation is not only associated with the residential environment but also shaped by the types of places people visit and the modes of social interaction they engage in (8, 9). For a long time, a prevailing “cosmopolitan mixing hypothesis” has suggested that population diversity, spatial density, and convenient transportation in large cities should facilitate cross-group encounters and thereby reduce socioeconomic segregation (10). However, empirical studies have shown that the actual diversity of groups encountered by urban residents in their daily mobility and travel patterns is lower than commonly assumed (11). Such segregation is reinforced by the homogeneity of social networks (12) and the triadic closure principle (13) (if two nodes are both linked to a third, they are likely to form a direct connection, creating a closed network), which further entrenches segregation within social networks (12, 14). Both developed and developing countries are increasingly facing the challenges of growing urban inequality (2, 15, 16). To address this, sustainable urban planning prioritizes the creation and enhancement of social hubs that encourage interaction among diverse groups, such as parks, markets, exhibitions, shopping centers, and restaurants. By optimizing these spaces to promote social mixing, we can foster more frequent interaction and connections between individuals from different socioeconomic backgrounds, thereby reducing social segregation in urban settings (11). This approach is crucial for building inclusive, diverse, and sustainable communities and cities.

Oldenburg and Brissett (17) proposed the concept of “third space” in 1982, referring to social places outside of home and work. These spaces can promote the gathering and accidental interaction of people from different social classes and are as important as places of residence and work. Studies have shown that residents from different social backgrounds will intersect at certain specific destinations (such as transportation hubs and commercial centers), which has an impact on segregation (18). In this context, we note that the food service industry has great potential as a gathering point for people. Dining consumption is a basic daily need for all socioeconomic groups (19). Studies have shown that streets with a high density of restaurants can promote interaction between social classes (20). By 2020, the proportion of dining out among Chinese households reached 46%, almost matching the proportion of dining at home (21). Food service facilities shape local tastes on a material level and imprint local culture through culinary concepts, customs, and techniques on bodies of customers, becoming an integral part of customer identity (22). Moreover, the presence of diverse and exotic restaurant types helps maintain connections to the hometowns of transient migrants and visitors (23). The function of food service spaces is no longer simply to provide food but rather to become potential “connection spaces” where people from different socioeconomic backgrounds converge in cities.

This research provides an empirical perspective for the study of urban social mixing. Extensive research has examined the location and spatial evolution of restaurants, but in-depth analysis of whether and how restaurants, as specific venues, foster substantive cross-group encounters is lacking. This study aims to uncover the potential role and characteristics of restaurant spaces in promoting integration across socioeconomic groups. Chengdu was chosen as a case study because it is a leading Sichuan cuisine hub and boasts a thriving restaurant industry. In 2010, Chengdu was awarded the title of “City of Gastronomy” by UNESCO, becoming the only city in China to receive this honor. Chengdu's permanent population reached 21.19 million in 2021, indicating a high level of urbanization. Chengdu's population density is ∼13,500 people per square kilometer, with some areas reaching 40,000 people per square kilometer. Our research highlights the potential of the restaurant industry to alleviate urban social segregation and explores strategies to enhance its positive impact.

Current measures of social segregation focus on capturing the actual (or absent) physical interactions between individuals, that is, the exposure index of individuals in different groups (24), which is closer to the essence of social segregation. Shifting the measurement of segregation from the static spatial distribution of residential groups to dynamic interpersonal encounters. Residential group composition is understood as one of the scenarios of social segregation, and changes in social segregation under multiple activity scenarios, such as work, daily, and irregular activities, should also be considered (25). These measures are called “experiential segregation.” Although the expression of “individual social exposure” is intuitive, it is almost impossible to confirm that two individuals in the same field interact. Fortunately, it has been found that even short, accidental encounters between different income groups can have a positive effect on social integration, such as increasing people's emotional support for wealth redistribution (26). A study based on Twitter geolocation data found (7) that different races and income groups showed high consistency in overall travel radius and number of destinations, but there were significant differences in the group composition of the communities they visited. Therefore, in past practice, the method of measuring empirical segregation was mainly to calculate the exposure index of the places visited by individuals and obtain it by weighted average (24, 25).

We first defined the social segregation index (SSI) at two levels: (i) the venue level, including residential and restaurant places, and (ii) the individual level, capturing segregation experienced through daily mobility. The essence of the SSI refers to the degree to which the structure of different social groups in the place deviates from the uniform distribution (8). When calculating the SSI of the place of residence, we added the influence of the neighboring community, given that the interaction between the boundary groups of the neighboring residential areas may decay with distance. Restaurants are regarded as independent closed units, and no spatial spillover effects are added. Personal contact is usually achieved through frequent contact in shared spaces (27). The individual travel SSI is the segregation index of each restaurant visited by the individual, which is the result of a weighted average according to the frequency of visit. Individual socioeconomic status (SES) is estimated by residential rent or house price. Our study used residential cell phone signal data and housing prices covering 4,031,858 people to determine their social experiences (SES) and calculate residential social segregation for each neighborhood. Within this sample, 889,000 users had cell phone signal data associated with restaurant visits, which we used to assess their social segregation when dining out. We compared the levels of social segregation experienced by individuals during dining-out and residential activities and further examined the variations in restaurant-level segregation and their potential to enhance the benefits of social mixing.

Results

Reduced socioeconomic segregation in dining contexts compared with residential settings

We matched individuals’ residential locations, identified from mobile data, with the nearest housing price data points to capture their housing values, which were then used to approximate SES. Considering the consistency of results across different groupings (Supplement S3), we ultimately selected n = 7 as the basis for classification. The seven groups were numbered SES1–SES7 according to average land price, from lowest to highest. We calculated the SSI for both residential areas (at the street level) and restaurants (treated as enclosed point locations) (Fig. 1A). At this fine-grained scale, the SSI of restaurants showed no evident correlation with that of residential communities. Two restaurants located on the same block can exhibit substantial differences in their venue-level social segregation. We further compared the differences in SSIs across individual activities in different contexts (Fig. 1B). Regardless of SES group, the boxplots of individual SSIs during dining-out activities, SSIi(rsti), were consistently and significantly lower than those observed in residential contexts SSIi(nbi). Within-group comparisons revealed substantial heterogeneity in residential-based social segregation (with an average coefficient of variation of 20.7), particularly among individuals in the middle SES groups. However, such heterogeneity diminished during dining-out activities, where the variation converged across groups (average coefficient of variation of 19.2). Moreover, the average levels of dining-out social isolation differed only marginally across SES groups, suggesting that dining-out spaces exhibit a consistent social structure irrespective of socioeconomic background.

To further quantify this relationship, we computed the standardized relative deviation of dining-out versus residential social segregation for each individual: Ii=SSIi(rsti)−SSIi(nbi)SSIi(nbi). A positive Ii indicates that individual i experiences greater segregation when dining out compared with at home, whereas a negative Ii indicates reduced segregation in dining-out contexts. When the number of SES groups exceeded six, the average relative deviation of dining-out versus residential segregation stabilized (Fig. 1C). Based on the chosen classification (n = 7), we found that individuals’ dining-out SSIs were, on average, 47.3% lower than their residential counterparts (95% CI = 47.2–47.3). Irrespective of whether individuals reside in segregated or diverse communities, their dining-out experiences are consistently characterized by markedly reduced levels of social segregation.

Social segregation patterns across restaurant types

Restaurants were classified based on three dimensions: cuisine type, service format, and per capita spending, and we compared the distribution characteristics of SSIs across different categories (Fig. 1D). The 25th percentile (Q0.25), median (Q0.50), and 75th percentile (Q0.75) of the overall sample were used as reference benchmarks. The results show that when per capita spending is below 100 RMB, the distribution of SSIs (Q0.25, Q0.50, and Q0.75) for full-service restaurants such as hotpot, regional Chinese cuisine, Southeast Asian cuisine, as well as for beverage-focused establishments, is generally slightly below the overall sample level, whereas fast-food restaurants tend to have relatively higher segregation indices. In contrast, when per capita spending exceeds 300 RMB, restaurant types are mainly full-service, and their social segregation levels are significantly lower than the overall benchmark. Considering consumer behavior patterns, this may reflect that high-end restaurants promote broader spatial exposure of high-SES groups, thereby reducing their social segregation.

Spatial scale differences in attracting socioeconomic groups

We further examined the spatial scale at which restaurants attract patrons to investigate how they draw individuals of different SESs—whether primarily from nearby neighborhoods or across a broader area. To quantify this, we used the dense service radius r_{0.75}, defined as the minimum radius covering 75% of a restaurant's visitors, representing its main service area. Initially, as the dense service radius increases, a restaurant's SSI decreases significantly. However, beyond a certain distance, further expansion of the service radius is associated with a slight increase in social segregation. Piecewise linear regression identified a significant breakpoint ψ^=5.76 (SE = 0.10, 95% CI = 5.56–5.95), indicating a clear change in slope. Before the breakpoint, social segregation decreases with expanding service radius (β = −0.06, P < 0.001), suggesting that for restaurants serving nearby communities, enlarging the service radius substantially enhances the socioeconomic diversity of patrons. Beyond ψ^, the slope becomes positive (β = 0.002, t = 25.87), indicating diminishing marginal benefits for social mixing; at distances exceeding 10 km, social segregation may even increase.

Spatially, restaurants with a dense service radius below 2 km are evenly distributed along urban streets, resembling neighborhood restaurants (Fig. 2B). In contrast, restaurants with larger dense service radius, sometimes exceeding 12 km, are concentrated in city centers or dispersed across high-rent districts, resembling destination-type restaurants. These findings suggest that neighborhood restaurants have greater potential to foster spatial social mixing.

For individuals, the social segregation experienced varies across SES groups depending on the distance traveled for dining (Fig. 2C). For SES1, the lowest socioeconomic group, the farther they travel to dine, the lower their average social segregation. For middle SES groups (SES2–SES5), visiting restaurants located 5–10 km away allows them to encounter a more diverse set of social groups. In contrast, for high-SES individuals (SES6 and SES7), dining nearby rather than traveling long distances increases their exposure to individuals from other groups.

Concentration of restaurant customer flow

Ideally, if community restaurants could attract a broader range of patrons, social exposure across different socioeconomic classes would be effectively enhanced. However, the spatial distribution of restaurant visits is highly clustered. The tail index of customer visits (α ≈ 0.39) indicates extremely heavy tails, and the Q95/Q50 ratio reaches 126.5, reflecting that customer flows are concentrated among a small subset of restaurants. The top 20% of restaurants account for 93.1% of total visitor traffic. Using the Moran's I index of restaurant flow, we identified distinct clustering patterns (Fig. 3A). High-traffic restaurants are concentrated in the city center and southern new districts, which feature integrated service industries, commercial pedestrian streets, and large office buildings. Low-traffic restaurants cluster in peripheral urban areas, where resident activity is limited. In most intermediate areas, spatial correlations of customer flows among restaurants are not significant.

We examined factors contributing to variations in restaurant traffic, including user ratings of restaurant prices, SSI of surrounding neighborhoods, housing prices, population density, and urban facilities (e.g. shopping mall density, the distance to parks, and transportation hubs; Fig. 3B). SSI of restaurants exerts a strong negative effect on customer flow (P < 0.001); each 0.1-unit increase in social segregation is associated with a 9.9% decrease in visits. Restaurants offering broadly popular services attract higher traffic, whereas per capita consumption has no significant impact.

Each additional unit increase in a restaurant's dense service radius is associated with a significant 4.9% rise in visits (P < 0.001), and each logarithmic unit increase in the surrounding permanent population corresponds to a 10.9% increase in visits (P = 0.002). Among environmental and locational variables, average land price has a significant negative effect, with each unit increase reducing visits by ∼0.16% (P < 0.001). Similarly, distance to transportation hubs negatively affects visitation, with each unit increase reducing visits by 0.06% (P < 0.001), highlighting the importance of accessibility. In contrast, shopping mall density is positively associated with customer flow, with each unit increase corresponding to a 0.03% rise in visits (P < 0.001).

Discussion

As urban mobility and social interactions become increasingly important in fostering sustainable and inclusive societies, the diversity of group types in urban public activity spaces directly shapes individuals’ perceptions of the surrounding social atmosphere. Promoting the contact between different groups is regarded as a key approach to fostering social mixing. Existing studies have shown that restaurants may exhibit a relatively high degree of dispersion in terms of income segregation and service catchment areas, and that place type and service range have relatively limited the explanatory power for location-level income segregation (8). On the contrary, we find that, in out-of-home dining activities, the differences in social segregation across socioeconomic groups tend to converge. From the overall distribution of the SSIrst (Fig. 2A), the pattern appears relatively concentrated. Further analysis shows that the relationship between location-level income segregation and service range is not simply a weak correlation, but displays certain nonlinear characteristics. Taking Chengdu as an example, when the service radius of a restaurant is <5.76 km, social segregation decreases significantly as the service range expands; when the service radius exceeds this threshold, the relationship between the two becomes statistically insignificant (β = 0.002). This result indicates that moderately expanding the service range of community-based restaurants (e.g. those with an SSIrst <5.76 km in Chengdu) may help release the positive effects of dining activities in enhancing social diversity.

There is a positive correlation between visitation frequency (foot traffic) and socioeconomic diversity, which is consistent with the findings of Yabe et al. (28) in four large US cities. Conversely, no significant association is observed between the social mixing effects of dining activities and the preexisting level of social diversity at the community level. To some extent, this suggests that the contact opportunities generated by individuals’ cross-spatial mobility may be more pivotal for social mixing than the internal structure of communities. In this regard, the spatial concentration of high-traffic restaurants in areas with dense commercial activities and high transport accessibility may help explain why these venues are associated with higher levels of social mixing: such locations are more likely to function as shared urban destinations that attract consumers from different residential and socioeconomic backgrounds. Based on these findings, future research in other urban contexts could further examine whether improving accessibility to key food service nodes, such as street corners or streets with concentrated restaurant clusters (11), and encouraging the co-location of diverse business types (29), may strengthen the relationship between dining activities and social mixing. Restaurants with per capita spending above 300 RMB show significantly lower levels of social segregation than the overall benchmark. In Chengdu, these predominantly full-service restaurants may serve as destination venues that attract consumers from a broader range of residential neighborhoods, thereby increasing spatial exposure among high-SES groups. This interpretation aligns with mobility-based studies (30), which show that nonresidential activity spaces and cross-neighborhood mobility shape exposure to social diversity. However, higher price points may still exclude lower-SES groups; therefore, high-priced restaurants should not be viewed as inherently inclusive spaces. Instead, this finding underscores the importance of restaurant type and price point as key dimensions for research on consumption spaces and social mixing.

The role of Chengdu's food service industry in social integration may be partly shaped by the broader cultural context of Chinese cuisine, where affordability and the wide availability of familiar food types can facilitate shared dining experiences. This cultural context helps contextualize the observed patterns of social mixing in Chengdu. According to the 2025 China Food service Industry Ecology White Paper (31), per capita food service consumption in China has remained at ∼40 RMB since 2022. Low-cost restaurants (with per capita expenditure below 50 RMB) consistently account for around 61% of the market, whereas high-end restaurants (over 300 RMB per capita) represent <1% (32). The prevalence of affordable restaurants provides most consumers with diverse and accessible options. Meanwhile, collectivist values rooted in traditional Chinese culture foster a strong orientation toward group belonging and harmonious relationships (33). Consequently, the food service sector has become a preferred venue for social activities such as family gatherings, social interactions, and business negotiations (34).

This study was conducted in Chengdu, where everyday food culture could enable restaurants to serve as informal “third places” supporting social interactions (17). While these findings are informative, caution should be exercised when generalizing them to other cities, and future multicity or cross-cultural studies could further examine how contextual differences shape the observed patterns. This analysis of social integration relied on available data and did not include certain sociodemographic variables (e.g. education, occupation, age, and gender). Incorporating social surveys in future research could provide a more nuanced understanding of urban social patterns. In addition, social diversity at restaurant venues was estimated from visit frequency proportions of different SES groups, which may not fully capture the quality of interactions among visitors. Complementing quantitative mobility data with qualitative approaches, such as ethnographic observations or in-depth interviews, could enrich insights into how outing activities contribute to social mixing and support more context-sensitive urban planning and social policy formulation.

Materials and methods

Mobility data and attributes information

The data used in this study comes from passive collection of mobile phone signaling. The mobile phone data used in this study is not publicly accessible and can only be accessed securely through the data provider. To minimize potential bias from restaurant employees and delivery riders, we excluded users with excessively frequent visits to the same location. Over the 18-week observation period, users with >60 visits to a single location were considered likely employee to reflect occupationals rather than patron behavior and were removed. We analyze mobile phone signaling data from August to October 2021 (see Supplement S1). Dianping (https://www.dianping.com/chengdu) is one of the leading urban life consumption guide platforms in China, with >200 million monthly active users and >14 million merchants, covering >2,500 cities in China. Merchant categories include food service, hotel accommodation, shopping, and other services (e.g. hairdressing); Dianping provides information on categories, prices, locations, and user reviews. Using the nearest neighbor method, locations and restaurant prices were matched, and 5,347 restaurants in the city center of Chengdu were included in our study. House prices are obtained from Lianjia.com (https://cd.lianjia.com/), with a total of 86,154 residential house price data collected. We use the city's main roads, downloaded from OpenStreetMap, to divide the blocks into distinct residential communities.

Identifying social economic status

We use the housing price of an individual's residence as a proxy for SES. The residence that an individual visits most frequently over the 3 months is considered their primary residence, and the mobile phone signaling data are matched with the housing price data based on proximity. If multiple residences have the same visit frequency, the median housing price is calculated as the final value. The natural breakpoint method is used to divide SES into seven levels (see the robustness test in Supplement S3).

SSI of places

This paper constructs an SSI at two levels: (i) the venue level, including residential and restaurant places, and (ii) the individual level, capturing segregation experienced through daily mobility.

Venue-based social segregation index (SSIa)

The venue SSI is used to measure the degree of balance among different socioeconomic groups in a particular location. Referring to the framework proposed by Moro et al. (8), assuming that there are n socioeconomic groups in a city, for a given venue a (which can be a residential community or a restaurant), the occurrence ratio τa,g of each group g in the venue is calculated (the proportion of residents belonging to group g in the residential community and the proportion of visits to group g in the restaurant). Given that people's daily visit behaviors are normally consistent and repeated (35), we consider the proportion of visit frequencies by different groups to restaurants as representative of the social group structure. The ideal social mixing state is defined as all groups are evenly distributed in venue a, that is, τa,g=1n, in which case the segregation index is 0; if only one group appears, it is completely isolated, and the index is 1.
