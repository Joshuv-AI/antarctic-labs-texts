Collagen dynamics in the breast cancer tumor microenvironment and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Danping Wu, Lu Liu, Ying Jiang, Zhiwen Qian, Yilan You, Xin Ning, Feng Zhang, Yida Wang, Yan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast Cancer (BC) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies among women globally, with its pathogenesis and clinical progression being profoundly regulated by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Collagen, the most abundant extracellular matrix (ECM) component, plays multifaceted roles in shaping the TME of BC. Aberrant cllagen deposition, crosslinking, and remodeling alter tissue stiffness and architecture, driving tumor initiation, growth, invasion, and metastasis through integrin and discoidin domain receptor-mediated mechanosignaling, growth factor regulation, and metabolic reprogramming. Collagen rich stroma also acts as a physical and biochemical barrier that restricts T cell infiltration, promotes macrophage polarization, and impairs natural killer cell (NK) cytotoxicity, thereby facilitating immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. Specific collagen isoforms, including types I, III, V, VI, X, and XI, exhibit context-dependent tumor promoting or tumor restraining effects, underscoring the complexity of roles. Recent advances in targeting collagen biology, such as enzymatic degradation, inhibition of crosslinking enzymes, blockade of collagen receptors, and immune modulation strategies demonstrates promising preclinical results, with several approaches progressing to clinical trials. Future perspectives emphasize the integration of collagen targeting therapies with immunotherapy and chemotherapy, alongside the development of predictive biomarkers to stratify patients most likely to benefit. Collectively, collagen represents both a critical regulator of BC pathogenesis and a promising therapeutic target with significant translational potential.

Introduction

BC constitutes a leading global malignancy in women and represents a predominant cause of cancer-associated mortality worldwide. Extensive research has revealed that BC demonstrates organotropic metastatic heterogeneity, with preferential dissemination to the principal metastatic targets, including bone, lung, liver, and brain, resulting in substantial variations in patient prognosis and therapeutic response [1]. Beyond these metastatic traits, TME and its dynamic components, particularly collagen, have emerged as critical regulators of BC progression, invasion, and therapeutic resistance, which are key hallmarks of cancer that continue to pose major therapeutic challenges [2].

Collagen, as the most abundant ECM component in the breast TME, undergoes profound remodeling during BC development: abnormal collagen deposition, fiber alignment, and stiffness, providing a physical scaffold for tumor cell migration, also modulating signaling crosstalk between tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune cells, thereby shaping tumor aggressiveness and compromising treatment efficacy [3]. Established risk factors encompass prolonged endogenous estrogen exposure, proliferative benign breast pathology, elevated mammographic density, and positive familial history [4], with growing evidence suggesting that dysregulated collagen dynamics may further intersect with these risk factors to exacerbate disease progression.

In recent years, significant progress has been achieved in stem cell therapy, targeted therapy, ablation therapy, nanoparticles, natural antioxidants, radiology, chemodynamic therapy, sonodynamic therapy, and ferroptosis-based therapy in the field of cancer treatment. However, the war against cancer still swings between hope and despair [5], particularly due to the lack of strategies that integrate TME components like collagen into therapeutic design. Systems level analyses, such as multi-omics and single-cell sequencing, have emerged as powerful tools to address this gap by unraveling the complex interplay between TME remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and treatment resistance, helping link collagen remodeling to other critical layers of BC biology, including lipid metabolism and TME microenvironmental regulation, thereby contextualizing collagen’s role within the broader landscape of BC pathogenesis [6].

In 1970, Tamoxifen is important non-cytotoxic small molecule therapeutic agent, and it used for the treatment of BC. The success of Tamoxifen ushered in the era of molecularly targeted cancer therapy [7], particularly since 2023, advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have brought about remarkable changes in the fields of biological sciences and medicine, and AI in the biomedical field is entering the era of clinical trials [8]. Collagen: functions in normal BC and in cancer

Within TME, collagens emerge as the most abundant structural proteins whose dynamic reorganization critically modulates both TME topology and neoplastic progression. These fibrillar components serve as architectural scaffolds, also as biochemical reservoirs that providing essential metabolic substrates, growth-regulatory cues, and immunomodulatory signals [9]. The collagen family represents the most abundant protein group in the ECM, comprising over 20 distinct types identified to date. These collagens play fundamental roles in organizing fibrillar and microfibrillar networks within the ECM, contributing to basement membrane formation and maintaining structural integrity [10].

Collagen in normal breast tissue

A large number of in vitro and organoid studies have shown that the branch elongation of breast epithelium is highly dependent on the nonlinear/plastic mechanical response of the surrounding collagen network, supporting the “collagen-epithelium bidirectional interaction” model; meanwhile, collagen related signals and matrix stiffness jointly regulate the stemness and pluripotency of basal epithelium, indicating the coupled effect of mechanical cues and ligand signals in maintaining glandular phenotype [11, 12]. Spatio-temporal studies on embryonic branching dynamics have provided a framework for understanding how the normal ECM guides terminal bifurcation [13], the basement membrane, mainly composed of Collagen IV and laminin, is responsible for maintaining the bilayer structure and polarity of breast epithelium, the folding, hydroxylation, and assembly processes of these components in the endoplasmic reticulum are crucial for basement membrane homeostasis [14].

In vitro models further suggest that the synergistic ratio of type I/II/III/V collagen best supports the formation of epithelial acini, indicating that the physiological matrix is a functional integrator of multiple collagen combinations [15]. In addition, physiological events in adulthood (menstrual cycle, pregnancy, lactation, and involution) are accompanied by significant ECM and collagen remodeling and coupled with immune regulation. Under obese conditions, even in the absence of tumors, the upregulation of type I/III/VI/VII collagen can be induced, forming pro-tumorigenic matrix characteristics, clinical drugs can “soften” the breast ECM by reducing the orderliness of fibrillar collagen and tissue stiffness, suggesting that endocrine intervention can exert measurable effects on tissue mechanics [16, 17].

Collagen in BC

During the occurrence and progression of BC, the composition, microstructure, cross-linking status, and post-translational modifications of collagen undergo systematic reprogramming. These changes act as driving factors to remodel the tumor mechanical and immune micro-environments. This dense fibrillar collagen network promotes cancer progression and metastasis, and is associated with low survival rates in cancer patients [18]. In triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), differences in collagen architecture among different patient populations suggest that ECM heterogeneity may mediate variations in clinical phenotypes [19]. Notably, high mammo-graphic density reflects increased stromal collagen deposition and immune cell infiltration within the mammary epithelium [20]. Fibroglandular tissue (FGT), which comprises both connective and epithelial elements, demonstrates a strong correlation with BC risk, and its epithelial components show marked spatial colocalization with collagen rich regions. Quantitative analyses confirm the predominance of collagen in the ECM composition of FGT [21]. Overall, collagen alterations are both participants in tumor biological processes and potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets. However, their biological effects and therapeutic plasticity require stratified studies at higher resolutions to guide clinical translation, which is a pressing challenge to be addressed in the current application of collagen targeted cancer therapy.

Collagen structural and signaling changes

The ECM serves as both a structural scaffold and an active mediator of tumor growth, local invasion, and metastatic spread [22]. Emerging evidence highlights its regulatory role in BC progression and metastasis [23]. A hallmark of BC development is ECM stiffening, resulting from excessive collagen deposition and cross-linking, which profoundly influences tumor behavior [24]. Compared to normal tissue, tumor associated ECM demonstrates elevated collagen deposition and remodeling activity, generating increased denatured collagen strands [25]. These aberrant collagen architectures represent characteristic features of invasive cancers [26]. The dual roles of collagen I

Collagen I (COL1) is the most abundant fibrous matrix component in the breast stroma, and its content, cross-linking degree, and spatial configuration exert bidirectional effects on the occurrence and progression of breast cancer. A large body of evidence indicates that LOX mediated cross-linking leads to collagen network stiffening and increased anisotropy, which in turn promotes epithelial-mesenchymal phenotypic transition, stemness maintenance, migration and invasion, and therapeutic resistance through the integrin β1-FAK/ILK-YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction axis. Meanwhile, tumor-associated collagen signatures are closely associated with poor outcomes (Fig. 2a) [28–30].

On the other hand, COL1 also profoundly shapes the immune ecosystem, the extracellular domain of DDR1 on tumor cells can bind and align collagen fibers, forming a geometric barrier that excludes T cell infiltration. Neutralizing antibodies that block DDR1-collagen binding can disrupt fiber alignment, restore immune infiltration, and inhibit tumor growth in BC models [31, 32]. Stromal TEM8/ANTXR1 mediated collagen binding and internalization support solid tumor growth [33]. Relatively, under specific biophysical and molecular contexts, COL1 exhibits tumor suppressive potential: its 3D matrix can trigger programmed cell death in epithelial BC cells via DDR1-BIK an effect attenuated by collagen aging and glycosylation. Histologically, dense lamellar and cuff-like collagen around tumor nests is associated with restricted in situ expansion and better prognosis. From a mechanical perspective, spatially uniform stiffening can inhibit collective invasion, whereas focal and gradational stiffening, in contrast, enhances collective migration suggesting opposing regulatory effects of “stiffness magnitude” and “stiffness distribution” on outcomes [34–36].

Enzymatic crosslinking

Enzymatic crosslinking by LOX family, P4Hs, and lysyl hydroxylases is key to tumor ECM remodeling, impacting TNBC behaviors. LOXL4 (LOX family) modulates TNBC metastasis: its knockdown alters collagen-dependent ECM, enhancing TNBC growth and lung metastasis, highlighting LOX-mediated crosslinking’s role in restraining TNBC dissemination [36]. Collagen prolyl hydroxylation (CPH), the most prevalent post-translational modification in humans, is catalyzed by P4H in a vitamin C dependent manner. CPH induces global DNA and histone methylation alterations in both pluripotent stem cells and tumor cells, thereby promoting cell state transitions [37]. C-P4H1 (P4Hs) drives TNBC chemoresistance via stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1α), its ablation disrupts HIF-1α signaling, sensitizing TNBC to chemotherapy [38]. Collagen prolyl hydroxylases are essential for BC metastasis, underscoring P4Hs’ roles in resistance and metastasis [39].

Lysyl hydroxylase PLOD2 regulates TNBC epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity (EMP) and stemness: the PLOD2/succinate axis drives EMP and maintains cancer stem cell traits, while PLOD2 mediated hydroxylation modifies ECM collagen and intracellular metabolism, promoting self-renewal and therapy resistance [40]. Enzymatic crosslinking also modulates TNBC adhesion and migration via P4Hs/lysyl hydroxylase-controlled intrafibrillar bonemimetic collagen mineralization [41]. Collectively, LOX/P4H/lysyl hydroxylase mediated crosslinking regulates TNBC growth, metastasis, chemoresistance, EMP, and stemness, targeting these pathways holds promise for novel TNBC therapies.

Receptor-mediated signal transduction

DDRs are prominent collagen responsive receptors in breast cancer. DDR2, activated by COL1, stabilizes the EMT regulator SNAIL1 via Src-dependent ERK2 phosphorylation, and facilitating invasion through collagen-dense stroma [42], exhibits multifaceted roles, with its signaling linked to cell survival and metastatic dissemination across BC subtypes (Fig. 2b) [43].

Other collagens modulate BC via distinct receptor axes. The α3 chain of type V collagen interacts with GPC1, a cell surface proteoglycan, to enhance GPC1’s function as a co-receptor for FGF2. This interaction amplifies FGF2 mediated Ras/Raf/ERK signaling, promoting tumor cell proliferation. COL8α1 promotes aggressive BC phenotypes via FAK/Src activation (Fig. 2c) [44]. Collagen X Alpha 1 (COL10A1) binds integrin β1 (ITGB1) to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, its knockdown abrogates chronic stress-stimulated TNBC progression (Fig. 2d) [45]. Collagen XVIII forms a complex with ErbB receptors and α6-integrin, activating MAPK/AKT cascades to maintain CSC properties. Targeting collagen XVIII improves the efficacy of ErbB inhibitors, overcoming intrinsic drug resistance (Fig. 2e) [46].

Beyond collagen receptors, Src family kinases (SFKs) and MEK/ERK pathways control dormant tumor cell outgrowth. Combined SFK/MEK inhibition induces apoptosis of dormant cells, preventing metastatic recurrence [47]. Additionally, ANTXR1, a stem cell enriched receptor, connects collagen signaling to CSC maintenance and metastasis [48], while GPVI interacts with cancer cell-derived galectin-3 to promote metastatic dissemination (Fig. 2f) [49]. These receptor-mediated pathways highlight the TME’s role in BC pathogenesis. Disrupting collagen-receptor interactions or downstream cascades suppresses tumor progression and enhances drug efficacy, offering novel strategies to improve BC patient outcomes.

Mechanisms of tumorigenesis and tumor growth

BC initiation and progression are closely associated with the mechanical cues of TME and metabolic interactions involving collagen. Mechanosensitive hormone signaling contributes to mammary progenitor expansion, elevating BC risk, while local mechanical loading of collagen networks is a prerequisite for BC cell invasion through the ECM [50, 51]. The dynamic mechanical stresses within and surrounding tumors influence cancer cell behavior and independently regulate disease progression, where mechanical stress relaxation may promote cancer progression through enhanced proliferation and invasion Neoplastic cell behavior is profoundly regulated by microenvironmental cues, particularly ECM topology and biomechanical properties [52]. The cytoskeletal membrane linker srGAP2 transmits mechanical signals in TNBC, where matrix stiffness-induced srGAP2 tension gradients direct migratory persistence and promote cancer progression [53]. Our multi-omics analysis of 260 ancestry diverse breast tissues identified spatially regulated, proline-hydroxylated collagen domains in TNBC, where site-specific hydroxylation drives epithelial proliferation, chemotaxis, and signaling, revealing ancestry-associated PTM defined collagen matrices as novel regulators of TME spatial organization and clinical heterogeneity [54].

Invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition

Full-length collagen VI (COL6) emerges as a novel BC invasion driver, with stromal abundance correlating positively with patient body mass index. Mechanistically, COL6 promotes invasion through NG2-EGFR crosstalk and MAPK signaling activation [55]. Obesity induces mammary gland ECM enrichment with pro-invasive collagen IV and VI, predominantly secreted by stromal vascular fraction cells and adipose-derived stem and progenitor cells, which collectively enhance cancer cell invasiveness [56]. Maternal obesity-induced collagen linearity alterations in ECM promote invasive epithelial phenotypes [57]. Obese adipose stromal cells facilitate tumor invasion through matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) dependent collagen fiber contraction [58].

Membrane-anchored MMP14/MT1-MMP serves as the principal proteolytic mediator for both physiological branching morphogenesis and pathological carcinoma invasion in vivo [59]. CRISPR-mediated MMP8 knockout induces an MMP expression signature shift, characterized by pro-invasive MMP1 downregulation and antitumorigenic MMP8 upregulation, ultimately reducing invasive capacity and collagen degradation [60]. Cancer cell invasion into collagen-dense ECM requires membrane-tethered MT1-MMP as the dominant collagenolytic protease. The ARF6-JIP-MT1-MMP-dynein-dynactin-kinesin1 axis orchestrates this invasive program [61]. MMP13 exhibits dual mechanoregulatory roles in BC progression, mediating microenvironmental softening through collagen degradation to promote invasion while paradoxically driving stromal stiffening via CAF and tumor associated macrophages (TAM) crosstalk and aberrant matrix deposition, amplified through transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and Runx2 dependent feedforward loops with EMT and CSC populations [62]. Despite its context dependent anti-fibrotic and metastasis suppressive effects, MMP13 concurrently promotes angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming to elevate tumor thermogenesis, suggesting spatiotemporal targeting of its biomechanical duality could disrupt tumor stroma coevolution [62]. ECM stiffening, a hallmark of BC progression resulting from excessive collagen deposition and crosslinking, stimulates thrombospondin-1 (THBS1) enriched exosome secretion via YAP/TAZ pathway activation. THBS1 subsequently mediates stiffness-dependent effects through MMP and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) engagement [24].

Hyaluronic acid (HA) alters collagen network architecture and mechanics through CD44-β1 integrin crosstalk, reducing tumor cell traction force generation, transmission, and invasion, revealing CD44 as a potential therapeutic target in HA-rich ECM pathologies like BC and glioblastoma [63]. Elevated ECM stiffness promotes EMT and metastatic dissemination in BC through mechanosensitive EPHA2/LYN complex formation. Stiffness induced ligand independent EPHA2 phosphorylation recruits LYN kinase, which phosphorylates the EMT transcription factor TWIST1, releasing it from cytoplasmic G3BP2 sequestration to initiate nuclear translocation and EMT activation [64].

Collagen linearization, a pathological feature of aggressive malignancies, is directly induced by tumor cell secretomes. The matricellular protein WISP1 plays a pivotal role by binding COL1 to promote linearization both in vitro and in vivo, thereby facilitating invasion and metastasis [65]. Mechanical stress in dense microenvironments causes nuclear envelope rupture and TREX1-dependent chronic DNA damage, inducing partial EMT and tumor progression [66]. Recurrent nuclear envelope compromise promotes senescence in normal cells but enhances invasive phenotypes in BC cells, including increased collagenolytic activity [66].

Chemotherapy-treated patients and murine models exhibit significant collagen IV upregulation in residual tumors. Integrin α1/α2-mediated Src/FAK signaling activation drives subsequent invasion [22]. Aging-associated matrix-bound vesicles acquire pro-tumorigenic properties that modify tissue microenvironments [67]. The poor prognosis marker EFA6B triggers ECM interaction machinery reprogramming and CDC42-dependent collective invasion in collagen matrices [68]. During malignant progression, extensive ECM remodeling generates radially aligned collagen fibers that facilitate tumor invasion. Notably, various invasive BC cell subtypes demonstrate differential responses to these epitaxially organized collagen fibrils [69].

Hypoxia-driven collagen regulation

Hypoxia promotes EMT through HIF1α-driven induction of CTCF, which mediates chromatin remodeling, epigenetic modifications, and alternative splicing [70]. Under hypoxia, HIF1α-mediated epigenetic activation of ATF3 promotes EMT and cancer invasion by upregulating P4HA1 transcription and inducing exon 9a inclusion through TET1-recruited demethylation and subsequent CTCF-dependent RNA Pol II pausing, enhancing collagen deposition and metastatic potential [71]. Metastatic niche formation involves collagen hydroxylation driven by pyruvate-derived α-ketoglutarate, which activates P4HA to remodel lung ECM [72].

Hypoxia significantly enhances CTCF occupancy at EMT-related genes and identified a CTCF-mediated mechanism promoting EMT, wherein CTCF binding to the COL5A1 promoter drives its hypoxic upregulation while orchestrating exon 64 A inclusion through promoter-exon looping, DNA demethylation, and RNA Pol II pausing, ultimately facilitating collagen remodeling and EMT progression [70]. Hypoxia drives aggressive tumor behavior, metastasis, and treatment resistance. The hypoxic TME promotes immune evasion and maintains the cold tumor phenotype [73].

Metastatic lesion formation and stemness maintenance

The metastatic cascade initiates with local invasion and dissemination of cancer cells from primary tumors, representing the most lethal aspect of malignancy [74]. In bone metastases, tumor osteocyte interactions differentially modulate dopamine receptor signaling, suppressing migration while paradoxically enhancing proliferation [75]. Intriguingly, tumor cells demonstrate preferential dissemination into COL1 rich environments, with microenvironmental signals capable of inducing epithelial cell dissemination independent of COL1’s fibrillar structure or specific adhesion motifs [76]. H3K27 acetylation-activated COL6A1 facilitates osteosarcoma lung metastasis via STAT1 repression and pulmonary fibroblast activation (Fig. 2g) [77]. Levated ECM protein expression in circulating tumor cells suggests cancer cell-derived ECM may facilitate metastatic colonization [44]. Heat shock protein 47, a collagen chaperone upregulated during EMT, mediates CTC-platelet interactions through COL1, potentiating metastatic spread [78].

The mesenchymal stem cell secretome’s impact on BC reveals PREX1 and CCL28 upregulation coupled with collagen downregulation (COL1A1/3, COL3A1), collectively promoting ECM remodeling and enhanced cell migration potential [79]. BC cells traversing COL1 matrix interfaces experience asymmetric mechanical forces that induce nuclear mechano-transduction dysregulation (via emerin/YAP mislocalization) and elevated contractility, triggering a phenotypic switch towards increased aggressiveness characterized by altered migration patterns and chemosensitivity [80].

LOXL1 overexpression drives the characteristic stiff, collagen rich ECM in lobular BC, supporting robust metastatic dissemination [81]. CoL10A1 drives BC brain metastasis by promoting proliferation, migration, invasion, and blood-brain barrier penetration via EMT pathway regulation [82]. Integrin-mediated ECM binding, particularly αvβ3 upregulation in TNBC models, drives tumor-stromal angiogenic processes through mechano-transductive signaling [83]. PyMT-induced mammary carcinomas lacking TGF-β receptor II exhibit increased stiffness, fibrillar collagen, and LOX expression, correlating with enhanced metastasis [84]. Gut microbiome dysbiosis preceding tumor initiation accelerates HR+ BC dissemination, with peritumoral collagen levels correlating positively with mast cell infiltration and recurrence [85]. The expression patterns of COL10A1 and its associated gene networks reveal inflammatory and immunosuppressive microenvironments that characterize aggressive breast/pancreatic cancers and promote metastatic potential in a sex-dependent manner [86]. COL1 triggers a force-dependent Yap-mediated feed-forward loop in basal-like BC cells, independent of K14, which transcriptionally amplifies collagen alignment and tension to drive collective invasion through leader cell formation [87].

Spatial stiffness mapping reveals increasing rigidity from tumor core to periphery, with soft microenvironments activating YAP1/AP1 signaling while stiff regions promote angiogenesis via FN1/MMP9 upregulation [88]. During intravasation, aligned collagen fibers facilitate metastatic cell migration through the ECM, basement membrane penetration, and vascular entry [89]. TM4SF1 mediates metastatic reactivation by coupling DDR1 to syntenin-2/PKCα, activating JAK2/STAT3 signaling to maintain SOX2/NANOG expression and stem cell traits across multiple organs [90]. This non-canonical DDR1 pathway enables exploitation of ubiquitous COL1 for metastatic resurgence [90]. ANTXR1, a stem cell marker, binds collagen VIα3 fragment C5A to activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling through LRP6, GSK3α/β phosphorylation, linking collagen remodeling to stemness and metastasis [48].

Collagen-ion channel interaction

The role of collagen remodeling in tumor biology is not limited to structural support, the mechano-transduction it mediates may also converge with electrophysiological changes at multiple nodes, particularly manifested in the crosstalk regulation between ion channels and tumor metabolism. The α-subunits of voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs and NaV1.1–1.9.9) initiate and propagate action potentials in neurons and muscle cells, while the NaV β-subunits (β1–4) have been shown to regulate the properties of α-subunits and facilitate specific functional interactions [91]. Through pan-cancer analysis, Jackson’s laboratory first revealed the aberrant expression patterns of the VGSC family in various cancers, providing a pan-cancer-level theoretical basis for collagen-ion channel crosstalk [92], expression of sodium channelβ3 (SCN3B) is positively correlated with the presence of key immune cells and negatively correlated with several immune checkpoint inhibitors [93]. Further verification via experiments on C6 glioma cells demonstrated that SCN3B can regulate cell motility independently of channel activity, suggesting that collagen mediated mechanical signals may trigger downstream pathways through SCN3B rather than relying on traditional electrophysiological mechanisms. Studies have also found that traditional drugs may regulate ion channels in cancer, however, relatively few studies have explored the effects of traditional toxin-based drugs on ion channels in cancer [94]. Emerging studies in this field have revealed that the collagen microenvironment can regulate ion channel activity via mechanical signals, thereby affecting tumor cell plasticity, stem cell like transition, and immune evasion, and ultimately exacerbating drug resistance. A key question is whether combined targeting research on mechanical signals, ion channels, and traditional drugs can be strengthened to improve their clinical application.

Collagen and the tumor immune microenvironment

The tumor ecosystem comprises malignant cells and a highly organized, heterogeneous microenvironment. The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), characterized by its complexity and plasticity, plays established roles in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis [95, 96]. Specifically, extracellular vesicles derived from cancer cells act as mediators among surrounding cells, such as immune cells, fibroblasts, and adipocytes. They provide energy substrates for the stromal component of the TME and promote macromolecular biosynthesis, thereby facilitating tumor progression [97]. Collagen-mediated T cell regulation

Regulatory T cells exhibit strong prognostic significance in BC, with elevated T cells infiltration correlating with worse outcomes. Increased collagen deposition in metastatic BC promotes T cells biomarker expression, fostering an immunosuppressive TME [99]. Collagen density differentially regulates cellular proliferation, while suppressing T cell activity in dense stroma, it paradoxically maintains cancer cell proliferation unaffected [100].

Spatial analysis reveals a U-shaped distribution of CD8+ T cells in BC, decreasing from periphery toward tumor clusters before increasing again centrally [102]. Early stage T cells may paradoxically restrain BC invasion [103], while anti-TGF-β/PD-L1 bispecific antibodies enhance T cells infiltration and anti-tumor efficacy in BC [104].

Collagen-binding domain interleukin12 (CBD-IL-12) fusion protein demonstrates superior tumor localization and sustained IFN-γ induction compared to native interleukin12 (IL-12), reducing systemic toxicity while enhancing checkpoint inhibitor efficacy in immunologically “cold” tumors [105]. High collagen density generally suppresses CD8 + T cell infiltration and activity, COL6 restricts T cell function primarily through physical barrier effect [106]. COL6 deposition is closely associated with metabolic reprogramming of TAMs, indirectly inhibiting T cell function. A team from the University of California, San Francisco demonstrated that under synergistic stimulation with TGF-β and IL-4, TAMs secrete large amounts of COL6 while upregulating arginase 1 expression to deplete arginine in the TME [107].

CAFs constitute a major TME component that promotes tumor progression through excessive ECM deposition and stromal remodeling [108]. Their collagen rich ECM production represents a hallmark feature driving cancer invasiveness [109]. Spatial fibroblast organization mediates T cell exclusion via distinct CAF subpopulations:

Activated fibroblasts overexpress COL1 and fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP), a collagenolytic peptidase promoting tumor progression [111]. Proline metabolism, crucial for collagen synthesis, is upregulated in BC stroma via PYCR1 overexpression [109]. CAFs modulate TGF-β signaling to promote fibrotic ECM accumulation [112], with GREM1 expression disrupting BMP/SMAD signaling in cancer cells [113]. Myeloid-fibroblast crosstalk through TGFβ/CD73 enhances collagen deposition while reducing fibronectin, facilitating tumor progression [114].

Autophagic fibroblasts promote desmoplasia through COL1 deposition and tissue stiffening, creating a pro-tumorigenic niche that supports angiogenesis and immune evasion [115, 116]. CAFs exhibit functional dichotomy:

Monocyte-derived S100A9 + myeloid cells activate CAFs and CXCL16 secretion, driving BC invasiveness [118]. Integrin α11β1, which is upregulated during myofibroblast differentiation, mediates fibroblast-collagen interactions, and its deletion impairs tumor growth and collagen organization [119]. CXCL12-secreting fibroblasts promote invasion via RhoA/ROCK/MLC-2 and MAPK pathway activation [120].

Notably, cancer cells produce abnormal collagen I homotrimers (α1/α1/α1) through COL1A2 epigenetic silencing, promoting proliferation via α3β1 integrin signaling [121]. ATF4, a stress response regulator, controls collagen synthesis and amino acid metabolism, driving CAF activation and metastatic progression [122]. Collectively, collagen-CAF interactions establish a feed-forward loop that enhances tumor growth and therapy resistance through TGF-β and other pro-tumorigenic factors.

Macrophages in TME

TAMs play a pivotal role in stromal remodeling by driving collagen crosslinking and tissue stiffening, thereby facilitating BC invasion. Biochemical analyses reveal that lysyl hydroxylase and lysyl oxidase-mediated collagen crosslinking is most abundant in highly invasive human BC subtypes characterized by rigid stroma [123]. High tumor stroma percentage and an RNA-seq-derived stromal score (AEBP1, COL6A3, CTSK, PLAC9) correlate with poor immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) response in breast cancer, mediated by collagen rich stroma driving tumor aggressiveness and M2 macrophage polarization via stroma and macrophage interactions [124]. TAMs exhibit functional heterogeneity: while pro-inflammatory macrophages may exert tumor-suppressive effects, anti-inflammatory macrophages predominantly promote tumor progression [125].

In normal human breast tissue, CD206+ macrophages expressing SERPINH1, COL1, or MORC4 are predominant and correlate with favorable patient survival outcomes. In contrast, tumor tissues exhibit a relative increase in CD206− macrophages, which are associated with poor prognosis [125]. Mechanistically, TAMs impair CD8+ T cell function through TGF-β driven collagen biosynthesis, arginine depletion, proline synthesis, and ornithine secretion, collectively suppressing anti-tumor immunity via mechanometabolic reprogramming [107].

Notably, TAMs express FAP and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), both induced by IL-6 dependent innate regenerative responses. IL-6 directly upregulates HO-1 and enables FAP expression in collagen rich microenvironments, promoting transendothelial migration and metastatic dissemination [126]. Furthermore, cytokine CCL5 is upregulated following HER2 downregulation and persists in residual tumors, recruiting CCR5+ macrophages that drive collagen deposition and tumor recurrence [127].

In TNBC, COL5A1 overexpression induces IL-6 production, promoting M2 macrophage polarization. Subsequently, TGF-β secreted by M2 macrophages enhances chemoresistance via the TGF-β/Smad3/COL5A1 axis by stabilizing COL5A1 through suppression of K48-linked ubiquitination, while concurrently elevating IL-6 secretion [128].

Paracrine interactions between BC cells (BCCs) and macrophages-mediated by macrophage derived EGF and BCC-secreted CSF-1, facilitating BCC migration through collagen matrices [129]. Preclinical studies in diabetic BC models demonstrate that tumors develop pronounced stromal remodeling, aberrant collagen architecture, and increased M2 macrophage infiltration, accelerating metastatic spread [130].

Macrophage-derived MMPs critically regulate ECM degradation. Notably, HER2 targeting CAR macrophages activate CD147 signaling upon antigen recognition, significantly inhibiting HER2+ 4T1 tumor growth in murine models while increasing MMP expression, reducing collagen deposition, and enhancing T cell infiltration [131]. Collectively, TAMs orchestrate collagen remodeling through dichotomous mechanisms: M2-polarized macrophages promote collagen synthesis, while collagen degradation products may reinforce immunosuppressive polarization.

NK cell dysfunction in collagenous microenvironments

Collagen mineralization upregulates mucin-type O-glycosylation and sialylation on tumor cells, increasing glycan shield thickness and impairing NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against BC cells [132]. Bone-mimetic mineralized COL1 hydrogels suppress BC metastatic behavior by reducing proliferation, downregulating metastatic markers, inhibiting migration, and promoting apoptosis, revealing an inverse relationship between bone mineralization and metastatic potential while suggesting additional skeletal factors mediate bone tropism [133].

NK cells can secrete MMP-9 and MMP-14 to degrade collagen fibers and improve the physical barrier of the TME. In TNBC models, the combination of NK cells and radiotherapy can enhance the activity of MMP-9, promote collagen degradation, and increase the infiltration efficiency of NK cells [134]. The combination of anti-PD-1 antibodies with collagen-degrading agents can synergistically enhance NK cell activity. Studies have shown that the combination of collagen crosslinking inhibitors and anti-PD-1 antibodies can improve immune cell infiltration. In TNBC models, this combined therapy significantly increases the infiltration and cytotoxicity of NK cells by reducing collagen deposition and alleviating the immunosuppressive microenvironment [135].

Collagen as a predictive biomarker and its molecular subtypes

Alterations in collagen density within TME can modulate cancer cell metabolism. Increased deposition of COL1 elevates breast tissue density, raising BC risk by 4 to 6 fold [136]. As a ECM component, COL1A1 overexpression in TNBC serves as an independent prognostic factor [137]. At the transcriptional level, elevated expression of COL1, COL3, and fibrillin1 correlates with reduced overall survival and progression free survival [138]. Tumor-associated collagen signatures (TACS) function as robust prognostic indicators, enabling stratification of high risk BC patients [139]. TACS is an independent prognostic factor for breast cancer. Pooled analyses of clinical and cohort studies have shown that tumor associated collagen signatures exhibit robust associations with patient survival, metastatic risk, and therapeutic resistance. Incorporating collagen-related indicators such as TACS into pathological stratification can significantly improve prognostic discriminative power [140, 141].

Integration of a complete collagen signature into the TNM staging system improves BC prognosis by dynamically reclassifying patients and enhancing stratification accuracy, as evidenced by superior hazard ratios and AUC values across training and validation cohorts, establishing collagen biology as an independent predictor of tumor behavior [141]. Additionally, adenosine, an oncogenic metabolite, influences tumor behavior, immune evasion, and angiogenesis. Elevated A2B adenosine receptor expression correlates with reduced COL1 levels and poor clinical outcomes [114].

ECM-derived mechanical forces regulate BC cell proliferation and phenotypic plasticity by modulating tissue stiffness. The fibrinogen/fibronectin/vitronectin/elastin axis has emerged as a prognosticpredictor [142]. Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) enables noninvasive quantificate of tumor viscoelasticity, demonstrating a positive correlation with collagen content, thus serving as a sensitive imaging biomarker for collagen deposition and therapeutic response monitoring [143]. In invasive carcinomas, inflammatory cell localization and collagen fiber alignment are associated with adverse survival outcomes [144], while collagen organization predicts recurrence risk in ductal carcinoma in situ [145]. Collagen subtypes exhibit distinct prognostic implications: COL10A1 is upregulated across BC subtypes, with expression positively correlated with ER, PR, HER2 status, and lymph node involvement [146]. COL11A1 overexpression is an independent risk factor, associated with stromal activation, CAFs enrichment, and reduced B-cell/CD4+/CD8+ T cell infiltration [147]. Stromal COL11A1 predicts invasive recurrence in DCIS and radiotherapy resistance [148]. COL12A1 is significantly elevated in breast tumors and inversely linked to patient prognosis [149]. Based on collagen-immune interplay, tumors can be classified into three subtypes: “Soft and hot” - low collagen activity, high immune infiltration best response to ICB; “Armor and cold”-high collagen activity, low immune infiltration; “Stagnant”-low collagen and immune activity. These subtypes exhibit prognostic relevance across multiple cancers, with “soft and hot” tumors showing superior ICB responsiveness [150]. Therapeutic strategies targeting collagen remodeling

Collagenase-mediated degradation

Elevated collagen levels in breast tumors are closely associated with disease progression and hinder therapeutic agent penetration in the TME, leading to poor clinical outcomes [151]. To address this, we engineered a probiotic strain that selectively colonizes tumors and releases bacterial collagenase in a lysis-dependent manner to degrade TME collagen, thereby enhancing the efficacy of BC treatments [151]. Additionally, a 20-amino acid peptide derived from collagen IV exhibits anti-tumor effects by inhibiting endothelial and tumor cell proliferation, adhesion, and migration, suggesting its potential as a standalone or adjunct therapy [152].

Crosslinking inhibition

Our systematic knockout screen of chromosome 11q13/14 in IntClust-2 breast cancers identified collagen modifying genes P4HA3 and SERPINH1 as novel drivers of tumor progression, whose pharmacological inhibition disrupts oncogenic signaling and reduces tumor growth, revealing collagen synthesis as a therapeutic vulnerability in this aggressive BC subtype [2]. LOX inhibition enhances drug penetration, suppresses FAK/Src signaling, and restores chemosensitivity by downregulating ITGA5/FN1 [153]. Furthermore, COX-2 inhibition reduces overall collagen deposition, offering another therapeutic avenue [154].

Receptor blockade

Collagen remodeling plays a crucial role in both physiological and pathological processes. Therapeutically, modulating the WISP2/WISP1 ratio in tumors suppresses collagen linearization, thereby inhibiting metastatic dissemination [155]. WRG-28, a selective extracellular inhibitor of DDR2, impedes tumor invasion, stromal support, and metastatic colonization in the lungs [156]. Dual-receptor-targeted MOF nanocarriers delivering siDDR2 and siITGAV synergistically disrupt the ECM barrier in TNBC by suppressing collagen deposition and TGF-β1 activation, thereby enhancing CD8+ T cell infiltration, downregulating PD-L1, and inhibiting tumor growth, offering a novel ECM reprogramming strategy to potentiate cancer immunotherapy [98].

Integrins, key mediators of cell adhesion and metastasis, α2β1 integrin, a major collagen receptor, drives BC metastasis. Alternagin-C, a disintegrin targeting α2β1 integrin, exerts anti-metastatic effects by suppressing inflammation, angiogenesis, and metalloproteinase activity, while inhibiting tumor adhesion to COL1 [157]. The protease inhibitor EcTI, isolated from Enterolobium contortisiliquum seeds, binds COL1 and disrupts tumor progression via integrin/FAK/SRC, Akt, ERK, and apoptotic regulators (BAX/BCL-2) [158].

Most tumors exhibit a stroma-rich microenvironment, characterized by fibrotic tissue, CAFs, and a dense ECM that hinders nanoparticle penetration [159]. The presence of dense collagen fibers, characteristic of TNBC, impairs drug penetration and compromises treatment efficacy, despite the potential therapeutic effects of epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) [160]. Targeting desmoplastic tumors through ECM modulation normalizes collagen and HA levels, recalibrates intratumoral mechanical forces, and enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy by improving perfusion [161].

Tumor-stroma ratio (TSR) emerged as an independent predictor of treatment response, with luminal subtype BC patients demonstrating higher neoadjuvant chemotherapy sensitivity in low-TSR cases, suggesting its potential as a reliable predictive biomarker [162]. Laminin-111 interaction induces a functional switch in ADGRF1 from oncogenic to tumor-suppressive activity through Gαs/STAT3 inhibition and senescence induction, while upregulating HER2 expression and enhancing anti-HER2 drug sensitivity in HER2+ breast cancer, suggesting therapeutic potential for targeting this pathway [163]. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, radiotherapy also remodels the TME into a low-tumorigenic state [164].

Immune regulation

B7-H3 as a biomarker linked to immuno-cold features and collagen accumulation in TNBC, demonstrating that B7-H3-targeting monoclonal antibody therapy suppresses tumor growth, reverses the armored-cold phenotype, enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy in preclinical models, and proposes a novel B7-H3/PD-L1-based subclassification stratifying TNBC patients by immune infiltration, collagen levels, and therapeutic responsiveness to guide precision immunotherapy [165]. B7-H3 blockade induces a “primed for death” stress state in TNBC cells by triggering metabolic rewiring through the AKT/SREBP1/FASN axis, and proposes a dual-targeting strategy combining B7-H3 and FASN inhibition to synergistically attenuate tumor progression, supported by an “immune attack-metabolic compensation” model highlighting the therapeutic potential of co-targeting immune and metabolic vulnerabilities in TNBC [166]. Metabolic dysregulation of lipids is an independent risk factor for BC. Studies have shown that fatty acid synthase expression is positively correlated with the immune cold TME of BC. Knockdown of fatty acid synthase inhibits the palmitoylation of ubiquitin specific protease 5, reduces its interaction with glutathione peroxidase 4, and increases the ubiquitination and degradation of glutathione peroxidase 4, thereby enhancing the response to immunotherapy [167].

A polymer-based combinatorial therapy integrating immunogenic cell death induction and CXCR4 inhibition enhances tumor immunogenicity, promoting T-cell recruitment. This approach reduces intratumoral fibrosis and collagen-mediated barriers, while diminishing immunosuppressive cell populations to restore T cell function [168].

Natural drugs and local anesthetics

As a core component of the ECM, collagen metabolic imbalance can induce diseases such as inflammation and cancer. Helicobacter pylori infection may enhance the migratory potential of gastric cancer cells, possibly because the genes it upregulates are associated with immunity and drug sensitivity in gastric cancer [169]. Among natural drugs, the aqueous extract can inhibit inflammation by regulating inflammasome signaling and the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway, thereby reducing inflammation-driven abnormal collagen remodeling. Additionally, theophylline acid isolated from chebulinic acid (a component of Terminalia chebula Retz) can inhibit Helicobacter pylori through potential binding to the Cag A protein and regulation of adhesion. This blocks Hp-induced activation of collagen-degrading enzymes and gastric cancer cell migration, ultimately protecting collagen structure [170, 171]. Local anesthetics are voltage-gated sodium channel blockers, primarily administered locally or to innervated nerves for anesthesia or analgesic purposes. However, studies have shown that local anesthetics can also reduce collagen cross-linking degree, promote moderate collagen degradation, and improve the penetration of therapeutic drugs into collagen-dense matrices. Meanwhile, they downregulate collagen synthesis, reduce ECM stiffness, and inhibit collagen-mediated cancer cell invasion [172, 173]. Furthermore, local anesthetics can preferentially inhibit cancer stem cells, thereby suppressing colon cancer invasion [174]. Currently, these two types of drugs face challenges: low purity of active components in natural drugs, and dosage-related side effects of local anesthetics. Further optimization is required to advance their clinical application.

Prospects and challenges

Collagen, as a core component of the TME in BC, plays an irreplaceable role in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Significant progress has been achieved in the research on the expression characteristics, functional mechanisms, and clinical significance of different types of collagen. Individualized therapy and combination therapy strategies based on collagen features have also shown promising application prospects in clinical studies. However, issues such as collagen heterogeneity, off-target effects, and drug resistance still restrict the clinical translation of collagen-targeted therapy, which need to be addressed by establishing a multi-dimensional biomarker system, developing novel collagen-targeted drugs, and promoting the integration of multi-disciplinary technologies.

In the future, with the deepening of basic research and continuous innovation of clinical technologies, collagen-targeted therapy is expected to become an important part of precise treatment for BC. Through accurate identification of collagen-related biomarkers, formulation of individualized treatment plans, and combination with novel targeted drugs and multi-disciplinary technologies, efficient and low-toxicity treatment of BC can be achieved, providing new hope for improving the prognosis of BC patients. Meanwhile, it is also necessary to strengthen multi-center, large-sample clinical studies to verify the efficacy and safety of collagen-targeted therapy strategies.
