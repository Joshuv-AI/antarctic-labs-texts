Anomalous Aortic Origin of the Left Coronary Artery Mimicking Recurrent Myocarditis in a Teenager
Authors: Minjung Bak, Ji Eun Lee, Sunki Lee, Jin Oh Na, Hwan Seok Yong, Eung Ju Kim
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA) is a rare but potentially catastrophic congenital anomaly, especially in the young and athletic population.1) Despite its clinical importance, diagnosing AAOCA in a timely manner remains a challenge. We report a rare case of a 16-year-old male whose left-AAOCA (L-AAOCA) was misdiagnosed and treated as recurrent myocarditis for 5 years before a definitive diagnosis was made following a life-threatening cardiac arrest.

CASE

The patient’s clinical history was notable for recurrent exercise-related cardiac events. At 11 years of age, he first presented with exercise-induced syncope and dyspnea. At age 14, he developed cardiogenic shock (blood pressure 55/44 mmHg) with severely depressed left ventricular systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] 23–29%). Based on elevated cardiac biomarkers and global hypokinesia on echocardiography, he was treated under a presumptive diagnosis of fulminant myocarditis with intravenous immunoglobulin and inotropic support, resulting in transient recovery of LVEF to 55%. Notably, inflammatory markers were unremarkable (C-reactive protein [CRP] 0.34 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR] 2 mm/hr, afebrile), with no antecedent viral prodrome and negative respiratory viral polymerase chain reaction panel including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), arguing against acute myocarditis.

At 16 years of age, he again developed blurred vision and a near-syncope sensation while swimming. He rested briefly but subsequently lost consciousness for approximately 5 minutes and was transported to the emergency department. On arrival, initial cardiac enzyme levels were within the normal range, but the initial lactate level was elevated at 6.1 mmol/L. Inflammatory markers were similarly unremarkable (CRP 1.00 mg/L, ESR 2 mm/hr, afebrile), with no viral prodrome and negative SARS-CoV-2 testing. Follow-up cardiac biomarkers demonstrated a marked increase, with creatine kinase muscle brain exceeding 300 ng/mL (0–4.87 ng/mL) and troponin T measuring 6.8 ng/mL (0–0.1 ng/mL). A prior outpatient electrocardiogram was normal (Figure 1A); however, the electrocardiogram obtained on arrival at the emergency department demonstrated QRS widening (Figure 1B). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a LVEF of 35%. His blood pressure progressively declined to 42/34 mmHg, and the wide-QRS rhythm deteriorated into pulseless electrical activity, prompting initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Following resuscitation, his vital signs failed to stabilize, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was instituted.

Coronary angiography performed immediately after extracorporeal membrane oxygenation insertion failed to selectively engage the left coronary artery. Subsequent aortography demonstrated the left coronary system. An endomyocardial biopsy was performed to evaluate possible inflammatory etiology and showed only mild lymphocytic infiltration, which was insufficient to explain the severity of the clinical presentation. Subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging revealed a subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) pattern in left coronary territory, strongly suggesting an ischemic etiology rather than inflammatory myocarditis (Figure 2). This pattern contrasts with the subepicardial or mid-wall enhancement typically seen in myocarditis. Coronary computed tomography angiography confirmed anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the right sinus with an interarterial course (Figure 3). The patient underwent surgical coronary artery re-implantation.2) Intraoperative findings revealed high-risk features, including an acute-angled ostium and a significant intramural segment. The patient was successfully discharged and remained asymptomatic during follow-up.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates a critical diagnostic pitfall in pediatric cardiology: the overlap between L-AAOCA and recurrent myocarditis. The intermittent nature of coronary compression in AAOCA can lead to a clinical course that mimics the “relapsing-remitting” pattern of inflammatory heart disease. In our patient, the repeated recovery of left ventricular function after rest and supportive care led to the erroneous diagnosis of myocarditis for 7 years. This highlights that clinical and biochemical markers of myocardial injury are not pathognomonic for myocarditis and must be interpreted alongside detailed anatomical evaluations.

A critical turning point in the diagnostic workup of this case was CMR imaging. The detection of a subendocardial LGE pattern in the anterior and anterolateral wall—a distribution confined to the left coronary territory—was pivotal in reframing the diagnosis from inflammatory myocarditis to ischemic myocardial injury. This distinction is essential: myocarditis characteristically produces subepicardial or mid-wall LGE in a non-coronary distribution, whereas ischemic injury produces subendocardial or transmural LGE following a vascular territory.3) Recognizing this difference directed the clinical team toward anatomical coronary evaluation, ultimately leading to diagnosis by coronary computed tomography angiography. We therefore propose that in any pediatric or adolescent patient presenting with recurrent myocarditis-like episodes—particularly when episodes are exertion-triggered, associated with syncope or hemodynamic compromise, or recurrent despite standard therapy—the LGE pattern on CMR should be carefully assessed to distinguish ischemic from non-ischemic myocardial injury, and should prompt an active search for coronary anomalies.1,3)

According to current guidelines, the diagnosis of myocarditis requires reasonable exclusion of coronary artery disease.3) In this patient, however, a comprehensive coronary evaluation was not initially pursued, likely because of the patient’s young age. This case illustrates an important clinical lesson: even in the absence of traditional atherosclerotic risk factors such as advanced age, diabetes, hypertension, or smoking history, myocardial ischemia due to congenital coronary anomalies remains a relevant possibility. Therefore, exclusion of coronary artery disease should be actively considered regardless of age when evaluating suspected myocarditis. Because invasive coronary angiography is often deferred in adolescents, noninvasive anatomic assessment—including focused evaluation of coronary origins by echocardiography or early coronary computed tomography angiography, guided by CMR when available—should be considered when myocarditis is suspected but the clinical pattern is exertional, recurrent, or unusually severe.

The pathophysiology of ischemia in L-AAOCA with an interarterial course is complex and dynamic. While right-AAOCA is more prevalent, L-AAOCA carries a significantly higher risk of myocardial ischemia and sudden cardiac death (SCD).4) Beyond simple compression between the aorta and pulmonary artery, several high-risk anatomical features determine the clinical outcome. An intramural course and an acute-angled takeoff are major determinants of ischemia and SCD.5) The marked systolic expansion of the aortic root during exercise can induce dynamic angulation or kinking of a coronary artery that originates at an acute or elevated takeoff angle. This geometric distortion may promote transient luminal narrowing. In addition, a proximal intramural segment is frequently underdeveloped relative to the distal coronary artery and is particularly vulnerable to lateral compression during systole in the setting of shear forces transmitted from the adjacent aortic wall.5,6) These mechanisms explain why ischemia is often transient and exercise-dependent, further confusing the diagnosis with other paroxysmal cardiac events.

Current expert consensus acknowledges some variability in management for asymptomatic patients with anatomical variants associated with a benign prognosis, such as intraseptal, prepulmonic, or retroaortic courses.6) However, surgical intervention is recommended in patients with a history of aborted SCD, objective evidence of ischemia on stress testing, or impaired coronary flow demonstrated by invasive or functional assessment.7) In the present case, surgical correction successfully relieved exercise-induced ischemia. Nevertheless, because a portion of the myocardium has already sustained injury, long-term management should focus on preventing progression of heart failure.

In conclusion, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for AAOCA in any young patient presenting with exercise-related syncope or unexplained heart failure. As demonstrated here, early anatomical diagnosis is the only way to avoid life-threatening complications.
