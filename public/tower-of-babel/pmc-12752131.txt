Inhibition of p38α MAPK increases short-term astrocyte reactivity: the exploratory VIP trial in early Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Dominique Gouilly, Alexandre Da-Costa, Agathe Vrillon, Aurélie Pistono, Marie Goubeaud, Elsa Bertrand, Johanne Germain, Nadéra Ainaoui, Stéphanie Bras, Hélène Catala, Mélanie Planton, Béatrice Lemesle, Anne Hitzel, Anne-Sophie Salabert, Leonor Nogueira, François Mouton-Liger, Déborah Méligne, Laurence Jasse, Marie Rafiq, Benjamine Sarton, Stein Silva, John Alam, Claire Paquet, Clovis Tauber, Claire Thalamas, Pierre Payoux, Patrice Péran, Jérémie Pariente
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuro-inflammation is an early mechanistic target in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, the effect of anti-inflammatory drugs on biomarkers of neuro-inflammation has yet to be described. Biomarkers of neuro-inflammation showed distinct evolution over the course of AD, suggesting that anti-inflammatory interventions could be beneficial or interfere with mechanisms of immune defense.MethodsWe conducted a pilot and exploratory phase II trial to evaluate the short-term effects of neflamapimod, a drug known to inhibit the neuro-toxic pro-inflammatory properties of the p38α MAPK. Participants with early AD were randomized to receive neflamapimod 40 mg (n = 17) or placebo (n = 16) twice-daily for 12 weeks. The primary endpoint was the treatment-related change in PET imaging of the translocator protein (TSPO) using [18F]-DPA-714. The main secondary endpoints were cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) markers of microglial and astrocyte reactivity. We hypothesized that neuro-inflammation would be reduced at three months in PET and in CSF markers.ResultsNeuro-inflammation was not reduced between the neflamapimod and placebo groups, and no change was observed in [18F]-DPA-714 PET. However, neflamapimod induced a significant increase in CSF GFAP levels compared to placebo. Multivariate analyses based on CSF biomarkers highlighted the contribution of sTREM2 in the pattern of variation that discriminated the two groups (accuracy = 71%). Patients who received neflamapimod and had baseline sTREM2 level above the median had the highest increase in GFAP compared to placebo, suggesting that neflamapimod may exacerbate reactive astrogliosis as a function of the TREM2-related microglial state. Safety analysis showed that three cases of CSF pleocytosis occurred during follow-up in participants receiving neflamapimod.ConclusionsNeflamapimod at a dose of 40 mg twice-daily interfered with mechanisms of immune defense in early AD. Although this study is exploratory, our results may provide an explanation for the failure of previous anti-inflammatory interventions in early AD. Biomarkers of neuro-inflammation should be required in the next-generation trials targeting neuro-immune mechanisms in AD.RegistryClinicalTrials.gov, Clinical Trial registration number: NCT03435861.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03625-x.

Background

Since the beginning of the study of neuro-inflammation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in the 1980 s, anti-inflammatory interventions have been the most investigated approach [1]. However, most of these trials have failed to reach their primary endpoint, and some of them even accelerated cognitive decline [1–3]. These failures have highlighted the need to refine the design of immunological trials, including the choice of target, the direction of the intervention, and the use of neuro-immune biomarkers during drug development [4–6].

To date, the effect of immunological interventions on biomarkers of neuro-inflammation has not been described in AD [7]. Multi-modal neuro-immune biomarkers can help to identify proof-of-mechanism, to track different immune pathways, and to untangle the relationship between neuro-inflammation and disease-modifying effects [1]. This includes brain endophenotypes using radiotracers of the translocator protein (TSPO) in positron emission tomography (PET), and measurements in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [6].

The response of microglia and astrocytes is an upstream event in AD that ultimately drives the progression of the triad of pathological changes of amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration [8, 9]. However, biomarkers of neuro-inflammation have demonstrated different evolutions in AD, and have exhibited varying relationships with other biomarkers of disease progression [7, 9, 10]. Notably, TSPO PET studies showed that neuro-inflammation may be protective in the early stages of AD, and that an increasing neuro-inflammation has a detrimental effect on disease progression [10–13]. This heterogeneity leads to the idea that intervention targeting neuro-inflammation in AD may be beneficial of interfere with mechanisms of immune defense [9].

Preclinical studies have identified the p38α mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) as a target for intervention aimed at reducing the pro-inflammatory neurotoxic properties of microglia and astrocytes [14, 15]. This multifunctional protein is expressed by microglia, astrocytes, and neurons and appears to be involved in neuro-inflammation, synaptic plasticity, tau hyperphosphorylation, and amyloid-mediated neuronal damage [16, 17]. During the neuro-immune response, the p38α MAPK regulates the production and the signaling of pro-inflammatory mediators in microglia and astrocytes [15, 18]. In addition, activation of the p38α MAPK can trigger overlapping neuro-immune pathways such as the extracellular-signal-regulated kinase (ERK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and the kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NFκB) [19, 20]. Preclinical studies of compounds that regulate the p38α MAPK have demonstrated various benefits including reduction of amyloid-related neurotoxicity, tau pathology, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and neuro-inflammation [16, 17].

In clinical studies, the MAPK pathway is related to PTK2B which is a common gene variant associated with an increased risk of AD [21, 22]. Recent proteomic studies have also associated the MAPK pathway with AD [23–25], one of which highlighted the association between the MAPK pathway and the rate of cognitive decline [26]. Another recent study showed that elevated CSF proteins in TSPO-positive individuals with AD were enriched in pathways such as the MAPK [25]. These results suggest that inhibition of p38α MAPK may target complex networks of multicellular pathologies and alleviate the burden of downstream processes related to amyloid and tau synergy. However, how the inhibition of this pathway modifies biomarkers of neuro-inflammation remains undescribed.

One hypothesis is that the inhibition of the p38α MAPK exerts neuroprotective effects by reducing neuro-inflammation, thereby toning down the self-amplifying cycle between neuro-inflammation and AD pathological changes. If this hypothesis is correct, the main short-term effect of p38α MAPK inhibition would be a reduction in neuro-inflammation compared to placebo, with no change in downstream biomarkers of neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment. However, neuro-inflammation may be protective in the early stages of AD [10, 11], and previous interventions with anti-inflammatory drugs other than neflamapimod have shown null results and sometimes even harmful effects [1, 2]. Therefore, another hypothesis would be that the inhibition of p38α MAPK interferes with mechanisms of immune defense and consequently increase neuro-inflammation.

We conducted a pilot and exploratory, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase II trial to assess the effect of neflamapimod, a selective inhibitor of the p38α MAPK [27]. The aim of this study was to assess how the inhibition of p38α MAPK modifies biomarkers of neuro-inflammation after a three-month follow-up in early AD. TSPO PET imaging was used as primary endpoint. The secondary endpoints were CSF biomarkers of neuro-inflammation, synaptic and axonal damage, as well as neuropsychological performances and gray matter volumes. We hypothesized that neuro-inflammation would be reduced at three months in PET and in CSF markers.

Methods

Study design

The V.I.P. study (V: VX-745; I: inflammation; P: PET scan) is a monocentric, double-blind, randomized and placebo-controlled phase II trial that was conducted at the Centre d’Investigation Clinique (CIC 1436) at the University Hospital of Toulouse (Clinical Trial registration number: NCT03435861). This study was approved by the French Ethics Committee “Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud-Est 1” (reference number: 2017–78) and the French Drug Safety and Health Products Agency (reference number: MEDAECNAT-2018–01–0034). All the participants provided written informed consent.

Participants were recruited at the Memory Clinic of the Neurology Department at the University Hospital of Toulouse (France). The inclusion criteria were an age range of 50 to 90 years, amnestic mild cognitive impairment with mini-mental state examination (MMS) > 20/30, and CSF biomarker evidence of AD [28]. Exclusion criteria were evidence of notable comorbidity and any other condition with the potential to affect cognition and neuro-inflammation. A detailed list of eligibility criteria can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

The prespecified recruitment objective was to enroll 40 participants and recruitment concluded when the drugs achieved the expiration date. Enrollment started on September 10, 2018, and the last visit was on June 30, 2021. The database was established throughout the study and was locked on June 3, 2022, prior to the unmasked analysis. An independent data monitor audited the study center at intervals and at close-out.

Participants were randomized to receive either neflamapimod 40 mg or a placebo twice-daily. Randomization was performed using blocks of random size based on the prespecified recruitment objectives using STATA software (v.14). The allocation of the participants was concealed by the pharmacy of the University Hospital of Toulouse. The treatment administration started the day after the last examination at the screening visit. Safety monitoring and review of adverse events were performed during a phone call two days after the first administration, each month during follow-up, and at the final trial visits.

All the participants followed the same regimen of 40 mg twice-daily, with a dose taken orally within 30 min of meals. This dosing regimen was similar to previous studies [27, 29]. Neflamapimod and placebo capsules were identical in appearance. Capsules were packaged in four-week supply kits and given to participants at each monthly visit during follow-up. Patients, caregivers, and investigators were blinded to treatment allocation.

The study design is shown in Fig. 1. The screening visit consisted of a complete neurological examination, a multi-domain neuropsychological assessment, electrocardiogram, blood sampling, lumbar puncture, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and TSPO PET imaging with [18F]-DPA-714. These examinations were performed over two days within one week, and replicated after a three-month follow-up. Blood samples were used to characterize APOE and TSPO genotypes. All the participants were classified as high (HAB), mixed (MAB), or low (LAB) binding affinity for TSPO.Fig. 1Study design. Outcome acquisition and measurement

Neuropsychological tests

Participants underwent neuropsychological tests over two days within one week at baseline (mean = 7 ± 3 days) and follow-up (mean = 7 ± 1 days). Cognitive functions were assessed with a comprehensive battery of tests shown in Supplementary Table 4 and described in detail elsewhere [30]. The efficacy analyses included all of these tests except for the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory scale and the Beck Depression Inventory, as these behavioral scales use a self-report notation. In addition, a few scores were missing due to cognitive impairment, refusal, or technical reasons (details can be found in Supplementary Tables 4, 8, 12 and 16).

CSF biomarkers

CSF was collected, handled, and assessed at baseline and follow-up, as previously described [31]. All the participants had CSF evidence of AD. In addition, CSF samples were stored at −80 °C and then transferred to Lariboisière Hospital, Paris (France) for measurement of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), chitinase-3-like protein 1 (YKL-40), soluble variant of the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cell 2 (sTREM2), interleukin (IL)−6 and IL-10, neurofilament light chain (NfL) and neurogranin. Samples were measured in duplicates over two analytical runs using commercial kits, according to the manufacturer’s instructions for all markers. Two control samples were run at the beginning and at the end of the plate. CSF NfL and GFAP were measured using the SIMOA technology with the Neurology 2-plexB #103,520 kit from Quanterix® on an HD-X analyzer (Quanterix, Lexington, MA) [32]. CSF neurogranin was measured using a commercial Elisa from Euroimmun (#EQ 6551–9601-L). CSF YKL-40 and sTREM2 levels were measured using a commercial Elisa assay from R&D system (sTREM-2: # DY1828-05, YKL-40 # DY2599). IL-6 and IL-10 levels were measured using the Human Cytokine Multiplex Panel A from R&D System (#QC11). All the samples were detected and displayed values above the lower limit of quantification reported for the assay. Intra- and inter-assays coefficients of variation were below 10% for all analytes.

MRI

For each subject, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was acquired at baseline and follow-up using on a 3T MRI scanner (Philips Achieva dStream), including 3D-T1-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI).

White matter hyperintensities were visually assessed by a trained rater (MP) using the 9-point Fazekas’ rating scale [33]. In addition, patients were classified as having possible or probable cerebral amyloid angiopathy by the same rater [34]. Two patients were excluded from these analyses due to severe artifacts or acquisition errors.

For efficacy analyses, volumetric measurements on 3D-T1-weighted images were performed at baseline and follow-up using the AssemblyNet pipeline on the Volbrain web platform (https://volbrain.net/). This allows the use of deep-learning to perform an accurate segmentation based on a large number of convolutional neural networks [35]. Bilateral regional volumes in cm3 were derived from the Neuromorphometrics atlas, and converted as a percentage of the total intracranial cavity for the bilateral whole cortex, frontal, parietal, temporal, precuneus, hippocampus, entorhinal and parahippocampal cortex.

[18F]-DPA-714 PET

A brain PET scanner was performed on a hybrid PET/CT tomograph (Siemens Biograph TruePoint 6.0) within one week of the MRI scan at baseline (mean = 7 ± 4 days) and follow-up (mean = 7 ± 4 days). The PET was acquired continuously over 60 min after intravenous injection of [18F]-DPA-714. The radiotracer dose was similar between the neflamapimod and placebo groups at baseline (mean = 238 MBq ± 35 and 248 MBq ± 57, respectively, P > 0.05) and follow-up (mean = 237 MBq ± 35 and 244 MBq ± 56, respectively, P > 0.05). All corrections (attenuation, radioactive decay, random, scatter-coincidences and a partial-volume correction) were incorporated in an iterative OSEM reconstruction (3 iterations, 21 subsets). Dynamic data were reconstructed in 32 time-frames (6 × 10 s; 8 × 30 s; 5 × 1 min; 5 × 2 min; 8 × 5 min).

SUVR was calculated to quantify [18F]-DPA-714 uptake. However, the distribution of TSPO is widespread in the brain [36], and the use of a reference region defined by anatomic criteria may introduce inter-individual variability as neflamapimod-related changes might occur in the reference region. Therefore, we performed a supervised clustering analysis (SVCA) [37]. Briefly, this method uses predefined reference time-activity curves derived from the images of healthy individuals to split the kinetic of the images from patients, voxels by voxels, as a linear sum of the predefined curves [37]. This allows to select cluster of voxels with the lowest activity as a data-driven reference region. Therefore, the reference was specific to each examination as the cluster was selected based on pharmacokinetic rather than anatomic criteria. This has the advantage to limit the susceptibility bias related to neflamapimod-induced changes in the reference. We used the neuroimaging examination of 20 healthy individuals (11 HAB and 9 MAB) recruited in a PET study of our group to assess neuro-inflammation in coma [38]. These subjects were comparable with respect to sex (P > 0.05, uncorrected) but age was lower (mean = 45; range = [22–63]; min = 20; max = 75) compared to AD patients receiving neflamapimod or placebo (P < 0.01, uncorrected). The acquisition parameters and the radiotracer dose were similar (mean = 242 MBq ± 62; P > 0.05, uncorrected).

Baseline T1-weighted MRI were segmented, spatially normalized and parcellated using the Destrieux and Desikan-Kiliany atlases on FreeSurfer. Frames 8 to 32 of the reconstructed PET images were motion-corrected on SPM (Matlab V. 2018 and 2019b). All frames were then co-registered to the corresponding T1-weighted in native space (for AD patients, the baseline T1-weighted was used). Dynamic PET images of healthy individuals were then used to generate two sets of classes for HAB (n = 11) and MAB (n = 9) following a previously described procedure [37, 38]. This allowed linear unmixing of the dynamic PET images of AD patients and the generation of the reference time activity curves separately for HAB and MAB [38].

Furthermore, mean SUV parametric images were calculated at the 50–60 min interval (e.g., frame 31 and 32), as previously described [30]. A binary inclusive mask of the entire brain segment was applied. Mean SUV values were then extracted from the bilateral binarized mask of the reference clustering region, supratentorial whole cortex, supratentorial whole gray and white matter (heretofore referred to as the whole brain), frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, precuneus, rostral and caudal anterior cingulate, posterior and isthmus cingulate, cerebellar cortex, and a temporal meta-ROI including the bilateral hippocampus, entorhinal and parahippocampal cortex, amygdala and fusiform gyrus. The mean SUV from the reference region mask was used to calculate the SUV ratio.

Statistical analyses

Prespecified analyses

The primary endpoint was the whole cortex SUVR in [18F]-DPA-714 PET imaging. The secondary endpoints were CSF biomarkers of neuro-inflammation, synaptic and axonal damage, gray matter volumes on MRI, and neuropsychological measurements.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effect of an anti-inflammatory drug on neuro-immune biomarkers in early AD. There was no prespecified hypothesis of a minimal delta difference between the neflamapimod and placebo groups in the primary and secondary endpoints. According to the guidelines for exploratory pilot studies [39], no prior sample size calculation and no formal power calculation was performed. Therefore, the analyses of this study should be interpreted with caution and were performed on a descriptive purpose. Our results may contribute to a methodological framework for using neuro-immune biomarkers in the next-generation trials of immunomodulatory therapies in AD [6].

For the PET and MRI measurements, percentages of variation were calculated using the following formula: “100 x (value at follow-up – value at baseline)/value at baseline”. For CSF biomarkers and raw cognitive scores, the delta differences were calculated using the following formula: “value at follow-up – value at baseline”.

The prespecified statistical analysis plan included the comparisons of the efficacy parameters between the neflamapimod and placebo groups. Paired comparisons were also performed within each group to analyze changes in values from baseline to follow-up. This enabled us to determine if the change in efficacy parameters from baseline was significant within each group. In addition, post-hoc analyses that were suggested after the initiation of data analysis included (1) multivariate analyses based on the pattern of variation of CSF biomarkers, and (2) analyses performed on CSF sTREM2.

Comparisons were performed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney’s test, Dunn’s test, Chi2 or Fischer’s test, when appropriate. Wilcoxon’s paired-test was used to compare baseline and follow-up values within each group. Significance was set at p < 0.05, two-tailed. These analyses were performed with the R software v.4.4.2.

In all the analyses, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using Benjamini-Hochberg’s correction for false-discovery-rate. Each modality was corrected separately. This resulted in a correction set of fourteen tests for baseline demographics; thirteen tests for [18F]-DPA-714 PET; five tests for CSF AD biomarkers; seven tests for CSF biomarkers of neuro-inflammation, synaptic and axonal damage; eight tests for gray matter volumes; twenty-seven tests for neuropsychological measurements; and eight tests in safety analysis. Only corrected results will be discussed in the following sections, and the reader is referred to the Tables and Supplementary Files for detailed uncorrected results.

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure that the results of the efficacy analyses were not driven by outlier values, we excluded the patients with a variation of ± 2 standard-deviation within each group (i.e., a null z-value corresponds to the mean value within the neflamapimod or placebo group). The results described below were unchanged for all the efficacy parameters (P > 0.05, HAB and MAB in combination for PET).

ANCOVA was performed to evaluate whether the variation in efficacy parameters was influenced by an interaction between the group (neflamapimod/placebo) and the following characteristics assessed separately: age, sex, education (years), time from diagnosis to baseline (months), apolipoprotein 4 carriership, and the use of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine). No interaction was observed (P > 0.05, adjusted for TSPO genotype for PET).

Multivariate pattern analyses

We performed multivariate pattern analyses (MVPA) to determine whether the neflamapimod and placebo groups could be discriminated based on the pattern of variation in CSF GFAP, sTREM2, YKL-40, IL-6, IL-10, NfL, and neurogranin. This approach is thought to increase sensitivity in differentiating between individuals or conditions, as shown by Pistono and colleagues in early AD [40]. Supervised classification was conducted using a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) classifier implemented in the Scikit-learn toolbox in Python [41]. The classifier was trained on labeled samples to separate groups (neflamapimod/placebo) and tested on unseen data to evaluate generalization. We used a leave-one-out cross-validation approach: in each fold, the classifier was trained on all but one participant per group and applied to the left-out participants to predict class membership. This procedure was repeated until every participant had been used once as the test case.

The significance of classification accuracy for predicting the group (neflamapimod/placebo) was then evaluated using permutation tests. We performed 100,000 random divisions of the entire dataset. This allowed us to calculate the P-value of the observed accuracy against chance level (e.g., 50%) and also the contribution of each biomarker to the classification accuracy. A detailed description of the multivariate analyses procedure can be found elsewhere [40].

Primary endpoint: effect of neflamapimod on [18F]-DPA-714 PET imaging

LAB patients were excluded from all PET analyses (neflamapimod, n = 3; placebo, n = 1). In addition, three patients receiving neflamapimod were excluded from PET analyses: two because of motion during acquisition, and one because the follow-up examination could not be performed.

There was no significant difference in standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) between patients receiving neflamapimod compared to placebo in the whole cortex (primary endpoint) as well as in other regions-of-interest (P > 0.05, HAB and MAB in combination, corrected). In paired comparisons, significant decreases were observed in all the regions-of-interest within the neflamapimod or placebo groups (P < 0.05, HAB and MAB in combination, corrected) except for the parietal and rostral anterior cingulate in patients receiving neflamapimod, and except for the rostral anterior and posterior cingulate and the cerebellar cortex in patients receiving placebo (P > 0.05, corrected). Most variations in SUVR values were contained within an interval of 0% to −5%, suggesting a very low variability at follow-up (Table 2).

To ascertain that the TSPO genotype did not influence our results, we performed these comparisons in HAB or MAB patients separately. There was still no significant result (P > 0.05, corrected, see Supplementary Table 6 for uncorrected results).

Effect of neflamapimod on secondary endpoints

LAB patients were included in all efficacy analyses of secondary endpoints because previous studies showed no effect of TSPO genotype on CSF biomarkers of neuro-inflammation [31], and on other biomarkers and neuropsychological measurements of AD [42].

CSF biomarkers of neuro-inflammation, synaptic and axonal damage

We found a significant increase in CSF GFAP in the neflamapimod group compared to placebo (P = 0.032, corrected, see Table 2 for uncorrected results). There was no significant difference in the CSF YKL-40, sTREM-2, IL-6 and IL-10, NfL, and neurogranin (P > 0.05, corrected). In paired comparisons, there was no difference from baseline within each group (P > 0.05, corrected).

Volumetric MRI

There were no significant differences between the neflamapimod and placebo groups in gray matter volume changes in the frontal, temporal, parietal, precuneus, hippocampal, parahippocampal, and entorhinal cortices (P > 0.05, corrected). In paired comparisons from baseline, there was a decrease in temporal volume within the placebo group (P = 0.02, corrected). There were no other significant differences from baseline in either group (P > 0.05, corrected).

Neuropsychological performances

There was no significant difference in the variation of neuropsychological raw scores between the neflamapimod and placebo groups (P > 0.05, corrected). However, we observed a decrease from baseline within the neflamapimod group on the free and cued selective reminding test (FCSRT) for the 7-day total delayed recall (P = 0.04, corrected), 20-min total delayed recall (P = 0.029, corrected), immediate total recall (P = 0.02, corrected), categorial (animal) and phonemic (P) verbal fluency (P = 0.02, corrected). There was no significant difference from baseline within the placebo group (P > 0.05, corrected).

CSF biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease

CSF AD biomarkers were measured in clinical practice at the University Hospital of Toulouse. However, at the time of the study, the quantification method was being upgraded, and two different quantification methods were used. The samples of the first 7 patients were measured by ELISA (INNOTEST), and the others by the Lumipulse G600 II system (Fujeribo, Ghent, Belgium). Therefore, exploratory statistics were performed after excluding the first 7 patients for compliance (placebo, n = 2; neflamapimod, n = 5).

There was no significant difference between the neflamapimod and placebo groups for amyloid-β 42 (Aβ42), the Aβ42/40 ratio, p-tau, the Aβ42/p-tau ratio and t-tau (P > 0.05, corrected). However, in paired comparison, we found a significant decrease from baseline in p-tau (P = 0.017, corrected) within the neflamapimod group. There was no significant difference from baseline within the placebo group (P > 0.05, corrected).

Multivariate pattern analyses

We performed multivariate pattern analyses to test whether the neflamapimod and placebo groups could be distinguished based on the pattern of variation in CSF GFAP, sTREM2, YKL-40, IL-6, IL-10, NfL and neurogranin. This enabled us to identify the pattern of variation that differentiated the two groups (e.g., an increase or decrease), as well as the contribution of each biomarker to that pattern. Details of the statistical procedure can be found in the Method section.

We performed a supervised classification analysis to predict the discriminative accuracy of the pattern of variation of CSF biomarkers. We obtained an accuracy of 71.2% which was significantly above the chance level (i.e., 50%; P = 0.017). This indicated that the neflamapimod and placebo groups appeared to be differentiated by an increase in GFAP, sTREM2, and a decrease in NfL and neurogranin, while almost no variation in YKL-40, IL-6, and IL-10. Interestingly, sTREM2 increase was the only marker with a significant contribution (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F). Effect of baseline sTREM2 value in the response to neflamapimod

We performed an exploratory sensitivity analysis to evaluate the effect of baseline CSF sTREM2 on the parameters of efficacy as previous studies have shown that sTREM2 levels can predict the subsequent evolution of AD [43, 44].

The median sTREM2 value at baseline in the entire population (neflamapimod and placebo in combination) was used to stratify the neflamapimod and placebo groups: patients with a baseline sTREM2 value above or equal to the median were classified as “high sTREM2”, whereas patients classified as “low sTREM2” had a baseline sTREM2 value below the median.

Again, only the results corrected for multiple testing will be discussed. The reader is referred to the Supplementary Files for details of the uncorrected results (Supplementary Tables 10 through 17). Due to the small number of patients in each subgroup, this analysis was performed for descriptive purposes and should be interpreted with caution.

At baseline, patients with high sTREM2 who received neflamapimod had higher CSF neurogranin (P = 0.049, corrected), p-tau (P = 0.028, corrected), and t-tau values (P = 0.017, corrected) compared to patients with low sTREM2 who received neflamapimod. These patients also showed lower scores of correct recognitions on the FCSRT at baseline compared to the low sTREM2 neflamapimod and the low sTREM2 placebo subgroups (P = 0.033, corrected). This suggests that patients receiving neflamapimod with the highest sTREM2 value at baseline might have a worse disease state prior to receiving neflamapimod compared to other subgroups.

Regarding the longitudinal changes, patients with high sTREM2 who received neflamapimod showed a smaller decrease in precuneus SUVR compared to the high sTREM2 placebo subgroup (P = 0.048, corrected, HAB and MAB in combination). In CSF, high sTREM2 patients receiving neflamapimod had a higher increase in CSF GFAP compared to the high sTREM2 placebo (P = 0.033, corrected) and the low sTREM2 placebo (P = 0.012, corrected) subgroups. They also showed a greater decrease in CSF Aβ42 compared to the high sTREM2 placebo subgroup (P = 0.043, corrected).

The other observations related to the low sTREM2 patients receiving neflamapimod. At baseline, these patients had lower CSF p-tau (P = 0.028, corrected), t-tau levels (P = 0.01, corrected) and entorhinal volume at baseline (P = 0.028, corrected) compared to the low sTREM2 patients receiving placebo. Regarding the longitudinal changes, there was no significant difference in the variation of CSF GFAP between the low sTREM2 patients receiving neflamapimod and both the low and high sTREM2 placebo subgroups (P > 0.05, corrected). This suggests that the effect of neflamapimod on GFAP in the whole population might be driven by patients with the highest sTREM2 value at baseline. In paired comparisons, a decrease in SUVR from baseline was observed in all region-of-interest within the low sTREM2 neflamapimod subgroup except for the caudal anterior cingulate, temporal meta-ROI and cerebellar cortex (P > 0.05, corrected).

In all the comparisons, there was no significant difference between high and low sTREM2 patients receiving placebo (P > 0.05, corrected). No other significant results were observed.

Discussion

The results of this exploratory trial in early AD can be summarized as follows: (1) no effect of neflamapimod 40 mg twice-daily is apparent on [18F]-DPA-714 PET, (2) CSF GFAP values were significantly increased at short-term, (3) this response might rely on the CSF sTREM2 profile at baseline, and (4) neflamapimod was well-tolerated although three cases receiving neflamapimod had CSF pleocytosis at follow-up.

Given the decades-long observation that the inhibition of p38α MAPK reduced neuro-inflammation in preclinical studies [16, 18], the observation that neflamapimod exacerbated short-term reactive astrogliosis in our study was unexpected. Phosphorylation pathways such as p38α MAPK have considerable crosstalk and interconnected loops that provide positive and negative feedback [14, 15, 18]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that these feedback loops can either amplify or suppress the inflammatory response [18, 45, 46].

Several lines of information from previous biomarker-based studies have indicated that neuro-inflammation may be protective in the early stages of AD, and that an increasing neuro-inflammation has a detrimental effect on disease progression [7, 10–13, 47]. Furthermore, studies using plasma or CSF GFAP have shown that astrocyte reactivity is an upstream event associated with synergistic changes in amyloid and tau burden and a worse cognitive prognosis at the pre-symptomatic and the early stages of AD [7, 8, 48–53].

In our study, one hypothesis is that inhibition of p38α MAPK counteracts reactive astrocytes response and interferes with mechanisms of immune defense resulting in an exacerbated pro-inflammatory response (Fig. 4). Previous studies showed that higher GFAP values was associated with worse cognitive performances in AD [48, 52, 54]. In our results, we observed a significant decrease from baseline in cognition in the paired comparison for patients receiving neflamapimod. This included scores of long-term memory and anterograde episodic memory scores based on verbal material, as well as phonemic and categorial verbal fluencies. Therefore, one possibility may be that the cognitive deterioration was mediated by an exacerbated reactive astrogliosis. However, the effect of neflamapimod on cognition may remain subtle as the variation in cognitive scores did not differ between the neflamapimod and placebo groups.

No major safety signal was reported in previous trial of neflamapimod in early AD [27, 29]. This consistent with our study showing no difference in any safety parameters between the neflamapimod and placebo group in statistical analyses. However, we observed a trend toward an increase from baseline in CSF white blood cell count within the neflamapimod group. The increase was superior to the threshold for CSF pleocytosis in three participants receiving neflamapimod, one of them showing a significant cognitive decline. These observations at the individual level are consistent with the notion of a treatment-induced inflammatory reaction.

Epidemiologic studies have shown that long-term use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs decreases the risk of AD [55]. However, most trials of anti-inflammatory drugs have shown null results and sometimes even harmful effects [1–3]. Although this study is exploratory, our results may reconcile these findings by suggesting that anti-inflammatory drugs might induce a short-term inflammatory reaction in early AD that may involve an astrocytic component. This could explain why some treatments such as rofecoxib, naproxen, and celecoxib, have shown an accelerated cognitive decline or an increase in adverse events compared to placebo [1–3].

Our exploratory results are consistent with previous studies using 40 mg neflamapimod twice-daily. The subtle cognitive decline observed within the neflamapimod group is consistent with prior results showing that AD patients in the lower three quartiles of plasma drug concentrations tend to perform worse on memory tests [27]. Furthermore, in our study, we found that CSF p-tau appeared to decrease at three-month relative to baseline in patients receiving neflamapimod. This is also consistent with prior finding showing that CSF p-tau and t-tau was reduced compared to placebo after a six-month follow-up [27]. This might be evidence of target engagement as the p38α MAPK is involved in the reduction of tau pathology [16, 17].

The results of previous studies of neflamapimod suggested that higher dose than 40 mg twice-daily might be beneficial. In a phase 1b of nine patients with early AD (eight at 40 mg and one at 125 mg twice-daily), a reduction of CSF IL-8 compared to baseline was observed in three patients who presented the highest plasma-drug concentration of neflamapimod [56]. Previous trials have also shown a trend toward dose-related improvement in anterograde memory performance for AD participants receiving 40 mg twice-daily within the highest quartile of plasma drug concentrations, or doses higher than 40 mg neflamapimod twice-daily [27, 29]. Therefore, another hypothesis is that 40 mg twice-daily remains sub-therapeutic, and that beneficial effects on neuro-inflammation might occur at a higher dose.

This idea is consistent with the results of a phase II trial in dementia with Lewy body (DLB) showing that the blood concentrations achieved in participants receiving 40 mg twice-daily was below the cellular potency for 50% inhibition (“EC50”) of p38α MAPK activity [57]. In this trial, participants receiving neflamapimod 40 mg thrice-daily showed improvement in functional and cognitive scores after a four-month follow-up, especially in participants with the lowest baseline p-tau levels [57, 58]. Further inspection of plasma biomarkers in this subgroup showed a moderate reduction of GFAP and a negative correlation between plasma GFAP changes and Clinical Dementia Rating sum-of-boxes [59]. However, it should be noted that the apparent differential effect of neflamapimod on GFAP between AD and DLB might also be related to difference in pathophysiology. Indeed, TSPO PET studies have suggested that the microglial response may be protective in early AD [10–12], while recent biomarker and neuropathological advances in DLB support the use of anti-inflammatory drugs [60–62].

In our study, the multivariate analyses highlighted the contribution of sTREM2 to the pattern of variation in CSF biomarkers that distinguished the neflamapimod and placebo groups. This was unexpected because there was no effect of neflamapimod on sTREM2. In addition, we observed that patients with baseline CSF sTREM2 above the median who received neflamapimod had the highest increase in CSF GFAP value. It suggests that neflamapimod 40 mg twice-daily may exacerbate reactive astrogliosis as a function of the TREM2-related microglial state. This hypothesis is consistent with preclinical studies showing that reactive astrocytes can be induced by activated microglia [63]. In addition, a recent study showed correlation between CSF proteins upregulated in TSPO-positive individuals including TREM2 and other proteins enriched in pathways such as the MAPK [25]. These results, including ours, suggest that the MAPK pathway may be part of a coordinated response between complex multicellular networks that includes TREM2.

Furthermore, the relationship between CSF sTREM2 levels and AD progression is still controversial. Some studies have shown that AD patients with high CSF sTREM2 levels at baseline have reduced amyloid burden, tau deposition, structural abnormalities, cognitive decline, and reduced likelihood of conversion to dementia [43, 44, 53, 64–67]. However, other studies have shown opposite results with high CSF sTREM2 levels at baseline correlating with cross-sectional CSF Aβ, P-tau, and T-tau levels, functional decline, and shorter time to conversion to dementia [68–74]. Two recent studies showed that CSF sTREM2 and TREM2 gene expression correlates with TSPO levels on PET [25, 75], and that microglial activation mediates tau propagation across Braak stages [75].

In addition, the interaction between TREM2 and astrocyte reactivity is difficult to interpret because the relationship between CSF sTREM2 and changes in cerebral tissue remains uncertain [5]. Indeed, an increase in CSF sTREM2 may reflect an increase in TREM2 expression and receptor signaling, but also an increase in shedding and decoy production leading to a decrease in signaling [5, 76]. Therefore, exploring the mutual interaction between CSF sTREM2 and astrocyte reactivity in AD remains a perspective of our study.

Although evidence supports TREM2 stimulation with agonistic antibodies as a new therapeutic approach for AD [77], the first results in humans remain negative. A recent phase I ascending-dose trial of a TREM2 agonist (DNL919) was discontinued after the observation of reversible hematologic effects at the highest dose tested in healthy volunteers [78]. In addition, a recent phase II trial of another TREM2 agonist (AL002) showed negative results on functional, cognitive and biomarker endpoints [79–81]. A discussion about the factors identified as critical for the development of TREM2-targeting therapies can be found elsewhere [6, 80, 81]. These failures are consistent with our exploratory results. It might point to the notion that increased TREM2 activity – as reflected by an increase in baseline CSF sTREM2 in our study – does not confer immuno-protection per se, and may be involved in susceptibility and variability in response to immunomodulatory therapies.

Limitations and perspectives

The important limitations of this study are the small number of patients and the descriptive nature of our analyses. These limitations are intrinsic to exploratory pilot studies [39]. In addition, we performed several statistical analyses. Although we applied a correction for multiple testing, this increases the risk of false-positive results.

Our study lays the groundwork for the next-generation clinical trials of disease-modifying immunomodulatory therapies in AD [6]. First, our results suggest that [18F]-DPA-714 PET may be less sensitive to short-term anti-inflammatory effects compared to fluidic biomarkers such as CSF GFAP and sTREM2. The variations in SUVR from baseline were contained within an interval of 0% to −5%, which is consistent with the range of test–retest changes reported in previous studies [82, 83]. In addition, a decrease in SUVR values were observed in the neflamapimod and placebo groups. This is consistent with other observational longitudinal studies that have shown that the uptake of [11C]-PK11195 or [11C]-PBR-28 can decrease in AD after various follow-up periods ranging from three months to two years [12, 83–86]. This is also in line with the notion that TSPO levels on PET may fluctuate during the course of AD [12]. Therefore, [18F]-DPA-714 PET may be valuable as a primary endpoint in trial with longer follow-ups and larger participant number compared to our study. In addition, we recommend the combination of TSPO PET with fluidic biomarkers to study the effect of the intervention on multiple cellular processes.

Since the choice of quantification method may affect our results, we replicated our analyses using the simplified reference tissue model to estimate non-displaceable binding potential, following the same procedure as previously reported [38]. Again, there was no significant effect of neflamapimod compared to placebo, and no significant change at follow-up compared to baseline within each group (Supplementary Table 19).

Another limitation of our study is the absence of control group to confirm that biomarker values are not within the normal range. However, the use of an external control group would not allow to account for the placebo effect. In addition, the presence of abnormalities in TSPO PET imaging and in CSF GFAP, sTREM2, YKL-40, and NfL have been widely demonstrated in early AD [11–13, 48, 53, 87].

Furthermore, the interpretation of the biological process visualized by TSPO PET has been the subject of several studies for several years [88]. Initially, the PET signal was thought to reflect a mixture of microglial density (e.g., the number of cells) and activity (e.g., pro or anti-inflammatory). Recent neuropathological studies have shown that TSPO expression can be observed in phagocytic microglial cells, and is correlated to neuro-immune and AD neuro-pathological changes [89–92]. These findings suggest that TSPO PET would be an appropriate endpoint for trial targeting neuro-immune mechanisms in AD, consistently with clinical PET studies [12, 13, 47]. However, some reports have questioned this idea [93], and a recent study showed that the TSPO PET signal in AD reflects the density of inflammatory cells rather than an activation state [94]. Therefore, in our study, the absence of results on TSPO PET might be explained by the fact that the predominant effect of neflamapimod lies in the activity rather than on the density of microglia. Further trials using TSPO PET as an endpoint will be helpful to clarify how TSPO PET can be modified by microglia-targeting therapeutics.

Finally, there were a few differences between the neflamapimod and placebo groups at baseline when the correction for multiple testing was not applied (Supplementary Tables 2 through 5), especially in [18F]-DPA-714 PET. The small number of patients probably resulted in an inclusion bias of participants despite the use of selective eligibility criteria and the double-blind randomization procedure.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have shown that the inhibition of the p38α MAPK using 40 mg twice-daily of neflamapimod increases short-term astrocyte reactivity in early AD. Although this study is exploratory, such a reaction could be a putative explanation for the failure of previous anti-inflammatory interventions in AD. Biomarkers of neuro-inflammation should be required in the next-generation trials targeting neuro-immune mechanisms in AD.
