Unraveling microbial signatures in the comorbidity of autoimmune diseases and depression
Authors: Fang Tang, Chengyuan Zhao, Yiting Cao, Changhong Liu, Guolin Mi, Jia Cao, Yongli Li, Cheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a common comorbidity in autoimmune diseases (ADs), including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a well-characterized AD with prominent gut involvement and strong host-microbiome interactions. Yet, its underlying microbial associations remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to explore the gut microbial composition and function in patients with ADs comorbid depression, including a subgroup with IBD comorbid depression, and to identify potential microbial and metabolic signatures associated with this comorbidity.MethodsWe analyzed two curated cohorts from the American Gut Project: an AD cohort (n = 344; AD with depression: n = 115, AD without depression: n = 120, healthy controls: n = 109), and an IBD subgroup (n = 75; IBD with depression: n = 25, IBD without depression: n = 30, healthy controls: n = 20). Gut microbial profiles were evaluated using 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V4 region). Microbial diversity was assessed via α- and β-diversity indices. Differential abundance analysis was conducted using Linear Discriminative Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) methods. Functional predictions were performed based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthology inference. Microbial co-occurrence networks were also constructed to explore taxonomic interaction patterns.ResultsIn the AD cohort, β-diversity was significantly reduced in patients with comorbid depression compared to those without, whereas α-diversity did not show statistically significant differences. A total of 40 microbial taxa were significantly different between patients with AD comorbid depression and AD without depression. In the IBD subgroup, 12 taxa were differentially abundant between patients with and without depression. Functional pathway predictions suggested disruptions in carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, and glycan biosynthesis in patients with AD and comorbid depression compared to those without depression. Microbial network analysis revealed distinct co-occurrence structures in patients with comorbid depression.ConclusionsThis exploratory study suggests that individuals with ADs or IBD and comorbid depression exhibit distinct gut microbiome compositions and functional potentials compared to non-depressed counterparts. These associations may offer insights into gut-brain interactions in autoimmunity and mental health. However, due to the cross-sectional design, mechanistic and clinical inferences remain speculative and require validation in future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05223-0.Keywords: Autoimmune diseases, Inflammatory bowel disease, Gut microbiome, Depression

Background

Depression is a common mental disorder, which causes individuals to suffer from long-term depressed mood, slow thinking and other symptoms, and has a wide range of effects on the physical, cognitive, emotional and social processes of patients [1]. An increasing body of evidence suggests that depression commonly co-occurs with autoimmune diseases (ADs), which involve aberrant immune responses targeting host tissues [2–4]. ADs comprise a heterogeneous group of chronic immune-mediated conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis, psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Among these, IBD is characterized by persistent inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract and has been consistently associated with alterations in gut microbial composition and function, making it particularly relevant for investigations of microbiome-related comorbidities [5]. Many epidemiological studies have found a high prevalence of comorbid depression in patients with different types of ADs, which can exacerbate disease severity, complicate treatment, and reduce quality of life [6, 7].

The gut microbiome has emerged as a potential factor linking depression and ADs through the gut-brain-immune axis [8]. Microbial dysbiosis has been associated with both inflammatory processes in ADs and neurobehavioral alterations seen in depression [9–11]. For instance, increased abundances of Veillonella and Bacteroides have been found linked to the inflammatory response, whereas reduced Prevotella has been associated with mood-regulated amino acid synthesis and modulation of host autoimmunity via bile acid pathways [8, 12–14]. This evidence suggests the important roles of gut microbes in the comorbidity of AD and depression. While mechanistic studies have provided insights into potential microbiota-mediated pathways in AD-depression comorbidity [8, 15], large-scale, population-based microbiome analyses remain limited, particularly for the broader spectrum of ADs beyond IBD. Given the distinct gastrointestinal involvement in IBD and its high depression comorbidity, we also aimed to explore whether IBD might present unique or shared microbial signatures compared to other ADs [7].

In this study, we analyzed two gut microbiome cohorts derived from the American Gut Project (AGP). The overall study population comprised individuals with ADs, with and without comorbid depression, as well as healthy controls. Within the AD cohort, an IBD subgroup was defined and analyzed separately to enable subgroup-specific comparisons. We have examined patterns of gut microbiota alterations across groups. We also predicted the functional pathways of differentially abundant microorganisms between groups, which may help identify potential biomarkers and provide new clues and methods for the diagnosis and treatment of comorbid AD and depression. By clarifying the intestinal microbial characteristics of patients with AD and depression comorbidities, it provides microbiological evidence for the pathophysiological mechanisms of the two comorbidities and facilitates the identification or discovery of biomarkers that can be used for the prediction, diagnosis, and treatment of the comorbidities-related biomarkers.

Results

Characteristics of study cohorts

In the AD cohort, an initial 1:1:1 propensity score matching was performed based on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diet type, smoking, and drinking status, yielding 150 individuals per group: (1) AD with comorbid depression, (2) AD without depression, and (3) healthy controls. The same matching strategy was applied to the IBD subgroup, yielding 33 individuals per group: (1) IBD with comorbid depression, (2) IBD without depression, and (3) healthy controls. A flowchart of cohort construction and matching is presented in Fig. 1. After propensity score matching, all baseline covariates achieved good balance among the three groups, with all standardized mean differences (SMD) values below 0.1 in the AD cohort. After quality control of microbiome data, several samples were excluded, resulting in slightly unequal group sizes. We reassessed covariate balance following this filtering step, and most covariates remained well balanced, with SMDs remaining below or near 0.1 (Table S1). Chi-square tests indicated no significant differences among groups for sex (P = 0.87), age (P = 0.49), or BMI (P = 0.70) in the AD cohort, suggesting that the groups were reasonably balanced for the available covariates. In the IBD subgroup, no significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed among the three groups after matching. Detailed baseline data and P-values are presented in Table 1 and Table S2. In the AD cohort, 150 samples per group were initially matched. After performing quality control and filtering out low-abundance taxa, 109 healthy control samples, 120 AD without depression samples, and 115 AD with depression samples were included for subsequent analysis. Similarly, in the IBD subgroup, 33 samples per group were initially matched. After the same quality control procedures, 20 healthy control samples, 30 IBD without depression samples, and 25 IBD with depression samples were included for subsequent analysis.

Distinct gut microbiome features were revealed in groups of autoimmune disease and depression cohort

We explored α-diversity, including microbial richness and evenness, among individuals with AD without depression, AD with depression, and healthy controls. The Shannon (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.06), Chao1 (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.48), ACE (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.26), and Simpson (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.15) indices showed no statistically significant differences among the three groups (Fig. 2A). However, when we analyzed β-diversity using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Jaccard distance metric, the permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) test results for pairwise comparisons between the three groups revealed statistically significant differences: AD without depression vs. healthy controls (R2 = 0.0056, P = 0.041), AD without depression vs. AD with depression (R2 = 0.0055, P = 0.041), and AD with depression vs. healthy controls (R2 = 0.0069, P = 0.003), as shown in Fig. 2B. However, the effect sizes were small, suggesting that group status explained only a limited proportion of the overall variation in microbial composition. Based on the Venn diagram of microbes in the three groups of the AD cohort (Fig. 2C), a total of 2,007 microbial features were identified. The AD with depression group had the most abundant number of gut microbes. Differential abundance analysis using the DESeq2 package identified dozens of gut microbes that were significantly different (P < 0.05) among the three groups, as visualized in the volcano plots (Fig. 2D-F). Linear Discriminative Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was further conducted to identify significantly differentially abundant taxa between groups, using a logarithmic Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score threshold of >= |2| and P < 0.05 (Kruskal-Wallis test). The LDA score reflects the effect size of each differentially abundant feature. Compared with the volcano plot analysis, LEfSe additionally evaluates the effect size and ranks taxa based on their discriminative ability. Twenty microbes were statistically different in AD without depression compared with the healthy controls (Fig. 3A), 55 microbes were statistically different in AD with depression compared with the healthy controls (Fig. 3B), and 40 microbes were statistically different in AD without depression compared with AD with depression (Fig. 3C). Some gut microbes were uniquely enriched in AD with depression group, including Prevotella copri (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), Veillonella parvula, Clostridium dispar, Aeromonas veronii, Eubacterium colinum, Phascolarctobacterium (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05). In addition to propensity score matching, we performed a sensitivity analysis stratified by exercise frequency (occasionally, regularly, and daily) to assess whether this variable influenced microbial composition and diversity results. Although the reduced sample sizes within subgroups limited statistical power, the β-diversity and differential abundance analyses showed broadly consistent patterns with the main results. For example, taxa such as Prevotella copri and Clostridium dispar exhibited similar trends across exercise frequency groups, suggesting that exercise frequency may have had limited impact on our overall findings (Figure S1-S3).

Functional analysis of the gut microbiome in the autoimmune disease and depression cohort

For the AD cohort, we performed functional annotation for gut microbes based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, as shown in Fig. 4, which presents the results at KEGG level 2. KEGG level 2 refers to functional subcategories, while level 3 refers to more specific pathways. Several metabolic pathways were significantly altered across group comparisons. Notably, pathways at KEGG level 2 involved in carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, and signal transduction were prominently affected. Regarding KEGG level 3 metabolic pathways, the most significantly different pathway between matched healthy controls and the AD without depression group was the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (P < 0.001). For the differential metabolic pathways between AD without depression and AD with depression, the phosphatidylinositol signaling system was the most significant pathway (P < 0.001) at level 3.

Compared with the IBD without depression group, the IBD with depression group showed significant enrichment in pathways related to glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, energy metabolism, biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, and signal transduction.

Association network of the gut microbiome in autoimmune disease without depression, autoimmune disease with depression and matched healthy controls

To better characterize microbial interaction patterns within each group, we constructed association networks for gut microbes in each group of the AD cohort (Fig. 5). These networks depict within-group microbial correlations, and differential abundance information was incorporated based on the corresponding volcano plot comparisons. Across the three groups, several microbial taxa were commonly observed in the association networks, such as the genus Oscillospira and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. In the association network of the AD with depression group (Fig. 5A), Phascolarctobacterium and Prevotella copri were identified as up-regulated relative to AD without depression. In the AD without depression network (Fig. 5B), Blautia and genus Ruminococcusin were up-regulated, whereas genus Bifidobacterium and Ruminococcus flavefaciens were down-regulated relative to healthy controls. When considering the comparison between AD with depression and healthy controls (Fig. 5C), Clostridium dispar was identified as an up-regulated taxon in the AD with depression group, while Prevotella copri was down-regulated.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated gut microbiota composition and predicted microbial functions in patients with ADs and IBD who reported comorbid depression. Individuals with depression exhibited alterations in microbial composition, mainly reflected by differences in specific microbial taxa compared to those without depression and healthy controls, while β-diversity differences were modest but statistically significant.

Some microbial features, such as alterations in Clostridium dispar, Prevotella copri, Veillonella, Odoribacter and members of the Paraprevotellaceae and Odoribacteraceae family, were observed across both the AD cohort and the IBD subgroup, suggesting potential shared microbial signatures associated with comorbid depression. These findings are consistent with accumulating evidence suggesting that specific gut microbial taxa may act as common factors underlying the gut–brain axis dysfunction observed in both ADs and depression. Prevotella copri has been increasingly associated with autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. Previous studies have shown that Prevotella species can trigger arthritis by activating the mucosal innate immune system in genetically susceptible hosts [16]. The presence of Prevotella copri has been strongly associated with the onset of rheumatoid arthritis [17], and it has been demonstrated that Prevotella copri can drive Th17 cell differentiation in an interleukin-6 (IL-6)-dependent manner, thereby promoting systemic inflammation [16]. In recent studies, Veillonella has been linked to depression. Integrated microbiome and metabolomic analyses have identified Veillonella as part of an aberrant microbial network in patients with major depressive disorder [18]. Enrichment of Veillonella has also been reported in patients with IBD, where it has been associated with pro-inflammatory potential [19]. Odoribacter and members of the Odoribacteraceae family appear to play protective roles in intestinal inflammation. For instance, Odoribacter splanchnicus has been shown to alleviate colitis by inhibiting neutrophil extracellular trap formation through secondary bile acid metabolism [20]. Its derived extracellular vesicles can also ameliorate IBD by regulating NLRP3 inflammasome activity and enhancing intestinal barrier function [21]. Notably, Odoribacter abundance has been reported to be negatively correlated with depression severity in IBD [22]. Additionally, Clostridium dispar has also been implicated in association with IBD [23].

The KEGG pathway analysis showed that the differential features identified in both the AD cohort and the IBD subgroup were consistently enriched in glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, energy metabolism, biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, and signal transduction pathways. Mucin-type O-glycans serve as essential barriers and immune anchors in IBD [24]. The gut microbiota has been demonstrated to alleviate depression by remodeling gut-brain energy metabolism [25]. It should be noted that the 16S rRNA-based functional inference is speculative and cannot capture strain-level variability, gene expression, or metabolic flux. These altered metabolism pathways may be relevant to comorbid depression in AD, which needs further validation by metagenomics, metabolomics, or experimental studies.

In the AD cohort specifically, we observed relative depletion of the phylum Firmicutes, classes Clostridia and Erysipelotrichi, and families Ruminococcaceae and Erysipelotrichaceae. The genus Faecalibacterium and species F. prausnitzii, both important butyrate producers, were also reduced. These patterns are consistent with reports of reduced Clostridia, Ruminococcaceae, and Faecalibacterium in multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and depression [26–31]. Such findings suggest that loss of these taxa may be associated with immune dysregulation and mood-related phenotypes, though causal inferences cannot be made. Conversely, enrichment of taxa such as order Caulobacterales, families Rhizobiaceae and Caulobacteraceae, genera Catenibacterium, Brevundimonas, Agrobacterium, and species Lactobacillus johnsonii was observed in AD patients with depression. Prior studies have reported increased Lactobacillus johnsonii in type 1 diabetes and alopecia areata [32, 33], and some probiotic interventions have suggested possible mood-modulating effects [34]. However, contradictory evidence exists for Catenibacterium, which was depleted in a Saudi Arabian IBD cohort [35]. Similarly, reports of Erysipelotrichaceae abundance vary across autoimmune and psychiatric conditions [36]. These discrepancies underscore the importance of interpreting microbial associations within the context of disease type, population, and methodology (Table S3).

This study has several limitations. First, since this is a cross-sectional study design, though we identified associations between gut microbial features and comorbid depression in AD and IBD, we cannot determine whether microbial changes precede, result from, or merely correlate with the disease state. Another limitation of our study is that both AD status and depression status were derived from self-reported AGP metadata. Importantly, while participants reported a diagnosis made by a medical professional, no independent clinical validation was performed. Moreover, due to limitations of the AGP survey, it was not possible to determine the specific type of autoimmune disease for each participant. Consequently, the accuracy and reliability of both AD and depression classifications may be limited, which could introduce misclassification bias and affect the interpretation of our findings. Future studies with clinically verified diagnoses are warranted to confirm these associations. In addition, although propensity score matching was employed to balance key demographic and lifestyle covariates such as age, sex, BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption, other important confounders could not be controlled. In particular, detailed dietary information beyond broad categories (omnivorous, vegetarian, vegan) was not available; differences in dietary habits may influence the gut microbiome and thus represent a potential uncontrolled confounder. Medication use, disease severity, disease duration, and treatment history are all known to influence the gut microbiome, but these variables were not available in the dataset. The differential abundance results presented in the volcano plots and related analyses were primarily based on nominal P-values rather than FDR adjusted P-values to retain sensitivity for detecting potential signals. In previous studies, similar approaches have also been employed, suggesting that this issue may be relatively common [37, 38]. However, the findings of the present study remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution, and further validation in independent cohorts with larger sample sizes is warranted.

All in all, we identified microbial taxa and predicted functional pathways potentially associated with comorbid depression in AD and IBD patients. While our results suggest distinct patterns of dysbiosis in these comorbidities, they remain exploratory and associative. Future studies should deploy longitudinal designs and incorporate multi-omics data, including metabolomic, transcriptomic, and clinical phenotyping, to more robustly characterize the gut-brain-immune axis in immune-mediated diseases with psychiatric comorbidities. These approaches may help to clarify the biological relevance of specific microbial features and their role in mediating host responses.

Conclusions

This study explored distinct gut microbial profiles and predicted functional alterations in patients with AD or IBD with comorbid depression, compared to those without depression and healthy controls. Key findings included significant differences in β-diversity and differential abundance of microbial taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Veillonella, and members of the Ruminococcaceae family. Functional prediction based on KEGG pathways suggested predicted alterations in carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, and glycan biosynthesis, which may reflect altered gut-brain-immune interactions in individuals with comorbid depression. These findings are associative and do not establish causality. The study is limited by its cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported diagnoses, and use of 16 S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V4 region, which restricts resolution and functional interpretation. Future longitudinal and multi-omics studies are needed to validate these observations and to further explore potential mechanistic links between the microbiome, immunity, and mental health.

Materials and methods

Data source and construction of study cohorts

The data for this study were obtained from the AGP, which is a global citizen science microbiome project that collected 15,096 samples microbial sequence data from 11,336 human participants from November 2012 through May 2017 [39]. Figure 1A shows a detailed flowchart about the construction of the study cohorts. In this study, individuals who reported a diagnosis of “AD,” “IBD,” or “type 1 diabetes” by a medical professional (doctor or physician assistant) were classified as AD patients. Participants were classified based on responses to specific questions in the AGP survey. For AD, participants were asked: “Have you been diagnosed with autoimmune disease such as Lupus (systemic lupus erythematosus), RA (rheumatoid arthritis), MS (multiple sclerosis), Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, or any other autoimmune disease?” Only those selecting “Diagnosed by a medical professional (doctor or physician assistant)” were included in the AD cohort. Participants reporting depression were identified from responses to: “Have you ever been diagnosed with mental health illness?” (Yes), followed by selecting “Depression” in the subsequent list. Participants who selected “I do not have this condition” were classified as not having depression. For IBD, participants were asked: “Have you ever been diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)?” Only those reporting diagnosis by a medical professional were included in the IBD subgroup. Based on mental health status, AD samples with a diagnosis of depression were identified, while those who selected “depression–bipolar–schizophrenia = I do not have this condition” were classified as not having depression. For healthy control samples, we included those aged 20–69 years with BMI between 18.5 and 30 without diagnosis of AD, IBD and diabetes, as well as no history of antibiotic use in the past year.

We further matched AD with depression, AD without depression and healthy samples with 1:1:1 propensity score based on age, gender, BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption. To assess covariate balance after matching, we calculated SMDs for each variables. An SMD < 0.1 was considered indicative of good balance. After implementing data preprocessing and quality filtering, the final sample size decreased from 150 per group to the numbers reported below. In total, the matched AD cohort consisted of 115 AD with depression samples, 120 AD without depression samples, and 109 healthy controls, yielding 344 fecal samples for analysis. The IBD subgroup was also matched using the same methodology, including IBD with depression (n = 25), IBD without depression (n = 30), and healthy controls (n = 20). The samples from the United States participants were collected by BBL culture swabs (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD) and returned by mail. Samples collected in Australia and the United Kingdom were first shipped via domestic post to a local aggregation site, stored at -80 °C and then shipped to the United States on dry ice using a certified shipping service. For all samples, Illumina HiSeq Rapid Run was used to sequence the V4 region of 16S rRNA. To protect the privacy of the participants, AGP processed the sequence data for de-identification and stored publicly in the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) database. Therefore, the data used in this study did not involve private information of study population. Batch effects and potential contamination cannot be completely avoided in large-scale, multi-site microbiome studies; however, we used identical protocols and the same sequencing platform to minimize their impact.

16S rRNA sequencing raw data processing

We downloaded the raw fastq files of the samples from the EBI database and used Qiime2 to process them [40]. First, we assessed the quality of these sequencing files using fastqc and then used cutadapt to remove primers and connectors from these sequences. Noisy sequences were removed using dada2 [41]. We removed reads with Q-scores of 35 or less to obtain a table of 16S rRNA amplicon sequence variants, which was finally annotated using the Greengenes_13_8_99%_515–806_classifier. To avoid the effect of zero inflation and reduce potential technical noise and improve statistical robustness, we removed low-abundance taxa with a total relative abundance of less than 0.01% from the feature table and excluded samples that failed to meet the quality control criteria or the minimum sequencing depth from downstream analyses. The details are shown in Fig. 1B.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (Version: 4.3.1). To explore the diversity of microbes in different groups, we performed α-diversity and β-diversity analyses after rarefying the sequencing data to depths determined from rarefaction curves to ensure adequate coverage (Figure S8). Specifically, 4,000 reads were used for the AD cohort and IBD subgroup, as these depths were sufficient to capture the majority of microbial diversity in each cohort while maximizing sample retention. α-diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and ACE) were calculated and compared across groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The results were visualized with ggplot2. As no overall differences were significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were not conducted. β-diversity was evaluated using the microeco package based on the Jaccard distance metric and visualized using PCoA. Group differences were evaluated using PERMANOVA [42] with 999 permutations, followed by pairwise comparisons to identify significant group pairs. LDA and LEfSe [43] were performed to identify microbes with statistically significant differences in pairwise comparisons between groups. LEfSe integrates non-parametric statistical testing and LDA to rank features according to their effect size. In this study, a microbial taxon was considered significantly differentially abundant when it met both criteria: P < 0.05 in the Kruskal-Wallis test and LDA score >= |2|. The volcano plot was used to show the significantly upregulated and downregulated microbes in pairwise comparisons between groups. Finally, functional prediction of microbial pathways was performed using Tax4Fun2. Predicted pathway abundances were aggregated at the sample level for each KEGG pathway, and then summarized by group. Group comparisons for KEGG pathways were conducted using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. KEGG level 2 refers to functional subcategories, while level 3 refers to more specific pathways. Pathway at level 2 with statistical differences were visualized by ggplot2. The variability analysis of the volcano plots was performed by the DESeq2 package, which reports Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P-values. Raw P-values were used to construct volcano plots, and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P-values were calculated to control for false discovery rate. Other analyses were performed using the microeco package [44]. Differences between the two groups in non-differential abundance analyses were tested by Wilcoxon Rank-Sum, and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis on the intergroup variable with significant differences (exercise frequency) to assess its potential impact on the results.

Ecological network analysis of gut microbes

We constructed microbial association networks to explore the co-occurrence patterns of gut microbes across groups. We used the microeco package in R (Version: 4.3.1) to perform Spearman rank correlation analysis between the abundance of gut microbes. Microbial association networks were constructed by including only pairs of microbes with statistically significant (P < 0.05) and absolute correlation coefficients above a threshold (r > = 0.3 in the AD cohort, r > = 0.4 in the IBD subgroup) [45]. Networks were visualized using Cytoscape (Version 3.7.2) [46]. The networks were treated as undirected graphs, where each node represents a microbial taxon. The size of each node and the thickness of connecting edges reflected the number of significant microbial correlations. Recurring microbes between groups were indicated in red. Other microbes with significant down-regulation and up-regulation were indicated in blue and yellow, respectively.
