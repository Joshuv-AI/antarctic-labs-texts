Optimization of the preparation of Gastrodia elata polysaccharide and investigation of their immunostimulatory activity in mice
Authors: Shengfang Wang, Baotang Zhao, Jing He, Yongpei Xiao, Jishan He, Yulong Huang, Mingze Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Gastrodia elata Bl. is a prized medicinal herb in China. As a perennial parasitic herb, also known as red arrow or tomorrow's gastrodia, and its dried rhizomes are of value for biological research[1], [2]. Other contemporary works have established that Gastrodia elata, as documented in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia and the Chinese Materia Medica, possesses the characteristics of sweetness moderating the liver, sweetness tonifying the spleen, and sweetness balancing the nature[3]. It is widely distributed across China, occurring in Jilin, Hebei, Gansu, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, and Tibet[4], [5],and is also found in East Asia, Southeast Asia, and Oceania[2]. In 2019, the substance was entered onto the pilot list for food and pharmaceutical products. Rich in nutrients, it possesses sedative properties and helps to calm the liver and subdue wind, exerting a regulatory effect on the central nervous system[6]. Gastrodia has been demonstrated to contain diverse bioactive constituents including phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, organic acids and sterols, whilst exhibiting multi-target pharmacological activity capable of influencing oxidative stress, immune regulation and various other physiological processes [7]. With systematic research into its biological activity, the development of related food products has encompassed alcoholic beverages, teas, biscuits, pastries, noodles, and other drinks[8]. Medicinal and health products made primarily from Gastrodia elata are available on the market in the form of injections, granules, capsules, tablets, pills and other conventional dosage forms. Different growing environments, geographical conditions and human factors can significantly affect the caliber and medicinal efficacy of Gastrodia elata[9]. Traditional Chinese medicinal herbs from Longnan, Gansu, form part of the symbiotic ‘ecological industry’ system in the western counties and districts, where the unique ecological environment fosters the authentic quality of Gastrodia elata[10]. In the future, the in-depth development of Gastrodia elata products and the enhancement of the added value of their extracts will remain one of the key directions for development.

Gastrodia elata polysaccharides possess a wide range of biological activities and potential applications, attracting considerable concerns across various fields. Existing research indicates that Gastrodia elata polysaccharides exhibit anti-tumor, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, neuroprotective, and cardiovascular disease-treating properties, as well as the ability to regulate intestinal barrier function[11], [12]. This extraction method is primarily based on traditional techniques, utilizing the solubility of polysaccharides to obtain a crude extract through hot-water reflux extraction. Although this method is simple to operate and relatively low-cost, it suffers from the drawbacks of low extraction efficiency and a time-consuming process[13]. In practice, approaches such as ultrasound, plant enzymes and microwave-assisted extraction have been integrated into traditional methods to enhance extraction efficiency. The cavitation effect of ultrasound and the thermal effect of microwaves are utilized to disrupt the cell wall structure of Gastrodia elata, thereby maximizing the retention of its bioactivity and accelerating the dissolution of polysaccharides[14], [15], [16].Enzymes commonly used in enzyme-assisted extraction include cellulase, pectinase, papain and amylase[17], [18]. By utilizing the specificity and high efficiency of enzymes, the cell wall structures or cross-linked matrices of the raw material can be disrupted under mild conditions, thereby reducing mass transfer resistance, accelerating the release and diffusion of target components, and consequently improving extraction yield[19]. Research on Gastrodia polysaccharides indicates that ultrasonically assisted enzymatic extraction of GEP-U yields a homogeneous, high-purity α-glucan with a final extraction rate of 13.30 % [20]. Ultrasound-enhanced enzymatic extraction using deep eutectic solvents (DES) achieved a GEPs yield of 25.22 %, representing a 3.11-fold increase over hot water extraction methods[21]. Ultrasound-assisted extraction of polysaccharides from three distinct Gynostemma varieties yielded rates of 11.32 %, 10.90 %, and 10.50 %, respectively, providing theoretical support for polysaccharide extraction from the aboveground parts of Gynostemma plants [22].

Although antibiotics are effective in treating intestinal infections and the associated immune dysfunction, they carry risks such as disruption of the gut microbiota, pathogen infection and the development of antibiotic resistance[23]. As natural compounds, plant polysaccharides offer low cost, low toxicity and high biocompatibility; they can adjust the structure of the gut microbiota and have demonstrated good potential for application in the prevention and control of metabolic diseases. With regard to gut modulation, polysaccharides derived from Gastrodia elata have been extensively studied. This study investigated alterations in the structural features of GEP during digestion and the effects of its digestion products on the gut microbiota during fermentation in human stools, demonstrating its potential efficacy as a prebiotic[24]. Li et al. used mouse colons to investigate the impact of GEPs on immunomodulatory activity, intestinal barrier function and gut microbiota regulation. The results indicated that these compounds significantly reversed the adverse effects of CTX on markers of immune organs in mice, modulated serum cytokine production and improved immunosuppression. Meanwhile, GEPs were able to restore the composition and abundance of the gut microbiota and increase the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the colon. They play a significant role in improving the gut microbiome and maintaining gut health, and may serve as raw materials for the development of functional foods that promote gut health[25]. Chen et al. isolated a specific fragment of Gastrodia elata polysaccharide and named it SRGP. Their research found that SRGP can increase CD4 + T-lymphocyte levels in colonic tissue and regulate intestinal immune function via the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. Using fecal samples, they investigated the physiological status of immunocompromised mice and found that SRGP promotes the proliferation of intestinal immune cells, regulates cytokine secretion, enhances intestinal barrier function, and aids in the restoration of the gut microbiota[7]. Using a sodium sulphate-induced mouse model of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), Xu et al. investigated the treatment value of GBP for IBD and its underlying mode of action, and found that disruption of the gut microbiota is a key factor in the onset and advancement of the disease[26]. GP treatment significantly improves the DSS-induced ulcerative colitis model in mice by a reduction in body loss and pathological damage, prolonging colon length, and enhancing intestinal barrier function through the suppression of inflammatory cytokines and associated proteins, as well as the promotion of the expression of functional proteins in colonic tissue; it also exerts a positive regulatory effect on the gut microbiota[27]. Selenium is an essential trace element that is widely distributed throughout various tissues and organs and plays a role in regulating numerous physiological functions[28]. Compared with traditional selenium supplements, Seleno-chitooligosaccharide offer the advantages of lower toxicity and higher bioavailability; nonetheless, differences in their composition and a lack of clinical validation limit their potential applications[29]. For example, the Seleno-chitooligosaccharide (SOA) studied by He et al. were found to modulate mechanical, biological and immune barrier functions in a mouse model, providing support for the use of SOA as a dietary supplement with prospective applications in improving intestinal mucosal permeability[30].

The gut is a crucial organ in the body, serving a crucial part in digestion, nutrient absorption, balance of the gut microbiota, and metabolizing immune functions[31]. The microbial community regulates the maturation of immune tissues and immune responses through its metabolic products and structural components, thereby maintaining the immunological homeostasis of the intestinal mucosa[32]. Immune regulation is closely linked to the balance of the gut microbiota; any disruption to this balance may trigger a range of immune-related conditions, including inflammatory bowel disease, allergies and autoimmune diseases[33]. As one of the key organs studied in gut microbiota, the caecum fulfils an important role in the metabolism of substances such as dietary fibre through fermentation by gut microbes. It also contributes to immune defense, water absorption and the maintenance of the gut microbiome balance, and is of significant importance to the body’s metabolism and gut health[34]. Moderate dietary consumption of selenium has been substantiated as having a supportive therapeutic benefit for various conditions. Given the excellent properties of Gastrodia elata Polysaccharide, this study aims to investigate their regulatory effects on the mouse organism following selenization, with a view to enhancing the body’s immune regulatory function.

Materials and methods

Reagents and consumables

Cellulase, pectinase (Beijing Jinming Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), acetone, anhydrous ethanol, n-butanol (Chengdu Jinshan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), chloroform (Sichuan Jinshan Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), phenol, concentrated sulphuric acid (Guangdong DaXiao Chemical Co., Ltd.), glucose, petroleum ether (Tianjin Hengxing Chemical Reagent Manufacturing Co., Ltd.), Cyclophosphamide (Hainan Puli Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), Astragalus polysaccharides (Shaanxi Angxu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), IL-1, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 assay kits (Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), SOD, CAT, MDA (Beijing Solabio Technology Co., Ltd.).

Preparation of Gastrodia polysaccharides

Fresh gastrodia slices are sun-dried and pulverized through an 80-mesh sieve. Petroleum ether is added, and the mixture is macerated and stirred to remove fatty impurities. The petroleum ether is replaced every 12 h. After 24 h, replace with anhydrous ethanol. Soak and stir to thoroughly remove small molecules, pigments, and oligosaccharides. Replace the ethanol every 12 h, continuing the soak until the ethanol is essentially colorless. Collect the precipitate and dry it to receive the pretreated gastrodia powder. Accurately weigh out 3 g of pre-processed Gastrodia elata powder, add deionized water in accordance with the specified solid-to-liquid ratio, add an appropriate proportion of mixed enzymes (cellulase: pectinase = 1:1), and carry out enzymatic hydrolysis at 45 °C for 2 h. Under the established conditions, perform ultrasonic extraction, centrifuge at 3800 r/min for 10 min, and collect the supernatant. The enzyme was inactivated by boiling for 10 min, followed by concentration and ethanol precipitation at 4 °C for 12 h.. Wash the solid precipitate three times with acetone and anhydrous ethanol. After evaporating the organic solvents, dissolve the precipitate in the solvent, then centrifuge and collect the upper supernatant. Protein removal was performed using the Sevag method, followed by 48 h dialysis. The resulting dried polysaccharide was freeze-dried and designated GP. Polysaccharide level was determined employing the phenol–sulphuric acid method.

Single-factor experiments

Using ultrasonic power of 300 W, ultrasonic temperature of 45 °C, solid–liquid ratio of 1:40, ultrasonic duration of 40 min, and enzyme addition of 1.8 % as standard conditions, the following parameters were investigated: ultrasonic temperatures of (35 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, 55 °C), solid-to-liquid ratios of (1:20, 1:30, 1:40, 1:50, 1:60), ultrasonication duration (30 min, 35 min, 40 min, 45 min, 50 min), and enzyme loading (0.6 %, 1.2 %, 1.8 %, 2.4 %, 3 %).

Ultrasound-Assisted composite enzyme extraction kinetic model for GP

This experiment utilized Gastrodia elata as the raw material. The polysaccharide extraction primarily entails solvent permeation, internal diffusion, and external diffusion. Consequently, Fick's second law was applied to investigate the kinetic model of GP extraction. Referencing the methodologies of Zhang [35] and Hou et al. [36], under conditions of 300 W ultrasonic power, 1.8 % enzyme addition, and a 1:40 mg/mL feed solution ratio, the effects of varying temperatures (35 −55 °C) on polysaccharide extraction rates were examined. Concurrently, thermodynamic parameters of GP were analyzed using the Arrhenius equation to evaluate thermodynamic behavior across these temperatures.

DSC measurement

Referencing the methodology of Zhu et al.[37], certain parameters were adjusted as follows: 10 mg of sample, a the temperature ramp rate at 10 °C/min, and the temperature interval at 30-250 °C. This investigation explored the thermal transformations and thermal effects occurring within the sample.

Uv–Vis and FT-IR

Prepare a sample solution at 2 mg/mL, using ultrapure water as the reference solution. Scan within the wavelength range at 200–400 nm, observing the absorption curves at 260 nm and 280 nm to determine whether GP contain substances such as proteins or nucleic acids. Mix the freeze-dried GP with dried KBr in a 1:100 ratio, grind into a fine powder, and press into a pellet. Perform a scan in the wavelength covering 4000–400 cm−1 to observe the infrared spectral characteristics of the sample.

Congo red (CR) and methylene blue (MB) assay

Following the methodology of Feng et al.[38], a preliminary assessment of triple-helix structures in various polysaccharide preparations was conducted. 1 mL of polysaccharide solution (2 mg/mL) was uniformly mixed with 1 mL of Congo red solution (80 μmol/L) in a test tube. NaOH solutions (0, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50 mol/L), reacted for 5–10 min, and then detected the maximum absorption wavelength between 200–600 nm using UV spectroscopy. Plot the maximum absorption wavelength against NaOH concentration on the x- and y-axes, correspondingly. Referencing the method of Wang et al.[39], use methylene blue to detect different polysaccharides and determine changes in polysaccharide molecular conformation.

SEM

After drying the samples, place small quantities onto conductive adhesive on the specimen stage. Following gold-coating treatment, observe the morphology of the polysaccharides at magnifications of 100×, 500×, and 1000 × under a 30 kV voltage.

XRD

A specific quantity of the freeze-dried sample was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer to examine the crystal structure of the polysaccharide. The parameters were set as follows: The experiments were conducted in the 2θ range of 5–40°, with a tube tension of 35 kV, a tube current of 25 mA, and a scanning rate of 2°/min.

In vivo experiments

Preparation of selenized polysaccharides

Place 0.5 g of GP in a conical flask, add 50 mL of nitric acid solution of a specific concentration (0.8 %), heat and stir gently to obtain a GP solution with a concentration of 10 mg/mL. Add an appropriate amount of sodium selenite (in a 1:1 ratio) and stir at 70 °C for 8 h. After the reaction, cool to room temperature and adjust the pH to 5–6 using sodium bicarbonate (0.4 %). Centrifuge the reaction mixture at 4000 rpm for 5 min. Take a small amount of the dialysate and add ascorbic acid for a color test; if no red color is observed, stop the dialysis and freeze-dry to obtain selenized Gastrodia elata polysaccharide (GP-SE).

Development of animal models

The mice were housed in a sterile environment maintained at a temperature of 22–26 °C and a humidity of 44–45 %, and were subjected to a 12/12-hour light–dark cycle. Following an acclimatization period, the KM mice were arbitrarily assigned into seven groups, each comprising 10 mice. Mice were first administered 60 mg/kg of cyclophosphamide (CTX) via intraperitoneal injection for 3 continuous days, with the exception of the normal control group (NC group). Mice in the NC group and the model group (CTX group) were administered saline via oral gavage, whilst mice in the Gastrodia elata polysaccharide group (KB group) and the positive control group (LC group) were administered 100 mg/kg of GP and Astragalus polysaccharide daily via oral gavage, respectively. mice in the low, medium and high dose groups of selenium-modified Gastrodia elata polysaccharides (GP-SE-L, GP-SE-M and GP-SE-H groups) were treated with 50 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg and 150 mg/kg, respectively. After 17 consecutive days of gavage, all mice were placed on a strict fast on the final day, although they were still allowed to drink water freely as usual. During this period, the mice’s coat color, body weight, general condition and excretion were observed. Blood was drawn from the orbital vein, after which the mice were anaesthetized, decapitated, dissected and their internal organs removed. These were rinsed with saline, drained of excess fluid, weighed and placed in anticoagulant tubes for storage at −80 °C until required.

Measurement of body weight and organ Indices

Mice were weighed every three days during the rearing period; following dissection, spleen tissue was collected and weighed. The formula (3) is as follows:(3)Organ index (%) = Organ mass/Mouse body weight × 100

Inflammatory cytokine assay

Assays were performed using kits to measure the levels of IL-1, IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 in the spleen.

Assessment of antioxidant factors

A kit was used to determine the levels of SOD, CAT and MDA in the spleen.

Effects on the caecum microbiota of immunocompromised mice

Genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using a commercial kit, and the purity and concentration of the DNA were assessed. Following the selection of the sequencing region, PCR amplification was performed on the selected V3-V4 variable regions. Library preparation was carried out using an Illumina library preparation kit, and quality control was performed using a multifunctional microfluidic bioanalyser and a fluorometer; sequencing was then conducted upon passing these checks. Following processing using Cutadapt software, the DADA2 package and Qiime2 software, in conjunction with the Silva (version 138) database and the Greengenes database, analyses were conducted on community diversity, inter-group differences and functional annotation.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized using Excel and plotted using OriginPro 2026. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Analysis of results

Results of single-factor experiments

Fig. 1(A) illustrates the variation in GP yield at ultrasonic power levels ranging from 200 to 400 W. At 250 W, the highest polysaccharide yield obtained was 17.49 %. Overall, as the ultrasound power increases, the polysaccharide yield rises initially and then falls. This may be attributed to insufficient cavitation effect at lower powers, leading to incomplete polysaccharide extraction, while excessive cavitation beyond 250 W caused polysaccharide degradation. Compared with the ultrasonic-assisted extraction of Selaginella tamariscina polysaccharides (STP), the effect of ultrasonic power on the STP extraction rate was consistent with the trend observed for GP. The higher yield of GP compared to STP may reflect not only differences in the raw materials but also the critical role of enzymatic hydrolysis as a key process step[42].Fig. 1Effect of ultrasonic power (A), ultrasonic temperature (B), ultrasonic time (C), solid-to-liquid ratio (D), and enzyme dosage (E) on the yield of GP.

Effect of ultrasonic power (A), ultrasonic temperature (B), ultrasonic time (C), solid-to-liquid ratio (D), and enzyme dosage (E) on the yield of GP.

Fig. 1(B) illustrates the variation in GP yield at ultrasonic temperatures ranging from 35 to 55 °C. At 40 °C, the highest yield of GP was achieved at 23.16 %. As the ultrasonic temperature rises, the yield of polysaccharides first increases and then decline. This may be due to inadequate enzymatic action at low temperatures to dissolve polysaccharides, while excessively high temperatures potentially accelerate solvent evaporation and diminish ultrasonic efficacy [43], thereby disrupting polysaccharide structure and enzymatic activity. For Tricholoma matsutake polysaccharides (TMP) extracted using the same method, the temperature range was set between 40 and 80 °C. The positive correlation between temperature and TMP yield may be attributed to the fact that higher temperatures enhance cavitation and diffusion effects. At temperatures exceeding 80 °C, the TMP yield decreases, likely because reduced surface tension and viscosity inhibit ultrasonic activity. The selection of a specific temperature range may be influenced by factors such as the characteristics of the raw material, the enzymatic system used, and the extraction target[44].

Fig. 1(C) illustrates the variation in GP yield over an ultrasonication time range of 20 to 60 min. The highest yield of 19.58 % was achieved at 40 min. As the duration of ultrasonic treatment is extended, the yield of polysaccharides first rises, then falls. It is likely that, during shorter treatment times, preventing adequate enzyme-substrate contact and hindering the dissolution of macromolecules like polysaccharides. By contrast, prolonged ultrasonic treatment accelerates the dissolution and diffusion of impurities, whilst leading to enzyme inactivation and polysaccharide degradation[17]. Studies have found that the trend in TMP yield under ultrasonication times of 5–30 min is consistent with that of GP. The decline in TMP yield may be due to adverse effects caused by prolonged exposure, such as damage to polysaccharides resulting from high system pressure and shear stress, as well as potential changes in enzyme activity. Differences in ultrasonication time are likely primarily influenced by variations in the structural complexity of the raw material cell walls and differences in mass transfer resistance[44].

Fig. 1(D) demonstrates the variation in GP yield at different solid-to-liquid ratios. When the solid-to-liquid ratio was 1:30, the yield reached a maximum of 22.26 %. If the solvent is insufficient, the polysaccharide concentration rapidly reaches saturation, thereby reducing the diffusion capacity and preventing further extraction of the polysaccharide. As the solid-to-liquid ratio raises, the polysaccharide continues to diffuse from the interior to the exterior of the raw material until a dissolution equilibrium is reached. Excessive solvent usage may lead to a reduction in the cavitation effect, decreased enzyme activity, and dilution of the polysaccharide concentration[45]. The trends in extraction yields of Selaginella tamariscina polysaccharides (STP) from ultrasonic-assisted extraction and Shatian pomelo peel polysaccharides (StPP) from ultrasonic-assisted enzymatic extraction as the solid-to-liquid ratio increases are consistent with the findings of this research[17], [42].

Fig. 1(E) displays the variation in GP yield at different enzyme concentrations. A maximum polysaccharide yield of 17.67 % was obtained at an enzyme concentration of 1.8 %. Increasing the enzyme dosage allows more enzyme molecules to act on the substrate, promoting further hydrolysis of the plant cell wall and accelerating the release of polysaccharides. However, excessively high enzyme concentrations may lead to substrate saturation, causing small protein molecules to become embedded within the polysaccharide components and reducing polysaccharide yield[46]. When preparing Ligustrum robustum polysaccharides (LRCP) using the same enzyme complex, the enzyme concentration range was set at 1–4 %. The highest LRCP extraction rate was obtained at an enzyme concentration of 3.0 %, with a trend similar to that observed for GP[47].

Optimization validation trial

The optimal process for ultrasonic-assisted composite enzyme extraction of GP was determined as follows: ultrasonication temperature of 39.58 °C, solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30.9 mg/mL, and ultrasonication duration of 38.48 min. yielding a maximum polysaccharide recovery rate of 21.73 %. Under practical conditions, the optimal process was adjusted to: ultrasonication temperature of 40 °C, solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30 mg/mL, and ultrasonication duration of 40 min. This yielded a polysaccharide recovery rate of 20.23 %, showing minimal deviation from the theoretical value. This demonstrates the feasibility of response surface modelling for optimizing GP extraction processes.

Extraction kinetics

Rate constant k

The polysaccharide mass concentration is typically maintained at a relatively stable level, i.e., the equilibrium concentration is attained. As shown in Table 4, at a constant temperature, the GP polysaccharide concentration exhibits an increasing trend with ultrasonication time, reaching equilibrium after 60 min. Under fixed solid-to-liquid ratio conditions, the apparent rate constant k increases gradually with rising temperatures between 309 and 329 K, indicating that elevated temperatures enhance the kinetic energy of solvent molecules, facilitating their penetration into the interior of solid particles and breaking the glycosidic bonds between polysaccharides. Relative extraction residue rate

The relative extraction rate is a measure of the degree to which the system approaches equilibrium during the extraction process, in the context of polysaccharide extraction kinetics. As shown in Table 5, the curves with R22 > 0.9 in the fitted exponential model demonstrate good fitting accuracy, suggesting that the GP extraction process conforms to the exponential model.

When extraction time is 0 min and the initial concentration is set as C0 = 0, the relative extraction rate simplifies to Y = (C_∞ − C) / C_∞. As shown in Fig. 3 (B), at a constant temperature, the relative extraction rate tends to decrease with increasing extraction time, indicating that the extraction rate of GP slows down during the process. The variation in relative extraction rate differs across temperatures. This experimental outcome contrasts with the findings of Zhang et al. [35], potentially due to temperature-induced alterations in enzyme activity and structure, thereby influencing polysaccharide extraction rates. Within the 309–324 K temperature range, elevated temperatures both accelerate polysaccharide dissolution and enhance initial release. This may result from improved solvent permeability and polysaccharide solubility at higher temperatures, coupled with damage of plant cell wall structures[21].

Effective diffusion coefficient

Research indicates that the isolation of bioactive compounds from plants is frequently constrained by internal diffusion, with the effective diffusion coefficient characterizing the mass transfer rate of the desired solute from the solid (or particulate) matrix towards the particle surface [48]. Using the formula k = π2Du/R2 and Table 5, Du can be calculated as Du = kR2/π2. Assuming that the radius R of the GP powder is 0.2 mm and π is 3.14, it can be seen from Fig. 3(C) that the Du value of the GP powder increases with rising temperature. This may be due to water molecules diffusing into the interior of the particles, dissolving the internal polysaccharides, and then diffusing outwards to the particle surface before ultimately entering the solvent. A plausible mechanism is that, simultaneously, the temperature promotes an increase in the rate of enzymatic reactions, thus accelerating mass transfer within the particles. Guo et al. [49] observed, using a two-site model, that an increase in temperature can effectively disrupt the structural integrity of Ganoderma lucidum. This disruption reduces the viscosity of the solvent, increases the diffusion coefficient and the solute partition coefficient, and facilitates the dissolution of more sugars into the solvent.

Half-life

The half-life t1/2 represents the time required to reach half of the equilibrium concentration, and the shorter this time is, the faster the extraction rate (Fig. 3(D)). In C = C∞/2; simplifying yields t1/2 = ln (1.2158/k). Table 5 indicates that the linear fit for t1/2 versus temperature is y = -0.0333x + 13.0489, with R2 = 0.9881. This confirms a good linear regression relationship, collectively demonstrating that the time required for polysaccharide extraction concentration to reach half the equilibrium concentration decreases with increasing temperature. This observation is in line with the findings of Fu et al. [50].

Activation energy

Ea denotes the energy required for a molecule to transit from its base state to an activated state; the lesser its value, the speedier the reaction rate [20]. As presented in Table 5, the rate constant follows the Arrhenius indefinite integral formula with temperature, namely ln k = -lnA + Ea/(RT). As shown in Fig. 3 (E), the fitted curve for −lnk versus 1/T is y = 1.3961x − 0.1861, with R2 = 0.9886. This indicates a good linear relationship for the regression equation. Taking R = 8.314, Ea is calculated to be 11.61 kJ/mol.

Uv–vis and FT-IR analysis

As depicted in Fig. 4 (B), no distinct characteristic peaks were observed for GP at 260 nm and 280 nm, implying that the majority of nucleic acids and proteins in the polysaccharide had been removed. The characteristic functional groups of polysaccharides produce distinctive absorption peaks in infrared spectroscopy scans (Fig. 4(C)). The positions of these characteristic absorption peaks can be used to infer the monosaccharide types, glycosidic bond types, and sugar ring types present in the polysaccharide[53]. The peak at 3400 cm−1 represents the O–H stretching vibration in the hydroxyl group[54]. The peak at 2928 cm−1 represents the C–H stretching vibration in the methyl group [41], while the absorption peak at 1648 cm−1 may correspond to the C=O stretching vibration in the carbonyl group (CHO), potentially indicating the presence of glucuronic acid[55]. The peak at 1368 cm−1 denotes C–H stretching vibrations, while the peak ranges from 1154 cm−1 to 1018 cm−1 originates from the stretching vibrations of the pyranose ring. The polysaccharide exhibits an α configuration at 850 cm−1[56].

CR analysis

The triple helix conformation enhances structural stability and improves resistance to degradation, which helps maintain and enhance biological activity [57], [58]. The triple helix structure in polysaccharides can provide a specific spatial conformation, influencing the recognition, binding, and signal activation with biological targets, thereby linking the structural characteristics of polysaccharides to their biological functions[59]. As depicted in Fig. 4 (D), with the increase of NaOH concentration, the λmax of the CR group undergoes a blue shift, while the GP sample group shows a red shift, indicating a change in conformation[37]. Under different concentrations of sodium hydroxide, the λmax of the GP sample group showed a trend of first rising, then falling, and then rising again, which was different from that of the CR control group. This indicates that the polysaccharide chain may have retained its triple helix structure [60]. These findings diverge from the results reported by Liu et al[61], which may be related to the species of Gastrodia elata used and its inherent characteristics.

MB determination

As shown in Fig. 4(E-F), solutions of various concentrations of methylene blue (MB) and polysaccharide (GP) exhibit a main peak at 665 nm and a shoulder peak at 615 nm. As the concentration of GP increases, the optical density (OD) corresponding to the 665 nm peak varies between approximately 0.3 and 0.83, whereas the OD of the 615 nm peak remains stable between 0.22 and 0.4. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), as linear polysaccharides containing periodic anions, interact with MB primarily through electrostatic forces, with sulfate groups playing a particularly important role[62]. In similar studies on polysaccharides, analysis of changes in surface charge in polysaccharide-based biopolymers such as starch before and after MB adsorption, combined with FTIR spectroscopy, has demonstrated that MB adsorption relies primarily on electrostatic interactions[63]. It is therefore likely that MB and GP form complexes via electrostatic interactions.

Morphological observation

As observed in Fig. 4 (G-H), under 100 × magnification, the GP surface exhibited a smooth, loose, and layered structure with visible fracture signs. At higher magnifications, distinct, irregular pores and cracks were observed on the GP surface, likely due to the destruction of polysaccharide structures by ultrasonic cavitation and enzymatic hydrolysis. Droplet-like protrusions may represent aggregates formed by partially degraded polysaccharide chains. The synergistic action of ultrasound and enzymes led to the disruption of glycosidic bonds both within and between polysaccharide molecules. This process decomposed the layered structure and aggregates of GP into small segments bearing filamentous branches, resulting in a loosely organized morphology [64]. Furthermore, the freeze-drying process may also influence the structure of this polysaccharide [65].

XRD analysis

As shown in Fig. 4 (I), the GP exhibits generally gentle fluctuations at 2θ = 15°-20°, with broad diffraction peaks that appear diffuse. This indicates that the polysaccharide is amorphous or weakly crystalline. The broadening and smoothing of the polysaccharide’s diffraction peaks may be due to the disruption of its ordered structure by enzymes or ultrasonication; the findings of this study in agreement with those of Wang et al. regarding plant polysaccharides[66], [67].

Antioxidant activity analysis

Conversely, non-active constituents within Gastrodia powder may dilute or inhibit the antioxidant activity. Biochemical tests

Assay of IL-1, IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6

IL-1 is one of the most crucial pro-inflammatory cytokines, responsible for regulating immune and inflammatory response[71]. It promotes the growth of B cell and T cells, upregulates the expression and formation of chemokines and inflammatory cytokines, and induces the production of ROS and NO; it is a potent adjuvant for antigen-specific antibody responses in living organisms[72]. IL-1β is a key immunomodulatory and pro-inflammatory cytokine, primarily secreted by monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells, and plays a significant role within the IL-1 family[73]. As displayed in Fig. 7 (A–B), the contents of IL-1 and IL-1β in the spleen increased following CTX injection, but returned to normal after oral administration of GP-SE. Compared with the changes in IL-1 levels in the GP-SE group, the differences in IL-1β levels between the different dose groups were more pronounced, possibly due to the different regulatory mechanisms of these cytokines. Relative to the KB group, the regulatory effect of the GP-SE group was closer to that of the control group; the chemical selenization process conferred greater regulatory capacity to GP in the spleen. In summary, GP-SE exerts a beneficial protective effect against CTX-induced splenic inflammatory responses and demonstrates immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects.Fig. 7Measurement of IL-1 (A), IL-1β (B), TNF-α (C), IL-6 (D), SOD (E), CAT (F) and MDA (G) levels.

Measurement of IL-1 (A), IL-1β (B), TNF-α (C), IL-6 (D), SOD (E), CAT (F) and MDA (G) levels.

TNF-α is a multifunctional cytokine involved in host defense, inflammation and apoptosis. It plays a twofold part in immune regulation, acting both as a pro-inflammatory mediator and an immunosuppressive factor; it maintains immune homeostasis by limiting the intensity and duration of inflammation, and is primarily secreted by macrophages[74]. Fig. 7(C) indicates that the level of TNF-α in the spleen decreases following CTX injection. This may be due to CTX inducing apoptosis in immune cells such as lymphocytes and macrophages within the spleen, whilst also inhibiting their proliferation and activation, thereby reducing both the number and function of cells secreting TNF-α. GP-SE can counteract the inhibitory effects of CTX by enhancing the activity of immune cells, promoting the functional recovery of macrophages and T cells, and alleviating CTX-induced oxidative stress and cellular damage. This leads to a rebound or normalization of splenic TNF-α levels, thereby exerting immunomodulatory and protective effects. There were significant differences between the various GP-SE dose groups and the NC group; TNF-α secretion in the GP-SE-M and GP-SE-H groups was higher than in the KB group, suggesting that different doses of the same selenized polysaccharide may have facilitated the recovery of TNF-α levels.

IL-6 is a polyfunctional cytokine that acts on a broad range of biological pathways and organs. It can induce B cells to differentiate into mature memory B cells and plasma cells, and prompt the differentiation of naive T cells into Th17 cells, thereby conferring the ability to produce antibodies[70], [75]. As shown in Fig. 7(D), the level of IL-6 in the spleen increased following CTX injection. Compared with the CTX group, IL-6 levels decreased significantly as the GP-SE dose increased. There were no significant differences in IL-6 levels between the GP-SE-M and GP-SE-H groups and the NC group. CTX induction may cause damage to splenic immune cells, trigger an inflammatory response, and affect IL-6 sretion. Concurrently, the expression of IL-6 is induced by pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1, IL-1β and TNF-α, thereby weakening the positive regulation of IL-6. GP-SE exerts an immunoprotected and regulatory effect by mitigating CTX-induced damage to immune cells, promoting the functional recovery of macrophages and lymphocytes, and restoring cytokine levels in the spleen to normal. Rhodotorula mucilaginosa JAASSRY1 significantly reduces CTX-induced IL-6 levels in the spleen. The study suggests that JAASRY may restore the secretion of cytokines and immunoglobulins to normal levels by activating lymphocytes and regulating cellular and humoral immune responses[76]. Artym et al. found that following cyclophosphamide treatment, certain macrophages, neutrophils, and mast cells may be responsible for the elevated IL-6 levels in the spleen, and that these elevated IL-6 levels contribute to the clearance of bacteria infecting the organs of mice [77]. Assay of SOD, CAT and MDA

Free radicals produced during oxidative damage can modulate the expression of relevant immune molecules, trigger inflammatory responses, and further induce cell apoptosis, leading to lipid peroxidation and causing damage to organs and tissues[78]. CAT is the main enzyme involved in the removal of H2O2, whilst SOD is not only a superoxide anion scavenger but also the main enzyme responsible for the production of H2O2; it supports immune tolerance and anti-inflammatory effects, and plays a vital role in the biological antioxidant system[79]. Fig. 7 (E–F) shows that SOD and CAT activities in the spleens of mice in the CTX group were markedly lower than those in the NC group, indicating that CTX notably impairs the antioxidant capacity of mice; these activities increased following oral administration of different types or doses of polysaccharides. Notably, an increase in the dose of GP-SE caused SOD and CAT activity to follow a pattern of initially increasing and then decreasing. This may be because low doses of selenized polysaccharides promote the proliferation of splenic lymphocytes, leading to an elevated splenic index, vigorous cellular metabolism and increased enzyme activity, whereas high doses may induce apoptosis or necrosis in splenic tissue. MDA is a product of the breakdown of lipid peroxides and is commonly used as a marker of the extent of lipid oxidation[80]. Fig. 7 (G) shows that MDA levels were higher in the CTX group than in the NC group, and that MDA levels decreased following polysaccharide treatment. Taken together, the SOD, CAT and MDA results indicate that CTX induces oxidative stress and immunotoxicity in the body, whereas polysaccharides can alleviate this oxidative damage and modulate the body’s immune status.

KEGG analysis

There were significant differences in the distribution of functional pathways between the GP-SE group and the CTX group. As shown in Fig. 10(C), CTX was more enriched in pathways related to genetic information processing and human diseases than the normal control group, suggesting that CTX may exhibit greater activity in functions associated with pathogen infection. The GP-SE group exhibited significant differences from the CTX group in metabolism, cellular processes and other functions, gradually converging with the NC group following regulation, suggesting that GP-SE possesses protective or immunomodulatory effects. According to Fig. 10(D), in terms of specific pathways, the CTX group exhibited higher enrichment in functional categories such as carbohydrate metabolism, the secretory system, bacterial infectious diseases, transcription and translation, reflecting multifaceted stress and functional disruption in the body following cyclophosphamide treatment. Concurrently, the GP-SE group also exhibited higher activity in pathways related to stress responses (such as environmental adaptation, cell growth and death), suggesting that this treatment may effectively activate the bodies or cells’ stress defense and repair mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 10(E), in the ‘Endocrine and Metabolic Diseases’ pathway, the GP-SE group was higher than the NC and CTX groups, indicating that the treatment may have improved the endocrine and metabolic disorders induced by CTX; in the ‘Bacterial Infections’ pathway, the CTX group had the highest abundance among the three groups. Fig. 10(F) illustrates the intergroup differences across three metabolic and regulatory pathways. In the ‘C5 branched-chain dicarboxylic acid metabolism’ pathway, the relative abundance in the CTX group decreased compared to the NC group, whilst the GP-SE group showed a rebound compared to the CTX group, suggesting that the treatment group may have promoted the catabolism of branched-chain amino acids. The three groups also exhibited the same trend in the “nitrotoluene degradation” pathway and the “multispecies longevity regulation pathway”. This may be because CTX caused an impairment in the catabolism of branched-chain amino acids, whilst simultaneously weakening the body’s ability to degrade exogenous harmful substances (such as nitrotoluene compounds) and inhibiting longevity regulation pathways associated with anti-ageing and stress adaptation. Overall, the STAMP results suggest that GP-SE can partially reverse CTX-induced metabolic alterations, particularly in the regulation of endocrine metabolism and branched-chain amino acid catabolism, although the differences in changes between groups were not entirely significant.

Discussion and conclusions

Single-factor experiments were conducted using ultrasonic-assisted composite enzymes (cellulase and pectinase) under varying ultrasonic power (200–400 W), ultrasonic temperature (35–55 °C), solid–liquid ratio (1:20–1:60), ultrasonication duration (30–50 min), and enzyme loading (0.6 %–3 %). Response surface optimization yielded an optimal yield of 21.73 % for GP.

By integrating the polysaccharide extraction process with Fick’s second law, the effects of temperature and time variations on the relevant parameters of polysaccharide extraction have been systematically demonstrated. An appropriate increase in temperature enhances the thermal motion of solvent molecules and enzyme activity, accelerating cell wall lysis and promoting the diffusion of polysaccharide molecules. Controlling the extraction time plays a crucial role in the industrial production of polysaccharides. When the polysaccharide concentration reaches dynamic equilibrium, prolonging the extraction time results in a waste of energy and time. It may also lead to structural damage to the polysaccharides and enzymes, causing polysaccharide degradation and enzyme inactivation, and potentially generating harmful substances. Thermodynamic analysis indicates that this extraction process is a non-spontaneous endothermic reaction, a finding consistent with the results obtained from differential scanning calorimetry (DSC).

In vitro antioxidant analyses indicate that, compared with the crude extract powder, the polysaccharide itself exhibits superior antioxidant properties, with its antioxidant capacity exhibiting a dose-dependent trend. This confirms the reliability and controllability of their antioxidant activity, laying the foundation for the precise development of antioxidant products. Future research into their specific mechanisms of action holds broad development prospects.

Immune modulation is one of the key biological activities of polysaccharides. As a commonly studied immune organ, the structure and functional status of the spleen can, to a certain extent, reflect the body’s immune regulatory state and are frequently used as key indicators for evaluating the efficacy of immune interventions. At the same time, there is a reciprocal regulatory relationship between the gut microbiota and the host immune system; the microbiota and its metabolites influence a variety of physiological processes in the host, including the maturation, maintenance and homeostasis of immune function[87]. Microbes play a significant role in digestion, immune and cognitive function, and are regarded as potential candidates for the diagnosis and therapeutic of immune-related diseases and cognitive impairments[88].Selenium deficiency has become a considerable public health issue and is strongly linked to diseases such as Keshan disease and Kasin-Beck disease[89]. Organic selenium is generally accepted as the form that the body can absorb. As a type of organic selenium compound, selenium polysaccharide combines the benefits of both selenium and polysaccharides, and research into their potential therapeutic applications is increasingly becoming a focus of scientific interest. Reports have already indicated that polysaccharide-modified selenium nanoparticles can be used to treat acute colitis[90]. This study demonstrates that GP-SE alters the immune status of the spleen in mice following CTX treatment, restoring health by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1, IL-1β and IL-6) and reducing oxidative damage (as measured by SOD, CAT and MDA).

CTX can lead to a decrease in the richness of the gut microbiota in normal mice and cause changes in species diversity; administration of GP-SE improved the gut microbiota structure and enriched Ruminiclostridium. CTX treatment reduced the abundance of the Bacteroidota and Firmicutes phyla, increased the F/B ratio at the phylum level, and simultaneously increased the abundance of conditionally pathogenic phyla (such as Proteobacteria), thereby leading to gut microbiota dysbiosis. At the genus level, the abundances of genera such as Blautia, Clostridia_UCG-014, Muribaculaceae, and Lachnospiraceae decreased, while those of Lactobacillus and Bacteroides increased; meanwhile, the abundance of potentially pathogenic genera, such as Helicobacter pylori, rose. Following GP-SE intervention, the abundance of the Bacteroidota phylum rebounded, the F/B ratio returned to normal, the abundance of commensal genera increased, and the microbial community structure tended toward normality, indicating that GP-SE can improve CTX-induced microbial dysbiosis and exert an immunomodulatory effect through the balance of potential beneficial bacteria and the F/B ratio. In other studies, Helianthus polysaccharide (HEP) has also been shown to improve gut microbiota structure and suppress CTX-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis by increasing the abundance of Alistipse, Muribaculaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococcaceae, while reducing the abundance of Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and Prevotella[91]. A link between Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology and the gut microbiota revealed elevated abundance of Clostridia_UCG-014 and reduced abundance of Blautia and Moryella[92]. Clostridia_UCG-014 is classified as a pro-inflammatory bacterium, while Blautia is considered to have probiotic potential and to reduce visceral fat accumulation[92], [93].

COG functional analysis indicates that GP-SE enhances functional diversity related to microbial metabolic adaptation, signal regulation, and structural synthesis. KEGG pathways reflect the fundamental functions of the microbiota in host metabolism, infection, and endocrine disease pathways, explaining the pathophysiological consequences corresponding to changes in microbiota function. The STAMP differential analysis statistically validated the trends observed above, identifying core effect pathways such as endocrine metabolism and branched-chain amino acid catabolism. Together, these findings help elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which GP-SE modulates CTX-induced intestinal microecological dysfunction and improves metabolic and immune damage by reshaping gut microbiota metabolism and stress-related regulatory functions. Research into selenized plant polysaccharides as a novel selenium supplement provides new potential targets and insights for the treatment of intestinal damage.
