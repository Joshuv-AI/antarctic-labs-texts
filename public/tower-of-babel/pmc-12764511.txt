Age-dependent increases in dorsal hippocampal postsynaptic α5GABA-a receptors may be lost in a rat model of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Jonique C. George, Allison E. Tipton, Natalia V. S. Bonfa, David H. Farb, Shelley J. Russek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Targeting amyloid for inhibition of neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) using selective therapeutics has been mildly successful, but an improved understanding of memory dysfunction on a neural circuitry level may be needed for development of acute nootropics for ongoing memory dysfunction. Here, we focus on the hypothesis that if early onset memory decline can be traced to the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit, then it could serve as a target for therapeutic modulation. Inhibitory GABAergic neurons participate in sharp wave ripple (SPW-R) oscillations in the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit as detected in CA1 during sleep and awake inactivity and are believed to function in memory replay and consolidation. Using the transgenic beta amyloid expressing TgF344-AD rat, which develops frank neurodegeneration and memory impairment with age, we previously discovered that ripple amplitude in 9mo and older TgF344-AD rats are insensitive to the nootropic drug α5IA, a negative allosteric modulator of α5-subunit containing γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptors (α5GABAARs). Here, we ask whether the loss of α5IA potentiation of ripple amplitude might reflect AD-specific downregulation of the predominant extrasynaptic α5GABAAR isoform. To our surprise, however, postsynaptic α5GABAAR levels decrease with aging in the dorsal dentate gyrus (DG) of TgF344-AD while increasing in wt littermates. The results support a model wherein age-associated beta-amyloid inhibits α5GABAAR trafficking. We posit that reducing α5GABAAR trafficking in excitatory specifically to the postsynaptic compartment in dorsal DG, and likely in CA1 neurons, may reduce their ability to shape local field potentials, including ripples, and blunt the pharmacological action of α5IA on ripple amplitude.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28973-9.

Introduction

Cognition encompasses higher level functions like thought, learning, and memory, which integrates sensory information for recall. For unknown reasons, cognitive function declines with age in some people but not others. Episodic memory, which involves the retrieval of personal experiences as well as their location and time frames, is susceptible to progressive dysfunction in human and animal models of many disorders associated with aging and/or neurodegeneration. Two examples where episodic memory dysfunction is a diagnostic marker are amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI), which primarily affects memory1, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Age related memory dysfunction is associated with hippocampal neural circuitry disruption and altered pattern separation in human dentate gyrus, CA3, and CA1 as shown by high-resolution fMRI2–4.

In an aMCI animal model, age related hippocampal hyperactivity in CA3 place cells is accompanied by an inability to remap place field representations in response to transitioning from a familiar to a novel environment, consistent with an increase in pattern completion in age-impaired rats5,6. Treatment with levetiracetam reduces hyperactivity in CA3, improves performance in tests of memory, and restores remapping of CA3 place cells, suggesting enhanced pattern separation5,7,8. In AD, hippocampal hyperactivity and episodic memory dysfunction is pronounced in concert with increased amyloid burden, tau tangles and ultimately neurodegeneration. These results point to a role for hyperactivity in memory dysfunction yet not all observations are consistent with this hypothesis: α5IA, a negative allosteric modulator (NAM) of α5GABAARs, increases the mean and peak firing rates of rat CA1 place cells9 and improves spatial learning in certain rat models of AD10.

Whether early-stage memory dysfunction may be separable from these consequences of frank neurodegeneration and subject to therapeutic treatment has yet to be elucidated11,12. The most prominent symptom of AD related dysfunction is memory impairment which is thought to result from synaptic transmission and neuroplasticity dysfunction13. Inhibitory chemical neurotransmission modulates synaptic plasticity in the central nervous system (CNS), where the balance between excitation and inhibition are the basis for synchronous neural network function in general and memory specifically14,15. In a healthy state, inhibition tempers excitation through the strategic localization of type A GABA receptors (GABAARs) on excitatory neurons16,17.

The α5-containing GABAARs (α5GABAARs) play a crucial role in learning and memory, as their activation can have a detrimental effect on learning tasks. Inhibition and downregulation of α5 subunit expression, including knock-down, knock-out, or point mutations, leads to increased performance in learning and memory paradigms18–20. Consistent with its role in learning and memory, α5GABAARs are found predominantly in the hippocampus, a brain region important for memory21–27. Moreover, the GABAAR system is the most prominent inhibitory system in the CNS, mediating two major types of inhibition: fast, and transient phasic postsynaptic inhibition and the slower, persistent tonic inhibition28,29. Phasic inhibition is generated by the vast majority of GABAAR subtypes located in the postsynaptic membrane28. Tonic inhibition is mediated by a small population of GABAARs located at extrasynaptic sites, or at the edge of the synaptic cleft, which are usually composed of the α5βxγ2 or the α4βxδ subunit composition23.

We previously demonstrated that α5IA increases the amplitude of sharp wave ripples (SPW-Rs) in Fisher 344 rats, consistent with an action of disinhibition increasing excitation9. Interestingly, their transgenic littermates (TgF344-AD), that overexpress the Swedish mutant human amyloid β precursor protein and Δ exon 9 human presenilin-1, show no change in SPW-Rs after α5IA administration9. It is unclear why α5IA loses its modulatory effect on SPW-Rs in the transgenic and we postulate that uncovering the molecular mechanism for this loss will be instrumental in finding the molecular basis for early HTC dysregulation in AD. We hypothesize that extrasynaptic α5GABAARs might be downregulated in AD. Because α5IA is an inverse agonist of GABAARs that contain the α5 subunit, we expect that a downregulation of α5GABAARs results in fewer α5IA—α5GABAAR binding sites, resulting in lower α5IA efficacy, i.e. lower memory improvement. A decrease in inhibition may cause hyperactivity as well as resulting memory dysfunction and resistance to modulation by α5IA with age. As a first step towards answering this question, we asked whether there was differential abundance and/or region-specific localization of α5 transcripts, α5 subunits, and α5GABAARs in the dorsal dentate gyrus and CA1 of TgF344-AD. However, contrary to expectation and the prevailing view concerning the preponderance of extrasynaptic α5GABAARs being the target of α5IA pharmacologically, our results are inconsistent with that hypothesis but support a key role for the much smaller proportion of α5GABAARs strategically located in the postsynaptic membrane.

Methods and materials

Animals

TgF344-AD rats30 were a gift from the Towne laboratory to the Farb laboratory at Boston University Chobanian and Avedisian School of Medicine and crossed with wild-type Fisher 344 rats (wt) to establish a TgF344-AD colony. TgF344-AD rats were originally generated by inserting two genes into rat pronuclei: Swedish mutant human amyloid β precursor protein and Δ exon 9 human presenilin-1. All animals were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle and were given free access to food and water. Female wt and TgF344-AD rats at 9 months (9mo) and 16 months (16mo) of age were used for subsequent experiments. All experimental protocols were approved by the Boston University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in accordance with guidelines from the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Our studies reported in this manuscript are in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). All studies were performed blinded by genotype and age although the rat sections often display phenotypic changes in some animals based on the variables. Power analysis was performed before experimentation, where possible, given the difficulty of breeding and maintaining aged littermate pairs in these studies. Post-experimentation power analysis and effect size (see Table 1) were also conducted to determine the strength of conclusions that could be made from the findings and to make important decisions about the future use of the animal colony. Co-immunofluorescence experiments used to determine the presence of extrasynaptic vs synaptic receptors were all fully powered. Tissue preparation and collection

RNAscope tissue preparation

Rats were anesthetized with CO2 until loss of pain sensation. The heads were decapitated with a guillotine and placed on ice where brains were removed, embedded in OCT, and flash frozen on dry ice.

Immunofluorescence tissue preparation

Rats were given light anesthesia using isofluorane and oxygen at a constant flow-rate [0.5/2]. They were weighed and given pentobarbitol to sacrifice, without stopping their hearts. Perfusion was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1X Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS). Brains were removed and placed first in 4% PFA for post-fixation and then in 10% sucrose in PBS, 20% sucrose, and 30% sucrose. Brains were then placed in cryomolds filled with OCT, frozen on dry ice, and stored in the -80 freezer until use.

Co-immunoprecipitation tissue preparation

Rats were anesthetized with CO2 until loss of pain sensation. Heads were severed via decapitation and placed on ice where brain tissue was removed. The hippocampi from both hemispheres were dissected and flash frozen. Frozen hippocampi were homogenized on ice with a dounce homogenizer followed by tissue disruption using a 21-gauge needle in 5 mL of M-PER reagent with NaCl, protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and PMSF. The sample was spun down, placed in a protein concentrator, and then spun down again. Concentrated extracted protein was collected and stored at -80 until use.

RNAscope

Flash frozen brains were removed from the -80 freezer and placed in a cryostat to acclimate. Brains were sectioned at 20 μm to reveal the dorsal hippocampus and mounted directly onto slides to dry at -20˚ for 60-120 min. The slides were post-fixed for an hour in 4% PFA on ice, dehydrated with 50%, 70%, and 100% ethanol baths, and treated with peroxide prior to probe administration. Slices were given probes for the α5 subunit of the GABAA Receptor and for the Solute Carrier Family 17 Member 7 (Slc17a7). HRP conjugated fluorophores were attached to the probe for visualization of transcripts on the Nikon Eclipse TE2000-S widefield microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Perfused brains were placed in a cryostat and sectioned at 14 μm and placed in cryoprotectant. Sections were washed in PBS and blocked in 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 2 h at room temperature. Slices were then placed in primary antibody [anti-synaptophysin, mouse monoclonal, Abcam, Cat No: ab8049, 1:500; anti-GABA-A receptor alpha5 polyclonal rabbit affinity purified antibody, Synaptic Systems, Cat No: 224 503, 1:100; anti-gephyrin recombinant human monoclonal antibody, Synaptic Systems, Cat No: 147 108, 1:100] overnight at 4 degrees. After washing, sections were placed in secondary Alexa Fluor antibodies (555 Donkey anti-mouse, Cat No: A31570; 647 goat anti-human, Cat No: A21445; 488 chicken anti-rabbit, Cat No: A21441) for fluorescent visualization on the Keyence BZ-X800 wide-field microscope and the Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope.

Microscopy

RNAscope

Four slices per brain were imaged on the Nikon Eclipse TE2000-S at 20 × magnification. Images were collected from a subfield at the apex of the CA1 and at the horn of the Dentate Gyrus (DG) for each channel: UV/blue = DAPI, 520 (green) = Slc17a7 (VGLUT), and 570 (red) = Gabra5. Each channel and subfield per slice was saved as individual image files prior to quantification. Note that in RNAscope the intensity of the individual signal does not confer any information regarding the number of molecules that are present. After background correction, a single dim signal or bright signal will be handled in the same manner, as one unique RNA molecule that was detected by the assay.

Immunofluorescence

Four slices per brain were imaged on the Keyence BZ-X800 at 63X oil immersion magnification. In each image, a snapshot was taken for the CA1 and DG in all four channels: 555 (red) = synaptophysin, 480 (green) = α5, 647 (magenta) = gephyrin, UV (blue) = DAPI. For co-localization analysis, imaging was performed on the Zeiss 700 at a magnification of 63X using 15 1um z-stacks. Each subfield was saved as one file consisting of all four channels in one merged image. Filter settings were adjusted on the confocal microscope to allow for the separation of bleed-through that comes from strong fluorescent signals. Oversaturation of signal was carefully checked between all channels before capturing the images. This was accomplished using exposure and gain settings that produced clear separation between the fluorophores. Fluorescent wavelengths used to detect colocalized proteins, using secondary antibodies, were well separated, with the emission wavelength for gephyrin set at 645nm and α5, 488nm. We chose the wavelengths of green and far-red, as opposed to green and red, where bleed-through is common. Next, we chose windows for the laser excitation that excluded areas where any fluorophores might overlap in cases of extremely high exposure. All slides were treated for autofluorescence by using Biotium True Black to control for nonspecific lipofusin.

Co-immunoprecipitation

The Pierce Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit (Cat. No: 26149, Thermo Fisher) was used for these experiments. After acclimating the column to RT, 50ug of γ2 antibody (anti-gamma-2, Alpha Diagnostics; Cat No: GAG21-A) was immobilized to the Pierce spin column. Then 1mg of hippocampal extracted protein was added to the column and incubated overnight at 4 degrees. The column was then washed, and the Co-IP was eluted. Eluted protein samples were analyzed using SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a PVDF membrane for visualization of α5 and γ2 (anti-alpha-5 antibody, Phosphosolutions, Cat No: 846A-GA5C; anti-gamma-2 antibody, Novus, Cat No: NB300-190).

Signal quantification and statistical analysis

RNAscope

Four images per brain were captured for each subfield and analyzed using Cell Profiler via the Erben Pipeline, Cell Profiler version 4.0.7 for Mac OS, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.cellprofiler.org31. Images were as follows: cell nuclei by DAPI stain, and fluorescent puncta representing transcripts of Gabra5, Slc17a7, and Gad1/Slc32a1. Cell Profiler was set to define a cell as any nucleus, determined by DAPI staining, and 1 pixel of space around said nucleus; this was done to include the cytoplasm in the definition of a cell. In the set of four images uploaded together, Cell Profiler related the fluorescent puncta to the particular cell in which it was found, thus providing information about which transcripts were in each cell and the number of puncta and cells were quantified. The number of puncta, or cells, were then averaged across biological replicates. Data was presented as the mean + /- the standard error of the mean (SEM).

Immunofluorescence

To measure the area of staining in um2, confocal z-stacks were collapsed using the Maximum Intensity function in FIJI. The threshold “Moments” was set for brightness in Color Thresholder. The collapsed image was then separated by channel. Colocalization of each channel with α5 signal was done one at a time. A composite of the α5 signal and the synaptophysin signal was generated. The α5 signal was kept as green and the synaptophysin as red. This allowed for the colocalized areas of the composite RGB image to be displayed as yellow. The threshold range for the yellow color was set between 35 and 45. A mask was generated based on the above threshold parameters and areas highlighted were measured in um2. A separate composite was generated with α5 and gephyrin, with the α5 signal kept as green and the gephyrin signal changed to red. This allowed for the same parameters and color thresholds to be used. A third composite was generated between synaptophysin and gephyrin, with the gephyrin signal changed to green. Finally, the α5 signal was measured by itself with the color threshold increased to the maximum range, as the only color in the image is green. A mask was generated and highlighted areas were measured. Postsynaptic receptors were calculated by subtracting the area of α5 colocalization with gephyrin from total α5 staining (Percentage of α5 that are postsynaptic = α5 staining – (α5 and gephyrin colocalized signal)), giving percent area. The percentage of extrasynaptic receptors was calculated by adding the α5 and gephyrin colocalization to the α5 and synaptophysin colocalization and then subtracting the synaptophysin and gephyrin colocalization. This was subtracted from 100: 100—(α5 and gephyrin colocalization + α5 and synaptophysin colocalization – synaptophysin and gephyrin colocalization).

To measure the staining intensity of overlapping signals that might correspond to potential receptor clusters, we used the FIJI plug-in ezColocalization. Confocal z-stacks were separated based on channel (see Methods for detail about experimental rigor). Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (PCC) and average signal intensity were used as the output for comparison across conditions. The immunofluorescent signals for detection of α5 and gephyrin were analyzed to determine colocalization. Correlation of postsynaptic neurons was calculated by averaging the PCC of each z. Data is displayed as mean + /- standard deviation of the mean, and a two-way ANOVA was used to measure the difference between age and genotype. Tukey’s Multiple Comparison Test was used if the ANOVA determined significance. Significance was set to p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.0 for Mac OS, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.graphpad.com.

Co-Immunoprecipitation analysis

To quantify the bands of the western blot, the files were analyzed in Image J using the “Gels” function. The lanes were plotted on the gel, and the plotted peaks were measured and transformed into numerical values, based on the area under the curve, for both γ2 and α5. An unpaired two-tailed t-test was used for determination of significance of γ2 between wt and TgF344-AD. The values for α5 were normalized to γ2, and a student t-test was used to determine significance between wt and TgF344-AD.

Results

Dorsal CA1 neurons in TgF344-AD contain fewer gabra5 transcripts/cell with age

To determine whether the expression of the α5 subunit, and therefore α5GABAARs, are downregulated in the dorsal hippocampus of TgF344-AD rats, we used high resolution in situ hybridization (RNAscope) to measure the number of transcripts in the CA1 and DG of littermate pairs at 9mo and 16mo of age (Fig. 1A). In CA1 (Fig. 1B, N = 4 littermate pairs), neither genotype nor age affected the abundance of gabra5 puncta (F3,12 = 0.766, p = 0.54; n = 4). The lack of any change in gabra5 transcript levels was also seen in the dentate gyrus (Fig. 1C). No significant difference in amount of total gabra5 (F3,10 = 0.57, p = 0.65; 9mo: n = 4, 16mo: n = 3) was detected. Extrasynaptic α5GABAARs decrease with age in both genotypes

We hypothesized that loss of ripple amplitude modulation by α5IA in the transgenic might reflect a decrease in the presence of α5GABAARs at membrane compartments, leading to decreased efficacy of the drug to modulate circuit function. To test this possibility, we used immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy with antibodies to gephyrin, a postsynaptic marker, and synaptophysin, a presynaptic marker, to denote locations around the synapse and to measure areas of highest colocalization with the α5 subunit (Fig. 5A, Suupplementary Fig. 1). First, the amount of area covered by extrasynaptic receptors in CA1 was determined. No difference was detected in the amount of extrasynaptic receptors between wt and TgF344-AD rats at either age (F1,16 = 1.375, p = 0.2581; n = 5 littermate pairs) (Fig. 5B). Analysis as a factor of age, however, showed a significant difference in the amount of extrasynaptic receptors between the groups (F1,16 = 4.587, p = 0.05), while the interaction between age and genotype revealed no significant difference (F3,16 = 2.06, p = 0.15). Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (PCC) was also used to measure differences in postsynaptic receptor populations by evaluating the correlation between gephyrin and α5 signals. Discussion

Understanding the molecular basis of learning and memory is an essential area of neuroscience and impacts the discovery of treatments for neurological disorders, like AD. The major symptom of AD is a loss in spatial memory processes that rely on the hippocampal formation, and in particular, the HTC. Recent studies suggest that the brain rhythms generated by this circuit underlie memory consolidation and replay. Synchronous rhythmic neuronal activities involved in cognitive spatial processes are theta, gamma, and sharp wave oscillations28,32. Sharp wave ripples (SPW-Rs) are fast oscillations found in the CA1 that associate with sharp waves, and the consolidation of learned information22. A recent study suggests that SPW-Rs may be altered in a rat model of Alzheimer’s Disease as evidenced by the altered response to the cognitive enhancer α5IA in TgF344-AD rats compared to wt littermates9. It has already been demonstrated that this cognitive enhancer has specificity for α5GABAARs, which are known to mediate most tonic inhibition in the hippocampus10,23,33–36.

Recent studies have suggested that SPW-R dysfunction may underlie significant losses in spatial memory processing seen in AD, however, the molecular basis behind this possibility remains unknown. The current study provides a detailed investigation into the potential molecular and synaptic reorganization of α5GABAARs in the HTC, using a transgenic model of AD, to begin to illuminate the source of the phenotypic differences between littermate controls and TgF344-AD. While α5βxγ2 GABAAR subtypes have been traditionally identified as extrasynaptic receptors that mediate tonic inhibition, more recent studies suggest that they have a role in both tonic and phasic inhibition18,37,38. The evidence to support phasic α5GABAARs also comes from studies using an α5 subunit knock-out mouse model where mice were shown to contain fewer neurons with the fast-inactivating kinetics of an inhibitory post-synaptic current, or phasic inhibition18,39. In fact, using a pharmacological optogenetic approach, it has been shown that LTP can drive extrasynaptic α5GABAARs into postsynaptic compartments in association with gephyrin and it is hypothesized that these receptors at the synapse are essential for the early stabilization of associative learning40. These findings have identified a new role for α5GABAARs in metaplasticity, the plasticity of plasticity necessary to prevent runaway hippocampal excitatory neurotransmission and to hold memories in place for further long-term processing.

Here, we reveal that increases in α5 subunits of the dorsal hippocampus that co-localize with gephyrin (a scaffold for postsynaptic GABAARs) were detected in wt F344 with age, but not in their TgF344-AD littermates. Since the GABAARs located at the synaptic cleft mediate an important component of phasic inhibition, the implication of these results is that the presence of a subpopulation of α5GABAARs in DG, and potentially CA1, may help to stabilize the representation of new memories during normal aging. It is also important to note that the results seen in our studies fall into concordance with the α5GABAAR synaptic localization literature, where both extrasynaptic and postsynaptic α5GABAARs have been described37,41. Surprisingly, results of a recent study show that α5GABAAR positive allosteric modulation, rather than negative modulation, can be beneficial in the context of aged-impaired memory42. Whether or not this enhancement is due to augmentation in the function of postsynaptic α5GABAARs that are progressively lost in disease is still unknown, but it may be an important area for future investigation as impaired synaptic inhibition in the background of increased excitatory drive during AD progression may disrupt hippocampal information flow even though α5GABAAR negative allosteric modulators are memory enhancing in normal rats.

Concurrently, we also measured the amount of extrasynaptic α5GABAARs, as it has been demonstrated that extrasynaptic α5GABAARs are also implicated in learning and memory in an AD model10. Ablation of these receptors responsible for tonic inhibition evokes positive performance on memory tasks, like the Morris Water Maze, and in hippocampal dependent learning in adult mice18,19. We used confocal microscopy to examine z-stacks throughout the field of view in our images and found no significant change in the amount of extrasynaptic receptors in wt vs TgF344-AD at either age (Fig. 5). These results are interesting to us as they reveal that the age-dependent increase in postsynaptic α5GABAARs in wt (Fig. 6) may not come from the extrasynaptic pool, which was a finding of Davenport and colleagues in 2021. Both genotypes show the same decrease in extrasynaptic α5GABAARs at 16mo, when compared to 9mo, while levels of postsynaptic receptors only increase in wt and not TgF344-AD.

To investigate whether the age-dependent differences seen in postsynaptic α5GABAAR levels between littermate controls and TgF344-AD in DG (Fig. 6B and D) are caused by alterations in α5 synthesis at a transcriptional level, we quantified Gabra5 transcript levels (Fig. 1). We first quantitated the total amount of Gabra5 in specific regions of CA1 and DG and saw no change across groups. These results, along with the lack of change in α5 subunit levels between controls and TgF344-AD (Fig. 4), suggest increased α5 synthesis may not be the source of increased postsynaptic receptors in wt with aging. Interestingly, we found that CA1 neurons of aged TgF344-AD contain fewer copies of the Gabra5 transcript compared to the neurons of 9mo TgF344-AD; this age-related decrease was not seen in wt and not seen in DG (Fig. 2). We hypothesize that this decrease in transcript production may be a compensatory mechanism in TgF344-AD in response to HTC dysfunction.

Limitations to our studies

We did not detect a genotype specific change in α5 subunit levels at 9mo, where impaired SPW-R modulation by α5IA has been reported. This could be attributed to our limited ability to detect individual α5 subunits in single cells of the dorsal CA1 and DG due to the resolution of immunofluorescence with currently available antibodies visualized with confocal microscopy. This technical limitation still leaves open the possibility that the higher resolution of RNAscope may have uncovered a biologically relevant response to toxic Aβ overexpression in CA1 that requires further study. Whether super-resolution, EM, or expansion microscopy will enhance the ability to detect limiting cluster numbers remains to be determined and will be ongoing in future studies.

Since GABAARs that contain the α5 subunit tend to also contain the γ2 subunit, and there are strong γ2 antibodies efficient for receptor pulldown assays, we asked whether the subunit ratios of α5 to γ2 remain the same between wt and TgF344-AD at 9mo. In healthy animals, α5GABAARs are usually found in an α5βxγ2 conformation. Using an antibody against γ2 to pull down all γ2-containing GABAARs (including those with α5βxγ2 subtypes), we discovered a potential, although not yet significant decrease in the total amount of hippocampal α5 subunits purified by their association with γ2. We were unfortunately unable to power these studies for a definitive conclusion given the limitation of obtaining and aging littermate pairs for molecular and electrophysiological studies using the TgF344-AD model, but the intriguing results suggest that it is an important research question that should be pursued in the future. There are important caveats of the approach, however, that need to be addressed before moving forward and lie in the fact that total hippocampus rather than region-specific areas was profiled due to the Co-IP assay that requires a large amount of tissue for efficient precipitation, as well as efficient detection of the small quantity of α5GABAARs in total γ2GABAARs.

In conclusion, we show that total changes in Gabra5 transcript levels and accompanying subunit protein in CA1 and DG of dorsal hippocampus, cannot account for the inability of α5IA to modulate SPW-Rs of 9mo TgF344-AD rats, although we have evidence suggesting that within the nuclei of individual cells, there may be a decreased presence of α5 subunit pools in cytoplasm. This possibility is also supported by the preliminary results of our co-IP studies where the presence of α5 subunits in γ2 containing GABAARs at 9mo was examined. Our most surprising finding, however, was the age based increase in DG postsynaptic α5GABAAR levels in wt that is lost in TgF344-AD littermates. This finding presents the idea that in normal aging, a proportion of receptors are relocated to postsynaptic sites for enhancing the stabilization of new memories in order to adapt to altered cellular function40,42. In the progression of AD, however, as cognitive abilities rapidly decline, localization of these receptors may be impaired, potentially from cellular transport deficits stemming from amyloid and tau tangle related disruptions. This research provides a major hypothesis that can be tested more specifically in future studies using high resolution proteomics and single cell electrophysiology. It also presents a new model for future therapeutic intervention.
