Reservoir operation impacts on floodplain wetland inundation and ecologically sensitive areas
Authors: Xi Dong, Chunming Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continuous construction of reservoirs worldwide has promoted socio-economic development and seriously affected the ecological and hydrological processes of rivers and floodplain wetlands. This study takes the Yimin River in Inner Mongolia, China as the research area, and based on the WIGR(Water inundation guarantee rate)model, quantifies the ecologically sensitive areas on both sides of the river under the operation of reservoirs, and proposes targeted protection suggestions. The research results show that due to the influence of reservoir construction, the areas with a WIGR of 0–20% are the most affected, with a reduction of 12.14 km2 in area. The average offshore distance before and after reservoir construction is 1050 m and 1125 m; The extremely low sensitivity reduction areas (1–4%), low sensitivity reduction area (5–16%), moderate sensitivity reduction area (17–46%) and high sensitivity area (47–80%) are 21.73 km2, 4.01 km2, 2.71 km2 and 0.18 km2 respectively, and the offshore distances are 975 m, 990 m, 630 m and 180 m respectively; We suggest that future water conservancy hub construction should consider releasing water at specific times to mimic natural flow patterns, and incorporating wetland restoration and protection work into dam design and operation to reduce the impact of dams on the environment and promote sustainable use of water resources. This study can provide new ideas for quantifying and protecting ecologically sensitive areas in floodplain wetlands.Keywords: Floodplain wetland, Water inundation guarantee rate, Reservoir, Yimin river, Remote sensingSubject terms: Riparian ecology, Wetlands ecology

Introduction

Wetlands are considered to be the most unique ecosystems in the world, providing many irreplaceable direct and indirect ecological products and services for humans. Wetlands cover 6% of the Earth’s area1 and provide 40% of its ecological services2. China has about 5.36 × 105 km2 of natural wetlands, accounting for nearly 10% of the world’s total 3. A total of 602 wetland reserves (including all local, provincial, and national reserves) have been established in China, with a protection ratio of 49.03%4. However, there is evidence that a considerable portion of wetland reserves are experiencing loss of function and degradation5–8. Human activities and their impacts, including climate change, agricultural encroachment, urban encroachment, wetland landscape fragmentation, and river dam construction are considered to be the main causes of wetland degradation8–12.

To meet the increasing water demands for social and economic development, a significant number of dams have been built around the world13,14. The construction of dams can alleviate regional water supply conflicts, improve soil moisture conditions, generate renewable energy through water conservancy and power generation, and have a series of positive effects on irrigation and flood control, the ecological environment surrounding river dams has garnered significant research attention over the years15–17. It has been shown that the construction of dams can alter the hydrological conditions of rivers and influence the surrounding wetlands11,12 by decreasing extreme flow, decreasing flooding frequency in active regions, and making flooding wetland areas smaller13,18–20. For dams, the development and utilization of water resources is the fundamental goal of their construction and operation, and how to maximize the benefits of water resources is undoubtedly an important issue 21,22.The floodplain wetlands downstream of the water conservancy hub are mainly in a natural state. Due to the influence of the water conservancy hub, the hydrological situation of the downstream wetlands changes, and there is a risk of wetland degradation22,23. In this study, the area downstream of a water conservancy hub that is susceptible to alternating flooding and drying up is defined as an ecologically sensitive area.

The construction and operation of dams have been unanimously recognized for their adverse effects on downstream ecosystems, but research has generally focused on the ecosystems within downstream river channels and little attention has been paid to the ecosystem outside the river24–26. Based on existing mature remote sensing technologies, modern scholars have conducted extensive research on reconstructing wetlands in different countries, regions, watersheds, and scenarios around the world27–31, And a model has been developed to quantify the relationship between river floodplains and hydrology32–36. Reservoirs alter downstream wetland inundation processes by affecting river flow and duration. The regulation of water resources in reservoirs can alleviate the hydrological connection between downstream wetlands and flood prone areas to a certain extent during a certain period of time. However, there are still some issues that need to be addressed to alleviate the risk of wetland degradation through the regulation of water resources in reservoirs: where are the ecologically sensitive areas on both sides of the river affected by changes in hydrological conditions? Is there a targeted protection opinion on the water resource regulation measures of reservoirs?

This study takes the Yimin River in Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China as the research object, focusing on the delineation of ecologically sensitive areas in floodplain wetlands under dam construction and operation, and exploring the impact of Honghuaerji Dam construction on the river and floodplain wetlands on both banks. In this study, wetlands specifically refer to intermittent submerged areas based on remote sensing image extraction results. This study aims to achieve the following main objectives: (1) quantitatively identify the degree of change in the inundation status of rivers and wetlands before and after dam construction; (2) Clarify the ecologically sensitive areas on both sides of the river; (3) Propose targeted suggestions for wetland protection in river floodplains.2. Study area and data.

Study area and data

Study area

The research area is located in the hinterland of Hulunbuir Grassland in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China. River runoff is an important natural force for the formation of grassland wetlands. The construction of reservoirs and other comprehensive factors have led to a decrease in river water levels, and wetlands near the edge of the riparian zone are affected by alternating flooding and drought cycles. Moreover, both sides of the river are mainly grazing areas with less human disturbance. Therefore, the floodplain wetland areas on both sides of the basin are mainly affected by river runoff. As shown in Fig. 1. It is situated between E 117° 00 ′ 10 ′′ ~ 117° 41 ′ 40 ′′ and N 48° 30 ′ 40 ′′ ~ 49° 20 ′ 40 ′′. The river is 359.4 km long, with a watershed area of 22,640 km2 and an average annual runoff of 1.3 billion m3. Winter is extremely cold and long, spring is dry and windy, summer is warm and cool and short, and autumn has a sharp drop in temperature and early frost. Spring has less precipitation and dry air, making it the season with the lowest relative humidity throughout the year, with an average of around 47%; In summer, there is a lot of precipitation, and the air is rich in water vapor, with a relative humidity of 65–73%; The temperature drops in autumn, and the relative humidity is slightly higher than in spring, ranging from 58 to 66%.

Experimental design

As shown in Fig. 2, the water body inundation area was determined by extracting the water body based on the MNDWI37 water body index from the 52-phase remote sensing data in the study area. Based on Matlab, the inundation frequency of all available images was calculated pixel by pixel and the submergence guarantee rate was calculated based on the flow frequency statistics. The shortest distance from the pixel to the main channel was then calculated and the IHA index system was used to determine the impact of changes in hydrological regimes on the submerged state. This provided an accurate assessment of water inundation in the study area so that the impact of reservoir construction on the river and its surrounding wetlands could be determined.

Research methods

Indicators of hydrologic alteration (IHA)

River flow involves considerable information and data so describing the process is quite complex. One effective approach to studying complex eco-hydrological processes is to establish an index system. Currently, the IHA (indicators of hydrologic alteration)28 index system is widely used around the world. This method is based on daily hydrological river data (series length > 20 a) to assess the degree of ecological and hydrological changes in the river.

In this study, some IHA indicators were selected to evaluate the impact of hydrological regime changes on the river inundation assurance rate. Among these, the monthly flow rate was used to describe the flow change characteristics of the river. Additionally, the magnitude and duration of extreme hydrological events were used to identify whether the ecological system requirements of the floodplain wetland were met. Discussion

Influence of hydrological changes on submerged state

This study used some parameters from the IHA index system38 to evaluate the changes in the hydrological conditions of the Yimin River from May to October before and after the construction of the reservoir (Table 3). The mean and maximum annual discharges were prioritized as they directly govern the inundation extent and duration in floodplains. While we acknowledge the ecological relevance of other IHA metrics, these two parameters suffice to capture the key reservoir-induced hydrological shifts relevant to floodplain connectivity.

Maintaining ecological flow in rivers to keep them as submersible riparian areas is crucial, not only for the ecological stability of the area but also for increasing the biodiversity of flora and fauna in the river-influenced area12. Following the construction of the reservoir, the impact of rainfall and summer runoff on the submerged area and the inundation frequency of the water body decreased, and the effects of extreme high and low flow were largely eliminated29,41,43, thereby decreasing the flooding of the river. That is, the construction of the reservoir reduced the inundation degree and frequency of wetlands in the downstream floodplain, which directly affected the wetland area in the floodplain by changing the runoff state13. In Table 3, the average runoff before the construction of the reservoir was higher than the average runoff after the construction, that is, the daily average flow and maximum flow of each month during the main runoff period (May to October) were correspondingly reduced, which directly led to a decrease in the runoff of the Yimin River, affecting the floodplain wetlands and reducing the frequency of wetland inundation on both sides of the river, resulting in a decrease in the coverage area of the inundation guarantee rate after the construction of the reservoir (Table 4). A reduction in average runoff affects the habitat range of aquatic species and plants. Reductions in runoff extremes, such as soil moisture required for growth, affect the dispersal conditions for plant growth and change the river topography and nutrient exchange between rivers and floodplains44,45.

Fig. 4 shows the statistical distribution of the WIGR pixel offshore distance before and after the construction of the reservoir in the study area. The mean value of the offshore distance of extremely low frequency 1–20% WIGR before and after the construction of the reservoir was 1050 and 1125 m, respectively. The average offshore distance of the 21–100% WIGR coverage area before and after the construction of the reservoir was 915 and 735 m, respectively. This indicated that the extremely low WIGR area was becoming further offshore and the 21–100% WIGR area was becoming closer to shore. The main reason for this is the peak shaving and valley filling effect of the reservoir, which makes the common flow more concentrated. This spatial reconstruction of “low-frequency expansion and high-frequency contraction” is essentially the homogenization of the flow process caused by the operation of the reservoir, leading to the contraction of the area with extremely high inundation guarantee rate towards the main river channel. The average offshore distance of the high-frequency inundation zone (21–100% WIGR) has decreased from 915 to 735 m, leading to a concentration of moisture tolerant plant communities towards the shoreline. The expansion of the low-frequency inundation zone has caused drought tolerant plants to invade the original riverbank wet zone, changing the vegetation community structure. Although reservoir operation dominates hydrological changes, climate change may exacerbate wetland degradation. For example, the regional drought cycle in Inner Mongolia may exacerbate water resource pressure on floodplain wetlands. Future research should use long-term climate data, such as precipitation anomalies and temperature trends, to distinguish these driving factors and delineate their relative contributions. At present, our research lacks long-term monitoring data on biological communities and soil, and there is a lack of quantitative models for ecological process response. This will be our next research focus.

Identification of degradation risk areas and degradation risk levels

Reservoir construction regulates river runoff, adjusts runoff by storing abundance and replenishing dryness, and controls floods through hubs—which can effectively reduce flood disasters and improve surface water resource utilization. Regulating the water supply of runoff significantly improved the ecological and hydrological conditions of the wetlands on both sides of the Yimin River. Usually, the runoff in the river channel was reduced due to hydrological changes; however, as the river state was regulated by the construction of the reservoir, the large fluctuations in the wetland area caused by the runoff change were decreased43,47.

The changes in the WIGR before and after the establishment of the reservoir are shown in Fig. 5. Fig. 5A shows the reduction of WIGR pixels and the decline probability. It can be seen that the higher the decline rate of WIGR, the fewer corresponding pixels, i.e. the smaller the area. There are up to 16,840 pixels elements with an decline rate of 1%, accounting for 58.28% of all pixel elements; The proportion of pixels with decline rates of 2%, 3%, and 4% is 8.01%, 9.64%, and 5.29%, respectively; The proportion of pixels with a decline rate of 5% or above varies from 0 to 1.96%. Fig. 5B represents the spatial distribution of WIGR reduction pixels. The main decline area was distributed in the flooding area on both sides of the river and far from the river. The area covered by the WIGR decreased or disappeared after the construction of the reservoir, which meant that the flood recharge of the wetland ecosystem in this area was insufficient.

Decrease in floodplain wetland inundation guarantee rate due to reservoir construction.

For floodplain wetlands located on both sides of meandering rivers, the geomorphological environment and hydrology are the two main abiotic drivers of floodplain wetland dynamics, spatial distribution, and species diversity5,48. The important ecological barrier function of wetlands on both sides of rivers faces threats from development by human society—such as the construction of water conservancy projects—which affects the hydrological dynamics of rivers and changes the transfer of nutrients and substances from source areas as a result8,46. These changes affect river running patterns, channel morphology, and the development of floodplain wetlands on both sides of riverbanks2,49. Therefore, in order to protect the floodplain wetlands on both sides of the research area, we need to explore the main development areas and sensitive areas of wetlands on both sides of the river. Based on this data, we need to establish ecological sensitive areas for protection and reduce the impact of water conservancy hub operations on floodplain wetlands.

Wetland protection under the influence of water conservancy hub operation

Wetland degradation occurs under the influence of a combination of factors and eventually leads to a decline in wetland structural stability, natural regulatory functions such as ecosystem stability, resistance, and resilience, and the overall decline of the wetland ecosystem44,51. Accordingly, this study analyzed the relationship between water and wetland degradation at different distances from the floodplain. The development and degradation of floodplain wetlands are usually a function of soil water availability and stress gradients related to inundation frequency52,53. Studies have found that the development and degradation of wetlands are closely related to flooding: if the location of the wetland becomes further, it will have an impact on wetland degradation53.

For floodplain wetlands downstream of reservoirs, the most effective protection method is to release artificial floods to ensure that the wetlands are regularly replenished by floodplain floods47.The regulation and storage operation of the Honghuaerji Reservoir changed the hydrological regime of the river, resulting in a reduction in the water volume of the downstream channel (Table 3), a reduction in the number of times the river flowed out of the trough floodplain, and a reduction in the inundation guarantee rate of the floodplain wetlands on both sides of the river—to varying degrees (Fig. 5).

Wetland-protected areas are different from other protected areas such as forest-protected areas and grassland-protected areas. Wetland protected areas are closely related to the hydrological conditions of rivers and the distance from the main river. The demarcation of protected wetland areas faces many challenges and difficulties. The unique and diverse nature of wetland biomes, ecosystems, geographies, and their social development environments makes it difficult to develop universal standards and techniques for assessing wetland-protected areas 22,48. Many studies have used multiple indicators to assess and delineate wetland reserves, but it is generally difficult to obtain all the necessary data, making it difficult to quickly apply protected area delineation methods54,55. Furthermore, there is no unified reference standard for the evaluation indicators to delineate protected areas. Different detection methods are required to obtain and evaluate the importance of each indicator and the calculations are complicated. The final comprehensive index may not be used as an indicator for the delineation of protected areas.

In this study, we only used statistical and mathematical models to evaluate the relationship between distance and wetland protection (WIGR and offshore distance), in order to delineate the scope of sensitive areas. The method of delineating sensitive areas is simple and universal. Based on our research results, we suggest taking active measures, such as regulating reservoirs, increasing river runoff, increasing flood inundation range and frequency through human intervention, and delineating reasonable protection areas to maximize the benefits of wetland ecological protection, and achieving a variety of compensation and protection mechanisms54.

For the limitations and future scope of this study, if information on several factors can be obtained spatially, such as nutrient content of wetland soil, plant community species, elevation, etc., then the protection of wetland ecological integrity, natural structure, and functionality in the future will be more perfect, which will also be the focus of our future work. We suggest that future water conservancy hub construction should consider releasing water at specific times to mimic natural flow patterns, and incorporating wetland restoration and protection work into dam design and operation. In addition, we believe that the focus of future research in the field of water resources will be on developing innovative technologies and strategies to reduce the impact of dams on the environment and promote sustainable use of water resources.

The shortcomings of this study

Based on the WIGR and the proposed offshore distance method, this study quantitatively identified the sensitive areas downstream of the water conservancy hub affected by inundation and assessed the impact of hydrological changes on inundation status.

However, this study has the following limitations: The selection of thresholds in this study primarily depended on inflection points in the curves. Fig. 5A illustrates the pixels with reduced WIGR and their corresponding decline probabilities. Although 1% decline is numerically small, it covers the largest area (accounting for 58.28% of the reduced pixels), representing a widespread but minor impact of reservoir operation on wetland inundation frequency. In Fig. 6A, the threshold selection was based on the slope inflection points of the cumulative frequency curve, where 5%, 17%, and 47% were identified as key turning points. These thresholds were used to classify four levels of wetland sensitivity. The data indicate that the smaller the decline probability, the larger the protectable area when delineating sensitive zones. Therefore, these key inflection points were used to determine the classification thresholds.

Additionally, there are data limitations in this study. The calculation of the distance between WIGR pixels and the main river channel was based on a two-dimensional plane, without incorporating DEM data, which may introduce errors in steep bank areas. The IHA analysis relied solely on daily flow data, lacking hourly data, making it impossible to calculate high-frequency indicators such as rise and fall rates.

Given these limitations, future research should integrate multi-source remote sensing data and ground-based observations, incorporate high-precision topographic and hydrological models, and explore machine learning methods to quantify ecological response thresholds. These improvements will enhance the applicability of the WIGR model across different watersheds and support more scientifically informed conservation decision-making.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the inundation status of the Yimin River and wetlands on both banks before and after the construction of the Honghuaerji Reservoir based on 52 Landsat data monitoring. Combined with flow observation data, the study analyzed and discussed the protection of floodplain wetlands, and the following conclusions were drawn:

After the construction of the Honghuaerji Reservoir, the changing trend in the inundation guarantee rate in different regions was different. The daily average flow and maximum flow for each month during the main runoff period (May–October) were reduced, resulting in the outflow of the Yimin River. The reduction of trough conditions reduced the inundation frequency of the wetlands on both sides of the river, resulting in a reduction in the coverage area of the inundation guarantee rate after the construction of the reservoir. The construction of the reservoir also changed the natural runoff process of the river, resulting in more stable and uniform hydrological conditions and an increase in the area with an extremely high inundation guarantee rate in the river. However, a negative impact was the less frequent inundation of the flood-dependent wetlands outside of the river channel, leading to the risk of degradation.

The application of the WIGR (Water Submersion Guarantee Rate) model in this study can be widely applied to watershed assessment under different geographical and climatic conditions. The method used in this study is compatible with multiple remote sensing data sources (such as Landsat, Sentinel-2, MODIS, etc.) and has elastic adaptability to image resolution and time series length. In areas with scarce data, reliable results can be obtained even with only 15–20 images.

The core parameters of the model adopt internationally recognized standards to ensure comparability of results across different regions. The mathematical expression of formula (3) is not limited by regional scales and can be applied to wetland systems of different scales ranging from 1 to 1000 km2.

This study quantified the ecological sensitivity of wetlands downstream of reservoirs based on hydrological indicators and remote sensing data, but key ecological parameters such as soil physicochemical properties, vegetation community structure, and biodiversity have not yet been integrated. This limits the comprehensive analysis of wetland degradation mechanisms by models, such as: (1) the impact of soil moisture and nutrient changes on the boundaries of sensitive areas; (2) The coupling relationship between vegetation succession and hydrological disturbance has not been fully evaluated. In addition, the potential regulatory effect of elevation changes on inundation patterns also needs further verification.

The research results of this article are of great significance for the management and planning of reservoirs, especially in understanding the impact on downstream wetlands. This research work can be applied to fields such as environmental impact assessment or planning regulations.
