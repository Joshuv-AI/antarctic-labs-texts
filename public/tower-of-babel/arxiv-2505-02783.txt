The H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus for right slice hyperholomorphic functions and right linear Clifford operators
F. Colombo, F. Mantovani, P. Schlosser

Abstract
. In 2016, the spectral theory on the S S -spectrum was used to establish the H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus for quaternionic or Clifford operators. This calculus applies for example to sectorial or bisectorial right linear operators T T and left slice hyperholomorphic functions f f that can grow as polynomials. It relies on the product of the two operators e ​ ( T ) − 1 e(T)^{-1} and ( e ​ f ) ​ ( T ) (ef)(T) , both defined via some underlying S S -functional calculus (also called ω \omega -functional calculus). For left slice holomorphic functions f f this definition does not depend on the choice of the regularizer function e e . However, due to the non-commutative multiplication of Clifford numbers, it was unclear how to extend this definition to right slice hyperholomorphic functions. This paper addresses this significant unresolved issue and shows how right linear operators can possess the H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus also for right slice hyperholomorphic functions.

1.
Introduction

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus applies to sectorial and bisectorial operators, and extends the holomorphic functional calculus, often referred to as the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus
[
32
]
, to functions that do not decay sufficiently fast at zero and infinity. For a comprehensive exploration of this calculus, we recommend the books
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
along with their associated references.

The spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum has fundamental differences compared to classical complex spectral theory, because it is derived from the classical resolvent series expansion, without assuming that the operator and the spectral parameter commute. Specifically, for bounded operators
T:V\rightarrow V
acting in a Clifford module
V
, the series expansion of the
S
-resolvent becomes

(1.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{n}s^{-n-1}=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}(\overline{s}-T),\qquad|s|>\|T\|,

where
s=s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}
is a paravector,
|s|
is its modulus and
\overline{s}
the conjugate of
s
. The reason why the value of the series (
1.1
) does not simplify to the classical resolvent operator
(s-T)^{-1}
is due to the noncommutativity
sT\neq Ts
. The explicit value of the sum in equation (
1.1
) motivates that, even for unbounded, closed operators
T
, the spectrum must be associated with the invertibility of the operator

Q_{s}[T]:=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2},\qquad\text{with }\operatorname{dom}(Q_{s}[T])=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2}).

This leads us to the definitions of the
S
-resolvent set
and the
S
-spectrum

\rho_{S}(T):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(T),

where
\mathcal{B}(V)
is the space of all bounded operators from
V
into itself (see
[
20
,
26
]
for more details). Motivated by (
1.1
), we define for every
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
the
left
and the
right
S
-resolvent operator

(1.2)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=(\overline{s}-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}.

which is then be used in the definition of the
S
-functional calculus

(1.3)

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s)\quad\text{and}\quad f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T),

for left resp. right slice holomorphic functions
f
. This is the Clifford analogue of the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus. Moreover, we remark that the spectral theorem has recently been extended from quaternionic operators in
[
2
]
to fully Clifford operators in
[
22
]
.

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, as an extension of the
S
-functional calculus (
1.3
), has been first explored in works such as
[
3
,
16
]
, while the recent paper
[
42
]
introduced the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for unbounded bisectorial operators within a Clifford module over the algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. This work leverages the universality property of the
S
-functional calculus, as explained in
[
21
]
, which demonstrates its applicability to fully Clifford operators. The idea of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for left slice holomorphic functions
f
, is to find a regularizer function
e
, such that we can define

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

where both
e(T)
and
(ef)(T)
are interpreted in the sense of the
S
-functional calculus (
1.3
). Thanks to
[
18
, Theorem 7.2.6]
, this definition is independent of the regularizer
e
. Since right slice hyperholomorphic functions
f
maintain slice hyperholomorphicity under multiplication with intrinsic functions
e
from the right, a natural attempt of defining
f(T)
would be as
(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
. However, it was already noted in
[
18
, Remark 7.2.2]
, that this definition is not independent of the regularizer function
e
. For left linear operators, the situation is of course the opposite.

In Section
4
, we provide a solution to this longstanding open problem, by defining the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for right linear, bisectorial, injective operators
T
and right slice holomorphic functions
f
with at most polynomial growth, as the closure

(1.4)

f(T):=\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}.

Since we a priori do not know that
(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
is closable, the closure in (
1.4
) is understood in the sense of multivalued operators, see Definition
2.2
. The independence of (
1.4
) from the regularizer
e
is proven in Theorem
4.8
and crucially depends on the fact that for bisectorial operators the Banach module
V
decomposes into
V=\ker(T)+\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
, see Theorem
3.3
. The fact that the right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
1.4
) coincides with the already existing left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for intrinsic functions, and with the right
\omega
-functional calculus for decaying functions, is proven in Theorem
4.5
and Theorem
4.6
.

Quaternionic and Clifford operators play an important role across various fields of mathematics and physics. For example, they are appear in quaternionic quantum mechanics (see S. Adler’s seminal work
[
1
]
), in vector analysis, differential geometry, and hypercomplex analysis.

Vector analysis.
The gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients in
n
dimensions represents various physical laws, such as Fourier’s law for heat propagation and Fick’s law for mass transfer diffusion. This can be expressed as

T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}},\qquad x\in\Omega,

and is associated with various boundary conditions, as discussed in
[
23
]
. Here,
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
represent the imaginary units of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, and the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are assumed to belong to
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
and satisfy appropriate bounds.

Differential geometry.
Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
n
, and
U
a coordinate neighbourhood of
M
. In this neighbourhood, we can find an orthonormal frame of vector fields
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
, such that the Dirac operator on
(U,g)
can be expressed as the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
differential operator

(1.5)

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla_{E_{i}}^{\tau},

where
\nabla_{E_{i}}^{\tau}
denotes the covariant derivative with respect to the vector field
E_{i}
. For more details see the book
[
35
]
. As a special case,
[
11
]
considers the Dirac operator in hyperbolic and spherical spaces, where (
1.5
) takes the explicit forms

\mathcal{D}_{H}=x_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-\frac{n-1}{2}e_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{D}_{S}=(1+|x|^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-nx,

for
(x_{1},...,x_{n-1},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively.

Hypercomplex analysis.
The well-known Dirac operator and its conjugate

D=\partial_{x_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{D}=\partial_{x_{0}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},

are widely investigated in
[
28
]
as well as in
[
35
]
. Slice hyperholomorphic functions can also be viewed as functions in the kernel of the global operator

G=|\operatorname{Im}(x)|^{2}\partial_{x_{0}}+\operatorname{Im}(x)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},

introduced in
[
19
]
, where
\operatorname{Im}(x)=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}
. For integers
\alpha,\beta,m
, the operators of the Dirac fine structure on the
S
-spectrum are defined as

T_{\alpha,m}=D^{\alpha}(D\overline{D})^{m}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{T}_{\beta,m}=\overline{D}^{\beta}(D\overline{D})^{m}.

For more information, see the concluding remarks in the last section of this paper, where we discuss the fine structures on the
S
-spectrum that consist of the function spaces and the related functional calculi.

2.
Preliminaries on Clifford Algebras and Clifford modules

Clifford algebras are associative algebras that generalize complex numbers and quaternions. They provide a mathematical framework to study operator theory in a noncommutative context. The fact that
n
-tuples, or more generally
2^{n}
-tuples, of noncommuting operators are embedded into a Clifford algebra, produces the benefit that for Clifford operators, the notion of spectrum turns out to be very clear and explicit, even if it differs from the classical notion in complex operator theory. Precisely, the real
Clifford algebra

\mathbb{R}_{n}
over
n

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
, which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\},

is given by

\mathbb{R}_{n}:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}e_{A}\;\Big|\;s_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\}.

Here we used the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\big\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\;\big|\;r\in\{0,\dots,n\},\,1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n\big\},

and the
basis vectors

e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note that for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}=1
. Furthermore, we define for every Clifford number
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
its
conjugate
and its
absolute value

(2.1)

\overline{s}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(-1)^{\frac{|A|(|A|+1)}{2}}s_{A}e_{A}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}^{2}.

An important subset of the Clifford numbers are the so called
paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\big\{s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}\;\big|\;s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

For any paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we define the
imaginary part

\operatorname{Im}(s):=s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n},

and the conjugate and the modulus in (
2.1
) reduce to

\overline{s}=s_{0}-s_{1}e_{1}-\dots-s_{n}e_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}+\dots+s_{n}^{2}.

The sphere of purely imaginary paravectors with modulus
1
, is defined by

\mathbb{S}:=\big\{J\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;J_{0}=0,\,|J|=1\big\}.

Any element
J\in\mathbb{S}
satisfies
J^{2}=-1
and hence the corresponding hyperplane

\mathbb{C}_{J}:=\big\{x+Jy\;\big|\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

is an isomorphic copy of the complex numbers. Moreover, for every paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we consider the corresponding
(n-1)
–sphere

[s]:=\big\{s_{0}+J|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\;\big|\;J\in\mathbb{S}\big\}.

A subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is called
axially symmetric
, if
[s]\subseteq U
for every
s\in U
.

In the hypercomplex context, several notions of holomorphic functions exist, each with distinct series expansions. For example, the classical theory related to the Dirac operator leads to monogenic function, which admit expansions in terms of Fueter polynomials. In this article we consider slice hyperholomorphic functions, which allow power series expansions, and are particularly suitable for use with Clifford operators.

Definition 2.1

(Slice hyperholomorphic functions)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be open, axially symmetric and consider

\mathcal{U}:=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;\big|\;x+\mathbb{S}y\subseteq U\big\}.

A function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
is called
left
(resp.
right
)
slice hyperholomorphic
, if there exist continuously differentiable functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
, such that for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}
:

i)

The function
f
admits for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
the representation

f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+Jf_{1}(x,y),\quad\Big(\text{resp.}\;f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+f_{1}(x,y)J\Big).

ii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
compatibility conditions

f_{0}(x,-y)=f_{0}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{1}(x,-y)=-f_{1}(x,y).

iii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
Cauchy-Riemann equations

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{1}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{0}(x,y)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{1}(x,y).

The class of left (resp. right) slice hyperholomorphic functions on
U
is denoted by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
(resp.
\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
). In the special case that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are real valued, we call
f

intrinsic
and the space of intrinsic functions is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(U)
.

Next, we turn our attention to Clifford modules over
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. For a real Banach space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the corresponding
Banach module

(2.2)

V:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\;\Big|\;v_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

and equip it with the
norm

\|v\|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad v\in V.

For any vector
v=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
s=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{A}}s_{B}e_{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we establish the left and the right scalar multiplication

\displaystyle sv:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(s_{B}v_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),\qquad\textit{(left multiplication)}

\displaystyle vs:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(v_{A}s_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).\hskip 23.9002pt\textit{(right multiplication)}

We recall the well known properties of these products

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|v\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|vs\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|v\|,\qquad\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}_{n},

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle=\|vs\|=|s|\|v\|,\hskip 137.42685pt\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

We will call
V
a
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive Banach module
, if
V
is reflexive with respect to its
\mathbb{R}
-linear dual space. For our particular space
V
in (
2.2
) this means that
V
is
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive, if and only if
V_{\mathbb{R}}
is reflexive.

Next, let us denote the set of
bounded right linear operators

\mathcal{B}(V):=\big\{T:V\rightarrow V\text{ right linear}\;\big|\;\operatorname{dom}(T)=V,\,T\text{ is bounded}\big\},

as well as the space of
closed right linear operators

\mathcal{K}(V):=\big\{T:V\rightarrow V\text{ right linear}\;\big|\;\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\text{ is right linear},\,T\text{ is closed}\big\}.

If an unbounded operator
T
is not closed, one may ask the question if there exists a closed extension. If this is the case we call the operator
closable
, and the smallest closed extension
\overline{T}
its
closure
.

In the case an operator is not closable, which happens to be the case for the right
H^{\infty}
-calculus in (
1.4
), the closure in some sense exceeds the notion of operators. We will now introduce the concept of
multivalued right linear operators
(also known as right linear relations) to overcome this problem. The idea is based on the fact that the graph

\text{graph}(T)=\big\{(v,Tv)\;\big|\;v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\big\},

of a right linear operator
T
, is a right linear subspace of
V\times V
.

Definition 2.2

(Multivalued operators)
.

Any right linear subspace
\mathcal{T}\subseteq V\times V
is called a
right linear multivalued operator
.

Let now
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}\subseteq V\times V
be two right linear multivalued operators, and
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
. Then the sum and product of multivalued operators is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{S}:=

\displaystyle\big\{(v,w_{1}+w_{2})\;\big|\;(v,w_{1})\in\mathcal{T},\,(v,w_{2})\in\mathcal{S}\big\},

(2.3)

\displaystyle s\mathcal{T}:=

\displaystyle\big\{(v,sw)\;\big|\;(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\circ\mathcal{S}:=

\displaystyle\big\{(v,u)\in V\times V\;\big|\;\exists w\in V\text{ such that }(v,w)\in\mathcal{S}\text{ and }(w,u)\in\mathcal{T}\big\}.

We can also equip any multivalued operator naturally with a domain

(2.4)

\operatorname{dom}(\mathcal{T}):=\big\{v\in V\;|\;\exists w\in V\text{ such that}(v,w)\in\mathcal{T}\big\}.

The great advantage of treating multivalued operators in our case is, that every right linear multivalued operator
\mathcal{T}
admits a closure

(2.5)

\overline{\mathcal{T}}:=\big\{(v,w)\in V\times V\;\big|\;\exists(v_{n},w_{n})_{n}\in\mathcal{T}\text{ such that }(v,w)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(v_{n},w_{n})\big\},

which is again a right linear multivalued operator. Note, that in the case that
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}
are graphs of right linear operator
T
and
S
, the definitions (
2.3
) coincide with the classical sum and product of operators. Also the closure (
2.5
) of multivalued operators coincides with the classical closure of operators in the case that
T
is closable. A more detailed introduction into multivalued operators can for example be found in
[
9
, Section 1]
.

3.
Properties of bisectorial operators

We collect in this section important properties of bisectorial Clifford operators. We state and prove some consequences of the bisectoriality estimate of the left
S
-resolvent in Lemma
3.2
. For reflexive Banach modules we show in Theorem
3.3
that bisectorial operators are always densely defined. Finally, in Theorem
3.5
we study also powers of a bisectorial operator.

In order to introduce bisectorial operators we define for every
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
the
double sector

\omega
D_{\omega}

(3.1)

D_{\omega}:=\big\{re^{J\phi}\;\big|\;r>0,\,J\in\mathbb{S},\,\phi\in I_{\omega}\big\},

using the union of intervals

I_{\omega}:=(-\omega,\omega)\cup(\pi-\omega,\pi+\omega).

Definition 3.1

(Bisectorial operators)
.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
is called
bisectorial of angle

\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, if its
S
-spectrum is contained in the closed double sector

\sigma_{S}(T)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}},

and for every
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
there exists
C_{\varphi}\geq 0
, such that the left
S
-resolvent (
1.2
) satisfies

(3.2)

\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\}).

We will now show that the estimate (
3.2
) on the left
S
-resolvent implies similar estimates on the right
S
-resolvent
S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)
and also on the pseudo resolvent operator
Q_{s}[T]^{-1}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and with the corresponding constant
C_{\varphi}
from (
3.2
), there holds the norm estimates

i)

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{|s|}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
;

ii)

\|Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}}{|s|^{2}}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
;

iii)

\|TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}+C_{\varphi}}{|s|}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
,

iv)

\|T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq 1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
.

Proof.

i)  Let us choose
J\in\mathbb{S}
, such that
s\in\mathbb{C}_{J}
. Then it is straight forward to check, by plugging in the definitions (
1.2
) of the
S
-resolvent operators, that

(3.3a)

\displaystyle S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)+S_{R}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)

\displaystyle=2(s_{0}-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)+S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{R}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)

\displaystyle=-2\operatorname{Im}(s)Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=-J\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)J.

By adding these two equations, we can express the right
S
-resolvent in terms of the left
S
-resolvent in the way

S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)=\frac{1}{2}\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)+S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)-\frac{J}{2}\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)J.

Using the given estimate (
3.2
) of the left
S
-resolvent, we can then estimate

\|S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}+\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|\overline{s}|}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}+\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|\overline{s}|}\Big)=\frac{2C_{\varphi}}{|s|}.

ii)  From the identity (
3.3a
), we get

(3.4)

\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)+S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)^{2}=4(s_{0}-T)^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-2},

and from (
3.3b
), we obtain

(3.5)

\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)J\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)J=-4\operatorname{Im}(s)^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-2}.

Adding now the equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) allows us to write
Q_{s}[T]^{-1}
in terms of the left
S
-resolvent in the form

\displaystyle\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)+S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle+\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)J\big(S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)-S_{L}^{-1}(\overline{s},T)\big)J

\displaystyle=4(s_{0}-T)^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-2}-4\operatorname{Im}(s)^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-2}

\displaystyle=4(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})Q_{s}[T]^{-2}=4Q_{s}[T]^{-1}.

Consequently, we can estimate

\|Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{4}\Big(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}+\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Big(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}+\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}\Big)\Big(\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}+\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}\Big)=\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}}{|s|^{2}}.

iii)  Rewriting the definition (
1.2
) as
TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)
, we can estimate

\|TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq\|Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}\|+\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|\leq\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}}{|s|}+\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}=\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}+C_{\varphi}}{|s|}.

iv)  Finally, since we can write
T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}=1-S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)s+TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}
, it follows immediately from (
3.2
) and iii), that

\|T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq 1+|s|\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|+|s|\|TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\|\leq 1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2}.\qed

The following theorem will be crucial in Section
4
for the well definedness of the right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus. It states that bisectorial operators are on the one hand densely defined, but more importantly that the Banach module
V
decomposes into the kernel and the closure of the range of
T
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
V
be a
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive Banach module and

T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then

i)

V=\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)
;

ii)

V=\ker(T)+\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
and
\ker(T)\cap\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

i)  Let
v\in V
and fix any
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then consider the family of vectors

v_{s}:=S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)sv,\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\}).

Due to (
3.2
), these vectors admit the uniform upper bound

\|v_{s}\|=\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)sv\|\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s|}|s|\|v\|=C_{\varphi}\|v\|,\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\}).

Since
V
is
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive, there exists
(s_{n})_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
and some
w\in V
, such that

(3.6)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}|s_{n}|=\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{s_{n}}=w,\quad\text{weakly in }V.

Note, that the weak convergence is understood with respect to the dual space of
\mathbb{R}
-linear functionals. From the second limit in (
3.6
), and the left
S
-resolvent identity

(3.7)

TS_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)=S_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)s_{n}-1,

which follows immediately from its definition (
1.2
), it follows that

(3.8)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}TS_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)v=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)s_{n}v-v=w-v,\quad\text{weakly in }V.

Moreover, from the first limit in (
3.6
) and the estimate (
3.2
), we also obtain the convergence

(3.9)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|S_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)v\|\leq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{\varphi}}{|s_{n}|}\|v\|=0.

Since
T
is a closed operator, it is also weakly closed, which means that from (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) one concludes
w-v=T0=0
. Hence, the weak convergence (
3.6
) turns into the weak convergence
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{s_{n}}=v
, i.e.
v
is weakly approximated by elements
v_{s_{n}}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
. Hence
v
is contained in the weak closure of
\operatorname{dom}(T)
, which is the same as being in the closure
v\in\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)
.

ii)   We take the same family
v_{s}
of vectors from (
3.9
), and analogously to (
3.6
) we choose this time a sequence
(s_{n})_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})
and some
w\in V
, such that

(3.10)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}|s_{n}|=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{s_{n}}=w,\quad\text{weakly in }V.

Using once more the identity (
3.7
) as well as the estimate (
3.2
), there also converges

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|Tv_{s_{n}}\|

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|TS_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)s_{n}v\|=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|(S_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)s_{n}-1)s_{n}v\|

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\big(\|S_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)\||s_{n}|+1\big)|s_{n}|\|v\|\leq(C_{\varphi}+1)\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}|s_{n}|\|v\|=0.

As above,
T
is closed, hence weakly closed, and we conclude from the two limits (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), that
w\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
and
Tw=0
, i.e.
w\in\ker(T)
. Moreover, with the
S
-resolvent identity (
3.7
), there converges

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}TS_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)v=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}S_{L}^{-1}(s_{n},T)s_{n}v-v=w-v,\quad\text{weakly in }V.

This shows that
w-v
is contained in the weak closure of
\operatorname{ran}(T)
, which is the same as
w-v\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
. Altogether we have decomposed
v
into

v=w+(v-w)\in\ker(T)+\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T).

In order to show that the sum is direct, let
v\in\ker(T)\cap\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
. Then, from the first limit in the upcoming Lemma
3.4
ii), we obtain

v=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow 0\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}(T-\overline{s})TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}v=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow 0\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}(T-\overline{s})Q_{s}[T]^{-1}0=0,

where we used that
TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}v=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}Tv
commutes since
v\in\ker(T)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
V
be a
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive Banach module and

T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every fixed
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
there exist the following limits:

i)

\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow\infty\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v=0
,
v\in V
;

ii)

\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow 0\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}|s|^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v=0
,
v\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
.

Proof.

i)  Let
v\in V
. By Theorem
3.3
i), there exists a sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
with

(3.12)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|v-v_{n}\|=0.

Since there commutes
TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}v_{n}=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}Tv_{n}
because of
v_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
, we can use the inequalities in Lemma
3.2
iii) & iv), to estimate

\displaystyle\|T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}(v-v_{n})\|+\|TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}Tv_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2})\|v-v_{n}\|+\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}+C_{\varphi}}{|s|}\|Tv_{n}\|.

Sending
s\rightarrow\infty
, the second term of the right hand side vanishes, and the estimate reduces to

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow\infty\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}\|T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\|\leq(1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2})\|v-v_{n}\|.

However, since this is true for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can also take the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
of the right hand side and with (
3.12
) we end up with the stated convergence

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow\infty\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}\|T^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\|=0.

ii)  Let
v\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
, i.e. there exists a sequence
(u_{n})_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
, such that

(3.13)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|v-Tu_{n}\|=0.

We can now use the inequalities in Lemma
3.2
ii) & iii), to estimate

\displaystyle\||s|^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\|

\displaystyle\leq\||s|^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}(v-Tu_{n})\|+\||s|^{2}TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}u_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\varphi}^{2}\|v-Tu_{n}\|+(2C_{\varphi}^{2}+C_{\varphi})|s|\|u_{n}\|.

Sending
s\rightarrow 0
, the second term on the right hand side vanishes and the estimate reduces to

\limsup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow 0\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}\||s|^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\|\leq 2C_{\varphi}^{2}\|v-Tu_{n}\|.

However, since this is true for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can also take the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
of the right hand side, and with (
3.13
) we end up with the stated convergence

\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\rightarrow 0\\
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus D_{\varphi}\end{subarray}}\||s|^{2}Q_{s}[T]^{-1}v\|=0.\qed

The next theorem describes properties of the powers of the bisectorial operator
T
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
V
be a
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive Banach module and
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is

(3.14)

\overline{\operatorname{dom}(T^{m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{m})}=\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T).

If
T
is also injective, then even

(3.15)

\overline{\operatorname{dom}(T^{m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{m})}=V.

Proof.

The inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
in (
3.14
) is trivial. For the inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
, let us consider for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the operator

(3.16)

r_{n}[T]:=n^{2}T^{2}(T^{2}+n^{2})^{-1}\Big(T^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)^{-1}.

Note that since we can write
T^{2}+n^{2}=Q_{Jn}[T]
and
T^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}=Q_{\frac{J}{n}}[T]
, for some arbitrary
J\in\mathbb{S}\subseteq\rho_{S}(T)
, the operator
r_{n}[T]
is well defined and bounded.

In the
first step
we want to show that for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there converges

(3.17)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{n}[T]^{m}u=u,\qquad u\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T).

For the induction start
m=1
, we rewrite the operator (
3.16
) as

\displaystyle r_{n}[T]

\displaystyle=1-T^{2}(T^{2}+n^{2})^{-1}-(T^{2}+n^{2})^{-1}\Big(T^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=1-T^{2}Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}-Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}Q_{\frac{J}{n}}[T]^{-1}.

Using the bounds of
Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}
in Lemma
3.2
ii), we can estimate

\displaystyle\|r_{n}[T]u-u\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T^{2}Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}u\|+\|Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}\|\|Q_{\frac{J}{n}}[T]^{-1}u\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T^{2}Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}u\|+\frac{2C_{\varphi}^{2}}{n^{2}}\|Q_{\frac{J}{n}}[T]^{-1}\|,\qquad u\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T).

Using the limits in Lemma
3.4
i) & ii), we then obtain

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|r_{n}[T]u-u\|\leq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|T^{2}Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}u\|+2C_{\varphi}^{2}\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}Q_{\frac{J}{n}}[T]^{-1}u\Big\|=0.

For the induction step
m\rightarrow m+1
, we estimate

\displaystyle\|r_{n}[T]^{m+1}u-u\|

\displaystyle\leq\|r_{n}[T]\|\|r_{n}[T]^{m}u-u\|+\|r_{n}[T]u-u\|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\varphi}^{2}(1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2})\|r_{n}[T]^{m}u-u\|+\|r_{n}[T]u-u\|.

where in the second line we used estimate

\|r_{n}[T]\|=n^{2}\big\|Q_{Jn}[T]^{-1}T^{2}Q_{\frac{J}{n}}[T]^{-1}\big\|\leq 2C_{\varphi}^{2}(1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2}),

which follows from Lemma
3.2
ii) & iv). Hence, using the induction assumption
m
as well as the induction start
m=1
, we conclude the converges

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|r_{n}[T]^{m+1}u-u\|\leq 2C_{\varphi}^{2}(1+2C_{\varphi}+2C_{\varphi}^{2})\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|r_{n}[T]^{m}u-u\|+\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|r_{n}[T]u-u\|=0.

Hence we have proven (
3.17
). Next, since we can write the
m
-th power of
r_{n}[T]
as

r_{n}[T]^{m}=n^{m}T^{2m}(T^{2}+n)^{-m}\Big(T^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{-m},

it is obvious that

(3.18)

\operatorname{ran}(r_{n}[T]^{m})=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{2m}).

Hence, for every
v\in\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)
there is
v_{n}:=r_{n}[T]^{m}v\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{2m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{2m})
, and we have already proven in (
3.17
) that there converges

v=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{n}.

This proves that
v\in\overline{\operatorname{dom}(T^{2m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{2m})}
, and we have verified (
3.14
) for all even powers of
T
. However, since the sets
\operatorname{dom}(T^{m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{m})
are getting smaller with increasing
m
, the equality (
3.14
) is then also true for every value
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
T
is also injective, there is
\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)=V
by Theorem
3.3
ii), and (
3.15
) follows from (
3.14
).
∎

4.
The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus right slice hyperholomorphic functions

This section contains the main results of this paper. We will start in Definition
4.2
by recalling the well established
\omega
-functional calculus for left and right holomorphic functions. We will do this in accordance with
[
42
, Definition 3.5]
and hence need the following spaces of slice hyperholomorphic functions.

Definition 4.1
.

For every
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
3.1
). Then we define the function spaces

i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;f\text{ is bounded},\;\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(re^{J\phi})|\frac{dr}{r}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta}\bigg\},

ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;f\text{ is bounded},\;\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(re^{J\phi})|\frac{dr}{r}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta}\bigg\},

iii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;f\text{ is bounded},\;\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(re^{J\phi})|\frac{dr}{r}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta}\bigg\}.

For those functions we then define the so called
\omega
-functional calculus.

Definition 4.2

(
\omega
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
(resp.
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{0}(D_{\theta})
) we define the
\omega
-functional calculus

(4.1)

\displaystyle f(T):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\bigg(\text{resp.}\;\;f(T):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\bigg),

where
\varphi\in(\omega,\theta)
and
J\in\mathbb{S}
are arbitrary and the integral (
4.1
) is independent of the choice.

As it is already worked out already in greater detail in
[
42
, Section 5]
, the
\omega
-functional calculus (
4.1
) can now be generalized to a larger class of left holomorphic functions. Since in this article we consider injective, sectorial operators
T
, we will define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for the following natural class of polynomially growing functions, see also
[
42
, Proposition 5.2]
.

Definition 4.3
.

For every
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
3.1
). Then we define the function spaces

i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists C,\alpha\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(|s|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{|s|^{\alpha}}\Big),\text{ for every }s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\};

ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists C,\alpha\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(|s|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{|s|^{\alpha}}\Big),\text{ for every }s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\};

iii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists C,\alpha\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(|s|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{|s|^{\alpha}}\Big),\text{ for every }s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\}.

Using the following regularization procedure, the so called
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus generalizes the
\omega
-functional calculus at least for left slice hyperholomorphic functions
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
, see
[
42
, Definition 5.3]
.

Definition 4.4

(Left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be sectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
. then we define the
left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

(4.3)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

using the function
e(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{(1+s^{2})^{m}}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m>\alpha
and
\alpha
from Definition
4.3
.

The main purpose of this section is to extend this left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus also to right slice hyperholomorphic functions
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
. Let us start with the following theorem, which gives a different representation of (
4.3
) for intrinsic functions.

Theorem 4.5

(Reformulation of the intrinsic
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus)
.

Let
V
be a
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive Banach module and
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
injective, bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, the operator
(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
is closable, and the left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.3
) can be written as

(4.4)

f(T)=\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}.

Here
e(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{(1+s^{2})^{m}}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m>\alpha
and
\alpha
from Definition
4.3
.

Proof.

For the inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
, we use the commutativity property
[
42
, Corollary 3.18 iii)]
of
e(T)
and
(ef)(T)
, to obtain the operator inclusion

\displaystyle(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}

\displaystyle=e(T)^{-1}e(T)(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T)e(T)e(T)^{-1}\subseteq e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T)=f(T).

Since the left
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
f(T)
is a closed operator, see
[
42
, Lemma 5.5]
, we conclude that
(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
is closable, and

\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}\subseteq f(T).

For the inverse inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
, let us consider the operators
r_{n}(T)
from (
3.16
). For any
v\in\operatorname{dom}(f(T))
and the fixed value
m
in the statement of the theorem, we consider the sequence of vectors

v_{n}:=r_{n}[T]^{m}v,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\overline{\operatorname{ran}}(T)=V
by Theorem
3.3
ii) and the injectivity of
T
, it is already proven in (
3.17
) that there converges

(4.6)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{n}[T]^{m}v=v.

By
[
42
, Theorem 3.16]
, the
\omega
-functional calculus of the regularizer function
e(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{(1+s^{2})^{m}}
can be written as

e(T)=T^{m}(1+T^{2})^{-m}.

Consequently, its range is given by

\operatorname{ran}(e(T))=\operatorname{dom}(T^{m})\cap\operatorname{ran}(T^{m}).

However, we also know the range of
r_{n}[T]^{m}
from (
3.18
), which then clearly gives us

(4.7)

v_{n}=r_{n}[T]^{m}v\in\operatorname{ran}(r_{n}[T]^{m})\subseteq\operatorname{ran}(e(T))=\operatorname{dom}\big((fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}\big).

Using once more the operator inclusion (
4.5
) as well as the commutation

f(T)r_{n}[T]^{m}\supseteq r_{n}[T]^{m}f(T),

from
[
42
, Corollary 5.11 i)]
, we can rewrite

(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}v_{n}=f(T)v_{n}=f(T)r_{n}[T]^{m}v=r_{n}[T]^{m}f(T)v.

The convergence (
3.17
) used for the vector
u=f(T)v
, then gives the limit

(4.8)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}v_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{n}[T]^{m}f(T)v=f(T)v.

Finally, combining the limits (
4.6
) and (
4.8
), shows that
v\in\operatorname{dom}\big(\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}\big)
and

\overline{(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}}\,v=f(T)v.\qed

Theorem 4.6

(Reformulation of the right
\omega
-functional calculus)
.

Let
V
be a reflexive Banach module and
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
injective, bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every

Proof.

For the inclusion

i’)

Theorem 4.11

(Product rule)
.

Let
V
be a
\mathbb{R}
-reflexive Banach module,
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
injective, bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then the left

-functional calculus (

) satisfies the product rule

i)

(fg)(T)\supseteq f(T)g(T)
,

,

;

ii)

(fg)(T)=f(T)g(T)

,

.

Moreover, the right
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.13
) satisfies the product rule

i’)

(fg)(T)=\overline{f(T)g(T)}
,
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{0}(D_{\theta})
,
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
;

ii’)

(fg)(T)\subseteq f(T)g(T)
,
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}^{\text{poly}}(D_{\theta})
,

.

Proof.

For the whole proof let
e(s)=\frac{s^{m}}{(1+s^{2})^{m}}
be a regularizer of
g
in the cases i), i’) and a regularizer of

is always a regularizer of the produce

i)  The commutation property

from
[

, Corollary 3.18 iii)]
, leads us to

and consequently there follows the product rule

is a bounded operator, there follows from the product rule of the
\omega
-functional calculus that

(fg)(T)=e(T)^{-1}(efg)(T)=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T)g(T)=f(T)g(T).

i’)  For the inclusion
\text{“}\supseteq\text{“}
, we note that

,

can also be written by the right

-functional calculus (

), and we get

where we used that

For the inverse inclusion
\text{“}\subseteq\text{“}
, we have

(fge)(T)e(T)^{-1}=f(T)(ge)(T)e(T)^{-1}\subseteq f(T)\overline{(ge)(T)e(T)^{-1}}=f(T)g(T).

Performing the closure on both sides of this inclusion, we get

ii’)  For the last product rule we consider

Theorem 4.14

V

First, it is already shown in

For the inclusion

∎

5.
Concluding remarks

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculi also have a broader context in the recently introduced fine structures on the
S
-spectrum. These are function spaces of nonholomorphic functions derived from the Fueter-Sce extension theorem, which connects slice hyperholomorphic and axially monogenic functions, the two main notions of holomorphicity in the Clifford setting. The connection is established through powers of the Laplace operator in a higher dimension, see
[
34
,
44
,
46
]
and also the translation
[
25
]
.

The fine structures on the
S
-spectrum constitute a set of function spaces and their related functional calculi. These structures have been introduced and studied in recent works
[
14
,
15
,
29
,
30
]
, while their respective
H^{\infty}
-versions are investigated in
[
24
,
31
]
.

Unlike complex holomorphic function theory, the noncommutative setting of the Clifford algebra allows multiple notions of hyperholomorphicity for vector fields. Consequently, different spectral theories emerge, each based on the concept of spectrum that relies on distinct Cauchy kernels. The spectral theory based on the
S
-spectrum was inspired by quaternionic quantum mechanics (see
[
12
]
), is associated with slice hyperholomorphicity, and began its development in 2006. A comprehensive introduction can be found in
[
20
]
, with further explorations done in
[
4
,
