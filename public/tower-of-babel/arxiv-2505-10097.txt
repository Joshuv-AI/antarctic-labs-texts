Odd Hadwiger’s conjecture for the complements of Kneser graphs
Meirun Chen, Reza Naserasr, Lujia Wang, Sanming Zhou

Abstract
A generalization of the four-color theorem, Hadwiger’s conjecture is considered as one of the most important and challenging problems in graph theory, and odd Hadwiger’s conjecture is a strengthening of Hadwiger’s conjecture by way of signed graphs. In this paper, we prove that odd Hadwiger’s conjecture is true for the complements K ¯ ​ ( n , k ) \overline{K}(n,k) of the Kneser graphs K ⁡ ( n , k ) K(n,k) , where n ≥ 2 ​ k ≥ 4 n\geq 2k\geq 4 . This improves a result of G. Xu and S. Zhou (2017) which states that Hadwiger’s conjecture is true for this family of graphs. Moreover, we prove that K ¯ ​ ( n , k ) \overline{K}(n,k) contains a 1-shallow complete minor of a special type with order no less than the chromatic number χ ​ ( K ¯ ​ ( n , k ) ) \chi(\overline{K}(n,k)) , and in the case when 7 ≤ 2 ​ k + 1 ≤ n ≤ 3 ​ k − 1 7\leq 2k+1\leq n\leq 3k-1 the gap between the odd Hadwiger number and chromatic number of K ¯ ​ ( n , k ) \overline{K}(n,k) is Ω ⁡ ( 1.5 k ) \Omega(1.5^{k}) .

Introduction

A graph
H
is said to be a
minor
of a graph
G
if it can be obtained from
G
by a series of operations composed of (i) deleting a vertex, (ii) deleting an edge, and (iii) contracting an edge. In such a case
G
is said to contain an
H
-minor
. Otherwise
G
is said to be
H
-minor-free
. An
H
-minor of
G
can be thought of as a family of pairwise vertex-disjoint connected subgraphs of
G
, each called a
bag
, such that the graph obtained from
G
by deleting all vertices outside these subgraphs and contracting all edges within each subgraph is isomorphic to
H
. The characterization of planar graphs as
K_{5}
- and
K_{3,3}
-minor-free graphs has inspired the study of relations between colorings and minors of graphs. In this regard Wagner
[
21
]
proved that if the four color theorem holds, then every
K_{5}
-minor-free graph is
4
-colorable. This motivated Hadwiger to conjecture that every
K_{t}
-minor-free graph is
(t-1)
-colorable. The
Hadwiger number
of a graph
G
, denoted by
h(G)
, is the largest
t
such that
K_{t}
is a minor of
G
. As usual, we use
\chi(G)
to denote the chromatic number of
G
. Hadwiger’s conjecture can be stated as follows.

Conjecture 1

(Hadwiger’s conjecture)
.

Every graph
G
satisfies
\chi(G)\leq h(G)
.

This conjecture is considered as one of the most central problems in graph theory. It has been proved for graphs with
\chi(G)\leq 6
(see
[
14
]
for the proof when
\chi(G)=6
). In the general case, a common approach towards establishing Hadwiger’s conjecture is to prove good upper bounds on the chromatic number of
K_{t}
-minor-free graphs in terms of
t
; see
[
11
]
and the references therein for results obtained by using this approach. Since it is challenging to prove Hadwiger’s conjecture in its general form, it is natural to attempt it for special classes of graphs; see, for example,
[
3
,
6
,
12
,
14
,
20
,
22
]
for several results along this direction.

Another venue of research is to consider possible strengthenings of Hadwiger’s conjecture by way of signed graphs. The importance of this direction lies in the following algorithmic consideration. Given a positive integer
t
, it can be decided in polynomial time if an input graph
G
has a
K_{t}
-minor or not. Upon receiving a “NO” answer for an input graph
G
, if Hadwiger’s conjecture is verified for the class of
K_{t}
-minor-free graphs, then the existence of a
(t-1)
-coloring of
G
is assured, whereas the decision problem of deciding whether a general graph is
(t-1)
-colorable is NP-complete for any fixed
t\geq 4
. As
K_{t}
-minor-free graphs have bounded average degree, this approach, unfortunately, applies only to families of sparse graphs. For example,
K_{3}
-minor-free graphs are forests, though in this particular case verifying 2-colorability of any graph can be done in polynomial time. To strengthen Hadwiger’s conjecture so that it works for a larger class of graphs, the notion of signed graphs and their minors are employed.

A
signed graph

(G,\sigma)
is a graph
G
together with a function
\sigma
that assigns a sign
\sigma(uv)
(
+
or
-
) to each edge
uv
of
G
. A
switching
at a vertex
x
of
(G,\sigma)
is to multiply the sign of all edges incident with
x
by a negative sign (that is, change the sign of each edge incident with
x
). Any signed graph obtained from
(G,\sigma)
by a series of switchings is said to be
switching equivalent
to
(G,\sigma)
. A cycle or closed walk
C
of
(G,\sigma)
is said to be
negative
or
unbalanced
if it contains an odd number of negative edges; otherwise it is
positive
or
balanced
. In other words,
C
is negative or positive depending on whether its sign is negative or positive, where the
sign
of
C
is the product of the signs of its edges. The concept of graph minors has been extended to signed graphs as follows. A signed graph
(H,\pi)
is said to be a
minor
of a signed graph
(G,\sigma)
if
(H,\pi)
can be obtained from
(G,\sigma)
by a series of the following operations: (i) deleting a vertex, (ii) deleting an edge, (iii) contracting a positive edge, (iv) switching at a vertex. Equivalently,
(H,\pi)
is said to be a minor of
(G,\sigma)
if there exist a signed graph
(G,\tau)
switching-equivalent to
(G,\sigma)
and
|V(H)|
pairwise vertex-disjoint subgraphs
B_{x}
of
G
, where
x\in V(H)
, such that (i) for each
x\in V(H)
, the set of positive edges of
B_{x}
under
\tau
induces a connected spanning subgraph of
B_{x}
, and (ii) for each edge
xy
of
H
, there exists an edge
uv
of
G
with
u\in V(B_{x})
and
v\in V(B_{y})
such that
\tau(uv)=\pi(xy)
. We call
B_{x}
the
bag
corresponding to
x
. This equivalent definition is based on the fact that one can obtain
(H,\pi)
from
(G,\tau)
by deleting all vertices outside these bags, deleting all edges between
B_{x}
and
B_{y}
except for one edge
uv
with
\tau(uv)=\pi(xy)
for each
xy
of
H
, and contracting all positive edges under
\tau
in each bag.

Denote by
(G,-)
be the signed graph with underlying graph
G
in which all edges are negative. Regarding each graph
G
as the signed graph
(G,-)
, the class of graphs becomes a subclass of the class of signed graphs. This point of view has led Gerards and Seymour to introduce independently a strengthening of Hadwiger’s conjecture which is now known as odd Hadwiger’s conjecture (see
[
10
,
15
]
). It was motivated by an earlier result of Catlin in
[
5
]
which can be considered as the first nontrivial case of the conjecture.

Conjecture 2

(Odd Hadwiger’s conjecture)
.

If
(G,-)
is
(K_{t},-)
-minor-free, then
\chi(G)\leq t-1
.

Note that in most literature a graph
G
for which
(G,-)
has no
(K_{t},-)
-minor is said to be odd
K_{t}
-minor-free and this definition is given without using signatures. In such a definition, vertices are 2-colored (corresponding to whether a switching is done at the vertex or not), contracted edges are bicolored (hence positive), and edges representing the connection of bags are monochromatic (hence negative). The above formulation of odd Hadwiger’s conjecture based on the notion of signed graphs is taken from
[
13
]
. To better present our work, we will restate odd Hadwiger’s conjecture using the following definition.

Definition 1
.

The
odd Hadwiger number
of a graph
G
, denoted
h_{o}(G)
, is the largest integer
t
such that
(K_{t},-)
is a minor of
(G,-)
.

Conjecture 3

(Odd Hadwiger’s conjecture restated)
.

Every graph
G
satisfies
\chi(G)\leq h_{o}(G)
.

Observe that if
(K_{t},-)
is a minor of a signed graph
(G,\sigma)
, then
K_{t}
is a minor of
G
. In other words, if
G
is
K_{t}
-minor-free, then
(G,-)
is
(K_{t},-)
-minor-free. (Note that the converse is not necessarily true.) So we have
h_{o}(G)\leq h(G)
. Thus, if
G
satisfies odd Hadwiger’s conjecture, then it satisfies Hadwiger’s conjecture. Furthermore, if true, odd Hadwiger’s conjecture bounds the chromatic number of a much larger class of graphs which, in particular, contains the class of bipartite graphs, whereas Hadwiger’s conjecture provides no bound for the chromatic number of this basic class. Hence in the study of odd Hadwiger’s conjecture one bounds the chromatic number of a family of graphs some of whose members are dense graphs, whereas in the study of Hadwiger’s conjecture one can only bound the chromatic number of sparse graphs. It is not hard to verify that in the case
t=3
odd Hadwiger’s conjecture says that every graph with no odd cycle is 2-colorable, which is trivially true. The case
t=4
is the above-mentioned result of Catlin
[
5
]
. The case
t=5
implies the four color theorem. A proof in this case, using the four color theorem, was announced by Gueinin in 2004 but no publication is available yet. All remaining cases are open.

While odd Hadwiger’s conjecture is stronger than Hadwiger’s conjecture, an asymptotic inverse implication is provided recently in
[
16
]
. More specifically, it is shown by Steiner
[
16
]
that if every
K_{t}
-minor-free graph admits an
f(t)
-coloring for some function
f
, then graphs with no odd
K_{t}
-minors admit a
2f(t)
-coloring. In particular, if Hadwiger’s conjecture is proved, then it would imply that every odd
K_{t}
-minor-free graph is
2(t-1)
-colorable. See
[
9
]
for more connections between odd Hadwiger’s conjecture and colorings of signed graphs.

A strengthening of Hadwiger’s conjecture, Hajós’ conjecture says that every graph
G
with
\chi(G)\geq t
contains a subdivision of
K_{t}
. This conjecture was proved to be false for every
t\geq 7
by Catlin
[
5
]
. In
[
18
]
, Thomassen gave several new classes of counterexamples to Hajós’ conjecture, including the complements of the Kneser graphs
K(3k-1,k)
for sufficiently large
k
. (Given integers
n
and
k
with
n\geq 2k\geq 4
, the
Kneser graph

K(n,k)
is the graph whose vertices are the
k
-subsets of
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
in which two such vertices are adjacent if and only if they are disjoint.) Meanwhile, he noticed that “it does not seem obvious” that these classes all satisfy Hadwiger’s conjecture. As a follow-up to this remark, Xu and Zhou
[
22
]
proved that indeed Hadwiger’s conjecture is true for the complement of every Kneser graph. Since odd Hadwiger’s conjecture is stronger than Hadwiger’s conjecture, it would be natural to ask whether the complements of all Kneser graphs satisfy odd Hadwiger’s conjecture. In this paper we prove that this is indeed the case. In a special case, our result confirms odd Hadwiger’s conjecture for a particular family of graphs with independence number 2, namely the complements of Kneser graphs
K(n,k)
with
2k+1\leq n\leq 3k-1
.
Among them the complement of
K(3k-1,k)
is especially interesting because not only does it provide a counterexample to Hajós’ conjecture for sufficiently large
k
but also the gap between its chromatic number
\binom{3k-1}{k-1}
and clique number
\binom{3k-2}{k-1}
can be arbitrarily large when
k
approaches infinity. In general, as with the case of Hadwiger’s conjecture, it is expected that the larger the gap between the chromatic number and clique number is, the harder it is to verify odd Hadwiger’s conjecture.

A
star
is the complete bipartite graph
K_{1,s}
for some
s\geq 1
. A minor of a graph is said to be
r
-shallow if each of its bags has radius at most
r
. A minor is said to be
strongly
1
-shallow
if each of its bags is either a single vertex or a star, the edge representing the connection between two star bags joins leaf vertices of the bags, and the edge representing the connection between a star bag and a single-vertex bag joins a leaf vertex of the star bag and the unique vertex of the single-vertex bag. Denote by
\overline{K}(n,k)
the complement of
K(n,k)
. Our main result, stated below, shows that not only does
\overline{K}(n,k)
satisfy odd Hadwiger’s conjecture but also it admits a strongly
1
-shallow
K_{t}
-minor for some
t
no less than
\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))
. Moreover, for
2k+1\leq n\leq 3k-1
, the gap between the odd Hadwiger number and chromatic number of
\overline{K}(n,k)
is
\Omega(1.5^{k})
; in particular, this gap can be arbitrarily large as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Theorem 4
.

For any integers
n
and
k
with
n\geq 2k\geq 4
, we have

\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))\leq h_{o}(\overline{K}(n,k)).

Moreover, there exists an integer
t=t(n,k)\geq\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))
such that
\overline{K}(n,k)
contains a strongly
1
-shallow
K_{t}
-minor. Furthermore, for
k\geq 3
, we have

h_{o}(\overline{K}(n,k))-\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))\geq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{12}(1.5^{k-1}-8k-2),&\text{ if }\ 2k+1\leq n\leq 3k-2\\
\frac{1}{6}(1.5^{k-1}-11),&\text{ if }\ n=3k-1.\end{cases}

Since
\overline{K}(n,2)
is isomorphic to the line graph of
K_{n}
, the statement that
\chi(\overline{K}(n,2))\leq h_{o}(\overline{K}(n,2))
is covered by a recent result of Steiner [18] which establishes odd Hadwiger’s conjecture for the line graphs of simple graphs.

The strongly
1
-shallow
K_{t}
-minor of
\overline{K}(n,k)
guaranteed by Theorem
4
gives rise to a
(K_{t},-)
-minor of
(\overline{K}(n,k),-)
with the same bags and connections (see Lemma
5
and its proof). It is worth mentioning that Bollobás, Catlin and Erdős
[
2
]
proved that Hadwiger’s conjecture holds for almost all graphs. In fact, they proved the existence of a strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minor in almost every
t
-chromatic graph, so they actually established odd Hadwiger’s conjecture for almost all graphs. However, even if we know a
t
-chromatic graph satisfies odd Hadwiger’s conjecture, in general it is difficult to find a strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minor in it or prove such a
K_{t}
-minor does not exist. In our proof of Theorem
4
, we will give an explicit construction of such a strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minor in
\overline{K}(n,k)
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section we introduce notation and present five preliminary results, including two lemmas which prove Theorem
4
when
k=2
and
k
is a divisor of
n
, respectively. In Section
3
, we give three constructions of strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minors of
\overline{K}(n,k)
using a lemma from
[
22
]
(see Lemma
9
in the next section). In Section
4
, we prove that for each pair of integers

with
n\geq 2k+1\geq 7
at least one of these constructions gives a strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minor with
t\geq\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))
, thereby completing the proof of Theorem
4
.

Preliminaries

Lemma 5
.

If a graph
G
contains a strongly
1
-shallow
K_{t}
-minor, then
h_{o}(G)\geq t
.

Proof.

Suppose that
H
is a strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minor of
G
. Then switching at the center vertex of each star bag of
(H,-)
results in a
(K_{t},-)
-minor of
(G,-)
. Hence
h_{o}(G)\geq t
.
∎

In what follows we always assume
n
and
k
are integers with
n\geq 2k\geq 4
. Set
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and denote by
\binom{[n]}{k}
the set of all

. Then both

and
\overline{K}(n,k)
have vertex set
\binom{[n]}{k}
.

Lemma 6
.

For any integers
s,k\geq 2
, we have
\chi(\overline{K}(sk,k))\leq h_{o}(\overline{K}(sk,k))
, and
\overline{K}(sk,k)
contains a strongly
1
-shallow
K_{t}
-minor, where

.

Proof.

The chromatic number and clique number of
\overline{K}(sk,k)
are equal. Turning each vertex in a maximum clique of
\overline{K}(sk,k)
into a single-vertex bag, we obtain a strongly 1-shallow
K_{t}
-minor of
\overline{K}(sk,k)
where
t=\chi(\overline{K}(sk,k))
. Hence the result follows.
∎

Lemma 7
.

For any integer
n\geq 4
, we have
\chi(\overline{K}(n,2))\leq h_{o}(\overline{K}(n,2))
, and
\overline{K}(n,2)
contains a strongly

-shallow

-minor, where

If

and the result follows from Lemma

. If

, and we can obtain a strongly 1-shallow

-minor of

by treating each vertex

∎

2(k-2)(s-6)n(2k-3)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)\cdots(n-k+1)\geq k!s(n+2k-4)(n+1).

We break this task into three pieces. First, it is easy to see

(n-5)(n-6)\cdots(n-k+1)\geq k(k-1)\cdots 7\cdot 6
by comparing factors. Second, since
n\geq sk\geq 7k
, we see that
(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)\geq(n+2k-4)(n+1)
. Finally, since
k\geq 5
and
s\geq 7
, we have
2(k-2)(2k-3)(s-6)n\geq 120s
. Putting these together, we obtain (
15
) immediately.

Case II.
k=4
.

II.1.
n=4s+1
.

We note again an upper bound for
l
:

l=\left\lceil\frac{4s+1-1}{6}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{2s}{3}\right\rceil\leq\frac{2s+2}{3}.

Hence, it suffices to show that

(s-l-1)\binom{4s+1}{4}\geq s\binom{\lfloor\frac{4s+1}{2}\rfloor}{4}+sl\left\lceil\frac{4s+1}{2}\right\rceil,

which holds if

\left(s-\frac{2s+2}{3}-1\right)\binom{4s+1}{4}\geq s\binom{2s}{4}+s\cdot\frac{2s+2}{3}\cdot(2s+1).

The latter inequality is further reduced to

\frac{s}{18}\left(52s^{4}-316s^{3}+99s^{2}-5s-22\right)\geq 0,

which holds for
s\geq 7.

II.2.
n=4s+2
.

In this case, we have
l=\lceil\frac{4s+1}{6}\rceil
and so the desired inequality (
13
) becomes

\left(s-\left\lceil\frac{4s+1}{6}\right\rceil-1\right)\binom{4s+2}{4}\geq s\binom{2s+1}{4}+s\left\lceil\frac{4s+1}{6}\right\rceil(2s+1).

This is reduced to

\frac{s(2s+1)}{6}\left(-4\left(8s^{2}+1\right)\left\lceil\frac{4s+1}{6}\right\rceil+30s^{3}-29s^{2}-3s+2\right)\geq 0.

One can verify this inequality for
s=7
by directly plugging in
s=7
. For
s\geq 8
, we may replace the ceiling by
(4s+6)/6
and then verify the simplified inequality straightaway.

II.3.
n=4s+3
.

In this case, we have
l=\lceil\frac{2s+1}{3}\rceil\leq\frac{2s+3}{3}
and the desired inequality (
13
) becomes

(s-l-1)\binom{4s+3}{4}\geq s\binom{\lfloor\frac{4s+3}{2}\rfloor}{4}+sl\left\lceil\frac{4s+3}{2}\right\rceil,

which holds if

\left(s-\frac{2s+3}{3}-1\right)\binom{4s+3}{4}\geq s\binom{2s+1}{4}+s\cdot\frac{2s+3}{3}\cdot(2s+2).

The latter inequality is reduced to

\frac{s}{18}\left(52s^{4}-276s^{3}-553s^{2}-321s-72\right)\geq 0,

which holds for
s\geq 7.

Case III.
k=3
.

III.1
n=3s+1
.

In this case, we have
l=\lceil\frac{3s}{4}\rceil\leq\frac{3s+3}{4}
and so the desired inequality (
13
) becomes

(s-l-1)\binom{3s+1}{3}\geq s\binom{\lfloor\frac{3s+1}{2}\rfloor}{3}+sl\left\lceil\frac{3s+1}{2}\right\rceil,

which holds if

\left(s-\frac{3s+3}{4}-1\right)\binom{3s+1}{3}\geq s\binom{\frac{3s+1}{2}}{3}+s\cdot\frac{3s+3}{4}\cdot\frac{3s+2}{2}.

The latter inequality is reduced to

\frac{s}{16}\left(9s^{3}-135s^{2}-31s+1\right)\geq 0,

which holds for
s\geq 16.

III.2
n=3s+2
.

In this case, we have
l=\lceil\frac{3s+1}{4}\rceil\leq\frac{3s+4}{4}
and so the desired inequality (
13
) becomes

(s-l-1)\binom{3s+2}{3}\geq s\binom{\lfloor\frac{3s+2}{2}\rfloor}{3}+sl\left\lceil\frac{3s+2}{2}\right\rceil,

(16)

which holds if

\left(s-\frac{3s+4}{4}-1\right)\binom{3s+2}{3}\geq s\binom{\frac{3s+2}{2}}{3}+s\cdot\frac{3s+4}{4}\cdot\frac{3s+3}{2}.

The latter inequality is reduced to

\frac{s}{16}\left(9s^{3}-144s^{2}-178s-56\right)\geq 0,

which holds for
s\geq 18.
In addition, one can directly verify (
16
) for
s=17
.

Case IV.
s=6
.

In this case, we have
6k\leq n\leq 7k-1
and

l=\left\lceil\frac{6k+r-1}{2(k-1)}\right\rceil\leq\frac{7k-1-1+2k-3}{2(k-1)}\leq\frac{9k-5}{2(k-1)}.

To prove (
13
), it suffices to show

(6-l-1)\binom{6k}{k}\geq 6\binom{\lfloor\frac{7k-1}{2}\rfloor}{k}+6l\left\lceil\frac{7k-1}{2}\right\rceil,

which holds if

\frac{k-5}{2(k-1)}\binom{6k}{k}\geq 6\binom{\frac{7k-1}{2}}{k}+6\cdot\frac{9k-5}{2(k-1)}\cdot\frac{7k}{2}.

Multiplying both sides by
2^{k+1}(k-1)k!
, the latter is equivalent to

\displaystyle(k-5)2^{k}(6k)(6k-1)\cdots(5k+1)

\displaystyle\geq 12(k-1)(7k-1)(7k-3)\cdots(5k+1)

\displaystyle\quad+21k(9k-5)2^{k}k!.

To conclude the proof, we only need to show the following two inequalities:

(k-5)2^{k}(5k)\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(6k-i)\geq 12(k-1)\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(7k-2i-1)

(17)

(k-5)2^{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(6k-i)\geq 21k(9k-5)2^{k}k!.

(18)

In fact, for
k\geq 7
, we have

\displaystyle(k-5)2^{k}(5k)\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(6k-i)

\displaystyle=(k-5)\left(\frac{12}{7}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{7}{6}\right)^{k}(5k)\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(6k-i)

\displaystyle\geq(k-5)\left(\frac{12}{7}\right)^{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(7k-2i-1)

\displaystyle\geq 12(k-1)\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(7k-2i-1).

Hence inequality (
17
) holds. For
k\geq 6
, we have

(k-5)2^{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(6k-i)\geq(k-5)2^{k}6^{k-1}k!\geq 21k(9k-5)2^{k}k!.

Hence inequality (
18
) holds.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Assuming
n=sk+r
and
k-s+1\leq r\leq k-1
, we have
t_{3}(n,k)\geq\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))
in each of the following cases:

(a)

s=2
(hence
r=k-1
);

(b)

s=3
,
k\geq 4
;

(c)

s=4
,
k\geq 7
;

(d)

s=4
,
k=6
,
k-3\leq r\leq k-2
;

(e)

s=5
,
k\geq 9
;

(f)

s=5
,
k=8
,
r=4
.

Moreover, when
s=2
, we have

h_{o}(\overline{K}(3k-1,k))-\chi(\overline{K}(3k-1,k))\geq\frac{1}{6}(1.5^{k-1}-11).

(19)

Proof.

The claim to be proved is

t_{3}(sk+r,k)\geq\chi(\overline{K}(sk+r,k))=\left\lceil\frac{1}{s}{sk+r\choose k}\right\rceil.

In view of (
5
), to prove
t_{3}(n,k)\geq\chi(\overline{K}(n,k))
it would be enough to show that

{sk+r-1\choose k-1}+\frac{1}{s+1}\left({sk+r-1\choose k}-s{(s-1)k+r-1\choose k}\right)-\frac{s}{s+1}\geq\frac{1}{s}{sk+r\choose k}.

Using the formulas
{sk+r-1\choose k}=\frac{(s-1)k+r}{k}{sk+r-1\choose k-1}
and
{sk+r\choose k}=\frac{sk+r}{k}{sk+r-1\choose k-1}
, this inequality can be rewritten as

\frac{(s^{2}-s)k-r}{sk}{sk+r-1\choose k-1}\geq s{(s-1)k+r-1\choose k}+s.

(20)

Multiplying both sides by
sk!
, the inequality can be further rewritten as

((s^{2}-s)k-r)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(sk+r-i)\geq s^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}((s-1)k+r-i)+s^{2}k!.

Since
k!\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}((s-1)k+r-i)
, it suffices to show that

\frac{(s^{2}-s)k-r}{(s-2)k+r}\cdot\frac{(sk+r-1)}{(s-1)k+r-1}\cdot\frac{(sk+r-2)}{(s-1)k+r-2}\cdots\frac{(s-1)k+r+1}{(s-2)k+r+1}\geq 2s^{2}.

Since
k-s+1\leq r\leq k-1
, we have
\frac{(s^{2}-s)k-r}{(s-2)k+r}\geq\frac{s^{2}-s-1}{s-1}
and, furthermore,
\frac{(s-1)k+r+i}{(s-2)k+r+i}\geq\frac{s+1}{s}
for any
1\leq i\leq k-1
. Therefore, to prove the inequality above it would be enough to prove

\frac{s^{2}-s-1}{s-1}\left(\frac{s+1}{s}\right)^{k-1}\geq 2s^{2}.

(21)

If
s=2
, the left-hand side of (
21
) is
(\frac{3}{2})^{k-1}
and
2s^{2}=8
, so the inequality holds for
k\geq 7
(and
r=k-1
). For
k=3,4,5,6
and
r=k-1
, we can verify inequality (
20
) directly. If
s=3
, the left-hand side of (
21
) is
\frac{5}{2}(\frac{4}{3})^{k-1}
while
2s^{2}=18
, and the inequality holds for
k\geq 8
and
k-2\leq r\leq k-1
. For
k=4,5,6,7
and
r=k-1,k-2
, once again we can verify inequality (
20
) directly.

Similarly, for
s=4
, (
21
) is reduced to
\frac{11}{3}(\frac{5}{4})^{k-1}>2s^{2}=32
which is valid for any
k\geq 11
and
k-3\leq r\leq k-1
. Once again for
k=7,8,9,10
,
r=k-1,k-2,k-3
, and
k=6,r=k-2,k-3
, we can verify inequality (
20
) directly.

Finally, for
s=5
, (
21
) is reduced to
\frac{19}{4}(\frac{6}{5})^{k-1}>2s^{2}=50
which holds for any
k\geq 14
and
k-4\leq r\leq k-1
. For
k=9,10,11,12,13
,
r=k-1,k-2,k-3,k-4
, and
(k,r)=(8,k-4)
, we can verify inequality (
20
) directly.

It remains to prove (
19
) in the case
s=2
. Since
s=2
, we have
r=k-1
,
n=3k-1
, and

\chi(\overline{K}(3k-1,k))=\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}{3k-1\choose k}\right\rceil\leq\frac{1}{2}\left({3k-1\choose k}+1\right).

Also, by Construction 3,
h_{o}(\overline{K}(3k-1,k))\geq t_{3}(3k-1,k)
, and
t_{3}(3k-1,k)
is bounded from below by (
5
). Thus,

\displaystyle\ h_{o}(\overline{K}(3k-1,k))-\chi(\overline{K}(3k-1,k))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ {3k-2\choose k-1}+\frac{1}{3}\left({3k-2\choose k}-2{2k-2\choose k}\right)-\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{2}{3k-1\choose k}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {3k-2\choose k-1}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{2k-1}{k}{3k-2\choose k-1}-2{2k-2\choose k}\right)-\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{3k-1}{k}{3k-2\choose k-1}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{6}\frac{k+1}{k}{3k-2\choose k-1}-\frac{2}{3}{2k-2\choose k}-\frac{7}{6}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left(\frac{1}{6}\frac{k+1}{k-1}\frac{3k-2}{2k-2}\frac{3k-3}{2k-3}\cdots\frac{2k}{k}-\frac{2}{3}\right){2k-2\choose k}-\frac{7}{6}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{6}\frac{k+1}{k-1}\frac{3k-2}{2k-2}\frac{3k-3}{2k-3}\cdots\frac{2k}{k}-\frac{2}{3}-\frac{7}{6}

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{6}\left(\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{k-1}-11\right),

as desired by (

), where in the last step we used the fact that

∎
