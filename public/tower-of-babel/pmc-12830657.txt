The effect of high-dose long-term therapy of intravenous immunoglobulins in autoimmune autonomic and sensory small fiber neuropathy: a retrospective open-label controlled study
Authors: Peter Novak, Alexandra Witte, Sadie P. Marciano, Donna Felsenstein, Long Ngo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intravenous Immunoglobulins (IVIG) are used to treat autoimmune autonomic and sensory small fiber neuropathy (ASFN). The long-term effects of a high immunomodulatory dose of IVIG in ASFN remain unclear. This retrospective controlled study evaluated the long-term effects of high dose (2 g/kg/monthly) IVIG compared to usual treatment in patients with ASFN. Inclusion criteria were: Survey of Autonomic Symptoms (SAS) score ≥ 10, abnormal autonomic testing and skin biopsy, and positive inflammatory/autoimmune markers or a history of acute illness preceding autonomic symptoms. Quantitative Scale for Grading of Cardiovascular Autonomic Reflex Tests and Small Fibers from Skin Biopsies (QASAT) was used to grade tests. Patients were treated with IVIG until their condition improved and reached a plateau. Linear models controlling for baseline were used to estimate the effect of IVIG. 41 ASFN patients were treated with IVIG and compared to 66 ASFN control patients treated with usual care. Both groups had evaluations at baseline and at the end of the trial. The average time IVIG therapy improved ASFN and reached plateau was 2.25 ± 0.99 years. The adverse effects of IVIG were frequent (prevalence 93%) but tolerable in most patients. IVIG improved SAS (p < 0.001) and QASAT total (p < 0.001), cerebral blood flow (p = 0.002) and autonomic failure (p = 0.035) scores. SAS and QASAT autonomic failure scores worsened in controls. Skin biopsy improved in both arms, but improvement was greater (p = 0.017) in the IVIG arm. This study provides evidence supporting the beneficial effect of long-term high-dose IVIG therapy for ASFN. Placebo-controlled, double-blinded studies are required to confirm our findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33059-7.

Introduction

An autoimmune autonomic and sensory small fiber neuropathy (ASFN) is a type of acquired neuropathy where patients develop symptoms and signs of neuropathy weeks or months after an infection or a precipitating event1–3. ASFN is associated with autonomic and sensory dysfunction of variable proportions. Although the exact mechanisms underlying autoimmune dysregulation in ASFN are debated, the acute/subacute onset, temporal relationship to preceding events, presence of inflammatory markers, and responsiveness to immunotherapy point to autoimmunity and/or low-grade inflammation1–5.

Previous uncontrolled studies showed the efficacy of intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) for the therapy of small fiber neuropathy associated with autoimmune dysautonomia2,6, positive trisulfated heparin disaccharide, fibroblast growth factor-3 or plexin D1 antibodies7, postural tachycardia syndrome (POTS)8,9, and post COVID-19 infection10,11. Nevertheless, randomized controlled studies using IVIG for painful small-fiber neuropathy12,13 and POTS14 did not show IVIG benefits. The cause of dichotomy in the effectiveness of IVIG between uncontrolled and controlled studies remains unclear. Differences in inclusion criteria, various outcome measures, IVIG dose and duration of therapy may all contribute to the observed discrepancies.

This retrospective study aimed to describe the long-term effects of a high dose (2 g/kg monthly) IVIG in ASFN as an add on therapy to usual care, as compared to ASFN controls receiving a usual symptomatic treatment.

Methods

Standard protocol approvals and patient consent

The Institutional Review Board of the Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Harvard Medical School, approved the study as a minimal-risk retrospective study (Protocol: 2024P002171), and the requirement for signing a consent form was waived. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study design, sample, and setting

This was a single-center, retrospective controlled study treating ASFN patients at the Autonomic Clinic and Laboratory at the Brigham and Women’s Faulkner Hospital conducted between 2016 and 2025. Data were prospectively collected into our database and were analyzed at the end of the period.

Inclusion criteria, established prior to the study onset, were: patients aged 18 years or older and those diagnosed with ASFN (Table 1). The ASFN was defined as a combination of the following (all conditions must be met): (1) Symptoms of autonomic impairment. These symptoms typically include chronic (> 6 months) combination of orthostatic intolerance (lightheadedness, dizziness, pre-syncope, dyspnea, fatigue, palpitations, anxiety, nausea, chest pain, headaches), gastrointestinal, urinary and sudomotor complaints3,5,15; (2) Abnormal autonomic testing; (3) Abnormal skin biopsy; 4) History of infectious illness that preceded the development of autonomic symptoms or elevated inflammatory or autoimmune markers. We searched for inflammatory/autoimmune markers that are typically associated with a preceding infection16. Exclusion criteria were: presence of disorders associated with small fiber neuropathy such as diabetes, pre-diabetes, parkinsonism, Parkinson’s disease, history of heavy alcohol use, B12 and/or folate deficiency, hepatitis C, HIV infection, chemotherapy exposure and/or cancer diagnosis, history of systemic autoimmune disease, and any comorbid conditions or use of medications that have been associated with small fiber neuropathy, evidence of large fiber neuropathy (loss of tendon reflexes, loss of proprioception, nerve conduction studies suggesting large fiber neuropathy), use of vasoactive medications that may affect autonomic nervous system during testing, and inability to complete the autonomic testing. We also excluded painful SFN, defined as SFN with prominent burning sensations, shooting pains, or prickling sensations, which may be accompanied by limited or subclinical autonomic symptoms12,17–19. Painful SFN differs from ASFN and may respond to IVIG treatment in a different way.

Patients reported outcome measures (PROMs)

Survey of autonomic symptoms (SAS)

Autonomic symptoms were assessed with the SAS, a self-administered, validated instrument for evaluating autonomic complaints over the past six months20. SAS evaluates the orthostatic, sudomotor, vasomotor, gastrointestinal, urinary, and sexual (men) domains. Higher scores reflect higher symptom presence and a higher impact on daily life. A cutoff score > 3 has 90% specificity and 65% sensitivity to detect autonomic dysfunction in early diabetic neuropathy.

COMPASS-31 is currently one of the most commonly used instruments to assess autonomic symptom burden21. We have previously demonstrated a strong positive correlation between SAS and COMPASS-3122, indicating that both instruments are comparable and capture similar information. We chose to use SAS because it is simpler, imposes less burden on patients, and is easier to administer. Given the high correlation (r = 0.74), SAS and COMPASS-31 scores can be used interchangeably for the inclusion criteria. COMPASS-31 scores can be estimated from SAS through regression where one point change of COMPASS-31 is equal to 0.48 change in SAS.

Neuropathy total symptom Score-6 (NTSS-6)

The NTSS was used to assess sensory complaints23. The NTSS-6 is a patient-reported validated instrument with a 0-21.96 point scale that assesses the sensory symptoms for the past 24 h. Evaluated symptoms include aching pain, allodynia, burning pain, lancinating pain, numbness, and prickling sensations. Higher scores reflect greater symptom frequency and intensity.

Pain, numerical rating scale (PNRS)

The pain for the last seven days was assessed using the commonly used 0–10 numerical rating scale24.

Small fiber neuropathy

SFN was defined by the combination of clinical signs suggestive of small fiber dysfunction including the autonomic symptoms as evidenced by abnormal SAS score and sensory symptoms as evidenced by abnormal NTSS-6 score and abnormal neurological examination indicative of small fiber dysfunction (pinprick and thermal sensory loss, allodynia, and hyperalgesia) and abnormal skin biopsy as evidenced by abnormal epidermal nerve fiber density (ENFD) or abnormal sweat gland nerve fiber density (SGNFD)16,19. In that definition of SFN, autonomic symptoms should prevail over sensory symptoms, although both can be present. Patients with painful small fiber neuropathy12,17 were not enrolled in this study.

Autonomic testing

Testing was performed following established standards and was previously described in detail25. Medications affecting the cardiovascular and autonomic nervous system were discontinued for five half-lives or longer before the testing. Cardiovascular reflex tests included deep breathing (evaluating cardiovagal parasympathetic function), the Valsalva maneuver (evaluating sympathetic adrenergic function using blood pressure responses and cardiovagal parasympathetic function using the heart rate responses), and the tilt test (evaluating both sympathetic and parasympathetic function). Deep breathing test was performed for six respiratory cycles with each inhalation and exhalation equal to ten seconds. Parasympathetic cardiovagal index was calculated as the average difference between expiratory and inspiratory heart rate. The Valsalva maneuver was performed as a forced expiration at the expiratory pressure of 40 mmHg for 15 s. The difference between baseline and end of phase 2 in mean blood pressure was used as a sympathetic adrenergic index26. Patients were tilted at 70 degrees for 10 min following 10 min of supine rest. Recorded signals included electrocardiogram, blood pressure, end-tidal CO2, and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFv) in the middle cerebral artery using Transcranial Doppler. Blood pressure was obtained intermittently every minute through brachial sphygmomanometry using an automated monitor Welch Allyn CVSM 6400 Monitor (Skaneateles Falls, NY) and continuously using the photoplethysmographic signal volume-clamped in the finger by servo control (Human NIBP Nano Interface MLA382, ADInstruments Inc., Colorado Springs, CO, USA and Human NIBP Nano Wrist Unit FMS910804, Finapress Medical Systems, Amsterdam, Netherlands). End-tidal CO2 was obtained using the Nonin Respsense Capnograph (Nonin Medical Inc. Plymouth, MN) via a nasal cannula. A pulse oximeter (part of the Welch Allyn monitor) was used to monitor the oxygen saturation throughout the testing. CBFv was obtained using a MultiDop T (Multigon, New York, NY) with the transducer attached to the head using a head frame with a three-dimensional positioner. The depth and angle of insonation were kept constant throughout the head-up tilt test. The physiologic signals were recorded using the PowerLab 16/35 data acquisition system with LabChart 8 software (ADInstruments Inc., Colorado Springs, CO, USA) and sampled at 400 Hz. Electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) was used to measure the sudomotor function27.

Skin biopsy

Density of epidermal nerves (ENFD) and sweat glands (SGNFD) were obtained using established standards19,28. Skin samples were taken from the proximal thigh 20 cm distal to the iliac spine and the calf 10 cm above the lateral malleolus using a 3-mm circular disposable punch tool. Skin biopsies were processed and immunostained at Therapath Neuropathology (NYC, NY). The specimens were immuno-stained with PGP 9.5, and epidermal nerve fibers were counted at high magnification in three different sections. Skin biopsies were evaluated blindly as the Therapath did not have information about the assignment in the treatment trial.

Quantitative scale for grading of cardiovascular autonomic reflex tests and small fibers from skin biopsies (QASAT)

Test results were graded using the Quantitative Scale for Grading of Cardiovascular Autonomic Reflex Tests and Small Fibers from Skin Biopsies (QASAT)25,29. QASAT is an objective instrument for grading the intensity of dysautonomia, small fiber neuropathy, and cerebral blood flow abnormalities. Each domain (heart rate, blood pressure, cerebral blood flow, end-tidal CO2) is analyzed separately, where a score equal to 0 is normal, and above 0 is abnormal.

The parasympathetic cardiovagal score is derived from the deep breathing test and is inversely proportional to the amplitude of respiratory sinus arrhythmia. The sympathetic adrenergic score from the Valsalva maneuver is proportional to the decline of mean blood pressure at the end of phase II of the Valsalva. Orthostatic scores (cerebral blood flow, capnography, orthostatic hypotension) are proportional to the duration of respective abnormalities during the head-up tilt test. For example, the score of three for orthostatic hypotension (range 0–10) means that the orthostatic hypotension duration was three minutes of the head-up tilt test. In analogy the CBFv score equal to five (range 0–10) means that the CBFv was reduced below the normal value for five minutes of the tilt test. QASAT-total score is defined as a sum of individual subscores, the range is 0–63.

QASAT autonomic failure score (QASATaf)29 is defined: QASATaf=QASATcardiovagal+QASATadrenergic+QASATsudomotor+QASATVr where QASATcardiovagal is the cardiovagal failure score and is obtained from heart rate responses in deep breathing test; the QASATadrenergic is the adrenergic failure score obtained as the summation of blood pressure responses to Valsalva maneuver and the head-up tilt scores; the QASATsudomotor is sudomotor failure score obtained from the ESC and QASATVr is the Valsalva ratio score obtained from the Valsalva maneuver. The range of QASATaf is 0–23. QASATaf is defined as normal (0), mildly abnormal (1–3), moderately abnormal (4–12), and severely abnormal (> 12). Autonomic, respiratory, and cerebral blood flow measurements from the tilt were previously described in detail25 and were calculated using the qpack package29.

Laboratory markers

Baseline laboratory tests were obtained at the discretion of the referring physicians, most commonly targeting metabolic, inflammatory, autoimmune, and hormonal profiles. We searched for variety of markers previously reported to be associated with preceding infections, post-infectious syndromes, and/or low-grade inflammation/autoimmunity. The complete list and range of these markers is provided in Supplementary Table S1 online. Post-treatment laboratory assessments were performed unfrequently, which precluded their inclusion in the final analyses.

Intervention

IVIG was used as an intervention. High-dose (2 gram/kg/month) was chosen since that dose is usually used for immunomodulatory therapy for various immune and inflammatory disorders30,31. The type of brand was determined by insurance and by market availability. IVIG was used as an add-on intervention to the usual medical care.

All patients were informed in detail regarding the potential effects of IVIG, e.g. immunomodulation and were informed about the potential side effects, including anemia, thrombosis, renal failure, and others32,33. According to our protocol, we initiated a monthly IVIG dose, divided into three or four administrations over three or four consecutive or non-consecutive days. As premedication, we administered oral acetaminophen 650 mg and loratidine 10 mg, with the option to give an additional acetaminophen 650 mg and 25 mg of diphenhydramine after the infusion. All IVIG patients received fluids, starting with 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride peri-infusion. IVIG was initially administered at a rate of 0.8 mg/kg/min, which was titrated up to 8 mg/kg/min based on the patient’s tolerance. Except for two patients, the infusions were administered at the patient’s home by infusion companies. All patients were monitored for potential IVIG side effects, including flushing, chills, headaches, and chest tightness, shortness of breath, nausea, heart or blood pressure changes. The infusion rate, and/or pre-post medication were adjusted, and/or brand was changed in the presence of side effects.

The patients were treated with IVIG until their condition improved and reached a plateau. Repeated autonomic testing was scheduled at least two months after the last dose of IVIG. ASFN patients in the control arm satisfied the criteria and were offered the IVIG therapy, but they either declined or the insurance did not approve IVIG.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary outcomes were SAS and QASAT-total score. Secondary outcome measures were NTSS-6, PNRS and autonomic testing.

Power analysis

This study was powered to detect a difference on SAS between the treatment groups as a change at the end of treatment from baseline. Since this was a retrospective study, we calculated post-hoc power analysis34. Using the achieved variability (means, standard deviations) and a sample size, the achieved power is 95.2% to detect a difference at a significance level of 0.05. Post-hoc power analyses are limited because they rely on the observed effect size. However, SAS was not used in the previous IVIG trials.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of IVIG and control arms were compared by the 2-sample t-test for continuous variables and by chi-square test for categorical variables. We used two models for assessments of the treatment effects. The first model was a linear mixed-effects model35 adjusted for baseline and with a random intercept. The time, intervention, and time*treatment interactions were considered as fixed effects, and subject was a random effect. Age and gender were covariates. The second model used the difference between post and pre-intervention and then we analyzed the differences by treatment groups using a linear model. This way the baseline (pre-treatment) value was directly adjusted for in the difference from the post-treatment value. Gender and age were covariates. Missing data were ignored.

Results

From a total of 197 patients with ASFN, and had repeated autonomic testing, 109 patients satisfied the inclusion criteria including self-reported and objective inclusion criteria, confirming the presence of ASFN, and were eligible for the IVIG study (Fig. 1). The IVIG was initiated in 43 participants. The dose was 2 g/kg for all patients except of one, in which the dose was capped at 80 kg. Two patients decided to discontinue IVIG due to IVIG-induced headaches after receiving only one dose of IVIG (0.67 gram/kg). These two patients who did not complete IVIG therapy were not included in the final analysis.

A total of 41 patients completed the treatment and were included in the final analysis. The IVIG group was compared to 66 control ASFN patients, who either declined IVIG or IVIG was declined by insurance, and thus were treated with usual medical care, including non-pharmacological (exercise, fluids, salt intake) as well as pharmacological medication (pressors, volume expanders, anti-tachycardic medication) if indicated.

Baseline comparisons

IVIG and control patients were of similar age, gender, body mass index and had similar durations of symptoms (Table 2). The IVIG patients had a higher proportion of abnormal inflammatory/autoimmune markers (p < 0.001). There was no difference in the use of medical therapy between the groups.

Objective measurements

At the baseline (Table 3), there were no significant differences between the groups in autonomic measurements, including the deep breathing test, the Valsalva maneuver and tilt table test results, nor in raw blood pressure, heart rate, or CBFv data. ESC unadjusted for weight was lower in the IVIG arm (p = 0.043); however, there was no difference between groups in ESC adjusted for weight. ESC adjusted for weight is the measure used for QASAT grading29. There was no difference in skin biopsies for both ENFD and SGNFD at both proximal and distal locations.

The QASAT-total score, QASAT-autonomic failure score, cardiovagal, adrenergic, CBFv orthostatic hypotension, orthostatic tachycardia, QASAT-sudomotor, ENFD and SGNFD scores were also similar between the groups.

Treatment effect

The time difference between initial and repeated autonomic testing was 2.36 ± 0.99 years in the IVIG and 2.78 ± 1.37 years in controls (p = 0.126). The average duration of IVIG therapy was 2.25 ± 0.99 years, the median was 1.89 years, the 25th percentile was 1.58 years, and the 75th percentile was 2.25 years.

Adverse events related to IVIG were common and reported by 93% of patients and were mild-to-moderate in most patients (Table 5). The most common side effect was headache affecting 60% of patients.

The total SAS score and all subscores improved in the IVIG arm while worsened in the controls (p1 < 0.001–0.005, p2 < 0.001–0.005, Table 6; Fig. 2A). The SAS score improved by 14.39 ± 31.65% in the IVIG arm while it worsened by 27.27 ± 51.19% in the control arm.

There was no difference in total NTSS-6 scores (p1 = 0.057, p2 = 0.049) (Fig. 2B), but several subscores were significantly reduced (burning frequency (p1 = 0.021, p2 = 0.02), numbness frequency (p1 = 0.005, p2 = 0.005), and numbness intensity (p1 = 0.032, p2 = 0.029) in the IVIG arm. The PNRS score was reduced in the IVIG arm by 27.49 ± 59.93% and worsened by 17.87 ± 109.99% in the controls (p1 = 0.015, p2 = 0.015) (Fig. 2C).

Autonomic testing

There was no difference in raw test data, including orthostatic heart rate, blood pressure and CBFv (Table 6). Heart rate responses to deep breathing improved in the IVIG group (p1 = 0.015, p2 = 0.12). The relative (percent) maximal decline of CBFv was worse in the IVIG group (p1 = 0.045, p2 = 0.051), although the absolute decline did not differ between groups.

ENFD at proximal thigh improved at both groups, with a greater improvement observed in the IVIG group (31.7 ± 112.9% in controls vs. 96.8 ± 186.6% in IVIG) (p1 = 0.035, p2 = 0.038). Skin biopsies for ENFD at the calf, and for SGNFD at both locations showed improvements, but there were no significant differences between both groups.

The QASAT-total score declined by 34.39 ± 21.43% in the IVIG arm and by 2.88 ± 46.92% in the control arm (p1 < 0.001, p2 < 0.001, lower score indicates a better outcome) (Table 6; Fig. 2D). The following QASAT scores also improved in the IVIG arm (Fig. 2E-H): orthostatic CBFv response (p1 = 0.002, p2 = 002), autonomic failure (p1 = 0.035, p2 = 0.032) and ENFD (p1 = 0.017, p2 = 0.018), QASAT Et-CO2 was not different.

Discussion

This retrospective open-label controlled study provides evidence supporting the beneficial effect of long-term high-dose IVIG therapy for ASFN. The linear models showed the benefit of IVIG therapy on subjective (SAS) as well as objective (QASAT) outcome measures. Two PROMS (SAS and PNRS) improved in the IVIG arm. The SAS score improved by 14.39 ± 31.65% in the IVIG arm while it worsened by 27.27 ± 51.19% in the control arm. The total QASAT score improved by 34.39 ± 21.43% in the IVIG arm and by 2.88 ± 46.92% in the control arm. Main QASAT scores (total, cerebral blood flow, autonomic failure, and ENFD) improved in the IVIG arm, while in the control group most of the QASAT scores improved less (total, cerebral blood flow, ENFD) or worsened (autonomic failure) compared to a baseline.

Baseline comparisons showed that PROMs and autonomic impairment were similar between the groups, or borderline worse in the IVIG group. The IVIG group had borderline worse peripheral neurodegeneration as evidenced by abnormalities in skin conductance, and a higher number of abnormal inflammatory markers at baseline compared to the control group. That difference may result from three factors. First, patients with worse symptoms and a higher number of inflammatory markers are more likely to decide on IVIG therapy as they may perceive their condition as more severe. Second, providers are also more likely to recommend IVIG in patients who are worse clinically. Third, objective evidence of neurodegeneration and autoimmunity may affect approval rates by insurance companies.

Importantly, although our study showed an improvement in the IVIG arm after the completion of therapy, patients continued to report autonomic and sensory symptoms albeit of lesser intensity. Objective testing also showed improvement in the IVIG group, but autonomic functions and skin biopsies did not normalize in most patients.

Improvement in QASAT skin biopsy scores following IVIG treatment is a key objective finding in this study. Skin biopsies improved in both arms, but QASAT ENFD scores improved more in the IVIG arm which is consistent with interpretation that IVIG may enhance regeneration of small fibers. Interestingly, IVIG induced greater improvement in ENFD in the proximal thigh compared to the controls (31.7% in controls vs. 96.8% in the IVIG group). This may be related to the closer proximity of proximal fibers to their cell bodies, with improvement in the proximal region preceding the distal regeneration.

The minimally clinically important differences for SAS and QASAT are unknown. The average SAS improvement observed in our study (14%) was lower than the improvement reported in the open-label IVIG study, in which the Compass-31 improvement of 20% was considered clinically meaningful6. Nevertheless, considering the worsening of SAS scores by an average 27% in the control group, the separation between the IVIG and controls in the SAS scores at the end of the trial may be clinically significant; however, this requires further study.

Adverse effects were common, necessitating frequent adjustment of concomitant fluids and premedication. The most common causes were headaches/chemical meningitis which is similar to the previous study14. Nevertheless, only two patients opted to discontinue IVIG therapy.

The intravenous volume expansion may improve the autonomic function14 including POTS36, and therefore may affect the autonomic testing. We eliminated the volume effect of sodium chloride infusions, which have a half-life of 80 min or longer37 by spacing the second autonomic testing several weeks apart from the previous IVIG infusion. The volume expansion does not affect skin biopsies.

Our study differentiates itself from previous research through its distinctive inclusion criteria, the use of specific subjective and objective measures, a high dose of IVIG, and a longer duration of therapy. All of these factors may influence the outcome14,32. While observational studies6–10 were suggestive of positive effect of IVIG, the controlled trials12–14 were negative (Supplementary Table S2). However, two controlled trials focused on painful SFN12,13, a diagnosis explicitly excluded in our study. Another controlled trial assessed POTS14. Although some POTS patients may have abnormal skin biopsy results14, POTS is generally considered a distinct diagnosis compared to condition studies in our trial. The main differences between our trial and available clinical trials – aside from different diagnoses – were the dose of IVIG and treatment duration.

In our subjects, the pattern of autoimmune and inflammatory markers was heterogeneous, various markers elevated. The heterogeneity of autoimmune and inflammatory markers in our study is not surprising, as optimal markers for chronic autoimmunity and/or low-grade inflammation are still lacking38. Even when a specific virus, such as COVID-19, is known to precede the sequelae, the described autoimmune and inflammatory responses remain heterogeneous, with immune features varying among patients39. Thus, ASFN can also be a heterogeneous condition involving multiple immunologic targets and mediators – an aspect of heterogeneity already observed in chronic large fiber autoimmune neuropathies40.

The improvement in the IVIG arm is consistent with immune dysregulation in ASFN. The suspected pathophysiology of ASFN includes neurodegeneration mediated by autoreactive B-cells, molecular mimicry, and/or dysimmunity. IVIG has numerous mechanisms of action and modulates both the innate and the adaptive immune systems41 and may affect each of the above-listed pathophysiological mechanisms. IVIG delivers neutralizing autoantibodies, reduces complement-mediated inflammation, and downregulates cellular autoimmunity and endothelial inflammation, promoting the regeneration of damaged small nerve fibers1,42.

Limitations

This study is limited by its single-center, retrospective, and open-label design. Referral bias may have influenced patient selection, as our institution is a tertiary academic center specializing in autonomic disorders, which may have led to a higher likelihood of more symptomatic or disabled patients being referred.

While the inclusion criteria were the same for both groups, the IVIG group had a higher degree of numbness intensity, though this difference is likely of minimal significance, as there was no corresponding difference in numbness frequency. Furthermore, burning pain—which is often the most disabling symptom in sensory small fiber neuropathy43 —was equally reported in both groups. The total NTSS-6 score, which evaluates overall sensory symptom burden, was also similar between the two groups, indicating comparable levels of sensory impairment.

No significant baseline differences were observed between the IVIG and control groups in demographic variables, symptom duration, medical therapies, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), autonomic symptoms, or autonomic test results (e.g., deep breathing test, Valsalva maneuver, tilt table test). Additionally, there was no difference in epidermal nerve fiber density (ENFD). Although the IVIG group had a slightly lower ESC, this difference is of minor significance, particularly since the ESC adjusted for body weight—used for classification of abnormalities27—was comparable between the two groups.

Another potential source of bias is the higher prevalence of inflammatory markers in the IVIG group. These markers may have influenced both patient agreements to receive IVIG therapy and physicians’ recommendations to initiate treatment. Additionally, the presence of these markers could have affected insurance approval decisions, as insurers typically consider baseline clinical data, including inflammatory markers, when reviewing treatment requests.

The lack of blinding in this study could have influenced the results, as the IVIG group received more fluids, premedication, and nursing support compared to the control group. These additional interventions could have impacted the outcomes, introducing potential bias. Conditioning to IVIG is also a concern10. The autonomic function tests which we used for objective assessment of autonomic functions can be influenced by placebo or nocebo interventions44 therefore inserting evaluations bias. However, the placebo effect cannot affect skin biopsies.

Heterogeneity in inflammatory markers is also a significant limitation of our study. Most of our earlier patients had a history of Lyme disease and many inflammatory markers became available later during the study. Nevertheless, there is still no inflammatory biomarker of chronic Lyme disease45. Due to limited availability, post-treatment laboratory data were not included in our study, which may have constrained our ability to assess treatment-related biological changes. We also did not look at the other antibodies such as Plexin-D17 and several newer recently reported antibodies46 in idiopathic SFN. These antibodies, however, are more likely associated with painful somatic SFN than with ASFN with predominant autonomic features that was studied in our trial. The effect of IVIG on inflammatory markers was not analyzed, because these markers were not available at the end to treatment. No direct evidence of autoimmunity has been found in skin specimens. We used SAS instead of the more commonly used Compass-31 autonomic symptom scale. However, we previously showed that SAS is a reliable alternative to Compass-31 since it captures similar information and both tests are highly correlated22.

The strengths of this study lie in the use of controls with ASFN, large sample size, the study duration, comprehensive objective evaluations, and consecutive enrolment of patients.

Conclusion

This retrospective open-label, controlled study provides evidence supporting the beneficial effects of long-term, high-dose IVIG therapy for ASFN. Placebo-controlled, double-blind studies are needed to validate our findings.
