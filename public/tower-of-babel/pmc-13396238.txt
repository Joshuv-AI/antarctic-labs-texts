Human angiotensin‑converting enzyme 2‑specific benzothiazole-based allosteric inhibitor against pan‑sarbecoviruses
Authors: Li Liu, Jinxin Bai, Runhong Zhou, Yao Ma, Dongyan Zhou, Terrence Tsz-Tai Yuen, Wenwu Liu, Pok Man Lai, Wenjun Yu, Jianxin Duan, Jingjia Ye, Xinhua Liu, Ruomei Gong, Ka-Yi Kwan, Tsz Tung Lee, Feifei Liu, Zhenglong Du, Pui Wang, Mengxiao Luo, Dawei Yang, Kong Hung Sze, Hin Chu, Zhiwei Chen, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging SARS‑CoV‑2 variants and related zoonotic sarbecoviruses rely on ACE2 for cell entry, motivating host‑directed antivirals that block spike-ACE2 interaction. Here, we characterize MB‑32, a benzothiazole small molecule that binds ACE2, selectively disrupts binding of SARS‑CoV‑2 spike receptor‑binding domain to ACE2, and preserves ACE2 enzymatic activity across species. MB‑32 potently inhibits entry of SARS‑CoV‑2 variants, SARS‑CoV‑1 and diverse bat/pangolin sarbecoviruses in ACE2‑expressing cells, while sparing vesicular stomatitis virus and authentic MERS‑CoV, indicating non‑virucidal, ACE2‑focused activity. Biochemical and biophysical analyses, supported by ACE2 mutagenesis, support a model in which MB‑32 engages a non‑catalytic surface pocket on the ACE2 N‑terminal helix to allosterically disrupt spike attachment. Intranasal MB‑32 achieves high airway concentrations, protects male ACE2‑transgenic mice and hamsters from SARS‑CoV‑2 disease, and prevents contact transmission of Omicron‑lineage viruses without detectable cardiovascular toxicity. These findings establish MB‑32 as a host‑targeted ACE2 entry inhibitor and provide a framework for small‑molecule ACE2‑directed antivirals against current and future sarbecovirus spillovers.Subject terms: Antivirals, SARS-CoV-2, Drug development

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) is the causative agent of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), a life-threatening illness1,2. Despite the success of vaccination efforts in reducing global hospitalization and mortality rates, the continuous evolution of SARS-CoV-2 through viral protein mutations allows the virus to evade existing vaccines, neutralizing antibodies, and antiviral treatments, leading to persistent waves of vaccine breakthrough infections, reinfections, rapid drug resistance, and long COVID3,4. By early March 2026, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated 779 million confirmed cases and 7.1 million deaths globally. Critically, SARS-CoV-2’s persistence is exacerbated by the emergence of highly transmissible variants like Omicron sublineages and zoonotic reservoirs in bats and pangolins, highlighting significant therapeutic challenges5–11.

Human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (hACE2) serves as the primary cell surface receptor for sarbecoviruses such as SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-212–19. Targeting this conserved host receptor, rather than mutable viral components, allows ACE2-targeting drug candidates to maintain efficacy against evolving variants and other sarbecoviruses20–22. For instance, soluble human ACE2 and engineered mutants can bind spike proteins of SARS-CoV-1 and animal-derived coronaviruses23–25. Additionally, anti-ACE2 monoclonal antibodies can prevent infection by all hACE2-binding sarbecoviruses tested, unlike monoclonal antibodies targeting the spike protein, which may become ineffective due to viral mutations26. Concerns about potential tissue injury, however, have led to the withdrawal of some ACE2-targeting trials27, as ACE2 plays a crucial role in maintaining organ function and physiological homeostasis28,29. Disruption of ACE2 enzymatic activity can result in adverse effects and diseases30,31. To date, a small molecule drug that specifically targets hACE2 with broad anti-pan-sarbecovirus activity while preserving its biological enzymatic function has yet to be identified.

This study introduces MB-32, a benzothiazole-based small molecule that targets the hACE2 receptor through noncatalytic, allosteric inhibition. MB-32 demonstrates pan-sarbecovirus activity in vitro, effectively neutralizing multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants (Alpha, Beta, Delta, Omicron), SARS-CoV-1, and diverse animal-derived sarbecoviruses. Functional and mutagenesis data indicate that MB-32 engages a putative allosteric pocket within the N-terminal helix of hACE2, near the spike binding interface, thereby blocking viral entry while preserving hACE2’s enzymatic function. In animal models, intranasal MB-32 administration markedly reduced Omicron viral load in the lungs and upper airway and prevented onward transmission following challenge. Together, these findings highlight MB-32 as a first-in-class small-molecule ACE2-targeted entry inhibitor with broad-spectrum activity against sarbecoviruses and support ACE2-directed strategies as a promising avenue for future pan-sarbecovirus countermeasures and therapeutics.

Results

A benzothiazole small molecule MB-32 inhibits pan-sarbecoviruses entry

To identify a broad-spectrum entry inhibitor against sarbecoviruses, we conducted high-throughput screening of a chemical library comprising 20,000 heterocyclic compounds with diverse benzofused scaffolds, including benzothiazoles32. Using a HEK293T-ACE2 cell-based pseudovirus assay, we simultaneously evaluated the library’s antiviral activities against SARS-CoV-1 and wild-type SARS-CoV-2 (SARS-CoV-2-WT), with Vesicular stomatitis Indiana virus (VSV) as a negative control (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 1). This screen identified compound YY-688 as a hit, which exhibited potent antiviral activity against both SARS-CoV-2-WT and SARS-CoV-1 in vitro (Fig. 1b, c). YY-688 showed IC50 values of approximately 450 nM for SARS-CoV-2 and 291 nM for SARS-CoV-1, with a CC50 of around 15 μM and selectivity index (SI) of 34 and 53, respectively (Fig. 1b). The absence of activity against VSV indicated that inhibition is specific to SARS-CoV-1/2 entry. These findings were corroborated using authentic SARS-CoV-2 strains (WT, D614G, Alpha, and Gamma), which showed a mean IC50 in the low‑micromolar range (16.4 µM, range 6.8–26 µM) and SI of 8.4 (range 5–20) (Fig. 1d), establishing YY-688 as the initial hit compound.

We next synthesized and screened 2,000 YY-688 analogs using the same assay, identifying 32 promising candidates (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among these, MB-32 displayed the greatest antiviral potency (IC50 = 105 ± 4 nM) together with reduced cytotoxicity (CC50 = 19.3 ± 2.7 μM) relative to YY-688, while retaining a lack of activity against VSV (Fig. 1e). MB-32 further exhibited broad-spectrum activity against SARS-CoV-2 VOCs, with a mean IC50 of 196 nM (range 13.4–532 nM) and a mean SI of 258 (range 36–1484) (Fig. 1f). In addition, MB-32 effectively inhibited entry of SARS-CoV-1 and related sarbecoviruses, including the bat strains Rs7327 and Rs423115, and the pangolins strain PCoV-GX33, with IC50 values ranging from 161 to 320 nM (Fig. 1g). When tested against authentic SARS-CoV-2 VOCs (BA.2, BA.5, XBB.1, and JN.1) and SARS-CoV-1 in multi-cycle infection assays, MB-32 showed a mean IC50 of 9.2 μM (range 5.7–13 μM), without detectable cytotoxicity at the highest tested concentration of 129 μM (Fig. 1h). MB‑32 therefore maintained consistent low‑micromolar activity against all authentic SARS‑CoV‑2 strains tested.

To directly compare the inhibitory activity of MB‑32 against ACE2‑using and non‑ACE2‑using coronaviruses, we tested SARS‑CoV‑2 and MERS‑CoV side‑by‑side in Calu‑3 cells using an identical pre‑incubation and washout protocol. Virus stocks were pre‑mixed with MB‑32 (0–8 µM) before infection, added to cells at an MOI of 0.1, and removed after 2 h, followed by three PBS washes and culture in fresh medium containing MB‑32 at the corresponding concentrations. Viral RNA levels were quantified at 24 hpi by RT‑qPCR for the N gene, normalized to GAPDH. Under these conditions, MB‑32 inhibited SARS‑CoV‑2 replication with an IC₅₀ of 1.615 µM, whereas no measurable inhibition of MERS‑CoV was observed across the same concentration range, indicating selective activity against ACE2‑using sarbecoviruses (Fig. 1i). Together with its lack of effect on VSV pseudoviruses (Fig. 1e), this selectivity indicates that MB-32 is not broadly virucidal or generally disruptive to enveloped viruses, but instead targets a feature specific to ACE2-using sarbecoviruses. Collectively, these data demonstrate that MB-32 is a potent and specific broad-spectrum entry inhibitor of pan-sarbecoviruses.

MB-32 blocks the interaction between hACE2 and SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain (RBD)

To elucidate the mechanism of action of MB-32, we examined whether it targets the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein or host cell components. SARS-CoV-2-WT pseudoviruses and HEK293T-ACE2 cells were separately pre-treated with serial dilutions of MB-32 for 2 h and 30 min, respectively, at 37 °C. The pre-treated viruses were then recovered and used to infect naïve HEK293T-ACE2 cells, whereas pre-treated HEK293T-ACE2 cells were exposed to virus either immediately or after a 2-h interval in the absence of MB-32. As controls, HEK293T-ACE2 cells were infected in the continuous presence of serially diluted MB-32. Antiviral activity was observed exclusively in HEK293T-ACE2 cells that had been pre-treated with MB-32 (Fig. 2a), indicating that MB-32 acts on cellular targets rather than directly on the viral spike protein. Moreover, upon removal of MB-32 and subsequent infection either immediately or after 2 h, the IC50 increased by approximately fourfold (Fig. 2a), suggesting that the inhibitory effect on cellular targets is partially reversible and that dissociation of MB-32 permits partial recovery of receptor availability for viral entry.

SARS-CoV-2 infection of HEK293T-ACE2 cells is mediated primarily by ACE2, which facilitates viral attachment and entry. Host proteases, including TMPRSS234 and Cathepsin L35, are essential for proteolytic activation of the spike protein to drive membrane fusion and productive infection. TMPRSS2 cleaves the spike protein at the S2’ site, enabling direct fusion of viral and cellular membranes at the cell surface36. When TMPRSS2 is absent or insufficient, the virus-ACE2 complex is internalized, and Cathepsin L processes spike within endosomes to promote fusion and release of viral RNA37.

To assess the specificity of MB-32, we performed ELISA-based binding assays showing that MB-32 inhibited binding of RBD and full-length spike to hACE2, with inhibition comparable to an RBD-specific neutralizing antibody (Fig. 2b). Non-neutralizing antibodies and diluent controls showed no effect. Consistently, MB-32 reduced binding of Alexa-647-labelled spike to HEK293T cells transiently expressing hACE2-GFP without observable alteration of ACE2 cell-surface expression (Fig. 2c). In a separate experiment using HEK293T-ACE2 cells co-cultured with Alexa 488-labelled spike and Alexa 594-labelled transferrin as endocytosis monitor, robust binding of spike and extensive co-localization of spike and transferrin were observed in nearly all cells, consistent with constitutive hACE2 expression and efficient endocytosis of both ligands. MB-32 diminished binding and internalization of spike into HEK293T-ACE2 cells but did not affect transferrin receptor-mediated endocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 2a), demonstrating that MB-32 specifically disrupts the ACE2-spike interaction rather than global endocytosis or membrane integrity. Enzymatic assays further revealed that MB-32 does not inhibit TMPRSS2 or Cathepsin L at concentrations that effectively block SARS-CoV-2 entry (17.5 μM) (Fig. 2d). Together with the lack of activity against MERS-CoV, these findings indicate that MB-32 inhibits SARS-CoV-2 infection primarily by blocking RBD engagement of ACE2, rather than by virucidal effects on the virion or by interfering with global endocytosis or TMPRSS2 and cathepsin L activity.

MB-32 directly binds ACE2

To investigate the direct binding interaction between MB-32 and hACE2, we employed complementary biophysical techniques including WaterLOGSY and saturation transfer difference nuclear magnetic resonance (STD-NMR) experiments38,39. The WaterLOGSY spectrum of MB-32 alone at 250 µM exhibited negative signals for its aromatic protons (Fig. 2e, green trace), indicative of a freely tumbling small molecule without self-aggregation under these experimental conditions. Upon addition of ACE2 to MB-32 at a molar ratio of 1:1000 (ACE2:MB-32), the WaterLOGSY signals reverted to positive (Fig. 2e, blue trace), consistent with formation of a ligand-protein complex. Corroborating these findings, STD-NMR revealed clear saturation transfer to MB-32 exclusively in the presence of ACE2 (1:1000 ratio), characterized by positive signals in the aromatic region (Fig. 2f, blue trace), whereas no STD signals were observed for MB-32 alone (Fig. 2f, green trace).

Further quantification by Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and biolayer interferometry (BLI) indicated dissociation constant (KD) of 7.078 and 3.997 µM, respectively, for the MB-32-hACE2 interaction (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2b). In line with its antagonistic effect, addition of MB-32 increased the apparent dissociation constant of the spike-hACE2 interaction from 3.66 to 212 nM (Fig. 2h, i). Cross-species SPR analyses showed MB-32 binding to ACE2 orthologs from nonhuman primates, golden hamster, mouse, and rat, with KD values ranging from 5 to 31.28 µM (Supplementary Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 2). Collectively, these orthogonal assays demonstrate that MB-32 directly engages ACE2 with micromolar affinity and modulates spike-ACE2 binding, consistent with interaction at a conserved binding pocket shared among mammalian ACE2 proteins.

Given that polymorphisms in the ACE2 coding sequence can modulate spike protein binding40,41, we further assessed whether single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) near the ACE2-spike interface40,42–45 impact MB-32-mediated entry inhibition (Supplementary Table 3). Testing 11 distinct hACE2 SNPs revealed that MB-32 consistently maintained inhibitory activity against pseudotyped SARS-CoV-2-WT as well as the Omicron variant BQ.1.1 (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results suggest that naturally occurring genetic variants in ACE2 at the spike-binding interface are unlikely to compromise the antiviral efficacy of MB-32.

MB-32 does not inhibit ACE2 enzymatic activity

ACE2 is a type I integral membrane glycoprotein featuring a single extracellular catalytic domain responsible for degrading angiotensin II into the heptapeptide angiotensin-(1-7), thus negatively regulating the renin-angiotensin system (RAS)28. Having confirmed the binding of MB-32 to ACE2, we assessed whether MB-32 affects ACE2’s enzymatic function using a fluorogenic substrate assay with the established ACE2 enzymatic inhibitor MLN-4760 as a positive control28. Across four concentrations of MB-32 (140 nM, 700 nM, 3.5 µM, and 17.5 µM), no inhibition of substrate turnover was observed, whereas MLN-4760 completely abolished hACE2 activity as expected (Fig. 2j)46. Consistently, MB-32 failed to inhibit enzymatic activity of ACE2 from Macaca fascicularis, mouse, or rat (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Furthermore, co-treatment with MB-32 and MLN-4760 in both enzyme assays and SARS-CoV-2 infection studies showed neither synergistic nor antagonistic effects (Fig. 2k, l), indicating that MB-32 engages a noncatalytic binding site distinct from the ACE2 active site. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MB-32 preserves the physiological enzymatic function of ACE2 while blocking viral entry, providing safety and mechanistic advantage for ACE2-targeted antiviral therapeutics.

Structural insight into MB-32 binding on hACE2

To gain mechanistic insight into MB-32 binding, we initially attempted co-crystallization of the ACE2-MB-32 complex but were unable to resolve a ligand-bound structure. We therefore employed formaldehyde (FA) crosslinking coupled with mass spectrometry to probe the MB-32 binding environment on hACE247. For FA crosslinking analysis, masses of 546.22 Da or 558.22 Da, corresponding to the monoisotopic mass of MB-32 (534.22 Da) plus a single-carbon (12 Da) or di-carbon (24 Da) bridge, were analyzed using PEAKS Studio 11 (Supplementary Table 4). Focusing on aspartic acid, tyrosine, arginine, and lysine, we detected a 558.22 Da adduct in peptides containing Y83 and K94 with high confidence scores (1000; Supplementary Table 4), indicating modifications of Y83 and/or K94 by MB-32. Given that crosslinking constraints require distances below 12 Å in accepted models, these findings suggest that MB-32 resides within 12 Å of these residues47.

Guided by these constraints, induced-fit docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggest that MB-32 can be accommodated within a pocket adjacent to Y83 and K94, distinct from the hACE2 catalytic site (Fig. 3a). This region has been previously proposed as an allosteric site on hACE2 and a potential target for COVID-19 therapeutics19. Within this modeled pocket, carbons on the MB-32 imidazole ring (red, green) are positioned 4–10 and 3–10 Å, respectively, from the Y83 hydroxyl oxygen of hACE2 (Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Movie 1), while the meta-position carbon (blue) on the MB-32 phenyl ring is 4–12 Å from the K94 amino group side chain (Supplementary Fig. 4a and Supplementary Movie 1). These spatial proximities are compatible with two potential FA-based crosslink events: one between the imidazole carbon and the Y83 phenol group, and one between the aromatic carbon and K94 ω-amino group, consistent with the observed 24 Da adduct (Supplementary Table 4).

In this model, MB-32 engages hACE2 through multiple interactions: the urea moiety forms hydrogen bonds with Q102 and Q98 (Fig. 3a), the nitro group on the nitroimidazole ring forms a hydrogen bond with Q86, and the imidazole nitrogen interacts with Y83. The latter interaction is predicted to promote an allosteric conformational change in Y83 from a “down” to an “up” conformation (Fig. 3a, b). Additionally, MB-32 methylbenzyl group forms hydrophobic contact with P565 (Fig. 3a). To functionally test the importance of these residues, we expressed mutant hACE2 (Gln18-Ser740) in HEK293T cells, generating Y83A, Q86A, Q102A, Q98A, and P565A variants (Supplementary Fig. 4b). To control for differences in protein source and potential post-translational modifications, which can impact binding affinity, we produced both wild-type and mutant ACE2 proteins and performed SPR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The home-made wild-type hACE2 displayed a binding affinity of 1.75 μM, whereas the P565A mutation caused more than a 10-fold reduction in MB-32 binding affinity (17.66 μM). In contrast, the Y83A, Q86A, Q98A, and Q102A mutations led to more moderate decrease in affinity (1.8- to 4.2-fold) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 4c). These results support a model in which MB-32 engages a noncatalytic pocket near the N-terminal helix of hACE2 that is stabilized by P565 and surrounding residues.

Among interacting residues, Y83 is located in a hydrophobic pocket critical for N-terminal RBD binding; it forms hydrogen bonds with the conserved RBD residues N487 and Y489, mediating sarbecoviruses spike engagement33,43–45,48–53 (Supplementary Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 4e). The Y83F mutation disrupts these hydrogen bonds, impeding spike binding and preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection54,55. Thus, MB-32 is predicted to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 binding to hACE2 by allosterically perturbing Y83 and its associated hydrogen-bond network. Consistent with this, transient expression of hACE2-Y83F or hACE2-Y83A in HEK293T cells reduced MB-32 antiviral efficacy against SARS-CoV-2-WT and the Omicron VOCs compared with wild-type hACE2 (Fig. 3d–g). Additional mutants at Q86A, Q98A, Q102A, and P565A also diminished MB-32-mediated inhibition, with an increase in IC50 values correlating with weakened binding affinity (Supplementary Fig. 4f–h). While a ligand-bound ACE2 structure will be required to define the binding pocket at atomic resolution, these combined biochemical, crosslinking, modeling, and mutagenesis data converge on an allosteric N-terminal helix pocket as the most likely site of MB-32 engagement, through which it alters the spike-binding surface and prevents viral entry into host cells.

MB-32 demonstrates potent antiviral efficacy in vivo and prevents natural transmission against SARS-CoV-2 VOCs

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of MB-32, we conducted a pharmacokinetic study in mice (6–8 weeks old male, n = 3 per group) following intranasal (i.n.) administration at 2.33 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 5a), the highest feasible dose for experimental animals. Quantitative tissue pharmacokinetics revealed high and sustained MB‑32 exposure in nasal turbinates (AUClast = 466,726 ng·h/g) and lungs (AUClast = 13,346 ng·h/g) (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). Adjusted for tissue water content (0.8 mL/g), MB‑32 concentrations in nasal turbinates and lungs exceeded the authentic‑virus IC50 (5–13 μM, ~2.6–6.9 μg/mL) by several fold (AUClast/IC50 ratios of 84–218-fold in turbinates and 2.4–6.3 fold in lungs), providing sustained local exposure well above the levels required for inhibition in vitro. Importantly, a single dose of 2.33 mg/kg did not result in significant body weight loss over 7 days compared to controls (n = 6/group) (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e).

We next assessed the protective efficacy of MB-32 against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron infections in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice and golden hamsters. In the intranasal inoculation model (Fig. 4a), groups of K18 mice were challenged with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.2, the predominant epidemic strain at that time in Hong Kong56. One group (n = 4) received intranasal MB-32 (60 µg/mouse, 2.33 mg/kg body weight), while the other group (n = 4) received vehicle as placebo control 30 min before virus exposure. All mice were then challenged intranasally with 2.5 × 104 plaque-forming units (PFU) of the Omicron BA.2. Pre-exposure administration of MB-32 provided significant protection, as shown by preserved body weight and a >2-log reduction in infectious virus load in lungs, as determined by quantitative RT-qPCR (P = 0.0005 for RdRp and P < 0.0001 for NP) (Fig. 4b, c). Consistently, N protein staining revealed abundant viral N protein in the lungs of vehicle-treated mice but none in MB-32-treated mice (Fig. 4d), indicating strong suppression of viral replication. H&E staining revealed no significant infection-related histopathological lesions in the lungs of either group (Fig. 4d). Further studies showed that MB-32 administered 30 min before virus exposure also provided robust protection against Omicron BA.5 and JN.1 in K18-hACE2 mice and golden hamsters, respectively (Fig. 4e–g and Supplementary Fig. 5f–i), consistent with the observed pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationship (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c).

Next, we evaluated the impact of MB-32 treatment on SARS-CoV-2 transmission using the golden Syrian hamster transmission model established by our group and others3. Briefly, index hamsters were intranasally inoculated with 5.6 × 104 PFU SARS-CoV-2 XBB.1.5 and rested for 2 days. On the third day, infected index hamsters were co-housed with naïve hamsters that had received either MB-32 or vehicle intranasally for 4 h to allow virus transmission. Contacts were then separated and individually housed for 2 additional days before tissue collection at 2 dpi (Fig. 4h). Nasal turbinates from index animals immediately after cohousing showed comparable viral titers (Fig. 4i). Compared with the vehicle-treated control, MB-32-treated contacts exhibited >2-log (p < 0.0001) reduction in infectious viral titres and viral gene copies in the lungs (Fig. 4j, k). Consistent with these viral-load measurements, viral N protein expression was undetectable by IHC in the lungs of the MB-32-treated hamsters, in contrast to the abundant viral N protein detected in vehicle controls (Fig. 4k). H&E staining revealed no severe infection-related histopathological lesions in the lungs in either vehicle- or MB-32-treated hamsters (Fig. 4k). Thus, the high local drug levels achieved in the upper and lower respiratory tract relative to the authentic-virus IC50 are sufficient to block early rounds of infection and limit onward spread, even though MB-32 displays only low-micromolar potency in multi-cycle cell culture assays. Together, these results demonstrate that MB-32 is effective in preventing SARS-CoV-2 transmission in vivo.

MB-32 rescues hACE2 transgenic mice from lethal SARS-CoV-2 infection

We next investigated whether MB-32 could protect animals from lethal SARS-CoV-2 challenge. hACE2 transgenic mice were infected with a lethal dose (4 × 103 PFU per mouse) of SARS-CoV-2 (HKU-001a) mixed with either MB-32 (60 µg) or vehicle, and survival was monitored for 14 days (Fig. 5a). Vehicle-treated mice experienced continuous body weight loss and began to succumb to lethal virus challenge as early as 7 dpi, resulting in 0% survival by 14 dpi. (Fig. 5b, c). In contrast, MB-32-treated mice maintained stable body weights throughout the study period, and survival was significantly improved to 100% (P = 0.0013) (Fig. 5b, c).

Quantification of viral genomes at 4 dpi demonstrated that MB-32 significantly suppressed SARS-CoV-2 infection in nasal turbinates and lung tissues by approximately 4.4 × 105-fold (P = 0.0051) and 8.6 × 104-fold (P = 0.0154), respectively (Fig. 5d–f). Plaque assay assessing infectious viral progeny titers revealed robust viral replication in nasal turbinates (2 × 103 PFU/mL) and lungs (1.83 × 106 PFU/mL) in vehicle-treated mice. In contrast, no plaques were detected in samples from MB-32-treated mice, indicating complete suppression of infectious virus replication below the limit of detection (Fig. 5f). Consistent with these results, N protein staining revealed abundant viral N protein in lung tissues of all vehicle-treated mice, whereas N protein was undetectable in any MB-32-treated mice (Fig. 5g), confirming significant suppression of viral replication by MB-32. Additionally, H&E staining demonstrated severe virus-induced pathologies in vehicle-treated mice, including bronchiolitis, vasculitis, and alveolitis with inflammatory infiltrates and hemorrhage, whereas these histopathological changes were largely alleviated or absent in MB-32-treated mice (Fig. 5h). Together, our in vivo data indicate that MB-32 robustly reduces viral burdens and infection-induced tissue damage in the respiratory tract, resulting in full survival of infected animals.

MB-32 shows no observable adverse effects in the cardiovascular system of experimental rats

MB-32 binds to ACE2 protein of rat with comparable affinity to human ACE2 (KD = 31.28 µM) (Supplementary Fig. 2c). To investigate the impact of MB-32 on the cardiovascular system, we monitored heart rate, blood pressure (mean arterial pressure [MBP], systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP]), and electrocardiogram (ECG) changes in two groups of rats (n = 6) that received a single intranasal dose of MB-32 (350 nmol/rat, a maximum dose that can be administered with the current formulation, equivalent to 2.853 mg/kg body weight and 1.2 times of effective dose in mice) or a vehicle control. Assessments were conducted before administering MB-32 and at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h post-inoculation. We did not observe any apparent abnormal changes in electrocardiographic indices such as the RR interval, heart rate, PR interval, P-wave duration, QRS duration, QT interval, and QTcF in animals receiving MB-32 compared to pre-drug administration baselines and those receiving the control vehicle (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). The blood pressure of the rats exhibited a slight decrease at each time point following saline or MB-32 administration, but remained relatively stable over time. No distinct abnormal changes in SBP, MBP, or DBP attributable to MB-32 were observed at any time points (Supplementary Fig. 6c). These results demonstrate that MB-32 is safe with no observed adverse effects on the cardiovascular system of experimental animals.

Discussion

In this study, we identify MB-32, a benzothiazole small molecule that targets hACE2 and exhibits broad antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 VOCs, SARS-CoV-1, and diverse bat- and pangolin-derived sarbecoviruses. MB-32 also prevents lung infections and natural transmission of SARS-CoV-2 VOCs in ACE2 transgenic mice and hamsters. Functional and biophysical assays show that MB-32 binds ACE2 and inhibits spike/RBD engagement without measurably impairing ACE2 enzymatic activity, consistent with a non-active-site, allosteric mechanism. This receptor-targeted strategy may reduce on-target toxicity associated with catalytic inhibition and offers a complementary approach to virus-directed antivirals against current and emerging SARS-CoV-2 VOCs and future pandemic threats linked to hACE2-dependent sarbecoviruses. Although our data strongly support ACE2 as the primary target, we acknowledge that, in the absence of a resolved MB-32/ACE2 co-structure, the exact location and residue-level composition of the binding pocket remain to be fully defined.

Most approved or late-stage small-molecule COVID-19 therapeutics act downstream of entry by inhibiting viral replication, such as main protease (Mpro/3CLpro) inhibitors (e.g., Nirmatrelvir) and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) inhibitors (e.g., Remdesivir and Molnupiravir). These agents are recommended for treating mild to moderate disease to reduce progression to severe COVID-19, but the rapid emergence of resistance to Nirmatrelvir57,58 and Molnupiravir59–61, as well as other 3CLpro inhibitors62,63, raises concerns about long-term efficacy and onward transmission of resistant virus. Current entry-directed strategies predominantly focus on the spike glycoprotein, including monoclonal neutralizing antibodies (NAbs)64 and peptide fusion inhibitors65,66. However, many VOCs have evolved resistance to clinically deployed antibodies, and peptide-based approaches, while attractive for topical delivery, are susceptible to degradation and often exhibit limited systemic exposure. Several small-molecule and peptide inhibitors that bind spike or the RBD have been reported67–74, but none has yet advanced to clinical use. In contrast, MB-32 targets the host entry receptor and blocks pan-sarbecoviruses entry, showing nanomolar potency against pseudoviruses in vitro and robust protection against SARS-CoV-2 VOC lung infection in vivo. Targeting the ACE2 ectodomain offers a complementary strategy with potential robustness to viral escape, as mutations that abrogate ACE2 binding typically compromise viral fitness. MB-32 exemplifies a benzothiazole small molecule that binds ACE2 and prevents pan-sarbecovirus entry both in vitro and in vivo while preserving ACE2 enzymic function, defining a tractable chemical starting point for ACE2-based entry inhibitors and a potential resistance-resilient component of combination regimens.

ACE2 is a zinc metalloprotease expressed in multiple organs, where it regulates angiotensin processing and confers cardiopulmonary protection28. A key requirement for ACE2-directed antivirals is that they do not disrupt this homeostatic function. The ACE2 ectodomain comprises an N-terminal metallopeptidase domain (residues 19–611) and a C-terminal collectrin-like domain (residues 612–740)28,75. The metallopeptidase domain is further subdivided into two subdomains (I and II), forming a claw-like structure connected by an α-helix (helix 17, residues 511–531). The catalytic zinc-binding site, coordinated by His374, His378, and Glu402, resides within subdomain I75. Known hACE2 enzymatic inhibitors, such as DX600 and MLN-4760, bind this active-site cleft and directly block catalytic activity46. In contrast, MB-32 did not inhibit ACE2 catalytic activity in human or animal ACE2 preparations even at high concentrations, whereas MLN-4760 fully suppressed substrate turnover. Consistent with preserved ACE2 function, high doses of MB-32 did not produce detectable cardiovascular toxicity in rats. These observations support the concept that MB-32 engages a noncatalytic site on ACE2, enabling antiviral activity while maintaining the receptor’s physiological role.

Structural analysis of the ACE2-RBD interface has identified at least 17 ACE2 residues critical for SARS-CoV-2 binding42,45,51,54,76,77, all located within the N-terminal region of subdomain I (residues 19-357) and forming two major interaction pockets. Seven RBD residues that contact ACE2 are conserved across SARS-CoV-2 VOCs and related sarbecoviruses, underscoring strong functional constraints and highlighting this interface as an attractive target for broad-spectrum entry inhibition42,45,51,54,76,77. Our mutagenesis and functional data implicate Y83 and P565 within ACE2 as contributors to MB-32 binding and antiviral activity and are consistent with MB-32 engaging a pocket that allosterically perturbs the ACE2-RBD interface. However, MB-32 retains partial activity when Y83 is mutated, and we have not yet mapped all residues that constitute the binding pocket. We therefore refer to a “putative allosteric site” and explicitly acknowledge that definitive identification of the MB-32-ACE2 interface will require higher-resolution structural studies of the complex.

We observed that MB-32 was approximately 77-fold more potent in the SARS-CoV-2 (WT) pseudovirus entry assay (IC50 ≈ 0.1 µM) than in the multi-cycle authentic virus assay in HEK293T-ACE2 cells (IC50 ≈ 7.7 µM). Such assay‑dependent shifts are not unexpected and have been reported by others. For example, the clinical antibody COV2‑2130 shows an ∼67‑fold higher IC₅₀ in a live‑virus neutralization assay (0.107 µg/mL) than in an HIV‑based pseudovirus assay (0.0016 µg/mL), and antibody 5F8 displays 557-fold weaker neutralization of authentic virus than of pseudovirus78,79. These differences likely reflect the inherent distinction between single‑cycle entry and multi‑cycle replication assays: The pseudovirus system measures a single round of entry using a highly sensitive luciferase readout with synchronized infection and constant compound exposure, whereas authentic virus assay integrates multiple rounds of replication and spread over 48 h, during which compound is subject to dilution, protein binding and possible metabolism, and only partial ACE2 occupancy is required for progressive infection. Consistent with this view, MB-32 displayed similar low-micromolar IC50 values against multiple authentic SARS-CoV-2 strains and VOCs in HEK293T-ACE2 and Vero E6 cells, yet these concentrations were sufficient to prevent lung infection and transmission in vivo. Moreover, when tested in lung-derived Calu-3 epithelial model using a 2h virus-compound pre-incubation followed by washout, MB-32 inhibited SARS-CoV-2 replication with an IC50 of ~1.6 µM, indicating that the compound retains low-micromolar potency under more physiologic, limited-exposure conditions in a human airway cell line. We therefore use the pseudovirus IC50 values as a sensitive measure of entry blockade and structure–activity relationships, and the authentic virus (including Calu-3) and animal data as a more conservative representation of MB-32’s translational antiviral potency.

Despite exhibiting a comparatively high in vitro IC₅₀ value against live virus in HEK293T‑ACE2 cells, intranasal administration of MB‑32 markedly reduces viral RNA and infectious titres in both upper and lower airways, preserves lung architecture, and prevents death in lethal challenge settings. We believe this apparent in vitro–in vivo disconnect is explained by two complementary factors. First, favorable respiratory‑tract biodistribution: intranasal delivery of MB-32 yielded AUClast/authentic‑virus IC50 ratios of 84–218‑fold in the nasal turbinate and 2.4–6.3‑fold in the lungs, above the AUC/IC50 ratio of 3–5 that is generally considered sufficient for effective viral inhibition in target tissues. Under these conditions, near‑saturating ACE2 occupancy at the mucosal surface can drive large reductions in viral replication even when the cell‑based IC₅₀ is in the low‑micromolar range. Second, differences in ACE2 expression between in vitro and in vivo target cells: HEK293T‑ACE2 cells overexpress ACE2, whereas epithelial cells in the turbinate and lung express more physiological levels, so the same free MB‑32 concentration is expected to yield higher fractional receptor occupancy and greater functional inhibition in vivo than in the overexpression system. Consistent with this notion, recent organoid‑based neutralization studies showed that multiple SARS‑CoV‑2 neutralizing antibodies display markedly greater potency in human nasal and lung epithelial organoids than in HEK293T‑ACE2 or other ACE2‑overexpressing cell lines, and that organoid assays better recapitulate in vivo and clinical protection80. These data support the concept that high‑ACE2 cell lines systematically underestimate the antiviral efficacy of receptor‑targeted biologics, in line with the in vitro–in vivo disconnect observed for MB‑32. Taken together, these considerations suggest that MB‑32’s apparent discrepancy between cell‑culture IC₅₀ and in vivo efficacy primarily reflects the experimental context rather than an intrinsically unusual mechanism of action.

Overall, our work establishes ACE2 as a viable host target for small-molecule entry inhibitors and positions MB-32 as a first-in-class chemical probe of ACE2-mediated sarbecovirus entry, while transparently recognizing the current limits of structural resolution for its allosteric binding site. We highlight the remaining uncertainties in binding kinetics, receptor modulation, and virion-receptor engagement as priorities for future mechanistic and PK-PD studies.

Our study has several limitations. First, although MB‑32 shows robust in vivo efficacy despite only low‑micromolar IC₅₀ values in HEK293T‑ACE2 cells against authentic virus, the quantitative PK-PD relationship, including ED₅₀, duration of protection, and therapeutic index, remains to be fully defined. Second, our mechanistic interpretation that high local drug levels and more physiological ACE2 expression in the respiratory tract explain the apparent in vitro–in vivo disconnect is supported by organoid data and PK measurements, but has not yet been validated directly using organoid models or conditional ACE2‑expression systems for MB‑32 itself. Third, while our pre‑incubation experiments argue against a strongly virucidal mechanism, we have not directly visualized potential structural effects of MB‑32 on virions.

MB-32 exhibits a KD of approximately 7 µM, which has implications for potency, therapeutic window, and duration of action. Our intranasal PK data indicates that effective local concentrations are readily achieved in the upper and lower respiratory tract while systemic levels remain low. This limited systemic exposure may be advantageous, as it reduces the likelihood of MB-32 being sequestered by ACE2 in the vasculature and may minimize potential off-target effects in other organs. At the same time, the relatively finite exposure suggests that longer-lasting protection, or alternative routes of administration, will likely require further optimization of MB-32 affinity and pharmacokinetic properties.

Rea‑Moreno et al. recently reported that extracellular vesicles (EVs) released from ACE2/TMPRSS2‑expressing airway epithelial cells can transfer these entry factors to otherwise resistant lung cells, unveiling an alternate pathway for SARS‑CoV‑2 infection that broadens the spectrum of ACE2‑positive targets in the respiratory tract81. This EV‑mediated redistribution of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 reinforces the rationale for host-directed ACE2 inhibitors and underscores the need to optimize benzothiazole-based compounds for potency at physiological ACE2 densities in complex multicellular environments. Future benzothiazole design and profiling should therefore incorporate EV‑transfer models similar to those used by Rea‑Moreno et al., to establish whether allosteric, non‑catalytic ACE2 binders such as MB‑32 can robustly suppress these alternate infection routes and approach true pan‑sarbecovirus coverage in vivo.

Future work incorporating airway organoid neutralization assays, detailed PK-PD modeling, and structural analyses (for example, TEM or cryo‑EM of ACE2-MB-32-spike complexes and virions incubated with MB-32, as well as covalent or photo‑affinity MB‑32 analogs for irreversible ACE2 labelling) will be important to refine MB‑32’s mechanism of action, exclude irreversible virions damage, and more precisely define the therapeutic index and binding site for MB-32 and related ACE2‑targeted biologics.

Methods

Chemical synthesis of MB-32 and its analogs

MB-32 and its derivatives were prepared from 1,5-difluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (DFDNB) according to previously reported procedures32. To improve the aqueous solubility of MB-32, the final compound was converted to its hydrochloride salt by treatment with 4.0 M HCl in dioxane. Detailed synthetic procedures and structural characterization data for MB-32, including NMR spectra and High-resolution mass spectra, are provided in Supplementary Figs. 7–10.

Cells and virus

HEK293T (ATCC CRL-3216), African green monkey kidney cells (Vero E6; ATCC CRL-1586™) were maintained in D10 (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium) containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% antibiotic/antimycotic, and 1% L-Glutamine. HEK293T-ACE2 cells (stable ACE2-expressing derivative of HEK293T) are maintained in D10 containing 250 μg/mL Hygromycin. Calu-3 cells (ATCC HTB-55) were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM; ATCC-formulated) supplemented with 10% FBS (and 1% penicillin–streptomycin). HEK293T and Calu‑3 cells (ATCC) were authenticated by STR profiling at the institutional core facility, whereas HEK293T‑ACE2 and Vero E6 cells were verified by documented origin, morphology, and virus‑susceptibility profiles; all cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed mycoplasma‑negative by PCR. The pseudotype virus were generated through cotransfection of HEK293T cells with 2 plasmids, pNL4-3Luc_Env_Vpr and pVax-1-Spike of SARS-CoV-2 (QHR63250), SARS-CoV-2 VOCs3,82,83, SARS-CoV-184, PCoV-GX spike (GenBank: QIA48614.1)33, Rs7327 (GenBank: KY417151), Rs4231 (GenBank: KY417146) or envelop of VSV15, as we previously described85. Authentic SARS-CoV-2 variants stocks were isolated from patients with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 in Hong Kong and prepared in Vero E6 or Vero-E6-TMPRSS2 cells3,86,87. Viral stocks were titrated by tissue culture infectious dose 50% (TCID50). WT SARS-CoV-2 HKU-001a (GenBank: MT230904), SARS-CoV-2 WT D614G (GISAID: EPI_ISL_497840), B.1.1.7/Alpha (GenBank: OM212469), B.1.351/Beta (GenBank: OM212470), B.1.617.2/Delta (GenBank: OM212471), Omicron BA.2 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_9845731), Omicron BA.5.2 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_13777658), Omicron XBB.1.5 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_17205250) and Omicron JN.1 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_18841631)3. Pre-treated viruses were recovered by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 2 h.

Animals

C57BL/6 mice were procured from Jihui Experimental Animal Co., Ltd, or Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME). Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were procured from Charles River Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. Heterogeneous K18-hACE2 C57BL/6J mice [2B6.Cg-Tg(K18-ACE2)2Prlmn/J] were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Hamsters were obtained from the Chinese University of Hong Kong Laboratory Animal Service Centre via the HKU Laboratory Animal Unit (LAU). The animals were housed in individually ventilated cages under 65% relative humidity, and ambient temperature of 21–23 °C, and a 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle. Group sizes were determined based on our prior experience examining viral replications in SARS-CoV-2-infected K18-hACE2 transgenic mice. Only male mice and hamsters were used in this study; sex was therefore not included as an experimental variable in the design or statistical analyses.

High-throughput screening of chemical compound library

A 20000 individual compounds of benzofused scaffolds library88 were dissolved in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to a stock concentration of 10 mM and stored at −20 °C. A primary screen to identify compounds with potential antiviral entry effects against pan-sarbecoviruses were performed by treating SARS-CoV-1, SARS-CoV-2, and VSV pseudotype virus with 10 µM of compound from the library at 37 °C for 1 h and followed by a luciferase-based assay as previously described89,90. Briefly, treated and untreated pseudovirus were added into pre-seeded HEK293T-ACE2 cells. After 48–72 h, infected cells were lysed to measure luciferase activity using a commercial kit (E1500, Promega, Madison, WI). Luciferase activity was measured using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed with Skanlt RE software 7.1. Compounds with potential anti-sarbecovirus entry activity were identified based on >50% reduction in entry efficiency against both SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 but not VSV, and were validated using dose-dependent inhibition and cell cytotoxicity assays. The IC50 is defined as the compound dilution that results in 50% inhibitory concentrations as determined by log (inhibitor) vs. normalized response–variable slope model.

Cytotoxicity assay in vitro

The cytotoxicity of the compounds is evaluated by microtiter assays with HEK293T-ACE2 and Vero-E6. Serial diluted compounds were tested in cells using a viability assay, which is commercially available (G7570, Promega, Madison, WI). The cytotoxicity data are reported as the quantity of drug required to reduce cell viability by 50% (cytotoxic concentration for 50%; CC50).

Antiviral assay using authentic SARS-CoV-2

Antiviral activity against authentic SARS-CoV-2 VOCs was performed according to previous studies91,92. In brief, Vero E6 cells were seeded in a 24-well culture plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, authentic SARS-CoV-2 VOCs (WT, D614G, Alpha and Gamma variants) (MOI = 0.005) and 3-fold serially diluted compound (from 25 μg/mL to 0.0061 μg/mL) were added into the Vero E6 cells. The culture supernatant of 48 h post-infection was collected to detect target gene RdRp/Hel by qRT-PCR. Forward primer (RdRp/Hel): 5′-CGCATACAGTCTTRCAGGCT-3′; reverse primer (RdRp/Hel): 5′-GTGTGATGTTGAWATGACATGGTC-3′3. IC50 values were determined by fitting a nonlinear four-parameter dose-response curve in GraphPad version 6.0. Alternatively, HEK293T-ACE2 cells were seeded in a 96-well culture plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, 3-fold serially diluted small compound (from 25 μg/mL to 0.308 μg/mL) and authentic SARS-CoV-2 VOCs (WT, BA.2, BA.5, XBB.1, and JN.1 variants and SARS-CoV-1) (MOI = 0.005) were added into cells, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The culture medium was then removed, and the plates were air-dried in a biosafety cabinet (BSC) for 20 min. Cells were then fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min and air-dried in the BSC again. Cells were further permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 and incubated with cross-reactive rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2-N serum for 1 h at RT before adding an Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody (Life Technologies). The fluorescence density of SARS-CoV-2-infected cells was scanned using a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems), and the neutralization effects were then quantified using Fiji software (NIH).

Antiviral assay using authentic MERS-CoV

MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 were pre-mixed with MB-32 with a range of concentrations (0–8 μM) prior to infection. Calu-3 cells were then infected with the virus-MB-32 mixtures at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. At 2 h post-infection, the inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, cells were maintained in fresh Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with MB-32 at the corresponding concentrations (0–8 μM). At 24 h post-infection, cell lysates were harvested for downstream analysis. RNA was extracted and subjected to quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) to quantify viral nucleocapsid (N) gene level, which was normalized with GAPDH expression.

Detection of S binding against ACE2 by ELISA

Binding between the purified SARS-CoV-2 S protein or RBD and the cellular entry factors ACE2 is detected by ELISA as previously described93. High-binding microtitre plates are coated with recombinant human ACE2 (CF933-ZN-010, R&D Systems, MN, USA) in PBS (100 ng per well) overnight at 4 °C. The plates are then blocked for 2 h at 37 °C with blocking buffer (PBS and 5% BSA) and washed with water for 3 times. Next, MB-32 diluted in 150 mM NaCl, 2% DMSO, and 0.02% Tween 20 is added to the plates in duplicates or triplicate and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, biotinylated SARS-CoV-2-S (100 ng mL-1) (SPN-C82E9, ACROBiosystems Group, DE, USA) or biotinylated SARS-CoV-2-RBD (40 ng mL-1) (793906, BioLegend) in 150 mM NaCl, 2% DMSO, and 0.02% Tween 20 is added to the plates in triplicate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Bound biotinylated protein is detected by adding Avidin-HRP (405103, BioLegend) diluted 1: 1000 in blocking buffer. Finally, the plates are developed with TMB turbo substrate for 5–15 min. The reaction is quenched using 0.5 M sulfuric acid, and the absorbance is measured at 450 nm.

Spike binding assay on ACE2‑expressing cells

Spike-ACE2 binding was assessed using HEK293T cells transiently expressing hACE2 and a stable HEK293T‑ACE2 cell line. Cells were seeded on poly‑L‑lysine-coated glass‑bottom dishes and cultured in D10 at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until ~70–80% confluent. For transient expression, HEK293T cells were transfected with a human ACE2-GFP expression plasmid 24–48 h before the assay. Recombinant biotinylated SARS‑CoV‑2 spike protein was pre‑mixed with avidin–Alexa Fluor 488 or avidin–Alexa Fluor 647 (final concentrations in the binding reaction: spike 250 ng/mL, avidin–Alexa Fluor 488/647 2 µg/mL) in D10 for 15–30 min at room temperature. Human transferrin (12.5 µg/mL; commercial source as listed in Reagents) was included as an internal control for clathrin‑mediated endocytosis. For MB‑32 treatment, cells were first incubated with MB‑32 (5 µg/mL in binding buffer) for 15 min at 37 °C. Without removing MB‑32, the pre‑formed spike–biotin/avidin–Alexa Fluor 488 or 647 mixture (spike 250 ng/mL, avidin 2 µg/mL, transferrin 12.5 µg/mL) was then added directly to the cells, and incubation was continued for 1 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Control wells received the spike–avidin mixture plus vehicle instead of MB‑32. After the 1‑h incubation, binding buffer was replaced with fresh D10 before imaging. Confocal images were acquired on a laser‑scanning confocal microscope using appropriate excitation and emission settings for Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 647, with identical acquisition parameters across conditions for quantitative comparison.

Protein expression and purification

A truncated extracellular form of human ACE2 (residues 1-740) and mutants were expressed in HEK293F cells. Briefly, the pCDNA3.1 plasmid containing the coding sequences of the human ACE2 or its mutations was transiently transfected into HEK293F cells. The supernatant was collected after 72 h culture, and soluble proteins were purified by using a 5-mL HisTrap Excel column. The sample was further purified via gel filtration chromatography with a Superdex™ 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) with a buffer consisting of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The binding kinetics and affinity of MB-32 for ACE2 from different species (human ACE2: Q9BYF1-1, ACROBiosystems Group, DE, USA; Macaca mulatta: AC2-C52H7, ACROBiosystems Group, DE, USA; hamster: 10578-ZN, R&D Systems, MN, USA; and rat: 80031-R08H, Sino Biology, TX, USA), human ACE2 single-site mutations, and SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein were analyzed by SPR using a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). ACE2 proteins were covalently immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip using an Amine Coupling Kit (Cytiva, BR100050) to an immobilization level of approximately 15000 response units (RU). SPR assays were performed at a flow rate of 30 µL/min in PBS (1:8 dilution) containing 2% DMSO and 0.02% Tween 20. Under a low-molecular-weight kinetic assay method, a concentration series of MB-32 was injected over the ACE2 surface for 60 s, followed by a 120 s dissociation phase in a multi-cycle format. No regeneration step was required; an additional wash with 50% DMSO was included between cycles. Affinity analysis of reference-subtracted sensorgrams was carried out using Biacore Evaluation software (Cytiva). For SPR analysis, transient injection-related spikes at the beginning of the injection, arising from bulk refractive index changes, were excluded prior to kinetic fitting in line with standard SPR data-processing practice. The interaction between MB-32 and ACE2 was evaluated using a steady-state affinity model with at least five analyte concentrations per series.

SPR analysis of ACE2-spike binding and competition by MB‑32

The binding kinetics and affinity of human ACE2 for SARS‑CoV‑2 spike were analyzed using a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). SARS‑CoV‑2 spike protein (ACROBiosystems, SPN‑C82E9) was covalently immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva, 29149603) using an amine coupling kit (Cytiva, BR100050) to a final response of ~1000 response units (RU). Experiments were performed at 25 °C at a flow rate of 30 µL/min in running buffer composed of 0.125× PBS, 2% DMSO, and 0.02% Tween‑20. For the ACE2-spike kinetic assay, a concentration series of human ACE2 (ACROBiosystems, AC2‑H52H8; 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256 nM) was injected over the spike‑coated surface for 180 s, followed by a 240 s dissociation phase, using a multi-cycle format. For competition assays, ACE2 was pre‑mixed with MB‑32 at a 1:40 molar ratio (ACE2:compound), and the resulting complexes were injected over the spike surface under the same association and dissociation conditions. After each cycle, the sensor surface was regenerated by two 30 s injections of 10 mM glycine‑HCl (pH 2.0) at 30 µL/min. Reference‑subtracted sensorgrams were analyzed using Biacore Evaluation software (Cytiva), and each injection series was globally fit to a 1:1 (Langmuir) binding model to obtain kinetic rate constants and equilibrium dissociation constants (KD).

Bio‑layer interferometry analysis of ACE2-spike interactions

Commercially biotinylated wild‑type ACE2–Fc (Acrobiosystems, AC2‑H82F9) were immobilized on streptavidin (SA) biosensors (Sartorius Octet, 18‑5019) until the loading response reached ~0.4 nm. SARS‑CoV‑2-WT spike proteins were diluted in Kinetics Buffer (Sartorius Octet, 18‑1105) to six concentrations (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 nM). ACE2-spike binding was measured on an Octet R2 2‑channel protein analysis system (Sartorius) at 30 °C with shaking, using a 240 s association step followed by a 180 s dissociation step. Reference sensor signals were subtracted from all traces, and the resulting curves were globally fit to a 1:1 binding model to determine kinetic and equilibrium affinity constants using Octet Data Analysis Studio 13.0 (Sartorius).

Human ACE2 enzymatic activity assay

ACE2 enzymatic activity was measured using the ACE2 Inhibitor Screening Assay Kit (MAK378, Sigma, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, various concentrations of MB-32 (10 μg/mL, 2 μg/mL, 0.4 μg/mL, and 0.08 μg/mL) were mixed with ACE2 in 96-well plates and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. After incubation, a fluorogenic ACE2 substrate was added, and the fluorescence intensity (320 nm/420 nm, excitation/emission), indicative of ACE2 enzymatic activity, was measured in kinetic mode for 1 h with Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems).

Human TMPRSS2 enzymatic activity assay

TMPRSS2 enzymatic activity was measured using the TMPRSS2 Fluorogenic Assay Kit (78083, BPS Bioscience, Inc., USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MB-32 (17.5 µM) was mixed with TMPRSS2 in 96-well plates and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. After incubation, a fluorogenic substrate was added, and the fluorescence intensity (383 nm/455 nm, excitation/emission), indicative of enzymatic activity, was measured with Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems).

Human Cathepsin L enzymatic activity assay

Cathepsin L enzymatic activity was measured using the Innozyme Cathepsin L Activity Kit (CBA023, Sigma, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MB-32 (17.5 µM) was mixed with Cathepsin L in 96-well plates and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. After incubation, a fluorogenic substrate was added and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C, and the fluorescence intensity (360 nm/460 nm, excitation/emission), indicative of enzymatic activity, was measured with Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems).

WaterLOGSY NMR binding assay of MB-32 with hACE2

All NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance-600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5mm PASEI probe with z-gradient. The WaterLOGSY experiment employing a pulse scheme “ephogsygpno.2” with water suppression using excitation sculpting with gradients. WaterLOGSY experiments were recorded using 2 K scans and 32 K points at 298 K. Control spectrum was also recorded under the same conditions, but no addition of hACE2 macromolecule to confirm no self-aggregation of MB-32 as macromolecule. Recombinant human ACE2 (Cat Q9BYF1-1, ACROBiosystems Group, DE, USA) was prepared to 0.25 µM concentration in 75 mM Tris and 1 M NaCl, pH 7.5 and mixed with 250 µM of MB-32 to attain a 1:1000 molar ratio for macromolecule vs ligand. To calibrate the actual effective concentration of MB-32 in the samples, all NMR samples were standardized by adding 175 µM Trimethylsilylpropanoic acid (TMSP) as internal standard. The 1D 1H NMR experiment was recorded using 2 K scans and 32 K points at 298 K, employing a pulse scheme “zgesgp” with water suppression using excitation sculpting with gradients.

STD-NMR binding assay of MB-32 with hACE2

The STD-NMR measurements were performed on a Bruker Avance-600 MHz spectrometer employing a pulse scheme “stddiff19.2” with WATERGATE water suppression. STD-NMR experiments were recorded with the saturation pulse set at 0.83 ppm (500 Hz) for on-resonance irradiation and 60 ppm (36,000 Hz) for off-resonance irradiation. The spectra were collected in an interleaved fashion to reduce temporal fluctuations. The STD-NMR binding experiment was performed on a sample with 250 µM MB-32 mixed with 0.25 µM human ACE2 (Cat Q9BYF1-1, ACROBiosystems Group, DE, USA) (hACE2:MB-32 = 1:1000) in 3% glycerol. Control spectra were recorded under identical conditions without hACE2 protein to confirm that the on- and off-resonance irradiation frequencies did not affect the MB-32 ligand resonances. Selective protein saturation (2 s) was accomplished using a train of 50 ms Gauss-shaped pulses, each separated by a 4 μs delay, at an experimentally determined optimal power (50 dB on a 5 mm PABBI probe). Experiments were recorded using a minimum of 4 K scans and 32 K points at 298 K. On- and off-resonance spectra were processed independently and subtracted to provide a difference spectrum using TOPSPIN software (Bruker).

Mass spectrometry experiments

Formaldehyde (FA) crosslinking was performed on a sample containing 10 µL of human ACE2 (18 mg/mL; Q9BYF1‑1, ACROBiosystems Group, DE, USA) and 0.6 µL MB‑32 (15.5 mM) in the presence of 0.9 µL FA (final FA concentration ~3%) at 30 °C for 30 min with shaking, essentially as described previously with minor modifications. The sample was vortexed every minute during incubation. The crosslinking reaction was quenched by adding 10 µL of 4 M Tris and incubating for a further 30 min at 30 °C in a water bath. A control crosslinking reaction was performed in parallel with ACE2 alone under identical conditions.

Crosslinked samples (ACE2 + MB‑32 and ACE2‑only control; n = 1 biological replicate each, with one technical LC‑MS/MS injections per digest) were separated by SDS–PAGE on a 3% stacking (3 cm wide) and 8% resolving gel. The gel was run with all proteins stacked as a single band at the boundary between the stacking and resolving gel. After Coomassie Blue staining, each band was excised and subjected to in‑gel digestion. Briefly, gel slices were reduced with 10 mM TCEP and alkylated with 55 mM 2‑chloroacetamide, then digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin (1.0 ng/µL). Tryptic peptides were extracted sequentially with 50% acetonitrile (ACN)/5% FA and 100% ACN. The peptide extracts were pooled and dried in a vacuum concentrator.

Peptides were desalted on C18 StageTips and analyzed by LC–MS/MS using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 nanoRSLC system coupled to a Thermo Fisher Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid Lumos mass spectrometer. Peptides were loaded onto a Nanotrap column (75 µm i.d. × 2 cm, 3 µm C18 particles; Thermo Fisher) and separated on an analytical C18 column (75 µm i.d. × 50 cm, 2 µm particles; Thermo Fisher) using a linear gradient of increasing buffer B (80% ACN, 0.1% FA) declining buffer A (0.1% FA) at a flow rate of 300 µL/min. Buffer B was increased from 3% to 27.5% over 30 min, from 27.5% to 44% over 16 min, ramped to 95% for 3 min, and then returned to 3% for column re‑equilibration.

The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode with a capillary temperature of 300 °C. Full MS survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 (at m/z 200) over an m/z range of 400–1,500, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 5 × 104 and a maximum injection time of 30 ms. Data‑dependent acquisition (top‑10 method) was performed with higher‑energy collisional dissociation (HCD). MS/MS spectra were acquired at 30,000 resolution with an AGC target of 200% (normalized), maximum injection time of 80 ms, isolation width of 1.6 m/z, and normalized collision energy of 30; dynamically selected precursor ions were excluded from re‑selection for 22 s.

Data Processing Protocol: Raw MS data were processed with MaxQuant (version 1.6.14.0). Spectra were searched against the human Swiss‑Prot FASTA database (May 2022 release; 20,373 entries) using the built‑in Andromeda search engine, with carbamidomethylation of Cys as a fixed modification and methionine oxidation and protein N‑terminal acetylation as dynamic modifications. A minimum peptide length of 7 amino acids was required. Confident proteins were identified using a target-decoy approach with a reversed database, strict false-discovery rate 1% at peptide and peptide spectrum matches (PSMs) level; minimum ≥1 unique peptide, ≥2 peptide spectral matches (PSMs).

To identify FA crosslinking‑related adducts, additional database searches were performed using PEAKS Studio 11 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.) with a custom variable modification corresponding to MB‑32 (534.22 Da) plus a two‑carbon (24 Da) bridge on Asp, Tyr, Arg, and Lys side chains. Search parameters otherwise matched those used in the MaxQuant analysis.

Induced fit docking and molecular dynamics analysis

The wildtype crystal structure of ACE2 complexed with the viral spike protein (PDB code: 6M0J)45 underwent initial preparation using the Protein Preparation Wizard (Schrödinger Release 2023-3) with default settings. Subsequently, the ACE2 structure was isolated and immersed in an orthorhombic SPC water box, maintaining a minimum buffer distance of 10 Å from the protein. Sodium ions were introduced to neutralize the simulation system. Equilibration procedures followed the default multi-step equilibration protocol within Desmond (Schrödinger Release 2023-3). To enhance the sampling of the loop spanning Y83 to K94, a 500 ns metadynamics simulation was conducted. This simulation utilized the phi and psi angle of Y83 as collective variables, set at a width of 5 degrees, a height of 0.03 kcal/mol, and intervals of 0.09 ps. Trajectory analysis involved visual inspection and selection of frames wherein Y83 exhibited a similar orientation as K94, clustering frames from 240 to 323 ns and from 420 to 500 ns. Four cluster representatives were identified. The ligand MB-32 was individually docked to these representative structures using Induced Fit Docking (Schrödinger Release 2023-3), defining the binding pocket by residues Y83, K94, Q98, and Y202. Subsequent visual inspection revealed only one pose, placing the ligand’s methylphenyl and nitroimidazole groups in proximity to K94 and Y83, respectively. Refinement of this pose employed the Refine Protein-Ligand Complex protocol in Prime (Schrödinger Release 2023-3), wherein residues within 5 Å of the ligand underwent sampling using a hierarchical optimization algorithm. All simulations adhered to the OPLS4 force field94.

MB-32 formulation for animal studies

MB-32 initially dissolves in 100% DMSO to create a stock concentration of 40 mg/mL. Subsequently, it is formulated in a mixture of DMSO/Tween 80/saline at a ratio of 5:5:90 (v:v:v) until it reaches its maximum concentration of 2 mg/mL.

Pharmacokinetics in mice

A total of 24 male C57BL/6 mice (6–8-week-old), weighing between 24 and 27 g, were utilized in the study. The mice underwent intranasal administration of MB-32 (2 mg/mL, 30 µL) under Avertin anesthesia. Blood, turbinate, and lung tissues were harvested at various time intervals: Pre-dose (0), 0.25, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 8, and 24 h post-dose. Blood samples (~250 µL per time point) were collected via Cardiac Puncture into EDTA-K2 tubes and subsequently centrifuged at 4 °C (6000 rpm for 5 min) for further analysis. Turbinate homogenization was carried out using an H-speed dispensator (26× dilution, Turbinate: 80%MeOH, 1:25, w/v) for subsequent processing and examination.

Toxicity of maximum dose of MB-32 in mice

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6) and subjected to a single intranasal administration of MB-32 (2 mg/mL, 30 μL) or its vehicle under intraperitoneal ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) anesthesia. Daily monitoring of the mice’s body weight was carried out for 7 days.

In vivo lethal challenge and MB-32 treatment in mice

MB-32 was formulated fresh immediately prior to administration. MB-32 was dissolved in DMSO at concentration of 40 mg/mL first, and was then dissolved in MB-32 solvent (Saline with 5% DMSO and Tween 80) at concentration of 60 μg/15 μL (4 mg/mL). For MB-32 treatment group, MB-32 at concentration of 60 μg/15 μL was mixed with SARS-CoV-2 at concentration of 4 × 103 PFU/15 μL at a ratio of 1:1. For control group, 14.25 μL MB-32 solvent (Saline with 5% DMSO and tween80) was first mixed with 0.75 μL DMSO, and then mix with WT SARS-CoV-2 at concentration of 4 × 103 PFU/15 μL at a ratio of 1:1. For virus challenge in mice, K18-hACE2 transgenic mice (aged 6–8-week-old male) were anaesthetized with ketamine and xylazine, followed by intranasal inoculation with 30 µL per mouse of MB-32-SARS-CoV-2 mixture. Animals from the control group received matched volumes of solvent-SARS-CoV-2 mixture. Mice were euthanized at 4 dpi for collecting lung tissues and nasal turbinates for virological assessment and histological examination as we previously described95,96. The body weight and survival of the infected animals were monitored for 14 days or until death of the animal. Viral RNA from infected mice lungs and nasal turbinates was extracted using the RNeasy Mini kit (74106, Qiagen). SARS-CoV-2 gene copies targeting the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) were quantified using the QuantiNova Probe RT-PCR Kit (208354, Qiagen). The primer and probe sequences are available on request.

Infectious virus titration by plaque assays

Organs collected from infected mice were homogenized in DMEM using the TissueLyser II system (Qiagen). The clarified supernatants were subjected to tenfold serial dilutions and inoculated onto confluent monolayers of VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells for 2 h at 37 °C. After inoculation, the inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were then overlaid with DMEM containing 1% low-melting-point agarose and 1% FBS. The cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde after 72 h incubation and stained with 0.5% crystal violet prepared in 25% ethanol for plaque visualization.

Prophylaxis studies in mice and golden hamster

6-week-old male K18-hACE2 mice were intranasally administered one dose of MB-32 (2 mg/mL, 30 μL) (n = 4) or its saline-based solvent (n = 4) alone under intraperitoneal ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) anesthesia 30 min before live virus challenge. Golden hamsters (8-week-old male) were intranasally administered one dose of MB-32 (2 mg/mL, 200 μL) (n = 4) or its saline-based solvent alone (n = 4). Animals were then intranasally inoculated with a challenge dose of 30 μL of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium containing SARS-CoV-2 (2.5 × 104 PFU of BA.2 and BA.5, or 1 × 105 PFU of JN.1). The animals were monitored daily for clinical signs of disease, and were sacrificed for analysis at day 4 after virus challenge. Half of the lung tissues were utilized for determining viral load through quantitative SARS-CoV-2-specific RdRp/Hel and sgNP reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction assay. The viral load was normalized by β-actin.

Contact transmission studies in golden hamster

To assess the effectiveness of MB-32 in preventing SARS-CoV-2 contact transmission in golden hamsters (10–12-week-old male), six donor hamsters were anesthetized with ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) through intraperitoneal injection and inoculated with 5.6 × 104 PFU of SARS-CoV-2 (strain XBB.1.5, GISAID: EPI_ISL_17205250). At 2 days post-inoculation (dpi), each of the inoculated donor hamsters (index animal) was moved to cohabitate with two naive hamsters: one was intranasally administered one dose of MB-32 (350 nmol/100 µL) in saline (n = 6), while another one received saline alone (n = 6), all 30 min before the cohousing. The cohousing period lasted for 4 h. Following the exposure, the donor hamsters were sacrificed for analysis. All naïve hamsters were individually housed in separate cages, monitored daily, and then sacrificed after 2 days for analysis. For nasal wash collection, hamsters were anesthetized using ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. Nasal washes were collected from both nostrils of each hamster using 160 μL of PBS containing 0.3% BSA. The collected nasal washes were diluted 1:1 and aliquoted for TCID50 assay in Vero E6 cells and quantitative real-time RT-PCR. Half of the nasal turbinates and lung tissues were utilized for determining viral load through quantitative SARS-CoV-2-specific RdRp/Hel and sgNP reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction assay. The viral load was normalized by β-actin, and the plaque-forming units (PFU) were normalized by the weight of tissue extractions.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The lung tissues were collected and fixed with 10% neutral-buffered formalin or zinc formalin and then processed into paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. The tissue sections (4 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for light microscopy examination as previously described with modifications97. For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections were stained with viral nucleocapsid (N) antigens and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI as previously described with slight modifications)98. Briefly, the tissue sections were first treated with antigen-unmasking solution (Vector Laboratories) in a pressure cooker. After blocking with 0.1% Sudan black B for 15 min and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS at RT for 30 min, the primary antibody, rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2-N serum (In-house, 1:4000 dilution with 1% BSA/PBS), was incubated at 4 °C overnight. This step was followed by FITC-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Cat # A-11034, clone: polyclonal) for 30 min and then mounted with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The images were acquired on a Carl Zeiss LSM780 fluorescence microscope using ZEN imaging software v3.3 (Zeiss).

Cardiovascular monitoring following MB-32 treatment in rats

SD rats weighing 220 ± 20 g, comprising an equal number of males and females, were procured from Charles River Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. These rats were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group, sex- and age-matched) and subjected to a single intranasal instillation of either saline or MB-32 (200 μg per rat). Subsequently, the heart rate and blood pressure metrics (MBP, SBP, DBP) of all animals were assessed utilizing a blood pressure monitor (Softron Biotechnology, BP-2010A) at specific intervals: prior to administration, and at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h post-administration. Simultaneously, electrocardiogram (ECG) readings for all animals were captured using an electrocardiograph (ADINSTRUMENTS, PowerLab 26T) at designated intervals: before administration, and at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h post-administration. Each ECG measurement was recorded over a 3-min duration at every time point. Analysis included examination of various parameters such as RR interval, PR interval, P wave duration, QRS duration, QT interval, and QTcF.

Analysis of N487 and Y489 conservation in spike glycoprotein of different lineages

The conservation of residues N487 and Y489 in the spike glycoprotein was examined across multiple SARS-CoV-2 lineages (Wuhan-Hu-1, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Omicron and its subvariants BA.1, BA.2, BA.4, BA.5.1, BA.2.75, CH.1.1, XBB, XBB.1.9.1, XBB.2.3, XBB.1.5, XBB.1.16 and EG.5), as well as related sarbecoviruses from bats (Rs4231, Rs7327), pangolins, Civets and SARS-CoV-1. Representative spike sequences for each lineage were retrieved from NCBI. Multiple sequence alignments were generated with MAFFT and displayed using a Clustal-style color scheme. Conservation scores and sequence logos were calculated and visualized in Jalview version 2.11.2.7.

Graphs/Statistical analyses

The data analyses and graphical representations were conducted employing GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was determined by a threshold of p < 0.05.
