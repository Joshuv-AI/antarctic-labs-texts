Rheological Origin of Aging
Vikash Pandey

Abstract
Most materials of practical applications age, and their properties change with time. The aging of materials is reflected in their mechanical responses to external stress and strain, which exhibit ubiquity in the form of logarithms and power laws, respectively. Such responses are usually described using complicated phenomenological models, including fractional viscoelastic models, but they lack physical insights. In this Letter, we postulate a linear time-varying rheological property whose constitutive relations are motivated from thermodynamic principles and experimental observations of the stick-slip-induced friction. The property satisfyingly explains the logarithmic relaxation and the three stages of universal creep behavior. Consequently, the fractional Maxwell model and the Mittag-Leffler function gain physical interpretation. Lastly, we show that viscosity emerges as a special case of the proposed property.

Any set of MRFs from a physically valid rheological mechanism must
adhere to the principles of thermodynamics and causality. This ensures
physically valid transient and dynamic behavior. The MRFs must satisfy
the following conditions at all times,
t\geq 0
,

\begin{cases}G\left(t\right)\geq 0,&\left(-1\right)^{n}\frac{d^{n}G\left(t\right)}{dt^{n}}\geq 0,\text{ and }\\
J\left(t\right)\geq 0,&\left(-1\right)^{n}\frac{d^{n}J\left(t\right)}{dt^{n}}\leq 0,\end{cases}

(1)

where
n
is a positive integer

. On the one
hand, the relaxation modulus is a complete monotone function, i.e.,
it is non-negative, non-increasing, and convex. On the other hand,
the creep compliance is a Bernstein function, i.e., it is non-negative,
non-decreasing, and concave. As the two conditions are rooted in general
Clausius–Duhem inequality, they guarantee the rate of entropy production
is nonnegative at all times, as expected from the second law of thermodynamics

Holm2017a
. These conditions are stricter than the causality
tests motivated by Kramers–Kronig relations. Such satisfying
G\left(t\right)

and
J\left(t\right)
when used in Boltzmann’s hereditary model correspond
to casual and fading passive memory kernels. They ascertain that inputs
from the recent past dominate the material behavior over the inputs
from the older past. For the same reason, the possibility of oscillatory
MRFs is discarded. Further, the two MRFs obtained from a LTI theory
are coupled to each other in the Laplace domain,
s
, as
\widetilde{G}\left(s\right)\widetilde{J}\left(s\right)=1/s^{2}

physical mechanism underlying universal MRFs is common, it has constrained
the search to LTI viscoelastic models.

The time-varying rheological property, jerkity, was introduced in
Ref.

\varepsilon_{d}

\dot{\sigma}_{d}\left(t\right)=\lambda\left(t\right)\varepsilon_{d}\left(t\right)

the dissipative nature of the jerk-mechanism. That study mostly focussed
on Andrade’s law and Omori’s law of earthquake aftershocks. The physical
justification and the relaxation behavior from the jerk mechanism
are yet to be studied. Beyond the mathematical curiosity to link
\dot{\sigma}\left(t\right)

with
\varepsilon
\left(t\right)
, the property is justified as
follows. There is sufficient in-situ experimental evidence that under
external actions of stress and strain, internal layers of almost all
materials develop microscopically rough surfaces due to localized
deformations
Galaz2018
;
Gu2024
. Those deformations, in the
case of metallic glasses and noncrystalline metals, create narrow
bands that slide past each other

. The actual contact
between the surfaces is mediated by an ensemble of microcontacts which
are often in the form of microasperities or nano-voids that hang out
from those layers
Suhr2005
;
Liu2021
. As the layers slide past
each other, the asperities from opposite facing-layers stick, they
lock against each other building the stress further. Eventually after
a certain threshold value of the stress, the static friction is overcome
followed by a sudden slip. As the lock between the asperities disengages,
the potential energy manifested from the accumulated stress is released
through short bursts of acoustic energy
Koivisto2007
. Such
acoustic emissions were referred to as copper-quakes by Andrade in
his observations about deformation in copper
Andrade1910
.
The friction changes from the static to the lower kinetic friction
enabling the motion temporarily, forming new lock-contacts, and the
process repeats. The stick-slip mechanism is also observed at atomic
levels due to local bond breaking and rebonding
Lee2010a
.
The stick-slip-induced friction is quite common yet one of the least
understood physical mechanisms. A common observation in stick-slip
type frictional interactions is a gradual increase in the contact
area, often
\sim\log t

the strengthening of interfacial bonds in lamellar materials too

We believe that relaxation in solids occurs through a stick-slip-induced
dissipation mechanism which is different than the conventional viscous
dissipation, the latter is better suited for liquids. The time-varying
aspect of

The logarithmic relaxation has an inherent connection with the fractional
Maxwell model (FMM). The FMM generalizes the classical Maxwell model
by using fractional derivatives and it is represented as a series
combination of a spring and a fractional dashpot. The constitutive
relation of a FMM is,
E\tau_{\sigma}^{\alpha}D_{t}^{\alpha}\varepsilon\left(t\right)=\tau_{\sigma}^{\alpha}D_{t}^{\alpha}\sigma\left(t\right)+\sigma\left(t\right)

and

denotes the convolution operation. Notably, the fractional
dashpot combines the behavior of both elastic solids and Newtonian
viscous fluids as evident from the limiting conditions
\alpha\rightarrow 0

and
\alpha\rightarrow 1

to the FMM, we input a step-strain, and then following the applications
of the Laplace transform and correspondence principle, see Chapter

(4)

\alpha=1/\left(E\theta\right)

which eventually provides physical interpretation to both the FMM

Historically, creep has been linked with viscosity, see Maxwell’s
definition of viscous fluids
Maxwell1871
. It is less likely
that a search for an alternative creep mechanism other than those
viscoelastic models that belong to the LTI system, would have been
attempted. In this Letter, we explored beyond the boundary of LTI
systems to discover the property of time-varying jerk-elasticity from
which the property of viscosity emerged as one of its special cases.
In the same vein, we stress that viscosity is mostly introduced in
constitutive relations and they rarely make an appearance in any of
the conservation laws. For example, the Navier-Stokes equation is
a viscous extension of Euler’s equation for inviscid fluid, only the
latter is fundamentally deduced from the laws of conservation of mass
and momentum, see Chapter 1 in
Holm2019
. In contrast, the
jerk-elasticity mechanism introduced here is firmly anchored in thermodynamic
laws. We may therefore infer that the property of jerk-elasticity
is relatively more fundamental than viscosity. We hope that the property
of jerk-elasticity gains the same regard as elasticity and viscosity.

A careful inspection of the common experimental observations and thermodynamic
requirements automatically led to the linearly time-varying property
of jerk-elasticity. The property satisfying described materials’ universal
relaxation and creep behavior. Notably, the MRFs from the jerk model
do not encounter any singularity at
t=0
in comparison to their
competing counterparts. The stick-slip mechanism is established as
the common physical mechanism responsible for memory exhibits, and
aging in materials. The findings from this work may further our understanding
of stick-slip-induced frictional interaction since the property of
jerk-elasticity stems from it.

The commonly observed logarithmic relaxation across complex media
is an outcome of the same mechanism. This is possibly the first work
that has given a physical insight into the fractional Maxwell model.
The associated Mittag-Leffler function offers a bridge for viewing
logarithmic memory kernels as limits of fractional dynamics but now
a physical interpretation is given to it. These results open an opportunity
to study and interpret fractional Maxwell wave equations and dielectric
relaxations which are described using an electrical analogue of the
Maxwell model.

Besides, the jerk-elasticity mechanism unifies all three stages of
creep with a temporal evolution of
\tau_{\varepsilon}
and
\alpha
.
This aligns with recent reports of different time constants in different
relaxation/deformation regimes
Barik2022
;
Wang2012a
. Lastly,
since an understanding of the three stages may help in forecasting
a possible failure of the material
Lavier2021
and avoid potential
catastrophe, we hope the results presented here find applications
in that direction. Moreover, a simple explanation with a few parameters
to a variety of observations is parsimonious at the least.
