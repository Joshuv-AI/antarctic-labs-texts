Neisseria meningitidis-associated chronic prostatitis following oral sexual exposure: a diagnostic challenge mimicking gonococcal urethritis
Authors: Zhang Jing, Xia Ming, Lyu XiaoMing, You Xu, Zhao Ming, Sun Hengbiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeisseria meningitidis is an uncommon cause of genitourinary infection, and chronic prostatitis caused by this organism has rarely been reported. A 34-year-old man presented with recurrent dysuria and urethral discharge after oral sexual exposure. Initially misdiagnosed as gonococcal urethritis, the patient achieved only partial improvement after empirical therapy. Culture of midstream urine and expressed prostatic secretions, confirmed by biochemical testing,MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rDNA sequencing, identified N. meningitidis. Targeted antimicrobial therapy resulted in clinical and microbiological resolution. This case highlights the importance of accurate pathogen identification in recurrent urethritis and prostatitis.Keywords: Neisseria meningitidis, Chronic prostatitis, Urinary tract infection, Sexually transmitted infection

Introduction

Neisseria meningitidis is a Gram-negative, kidney-shaped diplococcus that typically arranges in pairs and most strains possess a capsule and pili. This bacterium is a strict aerobe with high nutritional requirements, and it grows well on media containing blood or serum. It is sensitive to dryness, cold, heat, and common disinfectants, and has very low resistance in vitro. Based on differences in capsular polysaccharide antigens, Neisseria meningitidis has been classified into 12 serogroups, among which groups A, B, C, X, Y, and W are the main serogroups causing invasive meningococcal disease in humans.1

Traditionally, Neisseria meningitidis has been regarded as a significant pathogen responsible for central nervous system and systemic infections. It primarily colonizes the mucosal surfaces of the human nasopharynx and spreads via respiratory droplets. This bacterium can lead to several severe diseases, including epidemic meningitis, septicemia, pneumonia, arthritis, and pericarditis, with a high mortality rate.2,3 However, recent studies have increasingly shown that Neisseria meningitidis not only causes the aforementioned invasive diseases but can also, through sexual contact, lead to urinary and genital tract infections resembling gonococcal urethritis, emerging as a novel sexually transmitted pathogen.4

In recent years, the incidence of Neisseria meningitidis infections in the urinary and genital tracts has been increasing globally, becoming a significant issue in the field of sexually transmitted diseases. In 2015, the United States reported an outbreak of Neisseria meningitidis urethritis across multiple states, and the strain was later named US_NmUC.5 Subsequently, several countries worldwide reported cases of urethral infections caused by this strain, indicating that this pathogen poses a potential public health threat.6,7 Among men with Neisseria meningitidis urethritis, the vast majority are heterosexual, although some literature has reported that men who have sex with men (MSM) are also a high-risk population.8,9 Compared with Europe and North America, China has been slower to recognize and monitor Neisseria meningitidis infections in the urinary and genital tracts, as prior public health efforts primarily focused on the prevention and control of epidemic meningitis, with insufficient attention paid to sexually transmitted diseases caused by this pathogen. In recent years, with advancements in laboratory diagnostic technology for sexually transmitted disease pathogens, several regions in China have gradually reported cases of Neisseria meningitidis infections in the urinary and genital tracts, suggesting that the prevalence of this pathogen in sexually transmitted diseases in China may have been underestimated, and the lack of awareness among clinicians may lead to missed diagnoses and misdiagnoses.4

Recently, we isolated Neisseria meningitidis from the specimen of a patient with a urinary tract infection. This report combines the patient's clinical data, laboratory findings, and treatment process, aiming to enhance clinicians' awareness of Neisseria meningitidis infections in the urinary and provide a reference for the early diagnosis and standardized treatment of this disease.

Case presentation

Patient information

On November 29, 2025, a 34-year-old man visited to the Department of Urology at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University, Guangzhou, with a three-month history of intermittent dysuria and urethral discharge. Three months prior, he had sexual activity with a female partner, including atypical oral exposure (the partner used his saliva as a lubricant), in addition to conventional intercourse. Three days after the encounter, he developed dysuria and copious urethral discharge and sought care at a local clinic, where he was empirically diagnosed with gonococcal urethritis without laboratory confirmation. He received intramuscular ceftriaxone for three days, followed by a seven-day course of oral cefixime combined with azithromycin. Although his symptoms partially improved, he continued to experience occasional dysuria and increased urethral discharge approximately twice per month, which prompted him to seek further evaluation at our institution.

Laboratory findings

Midstream urine was collected for bacterial culture (including Neisseria gonorrhoeae), nucleic acid testing for N. gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis, and culture for Ureaplasma urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis. Subsequently, expressed prostatic secretions (EPS) were obtained via prostatic massage for routine EPS examination and bacterial culture (including N. gonorrhoeae). Nucleic acid testing of midstream urine was negative for C. trachomatis, and U. urealyticum and M. hominis were not detected. Routine microscopy of EPS revealed 50 WBC/HP. Both midstream urine and EPS specimens were inoculated onto blood and chocolate agar plates and incubated at 35 °C with 5% CO2 for 48–72 hours (Fig. 1A and B). Only 1 colony grew on each medium from midstream urine, which appeared as Gram-negative diplococci on Gram staining (Fig. 1C). In contrast, EPS samples yielded abundant colonies on both media, also appearing as Gram-negative diplococci (Fig. 1D). Colonies from both midstream urine and EPS were subcultured to new plates to obtain pure isolates.

Colony morphology on chocolate agar from midstream urine (A) and prostatic fluid (B), with corresponding Gram-stained microscopic images from midstream urine (C, derived from the single colony cultivated on blood agar) and prostatic fluid (D).

Biochemical testing identified the pure isolates as Neisseria meningitidis (GLU+, MAL+, GGT+, PROA+, NIT+), confirmed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and 16S rDNA sequencing (Fig. 2A–D). Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the broth dilution method according to CLSI guidelines. The strain was susceptible to minocycline, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, azithromycin, ceftriaxone, and meropenem; resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (>2/38 μg/mL); and intermediate to penicillin (0.25 μg/mL).

Microbial identification of the pure colonies via MALDI-TOF MS from midstream urine (A) and prostatic fluid (B), with corresponding 16S rDNA sequencing results from midstream urine (C) and prostatic fluid (D).

Treatment and outcome

Based on laboratory results and clinical presentation, the patient was diagnosed with chronic prostatitis caused by Neisseria meningitidis. He received intramuscular ceftriaxone (1 g once daily for 3 days) followed by oral cefixime (0.1 g twice daily for 30 days). By January 2026, urethral discharge and dysuria had resolved. At follow-up on February 7, 2026, routine microscopy of EPS showed only 2 WBC/HP, and bacterial cultures were negative, indicating microbiological and clinical resolution of the infection.

Discussion

In this case, the patient experienced symptoms for over three months, with EPS microscopy showing 50 WBC/HP and a markedly higher Neisseria meningitidis load in EPS than in urine, meeting criteria for chronic bacterial prostatitis.10 While Neisseria meningitidis is typically associated with invasive meningitis and septicemia, its role in urinary tract infections, particularly chronic prostatitis, is exceedingly rare.11 Most reports focus on acute urethritis, which closely resembles gonococcal urethritis and is frequently misdiagnosed. For instance, Alhetheel Faisal et al. described a 36-year-old man with persistent non-specific urethritis who remained symptomatic after levofloxacin and azithromycin therapy, and was ultimately diagnosed with Neisseria meningitidis using Thayer-Martin culture and MALDI-TOF MS, illustrating the potential for missed diagnoses of non-gonococcal pathogens with conventional molecular tests.12 In reports from Thailand and Vietnam, Neisseria meningitidis urethritis has typically presented as an acute, self-limiting infection associated with oral sexual exposure.13,14 In contrast, the present case was characterized by recurrent chronic prostatitis lasting three months rather than acute urethritis. This clinical course suggests that the initial empirical ceftriaxone–azithromycin therapy may have transiently alleviated symptoms but failed to eradicate prostatic colonization, allowing the infection to become chronic. This clinical challenge resembles that encountered in gonococcal prostatitis, although the causative pathogen is entirely different.

Although Neisseria meningitidis has traditionally been regarded as a colonizer of the oropharynx,15 its ability to establish chronic localized infection within the prostate suggests the involvement of specific virulence factors and host–pathogen interactions. Urogenital strains of Neisseria meningitidis have acquired genetic elements, such as the aniA–norB operon, which facilitate adaptation to the microaerophilic, nitrate-rich environment of the male urogenital tract.7,16 Additionally, anatomical features of the prostate, such as impaired glandular drainage, and the local immune microenvironment, including high expression of immunosuppressive factors like TGF-β, may provide a protective niche for immune evasion. Previous studies have also indicated that Neisseria meningitidis can form biofilms, conferring resistance to both antibiotics and host immune clearance.17,18 These factors may explain why the initial short course of ceftriaxone in this case transiently relieved symptoms but failed to eradicate the pathogen.

The diagnostic challenges and educational significance of this case are particularly notable. First, the patient had a clear history of oral sexual exposure, consistent with the sexual transmission route of Neisseria meningitidis, and literature confirms that oropharyngeal carriage is an important source for urethral infection.15 However, the initial clinician empirically diagnosed gonococcal urethritis based solely on symptoms, without performing any pathogen testing, which was a critical factor contributing to treatment failure and progression to chronic infection. Second, Neisseria meningitidis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are both Gram-negative diplococci morphologically, and Gram staining cannot reliably distinguish them; differentiation requires biochemical testing (e.g., glucose and maltose fermentation) or MALDI-TOF MS. In this case, the pathogen was definitively identified as Neisseria meningitidis using MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rDNA sequencing, and other common pathogens, including C. trachomatis, and U. urealyticum and M. hominis, were excluded.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of the Neisseria meningitidis isolate demonstrated intermediate susceptibility to penicillin (0.25 μg/mL) but susceptibility to ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and ciprofloxacin. This phenotype is consistent with recent observations in Neisseria meningitidis urethritis strains in China. For example, the GDSP2023025 strain described by Fu Bo et al. exhibited similar intermediate penicillin susceptibility and carried carry penA (PBP2) mutations and efflux pump genes.19 Genomic analyses of isolates from Shanghai revealed that ST-11026 and ST-1466 urogenital strains carry five PBP2 mutations (F50L, A510V, I515V, H541N, I566V) and a porB (A121D) mutation, which confer penicillin resistance.20 Although whole-genome sequencing was not performed for the present isolate, the MIC profile suggests a similar molecular mechanism. In this case, a prolonged sequential antibiotic regimen with ceftriaxone followed by oral cefixime for 30 days achieved both clinical and microbiological cure, highlighting the importance of extended therapy to ensure adequate prostatic penetration in chronic prostatitis.

Conclusion

Finally, this case underscores the need for heightened clinical awareness. In patients with recurrent urethritis or prostatitis—particularly those with atypical sexual exposures such as oral sex—comprehensive pathogen identification, including urine and EPS culture, biochemical testing, and MALDI-TOF MS, is critical. Neisseria meningitidis should be included in the routine differential diagnosis to prevent misdiagnosis, treatment failure, and progression to chronic infection.
