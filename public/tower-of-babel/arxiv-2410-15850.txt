Solving elliptic PDEs in unbounded domains
Doghonay Arjmand, Filip Marttala

Abstract
An accurate approximation of solutions to elliptic problems in infinite domains is challenging from a computational point of view. This is due to the need to replace the infinite domain with a sufficiently large and bounded computational domain, and posing artificial boundary conditions on the boundary of the truncated computational geometry, which will then pollute the solution in an interior region of interest. For elliptic problems with periodically varying coefficients (with a possibly unknown period), a modelling strategy based on exponentially regularized elliptic problem was previously developed and analysed. The main idea was to replace the infinite domain periodic problem with a regularized elliptic problem posed over a finite domain, while retaining an accuracy decaying exponentially with respect to the size of the truncated domain. In this article, we extend the analysis to problems, where no structural assumptions on the coefficient are assumed. Moreover, the analysis here uncovers an interesting property of the right hand side in the Fourier domain for the method to converge fast for problems beyond periodicity.



Keywords—
Elliptic PDEs, parabolic PDEs, infinite domain problems

\rho(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}1,\text{ }\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{\frac{3}{4}R},\\
0,\text{ }\boldsymbol{x}\text{ on }\partial K_{R},\end{cases}

(28)

and
\lvert\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq\frac{C}{R}
on
E
. Then
e_{R}
satisfies

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x}))&=F(\boldsymbol{x}),\text{ }\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{R},\\
e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})&=0,\text{ }\boldsymbol{x}\text{ on }\partial K_{R},\end{split}

(29)

where

F(\boldsymbol{x})=(\rho(\boldsymbol{x})-1)g(\boldsymbol{x})-a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{x})u(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{x})).

(30)

Clearly
e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})=u(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{u}_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})
in
K_{L}\subset K_{\frac{3}{4}R}
, and therefore,
\lVert e_{R}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K_{L})}=\lVert u-\tilde{u}_{R}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(K_{L})}
. Denote by
G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})
the Green’s function to (
4
) and let
G_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})
denote the Green’s function to (
1
). Then by the definition of the Green’s function we have

\begin{split}e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})=&\int_{K_{R}}G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})F(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}\\
=&\int_{K_{R}}(\rho(\boldsymbol{y})-1)G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})g(\boldsymbol{y})+\\
&-\int_{K_{R}}G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})a(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\nabla u(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}+\\
&-\int_{K_{R}}G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{y})u(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{y}))d\boldsymbol{y}.\end{split}

(31)

We note that the first term vanishes because
g
has support only on
K_{L}
and
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})-1=0
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{\frac{3}{4}R}\supset K_{L}
. We also note that because
\nabla\rho=0\text{ }\forall\boldsymbol{x}\notin E
, we can rewrite (
31
) as

\begin{split}e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})=&-\int_{E_{R}}G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})a(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\nabla u(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}+\\
&-\int_{E_{R}}G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{y})u(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{y}))d\boldsymbol{y}.\end{split}

(32)

Using
a(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\beta
,
\lvert\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq\frac{C}{R}
and Cauchy-Schwartz we get

\lvert e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq\frac{C\beta}{R}\left(\lVert G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x};\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{R})}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{R})}+\lVert\nabla G_{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{R})}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{R})}\right).

(33)

By restricting
\boldsymbol{x}
to
K_{L}
, and upon using Lemma
2
, and Corollary
2
, we obtain

\lvert e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq\frac{C}{R}\left(\lvert E_{R}\rvert\frac{1}{\left\lvert\frac{3}{8}R-\frac{L}{2}\right\rvert^{2d-3}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\frac{\lvert E_{R}\rvert}{R}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\frac{3}{8}R-\frac{L}{2}\right\rvert^{2d-4}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\right).

(34)

Moreover, since
\lvert E_{R}\rvert=\frac{4^{d}-3^{d}}{4^{d}}R^{d}
we get

\lvert e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq C\left(R^{d-1}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\frac{3}{8}R-\frac{L}{2}\right\rvert^{2d-3}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+R^{d-2}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\frac{3}{8}R-\frac{L}{2}\right\rvert^{2d-4}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\right).

(35)

Choosing
L\propto R
then finally gives

\lvert e_{R}(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq C\frac{1}{R^{d-2}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

(36)

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{L}
.
∎

Remark
.

Any choice of
L
that does not cause
L
to grow faster than
R
(which would not be allowed, since at some point the condition
L<\frac{1}{4}R
would be violated) leads to the same rate of convergence.

3
Improving the Decay by Regularization

As we saw in the previous section, a simple truncation of the infinite domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
K_{R}
, and choosing homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, results in a slow decay (of type
1/R^{d-2}
) for the
L^{2}
norm of the error. To improve over this slow decay, we will use another approach based on exponential regularization of elliptic PDEs, first proposed in
[
3
]
, whose analysis has been addressed in the context of periodic homogenization. In the present work, we relax the periodicity assumption on the coefficients, and still prove rates decaying much faster than
1/R^{d-2}
. In
[
3
]
, the authors begin by introducing the parabolic equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla\cdot\left(a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla v(t,\boldsymbol{x})\right)

\displaystyle=0,\hskip 9.24994pt(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(37)

\displaystyle v(0,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{x}).

In order to link this problem to (
1
) we further introduce
v_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{0}^{T}v(t,\boldsymbol{x})\;dt
. Notice then that if
v
is decaying in time then
v_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})=u(\boldsymbol{x})
where
u(\boldsymbol{x})
solves (
1
). Next we introduce
u_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{0}^{T}w(t,\boldsymbol{x})\;dt
, where
w
solves

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w(t,\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla\cdot\left(a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla w(t,\boldsymbol{x})\right)

\displaystyle=0,\hskip 9.24994pt(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T]\times K_{R},

(38)

\displaystyle w(0,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle w(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ on }(0,T]\times\partial K_{R}.

We observe that
u_{T}
satisfies

-\nabla\cdot\left(a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla u_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=g(\boldsymbol{x})-[e^{-T\mathcal{A}}g](\boldsymbol{x}),\hskip 9.24994pt\boldsymbol{x}\in K_{R},

(39)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the differential operator defined as
\mathcal{A}u=\nabla\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla u)
and
[e^{-T\mathcal{A}}g](\boldsymbol{x})
is the solution at time
T
of the equation (
38
). We refer to the extra term in the right hand side of (
39
) the exponential regularization term. We now want to show that (
39
) approximates (
1
) with a better accuracy than (
2
). Analogous to the analysis in the previous section, we define the error as the
L^{2}
norm of the difference
u_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})-u(\boldsymbol{x})
over the localized domain
K_{L}=[-L,L]^{d}
with
L<R
. Using
v_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})=u(\boldsymbol{x})
and the triangle inequality we can split the error as

\displaystyle\|u_{T}-u\|_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\leq\|u_{T}-v_{T}\|_{L^{2}(K_{L})}+\|v_{T}-v_{\infty}\|_{L^{2}(K_{L})}.

The first term
\lVert u_{T}-v_{T}\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}
we will call the boundary error because it originates from truncating the domain and choosing poor boundary conditions. In section
3.2
we will study this error term and show that for well chosen values of
T
this term decays exponentially. The second term
\lVert v_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})-v_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}
we call the modelling error because it appears due to the addition of the regularization term in (
39
). Notice how this term approaches
0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
, mirroring how (
39
) approaches (
2
) as
T\rightarrow\infty
. We will study this error term in Section
3.1
and compare the decay to the
d-2
-th order decay of the original elliptic problem.

3.1
Decay Rates of the Modelling Error

In this section we will study the second term
\|v_{T}-v_{\infty}\|_{L^{2}(K_{L})}
of the error bound. While the regularization term gives an improvement in the boundary error, the modification also creates a new error caused by the modification of the problem, which will be addressed here under two separate assumptions on
g
in the frequency domain.

Theorem 4
.

Let
v_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}v(t,\boldsymbol{x})\,dt
where
v
solves (
37
) with
a\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
a\in C^{k,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Furthermore, let
g(\boldsymbol{x})\in L^{1}(\Omega,1+\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert^{k+1})
and
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0
for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\leq k
. Then

\lVert v_{T}-v_{\infty}\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\leq CL^{d/2}T^{-(d+k+3)/2},

where
C
is a constant depending on
a
and
d
but independent of
L
and
T
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
v_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}v(t,\boldsymbol{x})\,dt
where
v
solves (
37
) with
a\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Furthermore, let
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0,\forall\boldsymbol{\omega}
such that
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}
. Then

\lVert v_{T}-v_{\infty}\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\leq Ce^{-2\alpha\omega_{0}^{2}T}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where
C=\frac{1}{2\alpha\omega_{0}^{2}}.

It is essential to compare these two theorems with respect to assumptions in the coefficient and the right hand side. 1) Theorems
4
and
5
provide bounds for the modelling error under different assumptions on the source term
g
; both meaning that
\hat{g}
has vanishing frequency components in the origin
\boldsymbol{\omega}=0
. The assumption in Theorem
4
is weaker, and results in a high order polynomial decay for the modelling error, while the second assumption is somewhat stronger and results in an exponential decay of the modelling error. 2) Theorem
4
necessitates a high regularity of the coefficient
a
, while Theorem
5
works under minimal assumptions on the coefficient, making it suitable for problems with rapidly varying coefficients.

Remark
.

To motivate the assumptions of vanishing frequency components in the origin, let
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a solution to (
1
) with constant
a
; say
a=1
. Then

-\lvert\boldsymbol{\omega}\rvert^{2}\hat{u}=\hat{g}.

(40)

Since
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
|\hat{u}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|\leq C,\forall\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This implies that
\hat{g}=\mathcal{O}(\lvert\boldsymbol{\omega}\rvert^{2}),\forall\boldsymbol{\omega}
, and that in particular
\hat{g}
vanishes in the origin.

In order to prove Theorems
4
and
5
we start by studying the decay rates of solutions of parabolic problems. We have

Theorem 6
.

Let
v
solve (
37
) with
a(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Furthermore, let
g(\boldsymbol{x})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},1+\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert^{k+1})
. Then

\displaystyle\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\lvert\Omega\rvert^{d/2}t^{-(d+k+1)/2}

where
C
is a constant depending on
a
and
d
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Using theorem
1
and
v=G*g
, the solution to (
37
) can be represented as follows

\displaystyle v(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\leq k}\dfrac{(-1)^{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|}}{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\;d\boldsymbol{x}D^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}G+\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|=k+1}F_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}*D^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}G.

The terms
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{x}^{\alpha}\;d\boldsymbol{x}
are connected to the derivatives of the Fourier transform of
g
at the origin
\boldsymbol{\omega}=0
, and will vanish if
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{g}(0)=0
, for
|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\leq k
. Now, if we can show that
D^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}G
decays in time in some appropriate norm, we will be able to prove a decay rate for the modelling error too. To achieve this, we use the fact that
a\in C^{k,r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of Hölder continuous functions with
k
continuous derivatives, and use Theorem
2
to see that

\displaystyle|v(t,\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|=k+1}\|F_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}|D^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}G|_{\infty}\leq Ct^{-(d+k+1)/2}.

From here we can establish the following interior estimate:

\displaystyle\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\lvert\Omega\rvert^{1/2}t^{-(d+k+1)/2}.

∎

Theorem 7
.

Let
v
solve (
37
) with
a(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Further let
\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0,\forall\boldsymbol{\omega}
such that
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq e^{-\alpha\omega_{0}^{2}t}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

To prove this theorem, we will need a variant of the Poincare inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is of the form

\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\lVert\nabla v(t,\cdot)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(41)

In a nutshell we will prove that indeed this inequality holds under the assumption
\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0
for all
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq w_{0}
. Assuming this inequality for the moment, we test (
37
) against
v
, which gives us

\langle v_{t},v\rangle=-\langle a\nabla v,\nabla v\rangle\leq-\alpha\langle\nabla v,\nabla v\rangle.

We then have

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq-\alpha\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq-\alpha\omega_{0}^{2}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

This implies that

\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq e^{-2\alpha\omega_{0}^{2}t}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

(42)

It remains to prove (
41
). We begin by showing
\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0,\;\forall|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}\rightarrow\hat{v}(t,\boldsymbol{\omega})=0,\;\forall t\in[0,T),|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}
. We have

\hat{v}(t,\boldsymbol{\omega})=\hat{G}(t,\boldsymbol{\omega})\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

Due to assumptions, we have that the right hand side is clearly
0
for any
t
and
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}
as long as
\hat{G}(t,\boldsymbol{\omega})
is bounded. To show that this is the case we will use the following Nash-Aronson estimates

\displaystyle G_{1}(t,\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y}):=ct^{-d/2}e^{-\beta\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}{4t}}\leq G(t,\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})\leq G_{2}(t,\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y}):=Ct^{-d/2}e^{-\alpha\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}{4t}}.

Whenever
t=0
we have that the Green’s function
G
is the Dirac delta. The Fourier transform is then constant and bounded. For
t\neq 0
we can use
\lVert\hat{G}(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\lVert G(t,\cdot;\boldsymbol{y})\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. By the Nash-Aronson estimate we have that the
L^{1}
-norm of the Green’s function is bounded and then so is the Fourier transform. We then have
\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=0\;\forall\boldsymbol{\omega}
such that
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}\rightarrow\hat{u}(t,\boldsymbol{\omega})=0\;\forall t\in[0,T)
. We now continue with

\lVert\nabla v(t,\cdot)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\int_{|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\geq\omega_{0}}\lvert\boldsymbol{\omega}\rvert^{2}\lvert\hat{v}\rvert^{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\omega}\geq\omega_{0}^{2}\int_{|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\geq\omega_{0}}\lvert\hat{v}\rvert^{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\omega}.

Here we used Plancherel’s theorem, Fourier transforms of gradients, as well as
\hat{v}(t,\boldsymbol{\omega})=0
for
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|\leq\omega_{0}
. Using Plancherel’s theorem again then gives

\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\lVert\nabla v(t,\cdot)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

which ends the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

The modelling error can be re-written as

\displaystyle\lVert v_{T}-v_{\infty}\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}=\left\lVert\int_{T}^{\infty}v(t,\cdot)\;dt\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\leq\int_{T}^{\infty}\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\;dt.

where we applied Minkowskii’s inequality. Since
k>1-d
, the integral is bounded and can be rewritten as

\int_{T}^{\infty}\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\;dt\leq\int_{T}^{\infty}CL^{d/2}t^{-(d+k+1)/2}\;dt=CL^{d/2}T^{-(d+k+3)/2},

where we used Theorem
6
in the last step.
∎

Theorem
5
is proved in the same way by using Theorem
7
instead of Theorem
6
on the last line. While Theorems
4
and
5
suggest that the optimal choice for
T
is
T=\infty
, this would be equivalent to the naive method of simply truncating the domain. We however know that this results in a boundary error of order
d-2
, which in most cases is worse than the modelling error caused by the regularization. As we will see in Section
3.2
, a better choice for
T
is
T\propto\lvert R-L\rvert
.

3.2
Decay of the Boundary Error

In this section we study the first term
\lVert u_{T}-v_{T}\rVert
of the error bound which is determined by the difference between the solutions of (

) and (

).

(Boundary layer)

(Cut-off function)

0\text{ on }\partial K_{R}\end{cases}\text{ and }\lvert\nabla\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rvert\leq C_{\rho}\text{ on }\Delta,

(43)

where

and

.

Definition 3
.

Define the function

where

and

solve (
38
) and (

\theta

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta(t,\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla\cdot(a(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\theta(t,\boldsymbol{x})))=F(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial K_{R}\times(0,+\infty),

\displaystyle\text{in }K_{R},

The Green’s function

\boldsymbol{y}
.

(Theorem 9 in
[

]
)
.

Assume that

and

(47)

for all

).

Proposition 2

(See
[

,

]
)
.

Let

With all the necessary definitions we may now state the key theorem for this section.

Theorem 8
.

Let

, and

. Moreover, let
u_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{0}^{T}w(t,\boldsymbol{x})dt

, where

and

) and (

. Then

\begin{split}\lVert u_{T}-v_{T}\rVert_{L^{2}(K_{L})}\leq&\lvert K_{L}\rvert^{1/2}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\frac{T}{\lvert R-L\rvert}+C_{3}\frac{T^{3}}{\lvert R-L\rvert}\right)\\
&\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}\lvert R-L\rvert}T^{\frac{2-d}{2}}e^{-c\frac{\lvert R-L\rvert^{2}}{T}},\end{split}

(49)

where

,

,

, and

The proof of Theorem

Lemma 4
.

Let
G(t,\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})
be the Green’s function of (

. Let

and
\Delta_{2}=K_{R}\setminus K_{2\tilde{R}-R}
. Then, for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in K_{R}
such that

, there exists a positive constant
