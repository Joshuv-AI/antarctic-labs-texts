Effect of metallic sealant thickness on leak arrest and composite repair performance in pipeline systems
Authors: Meshari Mohammed Alharthi, Ibrahim A. Alnaser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the combined effect of metallic sealant thickness and composite laminate configuration on the structural performance of pipeline repair systems. A total of 63 experiments were conducted on through-wall defects with diameters of 10.2 mm, 15.2 mm, and 25.4 mm diameters, three metallic sealant thicknesses (3.5 mm, 7 mm, and 10.5 mm) were evaluating under both dry and wet conditions. Additionally, two- and four-layer fiberglass composite overwraps were applied to assess their influence on pressure retention and energy release rate. Results showed that increasing sealant thickness significantly enhanced failure leak pressure, particularly in wet environments, where thinner sealants underperformed. The 10.5 mm thickness consistently delivered the best sealing capability across all conditions. Composite repairs improved performance further, with four-layer systems providing the highest-pressure resistance. However, only a marginal increase in interfacial energy release rate was observed, suggesting diminishing returns beyond a certain layer count. Experimental leak pressures exceeded theoretical predictions from ISO/TS 24,817, indicating the need to update current models. This research provides practical guidance for selecting optimal sealant thicknesses and laminate configurations based on defect size and environmental conditions, offering a more reliable, in-service repair solution for pipelines in the oil and gas industry.

Introduction

Pipelines serve as the primary mode of transportation for oil and gas, forming an extensive global network that is essential for energy supply and industrial development. Designed for high efficiency and long-distance delivery, pipelines are subject to significant mechanical stresses, corrosive environments, and aging-related degradation1,2. More than 60% of the global oil and gas pipeline infrastructure is over 40 years old, increasing the risk of failure and necessitating reliable, cost-effective repair strategies3.

Corrosion is the most common failure mechanism in metallic pipelines, often leading to through-wall defects, pinhole leaks, and full ruptures4–7. The U.S. Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA) identifies corrosion as a leading cause of pipeline failure, contributing to environmental damage, operational losses, and public safety risks8. The 2014 Kaohsiung pipeline explosion in Taiwan, which killed 27 people and injured over 280, underscores the severe consequences of corrosion-related failures (Taiwan Gas Blasts Likely Caused by Faulty Pipe - WSJ, n.d.). Another example occurred in 2015 when external corrosion caused a rupture that spilled approximately 500 barrels of oil into the Pacific Ocean, leading to clean-up costs exceeding $140 million9.

Recent studies have examined the performance and durability of FRP composites in harsh environments. For instance, Bazli M. et al.10 demonstrated the sensitivity of composite pipelines to combined thermal and pressure cycling, while Harle11 reviewed the long-term durability of FRP systems exposed to moisture, elevated temperatures, and chemical attack. In parallel to this, hybrid sealing and bonding systems have been investigated for structural applications. Hu et al.12 provided a critical review of adhesive bonding performance in aggressive environments, emphasizing the importance of sealant composition and bond-line design.

With respect to fracture mechanics and interfacial characterization, Savari et al.13 applied fracture mechanics concepts to composite-bonded metallic pipelines, showing that interface toughness plays a decisive role in repair effectiveness. Similarly, Rodriguez et al.14 analyzed polymer matrix composites, highlighting the complex relationship between interfacial energy release rate and laminate thickness.

Direct investigations of pipeline repair under active leakage conditions remain scarce. Mazurkiewicz et al.15 evaluated composite materials for live leakage scenarios and highlighted their limitations on wet surfaces, while Saeed et al.3 experimentally validated composite repair systems under pressurized leakage, noting reduced performance in uncontrolled conditions.

Traditional repair methods, such as welded sleeves, bolted clamps, or full-section replacements require shutdowns, pose risks in flammable environments, and are often impractical in offshore settings due to spark and heat restrictions16. These limitations have prompted the oil and gas industry to adopt alternative solutions—most notably fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) composite repair systems17–19.

FRP composites, typically comprising carbon or glass fibres embedded in polymer matrices, offer high strength-to-weight ratios, corrosion resistance, and fatigue durability20,21. Their most valuable feature is the ability to perform repairs on live pipelines without requiring shutdowns or hot work, making them particularly attractive in offshore and remote installations22. Industry standards such as ISO/TS 24,817 and ASME PCC-2 have recognized composite repair systems as validated techniques for reinforcing pressurized pipes with defects23–25.

However, composite repairs are most effective on clean, dry surfaces and perform poorly under active leakage conditions. Leaks interfere with adhesive bonding, reduce composite consolidation, and introduce voids that compromise mechanical performance26. This limitation restricts the use of composites in emergency repairs or high-moisture environments.

To address this critical gap, the current study explores a hybrid repair approach that employs metallic sealants of varying thicknesses (3.5 mm, 7 mm, and 10.5 mm) to first suppress active leaks, followed by the application of composite overwraps using two- and four-layer fiberglass laminates. This approach aims to evaluate the mechanical effectiveness of the sealant-composite combination under various defect sizes (10.2 mm, 15.2 mm, and 25.4 mm) and environmental conditions (dry and wet). By experimentally determining the failure leak pressure and comparing results to theoretical predictions based on fracture mechanics, the study assesses the validity of current predictive models and the role of sealant thickness in enhancing repair integrity27.

Furthermore, this study investigates the energy release rate associated with composite delamination to understand the fracture resistance provided by different laminate configurations. Previous studies suggest a non-linear relationship between laminate thickness and crack propagation resistance, particularly in wet environments28,29.

By integrating experimental insights with theoretical models, this research contributes to the development of safer and more adaptable pipeline repair strategies. The findings offer practical guidance for selecting optimal sealant thicknesses and composite configurations tailored to defect severity and environmental constraints. This hybrid method not only enhances pipeline reliability but also reduces downtime and improves safety—key priorities in the modern oil and gas industry.

Materials and methods

Design basis and repair standards

This study follows the design methodologies outlined in ASME PCC-2 and ISO/TS 24,817 for the composite repair of through-wall pipeline defects. For such defects, a fracture mechanics approach is employed, using the energy release rate (Gc) to characterize the interface strength between the composite laminate and the steel substrate under internal pressure loading. The metallic sealant used in this study was a commercially available epoxy adhesive reinforced with steel powder, commonly employed for structural repair in pressurized systems. According to the supplier’s datasheet, its typical properties, presented in Table 1, include a tensile strength of ~ 25 MPa, compressive strength of ~ 90 MPa, flexural strength of ~ 70 MPa, and hardness of 85 Shore D. The service temperature range is − 40 °C to + 120 °C, making it suitable for oil and gas pipeline environments. The sealant was applied in controlled thicknesses of 3.5 mm, 7 mm, and 10.5 mm to investigate its gap-filling and leak-arresting performance.

A blister-type fracture model describes the repair failure mechanism, with the composite layer behaving as an isotropic shell deforming under pressure. Failure occurs through interfacial delamination. The model links internal pressure, repair geometry, and material properties to the interface fracture energy.

Rationale for chosen spiral angles (7–20°)

The composite repair scheme uses two complementary configurations: (i) concentric (weld) wraps that provide near-circumferential reinforcement, and (ii) spiral/offset wraps applied at shallow helical angles (7–20° relative to the pipe axis). The concentric layers principally resist hoop stresses induced by internal pressure, while the shallow-angled spiral layers serve three functions: (a) they increase axial stiffness to resist longitudinal loads and bending, (b) they introduce an in-plane shear component that help redistribute stresses at the defect edge and reduce peak interfacial stresses (thereby delaying delamination), and (c) they improve practical manufacturability by reducing fibre wrinkling and easing hand lay-up under field-like conditions.

The chosen 7–20° range thus represents a deliberate compromise between structural performance and application practicality: concentric layers handle the primary hoop load, and the small-angle spirals provide axial/shear support and smoother load transfer across the interface. This combined architecture enhances overall repair robustness compared to single-orientation laminates while remaining suitable for on-site wet/dry applications.

Experimental overview

The experimental program was divided into three phases:

Phase I – failure pressure testing of metallic sealants

Metallic sealants with three thicknesses (3.5 mm, 7 mm, and 10.5 mm) were tested on steel pipes with through-wall defects (Ø10.2 mm, Ø15.2 mm, Ø25.4 mm). Each pipe was 2 m in length with blind flanges welded to both ends. Tests were conducted in both dry and wet conditions to assess sealing effectiveness. Failure pressure was recorded upon leak initiation or sealant detachment. Unlike typical thin-bond adhesive joints, the sealant in this study functions primarily as a gap-filling leak-arresting medium, not as a pure structural adhesive layer. The thickness was selected to ensure sufficient material volume to resist hydrostatic pressure in large through-wall defects and to maintain sealing capability under wet conditions where thin layers were observed to fail.

The sealant was allowed to cure for 24 h at ambient laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 40–50% relative humidity) before further testing or composite application. To maintain uniform thickness and prevent leakage during curing, a split mechanical clamp with a rubber backing was used to hold the sealant in place, as shown in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Application of metallic sealant before curing.

Application of metallic sealant before curing.

Prior to sealant or composite application, the steel pipe surfaces were mechanically abraded using 60-grit sandpaper to remove paint, oxide layers, and corrosion products, followed by cleaning with acetone to eliminate oils and debris. In dry condition tests, the sealant was applied directly to the prepared surface. In wet-condition tests, however, the sealant was applied under live leakage using a mechanical clamp equipped with a rubber backing to temporarily retain the material until curing. In these cases, the sealing mechanism depended less on surface adhesion and more on the sealant’s bulk pressure resistance and mechanical interlock with the pipe wall.

Phase II – measurement of energy release rate

This phase focused on determining the fracture toughness (Gc) of the interface between the composite overwrap and the steel pipe. Tests followed the pressure-to-failure method outlined in ASME PCC-2 (Repair of Pressure Equipment and Piping: An American National Standard, 2022) and ISO 24,81730,31. Composite laminates with with two and four fiberglass layers and two configurations, as illustrated in Fig. 2, were applied over drilled defects and pressurized until delamination. Each defect size was tested three times to ensure statistical relevance. The energy release rate was calculated from pressure data using fracture mechanics equations.

Phase III – composite repair system evaluation

The third phase examined the performance of composite repairs with and without metallic sealants. Pipes were wrapped using the two configurations describes above and tested under pressure. Performance was assessed by comparing experimental failure pressures with values predicted from measured Gc. The aim was to validate the sealing capability of metallic fillers and overall reinforcement integrity.

Experimental setup and procedure

The test rig comprised steel pipe specimens, pressure gauges, blind flanges, hydraulic pump, check and gate valves, and mounting stands as shown in Figs. 3 and 4. The internal pressure was gradually increased using hydrostatic water loading. Leaks and delamination failures were monitored to determine specimen performance. The pipe specimens were fabricated from ASTM A-106 Grade B carbon steel with an outer diameter of 219 mm, wall thickness of 10 mm, and length of 2 m. The material has a typical yield strength of approximately 240 MPa and tensile strength of 415 MPa, making it representative of common pipeline steels used in oil and gas transmission.

The composite overwraps were applied using a hand lay-up wet lay-up process. Glass fiber fabric was impregnated with an epoxy resin system (bisphenol-A epoxy with amine hardener, mixed in a 2:1 ratio by weight) and applied around the pipe either in a concentric “weld wrap” configuration or in a spiral offset pattern. The resin had a tensile strength of ~ 65 MPa, compressive strength of ~ 100 MPa, and flexural modulus of ~ 3 GPa, ensuring adequate stiffness and bonding capability. Wrapping was carried out under controlled tension to minimize voids, and each layer was allowed to partially cure before the next was applied to enhance interlaminar adhesion.

Specimens were visually inspected, flushed, and fitted with rubber gaskets before testing. Metallic sealants were applied using mechanical clamps and cured in situ. For wet tests, sealant application was done under live pressure. Pressure was gradually increased until failure was observed, with all critical values were recorded. For each test configuration, a minimum of three valid results were obtained. Mean values, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Student’s t-distribution, ensuring statistical robustness of the reported data.

Failure leak pressures were measured and compared across varying sealant thicknesses, defect sizes, and composite layer configurations.

The results under dry conditions demonstrate a clear, positive correlation between metallic sealant thickness and failure leak pressure. The highest performance was observed at 10.5 mm sealant thickness, indicating enhanced load distribution and void filling capability. This supports findings by37 and complements recent research by Budhe et al., which highlighted the significance of repair material volume in pressure resistance38. Interestingly, even the 3.5 mm sealant performed adequately for small defects, suggesting that thin sealants may still offer cost-effective options for minor repairs, provided they are installed in controlled environments.

The comparative analysis of sealant thickness also provides a clear explanation for the necessity of the thicker 10.5 mm application. As illustrated in Fig. 5, while the 3.5 mm sealant layer was able to withstand leakage in smaller defects, it consistently failed in larger defects (particularly 25.4 mm) because of insufficient material volume to fully resist hydrostatic loading and to fill voids at the defect edge. The 7 mm thickness showed intermediate performance but still demonstrated premature leakage initiation in certain wet-condition tests. In contrast, the 10.5 mm thickness provided sufficient depth to distribute pressure, resist crack initiation, and maintain sealing even in severe defect geometries. These observations confirm that, unlike traditional thin-bond adhesive layers, the sealant in this study operates as a gap-filling, pressure-resisting medium, and its increased thickness is justified by its functional role in leak arrest rather than simple adhesive bonding.

For wet-condition tests, the pipes were first filled with water and pressurized to ~ 0.2–0.3 MPa to simulate active leakage during sealant application. The sealant was applied under these live-leak conditions while a residual water film (~ 0.5–1.0 mm) remained on the pipe surface, representing the unavoidable moisture present during emergency field repairs. Curing was conducted under these conditions without surface drying.

In wet conditions, all sealant thicknesses showed a performance drop due to poor surface bonding, early delamination, and fluid interaction during curing. These issues are well documented in submerged composite repair studies22,39. The inability of the 3.5 mm sealant to arrest leaks in large defects under wet conditions further emphasizes the importance of adequate volume and sealing pressure. The results validate the need for a minimum threshold thickness, particularly for field repairs in offshore or humid environments, where pressure resistance is critical to avoid secondary failures.

Pressure vs. thickness in wet condition.

The observed failure mechanisms help explain the differences between sealant thicknesses. As shown in Fig. 6, for thinner sealants (3.5 mm), leakage often initiated through micro-voids formed during curing, which coalesced into preferential leakage paths under hydrostatic pressure. In wet-condition tests, incomplete displacement of water at the defect interface further reduced bonding efficiency, leading to premature leakage. Intermediate sealant thicknesses (7 mm) offered partial improvement but were still prone to early delamination at the pipe–sealant interface due to insufficient bulk material to resist crack initiation. By contrast, the 10.5 mm thickness provided enough volume to distribute stresses, delay crack propagation, and sustain sealing pressure even under adverse wet conditions. These findings confirm that the sealing mechanism depends not only on adhesion but also on the volumetric capacity of the sealant to resist pressure-driven void growth.

The relationship between sealant thickness and failure leak pressure exhibited a consistent, nearly linear trend across all defect sizes, with diminishing returns at higher thicknesses. Although regression fitting was not performed due to the limited number of experimental points, the trend clearly indicates that increasing sealant thickness significantly enhances pressure resistance. These data can serve as a basis for future empirical model development once a larger dataset is available.

Comparative analysis: wet vs. dry conditions

Failure leak pressures were consistently lower in wet conditions across all thicknesses and defect sizes. The performance gap widened with larger defects. However, the 10.5 mm sealant showed relatively stable behaviour, suggesting it as a viable candidate for both conditions.

Wet vs. dry performance comparison.

The performance differential between wet and dry conditions emphasizes environmental sensitivity in metallic sealant behavior. As illustrated in Fig. 7, while all sealants showed reduced pressures in wet settings, the 10.5 mm thickness maintained relatively high performance, suggesting that sealant volume can partially compensate for bonding challenges. This implies a potential design guideline: thicker sealants may provide redundancy in adverse conditions. These insights are vital for establishing field-applicable repair protocols and reinforce the need for improved surface preparation techniques when dry application is not feasible.

A closer examination of failure modes revealed that thinner sealant layers (3.5 mm) frequently failed through the formation of micro-voids at the defect interface, which rapidly propagated into leakage channels under pressure. In wet conditions, this effect was intensified by the inability of the thin layer to displace fluid during curing, resulting in incomplete sealing. For intermediate thicknesses (7 mm), partial leakage resistance was achieved, but delamination from the pipe wall was often observed at pressures lower than expected. The 10.5 mm sealant, by contrast, provided sufficient material to resist void coalescence and exhibited delayed crack initiation. These observations indicate that failure mechanisms in metallic sealants are not governed purely by adhesion but also by volumetric capacity to withstand hydrostatic pressure and prevent channel formation.

All reported values represent the mean of at least three independent tests and are presented with standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals. The observed improvements with the 10.5 mm sealant exceeded the variability margins, confirming that the differences are both statistically robust and practically meaningful.

This finding highlights the complexity of scaling composite repairs and indicates that improvements in laminate architecture may be more effective than simply increasing layer count.

Additionally, the improved performance of multi-layer repairs is not solely a function of added thickness: laminate architecture matters. Our combined concentric + shallow-angled spiral approach (7–20°) produces a multi-axial response where concentric layers resist hoop tension and the spiral layers carry axial/shear loads and smooth stress concentrations at the defect boundary. This synergy helps explain why additional spiral/concentric layers improve failure pressure more than would be predicted by thickness alone.

We note that this study did not include cyclic loading, temperature fluctuation, or long-term environmental degradation tests, which are critical for evaluating real-world repair performance. Future work should incorporate fatigue testing42, environmental degradation assessments43, and predictive long-term modeling44,45 to more comprehensively assess the durability of pipeline repairs under operational conditions.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the impact of metallic sealant thickness and composite laminate configurations on the performance of pipeline repair systems under both dry and wet conditions. Sixty-three experiments were conducted to assess failure leak pressures across defect sizes of 10.2 mm, 15.2 mm, and 25.4 mm, using sealant thicknesses of 3.5 mm, 7 mm, and 10.5 mm in combination with two- and four-layer fiberglass composite wraps. Results showed a strong inverse correlation between defect size and failure leak pressure. Thicker metallic sealants consistently outperformed thinner ones, with the 10.5 mm thickness providing the highest resistance under all test conditions. Although wet environments reduced sealant effectiveness—particularly for larger defects—the 10.5 mm sealant maintained substantial performance, demonstrating robustness under adverse conditions.

Integration of metallic sealants with composite laminates significantly enhanced repair efficacy. Four-layer wraps offered superior pressure resistance, especially when paired with thicker sealants, though the marginal increase in energy release rate between two- and four-layer configurations indicates a non-linear relationship between laminate thickness and interfacial fracture toughness. Comparison with ISO/TS 24,817 theoretical predictions revealed that experimental failure pressures consistently exceeded theoretical values—by up to 44%—highlighting the conservative nature of existing models and the need for refinement.

Overall, these findings provide practical guidance for optimizing repair strategies based on defect size, environmental conditions, and material configuration, contributing a scalable methodology for live pipeline repairs in offshore or moisture-prone settings. Future work should address long-term durability, cyclic pressure effects, and hybrid sealing materials to further enhance repair reliability and lifecycle performance.
