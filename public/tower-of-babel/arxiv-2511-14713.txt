nlKrylov : A Unified Framework for Nonlinear GCR-type Krylov Subspace Methods Thanks:
Tom Werner, Ning Wan, Agnieszka Międlar

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a unified framework for nonlinear Krylov subspace methods (nlKrylov) to solve systems of nonlinear equations. Building on classical GCR-like/type linear Krylov solvers such as GMRESR, we generalize these approaches to nonlinear problems via nested algorithmic structures. We present rigorous convergence results for problems, relying on relaxed assumptions that avoid the need for exact line searches. The framework is further extended to matrix-valued rootfinding problems using global nonlinear Krylov approaches. Extensive numerical experiments validate the theoretical insights and demonstrate the robustness and efficiency of our proposed algorithms.



keywords

nonlinear Krylov, quasi-Newton, nonlinear acceleration, Anderson Acceleration

Introduction

A classical problem in numerical analysis, with increasing popularity in recent data science and machine learning applications, is the root finding problem

\text{Find }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \text{ that satisfies }\ f(x)=0,

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuously differentiable and possibly nonlinear. Nonlinear equations like (
1
) can appear in a variety of problems such as the numerical solution of nonlinear ordinary differential equations

\dot{x}(t)=F(t,x),\ F:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},

using implicit integrators, the numerical solution of discretized nonlinear partial differential equations

\Delta u(x)=F(x,u),\ u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ F:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

or when solving unconstrained optimization problems

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi(x),\ \phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ \text{ twice continuously differentiable,}

(2)

using the optimality condition
\nabla\phi(x)=:f(x)=0
.
Since (
1
) frequently arises in practical applications, numerous methods have been developed and refined since the mid-20th century, most of which are based on fixed-point iteration

x_{j+1}=g(x_{j}),\ g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3)

Clearly, if we choose
g(x):=x+\beta f(x)
, for some
\beta\neq 0
, then

x=g(x)~\Longleftrightarrow~\beta f(x)=0~\Longleftrightarrow~f(x)=0,

i.e., any fixed point of
g
is a solution to the root finding problem (
1
) and vice versa. However, convergence of the fixed-point scheme (
3
) is generally not guaranteed and can be very slow in many applications. To improve or ensure convergence of (
3
), considerable effort has focused on acceleration schemes such as Anderson Acceleration (AA)
[
4
,
5
]
and the related Pulay mixing method
[
70
,
71
]
,
owing to their simplicity and effectiveness across a wide range of applications. Both methods rely on effectively sampling the next iterate from two sets of vectors, namely, the differences of consecutive function values
\Delta f_{j}:=f(x_{j+1})-f(x_{j})
, and of consecutive iterates
\Delta x_{j}:=x_{j+1}-x_{j}
. Lately, the use of Krylov subspace techniques for nonlinear acceleration has been discovered and explored in the form of nonlinear versions of GMRES
[
25
,
67
,
93
]
and GCR
[
50
]
. In
[
91
]
, the equivalence of AA, GMRES and GCR on linear problems has been established. The same study also formalizes the connection between Anderson Acceleration (AA) and multi-secant methods, a relation first identified in
[
39
]
and subsequently extended in
[
40
]
.
A second family of fixed-point schemes (
3
) for (
1
) is the family of Newton-type methods, which can be characterized by

x_{j+1}=x_{j}+\Delta x_{j},\ \ \text{where}\ \ B_{j}\Delta x_{j}=-f(x_{j})+t_{j}.

The classical Newton method
[
60
]
uses
B_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})
and
t_{j}=0
, resulting in the fixed-point scheme (
3
) with

g(x)=x-J_{f}(x)^{-1}f(x).

In practice, the update equation is usually solved approximately, introducing an error term
t_{j}\neq 0
. This inexactness leads to the class of inexact Newton methods
[
32
]
. Among those, Newton-Krylov methods have attracted significant attention across various research fields owing to their efficiency and scalability for large-scale and parallel computations
[
60
]
. When the exact Jacobian
J_{f}(x_{j})
is unavailable or too expensive to compute, an approximation
B_{j}\approx J_{f}(x_{j})
is employed and iteratively updated using information from previous steps, giving rise to quasi-Newton methods
[
34
]
.

In

Background

2.1
Krylov subspace methods for linear equations

In numerous applications, Krylov subspace methods have established themselves as a powerful and widely used class of iterative techniques for solving linear systems of the form

Ax=b,

(4)

involving large and sparse matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and the right-hand side
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. These methods construct a sequence of approximate solutions

x_{j+1}\in x_{0}+K_{j+1}(A,r_{0}),

where
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the residual corresponding to the initial guess
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

K_{j}(A,r_{0}):=\mathrm{span}\{r_{0},Ar_{0},A^{2}r_{0},\dots,A^{j-1}r_{0}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},

(5)

is the Krylov subspace of order (size)
j>0
. It is well-known
[
78
, §6.2]
that for a nonsingular
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the Krylov subspace
K_{n}(A,r_{0})
of size
n
contains an exact solution to (
4
). In practice, however, one typically seeks an approximate solution
x_{j}
with
j\ll n

that satisfies a user-specified accuracy.
Among the wide range of Krylov subspace methods,
this work focuses specifically on the minimal residual
[
63
, §2.5.5]
and conjugate residual methods
[
37
]
. Minimal residual methods seek a solution
x_{j+1}\in x_{0}+K_{j+1}(A,r_{0})
that minimizes the residual norm
\|r_{j+1}\|=\|b-Ax_{j+1}\|
over the Krylov subspace, i.e.,

x_{j+1}=x_{0}+p_{j},\quad p_{j}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{p\in K_{j+1}(A,r_{0})}\|b-A(x_{0}+p)\|_{2}.

The correction
p_{j}
is typically determined via the associated projected problem

x_{j+1}=x_{0}+V_{j}y_{j},\quad y_{j}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{j+1}}\|b-A(x_{0}+V_{j}y)\|_{2},

where
V_{j}=[v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{j}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times j+1}
is an orthonormal basis for
K_{j+1}(A,r_{0})

[
78
]
. Prominent examples of this class of methods include the MINimum RESidual method (MINRES,
[
68
]
) for symmetric matrices
A=A^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and the Generalized Minimum RESidual method (GMRES,
[
76
]
) for general matrices. A second class of Krylov subspace methods comprises variational iterative approaches derived from the Conjugate Gradient (CG) algorithm
[
52
]
, including the (Generalized) Conjugate Residual ((G)CR) method
[
37
,
84
]
and Orthomin(
k
)
[
89
]
. The two mentioned classes of methods fall within the general framework of (Truncated) Petrov Galerkin Krylov ((T)PGK) methods, where well-known equivalences hold in exact arithmetic, such as the equivalence of GMRES and GCR, and of Orthomin with truncated GCR
[
75
]
. Moreover, GMRES has been shown
[
90
]
to be closely related to the rank-one acceleration method of Eirola-Nevanlinna
[
36
]
, and together with GCR, both can be interpreted as Broyden-type methods when applied to linear problems
[
65
]
. See
Section 2.2
for a brief introduction to Broyden-type methods. Preconditioning plays a crucial role in enhancing the efficiency of Krylov subspace methods. While constant preconditioners based on incomplete factorizations are commonly used
[
78
, §10]
, variable preconditioners offer an adaptive and flexible alternative that can be adjusted dynamically during the iteration.
For instance, a few iterations of an appropriate solver can serve as a preconditioner
[
78
, §10.2]
, or one may employ an iteratively refined matrix
[
65
]
.
This concept led to the development of Flexible GMRES
[
77
]
. Around the same time, the recursive GMRES algorithm (GMRESR)
[
87
]
was introduced as a nested variant of GCR using a Krylov subspace method as a variable preconditioner. Subsequent extensions incorporated inner orthogonalization (GCRO)
[
26
]
and optimal truncation (GCROT)
[
27
]
. Related nested approaches, such as Loose GMRES (LGMRES)
[
12
]
and flexible versions of GCRO and LGMRES
[
53
]
, were later proposed to further enhance convergence.
The key algorithms are summarized in
Section

3
before extending them to the nonlinear case. With modern applications often involving the solution of linear systems with multiple right-hand sides, i.e.,

Ax_{i}=b_{i},~i=1,\dots,p\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad AX=B,~X=[x_{1},\dots,x_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p},~B=[b_{1},\dots,x_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p},

block variants of Krylov subspace methods have been developed to find solutions in the block-subspace

\mathcal{K}^{\square}_{j}(A,R_{0})=\mathrm{blockspan}\{R_{0},AR_{0},A^{2}R_{0},\dots,A^{j-1}R_{0}\},

which can be constructed using a block-Arnoldi or -Lanczos algorithm
[
74
,
78
,
79
]
. For more general linear operator equations

\mathcal{A}(X)=B,\quad X,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p},\quad\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\quad\text{linear},

global Krylov subspace methods were introduced in
[
57
]
, initially for large sparse Sylvester equations. Over time, global variants of GCRO, Flexible GMRES, the Quasi-Minimal Residual method (QMR), the Least SQuaRes method (LSQR), and related algorithms have been developed
[
66
,
86
,
92
,
95
]
.
Their effectiveness has recently been demonstrated in settings where only operator evaluations are available, such as in Newton-Krylov frameworks
[
94
]
.

2.2
Inexact Newton, Quasi-Newton and Newton-Krylov Methods

A standard approach for solving (
1
) is Newton’s method, i.e., given
x_{0}

x_{j+1}=x_{j}+\Delta x_{j},\ \mbox{ where }\ J_{f}(x_{j})\Delta x_{j}=-f(x_{j}).

(6)

While Newton’s method converges rapidly for a good initial guess, computing the Newton step
\Delta x_{j}
can be costly or infeasible. To improve efficiency, the Jacobian
J_{f}(x_{j})
(or its inverse
J_{f}^{-1}(x_{j})
) is often approximated using updates or estimates from previous iterates, leading to quasi-Newton methods
[
34
]
.
Broyden’s method
[
15
]
is a widely used quasi-Newton scheme that replaces the Jacobian
J_{f}(x_{j})
with an approximation
B_{j}
. The iterate is updated via

x_{j+1}=x_{j}+\Delta x_{j},\ \mbox{ where }\ B_{j}\Delta x_{j}=-f(x_{j}),

(7)

after which the Jacobian approximation
B_{j}
is updated using

B_{j+1}=B_{j}+\frac{(\Delta f_{j}-B_{j}\Delta x_{j})\Delta x_{j}^{T}}{\Delta x_{j}^{T}\Delta x_{j}},

(8)

which corresponds to the solution of
\min\|B-B_{j}\|_{F}
satisfying
B\Delta x_{j}=\Delta f_{j}

[
34
]
. Other quasi-Newton methods, such as Powell Symmetric Broyden method (PSB)
[
69
]
, Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) method
[
16
,
42
,
45
,
81
]
, and Davidon-Fletcher-Powell (DFP) method
[
24
]
,
use updates similar to Broyden’s, minimizing a Frobenius-norm change under specific conditions
[
34
]
, and are locally q-superlinearly convergent under standard assumptions
[
33
]
. Multisecant methods further generalize quasi-Newton updates by using several recent iterates and function evaluations rather than just the last one. Multisecant extensions of Broyden, BFGS, and DFP are discussed in
[
44
,
80
]
, and the Broyden-like class was introduced in
[
40
]
. These approaches avoid explicit Jacobian computation and support limited-memory implementations.
However, since these methods do not ensure that the approximate Newton step is a descent direction, they are often replaced by inexact Newton methods
[
32
]
, leading to the update

x_{j+1}=x_{j}+\Delta x_{j},\ \mbox{ where }\ J_{f}(x_{j})\Delta x_{j}=-f(x_{j})+t_{j},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\frac{\|t_{j}\|}{\|f(x_{j})\|}\leq\eta_{j}.

(9)

Here,
\{\eta_{j}\}_{j}
is a sequence of nonnegative scalars controlling the relative accuracy of the inner solve, and
t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the resulting inexactness. The approximate solution is typically obtained using an iterative solver, with
\eta_{j}
serving as relative tolerance.
Among the various iterative methods for linear systems, Krylov subspace solvers have proven to be particularly effective in inner iterations, as they enable matrix-free solution of the update equation without explicitly forming
J_{f}(x_{j})
. This capability underlies the broad class of (Jacobian-free) Newton-Krylov methods
[
62
]
.

2.3
The Nonlinear Truncated Generalized Conjugate Residual (nlTGCR)

In
[
50
]
, the Nonlinear Truncated GCR (nlTGCR) method was introduced, extending the linear GCR method
[
37
]
to systems of nonlinear equations. This approach closely relates to Anderson Acceleration and inexact Newton methods, offering a unified view of nonlinear iterative acceleration techniques.
Recall that GCR solves the linear system (
4
) using the Krylov subspace (
5
), where a basis
P_{j}=[p_{0},~p_{1},\dots,~p_{j}]
of
K_{j+1}(A,r_{0})
is constructed to satisfy
P_{j}^{T}A^{T}AP_{j}=I_{j+1}
, i.e.,
P_{j}
is
A^{T}A
-orthogonal, or equivalently,
V_{j}=AP_{j}
is orthogonal. Orthogonality of
V_{j}
is maintained using a modified Gram-Schmidt step after constructing a new direction
v_{j+1}
.
A truncated variant, GCR(
k
) with truncation window
k\geq 1
, was proposed in
[
37
]
(see
Algorithm

1
) to limit orthogonalization costs, where
k=\infty
recovers the full GCR. Here, only the
k
most recent directions
v_{j-k+1},v_{j-k+2},\dots,v_{j}
and
p_{j-k+1},p_{j-k+2},\dots,p_{j}
are stored and used for orthogonalization. Throughout this paper, we adopt the notation from
[
50
]
, defining
j_{k}:=\max\{j-k+1,0\}
and

P_{j}:=\left[p_{j_{k}},p_{j_{k}+1},\dots,p_{j}\right],\quad V_{j}:=\left[v_{j_{k}},v_{j_{k}+1},\dots,v_{j}\right],

so that
P_{j},V_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,k}
when
j\geq k
(truncated), and
P_{j},V_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,j+1}
otherwise. Hence, we will drop the "T" in nlTGCR(
k
) since all methods use truncation, and define
n_{j}:=j-j_{k}+1
as the number of columns in
P_{j}
(resp.
V_{j}
).

While extending the GCR method to nonlinear systems, the authors of
[
50
]
specified four key features that the generalized algorithm should exhibit.

F1

When applied to a linear problem, the nonlinear generalization
(in exact arithmetic) should be mathematically equivalent to the linear algorithm.

F2

The algorithm can be adapted to fit into the inexact Newton or multi-secant framework if desired.

F3

The algorithm should exploit a more accurate linear model than Newton or Quasi-Newton methods at the cost of potential extra function evaluations.

F4

The algorithm can be adapted to handle ’fuzzy’ functions appearing in stochastic or machine learning applications.

To achieve
F1
–
F4
, the linear algorithm is generalized to the nonlinear setting as follows. At step
j
of nlGCR(
k
), it is assumed that there exist two matrices
P_{j},V_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n_{j}}
with
J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}\approx V_{j}
that allow the local representation of the negative nonlinear residual
-r_{j+1}:=f(x_{j+1})
as

f(x_{j+1})=f(x_{j}+P_{j}y_{j})\approx f(x_{j})+J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}y_{j}\approx f(x_{j})+V_{j}y_{j},

(10)

with
y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
selected such that the residual norm
\|r_{j+1}\|
is minimized and

f(x_{j})+V_{j}y_{j}\ \perp\ \mathrm{span}\{V_{j}\}.

Note that, in the case of equality in (
10
),
y_{j}
is directly determined in terms of the normal equations as

y_{j}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}}\|f(x_{j})+V_{j}y\|=-V_{j}^{+}f(x_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\star)}}{{=}}V_{j}^{T}r_{j},

(11)

where we assume the columns of
V_{j}
to be orthonormal for
(\star)
.
Once
y_{j}
is computed, the update
x_{j+1}=x_{j}+P_{j}y_{j}
is performed and
r_{j+1}=-f(x_{j+1})
is set. The matrices
P_{j}
and
V_{j}
are then extended by setting
p_{j+1}=r_{j+1}
and
v_{j+1}=J_{f}(x_{j+1})p_{j+1}
as well as orthogonalizing
v_{j+1}
against all columns of
V_{j}
and modifying
p_{j+1}
accordingly. Notice that, in the linear case
f(x)=Ax-b
,
J_{f}(x)=A
and, by the formulation of the GCR-algorithm,

V_{j}=AP_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j},

(12)

and

P_{j}y_{j}=P_{j}V_{j}^{T}r_{j}=P_{j}(v_{j}^{T}r_{j})e_{n_{j}}=(v_{j}^{T}r_{j})p_{j}\equiv\alpha_{j}p_{j},

(13)

due to the orthogonality condition
r_{j}\perp\mathrm{span}\{V_{j-1}\}
in GCR, where
e_{n_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
is the
n_{j}
-th unit vector. However, equations (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
) are not true in the nonlinear version since generally,
J_{f}(x_{j+1})\neq J_{f}(x_{j})
and, as such,

V_{j}=\left[J_{f}(x_{j_{k}})p_{j_{k}},J_{f}(x_{j_{k}+1})p_{j_{k}+1},\dots,J_{f}(x_{j})p_{j}\right]\neq J_{f}(x_{j})\left[p_{j_{k}},p_{j_{k}+1},\dots,p_{j}\right]=J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}.

3
From Linear to Nonlinear Krylov methods

In this section, we will derive a unified framework for generalizing linear nested Krylov methods based on GCR to nonlinear equations and present three notable members of the resulting class of methods. Following
the terminology of
[
50
]
, which introduces nlGCR as the nonlinear analogue of GCR, we refer to methods that fit within our framework as
nonlinear Krylov
(
nlKrylov
) methods.

We first revisit the ideas from the original nlTGCR-paper presented in
Section 2.3
to develop a more general framework for this family of methods in
Section 3.1
. Afterwards, we will specify how to apply the introduced framework to three popular nested GCR-based algorithms, namely GMRESR, GCRO and LGMRES in
Section 3.2
,
Section 3.3
and
Section 3.4
, respectively, obtaining nonlinear extensions of these methods.

3.1
The general nlKrylov framework

In establishing the framework for nlKrylov methods, we recall that at step
j
of the nlGCR algorithm, the matrices
P_{j}
and
V_{j}
are employed as sets of directions for progressing the iteration, as shown in lines
5
and
6
of
Algorithm

2
. Since in the derivation of the nlGCR, the basis matrices
P_{j},V_{j}
seem rather arbitrary, one might also think of improving the local linear model (
10
) at the cost of some additional function evaluations, as indicated by
F3
. Inspired by the idea of nested GCR-type methods
[
26
]
, our aim is to compute
y_{j}
,
x_{j}
, and
r_{j}
as in nlGCR, but with
p_{j}
defined via a specified subroutine,
p_{j}=\mathcal{SR}_{j}(r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j}))
, which will involve the current residual
r_{j}
as well as applications of the Jacobian
J_{f}(x_{j})
. In the simplest case, where
\mathcal{SR}_{j}(r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j}))=r_{j}
, the standard nlGCR algorithm is recovered.
Building on the concept of nested Krylov methods,
\mathcal{SR}_{j}
represents a specific algorithm for solving the linear equation

J_{f}(x_{j})\widehat{p}=r_{j},

(14)

which requires only
r_{j}
as well as applications of
J_{f}(x_{j})
in the process. We will refer to every algorithm that uses nlGCR as an outer iteration and is equipped with a subroutine
\mathcal{SR}_{j}
for (
14
) as a
nonlinear Krylov method
. Observe that performing a few iterations of an iterative inner solver for (
14
) effectively incorporates the inexact Newton update direction
\Delta x_{j}

from (
9
) into the search space
P_{j}
, thereby improving the local linear model. However, as a large number of applications of
J_{f}(x_{j})
within
\mathcal{SR}_{j}
may introduce substantial computational overhead, their use should therefore be carefully balanced to enhance the local linear model (
10
) as effectively as possible without dominating the overall complexity.
In
[
26
]
, classical inner methods include Krylov subspace methods such as GMRES or the Bi-Conjugate Gradient Stabilized method (BiCGStab,
[
88
]
) if
J_{f}(x_{j})
is non-symmetric and CG for symmetric Jacobians. However, our framework is not restricted to Krylov subspace methods to obtain
p_{j}
. Different techniques such as classical iterative methods, randomized projection approaches or whatever algorithm is viable for subproblem (
14
) can be used. Nevertheless, in this paper, we will focus on three particular Krylov subspace approaches that will lead to nonlinear extensions of GMRESR(
m,k
)
Section 3.2
, GCRO(
m,k
)
Section 3.3
and LGMRES(
m,k
)
Section 3.4
.

3.3
nlGCRO

As noted in
[
26
]
, a limitation of GMRESR(
m
) is that it treats the subproblem (
14
) in isolation, without leveraging information from the outer GCR loop to improve the GMRES-solve step.
To address this, GCRO(
m
) has been developed as a refinement of GMRESR(
m
) that incorporates the GCR-basis
V_{j}
in the inner solve by introducing a projection onto its orthogonal complement, i.e., instead of solving
A\widehat{p}=r_{j+1}
in line
8
of
Algorithm

3
, GMRES is used to solve

\underbrace{(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})A}_{=:A^{\!\perp\!{V_{j}}}}~\widehat{p}=\underbrace{(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})r_{j+1}}_{=:\widetilde{r}_{j+1}}=r_{j+1},

(17)

where the second equation is true since, in the linear case,
r_{j+1}\perp V_{j}
. Given that, in the linear case,
AP_{j}=V_{j}
and

A^{\!\perp\!{V_{j}}}Q_{m}=Q_{m+1}\underline{H}_{m}

(18)

holds in the GMRES-solve, (
18
) yields

\widehat{p}=Q_{m}\gamma_{m},\quad\mbox{ with }\ \gamma_{m}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big\|\|r_{j+1}\|e_{1}-\underline{H}_{m}\gamma\Big\|,

and after orthogonalization we get

\|v_{j+1}\|p_{j+1}=A^{-1}A^{\!\perp\!{V_{j}}}Q_{m}\gamma_{m}=(I-A^{-1}V_{j}V_{j}^{T}A)Q_{m}\gamma_{m}=(Q_{m}-P_{j}B_{m})\gamma_{m},

(19)

with
B_{m}=V_{j}^{T}AQ_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j},m}

[
26
, Thm. 2.2]
.
In the nonlinear case, we generally do not have
J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}=V_{j}
, which means that the information in
V_{j}
is not solely associated with
J_{f}(x_{j})
but rather reflects contributions from all previously used Jacobians.
As such, including a projection onto
V_{j}^{\perp}
in the linear solve is only beneficial when the Jacobian is changing slowly, i.e., when
J_{f}(x_{j})\approx J_{f}(x_{j-1})
. If the the subspace
V_{j}
becomes outdated relative to the subproblem (
14
), projecting
J_{f}(x_{j+1})
onto
V_{j}^{\perp}
may fail to improve the local linear model and can even hinder convergence. Additionally, the second equation in (
17
) generally does not hold for nonlinear problems, since
r_{j+1}\not\perp V_{j}
. However, (
19
) remains valid with some minor modifications, namely

\|v_{j+1}\|p_{j+1}=(Q_{m}-P_{j}B_{m})\gamma_{m},

but now,
\displaystyle q_{1}=\frac{\widetilde{r}_{j+1}}{\|\widetilde{r}_{j+1}\|}
and

(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})J_{f}(x_{j+1})Q_{m}=Q_{m+1}\underline{H}_{m},\quad\gamma_{m}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\|\|\widetilde{r}_{j+1}\|-\underline{H}_{m}\gamma\right\|.

Following the derivations in
[
26
]
, we see that after the GCR-orthogonalization process, we have

\|v_{j+1}\|p_{j+1}=\widehat{p}-P_{j}V_{j}^{T}\widehat{c}=Q_{m}\gamma_{m}-P_{j}V_{j}^{T}J_{f}(x_{j+1})Q_{m}\gamma_{m}=(Q_{m}-P_{j}B_{m})\gamma_{m},

where
B_{m}=V_{j}^{T}J_{f}(x_{j+1})Q_{m}
, and similarly

\|v_{j+1}\|v_{j+1}=\widehat{c}-V_{j}V_{j}^{T}\widehat{c}=(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})J_{f}(x_{j+1})Q_{m}\gamma_{m}=Q_{m+1}(\underline{H}_{m}\gamma_{m}).

Consequently,
p_{j+1}
and
v_{j+1}
can be recovered directly from the inner solve (up to normalization), making orthogonalization in the outer GCR loop unnecessary, although it may still enhance numerical stability.

5
Convergence analysis of nlKrylov methods

The goal of this section is to examine the convergence behavior of nlKrylov methods, beginning with problems that have nonsingular Jacobians at the solution and then addressing those with singular Jacobians.

5.1
Problems with nonsingular Jacobian

Suppose that at iteration
j
, the spaces
P_{j},V_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n_{j}}
have been constructed, with
V_{j}^{T}V_{j}=I_{n_{j}}
. Let
W_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n_{j}}
denote the error matrix defined in (
24
), such that

W_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}-V_{j}~\Leftrightarrow~V_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}-W_{j}.

(26)

Then, assuming that the Jacobian is locally invertible in the neighborhood of
x^{*}
–and in particular invertible at
x_{j}
–we obtain from
Section 4.3
, upon defining

Y_{j}=P_{j}-J_{f}(x_{j})^{-1}W_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n_{j}},

directly the relation

J_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})Y_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}-W_{j}=V_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n_{j}}

from (
26
). Thus, we can compute
y_{j}
by solving
y_{j}=-J_{j}^{+}f(x_{j})=-V_{j}^{T}f(x_{j}),
which is
precisely how it is computed in the
nlKrylov
framework. This relation provides a natural interpretation of nlKrylov methods through the lens of preconditioned projection methods, where the dependence on the underlying subspaces is explicit and these subspaces are dictated by the choice of the subroutine
\mathcal{SR}_{j}
. We use this perspective to further analyze the convergence behavior of nlKrylov methods.
In
[
50
, Thm. 4.5]
, the convergence of nlGCR was established under the assumption of an exact line search, smoothness of
f(x)
, the existence of uniform bounds
\eta,\mu\in[0,1)
for the local linear model

\|W_{j}y_{j}\|\leq\mu\|f(x_{j})\|,

(27)

and a sufficient decrease of the linearized residual

\|f(x_{j})+V_{j}y_{j}\|\leq\eta\|f(x_{j})\|.

(28)

As we will see, in our analysis we do not require the first two assumptions and can relax the uniform bounds (
27
) and (
28
) as follows.

Assumption 1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mu_{j}:=\frac{\|W_{j}y_{j}\|}{\|f(x_{j})\|}
and
\displaystyle\eta_{j}:=\frac{\|f(x_{j})+V_{j}y_{j}\|}{\|f(x_{j})\|}
. Then, for all
j
there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mu_{j}+\eta_{j}=:c_{j}\leq c<1,

(29)

Note that, within the PAA framework,
\eta_{j}
can be interpreted as the angle between
f(x_{j})
and
V_{j}
, see (
25
). The convergence of the nlKrylov methods can now be established by leveraging
[
82

Theorem 1
.

Let
f(x)
be continuously differentiable in a neighborhood
B_{1}(x^{*})
of a solution
x^{*}
to
f(x)=0
, with

nonsingular. Suppose that
1

of

such that, for any

Proof.

Following the proof of
[
82
, Theorem 5]
, it is sufficient to show that nlKrylov methods are in fact inexact Newton methods, with the ratio
\displaystyle\frac{\|t_{j}\|}{\|f(x_{j})\|}
uniformly bounded by a constant less than
1
, where
t_{j}
denotes the deviation from the exact update (see (
9
)).
Local convergence then follows from
[
32
, Theorem 2.3]
.
Within our nlKrylov formulation, we have

s_{j}=x_{j+1}-x_{j}=-P_{j}V_{j}^{T}f(x_{j})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad t_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})s_{j}+f(x_{j})=(I-J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}V_{j}^{T})f(x_{j}).

Since (
24
) yields
J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}=W_{j}+V_{j}
, we obtain

t_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})s_{j}+f(x_{j})=(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T}-W_{j}V_{j}^{T})f(x_{j}).

(30)

Then, by (

1

\|W_{j}V_{j}^{T}f(x_{j})\|=\|W_{j}y_{j}\|=\mu_{j}\|f(x_{j})\|.

Thus, using (

the angle between

and

, gives

\displaystyle\|t_{j}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\;\|f(x_{j})^{T}(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})^{T}(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})f(x_{j})-f(x_{j})^{T}(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})^{T}W_{j}V_{j}^{T}f(x_{j})

\displaystyle-f(x_{j})^{T}V_{j}W_{j}^{T}(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})f(x_{j})+f(x_{j})^{T}V_{j}W_{j}^{T}W_{j}V_{j}^{T}f(x_{j})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\|f(x_{j})\|^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}+2\|f(x_{j})\|^{2}\mu_{j}\sin\theta_{j}+\|f(x_{j})\|^{2}\mu_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\|f(x_{j})\|^{2}(\sin\theta_{j}+\mu_{j})^{2}.

By
1

\eta_{j}\|f(x_{j})\|=\|f(x_{j})+V_{j}y_{j}\|=\|f(x_{j})-V_{j}V_{j}^{T}f(x_{j})\|=\|(I-V_{j}V_{j}^{T})f(x_{j})\|=\|f(x_{j})\|\sin(\theta_{j}),

and hence
\sin(\theta_{j})=\eta_{j}
.
Consequently, local convergence is guaranteed by (

[

, Theorem 2.3]
as

\displaystyle\frac{\|t_{j}\|}{\|f(x_{j})\|}\leq\eta_{j}+\mu_{j}\leq c_{j}\leq c<1.

Remark 2
.

Up to this point, we have relied on the implicit assumption that the Jacobian does not vary rapidly during the iteration process. However, this assumption has not been explicitly quantified. In our analysis, it is captured by (
24
) and
1
:
the alignment between
V_{j}
and
J_{f}(x_{j})P_{j}
and consequently the small norm of the error matrix

implicitly assumes a slowly varying linear model. Imposing stronger conditions, such as Lipschitz continuity of

, would lead to assumptions similar to

[
50

, which require a uniform bound on the quadratic term of the Taylor expansion of

– a condition that is too restrictive for our framework. Our result,
Theorem

, is formulated in terms of the subspaces

and
V_{j}
, allowing more flexibility in selecting the subroutine
\mathcal{SR}_{j}
.

5.2
Problems with singular Jacobian

Extensive results exist on convergence of Newton’s method for singular problems, where the derivative at the root is singular. Reddien
[
72
,
73
]
considered
C^{3}
functions with a one-dimensional null space of the Jacobian. These results were extended by Decker, Keller, Kelley, and Suresh
[
29
,
30
,
28
,
58
]
, who provided detailed convergence rate analyses under various scenarios. Griewank
[
46
,
47
]
showed a starlike domain for which Newton’s method converges linearly to the root.

For inexact Newton methods applied to singular problems, Kelley & Xue
[
59
]
provided convergence results analogous to
[
32
, Theorem 2.3]
,
while Shores and Tiahrt
[

]
showed that subspace projected inexact Newton methods converge whenever the corresponding exact Newton method does. Argyros
[
7
]
extended the results of Decker, Keller, and Kelley
[
28
]
to inexact Newton schemes. Convergence of quasi-Newton methods, such as Broyden’s method, for singular problems has also been studied in
[
31
,
64
]
. For singular systems, including underdetermined and overdetermined cases, several results
[
19
,
41
,
97
,
98
,
6
,
3
]
establish convergence of inexact Newton methods under weak Lipschitz or majorant conditions.
Although nlKrylov methods can be interpreted within the inexact Newton framework, the forcing sequence
c_{j}=\mu_{j}+\eta_{j}
is not explicitly user-controlled; rather, it is implicitly determined by the Krylov subspace construction and the associated projections at each iteration. Consequently, the aforementioned convergence results do not apply directly. Nevertheless, under additional assumptions on
t_{j}
, generalized convergence results for singular systems can be derived by leveraging the frameworks of
[
28
]
and
[
59
]
.

6.1
Linear and nonlinear updates

In

50

k

r^{nl}_{j+1}=-f(x_{j+1})

(40)

in line
6

Algorithm

2
by the linearized residual

r^{lin}_{j+1}=r_{j}-V_{j}y_{j}.

(41)

Assume we have used (
40
) so far, i.e.,
r_{j}\equiv r_{j}^{nl}
in (
41
). Then, if the cosine angle condition holds, i.e.,

\theta_{j+1}:=1-\frac{(r_{j+1}^{nl})^{T}r_{j+1}^{lin}}{\|r_{j+1}^{nl}\|^{2}\|r_{j+1}^{lin}\|^{2}}<\theta

(42)

for some predefined threshold
\theta
(see (
25
)), we adaptively switch to the linear version and check periodically, if the local linear model is still valid by computing the nonlinear residual (
40
) and checking (
42
) for a violation. If the angle grows too large, we switch back to the nonlinear update version. Since in that case, the subspaces
P_{j}
,
V_{j}
created by the linear update version are likely to contain inaccurate directions that can slow down or even impede convergence, we discard
P_{j}
and
V_{j}
and effectively restart the algorithm using
x_{0}=x_{j}
. This strategy can be directly tranferred to the nlKrylov framework as it depends only on the quality of the local linear model (
10

6.2
Automatic restarts

In the standard GCR-algorithm, we have
V_{j}^{T}V_{j}=I_{n_{j}}
. Moreover, since
V_{j}=AP_{j}
,
P_{j}
is orthogonal with respect to the inner product induced by
A^{T}A
, i.e.,

\delta_{i,j}=\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=\langle Ap_{i},Ap_{j}\rangle=p_{i}^{T}(A^{T}A)p_{j}:=\langle p_{i},p_{j}\rangle_{A^{T}\!A}.

If
A
is well-conditioned, orthogonality of
V_{j}
induces
A^{T}\!A
-orthogonality of
P_{j}
and allows for a numerically stable procedure. However, in the nonlinear case, we generally do not have
V_{j}=AP_{j}
since
A_{j}=J_{f}(x_{j})
changes in every step. To avoid ill-conditioning of
P_{j}
caused by the loss of
A_{j}^{T}\!A_{j}
-orthogonality, we employ the automatic restart strategy presented in
[
85
]
.
Consider the Gram-Schmidt process in the
j
-th step of the nlGCR, i.e., lines
7
-
12
of
Algorithm

2
. With
\beta_{i}=\widehat{v}^{T}v_{i}
, we expand
P_{j}
by

p_{j+1}=\frac{1}{\lVert\widehat{v}\rVert}\left(r_{j+1}-\sum_{i=j_{k}}^{j}\beta_{i}p_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{\lVert\widehat{v}\rVert}\left(\widehat{p}-\sum_{i=j_{k}}^{j}\beta_{i}p_{i}\right).

If we assume rounding errors on
p_{i}
, i.e., the numerically stored basis matrices are
\widetilde{p}_{i}=p_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
,
i=j_{k},\dots,j
, and an error on
p_{j+1}
denoted by
\delta_{j+1}
, which is assumed to be bounded by
\lVert\delta_{j+1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\lVert\widehat{v}\rVert}\lVert\widehat{p}\rVert_{\infty}
, we get an element-wise estimate of the error
\varepsilon_{j+1}
by

\lvert\varepsilon_{j+1}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{\lVert\widehat{v}\rVert}(C\cdot\lVert\widehat{p}\rVert_{\infty}+\sum_{i=j_{k}}^{j}\lvert\beta_{i}\rvert\lVert\varepsilon_{i}\rVert_{\infty}),

meaning we can keep track of the level of ill-conditioning by introducing the scalar sequence

w_{j+1}=\frac{1}{\lVert\widehat{v}\rVert}(C\cdot\lVert\widehat{p}\rVert_{\infty}-\sum_{i=j_{k}}^{j}\lvert\beta_{i}\rvert w_{i}),\quad w_{0}=0,

(43)

and restarting whenever
w_{j+1}>\tau
, where
\tau>0
is a user-defined tolerance. In the case of general nlKrylov methods, we have to replace
\widehat{p}=r_{j+1}
in (
43
) by the corresponding preconditioned residual
\widehat{p}=\mathcal{SR}_{j+1}(r_{j+1},J_{f}(x_{j+1}))
before the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization. If ill-conditioning is detected and the algorithm is restarted, we set
P_{j}=V_{j}=\emptyset
and restart using

P_{j+1}=\frac{\widehat{p}}{\|J_{f}(x_{j+1})\widehat{p}\|},\quad V_{j+1}=\frac{J_{f}(x_{j+1})\widehat{p}}{\|J_{f}(x_{j+1})\widehat{p}\|},\quad w_{j+1}=C\frac{\|\widehat{p}\|_{\infty}}{\|J_{f}(x_{j+1})\widehat{p}\|}.

In our implementation, we use
C=1
and
\tau=10^{3}
as default parameters and refer the interested reader to
[
85
]
for a discussion of different parameter choices.

6.3
Damping and choosing descent directions

Within the quasi-Newton framework,
d_{j}=P_{j}y_{j}=P_{j}V_{j}^{T}r_{j}
can be interpreted as an inexact update direction obtained using the approximation
J_{f}(x_{j})^{-1}\approx P_{j}V_{j}^{T}
. Hence, we can not guarantee that
d_{j}
is a descent direction for
f(x)

[
8
]
. Therefore, if
d_{j}
is not a descent direction, its negation
-d_{j}
should be used to ensure a decrease of
f(x_{j}+\alpha_{j}d_{j})
along the update direction. This condition can be verified by computing

\zeta_{j}:=\langle r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j})d_{j}\rangle.

If
\zeta_{j}>0
, then
d_{j}
is a descent direction, otherwise,

-\zeta_{j}=-\langle r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j})d_{j}\rangle=\langle r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j})(-d_{j})\rangle

is likely to be positive and we use
-d_{j}
as an update direction instead.
Additionally, we want to perform a simple line search to ensure that
\alpha_{j}\in(0,1]
satisfies the Armijo-Goldstein condition
[
9
]
, i.e.,

\lVert f(x_{j}+\alpha_{j}d_{j})\rVert^{2}\leq\|f(x_{j})\|^{2}-c_{1}\alpha_{j}\langle r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j})d_{j}\rangle=\|r_{j}\|^{2}-c_{1}\alpha_{j}\zeta_{j}.

(44)

Note that, within the backtracking procedure,
\zeta_{j}
can be inexpensively approximated using the finite difference approximation, i.e.,

\zeta_{j}=\langle r_{j},J_{f}(x_{j})d_{j}\rangle\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle r_{j},f(x_{j}+\varepsilon d_{j})-f(x_{j})\rangle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle r_{j},f(x_{j}+\varepsilon d_{j})+r_{j}\rangle,

where
\varepsilon=\alpha_{j}^{(0)}
is used before the backtracking loop to check if
d_{j}
is a descend direction and the estimate is refined inside the loop using
\varepsilon=\alpha_{j}^{(\ell)}=\frac{\alpha_{j}^{(0)}}{2^{\ell}}
. Also, we can split
\zeta_{j}^{(\ell)}
into

\zeta_{j}^{(\ell)}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{(\ell)}}\langle r_{j},f(x_{j}+\alpha_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{j})+r_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{(\ell)}}\left(\|r_{j}\|^{2}+\langle r_{j},f(x_{j}+\alpha_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{j})\rangle\right)

to safe one vector addition. If a more accurate estimate of
\zeta_{j}
is needed, numerical extrapolation can be used, e.g., by finding an interpolating polynomial
p(\alpha)
for the data points
(\alpha_{j}^{(i)},\zeta_{j}^{(i)})
,
i=0,\dots,\ell
, and evaluating it at
\alpha=0
, using the Neville-Aitken algorithm.
In our implementation, we use
c_{1}=10^{-3}
and adapt the initial step-size heuristic from
[
50
]
, where it is suggested to use
\alpha_{0}^{(0)}=1
and

\alpha_{j+1}^{(0)}=\begin{cases}\min\{1,2\alpha_{j}^{(0)}\},&\text{if $\alpha_{j}^{(0)}$ was accepted in iteration $j$}\\
\frac{\alpha_{j}^{(0)}}{2},&\text{else}\end{cases}

to avoid small steps while simultaneously reducing the number of line-search steps. The interested reader is referred to the appendix for a detailed statement of the
Algorithm

17
.

Note that, if the linear update is performed, the damped linear residual

r_{j+1}^{lin}=r_{j}-\alpha_{j}V_{j}y_{j}

is used on the left-hand side of (
44
) instead of
f(x_{j}+\alpha_{j}d_{j}))
.

Global nlKrylov methods for nonlinear
matrix equations

All strategies introduced in
Section

3
can be easily modified to solve nonlinear matrix equations of the form

F(X)=0,\quad F:\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,p},~p\leq n.

(45)

For problems of this form, the Newton iteration (
6
) can be generalized by replacing the system involving the Jacobian
J_{f}(x_{j})
with a linear matrix equation

L_{F}(X_{j},\Delta{X}_{j})=-F(X_{j}),

(46)

where
L_{F}(X_{j}):\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
is the Fréchet derivative of
F
at
X_{j}
. In
Section 7.1
, we briefly recall the idea of global Krylov solvers for linear operator equations and afterwards, in
Section 7.2
, we explain how to combine these ideas and the results on nlKrylov methods introduced in this paper to acquire global
nlKrylov
methods for nonlinear matrix equations. For the remainder of this section, we use capital letters to denote matrices and calligraphic letters the denote the columnwise concatenation of multiple matrices into a block matrix. Specifically, if
U_{1},\dots,U_{j}
are matrices of size
n\times p
, we define the corresponding block matrix
\mathcal{U}_{j}=\left[U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{j}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p\cdot j}
. On such block matrices, we define the matrix-vector product

\mathcal{U}_{j}~\Diamond~\gamma=\mathcal{U}_{j}(\gamma\otimes I_{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\gamma_{i}U_{i},\quad\gamma=(\gamma_{i})_{i=1,\dots,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{j}.

(47)

Finally, we denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
the Frobenius inner product and norm, respectively. For a matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
and a block matrix
\mathcal{U}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p\cdot j}
, we define

\langle\mathcal{U}_{j},W\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\langle U_{1},W\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle U_{j},W\rangle\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\mathcal{U}_{j},\mathcal{U}_{j}\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\langle U_{1},U_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle U_{1},U_{j}\rangle\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\langle U_{j},U_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle U_{j},U_{j}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{j,j}.

We call the block matrix
\mathcal{U}_{j}

F-orthogonal
, if
\langle\mathcal{U}_{j},\mathcal{U}_{j}\rangle=I_{j}
. Additionally, for a linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
, and an F-orthogonal block matrix
\mathcal{U}_{j}
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}^{\!\perp\!{\mathcal{U}_{j}}}:=(I-\mathcal{U}_{j}~\Diamond~\langle\mathcal{U}_{j},\cdot\rangle)\mathcal{A}
the operator such that

\mathcal{A}^{\!\perp\!{\mathcal{U}_{j}}}(X):=((I-\mathcal{U}_{j}~\Diamond~\langle\mathcal{U}_{j},\cdot\rangle)\mathcal{A})(X)=\mathcal{A}(X)-\mathcal{U}_{j}~\Diamond~\langle\mathcal{U}_{j},\mathcal{A}(X)\rangle,

i.e., the operator that orthogonalizes
\mathcal{A}(X)
against
\mathcal{U}_{j}
with respect to the Frobenius inner product. It is easy to see that this is the matrix-valued equivalent to the GCRO-operator introduced in (
18
), hence, we use the same notation.

7.1
Global linear methods

Global Krylov subspace methods
[
57
]
have been introduced in recent years as an efficient iterative tool for solving linear matrix equations

\mathcal{A}(X)=B,\quad\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,p},\quad B\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p},

(48)

where usually
p\ll n
and
\mathcal{A}
is a linear operator that is inexpensive to evaluate. Classical examples include Sylvester equations

AX+XB=C,

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p,p}

[
95
,
92
]
are large and sparse, or linear systems with multiple right hand sides
[
86
]
.
Let us recall that, given an initial residual
R_{0}=B-\mathcal{A}(X_{0})
,
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
, global GMRES
[
57
]
seeks to find a solution of the linear equation (
48
) in the block Krylov-subspace

\mathcal{K}^{\Box}_{j}(\mathcal{A},R_{0})=\mathrm{blockspan}(R_{0},\mathcal{A}(R_{0}),\dots,\mathcal{A}^{j-1}(R_{0})).

After
m
steps of global GMRES, we obtain the global Arnoldi-relation

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{Q}_{m}=\mathcal{Q}_{m+1}~\Diamond~\underline{H}_{m},

where
\mathcal{Q}_{m+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,(m+1)\cdot p}
is F-orthogonal and
\underline{H}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1,m}
is upper Hessenberg with an extra row (see (
15
)). Note that the size of
\underline{H}_{m}
depends only on the size of the Krylov subspace and is not affected by the block size
p
of the problem. The global Krylov methods rely on the fact that every linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
has a matrix representation
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot p,n\cdot p}
such that

\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{A}(X))=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(X).

Hence, solving (
48
) by
m
steps of global GMRES is mathematically equivalent to solving the vectorized equation

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}x=b,\quad\mbox{where}\quad x=\mathrm{vec}(X),~b=\mathrm{vec}(B)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\cdot p},

by a standard GMRES. Although, to the best of our knowledge it has not been done, GCR-based nested Krylov subspace methods can also be extended to linear matrix equations by using the Frobenius inner product in step
5
and
9
of
Algorithm

1
and the Frobenius norm for normalization. Analogously, GMRESR, GCRO and LGMRES can be adapted by adding global GMRES as the inner solver. As shown in

[
94
]
, global Krylov methods can serve as efficient inner solvers for (
46
) in the context of matrix-valued Newton methods. In the remainder of this section, we extend the nonlinear Krylov methods discussed in
Section

3
to matrix-valued problems.

Using the notation introduced in

7.3
Implementation details

In this section, we briefly comment on the necessary changes to the algorithms introduced in
Section

3
necessary to solve matrix-valued problems. First, as mentioned above, we have to replace the inner products and norms in every method by their Frobenius equivalents. Note that in
MATLAB
, the inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle
can be computed efficiently in
\mathcal{O}(np)
flops using

\langle X,Y\rangle=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}y_{ij}=\texttt{sum(X.*Y,'all')}.

Secondly, every matrix-vector multiplication involving the Jacobian

has to be replaced by the evaluation of the Fréchet derivative
L(X,\Delta{X})
, which can be performed efficiently using a finite difference approximation

L_{F}(X,\Delta{X})\approx\frac{F(X+\varepsilon\Delta{X})-F(X)}{\varepsilon}

if no explicit formula is available. If there is a need for a high-accuracy approximation, the complex step approximation

L_{F}(X,\Delta{X})\approx\frac{\Im(F(X+\imath\varepsilon\Delta{X}))}{\varepsilon},\quad\imath=\sqrt{-1},~\Im(a+\imath b)=b,~a,b\in\mathbb{R},

can be used if appropriate
[
2
]
.
Finally, one may apply the automatic restart strategy from
Section 6.2
when the maximum matrix norm
is used in (
43
).

9
Conclusion and future work

In this work, we have developed a unified framework for nonlinear Krylov methods derived from nested GCR-type linear solvers. Our approach systematically generalizes methods like GMRESR, GCRO, and LGMRES to the nonlinear problems, yielding algorithms such as nlGMRESR, nlGCRO, and nlLGMRES.
These methods preserve the essential structural properties of their linear counterparts while admitting an interpretation as inexact Newton schemes We have established convergence results under relaxed assumptions and clarified their relationships with other nonlinear solvers, including nlOrthomin. Moreover, the framework extends naturally to nonlinear matrix equations, which are increasingly important in data-driven and high-dimensional applications. Future directions include adapting the framework to stochastic or derivative-free settings, incorporating adaptive memory truncation for enhanced scalability, and exploiting the connection to Newton–Krylov methods by selecting
m
adaptively to further improve performance. Codes to reproduce the numerical experiments will be made publicly available upon publication.

C.1
A nonlinear eigenvalue problem

In this vector valued example, we consider a nonlinear eigenvalue problem (NEP) originating from the discretization of the partial delay-differential equation

\displaystyle\Delta u(x,t)+a(x)u(x,t)+b(x)u(x,t-2)-u_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega,

where
\Omega=[0,\pi]^{2}
,
t\geq 0
and

a(x)=\sin^{2}(x_{1})\sin^{2}(x_{2}),\quad b(x)=\sin(x_{1}+x_{2})+1.31.

The example is called the
pdde_symmetric
problem in the NLEVP-database
[
14
,
54
]
. Upon discretization, we obtain the NEP

T(\lambda)v=\lambda v,

(53)

with a nonlinear function
T(\lambda):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,n},~\lambda\mapsto(M+A)+e^{-2\lambda}B,
where
n=(N-1)^{2}
,
N
is the number of discretization points, and
M
,
A
and
B
are sparse symmetric
n\times n
matrices that discretize
\Delta(\cdot)
,
a(\cdot)
and
b(\cdot)
, respectively. The eigenvalue
\lambda^{*}
nearest to zero is of interest in this example.
Using an arbitrary normalization vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the eigenvalue problem (
53
) can be rewritten in terms of a root finding problem
[
48
]
, i.e.,

0=f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}T(\lambda)v-\lambda v\\
c^{T}v-1\end{bmatrix},\quad x=\begin{bmatrix}v\\
\lambda\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

(54)

Again, it is straightforward to see that

J_{f}(x)\Delta{x}=\begin{bmatrix}T(\lambda)-\lambda I&(T^{\prime}(\lambda)-I)v\\
c^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta{v}\\
\Delta\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(T(\lambda)-\lambda I)\Delta{v}+\Delta\lambda(T^{\prime}(\lambda)-I)v\\
c^{T}\Delta{v}\end{bmatrix},

where
T^{\prime}(\lambda)=-2e^{-2\lambda}B
.

Experiment 1:
In this experiment, we use
N=64
grid points in both directions, which results in a problem of size
n+1=(N-1)^{2}+1=3,970
. We set
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{1}_{n}
and initial guess
x_{0}=[v_{0}^{T},\lambda_{0}]^{T}
, where
(v_{0},\lambda_{0})
is the smallest magnitude eigenpair of
T(0)=M+A+B
. We use a truncation window of
k=20
for nlKrylov methods and
k_{AA}=30
for Anderson Acceleration. Nested nlKrylov methods use
m=30
, their adaptive versions use
\theta=10^{-3}
. Anderson Acceleration used damping parameter
\beta=10^{-3}
and Newton-Krylov method allows for a maximum of
200
GMRES steps. The iterations is stopped once the tolerance of
10^{-12}
is reached.
In
Figure

10
, all nested Krylov methods converge within
30
iterations. Among them, nlLGMRES and nlGCRO require the same number of iterations as Newton–Krylov, while nlGMRESR requires roughly ten additional iterations. The methods nlGCR, nlGCR-A, and nlOrthomin did not converge in this experiment.
The adaptive variants of nlGMRESR, nlGCRO, and nlLGMRES did not switch to linear mode. Considering function evaluations, all nested methods outperform Newton–Krylov: nlGCRO converges after approximately
500
function evaluations, nlGMRESR and nlLGMRES after about
800
, and Newton–Krylov requires roughly
1200
. The eigenvalue
\lambda^{*}\approx-0.2467
of interest is found by all converged methods.

C.2
A nonlinear eigenvector problem (NEPv)

For our last experiment, we consider a nonlinear eigenvector problem of the form

H(V)V=V\Lambda,\quad V^{T}V=I_{p},\quad\Lambda=\Lambda^{T},

(55)

where
H:\mathbb{R}^{n,p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
is a symmetric matrix function, i.e.,
H(V)=H(V)^{T}
, and
p\ll n
. Problems of this type arise frequently in quantum physics, most notably discretized Kohn-Sham- and Gross-Pitaevskii-equations
[
17
,
56
]
, in data-science applications such as Robust-Rayleigh-Quotient- or Trace-Ratio optimization
[
10
,
17
]
as well as the modeling of dissipative Hamiltonian DAEs
[
11
]
. Recently, the use of matrix-valued Newton methods to solve (
55
) via the root finding problem

F(X)=\begin{bmatrix}H(V)V-V\Lambda\\
I_{p}-V^{T}V\end{bmatrix},\quad X=\begin{bmatrix}V\\
\Lambda\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p),p},

(56)

has been studied in
[
94
]
. We consider the discrete 3D-Kohn-Sham-model

H(V)=L+\mathrm{Diag}(L^{-1}\rho(V)-\gamma\rho(V)^{1/3}),\quad\gamma\geq 0,

where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
is a 3D-Laplacian on a cube discretized using
N
equally spaced grid points in every direction, leading to
n=N^{3}
, and
\rho(V)=\mathrm{diag}(VV^{T})
is the charge density of electrons. Note that the Fréchet derivative of (
56
) is singular close to the solution due to the orthogonal invariance of
H(V)
, as was pointed out in
[
94
]
. However, if one chooses a different normalization criterion similar to (
54
), i.e., using a constant matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n,p}
, we can rewrite (
56
) to

\widetilde{F}(X)\begin{bmatrix}H(V)V-V\Lambda\\
I_{p}-C^{T}V\end{bmatrix},\quad X=\begin{bmatrix}V\\
\Lambda\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p),p},

(57)

to obtain a non-singular problem.

Experiment 1:
We first want to examine the singular case, i.e., the root finding problem (
56
). In our experiment, we use
N=16
and
p=2
, leading to
X\in\mathbb{R}^{4098,2}
. The iteration is started with an initial value generated by two steps of SCF initialized using the two smallest eigenpairs of
L
. We use a truncation window of
k=10
for the nlKrylov methods, the inner solve in nested nlKrylov methods is carried out by
m=20
steps of global GMRES, Newton’s method uses at most
400
steps of global GMRES. The iteration is terminated when the relative residual is below
\tau=(N+p)10^{-12}\approx 1.8\cdot 10^{-11}
or after
50
iterations. The adaptive methods use an angle of
\theta=10^{-3}
. For NEPv, acceleration schemes such as Anderson, Pulay or secant acceleration have proven to improve and stabilize SCF convergence when used as a mixing scheme
[
22
,
23
,
43
]
. As a consequence, here, we are using Anderson Acceleration as an accelerator rather than a solver, with SCF as the underlying fixed-point scheme. The window size is
k=30
and the update is damped using
\beta=-0.1
. The convergence results are displayed in
Figure

11
. Here we can see that only four methods managed to converge to the desired accuracy while all the others stagnated around
10^{0}-10^{-1}
. The four convergent methods are nlGMRESR-A, nlLGMRES-A, Newton-Krylov and Anderson Acceleration. Among these methods, the adaptive nlKrylov methods required the fewest iterations to convergence:
36
for nlGMRESR-A and
39
for nlLGMRES-A, while Newton’s method required
49
steps. Looking at the function evaluations, nlGMRESR-A performed the best with Anderson Acceleration being close, while Newton-Krylov and nlLGMRES required over
250
function evaluations more.
