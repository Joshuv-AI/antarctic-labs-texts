Abstract
The rapid advancement of embodied intelligence and world models has intensified efforts to integrate physical laws into AI systems, yet physical perception and symbolic physics reasoning have developed along separate trajectories without a unified bridging framework. This work provides a comprehensive overview of physical AI, establishing clear distinctions between theoretical physics reasoning and applied physical understanding while systematically examining how physics-grounded methods enhance AI’s real-world comprehension across structured symbolic reasoning, embodied systems, and generative models. Through rigorous analysis of recent advances, we advocate for intelligent systems that ground learning in both physical principles and embodied reasoning processes, transcending pattern recognition toward genuine understanding of physical laws. Our synthesis envisions next-generation world models capable of explaining physical phenomena and predicting future states, advancing safe, generalizable, and interpretable AI systems. We maintain a continuously updated resource at https://github.com/AI4Phys/Awesome-AI-for-Physics .

B-A

Scope

We focus on how frontier AI systems tackle physics and physical reasoning problems in the era of deep learning.
For physics reasoning, we specifically focus on how AI systems leverage the laws of physics to solve physics problems in end to end manner, which is as opposed to how physics inspired AI development.
As we aim to provide a coherent discussion on a central thesis, we do not discuss how physics inspire machine learning methods (such as the Boltzmann Machine and Hopfield Network) or how machine learning at large facilitates physics research, which is a vast and diversified field we leave for future work in AI for Science.

Visual Generation Models.

Image and video generation models are deep learning systems that learn to synthesize realistic visual content by modeling the complex distribution of natural images and videos. These models are mostly trained on large scale visual datasets, enabling them to generate content from noise or conditional inputs like text descriptions, sketches, or partial observations. A unified formulation for both:

p_{\theta}(x_{0:T}|c)=\prod_{t=0}^{T}p_{\theta}(x_{t}|x_{<t},c),

(3)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}

t

T=0

T>0
for video sequences, and
c
encodes conditioning signals (text prompts, class labels, initial frames, or latent codes). Modern implementations typically use diffusion models, GANs, or autoregressive transformers to approximate this distribution.

3D Reconstruction Models.

3D reconstruction models recover three-dimensional structure and appearance from 2D observations such as images or videos. These models solve the inverse rendering problem by inferring geometry (shape, depth, surface normals) and appearance properties (color, texture, materials) from partial visual data, often leveraging multi-view consistency, learned shape priors, or physics-based constraints. Formally:
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}:\{\mathcal{I}_{i},\mathcal{K}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\rightarrow(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{I}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
are input images with camera parameters
\mathcal{K}_{i}\in SE(3)

\mathcal{G}
(represented as meshes, point clouds, voxels, or implicit fields
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
) and appearance
\mathcal{A}
(texture maps, radiance fields, or material properties).

Vision Language Action Models.

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are embodied AI systems that directly map visual observations and language instructions to continuous control actions for physical manipulation tasks. These models enable robots to perform complex tasks by understanding natural language commands and visual context, outputting precise motor controls. Formally:
a_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(I_{t},l,h_{t})
where
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
represents continuous actions (e.g., end-effector position, joint angles),
I_{t}
is the visual input (RGB/depth images),
l
is the language instruction, and
h_{t}
captures temporal context or proprioceptive state.

Vision Language Navigation Models.

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) models enable agents to navigate through environments by following ninstructions while processing visual observations. VLN typically involves discrete action selection from a predefined set of navigation primitives. Formally:
a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{nav}}\pi_{\theta}(o_{t},l_{inst},m_{t})
where
\mathcal{A}_{nav}=\{\text{forward, left, right, stop}\}
represents discrete navigation actions,
o_{t}

l_{inst}

m_{t}

B-C

Taxonomy

To systematically understand how AI systems engage with physics, we propose a hierarchical taxonomy that reflects the natural progression from abstract reasoning to embodied action. Our framework organizes physics-aware AI capabilities into four interconnected domains that mirror human cognitive development in physical understanding.
Physical Perception
represents the ability to intuitively extract physical property from sensory data through increasingly sophisticated layers, including object recognition, spatial relationships and intrinsic. In the the inverse direction,
Physics Reasoning
encompasses symbolic manipulation and theoretical problem-solving, where AI systems leverage mathematical representations to solve physics problems ranging from textbook exercises to research-level challenges. These two complementary pathways converge in
World Modeling
, where AI systems integrate symbolic knowledge with perceptual understanding to construct predictive models of physical environments, enabling capabilities from video generation to reconstruction. Finally,
Embodied Interaction
grounds these cognitive capabilities in real-world action, where robots, autonomous vehicles, and navigation agents must reconcile theoretical understanding with the irreversible consequences of physical manipulation. Figure
3
shows our taxonomy. This taxonomy not only delineates current research boundaries but also reveals critical gaps where symbolic reasoning and embodied experience remain disconnected, highlighting the path toward genuinely physics-aware artificial intelligence.

C
Physical Perception

Understanding the physical world begins with perception. Before AI systems can reason about abstract physical laws or manipulate objects in complex environments, they must first acquire the fundamental capability to perceive, understand, and infer physical properties from sensory inputs. We term this capability
Physical Perception
, which represents the foundational layer of physical intelligence, where raw sensory data from cameras, sensors, and other modalities are transformed into structured representations of the physical world. In this section, we organize existing investigations into five hierarchical categories: Object Recognition, Spatial Perception, Intrinsic Property, Dynamic Estimation, and Causal & Counterfactual Inference, ordered by increasing demands of cognitive load.

Object Recognition

C-D

Dynamic Estimation

Crucially, these intrinsic properties serve as foundation for understanding how objects behave according to the laws of physics. Knowledge of mass and rigidity, for instance, directly informs whether/how an object might deform under external force, or how it influences motion during collisions. Building on this static perspective, dynamic property perception captures how objects behave and interact over time through contact, constraints, and forces such as support, occlusion, friction, and impact.
Unlike intrinsic estimation that answers what an object is in terms of characteristics, dynamic perception addresses how it behaves upon interaction with other objects.
Graph-based methods, especially graph neural networks (GNNs), have been central to this effort, with models such as Interaction Networks, Visual Interaction Networks, and the Neural Physics Engine
[
4
,
13
,
14
]
demonstrating how object relations can be captured to predict physical dynamics from visual inputs. More recent frameworks like I-PHYRE
[
6

further challenge agents to exhibit intuitive physical reasoning, multi-step planning, and in-situ intervention tasks, highlighting the importance of real-time dynamic perception. In addition, benchmarks such as DeepPHY
[
120
]
provide systematic evaluation environments that couple intrinsic attributes with dynamic outcomes, ensuring models are tested on both the static and temporal dimensions of physical understanding. By moving from the recognition of intrinsic object properties to the perception of their dynamic relations, AI systems develop a more comprehensive human-like understanding of physical environments.

C-E

Causal and Counterfactual Inference

Beyond perceiving object dynamics from massive aggregated data, causal and counterfactual inference represents the most sophisticated layer of physical reasoning, which focus on elucidating how underlying mechanisms that govern observed phenomena enable reasoning about hypothetical "what if" scenarios without direct observation. Rather than merely describing temporal event sequences, causal reasoning addresses more fundamental questions such as why events occur and how modifications to underlying conditions would alter seemingly irrelevant outcomes.
For instance, altering gravitational constants would fundamentally change the falling dynamics of objects, exemplifying the advanced reasoning capabilities required to address such counterfactual queries that transcend pattern matching from observational data.
Contemporary methodologies in this domain encompass causal graph modeling
[
23
,
24
]
, intervention-based learning frameworks
[
121

[

Evaluation of these approaches relies on the development of various physical reasoning benchmarks: the Causal3D benchmark
[

provides structured data with corresponding visual representations; the CLEVRER-Humans dataset
[
123

offers video-based reasoning tasks for causal judgment of physical events with human annotations, addressing the need for diverse event types and natural language descriptions; and the recent PhySense benchmark
[
124

emphasizes principle-based reasoning by systematically evaluating whether large language models can apply human-like physical principles.
By capturing latent causal structures, this layer provides interpretable, generalizable, and mechanistically grounded reasoning capabilities, representing the pinnacle of visual physical understanding.

Text-based Benchmarks.

Early benchmarks primarily focused on textbook physics problems in pure textual format, establishing baselines for symbolic manipulation and numerical reasoning.

Representative resources such as PhysicsEval
[

compile problems across multiple education levels and emphasize inference-time techniques, numerical calculation, and robustness under distributional shifts. These benchmarks provide systematic coverage from fundamental mechanics and kinematics to electricity, thermodynamics, and modern physics, enabling evaluation of both single-step calculations and multi-step reasoning chains. Other text-based datasets, including UGPhysics
[
125
]
, PHYBench
[

, GPQA
[

Empirical studies consistently show that even state-of-the-art language models often depend on superficial pattern matching and memorization, with sharp performance degradation on tasks requiring genuine physical reasoning, long multi-step inference, or generalization beyond the training distribution. Such findings highlight the persistent gap between current models and the robust reasoning capabilities expected from human-level or world-model-based physical understanding, motivating future benchmarks that more explicitly target multi-step reasoning, causal inference, and compositional problem-solving.

D-C

Theoretical Physics Solvers

The synergy between artificial intelligence and the physical sciences has evolved into two primary paradigms
[
146
,
2
]
: data-driven scientific discovery and physics-informed learning. These approaches provide the methodological foundation for the advanced reasoning systems discussed previously, each addressing the challenge of integrating physical principles in a distinct way.

Data-driven approaches aim to discover unknown physical laws directly from data. A prominent example is Symbolic Regression (SR), which extracts interpretable mathematical expressions without prior knowledge of the governing equations. This makes SR a powerful tool for generating human-readable models that overcome the "black-box" nature of many neural networks. The primary obstacle for SR is navigating its vast combinatorial search space while ensuring physical plausibility. To this end, recent advancements focus on incorporating hard physical constraints, such as unit consistency, to accelerate the learning process and guide the discovery toward physically meaningful equations
[
147
]
.

On the other hand, physics-informed learning is applied to problems where the governing equations are already established. This paradigm incorporates known physical laws as constraints within neural architectures. The most notable example is Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which embed the governing partial differential equations (PDEs) directly into the neural network’s loss function. This ensures that the model’s solution adheres to fundamental physical principles. This methodology has found widespread application across diverse fields, including the modeling of high-speed and hidden fluid mechanics
[
148
,
149
]
, solving the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
[

[

[

153

[

E
World Modeling

The previous sections have traced two key paths, namely Physical Perception and Physics Reasoning, towards AI systems that perceive and understand the law of physics.
Physical perception embodies the end-to-end learning approach, extracting physical patterns and regularities directly from sensory observations., while physics reasoning encodes scientific knowledge into symbolic representations and theoretical frameworks
Therefore, leveraging physical principles as constraints to construct and predict future states of the world has emerged as a natural idea, which has further evolved into a prominent research direction in frontier AI research, namely the rise of world models.

World Modeling
represents the capability of AI systems to generate, understand and predict the dynamics of simulated environments, enabling a wide range of applications from video generation and scene reconstruction to autonomous planning and decision-making.

Models with world modeling ability have three fundamental advantages for current AI development: (1) reducing the need for supervised data, (2) closing the simulation-to-reality gap, and (3) enabling low-cost, interpretable prediction of future states.
These advantages arise because world models enable agents to learn through interaction with simulated environments rather than relying on massive amounts of data, and they can provide more fine-grained sensory data for embodied agents
[
156
]
.
In this section, we categorize litertures of world models into three dimensions:
(1) Generative Models:
They rely on gradient descent algorithm to learn underlying dynamics of the world from large scale database;
(2) Physics-enhanced Approaches:
They are constructed based on the principles and laws of physics in symbolic representation, combining the principle of physics that govern our physical world with neural networks to simulate changes in world states;
(3) Benchmarks:
They provide the metrics used to evaluate model’s capabilities in qualitative and quantitative estimation for world modeling.

Scene Reconstruction.

Scene reconstruction is a computer vision technique that recovers the three-dimensional geometric structure and appearance information of a scene from two-dimensional images, point clouds, or other sensor data, aiming to construct a complete digital 3D representation of the scene. Unlike the forward process discussed earlier that transforms physical states into visual representations, scene reconstruction is an inverse process that requires inferring complete 3D geometry, physical properties, and scene dynamics from limited observational information.

This requires reconstruction algorithms to possess not only geometric perception capabilities, but also physical reasoning capabilities.
Modern scene reconstruction methods, particularly neural implicit representation-based approaches like PhyRecon

E-B

Physics-enhanced Modeling Approaches.

The core challenge facing existing world models is
insufficient out-of-distribution physical reasoning capability
. While large-scale systems like GAIA-1
[
184
]
and DINO-world
[
185
]
excel at in-distribution tasks and can generate realistic future scenarios, when encountering counterfactual reasoning, novel object interactions, or unfamiliar physical constraints, these models rely on "case-based" rather than "rule-based" generalization, leading to dramatic performance degradation
[
186
]
. Although early physics-based simulation methods can maintain consistency with real-world physics through predefined physics engines
[
26
]

[
27
]
, their reliance on manually designed parameterization limits their generalization capability to unmodeled physical effects.

To achieve genuine physical understanding, world models need to accomplish the following critical tasks: (1) Counterfactual physical reasoning: accurately predicting physical behaviors in unseen scenarios; (2) Novel interaction modeling: handling complex inter-object interactions that did not appear during training; (3) Symbolic-grounded knowledge bridging: connecting abstract physical laws with concrete perceptual experiences; (4) Physical constraint generalization: maintaining reasoning accuracy under new constraint conditions. These tasks require systems to maintain strict adherence to fundamental physical principles while adapting to complex and dynamic real-world environments.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed the concept of ’Neural Physics’, a hybrid framework that preserves fundamental physical laws while using neural networks to learn components that are traditionally difficult to model
[
187
]
. In this way, world models can perform accurate physical reasoning through the combination of physical principles and neural networks. Furthermore, they can create systems capable of bridging symbolic knowledge and embodied experience, representing a hallmark of human-level environmental understanding.

Neural physics methodologies merge explicit physical computations with neural network learning that captures complex material behaviors through data-driven optimization, while preserving end-to-end differentiability. Current approaches can be categorized into three main paradigms:

(1) Neuro-symbolic integration
directly embeds known physical laws into the neural network architecture to ensure strict compliance with fundamental physical principles, with the neural network solely responsible for learning uncertain components that are challenging to model accurately. For example, PhysORD
[
25
]
models vehicles as controlled Lagrangian systems by directly embedding the Euler-Lagrange equations as hard constraints within the network, where the neural network only needs to estimate quantities that are difficult to compute accurately, such as potential energy gradients and external forces. This approach ensures strict enforcement of fundamental physical laws like energy conservation, thereby maintaining good generalization capability even under data-scarce conditions.

(2) Physics-Structured Neural ODEs
retains the mathematical structure of physical equations in the neural ODE framework, where neural networks parameterize various components of the physical equations to enable continuous-time domain modeling. For example, MoSim
[
188
]
decomposes the rigid body dynamics equations into predictor and corrector parts. The predictor strictly follows the mathematical structure of rigid body dynamics, with inertia matrices, gravity terms, and control forces parameterized through specialized neural network modules. The corrector uses standard residual networks to address complex phenomena such as friction and collisions that are challenging to model explicitly. Time integration is performed via neural ODE solvers, enabling the system to maintain both the inherent structure of physical equations and the learning capacity of neural networks.

(3) Differentiable Physics Engines
employs a decoupled architecture in which neural networks specialize in predicting physical properties and interaction parameters from sensor data, with these predictions subsequently input into differentiable classical physics solvers for final system state computation. FusionForce
[
189
]
utilizes deep neural networks to predict terrain physical properties such as geometric shape, friction coefficients, and stiffness, along with robot-terrain contact forces from camera images and LiDAR data. These predictions are subsequently fed into a differentiable rigid body dynamics solver for robot motion trajectory computation. The end-to-end differentiable framework allows the model to refine terrain property predictions by backpropagating trajectory errors.

These methodologies demonstrate how physical reasoning emerges from the synergistic interaction between neural learning and constraints offered by the law of physics, moving beyond pattern memorization toward principled understanding of environmental dynamics.

G
Discussion

The Isolation of Perception and Reasoning.

This survey reveals a fundamental paradox: AI systems achieve superhuman performance on isolated physical tasks, e.g. olympiad-level problem solving, photorealistic video generation, yet lack the flexible, principle-based understanding that allows a child to predict whether stacked blocks will topple. This gap exposes not a quantitative shortfall in data or compute, but a qualitative misalignment between how current systems learn physics and how physical understanding actually works. The four capabilities examined (physical perception, physics reasoning, world modeling and embodied interaction) are not separate research directions but facets of a unified cognitive architecture that current approaches fail to integrate. Physical perception without reasoning remains trapped in correlation; physics reasoning without perceptual grounding produces detached symbolic manipulation; world models without embodied feedback generate plausible but physically inconsistent predictions. Therefore, we advocate that the research community fundamentally rethink how models can mutually inform and constrain these four capabilities through bidirectional coupling, rather than pursuing isolated task improvements.

Sim-to-real Gap.

The transition from simulation to embodied interaction exposes the field’s most critical vulnerability. World models trained on internet-scale video generate visually compelling predictions while systematically violating physical principles, e.g., objects floating without support, collisions without momentum transfer. This fragility stems from optimizing for perceptual plausibility rather than physical consistency. Without embodied consequences to enforce correct physical behavior, systems learn only superficial correlations. We should place greater emphasis on current world models’ simulation-to-reality transfer capabilities, for example, by actively refining physical models based on prediction errors encountered during interactions, treating real-world feedback as an essential component of learning rather than merely an end-to-end deployment task.

Internalizing Natural Laws.

Current approaches reveal a deeper architectural limitation: most systems, including frontier multimodal language models, rely on pattern matching over vast datasets rather than internalizing the compositional, causal structure of the law of physics. This explains why models achieve 50-80% accuracy on in-distribution benchmarks yet fail catastrophically on counterfactual scenarios or novel configurations. Physics is not a collection of statistical regularities but principles like conservation laws, symmetries, causal mechanisms that compose systematically. The path forward demands architectures that encode physical law to overcome inductive bias: differentiable physics engines maintaining hard constraints, neuro symbolic systems integrating learned perception with symbolic reasoning, or embodied learning paradigms acquiring intuition through active intervention rather than passive observation. These architectural innovations provide the possibility for internalizing natural laws. We have reason to believe that, beyond the scaling laws of data, parameter and inference time, a promising path forward may lie in scaling laws grounded in the rules of physical world.

H
Conclusion

This survey presents a comprehensive task-oriented taxonomy of AI systems for physics reasoning, organizing the landscape into four fundamental capabilities: physical perception, physics reasoning, world modeling, and embodied interaction. While our analysis reveals significant progress in specialized domains, most existing systems still rely on statistical pattern recognition rather than genuine physical understanding, with the most promising developments emerging from hybrid approaches that integrate physics-grounded architectures, physics-informed training methodologies, and explicit symbolic reasoning. The field stands at a critical juncture where recent breakthroughs in large language models, Graph Neural Networks, and multimodal systems offer unprecedented opportunities to develop unified frameworks that seamlessly bridge theoretical physics reasoning with embodied physical interaction. As AI systems increasingly operate in complex physical environments, the integration of robust physical reasoning capabilities becomes essential for safety, reliability, and interpretability, requiring continued collaboration between AI researchers, physicists, and domain experts to realize truly physical intelligent systems that comprehend and interact with physical reality.
