DG-Sensitive Pruning & a Complete Classification of DG Trees and Cycles
Hugh Geller, Desiree Martin, Henry Potts-Rubin

Abstract
. Given a squarefree monomial ideal I I of a polynomial ring Q Q , we show that if the minimal free resolution 𝔽 \mathbb{F} of Q / I Q/I admits the structure of a differential graded (dg) algebra, then so does any “pruning” of 𝔽 \mathbb{F} . In the language of combinatorics, this says that if Q / ℱ ⁡ ( Δ ) Q/\mathcal{F}(\Delta) , the quotient of the ambient polynomial ring by the facet ideal ℱ ⁡ ( Δ ) \mathcal{F}(\Delta) of a simplicial complex Δ \Delta , is minimally resolved by a dg algebra, then so is the quotient by the facet ideal of each facet-induced subcomplex of Δ \Delta (over the smaller polynomial ring). Along with techniques from discrete Morse theory and homological algebra, this allows us to give complete classifications of the trees and cycles G G with Q / I ⁡ ( G ) Q/I(G) minimally resolved by a dg algebra in terms of the length of the longest path in G G , where I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) is the edge ideal of G G .



1.
Introduction

Differential graded (dg) algebras are powerful tools in homological commutative algebra. For example, the existence of such structure reduces the difficulty in computing certain cohomologies and can be leveraged to give universal resolutions of modules, such as bar resolutions (see, for example,
[
22
,
25
]
). In
[
6
]
, Boocher et al. use dg algebras to study deviations related to edge ideals, and dg algebras make a significant appearance in the hot-topic study of cohomological support varieties (see, for example,
[
8
]
). Furthermore, commutative algebra is not the only area of mathematics in which one encounters dg algebras. First arising in algebraic topology, their use in homological commutative algebra was spearheaded by Avramov, Buchsbaum, and Eisenbud, to name only a few.

Unfortunately, the question of whether a given resolution admits the structure of a dg algebra is a difficult one. To show existence, one often has to describe the multiplication explicitly. The typical first examples of dg algebras one encounters are the Taylor resolution
[
19
]
and the Koszul complex. Tests for non-existence of dg algebra structure include results due to: Avramov
[
1
]
, in terms of the non-vanishing of kernels of maps between certain Tor modules; Katthän
[
23
]
, in terms of
f
-vectors of simplicial complexes; and Burke
[
10
]
, in terms of higher homotopies and
A_{\infty}
-structures. Notoriously, these obstructions are challenging to compute. In this paper, we contribute to the story of dg algebra structures on resolutions in the world of squarefree monomial ideals.

An active area of research within combinatorial commutative algebra is relating invariants of (finite simple) graphs to algebraic properties of their edge ideals. For example, Fröberg’s Theorem

Our main result (Theorem

) discusses the existence of a dg algebra structure, a notion we now define.

Definition 2.6
.

A
differential graded (dg) algebra
over a ring
Q
is a complex
(\mathcal{A},\partial^{\mathcal{A}})
of free
Q
-modules equipped with a unitary, associative multiplication
\mathcal{A}\otimes_{Q}\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
satisfying

(i)

\mathcal{A}_{i}\mathcal{A}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{i+j}
,

(ii)

a_{i}a_{j}=(-1)^{ij}a_{j}a_{i}
,

(iii)

a_{i}^{2}=0\text{ if $i$ is odd}
,

(iv)

\partial^{\mathcal{A}}(a_{i}a_{j})=\partial^{\mathcal{A}}(a_{i})a_{j}+(-1)^{i}a_{i}\partial^{\mathcal{A}}(a_{j})
,

where
a_{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
. Conditions (i)-(iii) are together called
graded commutativity
, and condition (iv) is called the
Leibniz rule
.

Observation 2.14

Let

Proof.

Units appear in the differential of the Taylor resolution if and only if

for some subset

Definition 2.27

A subcomplex

Remark 2.28
.

Developed separately, the proof of

Theorem 2.29
.

Let
A
be a Morse matching on the Taylor poset of an ideal
I
of

. If
A_{+}
is closed under taking supersets (i.e., whenever

, we have

for all

), then the subcomplex
\mathbb{J}
induced by

is a dg ideal of the Taylor resolution
\mathbb{T}
of

. Thus, the resulting quotient

admits the structure of a differential graded algebra.

Proof.

Suppose
(U,U^{\prime})\in A
, and let

be a basis element. Since

is closed under taking supersets, the multiplication on
\mathbb{T}
(Example

The same logic as above yields that

is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
.
∎

3.
Lyubeznik Edge Ideals

In this section, we show that Lyubeznik edge ideals are dg. The class of Lyubeznik graphs, i.e., graphs with Lyubeznik edge ideal, contains the class of trees of diameter three, and so this section provides part of the classification of dg trees (Theorem
6.1
). In
[
12
]
, graphs with Lyubeznik edge ideals are characterized as the graphs
L(a,b,c)
with vertex set

V=\{x,y\}\cup\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{a}\cup\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{b}\cup\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{c}

and edge set

E=\{\{x,y\}\}\cup\{\{x,x_{i}\}\}_{i=1}^{a}\cup\{\{y,y_{j}\}\}_{j=1}^{b}\cup\{\{x,z_{k}\},\{y,z_{k}\}\}_{k=1}^{c}.

Note that any combination of
a
,
b
, and
c
may be taken to be zero.

Example 3.1
.

Figures

and
2
show the Lyubeznik graphs

and
L(1,1,1)
, respectively.

Equivalent to Definition
2.15
, the Lyubeznik resolutions of a monomial ideal

may be described as follows (see
[

, Theorem 3.2]
and also
[

, the statement of which we reformulate to match our setting).

Theorem 3.2

(Batzies-Welker, 2002)
.

Let

be a total order on
G(I)=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}\}
. Without loss of generality, say
u_{1}<\cdots<u_{t}
. For
\sigma\subseteq G(I)
, set

M_{<}(\sigma)=\min\{u_{q}\in G(I)\colon u_{q}\textup{ divides }\lcm(\{u_{q+1},\dots,u_{t}\}\cap\sigma)\}.

Then the set

A(<):=\{(\sigma\cup M_{<}(\sigma),\sigma\setminus M_{<}(\sigma))\colon\sigma\subseteq G(I)\textup{ such that }M_{<}(\sigma)\neq\infty\}.

is a Morse matching inducing the Lyubeznik resolution
\mathbb{L}_{<}
of

.

As we have noted, different total orders need not induce the same Lyubeznik resolution of

. For the graphs

, the orders

is the minimal

.

Theorem 3.4

(Chau-Há-Maithani, 2024)
.

For total orders
<

in which

is the minimal

We track the drops in a Morse matching for a Lyubeznik resolution to reveal the Betti numbers of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
.

Corollary 3.5
.

The
i
th Betti number of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
is

\beta^{Q}_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&i=0,\\
\\
a+b+2c+1,&i=1,\\
\\
\displaystyle{\binom{a+c+1}{i}}+{\binom{b+c+1}{i}},&i\geq 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

We count the number of times an element drops from a source in the column of the Taylor poset corresponding to homological degree
i
,
i\geq 2
, in the Morse matching
A(<)
from Theorem
3.2
.

Note that monomials corresponding to leaves cannot drop, since each is divisible by a unique variable. Without loss of generality, if
xz_{k}
drops from
\sigma
, then
x\beta,yz_{k}\in\sigma
, where
\beta
is a variable such that
x\beta\in G(I(L(a,b,c)))
, whence
xy
divides
m_{\sigma}
. That is, the only element ever dropped in
A(<)
is
xy
. Moreover, whenever
xy
has the potential to drop, it does. Define

K_{i}:={\binom{a+b+2c}{i-1}}-{\binom{a+c}{i-1}}-{\binom{b+c}{i-1}}.

This value tells us how many
i
-element subsets of
G(I(L(a,b,c)))
drop
xy
: there are
{\binom{a+b+2c}{i-1}}
subsets containing
xy
, and in order for one of them, say
S
, to drop
xy
, we cannot take the other
i-1
elements in
S
to be either all
x\beta_{1}
(respectively,
y\beta_{2}
), since then
y
(respectively,
x
) would not divide the lcm of the elements in
S\backslash\{xy\}
. Thus, we subtract off these situations.

The
i
th Betti number of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
is thus equal to the number of
i
-element subsets of
G(I(L(a,b,c)))
minus the number of
i
-element subsets of
G(I(L(a,b,c)))
that drop
xy
minus the number of
(i+1)
-element subsets of
G(I(L(a,b,c)))
that drop
xy
, since both
A(<)_{+}
and
A(<)_{-}
contribute to the ranks of the free modules in the induced resolution (see the paragraph before Example
2.26
). That is,

\beta^{Q}_{i}={\binom{a+b+2c+1}{i}}-(K_{i}+K_{i+1}).

By Pascal’s Identity,

\beta^{Q}_{i}={\binom{a+c+1}{i}}+{\binom{b+c+1}{i}}

for
i\geq 2
. The cases
i=0
and
i=1
are clear.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we get the projective dimension of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
.

Corollary 3.6
.

The projective dimension of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
over
Q
is the maximum of
a+c+1
and
b+c+1
.

We note that the minimal free resolution of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
is linear, and so Corollary
3.5
provides not only the Betti numbers of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
, but the graded Betti numbers.

Remark 3.7
.

Lyubeznik graphs are examples of
split
graphs, i.e., graphs whose vertices may be partitioned into a clique and an independent set (take either
\{x,y\}
if there are no cycles or a triangle if one exists as the clique; the rest of the vertices form an independent set). Split graphs are cochordal (see, for example
[
14
, Proposition 3.10]
), and cochordal graphs are precisely the graphs with edge ideal having a linear resolution
[
17
]
.

By analyzing the Morse matching taken to produce the minimal free resolution
\mathbb{L}_{<}
of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
, we deduce that
\mathbb{L}_{<}
admits the structure of a dg algebra.

Theorem 3.8
.

Lyubeznik edge ideals are dg.

Proof.

The only element dropped in the Morse matching
A(<)
, where
<
is an order in which
xy
is taken least among all minimal generators of
G(I(L(a,b,c)))
, is
xy
. Thus, if
xy
drops from
\sigma
, then
xy
drops from
\tau\supseteq\sigma
. That is,
A(<)_{+}
is closed under taking supersets. The matching
A(<)
induces the minimal free resolution of
Q/I(L(a,b,c))
by Theorem
3.4
, and by Theorem
2.29
,
I(L(a,b,c))
is dg.
∎

The class of Lyubeznik graphs contains the class of diameter-three trees, and so we get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be a tree of diameter three. The minimal free resolution of
Q/I(\Gamma)
admits the structure of a differential graded algebra.

Proof.

Diameter-three trees are precisely the Lyubeznik graphs
L(a,b,0)
with
a,b\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

The Betti numbers and projective dimension of powers of edge ideals of trees of diameter three are computed in

, where they are called “whisker graphs.” The proof uses different methods, as the story there is about linear quotients. In

, edge ideals of trees of diameter three are shown to be minimally resolved by their Scarf complexes.

Lemma 4.3

Let

Proof.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let

. If neither of the multisets

has repeated elements and

\sigma(V,W)=\sigma(V_{z},W_{z})

\displaystyle\sigma(V,W)

\displaystyle=\left|\{(v,w)\in V\times W:v>w\}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\{(v_{z},w_{z})\in V_{z}\times W_{z}:v_{z}>w_{z}\}\right|

\displaystyle=\sigma(V_{z},W_{z}).\qed

Notation 4.9

Recall that if

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\emptyset\neq W\subseteq G(J)
. The chain map
\Phi:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{F}
is given by
\Phi_{0}(1)=z
and
\Phi(g_{W})=y_{W}f_{W_{z}}
, where
f_{W_{z}}=0
if there exists some
i
such that
zx_{i}
occurs more than once in
W_{z}
.

Proof.

We first consider elements of
\mathbb{G}_{1}
, which has basis
\{g_{\{x_{i}y_{i,j}\}}:x_{i}y_{i,j}\in J\}
. In this case, we find

\displaystyle\partial^{\mathbb{F}}_{1}\!\left(\Phi_{1}\!\left(g_{\{x_{i}y_{i,j}\}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=y_{i,j}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}_{1}\!\left(f_{\{zx_{i}\}}\right)

\displaystyle=zx_{i}y_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}\!\left(x_{i}y_{i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}\!\left(\partial^{\mathbb{G}}_{1}\!\left(g_{\{x_{i}y_{i,j}\}}\right)\right).

That is,
\partial^{\mathbb{F}}_{1}\circ\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{0}\circ\partial^{\mathbb{G}}_{1}
. We now consider higher degrees.

First, consider
W\subseteq G(J)
such that the multiset
W_{z}
has no repeated elements. In this case, we have
f_{W_{z}}\neq 0
. If any subset of
W_{z}
contains repeated elements, then we find
W_{z}
also contains repeated elements, contradicting our initial assumption. Thus, for any
w_{z}\in W_{z}
we also have
f_{W_{z}\backslash w_{z}}\neq 0
. We now compute the following, recalling that
x_{W}=x_{W_{z}}
:

\displaystyle\partial^{\mathbb{F}}_{|W_{z}|}\!\left(\Phi_{|W|}\!\left(g_{W}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=y_{W}\partial_{|W_{z}|}^{\mathbb{F}}\!\left(f_{W_{z}}\right)

\displaystyle=y_{W}\sum_{w_{z}\in W_{z}}(-1)^{\sigma(w_{z},W_{z})}\frac{m_{W_{z}}}{m_{W_{z}\backslash w_{z}}}f_{W_{z}\backslash w_{z}}

\displaystyle=y_{W}\sum_{w_{z}\in W_{z}}(-1)^{\sigma(w_{z},W_{z})}\frac{zx_{W_{z}}}{zx_{W_{z}\backslash w_{z}}}f_{W_{z}\backslash w_{z}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in W}(-1)^{\sigma(w,W)}\frac{x_{W}y_{W}}{x_{W\backslash w}y_{W\backslash w}}\left(y_{W\backslash w}f_{W_{z}\backslash w_{z}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in W}(-1)^{\sigma(w,W)}\frac{m_{W}}{m_{W\backslash w}}\Phi_{|W|-1}\!\left(g_{W\backslash w}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{|W|-1}\!\left(\partial^{\mathbb{G}}_{|W|}\left(g_{W}\right)\right).

We see that we have commutativity when
W_{z}
does not have repeated elements and turn our attention to when there are repeated elements.

In the case that
W_{z}
has repeated elements, we defined
f_{W_{z}}=0
and find
\Phi(g_{W})=0
. This then yields
\partial^{\mathbb{F}}_{|W_{z}|}\!\left(\Phi_{|W|}\!(g_{W})\right)=0
. Therefore, to finish the proof that
\Phi
is a chain map, we prove that
\Phi_{|W|-1}\!\left(\partial^{\mathbb{G}}_{|W|}\!(g_{W})\right)=0
whenever
W_{z}
has repeats. To do this, we note that
W_{z}
has repeated element
zx_{i}
if and only if there exist distinct
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
such that
x_{i}y_{i,j_{1}},x_{i}y_{i,j_{2}}\in W
. For simplicity, and without loss of generality, we re-index so that we are considering the case of
x_{1}y_{1,1},x_{1}y_{1,2}\in W
. We find

\partial^{\mathbb{G}}_{|W|}(g_{W})=y_{1,1}g_{W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,1}}-y_{1,2}g_{W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,2}}+\sum_{w\in W\backslash\{x_{1}y_{1,1},x_{1}y_{1,2}\}}(-1)^{\sigma(w,W)}\frac{m_{W}}{m_{W\backslash w}}g_{W\backslash w}.

Since
x_{1}y_{1,1},x_{1}y_{1,2}\in W\backslash w
for all the terms in the final summation notation, applying
\Phi
to the summation yields 0 and thus

\displaystyle\Phi_{|W|-1}\!\left(\partial^{\mathbb{G}}_{|W|}(g_{W})\right)

\displaystyle=y_{1,1}\Phi_{|W|-1}\left(g_{W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,1}}\right)-y_{1,2}\Phi_{|W|-1}\left(g_{W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,2}}\right)

\displaystyle=y_{1,1}y_{W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,1}}f_{(W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,1})_{z}}-y_{1,2}y_{W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,2}}f_{(W\backslash x_{1}y_{1,2})_{z}}

\displaystyle=y_{1,1}\frac{y_{W}}{y_{1,1}}f_{W_{z}\backslash x_{1}z}-y_{1,2}\frac{y_{W}}{y_{1,2}}f_{W_{z}\backslash x_{1}z}

\displaystyle=0.

Thus,
\partial_{i}^{\mathbb{F}}\circ\Phi_{i}=\Phi_{i-1}\circ\partial_{i}^{\mathbb{G}}
, and so
\Phi
is a chain map lifting multiplication by
z
.
∎

, i.e.,

\beta_{i,j}^{Q}(I(\Gamma))=\beta_{i,j}^{Q}(I)+\beta_{i,j}^{Q}(J)+\beta_{i-1,j}^{Q}(I\cap J).

Proof.

The formula for the graded Betti numbers of
Q/I(\Gamma)
follows from Remark
4.12
with the extra twist on the last term coming from
\mu^{z}
being multiplication by
z
. We now deduce the Betti splitting by considering the following two cases.
For
i=0
,

\displaystyle\beta_{0,j}^{Q}(I(\Gamma))

\displaystyle=\beta_{1,j}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{I(\Gamma)}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{1,j}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{I}\right)+\beta_{1,j}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{J}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0,j}^{Q}\left(I\right)+\beta_{0,j}^{Q}\left(J\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0,j}^{Q}\left(I\right)+\beta_{0,j}^{Q}\left(J\right)+\underset{=0}{\underbrace{\beta_{-1,j}^{Q}\left(I\cap J\right)}}.

For
i>0
,

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}^{Q}(I(\Gamma))

\displaystyle=\beta_{i+1,j}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{I(\Gamma)}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i+1,j}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{I}\right)+\beta_{i+1,j}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{J}\right)+\beta_{i,j-1}^{Q}\left(\frac{Q}{J}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i,j}^{Q}\left(I\right)+\beta_{i,j}^{Q}\left(J\right)+\beta_{i-1,j-1}^{Q}\left(J\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i,j}^{Q}\left(I\right)+\beta_{i,j}^{Q}\left(J\right)+\beta_{i-1,j}^{Q}\left(zJ\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i,j}^{Q}\left(I\right)+\beta_{i,j}^{Q}\left(J\right)+\beta_{i-1,j}^{Q}\left(I\cap J\right).\qed

Remark 4.15
.

While we previously addressed the case of trees with diameter less than four, we note that this case is encapsulated by Theorem
4.11
. Diameter-three trees are those for which, up to reindexing,
y_{i,j}
for all
i>1
and
y_{1,1}\neq 0
(see Equation

1

).

For a diameter two tree, we have
y_{i,j}=0
for all
i
and
j
. This results in
\mathbb{G}=Q
and
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}=0
, and thus
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)=\mathbb{F}
. This also holds for a diameter one tree, though this case is rather unexciting since the edge ideal is simply
(x_{1}z)
.

We now turn our attention to the dg structure of
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)
. Since
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{G}
are each Taylor resolutions, they are both dg algebras. To capitalize on their dg structures, we first define the following family of linear maps.

Definition 4.16
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{j}
such that either
j>1
or
\partial^{\mathbb{F}}\!(f)=x_{i}z
for some
i
. We define
\omega_{f}:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
by

\omega_{f}(g_{1}):=\begin{cases}-x_{i}g_{1},&\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f)=x_{i}z,\\
0,&|f|>1,\end{cases}

where
|f|
is the homological degree of
f
.

We extend
\omega_{f}
to a linear map for any
f\in\mathbb{F}_{>0}
by defining

\omega_{uf_{1}+vf_{2}}(g):=u\omega_{f_{1}}(g)+v\omega_{f_{2}}(g)

for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
. We define
\omega_{1}(g)=0
for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
.

We use the map
\omega_{f}
in the definition of a product structure on
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)
.

Definition 4.17
.

We define the following ten products on
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)
:

(0)

\displaystyle(q,0,0)\cdot(f_{1},g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle:=q(f_{1},g_{1},g_{2})=(qf_{1},qg_{1},qg_{2})\text{ for all }q\in Q,

(1)

\displaystyle(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle:=(f_{1}f_{2},0,0),

(2)

\displaystyle(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})\right),

(3)

\displaystyle(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(0,0,g_{2})

\displaystyle:=(0,0,0),

(4)

\displaystyle(0,g_{1},0)\cdot(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle:=(0,g_{1}g_{2},0),

(5)

\displaystyle(0,g_{1},0)\cdot(0,0,g_{2})

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{|g_{1}|}(0,0,g_{1}g_{2}),

(6)

\displaystyle(0,0,g_{1})\cdot(0,0,g_{2})

\displaystyle:=(0,0,0),

(7)

\displaystyle(0,g_{1},0)\cdot(f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{2},0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|}\omega_{f_{2}}(g_{1})\right),

(8)

\displaystyle(0,0,g_{1})\cdot(f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle:=(0,0,0),

(9)

\displaystyle(0,0,g_{1})\cdot(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle:=(0,0,g_{1}g_{2}).

Remark 4.18
.

If
(f,g_{1},g_{2}),(f^{\prime},g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)_{>0}
, then Definition
4.17
and the linearity of the direct sum combine to show the general product
(f,g_{1},g_{2})(f^{\prime},g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}^{\prime})
can be written as

\left(ff^{\prime}+\frac{1}{z}f\Phi(g_{1}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f^{\prime},g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime},\omega_{f}(g_{1}^{\prime})+(-1)^{|g_{1}|}g_{1}g_{2}^{\prime}+(-1)^{|g_{1}|}\omega_{f^{\prime}}(g_{1})+g_{2}g_{1}^{\prime}\right).

While a single expression initially seems preferable, it becomes cumbersome in our later proofs. As such, our proofs utilize a case-by-case approach to the product structure.

The next few results and definitions provide the tools needed to show that the product in Definition
4.17
yields a dg algebra structure on
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)
. The following lemma is meant to assuage any apprehension about the presence of
1/z
in our product definitions.

Lemma 4.19
.

If
f\in\mathbb{F}_{i}
and
g\in\mathbb{G}_{j}
, then
\frac{1}{z}f\Phi(g)\in\mathbb{F}_{i+j}
.

Proof.

Since
g\in\mathbb{G}_{j}
and
\Phi:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{F}
is a chain map, we have
\Phi(g)\in\mathbb{F}_{j}
. It then follows that
f\Phi(g)\in\mathbb{F}_{i+j}
. Thus, we need only address the presence of
1/z
.

Without loss of generality, suppose
f=f_{V}
and
g=g_{W}
for some
V\subseteq G(I)
and
W\subseteq G(J)
. By Proposition
4.10
, we have
\Phi(g_{W})=y_{W}f_{W_{z}}
, where
f_{W_{z}}=0
if
W_{z}
has repeated elements. In that case, we have

\frac{1}{z}f_{V}\Phi(g_{W})=\frac{y_{W}}{z}f_{V}\cdot 0=0.

On the other hand, if
W_{z}
has no repeated elements, then

\frac{1}{z}f_{V}\Phi(g_{W})=\frac{y_{W}}{z}f_{V}f_{W_{z}}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\sigma(V,W_{z})}y_{W}f_{V\cup W_{z}},&V\cap W_{z}=\emptyset,\\
0,&V\cap W_{z}\neq\emptyset.\end{cases}

That is, we either have
\frac{1}{z}f_{V}\Phi(g_{W})=y_{W}f_{V\cup W_{z}}
or
\frac{1}{z}f_{V}\Phi(g_{W})=0
. Since the product is well-defined in all cases, we have
\frac{1}{z}f_{V}\Phi(g_{W})\in\mathbb{F}_{i+j}
. The result extends to any
f
and
g
by the linearity of
\Phi
.
∎

To prove the products in Definition
4.17
satisfy the remaining properties of a dg algebra product, we introduce two lemmas. The first deepens our understanding of
\Phi
and shows that while it is not a dg morphism, it has a predictable interaction with the multiplicative structures of
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{G}
.

Lemma 4.20
.

The chain map
\Phi
is not a dg morphism, but for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbb{G}
, one has

z\Phi(g_{1}g_{2})=\Phi(g_{1})\Phi(g_{2})

or, equivalently,
\Phi(g_{1}g_{2})=\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})\Phi(g_{2})
.

Proof.

Since
\Phi
is
Q
-linear, it suffices to show the result for basis elements of
\mathbb{G}
. For that reason, we consider nonempty
V,W\subseteq G(J)
and prove that
z\Phi(g_{V}g_{W})=\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})
. We start by assuming
\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})=0
and consider three cases.

For the first case, suppose
\Phi(g_{V})=0
. Recall from Proposition
4.10
that this means that there exists some index
i
such that
zx_{i}
occurs more than once in the multiset
V_{z}
. That is, there exist two distinct indices
j
and
\ell
such that
x_{i}y_{i,j},x_{i}y_{i,\ell}\in V\subseteq V\cup W
. This tells us that
zx_{i}
occurs at least twice in the multiset
(V\cup W)_{z}
and thus

\Phi(g_{V}g_{W})\in\Span_{Q}(f_{(V\cup W)_{z}})=\{0\}.

That is, if
\Phi(g_{V})=0
then
\Phi(g_{V}g_{W})=0
.

For the second case, suppose that
\Phi(g_{W})=0
. Using the same argument as the first case, we find that
\Phi(g_{V}g_{W})=0
.

For the third and final case of vanishing, suppose that
\Phi(g_{V})\neq 0\neq\Phi(g_{W})
but

\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})=\left(y_{V}f_{V_{z}}\right)\left(y_{W}f_{W_{z}}\right)=y_{V}y_{W}f_{V_{z}}f_{W_{z}}=0.

Since
\Phi(g_{V})\neq 0\neq\Phi(g_{W})
, the multisets
V_{z}
and
W_{z}
do not contain repeated elements and so can be viewed as sets instead of multisets. Moreover, since
\mathbb{F}
is a Taylor resolution, the vanishing of this product means that
V_{z}\cap W_{z}\neq\emptyset
. That is, there exists some
i
such that
zx_{i}\in V_{z}\cap W_{z}
. This tells us that there must exist indices
j
and
\ell
such that
x_{i}y_{i,j}\in V
and
x_{i}y_{i,\ell}\in W
. If
j=\ell
, then
x_{i}y_{i,j}\in V\cap W
and thus
g_{V}g_{W}=0\in\ker\Phi
. If
j\neq\ell
, then
zx_{i}
occurs twice in the multiset
(V\cup W)_{z}
and thus
g_{V}g_{W}\in\ker\Phi
.

We see that whenever
\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})=0
, we also have
\Phi(g_{V}g_{W})=0
. To complete the proof, we now assume that
\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})\neq 0
. From our previous three cases, this means that neither
V_{z}
nor
W_{z}
contain repeated elements and so can be viewed as sets, as opposed to multisets. Furthermore,
\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})\neq 0
means that
V_{z}\cap W_{z}=\emptyset
, and so Lemma
4.8
tells us that
\sigma(V,W)=\sigma(V_{z},W_{z})
.

We note that
V_{z}\cap W_{z}
gives the repeated elements in
(V\cup W)_{z}
. Since
V_{z}\cap W_{z}=\emptyset
, there are no repeated elements in
(V\cup W)_{z}
and thus
(V\cup W)_{z}=V_{z}\cup W_{z}
. We are now able to observe the following:

\displaystyle\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})

\displaystyle=y_{V}y_{W}f_{V_{z}}f_{W_{z}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma(V_{z},W_{z})}y_{V}y_{W}\frac{m_{V_{z}}m_{W_{z}}}{m_{V_{z}\cup W_{z}}}f_{V_{z}\cup W_{z}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma(V,W)}y_{V}y_{W}\frac{zx_{V}zx_{W}}{zx_{V\cup W}}f_{(V\cup W)_{z}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma(V,W)}z\frac{x_{V}y_{V}x_{W}y_{W}}{x_{V\cup W}y_{V\cup W}}y_{V\cup W}f_{(V\cup W)_{z}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma(V,W)}z\frac{m_{V}m_{W}}{m_{V\cup W}}y_{V\cup W}f_{(V\cup W)_{z}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sigma(V,W)}z\frac{m_{V}m_{W}}{m_{V\cup W}}\Phi(g_{V\cup W})

\displaystyle=z\Phi(g_{V}g_{W}).

Rearranging, one obtains the equivalent form
\Phi(g_{V}g_{W})=\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{V})\Phi(g_{W})
.
∎

Our next lemma is necessary to understand how
\partial^{\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)}
interacts with the product structure.

Lemma 4.21
.

Suppose
f\in\mathbb{F}
and
g\in\mathbb{G}
. If
|f|>0
, then

\left(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f),0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f)\Phi(g),0,0\right)&|f|>1,\\
(0,\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f)g,0)&|f|=1\end{cases}.

Proof.

Since
\partial^{\mathbb{F}}
is
Q
-linear, it suffices to consider
f_{V}\in\mathbb{F}
where
V\subseteq G(I)
with
V\neq\emptyset
. In the case of
|V|=1
, the result follows from multiplication by scalars.

Next, suppose
|V|=2
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
V=\{x_{1}z,x_{2}z\}
. In this case, we observe the following:

\displaystyle\left(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{V}),0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)

\displaystyle=x_{2}\left(f_{\{x_{1}z\}},0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)-x_{1}\left(f_{\{x_{2}z\}},0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)

\displaystyle=x_{2}\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{\{x_{1}z\}}\Phi(g),0,-x_{1}g\right)-x_{1}\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{\{x_{2}z\}}\Phi(g),0,-x_{2}g\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\left(x_{2}f_{\{x_{1}z\}}-x_{1}f_{\{x_{2}z\}}\right)\Phi(g),0,-x_{1}x_{2}g+x_{1}x_{2}g\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{V})\Phi(g),0,0\right).

Now suppose
|V|>2
, and compute the following:

\displaystyle\left(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{V}),0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}(-1)^{\sigma(v,V)}\frac{m_{V}}{m_{V\backslash v}}\left(f_{V\backslash v},0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}(-1)^{\sigma(v,V)}\frac{m_{V}}{m_{V\backslash v}}\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{V\backslash v}\Phi(g),0,0\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\left(\sum_{v\in V}(-1)^{\sigma(v,V)}\frac{m_{V}}{m_{V\backslash v}}f_{V\backslash v}\right)\Phi(g),0,0\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{V})\Phi(g),0,0\right).

Since
|V|\geq 1
if and only if
V\neq\emptyset
, the proof is complete.
∎

Using Lemmas
4.20
and
4.21
, we can now prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.22
.

The product in Definition
4.17
is graded commutative, associative, and satisfies the Leibniz rule. Consequently, the product defines a differential graded algebra structure on
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)
.

Proof.

Due to the computational nature of the proof that the product is graded commutative, we relegate it to Appendix
A
.

Associativity can be proved using
4^{3}=64
cases. Of the 64 cases, 37 include at least one term of the form
(q,0,0)
where
q\in\mathbb{F}_{0}=Q
. Those 37 cases automatically hold since multiplication by
(q,0,0)
is the same as multiplication by
q
. Of the remaining cases, 17 of them vanish and we show the final 10 in Appendix
B
.

The Leibniz rule can be shown by investigating
4^{2}=16
cases. Of the 16 cases, 7 include at least one term of the form
(q,0,0)
where
q\in\mathbb{F}_{0}=Q
. Those 7 cases automatically hold since multiplication by
(q,0,0)
is the same as multiplication by
q
. Due to the computational nature of the proof, we relegate the remaining 9 cases to Appendix
C
.

∎

We now apply Theorem
4.22
to the case of edge ideals of trees.

Corollary 4.23
.

The edge ideal of any tree of diameter four is minimally resolved by a differential graded algebra.

Proof.

Theorem
4.11
tells us that the edge ideal of any tree of diameter four is minimally resolved by
\operatorname{Cone}\left({\Psi}\right)
while Theorem
4.22
tells us the resolution has the structure of a dg algebra.
∎

5.
The Pruning Process

In this section, we describe a process of “pruning” the minimal free resolution
\mathbb{F}
of
Q/I
, for any monomial ideal
I
, to the minimal free resolution of a smaller monomial ideal over a smaller polynomial ring (Definition
5.4
). We show that when
I
is squarefree, a dg algebra structure on
\mathbb{F}
descends to a dg algebra structure on the “pruned” resolution (Theorem
5.7
, Corollary
5.8
). In the language of combinatorics, dg-sensitive pruning implies that if the minimal free resolution of the quotient of a polynomial ring by the facet ideal of a simplicial complex
\Delta
admits the structure of a dg algebra, then so does the minimal free resolution of the quotient by the facet ideal of each facet-induced subcomplex of
\Delta
over the smaller polynomial ring (Corollary
5.13
). This allows us to conclude, for example, that induced subgraphs of dg graphs are dg (Corollary
5.14
), which is used to complete the classifications of dg trees and cycles in Section
6
.

In the case of a squarefree monomial ideal
I
, Katthän provides the following structure theorem for the minimal free resolution
\mathbb{F}
of
Q/I
when
\mathbb{F}
admits the structure of a dg algebra
[
23
, Theorem 3.6]
.

Theorem 5.1

(Katthän, 2019)
.

Let
I
be a squarefree monomial ideal, and suppose that the minimal
Q
-free resolution
\mathbb{F}
of
Q/I
admits the structure of a differential graded algebra. Then, there is an isomorphism of differential graded algebras
\mathbb{F}\cong\mathbb{T}/\mathbb{J}
, where
\mathbb{T}
is the Taylor resolution of
Q/I
and
\mathbb{J}
is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
.

Theorem
5.1
plays a key role in showing that the “pruning process” (Definition
5.4
) is “dg-sensitive.” We now introduce a dg ideal
\mathbb{I}
of the Taylor resolution which combines with the dg ideal
\mathbb{J}
from Theorem
5.1
to later formalize the notion of “pruning.”

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal of a polynomial ring
Q
in which
x_{k}
is a variable. Let
\mathbb{T}
be the Taylor resolution of
Q/I
. The subcomplex
\mathbb{I}
of
\mathbb{T}
given by

\mathbb{I}:=\bigoplus 0\to Qe_{V}\to Q\partial(e_{V})\to 0,

where the direct sum is taken over all
V\subseteq G(I)
such that
V
contains a monomial divisible by
x_{k}
, is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

For a basis element
e_{U}
of
\mathbb{T}
, we assume that
e_{U}e_{V}\neq 0
and notice that
e_{U}e_{V}
is a multiple of
e_{U\cup V}
. Since
U\cup V
contains a monomial divisible by
x_{k}
, we have that
e_{U\cup V}\in\mathbb{I}
. So,
e_{U}e_{V}\in\mathbb{I}
. Using the Leibniz rule,

\partial(e_{U}e_{V})=\partial(e_{U})e_{V}+(-1)^{|U|}e_{U}\partial(e_{V}).

Since
e_{U}e_{V}
is in
\mathbb{I}
, so is
\partial(e_{U}e_{V})
. By an argument similar to the above,
\partial(e_{U})e_{V}\in\mathbb{I}
. Thus,
e_{U}\partial(e_{V})
is in
\mathbb{I}
, and we have that
\mathbb{I}
is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
.

∎

The image of a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
in a quotient of
\mathbb{T}
by a dg ideal is again a dg ideal.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{J}
be a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
. Set
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{T}/\mathbb{J}
, and let
\pi:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{F}
be the quotient map. Then, the subcomplex
\pi(\mathbb{I})
of
\mathbb{F}
is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{F}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
from Lemma
5.2
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{J}
is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
,
\pi
is a dg morphism. Since
\mathbb{I}
is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{T}
and
\pi
is a surjective dg morphism, we have

\pi(\mathbb{I})=\pi(\mathbb{I}\mathbb{T})=\pi(\mathbb{I})\pi(\mathbb{T})=\pi(\mathbb{I})\mathbb{F}.

Thus,
\pi(\mathbb{I})
is a dg ideal of
\mathbb{F}
.
∎

We now describe a process, the “pruning process,” which we use to show that simplicial complexes with non-dg facet-induced subcomplexes cannot themselves be dg. The following definition/algorithm comes from
[
7
]
.

Definition 5.4

(Boocher, 2012)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a complex of free
Q
-modules, where
Q
is a polynomial ring, with choice of bases so that each differential is a matrix
A_{i}
. Let
Z
be a subset of the variables of
Q
. The
pruning of
\mathbb{F}
with respect to
Z
is the complex
P(\mathbb{F},Z)
of
Q/\langle Z\rangle
-modules obtained from
\mathbb{F}
by the following algorithm:

(1)

Let
i=1
.

(2)

For
i\leq\max\{j\mid\mathbb{F}_{j}\neq 0\}
, do:

(a)

In the matrix
A_{i}
, set all variables in
Z
equal to zero. Set
A_{i}
equal to this new matrix, and set
U
equal to the set indexing which columns of
A_{i}
are identically zero.

(b)

Replace
\{A_{i+1},\mathbb{F}_{i},A_{i}\}
with new maps and modules obtained by deleting rows, basis elements, and columns, respectively, corresponding to
U
.

(3)

Let
i=i+1
.

As an example, we prune the minimal
Q
-free resolution of
Q/I(L(1,1,1))
to the minimal
Q/(y_{1})
-free resolution of
\dfrac{Q/(y_{1})}{I(L(1,1,0))}
.

Example 5.5
.

Let
Q=\Bbbk[x,y,x_{1},y_{1},z_{1}]
. The minimal
Q
-free resolution
\mathbb{F}
of
Q/I(L(1,1,1))
is

0\xrightarrow{}Q^{2}\xrightarrow{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}y_{1}&0\\
0&x_{1}\\
0&-z_{1}\\
-z_{1}&0\\
x&0\\
0&y\end{smallmatrix}\right]}Q^{6}\xrightarrow{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-z_{1}&-z_{1}&-x_{1}&-y_{1}&0&0\\
x&0&0&0&-y_{1}&0\\
0&y&0&0&0&-x_{1}\\
0&0&y&0&0&z_{1}\\
0&0&0&x&z_{1}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}Q^{5}\xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}xy&yz_{1}&xz_{1}&xx_{1}&yy_{1}\end{bmatrix}}Q\xrightarrow{}0.

We prune by setting
y_{1}
equal to zero. The first loop of the pruning process deletes the fifth column of the first differential and thus the fifth row of the second differential, yielding the following sequence:

0\xrightarrow{}Q^{2}\xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}&0\\
0&x_{1}\\
0&-z_{1}\\
-z_{1}&0\\
x&0\\

Theorem 5.6

(Boocher, 2012)
.

Let

over

We now show that Boocher’s pruning process is dg-sensitive in the case of squarefree monomial ideals.

Theorem 5.7
.

Fix the following notation:

•

Q=\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

•

I
is a squarefree monomial ideal of
Q

•

\mathbb{F}
is the minimal
Q
-free resolution of
Q/I

•

R^{\prime}=\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}]=Q/(x_{k})

•

I^{\prime}=I/(x_{k})

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
admits the structure of a dg algebra over
Q
, so that
\mathbb{F}\cong\mathbb{T}/\mathbb{J}
for some dg ideal
\mathbb{J}
of
\mathbb{T}
(see Theorem
5.1
). Applying Boocher’s pruning process (Definition
5.4
) with

, we have that
P(\mathbb{F},Z)
, the minimal
R
-free resolution of
R/I^{\prime}

\mathbb{F}
by modding out by the image of the dg ideal

from Lemma

under the quotient map
\mathbb{T}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{F}\cong\mathbb{T}/\mathbb{J}
and tensoring down to

. Thus, the minimal free resolution

of

over

admits the structure of a differential graded algebra.

Proof.

We break down the proof into steps.

Step 1: generate
\mathbb{I}
by pruning
x_{k}
.
The differentials we list in what follows are those of
\mathbb{T}
. Set
x_{k}
equal to zero for pruning. In
\partial_{0}
, the columns with entries divisible by
x_{k}
are deleted. The corresponding rows of
\partial_{1}
are also deleted, killing the terms of the images of the
e_{W}
,
|W|=2
, that are supported on
e_{V}
for some
V\subseteq G(I)
containing a monomial divisible by
x_{k}
.

The columns of
\partial_{i}
corresponding to basis elements
e_{W}
, where
W
contains a monomial divisible by
x_{k}
, have nonzero entries corresponding to
e_{W^{\prime}}
, where
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
with
|W^{\prime}|=|W|-1
. The entries divisible by
x_{k}
go to zero in the quotient. The other entries correspond to those
W^{\prime}
containing monomials divisible by
x_{k}
. The columns of
\partial_{i-1}
corresponding to these

are deleted in the previous step, and thus these rows in
\partial_{i}
are also deleted, leaving a column of zeros in
\partial_{i}
.

The columns of
\partial_{i}
corresponding to basis elements
e_{W}
, where
W
does not contain a monomial divisible by
x_{k}
, has nonzero, not-divisible-by-
x_{k}
entries before deleting rows. But the rows in which these entries appear correspond to columns in
\partial_{i-1}
corresponding to
e_{W^{\prime}}
,
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
,
|W^{\prime}|=|W|-1
, satisfying that no monomial in
W^{\prime}
is divisible by
x_{k}
. So, these columns are not deleted by the previous step, and thus their corresponding rows in
\partial_{i}
are not deleted.

The columns and rows crossed out by pruning are the columns and rows corresponding to basis elements
e_{W}
of
\mathbb{T}
with

containing a monomial divisible by
x_{k}
. That is, we are setting

0\to Qe_{W}\to Q\partial(e_{W})\to 0

equal to zero for each such

, and thus the sum of these short exact sequences generate the dg ideal
\mathbb{I}
from Lemma
5.2
.

Step 2: tensoring with
R
yields a new Taylor resolution
.

Upon tensoring
\mathbb{T}/\mathbb{I}
with

, we get

\mathbb{T}/\mathbb{I}\otimes_{Q}R\cong\dfrac{\mathbb{T}\otimes_{Q}R}{\mathbb{I}\otimes_{Q}R},

which is the Taylor resolution on the minimal generators of

.

Step 3: modding out by

yields

, where

is generated by elements of

. Thus,

\frac{\dfrac{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{I}}\otimes_{Q}R}{\dfrac{\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{I}}{\mathbb{I}}\otimes_{Q}R}\cong\frac{\mathbb{T}}{\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{J}}\otimes_{Q}R\cong\frac{\mathbb{F}}{\pi(\mathbb{I})}\otimes_{Q}R\cong\frac{\mathbb{F}}{\mathbb{I}^{\prime}}\otimes_{Q}R\cong P(\mathbb{F},Z).

5.2

By Theorem
5.6
, the pruning process applied to the minimal free resolution of a monomial ideal produces the minimal free resolution of the pruned ideal over the pruned ring, and so we are done.
∎

.

Squarefree monomial ideals are in one-to-one correspondence with facet ideals of simplicial complexes, a generalization of edge ideals introduced by Faridi in

Let

Remark 5.10
.

In Definition

The induced subcomplex of the 2-simplex on any two of its vertices is a 1-simplex, whereas any proper facet-induced subcomplex of the 2-simplex is the empty set.

Theorem

yields the following result about facet ideals of facet-induced subcomplexes.

.

Let

be a simplicial complex such that the minimal

Our next result about induced subgraphs follows from Corollary

Corollary 5.14

Let

-free resolution of

The classes of facet-induced subgraphs of a (connected) graph

In particular, Corollary

Corollary 5.15

Let

In this appendix we prove the product in Definition

Theorem
.

Recall the products in Definition

(1)

\displaystyle(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle=(f_{1}f_{2},0,0)

(2)

\displaystyle(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})\right)\text{, where }\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})=\begin{cases}-x_{i}g_{2},&\text{if}\hskip 5.69054pt\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1})=x_{i}z,\\

(3)

\displaystyle(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(0,0,g_{2})

\displaystyle=(0,0,0)

(4)

\displaystyle(0,g_{1},0)\cdot(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1}g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{2},0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|}\omega_{f_{2}}(g_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=(0,0,g_{1}g_{2}).

We first note that

is the identity and

To understand the rest of the proof, it is helpful to recall that for

The rest of the products come in pairs, in the sense that proving one yields the other. We compare products (

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|f_{1}|\cdot|g_{1}|}\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{1},0,(-1)^{|f_{1}|\cdot|g_{1}|}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\left|(f_{1},0,0)\cdot(0,g_{1},0)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1}),0,0\right)\right|

(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{1},0)(f_{2},0,0)

(5)

(0,g_{1},0)(f_{1},0,0)(f_{2},0,0)

(6)

(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)

(7)

(0,g_{1},0)(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{2},0)

(8)

(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)(f_{1},0,0)

(9)

(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)(0,0,g_{3})

(10)

(0,0,g_{3})(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)

We note that the first two triples inherit associativity from
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{G}
, respectively.

For the third nontrivial triple, recall that since
|f_{1}f_{2}|>1,\omega_{f_{1}f_{2}}(g_{1})=0
, and so we find

\displaystyle[(f_{1},0,0)(f_{2},0,0)](0,g_{1},0)

\displaystyle=\left(f_{1}f_{2},0,0\right)(0,g_{1},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}f_{2}\Phi(g_{1}),0,0\right)

\displaystyle=(f_{1},0,0)\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{2}\Phi(g_{1}),0,\omega_{f_{2}}(g_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=(f_{1},0,0)[(f_{2},0,0)(0,g_{1},0)].

For the fourth triple, we find

\displaystyle[(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{1},0)](f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})\right)(f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1})f_{2},0,0\right)

\displaystyle=(f_{1},0,0)\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{2},0,\omega_{f_{2}}(g_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=(f_{1},0,0)[(0,g_{1},0)(f_{2},0,0)]

For the fifth triple, we see that
|f_{1}f_{2}|=|f_{1}|+|f_{2}|>1
, thus
\omega_{f_{1}f_{2}}(g_{1})=0
. We then find the following:

\displaystyle[(0,g_{1},0)(f_{1},0,0)](f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{1},0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})\right)(f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{1}f_{2},0,0\right)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1},0)(f_{1}f_{2},0,0)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1},0)[(f_{1},0,0)(f_{2},0,0)]

For the sixth triple, note if
\partial(f_{1})=x_{1}z
, then
\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1}g_{2})=-x_{1}g_{1}g_{2}=\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})g_{2}
.

\displaystyle[(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{1},0)](0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})\right)(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1}),0,0\right)(0,g_{2},0)+(0,0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1}))(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1})\Phi(g_{2}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})g_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{1}g_{2}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1}g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{1}g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(f_{1},0,0)[(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)]

For the seventh triple, we note that
\omega_{f_{1}}
is multiplication by the scalar
-x_{i}
, and so
\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})g_{2}=(-x_{i})g_{1}g_{2}=g_{1}(-x_{i}g_{2})=g_{1}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2}).
Thus,

\displaystyle[(0,g_{1},0)(f_{1},0,0)](0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1})f_{1},0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})\right)(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\Phi(g_{1})f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1})g_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\Phi(g_{1})f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|}g_{1}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1},0)\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1},0)[(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{2},0)].

For the eighth triple, we find:

\displaystyle[(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)](f_{1},0,0)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1}g_{2},0)(f_{1},0,0)=\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{1}g_{2})f_{1},0,(-1)^{|g_{1}g_{2}|}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{1}g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z^{2}}\Phi(g_{1})\Phi(g_{2})f_{1},0,(-1)^{|g_{1}|+|g_{2}|}g_{1}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1},0)\left(\frac{1}{z}\Phi(g_{2})f_{1},0,(-1)^{|g_{2}|}\omega_{f_{1}}(g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1},0)[(0,g_{2},0)(f_{1},0,0)]

For the ninth triple, we find:

\displaystyle[(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)](0,0,g_{3})

\displaystyle=(0,g_{1}g_{2},0)(0,0,g_{3})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|g_{1}g_{2}|}(0,0,(g_{1}g_{2})g_{3})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|g_{1}|+|g_{2}|}(0,0,g_{1}g_{2}g_{3})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|g_{2}|}(g_{1},0,0)(0,0,g_{2}g_{3})

\displaystyle=(g_{1},0,0)[(0,0,g_{2})(0,0,g_{3})]

For the tenth triple, we find:

\displaystyle[(0,0,g_{3})(0,g_{1},0)](0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(0,0,g_{3}g_{1})(0,g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(0,0,g_{3}g_{1}g_{2})

\displaystyle=(0,0,g_{3})(0,g_{1}g_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(0,0,g_{3})[(0,g_{1},0)(0,g_{2},0)]

Since all the non-vanishing triples are associative, we find the product formula is associative.
∎

Appendix C
Leibniz Rule

In this appendix we prove the products from Definition
4.17
satisfy the Leibniz rule. To do this, we recall from Lemma
4.21
that

\left(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f),0,0\right)\cdot\left(0,g,0\right)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f)\Phi(g),0,0\right)&|f|>1,\\
(0,\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f)g,0)&|f|=1.\end{cases}

Theorem
.

The products in Definition
4.17
satisfy the Leibniz rule.

Proof.

Recall that we set
(1,0,0)
to be the multiplicative identity. We need not check the Leibniz rule for elements of the form
(q,0,0)
for
q\in Q
, since the differential
\partial^{\text{Cone}(\Psi)}
is
Q
-linear.

For the ease of the reader, we recall that for homogeneous
(f_{1},g_{1},g_{2})\in\text{Cone}(\Psi)
of total degree
i
we have

\partial^{\text{Cone}(\Phi)}(f_{1},g_{1},g_{2})=\begin{cases}(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1})+\partial^{\mathbb{G}}(g_{1}),0,0),&i=1,\\
(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1})+\Phi(g_{2}),\partial^{\mathbb{G}}(g_{1})-zg_{2},0),&i=2,\\
(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1})+\Phi(g_{2}),\partial^{\mathbb{G}}(g_{1})-zg_{2},-\partial^{\mathbb{G}}(g_{2})),&i\geq 3.\end{cases}

The Leibniz rule holds for product (
1
),
(f_{1},0,0)(f_{2},0,0)
,
and follows from the dg structure of
\mathbb{F}
:

\displaystyle\partial((f_{1},0,0)(f_{2},0,0))

\displaystyle=\partial((f_{1}f_{2},0,0))

\displaystyle=(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1}f_{2}),0,0)

\displaystyle=(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1})f_{2},0,0)+((-1)^{|f_{1}|}f_{1}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{2}),0,0)

\displaystyle=(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1}),0,0)(f_{2},0,0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{2}),0,0)

\displaystyle=\partial((f_{1},0,0))(f_{2},0,0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)\partial((f_{2},0,0))

For product (
2
),
(f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{2},0)
, we consider four cases:

Case 1:
Let
\partial(f_{1})=x_{i}z
and
\partial(g_{2})=x_{j}y_{j,l}
.

\displaystyle\partial((f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{2},0))

\displaystyle=\partial\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,-x_{i}g_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}))+\Phi(-x_{i}g_{2}),-z(-x_{i}g_{2}),0\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{x_{i}z}{z}\Phi(g_{2})-\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(\Phi(g_{2}))-x_{i}\Phi(g_{2}),zx_{i}g_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-x_{j}y_{j,l}f_{1},x_{i}zg_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(x_{i}z,0,0)(0,g_{2},0)-(x_{j}y_{j,l}f_{1},0,0)

\displaystyle=(\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1}),0,0)(0,g_{2},0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)(x_{j}y_{j,l},0,0)

\displaystyle=\partial((f_{1},0,0))(0,g_{2},0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)\partial((0,g_{2},0)).

Case 2:
Let
\partial(f_{1})=x_{i}z
and
|g_{2}|>1
.

\displaystyle\partial((f_{1},0,0)(0,g_{2},0))

\displaystyle=\partial\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}),0,-x_{i}g_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1}\Phi(g_{2}))+\Phi(-x_{i}g_{2}),x_{i}zg_{2},x_{i}\partial^{\mathbb{G}}(g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(\Phi(g_{2})),x_{i}zg_{2},+x_{i}\partial^{\mathbb{G}}(g_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\partial((f_{1},0,0))(0,g_{2},0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)\partial((0,g_{2},0)).

Case 3:
Let
|f_{1}|>1
, and
\partial(g_{2})=x_{j}y_{j,l}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{z}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(f_{1})\Phi(g_{2}),0,0\right)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}\left(\frac{1}{z}f_{1}\partial^{\mathbb{F}}(\Phi(g_{2})),0,0\right)

\displaystyle=\partial((f_{1},0,0))(0,g_{2},0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)\partial((0,g_{2},0)).

\displaystyle=\partial((f_{1},0,0))(0,g_{2},0)+(-1)^{|f_{1}|}(f_{1},0,0)\partial((0,g_{2},0)).

For product (

Let

For product (

(0,0,g_{1})(0,0,g_{2})

\displaystyle\partial((0,0,g_{1})(0,0,g_{2}))

Case 2:
Let
\partial(g_{1})=x_{i}y_{i,l}
