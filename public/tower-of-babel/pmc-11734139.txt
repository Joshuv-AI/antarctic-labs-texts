Idiopathic Coronary Sinus Thrombosis
Authors: Corbin Ho-Fung Mok, Geri Ling-na Wong, Kent Chak-yu So, Alex Pui-wai Lee, Kevin Ka-ho Kam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 62-year-old man with acute coronary syndrome underwent an echocardiogram for evaluation. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed masses within a giant coronary sinus. This case highlights the role of multimodality imaging in investigating coronary sinus enlargement, coronary sinus thrombosis, and the approach to management.

A 62-year-old man presented to the emergency department with classical angina on exertion. Serial electrocardiograms showed T-wave inversion from leads V2 to V6 without dynamic changes. His high-sensitivity troponin T level was significantly elevated. The provisional diagnosis was non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with stable hemodynamics. Before invasive coronary angiography, an echocardiography was performed, demonstrating a giant coronary sinus (CS), 3.7 cm × 4 cm, with a heterogeneous adherent mass measuring 2.1 cm × 3.8 cm within the CS (Figures 1A to 1C, Videos 1 and 2). His left ventricular ejection fraction was mildly reduced, approximately 45%, with mid-anterior and anteroseptal hypokinesis, and his right-sided heart function and size were preserved. Results of an agitated saline test from the left arm were not suggestive of a left-sided superior vena cava (SVC). Parenteral anticoagulation was initiated given the likely diagnosis of CS thrombosis, followed by titration of a vitamin K antagonist (VKA). An emergency invasive coronary angiogram showed a critical stenosis at the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery with no evidence of coronary artery fistulas to the CS from multiple views. Balloon angioplasty was performed to the LAD lesion with a strategy of staged stenting to allow time for comprehensive work-up of CS thrombosis. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed 2 large, organized thrombi within the CS (Figures 1D and 1E, Videos 3 and 4). The interatrial septum was intact, with no features of unroofed CS. Positron emission tomography with computed tomography (PET-CT) showed no evidence of underlying malignant disease, anomalous pulmonary venous connection, or coronary fistulation to the CS. An absence of radiotracer uptake within the mass was confirmatory of CS thrombosis (Figure 1F, Video 5). Results of investigations for antiphospholipid syndrome, including anti–beta2-glycoprotein and anticardiolipin, were negative. Lupus anticoagulant testing was not feasible during anticoagulation. Considering the stagnant flow in the giant CS contributing to the thrombi formation, lifelong anticoagulation was planned, with no complications observed in 3-year follow-up.

The CS is located at the atrioventricular groove at the junction of the left atrium and left ventricle and is responsible for draining the coronary circulation into the right atrium. The usual size of the CS is 2 to 3 cm long and 1 cm in diameter. The CS can be dilated when there is elevated right atrial pressure or volume (eg, pulmonary hypertension, severe tricuspid regurgitation) or abnormal drainage into the CS (eg, persistent left-sided SVC or unroofed coronary sinus). CS thrombosis is rare and more often reported as a postprocedural complication of CS instrumentation such as central line or pacing lead placement or ablation catheter manipulation.1,2 In the presence of collateral flow in the venous system, symptoms are usually minimal. However, in an acute situation, the patient could present with acute heart failure or pulmonary embolism. Idiopathic CS thrombosis is a diagnosis by exclusion. Comprehensive echocardiography followed by PET-CT can help to confirm the diagnosis more definitively by excluding malignancy and anomalous drainage. Anticoagulation with a VKA is the mainstay of treatment. When patients have unstable hemodynamics secondary to CS thrombosis, referral for surgical thrombectomy may be indicated.2
