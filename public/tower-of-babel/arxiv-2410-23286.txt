Proof of nonintegrability of the spin- 1 1 bilinear-biquadratic chain model
HaRu K. Park, SungBin Lee

Abstract
Spin- 1 1 chain models have been extensively studied in condensed matter physics, significantly advancing our understanding of quantum magnetism and low-dimensional systems, which exhibit unique properties compared to their spin- 1 / 2 1/2 counterparts. Despite substantial progress in this area, providing a rigorous proof of nonintegrability for the bilinear-biquadratic chain model remains an open challenge. While integrable solutions are known for specific parameter values, a comprehensive understanding of the model’s general integrability has been elusive. In this paper, we present the first rigorous proof of nonintegrability for the general spin- 1 1 bilinear-biquadratic chain models. Our proof not only confirms the nonintegrability of widely studied models but also extends to offer deeper insights into several areas. These include the unification of nonintegrability proofs using graph theoretical methods and the identification of the absence of local conserved quantities in quantum many-body scar systems with perfect fidelity revivals, such as the AKLT model. This work marks a significant step toward understanding the complex dynamics of spin- 1 1 systems and offers a framework that can be applied to a broader class of quantum many-body systems.

Proof.
We provide a brief sketch of the proof here; see
[
35
]
for full details. Although not every operator string is a doubling product string, we can always identify a substring on the left that is part of a doubling product string. Using the condition

is proportional to

, the calculation simplifies, yielding the exact form of
f_{k}(\theta)
as follows:

f_{k}(\theta)=(k-1)\cos\theta\cos 2\theta.

(16)

If
\cos\theta\cos 2\theta\neq 0
, we find that
f_{k}(\theta)\neq 0
and therefore
\alpha=0
, which implies that
q(\bm{A}^{k}_{\text{DP}})=0
for all doubling product operators. The condition
\cos\theta\cos 2\theta\neq 0
holds when
\theta\not\in\{\pm\frac{\pi}{4},\pm\frac{3\pi}{4},\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\}
, and it is known that
\theta\in\{\pm\frac{\pi}{4},\pm\frac{3\pi}{4},\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\}
corresponds to the integrable cases of
H_{\theta}
. This completely classifies the integrability and non-integrability of
H_{\theta}
. A similar analysis also applies to
\overline{X},\overline{Y},\overline{Z}
by substituting
X,Y,Z
in Eq.
12
and Eq.
13
; for more details, see
[
35
]
.

Discussion.
— We have rigorously proven that the spin-
1
bilinear-biquadratic chain model lacks local conserved quantities, establishing its general nonintegrability, except at known integrable points. Our findings eliminates the possibility of hidden local symmetries in prominent spin-
1
chain models, such as the Haldane spin-
1
chain and the AKLT model. Notably, our proof shows the nonintegrable nature of the AKLT model, marking a significant result as it provides the first rigorous evidence of nonintegrability in quantum many-body scarring systems exhibiting perfect fidelity revivals. It also shows that local conserved quantities are not essential for the emergence of unique physical phenomena, such as topologically protected gaps or short-time fidelity revivals.

Our study extends beyond previous nonintegrability research in spin-
1/2
systems, addressing the unique challenges posed by spin-
1
systems’ complex operator commutation relations. The complexity of higher-spin systems has traditionally posed challenges to proving nonintegrability. This study demonstrates that nonintegrability can instead be established based on Hamiltonian locality rather than solely on single-site commutation relations, indicating potential applicability to a broader range of local Hamiltonians.
In addition, we believe that our approach can be generalized to prove nonintegrability in higher-spin Heisenberg-like models. For any spin-
S>1/2
, the quadratic spin operators
X^{2},Y^{2},Z^{2}
remain linearly dependent, satisfying
X^{2}+Y^{2}+Z^{2}=I_{2S+1}
, but are not proportional to the identity operator. This suggests that similar argument could play a crucial role in the proof of nonintegrability for higher-spin systems, similar to the role it plays in the spin-
1
model.

Furthermore, since we have shown that the Hilbert space dimension of the single-site operator does not affect the standard strategy for proving nonintegrability, we believe our approach could provide insight into the Grabowski-Mathieu conjecture, which claims that the absence of length
3
conserved quantities implies nonintegrability of the system. We leave this extension to higher-spin systems as future work.

Doubling Product operator

Before going through the detailed proof, we need to precisely define the concept of a doubling product operator and discuss its properties.

A doubling product operator is an operator expressed in the following doubling product form:

\displaystyle\{{\overbracket{A_{(1)}A_{(2)}\cdots A_{(n)}}}\}_{j}=

\displaystyle c\cdot(A_{(1)})_{j}([A_{(1)},A_{(2)}])_{j+1}([A_{(2)},A_{(3)}])_{j+2}\cdots

\displaystyle\cdots([A_{(n-1)},A_{(n)}])_{j+n-1}(A_{(n)})_{j+n},

(9)

where
c
is a normalization constant, and each
A_{(t)}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{B}
.

It is important to note that different doubling product forms can yield the same operator. For example,
{\overbracket{X\underline{X}Y}}={\overbracket{X\underline{Y}Y}}=X\overline{X}\overline{Y}Y
. This redundancy arises from the properties of
\underline{X},\underline{Y},
and
\underline{Z}
. As shown in Table
2
, the commutators
[\underline{X},A],[\underline{Y},A],[\underline{Z},A]
always yield the same operator up to a scaling factor. This property introduces flexibility in the notation for doubling product operators, allowing interchangeable use of
\underline{X},\underline{Y},
and
\underline{Z}
. While this arbitrariness could be restricted by allowing only
\underline{X}
for
A_{(t)}
and excluding
\underline{Y}
and
\underline{Z}
, we will later see that this flexibility helps simplify the conditions for
C|_{\len(C)}
in Step 1.

Furthermore, a doubling product operator does not always correspond to a single element in the basis of the operator strings, which are constructed from the single-site operators
\mathcal{B}_{0}
. For example,
{\overbracket{Z\overline{Z}}}=Z\underline{Z}\overline{Z}=-Z\underline{X}\overline{Z}-Z\underline{Y}\overline{Z}
. Thus, while the operator string
Z\underline{X}\overline{Z}
does not directly appear in doubling product form, it is part of a doubling product form and, therefore, cannot be assumed to have a zero coefficient in Step 1 of the main text. To formalize this, we denote
\bm{A}\leq\bm{B}
for an operator string
\bm{A}
and operator
\bm{B}
when
\bm{A}
has a nonzero coefficient in the basis representation of
\bm{B}
. For example,
Z\underline{X}\overline{Z}\leq{\overbracket{Z\overline{Z}}}
and
Z\underline{Y}\overline{Z}\leq{\overbracket{Z\overline{Z}}}
.

A natural question arises: how can we determine whether a specific operator string
\bm{A}=A_{(1)}A_{(2)}\cdots A_{(n)}
can be expressed in a doubling product form
{\overbracket{D_{(1)}\cdots D_{(n-1)}}}
, or at least as part of a doubling product form? Recalling the construction of the doubling product operators, this can be achieved through the following steps:

1.

First, set
D_{(1)}\in\mathcal{B}
to
A_{(1)}
.

2.

Next, choose an operator
D_{(2)}\in\mathcal{B}
that satisfies
A_{(2)}\leq[D_{(1)},D_{(2)}]
.

3.

Proceed inductively, selecting
D_{(j)}
such that
A_{(j)}\leq[D_{(j-1)},D_{(j)}]
, continuing this process until
j=n-1
.

4.

After completing these inductive steps, if the final single-site operator
D_{(n-1)}
satisfies
A_{(n)}\leq D_{(n-1)}
, then we have successfully found a doubling product operator such that
\bm{A}\leq{\overbracket{D_{(1)}\cdots D_{(n-1)}}}
. If this condition is not satisfied,
\bm{A}
cannot be represented as (or included in) a doubling product form.

In this process, the crucial point is as follows:
For given single-site operators
B
and
F
, with
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
and
F\in\mathcal{B}
, can we always find an operator
R\in\mathcal{B}
such that
B\leq[R,F]
?
Naturally, the answer depends on the specific operators
B
and
F
. For example, if
(B,F)=(X,Y)
then one can find
R=Z
, whereas if
(B,F)=(X,X)
, no such
R
exists. Crucially, whether such an
R
can be found is determined solely by the commutator properties of the single-site operators, without requiring any additional information, such as the commutator relations between two-site operators.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose an operator string
\bm{A}
is part of a doubling product operator. Additionally, assume the coefficient of
\bm{A}_{\text{base}}
in
C

q(\bm{A})
of
\bm{A}

Proof.

Suppose
\bm{A}
does not end with
\underline{X}
or
\underline{Y}
, and consider the following commutators:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}&\\
&&&&&&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\\
\hline\cr&&U_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&&U_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\\
&P_{L}&P^{\prime}_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&&U^{\prime}_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\end{array}

Here
P_{1}=X
if
A_{(k)}\in\{Y,Z,\overline{X},\overline{Y},\overline{Z}\}
and
P_{2}=Y
if
A_{(k)}=X
.

If
U_{L}\not\in\{\underline{X},\underline{Y}\}
, the last commutator does not contribute. Additionally, both the second and third commutators contain the operator string
U_{L}A_{(3)}\cdots A_{(k-1)}A_{(k)}/{\overbracket{P_{1}}}
. This setup provides a linear relationship between
q(U_{L}A_{(3)}\cdots A_{(k-1)}A_{(k)}/{\overbracket{P_{1}}})
and
q(\bm{A})
.

Now, consider the case where

. In this case, the third commutator does not contribute, and we need to consider the following three commutators:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}&\\
&&&&&&[A_{(k)},P_{1}]&P_{1}\\
\hline\cr&&\underline{X}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&&\underline{Y}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&A_{(k)}/P_{1}&P_{1}\end{array}

This setup yields a linear equation of the coefficients
q(\underline{X}A_{(3)}\cdots A_{(k)}/{\overbracket{P_{1}}})
,
q(\underline{Y}A_{(3)}\cdots A_{(k)}/{\overbracket{P_{1}}})
, and
q(\bm{A})
. By applying the same argument as above, we establish a linear relation between

, and

when

, and
P_{2}=X

. By combining these linear relationships, we obtain a direct linear relation between

q(\bm{A})
. Similar argue holds for

.

By repeatedly applying this argument, we continue to establish proportional relationships between the coefficients, such as

q(\bm{A})\sim q(U_{2}A_{(4)}\cdots A_{(k)}/{\overbracket{P_{1}P_{2}}})\sim q(U_{3}A_{(5)}\cdots A_{(k)}/{\overbracket{P_{1}P_{2}P_{1}}})\sim\cdots

where

indicates proportionality between two variables. Since
\bm{A}
is part of a doubling product operator, this argument can be extended until we reach

. Therefore,

q(\bm{A})\sim q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}}),

(12)

which implies that if
q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}})
is fixed, then

is also fixed.

Now, suppose that
\bm{A}
ends with
\underline{X}
, and consider the following commutators.

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\underline{X}&\\
&&&&&&X&X\\
\hline\cr&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\underline{Y}&\\

\hline\cr&&U_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{X}&X\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&&U_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{X}&X\\
&P_{L}&P^{\prime}_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&&U^{\prime}_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{X}&X\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{X}&X\end{array}

We have already shown that the operator strings in the last three commutators are uniquely determined when

is fixed. Therefore, we obtain

2q(\bm{A})-q(A_{(1)}\cdots\underline{Y})=c_{1}q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}})

for some constant

. Similarly, from the following commutators:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\underline{X}&\\

\hline\cr&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\underline{Y}&\\

\hline\cr&&U_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{Y}&Y\\

\hline\cr&&U_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{Y}&Y\\
&P_{L}&P^{\prime}_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&&U^{\prime}_{L}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{Y}&Y\\
&P_{L}&P_{L}&&&&&\\
\hline\cr&A_{(1)}&A_{(2)}&A_{(3)}&\cdots&A_{(k-1)}&\overline{Y}&Y\end{array}

one get

-q(\bm{A})+2q(A_{(1)}\cdots A_{(k-1)}\underline{Y})=c_{2}q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}})

for some constant

. Using these two equations, we find

\displaystyle q(\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{1}+c_{2}}{3}q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}})

\displaystyle q(A_{(1)}\cdots A_{(k-1)}\underline{Y})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1}+2c_{2}}{3}q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}})

showing that
q(\bm{A})
is uniquely determined when
q(\bm{A}_{\text{base}})
is fixed. The same argument applies for an operator string
\bm{A}
that ends with

∎

5
Translationally noninvariant operators

The argument treating about the translationally noninvariant operators, which we discuss in this section, follows the approach presented in
[
1
]
. Suppose that, for the Hamiltonian
H_{\theta}
, there exists a translationally noninvariant local quantity
C
of length
k>2
. Let
T^{(j)}
denote the translation operator shifting by distance
j
. From
C
, we can define a translationally invariant local quantity
C_{0}
as follows:

C_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{L}T^{(-j)}CT^{(j)}.

(28)

Due to the translational invariance of
H_{\theta}
,
C_{0}
is also conserved. Since we have eliminated the possibility of a translationally invariant local conserved quantity with length
k>2
, if the length of
C_{0}
exceeds
2
, a contradiction arises, implying that such
C
cannot exist. However, although
C
is of length
k>2
,
C_{0}
could still reduce to a conserved quantity with length
\leq 2
, such as
H_{\theta},I,
or
0
. We must therefore exclude this possibility.

Define the following quantities:

C_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{2\pi iaj/m}T^{(-j)}CT^{(j)},

(29)

where
m
is the smallest positive integer satisfying
T^{-m}CT^{m}=C
, which exists for finite
L
, and
a=1,2,\cdots,m-1
. Due to the translational invariance of
H_{\theta}
,
[C,H_{\theta}]=0
implies
[C_{a},H_{\theta}]=0
for all
a
. Furthermore, the
C_{a}
operators with length
k
must exist since
mC=C_{0}+\sum_{a=1}^{m-1}C_{a}
, and
C
is a length
k
operator.

Our goal is to show that
[C,H]=0
implies that every
C_{a}
has a length less than
k
, contradicting above and proving that such
C
does not exist. First, all arguments used in Section
3
remain valid for
C_{a}
; the only difference is the factors
e^{2\pi iaj/m}
multiplying the coefficients due to the phase difference between
q(\{\bm{A}\}_{1})
and
q(\{\bm{A}\}_{j+1})
in
C_{a}
. These factors only modify the phase of the coefficient in the linear relation and do not affect the existence of the relation, so the arguments in Section
3
hold.

Additionally, the arguments in Section
4
are not required here. For
J_{1}\neq 0
and
k=6m+1
, note that
q(\{{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}}}\}_{1})
and
q(\{{\overbracket{(XYZ)^{2m}}}\}_{6m+1})
are linearly related, as can be demonstrated using similar methods to those in Section
3
. We also have

q(\{{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}}}\}_{1})=-q(\{{\overbracket{(YX)^{3m}}}\}_{2})=q(\{{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}}}\}_{3})

and since
q(\{{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}}}\}_{3})=e^{4\pi ia/m}q(\{{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}}}\}_{1})
, it follows that
a/m=1/2
. However,

q(\{{\overbracket{(XYZ)^{2m}}}\}_{1})=q(\{{\overbracket{(YZX)^{2m}}}\}_{6m+2}))=q(\{{\overbracket{(ZXY)^{2m}}}\}_{6m+3}))=q(\{{\overbracket{(XYZ)^{2m}}}\}_{6m+4}))

and
q(\{{\overbracket{(XYZ)^{2m}}}\}_{6m+4}))=e^{6\pi ia/m}q(\{{\overbracket{(XYZ)^{2m}}}\}_{6m+1}))
imply
a/m=1/3
or
a/m=2/3
, leading to a contradiction. Thus,
q(\{{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}}}\}_{1})=0
, and all other coefficients must be zero. A similar argument applies for any
k
by adjusting operators in the doubling product form (e.g., using
{\overbracket{(XY)^{3m}X}}
and
{\overbracket{(XYZ)^{3m}X}}
for
k=6m+2
.), and for the
J_{1}=0
case by substituting
\overline{X},\overline{Y},\overline{Z}
for
X,Y,Z
.
