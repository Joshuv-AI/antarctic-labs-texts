Effects of online advance care planning (ACP) decision aid programmes on adults with cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhihao Han, Yong Fang, Jianjian Yang, Shengyang Jiang, Guiyue Ma, Xiaoqin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvance care planning (ACP) is important in cancer care, but discussions are often delayed or inconsistently implemented. Online ACP decision aid programmes may support ACP participation, although their effects in adults with cancer remain unclear.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, and supplementary sources was conducted from inception to 10 March 2026. Quantitative studies evaluating online ACP decision aid programmes for adults with cancer were included. Risk of bias was assessed using appropriate tools according to study design. Meta-analyses were performed using fixed- or random-effects models.ResultsTwelve studies met the eligibility criteria, including 10 randomised controlled trials, 1 pre-post study, and 1 single-arm quantitative evaluation study. Most studies were conducted in the USA. Pooled evidence showed that online ACP decision aid programmes significantly improved ACP documentation (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 1.29 to 3.27, p = 0.002) and ACP engagement (MD = 0.28, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.40, p < 0.0001). No statistically significant effects were found for AD completion (OR = 1.37, 95% CI 0.54 to 3.45, p = 0.50), satisfaction (MD = 0.15, 95% CI -0.25 to 0.55, p = 0.47), decisional conflict (MD=-0.72, 95% CI -5.87 to 4.43, p = 0.78), or anxiety (MD=-0.76, 95% CI -1.67 to 0.16, p = 0.11). Sensitivity analyses suggested that the findings for ACP documentation and ACP engagement were relatively robust, whereas the results for satisfaction and anxiety were less stable.ConclusionsOnline ACP decision aid programmes may support ACP in adults with cancer, particularly by improving ACP engagement and ACP documentation. However, evidence for benefits beyond ACP process outcomes remains limited or inconsistent. Further rigorous studies are needed to identify effective programme features and implementation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02184-z.Keywords: Advance care planning, Decision aid, Online intervention, Cancer, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Cancer care is often marked by disease progression, prognostic uncertainty and complex treatment decisions, requiring patients to balance potential survival benefits with quality of life while considering their future care preferences across the illness trajectory [1]. In this context, advance care planning (ACP) is a structured process through which adults are supported to clarify, communicate and, where appropriate, document their values, goals and preferences for future medical care, thereby promoting the delivery of medical care that is consistent with patients’ care preferences [2, 3]. For adults with cancer, ACP is particularly important because it can facilitate value-concordant care, strengthen informed and shared decision-making, and improve communication among patients, family members and healthcare professionals [4, 5]. However, ACP discussions in oncology are frequently delayed or inconsistently implemented, and many patients’ wishes remain insufficiently explored or documented despite evidence that patients with cancer generally prefer earlier conversations about future care [6–8]. These persistent gaps highlight the importance of developing effective approaches to support timely and meaningful ACP engagement in adults with cancer.

Despite the recognised importance of ACP, many adults with cancer may find it difficult to understand ACP-related information, clarify what matters most to them, prepare for discussions with family members and clinicians, and translate general wishes into concrete decisions and documentation; accordingly, ACP decision aid programmes have been developed to provide structured information, support values clarification, and facilitate communication and documentation [9]. Online programmes are particularly promising because they can be accessed at a time and place chosen by the patient, deliver standardised yet interactive and stepwise content, and allow users to proceed at their own pace and revisit materials over time, which aligns well with ACP as an evolving rather than one-off process [10–12]. In addition, some digital ACP tools can support the recording and review of preferences and, in some cases, generate a personalised written summary or translate elicited values and preferences into a more specific care plan, thereby reducing practical barriers to documentation and later updating [13–15]. Early oncology-related evaluations further suggest that web-based ACP resources such as PREPARE are acceptable and understandable for patients with cancer and may improve ACP engagement and ACP documentation [16–18].

In recent years, a growing number of original studies have examined online ACP decision aid programmes for adults with cancer. These studies suggest that digital ACP interventions may support patients in understanding ACP-related information, reflecting on personal values and future care preferences, preparing for discussions with family members and healthcare professionals, and, in some cases, documenting these preferences in a more structured manner [9, 18]. However, the available studies differ substantially in several important respects. The target populations vary by cancer type, disease stage and care setting, and the interventions themselves differ in their theoretical basis, content components, level of interactivity, duration, and degree of clinician or facilitator involvement [19, 20]. There is also considerable variation in how outcomes are assessed, with different studies focusing on decisional conflict, shared decision-making or patient involvement, communication-related outcomes, ACP engagement, advance directive completion, or ACP documentation [21, 22]. Furthermore, some programmes mainly aim to inform and clarify values, whereas others place greater emphasis on prompting conversations or facilitating document completion [20, 23]. Taken together, these differences mean that the findings from individual studies remain fragmented and do not yet provide a sufficiently clear picture of how, and to what extent, online ACP decision aid programmes benefit adults with cancer.

Although previous reviews have addressed ACP and decision support, they have not adequately answered whether online ACP decision aid programmes are effective for adults with cancer. Some reviews have examined ACP interventions across mixed disease populations, making it difficult to determine whether their findings are directly applicable to oncology [24–26]. Others have focused on only one component of ACP decision support, such as information provision or values clarification, rather than the broader ACP process of helping individuals understand and articulate their values, define goals and preferences for future treatment and care, discuss these with family members and healthcare professionals, and record and review them where appropriate [3, 27]. In addition, earlier reviews have often combined face-to-face, paper-based and web-based interventions, making it difficult to isolate the independent contribution of online decision support itself [26, 28]. As digital ACP tools and oncology-related studies have continued to emerge, a focused and up-to-date systematic review is needed to clarify the current evidence base and inform future programme development and implementation in cancer care.

This systematic review aims to assess the effects of online ACP decision aid programmes for adults with cancer and, where appropriate, to pool the findings in a meta-analysis. In particular, this review will examine whether these programmes influence key ACP-related outcomes reported across the included studies, including ACP knowledge, decisional conflict, communication-related outcomes, ACP engagement or preparedness, completion of advance directives or other ACP documents, and the documentation of care preferences. By focusing specifically on adults with cancer and online ACP decision aid programmes, this review aims to provide more precise evidence on intervention effects and to inform the future design, implementation and evaluation of ACP support in oncology.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number: CRD 420261375169). This study were conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [29]. The checklist is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Literature search

A systematic search of MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library was conducted from database inception to 10 March 2026. To identify ongoing or unpublished studies, we also searched ClinicalTrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. For supplementary and grey literature searches, Google Scholar, ResearchGate and ProQuest were searched using simplified combinations of the core terms. The titles and available abstracts or descriptions of potentially relevant records were screened, and the reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies were also checked manually. The search strategy combined terms related to cancer, advance care planning, decision aid, online, web-based and digital. The main concepts were combined using the Boolean operator AND, while synonyms and related terms within each concept were combined using OR. No language restrictions were applied during the search and screening process; however, all studies that met the final inclusion criteria were published in English. Before submission, we updated the search using the same search strategies, and no additional studies meeting the eligibility criteria were identified. Separate search strategies were developed for each database and adapted as appropriate. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria were defined a priori using the PICOS framework [30]. Population: studies involving adults aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of cancer were eligible. Studies including mixed clinical populations were also eligible when adults with cancer constituted a clearly relevant subgroup or when the intervention was delivered in settings relevant to cancer care. Where cancer-specific outcome data were not separately available, these studies were included only in the narrative synthesis because they still provided relevant contextual evidence for cancer-related or mixed clinical settings; however, they were excluded from the meta-analysis to avoid introducing indirectness and potentially biased effect estimates that could not be attributed specifically to adults with cancer. Intervention: eligible interventions were online, web-based or digital ACP decision aid programmes designed to assist patients in completing the ACP process. To be eligible, programmes had to include core ACP steps, including provision of ACP-related information, clarification of patients’ values and care preferences, preparation for communication with family members and healthcare professionals, and facilitation of preference documentation or completion of legally or institutionally recognised ACP-related documents where applicable. Comparator: for comparative studies, eligible comparators included usual care, standard education or information materials, wait-list control, or alternative interventions that did not provide equivalent online ACP decision aid support. Studies without a separate comparator were also eligible if they used a pre-post or single-arm quantitative evaluation design. Outcomes: studies were required to report at least one ACP-related or programme evaluation outcome, such as ACP knowledge, decisional conflict, communication-related outcomes, ACP engagement or preparedness, completion of ACP-related documents, documentation of care preferences, or programme acceptability, usability, or feasibility. Study design: original quantitative studies were eligible, including randomised controlled trials, non-randomised comparative studies, pre-post studies, and single-arm quantitative evaluation studies.

Studies were excluded if the intervention was not an online ACP decision aid programme, if it focused only on a single isolated activity (for example, communication training or document completion) without supporting the broader ACP process, or if mixed-population findings were not sufficiently relevant to adults with cancer. We also excluded publications that did not provide original quantitative outcome data, including protocols, reviews, qualitative studies, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts without full text, and case reports. Where multiple reports described the same study, the most complete and informative report was retained. No restrictions were applied regarding publication year or language.

Study selection

All records identified through the database and supplementary searches were imported into EndNote X9 for reference management, and duplicate records were removed. Based on the predefined eligibility criteria, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (ZH and YF). The full texts of potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same two reviewers (ZH and YF). Disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached; if necessary, a third reviewer (JY) was consulted. The study selection process was documented in a PRISMA flow diagram [29].

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form [31] was utilised to collect the following information from the included studies: (a) General study information (authors, publication year, country, and study design); (b) Participants’ characteristics (setting, sample, age, and cancer type); (c) Intervention characteristics (programme name, content components, mode of delivery, duration, and intervention provider); (d) Comparator details, outcome measures, and time points of data collection; (e) Outcomes of interest and numerical data required for meta-analysis, where available. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (ZH and SJ). The extracted data were checked for accuracy, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. In cases of uncertainty, a third reviewer (GM) was consulted.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers (ZH and YF). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus; when necessary, a third reviewer (XM) was consulted. For randomised controlled trials, the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2) was used to assess bias arising from the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result [32]. For non-randomised studies, including the pre-post study and the single-arm quantitative evaluation study, the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was applied, with judgements made according to the domains relevant to each study design [33]. The overall risk-of-bias judgements were taken into account in the interpretation of the review findings.

Certainty of the body of evidence

In addition, the certainty of the body of evidence for each meta-analysed outcome was assessed independently by 2 reviewers (ZH and YF) using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [34]. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus; when necessary, a third reviewer (XM) was consulted. The certainty of evidence was judged across the relevant domains, including risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias, as appropriate.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data synthesis and analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [35]. A narrative synthesis was first undertaken to summarise the characteristics and findings of the included studies. Where studies were sufficiently comparable in terms of participants, interventions, comparators and outcomes, meta-analysis was performed using RevMan version 5.4.1. For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were calculated when outcomes were measured on the same scale, and standardised mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs were calculated when different instruments were used to assess the same construct. Random-effects models were used to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Chi-square test and the I2 statistic. An I2 value of 0%-40% was considered to indicate low heterogeneity, 30%-60% moderate heterogeneity, 50%-90% substantial heterogeneity, and 75%-100% considerable heterogeneity. Where meta-analysis was not appropriate because of substantial heterogeneity or insufficient comparable data, the findings were synthesised narratively. Where sufficient studies were available, subgroup analyses were conducted according to key intervention characteristics and study design, and sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies at higher risk of bias. If at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome, publication bias was assessed using funnel plot asymmetry. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The PRISMA flow chart in Fig. 1 illustrates the study selection process. A total of records were identified through database and supplementary searches and imported into EndNote X9 for reference management. After duplicate records had been removed, the remaining records were screened by title and abstract, and potentially eligible articles were retrieved for full-text review. Full-text articles were excluded for reasons including ineligible population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, study design, or publication type. Ultimately, 12 studies met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in this review.

The included studies were conducted in a range of settings, including oncology clinics, medical centres, outpatient clinics, primary clinics, online samples, and online settings. Specifically, oncology clinic settings were reported in five studies [17, 20, 22, 36, 39], medical centre settings in three studies [19, 23, 38], outpatient clinics in one study [14], primary clinics in one study [18], and online setting in two study [16, 37].

Sample sizes ranged from 11 participants [16] to 29,357 participants [22]. Among the studies that reported age, the mean age ranged from 53.6 ± 11.0 years [37] to 74.5 ± 6.6 years [22]. In terms of participant characteristics, advanced cancer was the most common category and was reported in six studies [19, 20, 22, 23, 36, 38]. The remaining studies included ovarian cancer [17], metastatic breast cancer [37], gastrointestinal or lung cancer [39], and mixed participant samples [14, 16, 18].

The intervention programmes also varied across studies. MYWK was evaluated in four studies [19, 23, 36, 38], and PREPARE was reported in three studies [14, 18, 20]. The other intervention programmes were an ACP website [17], Four Conversations [37], a web-based AD [39], PACT [16], and facilitated ACP [22]. Overall, substantial variation was observed across the included studies in terms of study design, setting, sample size, participant characteristics, and intervention programme.

Intervention characteristics

The intervention programmes varied considerably across the included studies (Table 2). Most interventions were self-directed web-based or computer-based ACP tools, including an ACP website [17], MYWK [19, 23, 36, 38], PREPARE used at home or combined with an easy-to-read advance directive [14, 18], a web-based advance directive [39], and the PACT website [16]. By contrast, three studies incorporated additional support or facilitation, including a counsellor-supported online programme [37], facilitated ACP led by a trained facilitator [20], and a clinic-based intervention combining patient video decision aids with clinician communication skills training [22]. According to programme complexity, the interventions were further classified as single-component and multi-component programmes. Single-component programmes primarily consisted of an online, web-based or digital ACP decision aid tool, whereas multi-component programmes included an online ACP decision aid tool together with additional components, such as videos, printable documents, live sessions, facilitator-led ACP conversations, or clinician communication training.

Control conditions were similarly heterogeneous and included usual care, standard living will materials plus a brochure, standard ACP, wait-list control with usual care, paper advance directives, easy-to-read advance directives alone, and PREPARE for Your Care plus written materials; no separate comparator was used in the pre-post study or the single-arm quantitative evaluation study. Reported outcomes also varied across studies, spanning affective and decisional outcomes, such as anxiety, satisfaction, hopefulness, hopelessness and decisional conflict; ACP process outcomes, such as ACP engagement, AD completion, communication about end-of-life wishes and ACP documentation; and programme evaluation outcomes, such as acceptability, usability and perceived helpfulness. Overall, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies in intervention content, support, comparator conditions and outcome focus.

Risk of bias in included studies

The risk-of-bias assessments are summarised in Supplementary File 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1. Of the 10 included randomised controlled trials, two studies were rated as having a low risk of bias [20, 22], seven were judged as having some concerns [18, 19, 23, 36–39], and one study was judged as having a high risk of bias [17]. Among the two non-randomised studies, one study was judged as being at serious risk of bias [14], whereas the other was judged as being at critical risk of bias [16].

For the randomised trials, the most common source of concern related to the randomisation process. Some studies also had concerns regarding measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result, particularly those reporting self-reported outcomes or subgroup and secondary analyses [18, 23, 36, 38]. One study [17] was judged to have a high risk of bias, primarily because substantial attrition led to a high risk of bias arising from missing outcome data. In contrast, the two studies [20, 22] rated as low risk were characterised by clearer reporting of random sequence generation and allocation concealment, low concerns regarding missing data, and more consistent outcome reporting.

For the non-randomised studies, the principal sources of bias were confounding, selection of participants, and measurement of outcomes. A serious risk of bias [14] was identified in the pre-post study because it lacked a concurrent comparator, whereas a critical risk of bias [16] was identified in the single-arm study because participants were self-selected and the acceptability and usability outcomes were self-reported. These risk-of-bias judgements were taken into account when interpreting the review findings.

Intervention effects

Figure 2 presents the pooled effects of online ACP decision aid programmes on 6 outcomes: AD completion, ACP documentation, satisfaction, ACP engagement, decisional conflict, and anxiety. We did not calculate a single overall pooled estimate across all outcomes because these outcomes represented different domains and were analysed using different summary measures. According to Cochrane guidance, studies should be grouped for synthesis according to the specific outcome addressed, and continuous outcomes measured using different instruments should be combined only when they reflect the same underlying construct. Accordingly, separate meta-analyses were conducted for each outcome [35]. The extracted data used for the meta-analysis are presented in Supplementary File 4.

Forest plot showing the effects of online advance care planning decision aid programmes on patient outcomes

For AD completion, three studies [17, 20, 37], involving 687 participants, were included. The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups (OR = 1.37, 95% CI 0.54 to 3.45, p = 0.50). The heterogeneity test revealed a large level of heterogeneity across studies (I² = 83%, p = 0.002). The high heterogeneity may be partly explained by differences in intervention format, cancer population, level of interpersonal support and comparator conditions across the three studies.

For ACP documentation, five studies [18–20, 22, 39], involving 30,265 participants, were pooled. The intervention group was significantly more likely to achieve ACP documentation than the control group (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 1.29 to 3.27, p = 0.002). Moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I² = 72%, p = 0.007). The heterogeneity may be partly explained by differences in intervention design and outcome measurement, as the included studies varied in delivery mode, level of facilitation and how ACP documentation was defined or recorded.

For satisfaction, four studies [17, 18, 23, 39], involving 546 participants, were included. The pooled effect showed no statistically significant difference between groups (MD = 0.15, 95% CI -0.25 to 0.55, p = 0.47). The heterogeneity test indicated substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 71%, p = 0.01).

For ACP engagement, three studies [14, 18, 20], involving 714 participants, were pooled. The intervention effect was statistically significant, with higher ACP engagement in the intervention group than in the control group (MD = 0.28, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.40, p < 0.0001). No heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 0%, p = 0.65).

For decisional conflict, two studies [17, 37], involving 255 participants, were included. The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups (MD = -0.72, 95% CI -5.87 to 4.43, p = 0.78). No heterogeneity was detected across studies (I² = 0%, p = 0.49).

For anxiety, two studies [18, 23], involving 420 participants, were pooled. Although the pooled effect favored the intervention group, the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance (MD = -0.76, 95% CI -1.67 to 0.16, p = 0.11). Low to moderate heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 44%, p = 0.18).

The pooled evidence indicated that online ACP decision aid programmes significantly improved ACP documentation and ACP engagement, but no statistically significant effects were found for AD completion, satisfaction, decisional conflict, or anxiety.

Sensitivity analyses

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary File 5. For AD completion, exclusion of any single study did not materially alter the pooled estimate (RR range 0.957 to 1.273; p range 0.254 to 0.827), indicating that the non-significant result was not driven by any individual study. For ACP documentation, the pooled effect remained statistically significant and in the same direction after sequential omission of each study (RR range 1.374 to 1.735; p range < 0.001 to 0.015), suggesting good robustness of this finding.

For satisfaction, the pooled effect remained positive after exclusion of each study in turn (SMD range 0.071 to 0.269), but the p values varied from 0.007 to 0.511, indicating limited stability of statistical significance. For ACP engagement, both the direction and statistical significance of the pooled effect were maintained throughout the leave-one-out analyses (SMD range 0.306 to 0.341; p range < 0.001 to 0.004), supporting the robustness of this result.

For decisional conflict, the pooled effect remained non-significant after omission of each study (SMD range − 0.249 to -0.005; p range 0.468 to 0.969), although no material reversal in the direction of effect was observed. For anxiety, the pooled effect consistently favored the intervention group (SMD range − 0.261 to -0.133), but the statistical significance observed in the primary analysis disappeared after exclusion of either study (p range 0.066 to 0.326), suggesting that this finding was sensitive to individual studies and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Overall, the sensitivity analyses indicated that the findings for ACP documentation and ACP engagement were relatively robust, whereas the results for satisfaction and anxiety were less stable.

Publication bias analyses

Because all outcome-specific meta-analyses in this review included fewer than 10 studies, the publication bias analyses were considered to have limited interpretability and were therefore interpreted with caution. Accordingly, the results provided in Supplementary File 6 were treated as exploratory and for reference only, rather than as a basis for drawing firm conclusions regarding the presence or absence of publication bias.

Certainty of the body of evidence

Certainty assessments for the meta-analysed outcomes are presented in Supplementary File 7. The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for ACP documentation and ACP engagement, low for anxiety, and very low for AD completion, satisfaction, and decisional conflict. In particular, the certainty of evidence for AD completion and satisfaction was downgraded because of substantial heterogeneity and wide confidence intervals, whereas decisional conflict was downgraded mainly because of risk of bias and very serious imprecision. Overall, these findings suggest that the evidence supporting the effects of online ACP decision aid programmes is more certain for ACP process outcomes than for the other outcomes assessed.

Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis identified 12 studies evaluating online ACP decision aid programmes for adults with cancer. Despite substantial variation across studies in intervention content, delivery mode, level of support, comparator conditions, and outcome measures, the overall pattern of findings was cautiously favorable. The clearest benefits were observed for ACP documentation and ACP engagement, whereas no statistically significant effects were found for AD completion, satisfaction, or decisional conflict. Although anxiety showed a small reduction in the intervention group in the updated analysis, this result should be interpreted cautiously because only 2 studies contributed data and the sensitivity analysis indicated limited stability.

These findings suggest that online ACP decision aid programmes may have clearer effects on ACP process outcomes, particularly ACP engagement and ACP documentation, than on some other patient-reported outcomes. This pattern is plausible because ACP is generally understood as an ongoing process of clarifying values, discussing preferences, and, where appropriate, documenting them over time [2, 3]. Our findings also extend previous broader reviews, which suggested that web-based ACP interventions are promising but heterogeneous and that oncology-specific evidence remains limited [9, 24, 25].

Effects on process outcomes

The most consistent benefits of online ACP decision aid programmes in this review were observed for process outcomes, particularly ACP engagement and ACP documentation. This pattern is clinically plausible because ACP is widely understood as an ongoing process in which individuals are supported to clarify values, consider future care preferences, prepare for conversations, and document these preferences where appropriate, rather than as a single act of document completion [2, 3]. In the present review, interventions such as PREPARE, web-based advance directives, facilitated ACP, and programmes combining patient-facing tools with clinician communication support were intended to strengthen key ACP processes, which may partly explain the significant improvements observed in ACP engagement and ACP documentation. In addition, both outcomes remained stable in sensitivity analyses, suggesting that these findings are among the more robust results of the review.

By contrast, AD completion did not show a statistically significant pooled effect, despite its conceptual proximity to ACP documentation. One possible explanation is that online decision aid programmes may be particularly effective in improving readiness, prompting reflection, and facilitating discussion or documentation, whereas completion of a formal advance directive may also depend on additional legal, organisational, and clinical factors beyond the intervention itself [40, 41]. Taken together, earlier reviews suggest that ACP decision support interventions most consistently improve proximal ACP processes, such as knowledge, communication, engagement, and documentation, whereas effects on more distal psychosocial or clinical outcomes are less consistent, in the context of substantial heterogeneity in intervention content, format, and delivery [9, 24]. In the present review, the principal value of online ACP decision aid programmes for adults with cancer appeared to lie in strengthening the ACP process, particularly by increasing patient engagement and the likelihood that preferences were documented.

Limited effects beyond process outcomes

Beyond ACP process outcomes, the effects of online ACP decision aid programmes were limited or inconsistent. In the present review, no statistically significant pooled effects were observed for satisfaction or decisional conflict, and the small reduction in anxiety should be interpreted cautiously because it was based on only 2 studies and was not stable in sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that outcomes beyond ACP process measures, such as satisfaction, decisional conflict, and anxiety, may be less responsive to online ACP programmes alone than process outcomes such as engagement and documentation.

One possible explanation is that outcomes such as satisfaction, decisional conflict, and anxiety are influenced not only by structured information and values clarification, but also by when ACP conversations are initiated, how seriously ill patients are engaged in clinician-led communication, and how family members participate in these discussions [42–45]. In oncology, ACP and serious illness conversations are still often introduced late in the illness trajectory, even though many patients appear to welcome earlier discussions; however, patients’ readiness and preferred timing vary across individuals [46–48]. Although online ACP tools can support preparation and reflection, they cannot fully substitute for clinician-patient conversations that address prognosis, respond to emotional concerns, and help patients weigh treatment trade-offs, all of which may shape outcomes such as anxiety, decisional conflict, and satisfaction [49, 50]. Likewise, family involvement may either support understanding and shared preparation or, when preferences differ, contribute to uncertainty and distress [51].

A further consideration is that patient decision aids are primarily intended to support informed and values-congruent choices, rather than to directly improve all broader psychosocial outcomes [52]. Accordingly, benefits may be more readily detected for immediate ACP-related processes than for outcomes such as satisfaction, decisional conflict, or anxiety. As a result, an online ACP decision aid on its own may be sufficient to improve ACP-related processes, but insufficient to produce consistent improvements in broader non-process outcomes. These findings suggest that online ACP decision aid programmes may be more effective when integrated with clinician communication and follow-up support rather than used alone.

Clinical practice implications

The present findings suggest that online ACP decision aid programmes may have practical value in oncology care, but primarily as tools that support, rather than replace, clinician-led ACP. In this review, the clearest benefits were observed for ACP engagement and ACP documentation, indicating that these programmes may be particularly useful for helping patients prepare for ACP, reflect on their values and preferences, and move towards documented discussions and decisions. This interpretation is consistent with the consensus view of ACP as an ongoing process that supports individuals in understanding and sharing their values, goals, and preferences for future care, rather than as a single event [2].

A key implication for practice is that these programmes may be more useful when integrated into routine oncology care and followed by opportunities for clinician discussion, rather than being relied on as stand-alone educational resources or introduced only when illness has clearly advanced [7]. Evidence from cancer care suggests that many patients prefer earlier ACP discussions, although preferences regarding timing and readiness vary across individuals [42, 53]. In practice, online ACP tools may be most useful when they are introduced at a time that matches patients’ readiness and are accompanied by follow-up discussions with clinicians to review questions, clarify preferences, and support documentation [22].

The findings also suggest that successful implementation depends on more than simply providing access to a digital programme. The pattern of findings suggests that online ACP tools may be most useful when integrated into existing communication and documentation processes in oncology care. Within such workflows, oncology clinicians and nurses may help by introducing these tools, reinforcing ACP discussions, and supporting the translation of patients’ reflections into documented preferences and care plans [54–56]. Recent oncology nursing research likewise highlights the importance of role clarity, ongoing support, and enabling organisational conditions for ACP in cancer care [5].

Finally, implementation should remain person-centred. In this review, the included programmes varied substantially in content, level of support, and mode of delivery, and only some interventions explicitly incorporated family involvement or additional interpersonal support. This heterogeneity suggests that online ACP decision aid programmes are unlikely to suit all patients in the same way. Rather than adopting a single format, implementation may need to be tailored to patients’ informational needs, communication preferences, readiness, and desired level of support [12].

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. First, it focused specifically on online ACP decision aid programmes for adults with cancer, thereby providing a more targeted synthesis than previous broader reviews of ACP interventions or mediated decision support, which often included mixed populations, multiple delivery formats, or non-oncology contexts [9, 24, 25]. Second, the review used a comprehensive search across multiple databases and supplementary sources and was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidance [29]. Third, risk of bias was assessed using established tools appropriate to study design. Fourth, where studies were sufficiently comparable, meta-analysis was undertaken to quantify intervention effects, while the inclusion of both ACP process outcomes and other patient-reported outcomes allowed a broader understanding of the potential role of these programmes in oncology care.

This review also has several limitations. First, the number of studies contributing to each meta-analysis was small, which limited statistical power and reduced the precision of several pooled estimates. Second, substantial heterogeneity was present across studies in intervention content, degree of support, comparator conditions, participant characteristics, and outcome measurement, which may partly explain the inconsistency of findings across outcome domains. Third, although most included studies were randomised controlled trials, two non-randomised studies were also included, and several studies were judged as having some concerns, with some even rated as being at high, serious or critical risk of bias. In addition, the GRADE assessment indicated low or very low certainty of evidence for several outcomes. These methodological limitations may reduce confidence in some pooled estimates; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, most included studies were conducted in the USA, the findings may be influenced by the specific healthcare system, legal framework and cultural context in which ACP is implemented, and their transferability to other settings may therefore be limited.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that online ACP decision aid programmes may support ACP in adults with cancer, with the most consistent evidence observed for ACP engagement and ACP documentation. However, evidence for AD completion, satisfaction, decisional conflict, and anxiety remains limited or inconsistent. Overall, based on the current evidence, these programmes may be more appropriately viewed as tools to support, rather than replace, clinician-led ACP in oncology care. Given the small number of studies, heterogeneity in interventions and outcome measures, wide confidence intervals for several outcomes, and the predominance of US-based studies, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further adequately powered research is needed to confirm the effects of online ACP decision aid programmes and identify the programme features and implementation approaches most likely to benefit adults with cancer.
