Radon Partitions of Random Gaussian Polytopes
Moshe J. White

Abstract
In this paper we study a probabilistic framework for Radon partitions, where our points are chosen independently from the d d -dimensional normal distribution. For every point set we define a corresponding Radon polytope, which encodes all information about Radon partitions of our set - with Radon partitions corresponding to faces of the polytope. This allows us to derive expressions for the probability that a given partition of N N randomly chosen points in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} forms a Radon partition. These expressions involve conic kinematic formulas and intrinsic volumes, and in general require repeated integration, though we obtain closed formulas in some cases. This framework can provide new perspectives on open problems that can be formulated in terms of Radon partitions, such as Reay’s relaxed Tverberg conjecture.



1
Introduction

For many topics in mathematics, it is often natural to gain insight by exploring random objects, and convex polytopes are no exception. There are a number of methods to produce random polytopes. One widely explored method is selecting vertices uniformly at random from a convex body. Questions regarding the distribution of properties of these random polytopes, such as the volume and face numbers, go as far back as a question asked by Sylvester in 1864. Another method produces a “Gaussian polytope”, which is the convex hull of
N
points independently using the
d
-dimensional standard normal distribution (see
[
11
]
for a survey on random polytopes).

Radon’s classic theorem states that any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
may be partitioned into two sets with intersecting convex hulls, provided that
N\geq d+2
. We explore Radon partitions of the vertices of Gaussian polytopes - vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
chosen independently from a standard normal distribution. We succeed in principle in producing an expression for the probability that a given partition of such points is a Radon partition (depending on
N,d
, and the number of points in each part - see Proposition
3.6
). The goal of this paper is to introduce this model as a natural random setting for exploring problems related to Radon’s theorem.

As we explore in Section 2, all information about Radon partitions of a configuration of
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is encoded in a “Radon polytope”, which is obtained as the intersection between a fixed polytope (depending only on
N
) and a linear space
V
(whose dimension depends on
N,d
). A partition of our points is a Radon partition if and only if
V
intersects nontrivially with a certain cone (determined by the partition). In Section 3, we argue that vertices of the Gaussian polytope are a natural way to induce random Radon partitions, because in this setting the space
V
is chosen
uniformly
at random. We can therefore apply conic Kinematic formulas and obtain expressions for the probabilities that various Radon partitions will appear. In general these expressions involve repeated integration and thus rely on numeric approximation, but in some cases we obtain closed formulas. In Section 4 we give a very small example of open problems that can be described using Radon partitions.

For such problems we gain two new possible approaches: first, the problem is interpreted in terms of Radon polytopes. Second, we obtain a probabilistic version of our problem, which may be of independent interest.

2
Radon polytopes

In this paper, a polytope is the convex hull of a finite collection of points, and a cone is short for a convex polyhedral cone. We will make use of conic intrinsic volumes, and refer to
[
1
]
for all related definitions.

We will give a description of partitions of
N
points using a polytope in real
(N-1)
-dimensional space, but the standard coordinate system for
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
isn’t very convenient. Instead, we will use
N
coordinates with a single dependency between them:

Definition 2.1
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, denote

\mathbb{A}^{N}=\left\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}=0\right\}.

Informally, we consider
\mathbb{A}^{N}
as the space of possible “affine dependencies” among
N
points. Note that as a vector space,
\mathbb{A}^{N}
is just
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
. Throughout this paper,
N
will correspond to the number of points to be partitioned, not the dimension of spaces.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
denote the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
[N]
denote the first
N
natural numbers,
\left\{1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
. Given two disjoint subsets
A,B\subset[N]
, we define the
partition cone
of
A
and
B
as

C(A,B)=\textnormal{cone}\left\{e_{i}-e_{j}\ :\ i\in A,\ j\in B\right\},

and the
partition face
of
A
and
B
as

F(A,B)=\textnormal{conv}\left\{e_{i}-e_{j}\ :\ i\in A,\ j\in B\right\}.

We mention a basic property of partition cones:

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that for every
i\in I
, we have a partition cone
C(A_{i},B_{i})
. Then

\bigcap_{i\in I}C(A_{i},B_{i})=C(\cap_{i}A_{i},\cap_{i}B_{i}),

under the convention that
C(A,B)=\left\{0\right\}
if
A=\emptyset
or
B=\emptyset
.

Given
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{A}^{N}
, we have
\alpha\in C(A,B)
if and only if
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
for every
i\in A
,
\alpha_{j}\leq 0
for every
j\in B
, and
\alpha_{k}=0
for every
k\notin A\cup B
. We additionally have
\alpha\in F(A,B)
if and only if

\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}=1=\sum_{j\in B}-\alpha_{j}\ .

Recall the following proof of Radon’s classic theorem: if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
N\geq d+2
, the points are affinely dependent. That is, there exists
0\neq\alpha\in\mathbb{A}^{N}
such that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}x_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=0
. Then
\alpha\in C(A,B)
for some disjoint
A,B\subset[N]
:
A
(and
B
) are simply the sets of indices of negative (and positive) coordinates of
\alpha
. W.l.o.g. we may assume that
\alpha
is rescaled to satisfy
\alpha\in F(A,B)
. Now we rearrange the affine dependence as

\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{j\in B}-\alpha_{j}x_{j}\ ,

and this proves that
\textnormal{conv}\left\{x_{i}:i\in A\right\}\cap\textnormal{conv}\left\{x_{j}:j\in B\right\}\neq\emptyset
. We call this a Radon partition for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
:

Definition 2.4
.

A partition in
[N]
is an ordered pair
(A,B)
, where
A,B\subset[N]
are disjoint. Such
(A,B)
is a Radon partition for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if

\textnormal{conv}\left\{x_{i}\ :\ i\in A\right\}\cap\textnormal{conv}\left\{x_{j}\ :\ j\in A\right\}\neq\emptyset.

Remark 2.5
.

We refer to a partition
in

[N]
, to emphasize that
A\cup B
might be a proper subset of
[N]
.

We furthermore go against common terminology and regard a partition as an ordered pair - the first set specifies indices with positive values, and the second corresponds negative values. However the connection between
(A,B)
and
(B,A)
should be clear, e.g.
C(B,A)=-C(A,B)
, a partition
(A,B)
is a Radon partition if and only if
(B,A)
is a Radon partition, etc.

The proof draws a strong connection between
affine dependencies
and
Radon partitions
among any configuration of
N
points in any
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 2.6
.

Let
2\leq N\in\mathbb{N}
. The
complete Radon polytope
for
N
points is

P_{N}=\left\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{A}^{N}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\alpha_{i}\right|\leq 2\right\}.

Note that
P_{N}
is a polytope of dimension
N-1
, and the proper faces of
P_{N}
are precisely the partition faces
\left\{F(A,B)\right\}
(we may take
A=B=\emptyset
to obtain the empty face). See Figure
2
for the polytopes
P_{3}
and
P_{4}
.

2.1
Radon polytopes corresponding to point collections

We now define (general) Radon polytopes, which encode all relevant information about Radon partitions for some collection of points.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
N,d\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{N}
be a linear subspace of codimension
d
(i.e. dimension
N-d-1
). Then
P_{N}\cap V
is called a
Radon polytope
(for
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be some collection of points, not contained in any proper affine subspace. We define the corresponding
Radon polytope
as

P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\left\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in P_{n}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}x_{i}=0\right\}.

Indeed, for a point collection
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the linear map taking each
e_{i}
to
x_{i}
. Then
\ker(f)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a subspace of dimension
N-d
(unless the points are linearly dependent), and
V:=\ker(f)\cap\mathbb{A}^{N}
is a subspace of dimension
N-d-1
(unless the points lie on some affine subspace). Thus the
(N-d-1)
-dimensional polytope
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=P_{N}\cap V
, is a Radon polytope.

Any
\alpha\in V
indicates a Radon partition of the collection, as in the proof of Radon’s theorem above. Furthermore,
(A,B)
is a Radon partition for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
if and only if
F(A,B)\cap V\neq\emptyset
(or equivalently,
C(A,B)\cap V\neq\left\{0\right\}
). Since the proper faces of
P_{N}\cap V
are precisely those
F(A,B)\cap V
which are non empty, there is a one to one correspondence between proper faces of the Radon polytope and the (ordered) Radon partitions of the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
.

We summarize this as a Lemma:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a collection of points, and let
A,B\subset[n]
be disjoint subsets.

Denote
V=\ker\left((a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\mapsto\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}\right)\cap\mathbb{A}^{N}
. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

(A,B)
is a Radon partition for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
,

2.

F(A,B)\cap V\neq\emptyset
,

3.

C(A,B)\cap V\neq\left\{0\right\}
.

Furthermore, proper faces of the Radon polytope correspond to Radon partitions of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
.

3
Random polytopes

So far, we have proved that every
N
points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
have a corresponding Radon polytope. We will soon see that the converse is also true: given a subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{N}
of codimension
d
, there exists
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
P_{N}\cap V
is the corresponding Radon polytope, and describe how the subspace
V
can be chosen randomly.

Suppose we have a question stated in terms of the Radon partitions of a collection or collections of
N
points
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(perhaps asking for the existence of
X
with certain properties, or whether every such
X
has certain properties). In this case, it is sufficient to understand the Radon polytope
P(X)=P_{N}\cap V
, where
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{N}
is a linear subspace of codimension
d
. It is often useful to ignore the specific collection
X
altogether, and focus on the relevant subspace
V
and Radon polytope
P_{N}\cap V
. If we choose
V
randomly, we obtain a probabilistic version of our question.

One way to choose
V

uniformly
at random, is by taking
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the image of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, under a random orthogonal projection to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In more detail, choose
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1}\in\mathbb{A}^{N}
randomly (using any distribution which is invariant under orthogonal transformations). Let
w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the vectors obtained by applying the Gram-Schmidt process on
\mathbf{1},v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-1}
, where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the vector with
1
’s in each coordinate. For each
i=1,\ldots,N
, let
e_{i}=\sum_{j}a^{i}_{j}w_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the
i
-th standard basis vector. Define
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the linear map taking
e_{i}
to
x_{i}:=\left(a^{i}_{N-d},\ldots,a^{i}_{N-1}\right)
. Clearly,
\ker f=\text{span}\left\{w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{N-d-1}\right\}
, therefore
\ker(f)\cap\mathbb{A}^{N}=\text{span}\left\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{N-d-1}\right\}=\text{span}\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-d-1}\right\}
. For such randomly chosen
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we obtain the corresponding Radon configuration
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=P_{N}\cap V
, where
V
is chosen uniformly at random.

Also note that for any fixed subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{N}
of codimension
d
, we have
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
P(x_{1},\ldots,X_{N})=P_{N}\cap V
(simply take
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N-d-1}
as a basis for
V
).

Perhaps surprisingly, we could also choose
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
independently from a Gaussian distribution. While the collections of points obtained in these two methods have quite different distributions (e.g. in the second method the points are independent), the corresponding random Radon configuration is uniformly distributed in both cases
[
2
]
.

Henceforth, whenever
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and/or a subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{N}
of codimension
d
are chosen randomly, we assume that
V
is chosen uniformly at random, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
are chosen independently from a standard Gaussian distribution. We also say that the Radon polytope
P_{N}\cap V
is chosen uniformly at random.

3.1
Probability of obtaining specific Radon partitions

Consider a partition
[N]=A\cup B
, and denote
|A|=m,\ |B|=n
, so
N=m+n
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{d}(A,B)
denote the probability that
(A,B)
is a Radon partition for randomly chosen points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(equivalently, the probability that
F(A,B)
intersects a randomly chosen Radon polytope for
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

For a cone
C
in some ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, let
v_{0}(C),v_{1}(C),\ldots,v_{D}(C)
denote its conic intrinsic volumes (see
[
1
]
for definitions and any unproved statements regarding intrinsic volumes). Isometric cones have the same intrinsic volumes and in particular don’t depend on the ambient space, so it is useful to follow the convention
v_{k}(C)\equiv 0
for every
k>\dim(C)
. If
C
is not a vector space, the probability that a random linear subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
of codimension
d
intersects
C
nontrivially, is given by the conic Kinematic formulas (see
[
1
, §2.3.3]
):

\mathbb{P}(C\cap V\neq\left\{0\right\})=2\sum_{i\geq 1\text{ odd}}v_{d+i}(C).

(3.1)

Let
v_{k}(m,n)
denote
v_{k}\left(C(A,B)\right)
: this is well defined because any two partition cones
C(A,B)
and
C(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
are isometric if
|A|=|A^{\prime}|
and
|B|=|B^{\prime}|
. From Lemma
2.8
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{d}(A,B)=2\sum_{i\geq 1\text{ odd}}v_{d+i}(m,n),

(3.2)

noting that
v_{k}(m,n)=0
for
k\geq m+n
(because
C(A,B)
has dimension
|A|+|B|-1
). We also mention Gauss–Bonnet
[
1
, Corollary 4.4]
, from which it follows that for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{i\text{ odd}}v_{i}(m,n)=\sum_{i\text{ even}}v_{i}(m,n)=\frac{1}{2}.

(3.3)

We will now explore
v_{k}(m,n)
and
\mathbb{P}_{d}(A,B)
, for some values of
d,k,m,n
.

3.1.1
The case
d=1
and
k=0

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
[m+n]=A\cup B
be a partition with
|A|=m
and
|B|=n
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{1}(A,B)=1-\frac{2}{\binom{m+n}{m}}.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
be independent normal random variables, where
N=m+n
. The random configuration
P_{N}\cap\ker(f)
is uniformly distributed, where
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
takes each
e_{i}
to
x_{i}
. With probability
1
, we have
x_{\sigma(1)}<x_{\sigma(2)}<\cdots<x_{\sigma(N)}
for some permutation
\sigma:[N]\to[N]
. Furthermore,
\sigma
is uniformly distributed, since
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
are chosen independently. Clearly
(A,B)
is
not
a Radon partition for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
if and only if
x_{i}<x_{j}
for every
i\in A,j\in B

or
if
x_{i}>x_{j}
for every
i\in A,j\in B
. Thus the probability that
(A,B)
is not a Radon partition is
2\cdot{\binom{N}{m}}^{-1}
, and the claim follows.

3.1.3
The case
m=2

To obtain a formula for
v_{k}(m,n)
when
m>1
in terms of the functions
g_{0},g_{1},\ldots
, we will first prove a certain inclusion-exclusion principle for the intrinsic volumes. From (
3.1
), we learn that for a cone

that is not a vector space,

v_{k}(C)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{P}(C\cap V_{k-1}\neq\left\{0\right\})-\mathbb{P}(C\cap V_{k+1}\neq\left\{0\right\})\Big),

where each
V_{d}
is a randomly chosen vector subspace of codimension

. Note that the above equation holds even when
C
is a subspace of dimension
\ell\neq k,k+1
: in this case
v_{\ell}(C)=1
,
v_{d}(C)=0
for
d\neq\ell
(see
[
1
]
), and
\mathbb{P}(C\cap V_{d})
is
1
if
\ell>d
and
0
otherwise. We deduce:

Lemma 3.4

(Inclusion-exclusion)
.

Let
C,D
be cones in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, such that
C\cup D
is a cone. Then

v_{k}(C\cup D)=v_{k}(C)+v_{k}(D)-v_{k}(C\cap D),

provided that none of the cones
C\cup D,C,D,C\cap D
are a linear subspace of dimension
k
or
k+1
. More generally, if
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
and
\cup_{i}C_{i}
are cones, then

v_{k}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j+1}\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq n}v_{k}\left(C_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap C_{i_{j}}\right)\right),

(3.5)

provided that none of the inputs to the intrinsic volume function
v_{k}(\cdot)
that appear in the equation are a linear subspace of dimension
k
or
k+1
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that (
3.5
) holds if we replace
v_{k}
with the function

\widetilde{v}_{k}(C):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{P}(C\cap V_{k-1}\neq\left\{0\right\})-\mathbb{P}(C\cap V_{k+1}\neq\left\{0\right\})\Big),

which is defined on any cone
C
. If the assumptions of the lemma hold,
\widetilde{v}_{k}
and
v_{k}
are in agreement for every input appearing in the equation.
∎

Fix
n
, and let
D_{i}=C(\left\{i\right\},[n+2]-\left\{i\right\})
for
i=1,2
, and
D_{12}=C(\left\{1,2\right\},[n+2]-\left\{1,2\right\})
. Then
C=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup D_{12}
is itself a cone, and
C=L\oplus C_{0}
, where
L=\text{span}\left\{e_{1}-e_{2}\right\}
, and

C_{0}=\textnormal{cone}\left\{\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+e_{2})-e_{i}\ :\ i=3,4,\ldots,n+2\right\},

which is isometric to
C_{n}(\frac{1}{2})
. Recall that:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a linear subspace of dimension
\ell
, and let
C_{0}\subseteq L^{\perp}
be a cone. Then the intrinsic volumes of the cone
L+C_{0}
are given by

v_{k}(L+C_{0})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }k<\ell,\\
v_{k-\ell}(C_{0})&\text{ if }k\geq\ell.\end{cases}

This follows from the definition of the intrinsic volumes from
[
1
]
and the obvious bijection between
k
-dimensional faces of
L+C_{0}
and
(k-\ell)
-dimensional faces of
C_{0}
.

In our case,
D_{i}\cap D_{12}=C(\left\{i\right\},[n+2]-\left\{1,2\right\})
, and other intersections among
D_{1},D_{2},D_{12}
equal
\left\{0\right\}
(see Lemma
2.3
).

In particular, Lemma
3.4
holds for
k>0
, and we obtain

\begin{split}v_{k-1}\left(C_{n}(\frac{1}{2})\right)=v_{k}(C)&=v_{k}(D_{12})+v_{k}(D_{1})+v_{k}(D_{2})-v_{k}(D_{1}\cap D_{12})-v_{k}(D_{2}\cap D_{12})\\
&=v_{k}(2,n)+2v_{k}(1,n+1)-2v_{k}(1,n).\end{split}

Applying (
3.4
) and Proposition
3.3
and simplifying, we obtain for
k=1,2,\ldots,n+1
:

\begin{split}v_{k}(2,n)=&\binom{n}{k-1}g_{k-1}\left(-\frac{1}{k+1}\right)g_{n-k+1}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\right)\\
&+2g_{k}\left(-\frac{1}{k+1}\right)\left[\binom{n}{k}g_{n-k}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\right)-\binom{n+1}{k}g_{n-k+1}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}\right)\right]\end{split}

(3.6)

under the convention that
\binom{n}{\ell}=0
for
\ell>n
.

3.1.4
The case
m\geq 3

In principle, the method by which (
3.6
) is obtained using inclusion-exclusion, can be applied for every
m
:

Proposition 3.6
.

For every disjoint
A,B\subseteq[N]
such that
|A|=m,\ |B|=n
and every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a closed formula for
\mathbb{P}_{d}(A,B)
, consisting only of addition, subtraction, multiplication, integers, and the values
g_{\ell}\left(\pm\frac{1}{j}\right)
where
\ell=0,1,\ldots,m+n-1
and
j\geq d+2
.

Proof.

By applying (
3.2
), it suffices to prove that for every
k,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq k\leq m+n-1
, there exists a closed formula for
v_{k}(m,n)
in terms of the values
g_{\ell}\left(\pm\frac{1}{k+1}\right)
, for
\ell=0,1,\ldots,m+n-1
. This is done by generalizing the proof leading to (
3.6
), using inclusion-exclusion:

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the collection of cones

\mathcal{C}_{m}=\left\{C(A,[m+n]-A)\ :\ \emptyset\neq A\subseteq[m]\right\}.

Then
\cup\mathcal{C}_{m}=L+C_{0}
, where

L=\text{span}\left\{e_{1}-e_{i}\ :\ i=2,\ldots,m\right\}

and

C_{0}=\textnormal{cone}\left\{\frac{1}{m}(e_{1}+\ldots+e_{m})-e_{i}\ :\ i=m+1,\ldots,m+n\right\},

which is isometric to
C_{n}(\frac{1}{m})
. Thus Lemma
3.4
applies to
\cup\mathcal{C}_{m}
, and (
3.5
) becomes

v_{k}\left(\cup\mathcal{C}_{m}\right)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(-1)^{|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+1}v_{k}\left(\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\right),

(3.7)

where the sum is extended over all non-empty subcollections
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{m}
. From Lemma
3.5
,
v_{k}\left(\cup\mathcal{C}_{m}\right)=v_{k}\left(L+C_{n}(\frac{1}{m})\right)
is either
0
(if
k<m-1
) or

v_{k}\left(\cup\mathcal{C}_{m}\right)=v_{k-m+1}\left(C_{n}(\frac{1}{m})\right),

which has a formula as required from Proposition
3.3
(0). It follows from Lemma
2.3
that the right hand side of (
3.7
) consists of
v_{k}(m,n)
and a combination of terms
v_{k}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
with
m^{\prime}<m
. Our claim now follows by induction on
m
.
∎

For
m=3
, we obtain for
k=1,2,\ldots,n+2
:

\begin{split}v_{k}(3,n)=&\binom{n}{k-2}g_{k-2}^{-}g_{n-k+2}^{+}\\
&+3g_{k-1}^{-}\left[\binom{n}{k-1}g_{n-k+1}^{+}-\binom{n+1}{k-1}g_{n-k+2}^{+}\right]\\
&+3g_{k}^{-}\left[\binom{n}{k}g_{n-k}^{+}-2\binom{n+1}{k}g_{n-k+1}^{+}+\binom{n+2}{k}g_{n-k+2}^{+}\right]\end{split}

(3.8)

under the convention that
\binom{n}{\ell}=0
for
\ell<0
or
\ell>n
, where
g_{\ell}^{\pm}
is short for
g_{\ell}\left(\pm\frac{1}{k+1}\right)
.

3.1.5
The case
k=m+n-1

While the general formula of
v_{k}(m,n)
is quite complicated, we can simplify it when
k=m+n-1
:

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

v_{m+n-1}(m,n)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{m}{i}g_{n+i}\left(-\frac{1}{m+n}\right).

Proof.

We start by applying the inclusion-exclusion method on
\mathcal{C}_{m}
from above, and show that (
3.7
) simplifies when
k=n+m-1
. Indeed, a partition cone
C(A,B)
has dimension
|A|+|B|-1
. If
A\cup B
and
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
are two partitions of
[m+n]
, then
C(A,B)\cap C(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=C(A\cap A^{\prime},B\cap B^{\prime})
has dimension strictly less than
m+n-1
. Thus
v_{m+n-1}(\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=0
whenever
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
consists of more than one cone, and (
3.7
) for
k=m+n-1
simplifies to

\begin{split}v_{m+n-1}(\cup\mathcal{C}_{m})&=\sum_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[m]}v_{m+n-1}\left(C(A,[m+n]-A)\right)\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}v_{m+n-1}(j,m+n-j),\end{split}

Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
3.5
allow us to substitute
v_{m+n-1}(\cup\mathcal{C}_{m})=v_{n}\left(C_{n}(\frac{1}{m})\right)=g_{n}\left(-\frac{1}{m+n}\right)
, and rearranging gives us

v_{m+n-1}(m,n)=g_{n}\left(-\frac{1}{m+n}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\binom{m}{j}v_{m+n-1}\left(j,m+n-j\right).

(3.9)

We can now prove our claim by induction on
m
. For
m=1
, take (
3.4
) with
k=n
. For
m>1
, take (
3.9
) and substitute each
v_{m+n-1}(j,m+n-j)
using the induction hypothesis. The proof follows.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Clearly
v_{k}(m,n)=v_{k}(n,m)
, so proposition
3.7
implies the following curious identity: for every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{m}{i}g_{n+i}\left(-\frac{1}{m+n}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}g_{m+i}\left(-\frac{1}{m+n}\right).

3.2
Are balanced partitions more common?

Considering partitions
[N]=A\cup B
, which are more likely to appear as Radon partitions in our random setting: those where one of
A,B
is small and the other is large, or more “balanced” partitions, where
A,B
have roughly the same number of points? Assume w.l.o.g. that
|A|\leq|B|
. We say that
(A,B)
is balanced if
|A|=\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
(in the other extreme, we have
|A|=1
). There is some reason to suspect that balanced partitions are more common: if we take
N
points in convex position, none of the Radon partitions
A,B
have
|A|=1
. If our
N
points are on the moment curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, any Radon partition
(A,B)
has
|A|\geq\lfloor\frac{d+2}{2}\rfloor
(see
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
). On the other hand, every
N=d+3
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
have some balanced Radon partition (this is due to Flores and Van Kampen and holds even as a continuous analogue of Radon’s theorem, see e.g.
[
3
, Theorem 6.1]
, also see
[
12
]
).

In this sense, balanced partitions are unavoidable for large
N
, while non-balanced partitions can be avoided. We conjecture that for every
N,d
, the probability of finding a partition
[N]=A\cup B
as a Radon partition among
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is higher the more balanced our partition is:

Conjecture 3.9
.

Let
N,d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N\geq d+2
, and let
[N]=A\cup B=A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
be two partitions such that
|A|<|A^{\prime}|\leq|B^{\prime}|<|B|
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{d}(A,B)<\mathbb{P}_{d}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}).

Note that we require
N\geq d+2
because otherwise we have the trivial case
P_{d}(A,B)=0
.

4
Open problems described by Radon partitions

We consider two open problems around Radon partitions, and describe them as problems regarding Radon polytopes.

4.1
Radon with tolerance

Let
[N]=A\cup B
be a Radon partition of some
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
A\cup B
is a Radon partition
with tolerance
, if
A\setminus\left\{i\right\}\cup B\setminus\left\{i\right\}
is a Radon partition of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N}
for every
i\in[N]
. Let
t(d)
denote the minimal
N
such that
any
collection of
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
admits a Radon partition with tolerance. Larman
[
7
]
proved that
t(d)\leq 2d+3
, suggested that equality holds for every
d
, and proved it for
d\leq 4
. A lower bound of
t(d)\geq\lceil\frac{5d}{3}\rceil
follows from a result by Ramírez-Alfonsín
[
9
]
.

Clearly,
t(d)>N
if and only if there exist some points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which every Radon partition is
not
a partition with tolerance (we may assume the points are in general position). We can describe partitions with tolerance in terms of the Radon configuration: we have
P:=P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=P_{N}\cap V
, where
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{N}
is a linear subspace of dimension
N-d-1
. From Lemma
2.8
, we know that Radon partitions
[N]=A\cup B
correspond to facets
F=F(A,B)\cap V
of
P
(that is, faces of dimension
N-d-2
). Every subface of
F
is of the form
F(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\cap V
, where
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
. That is, subfaces of
F
correspond to a sub-partitions of the points, and therefore
(A,B)
is a Radon partition with tolerance if and only if
F
contains
N
ridges (subfaces of codimension
1
). To summarize, we obtain the following geometric interpretation for Radon partitions with tolerance:

Question 4.1

(Radon with tolerance)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and denote
N=2d+2
. Does there exist a linear subspace
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{N}
of dimension
d+1
, such that whenever
[N]=A\cup B
is a partition and
F=F(A,B)\cap V
has dimension
d
, then
F
has less than
N
faces of dimension
d-1
.

For every
d
, the answer to the above is positive if and only if
t(d)=2d+3
.

4.2
Reay’s relaxed Tverberg conjecture

Consider the following problem: given
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, can we find
three
disjoint sets
A,B,C\subseteq[N]
, such that
(A,B),\ (A,C),\ (B,C)
are all Radon partitions? We call
(A,B,C)
a Reay partition. Let
T(d,3,2)
denote the smallest
N
such that
every
collection of
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a Reay partition. This is one case in a problem introduced by Reay in 1979, who conjectured that
T(d,3,2)=2d+3
(in general
T(d,r,k)
is the minimal
N
such that every set of
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be partitioned into
r
disjoint sets, such that the convex hull of every
k
among them has non-empty intersection, see
[
10
]
). It follows from Tverberg’s theorem that
T(d,3,2)\leq 2d+3
, and equality was proved for
d\leq 5
by Perles and Sigron
[
8
]
.

Since Reay’s conjecture can be formulated using Radon partitions, it can be understood as a question about Radon polytopes:

Question 4.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and denote
N=2d+2
. Does there exist a linear subspace
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{N}
of dimension
d+1
, such that for every disjoint
A,B,C\subset[N]
, at least one of the intersections
V\cap F(A,B),\ V\cap F(A,C),\ V\cap F(B,C)
is empty?

For every
d
, the answer to the above is positive if and only if Reay’s conjecture holds for
(d,3,2)
.

It seems possible to obtain a kinematic formula for multiple cones, generalizing (
3.1
) and computing the probability that random collection of
N
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
obtains a certain Reay partition. For
6
points, simulations allow us to estimate

\mathbb{P}(6\text{ points in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ have a Reay partition})\approx 0.427

(more accurately, the probability is
0.42714\pm 0.00016
with
99.7\%
confidence, by simulating
82\cdot 10^{6}
times).
