Prevalence and factors associated with open defecation practices among household population in Tanzania
Authors: Jovinary Adam, Menti Ndile, Marietha A Holela, Pankras Luoga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Tanzania, limited scientific evidence exists regarding the national prevalence of households practicing open defecation (OD) and its associated factors. This analytical cross-sectional study used secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey (TDHS-MIS). A weighted sample of 70,615 households was included in the analysis. Descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted to estimate the prevalence of open defecation and identify associated factors. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between open defecation and selected covariates, including age, education level, wealth status, and place of residence. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value of < 0.05 with a 95% confidence level. The proportion of households practicing open defecation was 9.7%. The multivariable logistic regression results showed that households headed by individuals aged 15–24 years (AOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.39–2.62), those residing in rural areas (AOR = 2.52; 95% CI: 1.56–4.07), households headed by individuals with informal education (AOR = 2.59; 95% CI: 1.97–3.41) or primary education (AOR = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.14–1.63), households with low economic status (AOR = 3.62; 95% CI: 2.50–5.23), households with nine or more members (AOR = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.17–2.46), and households without a radio (AOR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.05–1.53) or television (AOR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.32–1.93) had significantly higher odds of practicing open defecation compared with their counterparts. Addressing open defecation in Tanzania requires a combined approach that includes sanitation infrastructure development, targeted behavior change communication, socioeconomic empowerment, and expanded dissemination of sanitation messages through mass media. Strengthening outreach in underserved rural communities and prioritizing households with limited educational and informational resources will be critical for accelerating progress toward open-defecation-free status and achieving national and global sanitation goals.

Background

Open defecation has been defined as improper disposal of human excreta in water bodies, forests, farmlands, garbage bins or other open spaces1. This practice contributes to higher transmissions of gastrointestinal infections such as diarrhea, cholera and typhoid fever, child undernutrition and morbidity1. Globally the World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) Joint monitoring program (JMP) 2021 reported 494 million people practicing OD2. OD is a public health concern attributed to increased prevalence of diarrheal diseases causing 1.7 billion morbidities in children under five years of age (WHO 2024). The burden of open defecation disproportionately affects developing regions, particularly Sub-Saharan Africa, where access to safe water and improved sanitation remains limited3. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the OD practice is reported to be high, ranging up to 10%3,4. The reasons range from individual to wider community level, including beliefs, poverty, low education and rural-urban dynamics.

In Tanzania, OD is a public health concern attributed to the low coverage of improved sanitation and hygiene facilities5. Despite national and international programs aiming to achieve adequate and equitable access to sanitation and hygiene for all and ending open defecation by 20306. Still open defecation remains prevalent in the country. Open defecation contributes to the prevalence of diarrheal diseases among children under-five that’s reported to be 9%-19%7. This challenges the efforts made by the government and other stakeholders to fight the practice in the community4. The efforts include the implementation of the National sanitation campaign “Nyumba ni Choo” from 2012 that aimed at improving access to basic sanitation and hygiene among rural households through adequate water and sanitation facilities6,8,10. Open defecation practice trend has observed urban-rural disparities due to limited access to improved sanitation and infrastructures in rural areas as opposed to urban setting2,11,12.

Previous studies have associated the prevalence of OD with rurality, poverty and limited access to education and mass media5,10. However the studies covered a limited area and reported inconsistency findings4. Nonetheless despite national campaigns, the public health burden posed by persistence OD is substantial. Where the prevalence of diarrheal diseases among children 6–23 months of age was reported to be 15%-19% TDHS 2022. Therefore, this study intended to fill that gap by using the 2022 TDHS-MIS data set.

Methods

Study area

Data used in this study were obtained from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey (TDHS-MIS), a nationally representative survey conducted in the United Republic of Tanzania. The survey was carried out from 14 February to 21 July 2022 and covered both Tanzania Mainland and Zanzibar. Tanzania is located in East Africa and is the largest country in the region, with a total land area of approximately 940,000 square kilometers, including about 60,000 square kilometers of inland water bodies. Administratively, the country is divided into 31 regions, of which 26 are in Tanzania Mainland and 5 are in Zanzibar.

Study design

This study employed an analytical cross-sectional design using secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey.

Sampling design

The sample design for the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey (TDHS-MIS) was structured to provide nationally representative estimates for the entire country, as well as for urban and rural areas in Tanzania Mainland and Zanzibar. The survey employed a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design.

In the first stage, sampling points (clusters) consisting of enumeration areas (EAs) delineated during the 2012 Tanzania Population and Housing Census (PHC) were selected. Enumeration areas were chosen using probability proportional to size within each sampling stratum. A total of 629 clusters were selected, of which 211 were from urban areas and 418 from rural areas.

In the second stage, a fixed number of 26 households were systematically selected from each cluster, yielding an anticipated sample size of 16,354 households. Of these, 16,312 households were successfully selected, and 15,907 were found to be occupied and eligible for interview. Prior to data collection, a household listing operation was conducted in all selected enumeration areas.

The Household Questionnaire was used to collect basic demographic information on all usual household members and visitors, including age, sex, marital status, education level, and relationship to the household head. All women aged 15–49 years who were usual residents or visitors in the household on the night preceding the survey were eligible for interview. In a subsample comprising half of the selected households, all men aged 15–49 years were also eligible for interview. Additionally, in this subsample, and with parental or guardian consent, anthropometric measurements were collected from children aged 0–59 months, as well as from eligible women and men. Further details on the survey design and implementation procedures are provided in the official TDHS-MIS 2022 final report13.

Sample size

The weighted sample size for this study comprised a total weighted 70,615 households drawn from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey household dataset, which is nationally representative of households across all regions of Tanzania. Sampling weights were applied to ensure that the estimates accurately represent the national household population. Specifically, the analysis used the DHS-recommended sampling weights (hv005/1,000,000) to account for the complex survey design, including unequal probabilities of selection, clustering, and stratification. The application of these weights enhances the precision, validity, and generalizability of the study findings.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The dependent variable in this study was open defecation, which includes households defecating in the fields, forests, bushes, open bodies of water, beaches, or other open spaces. The binary variable of defecation status was developed and coded as “0” for “yes” and “1” for “no”.

Exposure variables

The explanatory variables selected in this study included age, place of residence, sex of household head, marital status, education level, wealth status, number of household members, has radio and has television. The exposure variables were chosen based on their availability within the data set and relevant literature14–18. These variables were further categorized such as age in years (15–24, 25–34, 35–49 and ≥ 50), place of residence (urban, rural), sex of household head (male, female), marital status (never married, married, separated), education level (informal education, primary, secondary education and above), wealth status (poor, middle, rich), number of household members (1–3, 4–5, 6–8, 9 and above), has radio (yes, no), and has television (yes, no).

Data analysis

The study extracted the 2022 Tanzania Household Member Recode (PR) data set (TZPR81FL.DTA). The Data were analyzed using STATA software, version 18. Descriptive analysis was used by calculating weighted frequencies and percentages, through which prevalence of open defecation was obtained and described the background characteristics of the study participants. The “svy” command was used in STATA for assigning the sample weight and to adjust for the clustering effect and sample stratification. Additionally, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among independent variables, including age, wealth status place of residence and level of education. All VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 10, with the highest value being 4, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the predictors. Bivariate analysis using Pearson’s chi-square test was conducted to examine associations between open defecation and selected household characteristics. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. The multivariable model was used to identify factors independently associated with open defecation. The goodness of fit of the final multivariable logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, which indicated an adequate fit (p-value > 0.05). Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant. The strength of association was estimated using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

Background characteristics of the study participants

The study included a weighted sample of 70,615 households. About four-fifths (80.2%) of household heads were aged 35 years and above, of whom 42% were aged 50 years or older and 38.2% were aged 35–49 years. Nearly three-quarters of households were in rural areas (71%), and more than three-quarters were male-headed (76%). Regarding marital status, almost three-fifths of household heads were married (58.7%). In terms of education, about half had attained primary education (51.5%), while one-third had no formal education (33.3%), and less than one-fifth had secondary education or higher (15.2%). With respect to wealth status, households were equally distributed between the poor (40%) and rich (40%) categories, while one-fifth belonged to the middle wealth group (20%), as shown in Table 1.

The prevalence of open defecation among household population in Tanzania

The overall prevalence of open practices was 9.7% (95% CI = 7.9, 11.6) in Tanzania. The practice varies widely across regions, from less than 1% in Dar es Salaam and Mjini Magharibi to over 20% in Simiyu (24%), Manyara (23%), Tabora (22%), Arusha (21%), and Mara (21%) as shown in Fig. 1.

Open defecation practices across sociodemographic variables

Table 2 presents the distribution of open defecation practices across selected sociodemographic characteristics and household factors. The results show significant variations in open defecation practices by age group. The highest prevalence was observed among individuals aged 15–24 years (14.2%), while the lowest was among those aged 35–49 years (8.7%).

Place of residence was strongly associated with open defecation practices, with rural residents reporting a substantially higher prevalence (13.3%) compared to urban residents (1%). Sex of the household head also showed a significant association, where households headed by females had a higher prevalence of open defecation (10.8%) than those headed by males (9.2%).

The prevalence was lowest among never-married individuals (6.1%) and highest among separated individuals (9.4%). Education level demonstrated a strong inverse relationship with open defecation, with households having informal education exhibiting the highest prevalence (15.6%), compared to those with primary education (7.9%) and secondary or higher education (2.3%).

Open defecation was most prevalent among poor households (21.4%), followed by middle-income households (4.9%), and was least common among rich households (1.0%). Household size was also significantly associated with open defecation practices, with prevalence increasing as the number of household members increased, reaching 13.5% among households with nine or more members.

Access to mass media was significantly associated with open defecation practices. Households without a radio had a higher prevalence (13.7%) compared to those with a radio (4.4%). Similarly, households without a television reported a higher prevalence of open defecation (13.0%) compared to those with a television (1.0%).

Factors associated with open defecation practices among household population in Tanzania

As shown in the logistic regression output in Table 3, household heads aged 15–24 years had higher odds (AOR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.39–2.62) of practicing open defecation compared to those aged 50 years and above. Households residing in rural areas (AOR = 2.52, 95% CI = 1.56–4.07) also had higher odds of open defecation compared to those in urban areas. Additionally, heads of households with informal (AOR = 2.59, 95% CI = 1.97–3.41) and primary education (AOR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.14–1.63) had higher odds of practicing open defecation than those with secondary education. Moreover, households with lower economic status were three times (AOR = 3.62–95% CI = 2.50–5.23) more likely to practice open defecation than those in the wealthiest quantile. Furthermore, households with nine or more members (AOR = 1.70, 95% CI = 1.17–2.46), those without a radio (AOR = 1.27, 95% CI = 1.05–1.53), and those without a television (AOR = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.32–1.93) had higher odds of practicing open defecation.

Discussion

The study aimed to determine the prevalence of open defecation and the factors associated with the practice among households in Tanzania. The analysis revealed that 9.7% of households practiced OD. This represents a notable decline from 15.7% to 9.7% over a seven-year period since 20158,10. The observed reduction suggests meaningful behavioral change, likely facilitated by national sanitation campaigns; however, the remaining prevalence indicates persistent challenges among vulnerable populations. In comparison with other countries, the prevalence of open defecation in Tanzania is higher than that reported in The Gambia (0.8%)19, Kenya (7%), but lower than the prevalence observed in Ghana (24.6%)20, and Lesotho (16.3%)21. These differences may be attributed to variations in the effectiveness of community-based sanitation programs, levels of government commitment, and contextual socioeconomic and cultural factors influencing sanitation practices.

The study found that Simiyu region had highest prevalence of open defecation in Tanzania. The possible explanation could be limited access to proper sanitation and low latrine coverage22, which is often exacerbated by poverty that hinders investment in infrastructure. Additionally, the region’s geography which is semi-arid may contribute to seasonal water scarcity that hinders latrine use cleaning and handwashing23. Also soil characteristics may affect latrine construction leading to building of temporary latrines that are not climate resilient further contributing to open defecation11. Similar geographic and livelihood related patterns (i.e. agriculture dependent rural areas) have been observed in Haiti, where rurality and farming households were strongly associated with higher OD24.

From the findings, factors associated with practicing OD include households with a head aged 15–24 years, those led by individuals with informal education, households without a radio or television, those with nine or more members, poor households, and households in rural areas.

Our findings showed that households with household head aged 15–24 years, had almost twice the Odds of practicing open defecation. Younger household heads may have limited economic resources hence lack of social capital to invest in sanitation infrastructures, lower prioritization on sanitation and hygiene or inability to mobilize household improvement2,26. In contrast, households headed by older individuals tend to demonstrate greater adherence to evolving social norms that discourage open defecation, likely reflecting accumulated life experience, increased community integration, and stronger engagement with national sanitation campaigns in Tanzania. Evidence from rural Tanzania indicates that older household heads are more likely to adopt and consistently use latrines, partly due to stronger normative pressure and greater exposure to long-standing sanitation promotion efforts26. However, our findings differ from earlier evidence based on the 2015 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey, which reported higher odds of open defecation among households headed by older individuals5. The observed shift may reflect temporal changes in sanitation behavior, improved targeting of older populations through sanitation campaigns such as Nyumba ni Choo, and increased awareness of the health risks associated with open defecation among older household heads over time. This suggests that age related patterns in open defecation practices in Tanzania are dynamic and influenced by evolving socioeconomic conditions and public health interventions.

Educational attainment was a significant determinant of open defecation, with higher odds observed among households headed by individuals with informal or primary education compared with those with secondary education or higher. In Tanzania, education level has been consistently linked to improved sanitation behavior and reduced open defecation27. Evidence from the national evaluation of the National Sanitation Campaign identified the education level of the household head as a key determinant of access to improved latrines28. Similarly, previous studies in Tanzania reported that households headed by individuals with secondary or higher education were more likely to use improved toilet facilities, attributable to better understanding of health risks and informed decision-making29. These findings are consistent with other studies emphasizing the role of education in promoting positive sanitation behaviors, increasing awareness of the importance of latrine use, and improving hygiene practices23,24.

In this study, OD practices were more prevalent among households lacking access to mass media. Households without a radio or television had higher odds of practicing OD. Mass media in Tanzania such as radio and television are major channels for public health messaging in Tanzania, including campaigns on sanitation, hygiene, and disease prevention led by government and development partners. Previous studies conducted in Tanzania have shown that households with access to mass media are more aware of the health risks associated with open defecation and are therefore more likely to adopt improved sanitation behaviors29,32. Behavior change communication has been identified as a critical component of sanitation programs, as lack of media access limits exposure to health promotion messages2. These findings are supported by other studies reporting that media access increases awareness of the negative impacts of open defecation, as well as the preventive and health benefits of owning and consistently using a latrine, underscoring the important role of mass media campaigns in promoting sanitation-related behavior change29,30.

Moreover, household size was associated with open defecation, with households comprising nine or more members having higher odds of practicing open defecation. Larger households are more likely to experience resource constraints and increased strain on sanitation facilities, which can reduce consistent access to and use of latrines. As a result, the mere presence of a latrine does not necessarily guarantee its use, particularly due to overcrowding and poor maintenance35. In addition, household dynamics and entrenched cultural practices have been reported to influence latrine use among large families, where communities accustomed to open defecation may continue the practice regardless of facility availability28,31. Rural research in Tanzania identified “defiant households” that have the resources to construct toilets but nonetheless prefer open defecation or toilet sharing, highlighting how household dynamics and social norms can override infrastructure availability36. The findings of this study are consistent with previous studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa37,38.

Poor households, households with low wealth quantiles had almost thrice the odds of practicing OD as compared to rich households. Previous studies in Tanzania found that this is due to inability to cover the cost of constructing sanitation facilities including toilets26. Poverty has remained one of the most significant factors hindering the construction and use of sanitation facilities in sub-Saharan countries1. In addition, limited awareness of the health risks associated with poor sanitation and hygiene may lead to lower prioritization of latrine construction and use. These findings are consistent with studies from Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Ghana, which reported that households with middle and high wealth status were less likely to practice open defecation compared with poor and lower-income households7,24,27.

Our findings also reported that rural households had increased odds of practicing OD. In Tanzania most rural communities depend on basic or unimproved latrines, and in their absence, revert to OD6. This may be due to households being isolated, lacking improved toilet facilities and living far from each other4. Also, most rural households depend on agriculture for livelihood hence they spend more time in the fields where access to latrines is limited hence facilitating OD24. The higher likelihood of open defecation among rural households underscores the need for targeted, rural specific sanitation strategies. These should prioritize affordable and improved latrine coverage, strengthen community led behavior change initiatives, and integrate sanitation solutions within agricultural and rural livelihood settings to reduce persistent rural–urban disparities. Our findings on rural-urban disparities align with evidence from other settings, including Haiti, where rural agricultural households faced higher odds of OD due to infrastructural constraints including limited access to infrastructures, information and income25,28,34.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of this study lie in the use of a large, weighted, nationally representative dataset from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey (TDHS-MIS), which enhances the generalizability of the findings to households across Tanzania during the study period. In addition, the use of a standardized DHS questionnaire, which has been widely implemented in more than 90 low- and middle-income countries, ensured data quality and comparability across settings. Furthermore, the application of robust statistical methods, including multivariable logistic regression strengthened the validity of the findings and allowed for a comprehensive assessment of factors associated with OD. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between explanatory variables and open defecation practices. Second, the study relied on self-reported sanitation practices, which may be subject to social desirability or recall bias, potentially leading to underreporting of open defecation. Third, the DHS dataset does not capture cultural, or behavioral factors, such as social norms, beliefs, or attitudes toward sanitation, which are known to influence open defecation practices. This limitation restricted the ability to explore these important determinants in the current analysis. Additionally, the analysis was limited to variables available in the TDHS-MIS dataset; as a result, factors such as latrine quality, functionality, distance to sanitation facilities, seasonal variations, and community-level sanitation environments could not be assessed. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable and nationally representative evidence on the magnitude and determinants of open defecation in Tanzania, which can inform sanitation policies and targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Using nationally representative data from the 2022 TDHS-MIS, this study found that 9.7% of households in Tanzania continue to practice open defecation, indicating substantial progress but persistent public health challenges. Open defecation was disproportionately prevalent among rural, poor, large households, those headed by younger and less-educated individuals, and households with limited access to mass media. These findings highlight enduring socioeconomic and informational inequalities that continue to impede universal access to improved sanitation. Addressing open defecation in Tanzania will require integrated strategies that extend beyond infrastructure provision to address the broader social and economic determinants of sanitation behavior.

Implications of the study findings

The results underscore the need for equity-focused sanitation policies that prioritize poor and rural households facing structural barriers to latrine adoption and use. Strengthening behavior change communication, particularly through community-based channels, is essential for households with low educational attainment and limited media access. The higher risk among households headed by younger individuals highlights the importance of linking sanitation promotion with youth-focused livelihood and empowerment initiatives. Overall, achieving open-defecation-free status in Tanzania will require context-specific, multisectoral interventions that integrate sanitation improvements with education, poverty reduction, and rural development efforts.
