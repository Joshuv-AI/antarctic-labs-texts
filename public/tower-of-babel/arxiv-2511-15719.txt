Chain of Summaries: Summarization Through Iterative Questioning
William Brach, Lukas Galke Poech

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly using external web content. However, much of this content is not easily digestible by LLMs due to LLM-unfriendly formats and limitations of context length. To address this issue, we propose a method for generating general-purpose, information-dense summaries that act as plain-text repositories of web content. Inspired by Hegel’s dialectical method, our approach, denoted as Chain of Summaries (CoS), iteratively refines an initial summary (thesis) by identifying its limitations through questioning (antithesis), leading to a general-purpose summary (synthesis) that can satisfy current and anticipate future information needs. Experiments on the TriviaQA, TruthfulQA, and SQUAD datasets demonstrate that CoS outperforms zero-shot LLM baselines by up to 66% and specialized summarization methods such as BRIO and PEGASUS by up to 27%. CoS-generated summaries yield higher Q&A performance compared to the source content, while requiring substantially fewer tokens and being agnostic to the specific downstream LLM. CoS thus resembles an appealing option for website maintainers to make their content more accessible for LLMs, while retaining possibilities for human oversight.



1
Introduction

Figure 1:
Chain of Summaries (CoS) method implements Hegel’s dialectical method for document summarization, iteratively refining an initial summary (thesis) through challenging questions (antithesis) to produce an information-dense optimal summary (synthesis) capable of answering diverse queries.

Large language models (LLMs) are given increasing autonomy to navigate and process information on the Web (e.g., Search and Deep Research functionalities in commercial systems). However, most of the web content is not easy to digest for LLMs and challenges their context size, leading to necessary compromises in performance and potential instabilities. It has been proposed to make the content on the web more accessible to LLMs by running parallel versions of the website in an LLM-friendly markdown format
4
. But how can we turn the plethora of existing content on the Web into information-dense chunks of plain text that capture the essence of the original content along various, potentially unpredictable, aspects?

Here, we look at this problem from the angle of summarization – in the sense of making information better available for LLMs to consume. Concretely, if we had a robust summarization method that captured the essence of the content in various aspects, we could hope that a variety of future questions could be answered just based on the summary. If questions come up that cannot be answered from the summary, the full website could be consulted, and the summary could be revised accordingly for future requests. This way, the plain-text summary would effectively act as a cache for websites, which can be implemented both server-side (e.g., website maintainers) and client-side (e.g., Deep Research systems) and can be subject to human oversight as needed. The key idea of this paper is to simulate this process through an instantiation of Hegel’s dialectical method
17
with LLMs. We hypothesize that an initial summary draft (the thesis in Hegel’s terms) can be iteratively refined based on its capability to answer synthetically generated questions (the antithesis), ultimately crystallizing a general-purpose summary (synthesis) that facilitates answering as many unseen future questions as possible (cache hit, in the analogy). Representation of the summary must be in natural language for two reasons. First, it ensures human verifiability; website maintainers must be able to easily read and validate the information that LLM agents will consume, preventing the spread of inaccurate or outdated content. Second, a plain-text format is model-agnostic, creating a universal and durable representation that any LLM agent can use, in contrast to model-specific formats like dense embeddings. We denote this procedure as the Chain of Summaries (CoS; Figure
1
).

To test the effectiveness of CoS, we focus on the experiments with question-answering datasets because they allow us to take questions from the dataset that we can run against the summary of the content. In addition, QA datasets enable us to compare how helpful synthetic questions are compared to questions taken from the dataset of these datasets (human-crafted). To achieve representative selection, we run experiments with a large commercial model (GPT-4o-mini
19
) as well as publicly available models of smaller sizes: Llama-3.2:3B
2
and Qwen-2.5:7B
20
to perform the roles in the CoS approach: generate the initial summary, synthesize questions, determine whether they can be answered from the summary, and iteratively refine the summary. Although technically, models can be mixed and matched, we keep the LLM backbone constant for our experiments. It is expected that GPT-4o-mini will perform best, yet we are interested in how much performance can be retained using smaller, local models. We further experimented with several different hyperparameters of the CoS approach and found that iteratively refining the summary (e.g., iterate 10 times with 1 question per iteration) has a clear advantage over presenting all questions at once (e.g., 50 questions). Our experiments show that this method of creating information-dense summaries not only shortens the content but also makes the core information more accessible to LLMs. It enables higher question-answering performance than using the full source document.

Finally, we investigate how much the multi-step CoS summarization method can be reduced to a single step. For this, we first fine-tune an encoder-only model to estimate the quality of downstream QA performance (through regression on the main evaluation metric). Then we use this encoder-only model as a reward model for reinforcement fine-tuning Qwen2:0.5B-Instruct
29
and Qwen3:0.6B
28
via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
23
. Our results show that CoS consistently outperforms the strong zero-shot summarization baseline, given the same backbone LLM – suggesting that CoS improves summaries’ QA performance. Our reinforcement-finetuned Qwen2:0.5B-Instruct and Qwen3:0.6B models performed slightly worse than the pure CoS approach, indicating that the incremental procedure is beneficial to obtain a high-quality summary with the limitation of potential reward hacking. Similarly, CoS outperforms the established specialized summarization baselines, such as BRIO
12
and PEGASUS
33
, as well as strong zero-shot summaries generated by LLM.

In sum, this paper makes three contributions:

•

We propose and evaluate the Chain of Summaries (CoS) approach, which is based on Hegel’s dialectical method. In this CoS, summaries serve as machine-consumable-human-readable knowledge caches, and quality is measured by downstream QA performance.

•

We provide empirical evidence that the proposed method improves upon the naive-but-strong baseline of zero-shot summarization with an LLM, and outperforms strong dedicated summarization baselines.

•

We fine-tune a BERT model to predict a summary performance in a downstream QA task, which we then use as a reward model for reinforcement fine-tuning (GRPO) of an LLM, testing whether the multi-step procedure can be reduced to a single step.

2
Related Work

Text summarization has seen advances from both abstractive and extractive single-pass methods. Abstractive models include PEGASUS
14
;
34
, with its sentence-reconstruction pre-training, and BRIO
1
, which improves candidate summary ranking. In extractive summarization, recent work like DiffuSum
31
uses diffusion models to match summary representations with source sentences. summarization via Large Language Models is becoming more and more popular, but often suffers from the "middle curse"
22
;
11
, a strong positional bias where models struggle to utilize information from the middle of long contexts. Recent work has focused on improving LLM-retrieval integration through two main approaches. The first treats LLMs as black boxes while refining retrieved context: Replug
25
uses perplexity scores to train the retriever, Fit-Rag
16
employs bi-label scoring for relevance and LLM preferences, and Recomp
27
compresses documents to reduce noise. The second approach fine-tunes LLMs for better knowledge integration. Atlas
6
jointly pre-trains retriever and generator, while Raven
5
introduces Fusion-in-Context Learning. Ra-Dit
10
proposes dual instruction tuning, ChatQA
13
uses two-stage instruction tuning, and Rankrag
30
unifies ranking and generation in a single model. Genki
24
integrates knowledge directly into model parameters via fine-tuning. To address limitations of single-pass generation, iterative refinement approaches have emerged. SELF-REFINE
15
enables LLMs to generate, critique, and revise their outputs, while SummIt
32
combines summarization with self-evaluation to improve faithfulness without supervised training. SuRe
8
generates multiple answer summaries and uses the summarization process itself as a verification mechanism. Building on these advances, our work introduces a question-guided iterative framework that explicitly optimizes for information density by ensuring summaries can answer the most important questions about the source text, bridging QA-based evaluation with retrieval-augmented generation and iterative refinement.

3
Chain of Summaries

The Chain of Summaries (CoS) approach is inspired by Hegel’s dialectical method, whereby knowledge evolves through the stages of
thesis
,
antithesis
, and
synthesis
. As shown in Figure
1
, our system works in phases with several interconnected components that progressively enhance the summaries’ quality. Our proposed CoS approach works as follows (see also Figure
1
):

Initial summary generation (Thesis):
Given a source document, we first use an LLM to generate a zero-shot summary. This initial thesis provides a preliminary understanding that inevitably contains internal contradictions and limitations.

Question Generation (Antithesis):
Based on the original document, we generate synthetic question-answer pairs using the prompt in Appendix A. These questions act as the antithesis by challenging the summary’s claim to comprehensiveness, highlighting areas where key information is omitted.

Iterative Refinement Process (Movement towards Synthesis):
At the core of our approach lies a dialectical cycle that systematically resolves contradictions:

•

Evaluation Phase:
The Recognition of Contradiction component tests the current summary’s ability to answer questions from training and validation sets using the prompt in Appendix A. Questions that cannot be answered correctly reveal the thesis’s limitations.

•

Refinement Phase:
A ’synthesis engine’ component creates a new synthesis that incorporates missing information while maintaining conciseness. Using the source document, current summary, and unanswered questions, it creates an improved representation that transcends the previous version. The prompt for this phase is displayed in Appendix A.

Continued Dialectical Progression (Iteration):
This evaluate-refine cycle repeats for
i
iterations, with each cycle representing a dialectical movement towards the Absolute — a summary that maximizes information density for prospective downstream question-answering tasks. Once all iterations have been completed, the system selects the best synthesis based on its validation performance.

Hyperparameters for Chain of Summaries

The movement towards synthesis can be adjusted using several hyperparameters that govern the iterative approach. Number of iterations (
i
), this number determines how many iterations the summary undergoes. Higher values allow for a more comprehensive evolution, but there is a risk of diminishing returns or over-refinement. The number of questions for refinement (
iq
) controls how many unanswered questions from each evaluation phase are used to guide the subsequent synthesis. This parameter balances focused improvement (low values) against broader coverage (high values).

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Datasets

To directly assess our primary objective of creating summaries maximally useful for downstream QA tasks, we selected standard QA datasets. By testing question-answering using only the summary, we measure information density and utility. Our evaluation uses TriviaQA
7
, TruthfulQA
9
, and SQUAD v1.1
21
. To ensure a valid evaluation of our iterative approach, we select documents containing at least three test questions, providing sufficient representation to assess performance across iterations.

4.2
LLM Backbones

For experimental consistency and to isolate the effects of our iterative refinement process, we used the same LLM across all components (initial summary generation, QA pair generation, evaluation, and refinement) within each experimental run. This eliminated confounding variables from mixing models with different capabilities. We evaluated three language models:
GPT-4o-mini
,
Llama3.2:3B
, and
Qwen2.5:7B
for CoS.

4.3
Baselines

To provide context for the performance of our CoS approach, we compare it with a range of benchmarks designed to evaluate its effectiveness against direct LLM applications, alternative dense representations, and other summarization techniques. We use identical LLM backbones to generate a direct, one-pass summary. This is the simplest application of LLMs —
zero-shot summarization
— and shows how our iterative CoS process improves performance. The
Full Source Content
baseline represents a source document as context for the downstream QA task. This serves as a benchmark to determine whether our summarization process merely condenses information or actively makes it more accessible and useful for an LLM by mitigating issues such as the ’lost-in-the-middle’ effect, for example.
BRIO
and
PEGASUS
represent supervised abstractive summarization approaches, enabling comparison between our iterative method and specialised summarization techniques.

4.4
Evaluation Metrics

Given our goal of creating summaries optimised for downstream QA, our evaluation intentionally focuses on functional utility rather than traditional summarization metrics like fluency or coherence. Thus, our primary evaluation metric (Correct-F1) derives from the TriviaQA correctness measure using an F1 score for question-answering performance. The F1 score is computed as
\text{F1}=\frac{2\cdot\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}
, where
\text{precision}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|P|}
and
\text{recall}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|G|}
, with
P
representing prediction tokens and
G
representing ground truth tokens. The F1 calculation involves preprocessing prediction and ground truth strings by: converting to lowercase, removing punctuation and articles, replacing underscores with spaces, and standardizing whitespace. These normalized strings are then tokenized by spaces for F1 calculation, with common tokens determining precision and recall. For dataset-level evaluation, let
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{n}\}
be all summaries and
I=\{1,2,...,m\}
be all iterations. For each summary
s_{j}
and iteration
i
, we calculate

\text{MeanF1}(s_{j},i)=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{e\in E}\text{F1}(s_{j}^{i},e)

where
E
is all question-answer pairs and
s_{j}^{i}
is summary
j
at iteration
i
. We report the best-performing iteration for each summary
\text{Correct-F1}(s_{j})=\max_{i\in I}(\text{MeanF1}(s_{j},I))

This procedure enables each summary to achieve its best possible performance, as the optimal iteration may differ between summaries. For instance, summary
s_{1}
might achieve its best score at iteration
i=10
, while summary
s_{2}
peaks at iteration
i=9
.

Memorization

evaluates the summary’s performance (using Correct-F1) on the exact set of questions that were used to guide the iterative refinement process. The goal is to quantify how effectively each refinement step incorporates new information to cover identified knowledge gaps. A high score demonstrates that the iterative mechanism is successfully improving the summary based on the provided feedback, not just that the model is memorizing answers.

Generalization

measure of the final summary’s performance on the downstream QA task. We apply Correct-F1 to a completely separate, held-out set of validation questions that were never seen during the summary refinement loops. This evaluates the summary’s comprehensive knowledge capture, and a high score indicates that the summary has become a robust and broadly applicable knowledge source, useful for novel queries beyond those used for its creation. To ensure an unbiased evaluation, the set of questions used for the final generalization score was a held-out test set, kept separate from any questions used during the iterative refinement and validation selection loops.

Dataset-specific Evaluation Measures

For comparability with the literature, we also report standard evaluation measures for the datasets: F1 for TriviaQA and SQuADv1.1. For TruthfulQA, we report ROUGE-L to measure the textual similarity between the generated and ground-truth answers, acknowledging this is a proxy that does not fully capture truthfulness but serves as a consistent evaluation metric in our framework.

Summary Length

Our analysis of summary length versus generalization performance reveals distinct patterns across models within our CoS approach. As shown in Figure
2
and in more detail in Appendix B, CoS consistently outperforms traditional summarization methods while requiring substantially fewer tokens.

Using only 170 tokens, GPT-4o-mini enables CoS to achieve an F1 score of 0.80, which substantially outperforms zero-shot summarization (0.73), BRIO (0.63), and PEGASUS (0.72). The most striking comparison is with content-based approaches, which, despite achieving a competitive F1 score of 0.76, require over 11,000 tokens (full content), making them impractical for most applications. For Llama3.2:3B, CoS achieves an F1 score of 0.62, significantly exceeding both zero-shot approaches (0.52) and established methods with similar token efficiency. Qwen2.5 demonstrates the most impressive efficiency gains, with CoS achieving an F1 score of 0.60 using just 42–47 tokens, showing that our method can produce highly informative summaries in a very compact form.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis

We investigated three key hyperparameters in our CoS approach: number of iterations, questions per iteration, and integration of Chain of Draft (CoD) a prompting method where model is using reduced number of thinking output tokens. As shown in Appendix C, distributing questions over multiple iterations consistently outperforms presenting all questions simultaneously. For Llama3.2:3B, using 10 iterations with 1 question per iteration improved generalization from 0.52 to 0.64, while the single iteration approach with 50 questions reduced performance to 0.49. Similarly, for Qwen2.5:7B, the 10-iteration configuration improved generalization from 0.48 to 0.58, while the single-iteration approach reduced performance to 0.39. The inclusion of CoD showed model-dependent effects. For Llama3.2:3B, CoD achieved the highest generalization score of 0.68 with 10 iterations and 1 question per iteration - an improvement of 6%. However, for Qwen2.5:7B, no CoD performed better (0.60 versus 0.56). These results highlight the importance of balancing the number of iterations with the number of questions per iteration, and taking into account model-specific characteristics.

6
Fine-tuning Analyses

To further understand whether the iterative multi-step process can be compressed into a single step with an efficient language model, we fine-tune a reward model to estimate downstream QA performance, and then use GRPO to fine-tune a small Qwen model for summarization.

Fine-tuning ModernBERT for Downstream QA Performance Estimation

For the reward model, we fine-tuned a
AnswerDotAI/ModernBERT-base
model
26
with a context size of 8,192 using CoS intermediate and final summaries paired with their respective attained F1 scores. A detailed description of the training dataset is available in Appendix D. We employed binary cross-entropy loss with sigmoid activation to match the predicted distribution directly with the targets (range: 0–1).
Training the model with a batch size of 16, gradient accumulation steps of eight, and a learning rate of 0.008 over 15 epochs produced a model with training and validation losses of 0.434 and 0.508, respectively, and a test RMSE of 0.147, which demonstrates its strong predictive capability. This enables the Chain of Summaries process to be completed more quickly and allows existing summaries to be evaluated and selected for refinement if necessary.

Fine-tuning Qwen for summarization

We explored reducing our multi-step CoS methodology into a single-step operation by reinforcement fine-tuning Qwen3:0.6B and Qwen2:0.5B-Instruct using GRPO
3
;
23
with our ModernBERT estimator as reward model. The reward function was formulated as
-|1-\hat{s}|
, where
\hat{s}
represents the predicted downstream QA performance score, creating a gradient toward information-dense representations. We used 4 samples per batch with 4 candidate generations trained for three epochs with BF16 precision.
The prompt template is provided in Appendix A. The dataset of (interediate) summaries and their attained scores is provided in Appendix D.

Despite their small size, our fine-tuned smaller Qwen models produced strong results as shown in Table
1
. Fine-tuned Qwen2:0.5B-Instruct achieved a TriviaQA generalization score of 0.74 (GPT-4o-mini backbone), approaching the 0.80 of the larger CoS approach. In the Llama3.2:3B evaluation, the fine-tuned Qwen3:0.6B model matched the full-source performance on TriviaQA (0.50 vs. 0.51) and SQUAD1.1 (0.19 vs. 0.19). While these results demonstrate that small models can produce high-quality summaries that are comparable to those of larger approaches, the significant discrepancy on TruthfulQA (5.33 vs. 14.77) suggests that the dialectical refinement of CoS captures nuances that are difficult to distill into a single, compact model.

Downstream QA task comparison

Our Chain of Summaries approach, using a GPT-4o-mini backbone, achieves a competitive 80.0% F1-score on TriviaQA. This result in Table
3
outperforms context compression methods like RECOMP (59% F1) and surpasses several sophisticated RAG frameworks, including ATLAS (76.4%) and REPLUG (77.3%). CoS is a model-agnostic method that requires no fine-tuning. Since the context summary is generated before the final QA step, CoS adds no compute overhead at the inference stage. While fine-tuned models hold state-of-the-art (e.g., RankRAG at 92.3%), CoS offers a flexible and efficient black-box alternative that can enhance any general-purpose LLM by solely improving its input context, making it a highly accessible and adaptable solution that could be moderated by humans to ensure quality and safety.

7
Discussion

Our main finding is that CoS creates a textual summary that is more useful to a downstream LLM than the source text by condensing information and removing irrelevant context – suggesting that CoS makes information better accessible for downstream language model use.
Moreover, CoS consistently outperforms zero-shot summarization, as well as dedicated summarization methods such as BRIO and PEGASUS.

Our findings are in agreement with recent work showing that increased test-time compute (here, multiple LLM calls) can improve the quality of generated text
18
– which would amortize over multiple requests in our envisioned scenario, where website maintainers would use CoS to create information-dense summaries for downstream LLM agents to process. If implemented server-side, which is our primary recommendation, website maintainers can inspect the current summary of their website and/or specific subpages, and ingest manual corrections if needed. If they don’t have human-crafted Q&A pairs, website maintainers can use synthetic Q&A pairs, which we have shown to perform on par with real Q&A pairs. In contrast, client-side implementation (e.g., on the site of an LLM agent operating on the web) would induce redundancy as different systems would have to maintain their caches – and website maintainers would have little control over how their websites are summarized and whether the cache may have been invalidated. Our attempt at fine-tuning an LLM towards good one-step summarization was less effective than the multi-step summarization. We cannot exclude that this would be different with a larger model or through a more sophisticated reward model, which would change this result. However, as of now, we can conclude from this that the iterative procedure is
necessary
to obtain a high-quality summary.
In sum, we have shown how Hegel’s dialectical method can be leveraged with large language models to obtain a general-purpose summary that anticipates future questions, ready to be used as an LLM-friendly cache for content on the web.

8
Conclusion

We have introduced Chain of Summaries (CoS) as an iterative refinement method for summarization. We have shown that CoS consistently outperforms zero-shot summarization as well as dedicated summarization approaches.
As CoS works with synthetic questions, it is particularly suitable for applications with limited labeled data. Crucially, we showed that these information-dense summaries can even surpass the performance of the original documents, suggesting that CoS makes information better accessible for LLMs.
Our CoS method creates efficient textual summaries that serve as knowledge caches for LLMs, reducing redundant computation in retrieval-augmented systems.

9
Limitations

Our selection of datasets mainly consists of question-answering datasets. While we deem this a reasonable proxy for practical applications, it does not involve complex multi-stage synthesis of content on the web from various sources, which would need to be carried out by the client LLM. However, summarizing multiple possibly interlinked documents on the web for LLM use would be an exciting avenue for future work, testing how such links within the summaries could facilitate navigation of LLMs on the web. Future work may further look at more sophisticated question stratification and fine-tuning larger LLMs.

10
Ethical Considerations

We don’t expect that the proposed method would cause any extra harm compared to LLMs operating on the web on their own. In contrast, if implemented server-side as an LLMs.txt file, our approach allows website maintainers to validate what textual representation LLMs process.

Appendix D
Data Appendix

This appendix provides details of the dataset created to train the reward model for our Chain of Summaries (CoS) framework. The dataset comprises machine-generated summaries, each paired with an F1 score that quantifies its usefulness in subsequent question-answering tasks. This data was synthetically generated as an output of our iterative CoS procedure, in which candidate summaries were produced by applying our model to a diverse corpus of documents. These documents originate from TriviaQA, TruthfulQA, and SQUADv1.1. Each summary was then automatically evaluated against a set of validation questions from its source document that was provided in the dataset, and an F1 score was computed based on the proportion of questions that could be answered correctly using only the information within the summary. This process yielded a collection of
(summary, F1_score)
pairs. The dataset is structured for a regression task where the model learns to predict the F1 score, a continuous value in the range
[0,1]
, from the summary text. The resulting reward model can thereby estimate a summary’s capabilities for the QA task.

Preprocessing

To ensure the quality and suitability of the dataset, we applied a number of pre-processing steps. We filtered out any malformed entries or entries with missing F1 scores, and we performed deduplication based on the summary text. This meant that we retained only a single instance of any identical summaries. To guarantee that every entry could be processed by the reward model (AnswerDotAI/ModernBERT-base) without truncation, we removed all summaries that exceeded its context window of 8,192 tokens.
