“The missing link”: utility of brain health dashboards in supporting healthy ageing in primary care
Authors: Patrick James Adam, Joyce Siette
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs populations age, policymakers and researchers are investigating innovative systems to support healthy brain ageing and prevent dementia to reduce the personal, community and fiscal burden of age-related disease. Primary care is a routinely accessed part of global healthcare systems and could act as an avenue to introduce platforms to support healthy ageing. However, research suggests that many primary care professionals feel underprepared, lack confidence, knowledge, and resources for age-related practice. This exploratory study aims to explore the current state of healthy brain ageing management in primary care and investigate how digital technologies, particularly brain health dashboards, could be implemented and used to increase clinician and patient outcomes.MethodsHealthcare professionals (N = 9) with a range of primary healthcare expertise (general practice, specialist, nursing, health promotion and health administration) were interviewed on current practices used to support healthy ageing in older adults and use of digital technologies for brain health management. Semi-structured interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analysed thematically.ResultsThree core themes were identified: current management practices, facilitators and barriers to adoption and general acceptability. Core facilitators such as IT prevalence and clinician openness and barriers, including resource allocation, technological issues, breakdown of clinician-patient rapport and skills acquisition necessary for usage were identified by the healthcare professionals.ConclusionsThis study provides initial findings on the barriers and facilitators to adopting technologies for brain health management in primary care, which could be used to create systems to support clinicians’ management of brain ageing and improve patient outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03288-x.Keywords: Dementia, Digital dashboards, Digital technologies, Healthcare professionals

Introduction

Ensuring access to healthy ageing supports and services and fostering public systems to assist ageing individuals are global policy objectives [1, 2]. As life expectancies increase, the world’s population over 60 is expected to double to 2.1 billion by 2050 [3]. In 2024, an estimated 421,000 individuals were living with dementia in Australia [4], with the disease now being the leading cause of death [5]. Understanding the unique healthcare burdens of an ageing population, such as dementia and cognitive impairment, is essential for equipping practitioners and healthcare systems to mitigate the disease burden within our communities [6, 7]. A central component of this approach is to change perceptions of ageing to support older adults to lead healthier and longer lives [1, 7]. A complementary strategy is to invest in innovative systems to support healthy ageing that can assist in dementia prevention and disease modification by assessing and continuously monitoring the biopsychosocial needs of individuals who are ageing or at risk of dementia [8]. At the same time, healthcare organisations are increasingly adopting new technologies, such as dashboards, to support clinical decision making and the quality of care provided [9, 10]. Thoughtful development of these technological innovations in primary care settings may enable better detection, prevention, and management of age-related issues [11] and limit the fiscal and community burden of ageing and dementia [2, 12].

Research on mitigating risk factors associated with dementia is growing [12, 13], with evidence highlighting modifiable risk factors and prevention strategies. Livingston et al. [14] estimated that elimination of potentially modifiable factors would result in a 45% reduction in dementia prevalence. Of these factors, hearing loss, less education in early life, smoking, depression, and social isolation contributed the most to the modifiable risk factors [12, 14]. As such, risk reduction through modifiable factors is of paramount importance and may be achieved through lifestyle modification programs (LMPs) and social prescribing practices [15–18]. Common LMPs target risk factors such as physical inactivity, smoking, nutrition, and cognitive stimulating activities [15, 16, 19–21], and social prescribing methods to improve social connectedness and wellbeing, decrease loneliness, and link older individuals to other external supports [17, 18]. However, the role of prevention strategies is often under-recognised in reducing the personal and societal burden of dementia [22, 23]. This may be due in part to mixed evidence on the efficacy of LMPs and social prescribing and the overlapping nature of multiple risk factors in dementia aetiology [12]. However, it is widely accepted that increasing protective factors while mitigating risk factors will contribute to the reduction in prevalence of various dementias [12, 20].

Understanding where dementia risk can be most effectively and feasibly targeted is important in negating disease burden. Primary care is an accessible and cost-effective care route for older adults [24]. Whilst many healthcare professionals (HCPs) in allied health, nursing, pharmacy, and medicine more broadly play an important role in supporting community healthcare, general practitioners (GPs) are the most visited primary health provider in Australia [24, 25]. General practitioners are opportunely positioned for the coordination of care and continued support of individuals with or at risk of dementia. However, dementia diagnosis rates in primary care are low, with many remaining undiagnosed [26], which may contribute to poorer long-term outcomes and fragmented care for patients [27], particularly for culturally diverse individuals [28]. Key factors influencing HCPs’ knowledge and management of dementia include poor perceptions of treatment efficacy [29–32], dementia being outside of primary care’s scope [33], practitioner knowledge gaps [34–36], and time constraints [37]. Healthcare professionals require efficient tools to screen and manage dementia in primary care to allow for confidence in diagnosis and treatment. Healthcare technologies may help alleviate underdetection in primary care by aiding HCP’s assessment, clinical decision making and interpretation of results [38].

Attention must be given to how technology can be implemented to support HCPs in primary care to support patients with or at risk of dementia [39]. Technology for ageing has been successfully used to help older adults age in place by improving monitoring, support in daily functioning, and reducing social isolation [40, 41]. Despite this, technology for HCPs to support age-related primary care practice is still in its infancy (see Ludlow et al. [42] for a study protocol).

Recent work indicates a potential role of healthcare dashboards for improving clinicians’ adherence to evidence-based practice and improving patient outcomes in numerous healthcare settings [9, 10, 43]. Dashboards support clinical decision-making by providing relevant patient information concisely, and may also provide graphics, assessment and interpretation tools, and epidemiological or normative comparisons [9, 10, 38, 44, 45]. Healthcare dashboards have been used to manage risks associated with hypertension [46], diabetes [47, 48], and in aged care settings for allocating patients to groups based on their dementia progression [49]. A recent participatory design study by Esquer Rochin et al. [49] found that HCPs rated dashboard technology as highly feasible, useful, and easy to use. Additionally, a review by Siette et al. [50] examined a collection of studies on dashboard acceptability in aged care. Of the included studies, almost all reported medium to high acceptability levels but mixed usability levels [50].

Accordingly, dashboards may be a viable option to assist HCPs in their practice, but practitioners must be included within the design of such dashboards to ensure usability. Successful adoption, implementation, and enhanced patient outcomes stemming from emerging technologies hinge on active participation from healthcare professionals, particularly general practitioners, in co-creating the technology [42]. The present study aims to explore current brain health-related practices and perspectives within primary care settings to better understand the perceived role of primary care in supporting healthy ageing. As dashboard technology is only beginning to emerge in the management of healthy ageing, we sought to conduct an early exploration of perceptions towards dashboards for health ageing management in primary care.

Methods

Design

This exploratory, qualitative study employed a convenience sampling approach to recruit participants from a targeted pool of healthcare providers involved in dementia prevention or care. The study aimed to generate early insights into professional perspectives on culturally tailored dementia risk reduction, rather than to achieve thematic saturation. A total of nine participants were interviewed, which falls below the commonly recommended range for theme saturation in qualitative research. However, the sample provided sufficient variation in experience and role to identify recurring themes relevant to the study’s aims. A Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ; [51]) checklist for this study can be found in the electronic supplementary materials.

Participants

Participants were recruited through social media advertising, word of mouth, and flyer drops in New South Wales, Australia. Recruitment proved challenging due to several factors. Most prominently, healthcare professionals were under considerable time pressure during the study period, and outreach efforts, despite using multiple professional networks, generated limited responses. In particular, clinicians working in community and aged care services often reported limited capacity to participate in research activities due to staffing shortages and competing priorities. Nine HCPs participated in this study. Participants were required to be professionals working in primary care, or a role related to primary care. Additionally, participants needed to be over the age of 18 years, able to speak and understand English, and not be diagnosed with dementia. Participants were excluded if they did not work in primary care or did not fulfil all the study requirements. Each participant received $50 for their participation, which was approximately 40 min in duration. This study received ethics approval from the Western Sydney University Human Research Ethics Committee (H14895).

Materials

The materials for this study included a demographics survey, a semi-structured interview schedule, and an exemplar dashboard image.

Demographics survey

The demographics survey was presented after the participant information sheet and consent form on Qualtrics (www.qualtrics.com). The survey consisted of fifteen questions relating to either personal characteristics or professional history in healthcare. Participants were asked for their full name, gender, date and country of birth, home postcode, their highest level of education attained, cultural background and contact details. The professional history questions related to the participant’s healthcare role (e.g., GP), duration worked in current position, hours worked weekly, postcode of practice and completion of CME (continuing medical education) or other education in dementia or other age-related issues.

Semi-structured interview schedule

The semi-structured interview schedule consisted of questions related to ageing management in primary care, digital interventions for dementia management, and brain health dashboards for ageing. The questions were derived theoretically from the extant literature on ageing and digital healthcare interventions and from the proposed research questions a priori. Participants were also shown a static image of a hypothetical brain health dashboard to assist in questions regarding potential functionalities and features for future brain health dashboard technology. Some questions changed depending on the participant’s primary healthcare role. For instance, GPs were asked, “What information do you require for a diagnosis of dementia?”. In contrast, nurses and other professionals were asked, “Are you aware of any health information that is required to support a dementia diagnosis?”. The interview used closed- and open-ended questions to identify discussion areas and probe for further information, respectively. The questions were pilot tested on a member of the research team to ensure clarity before interviewing participants.

Example dashboard image

An example brain health dashboard was shared with the HCPs during the Zoom interview. The participants were shown a static image of the Cleveland Clinic’s [52] Brain Health Check-up dashboard (see Fig. 1). The purpose of the example dashboard image was to provide a visual reference point for the HCPs who were less familiar with dashboard technology. Moreover, the example dashboard was used to guide discussions on usability, particularly on what features would be required in brain health or dementia dashboards.

Procedure

Participants were asked to express interest in participating by providing their contact information on a Qualtrics form. Within the contact form, the participants were also asked questions on their eligibility to participate. If the participants met the eligibility criteria, they were then able to view and complete the consent form and demographics survey for the study. PJA and JS then contacted the eligible participants by email or text to provide them with a Calendly (calendly.com) link to book an interview.

All interviews were conducted by PJA online through Zoom (version 5.11.1). First, PJA briefly explained the study’s aims and the interview format. The participants were invited to ask questions prior to answering questions from the interview schedule. PJA then proceeded to ask the participants the questions outlined in the interview schedule. Additional probing questions were used at the discretion of the interviewer to further explore participants’ responses. During the interview, the participants were able to view the example dashboard image via the Zoom screensharing functionality. The participants were able to use the example dashboard image to help provide feedback on existing dashboard technologies and to provide a reference point for those unfamiliar with dashboard technology. The average interview time was 40 min (range: 20–50 min).

Analysis

The collected data was transcribed using Otter.ai (otter.ai) and manually checked by PJA for accuracy using the original audio recordings. After guaranteeing the accuracy of the interview transcripts, all responses were then anonymised and de-identified by PJA. Transcripts were then entered into Lumivero NVivo 13 for deductive thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke’s [53] 6-step approach, which involved data familiarisation, initial code generation, theme searching within the preliminary codes, revision of selected themes, theme definition and naming, and theme report production [54]. Given the limited sample size and the difficulty in reaching saturation, we employed a theory-guided analysis to support the interpretation of data and increase analytical rigour. Specifically, we drew on Levesque’s Access to Care Framework [55], which conceptualises access as the dynamic interaction between health system factors (e.g., approachability, availability, acceptability) and individual capacities to engage (e.g., ability to perceive, seek, reach and engage with care). This framework was used deductively to structure the coding process, with themes mapped to its core domains to explore how ethnocultural context influences access to brain health interventions. The dataset was independently coded by PJA and JS. Codes were compared and discussed in iterative sessions to resolve discrepancies and ensure consistency. Although the sample included a mix of general practitioners, nurses and allied health professionals, the aim was not to generalise across provider types, but to capture a range of insights about the accessibility and acceptability of dementia prevention in primary care. We acknowledge that the small and diverse sample limits the generalisability of findings and that data saturation was not reached. This study is best understood as a preliminary exploration intended to inform the design of more robust, theory-driven implementation models in future phases.

In addition to identifying themes around barriers and facilitators to brain health technologies in primary care, this study also aimed to explore the early-stage development of a process model for future implementation. This was done through an exploratory, abductive approach, in which emergent themes were mapped onto existing theoretical frameworks to support preliminary model construction. Specifically, data were analysed with reference to the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF; [56]) and the technology development stages outlined by Michie et al. [56], allowing us to iteratively build a model that reflected participant-identified priorities, constraints, and mechanisms for integrating brain health tools (such as dashboards) into practice. Themes relating to clinician motivation, patient engagement, and systemic capacity were examined in tandem to conceptualise a realistic, stepwise path from co-design to implementation.

Results

The sample included nine participants with a mean age of 43.49 years (SD = 12.55), comprising four males and five females (Table 1). Most participants were born in Australia (n = 6), with two from the Philippines and one from India. The group was highly educated, with all participants having at least three years of tertiary education. One participant held a bachelor’s degree, two with graduate diplomas or a bachelor’s degree with honours, four with master’s degrees, and two with doctoral degrees. Two HCPs had also completed specialist training in ageing-related issues or dementia. The cultural backgrounds were diverse, including English (n = 1), Indian (n = 1), Filipino (n = 1), and Chinese (n = 3), among others. Participants held various healthcare roles, such as general practitioners (n = 4), nurses (n = 2), and specialists (n = 1), with an average of 10.64 years (SD = 10.75) in their current positions, working 34 h per week (SD = 13.65).

Participant characteristics

Three core themes, containing a total of eight subthemes, were identified (see Fig. 2). The three core themes were: Current Practice and Management of Healthy Ageing, Facilitators and Barriers to Adoption, and General Acceptability of Digital Platforms.

Summary of identified primary and subthemes

Current practice and management of healthy ageing

This theme covers the current diagnostic and preventative practices utilised by the HCPs for healthy ageing in primary care. Two subthemes are included: diagnostic and preventative practices and awareness of digital platforms and resources.

Diagnostic and preventative practices

Many HCPs described memory as being one of the more significant diagnostic indicators for dementia, “Memory, obviously, the biggest red flag. So the loss of short- and or long-term memory, really, usually short term is what would indicate it (dementia)”. Numerous diagnostic tools were commonly utilised to support diagnosis, including the Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS), General Practitioner Assessment of Cognition (CPCOG) and Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA). Healthcare practitioners also relied on other tools, including brain scores, pathology reports and specialists for further guidance.

Regarding prevention strategies, HCPs discussed both lifestyle approaches, such as healthy eating and exercise, as well as the management of chronic health conditions. Lifestyle modification programs were also largely positively viewed by the HCPs, “I am a big believer that the three most important things are eating well, exercising and getting a…good quality and long enough sleep”; however, introducing LMPs during a new diagnosis of dementia was cautioned by one HCP to not overwhelm the patient, “If it’s a kind of out of the blue brand-new diagnosis of dementia? No, I wouldn’t at that point, you know, maybe further down the track”.

The HCPs expressed the need to improve current areas or practices within primary care, including discussions on providing early age intervention and additional education for HCPs, “That talk about brain health [needs to happen] along the way…when they are [in their] 40s or 50s.” and “education is obviously key in anything and there’s no exception here.”

Awareness of resources and digital platforms

HCPs highlighted a pressing need for streamlined, accessible, and easily navigable resources that can benefit both healthcare providers and their patients in the healthcare setting. The HCPs tended to refer to healthy ageing information resources developed by national bodies. However, practitioners had difficulties procuring resources for themselves and their patients to support their age-related practice. Current guidelines for age-related practice were described as sorely lacking, which was contrasted with other chronic health conditions, “I just wish that we had some sort of guideline or something that we can access with links, things that we can prescribe to patients and recommend them”, “The difficulty as a GP, there isn’t a central repository for us to get information for ourselves, let alone resources for our patient” and “The resources out there are either ‘I don’t know about it’ or ‘hard to access’ or sometimes you do get hit with a, you open up a blank browser and you [say] ‘Okay, where do I start?‘”.

Three of the nine HCPs who participated were aware of at least one digital platform supporting healthy brain ageing in primary care, but none of the HCPs were aware of dashboards for brain health or ageing, “We have dashboards through the paediatric setting. Yes, we do have that, but in aged care, I have not seen one ever. And in terms of brain health, I don’t think I have seen one”. Another HCP commented on the disappointment of lacking awareness of platforms to support brain ageing, “It’s really sad. It’s kind of, you know, that makes me feel guilty for the care that we provide to these patients…but there’s currently nothing [technological] in the workplace that we’re doing”.

Facilitators and barriers to adoption

Facilitators and barriers to adoption refer to the factors that either support or hinder the introduction of digital technologies, such as brain health dashboards, into primary care settings. This primary theme contains three subthemes: facilitators to adoption, barriers to adoption and identified needs.

Facilitators to adoption

The prevalence of technology and the openness of clinicians were both seen as key facilitators to the introduction of digital platforms. One HCP commented on digital solutions becoming the current standard in many areas of healthcare in primary care settings and at home:

I like the idea of patients being able to do things at home on their own computer in…a familiar environment, and that could include prevention exercises. But look, there’s certainly a place where and I think there’s a place for IT in every space now. We live in a world that’s based on technology these days. So it makes sense to be able to apply something like that (digital technologies for ageing) in this context.

Practitioner style, in particular HCPs who already rely heavily upon technology or those who are open to trying new technologies, was also seen as potentially facilitating:

Some [HCPs] might not like change or might not adapt. Whereas other people willing to try new things, early adopters, that sort of thing…we’ve had doctors that have a real thirst for technology and integrating that (technology) as well.

Barriers to adoption

Four clusters of barriers were suggested by the HCPs: resources, technological concerns, clinician-patient barriers, and education and skills barriers. How much the technology would cost and how well the technology could be disseminated amongst socio-economically disadvantaged groups was a concern for numerous HCPs:

The biggest problem and like many new interventions that are developed [are] access barriers… how much is it going to cost and who can afford it? There’s physical barriers as well. So in terms of implementation, it needs to be implemented utilising an inequality model. So everyone needs to be able to have access to it.

Technological difficulties included the ability to connect to digital platforms and the provision of privacy. Some HCPs commented on the nature of remote healthcare work, which included the temperamental or potentially non-existent connection to the Internet, which would limit the ability to use digital platforms, “Our main barriers [to using other technologies] was our was our connectivity…but also the local expertise required to maintain these digital platforms as well.” Regarding privacy, the HCPs were concerned about who could potentially access the data on the digital technologies and who would be able to input data into sensitive health records, “The accuracy of that [recorded data], and the people filling in, who are the people?…who can potentially key-in answers to this [technology]?”

Clinician and patient barriers included factors such as the breakdown of rapport using technology, rigid clinician styles and cultural appropriateness. One HCP was concerned that moving to digital technologies with some patients could reduce rapport by moving some services online:

[There would be] no connection, and there’s no relationship whatsoever. So that might be a little bit harder for them…a lot of our management is based on the…rapport that we build up [and] that is completely shut down when it’s electronic.

While openness was seen as a facilitator, HCP styles that were closed to the idea of using technology to support practice were seen as barriers, “Doctor personality, or style…they may not want to use all of that data [the technology collects and displays]”.

The cultural appropriateness of technologies was seen as a potential barrier. Numerous HCPs commented on the difficulty of translating healthcare technologies and the appropriateness of their measures, “there would be language difficulties. I mean, if I couldn’t read or speak English, there would obviously be that barrier”. Additionally, consistent technological support during the implementation of emergent digital platforms for healthy ageing was seen as necessary, “If it is too difficult to do…we will just give up, because [we] don’t have time to deal with it…once it’s rolled out, the support has to be there.”

Identified needs

Identified needs refer to areas of improvement within the domain of healthy brain ageing and management, and for technologies that could support brain ageing in primary care. Early age intervention was a key need. Many HCPs noted that healthy brain ageing should begin earlier to best track potential disease progression or aetiology:

Now we are [looking at] younger and younger [patients], 25 to 30 years, [or] 15 years or 20 years… Why? Because you’re thinking way ahead in prevention, [early screening] resonates very well within primary care in terms of dementia prevention.

General acceptability of digital platforms

This theme relates to the perceived appropriateness of digital platforms for healthy brain ageing management in primary care. Three subthemes are contained within: acceptability of digital platforms, features and functionality and perceived benefits of digital platforms.

Acceptability of digital platforms

All nine HCPs positively appraised digital technology for managing brain health and were accepting of its use in primary care. Digital technologies were generally seen to streamline consultation and diagnostic processes, “I think it would be very helpful [to have digital technologies]. I just find that this is an area that’s quite lacking in my practice, and I think it would be very helpful to have extra support”.

Although seven HCPs were not aware of brain health dashboards and only two had conceptual knowledge of brain health dashboards, all nine HCPs saw the technology type as highly acceptable for managing brain health in primary care. Brain health dashboards were seen as highly acceptable due to their ease of use, clarity, level of currency, and time-effectiveness, “It’s the missing link. This would be good”, “I can see this happening, this is what we need. Comprehensive, and it’s easy to navigate, and it’s just right at the tip of your fingers” and “It’ll give you an opportunity to feed in the information and then see how that information actually affects the outcomes. I think would be a great tool.”

Features and functionality

This subtheme contains the features and functionalities recommended by the HCPs to make digital technologies efficacious for managing brain health. A vast array of features and functionalities were proposed, including visuals, such as graphs and charts, planners and management tools, integrating with existing systems, note sharing, cross-platform and remote usage, and accessibility features for visually impaired individuals, “Good interface, possibly being able to contact someone like a contact number…[and details of] people that we can reach if we have any questions, referral pathways, information, printout resources”.

Features that guide clinicians or provide prompts to support their practice were also highly recommended. One HCP discusses the need for prompts to serve as an external audit when consulting an ageing patient:

We need prompts, decision making prompts…you need to have a programme that serves to this (increasing compliance). Have you done this? Have you thought about that? [Do] we have power of attorney yet? … If you have that available, it takes a problem of my hands, and I will run up to you quickly [and say] “Give me the program.”

Perceived benefits of digital platforms

Numerous perceived benefits were noted by the HCPs, including time efficiency, lifespan tracking, increased awareness of resources, increased compliance, facilitating advanced planning and inter-professional communication. HCPs spoke of dashboards providing them with a holistic overview and enabling efficient monitoring of patients over time.

Everything is there in the one, like, that’s why dashboards are good, because you can just have all the different aspects.

It’s just a nice, quick, easy summary, where you can sort of monitor somebody…if you can do that over time, then you can see how quickly they’re deteriorating with their dementia or their memory loss. And it’s good for the patient to show them that.

Model emergence and theoretical mapping

Informed by the themes identified, particularly those related to acceptability, feasibility, and clinician workflow integration, a draft implementation model was developed to guide the staged introduction of brain health technologies in primary care settings. This model (Fig. 3) reflects both the facilitators described by participants (e.g., integration with existing practice management systems, patient education support) and the barriers (e.g., time constraints, digital literacy, unclear role delineation). Components of the model align with the Theoretical Domains Framework [56–58], capturing domains such as knowledge, beliefs about capabilities, and environmental context, and are organised according to stages of technology innovation defined by Michie et al. [56, 57], from co-design to testing and iterative refinement. The model is not intended as a finalised framework, but as a theory-informed representation of the implementation process based on this exploratory phase.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore current brain health-related practices and perspectives within primary care settings to better understand the perceived role of primary care in supporting healthy ageing. In particular, this study aimed to investigate the potential role of dashboards in healthy ageing management in primary care. The results of this study included extracts on current age-related practice and dementia prevention, key barriers, and facilitators to introducing digital technologies for brain health management, and identified features and functionalities required by HCPs to assist in their practice.

Firstly, in relation to current practice, numerous assessments, including the MMSE and RUDAS, were commonly used in clinical practice to complement patient, caregiver, and clinician input. However, the HCPs often encountered challenges due to the scarcity of resources or a lack of awareness regarding where to access them. This highlights a need for educational support for HCPs in the context of age-related care [36]. Moreover, while utilising LMPs was generally seen as acceptable by the HCPs, some practitioners found it challenging to link these programs to specific risk factors and implement changes. This finding demonstrates the difficulties in diagnosis due to the numerous, often co-occurring biopsychosocial risk factors [12, 34], as well as HCPs low perceptions of treatment efficacy [29–32].

Our study also explored the perceived feasibility of brain health dashboards for HCPs in primary care. Brain health dashboards were seen as a time-efficient and intuitive way to manage patients’ healthy brain ageing, which has been previously demonstrated [49, 50]. However, the HCPs noted numerous barriers that must be overcome. These barriers were related to resources, difficulties integrating technology, concerns for the patient-clinician relationship, and educational challenges surrounding training with technology. These barriers must be considered alongside current barriers HCPs face in primary practice, such as time constraints [33, 34, 36]. Despite these barriers, the current prevalence of technology in some primary care settings and open or adaptive clinician styles were seen as potential facilitators. Co-design practices may further foster openness to technological advances in primary care, which must remain at the forefront of implementation studies [42].

Regarding usability, numerous essential features and functionalities for brain health technologies to assist HCPs in brain health management were identified. Features and functionalities such as accessible text and intuitive visualisations, tools for management and planning, and cross-platform usage were most discussed. However, other features, such as remote-access capabilities, wearable inputs, lifespan or risk trajectories, and paper-based backups, were also proposed by the HCPs. Of particular importance to HCPs were features that support decision-making when referring to interventions. These findings contribute to the growing body of research on features and functionalities required in dashboard technologies to best support HCPs in their practice [38, 49, 50, 59]. To be perceived as usable, brain health dashboards will require similar, if not better, features already available in current clinical decision-making systems [38] that HCPs have at their disposal.

Implications

Based on the findings generated by this study, we propose a preliminary process model for the implementation of brain health technologies in Australian primary care (Fig. 3). This model reflects a synthesis of the qualitative themes identified and is grounded in extant theories of behaviour change, such as the Theoretical Domains Framework [56–58] and has been constructed in line with the stages of technology creation and innovation listed by Michie et al. [57] and acceptance of technology in older adults [60]. Rather than a fixed blueprint, this model represents an early-stage conceptualisation to guide further development and testing.

Phase one of the model corresponds with the current study, which involved identifying barriers and facilitators through initial co-design discussions and prototyping. After utilising the facilitators present and mitigating the potential barriers, prototypes should be provided to HCPs to elicit responses on actual usability and efficacy. The data collected in future stages should then be reworked into more advanced digital platforms of brain health technologies, such as dashboards, through a recursive process of consultation and feedback implementation. It is hoped that more advanced implementations can elicit the benefits discussed by the HCPs herein and realise compliance [43], ease of accessibility and increased patient care benefits [9] for ageing patients in Australian primary care.

Strengths and limitations

Before concluding, the strengths and limitations of this study should be noted. Foremost, the sample size of this study (N = 9) was small and consisted of HCPs almost exclusively practising in Greater Western Sydney, which may limit the generalisability of the results. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling likely resulted in the sampling of HCPs with an interest in the topic area of healthy ageing management and technology, leading to a selection bias. Acquiring participants for this study was particularly challenging due to time constraints within the HCPs’ schedules. Accordingly, it is likely that thematic saturation was not reached, which necessitates further studies on this topic. Moreover, this study solely focused on the perspectives of HCPs. Future studies in this area will require the input of other key stakeholders, such as patients, carers, and policymakers. Finally, as this study was only on perspectives of perceived feasibility and usefulness, implementation studies are required to assess the real-world feasibility, usefulness, and scalability in clinical settings. Care should be taken in future studies to thoughtfully integrate technologies into primary care to assist HCPs in their practice, but not overwhelm or complicate their delivery of care. Despite these limitations, this study does provide a rich set of qualitative data on the thoughts of HCPs on technologies, particularly dashboards, for the management of healthy brain ageing in primary care. These early insights satisfy the exploratory aims of the study while providing directions and an impetus for future studies. The results of this study are of particular interest due to the diversity of HCPs included, which includes GPs, nurses, and a health promotion officer. Future research should continue to sample a range of healthcare professions as healthy ageing and dementia prevention are of multidisciplinary interest.

Conclusion

This study examined the current state of brain health management in primary care settings as well as the perceived feasibility and usefulness of technology, particularly dashboards, to support age-related practice. Primary care HCPs, while in a position to best identify and manage age-related issues and decline, may be under-supported by the lack of current guidelines, resources, and management technologies. Technology, especially brain health dashboards, was seen as a highly acceptable form of support if barriers to implementation were mitigated and the required functions and features needed by HCPs were thoughtfully integrated. Although this study was limited in some respects by its sample size, the exploratory nature of the study provides an impetus and direction for future research. It is hoped that this study complements the existing literature on age-related practice in primary care while providing emergent evidence on the acceptability and benefits of health management technology, such as brain health dashboards, to assist professionals in meeting the needs of ageing individuals within an ageing population.
