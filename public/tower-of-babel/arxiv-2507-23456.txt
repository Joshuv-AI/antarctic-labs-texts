Magnetically Programmable Surface Acoustic Wave Filters: Device Concept and Predictive Modeling
Michael K. Steinbauer 1,2,3,∗, Peter Flauger 1,2, Matthias Küß 4, Stephan Glamsch 4, Emeline D. S. Nysten 5, Matthias Weiß 5, Dieter Suess 1,2, Hubert J. Krenner 5, Manfred Albrecht 4, Claas Abert 1,2

Abstract
Filtering surface acoustic wave (SAW) signals of specified frequencies depending on the strength of an external magnetic field in a magnetostrictive material has garnered significant interest due to its potential scientific and industrial applications. Here, we propose a device that achieves selective SAW attenuation by instead programming its internal magnetic state. To this end, we perform micromagnetic simulations for the magnetoelastic interaction of the Rayleigh SAW mode with spin waves (SWs) in exchange-decoupled Co/Ni islets on a piezoelectric LiTaO 3 substrate. Due to the islets exhibiting perpendicular magnetic anisotropy, the stray-field interaction between them leads to a shift in the SW dispersion depending on the magnetic alignment of neighboring islets. This significantly changes the efficiency of the magnetoelastic interaction at specified frequencies. We predict changes in SAW transmission of 28.9 dB / mm 28.9\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m} at 3.8 GHz 3.8\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z} depending on the state of the device. For the efficient simulation of the device, we extend a prior energy conservation argument based on analytical solutions of the SW to finite-difference numerical calculations, enabling the modeling of arbitrary magnetization patterns like the proposed islet-based design.

Introduction

:

Surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices are integral to the modern telecommunications infrastructure, where they have already functioned as high-performance band-pass filters for decades
Morgan 1998
;
Morgan 2010
;
Campbell 2012
. The foundational principle for their application as filters was established with the introduction of specially shaped electrodes, called interdigital transducers (IDTs), on piezoelectric substrates
White and Voltmer 1965
.

An alternating voltage applied to a transmitting IDT excites SAWs within the substrate. These waves propagate across the substrate to a receiving IDT, where they are converted back to an electrical signal. The geometric configuration of the IDT dictates the resonance frequency at which SAWs can be excited, along with higher-order harmonics. This inherent frequency selectivity enables the precise bandpass filtering characteristic of SAW devices, while their small size and operating range in the radio frequency (RF) regime make them ideal for use in hand-held devices like smartphones
Ruppel et al. 2002
;
Chen et al. 2022
.

This basic functionality of SAW devices can be extended by modifying the substrate between the IDTs in various ways, enabling their application as lab-on-a-chip platforms for advancements across diverse disciplines, including physics, chemistry, and medicine
Delsing et al. 2019
;
Mandal and Banerjee 2022
;
Ding et al. 2012
;
Rufo et al. 2022
. A significant area of recent interest involves the interaction of these phononic devices with magnetism, since both can be excited in the RF range
Luo et al. 2024
;
Bozhko et al. 2020
. Specifically, the excitation of spin waves (SWs) via the magnetoelastic effect has emerged as a promising tool in magnonics, offering numerous potential applications
Yang and Schmidt 2021
;
Puebla et al. 2022
. One such application is the ability to absorb specific frequencies, dependent on the strength of an applied external magnetic field, when the SAW interacts with a magnetic thin film
Weiler et al. 2011
;
Dreher et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Casals et al. 2020
;
Kunz et al. 2024
.

This can be achieved, because SAW attenuation is only efficient at frequencies where its dispersion relation intersects with that of the SW. There, the SAW’s energy is transduced to a SW, where it then dissipates via Gilbert damping
Yamamoto et al. 2022
;
Küß et al. 2021
;
Bas et al. 2022
. The crossover point of the dispersion relations can be precisely tuned by adjusting the strength of an external magnetic field, as the field shifts the SW dispersion relation, thereby repositioning the resonance point to a desired frequency. By tuning this point to the band-pass frequency, signals are attenuated, whereas when detuned, the signal passes without attenuation, resulting in highly efficient signal filtering. However, implementing such a device would necessitate the continuous operation of a variable electromagnet, which is undesirable for many potential applications.

In this study, we propose a different method to deliberately shift the SW dispersion, where we utilize exchange-decoupled, but stray-field interacting, thin magnetic islets with out-of-plane (OOP) magnetization and a large magnetic moment (see Fig.
1
). The magnetization direction of each islet, influenced by the device’s magnetic history, can align along either the positive or negative z-axis. This orientation significantly alters the SW dispersion relation, which in turn impacts SAW absorption. This inherent variability allows for diverse device configurations to manipulate both the amount of absorption and the frequency band of its occurrence. Once programmed via a variable external magnetic field or spin torque methods, the islets maintain a stable magnetic state, requiring only a constant bias field to facilitate magnetoelastic interaction. We compare two device configurations: the A-state, where adjacent islets have antiparallel magnetic moments, and the P-state, where they are parallel. In the A-state, stray fields form flux closures, which enhance the internal magnetic fields within the islets. This increases the system’s stiffness and, consequently, its resonance frequencies compared to islets in the P-state, where magnetic field lines repel. Therefore, we anticipate a shift in the SW dispersion relation between the P- and A-states, leading to differences in SAW absorption, which we test using the Rayleigh SAW mode as an example.

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\Delta S_{\text{21}}(t)-\Delta S_{\text{21}}(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta S_{\text{21}}(t)}\right|

\displaystyle<10^{-5}

(20)

\displaystyle\left|\,\Delta S_{\text{21}}(t)-\Delta S_{\text{21}}(t-\Delta t)\,\right|

\displaystyle<10^{-3}\,$\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}$.

(21)

\Delta S_{\text{21}}
value is then determined by averaging
\Delta S_{\text{21}}
over these 50 time steps to further mitigate numerical noise.

and thus
\bm{\varepsilon}

III
Discussion

In this study, we have demonstrated the theoretical viability of a magneto-phononic device concept capable of switching SAW attenuation at a target frequency solely depending on its internal magnetic configuration. The shift in the SW dispersion relation resulting from the stray-field interactions of the parallel vs antiparallel aligned magnetic Co/Ni islets making up the device was shown to be significant enough to alter the SAW attenuation by as much as
28.9\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
between them.

The algorithm developed to carry out these simulations was successfully validated on the results presented in ref.
Küß et al. 2021
, showing that the assumptions made in its derivation are reasonable. Furthermore, due to the reduction in computational complexity by this approach, it is possible to sweep through a very large set of simulation parameters in a reasonable amount of time while maintaining great accuracy.

Methods

IV.1
Uni-Directional Model: Derivation

The principal assumptions of the model are:

•

The SAW only loses energy through a reduction in its amplitude
A
. Its velocity and mode are assumed to be constant and have to be known from simulations or analytical solutions. The simulation geometry then has to be chosen to be an integer multiple of the wavelength of that mode in the direction of travel.

•

Measurements are only taken, once the system is in a state of constant energy flow between the mechanical and magnetic systems (
\frac{\text{d}R_{\text{T}}}{\text{d}t}=0
). This can only occur for a sustained SAW signal of at least a couple periods (see Fig.
5
).

:
Example simulation of the magneto-phononic interaction within a thin magnetic film under influence of an external field. The system was in energetic equilibrium when at

) in red, the energy transferred from the phonon to the magnon (Eq.

\left(\text{approximated via}\,\frac{\text{d}E_{\text{M}}}{\text{d}t}(t)\approx\frac{E_{\text{M}}(t+\Delta t)-E_{\text{M}}(t-\Delta t)}{2\Delta t}\right)
. At
t=2.43\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}$
, the convergence criterion was reached. The bottom graph shows the validity of Eq. (
23
) with
R_{\text{T}}+R_{\text{L}}
(blue) indeed summing to
\frac{\text{d}E_{\text{M}}}{\text{d}t}
(black).

We start by examining the total change in energy of the magnetic system
\frac{\text{d}E_{\text{M}}}{\text{d}t}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}E_{\text{M}}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}U_{\text{M}}\big(t,\textit{{m}}(t)\big)\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta U_{\text{M}}\big(t,\textit{{m}}(t)\big)}{\delta\textit{{m}}}\frac{\text{d}\textit{{m}}}{\text{d}t}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}}_{\equiv R_{\text{L}}}+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial U_{\text{M}}\big(t,\textit{{m}}(t)\big)}{\partial t}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}}_{\equiv R_{\text{T}}}.

(23)

Here,
U_{\text{M}}
denotes the magnetic energy density and
R_{\text{L}}
are the dissipative losses due to Gilbert damping with the well known result
Abert 2019

\displaystyle R_{\text{L}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha\gamma}{1+\alpha^{2}}\mu_{0}M_{\text{s}}\int_{\Omega}(\textit{{m}}\times\textit{{H}}_{\text{Eff}})^{2}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}.

(24)

For
R_{\text{T}}
, we make use of the fact that, for constant
H_{\text{ext}}
, the magneto-elastic energy term (Eq.
2
) is the only one that explicitly depends on time in
E_{\text{M}}
. When the simulation geometry is an integer multiple of the wavelength,
\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\bm{\varepsilon}:C:\bm{\varepsilon}\right)\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}=0
. Given that
\bm{\varepsilon}_{\text{m}}
has no explicit time dependence,
\frac{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}_{\text{m}}}{\partial t}=0
. Thus, for a symmetric
C
,
R_{\text{T}}
simplifies to

\displaystyle R_{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}\right):C:\bm{\varepsilon}_{\text{m}}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}.

(25)

Using the law of energy conservation, we know that all energy gained by the magnetic system has to be compensated by a loss in the energy of the phonon,
E_{\text{Ph}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}E_{\text{Ph}}}{\text{d}t}=-R_{\text{T}}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\varepsilon}\right):C:\bm{\varepsilon}_{\text{m}}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}.

(26)

If the magneto-phononic interaction is weak enough, that the LLG stays in a linear regime, it is known, that
E_{\text{Ph}}
follows an exponential decay
Dreher et al. 2012
;
Küß et al. 2021
(in the appendix, a possible extension of this approach to the non-linear regime is discussed):

\displaystyle E_{\text{Ph}}(t)

\displaystyle=E_{\text{Ph}}(0)e^{\beta t}.

(27)

Using Eq.
26
, we can express the decay rate
\beta
as

\displaystyle\beta=\beta\frac{E_{\text{Ph}}(0)e^{\beta t}}{E_{\text{Ph}}(0)e^{\beta t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{E_{\text{Ph}}(t)}\frac{\text{d}E_{\text{Ph}}}{\text{d}t}(t)=\frac{-R_{\text{T}}(t)}{E_{\text{Ph}}(t)}

(28)

where we can see, that it is sufficient to know
R_{\text{T}}
and
E_{\text{Ph}}
for any single point in time, in order to obtain
\beta
for the full interaction.
R_{\text{T}}
can be calculated from Eq.
25
, where we simulate a segment of the magnetic material until
R_{\text{T}}
becomes constant (see fig
5
).
E_{\text{Ph}}
can be obtained in a pre-simulation step by summing the potential energy
\frac{1}{2}\bm{\varepsilon}:C:\bm{\varepsilon}
and kinetic energy
\frac{\rho\textit{{v}}^{2}}{2}
of the SAW over the full volume
V
, including possible non-magnetic layers
Landau et al. 1960
:

\displaystyle E_{\text{Ph}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{V}\bm{\varepsilon}:C:\bm{\varepsilon}+\rho\textit{{v}}^{2}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}.

(29)

Given that both
\bm{\varepsilon}
and
v
are derived from
u
, they are proportional to
A
and therefore:
E_{\text{Ph}}\propto A^{2}
. Using
P\propto A^{2}

Robbins 1977
, where
P
is the power of the SAW, we find:

\displaystyle P\propto E_{\text{Ph}}.

(30)

Finally, by viewing the phonon as a quasi-particle with a position
x(t)=ct
, where
c=v_{\text{t}}\xi
is its velocity, we can give its energy in terms of its position instead of time:

\displaystyle E_{\text{Ph}}(t)=E_{\text{Ph}}\left(\frac{x(t)}{c}\right).

(31)

Combining Eq.
26
to
31
, we obtain the magnetic transmission losses
\Delta S_{ij}
by setting the position of the phonon
x(t)
to the length of the magnetic material
l
:

\displaystyle\Delta S_{ij}(l)

\displaystyle=10\text{log}_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=10\text{log}_{10}\!\left(\frac{E_{\text{Ph}}(l/c)}{E_{\text{Ph}}(0)}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=10\text{log}_{10}\!\left(\text{exp}\!\left(\frac{l}{c}\beta\right)\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{10}{\text{ln}\!\left(10\right)}\frac{l}{c}\beta

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{10}{\text{ln}\!\left(10\right)}\frac{l}{c}\frac{-R_{\text{T}}}{E_{\text{Ph}}}

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{10}{\text{ln}\!\left(10\right)}\frac{l}{c}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{\varepsilon}\right):C:\bm{\varepsilon}_{\text{m}}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}}{\frac{1}{2}\int_{V}\bm{\varepsilon}:C:\bm{\varepsilon}+\rho\textit{{v}}^{2}\,\text{d}\textit{{x}}}.

(37)
