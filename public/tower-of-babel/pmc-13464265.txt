ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary artery perforation is an uncommon complication of right heart catheterization (RHC), with potentially lethal consequences.Case SummaryAn older woman undergoing transcatheter edge-to-edge repair under general anesthesia, had sudden bleeding from her endotracheal tube after RHC. This was followed by de-saturation and drop in blood pressure. Repeat RHC demonstrated left pulmonary artery perforation and pseudoaneurysm. Following wedge balloon occlusion of the perforation, a second femoral access was used to deliver a 12-mm Amplatzer vascular plug to seal the neck. The ping pong technique controlled active bleeding and allowed for successful device delivery for definitive management.DiscussionPulmonary artery perforation is both a rare and lethal complication that requires rapid diagnosis and treatment.Take-Home MessagesRespiratory tract bleeding following RHC should be considered pulmonary artery perforation until proven otherwise. The Ping Pong Guide technique is a feasible and safe treatment option.Key Words: complication, mitral valve, occluder

History of Presentation

Patient

An 89-year-old woman was admitted in September 2025 with signs and symptoms of heart failure. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated severe biventricular dysfunction, coupled with severe mitral regurgitation (MR). The mechanism of the MR was functional, with flail of the middle scallop of the posterior mitral valve leaflet (Video 1). Following assessment by the structural team during the index hospitalization, she was referred for transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, using the MitraClip G4 system after medical optimization.

Procedure

Access was obtained via the right femoral vein (primary access) and left femoral artery and vein (secondary access for anesthesia). The patient was then anaesthetized and intubated. A right heart catheterization (RHC) study was performed using a 7-F balloon wedge catheter. A J-wire was used to go from the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery. Following completion of the RHC study, we proceeded with the transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. Prior to the transseptal puncture with an SL-1 catheter, 3,000 units of heparin were given. After achieving access to the left atrium, an additional 4,000 units of heparin were given. Under TEE guidance, the septum was dilated using an 8 × 40-mm Mustang balloon. We then dilated the skin using a 22-F dilator and advanced the MitraClip guiding system. We entered the left atrium and de-aired the long sheath. Using 3-dimensional steering under TEE guidance, a MitraClip XT W was advanced to the plane of the mitral valve. Around this time we noticed some blood in the endotracheal tube.

Past Medical History

This 89-year-old female patient experienced mixed ischemic and nonischemic cardiomyopathy, with severe biventricular dysfunction. Her cardiac history included percutaneous coronary intervention, previously implanted cardiac re-synchronization therapy and defibrillator device, atrial fibrillation, and severe functional MR. Her medical history included hypertension, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, mixed asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, osteoarthritis, and gastrointestinal reflux disease. Her surgical history included a right total hip replacement more than a decade ago. Of note, she was on chronic direct oral anticoagulation therapy. Specifically, the apixaban was stopped 48 hours before her procedure.

Investigations

We first inquired about possible traumatic intubation and/or traumatic TEE insertion. We were assured that there was no prior trauma from either procedure. The amount of bleeding did not appear to be very significant at this time, and we elected to proceed. We checked clip orientation on TEE (Video 2), re-positioned as needed, and eventually locked, closed, and released the clip. Excellent result was observed on TEE, with only trace of MR (Video 3). Around this time the bleeding was thought to be worsening—there was more blood in the endotracheal tube, and issues with ventilation and oxygenation appeared. We then advanced a 7-F balloon wedge catheter from the left-sided venous sheath and up into the lung. Angiography demonstrated perforation of a pulmonary artery in the left lower lobe, with a large pseudoaneurysm (Figure 1, Video 4).

Management

The balloon wedge catheter was inflated, and the wedged catheter advanced into the feeding vessel to stop the bleeding. Simultaneously, anesthesia switched to a dual-lumen endotracheal tube and administered 50 mg of protamine. No specific reversal agent was used as the culprit was quickly identified, the perforation was controlled, and the last dose of apixaban was administered over 48 hours prior. The inner lumen of the MitraClip guiding system is not straight and is not intended to function as a vascular sheath for other catheters. Hence, it was removed and exchanged for a 20-F Gore dry seal sheath over the wire. First, we inserted a straight marker catheter through the Gore dry seal sheath and took an angiogram for calibration. We measured the target vessel at 7 mm. We then removed the straight marker catheter and advanced a 6-F Judkins Right 4 guiding catheter up through the new Gore dry seal sheath and into the pulmonary artery. We implemented the Ping Pong Guide technique to minimize bleeding while we were positioning the catheter to deliver a plug into the pulmonary artery. We were able to use a stiff exchange Glidewire delicately through the 6-F Judkins Right 4 guiding catheter and position it alongside the balloon wedge catheter that was through the left-sided femoral venous sheath. We brought the balloon down and were able to get the wire into the pseudoaneurysm. Over this, we advanced the 6-F guiding catheter into the damaged vessel. We advanced and deployed a 12 × 8-mm Amplatzer vascular plug device. The size of the device was chosen via the following logic: big enough to occlude without causing further damage. First angiogram revealed that we were positioned in the correct vessel, but as expected, there was still a tiny leak into the pseudoaneurysm. A few minutes later complete occlusion of this vessel was demonstrated (Video 5). Anesthesia performed bronchoscopy, which identified no active bleeding. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit.

Outcome and Follow-up

The patient was extubated the same night. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated only mild MR (Video 6). Computed tomography angiography ruled out active bleeding. The patient was transferred to the ward and later discharged home on optimal guideline-directed medical therapy.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a rare complication of RHC, causing active bleeding and subsequent hemodynamic compromise.

Among several iatrogenic complications related to insertion and maintenance of wedge catheters, pulmonary artery perforation (PAP) is an infrequent but lethal complication occurring with an estimated frequency of 0.01% to 0.47%. Risk factors for catheter-induced PAP include advanced age (>60 years), female gender, catheter stiffness, prolonged balloon inflation, multiple manipulations of the catheter, peripheral placement of the catheter, pulmonary hypertension, and anticoagulation therapy.1 PAP is associated with extremely poor clinical outcomes, with a reported mortality rate as high as 42%. The most common and severe complication is massive airway hemorrhage, which can rapidly lead to hypoxia, exsanguination, and cardiac arrest.2 The gold standard for diagnosis is pulmonary angiography, which can directly visualize the site of vascular injury, active extravasation, or pseudoaneurysm formation. Pulmonary angiography is also essential for planning intervention if indicated.1, 2, 3

Endovascular techniques, including arterial embolization and coil and plug deployment, have emerged as important options for hemorrhage control, particularly in cases in which surgical risk is prohibitive or the bleeding site is inaccessible.4,5 Operator familiarity with endovascular approaches is increasing, and these interventions are considered safe and effective for various pulmonary vascular injuries.5

First described by Ben-Gal et al6 for the treatment of coronary perforation, the dual catheter technique is implemented as follows: soon after the operator's recognition of the perforation, a sealing balloon is inflated, opposing the ruptured vessel wall or crossing over the perforated branch. While the sealing balloon is inflated, the guide catheter is withdrawn slightly from the coronary ostia, the contralateral femoral artery is instrumented, and a second guiding catheter is advanced up the aorta to engage the same coronary ostia, alongside the previous guiding catheter. A coronary covered stent is advanced on a new wire through that second guiding catheter and placed just proximally to the sealing balloon. The sealing balloon is briefly deflated and withdrawn proximally to allow wire passage, and the coronary covered stent is advanced to the site and deployed.

We describe an example of catheter-induced PAP in the context of several key risk factors, including advanced age, female gender, and the use of anticoagulation. The diagnosis was made promptly following the sudden onset of airway hemorrhage and hemodynamic compromise, with pulmonary angiography confirming the presence of a pseudoaneurysm and active vascular injury. Treatment followed best practices: airway protection, reversal of anticoagulation, and rapid transition to endovascular intervention with arterial plug deployment. The rationale for choosing the Amplatzer vascular plug over a coil or a covered stent consisted of several reasons: 1) a vascular plug is more predictable and is better suited for shorter segments; 2) a covered stent would potentially occlude multiple branches and should primarily be used for proximal branches where preservation of flow is essential; and 3) coils are unpredictable and are more likely to embolize. Moreover, the number of coils required for a good occlusion is hard to predict.

This case underscores the importance of recognizing predisposing factors, maintaining a high index of suspicion for vascular injury, and implementing a tailored, multidisciplinary approach to management (Figure 2).

Conclusions

PAP is a challenging and potentially dire complication of RHC. Rapid diagnosis and airway and hemodynamic stabilizations are crucial. Endovascular embolization using the Ping Pong Guide technique is an excellent choice in rapidly deteriorating, high-risk patients with accessible bleeding sites.
