176. One-month ronadside ambient PM2.5 does ot worsen lung or hippocampal inflammation in an LPS-induced COPD mouse model
Authors: Z Ma, H Akhlaq, S-Y Yau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) features chronic airway inflammation and is linked to elevated risks of anxiety and depression. While PM2.5 is epidemiologically associated with COPD exacerbations, it remains unclear whether real-world ambient exposure aggravates pulmonary inflammation and triggers brain inflammatory changes relevant to mood disorders.Aims & ObjectivesTo examine whether ambient PM2.5 exposure augments inflammatory protein expression in the lung and hippocampus in COPD compared to control mice.MethodMale and female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were assigned to filtered air + control, filtered air + COPD, ambient air + control, or ambient air + COPD. COPD-like inflammation was induced by intratracheal LPS once a week for four weeks. Ambient exposure used a custom chamber drawing roadside traffic air continuously for 4 weeks; controls received filtered air in the same setup. One week after the last LPS instillation, lungs and hippocampi were collected for western blot of inflammatory markers.ResultsLPS robustly increased pulmonary IL-17A versus vehicle in male mice, validating COPD-like inflammation. Four-week ambient PM2.5 did not significantly elevate IL-17A, IL-1β, IL-6, or NF-κB in lung or hippocampus in either control or COPD male mice compared with filtered air. No significant difference was observed between the LPS treatment and PM2.5 exposure in the female lung. Thus, under these conditions, roadside PM2.5 did not exacerbate pulmonary or hippocampal inflammatory protein signatures beyond LPS effects.Discussion & ConclusionsIn an LPS-induced COPD model, one month of real-world roadside PM2.5 exposure did not worsen inflammatory markers in lung or hippocampus. Findings suggest that exposure duration/composition and disease state may critically gate PM2.5 impact on lung–brain inflammation. Limitations include short exposure, variability in ambient PM2.5 composition, reliance on LPS rather than smoke-induced COPD, and absence of behavioral readouts. Future work should extend exposure, include controlled PM2.5 dosing and depression-relevant behaviors, and probe mechanistic pathways to define pollution–COPD–brain interactions.
