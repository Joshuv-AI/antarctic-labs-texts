Tumor-immune microenvironment and NRF2 associate with clinical efficacy of PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy in lung squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Jianchun Duan, Yun Zhang, Ran Chen, Liang Liang, Yi Huo, Shun Lu, Jun Zhao, Chunhong Hu, Yuping Sun, Kunyu Yang, Mingwei Chen, Yan Yu, Jianming Ying, Ruiqi Huang, Xiaopeng Ma, Shiangjiin Leaw, Fan Bai, Zhirong Shen, Shangli Cai, Daming Gao, Jie Wang, Zhijie Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe RATIONALE-307 study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03594747) demonstrates prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) with first-line tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy in advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC; N = 360). Here we describe an immune-related gene expression signature (GES), composed of genes involved in both innate and adaptive immunity, that appears to differentiate tislelizumab plus chemotherapy PFS benefit versus chemotherapy. In contrast, a tislelizumab plus chemotherapy PFS benefit is observed regardless of programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression or tumor mutational burden (TMB). Genetic analysis reveals that NRF2 pathway activation is enriched in PD-L1positive and TMBhigh patients. NRF2 pathway activation is negatively associated with PFS, which affects efficacy outcomes associated with PD-L1 and TMB status, impairing their predictive potential. Mechanistic studies demonstrate that NRF2 directly mediates PD-L1 constitutive expression independent of adaptive PD-L1 regulation in LUSC. In summary, the GES is an immune signature that might identify LUSC patients likely to benefit from first-line tislelizumab plus chemotherapy.Keywords: squamous NSCLC, PD-1 blockade, biomarkers, PD-L1, tumor mutational burden, gene expression signature, NRF2, tislelizumab, clinical trial, immunotheraphy

Introduction

Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade plus chemotherapy is an established first-line treatment option for all-comer patients with advanced lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).1,2,3,4 Nevertheless, despite the promising results observed in various clinical trials with anti-PD-1 and anti-programmed death ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) antibodies,1,2,3,4 some patients remain unresponsive or progress within a few months, suggesting the presence of primary resistance to this treatment regimen. For these patients, the addition of anti-PD-(L)1 therapy to chemotherapy may not bring extra benefit versus chemotherapy alone. Thus, identification of effective biomarkers for anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy combination therapy remains a key objective for the first-line treatment of LUSC.

Anti-PD-1 single-agent studies have successfully implemented the use of PD-L1 as a validated biomarker to select patients most likely to benefit from treatment.5,6 However, in anti-PD-1 plus chemotherapy studies in LUSC, PD-L1 has proven to be ineffective as a selection biomarker for LUSC, with treatment effects observed in both PD-L1positive and PD-L1negative patients.3,4,7 Additionally, tissue tumor mutational burden (tTMB) has also been demonstrated to have limited clinical utility, as reported in the KEYNOTE-407 study, in which tTMB was not significantly associated with the efficacy of pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy over chemotherapy alone as first-line therapy for metastatic squamous non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).8 Gene expression signatures (GESs) in tumor tissue have been explored as potential alternative biomarkers, with an 18-gene T cell inflamed signature (TIS) reported to predict response to pembrolizumab across multiple solid tumors9; however, this signature has not yet been validated in any phase 3 LUSC first-line therapy cohorts. More specifically, activation of the transcription factor NRF2 in LUSC tumors has been suggested to be associated with resistance to atezolizumab-based anti-PD-L1 therapy,10 but the prognostic role of NRF2 requires validation. Comprehensive analyses are therefore needed to identify and characterize potential biomarkers for the antitumor activity of PD-1 blockade plus chemotherapy in LUSC.8

The open-label, randomized phase 3 RATIONALE-307 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03594747) demonstrated significantly prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) with the combination of the anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone as first-line treatment for patients with locally advanced or metastatic LUSC.3 The positive results of the interim analysis led to its approval by the China National Medical Products Administration in 2021.11 To investigate the association between PFS (by independent review committee) and biomarkers assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), genomic profiling, and transcriptomic profiling, we performed a retrospective exploratory analysis among the 360 patients randomized in the RATIONALE-307 trial. The primary objective of this retrospective, exploratory analysis was to identify potential biomarkers correlated with clinical benefit and resistance to first-line treatment of patients treated with anti-PD-1 and chemotherapy in LUSC.

Results

Population definition of biomarker analysis

Between July 30, 2018 and June 13, 2019, 360 patients who met the study eligibility criteria were randomized. The median study follow-up time was 8.6 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 8.1–9.0 months). In the retrospective analyses reported here, patients randomized to the two tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arms (tislelizumab plus paclitaxel and carboplatin and tislelizumab plus nab-paclitaxel and carboplatin) were grouped as the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy combination therapy arm and compared with the chemotherapy-alone arm (paclitaxel plus carboplatin). Patients who received any study treatment were included in the efficacy analysis set (n = 355; 238 patients treated with tislelizumab plus chemotherapy and 117 patients treated with chemotherapy alone). The PD-L1 IHC assay and RNA panel sequencing were performed on tumor specimens collected at baseline. DNA panel sequencing was conducted on baseline tumor specimens and on plasma samples collected before the first treatment. The baseline characteristics and efficacy profiles of each biomarker evaluable population (BEP) were comparable with those of the efficacy analysis set (Table S1). The BEPs for PD-L1 expression, gene expression profiling (GEP), tTMB, and blood TMB (bTMB) included 349, 275, 263, and 111 patients, respectively.

PD-L1 and TMB did not predict the PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy

First, we examined the association of the established biomarkers PD-L1 and TMB (evaluated from tumor and blood samples) with treatment benefit. In total, 61.0% of patients in the PD-L1 dataset (n = 213) were PD-L1positive, as defined by 1% PD-L1 tumor cell (TC) expression (a cutoff commonly used in anti-PD-(L)1 therapy studies in NSCLC).2,12,13 The prevalence of PD-L1high patients, as defined by the 25% or 50% cutoff, was 42.4% (n = 148) and 35.0% (n = 122), respectively. We observed a trend toward enhanced PFS benefit in the PD-L1positive subgroup at a 1% cutoff (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.41 in PD-L1positive patients; HR = 0.64 in PD-L1negative patients; interaction p = 0.143; Figures 1A and 1B). When using a higher PD-L1 cutoff at 25% and 50%, no further increase of PFS benefit was observed, as indicated by the similar HRs in the PD-L1high versus PD-L1low subgroups (0.52 versus 0.46 and 0.47 versus 0.49, respectively) (Figure 1B).

In the tTMB dataset, 49.8% of patients (n = 131) had a tTMB score of 10 mutations (mut)/Mb (tTMBhigh) or greater; the prevalence of tTMBhigh patients as defined by the 12 or 14 mut/Mb cutoff was 32.7% (n = 86) and 20.5% (n = 54), respectively. A trend toward a better PFS benefit was found in tTMBhigh patients, along with a decrease of the PFS HR from 0.44 to 0.29 when the tTMB cutoff increased from 10 to 14 mut/Mb (Figures 1C and 1D). Similarly, a trend of smaller PFS HRs was observed in the bTMBhigh (bTMB ≥ 6 mut/Mb) versus the bTMBlow (bTMB < 6 mut/Mb) subgroup, while HR values in bTMBhigh patients were consistently at 0.30–0.33 regardless of cutoff (all interaction p > 0.05; Figures 1E and 1F). A potential poor prognostic effect of bTMB (≥6 versus <6 mut/Mb) was observed in the chemotherapy arm (the PFS HR of bTMB high versus bTMBlow was 2.36, p = 0.049). In summary, in LUSC, trends toward improved PFS benefit of the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm versus chemotherapy were observed in PD-L1positive, tTMBhigh, or bTMBhigh patients relative to the respective “low” subgroups; however, none of the differences in PFS benefit between the biomarker-defined subgroups reached statistical significance (interaction p > 0.05).

An immune GES differentiated the treatment-specific benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy on PFS

The status of the tumor immune microenvironment has been associated with the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and related GEP scores, such as the TIS9 and the T effector (Teff) signature,1 have been found to be promising biomarkers. Recently, TIS has been prospectively utilized for patient stratification in a biomarker-driven trial in advanced NSCLC (KEYNOTE-495).14 However, those established GEP scores were mostly derived from chemotherapy-free ICI treatment or were only knowledge based, and clinical utility has not been demonstrated in first-line NSCLC trials with anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy treatment. To better investigate the pre-existing tumor and host immune cell interactions within the tumor immune microenvironment specific to anti-PD-1 plus chemotherapy combination therapy, GEP was assessed on baseline tumor samples from 275 patients in the RATIONALE 307 study. Thirty-two genes were identified as being significantly associated with PFS benefit between treatment arms (HR in tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm < 0.9, HR in chemotherapy arm > 1.0, interaction p < 0.05) (Table S2). Subsequently, an 11-gene subset (Figure 2A) was identified from the genes ranked highest based on predictive effect (i.e., interaction p values [smallest to largest], corresponding to the ratio of PFS HR for tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in high and low expressors of the gene) and with immune-related functional annotations (STAR Methods; Figure S1A). This gene signature included regulators of both adaptive and innate antitumor immune responses, such as antiviral response (IFIT1 and OAS2), interferon signaling (SP110, JAK2, and STAT1), macrophage regulation (ESPTI1 and SLAMF8), and T cell-related functions (IL7R, CCL4, HLA.A, and SELPLG) (Table S3). Higher levels of expression of this 11-gene signature were observed in T lymphocytes and myeloid cells than in cancer cells and stromal cells (e.g., fibroblasts), using the lung cancer single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets (Figure S1B).15,16

In patients who had GES scores greater than or equal to the median (GEShigh), tislelizumab plus chemotherapy demonstrated longer PFS than chemotherapy (HR = 0.28, 95% CI 0.18–0.46, p < 0.001), with a median PFS of 7.8 versus 4.2 months, respectively (Figure 2B). Limited differences in benefit between treatments were observed in patients with GES scores below the median (GESlow HR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.51–1.30, p = 0.361, median PFS 5.7 versus 5.7 months, respectively; Figure 2B). A significant treatment-specific effect for the signature was confirmed by interaction analysis (p = 0.002), which suggested that the signature was predictive of PFS benefit with tislelizumab plus chemotherapy (Figure 2C). Next, we tested GES in a phase 3 immuno-oncology (IO) combination therapy trial (anti-PD-L1 plus anti-vascular endothelial growth factor receptor [VEGFR] therapy), the JAVELIN Renal 101 study17 (Figure S2A). Patients in the GEShigh subgroup showed greater PFS benefit with anti-PD-L1 plus anti-VEGFR therapy versus patients in the GESlow subgroup, and GES demonstrated a predictive effect (interaction p = 0.006; Figure S2A). Additional examination in two independent PD-(L)1 monotherapy-treated NSCLC cohorts18 showed consistent trends for PFS benefit with PD-(L)1 monotherapy (Figures S2B and S2C); a trend for overall survival benefit was also observed in patients in the GEShigh subgroup versus the GESlow subgroup in National Cancer Center of China (NCC; at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, Beijing, China) cohort 1 (Figure S2C; Table S4). To complement this gene expression analysis, we examined four published immune signatures in the RATIONALE-307 dataset, including the TIS,9 IMpower131 Teff signature,1 JAVELIN Renal 101 immuno signature,17 and a 4-gene inflammation signature (4-gene GES).19 Although there were very few overlapping genes between our immune signature (11-gene GES) and the published signatures (Table S5), a PFS benefit favoring tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone was consistently observed in the biomarker-positive subgroups, and the p values of the treatment and biomarker interactions were less than 0.05 in all immune signatures (Figure 2C). In an additional analysis using a quartile-based cutoff, subgroups with a higher GES score were associated with greater PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy (Figure S2D). Taken together, the 11-gene GES results, which were derived from a large combination therapy cohort, appeared to show a stronger correlation with the PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy in LUSC than the established tumor biomarkers (PD-L1, tTMB, and bTMB status) and showed non-overlapping features with four publicly validated immune signatures. These observations indicated the presence of additional factors in our study that may interfere with tumor-immune interplay.

NRF2 pathway activation was negatively associated with PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy and enriched in the PD-L1positive and tTMBhigh subgroups

To better identify the potential reason why PD-L1 and TMB could not predict the PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy, the oncogenic pathways20,21,22,23,24,25 were analyzed to investigate whether differentially mutated pathways among PD-L1-, TMB-, and GES-defined biomarker-positive patients may be linked to efficacy. By calculating the odds ratio of mutational frequency in each pathway, we found that the TP53 (69%), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K; 51%), cell cycle (48%), NRF2 (25%), and Wnt (4%) pathways showed enriched genetic alterations in the PD-L1positive and tTMBhigh subgroups but not in the GEShigh subgroup (Figure 3A). We further sought to identify the association between the aberrant oncogenic alterations and treatment effect. The Wnt pathway was excluded from subsequent analysis due to its low mutation frequency (<5%) in LUSC.

Genetic alterations in TP53, PI3K, or cell cycle pathways had no impact on PFS in either the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm or the chemotherapy arm (all p > 0.05). In contrast, activation mutations in the NRF2 pathway (including gain-of-function mutations in NFE2L2 and loss-of-function mutations in KEAP1 or CUL3) were observed to be associated with reduced PFS in the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm (HR = 1.91, 95% CI 1.20–3.05, p = 0.006; Figure 3B). A prolonged PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy was observed in the wild-type NRF2 (NRF2WT) subgroup (HR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.27–0.65, p < 0.001; Figure 3C), while the difference was not statistically significant in the mutated NRF2 (NRF2MUT) subgroup (HR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.38–1.33, p = 0.197; Figure 3D). This suggests that patients harboring activating mutations in the NRF2 pathway tend to be resistant to combination therapy, which was confirmed by a direct PFS association analysis in the NRF2MUT versus NRF2WT subgroups in the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm (HR = 0.52, 95% CI 0.33–0.83, p = 0.008; Figure S3A). Further characterization of immune cell populations revealed a lower expression of T cell gene signatures (The Cancer Genome Atlas [TCGA] LUSC dataset, n = 444, p = 0.047) as well as a lower density of CD3+CD8+ T cells (NCC cohort 2, n = 19 patients with NSCLC, p = 0.053) in the NRF2MUT subgroup than in the NRF2WT subgroup (Figures S3B and S3C; Table S6).

To further characterize the activation status of the NRF2 pathway at the transcriptome level, we investigated the key components of NRF2 downstream signaling by a published NRF2 pathway signature26 (Table S5) and found that the NRF2MUT subgroup had a significantly higher NRF2 signature score versus the NRF2WT group (p < 0.001; Figure S4A). To investigate the correlation between NRF2 pathway activation and treatment effect, we used the upper tertile as the cutoff to divide the patients into NRF2-high and -low subgroups (STAR Methods). A prolonged PFS benefit in the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm versus the chemotherapy arm was observed in NRF2low patients with reduced benefit in the NRF2high subgroup (NRF2high HR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.37–1.20, p = 0.178; NRF2low HR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.28–0.63, p < 0.001; interaction p = 0.203; Figures 3E and 3F). A direct PFS association analysis in the NRF2high versus NRF2low subgroups in the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm showed a trend similar to the NRF2 mutation status analysis results, although this was not statistically significant (HR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.50–1.61, p = 0.445; Figure S4B). Consistent with NRF2MUT, the percentage of NRF2high patients was significantly higher in the PD-L1positive and tTMBhigh subgroups (p = 0.004 and p = 0.003, respectively; Figures S4C and S4D) but similar in the GEShigh and GESlow subgroups (p = 0.447; Figure S4E). These results indicated that the effects of PD-L1 and TMB in predicting the efficacy of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy might be affected by NRF2 pathway activation.

NRF2 pathway activation may affect the PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy in the PD-L1positive and tTMBhigh but not GEShigh subgroups

The negative association of NRF2 pathway activation with PFS and its relationship to PD-L1 and TMB led to re-examination of the correlation of these biomarkers with efficacy by further stratification of NRF2high versus NRF2low patients. In the PD-L1positive subgroup, 39% (65 of 166) of patients had an NRF2high signature score. This PD-L1positive NRF2high subgroup of patients showed less PFS benefit than the PD-L1positive NRF2low subgroup (PFS HR = 0.55 versus 0.33, p = 0.067 versus p < 0.001; Figure 4A). A similar trend was observed in subgroups stratified by tTMB score and NRF2 signature score. In tTMBhigh NRF2high patients, there was less PFS benefit with tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy (HR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.22–1.35, p = 0.190; Figure 4B), while the tTMBhigh NRF2low subgroup showed an extended PFS benefit between treatments (HR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.14–0.55, p < 0.001; Figure 4B). It should be noted that patients with PD-L1negative and NRF2high tumors did not benefit from tislelizumab plus chemotherapy (PFS HR = 2.04), whereas those with PD-L1negative and NRF2low tumors had a modest PFS benefit (PFS HR = 0.52; Figure 4A). This differential impact of NRF2 activation was not observed in tTMBlow patients (Figure 4B).

In contrast, subgroups with a high GES score demonstrated a significant PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy regardless of NRF2 signature status (GEShigh NRF2high HR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.16–0.83, p = 0.016; GEShigh NRF2low HR = 0.24, 95% CI 0.13–0.43, p < 0.001; Figure 4C). NRF2 directly regulates PD-L1 transcription in LUSC cells

Considering the positive association of NRF2 pathway activation and PD-L1 expression shown in this study (Figure 3A), other LUSC datasets (Figures S3D and S3F), and a previously reported regulating relationship,27 we hypothesized that NRF2 may directly regulate the expression of PD-L1 in human LUSC. To this end, we initially performed the small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated NRF2 knockdown in the human LUSC cell lines SK-MES-1 and EBC-1. As shown in Figures 5A–5C, S5A, and S5B, similarly to NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1; a classic NRF2 target gene28), PD-L1 expression was reduced by NRF2 inhibition at both the mRNA and protein levels. Concurrently, we ectopically expressed NRF2WT or the NRF2-E79K mutant (a naturally derived NRF2 constitutively active mutant with amino acid alternation from Glu79 to Lys79) in the SK-MES-1 and NCI-H226 LUSC cell lines to further investigate the regulatory potential of NRF2 on PD-L1 expression. Western blot and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time qPCR) analyses revealed that the mRNA level and protein expression of PD-L1 were significantly elevated in NRF2-WT-expressing or NRF2-E79K-expressing cells (Figures 5D–5F, S5C, and S5D), indicating that NRF2 may directly regulate PD-L1 gene expression. To further investigate the transcriptional regulation of NRF2 on PD-L1, dual-luciferase reporter assays were generated using a PD-L1 gene promoter (pGL3-WT) or the PD-L1 promoter with putative NRF2MUT-binding site (pGL3-Mutant) (Figure 5G). Luciferase activity driven by the PD-L1 promoter was significantly activated by NRF2 overexpression or inhibited by knockdown of endogenous NRF2, as expected, while the PD-L1 promoter with the NRF2MUT binding site did not respond to NRF2 knockdown (Figures 5H and 5I). To further confirm the occupation of NRF2 on the PD-L1 promoter, we performed a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay in cross-linked SK-MES-1 cells (Figure 5J), which indicated that NRF2 was specifically enriched on PD-L1 promoters as well as NQO1 promoters (positive control) but not the PD-L1 exon region (negative control). In summary, our results demonstrate that NRF2 binds directly to the PD-L1 promoter region and mediates PD-L1 expression in TCs.

NRF2 pathway-mediated PD-L1 expression is independent of adaptive regulation in LUSC

PD-L1 expression is known to be regulated by inflammatory cytokines, such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ), in the tumor microenvironment.29 To investigate potential interactions between adaptive and NRF2-mediated constitutive PD-L1 expression, we treated cells with or without IFN-γ to mimic the inflammatory tumor microenvironment. In SK-MES-1 cells, PD-L1 mRNA levels of empty vector, NRF2-WT-, and NRF2-E79K-expressing cells were significantly elevated upon incubation with IFN-γ (Figure 5K). In addition, PD-L1 mRNA levels could be induced by NRF2-WT or NRF2-E79K expression in SK-MES-1 cells with or without IFN-γ incubation (Figure 5K). These results indicated that the NRF2 pathway regulates PD-L1 transcription independent of the inflammatory microenvironment.

To further confirm the association of these biomarker findings in clinical samples, we evaluated the relationship of PD-L1 expression with GES or NRF2 signature scores. A correlation between GES score (representing an inflamed tumor environment) and PD-L1 was observed in the GEShigh subgroup (Spearman r = 0.337, p < 0.001; Figure 5L) but not in the GESlow subgroup (Spearman r = −0.024, p = 0.783; Figure 5L). In contrast, NRF2 signature score and PD-L1 were correlated in the GESlow subgroup (Spearman r = 0.347, p < 0.001; Figure 5M) but not in the GEShigh subgroup (Spearman r = 0.156, p = 0.066; Figure 5M). These results suggested that the NRF2 pathway may be associated with constitutive PD-L1 expression and predominantly determine PD-L1 expression in a GESlow, non-inflamed microenvironment. The expression of PD-L1 in LUSC TCs may be a consequence of both TC NRF2 status and the immune microenvironment. Collectively, these results suggest that PD-L1 expression as a single indicator cannot effectively reflect the actual tumor inflammatory status and therefore determine the potential of whether patients would benefit from anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy combination therapy in LUSC.

Discussion

We analyzed the association between clinical outcomes and biomarkers from patients with advanced LUSC treated with tislelizumab plus chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone in the phase 3 RATIONALE-307 study.3 In line with similar findings relating to PD-L1 and TMB reported by KEYNOTE-407,2,8 we found a trend of association of PFS benefit with PD-L1 and TMB status, but the differences were not statistically significant for efficacy prediction. These observations suggest the potential contribution of other tumor- or immune-related factors that may impact the response to tislelizumab plus chemotherapy.

GEP analysis enabled the identification of an 11-gene immune GES that appeared to show a stronger correlation with the PFS benefit of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy than PD-L1 or TMB status. We also reported similar results with published immune-related signatures like the TIS. These data from a phase 3 study of first-line anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy treatment in LUSC demonstrate that immune-related signatures may have a predictive advantage over established biomarkers. The 11-gene signature comprised transcripts of genes functioning in T cells, B cells, and macrophages and related to antigen presentation, inflammation, and chemotaxis. Compared with published inflammation signatures derived mostly from ICI monotherapy (e.g., the TIS and Teff signature), 10 of 11 genes had not been reported previously in an immune signature for immunotherapy. This finding points to the importance of the inflamed tumor microenvironment, resulting from both adaptive (T cell, natural killer cell) and innate (macrophages) immunity, in response to anti-PD-1 plus chemotherapy combination therapy. In addition, the GES was further validated in independent datasets, including an IO-treated NSCLC cohort and a phase 3 study evaluating combination therapy with a PD-L1 inhibitor and anti-angiogenic agent in renal cell carcinoma.

Previous studies uncovered that NRF2 mutations facilitate the escape of NRF2 protein from proteosome degradation mediated by the CUL3-KEAP1 ubiquitin E3 ligase complex, which enables the translocation of NRF2 into the nucleus to initiate the transcription of target genes and strengthen its oncogenic functions.30,31,32 NRF2 activation promotes lung cancer tumorigenesis,10 accounts for multidrug resistance, and is associated with poor prognosis,33,34,35,36,37 including in patients receiving anti-PD-L1 treatment.10 High mutational frequency in the NRF2 pathway was found in a wide range of tumor types, including LUSC as the most affected (31%), followed by lung adenocarcinoma (25%), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (21%), esophageal carcinoma (20%), and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (17%).38 Previously, enrichment of NRF2 pathway mutations has been observed in “immune-cold” subtypes of LUSC,39 consistent with our finding of lower T cell gene signature expression and CD3+CD8+ T cell density in NRF2MUT versus NRF2WT subgroups. In the RATIONALE-307 study analyses, the treatment effect of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy was reduced in patients harboring activating mutations in the NRF2 pathway or with high NRF2 gene signature expression. This finding highlights the importance of NRF2 pathway activation as one of the potential resistance factors that may interfere with tumor-immune interplay in response to the combination of anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy in LUSC.

In RATIONALE-307, NRF2 pathway activation was found to be enriched in PD-L1positive and TMBhigh subgroups. We further demonstrated that NRF2 regulates PD-L1 expression via direct binding to the PD-L1 promotor region in vitro, independent of IFN-γ signaling, the key component of adaptive immunity. Stratification of the PD-L1 and TMB subgroups by NRF2 status led to an observation of reduced treatment effects in the PD-L1positiveNRF2high and TMBhighNRF2high subgroups versus their corresponding NRF2low subgroups, respectively. This suggested that NRF2 pathway activation status might affect the outcome of these two biomarkers in relation to the treatment effect of tislelizumab plus chemotherapy in LUSC.

Our 11-gene GES provides an immune signature with the potential to identify patients who could be more likely to benefit from anti-PD-(L)1 therapy and its combinations. Although tumors defined by median scores or greater for the previously defined TIS or our newly defined GES benefitted significantly more from tislelizumab plus chemotherapy treatment (PFS HR of 0.27 and 0.28, respectively, relative to scores lower than median), clinical assays measuring mRNA expression levels are currently not fully validated, and further development is still needed.14 The findings of this study support further evaluation of clinically available assays, such as PD-L1, TMB, or NRF2 mutations, for consideration as surrogate approaches to prioritize anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy treatment for patients with PD-L1positivetTMBhigh, PD-L1positiveNRF2WT, or tTMBhighNRF2WT status (PFS HR = 0.22–0.31). Recently, loss of function of LATS1/2, the YAP/TAZ upstream kinase in the Hippo pathway, has been implicated in paclitaxel resistance in NSCLC.40 In the current study, Hippo mutation (found in 12% of patients) was significantly associated with poor PFS with chemotherapy, while a strong treatment effect was observed with tislelizumab plus chemotherapy (PFS HR = 0.15; data not shown), indicating the potential for overcoming Hippo-mediated paclitaxel resistance with the addition of PD-1 blockade.

In conclusion, we identified an 11-gene immune signature as a potential predictive biomarker significantly associated with a treatment-specific PFS benefit with first-line tislelizumab plus chemotherapy combination therapy for LUSC. NRF2 activity was identified as a resistance factor to tislelizumab plus chemotherapy that was associated with an increase in PD-L1 expression and/or high TMB, which subsequently affected the outcome of the treatment efficacy associated with these biomarkers. These findings provide critical insights for biomarker-guided IO treatment of first-line LUSC and potentially for other indications.

Limitations of the study

These analyses have several limitations. Genomic alteration and expression profile assessments were based on biopsy tumor samples from each patient, which is unlikely to capture the entirety of the tumor genomic heterogeneity. For the archived tumor specimens, the somatic alteration landscape and genome expression profile may have further evolved prior to PD-1 blockade, and such changes may not have been captured by the current study. Furthermore, all analyses are retrospective, in the absence of an appropriate LUSC cohort for analysis; further independent and prospective validation is needed. Last, limited by the sample size, the impact of NRF2 mutations in the different biomarker-defined subgroups remains speculative, but this is worthy of further exploration and validation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

The patient eligibility criteria for the RATIONALE-307 trial have been described in detail previously.3 Briefly, eligible patients included those aged 18–75 years with treatment-naïve, histologically confirmed locally advanced (stage IIIB) or metastatic (stage IV) lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) (as classified by the American Joint Committee Cancer, Cancer Staging Manual 7th edition), without known EGFR-sensitizing mutations or ALK fusions. Mixed histology was permitted provided that squamous histology was the major component. In addition, patients were required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of ≤1 and measurable disease (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [RECIST] version 1.1), and to be ineligible for (or not amenable to) curative surgery or radiotherapy. Fresh or archival tumor tissue samples were required for PD-L1 expression assessment. Patient demographics and baseline characteristics have been described previously3 and are summarized in Table S1. Method details

Study design and procedures

The study design for the RATIONALE-307 trial has been described in detail previously.3 The primary endpoint was the comparison of PFS assessed by independent review committee (IRC) between tislelizumab combined with paclitaxel plus carboplatin or nab-paclitaxel plus carboplatin and paclitaxel plus carboplatin alone. Independent central review of radiologic images was performed by Bioclinica, Inc. Princeton, NJ. The exploratory endpoint was the analysis of the association between biomarkers and clinical outcome.

Patients were randomized (1:1:1) to treatment as described previously,3 with stratification by disease stage (stage IIIB versus IV) and level of TC PD-L1 expression (<1% versus 1–49% versus ≥50%). Patients were allocated to one of the following three regimens intravenously every three weeks (Q3W): tislelizumab plus paclitaxel and carboplatin; tislelizumab plus nab-paclitaxel and carboplatin; or paclitaxel and carboplatin. Treatment with tislelizumab was administered as a 200 mg infusion on day 1 [D1] and continued Q3W until a lack of clinical benefit or intolerable toxicity. Paclitaxel was administered as a 175 mg/m2 infusion on D1, carboplatin as infusion on D1 with an area under the concentration curve of 5, and nab-paclitaxel as infusions of 100 mg/m2 on D1, D8, and D15. Doublet chemotherapy was administered until the completion of 4–6 cycles (at investigator’s discretion), or the occurrence of disease progression or intolerable toxicity, whichever occurred first.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Biomarker analyses were performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue collected at screening. PD-L1 expression was assessed by the VENTANA PD-L1 (SP263) IHC assay (Ventana Medical Systems, Oro Valley, AZ, USA). PD-L1 expression levels were scored by the percentage of PD-L1 membranous staining at any intensity on tumor cells. The analysis of PD-L1 expression was performed prospectively at a central laboratory. A total of 354 randomized patients (98%) had evaluable PD-L1 expression.

Gene expression analysis

GEP data were generated on FFPE tumor tissue using HTG EdgeSeq Precision Immuno-Oncology Panel consisting of 1392 genes (PIP panel; HTG Molecular Diagnostics, Inc., Tucson, AZ, USA), per manufacturer’s instructions. In summary, RNA was extracted by HTG Lysis Buffer to subsequently bind to corresponding target-specific Nuclease Protection Probes on the HTG EdgeSeq processor. After the quantitative nuclease protection assay, samples were used as a template in individual PCR reactions with specially designed primers for PCR amplification. Unincorporated primer tags were removed from the library. The library concentrations were determined by qPCR, and normalized libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina Nextseq 500 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and the data were transformed into counts-per-million reads (CPM) using HTG EdgeSeq parser software. The CPM were log2 transformed and normalized by upper quantile normalization. Two hundred and eighty patients (78%) had evaluable GEP data. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the R package ‘GSVA’ to obtain individual sample-level gene signature scores.41 Gene signatures used in the RATIONALE-307 dataset were curated by pre-reported signatures9,26,47,48,49 (Table S5) with overlapping genes covered in the PIP panel. Full gene lists of the signatures used in the TCGA LUSC dataset are included in Table S5.

Immune signature development

GES genes were identified based on probe/gene performance, co-expression, immune functionality, and the association with PFS in RATIONALE-307 data (Figure S1A). Among the 1392 genes in the PIP panel, to obtain a reliable gene expression signal we first identified genes that had a high concordance across different GEP platforms (defined by Pearson correlation between PIP and RNAseq ≥0.5) from an evaluation of a benchmarking dataset.50 Among the 979 genes identified, those associated with prolonged PFS benefit were selected by fulfillment of three criteria: (1) the gene expression level demonstrates a positive association with survival in the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy arm (PFS HR < 0.9); (2) the gene expression level has no positive association with survival in the chemotherapy alone arm (PFS HR > 1.0); (3) the gene expression level correlates with significant PFS benefit between the tislelizumab plus chemotherapy and chemotherapy alone treatment arms (interaction p < 0.05). Candidate genes were chosen from the top of the gene list, ranked by predictive effect (i.e., interaction p values [smallest to largest], corresponding to the ratio of PFS HR for tislelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in high and low expressors of the gene). The immune gene signature was further refined from the candidate gene set based on immune-related functional annotations (Table S3).

Genomic profiling

Genomic profiling and tumor mutational burden (TMB) were assessed using an OncoScreen Plus panel consisting of 520 cancer-related genes (Burning Rock Biotech, Guangzhou, China P.R.) as previously described.51 Tissue DNA and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) were extracted with a QIAamp DNA FFPE tissue kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and a QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), respectively. DNA was fragmented by a Covaris M220 focused ultrasonicator (Covaris, Inc., Woburn, MA, USA) followed by end repair, phosphorylation, dA addition and adaptor ligation for library construction. Capture-based targeted sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with paired-end reads. Sequenced data were mapped to the human genome (hg19) by using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner 0.7.10 and Genome Analysis Toolkit 3.2 (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA). Variant calling was performed by using VarScan and variants were annotated with ANNOVAR and SnpEff v3.6. Mutations were then filtered against common single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) found in 1000 Genomes ExAC, dbSNP, ESP6500SIV2, and ClinVar databases. Tissue samples with maximum mutation AF (maxAF) ≥ 5% and plasma samples with maxAF ≥0.5% were screened for TMB calculation. TMB count was calculated as the number of mutations count (copy number variants and fusions were excluded) located at the coding region.

The gene lists for 10 oncogenic pathways were curated by comparing previously reported gene lists49 with overlapping genes covered in the OncoScreen Plus panel (Table S5). Previously described variants of unknown significance were excluded.52,53 Tumors with at least one functional mutation in an individual oncogenic pathway were defined as “pathway mutated”.

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed to examine the tumor immune microenvironment. Primary antibodies used in this study include CD3 antibody (clone LN10, ZM0417, RRID:AB_2890105, Zsbio; dilution 1:50), CD4 antibody (clone EP204, ZA0519, Zsbio; dilution 1:100), CD8 antibody (clone SP16, ZA0508, RRID: AB_2890107, Zsbio; dilution 1:100), CD39 antibody (clone EPR20627, ab223842, RRID: AB_2889212, Abcam; dilution 1:1000), and FOXP3 antibody (clone 236A/E7, ab20034, RRID: AB_445284, Abcam; dilution 1:100).

Sections of 4μm thickness were cut from lung biopsy FFPE tissue of squamous NSCLC patients (NCC Cohort 2, n = 19, Table S6). The slides were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated, and washed in tap water before boiling in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9; 643901; Klinipath) for epitope retrieval/microwave treatment (MWT). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked using Antibody (Ab) Diluent/Block (72424205, Akoya). One antigen requires one round of labeling, including primary antibody incubation, secondary antibody incubation, and tyramide signal amplification visualization, followed by labeling of the next antibody. Primary Abs of CD3, CD4, CD8, FOXP3, and CD39 were incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Next, incubation with Opal Ploymer HRP Ms+Rb (2414515; Akoya, Massachusetts, USA) was performed at 37°C for 10 min. The stained slides were scanned using the TissueFACS SL plus S system from Austria (Manufacture: TissueGnostics) and analyzed by Advanced Image Analysis software (StrataQuest V7.0). The entire slide was scanned at low magnification using a 5× objective. The low magnification scan was used to identify the location of the tissue on the slide. A region gate was drawn around the perimeter of the tissue which selects the entire biospecimen for acquisition in multiple sequential tiles at 20× high magnification. The high-magnification image was used for all downstream analyses. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of each marker expression in each stained cell membrane was quantified as previously described.54 Positive cells were defined as cells with true immunofluorescence signal detected (> median MFI of all stained cells in a given slide) and with the appropriate expression pattern. All the settings were saved within an algorithm and pipeline to allow batch analysis of multiple original images of the same tissue in different fluorescent channels.

Analysis of public and other independent datasets

Gene expression data and clinical data for TCGA LUSC were downloaded from the cBioportal.55 The Zhang et al., 2019,45 Motzer et al., 2020,17 and Hwang et al., 202018 datasets were downloaded from the respective online supplemental materials. These datasets were analyzed with the same methodology as described above for the RATIONALE-307 trial dataset. In addition, data from an independent NSCLC cohort from the corresponding authors’ institution that included both RNAseq and anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy efficacy data (referred to as the ‘NCC Cohort 1’ herein, n = 20, Table S4) were analyzed similarly. This additional cohort study was approved by ethics committees of the National Cancer Center Academy of Medical Sciences, and the Peking Union Medical College.

Functional experiments

Reagents and plasmids

Recombinant human interferon gamma (IFN-γ) protein was obtained from ABclonal (RP01038). The coding sequences of human NRF2 wild type (NRF2-WT) or E79K mutant (NRF2-E79K) were cloned into the lentiviral vector pLEX-MCS-CMV-puro (Thermo Scientific Open Biosystems) to generate expression plasmids.

RNA interference

All siRNAs were synthesized by Biotend Company. The siRNA-mediated gene knockdown transfections were performed with Roche X-tremeGENE siRNA Transfection Reagent (SITRAN-RO) at 50 nM final concentration according to the manufacturer’s instructions and cells were harvested 48 h after siRNA transfection. One siRNA against scramble sequence (siScr) was used as negative control, and all oligonucleotide sequences were as follows: siScr: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’; siNRF2-1: 5′-GCAGUUCAAUGAAGCUCAAdTdT-3’; siNRF2-2: 5′-GACAGAAGUUGACAAUUAUdTdT-3’.

Immunoblotting

Cells for immunoblotting analysis were lysed in EBC lysis buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, pH 8.0, 120 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet P-40) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Selleck) and phosphatase inhibitors (Selleck); 30 μg total proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel and blotted with indicated primary antibodies. Primary antibodies used for the immunoblotting analysis were: anti-NRF2 (1:1000; A11159; ABclonal), anti-PD-L1 (1:1000; E1L3N; 13684; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-NQO1 (1:1000; A0047; ABclonal), and anti-α-Tubulin (1:5000; sc-134237; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Secondary antibodies used were swine anti-rabbit Ig HRP antibody (polyclonal; 1:10000; No. P0217; Dako) and rabbit anti-mouse Ig/HRP antibody (polyclonal; 1:10000; No. P0260; Dako). All immunoblotting gel images were obtained with a Minichemi 610 chemiluminescent imager (Sagecreation, Beijing, China) and quantified using ImageJ (Version1.53).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and was reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the ABScript II RT Master Mix for qPCR Kit (ABclonal). The qPCR reactions were run in a Roche LightCycler 96 instrument. All samples were normalized to β-actin mRNA and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used for qPCR analyses were as follows: β-actin forward: 5′-AGAGCTACGAGCTGCCTGAC-3′, reverse: 5′-AGCACTGTGTTGGCGTACAG-3’; NRF2 forward: 5′-GAGAGCCCAGTCTTCATTGC-3′, reverse: 5′-TTGGCTTCTGGACTTGGAAC-3’; PD-L1 forward: 5′-TGGCATTTGCTGAACGCATTT-3′, reverse: 5′-TGCAGCCAGGTCTAATTGTTTT-3’; NQO1 forward: 5′-CCCTGCGAACTTTCAGTATCC-3′, reverse: 5′-CTTTCAGAATGGCAGGGACTC-3’.

Flow cytometry

Approximately 5×106 SK-MES-1 cells were collected and suspended in FACS buffer (1% BSA and 1% sodium azide in PBS) after washed with cold 1 × PBS buffer. The cells were incubated with primary antibody anti-PD-L1 (ab205921) or isotype (ab172730) for 30 min on ice, and then incubated with Goat anti-Rat IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (A-11008) for 20 min on ice in the dark. All samples were fixed with 1% formaldehyde before testing. CytoFlex3 (Beckman Coulter) was used to assay the samples, and data were analyzed with FlowJo software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The promoter sequence of human PD-L1 gene was obtained from the NCBI Gene database (Gene ID: 29126). A 922 bp PD-L1 promoter sequence starting from 872 bp upstream of the PD-L1 transcription start site (TSS) to 50 bp downstream of PD-L1 TSS (−872/+50) was cloned from the genome DNA (gDNA) of A549 cells, and further subcloned into pGL3-Basic Vector (Promega) to generate luciferase reporter plasmid (pGL3-WT). The potential NRF2 binding site sequence (5′-ATGACAAAGCA-3′, −593/–583) was analyzed by the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net), and the original binding site sequence was replaced by 5′-AAAAAAAAAAA-3′ to generate mutated luciferase reporter plasmid (pGL3-Mutant). All transfections for dual-luciferase reporter assay were performed by Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), and pRL-TK Vector (Promega) was used as transfection control. The luciferase activities in cell lysates were measured by GloMax Multi Jr (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

The ChIP assay was performed by using a SimpleChIPPlus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Agarose Beads, #9004, Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Specifically, 1×107 SK-MES-1 cells for each IP were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 mins, and then stopped by 125 mM glycine. The fixed cells were collected by PBS supplied with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (PIC), cross-linked pellets were suspended in ChIP lysis buffer, and digested by micrococcal nuclease for DNA fragments. Two percent of total DNA fragment was used as input. Immunoprecipitation was performed by anti-NRF2 (ab62352; abcam) and anti-rabbit IgG (#2729, Cell Signaling Technology). The chromatin complex was pulled down by A/G agarose beads and incubated in a 65°C vibrator for 30 min to reverse cross-link. The purified DNA was used for qPCR analysis. The enrichment percentage was calculated using the formula: Enrichment percentage = 2% × 2(C[T]Input Sample - C[T]IP Sample), C[T] = Threshold cycle of PCR reaction. An enrichment percentage of a gene desert region in human Chr.19 was used as reference. The primers used for qPCR analysis were as follows: PD-L1 promoter forward: 5′-AAGATGTAGCTCGGGATGGGAAGT-3′, reverse: 5′-GTGTGTGTGTGTGTGTATGGGTGT-3’; PD-L1 exon forward: 5′-TGTGCATGGAGAGGAAGACCTGAA-3′, reverse: 5′-GTACACCCCTGCATCCTGCAATTT-3’; NQO1 promoter forward: 5′-CCAGGACTCTCAGCCTTCCAAATC-3′, reverse: 5′-ATGGAGCAGAAAAAGAGCCGGATG-3’; Chr.19 forward: 5′-TGGGAAAACTCTCCAGGAC-3′, reverse: 5′-CTTTGGTTGCCTGTGCTT-3’.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sample size calculations and statistical considerations for the primary analyses have been reported previously.3 Patients with treatment assignment and any type of biomarker measurement (PD-L1, tTMB, bTMB, or GEP) were included in this retrospective exploratory analysis. The baseline characteristics and efficacy profiles in bTMB evaluable population were comparable to the intention-to-treatment efficacy analysis set.

The clinical data cutoff date for the present analyses was December 9, 2019. The analyses focused on the association of various types of baseline biomarkers with the RATIONALE-307 study primary endpoint of PFS per IRC. Patients were dichotomized into biomarker-high and -low groups based on certain cutoff values. Several cutoff values were used for PD-L1 (1%, 25%, and 50%), tTMB (10, 12, and 14 mut/Mb) and bTMB (6, 8, and 10 mut/Mb). The upper one-third value of NRF2 pathway signature score was used to define NRF2high and NRF2low subgroups, given the mutational frequency of NRF2 pathway was about 35% in LUSC, and a cutoff of 32% was used in a previously reported first-line therapy study in LUSC.10 For further validation, we performed the bimodal distribution of NRF2 signature in the TCGA LUSC dataset and found an upper threshold of 41% to distinguish high from low signature score, chosen as the trough between the two peaks in the bimodal distribution after kernel density estimation (data not shown). Binary forms of other transcriptional biomarkers were defined based on the median values of the evaluable cohort. Median PFS and 95% CI were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method. Associations between biomarkers and PFS were examined using stratified Cox proportional hazards (Cox PH) models with Efron’s method of handling ties. HRs and 95% CIs were calculated. In the Cox PH model, we included one biomarker, treatment assignment and the interaction between biomarker and treatment, which was used to assess if a biomarker was predictive, and the Wald test associated with the interaction term was calculated. Apart from the univariate biomarker analysis, we also explored treatment effects in subgroups defined by two biomarkers. The selection of the dual biomarker analyses was biology driven. In the model, besides stratification factors, we included the two selected biomarkers, treatment and a three-way interaction among those three main effects. In this report, since we would like to describe treatment effect under the subgroups only, no further model selections and simplifications were presented. Forest plots depicted the treatment or biomarker level (high versus low for GEP/signature scores, MUT versus WT for mutation) comparisons results for biomarker-defined sub-cohorts. Kaplan-Meier curves illustrated PFS change over time for biomarker subgroup by treatment, with testing on the rate of survival at risk by two-sided stratified log rank test. PD-L1 expression (<1% versus 1%–49% versus ≥50%) and disease stage (IIIB versus IV) were considered as stratification factors, according to the study design. However, for modeling of PD-L1, only disease stage was included as stratification factor. Unconfirmed objective response rate (ORR) assessed by IRC and two-sided binomial exact 95% CI were calculated. A variety of biomarkers were tested without adjusting for multiple comparisons. Since this is a retrospective exploratory analysis, all p values are descriptive.

For all boxplots, the data distribution was shown through the violin-plot, with the center line representing the median, the box limits representing the upper and lower quartiles, and the whiskers representing 1.5× the interquartile range (outlier points outside of this range shown as part of the boxplot). All comparisons of discrete variables between subgroups (biomarker-high versus -low or MUT versus WT) were performed with the two-sided non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (wilcox. test) or stat_compare_means (method = ’wilcox’) R function. Comparisons of the frequencies of mutational pathways by different subgroups were performed with two-sided Fisher’s exact tests (fisher.test) R function). Statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R 3.6.1 and SAS 9.4.

The results of the statistical analyses, including numbers of patients (N values), standard deviation (SD), standard error of the mean (SEM), 95% CI and p values, are reported where relevant within the manuscript text and figures. Figure footnotes include a description of the statistical tests used.
