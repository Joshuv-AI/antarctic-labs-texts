Biosensors reveal distinct cytosolic pH and redox dynamics across seedlings in response to danger signals and pathogen infection
Authors: Eliana Mor, Kaarthik Ramesh, Harika Bandaru, Silke Robatzek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants perceive danger signals, pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/DAMPs), to activate immune responses such as transient apoplastic alkalinization and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. However, how these pH and redox changes occur across organs and tissues during pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) remains poorly understood. Using genetically encoded biosensors (GEBs), we monitored cytosolic pH and redox dynamics across whole Arabidopsis seedlings with spatiotemporal resolution. Global treatments with diverse danger signals first induced cytosolic acidification and oxidation in roots, followed by petioles and, later, the hypocotyl, revealing organ-specific responsiveness and a bidirectional response gradient. By contrast, Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pto) DC3000 induced sustained cytosolic alkalinization and suppressed redox responses in inoculated shoots, even when co-treated with PAMPs. Mutants lacking either a functional flagellum (ΔfliC) or a Type-3 secretion system (ΔhrcC) induced opposite or no long-term responses in the cytosol, respectively. Together, these findings highlight that the immune response of the plant is not uniform but instead follows organ- and tissue-specific patterns which are trigger-dependent, and reflect distinct capacities of seedling cells to activate PTI.

Introduction

Pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) is a central component of plant innate defence, activated when cell surface-localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) detect pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs or DAMPs) (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Boller and Felix, 2009; Rao et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2020; DeFalco and Zipfel, 2021; Ngou et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2024). PTI triggers a suite of early responses, including ion fluxes, extracellular pH changes, bursts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and transcriptional reprogramming. These transient events occur within minutes of perception and serve to initiate and propagate defence signalling throughout the plant (Ben Khaled et al., 2015).

One of the earliest measurable PTI signatures is apoplastic alkalinization (Thor and Peiter, 2014; Moroz et al., 2017; Thor et al., 2020; Dora et al., 2022). This shift in extracellular pH is closely linked to ion transport across the plasma membrane (PM), in particular the inhibition of PM-localized H+-ATPases (Benschop et al., 2007; Nühse et al., 2007). Upon perception of PAMPs such as flg22 or elf18, immunogenic epitopes from bacterial flagellin and ELONGATION FACTOR-Tu (EF-Tu), respectively, the cognate receptors FLAGELLIN SENSING 2 (FLS2) and EF-Tu RECEPTOR (EFR) interact with the co-receptor BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1)-ASSOCIATED KINASE 1 (BAK1), leading to downstream phosphorylation events (Che et al., 2000; Kunze et al., 2004; Chinchilla et al., 2006, 2007; Zipfel et al., 2006; Schulze et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2020), including phosphorylation of a PM-localized H+-ATPases pump at Ser899 (Nühse et al., 2007). This ultimately inactivates the pump, reduces extracellular proton concentration, and causes rapid apoplastic alkalinization. Concomitantly, the cytosolic pH decreases (acidification), which is believed to influence protein conformation, enzyme activity, and the protonation state of phytohormones, thereby modulating downstream defence processes.

In tomato, binding of the peptide systemin to its receptor kinase SYR1 triggers activation of the protein phosphatase 2C family member POLTERGEIST-LIKE 2 (PLL2), which dephosphorylates the penultimate phosphoresidue of H+-ATPase (AHAs), thereby preventing 14-3-3 protein binding and enabling activation of H+-ATPases (R. Li et al., 2025). By contrast, the Arabidopsis homologues AtPLL4 and AtPLL5 act as negative regulators of PTI. Upon ligand perception, AtPLL4 and AtPLL5 become phosphorylated and dissociate from PRRs to relieve inhibition, indicating a distinct mode of controlling H+-ATPases activity (DeFalco et al., 2022).

RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR (RALF) peptides provide another route to extracellular pH modulation. These small secreted peptides regulate growth, development, and immunity in plants (Zhang et al., 2020). Plant-derived RALFs bind to the receptor-like kinase FERONIA (FER), triggering Ser899 phosphorylation of PM-localized H+-ATPases, thus resulting in pump inactivation and alkalinization (Haruta et al., 2014). RALF-induced alkalinization also involves H+ influx into the cytosol, further shifting pH homeostasis (Gjetting et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). Interestingly, FER is not only a RALF receptor but is also required for optimal assembly of the FLS2–BAK1 signalling-active PRR complex, highlighting a potential role for apoplastic pH and PRR complex signalling.

Changes in apoplastic pH have important consequences for cell wall mechanics and interaction with pathogens. Alkalinization can promote cell wall stiffening (Wan et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022), contributing to physical defence barriers and interference with pathogen virulence strategies. For example, elevated apoplastic pH causes the transcriptional down regulation of the Type-3 secretion system (T3SS), and flagellin and coronatine biosynthesis genes in Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pto) DC3000 (Yang et al., 2024). By contrast, acidification can loosen cell walls (Kesten et al., 2019) and induce stomatal opening, facilitating cell expansion but also increasing vulnerability to pathogen ingress.

Many plant-associated microbes modulate host pH to favour colonization. Microbes may secrete pH-modifying compounds or enzymes, such as organic acids or alkalinizing peptides including RALF-like peptides, to create conditions favourable for infection (Blackburn et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). For example, Pseudomonas syringae pv. phaseolicola (Pph)-induced apoplastic alkalinization is critical for lesion formation in Phaseolus vulgaris (Geilfus et al., 2020). Conversely, fungi such as Fusarium oxysporum, Botrytis cinerea, and Colletotrichum spp. acidify the apoplast to activate cell wall-degrading enzymes and enhance tissue maceration (Prusky and Yakoby, 2003; Kesten et al., 2019). In the interaction between barley and the beneficial fungus Piriformospora (or Serendipita) indica, rapid local apoplastic acidification is followed by systemic pH changes in leaves, reflecting activation of PM-localized H+-ATPases in both local and distal tissues (Felle et al., 2009). Similarly, rhizosphere-associated Pseudomonas can lower root extracellular pH through organic acid secretion, promoting root colonization (Yu et al., 2019).

Apoplastic pH changes are tightly connected to ROS production in plant immunity (Z. Li et al., 2025). Alkalinization favours the activity of type III peroxidases (PRXs), which generate hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the apoplast (Bolwell et al., 2002; Castro et al., 2021). PRR activation also stimulates NADPH oxidases, notably the respiratory burst oxidase homologs (RBOHs) RBOHD and RBOHF, which produce ROS as part of the oxidative burst (Castro et al., 2021). These enzymes are directly phosphorylated by BOTRYTIS-INDUCED KINASE 1 (BIK1) following PRR complex activation. Apoplastic ROS contribute to elevated cytosolic ROS levels via transport facilitated by the aquaporin PIP2;1 (Waszczak et al., 2018).

Extracellular ROS production by PRXs depends on elevated apoplastic pH (Bolwell et al., 2002), and activation of PM H+-ATPases suppresses the ROS burst in response to flg22 (Keinath et al., 2010). RBOHF also contributes to PAMP-induced apoplastic alkalinization and overall ROS accumulation in Arabidopsis guard cells, and is involved in dampening PAMP-induced cytosolic oxidation (Arnaud et al., 2023). Pathogen effectors can exploit this pH–ROS relationship. For example, Fusarium oxysporum secretes a RALF-like peptide to induce ROS production in tomato and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Thynne et al., 2017), while Ralstonia solanacearum exo-polygalacturonase PehC triggers extracellular alkalinization and ROS in tomato roots (Ke et al., 2023).

Beyond local responses, pH and ROS participate in systemic signalling. Long-distance defence communication involves coordinated waves of ROS, free calcium (Ca2+), electrical signals, and hydraulic changes that propagate through the vasculature and from cell to cell (Tian et al., 2020). DAMPs such as oligogalacturonides (OGs) and Glu can trigger defence responses via GLutamate Receptor-Like (GLR)-type ion channels, altering cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations. Glu in particular may act as a key signal for long-distance wound propagation, although systemic Ca2+ increases are not observed after OG or flg22 application (Toyota et al., 2018). These interconnected signalling modalities enable rapid communication between distant organs to coordinate whole-plant responses to local stress.

Despite extensive characterization of local and systemic pH and ROS dynamics, how these signals integrate into plant-wide patterns remains poorly understood. In particular, the spatial and temporal signatures of these responses across the entire plant, their initiation sites, and response gradients, have not yet been sufficiently described for a range of danger signals (Gust et al., 2017). To address this, we developed a quantitative imaging approach in Arabidopsis using the established genetically-encoded pUBQ10::pHGFP and p35S::roGFP2-Orp1 reporter lines to monitor, respectively, cytosolic pH and oxidation in seedlings (Kesten et al., 2019; Nietzel et al., 2019). This enabled visualization of temporal cytosolic acidification and oxidation patterns across seedlings in response to diverse danger signals, as well as the modulation or local induction of these responses by pathogenic bacteria with different lifestyles, providing new insights into the capacity of seedling tissues to mount PTI.

Materials and methods

Arabidopsis lines and seedling growth

The following GEBs were used to perform the experiments: (i) pUBQ10::pHGFP (Fendrych et al., 2014), a ubiquitously expressed pH-sensitive green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorophore that enables non-invasive monitoring of cytosolic pH through ratiometric analysis of protonation-dependent excitation shifts; and (ii) p35S::roGFP2-Orp1 (Nietzel et al., 2019), a constitutively expressed fluorescent redox reporter in which H2O2-dependent oxidation of the Orp1 peroxidase domain is reversibly transferred to roGFP2, altering its fluorescence properties and allowing ratiometric quantification of cytosolic thiol redox status and oxidative signalling in vivo. Seeds were surface-sterilized with multiple washes in 70% ethanol and one final rinse in pure ethanol. Seeds were stratified on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium, 10 mM MES monohydrate, and 1% agar for 2 d at 4 °C in the dark. Plates were exposed in a phytochamber to long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) at 22 °C during the day and 18 °C at night.

Elicitor preparation and treatment

A solution of 100 µM of elicitors (elf18, flg22, and OGs) dissolved in double-distilled water (ddH2O) was prepared and stored at −20 °C. After thawing the solution, the seedlings were subjected to global treatment or drop-dispense of the solution.

Bacterial growth and treatment

Pto DC3000 and its mutant ΔhrcC (Yuan and He, 1996) were grown on LB agar with 1% agar and 50 µg ml–1 rifampicin; the mutant ΔfliC (Parys et al., 2021) was grown on LB medium with 50 µg ml–1 rifampicin and 25 µg ml–1 chloramphenicol. After 2 d on solid medium at 28 °C, colonies of Pto DC3000 were resuspended in ddH2O for short-term experiments (minutes); for long-term experiments (days), all the strains were resuspended in filtered 10 mM MgCl2 to OD600=0.2. For short-term treatments (minutes), the suspension was added directly onto the seedlings on the slide. For long-term experiments, 10 µl of the suspension was applied directly onto the seedlings on the plate.

Imaging and ratiometric analysis

Seedlings were placed on the microscopy slide, where a microscopy chamber was created using several layers of tape as spacers on both sides to avoid compression of the seedling. For short-term experiments (0–120 min), global treatments of the seedlings were done directly on the slide and immediately imaged using the Leica DMi8 Thunder Imager equipped with a QUANTUM high speed stage, a Leica DFC9000 GT camera, LED5 laser, and objective ×20/0.40 Dry Universal. For long-term experiments (1, 3, and 5 d), a 10 µl drop of the treatment was applied directly on the shoot of the seedlings, followed by 10 min incubation, before transferring the seedlings onto new 1/2 MS agar plates for the following days. The seedlings were transferred on the slide at the reported time points, and 0.1% agarose medium was used to reduce shifting of the seedlings during imaging. For both biosensors, the fluorophores were sequentially excited at 390 nm and then at 475 nm; emission was collected at 535 nm for both excitation wavelengths. Laser intensity was kept at 40%. Autofluorescence was captured at excitation/emission 390 nm/460 nm, with laser intensity at 20%.

For each sample and time point, the composite images of three channels were processed using the Leica LAS-X software (version: 3.9.1.28433) and upload to ImageJ2 (version 1.54f) (Schindelin et al., 2012), the signal from autofluorescence was subtracted in both channels (390 nm, 475 nm) using the ImageCalculator plugin, and the mean grey value of a selected region of interest (ROI) was measured. Emission intensities were separately collected at 390 nm excitation, and after 475 nm excitation, respectively, for both pUBQ10::pHGFP and p35S::roGFP2-Orp1. Their ratio (I390 nm/I475 nm), or its natural logarithm (LN), was calculated to derive whether the tissues were overall relatively oxidized or alkalinized, which were visualized as heatmaps or box plots, respectively. Statistical significance was assessed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons using R (version 2025.05.0+496). For all the experiments, three independent biological replicates were performed; the total sample number is indicated in the figure legends. For representative microscopy images, merged raw-channel images are displayed using the Rainbow LUT to visualize intensity differences.

Gene expression analysis (quantitative reverse transcription–PCR)

Total RNA of seedling was extracted using the Trizol–chloroform protocol and treated using Turbo DNase (Invitrogen, AM2238). For cDNA synthesis, SuperScript III RT™ (Invitrogen) was used. AtEEF (5′-CCA AGG GTG AAA GCA AGA AGA-3′ forward and 5′-CCA AGG GTG AAA GCA AGA AGA-3′ reverse primers) and AtCDKA (5′-ATT GCG TAT TGC CAC TCT CAT AGG-3′ forward and 5′-TCC TGA CAG GGA TAC CGA ATG C-3′ reverse primers) were selected as reference genes. Primers for AtFRK1 were 5′-ATC TTC GCT TGG AGC TTC TC-3′ forward and 5′-TGC AGC GCA AGG ACT AGA G-3′ reverse. Expression values were analysed using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), significance was assessed by two-sided Student’s t-test (*P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001). Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Bacterial growth analysis of Pto DC3000

Five-day-old Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings (Col-0) inoculated with bacterial suspension applied on the shoot were harvested at 1, 3, and 5 days post-inoculation (dpi). Roots and shoots were separated and surface-sterilized in 70% ethanol for 10 min and then rinsed in ddH2O. The weight of the samples was measured to normalize the bacterial growth to the fresh weight of the plant sample. The collected samples were lysed with a tissue lyser in 100 µl of 10 mM MgCl2. A 10 µl aliquot of a serial dilution from 10−1 to 10−5 was dropped on LB plates with 1% agar and the appropriate antibiotics, and grown overnight at 28 °C. Individual colonies were counted at different dilutions to determine bacterial colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of fresh weight. For all the experiments, three independent biological replicates were performed.

Results

Global exposure of seedlings to danger signals induces pH and redox response gradients from roots and petioles towards hypocotyls

To monitor specific spatiotemporal signatures of cytosolic pH and oxidation in whole plants following global exposure to different danger signals, 5-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were used. Arabidopsis seedlings are widely employed as a model for plant immune responses and, at this young stage, they are well suited for fluorescence imaging. Seedlings were placed in a custom-made chamber on the slide to avoid seedling compression, and submerged in water with elf18 or flg22 at saturating concentrations of 100 µM. Samples were imaged at 10, 30, 60, and 120 min after elicitor addition using an LED-based microscope, enabling extended time-lapse imaging of whole seedlings with minimal impact on plant physiology and biosensor fluorophore stability (Fig. 1A). Measurements were calculated as the ratio between the fluorescence intensities collected at different excitations (I390/I475). Increased ratio values indicate alkalinization or oxidation, based on the used biosensors, pUBQ10::pHGFP or p35S::roGFP2-Orp1, respectively. Consistent with previously described response times, we observed that treatments with danger signals induced cytosolic acidification and oxidation within 10–120 min (Fig. 1B, C), thereby validating our experimental setup (Zipfel et al., 2006; Denoux et al., 2008; Beck et al., 2014). The complete set of measurements obtained in all experiments is given in Supplementary Dataset S1.

Each danger signal treatment elicited distinct stimulus-specific spatiotemporal patterns. After global incubation of seedlings with the bacterial-derived elicitors flg22 or elf18, we observed an early and strong cytosolic acidification in the root; no difference was detected in the hypocotyl, and responses in petioles were only detected at 120 min. Using the redox sensor, we observed an early oxidation in roots at 10 min, followed by petioles at 60 min, and the hypocotyl after 120 min incubation (Fig. 1B, C). These observations suggest that, although the whole seedling is exposed simultaneously to the treatment, the responses follow a bidirectional gradient, triggered first in the tissues at the edges, the root and petioles, and converging towards the hypocotyl (Fig. 1B).

To investigate whether the nature of the elicitor could be causing this bidirectional gradient, we used the same setup and biosensors to monitor the responses after treatment with OGs, a DAMP released upon pathogen-induced pectin degradation (Ferrari, 2013; Degli Esposti et al., 2025). OGs induced responses in the cytosol starting at 30 min (Fig. 2A). Compared with the response pattern of the tested PAMPs (Fig. 1), the onset of the responses was delayed but followed the same dynamics, with the acidification and oxidation first detected in the root and then in cotyledons and petioles, and later detected in the hypocotyl (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Video S1). Across all danger signals (elf18, flg22, and OGs), roots exhibited the earliest responses (10–30 min), followed by petioles (30–60 min) and, later, converging towards the hypocotyls (120 min) (Fig. 2C). Among the tested elicitors, flg22 induced the strongest pH and redox responses, exceeding those triggered by elf18 or OGs (Figs 1, 2; Supplementary Video S1), aligning with earlier reports (Zipfel et al., 2006; Denoux et al., 2008; Beck et al., 2014). Although the temporal resolution did not allow for a full description of the kinetics, these observations indicate that, despite global treatments, seedlings exhibited heterogeneous responses, with organ-specific pH and redox dynamics that initiated in the root tip and cotyledons, moving along the petioles and elongation zone of the root towards the hypocotyl, and appeared to develop as a bidirectional temporal gradient along the seedling (Fig. 2C). Importantly, this pattern was independent of the type of danger signal, whether bacterial or plant derived, suggesting a common spatiotemporal response to elicitors across whole seedlings.

Pto DC3000 induces sustained cytosolic alkalinization and suppresses redox responses in inoculated shoots

The typical flg22 and elf18 peptides are derived from sequences of Pseudomonas aeruginosa flagellin and Agrobacterium tumefaciens EF-Tu, respectively (Felix et al., 1999; Kunze et al., 2004). Pto DC3000 carries both immunogenic epitopes, with Pto-flg22 being highly immunogenic in A. thaliana, whereas Pto-elf18 triggers immune responses to a lesser extent (Zipfel et al., 2004, 2006; Boller and Felix, 2009; Gimenez-Ibanez and Rathjen, 2010). We next addressed the spatiotemporal dynamics of seedlings treated with the leaf apoplastic bacterial pathogen Pto DC3000. A commonly used inoculum density in infection assays (Zipfel et al., 2004) was directly applied to seedlings on the slide, and incubated for a few minutes before imaging over 10, 30, 60, and 120 min (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, this treatment induced only mild acidification in petioles and no oxidation over the recorded time period (Fig. 3B–D). Expression of the early defence gene FRK1 increased significantly after 30 min of Pto DC3000 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that transcriptional defence responses were nonetheless activated.

To further investigate whether Pto DC3000 modulates the cytosolic pH and redox immune responses of the plant, we performed a co-treatment with Pto DC3000 and 100 µM flg22, a concentration which induces cytosolic acidification and oxidation (Figs 1, 3A). Surprisingly, co-treated petioles and hypocotyls exhibited only mild cytosolic alkalinization of the petioles and no oxidation (Fig. 3B–D), suggesting that the presence of the pathogen suppressed the flg22-induced cytosolic responses. This is unlikely to result from the Pto-flg22 of the bacterium, since this epitope is highly immunogenic in A. thaliana and would otherwise trigger strong immune activation rather than suppression (Navarro et al., 2004; Bjornson et al., 2021).

Given that Pto DC3000 propagates in planta over several days, we also monitored pH and redox dynamics at 1, 3, and 5 dpi (Fig. 4A). At 3 d, shoots (cotyledons, petioles, and hypocotyls) exhibited pronounced cytosolic alkalinization (but no oxidation), while roots showed a mild oxidation compared with mock samples (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that Pto DC3000, a bacterial pathogen sensitive to alkaline pH (Yang et al., 2024), establishes and maintains a sustained acidic apoplastic environment, reflected by cytosolic alkalinization from 30 min to 3 dpi. This is consistent with activation of PM H+-ATPases, potentially mediated by effectors such as AvrB, which interacts with the host immune regulator RIN4, a known positive regulator of the PM H+-ATPases AHA1 and AHA2 (Liu et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2015). During the early stages of infection, it suppresses cytosolic oxidation, which becomes less pronounced at later time points.

Two key virulence determinants of Pto DC3000 are flagellum-mediated motility, which facilitates leaf invasion, and T3SS-secreted effectors that suppress PTI (Ramos et al., 2004; Boller and Felix, 2009; Kvitko et al., 2009; Lindeberg et al., 2012; Xin and He, 2013; Xin et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2018; Parys et al., 2021). We therefore investigated whether the sustained alkalinization and suppressed oxidation responses in the cytosol were affected by loss of motility or effector secretion, using the flagellum-deficient ΔfliC and the T3SS-deficient ΔhrcC mutants (Gimenez-Ibanez et al., 2018; Parys et al., 2021). Shoots were inoculated with wild-type or mutant bacteria at OD600=0.2 and imaged at 1, 3, and 5 dpi (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the ΔfliC mutant induced cytosolic acidification and oxidation of the shoot at 3 dpi compared with wild-type Pto DC3000 (Fig. 4B, C). Notably, the T3SS-deficient ΔhrcC mutant showed a weak PTI activation at 1 dpi, similar to the wild type, including in organs not directly inoculated, such as the root. However, this response was lost at 3 d and 5 d, consistent with the limited infection rate of ΔhrcC in planta (Fig. 4B, C; Supplementary Fig. S2). By contrast, the motility-deficient ΔfliC mutant still triggered cytosolic responses at 3 d and 5 d, which was mostly restricted to the directly inoculated tissues (cotyledons and petioles), reflecting its inability to move from the inoculation site (Supplementary Fig. S2).

These response patterns align with bacterial colonization success, namely higher in the shoot than in the root for all strains (Supplementary Fig. S2). Wild type Pto DC3000 bacterial numbers steadily increased in shoots up to 3d, before reaching a plateau. ΔfliC bacteria proliferated only in shoots at a lower rate compared with the wild type, and ΔhrcC proliferated less than the other strains and reached a growth plateau 1 dpi. Overall, long-term Pto DC3000 infection resulted in a sustained local alkalinization of the cytosol of the shoot, while short-term responses were weak and could even suppress those triggered by exogenous application of flg22. By contrast, ΔfliC caused acidification and oxidation of the cytosol, suggesting that despite lacking a flagellum, the strain still remains capable of triggering PTI responses but appears unable to suppress them.

Discussion

In plants, danger signals elicit both local and long-distance response patterns, including coordinated waves of self-propagating bursts of ROS, Ca2+, electrical signals, and hydraulic changes (Mittler et al., 2011; Farmer et al., 2014; Tian et al., 2016, 2020; Castro et al., 2021). Consistent with these patterns and recent single-cell transcriptome studies (Zhu et al., 2023; Nobori et al., 2025), our work revealed that PAMP- and DAMP-induced cytosolic acidification and oxidation were not uniformly initiated across organs but in limited cells in the root tip and elongation zone, then in petiole cells, and subsequently in the hypocotyl, establishing a bidirectional temporal response gradient across the whole seedling (Figs 1, 2; Supplementary Video S1). This response gradient might not represent a long-distance signalling wave, as seedlings were subjected to global treatments. Previous studies have found that neither OGs nor flg22 induces systemic cytosolic Ca2+ increases (Toyota et al., 2018), suggesting that systemic signals may rely on sensitized Ca2+ receptor modules rather than stronger Ca2+ signals (Jelenska et al., 2017; Furch et al., 2025; Köster et al., 2025).

It is likely that the initial responding cells are intrinsically more competent for PAMP or DAMP perception, similar to the recently described PRIMER cells, from which immune signals can propagate (Nobori et al., 2025). The enhanced immune competence may derive from elevated PRR expression (Beck et al., 2014), potentially influenced by developmental programmes that preserve meristematic activity (Emonet et al., 2021). For example, during early seedling development, FLS2 expression is suppressed because low levels of miR172b allow accumulation of the transcriptional repressors TARGET OF EAT1 and 2 (TOE1/2), which directly inhibit the FLS2 promoter (Zou et al., 2018). As seedlings mature, increasing miR172b levels diminish TOE1/2 abundance, thereby relieving repression and promoting FLS2 transcription. Alternatively, the elevated responsiveness of these cells could be associated with larger average cell size, as recently suggested (Zhang et al., 2025). However, it is unlikely that the bidirectional gradient described in our study would be correlated to the native or induced expression of the respective receptor genes, FLS2 and EFR. Indeed, FLS2 expression is reported in the shoot of Arabidopsis after flg22 treatment, and only very low expression is detected in the lateral root cap and mesophyll leaf without a trigger (Wendrich et al., 2020; Tenorio Berrío et al., 2025). Similarly, EFR is expressed in cotyledons with or without application of elicitors, while expression is not reported in other tissues, such as the hypocotyl and root, without a trigger (Gómez-Gómez and Boller, 2000; Zipfel et al., 2004; Wendrich et al., 2020). This would suggest that the observed bidirectional signal is transmitted to other tissues despite the expression pattern and the abundance of the receptors, indicating independence from the perception site. Further research is needed to address whether the delayed response in hypocotyl cells is released upon systemic signalling mechanisms such as membrane depolarization, Ca2+ gradients, or the propagation of apoplastic ROS, or involves the activation of phytocytokines (Miller et al., 2009; Mousavi et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2026).

Apoplastic pH can differently affect bacterial infection success. In Pph, apoplastic alkalinization promotes nutrient availability and bacterial colonization in bean (Geilfus et al., 2020). By contrast, in Pto DC3000, elevated apoplastic pH down-regulates transcription of T3SS genes as well as flagellin and coronatine biosynthesis (Yang et al., 2024), linking bacterial virulence to the inhibition of apoplastic alkalinization. Providing evidence for the ability of Pto DC3000 to suppress immunity, simultaneous global exposure of seedlings to Pto DC3000 and flg22 did not induce detectable responses within 10–120 min (Fig. 3). The suppression of redox responses at early stages further underscores the ability of the pathogen to dampen the host oxidative defences (Fig. 3B, C; Supplementary Fig. S3) (Lindeberg et al., 2012; Xin and He, 2013), for example through the release of catalases that can detoxify the apoplastic ROS accumulation (Guo et al., 2012). Unexpectedly, inoculation with the ΔhrcC mutant did not induce significant changes in apoplastic pH or redox status (Fig. 4), despite its defect in immune suppression (Huang et al., 1992; Roine et al., 1997; Mammarella et al., 2015; Lal et al., 2020), which aligns with its reduced bacterial proliferation compared with wild type Pto DC3000 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Given that Pto DC3000 employs additional strategies to dampen host immunity, such as the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs) (Janda et al., 2023), it is plausible that the ΔhrcC mutant retains some ability to suppress the pathogen-induced ROS burst and pH changes and to establish in the host, albeit with reduced virulence and proliferation success. Indeed, catalases released in EVs can detoxify ROS (Deng et al., 2025). Similar to rhizosphere-associated Pseudomonas, Pto DC3000 may lower extracellular pH through organic acid secretion (Yu et al., 2019), independent of the T3SS.

By contrast, the ΔfliC mutant induced cytosolic acidification and oxidation in the shoot 3 dpi (Fig. 4), despite its immune-suppressive capabilities and lacking a prominent immunogenic trigger (Zeng and He, 2010). However, although its immune-suppressive activity enables it to reach high bacterial loads (Supplementary Fig. S2), its non-motility prevents the bacterium from escaping the initial infection site (Misas-Villamil et al., 2011). This suggests that the ability of the mutant to suppress immunity may be offset by the confinement of bacteria at the infection site, which could eventually trigger localized immune responses. Consistent with this notion, transcriptomic analyses have shown that Pto DC3000 progressively reprograms its transcriptional landscape during infection to adapt to and manipulate the host environment (Lovelace et al., 2018). This dynamic transcriptional reprogramming is not observed in PTI-induced plants, in which Pto DC3000 exhibits a largely static transcriptional profile that is indicative of a failure to effectively remodel the host niche (Lovelace et al., 2018). In this context, altered regulation of bacterial virulence programmes during localized immune activation may contribute to the induced pH and redox responses observed for the ΔfliC mutant.

Collectively, our results reveal organ- and tissue-specific, trigger-dependent immune patterns (Supplementary Fig. S3), highlighting distinct immune competencies of seedling cells to initiate PTI. This work also highlights the power of live imaging with GEBs to reveal the dynamic patterns of plant immune responses, capturing how cellular and systemic signalling unfolds during interactions with danger signals and pathogens.
