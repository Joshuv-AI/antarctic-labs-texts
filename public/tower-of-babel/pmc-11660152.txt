Programming Surface Motility and Modulating Physiological Behaviors of Bacteria via Biosurfactant-Mimetic Polyurethanes
Authors: Zixi Chen, Apoorva Vishwakarma, Abraham Joy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Modulating microbial motility and physiology can enhance the production of bacterial macromolecules and small molecules. Herein, a platform of water-soluble and amphiphilic peptidomimetic polyurethanes is reported as a means of regulating bacterial surface behavior and the concomitant production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). It is demonstrated that carboxyl (−COOH)-containing polyurethanes exhibited 17-fold and 80-fold enhancements in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) swarming and twitching areas, respectively. Conversely, an amine (−NH2)-functionalized polyurethane reduces the P. aeruginosa swarming area by 58%. Similar influences on the surface motility of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and a nonswarming P. aeruginosa mutant strain are also observed. Notably, −COOH polyurethanes completely wet the agar hydrogel surface and promote bacterial surface proliferation, resulting in enhanced EPS and rhamnolipid production. The programming of bacterial spatial migration into designed patterns is achieved by leveraging the opposing influences of −NH2 and −COOH polyurethanes. The results highlight the potential of this synthetic polyurethane platform and potentially other polymer systems as an exciting approach to control bacterial surface behaviors and influence the production of engineered living materials.

Introduction

Bacteria, as living materials, have been exploited in diverse biotechnology applications, ranging from pollutant purification,1,2 eco-friendly biofuel production,3,4 and biobrick manufacturing,5 to healthcare.6,7 Modulating the life cycle of living systems offers an approach for designing advanced biomaterials for disease prevention,8,9 therapeutics,10,11 biomanufacturing of chemicals,12,13 and biocomputing.14 Genetic engineering of bacterial pathways has been the most common approach in such applications.15 For instance, genetically engineered Escherichia coli (E. coli) Nissle 1917, developed by Joshi and co-workers,16 expressing curli fibrous matrices of self-assembled CsgA proteins, can promote mucosal healing in situ. Pseudomonas fluorescens HK 44 was genetically engineered and used for bioremediation of polyaromatic hydrocarbon pollutants such as naphthalene.17 Other approaches have taken advantage of the ability of bacterial communities to sense and respond to external stimuli, including chemical signals,18,19 physical pressure,20 nutrient accessibility,21,22 surface conditions,23,24 and signal communications, such as those mediated via secondary messengers25,26 or quorum sensing.27 These instinctive properties endow bacteria with survival benefits and can be exploited to design engineered living materials (ELMs) tailored to specific needs.

Bacteria-surface interactions are found to affect bacterial surface behaviors by inducing changes in gene expression, resulting in altered cell morphology and surface adhesion.28 Consequently, the interactions trigger bacterial species to switch between a sessile state of dormant existence within biofilms and a motile state seen as surface motility. Biofilms, characterized as a single or a mixed bacterial species protected in a 3D-structured network of self-secreted extracellular polymeric substances (EPS),29 are key factors associated with antibiotic resistance30 and chronic infections.31 Alternatively, motivated by their adaptive response for recolonization, nutrient acquisition, and avoidance of harmful environments, bacteria migrate on surfaces and colonize new niches through various mechanisms.32 Modulating bacterial surface motility has been demonstrated through diverse extracellular approaches.33,34 For instance, the upregulation of biosurfactant rhamnolipid production during the initial surface migration of P. aeruginosa led to an increase in its surface twitching movement and deconstruction of its biofilm in an iron-deficient state.35 Organizing the movement of Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) through microfluidic patterns resulted in the formation of a dynamic biofilm.36 In most reported studies, bacterial surface motility could only be influenced unidirectionally, either through complete promotion or restriction,34 and in some cases, the desired outcomes were achieved through relatively complicated gene modifications.37,38 In contrast to genetic strategies, external chemical stimuli offer immediate and flexible control over bacterial behaviors. This control can be achieved via tuning concentration and chemical compositions. Although the effects of chemical stimuli may be less pronounced than those of genetic approaches, they are easier to apply to diverse bacterial strains. Controlling bacteria’s surface behaviors, including controlling surface motility and surface physiological activities, can have a role in biomanufacturing and is potentially a method for inhibiting bacterial colonization.

To this end, the use of synthetic polymers has the potential to be a straightforward and cost-effective counterpart to genetic approaches to modulate bacterial behavior. Here, we report the effects of a water-soluble and amphiphilic polyurethane platform with various pendant functional groups (−OH, −COOH, and −NH2) on bacterial surface behaviors. Our study indicated that −COOH-containing polyurethanes, when incorporated into agar hydrogel, significantly promoted multispectrum bacterial motility with a concomitant increase in EPS production of P. aeruginosa, while −NH2-containing polyurethane dramatically inhibited bacterial surface motility. Furthermore, we demonstrate that such −COOH polyurethanes rescue the swarming ability of P. aeruginosa that is deficient in rhamnolipid production. The increased production of EPS equates to an increase in protein and carbohydrate production. We also show that one can take advantage of the opposite behaviors of various functional groups on bacterial motility to create spatially programmed bacterial designs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that demonstrates the influence of polymer functional groups on modulating bacterial spatial movement and downstream effects on EPS production. This study outlines a framework for the design of polymeric approaches for controlling the motility and EPS production of bacterial communities through external chemical cues provided by polymers.

Polymer Synthesis

All peptidomimetic monomers (mLys,39 mSer,40 mAsp,41 and mC1541) described in this work were synthesized as described in our previous publications. Polymer P1-OH (Scheme S1) was synthesized using solution polymerization with mSer monomer (514.9 mg, 1.6854 mmol, 9 equiv), mC15 monomer (64.3 mg, 0.1873 mmol, 1 equiv), and hexamethylene diisocyanate (314.98 mg, 1.8727 mmol, 10 equiv) in a round-bottom flask (RBF) with CH2Cl2 (5 mL) as a solvent and tin(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (100 μL) as a catalyst in a N2 environment for 12 h. Postpolymerization, the reaction mixture was dialyzed against methanol for 24 h and then concentrated using a rotary evaporator and dried under reduced pressure to yield pure polymer. Postpolymerization, the resultant polymer (724 mg, 81.0% yield) was deprotected using 4 N HCl in dioxane (3 mL) in CH2Cl2 (6 mL) for 12 h in an RBF equipped with a stir bar. The deprotected polymer was dialyzed in methanol for 24 h to remove TBDMS and then dried under reduced pressure to yield P1-OH (yield = 70.5%, Mn = 6918 g/mol; Mw = 10 253 g/mol; PDI = 1.482).

Polymer P2-COOH (Scheme S2) was obtained via postpolymerization functionalization of P1-OH. In brief, to P1-OH (342.1 mg, 0.8506 mmol OH, 1 equiv) dissolved in a mixture of CH2Cl2 (5 mL) and DMF (2 mL) in an RBF equipped with a stir bar, succinic anhydride (127.7 mg, 1.2759 mmol, 1.5 equiv), DMAP (41.6 mg, 0.3402 mmol, 0.4 equiv), and TEA (86.1 mg, 0.8506 mmol, 1 equiv) were added and stirred overnight at room temperature. The resulting polymer was dialyzed against methanol for 24 h to yield pure P2-COOH, which was analyzed using 1H NMR (yield = 68.8%). As illustrated in Figures S1 and S2, after ring-opening polymerization, the hydroxyl group proton of P1-OH on the NMR spectrum completely disappeared, while a new peak emerged at approximately 12.2 ppm, corresponding to carboxylic acid groups. These spectral changes indicated that the modification percentage of P2-COOH was 100%.

For P3-COOH (Scheme S3), monomers mC15 (39.7 mg, 0.116 mmol, 0.5 equiv) and mAsp (648.5 mg, 2.2 mmol, 9.5 equiv) were dissolved in CH2Cl2 (10 mL) in an RBF equipped with a stir bar. To this mixture, hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI, 388.8 mg, 2.31 mmol, 10 equiv) and Tin(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (100 μL) were added. The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 12 h under a N2 atmosphere. The resulting polymer was purified via dialysis in methanol, concentrated, and dried under reduced pressure (yield = 83.3%, Mn = 7546 g/mol; Mw = 13 913 g/mol; PDI = 1.844).

Postpolymerization, the carboxylic acid functionalization in the polymer was deprotected via Pd/C-catalyzed hydrogenation to remove the benzyl-protecting group. To the polymer (549.7 mg) solution in a 1:1 mixture of methanol and CH2Cl2 (15 mL) in a reaction vessel, Pd/C (110 mg) was added. The reaction continued for 4 days at room temperature in a H2 atmosphere until complete deprotection was achieved, which was confirmed by 1H NMR. The reaction mixture was then filtered via a Celite plug to remove the Pd/C, and the resulting polymer was dried under reduced pressure to yield P3-COOH (yield = 40.7%). The peaks corresponding to the protons of the benzyl-protection group at approximately 5.0 and 7.0 ppm, shown in Figure S3, completely disappeared, indicating a 100% modification efficiency.

For the synthesis of P4-NH2 (Scheme S4), in an RBF equipped with a stir bar, monomers mLys (1000.0 mg, 3.141 mmol, 9.5 equiv) and C15 (56.8 mg, 0.165 mmol, 0.5 equiv) were dissolved in CH2Cl2 (20 mL). To this mixture, tin(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (100 μL) was added to catalyze the reaction along with hexamethylene diisocyanate (556.1 mg, 3.306 mmol, 10 equiv). The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 12 h in a N2 environment, and the resulting Boc-protected polymer was quenched by adding methanol (3 mL) and further purified by dialysis in methanol for 24 h. After dialysis, the polymer was concentrated and dried under reduced pressure (yield = 98%, Mn = 11 761 g/mol, Mw = 26 236 g/mol, and PDI = 2.231).

To deprotect the pendant Boc-protected amine group, the resulting polymer (1517.3 mg) was dissolved in CH2Cl2 (12 mL), reacted with 4 N HCl in dioxane (6 mL) at room temperature for 45 min, and then exposed to reduced pressure overnight. The polymer was once again dialyzed in methanol for 24 h, concentrated to remove methanol, and lyophilized to obtain a pure white solid (yield = 99%).

Measurement of Bacterial Growth in the Presence of Polymers

To determine the growth curve of P. aeruginosa PAO1 in the presence of polymers, an overnight culture of bacteria was used to inoculate BM2MM (Basal Medium 2 minimum medium) + 0.5% casamino acids (3 mL) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 3 h until the culture reached the mid-log phase. The culture was further diluted in BM2MM + 0.5% casamino acids to yield a working stock of ∼106 CFU/mL. A volume of 200 μL of the bacterial stock was then added to each well of a 96-well plate with the desired concentration (100 μg/mL) of polymers and controls (from a 20 mg/mL DMSO stock solution). The well plate was covered with a transparent lid and incubated inside a plate reader at 37 °C with 180 rpm shaking. The OD600 was measured every 10 min for 16 h. Growth curves were plotted as OD600 over time for each polymer and control. Each experiment was done in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Swarming and Twitching Motility Assays on Agar Plates

Swarming assays with P. aeruginosa PAO1 were performed on BM2MM swarming agar42 plates (62 mM pH 7 potassium phosphate buffer, 0.5 mM MgSO4, 10 μM FeSO4, 0.4% (w/v) glucose, 0.25% (w/v) casamino acids, 0.5% (w/v) agar) in the presence or absence of different concentrations of polymers and controls. The polymers and Triton X-100 stocks were prepared as 20 mg/mL solutions in DMSO, due to the limited solubility of P1-OH in water and growth media. The autoclaved agar solution was cooled to 65 °C in a water bath, and 20 mg/mL of sterile-filtered polymer solution in DMSO was added to the agar solution to make a final concentration of 100 μg/mL for polymers and Triton X-100. BM2 swarm agar plates were made by pipetting 30 mL of the polymer-agar mixture into 100 mm Petri dishes, which were then cooled to room temperature for 1 h in a laminar flow hood. Then, 1 μL of a mid-log phase culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 grown in BM2 minimal medium (BM2MM) that diluted to an OD600 of ∼ 0.1 was used to inoculate the agar surface at the center of each Petri dish, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. All resulting circular swarms were imaged with a Nikon D3500 camera at a fixed distance, and the resulting images were analyzed by measuring the swarm area using ImageJ. For swarming experiments with PAO1-ΔrhlA-gfp, appropriate antibiotic selection (20 μg/mL gentamycin) was used. The E. coli K12 swarming experiment was performed similarly, except on LB agar substituted with 0.5% glucose and 0.5% agar, and incubated for 36 h before image acquisition. Since polymers were dissolved in DMSO, the equivalent volume of pure DMSO (0.5% v/v) was used as a negative control for the experiments. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Time-lapse experiments of PAO1 swarming were recorded using a Nikon D5300 camera equipped with an intervalometer and an AF-S Micro NIKKOR 60 mm f/2.8G ED lens with the same preparation and incubation conditions described above. Images were acquired by the camera every 10 min for 21 h (Figure S5).

For twitching motility assays, 30 mL of LB supplemented with 1% (w/v) agar and the 100 μg/mL polymers or DMSO were poured into 100 mm Petri dishes and allowed to solidify at room temperature for 40 min. A mid-log phase culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 grown in BM2MM and diluted to an OD600 of ∼ 0.1 was used to stab through the agar to the plastic interface using an inoculation needle. The agar plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. After incubation, the agar was gently removed using a pair of tweezers to expose the twitching zone at the agar-plastic interface. The twitching zone was stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min and then imaged using a Nikon D3500 camera. The images were analyzed by quantifying the twitching area using ImageJ. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Time-Lapse Measurements of Spatially Programming Swarming Bacterial Motility

Swarming time-lapse experiments were performed with P. aeruginosa PAO1 on BM2 swarm agar plates. Images were acquired using a Nikon D5300 camera equipped with an intervalometer and an AF-S Micro NIKKOR 60 mm f/2.8G ED lens. A commercial USB-powered 10″ ring LED light equipped with a diffuser was placed under the agar plate and the camera such that it was outside the field of view. Images were acquired by the camera every 5 min for 20 h for P. aeruginosa PAO1 swarming. The acquired images were then processed using Adobe Photoshop to render time-lapse videos.

For creating an agar surface with two different polymers to direct and control the swarming of bacteria, sterile polycarbonate cutouts (5 mm wide and 10 mm high) in the shape of “U’ and ‘A” were used. The shapes were sterilized using 70% ethanol and allowed to dry inside a biosafety cabinet. Then, these cutouts were placed inside a 100 mm sterile Petri dish. BM2 swarm agar mixed with 100 μg/mL of P4-NH2 (25 mL) at 65 °C was poured on the outside of the cutouts such that the cutouts were level with the agar surface (this was visually determined). The agar was allowed to cool at room temperature for 30 min, after which the cutouts were carefully detached from the solid agar by running a scalpel along the edge slowly. The cutouts were carefully removed from the agar to get “blank spaces” in the agar gel, into which BM2 swarm agar + 100 μg/mL of P2-COOH was carefully pipetted (∼3 mL) such that the height or level of the agar was visually the same inside and outside the “blank space”. The agar inside was also allowed to cool at room temperature for 30 min before inoculating with 1 μL of ∼108 CFU/mL bacteria at the determined spots. This plate was then imaged using the time-lapse setup described above. To eliminate limitations and guidance on the bacterial surface swarming brought by potential discrepancies between the inside and outside of the “U” and “A” regions, a similar experiment was carried out, except that BM2 agar media (an equivalent volume of DMSO was used) were introduced into both the “U” and “A” outside and inside regions. The swarming time-lapse experiments of E. coli K12 were performed similarly, except that LB media containing 0.5% glucose and 0.5% agar were employed, and images were captured every 15 min for 30 h.

Surface Tension and Surface Energy of Polymer Solution, Water Contact Angle Measurements on Agar Surfaces

The surface tension of aqueous polymer solutions at different concentrations was determined by using a Ramé-Hart contact angle goniometer, along with DROPimage software. Surface tension was determined via the pendant drop method, and measurements were performed using polymers P1–P4 and the control, Triton X-100, dissolved in 10 mM phosphate buffer to buffer the pH of the solutions. Surface tension was reported as mean ± standard deviation of a minimum of five measurements and plotted against concentration. Data were fit using a first-order exponential decay function in Origin software.

To determine the water contact angle on polymer-agar surfaces, BM2 swarm agar with 100 μg/mL of polymers or controls (Triton X-100 and DMSO) was prepared as described in the section above. Ten mL of agar solution was poured into a 75 mm × 25 mm × 5 mm rectangular mold with detachable sides and allowed to solidify at room temperature for 30 min. Ten μL of DI water was carefully brought into contact with the agar surface, and the contact angle was measured on the goniometer. The water contact angle was reported as mean ± standard deviation of a minimum of five measurements and plotted against time.

Measurement of Zeta Potential of P. aeruginosa Cells in the Presence of Polymers

An overnight culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 was grown in TSB (6 mL) at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) until the culture reached the mid-log phase. The culture was then centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min at 25 °C to separate the TSB from bacteria. The bacterial pellet was washed twice in pH 7.0, 5 mM HEPES-buffered saline (HBS) and diluted to ∼0.1 OD600 to make a bacterial suspension in HBS. P1–P4 solutions in 5 mM HBS were quickly mixed with the bacterial suspension in HBS to obtain a final concentration of 100 μg/mL, gently shaken, and added to a disposable folded Zetasizer capillary cell (DTS 1070, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK) using a 1 mL syringe to measure electrophoretic mobility (25 °C, 150 V) in Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK) using the Helmholtz–Smoluchowski equation. Measurements were performed in triplicate, and the data are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Shear Modulus Measurement of BM2MM Swarming Agar in the Presence of 100 μg/mL Polymer/Controls

BM2MM swarming agar was prepared in the presence of 100 μg/mL polymer or controls (Triton X-100 and DMSO) as specified in the section above. This experiment was carried out in a TA rheometer (ARES G2) set at a system temperature of 37 °C with airflow. Basically, each tested agar sample was cut by a 25 mm punch and transferred onto 25 mm parallel stainless-steel plates. A thin layer of silicone oil was applied to the sides of the agar to avoid water evaporation during the measurement. After the system reached temperature equilibrium for 60 s, oscillation amplitude tests were then performed to measure the shear modulus of the agar under a 0.5% strain and 100.0 to 0.1 rad/s angular frequency setting. The shear modulus of each polymer agar sample was obtained by averaging at least three repeatable measurements.

Extraction of Extracellular Polymeric Substance (EPS) from P. aeruginosa PAO1 Swarmed Surface Growth

The experiment was carried out right after the bacterial swarming measurement described in 2.5. Basically, after 16h of swarming, the slime surface growth on each polymer-containing agar plate was carefully scraped by a sterile spatula and transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes. In each centrifuge tube, the slime surface growth was suspended in sterile saline (a volume ratio of 1:25). After which, a chelating agent, ferric ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), was introduced into the suspension medium to a final concentration of 1 mM and stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 1 h for releasing cell-associated EPS. The culture was then centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C to separate the saline solution with EPS from bacteria. After which, the cell-free supernatant was then precipitated via the addition of 95% ethanol dropwise, and the precipitation was repeated three times, followed by recovering the formed precipitate via centrifugation at 1000×g for 30 min. Dialysis of the crude product was performed for further purification against DI H2O overnight, and water was removed on a lyophilizer (LABCONCO, 2.5L SORVAL freeze-dryer). Product weight was then measured, and the EPS yield from the polymer-treated agar surface was plotted.

Compositions Ratio Quantification of Proteins and Polysaccharides of Extracted EPS

The experiment was carried out right after the bacterial swarming measurement described in 2.5 and 2.11. The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay was employed to determine the weight percentage of total proteins in the extracted EPS product. A series of bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard ampule solutions were prepared, covering a concentration range from 0.5 to 200 μg/mL and serving as a set of protein standards. Afterward, the BCA working reagent (WR) was made by mixing reagents MA, MB, and MC in a 25:24:1 volume ratio, which was followed by adding 150 μL of each standard and EPS sample solution (400 μg/mL) to 150 μL of WR in a 96-well plate. Then, the plate was covered and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h before cooling to room temperature. The signal absorptions were recorded at 562 nm on a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. The weight percentages of proteins were calculated based on the plotted BSA standard curve.

A modified phenol-sulfuric acid measurement43 was used to quantify the polysaccharide composition ratio in the extracted EPS product. EPS products were dissolved in ultrapure water at a concentration of 2 mg/mL, and D(+)-glucose was used to prepare a set of carbohydrate standard solutions, which ranged from 62 μg/mL to 4 mg/mL. Followed by the introduction of 30 μL of 5% (w/v) aqueous phenol solution, 50 μL of EPS sample solution or glucose standard solution was mixed well with 150 μL of 97% sulfuric acid in a 96-well plate. The plate was then heated in a 90 °C water bath for 5 min before cooling down to room temperature. The signal absorptions were recorded at 490 nm on a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. The weight concentration of polysaccharides was calculated based on the plotted glucose standard curve. Both experiments were carried out at least in triplicate.

Extraction of Rhamnolipid from P. aeruginosa PAO1 Swarmed Surface Growth

Rhamnolipid extraction (Scheme S5) was carried out similarly to EPS isolation. After having obtained the cell-free supernatant after 20,000 g centrifugation, the supernatant was adjusted to pH = 2 using 1 N HCl to reduce the rhamnolipid solubility in saline and kept at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, the concentrated supernatant was transferred to a 60 mL separatory funnel to carry out extraction at room temperature with a chloroform/methanol mixture (2:1, v/v) with equal volume. The precipitated white solid was then collected and dried through a rotary evaporator under high vacuum. The yield was then measured by weighing the rhamnolipid product.

Swarming of P. aeruginosa PAO1 in the Presence of Extracted Rhamnolipids

The experiments were carried out similarly to the procedure described in sec2.3. Instead of adding polymers to the swarming agar medium, 100 μg/mL of isolated rhamnolipid was introduced and uniformly mixed with the BM2MM agar medium. Afterward, 1 μL of a mid-log phase culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 grown in BM2 minimal medium (BM2MM) and diluted to OD600 ∼ 0.1 was used to inoculate the agar surface at the center of each Petri dish, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. All images were then taken through a Nikon D3500 camera at a fixed distance and processed using ImageJ.

CFU (Colony-Forming Unit) Counting of Swarmed P. aeruginosa PAO1

The experiment was carried out right after the bacterial swarming measurement described in 2.5. Basically, after 16h of swarming, the slime surface growth on each polymer-contained agar plate was carefully scraped by a sterile spatula and transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes. In each centrifuge tube, the slime surface growth was suspended in 10 mL of sterile saline (a volume ratio of 1:25) and vigorously stirred via a vortex. After that, the bacterial suspension stock was serially diluted, and 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was then applied to a 1/2 TSA agar plate surface and uniformly spread around using 10–15 sterile glass beads of Ø2.5 mm, followed by a 20 h incubation. Each concentration of bacterial culture was carried out in triplicate. The CFU on each agar plate was then counted. Colony numbers on agar plates between 30 and 300 were considered reliable results for the final CFU calculation.

Results and Discussion

Polyurethane Platform Design

Inspired by the amphiphilic structures and surface motility modulation effects of natural bacterial biosurfactants,24,44 we designed water-soluble compositions of low molecular weight polyurethanes. This was achieved through condensation polymerization of hexamethylene diisocyanate and N-functionalized diethanolamine-based diols. The diols were designed with varying polar or charged hydrophilic pendant groups and a hydrophobic 15-carbon alkane pendant group to tailor the amphiphilicity of the polymers (Figure 1A). Increasing the composition of the hydrophobic pendant group in the polymer above 5% led to a drastic decrease in solubility; hence, a ratio of 95/5 for hydrophilic/hydrophobic pendant groups was chosen for all functionalized polymers in subsequent experiments. All functional monomers mentioned in this work were synthesized as previously reported.39−41,45 The synthesis routes are depicted in Schemes S1–S4. Chemical compositions and polymer structures were confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S1–S4). The molecular weights of the polymers before deprotection were maintained within the range of 7–11 kDa, as determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC), to increase solubility and avoid potential toxicity.42

Modulating Bacterial Surface Motility

As the polymers were designed to be biosurfactant mimics, which influence bacterial surface motility,44,46 we examined the influence of polymers on bacterial surface swarming and twitching motility. To assess their effects, we measured the surface motility behavior of motile bacteria P. aeruginosa PAO1 in the presence of different polymers on semisolid agar surfaces. To ensure homogeneous mixing of polymers with bacterial growth media, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a carrier solvent for all microbiological measurements, with concentrations limited to a 0.5% volume ratio, a level at which DMSO shows negligible effects on microbial growth and behaviors.47,48 During a 16h swarming period, the migration of bacteria in the control group exhibited a radial outward movement from the incubation site in a circular pattern, covering an average area of 314 mm2 (Figures 1B and S5). Striking influences on bacterial swarming areas were observed in the presence of synthesized polymers and the commercial surfactant Triton X-100. We found that P2-COOH and P3-COOH promoted the bacterial swarming area by 17-fold and 16-fold, respectively. Meanwhile, the swarming area in the Triton X-100 group increased by 12-fold, aligning with observations in reported studies.24,45 Conversely, P4-NH2 inhibited swarming, resulting in a 58% decrease in the motility area. In our earlier studies,42,45 we demonstrated that polyurethanes containing positively charged amines exhibit notable antibacterial and antibiofilm properties against PAO1. In this study, the polyurethanes used are not effective antibacterials due to the low amount (5%) of cationic amines (−NH3+) in the total composition of the polymer.

To evaluate whether the polyurethane polymer platform has a broad-spectrum effect on bacterial surface movement, we examined the swarming behavior of E. coli K12. As shown in Figure 1C, the positively charged P4-NH2 inhibited swarming after a 36 h incubation. Unexpectedly, Triton also limited E. coli swarming, a surprising observation given reports suggesting that self-produced or exogenously added surfactants generally strengthen swarming motility.44,49 In comparison, polyurethanes with −COOH groups had a large impact on promoting swarming areas by up to 13-fold, demonstrating a unique effect compared to Triton. To validate if the significant increase in swarming by P2-COOH and P3-COOH is attributed to biosurfactant-mimetic properties, we examined the effect of these polymers on a nonswarming mutant, P. aeruginosa PAO1-ΔrhlA.50 This mutant lacks the genes necessary for generating the precursors for rhamnolipid biosurfactants.51 Our data (Figure 1D) indicate that −COOH-containing polyurethanes compensate for the lack of rhamnolipid production and promote swarming of the rhlA mutant, PAO1-ΔrhlA. We also observed that P4-NH2 did not have a significant impact on the swarming of the rhlA mutant. Meanwhile, Triton induced PAO1-ΔrhlA to rapidly move across the agar surface. It is interesting that the rhlA mutant did not swarm as significantly as the PAO1 wild-type strain with the assistance of P2-COOH and P3-COOH. This discrepancy highlights that there are other effects of polymers that extend beyond altering the surface properties of agar hydrogels. In brief, these observations imply a broad-spectrum effect of these polymer compositions on bacterial surface motility behaviors.

The influence of polymers on bacterial twitching behavior, which occurs in the interstitial space between the agar and solid Petri dish,42,46 was also examined. Significant differences in the twitching area of P. aeruginosa between polymer groups were noticed (Figure 1E). P1-OH and P4-NH2 moderately increased the twitching area, while substantial enhancements were observed in the P2-COOH and P3-COOH groups, with almost 80-fold and 70-fold increases, respectively.

Surface-Active and Modulus-Altering Properties of Polymers

The observations of various effects of polymers on modulating bacterial surface motility suggested that we investigate the mechanisms based on surface-active properties. We initiated the investigation by measuring water surface tension changes in the presence of gradient concentrations of polymers (Figure 2A). The experiments were carried out in a 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7), wherein the polymers contain −COO− and -NH3+ groups. A decrease in water surface tension with increasing polymer concentration was observed. However, at the bacterial surface motility-modulating concentrations (<125 μg/mL), we found no differences in surface tension. At 125 μg/mL, the largest decrease in the surface tension of water (γP3-COOH = 64.5 ± 0.3 mN/m) was observed for P3-COOH, whereas Triton X-100 lowered it to γTriton X-100 = 35.7 ± 0.4 mN/m. This indicates that the polymers do not significantly affect the surface tension in aqueous environments compared to Triton X-100.

Considering that bacterial surface motility takes place on semisolid agar interfaces, we further tested the influence of polymers on the wetting behavior of agar hydrogel surfaces. The polymers were evenly introduced into the agar medium, and the water contact angles over 120 s after droplet contact were measured on the agar hydrogels. Compared to the control BM2 swarm agar, we observed almost complete wetting of the agar surfaces by the incident water droplet in the presence of P2-COOH, P3-COOH, and Triton X-100 (Figure 2B,C). The addition of P1-OH or P4-NH2 to the agar also led to significant decreases in the water contact angles, although they did not result in complete wetting. However, this decrease of the contact angle in the presence of P1-OH and P4-NH2 did not result in a corresponding increase in bacterial swarming, suggesting a multiparameter effect that results in the observed exponential swarming. The differences in surface free energy at the polymer-agar and air interfaces among the tested groups, as illustrated in Figure 2D, were minor. Hence, the efficient wetting of agar surfaces as a result of mixing the polyurethanes in the agar is an important factor for the increased swarming area but is not the main or only reason for the increased swarming area.

In addition to surface properties, the matrix modulus of agar also plays a role in modulating initial bacterial attachment and then guiding bacterial motility.52,53 Therefore, the storage modulus of the swarm agar hydrogel in the presence of polymers was quantified via oscillatory shear measurements. The results in Figure 2E showed a slight increase in the storage modulus with an increase in angular frequency. Except for P4-NH2, polymers at 100 μg/mL showed minor effects on the agar hydrogel modulus compared to the storage modulus of the control group (356.6 ± 16.9 Pa at 100 rad/s). At the same frequency, due to the electrostatic forces between the positively charged P4-NH2 polymer and the negatively charged agar, the storage modulus was enhanced to 429.8 ± 21.6 Pa, which aligns with the reported literature.54 Hence, our result demonstrated that the significant differences in bacterial surface motility and inhibition among the various polymers are most probably not due to the differences in agar moduli.

Influences on Bacterial Physiological Behaviors

Subsequently, we focused on understanding the effect of polymer functional groups on the physiological behaviors of P. aeruginosa PAO1, as this strain has been extensively used for studies that examine its behavior in both motile and sessile states. Figure 3A shows the impact of polymers on bacterial growth in an aqueous medium. P. aeruginosa ceased to grow when the swarming medium contained 100 μg/mL of P4-NH2. This was due to the bacterial toxicity of the cationic groups of P4-NH2.39,45 The overall effect of P4-NH2 was predominated by its cationic charge, which emphasized that the inhibitory effect of P4-NH2 on bacterial growth is the primary parameter determining the reduction in the swarming area. Comparatively, P2-COOH and P3-COOH did not affect bacterial growth, while P1-OH showed a retardation in growth. Triton also exhibited retardation effects on bacterial growth, which is in line with the literature data.55 It should be noted that the concentration of bacterial inoculum and the effective local polymer concentration near bacteria are different for the growth and swarming assays. Hence, a direct comparison of polymer concentrations between these two experiments should not be made.

The interactions between bacteria and interfaces influence bacterial surface behavior through cellular signal sensing.56 Thus, a bacterial zeta-potential assay (Figure 3B) was carried out to measure the physiochemical interaction between the polyionic structure of polymers and the net negatively charged bacterial cell membrane. While untreated bacteria cells showed a potential of −25.3 ± 0.9 mV, the relatively neutral and ionic P1-OH, as well as negatively charged P2-COOH and P3-COOH, did not significantly change the bacterial membrane surface potential. In contrast, the positively charged P4-NH2 modified the zeta potential for cells to +19.3 ± 1 mV. These data underscore the antibacterial properties of P4-NH2 resulting from the strong affinity between polymers and bacterial cell membranes.

The second messenger cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP) plays a key role in the bacterial lifestyle, with high c-di-GMP levels aligning with biofilm formation and low c-di-GMP levels promoting biofilm dispersal and bacterial motility.25 To test if enhanced bacterial surface motility was regulated by c-di-GMP, a fluorescence-based reporter PAO1-pCdrA-gfp developed by Rybtke et al.57 was employed to monitor the green fluorescent protein (GFP) intensity, representing the proportional cellular level of c-di-GMP (see details in SI). The OD600 intensity of bacterial growth was employed here to normalize the secreted GFP between polymer samples. The normalized c-di-GMP of the control group started to increase after 6–8 h of incubation and reached the peak after 12 h, suggesting that a stationary state of bacterial cell growth was reached, aligning with the cell growth results (Figures 3C and S6). A significantly higher c-di-GMP expression level was observed in the P4-NH2 polymer, as the antibacterial properties create a stressful environment for the bacteria, resulting in the inhibition of swarming. The negatively charged P2-COOH and P3-COOH polymers induced a minor decline in c-di-GMP levels, compared to the control, indicating that the difference in c-di-GMP may only have a minor role in the observed surface mobility induced by these polymers. On the other hand, Triton reduced the expression of c-di-GMP peak levels, and it may be possible that this decrease is correlated to the increase in surface mobility on Triton-containing swarm agar surfaces.

We further examined bacterial proliferation behavior on agar surfaces during swarming via bacteria colony-forming unit (CFU) counting assays. As depicted in Figure 3D, the control exhibited an average CFU value of 2.46 ± 0.31 × 109 on the swarmed agar surface. In comparison, the CFU values of the P2-COOH and P3-COOH groups were two and one orders of magnitude higher, respectively. Triton enhanced bacterial surface proliferation, although not as effectively as the −COOH-containing polymers. Also, as indicated in Figure S8, the ratio of CFU on individual swarmed agar surfaces to their respective swarming areas was calculated. Polymer P2-COOH and P3-COOH significantly enhanced bacterial proliferation, with P2-COOH inducing more than a 6-fold increase in CFU compared with the control, while P3-COOH led to a 177% increase. The observed increase in CFU values can be attributed to the increased bacterial regeneration rate when bacteria move across −COOH-containing agar surfaces, allowing newborn bacteria to fully access the nutrients contained in the agar. Interestingly, the small molecule Triton only induced a much smaller increase of 110%. Consistent with our observations on cell growth assays, fewer bacteria were found on the P4-NH2-containing swarmed agar, and P1-OH showed limited influence. It is worth noting that the trend in CFU values among samples closely resembled the trend in the P. aeruginosa swarming area. Overall, our findings suggest that enhanced swarming motility in the presence of −COOH-containing polymers results from the synergistic effects of their wetting ability, the impact of promoting proliferation, and the moderate decrease of c-di-GMP expression levels.

Bacterial Surface Metabolic Products

Microbial EPS generally comprise polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and extracellular DNA,29 which are produced during swarming.58,59 As an eco-friendly, nontoxic product, EPS are used as food additives for their emulsification, water retention, and stabilizing properties.60,61 Moreover, the EPS serve as bioflocculant materials for biological wastewater purification and as organometallic associators for soil aggregation and plant–soil adhesion in environmental sustainability applications.62,63 Additionally, flagellin, an essential subunit protein of flagella secreted by E. coli , can activate Toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5) signaling, followed by triggering pro-inflammatory responses through increased IL-8 production, and therefore serves as a vaccine adjuvant.68 Here, we investigated whether enhanced swarming and bacterial proliferation increase EPS production. Given that P. aeruginosa swarming was most enhanced, we characterized its EPS production as described below. The EPS extraction workflow is illustrated in Figure 4A. Since no universal EPS extraction method exists for quantitative EPS characterization, a combination of physical (centrifugation) and chemical extraction (EDTA chelation and ethanol precipitation) methods was employed to obtain the maximum amount of EPS yield in this study. The white fibrous EPS products were weighed and plotted (Figure 4B). We found that swarming on agar containing −COOH polymers produced more than three times the EPS yield compared with the control agar, and Triton also elevated EPS production. Subsequently, the protein and polysaccharide composition ratios (weight percentage) in the extracted EPS samples were quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay and phenol-sulfuric acid measurement, respectively. Figure 4C,D suggests very limited weight percentage differences of proteins and polysaccharides between the polymers and the control. Thus, it can be concluded that P2-COOH and P3-COOH polyurethanes increased bacterial EPS production on agar surfaces without altering their EPS composition. Considering the nontoxic nature of such polymers, these results highlight the potential of these synthetic P2-COOH or P3-COOH polyurethanes as an alternative method for bacterial EPS production.

Biosurfactant rhamnolipid aids P. aeruginosa swarming, and it is also a biodegradable microbial product and has been employed as an antimicrobial against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and for the removal of metal pollutants.64,65 Since the −COOH polyurethanes effectively promoted bacterial swarming, the swarming slimes from both the control and P3-COOH-treated agar surfaces were collected for the extraction of rhamnolipids (Scheme S5). As illustrated in Figure S7, the yield of the rhamnolipid product from the P3-COOH group was more than 6-fold higher than that from the control group. Furthermore, to test if the extracted rhamnolipids accelerated P. aeruginosa to swarm faster, a swarming assay akin to the one discussed previously was conducted, where rhamnolipid extractions were introduced to the swarm agar at a concentration of 100 μg/mL. Comparable effects on promoting bacterial swarming were observed with the rhamnolipid extracted from the previously swarmed control agar plates (Figure 4F) or the −COOH polymer (Figure 4G) containing agar plates and subsequently added into the agar for swarming. No significant difference was found on the agar plate without the addition of rhamnolipid (Figure 4E). This suggests that the extracted rhamnolipid from the P3-COOH group has biosurfactant properties. These findings demonstrate the potential of synthetic −COOH-containing polyurethane in enhancing the efficiency of rhamnolipid production.

Programming Bacterial Motility Patterns

Next, we sought to explore the potential of functionalized polyurethanes to spatially control the movement of bacterial swarms. Recently, several studies demonstrated the spatial modulation of bacterial surface motility through genetic engineering,38 physical barriers,36,66 or light activation.67 However, studies that explore programming living bacterial surface motility patterns via synthetic polymers at a macro scale are scarce. In this study, we combined the inhibitory effect of P4-NH2 and the promoting effect of P2-COOH to explore selective bacterial migration on agar containing P2-COOH or P4-NH2 to generate desired bacterial patterns. Notably, in comparison to the small molecule Triton, these polymers stand out due to their high molecular weight, low diffusion coefficients, and charged functional groups. As demonstrated in Figure 5A, the medium agar contained P2-COOH within the “U” and “A” zones and P4-COOH outside the “U” and “A” patterns (see details in SI). Consistent with our hypothesis, P. aeruginosa swarmed along the created pattern during the 20 h incubation period. The bacterial movement started from the bottom side of the “A” zone and moved through the triangular zone containing the P4-NH2 inhibitor. They were able to actively diverge and move forward, avoiding the swarming inhibitor regions (Figure 5B and Movie S1). For the control group (Figure 5C and Movie S2), using agar that did not contain any polymers, outward bacterial movement in a circular pattern was observed. These observations point to the successful bacterial surface movement programming by the synthetic polyurethane platform. Similarly, identical experiments were carried out for E. coli swarming pattern programming, and we found that E. coli swarmed slowly but were also directed by the designed pattern (Figure 5D and Movie S3), while E. coli on the control agar surface (Figure 5E and Movie S4) swarmed outward in the form of tendrils from the inoculation points. Considering that bacterial swarming only happens on the agar-air interfaces, our synthetic polyurethanes can modulate bacterial surface motility at a very limited concentration. Moreover, the ease of this approach of utilizing a combination of different polymers with various functional groups to program bacterial swarming patterns opens a window to control living systems through simpler methods than the widely used synthetic biology approaches.

Conclusions

A platform of pendant-functionalized polyurethanes, inspired by biosurfactants, has been successfully used to demonstrate striking effects on the surface motility of P. aeruginosa and E. coli. Specifically, polyurethanes with −COOH groups significantly promoted the bacterial movement area by more than 17-fold, while the −NH2-containing polyurethane inhibited bacterial motility. Polyurethanes with −COOH groups enabled complete agar hydrogel wetting, encouraged bacterial proliferation on agar surfaces, and moderately reduced the c-di-GMP levels without altering bacterial cell growth and surface tension in an aqueous environment. These synergistic effects led to the exponential increase in bacterial surface movement. Moreover, our study demonstrated around four times higher bacterial metabolic EPS yields in the presence of −COOH-containing polyurethanes, without changing the EPS composition ratio. Leveraging the opposing effects of polyurethanes with −NH2 and −COOH groups, we provided an effective means to spatially program the swarming motility of P. aeruginosa and E. coli into desired patterns on semisolid agar surfaces. Therefore, this synthetic polyurethane platform exhibits a novel approach as a chemical scaffold for controlling bacterial mobility and enhancing the production of engineered living materials and bioactive molecules.
