Design, synthesis, characterization, biological investigation and docking studies of newly synthesized sulphonyl hydrazide and their derivatives
Authors: Muhammad Rahman, Rehman Zafar, Muhammad Saeed Jan, Hany W Darwish, Humayoon Khattak, Michael Aschner, Maria Daglia, Haroon Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerous medications, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids, are available to treat inflammation. However, due to their side effects, the search for new anti-inflammatory drugs remains a major research focus. The current study addresses the synthesis, characterization of sulphonyl hydrazide, followed by in vitro and in silico analyses. Sulphonyl hydrazide compounds (R1–R5) were synthesized and characterized using physicochemical and spectroscopic methods, followed by anti-inflammatory evaluation and in silico approaches. The COX-2 and 5-LOX pathways were used for anti-inflammatory potential of compounds. The test compounds (R1–R5) showed significant activity (P < 0.05) against various in vitro enzymes. The compound R3 exhibited remarkable potency in inhibiting both COX-2 and 5-LOX, at various concentrations with IC50 values of 0.84 µM and 0.46 µM, respectively. The compounds were also evaluated for their in vitro cytotoxicity in theHek293 cell line using the MTT assay to study. The synthesized compounds were further explored for in vivo anti-inflammatory potential, followed by an acute toxicity study. The compound R3 led to decreased paw edema from the 1st to 5th hour after carrageenan injection. All other compounds also showed reasonable to moderate anti-inflammatory potential. To address the compounds’ mechanisms of action, they were evaluated against various phlogistic mediators, including histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins, to confirm the anti-inflammatory pathway of the most potent synthesized compound. Their binding strategies were identified using molecular docking assays, which involved examining the interaction between the compounds and the amino acid residues in the binding pockets of the enzymes. Again, compound R3showedstrong binding affinity with the targeted receptors. The findings demonstrated that synthesized sulphonyl hydrazide complexes have significant efficacy in mitigating inflammation. Taken together, the new sulphonyl hydrazide compounds (R1–R5) elicited potential COX-2 and 5-LOX inhibition in vitro that was markedly augmented by molecular docking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23187-5.Keywords: Sulphonyl hydrazide, Synthesis/characterization, COX-2/5-LOX inhibition, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Inflammation is a complex biological process that is associated with the perception of pain and involves the increase in blood vessel permeability, alterations in cell membranes, and a rise in protein denaturation1,2. Inflammation can occur in normal cells or tissue when exposed to microbial, chemical, or physical agents, which can lead to injury3. Tissue inflammation arises from the body’s reaction to damage caused by certain foreign chemicals4. Neutrophils, when properly activated, convert arachidonic acid into leukotrienes via the 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) pathway, and into prostaglandins and thromboxane A₂ via the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) pathway5. The cell membrane contains phospholipids that supply arachidonic acid. This arachidonic acid is subsequently converted into prostaglandins and leukotrienes by the COX-2 or 5-LOX pathways. COX-2 and 5-LOX enzyme inhibitors can hinder the synthesis of prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Consequently, molecules that inhibit these enzymes can reduce adverse gastrointestinal effects while exerting anti-inflammatory and analgesic benefits6,7.

Organotin compounds have been recognized as a crucial group in chemistry due to their presence in key molecules such as alkaloids, peptides, natural chemicals, protein building blocks, and other agents8,9. Organotin compounds are gaining attention for their distinctive structural characteristics, which make them highly suitable for use as antimicrobial, antifungal, antimalarial, and anti-inflammatory medicines10. The structural characterization and pharmacological evaluation of hydrazide groups, in conjunction with metal complexes such as iron, nickel, and cobalt, have been of great interest11,12. These metal-based compounds have attracted a lot of interest because they act as antimitotic and anticancer agents13. The number of moieties attached to the central atom has a major impact on the properties and function of the organotin complex14,15. Orally administered metal complexes exert significant toxicity and neurotoxicity; however, increasing the length of the carbon chain often reduces their toxic effects, likely due to decreased bioavailability or altered metabolic pathways16,17. Resistance developed by microbes against antimicrobial agents poses a hurdle for the treatment of patients, especially those who are immunocompromised. Some derivatives have been prepared by the researchers to kill such microbes18. A new series of hydrazide compounds, i.e., 1, 2-benzisothiazole hydrazides with cyclic and acyclic structures, has also been synthesized and proven to exert antimicrobial activity19. Some derivatives of hydrazides are also highly active against the Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Several derivatives of hydrazides have been synthesized, consisting of heterocyclic substituted rings, and have been screened for anti-inflammatory activity using the paw edema assay in rats to test their efficacy20. These derivatives include the N’-((1 H-indol-3-yl) methylene) benzene sulfonohydrazide, 2- [2-(5-Methyl-2-benzoxazolinone-3 yl) acetyl] 4 chlorobenzylidene hydrazine and 2- [2-(5-Methyl-2-benzoxazolinone-3-yl) acetyl]-4- methoxybenzylidene hydrazine. Recently new cis- Tran’s derivatives of hydrazides have been synthesized and found to possess anti-inflammatory and anti-microbial activity.

Taking into consideration all of the information available on metal complexes, we have previously investigated the cytotoxic and anti-leishmanial activity of organotin complexes of N’-(2, 4-dinitrophenyl)-4-methylphenylsulphonylhydrazide21. This article addresses the synthesis of five new complexes and characterization, followed by investigation against COX-I, COX-2, and 5-LOX enzymes coupled with in silico approaches.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The reagents and chemicals used in this study were of high purity, free from contaminants or impurities. We purchased the following substances from Sigma Aldrich Ltd., USA: benzene, copper sulphate, ethyl acetate, ferric chloride, zinc acetate, p-toluene sulphonyl chloride, 2, 4-dinitrophenyl hydrazine, antimony trichloride, acetaldehyde, acetone, chloroform, hexane, methanol, and trimethylamine. Cyclooxygenase (COX-2) was acquired from human recombinant (Catalog Number C0858), COX-1 from sheep (CAS no: C0733), and lipoxygenase (5-LOX) from Human Recombinant (Catalog No. 437996) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Germany. Carrageenan, Histamine, Arachidonic acid, Prostaglandin E2, and Leukotrienes were procured from Sigma-Aldrich Co. LLC, USA. The solvents used in this investigation, which included acetone, glacial acetic acid, methanol, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and chloroform, were acquired from Merck, Germany. These solvents were dehydrated before use. The electrothermal melting point devices from Gallen Kamp were used to determine the melting points of the synthesized compounds. Utilizing a spectrophotometer with a wavelength range of 4000 to 400 cm− 1, the functional groups included in the synthesized molecules are examined and identified. Room temperature 1H spectra were obtained using the Bruker Advanced Digital 300 MHz NMR spectrometer from Switzerland. As the internal reference, DMSO was used, exhibiting chemical shifts of δ 1H (DMSO) = 2.5.

Enzyme assays

Anti-cyclooxygenase assay (COX-I/II)

A standard previously reported procedure22 was followed in performing the COX-I/II inhibitory test. A 300 U/ml concentration of the COX-I/II enzyme solution was created. For activation, a 50 µl cofactor solution containing 0.9 mM glutathione, 0.1 mM hematin, and 0.24 mM TMPD (N, N, N, N-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride) in 0.1 M Tris HCl buffer with a pH of 8.0 was added to the enzyme solution (10 µl) and refrigerated on ice for five minutes. After that, 20 µl of test samples with concentrations ranging from 125 to 3.91 µg/ml and 60 µl of enzyme solution were incubated for five minutes at room temperature. Similarly, 30 mM of arachidonic acid was added to start the reaction (20 µl). The reaction mixture was incubated for five minutes, and then a UV-visible spectrophotometer was used to measure the absorbance at 570 nm. The absorbance value per unit time was used to calculate the percentage of COX-2 inhibition. By plotting the inhibition vs. sample solution concentrations, the IC50 values (µM) were determined. As positive controls in the current study, celecoxib and indomethacin were used. Three different times were used to conduct the experiment.

5-Lipoxygenase (5-LOX) inhibitory assay

A previously described methodology was followed to perform the 5-lipoxygenase inhibitory activity23. In conclusion, different amounts of the synthesized molecules were produced, with a range of 125 to 3.91 µg/ml. Subsequently, an enzyme solution containing 10,000 U/ml of 5-lipoxygenase was generated. Linoleic acid was used as the substrate, and its concentration was 80 mM. Likewise, a pH 6.3 50 millimolar phosphate buffer was prepared. After dissolving various quantities of the fake compounds in 0.25 milliliters of phosphate buffer, 0.25 µl of lipoxygenase enzyme solution was added. After that, the mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 5 min. The absorbance at a wavelength of 234 nm was then measured after 1.0 ml of a 0.6 mM linoleic acid solution was added and well mixed. Docking studies

The Molecular Operating Environment was used to carry out the docking procedure (MOE 2016.08). The Protein Data Bank provided the crystallographic structure of cyclooxygenase-I (PDB ID: 1CX2), cyclooxygenase II (PDB ID: 3LN1), and lipoxygenase 5-Lox (PDB ID: 3V99). They were downloaded from the protein data bank http://www.rcsb.com and saved in PDB format. The structures of synthesized compounds were drawn in ChemDraw 8.0 and saved in mol. File. This file was reopened in BioVia Discovery Studio Visualizer and saved in PDB format after the addition of polar hydrogen atoms24. The targeted proteins were also opened in software BioVia Discovery Studio Visualizer, Version 4 (https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download) for removal of co-crystallized ligands, attached water atoms, and other unwanted atoms. The active site was determined so that chemical moieties could be attached to the target sites. Our previously reported methods, which included 3D protonation, energy minimization, and binding site identification, were used to produce the ligands and downloadable enzymes. The MOE Builder function was utilized to construct the ligand structures. A drug database with the name ligand.mdb was generated. The MMFF94X force field was then used to minimize the compound’s energy until a gradient of 0.01 was reached. The MOE window showed the structure of the enzyme. The attached water molecules were diminished. In an implicit solvated environment with a pH of 7, a temperature of 300 K, and a concentration of 0.1 M salt, every atom was protonated in three dimensions. The MMFF94X force field was used to minimize energy throughout the whole construction. In the end, the compounds were successfully inserted into the enzymes’ binding sites. Ten different conformations were produced for every molecule when the docking parameters were left at their defaults. To analyze enzyme complexes containing ligands with the lowest binding energy, the MOE ligand interaction module was employed. The software BioVia Discovery Studio Visualizer, Version 4, was used to create the aforementioned 3-D interactive graphic.

Molecular dynamic simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations (MD) are the fundamental tools to identify the stability patterns and stable conformations of synthesized compounds with targeted protein molecules at the active sites. These simulations were performed through iMODS, which is an internal coordinate normal mode analysis server that provides an adequate, quick, approachable, and authentic way of determining the stability patterns of proteins. These iMODS could be acquired to understand variance, covariance map, eigenvalues, elasticity data and deformability values. The best models after docking were identified for each synthesized compound, selected as a whole along with the protein molecule and saved in PDB format. These molecules were further analyzed and identified for molecular simulations through iMODS.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability assay

The effect of the test sample was evaluated using HEK 293 (human embryonic kidney) cell lines, obtained from the National Institute of Health, Islamabad, Pakistan. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). A stock solution of the sample was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The MTT assay was performed using various concentrations, along with a control. Cell viability was assessed in independent triplicates, and absorbance was measured at 545 nm using a spectrophotometer to determine the effective concentration.

Experimental animals

Male Swiss albino mice (25–30 g) were obtained from the National Institute of Health (NIH), Islamabad, Pakistan. Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions with free access to food and water, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee of the Bacha Khan University, Charsadda, KP, Pakistan (Approval No: BKUC-01/12024), which is in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines as well as accordance with the Animal Bye-Laws of 2008 (Scientific Procedures, Issue-I).

After completion of the experimental procedures, mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally with ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) to achieve deep anesthesia. Euthanasia was subsequently performed by exsanguination followed by disruption of the diaphragm.

Acute toxicity

The acute toxicity study of the synthesized compounds at various concentrations ranging from 25 to 2000 mg/kg body weight was examined. Mice were categorized into various groups on a random basis; each group contained six animals in each group, 3 male and 3 female mice, treated with various concentrations of each compound. Mice were observed for any adverse effects during the initial 24 h. Mortality was recorded after 24 h4.

In vivo anti-inflammatory potential of the synthesized compounds

Initially, the anti-inflammatory potential of the synthesized compounds was determined in mice weighing 25–30 g. The animals were divided into various groups with 6 animals per group. Normal saline (10 ml/kg) was used as a negative control (Group 1). Group 2 mice were treated with 100 mg/kg Aspirin (positive control). The other groups were treated with various concentrations of the synthesized compounds. Thirty minutes post-treatment, all groups received a single subcutaneous injection of 0.05 mL of 1% (w/v) carrageenan suspension into the subplantar region of the right hind paw of each animal. An initial reading of the inflammation was taken immediately with Plethysmometer (LE 7500 plan lab S.L) after the injection of carrageenan for 1st to 5th hr. The volume of the paw was observed at different time intervals, then compared with the mean paw volume of the negative control group.

Anti-inflammatory mechanism of the synthesized compounds

For the mechanistic approach, mice were randomly divided into various groups. Initially, the animals received a single intraperitoneal injection of one of the following treatments: vehicle control (0.9% normal saline or 10% DMSO), cetirizine (10 mg/kg), Icatibant (1 mg/kg), celecoxib (50 mg/kg B. wt.), Montelukast (10 mg/kg) or the tested synthesized compound at various concentration. Thirty minutes after treatment administration, inflammation was induced in the right hind paw of each mouse via a subplantar injection of either 1% histamine, 20 µg/mL bradykinin, 0.01 µg/mL prostaglandin (PGE2), or leukotriene (10 µg/mL). Paw edema was assessed by measuring paw volume for each animal before inflammation (baseline), immediately after injection (0 h), and subsequently at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5th hours post-injection.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Synthesis and characterization

In this experiment, 20 milliliters of ethanol were mixed with 5.2 g of 2, 4-dinitrophenyl hydrazine, and 10 drops of HCl were progressively added. It was then mixed with 5.2 g of tosyl chloride. To further raise the ethanol concentration, 10 ml more of ethanol and a few drops of HCl were added to the beaker. Using a reflux condenser, the reaction was refluxed for three to five hours while being magnetically stirred in parallel. Using thin-layer chromatography, the completeness of the reaction was confirmed. The product was poured into a rotating evaporator so that the solvent could evaporate.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of the synthesized compounds was assessed with the MTT assay. This method relies on the ability of viable liver cells with active mitochondria to metabolize MTT into formazan, producing a measurable signal. In contrast, non-viable cells do not carry out this conversion and therefore do not generate a signal. All synthesized compounds were tested for cytotoxicity in HEK 293 cells. In this study, it was found that compounds R1–R5 maintained over 70% cell viability even at the highest concentration tested (125 µg/mL) (see Table 1). At a lower concentration of 125 µg/mL, none of the compounds exhibited cytotoxic effects. The key strengths of these synthesized compounds lie in their minimal toxicity at elevated concentrations and promising pharmacological activity at lower doses.

Acute toxicity

The doses selected for the in vivo toxicity evaluation were based on preliminary range-finding studies, covering a spectrum from LD0 to LD100, and included ten different concentrations ranging from 25 to 2000 mg/kg body weight. Each dose was tested in triplicate, along with appropriate positive and negative controls. Specific dosing protocols and animal details are presented in Table 4. In the acute toxicity assessment, no mortality was observed in animals given a single oral dose of 1000 mg/kg of the test compounds. As a result, the estimated median lethal dose (LD50) for these compounds was determined to be approximately 1000 mg/kg. No abnormal clinical signs were noted in treated animals, including changes in the eyes, nasal mucosa, skin, or fur. Additionally, no irregularities were detected in respiration, autonomic functions (e.g., salivation, piloerection, sweating, defecation, urinary incontinence), cardiovascular parameters (blood pressure and heart rate), or central nervous system behavior (e.g., gait, ptosis, lethargy, convulsions, or tremors). According to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) guidelines on acute oral toxicity, a substance with an LD50 between 300 and 2000 mg/kg falls under toxicity Category 4, indicating that the tested compounds can be considered relatively safe at the administered doses.

Carrageenan-induced inflammatory activity

The preliminary in vivo anti-inflammatory activity of the synthesized compounds (R1–R5) was evaluated using a carrageenan-induced paw edema model to assess their potential to reduce inflammation. Carrageenan-induced inflammation is characterized by a time-dependent, biphasic response. The initial phase (0–1 h) primarily involves the release of histamine and bradykinin, while the later phase (beyond 1 h) is driven by the production of prostaglandins, leukotrienes, lysosomal enzymes, and proteases. Intraplantar injection of carrageenan triggered localized inflammatory responses. Treatment with the synthesized compounds significantly attenuated these inflammatory changes, indicating their potential anti-inflammatory efficacy. In this study, three (03) doses, i.e., 5, 10 and 20 mg/kg of each compound were applied. Compound R3was the most potent among the tested compounds. Compound R3 significantly reversed the inflammatory changes produced by carrageenan at the doses of 5, 10 and 20 mg/kg. R3 showed significant anti-inflammatory activity with 45.45 ± 2.10, 52.54 ± 1.32, 53.45 ± 2.35, 55.45 ± 1.51 and 56.78 ± 2.18 from 1st to 5th hour at 20 mg/kg, respectively, which shows that the R3activity was much closer to that of the standard drug aspirin Fig. 13C. Similarly, R4 significantly reduces the carrageenan-induced paw edema when administered at the dose of 20 mg/kg (25.76 ± 1.65 to 46.59 ± 1.71). All the other synthesized compounds displayed moderate anti-inflammatory potential, as shown in Fig. 13.

Mechanism of anti-inflammatory activity of a potent synthesized compound

Based on the promising in vitro and preliminary in vivo anti-inflammatory activities observed, further investigations were conducted to explore the potential mechanisms of action of the most active compound, R3. These studies focused on evaluating the response of R3 to different phlogistic agents, including histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandin E2, and leukotriene. The anti-inflammatory effect of compound R3 was first assessed using a histamine-induced inflammation model. In this setup, R3 demonstrated a mild anti-histaminic response, with a peak inhibition of 34.86 ± 1.19% observed at the 2-hour mark, after which the effect gradually declined. This moderate activity is likely due to the compound’s partial inhibition of mast cell degranulation and subsequent histamine release. As shown in Fig. 14a, the reference drug cetirizine exhibited a robust, dose-dependent anti-histaminic effect, with 71.64% inhibition at 1 h that remained significant up to the fifth hour. In contrast, compound R3 showed minimal efficacy in the bradykinin-induced inflammation model, achieving only 15.28 ± 2.19% inhibition at 3 h post-administration. These findings suggest a lack of significant activity against bradykinin-mediated pathways. Meanwhile, the reference bradykinin receptor antagonist, Icatibant (HOE 140), demonstrated strong, dose-dependent inhibition, with 65.54 ± 3.61% reduction in inflammation within the first hour (Fig. 14b). Hence, none of the tested compounds, including R3, showed meaningful inhibitory effects against bradykinin-induced inflammation. Cyclooxygenase (COX) plays a key role in the biosynthesis of prostaglandins from arachidonic acid, contributing to inflammation by increasing vascular permeability and potentiating the effects of other mediators such as histamine and kinins. In the prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)-induced inflammation model, compound R3 exhibited substantial inhibitory activity, with 61.02 ± 2.95% inhibition recorded at 1 h and a maximum inhibition of 81.08% achieved at 4 h post-administration (Fig. 14c), indicating strong suppression of COX-mediated inflammation. Leukotriene-induced inflammation was also used to assess the anti-inflammatory potential of compound R3. Results showed that R3 produced dose-dependent inhibition, with 67.65% reduction at 1 h and a peak inhibition of 74.11% at 3 h. This effect remained significant throughout the duration of the study. The standard drug Montelukast, a leukotriene receptor antagonist, demonstrated maximal inhibition of 77.02% at the 3-hour time point (Fig. 14d). These findings suggest that R3 exhibits notable efficacy in suppressing leukotriene-mediated inflammatory responses. Collectively, the data indicate that compound R3 is particularly effective in inhibiting inflammation mediated by prostaglandins and leukotrienes, while showing limited activity against histamine- and bradykinin-induced responses.

SAR of the synthesized compounds

The synthesized compounds are based on the diaryl sulfonyl hydrazide scaffold bearing an electron withdrawing p-nitro group on the terminal phenyl ring and a p-methyl substituent on the opposite aromatic ring. This arrangement provides the hydrogen bond donor /acceptor sites and aromatic surface for π–π stacking within the enzyme active sites. In the parent compound R1 the sulfonyl hydrazide moiety is free of metal coordination resulting in modest COX-2 (IC50 = 4.74 µM) and 5-LOX (IC50 = 1.33 µM) inhibition, but with the highest COX-2 selectivity index due to very weak COX-I inhibition. Complexation of this scaffold with transition metals produced substantial improvements in the biological potential. In particular, coordination with the Zn (R3) and Fe (R4) rigidified the structure, increased lipohilicity and stabilized the bioactive conformation which leads to significantly enhanced COX-2 and 5-LOX inhibition. The compound R3 displayed the most potent activity (COX-2 IC50 = 0.84 µM; 5-LOX IC50 = 0.46 µM), nearly matching the celecoxib and surpassing the zileuton, while the R4 displayed similarly strong dual inhibition (COX-2 IC50 = 1.70 µM; 5-LOX IC50 = 0.97 µM). The Cu (R2) and Sb (R5) complex produced intermediate effects, indicating that the nature of the coordinating metal governs both potency and selectivity.

Furthermore, the docking studies provided structural insight into these differences. The compound R3 and R4 having multiple hydrogen bonds, π–π and π–alkyl interactions, and additional stabilization through metal assisted contacts within the COX-2 and 5-LOX binding pockets. The nitro-substituted aromatic ring was consistently involved in π–stacking with the active site residues, while the sulfonyl oxygen and hydrazide NH groups formed key hydrogen bonds anchoring the scaffold. In vivo, these structural features translated into strong anti-inflammatory efficacy, where R3 significantly reduced carrageenan induced paw edema and suppressed PGE₂ and leukotriene mediated inflammation, confirming its dual enzyme inhibition. The compound R4 also produced marked invivo activity whereas the other complex displayed only moderate effects. Collectively the SAR indicated that the the diaryl sulfonyl-hydrazide core is responsible for baseline COX-2 selectivity, while metal coordination particularly with Zn optimizes geometry, strengthens protein interactions and confers potent dual COX-2/5-LOX inhibition, positioning R3 as the most promising lead compound.

Discussion

Our novel study focused on the synthesis and characterization of five new sulphonyl hydrazide compounds (R1–R5) using physicochemical and spectroscopic methods, followed by significant COX-1, COX-2, 5-LOX Inhibition, anti-inflammatory and mechanistic approach and molecular docking studies.

Schiff bases with a hydrazide group can be synthesized easily and possess favorable solubility, as well as unique structural characteristics. The biological activity of ligands often increases when they form complexes with tin atoms. This increase is influenced by the organic substituents connected to the ligands, the organotin group, and the coordination behavior of the metal29. Organotin compounds have garnered interest for their potential use as antifouling agents, medicinal medicines, and disinfectants for surface materials15,30. Additionally, they have been employed as preservatives for cloth, wood, leather, electrical equipment, and glass. In order to expand our understanding in the field of medical research, we have synthesized a series of innovative organotin complexes and investigated their potential for anti-inflammatory properties31.

Several studies have examined and analyzed inflammation extensively because of its correlation with various pathological disorders. Several reports addressed various types of medications to combat inflammation, targeting specific molecules and employing distinct mechanisms32.

The interaction between a drug and its receptor, along with the functional groups of the drug candidate, provides critical insights into its pharmacological fate and potential for adverse drug reactions33. In our study, some novel drug candidates of sulphonyl hydrazide complexes were synthesized as toto target inflammatory factors, including 5-lipoxygenase and cyclooxygenase-2, which are directly or indirectly involved in the inflammation34. The drugs demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory activity by exhibiting strong inhibitory effects against all tested targets. Similarly, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) has been extensively investigated for its potential role in pain and inflammatory conditions35. Among the synthesized compounds, R3 exhibited the most prominent inhibition against COX-1/2 at various test concentrations, with significant potency with respect to standard drugs indomethacin and celecoxib. The remaining compounds have also shown moderate activity36.

The pathophysiological role of 5-lipoxygenase is well-recognized, and its inhibitors have shown significant promise in the treatment of inflammatory conditions37,38. We have determined the anti-inflammatory potential of our recently synthesized compounds against 5-lipoxygenase inhibition. Notably, the test compounds illustrated marked inhibition against 5-lipoxygenase at various concentrations. In the synthesized compounds (R1-R5) here, the effects of compound R3 were again most promising as compared to other derivatives and established its marked potential as a drug candidate.

Histamine is a well-known vasodilator that significantly increases vascular permeability and stimulates smooth muscle contraction, gastric acid secretion, and respiratory mucus production. It exerts its effects on a variety of cell types, including endocrine and exocrine cells, smooth muscle cells, blood cells, neurons, and immune cells. Although primarily recognized for its role in allergic reactions and inflammation, histamine can also display anti-inflammatory properties through mechanisms such as inhibition of IgE-mediated histamine release from peripheral leukocytes, suppression of neutrophil lysosomal enzyme release, and activation of suppressor T-lymphocytes39. Bradykinin is a biologically active nonapeptide (composed of nine amino acids) synthesized from the precursor protein kininogen via the enzyme kallikrein. It is one of the most potent endothelium-dependent vasodilators and contributes to increased vascular permeability and contraction of nonvascular smooth muscle. Bradykinin plays a significant role in mediating inflammation and pain and can elevate intracellular calcium levels in neocortical astrocytes. Its receptor is typically upregulated in response to tissue injury and is implicated in the inflammatory cascade40. Arachidonic acid (AA) is a critical component of membrane phospholipids in nearly all cell types. Upon cellular activation, AA is released by the action of phospholipase A2 and subsequently metabolized via two main pathways, the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway, producing pro-inflammatory prostaglandins such as PGE2, and the lipoxygenase (5-LOX) pathway, leading to the formation of leukotrienes41. PGE2 contributes to inflammation by increasing vascular permeability and enhancing the effects of other mediators like histamine and bradykinin. Leukotrienes (LTs), derived from AA via the 5-lipoxygenase enzyme, are potent inflammatory mediators produced by various immune cells, including mast cells, basophils, neutrophils, monocytes, and eosinophils42. These compounds play a central role in the inflammatory response and exhibit biological activity similar to histamine43. Some evidence suggests that H1 receptor antagonists may modulate leukotriene release by interfering with G protein-coupled receptor activity. In the current study, compound R3 demonstrated strong inhibitory effects against both PGE2 and leukotriene-induced inflammation, indicating its potential as a dual inhibitor of COX-2 and 5-LOX. This dual inhibition strategy is a highly effective therapeutic approach, as it targets multiple inflammatory pathways simultaneously, offering potential for enhanced efficacy in the treatment of inflammation-related diseases44.

Molecular docking studies were performed to understand the binding potential of synthesized chemical moieties against the targeted macromolecular structures of cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase enzymes. Computational studies were carried out through MOE that gave detailed information about the best binding conformations along with values of negative binding energies45. The best postures were visualized through Discovery Studio Visualizer.

The molecules R1-R5displayedmolecular docking at the cyclooxygenase enzyme PDB ID: 1CX2 binding site. We have investigated docking on both R and S enantiomers and evaluated the predictive power of docking simulations. With very few exceptions, the (S, S)-isomer generally had higher negative binding affinity values than the (R, R)-isomer, (S, R)-isomer, and (R, S)-isomer. Surprisingly, compound R2 (R, R)- form showed an exceptionally high negative value for activity46.

Additionally, the synthesized compounds were put through a molecular docking investigation using the human 5-LOX binding site (PDB accession number: 3O8Y). Residues Leu367, Leu362, Ile406, Ala410, Val604, and Leu607 are the amino acids that make up the hydrophobic cavity that houses the human 5-LOX enzyme. At the center of the active site lies the iron atom, which is coupled to His367, His372, His550, and Asn554. One essential residue that stabilizes the complex is Phe177. We introduced our compounds into the lipoxygenase enzyme PDB ID: 3O8Y binding site47. Every conceivable isomer was encompassed, and their binding affinities were thereafter examined. Once again, it is generally seen that the (S, S)-isomer exhibits stronger negative binding affinity values compared to the (S, R)-, (R, S)-, and (R, R)-isomers, with only a few exceptions. The LOX inhibitor R2 has demonstrated significant activity, particularly in its (S, S)-2 form, with a notably high negative value.

Conclusions

In this novel study, we have successfully synthesized a novel set of five sulphonyl hydrazide derivatives (R1- R5). The structures of these compounds are elucidated through spectroscopic techniques. In enzymatic assays, these compounds exhibited strong inhibition of COX-2 and 5-LOX in vitro. Of compounds, R3 displayed the most potent effects against both enzymes and also displayed excellent potential in in vivo studies. Similarly, docking studies conducted within protein binding pockets demonstrated their efficacy against COX-2 and 5-LOX. These findings from the observed result suggested that the tested compounds possess dual inhibitory effect (COX-2/5-LOX) and have the potential to inhibit both lipooxygenases as well as cyclooxygenase pathways of arachidonic acid metabolites, supported by the mild anti-histaminic effect.
