Laser biospeckles as a speedy tool to investigate the effects of sound on lentil (Lens esculenta puyensis) seeds
Authors: Uma Maheswari Rajagopalan, Ayato Suzuki, Takeshi Baba, Takahiro Kono, Jun Yamada, Hirofumi Kadono
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe propose the application of laser biospeckles—a non-destructive, non-contact, real-time technique—to investigate the effect of sound pressure levels and different frequency sounds on lentil (Lens esculenta puyensis) seeds. Lentil seeds were illuminated by a laser diode source with a wavelength of 630 nm, and biospeckle patterns resulting from the interference of scattered laser light were captured as time-sequenced image frames. Biospeckles were recorded under white noise as well as under different frequencies of 100 Hz, 1 kHz, and 10 kHz at 80 dB. The time-sequenced frames were analyzed for the effects of sound by calculating the correlation between the frames and were characterized by a parameter called BA (Biospeckle Activity), which reflects the level of internal activity within the seeds.ResultsWe found that the BA value was lower under white noise, indicating reduced activity within the seeds. Furthermore, a clear dependence of BA on sound frequency was observed, with this trend becoming apparent after six hours—well before visible germination began. It was found that, depending on the frequency applied, lentil seed germination could either be accelerated (e.g., at 1 kHz) or decelerated (e.g., at 100 Hz or 10 kHz). These results suggest that the laser speckle method may enable faster characterization of the characteristic frequency response of different plant seed species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07580-x.Keywords: Lasers, Biospeckle, Scattering, Lentil seeds, Sound, Germination, Non-destructive testing

Introduction

Apart from water, light, carbon dioxide, temperature, and humidity, sound has also been established as playing an essential role in plant growth [1]. In 1973, Creath [2] reported that exposure to classical music promoted plant growth more effectively than exposure to rock music. Subsequent studies have further elucidated the effects of specific sounds on plant growth.

Gagliano et al. [3] reviewed the effects of sound on plants. For example, in one study, when corn roots were exposed to a 220 Hz sound, the roots bent toward the sound source. They reported that the roots responded to ambient sound in order to locate water, actively growing toward the source of flowing water, suggesting that plants may sense environmental sound and adjust their growth direction accordingly. Furthermore, Collins et al. [4] exposed green bean plants to a single 6 kHz frequency, which increased the average dimensions of the main leaves and promoted growth. In contrast, white noise was reported to inhibit growth.

Vanol and Vaidya [5] applied sounds of varying frequencies and types (classical music, rhythmic rock music, and non-rhythmic traffic noise) to guar plants and monitored parameters such as seed germination in Petri dishes, plant height, and number of leaves over a 13-day period. Their results showed a positive effect from classical and rhythmic rock music, whereas non-rhythmic traffic noise had a negative impact on growth compared to the control group.

Conversely, other studies have reported that, compared to silence, sound promoted growth in bean plants regardless of frequency. Sound exposure was also found to increase oxygen content and polyamine levels in cucumber and Chinese cabbage, thereby improving overall plant health [6, 7]. Cai et al. [8] exposed mung bean (Vigna radiata) to sound frequencies of 1000–1500 Hz, 1500–2000 Hz, and 2000–2500 Hz, and evaluated their development based on mean germination time, as well as stem and root lengths and weights. They reported a significant reduction in germination time and increased seedling growth at a frequency of 2000 Hz and intensity of 90 dB. Vicient [9] examined the effect of white noise on maize seed germination using the germination factor, which requires a seven-day observation period.

In the above studies reviewed, traditional methods such as measuring dry and wet weights, counting the number of tillers, assessing root and shoot lengths, and quantifying ROS levels were used. All of these techniques require considerable time for the effects to become observable. Moreover, due to the insufficient sensitivity of such techniques, continuous real-time measurement and monitoring are virtually nonexistent.

To address these limitations, we propose using the laser biospeckle technique to investigate seed–sound interactions—a study, to our knowledge, that has not yet been explored. Because the proposed method used light, it is a non-contact, non-destructive, and capable of detecting subtle changes within a short time frame.

In fact, we have previously reported the application of laser biospeckles for rapid assessment of plant responses to sound stimulation at various frequencies [10]. In the present study, we employed laser biospeckle imaging to investigate lentil seed germination under white noise at two sound pressure levels (80 dB and 100 dB), as well as under three different frequencies—100 Hz, 1 kHz, and 10 kHz—at a constant sound pressure of 80 dB. We found that sound exposure had a significant effect, delaying germination compared to the control (no sound).

Samples and experimental methods

Plant material and sound exposure

Lentils, Lens esculenta puyensis (organic crimson red lentils, Greenfield Project, Canada) were used as plant samples. Lentils were chosen due to their short germination time (approximately one day) and their uniform, flat shape, which makes them easy to observe. In addition, lentils are considered important worldwide as a versatile food with high nutritional value and are in high demand as a health food both in Japan and globally.

Lentils were disinfected by soaking in hydrogen peroxide solution (Kenei Pharmaceutical, 2.5–3.5 w/v% hydrogen peroxide) for 10 min. A schematic of the experimental paradigm is shown in Fig. 1. Three soundproof boxes were constructed, each containing 23 lentil seeds in Petri dishes along with a speaker. Each box was assigned to a particular frequency with a different sound source. Seeds were placed on paper towels soaked with distilled water in petri dishes.

A schematic of the soundproof box with the seeds and the measurement times, along with an inset of the soundproof box. The soundproof boxes at different sound exposure conditions were placed in a growth chamber that was maintained at 25 °C ± 1 °C and a humidity of 50%. Here, seeds for the control condition were also kept in a soundproof box without any external sound

The moment the seeds were placed in Petri dishes was designated as time zero, and measurements were subsequently conducted at 3-hour intervals for a total duration of 21 h. During this period, the lentils were continuously exposed to sound stimulus, except during the brief intervals required for biospeckle measurement. The soundproof boxes at different sound exposure conditions were placed in a growth chamber that was maintained at 25 °C ± 1 °C and a humidity of 50%. Here, seeds for the control condition (silence) were also kept in a soundproof box without any external sound.

White noise is a type of sound whose frequency components are uniformly distributed with equal intensity across the entire human audible range (approximately 20 Hz to 20 kHz). White noise, which masks ambient sounds while providing a stable background, is commonly used to evaluate acoustic equipment and to aid in relaxation. In this study, we investigated the effects of white noise on plants. Three experimental conditions were compared: white noise at two different sound pressure levels (80 dB and 100 dB), and a control condition (silence). Speckle measurements were taken every three hours using six seeds per condition.

To isolate the effect of sound frequency, single-frequency sounds were also employed. The sound pressure level was set at 80 dB, and frequencies of 100 Hz, 1 kHz, and 10 kHz were used. Speckle measurements were conducted using six lentil seeds for each frequency condition.

Sound generation and exposure protocol

To expose the seeds to sound, a speaker (Anker Soundcore 2) was employed. The sound pressure level (SPL) was measured prior to the experiment using a sound pressure sensor (SL-100U-M, INABA, Japan). For the white noise experiment, the speaker was connected to a PC via Bluetooth, calibrated to maintain consistent SPL at a specific level using a decibel meter. In the single-frequency experiment, a multifunctional instrument (Analog Discovery 2, Digilent, USA) was used to generate arbitrary waveforms. A sine wave at the desired frequency was produced using the WaveForms application on a PC and played through the speaker. The SPL was fixed at 80 dB across all frequencies used in this study.

Laser biospeckle imaging system and analysis

When a stationary, rough-surfaced object is illuminated by highly coherent interfering light, such as laser light, the scattered light from the surface interferes to form a granular pattern of bright and dark spots—commonly known as a speckle pattern—on the observation plane, such as a camera sensor (Fig. 2). When the object is a biological specimen, such as a live seed illuminated under laser light, a similar pattern of random black and white patches is formed. Such interference of scattered light from a living biological object is referred to as biospeckle and is observed on the camera or observation plane [11].

Experimental system with an inset of the laser biospeckle frames obtained from a lentil seed. The laser light illuminated the center of the lentil seed. During every measurement, the petri dish containing the seeds was taken out of the growth chamber and then returned back after completion of the measurements

Because the biological object is alive, the intensity of biospeckles exhibits temporal fluctuations that are caused by intracellular processes and non-cellular biological activities, such as cytoplasmic streaming and water transport. Larger changes in the biological system lead to greater intensity variations in the speckle pattern. Thus, analyzing the dynamic fluctuations of biospeckles can provide insights into the internal activity of the biological object. In this study, we investigated the effect of sound on lentil seeds by analyzing biospeckles.

A collimated laser beam measuring 1 × 3 mm² irradiated the lentil seed, and the reflected light was aligned to be captured by the camera. The entire experimental system was set up in an enclosed box to isolate it from air fluctuations in the laboratory. Additionally, the system was placed on an optical isolation table to minimize the influence of floor vibrations. A laser diode with a wavelength of 630 nm was used for illumination. The scattered light from the seed interfered to produce biospeckles on the camera plane.

A CMOS camera (DCC1545M-GL, 1024 × 1280 pixels, THORLABS, NJ, USA) was used to record biospeckle patterns resulting from laser light scattering. The patterns were captured as time-sequenced image frames or videos at 15 frames per second for 10 s, and the data were transmitted to a PC.

Data analysis and metrics

Several methods have been proposed to analyze biospeckles, including contrast-based evaluation of individual speckle images and assessment of speckle uniformity using the second-order moment (IM) [12, 13] . In this study, we employed the same method used in our earlier work [10, 14, 15]: calculation of the cross-correlation between frames of the acquired biospeckle videos [16] to evaluate the response of seeds to sound stimuli.

The video data were analyzed using MATLAB software (R2023b; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). In the analysis, the correlation coefficient, denoted by γ and shown in Eq. (1), was calculated between the first frame—used as the reference frame A—and each subsequent frame B. Amn and Bmn are the intensities, where m and n specify the pixels in the frame. Ā and respectively indicate the average of the intensities of each of the frames, A and B.

When the correlation is equal to one, this indicates no movement; when the correlation is less than one, movement is present. Within plant tissue, such as a leaf, significant internal movements lead to a decrease in correlation, whereas minimal movement results in an increase in correlation. In other words, a decrease in the correlation coefficient reflects greater biological movement, whereas an increase reflects reduced movement.

To express plant activity as a positive and directly proportional measure, we employed a metric known as Biospeckle Activity (BA), defined by Eq. (2) as one minus the correlation coefficient. This formulation is consistent with the approach used in our previous study [10]. BA provides a more intuitive representation of dynamic biological activity, thereby facilitating the visualization of changes in internal processes over time.

When the correlation is equal to one—indicating that there is no movement or that the object is stationary—Biospeckle Activity (BA) approaches zero. Conversely, when the correlation is close to zero, indicating substantial deviation due to the movement of the object under study, BA approaches one.

To quantitatively evaluate the BA variation, the time taken to reach 0.5 of the maximum BA value was used as a measure. We have designated this value as the “time to reach BA0.5.” Here, it is worth mentioning that we proposed the use of BA0.5 to compare the different frequency conditions.

In our study, although the fundamental mechanisms behind the origin of biospeckles is still unknown it has been proved to be useful [11]. Biospeckles originate from the interference of scattered fields from the seed surface and also structures within the seed. Such scattered fields are expected to undergo changes when the environments are changed, such as sound vibrations. It is presumed that the presence of sound vibrations could result in the movement of nutrients and water through different organelles within the seed. Such movement would result in physical changes such as expansion or changes in sizes and numbers of scatterers. So overall, there will be changes in BA measured in our case.

When BA approaches zero, then there is no movement or when the object is stationary. Conversely, when BA approaches one, it indicates substantial deviation due to the movement of the object under study. There are cases where the variation of BA is monotonous.

To quantitatively compare BA variation as a function obtained under different conditions, we needed another parameter. One way to characterize the change in BA as a function of time could be to investigate the time to reach 50% of the maximum value of BA. This characteristic time or time to reach BA0.5 could indicate how the environment has changed, either accelerating or slowing the physiological activity, and thus could be used as a factor to characterize the effect of the environment.

When the time to reach BA0.5 is shorter, that means the movements within the seed are faster, resulting in faster decorrelation between the frames. On the contrary, when the time to reach BA0.5 is longer, there is more correlated activity within the seed, resulting in a slower decay. As a result, time to reach BA0.5 can be used as way to characterize the BA quantitatively with respect to the changes in the environment.

Furthermore, to assess the statistical significance of the BA results, non-parametric hypothesis (NPH) tests for paired samples were conducted. This approach is appropriate when the sample size is small or when the data do not meet the assumptions of parametric tests such as a normal (Gaussian) distribution of the data. Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank tests (with p-value set as less than 0.05) were performed for both individual measurement hours and for the time required for BA to reach 50% of its maximum or BA0.5 for both conditions of different sound pressures and different frequencies. In order to test as a group, we applied the Friedman test, a statistical test for comparing three or more conditions. The tests were done for both sound pressure levels and sound pressure levels.

Results

Effect of white noise

The variations of BA (Biospeckle Activity) as a function of recording time when seeds were exposed to white noise for at different measurement hours of 3 h to 21 h at every 3 h were calculated and shown in Fig. 3a. Here, note that we show only from the 9th hour onwards till 18th hour at three-hour interval. The vertical axis represents BA, and the horizontal axis indicates the exposure time in seconds. The black, red, and green lines represent the results for seeds exposed to silence, 80 dB, and 100 dB of white noise, respectively.

a Variation of BA as a function of recording time with lentil seed under different duration from the start of germination under the presence of white noise with (b) BA at 0.5 as a function of hour for control and different sound pressure levels of white noise. White noise has been found affect the germination by delaying germination in comparison to control. Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank test for paired samples was done between different conditions of control, 80 dB and 100 dB revealed that there was a statistically significant difference between 80 dB and 100 dB while no significant difference between the other possible pairs of control and 80dB, control and 100dB. Error bars indicate standard error

Physiological changes begin to be observed from the sixth hour onward. At the ninth hour, a clear trend emerged, with the control group exhibiting the highest level of activity compared to the groups exposed to different levels of white noise. This suggests that the disparity in activity between sound-exposed and non-exposed groups increases with the duration of sound exposure.

At all the measurement hours, BA increased rather slowly in the seeds that were exposed to white noise in comparison to those seeds under control. This in turn indicates that the presence of white noise reduced the physiological activity within the seeds and BA increased slowly.

To quantitatively evaluate the variation in activity under different sound pressure levels over the measurement period of up to 21 h, the time taken for BA to reach 0.5 was given as a function of measurement hours in Fig. 3b. The data at hour 0 were obtained immediately after seed sterilization and primarily reflect the presence of residual water on the seeds’ surfaces. The data from the third and sixth hours indicate seed enlargement due to water absorption, a process likely unrelated to the effect of sound.

At individual hours 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, and 21, Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank tests revealed significant differences between the control group and both the 80 dB and 100 dB groups (p < 0.05) (refer Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, the 80 dB and 100 dB groups were significantly different from each other (p < 0.05).

Similarly, Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank tests performed on time to reach BA0.5 showed statistically significant differences between 80 dB vs. 100 dB yielded a statistically significant result (p < 0.05), while between the control vs. 80 dB and control vs. 100 dB were found to be not statistically significant (refer Supplementary Table 2). These findings suggest that higher sound pressure levels exert a more pronounced effect on seeds, indicating that white noise exposure negatively influences lentil seed germination.

In all the graphs shown, the black, red, and green lines represent results for seeds exposed to 100 Hz, 1 kHz, and 10 kHz frequency sounds, respectively.

a Variation of BA as a function of recording time with lentil seed under different duration from the start of germination under different frequencies at 80dB sound pressure with (b) BA at 0.5 as a function of hour. 1 kHz sound has been found to speed the germination in comparison to other frequencies employed. Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank test for paired samples was done between different frequency conditions revealed that there was a significant statistical difference between the 100 Hz and 1 kHz and 1 kHz and 10 kHz, but no statistical difference between 100 Hz and 10 kHz. c is shown for a dead seed and as seen no cleared frequency preference could be seen. There was no statistically significant difference between the three frequency conditions for the dead seed. Error bars indicate standard error

In all the results of BA shown at different hours of 6, 9, 12, and 15, there is a monotonous increase of BA as a function of the recording time reaching a plateau at approximately 3 s irrespective of the frequencies employed. However, a closer look reveals that the behavior of BA is different depending on the frequency. While at 6th hour, there is almost no difference in BA between the different frequencies employed. However, we could start seeing changes from the 9th hour. The rate of BA increase (i.e., steepness of the curve) varies depending on the sound frequency.

Beyond 9th hour, consistently across all the hours, the BA variation shown in red corresponding to 1 kHz, the increase in BA for 1kHZ was large with the largest change for BA seen at around 15 h. Alternatively, for other frequencies, the difference is not clear and for both 100 Hz (black) and 10 kHz (green), the variation in BA is slow, indicating the presence of possible sustained correlations resulting in slower physiological activity. As a result, it could be suggested that 1 kHz exhibits the most pronounced effect among the three frequencies tested.

Beyond 15 h, for example, between the 15th and 20th hours, the differences in BA diminished, likely because the seeds had already begun germinating and internal metabolic activity had started to decline. Notably, after the 18th hour, the frequency-induced differences in BA decreased sharply, likely due to physiological changes associated with the germination process.

Statistical test of the BA variations as a function of time at different measurement hours by Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank tests for paired samples were done. At individual measurement hours of 6, 9, 12, 15,18 and 21 h, the Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank tests revealed the significance between frequency combinations of 100 Hz and 1 kHz, 100 Hz and 10 kHz and 1 kHz and 10 kHz (Supplementary Table 3). Except for 100 Hz and 10 kHz, almost all the frequency combinations were statistically significant.

Next, to quantitatively evaluate the variation in activity over the measurement period, the time taken for BA to reach 0.5 was calculated, and its variation was analyzed as a function of measurement duration in hours and shown in Fig. 4b. From the sixth to the eighteenth hour, a clear dependence of seed activity on sound frequency was observed, with 1 kHz taking the least time for BA to reach 50% and this indicates that the activity is the fastest for 1 kHz of all the frequencies tested inducing the most pronounced activation. Paired Sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank tests (with p-value set as less than 0.05) was performed to compare the time to reach BA₀.₅ across different frequencies (100 Hz vs. 1 kHz, 100 Hz vs. 10 kHz, and 1 kHz vs. 10 kHz) at various measurement hours. All pairwise comparisons results are given in the Supplementary Table 4 (p < 0.05). As can be seen from the p-values, all possible comparisons between the different frequencies showed statistically significant differences.

To further verify whether the changes in the time required to reach BA₀.₅—observed in Fig. 4a, b—were attributable to sound-induced biological activity, experiments were conducted using non-viable seeds.

Roasted seeds were used as the sample, and the same sterilization procedure was applied during seed preparation. Figure 4c presents the experimental results, showing the time required for BA to reach 0.5 as a function of exposure time (in hours). As observed, aside from the initial BA measurement, no clear frequency-dependent differences were detected (Supplementary Table 5). The time required for BA to reach 0.5 remained nearly constant regardless of measurement hour.

In Fig. 4c, the variation observed in the later stages of the time course may be attributable to the degradation of non-viable seeds following prolonged water immersion. As seed decomposition progresses, increased microbial activity is expected, which may contribute to the observed speckle motion. The results obtained using non-viable seeds confirm the frequency-dependent activity observed in viable seeds, as shown in Figs. 4a, b. Friedman ANOVA test, a test done for a comparison to investigate the differences among all three different conditions of frequency and sound pressure, was also conducted. The results are provided in the Supplementary Table 6, indicating a significant difference between the frequency groups, while no significant difference was found in the sound pressure group under different conditions of the seed.

Discussion

Our study proposed the use of laser biospeckles as a non-invasive tool for rapidly monitoring lentil seed germination under different sound frequencies. This technique leverages the interference of scattered light from various organelles within the seed to assess changes in tissue activity in response to sound stimulation. The results align with previous research reporting enhanced germination at around 2 kHz in bean sprouts [8] and 70 Hz or higher in Arabidopsis thaliana [17]. In the present study, 1 kHz was identified as the most effective frequency for promoting lentil germination.

Sound waves have been increasingly recognized as a non-invasive and environmentally friendly method to enhance seed germination and plant growth. In plants, studies that use acoustic stimulation by specific sound frequencies from audible (20 Hz–20 kHz) to ultrasound (> 20 kHz) have been shown to influence seed germination across plant species. Such a phenomenon although not fully understood yet, there is increasing evidence for such a phenomenon to be mediated through several interrelated biophysical and biochemical mechanisms.

An experimental study based on scanning electron microscopy observations of soya beans under ultrasound treatments at 40 kHz and 100 W for durations of 10–30 min revealed cracking in the seed coat and thus increased water uptake thus demonstrating a change in the membrane permeability [18]. In another study [19], they showed, exposure of Bacillus atrophaeus endospores microbial systems to 5 kHz at 90dB significantly accelerated germination which was attributed to increased membrane permeability and enzymatic activation. In a review done by [20, 21], they examined the potential role of sound stimulation on the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and the consequent cascade of physiological changes in plants, from the perspective of transcriptional regulation and epigenetic regulation. They showed that a wide range of factors may regulate the production of secondary metabolites, including plant species, growth stage, sound types, sound frequency, sound intensity level and exposure time, etc. [17, 20] and the references therein).

Overall, these findings suggest that sound can act as a potential biophysical elicitor of seed germination by modulating membrane dynamics, activating calcium-dependent signaling cascades, and reprogramming gene expression via transcriptional and possibly epigenetic mechanisms. Further, such acoustic stimuli are dependent on frequency, sound pressure, and even more so on species.

Our findings also support the conclusion that the optimal stimulation frequency and sound pressure vary for the species chosen: lentil seeds. These results suggest that applying sound frequencies tailored to specific seed types may enable regulation of germination timing, contribute to the development of more efficient cultivation strategies, and highlighting the need for methods that can rapidly assess such species-specific frequencies.

Limitations

Although the underlying mechanisms by which audible sound affects plants remain unclear, this study demonstrated that the laser biospeckle method can be used to rapidly evaluate its impact on lentil seed germination. The results confirmed that sound at 1 kHz most effectively promoted germination, while white noise exerted a negative effect. Moreover, the presence of sound waves generates heat within the chamber. The correlation between heat and germination requires in-depth investigation. Despite the advantage of rapid assessment, the laser biospeckle method still requires frequency-specific testing, as the optimal stimulation frequencies vary among plant species.
