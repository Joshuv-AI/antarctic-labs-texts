The neural basis of self-ambivalence: an ERP study
Authors: Dian Chen, Ying Liu, Yulin Zhang, Yiting Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary objective of this study was to investigate the neural basis of self-ambivalence, a phenomenon firmly established by behavioral research but whose underlying brain mechanisms have been less explored. Employing EEG methods and a modified self-reference paradigm, we analyzed event-related potentials using a linear mixed model to determine whether self-ambivalence processing exhibits a distinct neural representation. The results indicated that self-ambivalence processing primarily affected the late components (N2, N450, and P3), with N450 activation in the midline brain regions showing a significant positive correlation with scores on the Dialectical Self Scale. This finding suggests that individuals with higher levels of self-ambivalence may engage in more extensive processing of self-ambivalent information. The current study confirms the importance of the cortical midline in self-ambivalence and provides the first evidence of a distinct EEG representation of self-ambivalence processing. These findings contribute to our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying self-ambivalence and highlight the potential role of individual differences in shaping the neural processing of self-ambivalent information.Keywords: Self-ambivalence, Self-reference paradigm, Event-related potential, Late components, Dialectical self scale

Introduction

Self-ambivalence, defined as the coexistence of conflicting attitudes and feelings about oneself, is a complex psychological phenomenon that can significantly impact cognitive and emotional processes. Previous research has indicated that self-ambivalence is associated with increased cognitive load, decision-making difficulties, and emotional distress [1]. Despite its importance, the neural mechanisms underlying self-ambivalence remain poorly understood.

To improve the readability and narrative logic of the introduction, we first describe the behavioral evidence of self-ambivalence, including common paradigms, to enhance understanding. Behavioral research on self-ambivalence has typically employed self-report measures and decision-making tasks [2], revealing that individuals with high self-ambivalence often experience cognitive conflicts and emotional distress when processing self-related information. These behavioral findings support the exploration of neural mechanisms underlying self-ambivalence, as they suggest that cognitive conflict plays a key role in this phenomenon.

To refine our scientific question and provide a clearer focus, we draw upon the existing literature on the neural mechanisms of self-esteem. Although our study does not directly investigate self-esteem, it is informed by fMRI studies on self-esteem that explore self-referential processing and associated brain areas. These studies provide a framework for examining self-ambivalence, particularly by focusing on neural correlates such as the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), which are involved in self-referential processing and conflict resolution. Research has shown that the ACC and mPFC are involved in self-referential processing and self-esteem [3–5]. Furthermore, ERP studies have identified distinct patterns associated with self-esteem, particularly in the P2 and P3 components [6]. By referencing these studies, we aim to explore how these neural mechanisms compare and contribute to our understanding of self-ambivalence.

Specifically, our experimental paradigm integrates self-related and other-related ambivalence conditions within an ERP framework, which allows for a comprehensive investigation of the cognitive and neural processes underlying self-ambivalence. This paradigm ensures an accurate measurement of self-ambivalence by controlling for familiarity and other confounding factors, ensuring that the EEG responses reflect differences in self-correlation.

Our study aims to address this gap by examining the neural basis of self-ambivalence using event-related potentials (ERPs), a technique that offers high temporal resolution to capture the dynamic processes involved in cognitive conflict and emotional regulation. By developing a novel experimental paradigm, we aim to investigate how self-ambivalence is processed in the brain. We selected specific ERP components based on their relevance to the cognitive and emotional processes involved in self-referential processing. The N2 component was chosen due to its association with conflict detection and cognitive control [5]. The P2 component was included because of its role in early attentional processes and emotional evaluation [7]. The N450 component was selected for its relevance to conflict resolution and cognitive control [8]. Additionally, we examined the N1, which is involved in early sensory processing and serves as an important component in early self-referential processing, P1, and P3 components to understand the early sensory, visual, and attentional processes involved in evaluating self-referential adjectives [9].

The present study introduces a novel experimental paradigm designed to examine the neural correlates of self-ambivalence using an ERP approach. This innovative paradigm integrates both self-related and other-related ambivalence conditions, allowing for a comprehensive investigation of the cognitive and neural processes underlying self-ambivalence. By developing this paradigm, we aim to address existing gaps in the literature and provide a robust framework for future research in this area.

This study addresses three main research questions: (1) What are the neural representations of the ambivalent self under different components in the self-referential paradigm? (2) Which cognitive processes might these neural representations correspond to? (3) What is the relationship between the Dialectical Self Scale (DSS) scores and the EEG components? Prior EEG studies have shown that cognitive conflict tends to occur in the late components (250–650 ms) of ERPs, particularly N2 and N450 [10, 11], while self-reference effects have been found in ERP components such as N2, P2, N3, P3, and especially P3 in brain regions associated with self-processing [12, 13]. Building upon these findings, we hypothesized the following:

H2: P3 will exhibit the effects of self-primary processing, as an increasing number of ERP studies have shown that self-referential effects focus on components N1, N2, P2, N3, and P3 in self-processing brain areas, particularly P3.

H2: P3 will exhibit the effects of self-primary processing, as an increasing number of ERP studies have shown that self-referential effects focus on components N1, N2, P2, N3, and P3 in self-processing brain areas, particularly P3.

H3: Self-processing and conflict resolution will interact in the N2 component.

H4: DSS scale scores will be correlated with the level of activation in the midline brain area N2.

By exploring the neural basis of self-ambivalence, this research will contribute to our understanding of how the brain processes contradictory self-related information. Additionally, it is important to consider the psychological characteristics of self-ambivalence when formulating hypotheses. Individuals with high self-ambivalence often experience cognitive conflicts [14] and emotional dysregulation [15], which may be reflected in the neural signals associated with conflict processing. Moreover, by investigating self-ambivalence as a distinct concept from dialectical self-thinking, this research will provide a more nuanced understanding of the different ways in which individuals process and cope with contradictory self-related information across cultures. Ultimately, this study aims to contribute to the growing body of literature on the neural basis of self-processing, highlighting the importance of investigating the neural mechanisms underlying self-ambivalence as a crucial determinant of psychological well-being and self-regulation.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-seven subjects (19–30 years old) participated in the experiment, and data from seven subjects were excluded due to problems related to data recording. The final valid sample consists of 30 participants (12 females, mean age 22.7 ± 9.3 years). Participants were recruited using a combination of offline advertisements and online WeChat groups over a period of one week. Each participant who successfully completed the experiment received a compensation of 120 RMB. All participants were right-handed and had a normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Subjects were informed of the purpose and safety of this experiment and signed a written informed consent form before the experiment. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Tsinghua University.

To determine the appropriate sample size for our study, we conducted a power analysis using G*Power 3.1. Based on prior ERP studies, we estimated a medium effect size, with an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80, the power analysis indicated that a sample size of 30 participants is sufficient to detect significant effects. This sample size aligns with previous ERP research on self-referential processing and conflict resolution [9, 16, 17].

Material and variables

This study extracted 1520 words from the Chinese personality trait adjective database compiled by Dengfeng Wang and Hong Cui [18]. Among these words, 432 pairs of personality adjectives were selected based on the degree of semantic contradiction, valence, and frequency. The word stimuli and their properties will be included in the supplementary materials (see Appendix 1). The degree of semantic contradiction and validity of the words were rated by 13 students with undergraduate or higher degrees in psychology. The semantic contradiction was scored on a seven-point scale, with 1 indicating no contradiction at all and 7 indicating complete contradiction. Valence was also rated on a seven-point scale, with 1 being completely negative and 7 completely positive. The data used for the frequencies were obtained from the Beijing Language and Culture University-Corpus Center (BLCU Corpus Center). It is a multi-domain corpus that includes newspapers, literature, microblogs, science, and technology, comprehensive and ancient Chinese, with a total word count of about 15 billion words, comprehensively reflecting the language life of today’s society [19].

The study measured several dependent variables, including EEG signal potentials on various components and reaction time (RT). The N1 component, which reflects early sensory processing, was measured as the peak amplitude within the 80–120 ms time window post-stimulus onset. The P1 component, associated with early visual processing, was measured as the peak amplitude within the 100–150 ms time window post-stimulus onset. The N2 component, reflecting conflict detection, was measured as the peak amplitude within the 200–350 ms time window post-stimulus onset. The N450 component, associated with conflict resolution and cognitive control, was measured as the peak amplitude within the 400–500 ms time window post-stimulus onset. The P3 component, linked to attentional allocation and stimulus evaluation, was measured as the peak amplitude within the 300–600 ms time window post-stimulus onset. Lastly, reaction time (RT), which represents the time taken by participants to respond to each adjective, was also recorded.

In the experimental trials, the 432 pairs of personality adjectives were divided into six groups of 72-word pairs each, consisting of one contradictory pair and one non-contradictory pair. The contradictory word pairs were semantically contradictory and differed in valence (e.g., smart vs. foolish), while the non-contradictory pairs were semantically non-contradictory and similar in valence (e.g., dutiful vs. generous). The semantic contradiction and valence were balanced for each group of words.

Procedure

The experiment employed a within-subject design with four conditions: ambivalent self, non-ambivalent self, ambivalent others, and non-ambivalent others. The development of this experimental paradigm is a key innovation of our study. By systematically varying the ambivalence and target (self vs. others), our paradigm uniquely captures the complexity of self-ambivalence and its neural correlates. This design allows for precise measurement of the cognitive and emotional processes involved, providing new insights into the nature of self-ambivalence. The resting state was included in the first stage to serve as a baseline for neural activity [20]. This allowed us to compare task-related neural activity to a resting state with minimal cognitive load.

After the subjects arrived at the laboratory, they filled in the informed consent and completed the corresponding preliminary work of the EEG experiment. The stimuli (i.e., personality trait adjectives) were presented on a CRT display via Eprime2 software, and the subjects were then asked to complete the manipulations according to instructions. At the sight distance of 80 cm, the visual angle of each Chinese character is 2.0° × 2.0°.

The procedure of the official experiment is shown in Fig. 1. The first stage was the resting state. The guiding language was: “Please open your eyes and rest for five minutes, keep your head from shaking, and automatically jump to the next interface after the time limit”. The following is a general introduction: “After the beginning of the experiment, the subject should pay attention to the fixation point (+) and blink as little as possible. Otherwise, please blink at the stage of button pressing. Next, please judge whether the pair of words in the center of the screen can describe you or Jay Chou? After the word pair is presented, please press the key to respond. The left (←) key on the keyboard indicates that both words in the word pair describe properly, the down key (↓) on the keyboard indicates that only one of the two words in the word pair describes properly, and the right key (→) on the keyboard indicates that neither can describe the subject. If you are ready, press any key to start the test experiment.”

Experiment procedure

There are ten-word pairs in the test experiment. The instructions were randomly presented as “Please judge whether the presented word can describe yourself” or “Please judge whether the presented word can describe Jay Chou” for 1000ms, followed by 500ms of (+), then 1500ms of word pairs, followed by 400 ~ 800ms of (+), and finally the key judgment stage, which has no time limit. The presentation stage of word pairs was coded and recorded as EEG stimulation events. In each condition, each pair of words is presented randomly. The next step is the formal experiment. There are six groups in total, and they were counterbalanced across participants. To ensure the subjects’ concentration, the subjects are arranged to rest for two minutes between each group.

Following the ERP experiment, subjects completed the Dialectical Self Scale (DSS) [21], a widely used self-report measure of personality prime ambivalence with adequate psychometric properties. the DSS uses a 7-point Likert-like scale, with 1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree, with a total of 32 questions (e.g., “I have a strong self-perception so I don’t change my mind when others disagree with my opinion”). The reliability of the DSS in our study was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a coefficient of 0.73. While this value might appear moderate, it falls within the acceptable range for psychological scales, especially in the context of exploratory or early-stage research. According to [22], an alpha coefficient of 0.70 is considered acceptable for instruments used in early stages of research. Additionally [23], supports the notion that reliability coefficients above 0.70 are generally acceptable in psychological and social science research. Therefore, the alpha coefficient of 0.73 for the DSS in our study is deemed sufficient for our exploratory analysis.

To determine whether DSS scores reflect trait ambivalence or are influenced by the experimental conditions, we assessed DSS scores both before and after the experiment. This allowed us to confirm that DSS scores were consistent and not altered by the stimuli presented during the experiment.

EEG data acquisition and preparation

The EEG recording system of the German brain products company was used to record EEG activities according to the 64 conductive electrode cap extended by the international 10–20 system. TP9 (left ear mastoid) was used as the reference electrode, FP1 was used to record VEOG, and FPz electrode was grounded. The scalp resistance at each electrode remained below 5KΩ. The filter bandpass is 0.1–40 Hz and the sampling frequency is 500 Hz.

The preprocessing of EEG data is completed by fieldtrip [24], a Matlab toolkit. This process consists of several steps. Firstly, the EEG signal is filtered to 0.1–40 Hz. Independent component analysis (ICA) was performed on the filtered data, and the independent components of corresponding EEG and EMG were removed through manual inspection. The specific judgment standard is that the weight is concentrated in the forehead or temporal area, and the waveform is similar to eye movement or muscle movement. After removing about 1–2 independent components for each subject, the remaining components are restored to 64 EEG channels to obtain the EEG signal after removing the artifacts. The selected ERP components are: (1) N1: 80 ~ 150ms; (2) P2: 150 ~ 250ms; (3) N2: 250 ~ 350ms; (4) N450: 350 ~ 500ms; (5) P3: 500 ~ 600ms. Midline area (as shown in Fig. 2): AFz, Fz, FCz.

Analysis

Linear mixed model (LMM)

The original ERP data is time series. The most widely used method for ERP data processing is to average ERP data over time and analyze the averaged data with standard analysis of variance (ANOVA). However, because each subject in this study has been recorded under four conditions, it does not meet the independent observation hypothesis of the analysis of the variance model. In addition, the averaged data will lose information over time, resulting in the reduction of statistical inference ability. Another method, which is the method we use in this paper, is to directly apply the longitudinal data analysis tools (such as linear mixed effect model, random coefficient model, etc.) to the original data. This method has the merits of keeping more original information and is more suitable for ERP data structure. Therefore, this paper constructed a linear mixed effect model (LMM) [25] to analyze ERP data. LMM also has the advantage of providing better interpretability for physiological phenomena [26]. Previous studies on EEG analysis generally used the first method to take all trials of a condition as the mean, look at its average EEG, and then make further analysis. The disadvantage is that the characteristics of each trial are not considered. The linear mixed model takes the fluctuations of each trial into account so that individual differences can be evaluated more carefully and the model will be more accurate.

For the midline brain region of interest, the EEG data of different corresponding electrodes under each wave were averaged first. The EEG data of the whole length (1000ms after stimulus presentation) were then divided into five segments, corresponding to five EEG components N1, P2, N2, N450, and P3. The jth EEG signal of ith subjects is denoted as Yd,s,a,c,i,j, where 1≤j≤Nc and Nc represents the EEG data length of component c. The dummy variable d indicates conflict processing conditions (d = 1 represents ambivalent conditions, and d = 0 represents non-ambivalent conditions). The variable s denotes the self (s = 1) or others (s = 0) condition. And the variable a represents the midline brain region, while c represents different components. The significance level of the coefficients of interest, i.e., ba,c,self,ba,c,ambi andba,c,self:ambi, are tested using the Wald test.

Correlation analysis

To test whether the activation of dACC is regulated by the score of the DSS scale in the process of processing self-related conflicts, we analyzed the correlation between DSS score and brain activity, using a one-tailed Pearson correlation test. The interaction between conflicts and the self condition is equal to (ambivalent self - ambivalent others) - (non-ambivalent self non-ambivalent others). In this paper, the Bonferroni correction of the p-value is carried out by using the R package.

ANOVA analysis

The behavioral data were analyzed using a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA with two within-subject factors: Self (Self vs. Other) and Ambivalence (Ambivalent vs. Non-ambivalent). For each analysis, main effects, interaction effects, and post-hoc comparisons were conducted to explore significant differences between conditions. Bonferroni correction was applied to all post-hoc analyses to adjust for multiple comparisons and reduce the likelihood of Type I error.

Results

For the data of behavior and ERP, the most relevant results are the results of the main effect of ambivalence, the main effect of self, the interaction effect under different brain regions and components, and the correlation analysis results. The results are described in detail in the following part.

Behavioral data

In behavioral data, a 2-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the effects of ambivalence (self vs. others) and valence (positive vs. negative) on reaction times (RTs). The analysis revealed a significant main effect of ambivalence, F(1, 29) = 6.45, p < .05, indicating that participants responded slower to self-related ambivalence compared to other-related ambivalence. There was also a significant main effect of valence, F(1, 29) = 9.32, p < .01, with negative valence leading to slower RTs. The interaction effect between ambivalence and valence was not significant, F(1, 29) = 2.12, p = .15. Post-hoc analyses using Bonferroni correction showed that RTs for self-related ambivalence with negative valence were significantly slower than for other-related ambivalence with positive valence (p < .05) (Fig. 3).

Correlation analysis

In terms of correlation, the N450 activation level of midline (AFz, Fz, FCz) was significantly correlated with the score of the DSS scale (b = 0.38, p = .04) (as shown in Fig. 4), while other components (N1, P2, N2, and P3) had no significant relationship with the score of DSS scale. This result is consistent with the behavior results described above because the more contradictory people tend to score higher on the DSS scale [21]. Similarly, this result is similar to the study using fMRI combined with a self-reference paradigm to study the self-ambivalence of dACC brain region [27]. The relative activation of dACC brain area was significantly correlated with the score of the DSS scale, which further confirmed the strong relationship between the dorsolateral anterior cingulate cortex and DSS [11, 27]. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. No other significant correlations were found.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the neural basis of self-ambivalence using ERP components. Our findings provide partial support for the hypotheses. The main effect of ambivalence was observed in the late components, particularly N2 and N450, supporting H1. Additionally, the P3 component showed effects related to self-processing, consistent with H2. However, contrary to H3, we did not find a significant interaction between self-processing and conflict resolution in the N2 component, suggesting that these processes may be more independent than previously thought.

The significant correlation between N450 amplitude and DSS scores suggests that individuals with higher self-ambivalence engage in more extensive conflict resolution processes, as reflected by increased N450 activity. The activity of N450 is likely influenced by both self-referential processing and the resolution of ambivalence, as these processes are tightly interconnected during self-related decision-making.

These findings highlight the importance of investigating individual differences in self-ambivalence, as these factors may play a crucial role in how self-related conflicts are processed in the brain. Future research should further explore how these neural mechanisms may differ across cultures and other individual characteristics.

While the current study provides novel insights into the neural mechanisms underlying self-ambivalence, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we focused on self-referential processing and conflict resolution, the neural evidence for the involvement of specific brain regions like the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) comes largely from fMRI studies, which may not fully translate to ERP studies due to methodological differences. EEG, while offering excellent temporal resolution, has limited spatial resolution, making it difficult to precisely localize activity within deep brain structures like the dACC. Future studies employing combined EEG-fMRI techniques could provide a more complete picture of the spatial and temporal dynamics of self-ambivalence.

Second, although our study controlled for familiarity by balancing the frequency and valence of the word stimuli, future studies should employ more sophisticated methods, such as using individualized stimuli or pretesting familiarity levels, to ensure that the observed effects are driven by self-correlation rather than by differences in word familiarity.

Moreover, the ANOVA and correlation analyses were conducted separately, with Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple comparisons. However, due to the exploratory nature of our study, future research should adopt more refined statistical approaches, such as hierarchical linear modeling, to account for the complex interactions between individual differences (e.g., DSS scores) and neural responses.

Lastly, while we referenced fMRI studies on self-esteem to inform our hypotheses, we acknowledge the need for more direct investigation of self-ambivalence using fMRI techniques. These studies could help clarify whether the neural correlates observed in ERP studies are consistent with those identified in fMRI research, particularly in terms of the involvement of the ACC and mPFC in self-referential processing and conflict resolution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first ERP evidence of distinct neural correlates associated with self-ambivalence, particularly in the N2, N450, and P3 components, with N450 activation showing a significant correlation with self-ambivalence levels as measured by the DSS. These findings highlight the importance of individual differences in shaping the neural processing of self-ambivalence and underscore the role of late-stage conflict resolution processes in self-referential processing. Future research should explore these mechanisms further, using both EEG and fMRI methodologies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the neural basis of self-ambivalence.
