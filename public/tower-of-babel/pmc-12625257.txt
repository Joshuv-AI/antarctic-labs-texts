Retained blood syndrome after cardiac surgery: incidence, risk factors, and outcomes; single centre experience Taiz Yemen
Authors: Ismail Al-Shameri, Abudar Al-Ganadi, Naseem Al-wsabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective evacuation of shed blood around the heart and lungs is essential in the early postoperative period following cardiac surgery. Incomplete evacuation can lead to retained blood syndrome (RBS), which may necessitate reintervention. This study aimed to determine the incidence of RBS requiring reintervention and assess its impact on early clinical outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, cross-sectional observational study including all adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery between January 1, 2022, and June 30, 2024. Patients who underwent procedures for retained blood were compared to those who did not. Univariate and multivariate analyses were used to evaluate the impact of RBS on postoperative outcomes.ResultsAmong 330 enrolled patients, 49 (14.8%) underwent a total of 65 reintervention procedures (19.5%) for removal of retained blood from the pericardial or pleural spaces. RBS was associated with an increased hospital stay > 11 days (OR 1.379; 95% CI 1.255–1.519; p < 0.001), ICU stay > 2 days (OR 1.035; 95% CI 1.020–1.050; p < 0.001), and ventilation time > 4 h (OR 1.158; 95% CI 1.078–1.244; p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in postoperative mortality between the two groups. RBS was an independent predictor of postoperative pulmonary complications (26.5.6% vs. 5.7%, OR 0.167, 95% CI 0.74 − 0.376), deep sternal wound infection or mediastinitis (20.4% vs. 3.9%, OR 0.159, 95% CI 0.063–0.399), and postoperative renal impairment (16.3% vs. 5.3%, OR 0.289, 95% CI 0.115–0.724).ConclusionsRetained blood after cardiac surgery is common and significantly associated with prolonged ICU and hospital stays, increased ventilator time, and higher rates of postoperative complications. Early recognition and appropriate management may improve outcomes.Keywords: Pericardial effusion, Pleural effusion, Reexploration, Retained blood, Tamponade

Introduction

Postoperative bleeding is a common complication following cardiac surgery [1, 2]. Current data suggest that the incidence of retained blood requiring reintervention—such as surgical re-exploration, insertion of new chest tubes, or percutaneous drainage of pericardial or pleural spaces—ranges from 13.8% to 22.7% [3].

Chest drainage tubes are routinely used to evacuate shed blood and prevent fluid accumulation. However, they are prone to occlusion by clotted blood, leading to retained blood around the heart and lungs. A recent survey of cardiothoracic surgeons and cardiac surgery nurses reported that 100% had encountered chest tube clogging in clinical practice, and the majority linked it to adverse outcomes [4]. In a prospective observational study, 36% of chest tubes were found to be occluded after routine cardiac surgery, with most obstructions occurring within the thoracic cavity—unnoticed by ICU staff [5]. In addition to occlusion, suboptimal positioning, premature removal, and kinking of chest tubes may further impair drainage efficiency.

When blood clots accumulate outside of functioning chest tubes, they can collect in the pericardial and/or pleural spaces. This may cause acute mechanical compression of the heart or lungs, or in smaller volumes, may induce local inflammation, leading to effusions or delayed fibrosis [6].

Retained blood was defined as the accumulation of blood, clots, or bloody fluid requiring therapeutic intervention—such as re-exploration or drainage—for conditions including cardiac tamponade, pericardial effusion, hemothorax, or pleural effusion occurring during the index hospitalization [7, 8].

Several risk factors for bleeding have been identified in the literature, such as advanced age, urgent or emergent surgery, low body surface area, prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass (>150 min), complex procedures, use of multiple bypass grafts (>5), and preoperative use of antiplatelet therapy [9–12]. Postoperative bleeding and retained blood are associated with worse clinical outcomes, including prolonged mechanical ventilation, ICU stay beyond 72 h, higher 30-day mortality, increased risk of stroke, and significantly higher hospital costs [1, 12].

To the best of our knowledge, there have been no published studies specifically addressing retained blood syndrome in Yemen. This study aimed to assess its incidence, identify associated risk factors necessitating reintervention, and evaluate its impact on clinical outcomes at our center in Taiz, Yemen.

Patients and methods

This prospective, cross-sectional, observational study included 330 adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery between January 1, 2022, and June 30, 2024, at the Cardiovascular and Kidney Transplant Centre in Taiz, Yemen. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and informed consent was secured from all patients prior to participation.

Inclusion criteria included all adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing open cardiac surgery during the study period. Exclusion criteria were patients with incomplete clinical data, prior cardiac surgery within 30 days, or those lost to follow-up during index hospitalization.

Comprehensive data on preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables were collected prospectively. The use of antithrombotic agents and the volume of chest drainage were retrospectively reviewed from medical records.

Until 2023, aspirin was continued until the day of surgery. Starting in 2023, aspirin was discontinued at least five days preoperatively. Vitamin K antagonists were stopped at least three days before surgery, and bridging with heparin was not performed. Patients with acute coronary syndrome or mechanical heart valves received preoperative enoxaparin. P2Y12 inhibitors were discontinued at least five days before surgery.

Operative protocol

All patients received a pre-cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) loading dose of heparin (3 mg/kg) to achieve an activated clotting time (ACT) > 480 s. Arterial and venous cannulation was performed according to the type of surgery. Moderate hemodilution (hematocrit 20–26%) and systemic hypothermia (28–32 °C) were applied using a roller pump with non-pulsatile flow (2.0–2.4 L/m²/min) and a membrane oxygenator. ACT was maintained above 480 s throughout the procedure.

At the conclusion of surgery, two chest tubes were inserted in all patients: a 28 F tube in the left pleural cavity and a 32 F tube in the anterior mediastinum. In coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) cases, the left pleural space was opened during internal mammary artery harvesting. In valve procedures, a small pleural opening was created for drain placement. If the right thorax was incidentally opened, the anterior mediastinal tube was passed through the right pleural opening and placed retrosternally. Posterior left pericardiotomy was performed in 165 patients.

Postoperative management

Following routine chest closure, all patients were transferred to the ICU. Chest drains were routinely milked and stripped to maintain patency. Low-intermittent suction (−20 cmH₂O) was applied. Drainage output was measured hourly in the ICU. Chest tubes were removed on postoperative day 2 if drainage was < 100 mL over the previous 12 h or if the fluid was serous. If not, re-evaluation was conducted every 12 h.

All patients underwent a postoperative chest X-ray (CXR) upon ICU admission and every morning during hospitalization. Patients discharged with moderate pleural effusion were scheduled for follow-up CXR 2–3 weeks post-discharge.

Postoperative anticoagulation followed institutional protocol. Aspirin (100 mg) and P2Y12 inhibitors (75 mg) were resumed on postoperative day 1 for CABG patients. After mechanical valve surgery, low-dose heparin (250 IU/h) was initiated once hemodynamic stability was achieved and drainage was < 100 mL/h. Warfarin was restarted on the first postoperative day in valve surgery patients.

Red blood cell transfusion was given intraoperatively if hemoglobin dropped below 9 g/dL, and postoperatively if < 8 g/dL. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and platelets were administered based on bleeding severity, INR, and platelet count. Fresh whole blood was used in cases of massive bleeding.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was RBS, defined as any intervention during the index hospitalization to evacuate retained blood, clot, or bloody fluid. This included re-exploration for tamponade or hemothorax, pericardial window, pericardiocentesis, or insertion of pleural drains/thoracentesis [7, 8].

Secondary outcomes included: Duration of ICU and hospital stay, volume of chest drainage, postoperative blood transfusion, and postoperative complications: mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, pulmonary complications, renal impairment, and sternal wound infection/mediastinitis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 24.0 (RRID: SCR_002865). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variables: categorical data as frequencies/percentages and continuous data as means (± SD) or medians (IQR), based on data distribution. Univariate analysis was conducted to compare the RBS and non-RBS groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-squared test. Continuous variables were analyzed with Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the independent association of RBS with outcomes, logistic, linear, and ordinal regression analyses were performed. Variables with clinical relevance or significance in univariate analysis were included in multivariate models. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion where applicable.

Results

During the two-half-year study period from 1 st January 2022 to June 2024, 330 adult patients underwent cardiac surgery and qualified for study inclusion. Retained blood was defined as any intervention required to remove blood, blood clots, or bloody fluid, including procedures for tamponade, pericardial effusion, hemothorax, or pleural effusion during the index hospitalization. Overall, 49 patients (14.8%) underwent a total of 65 (19.5%) reintervention procedures for the removal of retained blood from the pericardial or pleural cavity. A single procedure was performed in 38 patients (11.5%), while two procedures were required in 8 patients (2.4%), three procedures in 2 patients (0.6%), and five procedures in 1 patient (0.3%).

Patients with small pericardial effusion were treated with diuretics and anti-inflammatory drugs for two weeks, and no further intervention was required. Follow-up echocardiography performed during the first postoperative month confirmed the complete resolution of pericardial effusion in all cases. Among the 27 patients (23.2%) with moderate pericardial effusion, treatment with diuretics and anti-inflammatory drugs was extended for two to four weeks. Of these, only two patients (7.4%) developed pericardial tamponade, necessitating intervention. Follow-up echocardiography at one month postoperatively identified mild residual pericardial effusion in three cases. Clinical manifestations of cardiac tamponade, including hypotension, tachycardia, and decreased urine output, were observed in 23 patients (20% of cases with pericardial effusion). Among patients with progressive large pericardial effusion, 21 out of 24 developed cardiac tamponades, necessitating emergency intervention. Management included subxiphoid pericardiotomy in six patients and emergent resternotomy in 15 patients. Tamponade occurred early (< 10 days postoperatively) in six patients, whereas late-onset tamponade (day 14 to day 23) was documented in nine patients. The incidence of cardiac tamponade was significantly higher following valve replacement (17 patients) compared to coronary artery bypass grafting (5 patients) or combined surgical procedures (1 patient).

Regarding the distribution of surgical procedures related to retained blood, patients undergoing isolated CABG had a retained blood rate of 8.6% (15/175), those undergoing isolated valve surgery had a rate of 23.2% (32/138), and patients undergoing combined CABG/valve surgery had a rate of 11.2% (2/17). The difference between the study groups was statistically significant (P = 0.001). However, the urgency of surgery (emergency, urgent, or elective) did not significantly affect the rate of retained blood (P = 0.206).

Among the specific interventions performed, a pericardial window was created in 6 patients (1.8%), pleural drainage was required in 27 patients (8.2%)—with 3 patients undergoing the procedure twice—and thoracocentesis was performed in 3 patients (0.9%), with one patient requiring it twice. Thoracotomy was necessary in 4 patients (1.2%). Resternotomy for surgical drainage was performed in 21 patients (6.4%), with 4 patients requiring the procedure twice. Emergent resternotomy in the intensive care unit was performed in 2 patients. Regarding the causes of re-exploration, 21 patients (6.4%) required surgical intervention: 14 patients (4.3%) due to tamponade and 7 patients (2.1%) due to surgical bleeding. Among the 7 patients requiring reoperation for excessive hemorrhage, 3 developed both excessive mediastinal bleeding and cardiac tamponade, while the remaining 4 underwent reoperation for excessive bleeding in the early postoperative period before developing tamponade (Table 3].

Discussion

Retained blood syndrome is a postoperative complication characterized by the accumulation of blood or clots within the pericardial or pleural spaces after cardiac surgery. Despite improvements in surgical techniques and postoperative management, RBS continues to be a clinically significant issue due to its association with adverse outcomes such as cardiac tamponade, pleural effusions, increased morbidity, and prolonged hospital stays. This discussion outlines the incidence, risk factors, and clinical outcomes associated with RBS, drawing comparisons with existing literature and highlighting potential areas for improvement in clinical practice.

The reported incidence of RBS varies widely in the literature, largely due to inconsistencies in diagnostic criteria and patient population differences. Boyle et al. [6] reported RBS incidence ranging from 16% to 34%, depending on the definition and detection method used. Borracci et al. [7] found that approximately 20% of patients undergoing CABG or valve surgery developed clinically significant retained blood requiring intervention. In our study, the incidence of RBS was 19.5%, which aligns closely with previously reported figures [8, 13], supporting the need for standardized criteria for diagnosing and reporting RBS.

Several patient-related factors contribute to the risk of developing RBS. Consistent with previous findings [1]. Our study identified the use of perioperative antiplatelet agents such as aspirin and clopidogrel as significant risk factors. Interestingly, we also observed a higher incidence of RBS among younger patients, which we believe is due to the higher frequency of valvular procedures in this age group, often related to rheumatic heart disease.

RBS has been shown to negatively impact recovery. Prior studies have reported significantly prolonged hospital stays among RBS patients, driven by the need for additional interventions, imaging, and intensive monitoring [6]. , [8] Similarly, our findings indicate that patients with RBS experienced significantly longer durations of mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and total hospitalization. In addition, RBS patients had higher rates of readmission, often due to delayed effusions or infections. These findings are in line with previous reports that highlighted complications such as pericardial and pleural effusions or tamponade requiring further invasive procedures [1, 3]. We also found a higher 30-day morbidity among patients with RBS, including increased rates of pulmonary complications, acute kidney injury, and sternal wound infections. Dixon et al. [14] likewise demonstrated that higher chest tube output was associated with adverse outcomes such as longer ICU/hospital stay, increased ventilator support, and greater risk of infections and readmissions.

Poor chest tube function, including clogging, inadequate suction, or malpositioning, is a well-recognized contributor to blood retention. Prospective studies have found chest tube obstruction in up to 36% of patients post-cardiac surgery [5]. When tubes clog while bleeding is ongoing, blood accumulates in the pericardial or pleural space, potentially leading to tamponade or effusion. However, the frequency and consequences of this specific mechanism remain underexplored. In addition, chest tubes may not be optimally placed or may become kinked, which also limits effective drainage [8].

Given the clinical impact of RBS, efforts to improve blood evacuation after cardiac surgery are warranted. Impaired chest tube patency is a potentially modifiable factor. However, there are currently no formal guidelines for maintaining chest tube patency postoperatively. Traditional practices such as chest tube stripping or milking are widely used, but their efficacy remains controversial [15]. Day et al. [16] reviewed these techniques and concluded that they may be harmful due to the generation of excessive negative intrathoracic pressure. Other techniques, such as manually opening chest tubes to break up clots, pose significant infection risks [17, 18] Newer technologies that allow for active clearance of chest tubes without compromising sterility may provide a safer and more effective approach [19, 20] Sirch et al. [21] demonstrated a 43% reduction in RBS using active chest tube clearance in a propensity-matched cohort.

In our study, we employed posterior pericardiotomy as a preventive strategy. This technique was found to significantly reduce the risk of RBS (OR 0.430; 95% CI, 0.226–0.816), supporting previous evidence that it can enhance pericardial drainage and reduce effusion-related complications.

Despite the strengths of this prospective observational study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, our analysis did not account for the timing of adverse events relative to reintervention. Complications such as sternal wound infection may arise days after surgery, while others like acute kidney injury may precede the diagnosis of RBS, complicating causality assessment. Future studies should consider a time-to-event analysis to better understand the temporal relationship between RBS and associated complications.

Conclusions

Retained blood syndrome, defined as the need for any procedure to evacuate blood or clots from the pericardial or pleural spaces, was observed in 18.5% of patients in our cohort and remains a significant complication following cardiac surgery. Its presence was strongly associated with increased postoperative morbidity. Specifically, RBS patients had a 45% longer ICU stay (mean 4.5 vs. 3.1 days), required 55% more prolonged mechanical ventilation (26.4 vs. 17 h), and experienced a 4-fold higher rate of reoperation for bleeding (15% vs. 3.8%) compared to non-RBS patients. Future studies should aim to validate standardized diagnostic criteria and drainage protocols to improve patient outcomes and reduce complication rates associated with retained blood after cardiac surgery.
