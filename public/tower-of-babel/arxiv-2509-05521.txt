Abstract
We consider port-Hamiltonian systems from a geometric perspective, where the quantities involved such as state, flows, and efforts evolve in (possibly infinite-dimensional) Banach spaces. The main contribution of this article is the introduction of a weak solution concept. In this framework we show that the derivative of the state naturally lives in a space that, for ordinary evolution equations, plays the role of an extrapolation space. Through examples, we demonstrate that this approach is consistent with the weak solution framework commonly used for partial differential equations.

Port-Hamiltonian systems generally lead to differential inclusions, which, in important special cases, reduce to ordinary differential equations (ODEs), PDEs, DAEs, or, in an even more general case, partial differential-algebraic equations (PDAEs).
These differential inclusions are typically formulated in a strong sense, and a connection to weak solution theory has not yet been established. This article addresses precisely this gap: we leverage the self-orthogonality property of the Dirac structure (with respect to a certain indefinite inner product) to develop a weak solution concept. Through examples, we demonstrate that this approach aligns with the weak solution framework commonly used for PDEs.

To clarify from the outset, we do not address the question of existence of solutions in this article. The reason is that port-Hamiltonian systems in geometric form fall within the “behavioral approach” in the sense of
[
36
]
, which focuses on trajectories rather than systems with explicitly defined inputs and outputs. The definition of inputs and outputs, and the subsequent analysis of existence of solutions, is simply a separate line of investigation.

However, we show that the proposed solution concept is relevant not only for infinite-dimensional systems but also for the finite-dimensional case. This is due to two reasons. First, the weak solution concept naturally accommodates discontinuous trajectories at the external ports, as occur for instance in mechanical or electrical systems when an external force or voltage is switched on or off. Second, the weak solution concept ensures that components of the state which are annihilated by the Dirac structure, in the sense that parts of the state derivative do not actually enter the equations, are not required to be differentiable.

Background on port-Hamiltonian systems, their solution concepts, and the relations among them is provided in
Section

2
.
The basic idea for our weak solution concept is relatively straightforward: the differential inclusion is formally multiplied by a test function taking values in the Dirac structure. After integration in time, the derivative of the state is transferred to the test function. We further show that a part of the state is indeed weakly differentiable when regarded as a trajectory in a larger space. This extends the concept of the extrapolation space, which is well established in the context of evolution equations
[
46
, Sec. 2.10]
.
In
Section

4
, we then turn to the energy balance. Under mild additional assumptions on the underlying Banach spaces and square-integrability of the trajectories, we show that the difference of the Hamiltonian between initial and final times (the stored energy gained) can be estimated by the supplied energy, that is, the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product of the flow and effort variables at the external ports.
Finally, our theory is applied to two infinite-dimensional examples in
Section

5
.

2
Port-Hamiltonian systems an their solution concepts

The fundamental ingredient of port-Hamiltonian systems is the Dirac structure.

Definition 1
(Dirac structure).

For a Banach space
\mathcal{F}
, a subspace
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is called a
Dirac structure
, if
for all
\mathfrak{f}\in\mathcal{F}
,
\mathfrak{e}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, we have

(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{e})\in\mathcal{D}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall\,(\hat{\mathfrak{f}},\hat{\mathfrak{e}})\in\mathcal{D}:\;\langle\hat{\mathfrak{f}},\mathfrak{e}\rangle+\langle\mathfrak{f},\hat{\mathfrak{e}}\rangle=0.

For
(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{e})\in\mathcal{D}
, we call
\mathfrak{e}
an
effort
and
\mathfrak{f}
is termed a
flow
. By equipping the so-called
Bond space

\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
with the indefinite and non-degenerate inner product

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle\;:

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\big)\times\big(\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\big)

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}},

(2)

\displaystyle\big((\mathfrak{f}_{1},\mathfrak{e}_{1}),(\mathfrak{f}_{2},\mathfrak{e}_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\langle{\mathfrak{f}}_{1},\mathfrak{e}_{2}\rangle+\langle\mathfrak{f}_{2},{\mathfrak{e}}_{1}\rangle,

we can conclude that
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a Dirac structure if, and only if,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{\bot\!\!\!\bot}
, where the latter denotes the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
. In particular, any Dirac structure is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Remark 2
.

In the case
\mathcal{F}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Similarly, for matrices

\rank[F,\,G]=n

\mathcal{D}=\ker[F,\,G]=\im\left[\begin{smallmatrix}G^{\top}\\

(3)

We are now ready to introduce port-Hamiltonian systems. In addition to a Dirac structure, these systems also include Hamiltonians and modulated resistive relations, whose definition is included in the following one.
As mentioned in the introduction, we focus here on a specialized version of port-Hamiltonian systems, while a more general class will be discussed in the next section. Here, we note that for a Fréchet differentiable mapping

Definition 3
(Port-Hamiltonian system).

Let

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}

A

•

\forall\,(\mathfrak{f}_{\mathcal{R}},\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}})\in\mathcal{R}:\quad\langle\mathfrak{f}_{\mathcal{R}},\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\rangle\leq 0.

Remark 7
(Further generalizations of the Hamiltonian).

In general, the philosophy of port-Hamiltonian systems can be summarized as: whatever does not fit is adapted to fit. Concretely, this means that the class is further generalized whenever a physical system arises that cannot be captured by the existing definitions. This has led to numerous generalizations of the class introduced in
Definition

3
, some of which we briefly discuss below.

(a)

In a more general formulation of port-Hamiltonian systems, implicit energy storage is allowed
[
28
,
29
]
. This is, for instance, required for a direct treatment of mechanical systems with holonomic constraints. Mathematically, this means that instead of specifying a Hamiltonian, one prescribes a Lagrangian submanifold
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Consequently, the co-energy variable
e(\cdot)
need not coincide with the Fr’echet derivative of a Hamiltonian; rather, the pair
(x(\cdot),e(\cdot))
takes values in the prescribed manifold
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. This generalization does not affect the definition of weak solutions: our concept extends in a straightforward manner, except for the energy balance discussed in
Section

4
, which explicitly relies on the presence of a Hamiltonian. To keep the exposition concise, we do not elaborate on Lagrangian submanifolds in this work.

(b)

In a generalization of
Definition

3
, the resistance may depend on the state.
That is, one has to consider modulated resistive relations, which are families

\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathfrak{x}})_{\mathfrak{x}\in U}
with the property that

\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathfrak{x}}\subset\mathcal{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{R}}\times\mathcal{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}
is a resistive relation for all
\mathfrak{x}\in U
.
Accordingly, in (
4
), the condition
(f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t))\in\mathcal{R}

has to be replaced with
(f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t))\in\mathcal{R}_{x(t)}
.
An example is a car driving on a road with varying surface conditions, where the rolling resistance depends on the position of the vehicle, which in turn is part of the state.

The definition of a classical solution for this type of generalization is immediate.
For the definition of a weak solution, one simply replaces in
Definition

5

(c)
with

With this assumption, the corresponding argument in the proof of
Theorem

6

(b)

(c)

The system may evolve on a (Banach) manifold
\mathcal{M}
. More precisely, the state evolves on a manifold, as is common in rational mechanics

The definition of weak solutions (that is, multiplying by test functions and formally integrating by parts) leads to highly advanced questions in differential geometry and, to the best of the author’s knowledge, is completely unexplored, even in the finite-dimensional setting. The author intends to consider this problem in the future, although no outcome can be promised.

2.1
The finite-dimensional case

One of the main motivations for introducing weak solutions, as already mentioned, is the search for a solution concept that is consistent with the weak solution framework for partial differential equations. We will discuss this in more detail by means of examples in
Section

5
.
However, the weak solution concept also offers several interesting features in the finite-dimensional setting, which we will explore in the following.

If all flow and effort spaces are finite-dimensional, we can set (by using suitable isometric isomophisms), choose

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}={\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}={\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathrm{r}}},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}={\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}.

Then
Remark

2
gives that any Dirac structure

\mathcal{D}\subset(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}})\times(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime})\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{2(n_{\mathrm{s}}+n_{\mathrm{r}}+n_{\mathrm{p}})}

can be represented as

\mathcal{D}=\ker[F_{\mathrm{s}},\,F_{\mathrm{r}},\,F_{\mathrm{p}},\,G_{\mathrm{s}},\,G_{\mathrm{r}},\,G_{\mathrm{p}}],

where
F_{\mathrm{s}},G_{\mathrm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n_{\mathrm{s}}}
,
F_{\mathrm{r}},G_{\mathrm{r}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n_{\mathrm{r}}}
,
F_{\mathrm{p}},G_{\mathrm{p}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n_{\mathrm{p}}}
,
n:=n_{\mathrm{s}}+n_{\mathrm{r}}+n_{\mathrm{p}}

F:=[F_{\mathrm{s}},\,F_{\mathrm{r}},\,F_{\mathrm{p}}],\qquad G:=[G_{\mathrm{s}},\,G_{\mathrm{r}},\,G_{\mathrm{p}}],

the matrix
[F,\,G]
has full row rank with
FG^{\top}+GF^{\top}=0
. The strong form of the port-Hamiltonian system (

(f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t))\in\mathcal{R}.

(6)

Note that we need to take the gradient of the Hamiltonian rather than its derivative; the two are simply related by a transpose.

Next we characterize weak solutions. To this end, we use the identity (

\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
\varrho_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{D}\quad\forall\,t\in I,

then there exists some

\begin{pmatrix}\chi(t)\\
\chi_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
\chi_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}G_{\mathrm{s}}^{\top}\\

3
The state extrapolation space

Our concept of a weak solution essentially involves testing in time and in the relevant function spaces, the latter corresponding to testing over spatial domains in the case of PDEs. In the following, we show that - loosely speaking - the state trajectory can be interpreted as a weakly differentiable function taking values in a larger space. More precisely, though still not in an absolute sense, we factor out the space of states whose derivatives are annihilated by the Dirac structure and consider a superspace in which the state derivative evolves.
This extends the common concept of the
state extrapolation space
, for example, in the context of evolution equations
[
46
, Sec. 2.10]
.

In the following, to avoid overly bulky expressions, we will group the spaces of resistive and external flows and efforts together. In particular, we define

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}.

(7)

We do not explicitly use Hamiltonians and resistive relations here; instead, we allow for general trajectories of the co-energy variables, resistive flows, and efforts. All the following results and definitions apply to port-Hamiltonian systems in the sense of
Definition

5
by setting (
7
) and additionally imposing
e(t)=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(x(t))
and
(f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t))\in\mathcal{R}
.

Next, we show that (at least part of) the state trajectory of a port-Hamiltonian system
is weakly differentiable when viewed as a trajectory in a suitable
state extrapolation space
. This requires considering the space of occurring co-energy variables, which are precisely those obtained by projecting the Dirac structure onto the component that represents the co-energy variables.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Q}}
be Banach spaces, and let

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{e}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{D}\,\right.\right\}.

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathcal{Q}}\\

\mathfrak{f}_{\mathcal{Q}}\\
\mathfrak{e}\\

\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{e}\\

Now consider the annihilator

{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}{}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}=\left\{\,\mathfrak{f}\in\mathcal{X}\,\left|\vphantom{\mathfrak{f}\in\mathcal{X}}\,\langle\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}=0\;\forall\,\mathfrak{e}\in\mathcal{E}\,\right.\right\}.

Definition 11

A closed subspace
\mathcal{U}
of a Banach space
\mathcal{V}
is called splitting subspace, if there exists some closed subspace
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{V}
with

\mathcal{U}

For a splitting subspace
\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{V}

\mathcal{U}
is canonically isomorphic to the quotient space
\mathcal{V}/\mathcal{U}
. In particular, all complementary subspaces are mutually isomorphic. The additional advantage of the space of co-energy variables having a closure that is a splitting subspace is discussed in the following remark.

Remark 12

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, and let
\mathcal{E}_{c}
be a complementary subspace of
\mathcal{E}
. Then
{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}{}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}
is also a splitting subspace of
\mathcal{X}
via

\mathcal{X}={\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}\oplus{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}_{c}}}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}_{c},

(16)

and
{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}_{c}}}{}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}_{c}
can be regarded as a subspace of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.
Consequently,
\mathcal{X}
can be regarded as a dense subspace of

\mathcal{X}_{-1}:=\big({\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}\big)\oplus\mathcal{E}^{\prime},

(17a)

where the latter is a Banach space provided with the norm

\|x_{1}+x_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{-1}}=\sqrt{\|x_{1}\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\|x_{2}\|_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}^{2}}\quad\forall\,x_{1}\in{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}^{\bot}\mathcal{E},\;x_{2}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}.

(17b)

Note that, if
\mathcal{E}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
(which for instance arises if
\mathcal{X}
is finite-dimensional), then
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{-1}
. Further, since any two complemetary spaces are isomorphic,
the space
\mathcal{X}_{-1}
is well-defined up to isomorphy. If
\mathcal{E}
is reflexive, then we have, under the preliminaries and notation from
Theorem

9
that
x
in (
11
) can be split uniquely into

x=x_{c}+x_{e}\text{ with }x_{c}\in\mathrm{C}(I;{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}^{\bot}\mathcal{E})\text{ and }x_{e}\in\mathrm{C}(I;{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}_{c}}}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}_{c})\cap\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(I;\mathcal{E}^{\prime}).

(18)

In the case where
\mathcal{X}
is a Hilbert space, we clearly have that
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
is splitting, with complementary subspace given by
\mathcal{E}^{\bot}=\overline{\mathcal{E}}^{\bot}
. In the subsequent remark, we consider a further special case which allows to relate our concepts to the extrapolation space that is commonly used in infinite-dimensional linear systems theory
[
44
,
46
]
.

Remark 13
(Extrapolation spaces in a special case).

Let us consider a special case where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\{0\}
,
\mathcal{X}
is a Hilbert space identified with its own dual, and
\mathcal{E}
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
. Then the canonical embedding
\iota
in (
10
) is actually the identity map, and the definition of the Dirac structure implies the existence of a (possibly unbounded) operator
J:\dom(J)\subset\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
that is skew-adjoint (i.e., its adjoint satisfies
J^{*}=-J
), such that

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\,(Je,e)\,\left|\vphantom{(Je,e)}\,e\in\dom(J)\,\right.\right\}.

This yields
\mathcal{E}=\dom(J)
, so that the state extrapolation space satisfies
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\dom(J)^{\prime}
, where the latter denotes the dual of
\dom(J)
with pivot space
\mathcal{X}
.

Note that, by
[
46
, Prop. 2.10.2]
, for all
\lambda
in the resolvent set of
J
, the state extrapolation space is topologically isomorphic to the completion of
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the norm

\|(\lambda I-J)^{-1}x\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

4
The energy balance

The Hamiltonian often (though not always) admits a physical interpretation as an energy, whereas the dual pairings of efforts and flows represents power.
For a port-Hamiltonian system as defined in
Definition

3
, we can apply the chain rule and the fundamental theorem of calculus to show that any classical solution on an interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

\forall\,t_{0},t_{1}\in I\text{ s.t.\ }t_{0}\leq t_{1}:\\
\mathcal{H}(x(t_{1}))-\mathcal{H}(x(t_{0}))=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\mathcal{H}(x(t)){\rm d}t=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\langle{\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}x(t),\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(x(t))\rangle_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}{\rm d}t\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\underbrace{\left\langle\!\left\langle\left(\begin{pmatrix}-{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}x(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(x(t))\\
e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right),\left(\begin{pmatrix}-{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}x(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(x(t))\\
e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right)\right\rangle\!\right\rangle}_{=0}{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\underbrace{\langle f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}}}_{\leq 0}{\rm d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\langle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t),e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}{\rm d}t\\
\leq-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\langle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t),e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}{\rm d}t.

Here,
\langle f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}}
represents the dissipated power, while

\langle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t),e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}
denotes the power supplied to the system at time
t\in I
.

Our aim here is to show that the energy balance also holds for weak solutions.
To this end, we impose the natural assumption that all efforts and flows lie in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
.
We consider two cases:
First, we establish the energy balance in the case where the Hamiltonian is quadratic.
Next, we consider the setting where
\overline{\mathcal{E}}
is a splitting subspace, but the Hamiltonian is not necessarily quadratic. However, we assume that it extends to a continuously differentiable mapping from
\mathcal{X}_{-1}
, as described in
Remark

12
. This assumption, for instance, is always satisfied in the finite-dimensional case.

The following lemmas are essential for both cases. This involves the convolution, which is defined as

\delta\ast z:\quad t\mapsto\int_{I}\delta(t-\tau)x(\tau){\rm d}\tau

for suitable functions
\delta:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathcal{X}

Proof.

Since

\forall\chi\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I_{\varepsilon};\mathcal{X}),\;\varrho\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I_{\varepsilon};\mathcal{X}^{\prime}),\;\chi_{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I_{\varepsilon};\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Q}}),\varrho_{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I_{\varepsilon};\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime})\\

Now, by using that the convolution with a smooth function is smooth, the result follows by the same argumentation as in the proof of

(b)
.

Lemma 15
.

Assume the conditions of

(x,f_{\mathcal{R}},f_{\mathcal{P}},e_{\mathcal{R}},e_{\mathcal{P}})

\forall\,t_{0},t_{1}\in I_{\varepsilon}\text{ with }t_{0}\leq t_{1}:\\

the result is proven.

Next we prove the energy balance under two alternative scenarios. One of them is quadraticity, which is defined below.

Definition 16
(Quadratic function).

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach space. A function
\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called quadratic, if

\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X}:\quad\mathcal{H}(x)=\langle x,\tfrac{1}{2}Hx+b\rangle_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}+c

(20)

for some
b\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
,
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
and a linear bounded operator
H:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with the property that it is self-dual in the sense that

\forall\,x,y\in\mathcal{X}:\quad\langle x,Hy\rangle_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}=\langle y,Hx\rangle_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}.

Next we prove an energy balance for weak solutions.

Theorem 17
.

Assume the conditions of
Definition

3
hold, and let

(x,f_{\mathcal{R}},f_{\mathcal{P}},e_{\mathcal{R}},e_{\mathcal{P}})
be a weak solution of (
4
) on
I
, with

f_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}(I;\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}})
,

e_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}(I;\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime})
,

f_{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}(I;\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}})
, and

e_{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}(I;\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime})
.
In addition, assume that at least one of the following two conditions is satisfied:

(a)

\mathcal{H}
is quadratic, or

(b)

the space of occurring co-energy variables is reflexive, its closure is splitting subspace of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{H}
extends to a continuously differentiable mapping
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{X}_{-1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and

, with

as in

.

Then

\forall\,t_{0},t_{1}\in I\text{ with }t_{0}\leq t_{1}:\quad\mathcal{H}(x(t_{1}))-\mathcal{H}(x(t_{0}))\\

(21)

and

\forall\,t_{0},t_{1}\in I\text{ with }t_{0}\leq t_{1}:\qquad\mathcal{H}(x(t_{1}))-\mathcal{H}(x(t_{0}))\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\langle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t),e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}\,{\rm d}t.

(22)

Proof.

We prove the statement separately for the two cases. Both proofs rely on a mollification of the weak solutions. To this end, let
\delta\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(I)
be a nonnegative function with

and
\delta(t)=\delta(-t)
for all

. Define

by

for

.
Further, define

,

,

,

5
Examples

We present two examples to illustrate the concept of weak solutions for port-Hamiltonian systems.
The first example is a nonlinear elasticity problem in a one-dimensional spatial domain.
As a second example, we consider a diffusion equation on a higher-dimensional spatial domain.
In both cases, the systems are infinite-dimensional and impose no constraints on the co-energy variables (i.e.,
\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
).
It turns out that our notion of weak solution coincides with the classical weak solution in the sense of standard PDE theory.

5.1
Nonlinear vibrating string

Consider a vibrating string with nonlinear elasticity.
We assume that the mass density
\rho:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is positive with
\rho^{-1}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}([a,b])
.
The restoring force depends on the spatial variable
\xi\in[a,b]
and on the strain
\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi)=\tfrac{\partial\bm{w}}{\partial\xi}(t,\xi)
.
This strain is simply the spatial derivative of the displacement
\bm{w}
.
We denote the restoring force function by
\bm{F}:[a,b]\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose there exists
L>0
such that, for each
\xi\in[a,b]
, the mapping
\epsilon\mapsto F(\xi,\epsilon)
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L
.
Then it follows that

\displaystyle\bm{F}:\quad\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b]),

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\mapsto\big(\xi\mapsto\bm{F}(\xi,\bm{\epsilon}(\xi))\big)

is a well-defined mapping.
Moreover,
\bm{F}
is the Fréchet derivative of the functional

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}:\quad\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle\epsilon(\cdot)

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{a}^{b}\int_{0}^{\epsilon(\xi)}F(\xi,\zeta){\rm d}\zeta,{\rm d}\xi

which represents the potential energy. The overall system is then described by the PDE

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{p}(t,\xi)\\
\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\begin{pmatrix}\rho(\xi)^{-1}\bm{p}(t,\xi)\\
\bm{F}(\xi,\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi))\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{p}(t,\xi)
is the infinitesimal momentum at
(t,\xi)
.

Furthermore, we consider boundary values, which, as will become clear later, are treated as external ports. These are given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-\bm{F}(\xi,\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi))|_{\xi=a}\\
\phantom{-}\bm{F}(\xi,\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi))|_{\xi=b}\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle\text{strain forces},

\displaystyle e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(\rho^{-1}\bm{p})(a,t)\\
(\rho^{-1}\bm{p})(b,t)\end{smallmatrix}\right),\qquad

\displaystyle\text{displacement velocities}.

The above equations indeed form a port-Hamiltonian system in the sense of
Definition

3
, with state

x(t)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{p}(t)\\
\bm{\epsilon}(t)\end{smallmatrix}\right):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{p}(t,\cdot)\\
\bm{\epsilon}(t,\cdot)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b];{\mathbb{R}}^{2}),

and Hamiltonian given by the sum of potential and kinetic energy, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}:\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad\mathcal{H}(\bm{p},\bm{\epsilon})=\int_{a}^{b}\rho(\xi)^{-1}\bm{p}(\xi)^{2}{\rm d}\xi+\Psi(\bm{\epsilon}).

The underlying Dirac structure is

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\,\left(\begin{pmatrix}{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\mathfrak{e}_{2}\\
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\mathfrak{e}_{1}\\
-\mathfrak{e}_{2}(a)\\
\phantom{-}\mathfrak{e}_{2}(b)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{e}_{1}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{2}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)\\
\mathfrak{e}_{1}(b)\end{pmatrix}\right)\,\left|\vphantom{\left(\begin{pmatrix}{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\mathfrak{e}_{2}\\
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\mathfrak{e}_{1}\\
-\mathfrak{e}_{2}(a)\\
\phantom{-}\mathfrak{e}_{2}(b)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{e}_{1}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{2}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{1}(a)\\
\mathfrak{e}_{1}(b)\end{pmatrix}\right)}\,\mathfrak{e}_{1},\mathfrak{e}_{2}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}([a,b])\,\right.\right\}\\
\subset\big(\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\big)\times\big(\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\big).

(24)

This is indeed a Dirac structure, as shown in
[
26
]
.
Here, the Hilbert spaces
\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])
and
{\mathbb{R}}
are identified with their respective duals (which is possible by the Riesz representation theorem
[
3
, Sec. 6.1]
).
The resistive port is trivial in this example, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}=\{0\}
. Hence it is omitted in the sequel.
We further have
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])^{2}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Let us now investigate weak solutions.
Namely, according to
Definition

5
, we have, for some interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, that

\left(\begin{pmatrix}\bm{p}\\[2.84526pt]
\bm{\epsilon}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}f_{\mathcal{P}1}\\
f_{\mathcal{P}2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}e_{\mathcal{P}1}\\[2.84526pt]
e_{\mathcal{P}2}\end{pmatrix}\right)

is a solution, if
\bm{p},\bm{\epsilon}:I\to\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])
are continuous
e_{\mathcal{P}1},e_{\mathcal{P}2},f_{\mathcal{P}1},f_{\mathcal{P}2}:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
are locally integrable, and, for
\bm{F}(t):=\bm{F}(\cdot,\bm{\epsilon}(\cdot))
,
\bm{v}(t):=\rho(\cdot)^{-1}\bm{p}(t,\cdot)
it holds for all
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}([a,b]))
that

\int_{I}\left\langle\!\left\langle\left(\begin{pmatrix}\bm{p}(t)\\
\bm{\epsilon}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}1}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}2}(t)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}(t)\\
\bm{F}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{P}1}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{P}2}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right),\left(\begin{pmatrix}{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\varrho_{2}(t)\\
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\varrho_{1}(t)\\
-(\varrho_{2}(t))(a)\\
\phantom{-}(\varrho_{2}(t))(b)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\varrho_{1}(t)\\
{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\varrho_{2}(t)\\
\phantom{-}(\varrho_{1}(t))(a)\\
\phantom{-}(\varrho_{1}(t))(b)\end{pmatrix}\right)\right\rangle\!\right\rangle{\rm d}t=0.

Let us denote
\varrho(t,\xi):=(\varrho(t))(\xi)
. Then we see that weak solutions fulfill, for all
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}[a,b])
, it holds that

\int_{I}\int_{a}^{b}\bm{p}(t,\xi){\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varrho_{1}(t,\xi)+\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi){\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varrho_{2}(t,\xi)+\bm{v}(t,\xi){\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\varrho_{2}(t,\xi)\\
+\bm{F}(t,\xi){\textstyle{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}}\varrho_{1}(t,\xi){\rm d}\xi+f_{\mathcal{P}1}(t)\varrho_{1}(t,a)+f_{\mathcal{P}2}(t)\varrho_{1}(t,b)\\
-e_{\mathcal{P}1}(t)\varrho_{2}(t,a)+e_{\mathcal{P}2}(t)\varrho_{2}(t,b){\rm d}t=0.

If we successively choose
\varrho_{1}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}([a,b]))
with
\varrho_{2}=0
, and then
\varrho_{1}=0
with
\varrho_{2}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}([a,b]))
, the boundary terms in the above integral vanish.
This yields that the two equations

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\bm{p}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\bm{F}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\bm{\epsilon}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}}\bm{v}(t,\xi),

hold in the weak sense, that is, now, in the sense of classical PDE theory
[
16
]
. General test functions
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}([a,b]))
ensure that the boundary values are correctly imposed.

We further take a look at the extrapolation space that is introduced in
Section

3
.
The representation (
24
) directly implies that the space of occurring co-energy variables is given by
\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{H}^{1}([a,b])^{2}
, and the norm (
8
) is equivalent to the standard one in
\mathrm{H}^{1}([a,b])^{2}
. Since the latter is dense in
\mathrm{L}^{2}([a,b])^{2}
, we have a trivial annihilator, that is,
{\vphantom{\mathcal{E}}}{}^{\bot}\mathcal{E}=\{0\}
. This implies that the canonical projection
\iota
in (
10
) is the identical map. Since all involved spaces are Hilbert spaces, they are all reflexive. Consequently, we can apply
Theorem

9
to see that the derivative of the state in a weak solution evolves in the dual of
\mathcal{E}
, which is given by the Sobolev space
\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{0}([a,b])^{2}
with negative exponent
[
2
]
. This particularly leads to

\bm{p},\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{0}([a,b])).

5.2
Diffusion equation

Let us consider the diffusion equation on some Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The external port is given by the Dirichlet and Neumann trace at
\partial\Omega
. That is, for some
a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
with values in the cone of positive definite matrices and the additional property that
a^{-1}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
, we consider

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\divg\big(a(\xi)\grad x(t,\xi)\big),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\Omega,

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle={x}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle e_{\mathcal{P}}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=n(\xi)^{\top}a(\xi)\grad{x}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\partial\Omega,

where
n(\xi)
is the outward normal vector of
\Omega
at
\xi\in\partial\Omega
. By introducing
e_{\mathcal{R}}=-a\grad x
,
f_{\mathcal{R}}=\grad x
, we rewrite the above system as

\displaystyle-\dot{x}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\divg e_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\Omega,

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\grad x(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\Omega,

\displaystyle e_{\mathcal{P}}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=-n(\xi)^{\top}e_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P}}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=x_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle a(\xi)f_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=e_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\,\xi\in\Omega.

This is a port-Hamiltonian system with spaces
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}=\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
\mathcal{H}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
. Further, the resistive relation is given by

\mathcal{R}=\left\{\,(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{e})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,\left|\vphantom{(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{e})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\,a\mathfrak{f}=-\mathfrak{e}\,\right.\right\},

and the Dirac structure reads

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\,\left(\begin{pmatrix}\divg\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\grad\mathfrak{e}\\
\gamma_{n}\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{e}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\gamma\mathfrak{e}\end{pmatrix}\right)\,\left|\vphantom{\left(\begin{pmatrix}\divg\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\grad\mathfrak{e}\\
\gamma_{n}\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{e}\\
\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\gamma\mathfrak{e}\end{pmatrix}\right)}\,\mathfrak{e}_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathrm{H}_{\divg}(\Omega),\,\mathfrak{e}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\,\right.\right\},

(25)

where
\mathrm{H}_{\divg}(\Omega)
denotes the space of all elements of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
whose weak divergence is in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\gamma:\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Omega)
is the trace operator,
and
\gamma_{n}:\mathrm{H}_{\divg}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Omega)
is the normal trace operator, see

[
45
]
. Note that this is indeed a Dirac structure, as can be seen using the results from
[
1
]
. The structure above is the same as the one used for the higher-dimensional wave equation as considered in
[
1
]
, with the subtle but important difference that the second port is used for the resistive part rather than for another state.

To determine the properties of weak solutions of the above system, we make use of
Definition

5
to see that, for some interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, that weak solutions

(x,f_{\mathcal{R}},f_{\mathcal{P}},-e_{\mathcal{R}},e_{\mathcal{P}})

fulfill that
x:I\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is continuous,
e_{\mathcal{R}},f_{\mathcal{R}}:I\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
e_{\mathcal{R}}=-af_{\mathcal{R}}
,
f_{\mathcal{P}}:I\to\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and
e_{\mathcal{P}}:I\to\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
are locally integrable, and
it holds for all
\varrho\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
,
\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
that

\int_{I}\left\langle\!\left\langle\left(\begin{pmatrix}x\\
f_{\mathcal{R}}\\
f_{\mathcal{P}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}x\\
-af_{\mathcal{R}}\\
e_{\mathcal{P}}\end{pmatrix}\right),\left(\begin{pmatrix}\divg{}\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
\grad{}\varrho(t)\\
\gamma_{n}\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\varrho(t)\\
\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
\gamma\varrho(t)\end{pmatrix}\right)\right\rangle\!\right\rangle{\rm d}t=0.

An expansion of the above integral yields

0=\int_{I}\int_{\Omega}x(t,\xi)\big({\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varrho(t,\xi)+\divg{}\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)\big)+f_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)\big(\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)-a(\xi)\grad\varrho(t,\xi)\big){\rm d}\xi\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega}f_{\mathcal{P}}(t,\xi)\varrho(t,\xi)+e_{\mathcal{P}}(t,\xi)n(\xi)^{\top}\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi){\rm d}\xi{\rm d}t

Now choosing
\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}\equiv 0
we obtain for all
\varrho\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega))
that

0=\int_{I}\int_{\Omega}x(t,\xi){\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varrho(t,\xi)-f_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)a(\xi)\grad\varrho(t,\xi)\big){\rm d}\xi{\rm d}t.

which particularly means that the distributional gradient of
af_{\mathcal{R}}
equals to the distributional time derivative of
x
. On the other hand,
the choice
\varrho\equiv 0
we obtain for all
\varrho:\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{1}(I;C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega))
that

0=\int_{I}\int_{\Omega}x(t,\xi)\divg{}\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)+f_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi)\varrho_{\mathcal{R}}(t,\xi){\rm d}\xi{\rm d}t,

which means that the distributional gradient of
x
equals to
f_{\mathcal{R}}
. Moreover, without going into details, the boundary terms in the overall integral expansion mean that, in the weak sense, the normal trace of
x
is given by
f_{\mathcal{P}}
, while the boundary trace of
x
is given by
e_{\mathcal{P}}
.

It can be directly seen from (
25
) that the space of occurring co-energy variables is given by
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, whence the extrapolation space is given by

\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{0}(\Omega):=\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}
. For the same reasons as in the previous example, we can use
Theorem

9
to infer that the weak solutions fulfill

x\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}(I;\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{0}(\Omega)).
