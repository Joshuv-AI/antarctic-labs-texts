. Introduction

The study of discrete versions of classical problems in geometric measure
theory, harmonic analysis, number theory, or probability
has been a central point of research in recent decades.
For a brief selection of papers in the area of interest of this work,
where the problems are investigated across different dimensions and
underlying structures
see
[

,

,
18
,

,

.
The discrete nature of these problems have found diverse
practical applications
in optimizing communication networks
[

,
37
]
,
data mining
[
1
]
,
the design of neural networks
[
13
]
,
or statistical mechanics and string theory
[
35
,

.

Within multidimensional spaces, common sense-trained intuition can be tricked when faced with the truth.
Instances where this can happen include phenomena similar to the one used
by Bubeck and Sellke
[
13
]
to argue the necessity
for neural networks to be larger than previously expected in order to
avoid fundamental mishaps.
Their proof is based on the observation that almost all pairs of points
placed on a high-dimensional sphere are almost a diameter apart
from each other.
Related phenomena are also known to occur in high-dimensional comparable-size normalized hypercubes.
There, despite the huge number of triangular shapes, which grows fast
as the dimension increases, it is found that almost all triangles are
almost equilateral
[
4
,
6
]
.

In this paper, we show that this phenomenon
can be encountered concealed even in two dimensions,
within a complex plane setting.
Let
p
be an odd prime and let
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)

denote the cyclotomic field generated by the
primitive
p
-th root of unity
\omega=\exp\big(\frac{2\pi i}{p}\big)
.
For a positive integer
N
, we denote by
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
the
symmetric
box of cyclotomic lattice points

\mathscr{B}(p,N):=\big\{a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}:a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1}\in[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big\}.

The vertices of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
are in the set

\mathcal{V}(p,N):=\big\{a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}:a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1}\in\{-N,N\}\big\}.

Notice that the size of these sets increase exponentially with
p

since
\#\mathscr{B}(p,N)=(2N+1)^{p-1}
and
\#\mathcal{V}(p,N)=2^{p-1}
.
An indication of how these
cyclotomic hypercubes
look like can be seen
in Figures
1
and
2
.
For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
,
after its successive rotation through the elements of the

\omega
-basis
\omega,\dots,\omega^{p-1}
, we let

\displaystyle\psi(\gamma):=\big(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\gamma\omega),\dots,\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\gamma\omega^{p-1})\big)

be the
trace-map image
of
\gamma

in the vector space
\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
.
Then, let
d(\alpha,\beta)
be the
cyclotomic distance
between

\alpha
and
\beta
, which is defined as the
the Euclidean distance between their
\psi
-embeddings, that is,

\displaystyle d(\alpha,\beta):=\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\psi(\beta)-\psi(\alpha)\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}.

(1)

This cyclotomic distance, referred to henceforth briefly as distance, is a suitable distance in
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
,
which finely complies with the algebraic-arithmetic structure.
Then the associated norm of
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
, which is the distance from
\alpha
to the origin
O
,
can be nicely expressed (see Lemma
2.1
)
in terms of the trace of
\alpha

and the Euclidean norm of
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
,
the vector determined by
the coefficients of
\alpha
in the
\omega
-basis.
What is noteworthy is that while the properties of the norm are preserved for any
p
, the contribution of the trace term
in the norm of a vetrex
\alpha\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)
diminishes
towards zero as
p
increases.
This is happening because almost all vertices are balanced,
meaning they have approximately equal numbers of components
equal to
N
or
-N
.
The reason lies in the combinatorial property of binomial coefficients,
which clusters their weight around their middle
(for a further in-depth discussion see the proof and an application of the argument to establish a property of equidistribution on the rays cutting through the Proth-Gilbreath triangles in the proof of
[
9

Next, we scale the cyclotomic distance so that the points furthest apart in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

are at a distance of exactly

This gives us the
normalized distance

\displaystyle\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,\beta):=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\diam(\mathscr{B}(p,N)}$}d(\alpha,\beta),

(2)

which additionally serves as a unitary means of comparing

1.1.
The main results

We outline here our main findings derived from exploring topics that include: the evaluation of the distances between an arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)

and the vertices
\beta\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)
,
finding the common size of the central angle

\widehat{\alpha O\beta}
, and
measuring distances between vertices.

First we point out that almost all triangles

\triangle=(\beta_{1},\alpha,\beta_{2})

with
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)

and
\alpha
anywhere in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

are almost isosceles.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a prime
p_{\varepsilon}
such that for all primes
p\geq p_{\varepsilon}
,
for all integers
N\geq 1
, and for every point
\alpha\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)
,
the proportion of triangles

(\beta_{1},\alpha,\beta_{2})

such that

\left|\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,\beta_{1})-\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,\beta_{2})\right|\leq\varepsilon,

where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)
,
is greater than or equal
to
1-\varepsilon
.

Next we show that for almost all
\alpha\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)
,
nearly all angles
\widehat{\alpha O\beta}
with

\beta\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)
are close to right angles.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a prime
p_{\varepsilon}

and a function
f(p)\leq 1/2

that tends to zero as
p
tends to infinity,
such that, for all
primes
p\geq p_{\varepsilon}
, for all integers
N\geq 1
,
and for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)
with

\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,O)\geq f(p)
(where

is the origin), the proportion of
angles
\widehat{\alpha O\beta}
,
with
\beta\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)
, for which

\mid\cos(\widehat{\alpha O\beta})\mid\leq\varepsilon,

is greater than or equal to
1-\varepsilon
.

An explicit expression for the function
f(p)

from Theorem
2
,
whose role is to keep triangles not too close to being degenerate, is given in
Theorem
7
.

The following
is a super-regularity statement on the vertices of the box
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
, proving that the exceptional set of
segments whose normalized lengths are not in the vicinity
of
1/\sqrt{2}

becomes arbitrarily small as

approaches infinity.

Theorem 3
.

There exist absolute positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
all integers
N\geq 1
, and all prime
p>C_{1}/\varepsilon
,
we have

\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}(p,N)^{2}}$}\#\Big\{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)^{2}:\Big|\distance_{p,N}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$}\Big|\geq\varepsilon\Big\}\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}p}$}\,.

(3)

Note that for a non-trivial result in (
3
), we need to take

p\gg 1/\varepsilon^{2}
.

By repeatedly applying Theorem
3
,
we derive a statement that contains it and is at the same time more general.

Corollary 1
.

There exist absolute positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
all integers
N\geq 1
, all integers
K\geq 2
, and
all primes

,
we have

\begin{split}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}(p,N)^{K}}$}\#\Big\{(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\in\mathcal{V}(p,N)^{K}:&\max_{1\leq j<k\leq K}\Big|\distance_{p,N}(\beta_{j},\beta_{k})-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$}\Big|\leq\varepsilon\Big\}\geq 1-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{C_{2}K^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}p}$}\,.\end{split}

It should be noted that for a non-trivial result in Corollary
1
, we need to take

p\gg K^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}
.

Let us remark that if
p
increases while

may not necessarily change,
the densities of both
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
and
\mathcal{V}(p,N)
, as sets of complex numbers,
increase much faster than their diameter.
In order to illustrate the resulting variety of polytopes
with vertices based on

In the right-hand side figure,

Nevertheless, Corollary

proves that

and the wide variety of

In other words, if we call

Corollary 2

Let

1.2.
Pyramids with leveled heights

Let us now level the pyramids by allowing the vertices of the bases to no longer be confined in the set
\mathcal{V}(p,N)
.
Now, in the significantly larger set of all points within
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
,
a result, which can be proven by following the analogous steps in the proof of Corollary
1
,
takes place.
The only difference in the result lies in the fact that the pyramids mentioned in Corollary
2
no longer have a distinguished vertex as the apex.
Thus, all the lateral faces of the pyramids are nearly identical, being almost equilateral triangles, just like all triangles formed by the vertices of the bases. Additionally,
and here lies the key distinction,
the normalized distance between almost any two points
in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

is about
1/\sqrt{6}\approx 0.408
instead of
1/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.707
, as it was in the case of triangles with vertices in
\mathcal{V}(p,N)
.

It is noteworthy that this
1/\sqrt{6}
is a typical occurrence in the context of normalized distances in various multidimensional settings (see
[
6
,
4
]
).
A particular instance is related to the phenomenon of visibility.
Extensive research has been conducted on the various visibility problems
in sets of lattice points in different dimensions, when viewed from a fixed point
or while walking along curves. For a limited selection of some of the relatively
recent works that are closer to our subject see Anderson et al.
[
3
,
2
]
,
Boca et al.
[

,

,

]
,

[

Given two points in the cyclotomic hypercube

, we say that

is

if their associated vectors in

have no other

lying on the same straight line between them.
Then, a

-polytope with vertices in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

is called

For any integer

, let
\Omega_{K}(p,N)
denote the set of all self-visible

-polytopes with vertices in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.
Then, knowing that, in the limit as

the set of self-visible

is a large set, having a positive proportion in

(see

proves that almost all self-visible

Theorem 4
.

For any
\varepsilon>0

and any integer

there exists an effectively computable

such that for any prime

, and any positive integer

\frac{1}{\#\Omega_{K}(p,N)}\#\left\{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{K})\in\Omega_{K}(p,N):\max_{1\leq j<k\leq K}\Big|\mathfrak{d}_{p,N}(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k})-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$}\right|\leq\varepsilon\Big\}\geq 1-\varepsilon.

2.
The norm, the distance and the normalized distance

Cyclotomic fields have interesting properties that make them
useful in the study of algebraic integers, class field theory, the theory of modular forms, while also having ties to other areas of mathematics such as algebraic geometry and representation theory.
In this section we will address only
the essential topics needed to reach our objectives, while for other
basic or advanced elements, we refer to the works
by Marcus
[
30
]
and Washington
[
38
]
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/p)
be a primitive
p
-th root of unity.
The cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
is a subfield of complex numbers obtained by adjoining
\omega
to the field of rational numbers.
The permutations of any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)

given by the automorphisms in the
Galois
group

Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q})
, which is isomorphic with the
multiplicative group
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,
are weighed by the trace
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}}=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits

defined as the additive map

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits:\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}

and

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)=\sum_{\sigma\in Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q})}\sigma(\alpha).

(4)

In particular, if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]=\{a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}:a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
,
which is the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Z}
.

2.1.
The norm

Let
\omega,\omega^{2},\dots,\omega^{p-1}

be a basis in
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
.
Then any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
can
be mirrored in the
(p-1)
-dimensional vector space

\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
by the map

\psi:\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}

that associates the trace
coordinates

\psi(\alpha):=\big(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega),\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{2}),\dots,\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{p-1})\big).

This linking induces naturally in
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
a
norm

through the Euclidean norm of
\psi(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
.
Thus, we define

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|:=\Big(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega)^{2}+\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{2})^{2}+\cdots+\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{p-1})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

(5)

To ascertain that
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\cdot\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|
defined by (
5
) is a norm,
we need to check that it is positive definite.
To start with, we observe that
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|
is always non-negative and
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|0\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|=0
.

Now suppose that
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|=0
and
\alpha\neq 0
.
Then, on one hand,
\psi(\alpha)=0
, which means that

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{2})=\cdots=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{p-1})=0
.
On the other hand, writing
\alpha^{-1}
in the

\omega
-basis as
\alpha^{-1}=b_{1}\omega+\cdots+b_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}

with rational coefficients
b_{1},\dots,b_{p-1}
, it follows that

b_{1}\alpha\omega+\cdots+b_{p-1}\alpha\omega^{p-1}=1
.
Taking trace on both sides, this implies the contradiction

0=b_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega)+\cdots+b_{p-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{p-1})=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(1)=p-1.

In conclusion,
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\cdot\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|
is positive definite.

Next, the homogeneity

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|b\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|=|b|\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|

holds for any
b\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)

because the trace is
\mathbb{Q}
-linear.

Finally, the triangle inequality for
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\cdot\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|

is a consequence of the triangle inequality in

\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
.

Consequently, we have ascertained that
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\cdot\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|

defined by (
5
) is indeed a norm.

2.2.
The distance

The key outcome of the previous remarks is that
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
becomes a metric space with the
norm-induced distance

d(\alpha,\beta):=\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\beta-\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|,

(6)

which satisfies the required axioms, as the norm provides the necessary properties.

It is worth noting that the distance defined in this manner
closely resembles the Euclidean distance in vector spaces and also
has properties that are well-suited to the characteristics of cyclotomic fields.
We mention here only two of them, which will be further explored in an upcoming paper.

Thus one can show that it is invariant under the action
of the Galois group

\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q})
, that is,

d(\alpha,\beta)=d\big(\sigma(\alpha),\sigma(\beta)\big)

for any automorphism
\sigma\in\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q})
.

Another noted property establishes a connection between
the distance between two points and the layout of the entire fields they generate.
More precisely, the property that is reminiscent of
Krasner’s fundamental Lemma
[
32
, Lemma 8.1.6]
)

states that if
\alpha
and
\beta
are two elements in
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)

and if
\alpha
is closer to
\beta

than half the distance to its closest away conjugate, then the entire
field generated by
\alpha
is included in the field generated by
\beta
.

2.3.
The central hypercube and the Euclidean norm

For any odd prime
p
and positive integer
N
, denote

\omega=\exp(2\pi i/p)
and let

\mathscr{B}(p,N)
be the symmetric hypercube defined by

\mathscr{B}(p,N):=\left\{a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}\in\mathbb{C}:(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1})\in\big([-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)^{p-1}\right\}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\omega].

Because
\{\omega,\omega^{2},\dots,\omega^{p-1}\}
is a basis in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
,
we know that
{\mathscr{B}}(p,N)
contains

\#{\mathscr{B}}(p,N)=(2N+1)^{p-1}

(7)

distinct elements.

For any
\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
,
we always write its associated vector of coordinates

\bm{\alpha}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}

in boldface without further additional repetitions.
Then, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
, we denote by
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}

the
Euclidean norm
of
\alpha
, which is
the Euclidean norm of
\bm{\alpha}
defined by

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}:=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}a_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(8)

2.4.
The key formula for the norm

From the definition of the trace (
4
) it follows that

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(1)=p-1
, and
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\omega)=\cdots=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\omega^{p-1})=-1
.
These imply two simple formulas for the trace. Thus, if

\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}
, then

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)=-(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{p-1}),

(9)

and

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{j})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq p-j\end{subarray}}^{p-1}(-a_{k})+(p-1)a_{p-j}=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)+pa_{p-j},\ \ \text{ for $j=1,\dots,p-1$.}

(10)

Then, by combining definition (
5
) and
relation (
10
), we see that

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha\omega^{j})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)+pa_{p-j}\right)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)+pa_{j}\right)^{2}.

Next, by expanding the square, employing (
9
) and
simplifying the expression, it yields:

\begin{split}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}&=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}+2pa_{j}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)+p^{2}a_{j}^{2}\right)\\
&=(p-1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}+2p\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}+p^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}^{2}\\
&=(p-1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}-2p\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}+p^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}^{2}\\
&=p^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}.\end{split}

Thus, we have obtained a convenient formula that we state
in the next lemma for later reference.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
,
and denote by
\bm{\alpha}
the associated
vector
\bm{\alpha}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
.
Then

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}=p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}.

(11)

As a point of reference, let us see the relation of size between the norms

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\cdot\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}
and
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\cdot\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|
.

Remark 2.1
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
,
with the associated vector
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Q}^{p-1}
,
we have

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}\leq\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|.

(12)

To prove (
12
), let

\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}
and appeal to the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality to get

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}\bigg)^{2}\leq(p-1)\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}^{2}=(p-1)\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}_{E}.

Then insert this inequality in (
11
) to find that

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}=p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}\geq p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)(p-1)\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}=\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2},

from which (
12
) follows.

2.5.
The Euclidean diameter of the box

Let
N\geq 0
and
q\geq 3
be integers. Let
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/q)
be the fundamental
q
-th root of unity.
(Our results are specifically for the case where
q
is an odd
prime, and as the concepts self-support for a general
q\geq 3
, a more comprehensive discussion is needed elsewhere.)

Let
\mathscr{B}(q,N)\subset\mathbb{C}
denote the set
of complex numbers represented as
f(\omega)
,
where
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
,
f(X)=a_{1}X+\cdots+a_{q-1}X^{q-1}
and the coefficients of
f
are in
[-N,N]
, that is,

\mathscr{B}(q,N):=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{q-1}\omega^{q-1},\ a_{1},\dots,a_{q-1}\in[-N,N]\big\}.

Then the set of vertices
\mathcal{V}_{q,N}\subset\mathscr{B}(q,N)
is obtained
when the coefficients take values only at the endpoints of the interval:

\mathcal{V}(q,N):=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{q-1}\omega^{q-1},\ a_{1},\dots,a_{q-1}\in\{-N,N\}\big\}.

Note that the geometrical shape of
\mathcal{V}(q,N)
is not changed when scaling by
N
, so that we may assume
N=1
.

Figure 2.

The set of vertices
\mathcal{V}{(q,1)}
,
their encircling circle and
the poles highlighted for
q=13,14,15,16
.

Two significant points in the box are the vertices North Pole
and the East Pole, which we denote by
\NP(q)
and
\EP(q)
.
While
\EP(q)
is real for every
q
,
\NP(q)
is purely imaginary only if
q
is odd.
When
q
is even,
\NP(q)
is placed in the first quadrant closest to the imaginary axis among the vertices above the real axis farthest from the origin, and has a mirror double across the imaginary axis.
The set of vertices and the poles are shown in Figure
2
for
q=13,14,15,16
.
The southern and western poles
\SP(q)
and
\WP(q)
are the opposites of
\NP(q)
and
\EP(q)
and are their
mirror reflections with respect to the origin.

The North Pole is the purely imaginary complex number:

\NP(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{(q-1)/2}\omega^{j}-\sum_{j=(q+1)/2}^{q-1}\omega^{j}.

(13)

The East Pole is the real number:

\EP(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor{q/4}\rfloor}\omega^{j}-\sum_{j=\lfloor{q/4}\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor{3q/4}\rfloor}\omega^{j}\sum_{j=\lfloor{3q/4}\rfloor+1}^{q-1}\omega^{j}.

(14)

Then the Euclidean diameter of
\mathscr{B}(q,N)
is

\diam\nolimits_{E}(\mathscr{B}(q,N))=N\cdot\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\NP(q)-\big(-\NP(q)\big)\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{2N}{i}$}\cdot\NP(q),\ \text{ if $q$ is odd}.

(15)

2.6.
The normalized distance

To ensure a unified reference for comparison from hypercube to hypercube as
N
or
p

change, we will use in each hypercube
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

a normalized distance
\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,\beta)
,
which depends on both
N
and
p
, but has the property that the diameter of

\mathscr{B}(p,N)
measured by
\distance_{p,N}
is equal to
1
.
In order to do this, we need to know the diameter of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
,
which we will calculate in the following lemma.

Let
\diam\big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\big)
denote the
diameter
of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
,
which is defined as the maximum distance between any two points in the hypercube,
that is,

\diam\big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\big):=\max_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\beta-\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|\,.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
N
be a positive integer. Then

\diam\big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\big)=2Np(p-1)^{1/2}.

(16)

Proof.

From the expression (
11
) of the norm, we see that

\alpha
and
\beta
are diametrically opposite points in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

if they are in a certain position that makes

p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}
as large as possible,
where
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
are the coordinate vectors associated
with
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Next let us note that

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}\leq(p-1)(2N)^{2}\ \text{ and }\ \big(\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\beta-\alpha)\big)^{2}\geq 0

(17)

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.

But, if we take
\alpha
and
\beta
, for example, with alternating
coefficients, such as

\alpha_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(-1)^{j}N\omega^{j}\ \text{ and }\ \beta_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(-1)^{j+1}N\omega^{j},

we see that
\beta_{0}=-\alpha_{0}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha_{0})=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\beta_{0})=0
,
because
p
is odd.
Then,
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\beta_{0}-\alpha_{0})=0
and also
we have

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\alpha}_{0}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}=(p-1)(2N)^{2}
,
that is, both inequalities in (
17
) are satisfied
with equalities.
As a consequence,

\diam\Big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\Big)=\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\beta_{0}-\alpha_{0}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|=\big(p^{2}\cdot(p-1)(2N)^{2}\big)^{1/2}=2Np(p-1)^{1/2},

which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now, taking into account formula (
16
), we can define
the normalized measure that allows for a standardized comparison of
the distances in different boxes
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
when
N
and
p
vary.
Thus, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathscr{B}(p,N)
, we define the
normalized distance

\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,\beta)
by

\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,\beta):=\frac{1}{2Np(p-1)^{1/2}}d(\alpha,\beta).

(18)

Note that, while this definition guarantees that the normalized distance
between any two points
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
is at most
1
, the formula can also be used for any pair of points
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, even if their mutual normalized distance may be greater than
1
.
Also, from the proof of Lemma
2.2
we may say that
the normalized diameter of both
\mathscr{B}(p,N)

and
\mathcal{V}(p,N)
is equal to
1
.

3.
On the distances from an arbitrary point to the vertices

3.1.
The average distance from a point to the vertices

Let
A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})
denote the average normalized
square distances from
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
to the vertices of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.
The next lemma provides an exact formula for

A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime, let
N\geq 1
be integer
and let
\mathcal{V}
be the set of the vertices of the origin-centered hypercube
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.
Then, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, the average of the
square normalized distance from
\alpha
to
\mathcal{V}
is

\begin{split}A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})&:=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\big(\distance_{p,N}(\alpha,x)\big)^{2}=\big(\distance_{p,N}(0,\alpha)\big)^{2}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}p^{-1}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}p^{-2}\,.\end{split}

(19)

Note that the average
A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})
depends on the position of

\alpha
in
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
but not on the size
N
of the box.

Proof.

In the
\omega
-basis,
let
\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}

and denote a generic element of
\mathcal{V}
by

x=x_{1}\omega+\cdots+x_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}
.
Correspondingly, we denote by

\bm{\alpha}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1})
and

\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-1})\in\{-N,N\}^{p-1}
the associated vectors.
Let
D=2Np(p-1)^{1/2}
denote the diameter of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.
Then, employing formula (
11
),
we have

\begin{split}A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})&=\frac{p^{2}}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(a_{j}-x_{j})^{2}-\frac{p+1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}(a_{j}-x_{j})(a_{k}-x_{k})\\
&=\Sigma_{A}^{\prime}-\Sigma_{A}^{\prime\prime},\end{split}

(20)

where
\Sigma_{A}^{\prime}
and
\Sigma_{A}^{\prime\prime}

stand for the minuend and the subtrahend above.

The minuend is:

\begin{split}\Sigma_{A}^{\prime}&=\frac{p^{2}}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(a_{j}^{2}-2a_{j}x_{j}+x_{j}^{2})\\
&=\frac{p^{2}}{D^{2}}\big(\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}+(p-1)N^{2}\big),\end{split}

(21)

because
x_{j}^{2}=N^{2}
for any
x\in\mathcal{V}
and the number of
vertices with the
j
-th component equal to
N
or
-N
are the same,
so that the sum of the terms with
x_{j}
at the first power vanishes.

Next to calculate
\Sigma_{A}^{\prime\prime}
, note that
using the same fact on the cancellation in the sum
of the terms with components at the first power, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}(a_{j}-x_{j})(a_{k}-x_{k})&=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}\bigg)^{2}-2\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}x_{k}+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}x_{j}x_{k}\\
&=\#\mathcal{V}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}x_{j}x_{k}.\end{split}

(22)

In the last multiple sum, we separate the terms on the diagonal from the off-diagonal ones, to obtain:

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}x_{j}x_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{p-1}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}x_{j}x_{k}=\#\mathcal{V}\cdot(p-1)N^{2}.\end{split}

(23)

(Here, to see that the sum of the off-diagonal terms is zero, note on one hand that for each
i,j
fixed, there are four possible values for the pairs of components
x_{j},x_{k}
, namely
-N,-N
;
-N,N
;
N,-N
;
N,N
,
and in two of these instances the product
x_{j}x_{k}
is
N^{2}
,
while in the other two the product
x_{j}x_{k}
is
-N^{2}
.
On the other hand, the number of vertices
x\in\mathcal{V}
with the components
x_{j},x_{k}
being in any of these four instances is the same.)

Thus, using (
22
) and (
23
), we obtain

\begin{split}\Sigma_{A}^{\prime\prime}&=\frac{1}{D^{2}}(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}+\frac{1}{D^{2}}(p-1)(p+1)N^{2}.\end{split}

(24)

Inserting both (
21
) and (
24
) into (
20
) yields

\begin{split}A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})&=\frac{1}{\left(\diam\big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\big)\right)^{2}}\big(p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}+N^{2}(p^{3}-2p^{2}+1)\big).\end{split}

(25)

On using (
11
) and (
18
),
formula (
25
)
can be rewritten as (
19
),
thus completing the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

3.2.
A moment about the mean

Here we calculate the second moment

M_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})

of the square normalized
distance from
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
to
\mathcal{V}(p,N)
about the average
A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime, let
N\geq 1
be integer,
let
\mathcal{V}
be the set of the vertices of the
origin-centered hypercube
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
,
and let
D=\diam\big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\big)
.
Then, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, the second moment
about the mean of the square normalized distance from
\alpha

to
\mathcal{V}
is

\begin{split}M_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})&:=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\big(\distance_{p,N}^{2}(\alpha,x)-A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})\big)^{2}\\
&\phantom{:}=\frac{2N^{2}}{D^{4}}\Big((N^{2}+2\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2})p^{4}-(N^{2}+2\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2})p^{3}-(3N^{2}+2\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2})p^{2}\\
&\phantom{=\frac{2N^{2}}{D^{4}}\Big((N^{2}+2\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2})p^{4}-(N^{2}.}+(N^{2}-2\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2})p+2(N^{2}-\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2})\Big).\end{split}

(26)

Note that since

\displaystyle\frac{2N^{2}}{D^{4}}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{8N^{2}p^{4}(p-1)^{2}}$}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{8N^{2}p^{6}}$}+O\big(N^{-2}p^{-7}\big),

while
\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}\leq N^{2}p

and
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}\leq N^{2}p^{2}

it follows that
M_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})
is
O(p^{-1})
,
more precisely

\displaystyle 0\leq M_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{3}{p}$}

(27)

for all integer
N\geq 1
and all odd primes
p
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}
be fixed,
let
x=x_{1}\omega+\cdots+x_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}
denote the elements of
\mathcal{V}
,
and let
\bm{\alpha}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1})\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, and

\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-1})\in\{-N,N\}^{p-1}
be the associated vectors.

Expanding the square, the second moment can be rewritten as

\begin{split}M_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})&=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{4}(\alpha,x)-2A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})\cdot\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{2}(\alpha,x)+\big(A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})\big)^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{4}(\alpha,x)-\big(A_{p,N}(\alpha,\mathcal{V})\big)^{2},\end{split}

(28)

where
D=\diam\big(\mathscr{B}(p,N)\big)
.
We employ formula (
11
) to express the sum above as

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{4}(\alpha,x)&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{4}}$}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha-x\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{4}\\
&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{4}}$}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{x}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha-x)^{2}\Big)^{2}\\
&=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\prime}+\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}+\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime\prime},\end{split}

(29)

where

\begin{split}\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\prime}&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{p^{4}}{D^{4}}$}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{x}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{4}\\
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}&=-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{2p^{2}(p+1)}{D^{4}}$}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{x}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha-x)^{2}\\
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime\prime}&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{(p+1)^{2}}{D^{4}}$}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha-x)^{4}.\end{split}

(30)

Next, in the following subsection, we bring out a few formulas that will be used several times afterwards.
The formulas are based on sums that cancel out due to the symmetry of the vertices and
either the fact that the order of the added monomials is odd or that, in the case of an even order, the summation is only done over the ‘off-diagonal’ terms.

3.2.1.
Multinomial sums on vertices that cancel out

For an arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
we write

\alpha=a_{1}\omega+\cdots+a_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}

and let
\bm{\alpha}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p-1})
be its associated vector.
We also let

x=x_{1}\omega+\cdots+x_{p-1}\omega^{p-1}\in\mathcal{V}
denote a generic vertex of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.

The following sum of linear terms cancels out as seen in the derivation of relations (
21
):

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}x_{j}=0.

(31)

In the analogous quadratic form, we separate the diagonal and the off-diagonal terms. Then, since the sum of the latter cancels out, as noted while deriving formula (
23
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}a_{j}a_{k}x_{j}x_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}^{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{p-1}a_{k}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}x_{j}x_{k}=\#\mathcal{V}\cdot\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}N^{2},

(32)

and

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}a_{j}a_{k}x_{m}x_{n}&=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}a_{k}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}}\bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}x_{m}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{p-1}x_{m}x_{n}\bigg)\\
&=\#\mathcal{V}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}N^{2}(p-1).\end{split}

(33)

For a sum of cubic monomials in the components of
\bm{x}
, we have:

4.
Distances from vertices to vertices

4.1.
The average distance between two vertices

Let
A_{p,N}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V})
denote the average normalized
square distances between two vertices of
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.
The next lemma provides an exact formula for

A_{p,N}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime, let
N\geq 1
be integer
and let
\mathcal{V}
be the set of the vertices of the origin-centered hypercube
\mathscr{B}(p,N)
.
Then

\begin{split}A_{p,N}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V})&:=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\big(1-p^{-1}-p^{-2}\big)\,.\end{split}

(41)

Proof.

Using formula (
19
), we have

\begin{split}A_{p,N}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V})&=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\\
&=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(\distance_{p,N}^{2}(0,\alpha)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}p^{-1}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}p^{-2}\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}^{2}(0,\alpha)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}\big(1-p^{-1}-p^{-2}\big)\,.\end{split}

(42)

With the diameter
D
given by
D^{2}=4N^{2}p^{2}(p-1)

and formula for the norm (
11
),
the first term above is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}(0,\alpha)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\alpha\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\big(p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{2}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\alpha)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}p^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\alpha_{j}^{2}-\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}(p+1)\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}.

Changing the order of summation and observing as in (
23
)
and in the remark that followed that the sum of the off-diagonal
in the second term cancels out, we further obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}}\distance_{p,N}(0,\alpha)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}p^{2}(p-1)\Big(2^{p-2}N^{2}+2^{p-2}(-N)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}D^{2}}(p+1)(p-1)\Big(2^{p-2}N^{2}+2^{p-2}(-N)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}$}\big(1-p^{-1}-p^{-2}\big)\,.

The lemma then follows on inserting this result into
the right hand-side of (
42
).
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime, let

be integer
and let
\mathcal{V}
be the set of the vertices of the origin-centered hypercube

&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{4(p-1)}$}\big(p-2+p^{-1}+2p^{-2}-5p^{-3}-4p^{-4}\big)\,.\end{split}

(43)

Proof.

With the diameter

satisfying

and using formula (

In the following, let

4.1.1.

\begin{split}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\alpha}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|_{E}^{4}&=\big((b_{1}-a_{1})^{2}+\cdots+(b_{p-1}-a_{p-1})^{2}\big)^{2}\\

(45)

\begin{split}\Sigma_{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime\prime}&=-2p^{2}(p+1)2^{2p-3}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{a_{j}\in\{-N,N\}}a_{j}^{4}\cdot 2\\
&\quad-2p^{2}(p+1)2^{2p-4}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

\begin{split}\Sigma_{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime\prime}&=-4p^{2}(p+1)2^{2p-3}(p-1)\cdot 2N^{4}-4p^{2}(p+1)2^{2p-4}(p-1)(p-2)\cdot 4N^{4}\\

(46)

4.1.3.
The estimation of
\Sigma_{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime\prime\prime}

The terms added in

5.3.

Although there are many similarities, there are also differences between the super-regularity properties in high dimensional hypercubes in Euclidean spaces

One such difference is that in the Euclidean hypercube

\displaystyle\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\beta}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}_{E}=(p-1)N^{2}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ 0\leq\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\beta)^{2}\leq(p-1)^{2}N^{2}.

\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\beta\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}=p^{2}\left|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\left|\bm{\beta}\right|\hskip 2.5pt\!\!\right|^{2}_{E}-(p+1)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\beta)^{2}

\displaystyle(p-1)N^{2}\leq d(O,\beta)^{2}\leq(p-1)p^{2}N^{2}.

(52)

\beta

This is due to the fact that the binomial coefficients near the middle overwhelmingly dominate the sum of the others (see the proof and the application discussed in
[

Remark 5.1
