Abstract
. We characterize all time-frequency representations that satisfy a general covariance property: any weak*-continuous bilinear mapping that intertwines time-frequency shifts on the configuration space with time-frequency shifts on phase space is a multiple of a metaplectic time-frequency representation.

81S30, 22E46

1.
Introduction

Many important time-frequency representations possess the covariance property: a time-frequency shift of a
function is transformed to a shift of the time-frequency representation. For one of the most
central time-frequency representations, namely the Wigner distribution, this property looks as
follows. Let

(1.1)

W(f,g)(x,\omega)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x+\tfrac{t}{2})\overline{g(x-\tfrac{t}{2})}e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot t}\,dt,\quad x,\omega\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,,

be the (cross-) Wigner distribution of two functions
f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The translation operator or shift on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is defined by
T_{x}f(t)=f(t-x)
for
t,x,\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
the frequency shift or modulation operator is given by
M_{\omega}f(t)=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot t}f(t)
. Its Fourier transform is
\widehat{M_{\omega}f}=T_{-\omega}\hat{f}
, whence
frequency shift. Writing
\lambda=(x,\omega)
for a point in the
time-frequency space, the symmetrized composition is the time-frequency shift

\rho(\lambda)=\rho(x,\omega)=T_{x/2}M_{\omega}T_{x/2},\quad\lambda=(x,\omega)\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\,.

Then the covariance property states that for all
\lambda,z\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}},f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

(1.2)

W(\rho(\lambda)f,\rho(\lambda)g)(z)=W(f,g)(z-\lambda)=(T_{\lambda}W(f,g))(z)\,.

Many other useful time-frequency representations possess this useful and intuitive property, for instance, the
spectrogram or the Born-Jordan distribution.
Therefore, covariant time-frequency representations have been studied
intensely in the engineering literature and in physics, and ultimately
were completely classified by L. Cohen
[
3
,
4
]
. If a
time-frequency representation
{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)
is covariant, then
{\mathfrak{R}}
must have the form

{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)=W(f,g)\ast\Psi
for some distribution
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
. See also
[
14
, Sec. 4.5]
for a mathematical treatment of
Cohen’s class.

A second look at the Wigner distribution reveals a more delicate structure.
By viewing the Wigner distribution as a sequilinear mapping, we can apply
different time-frequency shifts to
f
and
g
and obtain the following
formula (e.g.,
[
11
, Prop. 1.94]
or
[
14
, Prop. 4.3.2]
)

\displaystyle W(\rho(\lambda)f,\rho(\mu)g)(z)

\displaystyle=e^{\pi i\lambda\cdot{\mathcal{J}}\mu}e^{2\pi iz\cdot{\mathcal{J}}(\lambda-\mu)}W(f,g)\big(z-\frac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\big)

(1.3)

\displaystyle=\rho\Big(\tfrac{\lambda+\mu}{2},{\mathcal{J}}(\lambda-\mu)\Big)W(f,g)(z)\,,

with
{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}
.
Thus the Wigner distribution with two
different
time-frequency shifts is not
only
a shift of the Wigner distribution, but also contains a modulation, in other words, the result is a time-frequency shift of
the original Wigner distribution. Formula (
1.3
) is a general
covariance property, and it says that the transform of time-frequency shifts is
again a time-frequency shift. Note, however, that on the
right-hand side the time-frequency shift has twice as many variables. The general
covariance property (
1.3
) is a fundamental property of the Wigner
distribution and plays an important role in the time-frequency approach
to pseudodifferential operators, see e.g.,
[
15
]
and the recent
monograph
[
5
]
.

We say that a time-frequency representation, i.e., a bilinear mapping
{\mathfrak{R}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, possesses the general covariance property, if it
maps time-frequency shifts of
f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
to a time-frequency shift of
{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)
.
Formally,
there exists a function
\Phi_{{\mathfrak{R}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}
, such that for all
f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and all
\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}
one has

(1.4)

{\mathfrak{R}}(\rho(\lambda)f,\rho(\gamma)g)=c\rho(\Phi_{\mathfrak{R}}(\lambda,\gamma)){\mathfrak{R}}(f,g).

Note that in this definition
\rho
occurs with different dimensions.
On the left-hand side

\rho(\lambda)f
acts on a function of
d
variables, on the right hand side

\rho(\Phi_{{\mathfrak{R}}}(\lambda,\mu))
acts on a distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
.
In the sequel we will use only the latter version, therefore, we will not introduce a different notation.

It is natural to assume that
\Phi
is measurable.
In this paper, we derive a complete classification of all time-frequency representations that satisfy the
general covariance property (
1.4
).

For the formulation of the main result, we recall the definition of a
metaplectic operator. By the theorem of Stone-von Neumann
[
11
]
for every
symplectic matrix
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
there exists a unitary
operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, such that

(1.5)

\rho(\mathcal{A}\lambda)=\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rho(\lambda)\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1},

that is,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
arises as an intertwining operator, and is called
the
metaplectic operator
associated to
\mathcal{A}
.

With these preparations, the main result about time-frequency representations obeying the general covariance property goes as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Main result)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{R}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero, bilinear,
separately weak*-continuous mapping satisfying the general
covariance property (
1.4
) with a measurable function
\Phi
.
Then there exist
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
a\neq 0
, and
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
such that

(1.6)

{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)=a\,\hat{\mathcal{A}}(f\otimes g),\qquad f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}.

The general covariance property seems to be very strong. Although we
start with a mapping from
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, the actual
mapping properties are much stronger.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{R}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
. Under the assumptions of
Theorem
1.1
,
{\mathfrak{R}}
has the following properties.

(a)

The function
\Phi_{\mathfrak{R}}
is unique and linear.

(b)

{\mathfrak{R}}
maps
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
into
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.

(c)

{\mathfrak{R}}
is non-degenerate.

(d)

{\mathfrak{R}}
extends to an isometry
{\mathfrak{R}}:{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\times{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\to{L^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, i.e.,

(1.7)

\lVert{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)\rVert_{2}=|a|\,\lVert f\rVert_{2}\lVert g\rVert_{2},\quad f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

(e)

The range
\{{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g):f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\}
spans a dense
subspace of
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and a weak
∗
-dense subspace
of
{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.

Note that we only assume that
\Phi_{\mathfrak{R}}
is
measurable!

It turns out that time-frequency representations of the form
\hat{\mathcal{A}}(f\otimes\bar{g})

were recently introduced by Cordero and
Rodino
[
9
]
as a generalization of the Wigner
distribution, the short-time Fourier transform,
or Beyer’s time-frequency representations
[
1
]
. They are called metaplectic time-frequency representations or
the metaplectic Wigner distributions associated to
\mathcal{A}
, or shortly,
the
\mathcal{A}
-Wigner distributions.
In a series of papers, Cordero, Rodino, and their
collaborators
[
8
,
6
,
7
]
have shown that metaplectic time-frequency representations are a versatile tool for the refined investigation of pseudodifferential operators and function spaces.
Uncertainty principles for metaplectic time-frequency representations were investigated
in
[
16
,
17
]
.

Theorem
1.1
provides an intrinsic, structural argument as
to why metaplectic time-frequency representations are natural and are bound to appear in time-frequency analysis.

Remark 1.3
.

Usually, a time-frequency representation is treated as a sesquilinear form rather than
as a bilinear form.
Theorem
1.1
can be equivalently restated in terms of
sesquilinear forms by a tiny modification. If
{\mathfrak{R}}
is bilinear,
then
the corresponding sesquilinear form
\overline{{\mathfrak{R}}}(f,g)\coloneqq{\mathfrak{R}}(f,\overline{g})

is non-zero, continuous, and satisfies

\overline{{\mathfrak{R}}}(\rho(\lambda)f,\rho(\gamma)g)=c\rho(\Phi_{\overline{{\mathfrak{R}}}}(\lambda,\gamma))\overline{{\mathfrak{R}}}(f,g),\quad\Phi_{\overline{{\mathfrak{R}}}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\Phi_{{{\mathfrak{R}}}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\gamma_{1},-\gamma_{2}).

The change of the intertwining function is due to

\overline{\rho(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})g}=\rho(\gamma_{1},-\gamma_{2})\overline{g}
. The conclusion of Theorem
1.1

\overline{{\mathfrak{R}}}(f,g)=a\,\hat{\mathcal{A}}(f\otimes\bar{g})
remains the same.

The remainder of the paper is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
We first use the continuity assumptions of
{\mathfrak{R}}
to identify it with a continuous linear mapping
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
that satisfies a general covariance property.
Here, the arguments are analytical and are based on a bilinear
version of the Schwartz kernel theorem.
In the next step, we exploit the underlying Lie group structure of the problem to
deduce that
\Phi
must be a linear
mapping.
Finally,
the general covariance
property (
1.4
)
implies that
\Phi
is symplectic. The arguments here are purely algebraic.
We put these three aspects together to derive the final form of

{\mathfrak{R}}
.

We would like to offer our thanks and appreciation to Christian Bargetz whose
advice helped us to compress our original proof of Lemma
3.1
to a few lines.

2.
Symplectic matrices and metaplectic operators

The symmetric time-frequency shifts

\rho(\lambda)=\rho(x,\omega)=T_{x/2}M_{\omega}T_{x/2},\quad\lambda=(x,\omega)\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}},

are a projective unitary representation of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}
.
They satisfy

(2.1)

\rho(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})=e^{\pi i\lambda_{1}\cdot{\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{2}}\rho(\lambda_{1})\rho(\lambda_{2}),\qquad{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
holds

(2.2)

\rho(\lambda)^{-1}=\rho(-\lambda),\qquad\text{ and }\quad\rho(n\lambda)=\rho(\lambda)^{n}

The symplectic matrices are those matrices which preserve the standard symplectic form
[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]=\lambda_{1}\cdot{\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{2}
, i.e.,

(2.3)

{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}=\left\{\mathcal{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d}:\mathcal{A}^{t}{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{J}}\right\}.

This is a distinguished group of matrices in time-frequency analysis

[
14
,
11
,
10
]
. By the Stone-von
Neumann theorem, for every symplectic matrix
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d\times 2d}
,
there exists a unitary operator
\mathcal{A}
that is unique up to scaling
with
c\in{\mathbb{T}}

[
11
,
14
,
10
]

satisfying

(2.4)

\rho(\mathcal{A}\lambda)=\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rho(\lambda)\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\,.

Thus
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
arises as an intertwining operator, and is called the
metaplectic operator
associated to
\mathcal{A}
.
The metaplectic operators are isomorphisms on the Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
, the space of rapidly decaying functions
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
,
and by duality, on its dual space
{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
.

3.
Analytic consequences

In this section, we exploit the weak*-continuity of the bilinear form
{\mathfrak{R}}
.
Explicitly this means that for every sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
converging to
f\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
in the topology of
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and every
g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
, we have

(3.1)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}{\mathfrak{R}}(f_{n},g)={\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)\quad\text{ in the weak*-topology on }{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})},

and, likewise, for all
(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
converging to
g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and all
f\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}

(3.2)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g_{n})={\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)\quad\text{ in the weak*-topology on }{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}.

Throughout, we denote with
\langle T,f\rangle=T(f)
the evaluation of the tempered distribution
T\in{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
at
f\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
.
We will use the following bilinear version of the Schwartz kernel theorem
[
22
, p. 531]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every separately weak*-continuous bilinear mapping

{\mathfrak{R}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})},

there exists a unique weak*-continuous linear operator
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}

such that for all
f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and all
H\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}

(3.3)

\langle{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g),H\rangle=\langle{\mathfrak{T}}(f\otimes g),H\rangle.

In addition, there exists a kernel
K=K_{\mathfrak{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}})
such that

(3.4)

\langle{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g),H\rangle=\langle K,f\otimes g\otimes H\rangle,\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})},\,H\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}.

Proof.

As we are unaware of an explicit reference, we offer a proof sketch
for completeness. The proof uses the same arguments as the numerous
kernel theorems in functional analysis.
We recall that
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
is a
nuclear Fréchet space and hence a Montel space, i.e., bounded,
closed sets in
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
are compact and vice versa, see
[
22
, Prop. 34.4]
. This
property implies that a weak
∗
-continuous map from
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
to
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}^{\prime}

is automatically continuous, when
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}^{\prime}
is endowed with the strong
topology (topology of uniform convergence on bounded
sets)
[
22
, p. 358]
. Consequently, for fixed
g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
the map
f\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\mapsto{\mathfrak{T}}(f,g)\in{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
is strongly continuous, and likewise
g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\mapsto{\mathfrak{T}}(f,g)\in{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
is strongly continuous for every
f\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
. Since
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
is a Fréchet space, the separate continuity implies
the joint continuity of
{\mathfrak{T}}
on the Cartesian product
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}

[
20
, Thm. 2.17]
.

Now we extend
{\mathfrak{T}}
to a linear map on the algebraic tensor product

{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
. We endow
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
with the projective
tensor product topology. This is by definition the finest topology
such that the map
(f,g)\mapsto f\otimes g
is continuous.
As a consequence,
{\mathfrak{T}}
is (strongly) continuous on
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
. The completion of
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
with respect to this topology is
the projective tensor product
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\otimes_{\pi}\,{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
and is
canonically isomorphic with
\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})

[
22
, Thm. 51.6.]
. By continuity,

{\mathfrak{T}}
can now be extended to a (strongly) continuous map from
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\otimes_{\pi}\,{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\cong{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
to
{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
. Now the Schwarz kernel theorem
provides a kernel proving the representation (
3.4
).
∎

We now relate the bilinear kernel theorem to the setup in Theorem
1.1
.
We show that the linear extension
{\mathfrak{T}}
of
{\mathfrak{R}}
also satisfies a covariance property.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{R}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero, separately weak*-continuous bilinear form satisfying the covariance property (
1.4
).
Then there exists a non-zero weak*-continuous operator
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
and
a function
\Phi:{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
satisfying

(3.5)

{\mathfrak{T}}(f\otimes g)={\mathfrak{R}}(f,g),\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}

and the covariance property

(3.6)

{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda)F=c\rho(\Phi(\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}F,\quad\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}},\,F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}.

The function
\Phi
is measurable if and only if
\Phi_{\mathfrak{R}}
is measurable.

Proof.

Since
{\mathfrak{R}}
is separately weak
∗
-continuous, Lemma

3.1
asserts the existence of the associated linear operator

{\mathfrak{T}}
.
We claim that

(3.7)

\Phi(\lambda)=\Phi(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})=\Phi_{\mathfrak{R}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{4}),\quad\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

has the desired property.
The modification of
\Phi
is a
consequence of the reordering of time-frequency shifts for
\rho
on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}
as follows:

(3.8)

\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4})(f\otimes g)=\rho(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3})f\otimes\rho(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{4})g,\quad\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

This implies that (
3.6
)
is satisfied by the elementary tensors, and by linearity, on their linear span.
Since time-frequency shifts are weak*-continuous on
{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}

[
14
, Cor. 11.2.22]
, the identity
{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda)=\rho(\Phi(\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}

extends from
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\otimes{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
to
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
by continuity and density.
∎

4.
Consequences of the General Covariance Property

In this section, we focus on the implications of the
covariance property (
1.4
), and by extension, of the covariance property (
3.6
),
and show that
\Phi
is necessarily a symplectic linear
transformation. For the proof we will use some basic, but deep facts
about homomorphisms between Lie groups.

We split the proof into several lemmata.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero continuous linear operator satisfying the general covariance property (
3.6
). Then
{\mathfrak{T}}
is one-to-one.

Proof.

If
{\mathfrak{T}}F_{0}=0
for a non-zero
F_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, then for all linear combinations
F=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\rho(\lambda_{j})F_{0}
we obtain

(4.1)

{\mathfrak{T}}F=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda_{j})F_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\rho(\Phi(\lambda_{j})){\mathfrak{T}}F_{0}=0.

Therefore,
{\mathfrak{T}}
vanishes on the dense subspace of finite linear combinations. By continuity,
{\mathfrak{T}}=0
, contradicting the assumption.
∎

Lemma
4.1
implies the no-degeneracy stated in Corollary
1.2
(c).

Recall that we denote the skew-symmetric form with
[\lambda,\mu]=\lambda\cdot{\mathcal{J}}\mu
,
\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero continuous linear operator satisfying the general covariance property (
3.6
).
Then

(4.2)

\rho(\Phi(\lambda+\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F=e^{\pi i([\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)])}\rho(\Phi(\lambda)+\Phi(\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F,\quad F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})},\ \lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}},

and

(4.3)

[\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)]\in{\mathbb{Z}},\quad\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}.

Proof.

Let
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero function,
\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
. Then

(4.4)

\begin{split}\rho(\Phi(\lambda+\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F&=c^{-1}\,{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda+\mu)F=c^{-1}\,e^{\pi i[\lambda,\mu]}{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda)\rho(\mu)F\\
&=c\,e^{\pi i[\lambda,\mu]}\rho(\Phi(\lambda))\rho(\Phi(\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F\\
&=c\,e^{\pi i([\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)])}\rho(\Phi(\lambda)+\Phi(\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F\\
&=c\,e^{-\pi i([\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)])}\rho(\Phi(\lambda)+\Phi(\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F.\end{split}

The last equality here is obtained by switching the places of
\lambda
and
\mu
(recall that
[\lambda,\mu]=-[\mu,\lambda]
).
By Lemma
4.1
, the chain of equalities can only hold if the phase factors are equal, i.e.,

(4.5)

e^{2\pi i([\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)])}=1.

This is equivalent to (
4.3
).
∎

To show that
\Phi
is linear, we introduce the subgroup

(4.6)

H=\{\nu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}:\ \exists c_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{C}}\text{ with }\rho(\nu){\mathfrak{T}}F=c_{\nu}{\mathfrak{T}}F\text{ for all }F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\},

This is the
projective kernel
of the representation
\rho
restricted to the subspace
\mathrm{ran}\,{\mathfrak{T}}=\{{\mathfrak{T}}F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}:F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\}
, and by continuity of
{\mathfrak{T}}
and
\rho(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
,
to its closure
\overline{\mathrm{ran}\,{\mathfrak{T}}}
in
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.
Let

(4.7)

q_{H}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}/H

denote the quotient map.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero continuous linear operator satisfying the general covariance property (
3.6
).
Further, let
H
be the projective kernel of
\rho
defined in (
4.6
).
Then
H
is a closed subgroup of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
.

Proof.

Since
\rho
is a projective representation of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
, it follows that
H
is a group:

(4.8)

\rho(\nu+\eta){\mathfrak{T}}F=e^{\pi i[\nu,\eta]}c_{\nu}c_{\eta}{\mathfrak{T}}F,\quad F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})},\,\nu,\eta\in H.

The continuity of the mapping

\lambda\mapsto\rho(\lambda){\mathfrak{T}}F

implies that
H
is closed and the mapping
H\to{\mathbb{C}},\ \nu\mapsto c_{\nu},

is continuous.
To see this, let
(\nu_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq H
be a convergent sequence with limit
\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
. Then for all
F,G\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}

(4.9)

\langle\rho(\nu){\mathfrak{T}}F,G\rangle=\langle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\rho(\nu_{n}){\mathfrak{T}}F,G\rangle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}c_{\nu_{n}}\langle{\mathfrak{T}}F,G\rangle.

Since the limit in the middle converges, so does the limit on the right-hand side. Since
{\mathfrak{T}}
is not zero, there exist
F,G
with
\langle{\mathfrak{T}}F,G\rangle\neq 0
. Thus,

c_{\nu}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}c_{\nu_{n}}

is
well-defined and satisfies
\rho(\nu){\mathfrak{T}}F=c_{\nu}{\mathfrak{T}}F
,
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero continuous linear operator satisfying the general covariance property (
3.6
).
Further, let
H
be the projective kernel of
\rho
defined in (
4.6
).
Then
the map

(4.10)

q_{H}\circ\Phi:{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}/H,\quad\lambda\mapsto(q_{H}\circ\Phi)(\lambda)

is one-to-one.

Proof.

We want to show that for all
\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
and
\nu\in H

(4.11)

\Phi(\lambda)=\Phi(\mu)+\nu\quad\implies\lambda=\mu.

In a single chain of inequalities, we have for all
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}

(4.12)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{T}}(\rho(\lambda)F)

\displaystyle=c\,\rho(\Phi(\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}F=c\,\rho(\Phi(\mu)+\nu){\mathfrak{T}}F

(4.13)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:rho_of_sum}}{=}c\,e^{\pi i[\Phi(\mu),\nu]}\rho(\Phi(\mu))\rho(\nu){\mathfrak{T}}F

(4.14)

\displaystyle=c\,c_{\nu}\,e^{\pi i[\Phi(\mu),\nu]}\rho(\Phi(\mu)){\mathfrak{T}}F

(4.15)

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{T}}(c\,c_{\nu}\,e^{\pi i[\Phi(\mu),\nu]}\rho(\mu)F).

By Lemma
4.1
,

(4.16)

\rho(\lambda)F=c\,c_{\nu}\,e^{\pi i[\Phi(\mu),\nu]}\rho(\mu)F,

or equivalently,

(4.17)

\rho(\lambda-\mu)F=c\,c_{\nu}\,e^{\pi i([\Phi(\mu),\nu]+[\lambda,\mu])}F.

Since (
4.17
) holds for all
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, the time-frequency shift
\rho(\lambda-\mu)
must be a multiple of the identity, and therefore

\lambda=\mu
.
∎

We now prove that
\Phi
is an invertible linear map.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero continuous linear operator satisfying the covariance property (
3.6
) with a measurable
\Phi
.
Then
\Phi
is an invertible linear map, i.e., there exists a matrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{GL}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\Phi(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}\lambda
,
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the projective kernel of
\rho
defined in (
4.6
), and
q_{H}
the associated quotient map
to
G={{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}/H
.
Since we are in the abelian setting and
H
is closed by Lemma
4.3
,
H
is a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}

[
19
, p. 51.]
.
Furthermore,

G
is a connected abelian Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}={{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}/\mathfrak{h}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
for some
k\leq 4d
.

An injective Lie group homomorphism.

By Lemma
4.2
, for all
\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}

(4.18)

\Phi(\lambda+\mu)-\Phi(\lambda)-\Phi(\mu)\in H.

Thus,

(4.19)

(q_{H}\circ\Phi)(\lambda+\mu)=(q_{H}\circ\Phi)(\lambda)+(q_{H}\circ\Phi)(\mu),

that is,
\pi=q_{H}\circ\Phi
is a group homomorphism. By Lemma
4.4
, it is one-to-one.

Since
q_{H}
is continuous and
\Phi
is measurable,
\pi
is
measurable. By
[
18
, Thm. 22.18]
, this implies
that
\pi
is continuous. The continuity of
\pi
implies that it
is even
real analytic
[
2
, Chap. 4 § XIII, Prop. 1]
,
see also
[
2
, Chap. 4 § I]
.
In particular,

\pi
is a Lie group homomorphism from
{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
to
G={{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}/H
. It induces a
Lie algebra homomorphism
between the corresponding Lie algebras
{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
and
\mathfrak{g}={\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

Dimension count.

Since
\pi
is one-to-one, so is
d\pi

[
23
, Cor. 2.7.4.]
. Thus,
4d\leq k
, i.e.,
k=4d
.
This implies that
d\pi
is actually invertible and
\mathfrak{h}=\{0\}
.
The latter implies that
H
is
discrete
, i.e.,
H\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{l}
for some
0\leq l\leq 4d
.

Inverting
\pi
.

Since
d\pi
is onto, the image
\pi({{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}})
is an open connected subgroup of
G

[
23
, Cor. 2.7.4.]
.
However,
G
is connected, so the only open connected subgroup of
G
is
G
itself.
Thus
\pi
is invertible, with a real analytic inverse. Therefore,
\pi
is a Lie group isomorphism.
This implies that the simply connected Lie group
{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
is topologically isomorphic to
G\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{4d-l}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{l}
.
But the torus is not simply connected, hence
l=0
, i.e.,
H=\{0\}
.
This also implies that
\pi=\Phi
is an automorphism on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
, i.e., there exists an invertible matrix

\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{GL}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\Phi(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}\lambda
,
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

It remains to prove the main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemma
3.2
, there exists a weak*-continuous operator
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
and a measurable function
\Phi:{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
such that for all
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
and all
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
holds

(4.20)

{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda)F=c\,\rho(\Phi(\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}F.

By Proposition
4.5
, there exists an invertible matrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{GL}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\Phi(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}\lambda
,
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
.
By Lemma
4.2
,
\mathcal{A}
satisfies

(4.21)

[\lambda,\mu]-[\mathcal{A}\lambda,\mathcal{A}\mu]\in{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}.

Since the expression is continuous in
\lambda
and
\mu
, this implies that

(4.22)

[\lambda,\mu]-[\mathcal{A}\lambda,\mathcal{A}\mu]=0,\qquad\lambda,\mu\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}.

i.e.,
\mathcal{A}
is symplectic.

Define
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
as

{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathfrak{T}}\,.

Then by (
2.4
),
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}
satisfies the
identity

(4.23)

{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}\rho(\lambda)F=\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda)F=\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\rho(\mathcal{A}\lambda){\mathfrak{T}}F=\rho(\lambda)\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathfrak{T}}F=\rho(\lambda){\mathfrak{T}}_{0}F,

that is,
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}
commutes with all time-frequency shifts.
This fact implies that
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}
is a multiple of the identity
operator on
{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.

If
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}
were bounded on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, then this conclusion would follow from
Schur’s lemma. On the level of distributions, one may argue as follows.
Since
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}
commutes in particular with all time shifts
T_{x}=\rho(x,0)
, it is a convolution operator
[
12
, p. 169]
, i.e., there exists a
K_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
such that

(4.24)

{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}F=K_{0}*F,\quad F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}

holds in the distributional sense.
Since
{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}
also commutes with modulations,

(4.25)

K_{0}*F=M_{-\omega}M_{\omega}{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}F=M_{-\omega}{\mathfrak{T}}_{0}M_{\omega}F=M_{-\omega}(K_{0}*M_{\omega}F).

Since
\langle M_{\omega}K_{0},G\rangle=\langle K_{0},M_{\omega}G\rangle
and

\langle K_{0}*F,G\rangle=\langle K_{0},\tilde{F}*G\rangle
, where
\tilde{F}(t)=F(-t)
denotes the reflection of
F
,
the last equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle K_{0},\tilde{F}*G\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle M_{\omega}(K_{0}*M_{-\omega}F),G\rangle=\langle K_{0}*M_{-\omega}F,M_{\omega}G\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle K_{0},(M_{-\omega}F)\,\tilde{}*(M_{\omega}G)\rangle=\langle K_{0},(M_{\omega}\tilde{F})*(M_{\omega}G)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle K_{0},M_{\omega}(\tilde{F}*G)\rangle=\langle M_{\omega}K_{0},\tilde{F}*G\rangle.

Now,
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}*{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
spans a dense subspace of
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, because it
contains all Gaussian functions. Therefore it follows that
K_{0}=M_{\omega}K_{0}
for all
\omega\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}
.
This implies that
K_{0}
is supported on
\{0\}
. The only distribution supported on
\{0\}
which commutes with all frequency shifts is a multiple of the identity, i.e., there exists an
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

(4.26)

\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}{\mathfrak{T}}F={\mathfrak{T}}_{0}F=aF,\qquad F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}.

Equivalently,
{\mathfrak{T}}=a\hat{\mathcal{A}}
on
{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}
. This concludes the proof of
the main theorem.
∎

It seems that a homomorphism from a locally compact group
G
to a
quotient
G/H
with respect to a closed normal subgroup
H
can be
one-to-one only if
H=\{e\}
, but currently we do not have an argument
for this more general result.

5.
Final Remarks

To appreciate the proof above, we present some alternative arguments. They do not
require assume the measurability of
\Phi
, but require additional assumptions on the
bilinear form
{\mathfrak{R}}
. We note that in our formulation of
Theorem
1.1
these assumptions appear as corollaries.

Eigendistributions.
The following argument is based on easy facts about the
eigen-distributions of time-frequency shifts. It is easy to see that an
eigen-distribution
\rho(\lambda)F=F
must be in
F\in{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\setminus\bigcup_{p\leq\infty}L^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})
. Just write
\lambda=\mathcal{A}(x,0)
for
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(2d,{\mathbb{R}})}
and
\rho(\lambda)F=\hat{\mathcal{A}}\rho(x,0)\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}F=F
, then
\hat{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}F
is periodic, and
periodic distributions cannot be in
L^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})
for
p<\infty
.
Using this observation, we sketch a different version of
Proposition
4.5
under additional hypothesis.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero linear operator
satisfying the covariance property
{\mathfrak{T}}\rho(\lambda)F=c\rho(\Phi(\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}F
for all
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
and
all
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.

Assume in addition that

(i) either there exist
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
with a non-zero
image
{\mathfrak{T}}F\in L^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})
for some
p<\infty
, or

(ii)
\{{\mathfrak{T}}F:F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}\}
is a
weak
∗
-dense subspace of
{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
.

Then
\Phi
is an additive map.

Note that we do not assume that
\phi
is measurable. Assumption (i) is quite reasonable, because a time-frequency representation of
Schwartz functions should be “nice”. Assumption (ii) is very strong,
but difficult to verify. It actually follows as a corollary from Theorem
1.1

provided that we assume the measurability of
\phi
.

Sketch of proof.

By Lemma
4.2

\rho(\Phi(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})-\Phi(\lambda_{1})-\Phi(\lambda_{2})){\mathfrak{T}}F=c{\mathfrak{T}}F

with some phase factor
c
. Thus
{\mathfrak{T}}F\neq 0
is an eigen-distribution
of a time-frequency shift. For
{\mathfrak{T}}F\in L^{p}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})
this is only possible, if
the time-frequency shift is a multiple of the identity, whence

\Phi(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})-\Phi(\lambda_{1})-\Phi(\lambda_{2})=0
and
thus
\Phi
is additive.

If
{\mathfrak{T}}({\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\otimes{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})})
spans a weak
∗
-dense subspace of

{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
, then again the time-frequency shift on the left-hand side must be a
multiple of the identity, and
\Phi
is additive.
∎

Uniqueness.
Some arguments would simplify, if we knew that
the distortion function
\Phi
were unique.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{R}}:{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\times{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\to{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
be a non-zero, bilinear,
separately weak*-continuous mapping satisfying the general
covariance property (
1.4
).
Further, assume that
\Phi_{\mathfrak{R}}
is unique.
Then there exist
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
a\neq 0
, and
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{Sp}(4d,{\mathbb{R}})}
such that

(5.1)

{\mathfrak{R}}(f,g)=a\,\hat{\mathcal{A}}(f\otimes g),\qquad f,g\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}.

Proof sketch.

The only part where measurability plays a role in the proof above is
related to Proposition
4.5
. We indicate how to use
uniqueness instead.
The rest of the proof is the same as the proof of Theorem

1.1
. A simple argument, which we omit, shows that the
constant in (
1.4
) can be taken to be
c=1
.

Now for
\lambda\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{4d}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
F\in{\mathcal{S}({{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}})}
we have

\displaystyle\rho(\Phi(n\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}F

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{T}}\rho(n\lambda)F=\rho(\Phi(\lambda)){\mathfrak{T}}\phi\big((n-1)\lambda\big)F=\dots

\displaystyle=\rho(\Phi(\lambda))^{n}{\mathfrak{T}}F=\rho\big(n(\Phi(\lambda)\big){\mathfrak{T}}F.

By uniqueness of
\Phi
we obtain

\Phi(n\lambda)=n\Phi(\lambda)\,,

which implies even that
\Phi
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-homogeneous.

We now apply (
4.3
), where the measurability of
\Phi
is
not used, and obtain for all
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(5.2)

2^{m+n}([\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)])=[2^{m}\lambda,2^{n}\mu]-[\Phi(2^{n}\lambda),\Phi(2^{n}\mu)]\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

This is equivalent to

(5.3)

[\lambda,\mu]-[\Phi(\lambda),\Phi(\mu)]=0,

which already implies that
\Phi
is a linear symplectic map.

The rest of the proof is identical to the Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎
