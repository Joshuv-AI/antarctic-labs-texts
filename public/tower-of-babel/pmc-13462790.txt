Acute adrenal crisis after orchiectomy for testicular adrenal rest tumors in untreated congenital adrenal hyperplasia
Authors: André Gustavo Pires de Sousa, Alana de Araújo Bezerra, Carlos Eduardo de Paiva Chaves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 22-year-old man with untreated congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) developed an acute adrenal crisis following bilateral orchiectomy for testicular adrenal rest tumors. The patient survived into adulthood without treatment until progressive testicular masses led to surgery due to misinterpretation of malignancy. Eighteen days postoperatively, he presented with severe asthenia, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. Diagnostic evaluation revealed markedly elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone, androstenedione, and adrenocorticotropic hormone levels, with bilateral adrenal hyperplasia on imaging. Genetic testing identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the CYP21A2 gene. Following glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement, the patient achieved clinical stability. This case illustrates that surgical removal of testicular adrenal rest tumors in untreated individuals can precipitate severe acute adrenal insufficiency, highlighting these tumors as a critical sentinel finding for late-diagnosis congenital adrenal hyperplasia.

Introduction

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) comprises a group of disorders characterized by impaired cortisol biosynthesis resulting from pathogenic variants in genes encoding enzymes involved in adrenal steroidogenesis, most commonly CYP21A2. The clinical presentation varies along a broad spectrum—including classic salt-wasting, classic simple virilizing, and nonclassic forms—depending on the degree of residual enzymatic activity [1].

Testicular adrenal rest tumors (TARTs) are a well-recognized CAH complication, composed of vestigial adrenal cells within the testes [1, 2]. Testicular adrenal rest tumors typically occur in severe CAH forms associated with chronic suboptimal therapeutic control and persistently high levels of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) [2-4]. Accurate differential diagnosis between TARTs and primary testicular malignancies is essential; while malignant neoplasms necessitate radical orchiectomy, TARTs are primarily managed with intensified glucocorticoid therapy to suppress ACTH-driven tumor growth.

No previous reports document acute adrenal insufficiency following TART resection. Although these tumors exhibit functional steroidogenic capacity [5, 6], their contribution to systemic hormonal homeostasis remains uncertain. The present case describes a patient with CAH who remained untreated for over 2 decades—an uncommon clinical scenario. Surgical removal of a substantial mass of ectopic adrenal tissue precipitated an acute adrenal crisis. This rare presentation highlights the potential for TART resection to precipitate an Addisonian crisis in patients with previously unrecognized CAH.

Case presentation

A 22-year-old man presented to a urological service with a history of bilateral testicular masses first noted at 19 years of age, with progressive enlargement. Physical examination using an orchidometer revealed bilateral testicular volume estimated at >30 mL, with firm, irregular consistency. Based on the initial evaluation of suspicion of malignancy, bilateral radical orchiectomy was performed, with no immediate perioperative complications. The patient was discharged on the following day. Prior to surgery, sperm cryopreservation was performed.

Eighteen days after surgery, the patient was admitted with severe asthenia, nausea, vomiting, and dehydration. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed serum sodium of 126 mEq/L (SI: 126 mmol/L; reference range [RR], 135-145 mEq/L [SI: 135-145 mmol/L]) and serum potassium of 5.5 mEq/L (SI: 5.5 mmol/L; RR, 3.5-5.0 mEq/L [SI: 3.5-5.0 mmol/L]). He denied fever or urinary symptoms. On the following morning, a baseline serum cortisol level was obtained, and intravenous hydrocortisone (50 mg every 6 hours) was initiated, resulting in clinical improvement. An endocrinology consultation was requested. Physical examination was notable for short stature (1.58 m, <3rd percentile; target height: 1.75 m; range: 1.665-1.835 m) and hyperpigmented macules on the lips. Weight was 63.5 kg. Glucocorticoid replacement was transitioned to oral prednisolone (7.5 mg/day), and the patient was discharged. However, 8 days later, he was readmitted with symptomatic hyponatremia (serum sodium 118 mEq/L [SI: 118 mmol/L]). Mineralocorticoid replacement with fludrocortisone 0.1 mg/day was initiated, leading to correction of electrolyte abnormalities.

The patient was not screened for CAH at birth. Review of medical records from another institution revealed that he had been evaluated by pediatric endocrinology at 3 years of age due to macrogenitosomia. Physical examination demonstrated testicular volumes of 2 to 3 mL and phallic length of 8 cm. He exhibited accelerated linear growth of 13 cm/year, advanced bone age, and elevated serum testosterone and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) levels. There was an initial misdiagnosis of testotoxicosis, and ketoconazole (400 mg/day) was prescribed. However, treatment was discontinued due to recurrent vomiting, and the patient was lost to follow-up for nearly 20 years. During this period, he reported no salt-craving behavior or hospitalizations suggestive of previous adrenal crises. Family history includes a brother with precocious puberty and short stature whose diagnosis of CAH was only confirmed recently. Notably, scrotal ultrasonography did not reveal TARTs in the brother. A sister died at 2 months of age of an undetermined cause.

Diagnostic assessment

At the initial evaluation at age 3, laboratory findings showed elevated serum testosterone of 77.6 ng/dL (SI: 26.9 nmol/L; RR for age, <2.5 ng/dL [SI: <0.87 nmol/L]). Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate was 286.5 µg/dL (SI: 7.7 µmol/L; RR for age <4 years, <19 µg/dL [SI: <0.5 µmol/L]). Gonadotropins were suppressed, with follicle-stimulating hormone 0.64 U/L (SI: 0.64 U/L) and luteinizing hormone 0.05 U/L (SI: 0.05 U/L). Bone age was significantly advanced, corresponding to 13 years.

Preoperatively, scrotal ultrasonography demonstrated increased testicular volume (right testis 49.8 cm3; left testis 76.0 cm3), with markedly heterogeneous parenchyma containing calcifications (Fig. 1). Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed no abnormalities other than solid, heterogeneous lesions in both testes. Serum levels of β-human chorionic gonadotropin, α-fetoprotein, and lactate dehydrogenase were within normal limits.

Upon admission in acute adrenal crisis, early morning serum cortisol, collected on the first day of hospitalization, was 0.55 μg/dL (SI: 15.1 nmol/L; RR, 5.0-23 μg/dL [SI: 138-635 nmol/L]). A repeat abdominal CT scan demonstrated diffuse bilateral adrenal hyperplasia, which had not been identified on the preoperative imaging (Fig. 2). Adrenal laboratory evaluation performed 25 days after orchiectomy revealed a markedly elevated serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone 49 000 ng/dL (SI: 1484 nmol/L; RR, <200 ng/dL [SI: <6.0 nmol/L]) and androstenedione 1350 ng/dL (SI: 47.1 nmol/L; RR, 40-150 ng/dL [SI: 1.4-5.2 nmol/L]), as shown in Table 1. Adrenocorticotropic hormone was >1250 pg/mL (SI: >275 pmol/L; RR, 7.2-63.3 pg/mL [SI: 1.6-13.9 pmol/L]). Levels of 11-deoxycortisol, DHEA-S, and DHEA were normal. Genetic testing identified a homozygous c.293-13 C > G [I2G] pathogenic variant in the CYP21A2 gene, confirming CAH due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency.

Treatment

The patient was maintained on prednisolone (successfully tapered to 5 mg/day) and fludrocortisone (0.1 mg/day).

Outcome and follow-up

Three months postorchiectomy, androgen replacement therapy was initiated with testosterone undecanoate. At the most recent follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic on stable hormonal replacement therapy. Clinical and biochemical control has been consistently achieved, with plasma androstenedione, total testosterone, serum electrolytes, and plasma renin activity maintained within their respective target therapeutic ranges.

Discussion

The case describes a patient with CAH due to a pathogenic variant in the CYP21A2 gene who remained untreated throughout life. During childhood, he was misdiagnosed with testotoxicosis and received short-term ketoconazole therapy. At that time, the reported vomiting was likely related to ketoconazole-induced suppression of adrenal steroidogenesis, exacerbating an already compromised hormonal state. This misdiagnosis contributed to decades of untreated adrenal insufficiency and the development of massive TARTs. Furthermore, this case underscores the importance of a thorough workup for CAH in any male presenting with bilateral testicular masses and signs of early androgen excess.

In adulthood, the patient developed TARTs, which were initially misinterpreted as malignant testicular neoplasms, resulting in bilateral orchiectomy. Approximately 2 weeks postoperatively, an Addisonian crisis occurred, potentially precipitated by the removal of these tumors, which may have overwhelmed the adrenal functional reserve. Additionally, there was bilateral adrenal enlargement observed on postoperative abdominal CT (Fig. 2), which may reflect an acute trophic response to a massive compensatory ACTH surge following the TART resection. This marked bilateral adrenal enlargement prompted the inpatient care team to suspect acute adrenal insufficiency. Physiological surgical stress also likely contributed to the adrenal crisis. However, in patients with adrenal insufficiency without perioperative stress-dose glucocorticoids, such events typically occur in the immediate postoperative phase [8]. Given that the plasma half-life of cortisol is short [9], potential TART-derived glucocorticoids, if any, would decline within the first 24 hours postoperatively. Therefore, the exact mechanisms explaining the 18-day delay until admission remain unknown. One plausible explanation is that the rapidly enlarging adrenal glands, stimulated by a massive postoperative ACTH surge, initially succeeded in producing a compensatory amount of glucocorticoids that provisionally sustained metabolic balance.

Testicular adrenal rest tumors represent a well-recognized complication of CAH, with prevalence ranging from 14% to 89% depending on age and severity [2]. Their development has been primarily attributed to inadequate glucocorticoid treatment, leading to persistently elevated ACTH levels that stimulate the growth of adrenal rest tissue within the testes [4]. Furthermore, patients with TARTs typically exhibit clinical markers of long-term poor hormonal control, including reduced adult height and advanced bone age, compared with patients without these tumors [3]. Nevertheless, TARTs can also occur in patients with adequate hormonal control, underscoring the need for regular screening in all male patients with CAH, beginning in late childhood or early adolescence, regardless of biochemical status [10].

Histologically, TARTs feature large polygonal cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm, hyperchromatic nuclei, intersected by dense fibrous stroma, often featuring calcification or hyalinization in long-standing cases [11]. Distinguishing TARTs from LCTs is challenging due to shared histopathological and molecular features [12]. Immunohistochemistry is a valuable tool in this differentiation. Testicular adrenal rest tumors generally exhibit strong, diffuse CD56 positivity and synaptophysin reactivity, whereas LCTs typically demonstrate focal or negative staining for these markers [11, 13]. In this case, the entire initial immunohistochemical panel focused on excluding common testicular malignancies; therefore, a specific TART panel (including steroidogenic enzymes) was not performed. However, although MART-1 positivity does not differentiate TART from LCT, it is compatible with the steroidogenic capacity. Additional features can contribute to differentiating TARTs from LCTs: prior CAH diagnosis, bilateral masses, negative serum tumor markers, and younger age favor TARTs [2, 14]. However, distinction may not be possible, as data suggest these tumors share the same totipotent embryonic cell origin [12]. In this case, although synaptophysin staining was negative, the diagnostic revision from LCT to TART is substantiated by the laboratory findings, rapid adrenal hyperplasia, and favorable response to steroid therapy. Distinguishing TARTs from LCTs is clinically essential: while LCTs often require surgical resection, TARTs should be managed medically. Optimizing glucocorticoid therapy is the primary treatment for TARTs, aiming to suppress ACTH levels, reduce tumor size, improve Leydig cell function, and potentially preserve fertility [2, 10]. In retrospect, the preoperative diagnosis deserves critical reflection. Bilateral testicular involvement, negative serum tumor markers, characteristic ultrasonographic findings, and the patient's history of androgen excess and short stature should have prompted consideration of TART and underlying CAH before radical surgery. Earlier endocrine evaluation might have avoided unnecessary bilateral orchiectomy.

The identified homozygous pathogenic variant is classically associated with the classic salt-wasting phenotype, characterized by high mortality during infancy if untreated [10]. However, the Int2G variant exhibits notable phenotypic heterogeneity [15]. Intriguingly, this patient survived without severe manifestations for over 2 decades without glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement. The most accepted hypothesis suggests that a fraction of transcripts escapes aberrant splicing, allowing production of small amounts of 21-hydroxylase, which may result in a milder presentation. Furthermore, extra-adrenal 21-hydroxylation may represent a phenotypic modifier in CAH [15]. In addition, a third RP1-C4-CYP21-TNX (RCCX) module copy, which may harbor an additional functional CYP21A2 gene, can mitigate hormonal deficiency, potentially delaying adrenal crisis [16]. Nevertheless, sporadic reports of patients with limited treatment suggest exceptional compensatory mechanisms may permit survival. Translational studies demonstrate that accumulated adrenal steroid precursors may exert partial glucocorticoid activity by activating the glucocorticoid receptor [5, 6, 17]. This can provide sufficient physiological compensation to prevent lethal adrenal crises, despite failing to mitigate androgen excess.

A clinical presentation similar to the current case is uncommon. Table 2 summarizes reported cases in which TART was the sentinel finding for CAH, including those involving unnecessary surgical interventions [18-20]. Therefore, in any case of bilateral testicular tumors, TART must be considered in the differential diagnosis, raising immediate clinical suspicion of previously undiagnosed or untreated CAH [1, 14].

We found no documented cases of patients progressing to acute adrenal crisis following resection of these ectopic adrenal tumors. This suggests that our patient represents a unique manifestation. Clinicians must be aware that performing bilateral orchiectomy in untreated CAH patients can trigger a life-threatening acute adrenal crisis. Awareness of this complication is essential given the diagnostic challenges and significant mortality risk if not promptly managed.
