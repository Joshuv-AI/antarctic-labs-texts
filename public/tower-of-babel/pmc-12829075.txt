Prevalence and predictors of fibromyalgia symptoms in an academic setting: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maram H Alshareef, Ghalia Almutair, Waleed Hafiz, Shoroug Ibrahim, Nahla H Hariri, Mokhtar Shatla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFibromyalgia (FM) is a complex, chronic syndrome characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain and cognitive difficulties. Occupational and psychosocial stressors may contribute to the development and exacerbation of FM symptoms. This cross-sectional study was conducted to determine the prevalence of FM symptoms and associated factors among students and faculty at Umm Al Qura University in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross sectional study using a valid questionnaire that included the Fibromyalgia Survey Diagnostic Criteria and the General Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale among Umm Al-Qura university members.ResultsOf the 580 participants, 14.1% met the criteria for FM and 22.1% exhibited anxiety symptoms. Univariate and multivariate analyses revealed that FM symptoms were significantly associated with age ≥ 25 years, female sex, faculty/employee status, family history of FM, lack of regular weekly exercise, regular use of pain relief medications, and presence of anxiety symptoms. Anxiety was a particularly strong predictor, with affected individuals being over eight times more likely to have FM symptoms than those without anxiety (AOR, 8.035; p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe study emphasizes the importance of identifying modifiable lifestyle and psychological factors associated with the development of FM, particularly in academic settings where stress is prevalent. The high prevalence of FM symptoms among students and faculty and the strong link between FM symptoms and anxiety highlight the need for early screening and intervention strategies. Recommendations include implementing campus-wide mental health and physical activity programs, educating the university community about FM, and offering personalized support to individuals with high risk of developing FM.Keywords: Fibromyalgia, Prevalence, Students, Faculty members, Anxiety

Background

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a complex, chronic syndrome characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive difficulties, and heightened sensitivity to stimuli such as touch, sound, and temperature. It is classified as a central sensitivity syndrome, believed to be caused by dysregulation in the processing of pain signals by the central nervous system [1]. The pathophysiology of FM is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, neuroendocrine dysfunction, autonomic nervous system abnormalities, psychological stress, and environmental triggers [2, 3].

The global prevalence of FM is estimated to be 2%–8%, with estimates varying greatly based on the demographic and diagnostic criteria used [4, 5]. Numerous studies have indicated that the female-to-male ratios in the prevalence of FM vary from 7:1 to 9:1, suggesting that women are disproportionately affected [6]. Although FM can affect individuals of all ages, it is commonly diagnosed between the ages of 30 and 50 years [7]. However, emerging data suggest that FM symptoms may also be present in younger populations, including university students, who often experience high levels of psychological distress and academic pressure and exhibit negative habits such as irregular sleep and sedentary lifestyles, all of which are known risk factors for FM [8, 9].

Academic and workplace environments, particularly in universities, are potential sources of chronic stress, long working hours, and reduced opportunities for physical activity. These occupational and psychosocial stressors may contribute to the development and exacerbation of FM symptoms [10]. However, despite the growing recognition of FM in diverse population groups, region-specific data, particularly in the Middle East, remains scarce.

Only a few studies have been conducted to examine the epidemiology of FM in Saudi Arabia. In one study, the prevalence rate of FM in the general population was reported as 7%–13%, whereas another study indicated a higher rate for patients who visit primary healthcare centers [11, 12]. However, data on the prevalence of FM in university populations, comprising both students and faculty, are lacking. Given the unique stressors in academic settings and the cultural, social, and occupational dynamics in the Middle Eastern region, it is essential to understand the burden of FM in the context of university settings in the Middle East.

This study was conducted to determine the prevalence of FM symptoms among students and faculty members at Umm Al Qura University in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, and identify the key demographic, psychological, and lifestyle factors associated with FM in the study population. By identifying at-risk groups and factors that contribute to the development of FM, this study could inform future prevention strategies, early screening efforts, and holistic management approaches within university environments.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a descriptive, cross-sectional study conducted at Umm Al Qura University in Makkah, Saudi Arabia. The data collection period was from [January - March], 2024. The study population included both students and faculty members across various colleges within the university.

Study population and sampling

A total of 580 individuals were included in this study using a non-probability convenience sampling technique. This. This approach was selected because the university population is very large, and conducting clinical screening for all members was not feasible at this exploratory stage. Our intention in this initial phase was to broadly estimate the potential prevalence of the condition. The survey was disseminated to the entire university community using a standardized Google Forms link. Distribution was facilitated through designated volunteer representatives in each college to ensure wide reach. Participation was open to all eligible individuals, students, employee or faculty members of Umm Al Qura University, aged 18 years or older, regardless of whether they had fibromyalgia symptoms or not to minimize selective participation. Inclusion criteria include aged 18 years or older, who voluntarily consented to participate in the study. Individuals with known psychiatric or neurological disorders that could confound the assessment of FM symptoms were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected using a self-administered, structured questionnaire disseminated online via institutional emails and social media platforms. The questionnaire consisted of four main sections:

Socio-demographic Information: Included items on age, sex, occupation (student or faculty), nationality, and body mass index.

Lifestyle and Behavioral Factors: Included items on smoking status, sleep duration, regular exercise, use of pain medications, chronic comorbidities, family history of FM, and social support.

Fibromyalgia Survey Diagnostic Criteria (FSDC): The FSDC was used to assess FM symptoms. The FSDC includes the Widespread Pain Index (WPI; score range: 0–19) and the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS; score range: 0–12). FM was diagnosed if the respondent had either a WPI ≥ 7 and an SSS score ≥ 5 or a WPI between 3 and 6 and an SSS score ≥ 9, with symptoms present for at least three months and no other disorder to explain the pain [13].

Anxiety Assessment: Anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7), a validated instrument that consists of seven questions rated on a 4-point Likert scale (from 0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”). Scores were classified as follows: 0–4 (no anxiety), 5–9 (mild anxiety), 10–14 (moderate anxiety), and 15–21 (severe anxiety). A cutoff score of ≥ 10 points indicated clinically significant anxiety symptoms [14].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). For categorical variables, descriptive data are presented as percentages and frequencies. The chi-square test was used to examine associations between FM symptoms and potential predictors identified in a univariate analysis. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify significant independent predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 580 people participated in this study. Most of the participants were females (352, 60.7%), aged between 18 and 24 years (230, 39.7%), faculty members or employees (368, 63.4%). Regarding lifestyle factors, 310 (53.4%) do not exercise, and 264 (45.5%) sleep 6 h or less per day. In addition, 330 (56.9%) participants reported that they feel energetic in the morning, 334 (57.6%) receive family support, and 189 (32.6%) use pain relief medications regularly (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of factors associated with FM symptoms

The univariate analysis revealed that several socio-demographic and health-related factors were significantly associated with FM symptoms. The results showed that age ≥ 25 years, regular use of pain relief medications, and female sex were associated with a significantly higher prevalence of FM symptoms (p = 0.001). Moreover, being a faculty member/employee, not living with family, lack of regular exercise and family history of FM were associated with FM (p < 0.05).

Other variables such as obesity, smoking, chronic comorbidities, sleep duration, and feeling energetic upon waking were not significantly associated with FM symptoms (Table 3).

Multivariate regression analysis

The multivariate analysis identified several significant independent predictors of FM symptoms. These predictors included age ≥ 25 years (AOR: 9.314; 95% CI: 4.973–24.001; p = 0.001), female sex (AOR: 16.212; 95% CI: 5.973–44.001; p = 0.001), faculty/employee status (AOR: 11.663; 95% CI: 5.331–16.201; p = 0.001), family history of FM (AOR: 3.126; 95% CI: 1.326–8.311; p = 0.026), lack of regular weekly exercise (AOR: 3.327; 95% CI: 1.215–9.116; p = 0.019), regular use of pain relief medications (AOR: 3.551; 95% CI: 1.816–6.946; p = 0.001), and presence of anxiety symptoms (AOR: 8.035; 95% CI: 3.668–17.601; p = 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study was conducted to assess the prevalence and associated risk factors of FM among students and faculty members at Umm Al Qura University. The results revealed that 14.1% of participants met the criteria for FM, a finding consistent with those of some international and regional studies, which have reported prevalence rates ranging from 2% to 17% depending on the population and diagnostic criteria used [4, 11]. The prevalence observed in the present study is higher than that reported for the general population globally, possibly due to the unique stressors and occupational burdens present in academic environments [5].

Sex- and age-related associations

Female sex was a strong independent predictor of FM symptoms in the present study. This finding aligns with the results of several studies that have highlighted a higher prevalence of FM symptoms among women [15, 16]. This sex-specific disparity is often attributed to hormonal differences, increased central sensitization, and psychosocial factors that disproportionately affect women [6]. Similarly, the results of this study indicated that participants aged 25 years or older were significantly more likely to report FM symptoms than younger participants. This may be due to the cumulative increased exposure to stressors and physical strain with increasing age [17].

Occupational influence

Faculty members and employees exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of FM symptoms than students. This may reflect increased work-related stress, long working hours, and possibly reduced time for self-care or physical activity among working adults [10]. Occupational stress has long been implicated in the development and exacerbation of FM symptoms, particularly in high-responsibility professions [18].

Psychological factors

The univariate and multivariate analyses conducted in this study revealed a significant association between anxiety and FM. Specifically, the results showed that individuals with anxiety symptoms were more than eight times more likely to report FM than those without anxiety symptoms. This finding supports existing evidence on the strong link between FM and psychological distress, particularly anxiety and depression [19, 20]. Dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and altered neurotransmitter levels are proposed mechanisms underlying this association [21].

Lifestyle and behavioral factors

The results of this study indicated that lack of regular exercise was independently associated with FM symptoms. Exercise reduces the severity of FM symptoms by improving sleep, reducing fatigue, and enhancing mood [22]. The results also indicated that participants who used pain relief medications regularly were more likely to report FM symptoms than those who did not. Although this finding may indicate that the individuals with FM experience more severe or chronic pain, it also raises concerns about potential over-reliance on pharmacological management without addressing underlying lifestyle contributors [23].

Interestingly, variables such as obesity, smoking, sleep duration, and perceived energy levels upon waking were not significantly associated with FM in this study, despite being frequently reported as potential risk modifiers in previous studies on FM [24, 25]. This may reflect population-specific factors or limitations in the accuracy of self-reported data.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in the use of validated instruments such as the FSCDC and GAD-7, and the inclusion of both students and staff in the study cohort, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of the prevalence of FM within a university setting. However, the notable limitations of the study include its cross-sectional design, which prevented causal inference, and potential response bias attributable to the use of self-reported data. Additionally, the single-center setting may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, we propose the following recommendations to help address the prevalence and impact of FM among university students and staff:

Implementation of periodic Heal screening program: Establish routine health assessments for all university members in line with national and international guidelines. These screenings should include anxiety evaluation and consider fibromyalgia as a possible differential diagnosis for individuals reporting prolonged, generalized body pain, with referral to specialists when appropriate to support early identification and intervention. In addition, the university should promote holistic, accessible support services for students and staff experiencing personal or family health challenges, ensuring comprehensive care and well-being.

Conclusions

This study highlights a notable prevalence of FM symptoms among individuals working or learning in universities, particularly females, older adults, and those with anxiety. The results of this study indicate that promotion of physical activity, initiation of early mental health interventions, and development of workplace wellness programs and the importance of establishing periodic health screening programs lies in their ability to detect and manage health conditions that may be silent yet have long-term impacts on individual well-being and the overall economic burden on the community.
