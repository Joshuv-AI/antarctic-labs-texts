Genomic and phenotypic characterization of indigenous violacein-producing Chromobacterium amazonense SAM215 from lake sediment in Bangladesh
Authors: Mithu Rani Tarafdar, Md. Al Muid Khan, Atiqua, Nabila Khanam, Shah Minhajul Abedin, Avirup Saha, Zahidul Islam, Arafat Al Mamun, Mahmud Hossain, Muhammad Manjurul Karim, Shakila Nargis Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The genus Chromobacterium, found in diverse ecosystems, is well known for its production of violacein, quorum-sensing ability, and considerable biotechnological importance. While C. violaceum has been extensively studied, other species, including C. amazonense, have received relatively little attention. This study reports phenotypic characterization and genomic analysis of the indigenous Chromobacterium amazonense strain SAM215, isolated from Bijoypur Lake, Netrokona, Bangladesh. The violacein-producing purple colony was selected and characterized, exhibiting high salinity tolerance (up to 12.5% NaCl), resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics, tolerance to metal ions, and strong biofilm formation. Notably, disruption of the biofilm under shaking conditions significantly diminished violacein production. The de novo-assembled genome (4.14 Mbp), identified as C. amazonense, comprised 4104 protein-coding genes and 53 RNA genes. Genomic analysis revealed genes associated with metal tolerance, stress adaptation, antimicrobial compound production, plant-growth-promoting traits, and enzymes such as chitinases, suggesting a genomic repertoire of biotechnological interest. However, multiple genes associated with antibiotic resistance and virulence factors, including secretion systems and hemolysin, were also identified. Preliminary in vivo pathogenicity assessment in Swiss albino (Mus musculus) mice (n = 3) showed reduced survival at 106 and 108 CFU/mL doses (i.p.), although the study was not statistically powered to determine significance. Integrating the available genomic data with the observed phenotypic characteristics provided a better understanding of the biology of Chromobacterium amazonense.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55922-x.

Introduction

Chromobacterium is a soil- and water-borne free-living Gram-negative Betaproteobacterium of the family Chromobacteriaceae1 and is found in a variety of environments within tropical and subtropical ecosystems2. Chromobacterium violaceum, the type species of the genus, was the only validated species until 2007 since its discovery in 18813; later ten new species have been added to the taxonomy, including Chromobacterium amazonense4. The sequencing of the genomes of Chromobacterium has revealed gene sets that may yield several pharmacological, biotechnological, and industrial products2,5. To date, the focus of research has been mostly on violacein, a blue-violet colored signature pigment of Chromobacterium with numerous bioactive properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, and antiproliferative activities6–12. However, scientists have begun to explore other products of the genus Chromobacterium, including polyhydroxyalkanoates (biodegradable plastics)13, 2,4-Diacetylphloroglucinol (Antibiotic and biocontrol agent)14, Cyanide (lixiviant for gold recovery from electronic waste)15 and multiple industrially important enzymes, for example, chitinase16, lipase17, azoreductase18, phenylalanine hydroxylase19, etc. Nevertheless, the potential of other species within the Chromobacterium genus, such as C. amazonense, has remained largely unexplored compared to C. violaceum, which has been the most extensively studied species for violacein production and quorum-sensing research.

Chromobacterium is an opportunistic bacterium; hence, pathogenicity associated with it has been infrequent. While uncommon, infection with C. violaceum has been reported as lethal20 and C. haemolyticum has been shown to cause bacteremia21. Several Chromobacterium isolates are multidrug-resistant, showing resistance to beta-lactams, clindamycin, and many other antibiotics22. The Chromobacterium genus is frequently used in quorum sensing research, on which the biofilm formation and production of various metabolites, including violacein, and several virulence factors depend; this may collectively enhance resistance to medications and host responses23. Although the violacein-producing C. amazonense genome also harbors several pathogenic determinants4, no clinical cases have been reported yet.

The first strain of C. amazonense was isolated in 2015 from the Rio Negro River in Amazon, Brazil24, and was subjected to detailed phenotypic characterization. Subsequently, a complete genome analysis of the species was conducted by Santos A. B., et al. in 20184, revealing extensive pathways for energy generation, genes for transport proteins, stress adaptation, and quorum sensing for the control of inducible systems, all of which contribute to the versatility and adaptability of the organism. However, a more comprehensive understanding of C. amazonense, particularly its biotechnological potential, has required integrated analyses of both phenotypic characteristics and genomic features.

The current study presented the isolation, identification, characterization, whole genome analysis, and pangenome analysis of an indigenous Chromobacterium amazonense strain SAM215 from the carbonate-rich lake sediment of Bijoypur Lake, Susang Durgapur, Netrokona, Bangladesh, which is expected to enrich our understanding of species-level diversity and to pave the way for further comparative studies involving Chromobacterium-related isolates.

Materials and methods

Sampling and Isolation

Bijoypur Lake (Lake Birishiri), with the geographical coordinates 25° 9’ 29" N to 25° 9’ 31" N, 90° 38’ 19" E, is a natural body of water situated in Susong Durgapur, Netrokona, Bangladesh, and known for its bluish-green water. Water and sediment samples from the littoral zone were collected in triplicate during the rainy season in July and subsequently analyzed in the laboratory. The water was collected at a depth of 3 meters, corresponding to 1% light penetration. Water temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen concentration (DO) were measured in situ. Collected samples were serially diluted from 10−1 to 10−8, and 0.1 mL was spread over Luria Bertani (LB) agar plates (10 g/L of tryptone, 5 g/L of yeast extract, 10 g/L of NaCl, and 1.7% agar) and incubated at 37°C for 24–48 h. The violet colonies on the medium were primarily selected as violacein producers and preserved at -26°C.

Characterization of the strain

After 24 h of incubation on LB agar, the isolate was subjected to Gram staining, followed by microscopic observation of morphology. The physiological parameters of the isolate were qualitatively assessed by examining their growth and pigmentation across different temperature ranges (22 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, 37 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, 55 °C) and salt concentrations (0%, 1.5%, 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, 10%, 12.5% and 15%). For both experiments, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h and observed for bacterial growth. The isolate was subjected to the Motility Indole Urease (MIU) test, citrate utilization, catalase production, Indole Acetic Acid (IAA) production, nitrate production, casein degradation, and starch hydrolysis for biochemical characterization of the bacteria25. The hemolytic activity was tested by culturing the bacteria on freshly prepared blood agar plates. Fermentative activity against various sugars (mannose, fructose, sucrose, lactose, and mannitol) was evaluated to determine the carbohydrate utilization pattern of the strain. All experiments had negative controls and replicates. The biofilm-forming ability of the isolate was assessed by microtiter plate assay26 using crystal violet staining and classified using the scheme proposed by Stepanović et al.27. Cyanide (HCN) production test was done using paper soaked in an alkaline picrate solution on the lids of the LB agar plate where the bacterium was streaked, as described by Lork28. Cyanogenic potential was indicated by a change in the color of the filter paper from yellow to orange-red, brown, or reddish-brown after incubation. Siderophore production was detected using the universal chrome azurol S (CAS) agar plate assay with minimal M9 salt medium. The bacterium was streaked onto CAS agar plates, and siderophore production was assessed following incubation for 72 h at 37 °C by the appearance of an orange halo29.

Violacein production and extraction

Crude Violacein was extracted from the liquid culture following a previously described method30 with some modifications. Violet colonies from a fresh bacterial culture were picked from agar plates and inoculated into 100 mL LB broth in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask for 72 h at 37 °C in the incubator under two conditions: static and shaking (120 rpm). Upon incubation, the pigmented broth was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was mixed thoroughly with 96% ethanol (20% of the initial culture volume). The mixture was centrifuged again at 10,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min to obtain violacein in the ethanol supernatant, while the cells remained in the pellet. The extraction process was repeated multiple times until the pellet was colorless. Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The antibiotic susceptibility of the isolate was performed and interpreted against nine commercial antibiotics (penicillin, ampicillin, tobramycin, pefloxacin, levofloxacin, amoxicillin, erythromycin, cefotaxime, and ceftriaxone) using the standard Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion assay31 on Mueller-Hinton Agar by following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards32.

Metal tolerance

The isolate was allowed to grow in 96-well microtiter plates in LB broth supplemented with 14 different metal salts (arsenic, chromium, lead, cadmium, mercury, zinc, copper, potassium, calcium, magnesium, manganese, aluminium, cobalt, and iron) of varied concentrations ranging from 256 µg/mL serially diluted two-fold up to 1 µg/mL. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial growth and pigment production were observed visually.

Whole genome sequencing and analysis

For the extraction of genomic DNA (gDNA), the isolate was grown in LB broth (Laboratories, USA) at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm for 24 h. The gDNA was extracted from the culture using the Qiagen Genomic DNA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. gDNA quantification was performed using a Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The genome sequence was prepared by the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr, b) using the Nextera DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc., USA) and sequenced on the Illumina iSeq 100 platform in paired-end mode. FastQC v0.12.1(https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc), Fastp (v0.26.0)33, and SPAdes(v4.2.0)34 were employed for the quality assessment of the reads, adapter trimming, and de novo sequence assembly, respectively. Several assemblies were computed using k-mer values from 31 to 119. The outputs were assessed and compared in QUAST (5.3.0)35, and the best assembly (k-mer 95) was selected based on the N50, the largest contig length, and the lowest number of contigs. Proksee was used to visualize the genome map of the draft assembly, and RStudio was used to create the graphs.

The assembled genome was uploaded to the Type Strain Genome Server36 for taxonomic inference and identification (dDDH cut-off value 70% for species delimitation), further confirmed by PubMLST37 and SpeciesFinder 2.038, and annotated in Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology (RAST)39 for subsystem analysis and ORF prediction. Additional functional annotations were performed with the GO (Gene Ontology), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes)40, NR, COG (Cluster of Orthologous Genes), and Enzyme Class databases with OmicsBox v3.4 (OmicsBox – Bioinformatics Made Easy, BioBam Bioinformatics, March 3, 2019, https://www.biobam.com) and BLAST2GO41. Carbohydrate-active enzymes were identified by using CAZy databases in CUPP (Conserved Unique Peptide Patterns)42. The pathogenicity of the isolate was evaluated using PathogenFinder43 and the VFDB database, while the RGI 6.0.5 web portal (default thresholds) with the CARD 4.0.1 database44 was used to identify antibiotic-resistance genes. AntiSMASH (version 7.0)45 was used to identify biosynthetic gene clusters with ‘strict’ strictness. The genome is visualized with Proksee46.

Intra-species genome analysis

The genomic features of SAM215 were compared to the genomes of six available strains of C. amazonense (DSM 26508, 54AF, BASUSDA_45, Cv1, CV8) in the NCBI GenBank Data Library using IPGA47. Synteny analysis was done using the Mauve software package48.

In vivo pathogenicity testing

A pilot mouse survival study was conducted to preliminarily evaluate the pathogenicity of the strain in Swiss albino (Mus musculus) mice, following a similar method used by Batista et al.49. All mice were handled according to guidelines approved by the Committee on Animal Research at the University of Dhaka (Ref: 282/Biol.Scs). Results and discussion

Screening of pigment-producing bacteria

In this study, a violacein-producing strain, named SAM215, was isolated from water and sediment samples from Lake Birishiri (Fig. 1a and b). During sample collection, the lake water exhibited an average pH of 7.5 ± 0.5 and a temperature of 28.5 ± 0.8 °C. On LB agar medium, colony morphology was observed (Fig. 1c) to be violet colored, convex, smooth, and round, with an average colony diameter of 1.5 mm after 48 h. Colonies formed at 24 h were small and cream-colored, which gradually turned purple to dark violet over the next 24 h. This coloration was caused by the production of violacein, a tryptophan derived violet colored bisindole metabolite. Upon Gram staining, the bacteria appeared as long, straight, rod-shaped, Gram-negative bacilli (Fig. 1d). The stained cells were mostly found in pairs and short chains under a microscope.

Biochemical and physiological traits

The biochemical properties of the isolate, summarized in Table 1, were well aligned with the genus Chromobacterium50, and the catalase-negative result was a distinguishable property of Chromobacterium amazonense24. It fermented glucose and mannitol. Although indole-negative, it produced indole-3 acetic acid (IAA), suggesting a tryptophan-independent pathway for IAA production51. β-hemolysis of the isolate on sheep blood agar indicated pathogenic potential, as observed in two other species of the genus.

Visible growth of the isolate was observed up to 45 °C, which was consistent with the previously reported upper growth limit of 45°C24. However, pigment production ceased above 40 °C, consistent with the reported optimal range for violacein production (30–35 °C52). The isolate exhibited tolerance to high salt concentrations, up to 12.5%, surpassing previous reports of 2.5%53 but pigment production ceased at 7.5% NaCl. Although the exact mechanism was not determined, the inhibition of the isolate’s pigment production at higher temperatures or salinity may have been due to reduced biofilm formation under stress, and the ability of this strain to survive high salinity might have been related to its mineral-rich habitat and metabolic adaptability. The isolate was a strong biofilm former, a trait linked to quorum sensing that significantly influenced secondary metabolite production and stress response23. Although quantitative growth kinetics and violacein yield measurements were not performed, which could have strengthened these observations, the qualitative results still defined the physiological tolerance range of the isolate and supported its ecological fitness.

Production and extraction of violacein

The yield of violacein under static conditions was 294.15 µg/g, approximately ten times higher than the yield of 31.09 µg/g observed under shaking conditions (120 rpm), and this difference could be visually distinguished (Fig. 1e). In static culture, violacein accumulated at the upper surface, producing a film on top of the broth. This phenomenon could be explained by the tendency of hydrophobic violacein to accumulate in the biofilm formed at the air-liquid interface. It also indicates a relationship between biofilm formation and the isolate’s pigment-producing capacity. This may have been due to shear stress on cells in shaking culture54 that may have inhibited the formation of biofilm55, which is linked to quorum sensing in Chromobacterium and eventually leads to violacein production56. Hence, static cultures provided a conducive environment for biofilm formation, thereby enhancing violacein production through cell-to-cell communication. The UV-Vis spectrogram of the extracted violacein exhibited a strong, single absorption peak in the visible range of the electromagnetic spectrum at 575 nm (Fig. 1f).

Antibiotic susceptibility

The isolate SAM215 showed resistance towards all beta-lactam antibiotics tested (penicillin, ampicillin, and amoxicillin) and the aminoglycoside (tobramycin), but was completely susceptible to macrolides (erythromycin) and fluoroquinolones (pefloxacin and levofloxacin), suggesting possible therapeutic options in case of infection (Table 2). The formation of biofilm may have contributed significantly to the drug resistance and susceptibility of the isolate57. Fluoroquinolones and macrolides have been reported to possess biofilm-disrupting ability58,59, which may have contributed to the susceptibility of the isolate to these antibiotics.

Metal tolerance

Twelve different metal salts were used to observe the effect of metal ions on bacterial growth and pigment production. As shown in Fig. 1g, mercury impeded both growth and pigmentation at concentrations above 2 µg/mL. In contrast, high concentrations (256 µg/mL) of iron were favorable for both growth and violacein production. Chromium and arsenic were also toxic to the bacteria, but zinc, cadmium, barium, copper, and cobalt were better tolerated.

Manganese, magnesium, and aluminum did not inhibit growth within the tested range but affected violacein production. Tolerance to concentrations of heavy metals exceeding 1000 µg/mL has been reported for other Chromobacterium species60. However, in this strain, differences in the metal concentrations required for pigmented versus non-pigmented growth may provide a useful basis for studying quorum sensing in heavy metal-resistant bacteria.

Identification and annotation of the genome

The SAM215 genome (4.14 Mbp in length, comprising 114 contigs) (Table 3) was identified as Chromobacterium amazonense based on whole-genome comparison-based taxonomy using the Type Strain Genome Server (Fig. 1h) and 16 S rRNA-based phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. S1) with an ANI value of 97.7% and a dDDH value of 89.3%, surpassing species delineation cutoffs of 96% and 70%, respectively61. This classification was further confirmed by SpeciesFinder2.0 and PubMLST. The GC content of the sequence was 62.5%, consistent with the values reported for the Chromobacterium genus, which range from 61.95 to 64.83%4.

A total of 4104 protein-coding sequences and 53 RNA genes, including 47 tRNAs and 5 rRNAs, were predicted in the genome using RAST for annotation (Table 3). Coding sequences had GC content ranging from 30% to 75%, with most clustered around 64.5%, and lengths ranging from 600 to 800 bp (Supplementary Fig. S2). Approximately 27% of the coding sequences were annotated as hypothetical proteins, and 2% corresponded to phage-related protein coding sequences. Moreover, two CRISPR arrays, 37 CRISPR repeats, and 35 CRISPR spacers were identified in the genome. Figure 2a depicts the genomic features of C. amazonense SAM215, additionally showing mobile genetic elements and 6 prophage regions. Two of the prophage regions contained genes encoding holin and integrase, while three harbored endolysin-encoding genes. Two insertion sequences belonging to the IS903 group (IS5 family) were identified at Node 7 (93616–94651) and Node 11 (95706–96741). No plasmids were detected in the draft assembled genome.

Functional annotation of the genes

Predicted protein-coding sequences of SAM215 were annotated using multiple functional databases. RAST identified 310 subsystems with 1,231 features unique to the genome. Figure 2b shows numerous physiological function-related genes, including stress response, virulence factors, pigment production, and cell regulation. Metabolism could be broadly divided into sequential metabolism and genetic material metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S3). A total of 2975 genes were classified into 19 functional categories using the COG database (Fig. 2c), although a significant proportion remained of unknown function. The nr database assigned the majority of genes (3,782) to C. amazonense, placing it at the top of the five most-represented species in the genome of SAM215 (Fig. 2d).

GO annotation assigned 2,980 genes across 13,551 GO terms, grouped into three principal categories: Cellular Components, Molecular Function, and Biological Processes (Fig. 2e). Major functional classes included cellular processes (12.1%), catalytic activity (11.7%), metabolic processes (10.8%), cell and cell part (19.7%), and binding (8.8%). KEGG pathway analysis mapped 3,517 genes to 43 pathways, mostly related to metabolism (74%), including 36 genes involved in xenobiotic biodegradation (Fig. 2f). Rahman et al. reported the isolation of a pesticide-degrading C. amazonense strain (BASUSDA_45) from a pesticide-contaminated site, supporting the species’ ability for xenobiotic degradation, which was also reflected in the genome of the SAM21562 (Supplementary Table S1).

Moreover, 1,876 genes were assigned to enzyme classes such as oxidoreductases and hydrolases, while 177 genes were identified encoding for carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), which are of significance in biotechnology and health63, distributed across five major classes (Supplementary Fig. S4), including chitinases, belonging to the glycoside hydrolase (GH) family64. The high number of annotated genes across diverse functional classes underscored the metabolic versatility of SAM215.

Further insights into the genome

Additionally, the genome contained several genes associated with pathogenicity, stress adaptation, and resistance to antibiotics and heavy metals, as well as genes encoding industrially important enzymes and secondary metabolites (Fig. 3a). The presence of many of these genes highlighted the strain’s biotechnological potential. Although virulence genes were present and β-hemolytic activity was observed, no clinical cases of C. amazonense infection have been reported to date, suggesting a lower risk profile for its use in biotechnological applications than C. violaceum.

Pathogenicity

PathogenFinder predicted C. amazonense SAM215 as a human pathogen with a probability of 0.75, based on matches to 28 pathogenic families. An arsenal of virulence determinants (Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. 3b), including those promoting invasion, adherence, immune evasion, toxins, and secretion systems, was identified in the annotated genome. The presence of pili and EF-Tu genes, previously reported in C. violaceum65, along with a type III secretion system similar to that described in Salmonella66,67, suggests potential roles in adhesion and invasion. Genes potentially associated with evasion of phagosomal killing, including CipC67 and OhrR68-mediated synthesis of O-antigen and capsule, were also detected69–71. Additionally, the presence of toxin genes, including hemolysin, may be associated with the beta-hemolysis seen on the blood agar plate. Quorum sensing has been reported to contribute to pathogenicity in Chromobacterium72 and associated genes were found in the genome of SAM215. Genes encoding collagenase and elastase may be associated with tissue damage73,74. The presence of genes encoding multiple secretion systems in this bacterium, particularly T3SS, suggests potential pathogenic traits67,72. The likelihood of pathogenicity was preliminarily assessed in vivo by intraperitoneal injection of the strain in Swiss albino mice. As shown in Fig. 3c, a difference in survival was observed between mice injected with C. amazonense SAM215 and the untreated control group during the observation period. However, this experiment was conducted as a pilot study and was not designed to support a statistically powered comparison. Therefore, definitive conclusions regarding the significance of this observation cannot be drawn without additional experiments using larger group sizes and complementary endpoints such as bacterial load and histopathological analysis.

Stress adaptation

Several genes associated with protection against various abiotic stresses, including oxidative, osmotic, heat, envelope, and DNA damage, were identified in the C. amazonense SAM215 genome (Fig. 3d). These included genes encoding SoxR, OxyR, and YaaA, which are associated with oxidative and peroxide stress responses in Escherichia coli, 75, as well as the BigR operon, previously reported to support growth under hypoxic conditions in some plant-pathogenic bacteria76. Genes encoding outer membrane lipoproteins, the mechanosensitive channel MscL, and osmoprotectant ABC transporters (OsmV, OsmW, OsmX, and OsmY), which are associated with osmotic stress adaptation in other bacteria, were also detected in the genome77,78. The presence of 14 heat-shock-related coding sequences, including DnaJ-DnaK-GrpE, ClpA/B, HscA/B, Hsp90, Hsp20, and HtpX, suggests potential roles in adaptation to elevated temperature, as well as oxidative and envelope stress79–82. The alkylating agent-inducible gene, aidB, involved in adaptive responses to alkylation damage83, was also detected. Most notably, the operon for violacein production, the signature compound of the genus that has been reported to function as a defense mechanism against predators, was likewise detected84. In addition, genes encoding glutathione peroxidases, catalases, aldolases, and efflux transporters identified in the genome may contribute to resistance against a wide range of toxic compounds.

Resistance to antibiotics and heavy metals

As shown in Table 4, multiple genes encoding β-lactam-inactivating proteins, which hydrolyze many clinically used β-lactams, were identified in the genome. This was phenotypically supported by the antibiotic susceptibility test, in which the isolate was resistant to all tested β-lactams. Although the bacterium contained resistance genes to fluoroquinolones, it remained susceptible to these antibiotics. Additionally, the presence of coding sequences encoding multidrug resistance (MDR) efflux pumps and resistance to other antibiotics may contribute to clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance85.

Several heavy metal resistance genes were also identified in the genome. These included genes encoding ArsC and Acr3, which are associated with arsenic resistance through arsenate reduction and arsenite export, respectively86. Putative transporters for other heavy metals (cadmium, cobalt, copper, chromium, lead, mercury, nickel, and zinc) were also identified in the genome (Table 4). Consistent with this, the isolate exhibited a degree of tolerance to several metals in the experimental observations. These elements occur naturally at low concentrations in rocks, soil, water, and the atmosphere; however, at high concentrations, they may become toxic to organisms87. The relatively high tolerance to iron may be associated with the presence of genes encoding ferric iron-type ABC-transport systems and the fur (ferric uptake regulator) gene, which is involved in the regulation of iron homeostasis and protection against oxidative damage caused by excess iron88.

Biotechnological Potential

Genome annotation of antiSMASH-predicted biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) for several important secondary metabolites, including violacein, hydrogen cyanide, fengycin, surfactin, chromobactin, and 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG) (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table S3), indicates the genomic potential of Chromobacterium amazonense SAM215 to synthesize diverse bioactive compounds. Violacein, the signature pigment produced by Chromobacterium species has been considered a compelling candidate for drug development due to its antimicrobial, antioxidant, and antitumor activities, among other properties84,89.

Violacein production in the strain, as predicted by the presence of the vioABCDE operon, was confirmed by LC-MS/MS analysis (Supplementary Work). The genome also harbored a predicted BGC corresponding to DAPG, an antifungal compound synthesized by the phlABCDE operon in bacteria14. However, experimental validation of DAPG production was not performed in this study. The hydrogen cyanide biosynthesis gene cluster hcnABC was also predicted in the strain. Cyanide production was presumptively detected using a qualitative assay using alkaline picrate paper (Fig. 4b), but quantitative or chemical confirmation was not performed. Hydrogen cyanide has been reported to have applications in gold mining, synthetic fiber industries, and agriculture as an insecticidal compound90.

Additionally, antiSMASH predicted a BGC putatively associated with chromobactin, a catecholate-type siderophore involved in iron acquisition in Chromobacterium species49. Siderophore production by C. amazonense SAM215 was supported by a positive chrome azurol S (CAS) agar assay (Fig. 4c); however, the specific chemical identity of the siderophore could not be confirmed. Nonetheless, while these predictions highlighted the bacterium’s biotechnological potential, it should be noted that BGCs are often poorly resolved in draft genomes generated using short-read Illumina sequencing compared to long-read assemblies. This limitation, largely due to the repetitive sequences present in BGCs, may result in fragmented, incomplete, or even undetected clusters in the present assembly91.

C. amazonense SAM215 genome also possessed genes encoding several industrially relevant enzymes, including chitinase, penicillin acylase, elastase, laccase, quinone oxidoreductase, thermostable carboxypeptidase, and glucoamylase. The strain exhibited multiple plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR)-associated traits, including tolerance to salinity and heavy metals, and the production of cyanide, DAPG, IAA, siderophores, and quorum-sensing capability. However, two key considerations regarding its application include its potential pathogenicity and the possible inhibitory effects on indigenous microbial communities.

Comparative analysis of genomes.

Six C. amazonense genomes, including SAM215, were used for pan-genome analysis. Figure 5a shows that the number of core gene clusters decreased to 3,117, where the curve gradually flattened, while pan gene clusters increased to 6,457. The core-to-pan genome ratio was 0.33, and according to Heap’s law, the beta value was approximately 0.196, suggesting that the C. amazonense pan-genome was relatively closed (Supplementary Fig. S5).

The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5b) revealed BASUSDA_45 and Cv1 as the closest-related strains, also supported by ANI values of 98.6% and 98.8%, respectively (Fig. 5c). The pan-genome COG profile (Fig. 5d) indicated that core genes spanned all functional classes, highlighting their essential housekeeping roles, whereas strain-specific genes were mostly poorly characterized or unannotated, suggesting roles in niche adaptation and strain-level functional diversification. While C. amazonense strain Cv1 harbored the highest number of strain-specific genes, C amazonense SAM215 harbored the lowest number, potentially reflecting differences in ecological specialization. These hypothetical proteins may play roles in adaptation and cell-to-cell communication, indicating the need for further exploration to uncover novel metabolic pathways. These findings were further reflected in the genome synteny analysis (Fig. 5e), which revealed regions of both conservation and variation, with possible hotspots of genomic rearrangement.

Conclusion

The study isolated and characterized an indigenous strain of Chromobacterium amazonense, providing detailed insights into its biochemical, physiological, and genomic properties. Integrating genomic data with observed phenotypic characteristics provided a better understanding of the biology of C. amazonense. This work provided new insights into the ecological and functional significance of this indigenous strain, which may be utilized for future characterization and comparative studies of Chromobacterium and related isolates.

The antibiotic resistance profile and presence of putative pathogenic determinants in the genome warrant further investigation into its clinical relevance. Its ability to grow without producing violacein under various stress conditions can be exploited to study quorum-sensing mechanisms. The presence of several industrially exploitable enzymes and metabolites demonstrates the isolate’s biotechnological potential beyond violacein production. Overall, the observed environmental resilience and metabolic versatility established a foundation for future studies aimed at fully exploring its biotechnological applications.
