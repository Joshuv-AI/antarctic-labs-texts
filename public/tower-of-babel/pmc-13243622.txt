Laser-assisted scanning electrochemical machining of Ti-6Al-4 V for localized patterning
Authors: Shuhei Kodama, Ibuki Okuma, Wataru Natsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTitanium alloys are widely used in aerospace and other industries owing to their low density, high strength, and excellent corrosion resistance, yet their machinability remains challenging. Electrochemical machining (ECM) is a promising non-contact approach; however, the formation of a stable passive film on titanium alloys often suppresses anodic dissolution in scanning ECM. In this study, we propose laser-assisted scanning ECM using an electrolyte suction tool, in which nanosecond laser irradiation locally disrupts the passive film and activates electrochemical dissolution. Parametric experiments identified conditions that produced a V-shaped groove along the laser path even when ECM alone was ineffective. The optimized parameters were an applied voltage of 9.0 V, a laser fluence of 0.5 J/cm2, a scanning speed of 0.25 mm/s, and a 2.0 s irradiation followed by a 2.0 s interval. In addition, extending the voltage application time to 12.0 s after laser irradiation improved groove uniformity. High-speed observation of the electrolyte film during scanning indicated that vigorous bubble generation at higher laser fluences disturbed the current distrubusion, whereas stable machining was achieved by using a lower laser fluence and a slower scanning speed. These results demonstrate that controlling laser activation and post-irradiation voltage duration enables localized pattern machining of Ti-6Al-4 V.Keywords: Ti-6Al-4V, Laser-assisted scanning ECM, Passive film, Bubble dynamicsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Titanium alloys are widely used in aerospace, power generation, and biomedical applications due to their high specific strength and excellent corrosion resistance1. However, they are notoriously difficult to machine by conventional methods. This poor machinability is mainly attributed to low thermal conductivity, high chemical reactivity at cutting temperatures, and a low elastic modulus. Consequently, machining titanium typically requires reduced cutting speeds and feeds to avoid overheating, which lowers productivity while still causing shortened tool life and poor dimensional accuracy2–4. These challenges have been repeatedly emphasized in the machining and manufacturing literature as a key motivation for adopting non-traditional or hybrid processes for titanium alloys5,6.

Electrochemical machining (ECM) has emerged as a promising alternative for such difficult-to-cut metals because it removes material via anodic dissolution without direct tool-workpiece contact or cutting forces7,8. In ECM, a shaped tool (cathode) is positioned close to the workpiece (anode) in a conductive electrolyte; upon applying a DC or pulsed voltage, the workpiece dissolves electrochemically, thereby replicating the tool shape. This process is largely insensitive to material hardness and causes negligible tool wear. Accordingly, ECM and its variants have been widely recognized as viable for producing complex features in difficult-to-cut alloys used in aero-engine components9–11. However, applying ECM to titanium alloys is challenging because titanium naturally forms a tenacious passive oxide film that inhibits anodic dissolution8,12,13. Once formed, the passive film acts as a barrier until it is disrupted; only after local film breakdown can the underlying metal be exposed to fresh electrolyte and begin dissolving again. In scanning ECM, this passivation issue becomes more severe8. During scanning of a titanium alloy, the area just beneath the tool experiences high current density and dissolves, but regions slightly offset from the tool or ahead of the tool before it arrives experience much lower current densities and can passivate due to stray currents. As the tool continues to scan, it encounters a surface that is already passivated in adjacent zones, resulting in non-uniform material removal. Therefore, conventional scanning ECM struggles to produce accurate and uniform features on titanium alloys. In addition, chloride electrolytes and local electrochemical conditions can further influence passive-film stability and breakdown behavior, directly affecting localization and uniformity in scanning ECM14.

To mitigate passivation in ECM, researchers have explored electrical parameter control, electrolyte management, and chemistry modifications. For example, pulsed and bipolar power strategies have been used to promote repeated activation and improve process stability15. Tailoring electrolyte flow and renewal can also reduce reaction product accumulation and help maintain active dissolution; for Ti-6Al-4 V, pulsating electrolyte flow has been reported to influence surface quality and machining behavior16. Speidel et al. reported successful machining of titanium alloys using novel electrolyte compositions (e.g., a NaCl–ethylene glycol mixture), which improved dissolution uniformity by widening the passive breakdown range17. While these approaches can alleviate passivation, they often introduce additional complexity or remain limited in effectiveness.

Another route is hybridization with auxiliary energy fields. Pawar et al. achieved more uniform deep holes in a Ti alloy using ultrasonic-assisted ECM, attributing the improvement to enhanced electrolyte circulation and disruption of the oxide layer7. Thermal assistance can also be introduced, most notably via laser irradiation. Laser-assisted ECM (LECM) has emerged as a viable hybrid method for difficult-to-cut materials, in which localized laser heating can accelerate reaction kinetics and improve localization by directing dissolution to targeted regions18,19. Laser-assisted jet ECM has also been reported to improve precision and removal efficiency in titanium alloys, suggesting that synchronized laser input can localize and stabilize electrochemical dissolution.

Despite these advances, a gap remains for scanning ECM of titanium alloys. Existing approaches either rely on electrical/electrolyte adjustments alone or employ laser assistance in configurations that do not explicitly address (i) stray-current-induced pre-passivation ahead of a moving tool and (ii) practical electrolyte confinement and waste removal during scanning. Here, we integrate laser assistance into scanning ECM using an electrolyte suction tool, which suppresses electrolyte splashing, eliminates the need for a processing chamber, and facilitates waste electrolyte handling. Hizume et al. introduced an electrolyte suction tool for scanning ECM to confine the current path and reduce stray current effects on titanium8. Nevertheless, repassivation can still occur when the local current density decreases during scanning, limiting feature uniformity. To address this, we couple a coaxial laser with suction-tool scanning ECM so that the passive film is removed or destabilized immediately before and during electrochemical dissolution at the tool–workpiece interface. Using this synchronized laser-assisted scanning ECM approach, we investigate efficient, high-precision localized patterning of titanium alloys. The effectiveness of the proposed hybrid method is demonstrated via actual machining experiments. The results are analyzed to verify the synergistic effects of laser and electrochemical dissolution on titanium’s machinability, and the technical feasibility of the laser-assisted scanning ECM process is demonstrated.

Principle of laser-assisted scanning ECM

Laser–material interactions are commonly categorized as (i) non-thermal ablation, in which laser energy is deposited faster than thermal diffusion, and (ii) thermal processing, where laser absorption raises the temperature and can induce melting, evaporation, and plasma formation20–23. This framework is introduced here to clarify the role of the nanosecond laser in laser-assisted scanning ECM (LAS-ECM): the laser is not intended to form the groove by bulk ablation, but to locally disrupt or remove the TiO2-based passive film and transiently activate the titanium surface for electrochemical dissolution. Thus, the laser provides a localized activation step, whereas most of the material removal is achieved electrochemically.

For nanosecond irradiation at 532 nm, disruption and removal of oxide films on titanium alloys have been widely reported to be governed predominantly by photothermal mechanisms, exhibiting threshold-type behavior as a function of fluence24–30. Accordingly, in the present LAS-ECM configuration, laser irradiation is used primarily to destabilize the TiO2-rich passive film and activate the surface immediately prior to dissolution.

Figure 1 illustrates the working principle. When a voltage is applied using an electrolyte suction tool, anodic dissolution occurs mainly beneath the tool electrode, whereas regions exposed to low current density in the surrounding electrolyte tend to repassivate due to stray currents. As the tool scans, such pre-passivation ahead of the tool can hinder uniform dissolution in conventional scanning ECM of titanium alloys. In LAS-ECM, the laser beam is delivered through the tool and irradiates the tool–workpiece interface in a co-moving manner. The laser locally removes or destabilizes the passive film immediately before and, when relevant applicable, during electrochemical dissolution, exposing a temporarily active titanium surface. Consequently, dissolution proceeds preferentially along the laser-activated path, improving stability and reducing interference from passivation.

Laser-assisted electrolytic machining model.

Under the conditions of this study, the process is designed to prioritize oxide-film modification or removal over bulk material ablation. The processing depth achieved by laser irradiation alone is small compared with the final groove depth obtained by LAS-ECM, indicating that the laser primarily acts as a surface activation step rather than a direct material-removal step. Therefore, the groove geometry is governed mainly by electrochemical dissolution, while laser irradiation controls where and when dissolution is initiated by managing the passive film state at the interface.

Verification of laser-assisted scanning electrochemical machining of titanium alloys

Laser-assisted scanning ECM system

Figure 2 shows a schematic of the machining system. The tool and the workpiece were mounted on a translation stage. An electrolyte suction tool was used to confine the electrolyte flow and localize the machining area31. Electrolyte was supplied from a reservoir to the machining zone and then drawn by a pump to a waste tank. The cathode (negative terminal) of the power supply was connected to the tool electrode, and the anode (positive terminal) was connected to the workpiece. A SUS304 bar electrode (1 mm × 1 mm cross-section) was used as the cathode facing the workpiece surface. SUS304 was selected for its electrical conductivity and corrosion resistance in concentrated NaCl electrolytes, which helps maintain a stable electrode geometry during repeated scans. A dedicated laser channel was positioned adjacent to the electrode, and a 1-mm-thick glass plate was installed at the bottom of the channel to prevent electrolyte intrusion. ECM and laser irradiation were applied simultaneously. During scanning in the X direction, the laser irradiated the surface immediately upstream of the electrode so that electrochemical dissolution proceeded while the passive film was locally removed or destabilized, thereby minimizing the influence of repassivation during scanning.

Laser-assisted scanning ECM system.

Experimental conditions

The Nd: YAG laser (Spectra-Physics, Explorer One 532) was operated at a wavelength of 532 nm with a pulse width of 10 ns and a repetition rate of 1 kHz. The laser beam had a Gaussian spatial intensity profile. The 532 nm wavelength was chosen to efficiently heat and modify the TiO2-based passive film on Ti-6Al-4 V in electrolyte, and the 10 ns pulse width was selected to disrupt the passive film with minimal bulk ablation. The beam was steered by mirrors, focused by a focusing lens, delivered through a passage in the tool, and focused to a spot diameter of 100 μm on the workpiece surface in the electrolyte. The spot size was selected as a practical compromise between spatial localization and robust laser delivery through the tool: it is sufficiently small to confine activation to a localized region for patterning, while being large enough to tolerate minor alignment/runout errors and to maintain a consistent irradiated area during scanning. In addition, the spot size is comparable to the characteristic width scale of the grooves targeted in this study, facilitating direct comparison between the laser-activated zone and the resulting ECM groove profile.

The workpiece material was Ti-6Al-4 V. The electrolyte was an aqueous NaCl solution (20 wt%), which is commonly used in ECM and is easy to handle. The initial interelectrode gap was set to 50 μm, and SUS304 was used as the electrode material. Scanning was performed by translating the workpiece mounted on an X-axis stage in the direction of electrolyte flow at scanning speeds of 0.10, 0.25, and 0.50 mm/s. The scanning distance was fixed at 3.0 mm for all conditions. The laser fluence was varied from 0.1 to 5.0 J/cm2, and the ECM voltage was set to 10–20 V. This fluence range was selected to cover (i) a sub-threshold regime where the passive film is expected to remain largely intact and (ii) a regime where passive-film modification/removal enables electrochemical dissolution. Considering the repetition rate and scanning speed, the pulse-to-pulse spacing along the scan direction was 0.10, 0.25, and 0.50 μm at 0.10, 0.25, and 0.50 mm/s, respectively, indicating effectively continuous irradiation relative to the 100 μm spot diameter. Therefore, the present conditions provide quasi-continuous irradiation along the scan path, and differences in machining behavior with scanning speed are attributed primarily to the residence time and the electrochemical/flow response rather than to insufficient pulse overlap. For comparison, experiments were also conducted using ECM alone without laser irradiation.

Influence of laser fluence, ECM Voltage value, and scanning speed on geometry

To evaluate the effects of laser fluence, applied voltage, and scanning speed in the hybrid process, laser irradiation was performed at fluences of 1.0, 2.5, and 5.0 J/cm2. ECM was conducted at two constant voltages (10 and 20 V), and the scanning speed was set to 0.10, 0.25, or 0.50 mm/s. Experiments using ECM alone (without laser irradiation) were also performed for comparison. After machining, cross-sectional profiles were measured along the scan line at a position 1.5 mm from the center of the machined trace.

Figure 3 summarizes the resulting groove profiles. With ECM alone, material removal decreased as the scanning speed increased, whereas the laser-assisted process produced grooves under all tested conditions. This indicates that laser-induced depassivation promotes anodic dissolution near the irradiation site and enables scanning ECM even at 10 V, where machining is difficult without assistance. In addition, the removed volume generally increases with increasing laser fluence at each scanning speed. These trends are consistent with prior studies showing that pulsed laser irradiation can selectively modify or remove oxide films on titanium alloys with limited substrate damage32,33, supporting the effectiveness of the approach used in this study. Moreover, laser irradiation not only removed passive films physically but also transiently enhanced electrolyte properties near the machining interface34,35. Localized heating may reduce electrolyte viscosity and increase ion mobility, further accelerating electrochemical reactions. The preferential material removal confined to the laser-irradiated track provides consistent indirect evidence that the laser locally disrupts the passive film and activates anodic dissolution. Quantitative chemical analysis of oxygen distribution across the machined region will be addressed in future work.

At higher scanning speeds, a W-shaped profile with reduced removal at the center is occasionally observed. One plausible explanation is that higher scanning speeds and/or higher laser fluence promote laser-induced gas/vapor bubble generation near the irradiation point, intermittently disturbing electrolyte contact and the current distribution beneath the electrode. This tendency was more apparent at 10 V, where the dissolution rate is lower and thus more sensitive to transient shielding. Accordingly, electrolyte behavior under laser irradiation was examined using high-speed imaging in Sect. “Observation of electrolyte behavior by laser irradiation of liquid film in scanning state”.

To identify the minimum laser fluence required to initiate laser-assisted dissolution via passive-film disruption, additional experiments were performed at 0.1 and 0.5 J/cm2 under 10 V, a condition under which scanning ECM does not readily proceed without laser assistance. Figures 4 and 5 show optical images of the resulting tracks, and Fig. 6 shows representative cross-sectional profiles. At 0.1 J/cm2, no clear linear track is observed, indicating insufficient depassivation. In contrast, at 0.5 J/cm2, a continuous machined track appears along the laser path, and V-shaped grooves were obtained for all scanning speeds. These results suggest that ~ 0.5 J/cm2 is the minimum fluence for reproducible initiation of laser-assisted scanning ECM under the present conditions, whereas laser irradiation alone at 0.1 and 0.5 J/cm2 did not produce measurable grooves. With a spot diameter of 100 μm, the pulse energy is 7.85 µJ at 0.1 J/cm2 and 39.3 µJ at 0.5 J/cm2. Using the thermal diffusivity of water α = 1.43 × 10− 7 m2/s36, the thermal diffusion length lth = is 37 nm during a 10 ns pulse and 12 μm over the 1 ms interpulse period, indicating negligible heat diffusion during the pulse and dissipation mainly between pulses. Assuming an effective heated liquid thickness of 10–100 μm and a volumetric heat capacity Cv = 4.18 MJ/m3K37, the near-interface temperature rise per pulse at 0.5 J/cm2 is estimated to be on the order of several kelvin to several tens of kelvin depending on the absorbed fraction. These considerations support a predominantly photothermal contribution under the present nanosecond irradiation in electrolyte.

This threshold-type behavior agrees with prior nanosecond-laser studies reporting that TiO2-based films on titanium alloys are removed or significantly destabilized only when the fluence exceeds a critical, predominantly thermally driven removal threshold; below this threshold, the oxide film tends to remain largely intact or is only partially cracked, leading to incomplete and less reproducible activation of the surface24–30. Given the well-known stability of TiO₂-based passive films in chloride-containing electrolytes38–40, the transition from no clear track at 0.1 J/cm2 to continuous linear dissolution at 0.5 J/cm2 is reasonably interpreted as the onset of effective depassivation rather than bulk laser ablation. Therefore, the depassivation threshold in the present setup is concluded to lie between 0.1 and 0.5 J/cm2, and stable laser-assisted scanning ECM becomes feasible at 0.5 J/cm2 and above.

Observation of electrolyte behavior by laser irradiation of liquid film in scanning state

Under the low-voltage condition (10 V), the machined profiles tended to become W-shaped at higher scanning speeds, and this tendency depended on the applied laser fluence. To investigate a possible physical origin, the effect of laser irradiation on the electrolyte was examined by observing a liquid film on Ti-6Al-4 V under scanning-like conditions. A liquid film approximately 2 mm thick was prepared on the workpiece surface, and the laser-irradiated region was imaged at 45° using a high-speed camera. The laser fluence was set to 0.5, 1.0, and 2.5 J/cm2.

Figure 7 shows representative snapshots during irradiation, and Fig. 8 shows snapshots immediately after the end of irradiation; the blue box indicates the laser-line irradiation region. At 0.5 J/cm2, bubble generation is limited, whereas clear bubble formation is observed at 1.0 J/cm2 and becomes more vigorous at 2.5 J/cm2. This trend is consistent with prior reports on nanosecond laser irradiation in liquids, in which higher fluence promotes more intense bubble activity12,41. With increasing scanning speed, the irradiation time at a given location decreased, and the overall bubble presence within the field of view tended to decrease. After irradiation of the liquid, bubbles nucleated near the irradiated region, grew to a characteristic size on the order of 100 μm, and subsequently merged and split while being advected by the flow induced by rapid expansion.

To visualize the spatial scale of bubble activity, a magnified view extracted from Fig. 7(h) is presented in Fig. 9. Figure 9 shows that bubbles rise within a lateral range of approximately 100 μm, which is comparable to the laser spot diameter of 100 μm. This length-scale agreement is consistent with the reduced material removal observed near the groove center in W-shaped profiles, suggesting that laser-induced bubbles can intermittently shield the electrolyte pathway and locally increase the effective resistance in the interelectrode gap, thereby perturbing the current distribution beneath the electrode.

Magnified image enlarged from Fig. 7(h).

The high-speed frame sequences further indicate that the characteristic bubble size is on the order of 100 μm and that bubbles appear, move radially, and then stagnate before being advected with the workpiece motion. These observations provide qualitative but direct evidence that bubble activity occurs on a spatial scale comparable to the laser spot and can intermittently interfere with electrolyte contact near the machining interface. Quantifying bubble size and lifetime statistics under flow remains an important topic for future work.

Bubble-induced shielding and current-distribution disturbances in electrochemical machining have been discussed in prior studies26,42. In addition, laser-induced passive-film disruption creates a locally depassivated metal surface that can behave as a low-resistance, high-exchange-current reaction hotspot. Once such an activated site is created, anodic dissolution preferentially initiates there, and subsequent material removal may appear to spread laterally via two coupled mechanisms: (i) geometric growth of the dissolution pit/groove from the activated site and (ii) an increased probability of passive-film breakdown in the vicinity due to changes in local potential distribution, including field and IR-drop effects and local chemistry (e.g., chloride-driven destabilization). Such localized breakdown/growth behavior and the stability of anodic films on Ti-6Al-4 V in chloride-containing environments have been documented in electrochemical studies43–45. When bubbles transiently suppress dissolution near the groove center, the current can be redistributed toward neighboring laser-activated regions. Dissolution can then develop and broaden via hotspot growth and nearby breakdown, ultimately yielding a W-shaped cross-sectional profile.

Machining with controlled machining area

Investigation of influence of laser presence during machining

To clarify the role of laser irradiation in laser-assisted scanning ECM (LAS-ECM), we implemented a controlled scanning strategy in which irradiated and non-irradiated segments were alternated during continuous processing. The applied voltage was kept constant with and without laser irradiation. In this section, the laser fluence and applied voltage were set to 0.5 J/cm² and 10 V, respectively, and the workpiece was scanned at 0.25 mm/s. Two interval conditions were examined: (1) 4.0 s irradiation followed by a 4.0 s non-irradiation interval (corresponding to a 1.0 mm spacing) and (2) 2.0 s irradiation followed by a 2.0 s non-irradiation interval (0.5 mm spacing). Figure 10 shows optical images of the machining traces, and Fig. 11 shows the corresponding contour profiles measured along the red dashed line in Fig. 10.

Measured profiles of interval patterning. (a) Y-direction (1.0 mm interval); (b) X-direction (1.0 mm interval); (c) Y-direction (0.5 mm interval); (d) X-direction (0.5 mm interval).

For both interval conditions, localized discoloration appeared only in the irradiated segments, whereas no apparent change was observed in the non-irradiated segments. A relatively broad discoloration zone was also observed adjacent to the first irradiated segment, which likely resulted from electrochemical dissolution immediately after voltage application when the surface was not yet fully passivated. The contour profiles confirmed that the discolored regions corresponded to actual groove formation.

Machining depth varied along the scan path, with shallower grooves near the start and end of the scan. This variation can be explained by an imbalance in the effective dissolution time and current distribution at the scan boundaries. At the start of scanning, the current distribution is not yet stabilized and part of the current can spread to nearby regions (consistent with the broader discoloration adjacent to the first irradiated segment), reducing the effective dissolution rate within the intended laser-activated track. At the end of scanning, the last irradiated segment has the shortest available time for electrochemical dissolution before the process terminates, resulting in a shallower depth.

Based on this interpretation, two practical strategies can improve uniformity: (i) suppress undesired dissolution at the moment of voltage application by selecting conditions that limit electrolysis outside the laser-activated zone, and (ii) extend the post-irradiation voltage application time to ensure sufficient dissolution time for the terminal irradiated segment. These results demonstrate that, under low-voltage conditions where scanning ECM alone is ineffective, spatial modulation of laser irradiation enables selective control of the machining area, allowing localized and programmable material removal through deliberate depassivation control.

Investigation of Voltage Conditions to Control Occurrence of Pits

Scattered point-like machining marks were observed outside the laser-irradiated regions. These features are attributed to pit formation caused by unintended electrochemical reactions initiated at weak points in the passive film, which degrades machining selectivity and surface quality. To mitigate pit formation, additional experiments were performed at reduced applied voltages (7.0, 8.0, and 9.0 V) while maintaining a laser fluence of 0.5 J/cm² and an irradiated/non-irradiated spacing of 1.0 mm (Sect. “Investigation of influence of laser presence during machining”). At 7.0 V, no visible machining marks were observed, indicating that the voltage was insufficient to sustain dissolution even after laser-induced depassivation. Therefore, the following analysis focuses on 8.0 and 9.0 V.

Figure 12 shows optical images of the machined regions. Compared with 10 V, where numerous circular pits were observed, both 8.0 V and 9.0 V exhibit markedly improved surface integrity with pit formation largely suppressed. This suggests that reducing the applied voltage confines electrochemical activity primarily to the laser-irradiated zones where the passive film has been disrupted, thereby enhancing machining selectivity. Representative contour profiles along the scan path are shown in Fig. 13. At 8.0 V, grooves are formed along the irradiated regions, although the depth is relatively shallow and somewhat variable. At 9.0 V, both the machining depth and uniformity improve; however, localized deepening is occasionally observed near the ends of some irradiated segments, which may reflect continued electrochemical activity near irradiated/non-irradiated transitions where electrolyte access and surface state differ.

Optical micrographs of machining marks under different voltages. (a) 8.0 V; (b) 9.0 V.

Measured profiles under different voltages. (a) Y-direction (8.0 V); (b) X-direction (8.0 V); (c) Y-direction (9.0 V); (d) X-direction (9.0 V).

Pit formation under conventional ECM at higher voltages has been attributed to localized passive-film breakdown and subsequent concentrated dissolution46. By reducing the applied voltage, anodic dissolution can be preferentially confined to laser-activated regions, thereby minimizing pit formation and improving precision47. This demonstrates that voltage control is essential for ensuring selective material removal and enhancing machining precision.

Applied voltage is a key factor that governs how far and how uniformly dissolution will spread from the laser-activated sites. In ECM of Ti-6Al-4 V, a critical potential can exist beyond which the passive film undergoes widespread breakdown. In a chloride-rich electrolyte, this breakdown can occur at relatively low voltages due to chloride’s aggressive attack on TiO₂48. At 8 V slightly below breakdown, the intact passive oxide remains stable over most of the surface; consequently, only the laser-created oxide-free spots dissolve actively. The etching is highly localized, essentially confined to where the laser seeded the dissolution by removing the film. However, when the voltage is raised to 9 V, the surrounding oxide film can undergo spontaneous breakdown and chemical attack, and dissolution begins to spread beyond the initial laser spots. This results in a greater spatial extent of anodic dissolution and a more uniform erosion of the surface49,50. Namely, raising the applied voltage in laser-assisted ECM of Ti-6Al-4 V tends to expand the corrosion front from the laser-ablated nuclei to a broader area by breaking down the surrounding passive film. This yields more uniform material removal at slightly higher voltages due to a more even current density distribution and enhanced oxide dissolution kinetics.

Additionally, the progressive reduction in machining depth toward the terminal end of the scanning path was again observed, consistent with earlier findings. Raising the voltage to 10 V causes the potential to reach the critical level. As shown in Fig. 9, this leads to the breakdown of the passive film directly beneath the tool, resulting in its localized breakdown. During scanning, current concentrates at the locations where the passive film was removed by the laser, causing localized processing to proceed.

Overall, under the laser-assisted conditions employed here, scanning ECM proceeded at 8.0 V, and pit formation outside the laser-irradiated zones was effectively suppressed at 9.0 V. These findings highlight that careful optimization of the applied voltage is critical for achieving selective, high-precision machining in laser-assisted scanning ECM of Ti-6Al-4 V.

Investigation for stabilization of end-stage machining in laser-assisted scanning ECM

Evaluation of behavior at end of processing by different number of irradiations

To further examine the hypothesized mechanism, additional experiments were conducted by varying the number of laser irradiation cycles. The applied voltage was fixed at 9.0 V, which was identified as an optimal condition for stable machining, while all other parameters were kept the same as those used for the uniform 1.0 mm–spaced machining condition. Figure 14 shows cross-sectional profiles measured along the X- and Y-directions through the center of the machined traces for each irradiation condition.

Measured profiles with different numbers of laser irradiations. (a) X-direction; (b) Y-direction (first irradiation section); (c) Y-direction (second irradiation section); (d) Y-direction (third irradiation section).

Regardless of the number of irradiation cycles, the machining depth consistently decreases toward the end of the final irradiated section. In addition, within each irradiated section, the machining depth decreases from the first to subsequent passes; the first pass produced a deeper groove than the second and third passes.

To clarify the roles of laser activation and subsequent electrochemical removal, we distinguish (i) the activation time, during which the laser disrupts the passive film and creates a temporarily active surface, from (ii) the reaction time, during which anodic dissolution proceeds under the applied voltage while electrolyte contact is maintained. In the present process, laser irradiation primarily defines where dissolution can initiate (via local depassivation), whereas the accumulated depth is governed mainly by the effective reaction time available after activation.

This behavior is attributed to the increased scanning distance and processing time afforded by multiple irradiation cycles, allowing cumulative electrochemical dissolution. Moreover, the results indicate that electrochemical dissolution does not occur exclusively at regions of freshly removed passive film; rather, electrochemical activity persists in previously irradiated areas, gradually consuming material even after the electrode has moved past. Therefore, repeated irradiation does not simply remove the film again, but effectively extends and redistributes the ECM reaction time over the already-activated track, leading to larger cumulative removal in earlier sections while the terminal region still suffers from limited reaction time before processing ends.

These findings support the interpretation that insufficient processing time immediately after passive film removal leads to a decline in machining depth toward the terminal regions, emphasizing the critical need to optimize scan strategies for uniform material removal.

Improved uniformity by adapting reciprocal scanning

Because insufficient end-stage processing time was identified as a limiting factor, an alternative scanning strategy was evaluated: reciprocating (round-trip) scanning. In this experiment, continuous laser irradiation was applied without discrete irradiated and non-irradiated sections. The workpiece was scanned under two conditions: (1) one-way scanning over a 2.0 mm length, and (2) reciprocating scanning over the same 2.0 mm length, with either one or three round trips. Figure 15 presents the machining trace images and corresponding schematics. Contour profiles along the scanning direction, indicated by the red dashed arrows, are shown in Fig. 16. Under one-way scanning conditions, a pronounced decrease in machining depth toward the scan endpoint was confirmed, consistent with previous results. In contrast, round-trip scanning markedly improved machining depth uniformity at the center of the trace, particularly in the region experiencing repeated laser passes and cumulative electrochemical activation.

Measured profiles for reciprocal scanning. (a) X-direction; (b) Y-direction (one-way); (c) Y-direction (1 round-trip); (d) Y-direction (3 round-trips).

However, new non-uniformities emerged: the machining width near the scan start point was consistently wider than at the turnaround point (scan reversal location), leading to asymmetric groove profiles. This discrepancy was more pronounced under the three-round-trip condition, likely due to cumulative material removal accentuating width expansion at regions exposed to repeated processing. Thus, while round-trip scanning mitigates depth non-uniformity across the scan center, it simultaneously introduces width non-uniformity, indicating that simple reciprocating strategies alone may be insufficient to achieve fully uniform machining profiles. Further optimization, such as tailored voltage modulation, dynamic scan speed control, or localized electrolyte flow management, may be required to fully stabilize the machining profile during laser-assisted scanning ECM.

Improved uniformity at end of processing by extending voltage application time

As an alternative strategy to improve machining uniformity, we investigated the effect of extending the voltage application time after the end of laser irradiation during one-way scanning. By sustaining anodic dissolution after laser exposure, sufficient dissolution time may be provided even in terminal regions. Experiments were conducted under three conditions, in which the voltage was continuously applied for 4.0, 8.0, or 12.0 s after the end of laser irradiation. Figure 17 shows images and schematics of the resulting traces, and Fig. 18 compares the contour profiles with those obtained under one-way scanning without post-irradiation voltage extension.

Extending the post-irradiation voltage duration increases the overall removal volume. Along the X-direction, the bottom-surface slope becomes more gradual, indicating improved depth uniformity. Along the Y-direction, width variation is reduced compared with the pronounced width changes observed under reciprocating scanning. These results indicate that extending the post-irradiation voltage duration improves both depth and width uniformity.

Uniform pattern processing by adjusting voltage application time at end of processing

Building upon these findings, we investigated whether post-irradiation voltage extension can be applied to patterned machining to achieve precise and uniform features. Based on the previously optimized conditions (applied voltage: 9.0 V; laser fluence: 0.5 J/cm2; irradiation/interval: 2.0 s/2.0 s; scanning speed: 0.25 mm/s), the post-irradiation voltage duration was extended to 8.0–12.0 s as shown in Fig. 19. With an 8.0 s extension, the machining width remains consistent across all irradiated segments; however, the machining depth gradually decreases toward the end of the scan, indicating that the available dissolution time is still insufficient to fully stabilize removal. In contrast, with a 12.0 s extension, both machining depth and width remain uniform throughout the scan path, indicating that sufficient post-irradiation dissolution time is secured.

Repeatability under the final optimized condition (12.0 s extension) was evaluated by repeating the experiment three times under identical parameters. The resulting groove depth was 15.2 ± 1.4 μm, and the groove width was 282.8 ± 21.1 μm. These results demonstrate repeatable, uniform pattern formation under the optimized condition.

Overall, extending the post-irradiation voltage duration is effective not only for improving one-way scanning uniformity but also for achieving consistent depth and width in patterned machining. Careful control of voltage timing is therefore a key parameter for optimizing laser-assisted scanning ECM, particularly for high-precision patterning.

Conclusions

This study proposed laser-assisted scanning electrochemical machining (LAS-ECM) for titanium alloys to overcome passivation that suppresses anodic dissolution in scanning ECM. The results demonstrate that synchronized nanosecond laser irradiation enables local depassivation and promotes stable scanning electrochemical dissolution.

V-shaped grooves were achieved by optimizing the laser fluence, while excessive laser-induced bubble generation hindered uniform removal, occasionally producing W-shaped profiles. Spatial selectivity was demonstrated by alternating irradiated and non-irradiated segments, and pit formation outside the irradiated regions was largely suppressed by optimizing the applied voltage. In addition, extending the post-irradiation voltage duration improved depth and width uniformity, particularly near the scan endpoint, and enabled repeatable, uniform patterned machining. These findings indicate that LAS-ECM is a promising approach for materials in which passive films limit conventional electrochemical machining.

Limitations of this study include the lack of quantitative chemical characterization of the passive film state (e.g., oxygen distribution measured by XPS and EDS) and the absence of statistical quantification of bubble dynamics (size and lifetime) under scanning flow conditions. Future work will focus on (i) correlating surface chemistry with machining response using ex situ and in situ analyses, (ii) quantitatively characterizing bubble behavior and its influence on current distribution, and (iii) further optimizing the synchronization between laser activation and post-irradiation voltage duration to improve uniformity and patterning accuracy.
