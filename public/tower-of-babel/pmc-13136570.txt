Impact of Thyroid CT on Detecting Macroscopic Nodal Metastasis in Patients With Papillary Thyroid Microcarcinoma
Authors: Young Hun Jeon, Ji Ye Lee, Taehyuk Ham, Kyu Sung Choi, Inpyeong Hwang, Roh-Eul Yoo, Koung Mi Kang, Ji-hoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of adding CT to ultrasound (US) for nodal assessment in patients with papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC), particularly in those with US-node-negative disease.Materials and MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included consecutive patients with PTMC (≤1 cm on US) who underwent both US and CT for PTMC staging between August 2016 and January 2020, and subsequently underwent surgery including neck dissection. The number of patients with clinical N1 and pathological N1 disease was assessed. The diagnostic performance of US, CT, and combined US + CT (positive if either was positive) for macroscopic lymph node metastasis (LNM) (i.e., metastatic tumor foci >2 mm) was evaluated. Cases with discordant nodal staging between US and CT were identified. The diagnostic utility of CT was also assessed in a subgroup of patients with node-negative findings on US.ResultsAmong 982 patients (mean age ± standard deviation, 47.3 ± 11.5 years; 774 female), pathological analysis confirmed cervical LNM in 377 patients, including macroscopic, microscopic, and size-unknown LNM in 187, 175, and 15 patients, respectively. The addition of CT to US improved sensitivity for detecting macroscopic LNM compared to US alone (68.4% [128/187] vs. 26.7% [50/187]; P < 0.001), while maintaining high specificity despite a significant decrease (90.9% [709/780] vs. 97.2% [758/780]; P < 0.001). Discordant nodal staging between US and CT regarding macroscopic LNM was observed in 149 cases (15.2% [149/982]), with 131 patients (87.9% [131/149]) being upstaged by CT. In patients with node-negative US findings, CT detected US-undetected macroscopic LNM in 78 patients (8.7% [78/895]) and exhibited a sensitivity of 56.9% (78/137) and specificity of 93.5% (709/758) for macroscopic LNM.ConclusionThe integration of CT with US improved sensitivity for detecting macroscopic LNM in patients with PTMC, identifying those who would otherwise be inappropriately considered candidates for active surveillance based solely on US findings. This may assist in refining patient management.Keywords: Thyroid, Papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, Computed tomography, Lymph nodes, Neoplasm metastasis

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC; papillary thyroid carcinoma [PTC] with a maximal diameter ≤10 mm) has rapidly increased with the widespread adoption of imaging modalities [1,2]. National guidelines from Japan [3], the United States [4], and South Korea [5] increasingly support active surveillance (AS) as an alternative to immediate surgery in patients with low-risk PTMC. These guidelines commonly define “low-risk” as tumors without clinically evident lymph node metastases (LNM), distant metastases, local invasion, high-risk tumor location, or aggressive cytology [6]. Although the presence of cervical LNM has traditionally been regarded as an indication for immediate surgery, its prognostic relevance is now recognized as multifactorial [4,7]. Notably, the 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines recommend against prophylactic central neck dissection (CND) for clinically node-negative PTC [8], aligning with previous studies demonstrating that microscopic LNM (metastatic tumor foci ≤2 mm) exerts minimal prognostic impact [9,10,11,12]. Consequently, accurate detection of macroscopic nodal metastasis is essential for appropriate risk stratification and the correct identification of patients suitable for AS.

Ultrasound (US) is the primary imaging modality for nodal evaluation in patients with thyroid cancer; however, its limited sensitivity often leads to uncertainty in clinical decision making [13,14,15,16]. To address this limitation, CT has been adopted as a complementary imaging tool [11,17,18,19]. Recent meta-analyses have shown that combining CT with US increases sensitivity for detecting central compartment LNM (41%–46%) compared to US alone (28%–35.3%) [15,16]. A recent multicenter study further supported the value of combined imaging by demonstrating that patients with negative US and CT findings rarely had clinically significant nodal disease [14].

Although AS is gaining clinical attention as a management option for low-risk PTMC, the limited sensitivity of US remains a potential barrier to its broader adoption. Although several guidelines recommend complementary CT in advanced thyroid cancer [4,20], its benefit in the routine evaluation of US-node-negative PTMC, which is often considered low risk, remains unclear. Moreover, most previous studies focused on the diagnostic performance of CT for overall LNM rather than distinguishing macroscopic diseases, thereby limiting its clinical utility [11,18,21,22]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess whether combining CT with US improves the detection of macroscopic LNM and determination of the appropriateness of AS in patients with PTMC, particularly those with US-node-negative disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2410-085-1576), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study Population

Consecutive patients with PTC who underwent preoperative US and CT between August 2016 and January 2020 were included. At our institution, preoperative CT is routinely performed in all patients with PTC to evaluate nodal staging. In this study, PTMC was defined as PTC with a maximal diameter ≤1 cm, as measured on preoperative US. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) primary tumor with a maximal diameter >1 cm on US, 2) no further management after diagnosis at our hospital, and 3) concurrent malignancy. For patients with low-risk PTMC, endocrinologists with >10 years of thyroid-specific experience provide detailed counseling on current management options, surgery, and AS using a decision aid [23]. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has been discussed as an alternative management strategy [24]. Patients who underwent nonsurgical management (AS, n = 173; RFA, n = 26) were subsequently excluded. For the present analysis evaluating the diagnostic performance of CT in addition to US for detecting cervical LNM, 49 patients who did not undergo neck dissection were also excluded (total thyroidectomy, n = 13; lobectomy, n = 36). Neck US and CT Image Acquisition

All US images were acquired using high-resolution US units with linear transducers (7.5–10.5 MHz) by experienced radiologists or chief radiology residents under the supervision of attending radiologists. Grayscale and color Doppler images were obtained before US-guided biopsy. For LNM assessment, a thorough US examination was performed across the bilateral cervical compartments (levels I–VI) [25].

CT scans were obtained using multidetector CT scanners (16–256 channels) based on the thyroid cancer-dedicated neck CT protocol recommended by the Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology (KSThR) [5]. Detailed imaging protocols and workflows are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Image Analysis and LN Diagnosis

Nodal classifications for US were based on official radiology reports generated at the time of the initial examination. Two board-certified radiologists (reader 1: Y.H.J. and reader 2: J.Y.L., with 4 and 10 years of experience in thyroid, head, and neck imaging, respectively) retrospectively reviewed all CT images and were blinded to the clinical information, US reports, and pathology results. Interobserver agreement was also recorded. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus during the joint review sessions.

Based on the KSThR guidelines [5], lymph nodes (LNs) were classified as probably benign, indeterminate, or suspicious. The detailed diagnostic criteria for each category of US and CT are provided in Supplementary Material 2. A test-positive LN diagnosis was defined as the presence of any suspicious features on a single modality (US alone or CT alone) or on either modality in the combined US + CT analysis [22]. To correlate with the histopathological reference standard, the anatomical levels of suspicious nodes detected on US or CT were carefully matched to the levels of metastatic nodes confirmed in the final surgical pathology report.

US-Guided Biopsy and Surgical Pathology Analysis

Board-certified radiologists performed US-guided fine-needle aspiration and thyroglobulin measurement (FNA-Tg) or core-needle biopsy for suspicious LNs identified on either US or CT, regardless of size, and for indeterminate LNs with a short-axis diameter >5 mm [5]. Biopsy of the probably benign LNs was performed at the discretion of the operator or upon request from the referring physician. Details of the procedures are provided in Supplementary Material 3.

All patients underwent a total thyroidectomy or lobectomy with neck dissection. In cases where clinical N1a was confirmed during preoperative evaluation, total thyroidectomy and CND were performed. In cases with preoperative confirmation of lateral neck metastasis (cN1b), modified radical neck dissection was performed on the affected side. Surgical pathology reports documented tumor characteristics as follows: 1) type of surgery, 2) primary tumor characteristics (location, tumor size, histopathologic subtype, lymphatic and vascular invasion, and extrathyroidal extension), 3) LN characteristics (location, number of metastatic LNs, and maximum diameter of metastatic tumor foci within the LN), and 4) pathological TNM staging. The reference standard for the LN status (final diagnosis) was determined by pathological examination of the surgical specimen, independent of the preoperative biopsy results. LN size was categorized as microscopic LNM (≤2.0 mm) and macroscopic LNM (>2.0 mm) based on the largest dimension of metastatic tumor foci [25].

Statistical Analysis

The number of patients with clinical N1 and pathological N1 disease was assessed. To compare the diagnostic performance of imaging tests for detecting cervical LNM, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value, and diagnostic accuracy were calculated using surgical histopathology as the reference standard. Sensitivity and specificity were compared between US, CT, and combined US + CT using McNemar’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple pairwise comparisons. Further analysis was conducted specifically for macroscopic LNM. For this analysis, the reference standard was defined by categorizing macroscopic LNM cases as positive and microscopic LNM and node-negative cases as negative. To evaluate the added value of CT in cases of discordant nodal staging between US and CT (upstaging or downstaging), we assessed which modality correctly predicted the presence of macroscopic LNM. To assess the impact of adding CT in cases missed on US, the diagnostic performance of CT was evaluated in patients with clinically node-negative findings on US (UScN0) according to final nodal staging (N1a vs. N1b) and metastasis size, as determined by histopathology. The diagnostic metrics were calculated on a per-patient basis. Interobserver agreement between the two CT readers was assessed using kappa (κ) statistics. All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc software (version 20.112; MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium).

Diagnostic Performance for Detecting Cervical LN Metastasis Based on Imaging Tests

Table 2 summarizes the diagnostic performances of US, CT, and combined US + CT for detecting LNM. For overall LNM, CT alone (41.4% [156/377], 95% CI: 36.4%–46.5%) demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity compared to US alone (14.9% [56/377], 95% CI: 11.5%–18.9%; P < 0.001). The specificity of CT was 94.7% (573/605, 95% CI: 92.6%–96.4%). Integrating CT with US increased the sensitivity compared to US alone, from 14.9% (56/377, 95% CI: 11.5%–18.9%) to 42.7% (161/377, 95% CI: 37.7%–47.9%; P = 0.005), although US alone maintained slightly higher specificity (97.2% [588/605]) than CT alone (94.7% [573/605]) and combined US + CT (93.2% [564/605]; P < 0.001). Notably, all imaging modalities demonstrated high specificity (>93%) in detecting LNM.

For macroscopic LNM, the addition of CT to US significantly increased the sensitivity (68.4% [128/187] vs. 26.7% [50/187]; P < 0.001) and overall diagnostic accuracy (86.6% [837/967] vs. 83.6% [808/967]; P < 0.001) compared with US alone, while maintaining a high specificity despite significant reduction (90.9% [709/780] vs. 97.2% [758/780]; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Among macroscopic metastases missed by both imaging modalities, most were small lesions (≤5 mm) located in the central compartment (76.3% [45/59]) (Supplementary Table 1). Interobserver agreement for CT-based nodal classification demonstrated substantial concordance (κ = 0.749; 95% CI: 0.692–0.806) (Supplementary Table 2).

Comparison of Nodal Staging Based on US and CT Imaging

Table 3 summarizes the discordant nodal staging findings between CT and US based on the presence of macroscopic LNM. Discordant staging was observed in 149 of the 982 patients (15.2%). Among these, 131 (87.9% [131/149]) were upstaged based on CT findings compared to US findings, while 18 (12.1% [18/149]) were downstaged by CT. In the CT-upstaged group (n = 131), CT correctly identified macroscopic LNM in 80 patients (61.1% [80/131]). Specifically, among patients with US-cN0 tumors, CT correctly identified US-undetected macroscopic LNM in 78 cases (67 central and 11 lateral) (Figs. 3, 4). In contrast, CT overstaged 41 patients (31.3% [41/131]). In the CT-downstaged group (n = 18), CT correctly excluded macroscopic LNM in 13 patients (72.2% [13/18]), thereby correcting false-positive diagnoses made using US alone. However, CT failed to detect macroscopic LNM in 2 patients (11.1% [2/18]) who were correctly identified by US. A detailed analysis of the 127 US-negative, CT-positive cases revealed that 59.8% (76/127) of the cases resulted from LNs not being detected on US, rather than being interpreted differently (Supplementary Table 3).

Impact of Adding CT in Patients With Clinically Node-Negative Findings on US According to LN Location and Size

Table 4 summarizes the diagnostic performance of CT for detecting central and lateral LNM in 909 patients with clinically UScN0. Across cervical compartments, CT demonstrated high specificity (95.9% [564/588]) and positive predictive value (PPV; 81.4% [105/129]) for identifying pathologic N1 disease, with a corresponding trade-off of relatively low sensitivity (32.7% [105/321]). Notably, CT was particularly effective in identifying lateral LNM in UScN0 patients, with a sensitivity of 81.3% (13/16) and a PPV of 100% (13/13), without any reduction in specificity (100% [3/3]). For macroscopic LNM, CT demonstrated a sensitivity of 56.9% (78/137) and a specificity of 93.5% (709/758), detecting additional macroscopic metastases in 78 patients (8.7%) among 895 UScN0 cases.

Analysis of the characteristics of US-undetected metastases revealed distinct size distributions by compartment. In the central compartment (n = 75), most metastatic tumor foci measured 2.1–5.0 mm (72.0% [54/75]). In contrast, in the lateral compartment (n = 11), metastatic foci measuring 5.1–10.0 mm predominated (63.6% [7/11]) over those measuring 2.1–5.0 mm (36.4% [4/11]). No metastatic tumor foci larger than 10 mm were identified in either compartment (Supplementary Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Accurate nodal staging is essential for selecting candidates for AS in patients with PTMC. However, US alone still shows limited diagnostic accuracy in identifying clinically significant nodal disease. In this study, we evaluated the added diagnostic value of preoperative CT, particularly in patients with node-negative US findings. The integration of CT with US significantly improved the sensitivity for detecting macroscopic LNM (68.4% vs. 26.7%, P < 0.001) compared to US alone. Most importantly, CT identified additional macroscopic LNM in 8.7% of patients with node-negative findings on US. These findings highlight that CT can further optimize the selection of AS candidates among patients initially screened using US and considered for AS.

LNM is a well-established prognostic factor for locoregional recurrence and persistent disease in patients with PTC [7,26,27]. Large cohort studies have shown that PTMC also carries a wide range of pathologic LNM rates and that preoperative assessment performed by US alone may fail to detect clinically significant nodal involvement [13,28,29,30,31]. For instance, Zhao et al. [32] reported that US missed 149 of 392 (38.0%) LNM in pathologically confirmed PTMC cases. CT has been employed as a complementary modality to address this limitation, and recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that combining CT with US significantly enhances the sensitivity of detecting LNM, particularly in the central compartment, without compromising accuracy [15,16,33]. However, few studies have evaluated the diagnostic value of adding CT specifically in patients with PTMC undergoing AS evaluation [11]. Our findings address this gap by showing that adding CT to US markedly improves the sensitivity for detecting macroscopic LNM. Enhanced diagnostic accuracy is critical for precise staging and the identification of truly “low-risk” cases, thereby supporting optimal treatment selection.

Our study revealed that CT detected macroscopic LNM in 78 (8.7%) of the 895 patients who were initially considered node-negative on US. This result contrasts with the low nodal recurrence rate (1.6% at 20 years) reported in Japanese AS cohorts managed primarily using US [34]. This apparent discrepancy is likely explained by the indolent natural history of small-volume macroscopic LNM, which are negative on US but detectable on CT; the low long-term recurrence rate in these cohorts may reflect the slow progression of this preexisting disease [27]. However, given that the presence of macroscopic LNM is generally considered a contraindication for AS, our findings suggest that the addition of CT facilitates accurate identification of patients with clinically significant nodal disease who may require immediate surgery, thereby reducing the likelihood of including suboptimal candidates in AS protocols.

The central compartment is the most common site of nodal metastasis frequently missed by US alone [13,21]. In this study, CT correctly identified central macroscopic LNM in 59.1% of patients who were node-negative on US but node-positive on CT. This finding aligns with previous studies that reported that the addition of preoperative CT altered surgical planning in 22.5%–25% of patients with PTC [19,35]. Given that the nodal tumor burden is a key determinant of disease progression during AS in PTMC [19,35], these results underscore the role of CT in overcoming the inherent sensitivity limitations of US in the central neck.

While the integration of CT with US results in a modest reduction in specificity, this tradeoff is offset by a substantial gain in sensitivity for detecting cervical LNM. Novel LNM, a major driver of disease progression in AS, often necessitates extensive nodal dissection and increases the likelihood of adjuvant radioactive iodine therapy [34,36]. Notably, previous studies have reported that patients undergoing conversion surgery due to disease progression exhibit a higher incidence of unfavorable pathological features and more extensive nodal involvement than those undergoing immediate surgery [37]. Moreover, although false-positive CT findings can often be clarified through preoperative cytological assessment, no comparable safeguard exists for false-negative cases. Therefore, the improved sensitivity of CT may help reduce the risk of AS failure and minimize the need for delayed and more extensive surgical interventions.

In UScN0 patients, CT demonstrated high specificity and a PPV of 100% for detecting lateral LNM. However, this likely reflects selection bias, as MRND was performed only when preoperative biopsy or intraoperative frozen sections confirmed LNM [11]. In 55.6% of the discordant cases, lateral LNM identified solely on CT was considered of questionable clinical significance, given that no recurrence was detected during postoperative follow-up despite the absence of surgical resection of these nodes. These discordant CT-positive findings in the lateral compartment may increase the risk of overtreatment in the absence of a US correlation or cytological confirmation. Therefore, in lateral nodal evaluation, CT should be interpreted with caution and used primarily to corroborate suspicious US findings rather than serving as a standalone determinant in surgical planning.

This study had some limitations. First, the study population may have been subject to a selection bias, as patients with nodal metastasis are more likely to undergo surgery for thyroid cancer. Although prophylactic CND was routinely performed in most PTMC cases at our institution during the study period, potentially mitigating this bias, it may have influenced the observed diagnostic performance for LNM. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, the diagnostic accuracy was assessed on a per-patient rather than a per-lymph node basis, limiting the ability to evaluate nodal characteristics such as number, size, or anatomical location, which could affect sensitivity and specificity. Third, the retrospective nature of this study limits the ability to directly assess the influence of CT findings on clinical decision making. Further prospective studies are required to validate these findings.

In conclusion, the integration of CT and US improved the sensitivity of detecting macroscopic LNM in patients with PTMC, identifying those who would be inappropriately considered candidates for AS by US alone. These results support the use of CT as a complementary modality to US for differentiating between AS and immediate surgery in patients with PTMC. Prospective multicenter studies are required to further define the clinical utility of CT.
