Piecewise Deterministic Markov Processes for Bayesian Inference of PDE Coefficients
Leon Riccius, Iuri B.C.M. Rocha, Joris Bierkens, Hanne Kekkonen, Frans P. van der Meer

Abstract
We develop a general framework for piecewise deterministic Markov process (PDMP) samplers that enables efficient Bayesian inference in non-linear inverse problems with expensive likelihoods. The key ingredient is a surrogate-assisted thinning scheme in which a surrogate model provides a proposal event rate and a robust correction mechanism enforces an upper bound on the true rate by dynamically adjusting an additive offset whenever violations are detected. This construction is agnostic to the choice of surrogate and PDMP, and we demonstrate it for the Zig-Zag sampler and the Bouncy particle sampler with constant, Laplace, and Gaussian process (GP) surrogates, including gradient-informed and adaptively refined GP variants. As a representative application, we consider Bayesian inference of a spatially varying Young’s modulus in a one-dimensional linear elasticity problem. Across dimensions, PDMP samplers equipped with GP-based surrogates achieve substantially higher accuracy and effective sample size per forward model evaluation than Random Walk Metropolis algorithm and the No-U-Turn sampler. The Bouncy particle sampler exhibits the most favorable overall efficiency and scaling, illustrating the potential of the proposed PDMP framework beyond this particular setting.

Introduction

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods form a cornerstone of numerous scientific and engineering disciplines, including statistical physics, computational biology, and machine learning.
These methods are widely used for sampling from complex, typically unnormalized probability distributions that frequently arise in these contexts.
A particularly prominent application of MCMC is Bayesian inference, where samples are drawn from posterior distributions to quantify uncertainties in model parameters based on observed data.

The majority of established MCMC methods are built upon on the Random Walk Metropolis (RWM) algorithm
27
and its generalization to non-symmetric proposals, the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
21
.
Despite their popularity due to simplicity and guaranteed asymptotic convergence, these algorithms suffer from poor sampling efficiency in high-dimensional parameter spaces due to their inherently diffusive nature
33
.
To address these limitations, various advanced methods with improved sampling efficiency have emerged.
Notable examples include the first-order Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm
31
, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)
14
, and their second-order manifold-aware extensions
18
.
The No-U-Turn sampler (NUTS)
22
further enhances HMC by promoting large transitions without the need for manual tuning of trajectory lengths, and is widely adopted in probabilistic programming frameworks such as Stan
10
and PyMC
34
.
Empirical evaluations of these methods on practical engineering problems
18
;
12
;
19
confirm that their theoretically superior convergence properties observed on canonical test distributions
17
;
32
often carry over to realistic engineering applications.

All aforementioned methods share a common characteristic: reversibility.
Specifically, they satisfy the detailed balance condition, which ensures convergence to the target distribution but limits their sampling efficiency due to diffusive dynamics
(
20
, ch 6.1)
.
Recently, there has been growing interest in irreversible sampling algorithms, motivated by theoretical results demonstrating their potential for faster convergence and lower asymptotic variance
11
;
8
.

Irreversible algorithms can be broadly categorized into two types.
One approach involves lifting reversible algorithms onto higher-dimensional state spaces
26
.
The second approach relies on piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs).
PDMP-based samplers include the Zig-Zag sampler
7
, the Bouncy Particle sampler
9
, and the Boomerang sampler
4
.
These samplers evolve deterministically and are occasionally interrupted by stochastic events that abruptly alter their trajectories.
While these algorithms have favorable theoretical properties, such as good mixing, low autocorrelation, and error-free subsampling
3
, their simulation is prohibitively expensive beyond a few simple examples of academic nature.
To date, PDMP samplers have only seen limited application to practical problems, e.g. linear Bayesian inverse problems
23
.

The PDMP samplers define a non-homogeneous Poisson process.
In such a process, the time until next event is exponentially distributed with a rate that depends on the current state and the gradient of the log-density of interest.
A direct simulation of the next event time involves solving an integral equation, which has a closed form only for a few simple distributions, such as the Gaussian distribution with its quadratic log-density.
For more complex distributions, the next event time can be obtained via numerical integration of the event rate.
In
2
, the Zig-Zag sampler and Bouncy particle sampler are discretized with a forward Euler scheme, essentially defining a Markov chain on a grid of states.
Higher-order discretizations were considered in
28
.
While numerical integration opens PDMP sampling to a wide range of distributions, it is still not competitive in terms of computational cost in most cases.

The events can also be generated indirectly via Poisson thinning
3
.
The thinning is applicable if an upper bound process is available, i.e. a Poisson process with a rate strictly higher than the one of the process of interest.
A candidate event generated from this upper bound process is accepted with a probability equal to the ratio of the true rate and the upper bound.
Poisson thinning is only viable if events from the upper bound process can be generated efficiently.
In
13
, a constant upper bound for a given time horizon is found by a polynomial fit to the switching rate of the Zig-Zag sampler.
This process is repeated for each new state.

The direct inversion, as well as the thinning in
13
can be summarized as purely local approaches for simulating PDMPs, as they only consider the log-density and the event rate in the vicinity of the current state.
In this work, we propose a more global approach to PDMP simulation, attempting to find an approximation of the event rate that can be used throughout the domain.
In theory, the event rate along the trajectory of the PDMP is sufficient to generate the next event.
In practice, the information about the event rate in the rest of the domain can aid this event generation.

Our approach uses surrogate models to construct proposal rates for Poisson thinning for approximate PDMP sampling.
A key feature is a correction mechanism that dynamically adjusts the surrogate whenever a violation of the upper bound condition is detected during simulation.
This correction mechanism renders the thinning procedure agnostic to the specific surrogate used:
the only requirement is that the surrogate induces a computable proposal rate.
This flexibility allows us to explore a range of surrogate families—from simple constant potentials or Laplace approximations to more expressive GP models—and to devise adaptive versions that incorporate information from the sampling process itself.

Our approach is tailored to unimodal, smooth posterior distributions defined on continuous parameter spaces.
Such distributions typically arise in the context of elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs)—e.g. heat conduction, electrostatics, or diffusion—and the inference of their coefficients.
We demonstrate and test our method on a simple yet illustrative example problem in linear elasticity, where we infer parameter fields from noisy displacement observations.
Our method is agnostic to the specific choice of PDMP sampler.
We apply it to both the Zig-Zag sampler and the Bouncy particle sampler and compare their performance.
Both samplers outperform a well-tuned RWM baseline and the popular NUTS across all considered metrics and settings, marking a step towards greater application of PDMP sampling in Bayesian inference.

Piecewise-Deterministic Markov Process Samplers

PDMPs are continuous-time Markov processes characterized by deterministic trajectories interrupted by random events.
We limit ourselves to PDMPs designed for sampling from a target distribution
\pi\left\lparen{\bm{x}}\right\rparen\propto\exp\left\lparen-\Psi\left\lparen{\bm{x}}\right\rparen\right\rparen
on
\mathcal{X}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
\Psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a continuously differentiable potential.
A widely used subclass of PDMP samplers, including the Zig-Zag and Bouncy particle samplers, is obtained by augmenting the position variable
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
with a velocity variable
{\bm{v}}_{t}\in\mathcal{V}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}

Following
15
, a PDMP is defined by these three components:

1.

The deterministic dynamics
: Between events, the position
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and velocity
{\bm{v}}_{t}\in\mathcal{V}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
evolve deterministically according to

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{\Phi}_{{\bm{x}}}\lparen{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{t}\rparen,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{v}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{\Phi}_{{\bm{v}}}\lparen{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{t}\rparen,

(1)

where
\bm{\Phi}_{{\bm{x}}}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{{\bm{v}}}
are vector fields governing the evolution.

2.

The event rate
: At switching times
\tau
, the PDMP undergoes a sudden transition.
These events happen according to an inhomogeneous Poisson process with state-dependent, non-negative rate
\lambda\lparen{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{t}\rparen
.

3.

The transition kernel
: When an event occurs, the PDMP transitions from
({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{t})
to
({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\prime})
according to a transition kernel
q\left\lparen{\bm{x}}_{t}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\prime}|{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{t}\right\rparen
.

Different choices for
\bm{\Phi}_{{\bm{x}}}
,
\bm{\Phi}_{{\bm{v}}}
,
\lambda
, and
q
, together with an initial condition
({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{v}}_{0})
, fully define the PDMP.

The Zig-Zag and Bouncy particle samplers feature piecewise constant velocities.
Between events, the dynamics are

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}={\bm{v}}_{t},\hskip 56.9055pt\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{v}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=0.

(2)

The position
{\bm{x}}_{t+s}
at time
t+s
is straightforward in this case:

{\bm{x}}_{t+s}={\bm{x}}_{t}+s\cdot{\bm{v}}_{t}.

(3)

We will drop the time subscript for brevity in the following when there is no ambiguity.

These simple dynamics allow us to representgg the trajectory of the PDMP entirely in terms of a skeleton
\{\lparen t^{k},{\bm{x}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen\}_{k\geq 0}
, which consists of event times
t^{k}
and the corresponding states
({\bm{x}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k})
immediately after each event.
Using
Eq.

3
, any position
{\bm{x}}_{t}
at time
t\in[t^{k},t^{k+1}]
can then be linearly interpolated as
{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}^{k}+{\bm{v}}^{k}\cdot\lparen t-t^{k}\rparen
.

2.1
The Zig-Zag Sampler

The velocity space of the Zig-Zag sampler is discrete:
\mathcal{V}=\{-1,+1\}^{d}
.
Events occur independently in each coordinate direction
i
with state-dependent intensity

\eta_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}})=\max\left\{0,v_{i}\frac{\partial\Psi({\bm{x}})}{\partial x_{i}}\right\}.

(4)

yielding a total event rate of

\lambda({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\eta_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}}).

(5)

Events can be simulated from the total event rate (
5
).
The component to be flipped can then be selected with probability
p_{i}
proportional to its event rate

p_{i}=\frac{\eta_{i}({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}})}{\lambda({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}})}.

(6)

Alternatively, the events can be simulated directly from the individual component rates.
The next switching time
\tau
is then given by the minimum of the switching times
\tau_{i}
across all components,

\tau=\min_{i=1,\dots,d}\tau_{i},

(7)

and the process moves forward to
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}={\bm{x}}+\tau\cdot{\bm{v}}
.

At the occurrence of an event, the transition kernel
q
flips the sign of a single component
v_{i}
of the velocity vector
{\bm{v}}
.
The velocity right after the event
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle v_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-v_{i},

(8)

\displaystyle v_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v_{j}\quad\forall j\neq i,

which yields the transition kernel
q
:

q\left\lparen{\bm{v}}^{\prime}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}\right\rparen=\delta\left\lparen{\bm{v}}-2{\bm{e}}_{i}{{\bm{e}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}}{\bm{v}}\right\rparen,

(9)

where
{\bm{e}}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The position remains unchanged during the state update.
This construction yields a process with stationary distribution
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{v}})=p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{v}})
, where
p({\bm{v}})
is uniform over
\{-1,+1\}^{d}
and
p({\bm{x}})=\pi({\bm{x}})
.
The Zig-Zag sampler is ergodic under mild conditions on the target distribution
6
.

The movement of the Zig-Zag sampler is guided by the gradient of the potential function
\nabla\Psi({\bm{x}})
.
When the sampler moves towards regions of higher density (i.e. lower potential), the event rate is zero.
However, when the sampler moves towards the tails of
\pi({\bm{x}})
(i.e. higher potential), the event rate increases, leading to a higher chance of encountering an event.
Upon such an event, the respective velocity component flips, and the sampler is redirected back towards regions of higher density.

2.2
The Bouncy Particle Sampler

The Bouncy particle sampler employs a continuous velocity space
\mathcal{V}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Upon an event, the velocity is reflected against a hyperplane tangential to an isocontour of the target potential:

q\left\lparen{\bm{v}}^{\prime}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}\right\rparen=\delta\left\lparen{\bm{v}}-2\frac{\left\langle{\bm{v}},\nabla\Psi\left\lparen{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\right\rparen\right\rangle}{\left\|\nabla\Psi\left\lparen{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\right\rparen\right\|^{2}}\nabla\Psi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right\rparen.

(10)

This reflection flips the component in the direction of the gradient of the potential, effectively returning the particle towards regions of higher probability density.
Again, the position
{\bm{x}}
is unaffected by the event.
The reflection events occur with state-dependent intensity

\lambda\lparen{\bm{x}},{\bm{v}}\rparen=\max\left\{0,\left\langle{\bm{v}},\nabla\Psi({\bm{x}})\right\rangle\right\}.

(11)

The bounce mechanism and event rate of the Bouncy particle sampler are illustrated on a two-dimensional Gaussian target in
Fig.

2
.

Because the original version of this algorithm
29
is not ergodic in certain scenarios,
9
introduced random refreshments to ensure this sampler can visit the entire state space.
These random refreshment events, where the velocity is resampled from its stationary distribution
p\lparen{\bm{v}}\rparen=\mathcal{N}\lparen{\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}\rparen
, are exponentially distributed with rate
\lambda_{\text{ref}}>0
.
If
\lambda_{\text{ref}}
is too low, the sampler may struggle to reach certain regions of the state space.
If
\lambda_{\text{ref}}
is too high, the sampler may exhibit diffusive behaviour, reducing its efficiency.
The optimal choice of
\lambda_{\text{ref}}
is problem-dependent and often requires empirical tuning.

The Bouncy particle sampler can in theory find better paths through the state space on isotropic targets due to its continuous velocity space
5
.
However, one needs to carefully tune the refreshment rate
\lambda_{\text{ref}}
to ensure ergodicity.
The Zig-Zag sampler, on the other hand, does not require such tuning and runs out-of-the-box for a wide range of targets.

2.3
Simulating Events with Poisson Thinning

Simulating event times from an inhomogeneous Poisson process with rate function
\lambda({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{v}}_{t})
can be challenging.
Two common methods for simulating these event times are the inverse method and the thinning algorithm (both described in
25
).
The inverse method involves computing the integrated rate function

\Lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda({\bm{x}}_{s},{\bm{v}}_{s})\mathrm{d}s,

(12)

and then sampling the next event time
\tau
by solving

\Lambda(\tau)=-\log u,

(13)

where
u\sim\mathcal{U}\lparen 0,1\rparen
.
This method requires that the integrated rate function
\Lambda(t)
can be computed and inverted efficiently, which is generally not the case for non-linear PDE-governed Bayesian inverse problems.

The thinning algorithm provides an alternative approach that does not require the inversion of the integrated rate function.
It involves simulating a candidate switching time
\tau
from a Poisson process with an upper bound
\bar{\lambda}
on the rate function
\lambda
.
The candidate switching time
\tau
is accepted with probability

a(\tau)=\frac{\lambda({\bm{x}}_{t+\tau},{\bm{v}}_{t+\tau})}{\bar{\lambda}({\bm{x}}_{t+\tau},{\bm{v}}_{t+\tau})}.

(14)

If the candidate is rejected, we restart the event generation from time
t+\tau
without updating the velocity.

The thinning procedure is illustrated in
Fig.

3
with a loose (left) and a tight (right) upper bound
\bar{\lambda}
.
A tighter the upper bound, such as in
Fig.

3(b)
, yields a more efficient algorithm, as fewer candidate events will be rejected.
As
Eq.

14
must be evaluated for each candidate event, having a smaller number of rejections is particularly attractive when each evaluation of the true event rate
\lambda
is computationally expensive, e.g. solving a PDE for a realization of the parameter vector.

2.4
Affine Transformation

Both the Zig-Zag sampler and the Bouncy particle sampler preserve their target distribution under affine reparameterizations of the state space
2
.
More precisely, let

{\bm{\xi}}={{\bm{A}}^{-1}}\,({\bm{x}}-{\bm{b}}),

(15)

for some invertible matrix
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
and vector
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Running the Zig-Zag sampler or Bouncy particle sampler in the
{\bm{\xi}}
-coordinates with the transformed density

\tilde{\pi}({\bm{\xi}})\propto\pi\left\lparen{\bm{A}}\,{\bm{\xi}}+{\bm{b}}\right\rparen

(16)

and then mapping the resulting trajectory back via

{\bm{x}}={\bm{A}}\,{\bm{\xi}}+{\bm{b}},

(17)

yields a process in the original coordinates whose invariant distribution is
\pi({\bm{x}})
.
This affine equivariance property may be exploited to improve sampling efficiency on anisotropic posterior distributions
5
.

In practice, we construct a local preconditioning transformation based on a Laplace approximation.
Let
{\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}}
denote the maximum a posteriori estimate and let
{\bm{H}}=\nabla^{2}\Psi({\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}})
be the Hessian of the potential at that point.
We compute the Cholesky factorization
{\bm{H}}={\bm{L}}\,{{\bm{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}}
and define the affine change of variables

{\bm{\xi}}={{\bm{L}}^{\mathsf{T}}}\,({\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}}),

(18)

or, equivalently,
{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}}+{{\bm{L}}^{-\mathsf{T}}}\,{\bm{\xi}}
, which centres the posterior at the MAP and locally whitens it with respect to the curvature of
\Psi
.
The transformed potential is then

\tilde{\Psi}\left\lparen{\bm{\xi}}\right\rparen=\Psi\left\lparen{\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}}+{{\bm{L}}^{-\mathsf{T}}}\,{\bm{\xi}}\right\rparen.

(19)

In the general mapping
{\bm{x}}={\bm{A}}\,{\bm{\xi}}+{\bm{b}}
above, this choice corresponds to
{\bm{A}}={{\bm{L}}^{-\mathsf{T}}}
and
{\bm{b}}={\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}}
.
We run the Zig-Zag sampler or Bouncy particle sampler in the transformed space with density proportional to
\exp(-\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}}))
, obtaining a trajectory
\Xi_{t}
, and then map back via
{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}_{\text{MAP}}+{{\bm{L}}^{-\mathsf{T}}}\,{\bm{\xi}}
.

3
Poisson Thinning with Surrogate Models

Thinning requires an upper bound on the true PDMP event rate.
For the transformed potential
\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})
, this transformed rate takes the form
\tilde{\lambda}({\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}})
.
The main idea is to use a surrogate model
\bar{\Psi}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}\rparen
to approximate the transformed potential
\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})
.
We can then approximate the gradients of the potential
\nabla\bar{\Psi}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}\rparen\approx\nabla\tilde{\Psi}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}\rparen

and the process rate
\bar{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen\approx\tilde{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen
.
The cost of evaluating the surrogate is often orders of magnitudes lower than the cost of evaluating the true potential in PDE-governed inverse problems.
Hence, we can use the direct inversion method and solve
Eq.

13
numerically to produce event time candidates efficiently with the surrogate rate.
We then thin these candidates against the true event rate
\tilde{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen
to obtain valid event times for the PDMP.

In theory, the upper bound process must satisfy the global constraint

\bar{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen\geq\tilde{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen,

(20)

for all
\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen
, which guarantees the correctness of the thinning procedure.
In addition, we want this upper bound process to be as tight as possible for an efficient thinning procedure.

In practice, it is challenging to construct surrogate models that are both tight and satisfy the constraint in
Eq.

20
globally.
We instead enforce the upper-bound condition only locally along the realized trajectory.
This is sufficient for correctness because event times in PDMPs depend solely on the rate integrated along the current path.
A similar route is taken in
13
, where local constant bounds for the event rate are constructed for a given time horizon.
We extend this idea by using global surrogate models that can adapt to the local behaviour of the potential function.
To ensure the correctness of the thinning procedure, we introduce a correction mechanism that adjusts the approximate rate
\bar{\lambda}
whenever a violation of the upper bound condition is detected during the simulation.

Assuming that we violated the upper bound condition at some time
t^{k}
with position
{\bm{\xi}}^{k}
with velocity
{\bm{v}}^{k}
, i.e.
\bar{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen<\tilde{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen
,
we can correct the surrogate model locally by adding an offset
\gamma
to the event rate.
Formally, we define the corrected event rate as

\bar{\lambda}^{\text{c}}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen=\max\left\{0,\langle{\bm{v}},\nabla\bar{\Psi}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}\rparen\rangle+\gamma\right\},\\

(21)

for the Bouncy particle sampler.
We initialize the offset to
\gamma=0
at the start of the simulation.
In case of a violation of
Eq.

20
at
\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen
, we increase the offset
\gamma
as much as needed to satisfy the upper bound condition at the associated state:

\gamma=\max\left\{0,\tilde{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen-\bar{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen\right\}.

(22)

The Zig-Zag sampler is equipped with a vectorial offset
{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with components updated similarly to the scalar offset in the Bouncy particle sampler, exploiting the additive structure of the Zig-Zag sampler event rate:

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}^{\text{c}}_{i}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,v_{i}\frac{\partial\bar{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})}{\partial\xi_{i}}+\gamma_{i}\right\},

(23)

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}^{\text{c}}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bar{\eta}^{\text{c}}_{i}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen,

(24)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\tilde{\eta}_{i}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen-\bar{\eta}^{\text{c}}_{i}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}^{k},{\bm{v}}^{k}\rparen\right\},

(25)

where
\bar{\eta}^{\text{c}}_{i}
is the component-wise proposal rate induced by the surrogate
\bar{\Psi}
.

We revert the process to time
t^{k-1}
, as the event that triggered the correction was invalid.
We then re-simulate the next event using either the corrected total event rate
\bar{\lambda}^{\text{c}}
for the Bouncy particle sampler or its components
\bar{\eta}^{\text{c}}_{i}
for the Zig-Zag sampler.
Note that we must also re-use the same outcome of the uniform random variable used for the candidate generation (see
Eq.

13
).
Otherwise, we incur a bias towards sooner events, as these corrections occur more frequently when the PDMP travels further away from the mode of the target distribution.
Such long traverses are more likely for larger outcomes of the uniform random variable
u
.

In its basic form, the offset
\gamma
increases over time, making the thinning procedure less efficient.
To mitigate this issue, we implement a decay mechanism for the offset.
Denoting
\gamma^{k}
the offset after the
\lparen k-1\rparen
-th event occurring at time
t^{k-1}
,
we obtain the offset
\gamma^{k}
that we use for the next interval
[t^{k},t^{k+1})
as

\gamma^{k}=\gamma^{k-1}\cdot\exp\left\lparen-\beta\left\lparen t^{k}-t^{k-1}\right\rparen\right\rparen,

(26)

which means the offset decays exponentially over time with rate
\beta>0
.
The discrete nature of the offset decay
Eq.

26
means that the offset remains constant between events, and that we do not violate
Eq.

20
when re-doing an event that triggered a correction.
We note that even with this correction, there is still no guarantee that the upper bound condition
Eq.

20
holds for all
\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen
along the trajectory, as we might miss fast oscillations of the rate between events.
Our method is therefore approximate in nature.

holds a detailed discussion of the bias introduced by the approximate event generation scheme.

3.1
Surrogate-Agnostic Design and Surrogate Families

A key advantage of the correction mechanism is that it guarantees correctness regardless of the surrogate model.
The only requirement is that the surrogate induces a computable proposal rate
\bar{\lambda}\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{v}}\rparen
; whenever this is not an upper bound, the local offset
\gamma
in
Eq.

21
restores correctness.
Consequently, the approach works even with extremely crude surrogates.
For example, taking a constant potential
\bar{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})\equiv c
yields
\nabla\bar{\Psi}\equiv{\bm{0}}
and thus an initial proposal rate of zero.
In this case, we must initialize with a small non-zero offset (e.g.
\gamma=1
) to generate candidate events from
\bar{\lambda}
; the first detected violation then raises the offset, after which the process proceeds correctly.
While such a baseline will be inefficient—offsets grow quickly, thinning acceptance is low, frequent evalutation of
\tilde{\lambda}
—it is valuable as a sanity check and lower-bound benchmark.

We consider the following surrogate families, ordered by increasing expressiveness and cost:

•

Constant potential (baseline):
\bar{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})\equiv c
.
No training; correctness entirely via offsets.

•

Laplace (trivial in transformed coordinates): after the affine transformation, the Laplace model reduces to the standard normal potential,
\bar{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}{\bm{\xi}}+c
, with gradient
{\bm{\xi}}
and Hessian
{\bm{I}}
, and arbitrary constant
c
.
This provides an inexpensive, stabilizing baseline around the mode.

•

Laplace + GP residual: model the discrepancy
r({\bm{\xi}})=\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}})-\bar{\Psi}_{\text{L}}({\bm{\xi}})
with a GPpretrained on
N_{0}
realizations of
r({\bm{\xi}})
.
This combined model captures non-quadratic structure while preserving good local behaviour.

•

Laplace + GP with gradient observations: enrich the GP training set with gradients of
\tilde{\Psi}
where available, using derivative kernels.

•

Adaptive GP (thinning-driven): start with
N_{0}
initial training points and expand the training set at milestone sizes
N_{0}\,2^{n}
for
n=1,\ldots,5
, using data gathered from the thinning/simulation (e.g., locations of corrections or along accepted paths).
This progressively tightens local rate proposals.

While we acknowledge the vast body of literature on more elaborate active learning schemes for GPs, we use the simple heuristic of the Adaptive GP model to demonstrate the potential of adaptive surrogates in this context.
Here, we include
all
data gathered during simulation to expand the training set.
We therefore treat this model as an upper performance bound for active learning.

We follow a simple, consistent pipeline across surrogates:
first, we apply the affine transformation of
Section

2.4
to whiten the state so that the Laplace approximation is standard normal in the transformed coordinate, as described in
Algorithm

3
.
In these coordinates, the Laplace potential is trivial:
\bar{\Psi}_{\mathrm{L}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}{\bm{\xi}}+c
, with gradient
\nabla\bar{\Psi}_{\text{L}}({\bm{\xi}})={\bm{\xi}}
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}\bar{\Psi}_{\text{L}}({\bm{\xi}})={\bm{I}}
, see
Algorithm

4
.
For all GP-based surrogates, we initialize by drawing
N_{0}
samples
\{{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{0}}\sim\mathcal{N}\lparen{\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}\rparen
, evaluating
\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}}_{i})
, and training a GP on residuals
r({\bm{\xi}}_{i})=\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}}_{i})-\bar{\Psi}_{\text{L}}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\rparen
, as in
Algorithm

5
.
for the gradient-observing variant, we additionally include derivative observations
\nabla r({\bm{\xi}}_{i})=\nabla\tilde{\Psi}({\bm{\xi}}_{i})-\nabla\bar{\Psi}_{\text{L}}\lparen{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\rparen
.
For the adaptive, the GP hyperparameters are updated at each milestone, as long as the total number of data points does not exceed 1000,
as the computational costs grows cubically with the number of data points and further improvements are expected to be small in this data regime.

.
We place a constant-mean
1
1

1

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{\xi}}}m_{*}({\bm{\xi}})

4.1
Test Case

To demonstrate the proposed approach for PDMP sampling, we introduce a scalable case study.
The problem is chosen to be computationally inexpensive, enabling direct comparison with long reference runs obtained using the Random-Walk Metropolis (RWM) algorithm.
At the same time, the case study is motivated by its resemblance to practical engineering problems.
Its nonlinear parameter-to-observable map leads to a non-Gaussian posterior distribution, well-suited to test the capabilities of the PDMP samplers and the surrogate-assisted framework.

Specifically, we consider the mechanical equilibrium of a one-dimensional linear elastic bar with a spatially varying and uncertain Young’s modulus, which is to be inferred from displacement measurements.
The bar with length L = 1 and constant cross-sectional area A = 1 is depicted in
Fig.

4
.

The bar is fixed at the left end and subjected to a concentrated load
\hat{F}=1
at the right end.
Assuming a linear elastic material, the displacement
u(x)
along the bar is governed by the following boundary value problem:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left\lparen E(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}u(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=0,\quad x\in(0,1),

(38)

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(39)

\displaystyle E(1)\frac{\mathrm{d}u(1)}{\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle=1,

(40)

where the Young’s modulus
E(x)
depends on the position
x
.
We consider this parameter field
E(x)
to be uncertain.
In many situations however, some prior knowledge about the parameter field is available.

Here we adopt a smooth, spatially correlated prior field, which we model as a log-Gaussian random field, owing to the positivity constraint of the Young’s modulus.
Such a field can be expressed as a Gaussian process:

\displaystyle\log E(x)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{E}(x),k_{E}(x,x^{\prime})),

(41)

\displaystyle k_{E}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{E}^{2}\exp\left\lparen-\frac{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}{2l_{E}^{2}}\right\rparen.

(42)

Its three parameters are the mean function
\mu_{E}(x)
, the signal variance
\sigma_{E}^{2}
, and the length scale
l_{E}
of the squared exponential covariance function
k_{E}(x,x^{\prime})
.
In practice, these parameters are estimated from data, e.g. using maximum likelihood estimation, set based on expert knowledge, or treated as hyperparameters in a hierarchical Bayesian model.
For our testing purposes, we set
\mu_{E}=1
,
\sigma_{E}=1
and
l_{E}=0.3
, yielding a moderately varying field.

In theory, we would like to infer the continuous field
E(x)
.
In practice, however, we need to discretize the field to represent it with a finite number of parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
.
We discretize the field with piecewise-constant basis functions
\phi_{i}

\displaystyle\log E(x;{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\theta_{i}\phi_{i}{x},

(43)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\dfrac{i-1}{d}\leq x<\dfrac{i}{d},\\
0,&\text{elsewhere}.\end{cases}.

(44)

leading to a parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of dimension
d
.
We obtain the prior distribution of the parameters
p\lparen{\bm{\theta}}\rparen=\mathcal{N}\lparen\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}},\text{Cov}({\bm{\theta}})\rparen
by projecting the Gaussian process onto the piecewise-constant basis functions.
With
M_{ij}=\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle
, the covariance matrix of the parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
in the general case is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})

\displaystyle={{\bm{M}}^{-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}(x)k_{E}(x,x^{\prime})\phi_{j}(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}{{\bm{M}}^{-\mathsf{T}}}.

(45)

Since the basis functions
\phi_{i}
are non-overlapping, and the basis is orthogonal with
M_{ij}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}\,\bm{I}
, the covariance simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=d^{2}\int_{(i-1)/d}^{i/d}\int_{(j-1)/d}^{j/d}k_{E}(x,x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.

(47)

With the constant mean function
\mu_{E}(x)=1
of the underlying process, the mean of the coefficients
\theta_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i}]

\displaystyle=d\int_{(i-1)/d}^{i/d}E(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1.

(48)

Together with the feature vector
\bm{\phi}(x)={[\phi_{1}(x),\ldots,\phi_{d}(x)]^{\mathsf{T}}}
, the covariance of the discretized logarithm of the parameter field is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\log E(x;{\bm{\theta}}),\log E(x^{\prime};{\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle={\bm{\phi}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}}\,\mathrm{Cov}({\bm{\theta}})\,\bm{\phi}(x^{\prime}),

(49)

which for
d\to\infty
converges to the original covariance function
k_{E}(x,x^{\prime})
of the Gaussian process.

We acknowledge that the piecewise-constant basis functions are not ideal for representing a smooth Gaussian process.
For most applications, other basis functions, such as Chebyshev polynomials or Karhunen-Loève basis functions, would be more suitable.
However, the piecewise-constant basis functions have a key advantage for our testing purposes:
they allow for an analytic solution of the forward problem, which reads

\displaystyle u(x;{\bm{\theta}})=\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor xd\rfloor}\frac{\frac{1}{d}}{\exp\theta_{i}}+\frac{x-\frac{\lfloor xd\rfloor}{d}}{\exp\theta_{\lfloor xd\rfloor+1}}\right\rparen,\quad x\in[0,1].

(50)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor function.
This solution is continuous and piecewise-linear, with kinks at the segment boundaries.
Most notably, the displacements are a non-linear function of the latent variables
{\bm{\theta}}
, leading to a non-linear inverse problem.
Due to the analytic solution, we can evaluate the forward model and run the inference at negligible computational cost, allowing us to focus on the analysis of the sampling algorithms themselves.

First, we draw a sample from the prior
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\sim p\lparen{\bm{\theta}}\rparen
as the ground truth which we then try to infer from noisy displacement measurements
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}
.
We generate these displacement measurements
\tilde{u}_{i}
by evaluating the forward model at
m
equidistant sensor locations
x_{i}=i/(m+1)
for
i=1,\ldots,m
and adding independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}=0.025
.
We therefore adopt a Gaussian likelihood model

\displaystyle p(\tilde{{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{u}}({\bm{\theta}}),\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{2}\,\bm{I}),

(51)

where
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}={[\tilde{u}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{u}_{m}]^{\mathsf{T}}}
are the noisy measurements, and
{\bm{u}}({\bm{\theta}})={\left[u(x_{1};{\bm{\theta}}),\ldots,u(x_{m};{\bm{\theta}})\right]^{\mathsf{T}}}
are the model predictions at the sensor locations.
Following
30
, we set the number of sensors to
m=\lfloor\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}d\rfloor
.
Fixing the observation-to-parameters ratio in this manner helps to maintain a consistent level of problem difficulty across different parameter dimensions
d
.

Finally, we summarize the Bayesian inference problem as

\displaystyle p\lparen{\bm{\theta}}|\tilde{{\bm{u}}}\rparen

\displaystyle\propto p\lparen\tilde{{\bm{u}}}|{\bm{\theta}}\rparen\,p\lparen{\bm{\theta}}\rparen,

(52)

where the prior
p\lparen{\bm{\theta}}\rparen
is the Gaussian distribution derived above.
We can now estimate the posterior distribution using the PDMP methods described in
Section

2
, i.e.
\pi({\bm{\theta}})=p\lparen{\bm{\theta}}|\tilde{{\bm{u}}}\rparen
.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

First, we compute a reference solution for each setting
d=2,\dots,10
using a long RWM run with
{10}^{7}
samples.
Let
\mu_{i}^{\star}
and
\sigma_{i}^{2,\star}
denote the coordinate-wise posterior mean and variance of this reference posterior.
For any method
\mathcal{M}
, let
\hat{\mu}^{(\mathcal{M})}_{i}
and
\hat{\sigma}^{2,(\mathcal{M})}_{i}
denote the corresponding estimates.
We then evaluate the following metrics:

Posterior Mean Error:

\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{mean}}^{(\mathcal{M})}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(\hat{\mu}^{(\mathcal{M})}_{i}-\mu_{i}^{\star}\bigr)^{2}}

(53)

Posterior Variance Error:

\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{var}}^{(\mathcal{M})}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(\hat{\sigma}^{2,(\mathcal{M})}_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{2,\star}\bigr)^{2}}

(54)

Wasserstein Distance:
We compute the 2-Wasserstein distance (WD) between the reference solution
\hat{p}^{*}
and an approximation
\hat{p}^{(\mathcal{M})}
resulting from method
\mathcal{M}
.

W_{2}^{2}(\hat{p}^{*},\hat{p}^{(\mathcal{M})})=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\hat{p}^{*},\hat{p}^{(\mathcal{M})})}\iint\|{\bm{\xi}}-{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi\left\lparen{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}\right\rparen\\

(55)

where
\Pi(\hat{p}^{*},\hat{p}^{(\mathcal{M})})
denotes the set of all couplings of the two distributions.

Effective Sample Size (ESS) per Model Evaluation:
We compute the ESS per model evaluation to assess the sampling efficiency of the PDMP methods.
The ESS is calculated using the formula

\displaystyle\text{ESS}=\frac{N}{1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_{k}},

(56)

where
N
is the total number of samples and
\rho_{k}
is the autocorrelation at lag
k
.
We normalize the ESS by dividing it by the number of model evaluations
N_{\mathrm{eval}}
to account for computational cost.

Remarks

We compute all metrics in the affine space to obtain standardized results.
The posterior mean and variance of the PDMPs can be computed analytically, as shown in
6
.
We display these quantities as functions of PDE model evaluations
N_{\mathrm{eval}}
, as we assume these evaluations to be the computational bottleneck of our problem.
This way, we can compare convergence of the different methods on equal footing.
We therefore need to keep track of the number of model evaluations performed by the PDMP samplers.
Markov chains are typically evaluated in terms of number of samples drawn, which is directly proportional to the number of model evaluations for the RWM sampler.

In
6
, a method to compute the ESS directly from the PDMP skeleton without a need to discretize is also provided.
However, we observe in the shorter runs considered here that the ESS computed from the discretized process produces more stable and accurate results.
We therefore approximate the PDMP until time
T
and discretize it into
N
samples equally spaced in time, i.e.
{\bm{\xi}}_{i}={\bm{\xi}}(i\cdot T/N)
for
i=1,\ldots,N
.

In a similar manner, we discretize the PDMPs for the empirical WD, as no analytical solution for this metric is available.
The exact empirical WD becomes prohibitively expensive to compute for large sample sizes and dimensions.
We therefore resort to the debiased Sinkhorn divergence, as described in
16
, as an efficient approximation.
We set regularization parameter
\varepsilon=0.02
for numerical stability while keeping the bias low.

The WD is also reported as a function of model evaluations, similar to the posterior mean and variance.
The empirical WD is a biased and noisy estimator of the true WD between the PDMP and the reference posterior, especially for small sample sizes.
We do not correct for the bias in our experiments, but ensure comparability by using the same number of samples for all methods.
This means that we discretize the PDMPs with
N
points when computing the WD distance after
N
model evaluations.
We reduce the variance by taking multiple random draws of
N
samples from the reference posterior.

We repeat each numerical experiment 50 times with 50 different seeds, and then report the average for each of the four metrics.
For each seed, the initial position of the sampling algorithm is drawn from the Laplace approximation of the posterior.
Note that we run the MCMC algorithms in the affine space as well to put all algorithms on an equal footing.
This way, we expect a short burn-in phase regardless of the algorithm.
We obtain the affine transformation only once per setting and use it for all methods and seeds.

We provide a RWM baseline for all experiments, which is not to be confused with the reference solution with
{10}^{7}
samples.
It is well known that the performance of RWM depends heavily on the choice of the proposal distribution.
We start out with a standard Gaussian proposal, reflecting the isotropic nature of the posterior in the affine space.
After each
100
iterations, we multiply the current proposal covariance with
0.9
if the acceptance rate is below
0.2
, and with
1.1
if the acceptance rate is above
0.25
.
After
1000
iterations, we set the proposal covariance matrix to the empirical covariance of the samples collected so far, scaled by the optimal factor
\nicefrac{{2.38^{2}}}{{d}}
derived in
17
.
We then keep rescaling the proposal covariance every
100
iterations as before, and leave it unchanged after
2000
iterations to satisfy vanishing adaptation
1
.
It is also this version of the RWM algorithm that we use to compute the reference solutions, albeit with a different seed and a much longer run where we discard the first
5000
samples as burnin.

4.3
Convergence of the Zig-Zag Sampler

First, we would like to elucidate when the Zig-Zag sampler converges to the true posterior distribution.
This is particularly important as we move beyond established means to simulate the process that are well studied and known to converge.
There is a number of potential violations of the assumptions that guarantee convergence:

1.

The surrogate model does not produce a strict upper bound process for the thinning.

2.

We constantly change the offset
\gamma
to restore the upper bound property (although to a lesser extent at later times).

3.

We constantly reduce the offset to recover from a potentially large gap between the surrogate and the true model.

4.

We revert and redo events when the surrogate is found to be an invalid upper bound and the offset
\gamma
is increased.

We compare the following three scenarios to investigate the influence of these potential violations on performance and accuracy of the sampler:

•

Constant potential:
We use a constant surrogate model and increase the offset
\gamma
when needed.
Additionally, the offset shrinks exponentially with time
t
.
This tests points 2, 3, and 4.

•

Constant potential with no shrinkage:
We use a constant surrogate model and increase the offset
\gamma
when needed, but do not allow it to shrink.
This tests points 2 and 4.

•

Random gradient:
We use a surrogate model that produces a random value for each iteration of the Zig-Zag sampler.
We sample a value from
\mathcal{U}\lparen-0.5,0.5\rparen
for each component of the potential gradient.
We increase the offset
\gamma
when needed, and allow it to shrink.
This tests the limits of admitting a non-static upper bound process, i.e. tests points 1, 2.

The two constant surrogate models represent the most naive approach to surrogate modelling, where we simply use a constant value as an upper bound for the rate function.
In theory, such a model would only be applicable to bounded domains, or a small number of distributions on unbounded domains, such as the Cauchy distribution.
A Gaussian distribution, e.g. produces an unbounded rate function.
In practice, however, all realizations of the Zig-Zag sampler are finite in time, and thus the rate function is bounded along any simulated trajectory.
We would like to find this maximum value of the rate function with this constant model.

The random surrogate model represents a highly inaccurate model that does not provide much useful information about the rate function.
Further, it is an extreme case of an ever-changing upper bound, which helps to isolate the influence of tampering with the upper bound process over time.

4.5
Adaptive Refinement of the GP Surrogate Model

The last extension to the surrogate model is to adaptively refine the surrogate model during the PDMP simulation.
Since we need to evaluate the model at each proposed event time, we can use the additional information from these points to update the surrogate model.
We compare the pretrained GP surrogate model with an adaptive GP version (Ada GP) in

The Ada GP and GP curves fully overlap at the start of the simulation, as the Ada GP model is initialized with the same training data as the GP model.
The adaptive refinement becomes apparent in later stages of the simulation, where the RMSE curves of the two models become distinct.
Counterintuitively, the Ada GP does not consistently outperform the GP model.
In fact, the two curves cross multiple times in all three metrics.
The ESS per model evaluation is slightly higher for the Ada GP model than for the GP models, but only by the smallest of margins, as shown in
Fig.

We hypothesize that the additional training points added during the simulation lie in regions well covered by the initial training set, and thus do not contribute much new information to the model.
While the initial sampling from the Laplace approximation yields high-quality training points for this problem with moderate non-linearity, the situation may differ when strong non-linearities are present.
We therefore assume that the benefits of adaptive refinement are strongly problem-dependent, and that more sophisticated adaptive sampling strategies are required to fully leverage its potential in more challenging scenarios.

Simply put, the NUTS does not excel in the settings considered here, even though it does in many others.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced and analyzed a surrogate–assisted framework for simulating piecewise-deterministic Markov process (PDMP) samplers in non-linear Bayesian inverse problems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs).
Focusing on the Zig-Zag sampler (Zig-Zag sampler) and the Bouncy Particle sampler (Bouncy particle sampler), we designed a global thinning strategy that uses a surrogate for the potential together with a simple, yet effective, correction mechanism for the resulting event rate.
This construction makes the method agnostic to the particular surrogate family: whenever the surrogate underestimates the true rate locally, the offset is increased to restore the upper–bound property required for valid thinning.

Our numerical experiments—the inference of a coefficient field in linear elasticity—demonstrated that this approach yields PDMP samplers that were both robust and efficient.
Even very crude surrogates, such as a constant model or a pure Laplace approximation in whitened coordinates, can be corrected on the fly and were empirically observed to converge to the reference posterior.
More expressive Gaussian process (GP) surrogates on top of the Laplace approximation further tightened the rate approximation and improved sampling efficiency across the board.
While the Gaussian process model offered clear benefits, incorporating derivative observations and adaptive refinement yielded only modest additional gains in performance for our benchmark problem.

We concluded with a comparison of the Zig-Zag sampler and the Bouncy particle sampler within the surrogate–assisted framework.
The Bouncy particle sampler had the upper hand regarding the posterior mean estimation error, the Zig-Zag sampler attained better performance in terms of variance estimation, and both algorithms were largely on par regarding the Wasserstein distance.
The Bouncy particle sampler exhibited a higher effective sample size per model evaluation, particularly evident in higher dimensions.
Both PDMP samplers outperformed a well tuned Random Walk Metropolis baseline and the popular No-U-Turn Sampler in all considered metrics, across all settings.

This study was restricted to smooth, unimodal posteriors arising from an elliptic PDE with an analytic forward map and moderate parameter dimensions.
A limitation of the employed Gaussian process surrogates is their cubic training cost in the number of design points, which limits their applicability in very high dimensions.
Moreover, while our empirical evidence supported convergence of the surrogate–assisted PDMP samplers under the proposed correction scheme, a theoretical analysis of convergence for adaptive and non-strict upper bounds remains an open problem.

Future research directions include extending the approach to more realistic PDE-governed inverse problems, higher-dimensional parameter spaces, and possibly multimodal posteriors.
On the surrogate side, sparse or low-rank Gaussian process constructions or neural surrogates could alleviate the cubic scaling while preserving global accuracy.
Finally, a rigorous theoretical treatment of convergence properties for the proposed surrogate–assisted PDMP samplers would provide valuable insights and guidance for practical applications.
Nevertheless, this work establishes the first successful application of PDMP sampling to PDE-governed Bayesian inverse problems, demonstrating their viability for a wide range of scientific and engineering applications.
