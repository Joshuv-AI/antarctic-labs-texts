Validity of the Thai version of the Western Ontario shoulder instability index in shoulder instability
Authors: Pattanaket Cheewakongkiat, Fukit Cheevasuvit, Nattha Kulkamthorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeShoulder instability affects quality of life and function. The Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) is widely used; however, a validated Thai version is not available. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the Thai WOSI.MethodsThe WOSI was cross-culturally adapted to the Thai version (Thai WOSI) following guidelines. Sixty-two patients comprising 2 women and 60 men, with unilateral shoulder instability, were recruited for this study. All patients completed Thai WOSI and Thai QuickDASH for internal consistency and validity.Additionally, all patients repeated the Thai WOSI two weeks after the initial examination to assess test-retest reproducibility.ResultsSixty-two subjects, with a mean age of 28.76 years (SD, 9.7), were included in the study. Internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.956) and test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.97) were excellent. A strong correlation was observed between the Thai WOSI and the Thai QuickDASH (Pearson's r = 0.963), supporting good criterion validity.ConclusionsThe results of this study demonstrate that the Thai version of the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing shoulder-related function and quality of life in Thai-speaking patients with shoulder instability. Following linguistic translation and cultural adaptation, the Thai WOSI can be confidently used in both clinical practice and research settings to evaluate patients with shoulder instability in the Thai population.

Introduction

Shoulder instability is defined as the failure to maintain congruency of the humeral head within the glenoid fossa, resulting in pain, apprehension, and functional limitation, particularly during overhead activities.1 The most commonly follows traumatic anterior glenohumeral dislocation, with an incidence of approximately 1–2.5% in the general population.2 Many patients, particularly younger individuals, develop recurrent instability, with up to two-thirds progressing to chronic instability within five years. 3, 4, 5.

Shoulder instability significantly impairs participation in sports and activities of daily living, leading to a decline in shoulder-related quality of life.6 Evaluation of treatment outcomes, whether nonoperative or operative, should therefore encompass both objective and subjective parameters. Objective assessment includes range of motion, rotator cuff strength, provocative instability tests, and recurrence rates.7 However, Patient‐reported outcome measures assess perceived limitations and are important for evaluating treatment in shoulder instability.8

Many instruments have been developed to assess the clinical outcomes of treating shoulder instability. However, most of these are derived from clinical data and depend on the examiner's judgment. Specifically for shoulder instability, assessment tools that prioritize the patient's subjective viewpoint are necessary to truly evaluate their disability in their daily activities.

Self-administered questionnaires are widely recommended for evaluating functional status and symptoms based on patients’ perceptions. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) allow clinicians to assess subjective outcomes in a standardized manner. The use of PROMs has increased substantially, with an 18% rise in overall utilization and a 20% increase in the use of multiple PROMs. Moreover, PROMs without a clinician-dependent component are being adopted at higher rates than clinician-administered instruments.9

The Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) is a patient-administered, disease-specific quality-of-life scoring system designed to evaluate symptoms and their impact on physical activity and overall quality of life. It can also be utilized in clinical settings to monitor an individual's progress. This 21-question index is divided into four domains: physical symptoms/pain, sports/recreation/work, lifestyle, and emotions. Patients respond on a visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 0 mm.

(no discomfort) to 100 mm (maximum discomfort). The overall score is calculated by summing the different items, with a range from 0 (best score) to 2100 (worst score).8 The WOSI is highly recommended for evaluating shoulder instability due to its high reliability and validity.10 The WOSI has been translated and culturally adapted into several languages.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 However, no Thai version of the WOSI has previously been available.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to translate and culturally adapt the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) into the Thai language and to evaluate its validity and reliability in Thai patients with shoulder instability. We hypothesized that the Thai version of the WOSI would demonstrate reliability and validity comparable to the original instrument.

Materials and methods

Translation and cultural adaptation

Permission to translate and use the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) was obtained from Dr. S. Griffin, the original developer of the questionnaire. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Royal Thai Army Medical Department (approval date: 19 May 2021; reference number: R043q/64).

The translation and cultural adaptation process followed the standardized guidelines proposed by Beaton et al.16 to ensure conceptual, semantic, and cultural equivalence. The process consisted of seven steps:1.Forward translation of the original English WOSI into Thai by two independent native Thai-speaking translators (T1 and T2)2.Synthesis of the two forward translations into a single consensus Thai version (T1–2)3.Back-translation of the consensus version into English by two independent native English-speaking translators (ET1 and ET2) who were blinded to the original questionnaire4.Comparison of the back-translated versions with the original WOSI by the research team5.Review and finalization of the Thai version by an expert committee comprising translators and a Thai linguist6.Pre-testing and cognitive interviewing in five patients with shoulder instability to assess clarity and comprehensibility7.Acceptance of the final Thai version of the WOSI.

Study population

Participants were recruited from the Orthopedic Department of Phramongkutklao Hospital. A total of 62 patients diagnosed with shoulder instability were invited to participate. Patients with anterior, multidirectional, and posterior shoulder instability were included, provided that the diagnosis was confirmed by clinical examination and imaging studies. All participants received written and verbal information regarding the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any impact on their future medical care. All data were anonymized and analyzed collectively.

Definition and diagnosis of shoulder instability

Shoulder instability was defined as symptomatic abnormal translation of the humeral head relative to the glenoid, resulting in pain, apprehension, or functional limitation. Diagnosis was established by an orthopedic surgeon based on a combination of patient history, physical examination, and imaging findings when available.

Patients with anterior instability (ADI) were identified by a history of traumatic anterior dislocation or subluxation and positive apprehension and relocation tests. Multidirectional instability (MDI) was diagnosed based on symptomatic instability in two or more directions with evidence of generalized ligamentous laxity or capsular redundancy. Patients with atraumatic instability associated with hyperlaxity were included if symptoms were reproducible during daily or sports activities. Associated structural lesions, such as labral tears or long head of the biceps tendon pathology, were documented when identified on magnetic resonance imaging or during clinical evaluation.

Outcome measures and test–retest reliability

Participants were instructed to independently complete the Thai version of the WOSI and the Thai version of the QuickDASH questionnaire during their outpatient visit. The Thai QuickDASH was selected as a comparator instrument because it has been widely used in previous validation studies of the WOSI in other languages.13 The Thai version of the QuickDASH, which was cross-culturally adapted by Rapipong et al., has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity.17

To assess test–retest reliability, participants completed the Thai WOSI questionnaire again two weeks after the initial administration. Participants were instructed not to undergo any new treatment, surgical intervention, or significant changes in physical activity during the 2-week test–retest interval. The two-week interval for the test–retest assessment was selected to minimize recall bias while ensuring that patients’ clinical conditions remained stable. This interval is commonly used in reliability studies of patient-reported outcome measures and is considered sufficient to reduce the likelihood that participants recall their previous responses, while minimizing the chance of true clinical change.11, 12, 13, 14, 15,18,19.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on reliability and validity analyses using data from a previous validation study of the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI) in the Japanese population, which reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.84, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.91 for test–retest reliability, and a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.63 between the WOSI and the QuickDASH.13 Sample size estimation for internal consistency was calculated according to Bonett's method for testing Cronbach's alpha, yielding a minimum required sample size of 9 participants.(Bonett, 2002) For test–retest reliability, the required sample size to estimate the ICC with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a desired confidence interval width of 0.10 was calculated using Bonett's formula, resulting in a required sample size of 47 participants.(Bonett, 2002) For criterion validity assessed by Pearson's correlation coefficient, a minimum sample size of 23 participants was required.(Bland, 2000) Based on these calculations, the largest estimated sample size of 47 participants was selected, and an additional 20% was added to account for potential dropout, resulting in a final target sample size of 57 participants.

All data were checked for completeness and accuracy and analyzed using STATA/MP version 12 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Demographic variables and participant characteristics, including age, sex, occupation, involvement of the dominant or non-dominant side, duration of symptoms, and treatment modality, were summarized using descriptive statistics. Internal consistency of the Thai version of the WOSI was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficient. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) derived from repeated questionnaire administration at baseline and 2 weeks later in participants who reported no change in clinical status. Criterion validity was examined by calculating Pearson's correlation coefficient between the Thai WOSI and the Thai version of the QuickDASH. Construct validity was assessed by comparing WOSI scores across relevant clinical subgroups using the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05.

Result

Patient demographic data

A total of 62 patients with shoulder instability participated in this study to evaluate the reliability and validity of the Thai version of the WOSI. The study population predominantly consisted of male patients, with 60 men (96.77%) and 2 women (3.23%), and a mean age of 28.76 ± 9.78 years. All participants completed the questionnaires independently, with no missing or unanswered items. Test–retest reliability analysis demonstrated excellent reproducibility of the Thai WOSI. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the total WOSI-TH score was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.98). ICC values for the individual domains ranged from 0.89 to 0.93, with the highest ICC observed in the emotion domain and the lowest in the lifestyle domain (Table 3). The higher ICC observed for the total score compared with individual domain scores is expected, as the total score aggregates multiple items. Validity

Criterion validity was evaluated using Pearson's correlation coefficient to assess the relationship between the Thai WOSI and the Thai version of the QuickDASH. An excellent correlation was observed between the total WOSI-TH score and the total QuickDASH-TH score (r = 0.963, n = 62, p < 0.001). In contrast, correlations between individual WOSI domains and the QuickDASH-TH ranged from 0.722 to 0.783, with correlation coefficients of 0.783 for disability and symptoms, 0.730 for work, and 0.722 for sports and recreation domains (Table 4). The higher Pearson correlation for the total score is expected, as the total WOSI encompasses more items (a broader construct) than individual domains. Discussion

This study demonstrated that the Thai version of the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI-TH) has excellent psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, very good test–retest reliability, and strong criterion validity when compared with the Thai version of the QuickDASH. These findings indicate that the WOSI-TH is a reliable and valid patient-reported outcome measure for assessing shoulder-related quality of life in Thai patients with shoulder instability. The overall reliability and validity indices observed in this study are comparable to those reported in previous cross-cultural adaptations of the WOSI, including the Japanese, Korean, Italian, and Chinese versions, which also demonstrated high Cronbach's alpha values and strong correlations with established functional outcome measures.

The cross-cultural adaptation process in this study followed internationally accepted guidelines to ensure not only linguistic equivalence but also cultural relevance. Several items in the original WOSI required cultural modification rather than direct translation. For example, items related to sports, recreational activities, and work-related tasks were adapted to reflect activities commonly performed in Thai daily life and occupational settings. Certain emotional expressions and lifestyle-related phrases were adjusted to align with Thai cultural norms, ensuring that patients could clearly understand and relate to the content without altering the original conceptual meaning. These adaptations were refined through expert committee review and patient pre-testing, which confirmed the clarity and appropriateness of the final Thai version.

The Thai WOSI can be readily implemented in daily clinical practice and sports medicine settings to assess baseline disability, monitor treatment outcomes, and guide return-to-sport decision-making. As a self-administered, disease-specific PROM, it provides clinicians with valuable insight into patients’ perceived functional limitations and quality of life.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population consisted predominantly of male participants, which reflects the epidemiology of traumatic shoulder instability in young, active individuals but limits the generalizability of the findings to female patients. Gender-related differences in pain perception, activity level, and psychosocial responses may influence patient-reported outcome measures, and future studies with a more balanced gender distribution are warranted.

Second, the two-week interval used for test–retest reliability assessment was selected to minimize recall bias while ensuring clinical stability of symptoms. This interval has been widely used in previous validation studies and is generally considered sufficient to reduce the likelihood that patients remember their previous responses. However, subtle symptom fluctuations within this period cannot be entirely excluded and may have influenced individual scores.

Third, shoulder instability encompasses a heterogeneous group of pathologies, including anterior, multidirectional instability, and associated lesions such as labral or biceps pathology. This variability may contribute to differences in symptom severity and functional limitation, which could influence PROM responses. Although the WOSI is designed to capture the overall impact of instability on quality of life, subgroup analyses based on specific instability patterns or associated lesions were not performed in this study and should be considered in future research.

Fourth, this study did not evaluate the responsiveness of the WOSI-TH or determine the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), which are essential properties for assessing longitudinal change. Future studies should address these aspects to enhance the clinical utility of the instrument.

Although no formal a priori power analysis was conducted for hypothesis testing, the sample size of 62 participants is consistent with those used in previous cross-cultural validation studies of patient-reported outcome measures and was sufficient to evaluate reliability and criterion validity. Nevertheless, future studies with larger and more gender-balanced cohorts may further strengthen the statistical robustness and generalizability of the findings.

In summary, after careful translation and cross-cultural adaptation, the WOSI-TH demonstrates strong reliability and validity and can be confidently used in both clinical practice and research settings to evaluate Thai patients with shoulder instability. Further studies involving broader patient populations and longitudinal outcome assessments are recommended to enhance its applicability and generalizability.
