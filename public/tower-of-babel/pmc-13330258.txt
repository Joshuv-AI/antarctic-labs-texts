Primary Human Papillomavirus Screening Versus Cytology for Detection of Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia Grade 2 or Higher in Poland: a Parallel-Group, Randomized Controlled Trial

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Prior to this study, we conducted a comprehensive review of the literature to compare cervical cancer (CC) screening outcomes based on cytology versus high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) testing. We searched databases including PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library up to May 2019, using keywords such as “cervical cancer screening,” “cytology,” “HPV testing,” and “hrHPV”. The research revealed a growing body of evidence suggesting that primary hrHPV-based screening offered greater sensitivity in detecting cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) compared to cytology. However, findings regarding specificity, applied triage algorithms, and colposcopy referral rates varied across studies. No randomized healthcare policies comparing primary HPV-based screening and cytology had been conducted in Eastern Europe. Data on the use of immunocytochemistry and methylation tests in triage were emerging.

Added value of this study

This study contributes to the existing body of knowledge by providing reliable real-world data demonstrating that primary HPV-based screening with LBC triage nearly doubles CIN2+ and CIN3+ detection rates compared to cytology (conventional or LBC) at the expense of higher colposcopy referral and CIN1 detection rates. Residual risk of CIN2+ and CIN3+ in women with an HPV-positive test and normal reflex and 6-month repeat LBCs seems to be very low and FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation test seems to detect more CIN2+ cases than p16/Ki-67 immunocytochemistry in these women.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of our study, combined with existing evidence, urge countries with cytology-based programs to transition to primary HPV-based screening with reflex LBC. Additional colposcopy services should be available, at least in the first HPV-based screening round, and careful protocols must be developed to monitor HPV-positive women without CIN2+ and avoid overtreatment. Methylation markers have the capacity to be developed as sensitive triage methods in HPV-based screening.

Introduction

World Health Organization advocates high-performance cervical cancer (CC) screening tests.1 Although several countries have already switched from cytology to human papillomavirus (HPV)-based screening, many high-income countries still run cytological screening.2 Pilot studies were recommended before implementing HPV-based screening to assess feasibility, sustainability and other factors which may impact screening programs and healthcare systems.3

Data on the superior sensitivity (and lower specificity) of HPV tests compared with cytology comes from different types of studies,4,5 most of them comparing co-testing (concurrent examination with both HPV test and cytology) versus cytology. However, analyses considering only HPV testing and ignoring cytology in case of co-testing, consistently showed increased detection of prevalent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grade 2 or worse (CIN2+) and CIN grade 3 or worse (CIN3+) and subsequent lower CIN3+ detection in the second screening round among women who were HPV-negative in the first screening round in the experimental arms compared to those cytology-negative in the control arm.5 On the other hand, majority of pilot and implementation studies comparing performance of HPV-based to cytology-based screening did not use randomization of subjects but rather cluster allocation to screening tests6,7 which may have introduced some bias. One randomized public health trial showed similar detection rates (DRs) of CIN2+ in primary HPV and cytology arms8,9 but a higher protective effect of HPV-based screening against subsequent cancer.

Colposcopy referral of all HPV-positive women is neither feasible nor justified because of high positivity rates and consequently low positive predictive values (PPVs). Therefore, triage strategies including reflex liquid-based cytology (LBC), limited (genotypes 16/18) or extended genotyping (distinguishing more types or type groups), p16/Ki-67 immunocytochemistry on reflex LBC samples, methylation markers and other tests can be used to select HPV-positive women with high risk of cervical precancer and cancer.10, 11, 12, 13 Virtually all HPV-based screening programs utilize reflex LBC and/or other triage strategies but there is no consensus on an optimal triage approach. Although some risk of CIN2+ after a positive HPV test and normal reflex LBC remains, recommended strategies differ from one-year recall for these women currently in the USA10 to five-year recall for those testing HPV-positive with two normal LBCs six months apart in the previous Dutch algorithm14 that was in place at the time of initiation of our study. Strategies recommended for HPV-positive women with normal LBC triage results include repeated testing with HPV assays or cytology.3

To assess feasibility of switching from cytology-based to primary HPV-based CC screening and its performance in Poland we performed a multicenter randomized public health trial based on on-site randomization to compare effectiveness of two strategies: (i) primary HPV testing with reflex LBC triage, and if normal, repeated LBC after six months and colposcopy referral in cases of any cytological abnormality and (ii) current cytology-based (conventional or LBC) screening with a standard protocol including early recall cytology after 6 months and colposcopy referral. We also aimed to compare performance of a methylation test versus immunocytochemistry in proper identification of the residual risk of CIN2+ in HPV-positive women with two normal LBCs.

Methods

Study design

The HIPPO (HPV testing In Polish POpulation-based cervical cancer screening program) study is a parallel-group superiority randomized public health trial comparing performance of screening with (i) high risk (hr) HPV DNA testing, and (ii) cytology, with appropriate triage protocols in everyday clinical practice of CC screening in Poland. The detailed study protocol has been published elsewhere.15 The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (study ID: NCT04111835). Neither patients nor public were involved in the design, conduct or reporting of trial results.

The study started in October 2019 at the Maria Sklodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology (MSCNRIO) in Warsaw, Poland. In 2021, seven additional consortia joined the study, expanding it to 27 screening sites, 8 laboratories and 11 colposcopy clinics countrywide (see Supplementary Appendix for detailed list). Consortia included sites routinely participating in the screening program.

Participants

Insured women aged 30–59 years and eligible to participate in the CC Screening Program (CCSP) were enrolled in the trial. Women aged 25–29 although eligible for screening in CCSP were not included since primary screening with hrHPV DNA testing which was not recommended for them at the time of initiation of the study.3,16 Exclusion criteria were: (i) having up-to-date primary screening test in the CCSP (within the previous 3 years or within the past year in case of elevated CC risk due to ongoing immunosuppressive treatment, HIV infection or hrHPV infection), (ii) CC diagnosis with ongoing oncological treatment or surveillance. Routine self-referral of women, advertising campaigns, and direct personal and telephone invitations were used to recruit women. Written informed consent to participate was required by the decision of the Ministry of Health since an experimental screening test was offered in CCSP. Residual biological material was banked from consenting women in the HPV arm. Since the female population in Poland is ethnically homogeneous, no data on race was collected.

Randomization and masking

Consenting women underwent individual real-time centralized randomization within 10-year-age strata (30–39, 40–49, 50–59) by the electronic data capture system called Electronic System for Prevention Monitoring (pol. System Informatyczny Monitorowania Profilaktyki, SIMP). SIMP verified eligibility criteria based on screening and medical history records, and after signing the informed consent form women were randomized on-site in 1:1 ratio to screening with either HPV test or cytology as a primary method. Patient questionnaires, screening, colposcopy, and histology results were stored in SIMP. Both participating women and study personnel were aware of allocation of participants to arms.

Procedures

Participants completed a medical questionnaire routinely required in CCSP. Next, women were randomized to screening with: (i) hrHPV molecular test (experimental arm) or (ii) cytology. Either LBC or conventional cytology was offered in arm (ii) depending on the current practice in the particular screening site. Cervical samples were taken by gynecologists or midwives with the use of vaginal speculum and a certified brush and placed in a certified storage medium. Storage medium was not predetermined in cytology arm. In HPV arm SurePath™ medium (BD Totalys Slide Prep, Becton Dickinson TriPath) was used. Data on macroscopic evaluation of the cervix were recorded in SIMP.

Sites were obliged to use HPV assays validated according to international criteria for primary HPV screening17 and validated within VALGENT framework,18 certified, with ability to detect 14 high-risk HPV genotypes. Cytological brushes had to adhere to the European Guidelines for quality assurance in cervical cancer screening.16 Procedures performed in the cytology arm were expected to be in accordance with recommendations of Polish Gynecological Society.19 Cytology in both arms was evaluated according to the modified Bethesda system.20

Experimental arm—hrHPV test

In case of HPV-negative result, the next screening round was scheduled in 5 years. Triage for HPV-positive women was based on immediate reflex LBC, and, if negative (no intraepithelial lesions or malignancy, NILM), early recall LBC in 6 months. In case of abnormalities in triage or recall cytology, women were referred for colposcopy with mandatory histological verification.

In HPV-positive, twice LBC normal women additional triage with CINtec® PLUS Cytology (Roche Molecular Systems, Pleasanton, CA, USA, Diagnostics) and QIAsure™ Methylation Test (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands) was performed. The first one is an immunocytochemical test, identifying simultaneous expression of p16/Ki-67 proteins. The second one identifies hypermethylation of FAM19A4 and hsa-miR124-2 genes. Both tests, called collectively additional triage biomarkers, were performed on early recall LBC residual cell pellet. In case of either positive result, women were referred to colposcopy; remaining women being negative for both additional biomarkers were scheduled for the next screening round in 3 years.

Control arm—cytology

According to the recommendations at the time of study initiation,21 all women with high-grade abnormalities (atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions, or more severe diagnosis, ≥ASC-H) were referred for colposcopy, with biopsy at the discretion of colposcopists. By the decision of the pathologist low-grade cytological abnormalities (atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance, ASC-US, or low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions, LSIL) usually led to a repeat cytology in 6 months or colposcopy referral. Two abnormal cytologies resulted in colposcopy referral.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of the study was DR of histologically confirmed CIN2+ in intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis in the first screening round. Histological outcomes in HPV-positive women with two normal LBCs in whom either of the additional triage biomarker was positive were not included in primary analysis but were included in secondary analyses according to the amendment of the study protocol. Secondary outcomes were: (i) DR of histologically confirmed CIN3+, CC and CIN grade1 (CIN1); (ii) rate of abnormal primary screening test results; (iii) colposcopy referral rate (a measure of adverse events and harms in CC screening) and compliance with colposcopy referral, (iv) PPV of colposcopy referral, colposcopy and HPV-positive results by histology of detected lesions (CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, CC, CIN2+, CIN3+). DR and DR ratios were also calculated including the additional DR of testing with additional triage biomarkers in the HPV arm.

Statistical methods

Sample size estimations

Based on data retrieved from SIMP, we presumed 0.5% CIN2+ DR in cytology arm. Considering meta-analysis indicating 1.5 times higher sensitivity for CIN2+ detection with hrHPV molecular test compared to cytology,22 we expected 0.75% DR for CIN2+ in HPV arm. With 0.05 significance level and 80% power, 16,500 women should have been included in each study arm to demonstrate the presumed difference in CIN2+ detection.

To account for multicentre nature of the data, mixed-effects logistic regression model with random intercept with clustered variance estimator was implemented to estimate odds ratios for histological endpoints. Since DR of CIN lesions are low, odds ratios are comparable with rate ratios in these cases. For non-rare events, rate ratios were estimated.

Few missing data distributed similarly in both study arms were expected. No imputation was performed due to negligible impact of missing values on results. Two-sided statistical tests were applied to compare demographical covariates (chi-squared or Fisher exact test for discrete, Kruskall-Wallis or Mann–Whitney U for non-normally distributed continuous variables). In case of imbalanced distribution of demographic characteristics between study arms, adjusted models were prepared to control for potential confounders. Descriptive statistics included: absolute numbers and percentage for discrete, mean and standard deviation for normally distributed continuous, and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Continuous variables were checked for normality with Shapiro–Wilk test. All analyses were performed in Stata 15.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) and plots were prepared with R software (R Core Team (2021). R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Interim analysis was conducted with Data Safety and Monitoring Board after one year of study proceeding with 1213 and 1184 participants included in HPV and cytology arm, respectively.23 To reveal possible need for sample size increase after interim analysis, we used alpha-spending function by O'Brien-Fleming. Due to low rate of participants included in interim compared to the projected sample size (2397/33,000, 7.26%), alpha spent was negligible (5.72∗10−10) and increasing study size or changes in recruitment duration were not necessary.

Ethics committee approval

The study was approved by Research Ethics Committee at the MSCNRIO, Warsaw, Poland (no. 28/2019—main protocol, approved on 27 June 2019, and no. 28/2019/1/2020—amendment, approved on 16 July 2020). All participants provided written informed consent.

Role of funding source

The funding body had no role in trial design, conduct, data analysis or reporting.

Results

Between the 28 October 2019 and the 31 December 2022, 33,506 participants were recruited and followed-up until 28 October 2023. Among them, 16,799 were randomized to HPV and 16,707 to cytology arm. Four women in the HPV arm withdrew consent and finally 16,795 women with primary HPV test results and 16,707 with primary cytology result were analyzed (Fig. 1).

HIPPO study flowchart.

The median age of women was 45 years (IQR 38–52), 27,315 had never smoked (81.5%), 4800 were nulliparous (14.3%), and the median age at menarche was 13 years; 2330 used oral contraceptives (7.0%), and 2313 used hormone replacement therapy (6.9%). Small but significant differences between arms were noted for intrauterine device use, immunosuppressive treatment and presence of CC risk factors (HIV infection, HPV infection and immunosuppressive treatment; Table 1).

Histological endpoints

Overall, 137 CIN2+ lesions were detected in the HPV arm (DR 0.82%, 95% CI: 0.69%–0.96%) and 71 CIN2+ lesions in the cytology arm (DR 0.42%, 95% CI: 0.34%–0.54%), resulting in 93% more CIN2+ lesions in the experimental arm than in the control arm (odds ratio (OR) 1.93, 95% CI: 1.34–2.77). Significantly more CIN3+ lesions were detected in the HPV arm (n = 69, DR 0.41%, 95% CI: 0.32%–0.52%) than in the cytology arm (n = 35, DR 0.21%, 95% CI: 0.15%–0.29%; OR 1.99, 95% CI: 1.16–3.42). Similarly, CIN1 was more than twice as frequently diagnosed (OR 2.11, 95% CI: 1.56–2.85) in the experimental arm (n = 237, DR 1.41%, 95% CI: 1.24%–1.60%) than in the control arm (n = 112, DR 0.67%, 95% CI: 0.56%–0.81%). The difference in CC DR between study arms was not significant (3 and 5 cases in the cytology and in the HPV arm, respectively; OR 1.66, 95% CI 0.68–4.04; Fig. 2).

Detection rates of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grade 1 (CIN1), CIN grade at least 2 (CIN2+) and CIN at least 3 (CIN3+) in HPV arm (light gray color) and cytology arm (dark gray color) with 95% confidence intervals indicated with bars. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals in brackets are annotated below the bars.

After inclusion of the additional detection of histological outcomes in HPV-positive women with two normal LBCs and positive results of additional triage methylation and immunocytochemistry biomarkers, DRs increased to 0.87% for CIN2+ (9 more cases), 0.42% for CIN3+ (1 more case), and 1.54% for CIN1 (22 more cases) in HPV arm; differences between study arms persisted similar, with OR 2.05, 95% CI: 1.44–2.93 for CIN2+, OR 2.02, 95% CI: 1.18–3.44 for CIN3+ and OR 2.31, 95% CI: 1.70–3.13 for CIN1.

Other endpoints

Significantly more abnormal primary screening results were in the HPV arm (n = 1228, 7.3%) compared to the cytology arm (n = 1114, 6.7%, RR 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19). After the primary screening test, 65% more participants (RR 1.65, 95% CI: 1.44–1.90) were directly referred for colposcopy in the HPV arm (n = 555, 3.3%) compared to cytology arm (n = 334, 2.0%). Including all colposcopy referrals (which is the current measure of harms and adverse events in CC screening24), the difference between study arms was smaller but still significant (n = 702, 4.2% in HPV arm versus n = 499, 3.0% in cytology arm; RR 1.40, 95% CI: 1.25–1.57). Difference in compliance to colposcopy referral was borderline between the arms (RR 1.09, 95% CI: 0.96–1.23), see Table 2.

Positive predictive values

Among HPV-positive women, 237 were diagnosed with CIN1 (19.3%, 95% CI: 17.2%–21.6%), 68 with CIN2 (5.5%, 95% CI: 4.4%–7.0%), 64 with CIN3 (5.2%, 95% CI: 4.1%–6.6%), and 5 with invasive cancer (0.4% 95% CI: 0.2%–1.0%) resulting in 11.2% being diagnosed with CIN2+ (95% CI: 9.5%–13.0%), and 5.6% with CIN3+ (95% CI: 4.5%–7.1%).

Among those referred for colposcopy, 137 were subsequently diagnosed with CIN2+ in HPV arm (19.5%, 95% CI: 16.7%–22.6%) and 71 in cytology arm (14.2%, 95% CI: 11.4%–17.6%). PPV for detecting CIN3+ was 9.8% (95% CI: 7.8%–12.3%) in experimental and 7.0% (95% CI: 5.1%–9.6%) in control arm, while for CIN1 they were 33.8% (95% CI: 30.4%–37.3%) and 22.4% (95% CI: 19.0%–26.3%), respectively.

Among participants who underwent colposcopy, CIN2+ was identified in 21.1% (95% CI: 18.1%–24.4%) of women in the HPV arm and 16.7% (95% CI: 13.5%–20.6%) in the cytology arm. CIN3+ was diagnosed in 10.6% (95% CI: 8.5%–13.3%) and in 8.3% (95% CI: 6.0%–11.3%) of women in the HPV and cytology arms, respectively. CIN1 was diagnosed in 36.5% (95% CI: 32.9%–40.3%) and 26.4% (95% CI: 22.4%–30.8%) of women examined by colposcopy in the HPV and cytology arms, respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

Within the Polish CCSP, HPV testing with a protocol including reflex LBC as triage and colposcopy referral of cytology-positive women led to detection of almost twice as many CIN2+ and CIN3+ cases as cytology-based screening with the usual follow-up of positive results by repeat cytology and/or colposcopy. The HIPPO trial shows a considerable amount of missed pre-cancerous lesions among women assigned to cytological screening. Despite the higher screen-test positivity rates observed in the HPV arm, the PPVs for CIN2+ and CIN3+ among HPV-positive women exceeded those in the cytology arm.

Our results align with those from pilot studies in Denmark,6 UK7 and Norway25 and contrast with data from implementation study with a comparable protocol in Sweden,8,9 where DRs were similar in both arms. However, the extension phase of the Swedish study showed that HPV testing gives a greater reassurance against development of invasive CC than cytology in long-term follow-up.26 This was not found in a similar trial in Finland,27 where a high-quality cytology-based screening program has long been in place. It is possible that similar performance of HPV testing and cytology resulted from the high quality of the latter. In Finland many women participate in opportunistic cytological screening between organized screening rounds which may have contaminated results in cytology and HPV arms. In the first round of the Artistic trial in UK,28 CIN3+ DRs were similar in cytology and HPV arms. However, subsequent rounds resulted in lower CIN2+ DRs in the HPV arm revealing higher protective effect of HPV testing in the first round.29 A recent review of randomized trials4 indicates a range of differences in performance of cytology and HPV testing might depend on the quality of cytology. In our pilot the advantage of HPV-based screening over cytology is obvious. The differences are visible and consistent over three analyzed age strata however not all statistically significant (p-values for CIN2+ DR of <0.0001, 0.11 and 0.024 for 30–39-, 40–49-, and 50–59 age groups, respectively) since the study was not powered for stratified analyses and these should be treated as exploratory [Supplementary Appendix, Figs. 1–3].

We have encountered higher colposcopy referral rates (RR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.25–1.57) and CIN1 DR (RR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.67–2.66) in the HPV versus cytology arm. This might be considered as an adverse effect of HPV-based screening but is in line with previous data5,22 showing lower cross-sectional specificity of HPV testing compared to cytology. Therefore, countries transitioning from cytology to HPV-based screening should implement adequate triage protocols and ensure sufficient colposcopy services also covering treatment of CIN2+. Capacities and strict adherence to guidelines are required also to follow-up women with CIN1 as it is recommended as the first approach to avoid deleterious effects of overtreatment10,30 and since the risk of cancer is not different in women with CIN1 and HPV-positive women with normal biopsy results.

The higher CIN DR in the HPV arm is to some extent attributable to marginally higher compliance with colposcopy referral in the HPV versus cytology arm. Borderline significant difference suggests slightly higher motivation among HPV-positive women to undergo colposcopy than in the cytology arm. High compliance with further diagnostic steps is crucial for overall effectiveness of a fully implemented HPV-based program. The PPVs of colposcopy referral and colposcopy were higher in the HPV arm despite lower specificity of HPV testing compared to cytology which shows that the triage of HPV-positive results was effective.

We used LBC triage for HPV-positive results and early 6-month recall in HPV-positive cases with normal reflex LBC following triage policy in the first edition of HPV-based screening in the Netherlands. We have done this for two reasons: 1) single reflex LBC may not be sufficiently sensitive to exclude CIN2+ in HPV-positive women and 2) 12-month recall was considered too long for Polish women and might cause anxiety, since a 6-month recall is routinely used for ASC-US and LSIL in the current cytology-based screening program. Out of 1228 HPV-positive women, triage LBC was abnormal in 555 cases and resulted in CIN2+ and CIN3+ DRs of 0.74% and 0.37%, respectively. Of 671 referred women, 617 attended the early recall LBC after 6 months (92.0%), which is an acceptable retention rate. The recall LBC after six months and subsequent colposcopy with histological verification resulted in an additional yield of 0.08% and 0.04% in CIN2+ and CIN3+, respectively. Although recent data from a similar large study in Sweden indicate that cytology triage for HPV-positive women is not sensitive enough,26 LBC is used in virtually all high-income countries with HPV-based screening.

Two new triage technologies—p16/Ki-67 immunocytostaining and FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation test—became available and gained popularity, so we amended the protocol during the first year of the study. We used these tests as safety measures for HPV-positive women with two normal LBCs. By histological verification of either immunostaining or methylation positive women in this group, we estimated the residual risk of CIN2+ at the minimum of 3.6% since concurrent false negative results of both biomarkers cannot be excluded. Among 9 CIN2+ cases detected in this pathway, only one was CIN3. This risk is below the colposcopy referral threshold in the very stringent American10 and derived Polish guidelines.30 We can therefore conclude that cross-sectional risk of CIN2+ is low in HPV-positive women with two normal LBCs six months in our screening program. However, HPV test as the most sensitive tool is currently used to close the screening episode and as an “exit-screening test” in most countries using HPV-based screening. Therefore, we should follow HPV-positive women with two negative LBCs or two negative LBCs and negative additional triage markers to assess long term CIN2+ risk. Interval until the next HPV screening round was shortened from 5 to 3 years for safety reasons in this group.

Our data suggest higher CIN2+ DRs but lower specificity of FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation than p16/Ki-67 immunocytochemistry in HPV-positive LBC normal women. Methylation tests are especially attractive for HPV-based programs utilizing self-collection of material which is not suitable for cytology. Based on our findings and other data, switching from cytology-based to HPV-based screening reduces cytotechnicians’ workload by 80–90%. On the other hand, it requires installation of validated, certified, high-throughput, and automated HPV testing platforms for high quality and efficiency. Centralization of laboratory work-up, changes in staffing to maintain a sufficient number of cytology examinations needed for the proficiency of cytotechnicians are strongly recommended. European guidelines advocate a minimal threshold of 10,000 tests per laboratory.16 Countries such as Turkey, England, Ireland and the Netherlands31, 32, 33, 34 centralized screening in labs which process hundreds of thousands of samples annually. No minimal requirements are imposed in our program, but they are highly needed also in countries planning to transition from cytology to HPV-based screening.

Population based HPV-vaccination of adolescents started in 2023 in Poland, so our results are obtained in an unvaccinated cohort. CIN DRs should be considered as for a screened, not a hard-to-reach population since our unpublished data show that many women participating in the screening program participated in previous rounds and in opportunistic screening.

The strengths of the HIPPO trial include randomized design, high number of women included, multicentered execution in different regions of the country, and embedment in the CCSP. Therefore, our results and conclusions should be representative of the whole country and applicable for other countries with cytological screening.

There are potential limitations of our study. Since it was not blinded and the cytotechnicians were aware of their participation in the trial, expectation bias cannot be excluded. This might have increased DRs of cytological abnormalities both in the HPV arm (being aware of higher risk of abnormalities in HPV-positive samples) and the cytology arm. Indeed, abnormality rate in the cytology arm was more than twice as high in HIPPO (6.7%) than in the OCCSP in 2016–2020 (2.6%). However, rates of abnormal LBCs after HPV-positive tests in the HPV arm were comparable to those observed in the Dutch screening program based on a similar algorithm.35 In all screening programs cytotechnicians are aware of assessing HPV-positive cytology slides; that is why “informed of HPV-positive” assessment of LBC in our protocol should be regarded as a part of intervention rather than a bias or limitation. Nevertheless, LBC positivity rates in HPV-positive samples should be monitored as a measure of quality assurance. In the HPV arm reimbursement was provided only for colposcopies with histological verification whereas in the cytology arm both colposcopy alone and colposcopy with histological verification were reimbursed. The histological verification rate was higher in the HPV-arm (99.3%) versus cytology arm (94.3%; p < 0.0001) which could have an impact on CIN DRs. Some biopsies in the HPV arm might have accompanied normal colposcopic impression and detected occult CIN2+ (e.g. so called “thin”36). However, this practice is in line with current Polish recommendations which advocate histological verification generally in all women referred to colposcopy.37

Decisions in public health interventions such as screening programs should be evidence-based. The European Commission is finalizing works on new, evidence-based recommendations for CC screening.24 Our study provides evidence supporting primary HPV-based screening. The Ministry of Health in Poland decided to implement HPV-based screening from 1 July 2025 but unfortunately retains cytology in parallel and leaves the choice of screening test to women. Hopefully, Poland and other countries with cytology-based screening will soon transition to primary HPV testing only. We have demonstrated that it is feasible and substantially more effective in identifying women with cervical precancer.

Prospective evaluation of HPV-based cervical screening programs in Poland and other countries are required. Reductions in CIN2+ detection/prevalence in subsequent rounds of screening and ultimate impact on CC incidence and mortality should be evaluated. Although co-testing might be slightly more sensitive than HPV testing alone, it is substantially less cost-effective due to a significant drop in specificity.38,39 HPV testing has very high sensitivity, but triage of positive results is required to compensate for lower specificity and precise identification of women with underlying or incipient precancer. LBC used for direct triage and early recall in our study performed well but worldwide studies are underway to identify a more precise triage test or protocol.40

We confirm, for the first time, in an Eastern European country that primary HPV-based screening significantly outperforms cytology in identification of women with CIN2+ and CIN3+. It however requires more colposcopies with histological verification and identifies HPV-positive women without CIN2+ requiring follow-up and resources. Therefore, adequate triage of HPV-positive women is needed to reduce over-referral. Based on the body of evidence produced to date by the scientific community and our findings we advocate transitioning from cytology-based to HPV-based screening programs and implementation of HPV-based CC screening in countries without screening programs. Prospective monitoring of the impact of subsequent rounds of HPV-based screening on CC incidence and mortality is needed.
