Blinding and quality of life endpoints in randomised trials of stable angina: a meta-epidemiological study
Authors: Dominic Jelf, Maya Jelf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQuality of life (QoL) measures are commonly used in randomised trials assessing therapies for stable angina. As these outcomes rely on patient reporting, they are susceptible to placebo and expectation effects when blinding is absent. It remains unclear whether trials investigating QoL outcomes in stable angina routinely incorporate blinded designs.MethodsWe performed a meta-epidemiological analysis of randomised controlled trials evaluating therapeutic interventions for stable angina, examining the association between QoL endpoint use and trial blinding. Background

Stable angina is a common manifestation of coronary artery disease, affecting approximately 3% of the population in the UK.1 Treatment primarily aims to provide symptomatic relief and enhance quality of life (QoL). As symptom burden often drives clinical decision-making, trials assessing cardiovascular therapies should use clinical outcomes reflecting symptom severity and impact on QoL.2 In this context, QoL can be defined by the impact of stable angina and its treatment on physical functioning, daily activities and emotional well-being. Angina symptom burden has classically been assessed using exercise testing, patient-reported angina frequency and physician-assessed classifications such as the Canadian Cardiovascular Society grade. However, validated QoL questionnaire tools such as the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ) and the 36-Item Short Form Survey(SF-36) provide a broader patient-centred assessment of the lived impact of stable angina. This was recognised in 2006, when the European Medicines Agency recommended the use of QoL measures as primary endpoints in trials evaluating therapies for stable angina, reflecting their importance in assessing treatment benefit from the patient perspective.3 Although these outcomes are distinguished from hard clinical endpoints such as mortality, they are more susceptible to bias arising from trial design, particularly when blinding is absent.

QoL endpoints are inherently subjective, relying on patient perception that is shaped by prior beliefs and expectations. Consequently, without blinding, these endpoints are more likely to be influenced by placebo effects and recall bias, potentially inflating treatment effect estimates and thus overestimating the apparent benefit of an intervention.4–6 This raises an important incongruence whereby endpoints most relevant to clinical decision-making and patients’ daily functioning may also be those most vulnerable to bias when blinding is omitted.

However, implementing blinded designs can be challenging, particularly in trials assessing interventional procedures. Unlike pharmacological studies, where placebo controls can often be used to facilitate double blinding, interventional trials may involve procedural differences that are difficult to conceal from patients and investigators. Although sham-controlled designs can address this challenge, they can be logistically complex, may raise ethical concerns and are often more resource-intensive than conventional trial designs.7 8 Nevertheless, studies have demonstrated that blinding in placebo-controlled trials of procedural interventions is both practical and instrumental in influencing estimates of treatment benefit across a range of specialties including for the management of stable angina.9–11

Indeed, the ORBITA trial demonstrated that symptomatic benefit from percutaneous coronary intervention in stable angina may be attenuated when assessed within a blinded, sham controlled design.10 This was reflected in the primary endpoint of change in treadmill exercise time and secondary QoL endpoints including the SAQ and EQ-5D-5L questionnaire. Its successor, ORBITA-2, reinforced the importance of blinded, placebo controlled designs.11 It addressed limitations of retrospective symptom recall by using a smartphone application to contemporaneously record patient-reported daily angina symptoms and antianginal medication use, which were incorporated into an angina symptom score. Together, these trials have illustrated not only the feasibility of blinded, sham controlled designs but also why careful and robust trial design is particularly crucial when outcomes depend on patient reported QoL tools.

Given the shift towards incorporating QoL outcomes in research, understanding whether these endpoints are typically evaluated under blinded conditions is important for interpreting the reliability of evidence. Despite this, the prevalence of blinding in stable angina randomised controlled trials using QoL endpoints has not been systematically explored. We therefore conducted a meta-epidemiological study of randomised controlled trials evaluating therapeutic interventions for stable angina in order to assess the association between QoL endpoint use and reported participant blinding.

Methods

This study was a meta-epidemiological analysis of randomised trials evaluating therapeutic interventions for stable angina to examine the association between QoL endpoint use and trial blinding. The exposure of interest was inclusion of a QoL endpoint. The primary outcome was reported participant blinding. The unit of analysis was the individual trial.

Eligibility criteria

Randomised clinical trials enrolling patients with chronic or stable angina syndromes were eligible, including microvascular angina where studied. Trials focusing on acute coronary syndromes or unstable angina were excluded. Eligibility was not restricted by publication year or sample size. Included studies were eligible if they reported at least one angina related patient centred outcome, including symptom scores, exercise tolerance or QoL measures. Trials in which the primary endpoint consisted exclusively of major adverse cardiovascular events or other hard clinical outcomes were excluded, as these outcomes do not assess angina symptoms. We restricted inclusion to trials reporting symptom related angina outcomes because the relevance of blinding is the greatest when outcomes depend on patient or clinician assessment of symptoms. Non-randomised studies and those that did not evaluate therapeutic interventions for angina were also excluded.

Search strategy

We searched MEDLINE from database inception to February 2026 for randomised trials evaluating therapies for stable angina. The search combined controlled vocabulary related to stable angina, randomised controlled trials and therapeutic interventions. The full search strategy is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (DJ and MJ). Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then assessed independently by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. The study selection process is summarised in a flow diagram (figure 1).

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardised form. Extracted variables included sample size, publication year, intervention type, blinding status and the presence of QoL endpoints. As reporting of specific blinding domains varied substantially across trial reports, trials were classified as blinded if participant blinding was reported in the trial publication. This pragmatic approach was chosen to allow consistent classification across trials published over several decades. Trials described as open label or lacking participant blinding were classified as unblinded. When blinding status could not be determined from the study report, trials were classified as having unavailable blinding information.

Endpoint and intervention classification

Trial endpoints were classified based on the outcomes reported in each study. QoL endpoints were defined as questionnaire-based patient-reported health status instruments assessing health-related QoL, functioning or psychological well-being. This category included both formal health-related QoL instruments and related multidomain patient-reported measures relevant to patients’ perceived health status and daily functioning in stable angina. Examples included validated instruments such as the SAQ. Isolated measures of angina frequency, angina severity, exercise tolerance or physiological ischaemia were classified separately and were not considered QoL endpoints unless incorporated within a multidomain QoL or health-status instrument. A full list of endpoints is provided in online supplemental table 2.

Where a primary endpoint was explicitly specified in the trial report, only this endpoint was recorded and classified. If no primary endpoint was specified, all reported endpoints were extracted and categorised in order to avoid excluding older studies with less standardised reporting. Trials were classified as including a QoL endpoint if the primary endpoint (where specified) or any reported endpoint (where no primary endpoint was specified) fell within the QoL category. Interventions were categorised as pharmacological, interventional or other therapies based on the primary treatment evaluated in the trial. A full list of therapies is provided in online supplemental table 3.

Statistical analysis

Trial characteristics were summarised using categorical groupings or proportions as appropriate. Associations between the presence of QoL endpoints and trial blinding were evaluated using regression models estimating adjusted prevalence ratios. Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios, as ORs from logistic regression may overestimate associations when outcomes are common. Trials with unavailable blinding information were excluded from regression analyses. Models were adjusted for intervention type, publication year and log-transformed trial sample size. To assess the robustness of the primary findings, we undertook a sensitivity analysis limited to trials that explicitly specified a primary endpoint. Endpoint classification in this analysis was based solely on the stated primary endpoint, and the adjusted Poisson regression model was refitted within this subset.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value <0.05. Analyses were performed using R V.4.5.1.12 This meta-epidemiological study is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. The study was registered in PROSPERO prior to conducting the review (CRD420261336319). The dataset and full R analysis code used in this study are available in a public repository to facilitate reproducibility and can be accessed online (https://github.com/dominicjelf/angina_qol_blinding_analysis).

Results

Study selection

The database search identified 2410 records. After screening titles and abstracts, 2029 records were excluded because they were not original randomised controlled trials, did not report relevant symptom-based angina endpoints or had unavailable full text. A total of 381 trials met eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis.

Most trials evaluated pharmacological therapies (83%), followed by interventional therapies (11%) and other interventions (6%). Trials including QoL endpoints tended to be larger, with a smaller proportion enrolling fewer than 50 participants than trials without QoL endpoints (45% vs 61%).

Blinding status was available for 355 trials, of which 308 (87%) were blinded and 47 (13%) were unblinded. Trials including QoL endpoints were less likely to be blinded compared with trials without QoL endpoints (54% vs 92%).

Temporal trends in endpoint use and blinding

The use of QoL endpoints increased over time, rising to 50% of included trials in both the 2010s and 2020s. In contrast, the proportion of trials incorporating blinding declined from over 90% of trials with available blinding data before 2000 to 77.6% of trials in the 2000s, 72.4% of trials in the 2010s and 68.8% of trials in the 2020s (figure 2).

Overall association between trials using QoL endpoints and trial blinding

After adjusting for treatment, publication year and log sample size, trials including QoL endpoints were significantly less likely to be blinded than trials without such endpoints (adjusted prevalence ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.87, p=0.001).

Pharmacological trials were more likely to incorporate blinding compared with intervention trials (adjusted prevalence ratio 3.01, 95% CI 1.93 to 4.69, p<0.001). There was no significant association between trial blinding and publication year or trial sample size. Full regression results are presented in table 2.

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to trials with an explicitly stated primary endpoint, the association between QoL endpoint use and lower likelihood of participant blinding remained consistent with the primary analysis. Stratified blinding by treatment type

Blinding prevalence varied according to treatment type and the inclusion of QoL endpoints (figure 3). Among interventional trials, blinding was uncommon and was particularly rare in trials that included QoL endpoints (1/10 (10%) vs 11/28 (39.3%)). A similar pattern was observed among trials evaluating ‘other’ therapies (3/12 (25%) vs 7/8 (87.5%)). In contrast, pharmacological trials frequently implemented blinding regardless of endpoint choice, although trials including QoL endpoints were less likely to be blinded (22/26 (84.6%) vs 264/271 (97.4%)). A consistent pattern was observed across treatment types, with trials including QoL endpoints less frequently blinded than those without QoL endpoints.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this meta-epidemiological analysis of 381 randomised trials of stable angina, we found that trials using QoL endpoints were significantly less likely to employ blinding than trials without QoL endpoints. This association persisted after adjustment for treatment type, publication year and sample size.

QoL endpoints were used in a minority of trials but have become increasingly common over time. Blinding prevalence also varied across treatment types, being most common in pharmacological trials and less frequent in interventional studies. Across these treatment categories, trials incorporating QoL endpoints were consistently less likely to report blinding.

Interpretation and implications for clinical evidence

Studies across multiple medical fields have shown that trials lacking blinding often report larger effect sizes, particularly when outcomes are subjective.4 13 In this context, our findings raise concerns about the reliability of evidence generated from trials evaluating symptom-based outcomes when blinding is absent. If subjective endpoints are assessed in unblinded settings, treatment effects may be more vulnerable to systematic overestimation, potentially influencing guideline development and subsequently clinical decision making.

Our study showed that QoL outcomes are increasingly used in angina trials. This is in keeping with their growing use across other areas of healthcare research14 and likely reflects a broader shift towards patient-centred assessment of treatment benefit. Their uptake has been supported by the wider availability of validated patient-reported outcome measures and increasing regulatory emphasis on patient-centred trial outcomes.

Several factors may explain why trials using QoL endpoints were less likely to be blinded. First, QoL measures are frequently used in studies of interventional procedures, which in our analysis were substantially less likely to employ blinding than pharmacological trials. This may reflect the practical difficulties of masking procedural interventions, particularly where creating a credible sham comparator is logistically challenging, financially costly and may introduce ethical concerns around exposing patients to unnecessary procedural risk. Despite this, ORBITA and ORBITA-2 provide recent examples of how blinded, sham-controlled designs can shape interpretation of symptomatic benefit in stable angina. These trials also illustrate the importance and limitations of patient-reported outcome tools. Validated questionnaires such as the SAQ and newer app-based symptom measures can improve the standardisation and granularity of assessment for both QoL and symptom endpoints. Nonetheless, questionnaire-based tools may be affected by retrospective recall and item interpretation, while app-based measures depend on adherence, digital access and accurate symptom attribution. Neither approach removes the need for blinding when outcomes remain dependent on patient reporting.

The historical context of stable angina trials may also partly explain the difference in blinding between trials that incorporated QoL endpoints and those that did not. Earlier trials in our dataset predominantly evaluated pharmacological treatments, for which participant blinding was comparatively straightforward through the use of matched placebo. These trials often used exercise-based, Holter-derived and physician-assessed endpoints rather than broader QoL measures. As QoL outcomes have become more common, they have increasingly been used in trials of interventional and other complex therapies, where blinding may be more difficult to achieve but remains no less important.

However, our finding that trials using QoL endpoints were less likely to be blinded cannot be fully explained by treatment category alone, as this association persisted after adjustment for treatment category, publication year and sample size. Additional factors may be relevant, including changing trial priorities over time and historical differences in reporting standards for patient-reported outcomes. As the use of QoL outcomes has expanded, reporting standards such as the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials - Patient Reported Outcomes (CONSORT PRO) extension have emphasised the importance of rigorous trial design and transparent reporting when these endpoints are used as primary or key secondary outcomes.15 It is also possible that, because QoL instruments are standardised and validated, their vulnerability to bias in unblinded settings is sometimes underappreciated. While validation establishes that an instrument measures the intended construct in an interpretable way, it does not eliminate bias arising from an unblinded trial design.

Against this background, our findings suggest a mismatch between endpoint choice and trial design, with QoL endpoints being used more often in trials that are less likely to employ blinding. The combination of subjective outcomes and unblinded trial design may increase the risk that treatment benefits are overestimated, thereby affecting the reliability of the evidence base used to guide clinical practice. These findings have implications for the design, appraisal and interpretation of angina trials. Trialists should adopt blinding or sham controlled designs where feasible. Reviewers and editors should scrutinise subjective endpoints particularly carefully in unblinded trials. Guideline developers should explicitly consider blinding when interpreting evidence for symptom based benefit.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Classification of blinding relied on descriptions reported in trial publications and may therefore be subject to misclassification. Our search was restricted to MEDLINE and therefore may not have captured all eligible trials. However, MEDLINE indexes the majority of randomised controlled trials in cardiovascular medicine, and our meta-epidemiological objective was to obtain a broad sample of angina trials in order to characterise trial features.

The observational design of this analysis means that causal relationships between endpoint selection and blinding practices cannot be established. Furthermore, other trial characteristics not examined in this study may influence trial design decisions. Trials were classified as blinded if participant blinding was reported. However, the degree of blinding varied between trials, and single blind designs provide less protection against bias than fully double blind trials. Our classification did not distinguish between different degrees of blinding, as reporting of these features varied substantially across trial reports. Importantly, the study was deliberately designed to examine associations between trial design features, not to evaluate intervention effects. Accordingly, we did not assess treatment effect estimates within individual trials and cannot determine the magnitude of bias associated with lack of blinding. Formal risk of bias and certainty assessments were therefore not performed, as these are more relevant to syntheses aimed at estimating pooled treatment effects.

Conclusion

In randomised trials evaluating therapies for stable angina, QoL endpoints are increasingly used but are less frequently accompanied by blinded trial designs, highlighting a potential blinding paradox whereby outcomes most vulnerable to expectation and placebo effects are often evaluated in studies that do not employ blinding. Consequently, the absence of blinding may increase the risk of biased estimates of treatment benefit. These findings support closer scrutiny of blinding in trials that evaluate symptom based benefit and reinforce the importance of blinded or sham controlled designs wherever feasible.
