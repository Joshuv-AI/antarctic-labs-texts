The Ultra-Radical: Analytic Continuation, Branching, and Stability of the Principal Branch
Sergey V. Berezin

Abstract
We study the ultra-radical x n ; a ; b \sqrt[n;a;b]{x} , the multi-valued solution to y a = 1 + a ​ x ​ y b y^{a}=1+axy^{b} . Inside the convergence radius | x | < R |x|<R , every branch is given by a Master–J power series; for | x | ≥ R |x|\geq R , analytic continuation requires switching to one of two conjugate series . We introduce a deterministic geometric criterion that selects, for each branch index n n , the correct conjugate series, thereby eliminating heuristic search and guaranteeing branch continuity across | x | = R |x|=R . Key finding: Only the principal branch ( n = 0 n=0 ) remains continuous when the parameters a a , b b , and x x vary smoothly. This includes the critical limits a → 0 a\to 0 (transition to an exponential equation) and b → 0 b\to 0 (transition to a binomial root), where the principal branch converges to the corresponding classical solution. In contrast, branches with n ≠ 0 n\neq 0 exhibit oscillatory divergence as a → 0 a\to 0 and lose their identity in these limits. This structural continuity singles out the principal branch for applications where parameters may vary with the system’s state, such as in nonlinear media with field - dependent exponents or adaptive dynamical systems.

Introduction

Consider the
master function

y=M(m;\alpha;\beta;x)
, defined by the series

M(m;\alpha;\beta;x)=m+x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(m-\alpha\gamma+\beta\ell).

(1.1)

This is a multi-parameter family of solutions, continuously depending on parameters
\alpha,\beta
and the independent argument
x
.

When
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
, the master function is a power series representing solutions of classical elementary or transcendental equations, where
\alpha=a
,
\beta=b
:

•

For
m=0

y=x
(
a=b=0
),

(
a=0,b\neq 0
),

(
a\neq 0,b=0
).

•

For
m=1

y=e^{x}
(
a=b=0
),
y=e^{xy^{b}}
(
a=0,b\neq 0
),
y=(1+ax)^{1/a}
(
a\neq 0,b=0
).

For
a\neq 0
and
b\neq 0
with
m=1
, the master function is the power series expansion of the
ultra-radical

y_{n}=\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}\equiv U_{n}(a;b;x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(1.2)

defined as the multivalued solution of the algebrao-transcendental equation

y=(1+axy^{b})^{1/a}.

(1.3)

Its power-series representation possesses a
finite radius of convergence

R=\frac{|1-a/b|^{b/a}}{|b-a|},\qquad a\neq 0,\;b\neq 0,\;b\neq a.

(1.4)

The ultra-radical is always defined via the master series
y=\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}=vM(1;\alpha;\beta;X_{J}V/(\alpha q_{J}))
,
where
v=e^{f}
,
V=e^{\beta f}
,
f=\frac{\ln|q_{J}/p_{J}|+i[\arg(q_{J}/p_{J})+2\pi N]}{\alpha}
. For
|x|<R
, the direct expansion is used,
while for
|x|\geq R
, one of two conjugate representations with different
parameters
\alpha,\beta
is employed, the choice depending on the branch number
n
:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}\text{Domain}&\text{Param.}&\alpha&\beta&p_{J}&q_{J}&X_{J}\\
\hline\cr|x|<R\ (\text{inside})J=1&N=n\in\mathbb{Z}&a&b&1&1&ax\\[10.0pt]
|x|\geq R\ (\text{case h})J=2&N=h\in\mathbb{Z}&b-a&-a&-ax&-1&1\\[10.0pt]
|x|\geq R\ (\text{case k})J=3&N=k\in\mathbb{Z}&-b&a-b&-1&ax&-1\end{array}

Sector centers
and
candidates
on the imaginary axis:

L(n)=\operatorname{Im}(bf(n)),\quad L(h)=\operatorname{Im}(bf(h)),\quad L(k)=\operatorname{Im}(bf(k)),

For real
a,b

L(n)=\frac{2\pi bn}{a},\quad L(h)=\frac{b[\arg(1/(ax))+2\pi h]}{b-a},\quad L(k)=\frac{b[\arg(-ax)+2\pi k]}{-b}.

The centers
L(n)
partition the imaginary axis into sectors
[g_{n},G_{n}]=[L(n)-\pi b/a,\ L(n)+\pi b/a]
.

The
angular criterion
selects those
h
or
k
for which
L(h)
or
L(k)
fall into the sector
[g_{n},G_{n}]
of branch
n
, ensuring continuous analytic continuation.

Note also the connection between the behavior for
|x|\geq R
and the limit
a\to 0
. The two conjugate representations of the principal branch —
h=0
and
k=0
— tend, as
a\to 0
, to the two distinct real branches of the generalized Lambert function
y=e^{xy^{b}}

3.2
Structural Stability of the Principal Branch

The special behavior of the
n=0
branch reveals a profound structural property:
among all branches, only this one evolves
smoothly
as the parameters
a
and
b
vary continuously.

Observation:
When
a
is varied continuously from, say, 3 to 12,
the
n=0
branch changes gradually — its graph simply adjusts its curvature and asymptotic behavior.
All other branches, however, appear and disappear abruptly as the number of solutions changes
discontinuously with
a
. For irrational
a
, the equation technically possesses infinitely many
branches, yet only the
n=0
branch remains stable and well
-
defined across the entire parameter range.

Physical and computational implications:

•

In physical systems where exponents
a
,
b
themselves vary with time or state
(e.g., non
-
linear media with field
-
dependent exponents, adaptive systems),
only the principal branch can be tracked continuously.

•

Iterative methods (Newton, Halley, etc.) have no inherent mechanism to identify
which of the infinitely many numerical roots corresponds to the principal branch —
they simply converge to whichever root lies closest to the initial guess.

•

The Master
-
J method, in contrast,
identifies
the principal branch explicitly:
it is the one for which the phase factor
e^{2\pi in/a}
reduces to unity (
n=0
),
eliminating the oscillatory term that complicates all other branches.

Thus, for the principal branch, the solution simplifies to:

\sqrt[0;a;b]{x}=M\!\left(1;a;b;x\right)\quad(\text{for }|x|<R),

with the analytic continuation automatically selecting
h=0
(for
x>0
) or
k=0
(for
x<0
)
when
|x|\geq R
. No other branch enjoys such algorithmic simplicity.

3.3
Geometric Criterion for the Principal Branch (n=0)

For the principal branch
y_{0}(x)=\sqrt[0;a;b]{x}
, the branch
-
selection algorithm simplifies drastically. When
|x|\geq R
(outside the convergence radius), the branch
n=0
always admits two continuation candidates:
h=0
and
k=0
. The choice between them is governed by a single geometric inequality:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} &\text{Use the $h$-series with }h=0\quad\text{if }|\arg(x)|\leq\frac{\pi\,|b-a|}{a},\\[6.0pt]
&\text{otherwise use the $k$-series with }k=0.\end{aligned}}

4
Generalizations

4.1
Equations with Arbitrary Coefficients

For equations with arbitrary coefficients:

pu^{a}=q+zu^{b}

we apply the substitution:

u=y\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}},\quad z=aqx\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{\frac{b}{a}}

to obtain the canonical form:

y^{a}=1+axy^{b}

4.2
Equations with Arbitrary Number of Terms

For equations with multiple terms:

py^{a}=q+x_{1}y^{b_{1}}+x_{2}y^{b_{2}}+\cdots

such equations are solved by merging several ultra-radicals:

y=v\cdot_{a}^{1}:_{\frac{x_{1}v^{b_{1}}}{aq},\ \frac{x_{2}v^{b_{2}}}{aq},\ \cdots}^{b_{1},\ s_{2},\ \cdots},\quad v=\sqrt[a]{\left|\frac{q}{p}\right|}e^{\frac{\left(\arg\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)+2\pi n\right)i}{a}},\ n\in\mathbb{Z}

The operation of merging master series will be detailed in the appendices.

5
The Ultra
-
Radical as a Solution of Nonlinear ODEs

The differential properties of the ultra
-
radical reveal its important role in the theory of nonlinear ordinary differential equations.

Let
y=\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
be the ultra
-
radical defined by the equation
y^{a}=1+axy^{b}
. Differentiating this identity with respect to
x
yields

ay^{a-1}y^{\prime}=ay^{b}+abxy^{b-1}y^{\prime}.

Cancelling
a
(assuming
a\neq 0
) and solving for
y^{\prime}
, we obtain the compact algebraic formula

\boxed{\,\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}=\frac{(\sqrt[n;a;b]{x})^{b-a+1}}{1-bx(\sqrt[n;a;b]{x})^{b-a}}=\frac{\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}}{(\sqrt[n;a;b]{x})^{a-b}-bx}.\,}

(5.1)

Thus the ultra
-
radical satisfies the nonlinear differential equation

\boxed{\,y^{\prime}=\frac{y^{b-a+1}}{1-bxy^{b-a}},\qquad y(0)=1.\,}

(5.2)

Conversely, any ODE that can be reduced to the form (
5.2
) has the solution
y(x)=\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
.

5.1
Practical Use

•

Computing the derivative:
Once the value of the ultra
-
radical is known, its derivative follows directly from (
5.1
).

•

Solving ODEs:
Equations of the form (
5.2
) are solved explicitly via the ultra
-
radical.

•

Verification of numerical methods:
The ultra
-
radical provides a benchmark analytical solution for testing numerical ODE solvers.

•

Behaviour analysis:
Formula (
5.1
) allows one to study monotonicity, convexity and asymptotic behaviour of the solution.

6
Integral of the Ultra-Radical

Similar to the derivative, the indefinite integral of the ultra-radical can also be expressed through the ultra-radical itself. This extends the analytic capabilities of the method and enables, in particular, the derivation of explicit expressions for pseudopotentials in nonlinear wave problems. The integral is most conveniently obtained by the method of integration of the inverse function, since the inverse relation
x=x(y)
for the ultra-radical has a simple power
-
law form.

6.1
Method of integration via the inverse function

If
y=y(x)
is an invertible function and
x=x(y)
is its inverse, the identity

\int y(x)\,dx=xy(x)-\int x(y)\,dy

holds; it follows from integration by parts or directly from geometric considerations (see, e.g., standard textbooks of mathematical analysis). This method is particularly efficient when
x(y)
has a simpler expression than the original dependence
y(x)
.

6.2
Derivation of the integral formula

Let
y=\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
be the ultra
-
radical satisfying the defining equation

y^{a}=1+axy^{b},\qquad a\neq 0,\;b\neq 1.

Solve it for
x
as a function of
y
:

x=\frac{y^{a-b}-y^{-b}}{a}.

(6.1)

Now apply the integration
-
via
-
inverse
-
function formula:

\int y\,dx=xy-\int x\,dy.

Substituting
x
from (
6.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int y\,dx

\displaystyle=xy-\frac{1}{a}\int\bigl(y^{a-b}-y^{-b}\bigr)\,dy

\displaystyle=xy-\frac{1}{a}\Biggl[\frac{y^{a-b+1}}{a-b+1}-\frac{y^{1-b}}{1-b}\Biggr]+C,

where
C
is an arbitrary constant of integration, and we assume
a-b+1\neq 0
and
b\neq 1
(the case
b=1
is treated separately).

Thus we arrive at the general formula for the indefinite integral:

\boxed{\,\int\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}\,dx=x\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}-\frac{1}{a}\Biggl[\frac{(\sqrt[n;a;b]{x})^{a-b+1}}{a-b+1}-\frac{(\sqrt[n;a;b]{x})^{1-b}}{1-b}\Biggr]+C.\,}

(6.2)

6.3
Normalized form used in physical applications

In many physical problems one requires the integral to vanish at
x=0
, which corresponds to the condition
y(0)=1
. Substituting
x=0
,
y=1
into (
6.2
) fixes the constant
C
and yields a formula for the definite integral from
0
to
x
:

\boxed{\,\int_{0}^{x}\sqrt[0;a;b]{t}\,dt=\frac{1}{a}\Biggl[\frac{a-b}{a-b+1}\Bigl(y^{a-b+1}-1\Bigr)+\frac{b}{1-b}\Bigl(y^{1-b}-1\Bigr)\Biggr],\qquad y=\sqrt[0;a;b]{x}.\,}

(6.3)

Exactly this normalized form arises naturally when deriving a pseudopotential from Poisson’s equation after substituting the concentration expressed via the ultra
-
radical.

6.4
Special case b=1

For
b=1
formula (
6.2
) becomes

\int\sqrt[n;a;1]{x}\,dx=x\sqrt[n;a;1]{x}-\frac{1}{a}\Biggl[\frac{(\sqrt[n;a;1]{x})^{a}}{a}-\ln\!\bigl(\sqrt[n;a;1]{x}\bigr)\Biggr]+C,

(6.4)

which can be obtained either by direct computation or by taking the limit
b\to 1
in (
6.2
).

6.5
Verification and an example

To verify, differentiate the right
-
hand side of (
6.2
) using the derivative formula for the ultra
-
radical (
5.1
) and the defining equation. After algebraic simplification one confirms that the derivative equals
\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
, which validates the integral.

As an illustration take
a=3
,
b=2
(the equation
y^{3}=1+3xy^{2}
). From (
6.2
):

\int\sqrt[0;3;2]{x}\,dx=xy-\frac{y^{2}}{6}+\frac{y^{-1}}{3}+C,\qquad y=\sqrt[0;3;2]{x}.

6.6
Conclusion

The obtained formulas allow one to express integrals of solutions to a wide class of power
-
law equations in closed analytic form. This opens the possibility of direct symbolic analysis in problems where previously a parametric representation or numerical integration was required.

7
Physical Interpretation: Non-Linear Circuits and the Ultra-Radical

Although the ultra-radical
\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
was introduced as a solution to the algebrao-transcendental equation
y^{a}=1+axy^{b}
, its mathematical structure turns out to be naturally connected to the description of a wide class of physical systems — in particular, electric circuits containing non-linear elements. In this section we show how equations arising in circuit theory reduce to the canonical form solvable by the ultra-radical, and reveal the physical meaning of the conditions imposed on the parameters
a
and
b
.

7.1
From Ohm’s Law to Power-Law Non-Linearity

For a linear resistor the relation between voltage
U
and current
I
is given by Ohm’s law:

U=RI,

where
R
is a constant resistance. For many real devices (incandescent lamps, semiconductor elements, varistors) this dependence is strongly non-linear. It is often approximated by a power-law function

U=\alpha I^{\,k},\qquad k>0,\;k\neq 1,

(7.1)

where
\alpha>0
is a coefficient and the exponent
k
characterises the degree of non-linearity. Expression (
7.1
) can be rewritten in a form reminiscent of Ohm’s law by introducing a
current-dependent resistance

R(I):=\frac{U}{I}=\alpha I^{\,k-1}.

Then

U=R(I)\,I,\qquad R(I)=\alpha I^{\,k-1}.

The physical meaning of the parameter
k
is transparent:

•

For
k>1
the function
R(I)
increases with current (example — an incandescent lamp: heating raises the resistance);

•

For
k<1
the function
R(I)
decreases with current (example — a semiconductor thermistor: heating generates additional charge carriers, resistance drops);

•

For
k=1
we recover an ordinary linear resistor with constant resistance
R(I)\equiv\alpha
.

7.2
Equation of a Simple Non-Linear Circuit

Consider a circuit consisting of a constant voltage source
U_{0}
, a linear resistor
R_{0}
and a non-linear element with the volt-ampere characteristic (
7.1
). By Kirchhoff’s second law

U_{0}=U_{\text{lin}}+U_{\text{nonlin}}=R_{0}I+\alpha I^{\,k}.

Bringing all terms to one side we obtain a three-term algebraic equation for the current
I
:

\boxed{\;\alpha I^{\,k}+R_{0}I-U_{0}=0\;}

(7.2)

Equation (
7.2
) has exactly the same structure as the canonical equation for the ultra-radical
y^{a}=1+axy^{b}
after a suitable change of variables. Indeed, comparing with the general three-term form
Ay^{a}+By^{b}+C=0
, we see the correspondence

A=\alpha,\quad a=k;\qquad B=R_{0},\quad b=1;\qquad C=-U_{0},

where
I
plays the role of the unknown
y
.

7.3
Practical Value of the Ultra-Radical: Overcoming Infinite Multi-Valuedness

Consider a non-linear element with the voltage-current characteristic
U=\alpha I^{k}
, where the exponent
k
is a fractional (possibly irrational) number. Together with a series linear resistor
R_{0}
and a voltage source
U_{0}
, the current satisfies the equation

\alpha I^{k}+R_{0}I-U_{0}=0.

(7.3)

For fractional
k
this equation possesses an
infinite
set of complex solutions – a typical situation for power-type equations with a non-integer exponent. All modern numerical methods (Newton, secant, fixed-point iterations) face fundamental difficulties in such a situation, which the ultra-radical
\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
resolves in a natural and unified way.

1.

Identification of solutions within an infinite set.
For irrational
k
equation (
7.3
) has a continuum of solutions. A numerical method started from some initial guess will find
one
root, but it provides no information about which particular family (branch) this root belongs to. The ultra-radical
\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
explicitly parameterizes the whole solution set by the integer index
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. This allows one not only to obtain a specific value, but also to
know its place in the complete solution structure
.

2.

Continuous branch tracking under parameter variation.
In a real device the exponent
k
may slowly vary (e.g., due to temperature drift). The physically observed current must change continuously. Numerical algorithms, having no notion of branch numbering, may unexpectedly “jump” to a different branch when parameters are slightly altered, leading to a discontinuous jump in the solution. The ultra-radical, thanks to the geometric criterion for selecting the conjugate series (Section
1
), guarantees that the principal branch (
n=0
) remains continuous under smooth variation of all parameters. This property is critically important for modelling devices with “floating” characteristics.

3.

Analytic operations: derivatives and integrals.
Stability analysis, calculation of sensitivity
dI/dU_{0}
, or the energy stored in the element require derivatives and integrals of the solution. With a purely numerical approach these must be computed via implicit differentiation of the equation or finite
-
difference approximations, which complicates the analysis and accumulates errors. The ultra-radical, being an explicitly defined function, admits direct symbolic differentiation (Formula (
5.1
)) and integration (Section
6
). For instance, the derivative of the current with respect to voltage is obtained simply by substituting
y=\sqrt[0;k;1]{x}
into (
5.1
), and the integral
\int I\,dU_{0}
is expressed in elementary functions of
y
via (
6.2
).

4.

Inheritance of the general properties of master functions.
The ultra-radical is a particular case of the master series
M(m;a;b;x)
and therefore inherits all its identities: power, logarithmic, isoroot, etc. (see Appendix
C
). This allows one to perform with the solution of (
7.3
) the same algebraic transformations as with elementary functions, opening the possibility of symbolic simplifications and analytic continuation.

Concrete example: a varistor with a fractional exponent

A typical varistor has a power
-
law characteristic with exponent
k=3.5
(fractional). For
\alpha=10^{3}\;\text{V·A}^{-k}
,
R_{0}=100\;\Omega
,
U_{0}=220\;\text{V}
equation (
7.3
) becomes

10^{3}\,I^{3.5}+100\,I-220=0.

It cannot be solved in radicals, and numerical methods yield only a single number without indicating how it relates to the complete set of solutions.

Reducing the equation to the canonical form
y^{3.5}=1+3.5\,x\,y
, we obtain an explicit representation of the current via the ultra-radical:

I=\left(\frac{U_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{\!1/3.5}\cdot\sqrt[0;3.5;1]{\,x\,},\qquad x=-\frac{R_{0}}{3.5\,U_{0}}\left(\frac{U_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{\!1/3.5},

where the root
\left(\frac{U_{0}}{\alpha}\right)^{1/3.5}
is taken on its principal branch. This formula automatically ensures the continuity of
I
as
U_{0}
,
R_{0}
,
\alpha
, and even the exponent
k
itself are varied.

Concluding remark

The ultra-radical
\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
is not merely an alternative computational trick. It provides a
functional representation
of the solutions of power
-
type equations, analogous to how the Lambert
W
-
function represents the solutions of
ye^{y}=x
. Thanks to the explicit parameterization of all branches (
n\in\mathbb{Z}
) and the inheritance of the algebraic properties of the master series, the ultra-radical turns the analysis of equations like (
7.3
) from a numerical procedure with uncontrolled multi
-
valuedness into a deterministic operation with guaranteed continuity and full support for analytic manipulations. For problems where the exponents are fractional (let alone irrational) and may vary in time, such an approach is not just convenient – it is the only consistent way to correctly account for multi
-
valuedness.

8
The Ultralogarithm: The Logarithmic Core of the Theory

The presented theory of the ultra radical possesses a profound and symmetric "logarithmic" counterpart. This counterpart not only simplifies the formalism but also directly bridges it with classical analysis, revealing it as a natural generalization of familiar functions.

8.1
From Ultra Radical to Ultralogarithm

The fundamental identity linking master series,
M(1;a;b;x)=\exp(M(0;a;b;x))
, suggests that
M(0;a;b;x)
is the "logarithm" of the ultra radical. Crucially, this is not merely a formal composition—it is an
independent canonical function
solving its own defining equation. For
a=1
, this equation takes an elegantly simple form, which we term the
b
-logarithm equation
:

\boxed{\,y=\ln\!\bigl(1+xe^{\,by}\bigr)\,}

(8.1)

Its solution,
y=\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x):=M(0;1;b;x)
, is called the
b
-logarithm
(or ultralogarithm).

This reveals
two parallel families
of canonical functions:

Type

General Equation

Canonical Form (
a=1
)

Power-type

y^{a}=1+axy^{b}

y=(1+xy^{b})^{1}
(trivial)

Logarithmic-type

y=\dfrac{\ln(1+axe^{by})}{a}

\boxed{y=\ln(1+xe^{by})}

The key insight is that the **canonical logarithmic form is non-degenerate**. It defines a nontrivial one-parameter family
\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x)
, which generalizes the natural logarithm:
\ln(1+x)=\operatorname{ulog}_{0}(x)
. Furthermore, specific parameter choices recover other classic functions. For instance, with
a=2,b=1
, the general logarithmic master function
M(0;2;1;x)
yields the
inverse hyperbolic sine
:
\operatorname{arsinh}(x)=M(0;2;1;x)
. This is not a coincidence but a consequence of the underlying structure: the condition
a-b=1
(as seen in the graphs of the corresponding ultra radical) and
b=1
(which simplifies integration formulas) mark this function as a distinguished, well-behaved node within the parametric space.

8.2
Branch Relationships: From Ultra Radical to Ultralogarithm

The defining equations of the ultra radical and the ultralogarithm form a coupled system via the logarithm operation:

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\bigl(1+axy^{b}\bigr)^{1/a}\quad\text{(Ultra radical $\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}$)},

(8.2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln\!\bigl(1+axe^{bu}\bigr)}{a}\quad\text{(Ultralogarithm $M(0;a;b;x)$)}.

(8.3)

The substitution
y=e^{u}
(or
u=\ln y
) transforms (
8.2
) into (
8.3
). However, due to the multivalued nature of the complex logarithm, this connection generates the complete family of branches.

For the canonical form, the general solution is given by parametric formulas:

\displaystyle y_{n}

\displaystyle=v_{n}\cdot M\!\bigl(1;\;a;\;b;\;xV_{n}\bigr),

(8.4)

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle=\ln(v_{n})+M\!\bigl(0;\;a;\;b;\;xV_{n}\bigr),

(8.5)

where

v_{n}=\exp\!\left(\frac{\ln|1|+i\bigl(\arg(1)+2\pi n\bigr)}{a}\right)=e^{2\pi in/a},\quad V_{n}=v_{n}^{b}=e^{2\pi ibn/a},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Interpretation and Implications

•

Formulas (
8.4
) and (
8.5
) demonstrate that each branch
u_{n}
of the ultralogarithm is obtained not by simply taking the logarithm of the corresponding branch
y_{n}
of the ultra radical (
u_{n}\neq\ln y_{n}
), but by a shift of the constant
\ln(v_{n})
and a change of the
argument
in the master series (
xV_{n}
instead of
x
). This reflects the algebraic independence of the two solution families.

•

The expression
M(0;a;b;xV_{n})
in (
8.5
) represents the
power series for any branch of the ultralogarithm
. Thus, taking the logarithm of the ultra radical yields not an arbitrary transcendental function, but a specific, computable power series shifted by an integer imaginary constant.

•

For
n=0
, we obtain the
principal branches
:
v_{0}=1
,
V_{0}=1
, and the relationship simplifies to the expected one:
y_{0}=M(1;a;b;x)
and
u_{0}=M(0;a;b;x)
, with
y_{0}=e^{u_{0}}
.

•

This formalism makes the
verification rule
explicit: for the substitution
y_{n}
into equation (
8.2
) to yield an identity, it is necessary to compute
y_{n}^{a}
as
\exp\bigl(a\cdot u_{n}\bigr)
, where
u_{n}
is given by formula (
8.5
). The integer
n
in the expression for
v_{n}
corresponds precisely to the verification parameter
u
computed automatically by the
SolverABC
algorithm.

This representation not only clarifies the multivalued structure but also provides a unified computational basis: an algorithm constructed for the ultra radical simultaneously computes the corresponding branches of the ultralogarithm via formulas (
8.4
)–(
8.5
).

8.3
Differential Properties and Relation to the Ultra Radical

As follows from the exponential identity
M(1;a;b;x)=\exp(M(0;a;b;x))
, the derivatives of the master functions are linked by a fundamental relation:

\frac{d}{dx}M(1;a;b;x)=M(1;a;b;x)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}M(0;a;b;x).

(8.6)

This relationship reflects the logarithmic nature of the connection between the power-type and logarithmic-type series. For the
b
-logarithm (
a=1
), equation (
8.6
) takes a concrete and illustrative form. Introducing
u=e^{\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x)}=M(1;1;b;x)
, which is equivalent to
u=1+xu^{b}
, one can derive an explicit formula for the derivative that avoids cumbersome expressions:

\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x)=\frac{u^{\,b}}{b+(1-b)u}.

(8.7)

Key observation:
Formula (
8.7
), unlike the direct expression for the derivative of the corresponding ultra radical,
contains no explicit dependence on the independent variable
x
. It expresses the rate of change of the
b
-logarithm solely in terms of its exponent
u
and the parameter
b
, representing a significant algebraic simplification. This demonstrates that switching to the logarithmic parametrization does not merely alter the notation but
simplifies the algebraic structure
of the fundamental relations.

8.4
The Significance of the b-Logarithm

1.

Minimalism and Classical Bridge
. Like the ordinary logarithm,
\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x)
is defined by a single parameter
b
. It provides the most direct and intuitive entry point into the theory, seamlessly extending the familiar function
\ln(1+x)
.

2.

Computational Advantages
. For small
|x|
, the series
M(0;1;b;x)
often exhibits superior convergence and numerical stability compared to the power series of the corresponding ultra radical with parameters
(1,b)
.

3.

A Transformational Tool
. Many equations involving exponentials can be reduced to the form solvable by the
b
-logarithm. Thus,
\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x)
acts as a
universal solver
for a class of exponential-power equations, analogous to the role of the logarithm in linearizing multiplicative relationships.

4.

A New Research Pathway
. The theory of the
b
-logarithm as a standalone object—its analytic properties, integrals, asymptotic behavior, and applications—constitutes a vast and nearly unexplored field. It offers a more accessible and focused domain for initial investigation than the full two-parameter theory of the ultra radical.

8.5
Methodological Impact

Introducing the
b
-logarithm
\operatorname{ulog}_{b}(x)
as a primary object serves a crucial purpose: it
grounds the theory in a simple, familiar core
. Instead of presenting the complete two-parameter apparatus immediately, one can first demonstrate how the well-known natural logarithm naturally extends by introducing a single parameter
b
. The subsequent generalization to the ultra radical (by introducing the parameter
a
) then appears as a logical and necessary step to capture the full algebraic structure, rather than an arbitrary abstraction.

Therefore, the ultralogarithm is not merely a corollary of the theory; it is its
conceptual keystone
. It facilitates understanding, strengthens connections to classical analysis, and provides a clear pathway for pedagogical exposition and practical application, all while highlighting profound connections to special functions like the inverse hyperbolic sine.

9
Conclusion

This work presents a systematic approach to solving the fundamental problem of analysis — ensuring continuity of branches of multi
-
valued functions during analytical continuation beyond the radius of convergence of power series.

Main results:

1.

For the ultra
-
radical
\sqrt[n;a;b]{x}
— solution of the equation
y^{a}=1+axy^{b}
— we propose a
geometric criterion
for branch selection. The criterion is based on the sector structure of the unit circle determined by parameter
a
, and guarantees root continuity when crossing the circle
|x|=R=\frac{|1-a/b|^{b/a}}{|b-a|}
.

2.

We developed and tested on examples a
deterministic algorithm
for analytical continuation, including standards for resolving ambiguities in degenerate cases (on sector boundaries).

3.

The framework
suggests a pathway for generalization
to equations with arbitrary coefficients and multiple terms through the merge operation, potentially extending its applicability to a broader class of equations.

Appendix A
Derivation of Master Series

Given the equation:

y^{a}=1+axy^{b}

Assume a solution as a power series:

y=1+k_{1}x+k_{2}x^{2}+k_{3}x^{3}+k_{4}x^{4}+\cdots

A.1
Substitution of Series into Equation

Expand the left side
y^{a}
using the binomial formula:

y^{a}=1+ak_{1}x+\left(ak_{2}+\frac{a(a-1)}{2}{k_{1}}^{2}\right)x^{2}+\left(ak_{3}+a(a-1)k_{1}k_{2}+\frac{a(a-1)(a-2)}{6}{k_{1}}^{3}\right)x^{3}+\cdots

Expand the right side
1+axy^{b}
:

y^{b}=1+bk_{1}x+\left(bk_{2}+\frac{b(b-1)}{2}{k_{1}}^{2}\right)x^{2}+\left(bk_{3}+b(b-1)k_{1}k_{2}+\frac{b(b-1)(b-2)}{6}{k_{1}}^{3}\right)x^{3}+\cdots

1+axy^{b}=1+ax+abk_{1}x^{2}+\left(abk_{2}+\frac{ab(b-1)}{2}{k_{1}}^{2}\right)x^{3}+\cdots

A.2
Equating Coefficients

For
x
:

ak_{1}=a\Rightarrow k_{1}=1

For
x^{2}
:

ak_{2}+\frac{a(a-1)}{2}=ab

k_{2}=b-\frac{a-1}{2}=\frac{1+2b-a}{2}

For
x^{3}
:

ak_{3}+a(a-1)k_{2}+\frac{a(a-1)(a-2)}{6}=abk_{2}+\frac{ab(b-1)}{2}

Substituting
k_{2}=\frac{1+2b-a}{2}
:

k_{3}=\frac{(1+3b-a)(1+3b-2a)}{3!}

For
x^{4}
:

k_{4}=\frac{(1+4b-a)(1+4b-2a)(1+4b-3a)}{4!}

A.3
General Coefficient Formula

The general term of the series:

k_{\ell}=\frac{\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(1-a\gamma+b\ell)}{\ell!}

Thus, the solution is:

y=1+x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(1-a\gamma+b\ell)\right)

Introducing unified notation:

M(m;a;b;x)=m+x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(m-a\gamma+b\ell)\right)

=m+x+(m-a+2b)\frac{x^{2}}{2}+(m-a+3b)(m-2a+3b)\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+\cdots

We obtain the identity:

y=\left(1+axy^{b}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}=M(1;a;b;x)

Fundamental Identities

Exponential identity

Scaling identity

c\cdot M(0;a;b;x)=M(0;a/c;b/c;cx)

:

:

\frac{dM(1;a;b;x)}{dx}=M(1;a;b;x)\frac{dM(0;a;b;x)}{dx}

Master series inherit from master numbers:

Appendix E
Convergence Radius of Master Series

The power series
M(m;a;b;x)=m+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}{k_{\ell}x^{\ell}}
, where

k_{\ell}=\frac{\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(m-a\gamma+b\ell)}{\ell!}

has convergence radius
R
, determined by coefficient asymptotics via Cauchy-Hadamard formula:

\frac{1}{R}=\limsup_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\left|k_{\ell}\right|^{\frac{1}{\ell}}

For
\ell\rightarrow\infty
the main contribution comes from factor
\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(b\ell-a\gamma)
, since
m
is constant. Replacing sum with integral:

\ln\left|k_{\ell}\right|\sim\ell\int_{0}^{1}{\ln|b-au|du}-(\ell\ln\ell-\ell)+o(\ell)

The integral computes analytically:

I=\int_{0}^{1}{\ln|b-au|du}=\frac{b\ln|b|-(b-a)\ln|b-a|}{a}-1\quad(a\neq 0)

Then:

\ln\left|k_{\ell}\right|\sim\ell[I+1]-\ell\ln\ell+o(\ell)

\left|k_{\ell}\right|^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\sim e^{I}

Consequently:

R=e^{-I}=|b|^{\frac{-b}{a}}\cdot|b-a|^{\frac{b-a}{a}}=\frac{|1-a/b|^{\frac{b}{a}}}{|b-a|},\ a\neq 0,b\neq a

R=\frac{1}{|be|},\ a=0

R=\frac{1}{|a|},\ b=0

M(m;a;b;x)
converges if
|x|<|R|

If
b/a
is complex, for correct
R
definition, take only principal branch of complex logarithm when computing complex power.

E.1
Behavior at the Convergence Boundary

The behavior of power series at the boundary
|x|=R
depends on the specific equation. For example:

•

The series for
\sqrt{1+x}
converges for
|x|\leq 1

•

The series for
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}}
converges for
-1<x\leq 1

In this work, for brevity we use a single convergence condition
|x|<R
. It should be understood that the actual behavior depends on the specific equation:

•

For some equations:
x<R

•

For other equations:
x\leq R

Derivatives and integrals of master series possess the same radius of convergence
R
as the original series; the only possible difference lies in whether the series converges strictly inside the disk
|x|<R
or also admits convergence on the boundary
|x|=R
.

Two different master equations are called

For example, if master equation

,

Appendix G
Analytical Continuations (Conjugate Master Series)

Convergence radius of exponentials, sines and cosines is infinite. Binomial series has limited number of terms, since all subsequent master series terms equal zero. Therefore convergence question for this series is irrelevant.

(1+x)^{a}=M(1;1/a;0;ax)\quad,\quad a\in\mathbb{N}

M(1;1/2;0;2x)=1+2x+\frac{4x^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{8x^{3}}{3!}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{2}\right)=1+2x+x^{2}

M(1;1/3;0;3x)=1+3x+\frac{9x^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)+\frac{27x^{3}}{3!}\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{3}\right)+0+0+\cdots

For other functions, question of convergence radius limitation is important. When one power series converges in region where original series diverges, and coincides with original function in common convergence region - this is called analytical continuation.

Master series
M(m;a;b;x)
of some function converges for
|x|<|R|
, where
R
given by formula
\frac{|1-a/b|^{\frac{b}{a}}}{|b-a|}
. For

Clarification:
Master numbers

are
not

that can be computed directly without series expansion.

A master number of order
\ell

(m-a\gamma+b\ell)
. The number of factors equals
\ell-1
. Therefore for
\ell<2
the master number returns 1. We denote master numbers inline (N), and use a short notation analogous to that employed for various factorial generalizations, powers, and other special numbers.

N(m;a;b;\ell)=\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b}_{\ell}{}=\prod_{\gamma>0}^{\gamma<\ell}(m-a\gamma+b\ell)

\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b}_{\ell}{}=(m-a+b\ell)(m-2a+b\ell)\cdots\left(m-a(\ell-1)+b\ell\right)

\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b}_{\ell}{}=(m-a+b\ell)(m-2a+b\ell)\cdots\left(m-a(\ell-2)+b\ell\right)\left(m-a(\ell-1)+b\ell\right)

Conclusion:

N(m;a;b;\ell)=N(m;-a;b-a;\ell)

\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b}_{\ell}{}=\prescript{m}{-a}{!}^{b-a}_{\ell}{}

Example:

\prescript{1}{-2}{!}^{0}_{4}{}=(1+2)(1+4)(1+6)=3\cdot 5\cdot 7

Appendix I
Function Merging Operation

The merge operation (denoted by symbol
@
) allows combining multiple series into a single unified structure. This operation is essential for solving equations with multiple terms, where each term requires its own master series with distinct independent arguments.

The merge operation for master numbers is defined as:

\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b_{1}}_{\ell_{1}}{}@\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b_{2}}_{\ell_{2}}{}@\cdots=\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\cdots}{}=\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots-1}\left(m-a\gamma+b_{1}\ell_{1}+b_{2}\ell_{2}+\cdots\right)

I.1
Merging Power Series

For power series (
m=1
), the merge operation is performed as follows:

\prescript{1}{a}{:}^{b_{1}}_{x_{1}}{}@\prescript{1}{a}{:}^{b_{2}}_{x_{2}}{}@\cdots=\prescript{1}{a}{:}^{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots}_{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots}{}

Merging of power series is analogous to ordinary multiplication of power series, where each term of one series is multiplied by each term of all other series, with the exception of the master-number factors.

I.2
Features of Term Merging

1.

Independent fractions are multiplied

\frac{x_{1}^{\ell_{1}}}{\ell_{1}!}\cdot\frac{x_{2}^{\ell_{2}}}{\ell_{2}!}\cdot\frac{x_{3}^{\ell_{3}}}{\ell_{3}!}

2.

Master numbers form a unified monolith

\prescript{m}{a}{!}^{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}{}=\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}-1}\left(m-a\gamma+b_{1}\ell_{1}+b_{2}\ell_{2}+b_{3}\ell_{3}\right)

3.

Zero term handling
: For power series (
m=0
), the zero term is
w_{0}=0
. During merging, the zero term is temporarily set to
w_{0}=1
. After completing the merge operation, 1 is subtracted from the resulting monolith.

4.

Appendix K
Relationship Between Master Series and Classical Hypergeometric Functions

The master series
M(m;a;b;x)
and the hypergeometric function
{}_{p}F_{q}
are two distinct ways of parameterizing power series. In this section we show that in certain degenerate cases the two formalisms yield identical expansions for classical functions, thus establishing precise correspondences between their parameters.

K.1
Definitions

The master series is defined as

M(m;a;b;x)=m+x+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{\ell!}\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\ell-1}(m-a\gamma+b\ell),

where
m,a,b\in\mathbb{C}
.

The hypergeometric series:

{}_{p}F_{q}\!\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{p};b_{1},\dots,b_{q};x\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{k}\cdots(a_{p})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k}\cdots(b_{q})_{k}}\frac{x^{k}}{k!},

where
(u)_{k}=u(u+1)\dots(u+k-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol.

K.2
Examples of Exact Correspondences

Binomial series

(1+x)^{\alpha}={}_{1}F_{0}(\alpha;;-x)=M\!\left(1;\frac{1}{\alpha};0;\alpha x\right).

Inverse power function

(1-x)^{-\alpha}={}_{1}F_{0}(\alpha;;x)=M\!\left(1;-\frac{1}{\alpha};0;\alpha x\right).

Natural logarithm

\ln(1+x)=x\;{}_{2}F_{1}(1,1;2;-x)=M(0;1;0;x).

Exponential function

e^{x}={}_{0}F_{0}(;;x)={}_{1}F_{1}(1;1;x)=M(1;0;0;x).

K.3
Interpretation of the Coincidences

The equalities above reflect the fact that when the parameters

or

take special values (zero or related to the exponent
\alpha
), the master series

reduces to elementary functions — the binomial

It is crucial to note that this correspondence occurs only in these

K.4

The coincidence of master series with hypergeometric series in degenerate cases confirms that the master formalism correctly generalizes classical analysis. However, the complete theory of analytic continuation, branching, and stability of solutions to nonlinear master equations presented in this work is self

This demonstrates why quintic equations cannot be generally solved in radicals: the structural approach necessarily leads to resolvents of higher degree than the original equation. Historical Note: The Search for the Ultra-Radical and the Role of Computational Tools

The quest for an analytic solution to generalized trinomial equations has a long and distinguished history. The problem essentially revolves around inverting the function defined by
y^{a}=1+axy^{b}
, a quest for what we now term the
ultra-radical
.

The journey began in the 18th century with the pioneering work of **Johann Heinrich Lambert** (1758)
[
11
]
, who systematically studied the trinomial
x^{m}+px=q
. He derived successive approximations and recognized the structure of a series solution, laying the groundwork for later investigations. **Leonhard Euler**, inspired by Lambert, examined the more general equation
x^{\alpha}-x^{\beta}=(\alpha-\beta)vx^{\alpha+\beta}
and its limiting logarithmic form. These efforts highlighted the inherent complexity of the problem but stopped at deriving series expansions for specific instances, lacking a unified, parametrized formalism and a method for analytic continuation.

In the late 18th century, **Erland Samuel Bring**, in his work on the quintic equation, discovered a crucial reduction: the general quintic can be transformed into the form
y^{5}+py+q=0
. This is equivalent to the ultra-radical equation with parameters
a=5,b=1
. Thus, Bring’s radical, a key element in the theory of quintic solvability, is a distinct, isolated point
(5,1)
in the two-dimensional parametric space of the ultra-radical.

For centuries, these contributions remained as separate, insightful fragments. A complete, unified theory required not only conceptual leaps but also a tool capable of handling the immense symbolic complexity involved. The manual derivation of the general series coefficients, the identification of the three isoroot transformations (AB, BC, CA), and the development of the merge operation (@) for multi-term equations represented a combinatorial and algebraic challenge of staggering proportions.

The modern resolution of this centuries-old search became possible only with the advent of powerful computational tools. The author’s investigation illustrates this point dramatically:

•

The complete parametric power series and the three fundamental transformations (defining the analytic continuation) were **automatically discovered and verified** by symbolic computation (using Maple) within a week in 2018.

•

In contrast, the **merge operation (@)**, a generalization essential for solving equations with an arbitrary number of terms, eluded manual derivation for five years. It was finally identified and formulated in 2023, again through an iterative cycle of computational experimentation and pattern recognition aided by the structural insights provided by the system.

•

The recent preparation of this manuscript, including the verification of complex identities, branch continuity proofs, and structuring of the theory, was significantly accelerated by using large language models (LLMs) specialized in mathematical reasoning. These tools acted as tireless assistants in code generation, consistency checking, and suggesting expositional structures.

This historical perspective underscores a key thesis: the ultra-radical, as a complete analytic function with its branching rules and continuation algorithm, is a
computationally native
object. Its full description was historically int noractable not due to a lack of mathematical insight from giants like Lambert, Euler, or Bring, but due to the absence of the necessary symbolic and computational power. The present work, therefore, stands on the shoulders of these pioneers, fulfilling the goal they could clearly see but not fully reach, empowered by the computational tools of our time.
