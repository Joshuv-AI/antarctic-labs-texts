Exact Solutions to the Quantum Schrödinger Bridge Problem
Mykola Bordyuh, Djork-Arné Clevert, Marco Bertolini

Abstract
The Quantum Schrödinger Bridge Problem (QSBP) describes the evolution of a stochastic process between two arbitrary probability distributions, where the dynamics are governed by the Schrödinger equation rather than by the traditional real-valued wave equation. Although the QSBP is known in the mathematical literature, we formulate it here from a Lagrangian perspective and derive its main features in a way that is particularly suited to generative modeling. We show that the resulting evolution equations involve the so-called Bohm (quantum) potential, representing a notion of non-locality in the stochastic process. This distinguishes the QSBP from classical stochastic dynamics and reflects a key characteristic typical of quantum mechanical systems. In this work, we derive exact closed-form solutions for the QSBP between Gaussian distributions. Our derivation is based on solving the Fokker-Planck Equation (FPE) and the Hamilton-Jacobi Equation (HJE) arising from the Lagrangian formulation of dynamical Optimal Transport. We find that, similar to the classical Schrödinger Bridge Problem, the solution to the QSBP between Gaussians is again a Gaussian process; however, the evolution of the covariance differs due to quantum effects. Leveraging these explicit solutions, we present a modified algorithm based on a Gaussian Mixture Model framework, and demonstrate its effectiveness across several experimental settings, including single-cell evolution data, image generation, molecular translation and applications in Mean-Field Games.

Introduction

The

;

;

;

;
vincent2011connection
;

;

;

Under the transition density of Brownian motion, paths that transport mass from arbitrary
\pi_{0}
to
\pi_{1}
are highly unlikely; the SBP addresses this by selecting, among all such low-probability trajectories, the one that is most likely. This optimal stochastic process has time-marginal density
p(\mathbf{x},t)
that admits the factorization

p(\mathbf{x},t)=\phi(\mathbf{x},t)\,\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
where the potentials

and

satisfy heat equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle=-\beta\Delta\phi~,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{\phi}

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta\widehat{\phi}~,

(1)

with

This structure closely mirrors quantum mechanics, where the probability density is given by
p(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\psi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\overline{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, with
\psi
satisfying the (imaginary-time) Schrödinger equation

i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\beta\Delta\psi
,
highlighting a deep mathematical connection between stochastic control and quantum dynamics

;
schrodinger1926undulatory
.
This analogy was rigorously formalized by Guerra and Morato

, and extended in subsequent works

;

;

, which demonstrated that the SBP admits an alternative formulation as a stochastic control problem, governed not by classical optimal transport dynamics

;
kantorovich1942translocation
, but by the kinematics of the Schrödinger equation.

In this work, we aim to bridge the conceptual and methodological gap between the “classical” SBP and the Guerra-Morato (GM) formulation, referred to as the Quantum Schrödinger Bridge Problem (QSBP), following the terminology of
pavon2002quantum
. Our first contribution is to reformulate the GM Lagrangian in terms of a forward-backward stochastic differential equation (SDE) system and derive the necessary conditions that the associated quantities must satisfy. This reformulation enables the adaptation of various techniques originally developed for solving the classical SBP

;
shi2023diffusion
;
chen2021likelihood
;
vargas2021solving
;
tong2023simulation
;
neklyudov2022action
to the quantum setting.
These methods typically involve learning forward and backward stochastic processes between the marginal distributions
\pi_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{x})
using two neural networks, with a Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure (IPFP)
ruschendorf1995convergence
;
ireland1968contingency
;
essid2019traversing
optimization procedure. However, such approaches are often computationally intensive due to the necessity of sampling the whole trajectory, which can hinder their usability in practice

.
To address these limitations, a complementary line of research has focused on the analytical tractability of Gaussian distributions to develop more efficient SBP solvers
chewi2020gradient
;
altschuler2021averaging
;
korotin2023light
. Exact solutions to the SBP are known in only a few cases, with the Gaussian setting being one of the most prominent

;
bunne2023schrodinger
.

In this work, we extend the set of exact solutions by deriving the closed-form expression of the QSBP for Gaussian marginals. Our approach differs significantly from that of

, which employ tools from Riemannian geometry. In contrast, we adopt a Lagrangian framework and solve both the Fokker–Planck equation and the quantum Hamilton–Jacobi equation explicitly, offering a distinct theoretical perspective for addressing bridging problems with tractable distributions.

In short, this work places the
quantum Schrödinger Bridge on equal footing with the classical SBP in both theoretical understanding and practical usability
. Specifically, we make the following contributions:

•

In Section

, we introduce the optimality conditions for the QSBP and show that they lead to the Quantum Hamilton-Jacobi Equation, whose solution is governed by the Schrödinger equation. While these results are not novel per se, we reformulate them in a manner that is suitable for generative score-matching models.

•

In Section

•

In the subsequent sections, we extend the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) algorithm to our exact solution and apply it to a variety of bridging tasks, including image generation, single-cell data modeling and latent molecular properties translation. We also demonstrate an application to Mean Field Games via a variational formulation of the quantum Lagrangian.

In this section, we explore the solution of the QSBP and its connection to fundamental equations of physics and probability. Specifically, we derive the Hamilton-Jacobi equation that governs the dynamics of the system, enriched by the inclusion of the Bohm potential. This quantum potential introduces non-local effects, reflecting how each particle’s behavior depends not only on local properties but also on the global configuration of the system. Further, we establish links between the optimal transport framework and quantum mechanics, as we show that the governing equation of motion is the Schrödinger equation.

Proposition 2
.

The solution of the QSBP (Definition
1

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}=-Q(\boldsymbol{x})~,

is known as the Bohm potential (or quantum potential) and is given as

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{x})=-2\beta(\boldsymbol{x})^{2}\frac{\Delta\sqrt{p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}}{\sqrt{p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}}=-\beta(t)^{2}(\Delta\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2})~.

(10)

We note that the quantum potential

5.4
Mean Field Games, Population Dynamics, and Quantum Lagrangian Minimization

Mean Field Games (MFG)
lasry2006jeux
;
lasry2007mean
provide a theoretical framework for modeling complex game-theoretic problems involving a large number of interacting agents, where the number of players tends toward infinity.
The QSBP is closely related to a particular instance of MFG where interactions between agents are important, for example, when a school of fish clusters to enhance collective security. In this case, the dynamics are governed by the Schrödinger equation
swiecicki2016schrodinger
.
For our mean field experiments, we consider classical crowd navigation environments: the “S-tunnel” with asymmetric obstacles, and the “U-tunnel” environment with a narrow passage, previously studied in works on MFG
ruthotto2020machine
;
lin2021alternating
;
liu2022deep
;
liu2023generalized
;
liu2024deep
. In our setup, we model the population evolution between the initial and final distributions using a single Gaussian, evolving through a predefined obstacle configuration.
We adopt a variational Lagrangian formalism to find the optimal trajectory. We initialize the trajectory using the RRT* path planning algorithm
karaman2011sampling
, which builds a path connecting the start and target distributions.
The trajectory is modeled by a time-discretized set of Gaussian distributions parametrized by the mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)
and variance
\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)
for
t=0,\mathrm{d}t,\dotsc,1
. For simplicity, we consider the case of a diagonal Gaussian. Given
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t_{i})
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}(t_{i})
, the kinetic and potential energies along the trajectory in the diagonal case are computed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{i}=0}^{1}\left[\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t_{i})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Tr}\,\bigl(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t_{i})\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t_{i})\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t_{i})\bigr)\right]~,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}\,\sum_{t_{i}=0}^{1}\,\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t_{i})\bigr)~.

(21)

The evolution of individual samples is governed by the stochastic update (see Appendix
E
)

\boldsymbol{x}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}(t_{i}+1)+\sqrt{1-2\beta}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}(t_{i+1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{\Sigma}(t_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t_{i}))+\sqrt{2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}~,

(22)

ensuring that at each time
t_{i}
the sample population has mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t_{i})
and variance
\mathbf{\Sigma}(t_{i})
.
The final population dynamics trajectory is obtained by minimizing the following Lagrangian objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{QSB}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl[\|\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\|^{2}\Bigr]\,p(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{U}~.

(23)

We minimize the objective (
5.4
) with respect to the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)
over the time interval
t=0,\mathrm{d}t,\dotsc,1
.
At each optimization step, we recompute the derivatives

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}(t+1)-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}~,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}(t+1)-\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}~,

(24)

evaluate the updated kinetic and potential energy contributions, and propagate the individual samples according to the stochastic update rule (
22
).

To discourage the population from entering obstacle regions, we include an additional penalty term
\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}
based on the log-likelihood of samples overlapping with obstacles.

6
Discussion and Conclusions

Our work represents a step toward greater flexibility in modeling generative processes between two arbitrary distributions. By selecting a different optimality condition, formulated in our framework as a distinct Lagrangian, we derive a bridging problem that follows the Schrödinger equation from quantum mechanics. In the case of Gaussian measures, we provide exact closed-form solutions, effectively placing our models on the same footing as traditional Schrödinger bridge problems in terms of both usability and theoretical understanding. A particularly promising avenue of research is to apply these processes to physical data governed by similar dynamics, such as in molecular dynamics simulations, where the system’s time evolution is described by the Schrödinger equation.

Proposition 5
.

The solution of the QSBP (Definition
1
) is described by the quantum Hamilton-Jacobi equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}=-Q(\boldsymbol{x})~,

(25)

where
Q(\boldsymbol{x})
is known as the Bohm potential (or quantum potential) and is given as

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{x})=-2\beta(\boldsymbol{x})^{2}\frac{\Delta\sqrt{p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}}{\sqrt{p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}}=-\beta(t)^{2}(\Delta\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2})~.

(26)

Proof.

As mentioned above, the FPE (
5
) is valid no matter which Lagrangian is chosen for optimality.
Therefore, it must be satisfied also in our case. We impose the constraint directly in the Lagrangian through a Lagrange multiplier

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{QSB}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{b}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}+\beta(t)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\right)\,p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+S(\boldsymbol{x},t)\left[\partial_{t}p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right]\right]\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left[\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{b}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}-\beta(t)\mathbf{b}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\cdot\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\right]p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t

Plugging in the definition of
\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
(
6
) and setting to zero the derivative of the above Lagrangian with respect to
\mathbf{b}_{+}
, we get its optimal value

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{+}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\beta(t)\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)~,\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)~.

(28)

Substituting the above expressions into the minimization objective, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{QSB}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta(t)^{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}+|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}+2\beta(t)\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left.-\beta(t)^{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}-\beta(t)\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)\cdot\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}\right]p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)^{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}-\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}\right]p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left\{\left[\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)^{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}-\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}\right]p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\beta(t)^{2}\nabla p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\left[\beta(t)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}+\Delta\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}\right]p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t~,

(29)

which vanishes when

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S(\boldsymbol{x},t)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla S(\boldsymbol{x},t)|^{2}=\beta(t)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}+\Delta\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=-Q(\boldsymbol{x},t)~,

(30)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Main Theorem

Our main result is given by the following

Theorem 7
.

Given a probability distribution of the form

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)(\boldsymbol{x}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))\right)~,

(34)

it solves the QSBP (
7
) with boundary conditions

\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})
and
\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{0}\,t~,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigl[(1-t)\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}+t\Bigl(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}-\beta^{2}\mathbb{I}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr]^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\;+\;\,t\beta^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}.

(35)

Proof.

We starts by considering the constrains imposed by the continuity equation.
Then, we will solve the different Hamilton-Jacobi equation.
The continuity equation takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))=0~.

(36)

We first compute the quantity
\partial_{t}p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
. The pre-factor
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)}}
depends on the determinant of the covariance matrix. Taking the time derivative of this term we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\left(\det\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)\right)^{3/2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\det\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)\right)~,

(37)

where we used the Jacobi formula for the derivative of the determinant and
\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)=\partial_{t}\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)
.
Taking the time derivative of the exponential term involves differentiating both with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)
.
Putting all these components together, the time derivative of
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\left[-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)\right)+(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))\right]~,

(38)

where we used the identity

\partial_{t}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)=-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)
as well as the fact that
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a symmetric matrix.
The gradient of
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}
is

\displaystyle\nabla p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})~,

(39)

and thus the divergence term

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\right)=-\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})+p_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)~.

(40)

Plugging everything into (
36
) we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)\right)+(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\dot{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(t)\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}(t)\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)~.

(41)

Since both sides of the equations are polynomials in
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)

Bohm Potential of a Gaussian Mixture

We consider a Gaussian mixture distribution:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}),

(82)

where
\alpha_{k}\geq 0
,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}=1
, and

\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k})
is the
k
-th Gaussian component.
We define the
responsibilities
(posterior mixture weights):

\displaystyle w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\frac{\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k})}{p(\boldsymbol{x})},\quad\text{so that}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;1.

(83)

Single-Gaussian Bohm Potential.

if
p(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})
, the

Bohm potential
is defined as

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;-\,\beta^{2}\Bigl[\Delta\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl\|\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr].

(84)

For a single Gaussian,

\displaystyle\nabla\log\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=-\,\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\,(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})~,

\displaystyle\Delta\log\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\;-\,\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}).

(85)

Hence

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\beta^{2}\Bigl[\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bigr)\;-\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bigr)^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\Bigr].

(86)

We denote this single-Gaussian Bohm potential by
Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
for the
k
-th component.

Score
\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})
of the Mixture.

For the mixture density
p(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
, we have

\displaystyle\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\log\!\Bigl(\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigr),\quad\text{where we abbreviate }\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}).

(87)

Then

\displaystyle\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\boldsymbol{x})}\;\nabla\!\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\boldsymbol{x})}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\nabla\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigl[-\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\,(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})\bigr]~.

(88)

Score squared of the Mixture.

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}w_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{\top}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})

(89)

(91)

Using the Laplacian identity above, we find

\displaystyle\Delta\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl\|\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\Delta\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\;+\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\bigl\|\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\;-\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl\|\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}.

(92)

\displaystyle=\;-\,\beta^{2}\biggl[\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigl(\Delta\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\;+\;\bigl\|\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr)\;-\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl\|\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\;\underbrace{\bigl[-\,\beta^{2}(\,\Delta\log\mathcal{N}_{k}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}\|^{2})\bigr]}_{=\,Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})}\;+\;\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{2}\Bigl[\bigl\|\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\;-\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\bigl\|\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr].

(93)

\displaystyle\boxed{Q(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\;\;+\;\;\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{2}\Bigl[\bigl\|\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\;-\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\bigl\|\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr].}

(94)

This shows that the Bohm potential of a Gaussian mixture is given by a

plus an extra “coupling term” that reflects the nontrivial mixture-log-density structure.

Appendix D
Molecule Translation in Latent Space

The entire experiment was performed on a standard laptop using only the CPU, without any GPU acceleration.
We use a 512 dimensional latent space representation of 7,831 molecules, each annotated with up to 12 toxicity endpoints. For this experiment, we focus on two endpoints with the most abundant annotations: SR-MMP (918 measurements) and NR-AhR (768 measurements). Each molecule is labeled as toxic or non-toxic, corresponding to class indices 1 and 0, respectively.

Classifier Architecture.

To distinguish toxic from non-toxic molecules, we train a multitask binary classifier using a fully connected feedforward neural network. The model consists of five linear layers with decreasing hidden dimensions: 256, 128, 64, and 32 units. Each layer is followed by a LeakyReLU activation. The final output layer produces a 12-dimensional logit vector, one per toxicity endpoint. The network is trained using the Adam optimizer (default parameters), with a learning rate of
3\times 10^{-4}
and a batch size of 1024. Training is performed on a random train/test split using binary cross-entropy loss until convergence.

Classifier Performance.

The classifier achieves the following performance on the two target endpoints:

NR-AhR: F1 = 0.584, PR-AUC = 0.689, Balanced Accuracy = 0.723, MCC = 0.530

SR-MMP: F1 = 0.674, PR-AUC = 0.740, Balanced Accuracy = 0.793, MCC = 0.611

Latent Space Translation.

We apply our GMM-based Schrödinger Bridge model to translate molecules in latent space between the non-toxic and toxic classes. The model is trained with 30 Gaussian wavepackets, using
\beta=0.01
, a batch size of 10, and a learning rate of
10^{-3}
. Training converges in approximately 10,000 epochs.

Due to the broader distribution of non-toxic molecules in latent space and the concentrated nature of toxic molecules, we focus on forward translation: from non-toxic to toxic. A classification threshold of 0.5 is used to distinguish class membership. For each non-toxic molecule, we generate 1, 5, and 10 samples from the learned transport distribution to evaluate the likelihood of crossing into the toxic region and study saturation effects. An interesting application of our approach would be to combining it with explainability approaches
[
63
,
64
]
to gain further insights into the model rationale for generating the chosen trajectory in latent space, which should amount to explain which chemical matter modes are associated a high likelihood in the learned “toxic” distribution.

Appendix E
Mean-Field Games and Lagrangian Minimization

Our approach is based on Lagrange minimization principle. Among all admissible trajectories
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
connecting two fixed points
\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}(1)=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
over a fixed time interval
t\in[0,1]
, the optimal trajectory minimizes the action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{x}(t)]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t),t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(95)

where
\mathcal{L}(x,\dot{\boldsymbol{x}},t)
is the Lagrangian, typically of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,\dot{\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\|^{2}-V(\boldsymbol{x}(t),t),

(96)

with
V(\boldsymbol{x},t)
denoting a potential energy function. Considering a single gaussian, we can significanly simplify our formulas for kinetic and potential energies, making the algorithm attractive in its simplicity and flexibility.

Let the population density be Gaussian

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),\mathbf{\Sigma}(t)\bigr),\qquad\;t\in[0,1],

(97)

with boundary marginals
p_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})

E.1
Experiment Specifics

The entire experiment was performed on a standard laptop using only the CPU, without any GPU acceleration.
The objective
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QSB}}
serves as the loss function in our search for the optimal trajectory. In our two-dimensional "S-tunnel" and "V-neck" experiments, we fix the initial and final means of the population distributions at
t=0
and
t=1
, respectively. We introduce learnable parameters
\mu(t_{i})
and
\Sigma(t_{i})
for discrete time points
t_{i}=0,\mathrm{d}t,\ldots,1
. For trajectory learning, we use 100 time steps.

To prevent particles from passing through obstacles, we add a penalization term
\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, resulting in a total loss function of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QSB}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}~.

(105)

The hyperparameter
\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}
is chosen so that both terms in the loss are of comparable magnitude, which empirically yields the best performance (in our case
\lambda_{\text{obs}}=5000
).

In S-tunnel experiment initial population is given by
\mu(0)=(0,0)
and
\mu(1)=(20,0)
. Two elliptical obstacles are placed at
(6,-4.5)
and
(14,4.0)
, each with semi-axes
a=2.0
and
b=10.0
, and the domain is bounded within
[0,20]\times[-10,10]
.

In the U-tunnel experiment, the initial population is given by
\mu(0)=(0,0)
and
\mu(1)=(20,4)
. Two circular obstacles are placed at
(10,8.0)
and
(10,-4.0)
, each with semi-axes
a=5.0
and
b=5.0
, and the domain is bounded within
[0,20]\times[-10,10]
.

A collision-free reference path is generated via RRT*, reparametrized by arc length, and used to initialize the mean trajectory
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)
over
n=100
time steps. Both
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)

\log\Sigma(t)

We use
