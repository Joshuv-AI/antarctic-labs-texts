Genetic profiling of bacterial leaf blight resistance genes in Bangladeshi rice varieties
Authors: Md Shahidul Islam, Pengfei He, Pengbo He, Yixin Wu, Shahzad Munir, Yueqiu He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough bacterial leaf blight (BLB) poses a threat to rice yields in Bangladesh, locally validated resistance profiles are limited. This study addresses this gap by identifying key resistance genes and evaluating their distribution in widely cultivated rice varieties in the Mymensingh region of Bangladesh. This study aimed to identify bacterial leaf blight resistance genes (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21) present in rice varieties cultivated in Mymensingh, a prominent rice-producing region in Bangladesh.MethodsWe genotyped symptomatic leaves (n = 187) from 10 varieties at 14 locations in Mymensingh using diagnostic markers for Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, xXa13, and Xa21; selected amplicons were Sanger-sequenced and analyzed (NJ trees, 1,000 bootstraps).ResultsThe results revealed distinct resistance gene profiles: BRRI Dhan-49 presented the recessive gene Xa13; BRRI Dhan-51 possessed multiple resistance genes ( Xa2, Xa7, and Xa5); and BRRI Dhan-34 contained the Xa4, Xa5, and Xa7 genes. Compared with single-gene varieties, varieties characterized by different resistance genes demonstrated superior protection against bacterial blight, outperforming. Phylogenetic analyses revealed that the Bangladeshi varieties exhibit a close genetic relationship, reflecting a localized breeding history and adaptation, which distinguishes them from other international rice varieties.ConclusionsMulti-gene varieties (BRRI Dhan-51, Dhan-34) offer stronger field protection than single-gene materials, supporting gene pyramiding and germplasm diversification for durable BLB resistance in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07928-2.

Introduction

Bacterial leaf blight (BLB), caused by Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo), is a rice disease that inflicts significant yield losses on a global scale, particularly in Asian regions characterized by humid and warm climates, such as Bangladesh. This disease reduces photosynthetic capacity and can cause severe yield losses during epidemics [1], while Bangladesh’s warm, humid monsoon climate poses a persistent risk [2]. Mymensingh, a central rice-producing area with rainfed (T Aman) and irrigated (Boro) systems, experiences recurrent BLB outbreaks linked to high humidity, monsoon rainfall, and monoculture; local investigations have reported frequent disease and yield impacts, underscoring the need for region-tailored, sustainable solutions [3]. Genetic resistance preferably via stacked (pyramided) loci remains the most sustainable strategy for managing BLB in this context [4–6]. Rice production in tropical and subtropical regions continues to face BLB emergencies, with outbreaks documented across South and Southeast Asia [7, 8]. Several resistance (R) genes, including Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21, have been widely identified and utilized globally; these genes significantly increase bacterial blight resistance in various rice cultivars [7, 9–11]. However, the virulence of the local pathogen and its population affect its effectiveness. Selection and evolutionary pressures on pathogens can lead to rapid breakdown [12–15]. Recent advances, including genome-wide association studies (GWAS), genome editing, and CRISPR/Cas9, have accelerated the identification and validation of BLB resistance. In addition, marker-assisted selection (MAS) enables the routine introgression of multiple Xa loci into high-yielding backgrounds [16]. Despite this progress, translation to locally grown materials in Bangladesh is uneven, with farmer seed systems and admixture potentially obscuring varietal profiles.

Few comprehensive studies have focused on the genetic makeup and resistance profiles of locally adapted rice varieties. However, localized screening for varieties cultivated in specific agroecological environments remains limited. Existing research predominantly relies on hybrid varieties released nationally or commercially, without considering landrace varieties or farmer preferences. Unfortunately, there is insufficient integration among molecular diagnostics, field resistance genotyping methods, and genotype assessment. Furthermore, the diversity and evolution of Xoo resistance genes have yet to be adequately evaluated for their effectiveness due to a lack of alignment between resistance gene profiles and regional pathogens, rendering deployment more complex. Despite national releases carrying Xa loci, three uncertainties persist for Mymensingh, a major rice-growing zone in Bangladesh: (i) the current frequencies of key Xa genes in widely grown varieties under farmer seed systems; (ii) the spatial distribution of these genes across production subregions; and (iii) the field relevance of detected genes when viewed alongside observed BLB incidence/severity. Prior studies in South Asia have documented Xa effectiveness and Xoo virulence diversity [2, 3, 5], but area-specific, variety-focused molecular screens linked to field observations are scarce for Mymensingh, where admixture of farmer-saved seeds may blur formal varietal profiles.

To address this gap, this study aims to (i) determine the presence and geographical distribution of key bacterial blight resistance genes (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21) in rice varieties commonly grown in Mymensingh, Bangladesh; (ii) evaluate their field-level performance and associated disease severity; and (iii) assess phylogenetic relationships to understand local genetic diversity. The rationale for this study stems from the increasing prevalence of BLB outbreaks in Bangladeshi rice-growing regions, the economic losses faced by farmers, and the declining efficacy of chemical treatments. Although resistance genes are available globally, their effectiveness varies regionally depending on local Xoo pathotypes and farming methods. Currently, Mymensingh, a key rice-producing area in Bangladesh, lacks systematic molecular screening. This study aims to address this gap by providing scientific insights and practical guidance for sustainable rice breeding.

Materials and methods

Study areas

This study was conducted in Mymensingh, Bangladesh, a district renowned for its rice cultivation, from January 2023 to October 2024. Bacterial leaf blight is caused by Xanthomonas oryzae pv. Oryzae (Xoo), which thrives in favorable climatic conditions (high humidity and frequent rainfall). Mymensingh reported significant economic losses due to bacterial leaf blight in the past, highlighting the importance of examining disease resistance in locally cultivated rice varieties. This district is located between latitudes 24°15′ N and 25°10′ N and longitudes 90°15′ E and 90°45′ E. It is characterized by a humid subtropical climate influenced by monsoonal patterns, distinct dry and wet seasons, and varied terrains averaging 15 m above sea level.

Sample collection

Rice leaves were collected from 14 locations across Mymensingh (Table 1). The sampling locations were selected based on historically documented BLB incidence rates, farmer reports of disease prevalence, and the representativeness of popular rice varieties cultivated in the region. Leaf samples exhibiting typical symptoms of bacterial blight characterized by yellowing lesions along leaf margins, water-soaked streaks extending toward the midrib, and necrotic tips were collected for analysis (IRRI Standard Evaluation System, IRRI, 2013). A total of 187 symptomatic leaf samples representing 10 widely cultivated rice varieties were collected. Each leaf sample was individually placed in a sterile polyethylene bag, labeled precisely with the variety name and location coordinates, transported immediately on ice, and stored at 4 °C until DNA extraction. Although R-genes can be genotyped from any tissue, we sampled symptomatic plants to link field-confirmed BLB pressure (IRRI SES) with host genotypes and minimize off-type sampling; DNA was taken from lesion-adjacent green tissue to ensure host DNA quality.

We sampled symptomatic plants to confirm active BLB pressure and strengthen phenotype–genotype linkage while reducing off-type sampling in farmer saved seed systems; because Xa/xa assays target host DNA, this choice does not bias locus detectability but does not measure resistance in asymptomatic plants. Future work will pair randomized asymptomatic samples with controlled inoculations. Because BRRI varieties are genetically fixed at release but are distributed through heterogeneous farmer-managed seed systems, we used multilocation PCR profiles to assess the genetic purity of samples labeled with specific varietal names (BRRI Dhan-49, BRRI Dhan-51, BRRI Dhan-34) in the field rather than to imply that location itself alters Xa/Xa alleles.

Molecular identification

DNA extraction

Symptomatic leaf tissues were surface-sterilized using sterile distilled water and then blotted dry. DNA extraction was performed using a commercial plant DNA extraction kit (Favorgen Biotech Corporation, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, which has been previously validated for efficient genomic DNA extraction from rice leaves [17]. The DNA concentration and purity were evaluated via a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and confirmed via gel electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Screening for resistance genes

PCR-based genotyping was performed on all 187 symptomatic leaf samples collected, ensuring complete representation of the 10 rice varieties and 14 locations under study. These symptomatic rice leaf samples were genotyped via PCR assays targeting five bacterial leaf blight resistance genes (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21). We targeted these genes for (i) documented effectiveness in the South Asian/Bangladeshi germplasm, (ii) widely validated diagnostic primers (RM224, Xa5FM, X7, Xa13-promoter, pTA248), and (iii) complementary mechanisms supporting robust, comparable field genotyping across 14 sites. PCR amplification was conducted via gene-specific primers targeting five well-characterized bacterial leaf blight resistance genes—Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21 based on previously validated molecular markers reported in peer-reviewed literature [17–19]. This targeted approach ensures high specificity and reproducibility for each resistance locus, reducing the risk of false positives or amplification bias. Both functional markers (Xa21, Xa13) and validated SSRs (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7) were used. The functional markers provided gene-specific detection, while linked SSRs ensured robust amplification and comparability with earlier studies in the Bangladeshi germplasm. Table 2 summarizes the sequences of primers used for genotyping, their annealing temperatures, target loci, expected amplicon sizes, and diagnostic alleles. Additional SSR markers (K39512, RM27360, and RM7443) were included only for phylogenetic and diversity analyses, not for direct R-gene detection. K39512, RM27360, and RM7443 were used solely for phylogenetic and background diversity purposes and were not employed to detect Xa loci. Throughout, we report the detection of resistance-associated (diagnostic) alleles at Xa loci, not the confirmation of intact gene structure/expression.

To maintain experimental integrity and validate amplification fidelity, each PCR assay incorporated internal controls: (i) a positive control using IRBB60, a rice line known to harbor the target resistance genes, and (ii) a negative control containing nuclease-free water to rule out contamination. The reproducibility of amplification was confirmed across replicates to ensure consistent detection of each gene target (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Sequencing and cluster analysis

The PCR products were purified and Sanger sequenced to verify amplicon identity (assay specificity/QC). The sequences were aligned (ClustalW, MEGA v11), and a Neighbor-Joining dendrogram was constructed to visualize marker-based clustering; given the limited marker set, we do not interpret this as an evolutionary phylogeny. Sanger sequencing was chosen for its accuracy and reliability in targeted gene analysis [16]. The obtained sequences were aligned using ClustalW in MEGA v11 software [26], compared with reference sequences in GenBank (NCBI) via BLAST, and analyzed to construct phylogenetic trees using the Neighbor-Joining method with 1000 bootstrap replications [26]. Representative sequences were submitted to GenBank (Supplementary Table 1). To complement molecular identification, field-level observations of disease symptoms were recorded at the time of sampling, following the IRRI’s Standard Evaluation System [27]. Although not quantitative, these assessments provide a practical reference for correlating gene presence with resistance performance. This dual approach combining molecular screening with field observation ensures that gene detection is not only technically accurate but also contextually meaningful for field performance.

Disease severity scoring

Visual observations of the collected samples confirmed the presence of bacterial leaf blight. The IRRI Standard Evaluation System 2013 was utilized [27] to assess its severity in the field. It focuses on symptoms such as water-soaked streaks and yellowing leaf margins as indicators. Although no precise lesion length or incidence rate measurements were recorded, leaf samples representative of clearly symptomatic plant species were selected to verify the molecular detection of genes related to resistance correlated with symptoms under field conditions. Additionally, symptomatic presence was a qualitative indicator to guide sample selection and gene analyses. Significantly, this study relied on naturally infected symptomatic samples from field sites and did not include controlled inoculation trials with local Xoo isolates. While this approach captures field-relevant resistance responses, it limits the accuracy of evaluating host–pathogen interactions under uniform infection pressure. Symptom-anchored sampling enabled phenotype–genotype linkage at the plant/site level without compromising the validity of host genotyping.

Bioinformatics

The frequency of resistance genes was calculated for the 10 rice samples collected from 14 different locations. The PCR amplification result was recorded as binary presence (+), or absence (-), of each resistance gene. ClustalW was used to align Sanger sequencing data for selected PCR products in MEGA v11 [26]. BLASTn search results were performed in the NCBI GenBank to validate the amplified target genes. To illustrate the relationships between resistant alleles found in different varieties, phylogenetic trees were created via the Neighbor‒Joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replications.

Data analysis and statistics

We estimated detection frequencies for Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21 overall and by variety/location; differences by variety were tested with χ² (or Fisher’s exact when expected counts < 5), reporting Cramér’s V and 95% Wilson CIs, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (q = 0.05) for multiple comparisons. The sensitivity checks identified rare varieties (n < 10) and contrasted multigene (≥ 2) and zero/one-gene profiles; the results were consistent. For field observations (Table 4), we used two-proportion tests (incidence, Wilson CIs), Kruskal–Wallis (severity), and Spearman’s ρ between gene count and yield (t ha⁻¹), noting the observational, noncausal design. For phylogenetic alignment, ClustalW (MEGA v11) with neighbor-joining and 1,000 bootstraps (descriptive only) were used. Analyses were run in R v4.3.x (base statistics); CIs for proportions used Wilson intervals.

Results

Identification and distribution of resistance genes

PCR-based screening of 187 symptomatic rice leaf samples from 14 locations in Mymensingh revealed significant variability in the distribution of bacterial blight resistance genes (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, Xa21) across ten commonly cultivated rice varieties. Table 3 presents this gene-wide distribution, allowing a comparative view of resistance gene presence across genotypes. This structured format facilitates the identification of high-value varieties (BRRI Dhan-51, Dhan-34) for breeding programs, while highlighting susceptible varieties that lack target resistance loci. Notably, while BRRIs release these varieties as genetically uniform, farmer-saved seeds often undergo mixing, local adaptation, and seed replacement over time. This can introduce heterogeneity within the same-named variety, which may explain the detection of different R-gene profiles in samples labeled under the same varietal name. In this study, therefore, any variation in PCR allelic profiles under a common varietal name across locations is interpreted as evidence of seed lot heterogeneity or mislabeling in farmer seed systems rather than as location-driven changes in the underlying DNA segment. BRRI Dhan-49 exhibited the Xa13 (13.4% prevalence) resistance-specific allele, a gene previously reported to contribute to partial resistance by restricting pathogen-induced nutrient flow. BRRI Dhan-51 carried the most significant number of resistance-specific alleles at the targeted Xa loci (diagnostic alleles at Xa21, Xa7, and Xa5) among the tested varieties, including Xa21 (18.7%), Xa7 (11.8%), and Xa5 (9.1%). BRRI Dhan-34 revealed the Xa4 (6.4%), Xa5 (4.8%), and Xa7 (7.0%) loci. Notably, other varieties (BRRI Dhan-28, −29, −45, −58, −89, −92, and − 100) presented no amplification of these targeted resistance genes. Because markers target rice loci, sampling symptomatic plants does not bias R-gene detection; it provides context under documented field disease.

The data in Table 3 clearly demonstrate that only a subset of varieties carry multiple R genes, which correlates with a lower field-level disease burden. Notably, BRRI Dhan-28, 29, 58, and others exhibited no amplification of any of the tested resistance genes, indicating potential vulnerability. These findings emphasize the heterogeneity of resistance within current cultivation systems and the need for targeted varietal replacement or gene pyramiding strategies. Despite the absence of PCR amplification, some varieties may still be susceptible to resistance genes that were not tested in this study. Xa21 (known for its broad-spectrum resistance properties), particularly BRRI Dhan-51, was detected most often. This suggests that it may play a role in the current field resistance. The observed variation likely reflects farmer-level seed heterogeneity, varietal admixture, and local seed management practices, which should be carefully distinguished from the outputs of formal breeding programs. BRRIs Dhan-51 and BRRIs Dhan-34 could be promising sources for breeding resistance.

Geographical distribution of resistance genes

The geographical analysis revealed varying frequencies of resistance genes across the sampling locations (Fig. 1). BRRI Dhan-51 and BRRI Dhan-49 showed widespread geographical distributions, suggesting broader adoption or dissemination. However, these patterns must be interpreted with caution, as farmer-level seed heterogeneity may contribute to the apparent variability in R-gene profiles across sites rather than reflecting true genetic divergence within breeder-pure lines. BRRI Dhan-34 was primarily localized in Mymensingh’s northern and eastern regions, highlighting specific regional preferences or breeding practices.

Marker-based clustering (NJ dendrogram) of diagnostic amplicons

Marker-based clustering (using an NJ dendrogram) of diagnostic amplicons revealed a tight grouping among BRRI-developed materials, consistent with their shared breeding origins and regional adaptation. Because this analysis is based on a limited set of locus-specific amplicons/SSRs, it should be interpreted as a similarity dendrogram rather than an evolutionary phylogeny. Within this constraint, BRRI Dhan-51 and BRRI Dhan-34 (each with multiple resistance-specific alleles at Xa loci) clustered together, whereas BRRI Dhan-49 (Xa13 only) formed a nearby but distinct branch, reflecting differences in diagnostic allele stacks. The international accessions used as outgroups separated as expected, suggesting their utility for increasing diversity, but we do not infer evolutionary relationships. The results revealed distinct clustering patterns, highlighting the genetic relatedness among local varieties and their divergence from international rice accessions (Fig. 3). Supplementary SSR markers (K39512, RM27360, and RM7443) contributed to the assessment of background diversity but were not considered to be BLB resistance genes.

PCR amplification profiles of resistance genes

The PCR results were validated through gel electrophoresis visualization (Fig. 3). The amplification of distinct bands at ~ 150–800 bp, consistent with the expected amplicon lengths, confirmed the presence of resistance genes (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21) in the respective rice varieties. Each gel contained a 100 bp ladder (lane M), negatively controlled samples (nuclease-free water), and amplified rice samples. The expected band sizes were determined by published primer designs [17, 19]. The bands were considered positive only if they were consistent and reproducible in multiple replicates and matched the expected sizes. Representative PCR products for the Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21 assays were Sanger sequenced to confirm amplicon identity. BLASTn top hits corresponded to the intended rice loci with high identity and e-values of ~ 0, supporting assay specificity (see Supplementary Table S1). The accession numbers LC861695–LC861704 contain the deposited sequences referenced in the Data Availability statement. These data serve as amplicon-level QC rather than a separate comparative genomics analysis.

The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4) revealed that the Bangladeshi rice varieties formed a tight genetic cluster, suggesting a common breeding history with limited genetic diversity. BRRI Dhan-49, BRRI Dhan-51, and BRRI Dhan-34 clustered closely, indicating their shared resistance gene profiles, which may have originated from similar breeding programs. However, international varieties, such as those from China (DQ421395.1) and India (JN564603.1), displayed greater genetic divergence, implying their potential utility for introducing novel resistance genes into local breeding programs. These findings provide hope for the introduction of novel resistance genes into local breeding programs. Interestingly, BRRI Dhan-51 and BRRI Dhan-34, both of which carry multiple resistance genes (Xa21, Xa7, and Xa4), were positioned in a distinct subcluster, suggesting strong genetic stability and adaptability to bacterial blight pressures. In contrast, BRRI Dhan-49, possessing only Xa13, formed a separate branch, further supporting the need for gene pyramiding strategies to increase resistance durability. This study’s clustering of genetic markers aligns with previous studies that have demonstrated how homogeneity between local rice varieties may increase their vulnerability to pathogens [20, 28]. These findings indicate that breeders should utilize germplasms from distant populations to enhance resistance and yield stability under changing environmental conditions [29].

Field performance and disease incidence observations

To assess the practical relevance of resistance gene profiles, we compared field observations of disease incidence, severity, and yield across five selected varieties. Compared with single-allele or zero-allele lines, varieties carrying multiple resistance-specific alleles consistently presented lower disease burdens and higher yields (Table 4). This linkage offers practical value for agronomists and breeders seeking both disease resistance and productivity. Disease incidence and severity varied significantly by genotype, supporting the molecular results.

Based on farmer reports and observations made during the 2023 growing season, yield values and disease observations were calculated. Varieties such as BRRI Dhan 34 [30, 31] and BRRI Dhan 51 [32], which contain multiple resistance genes, are associated with fewer disease symptoms. These findings suggest that stacking genes may have an additive effect on field resistance. The higher yields of BRRIs Dhan-51 and Dhan-34 are consistent with the lower disease incidence observed, but further controlled trials are needed to confirm causality [31–33]. The observed resistance cannot be definitively linked to gene‒pathogen interactions owing to a lack of characterization of the pathogen strain and controlled inoculation. This underscores the need for further research to confirm the observed resistance and the potential for future studies in this area.

The integration of molecular tools (PCR, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis) with real-world field observations offers a comprehensive framework for evaluating resistance. The detection of a resistance-associated (diagnostic) allele at the Xa21/Xa7/Xa5 locus in varieties such as BRRI Dhan-51 was consistently associated with lower disease severity and higher yield in the field. Conversely, single-gene varieties such as BRRI Dhan-49, although genetically resistant (Xa13), demonstrated relatively high susceptibility in field settings, highlighting the limitation of relying on a single resistance mechanism. This highlights the methodological strength of this study: combining genotype profiling with agronomic performance yields insights relevant for both breeders and farmers.

Discussion

This research has successfully achieved the primary objective of identifying bacterial blight resistance genes in rice varieties cultivated in Mymensingh. The identification of five significant resistance genes (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21) notably enhances our understanding of the genetic basis of resistance to bacterial blight among local cultivars. While functional markers are available for all five Xa loci, we included linked SSRs because of their validation in local germplasms and reproducibility under field conditions. This balanced specificity with robustness and cross-study comparability. This knowledge is essential for the development of targeted and effective breeding strategies tailored to each specific region. The objectives of this study were to detect key resistance genes via PCR, confirm their identity by sequencing, and evaluate the performance of varieties that possess these genes. However, it is essential to recognize that strain-level pathogen characterization or controlled pathogenicity tests may alter the interpretation of these findings. Each resistance gene offers its own way to combat bacterial blight. R-genes are detectable from any tissue; our symptom-anchored design was chosen solely to strengthen the phenotype–genotype linkage under real BLB pressure. For example, Xa13 inhibits pathogen growth by interfering with sugar transport pathways initiated by pathogens [6]. In the long term, the single-gene resistance of BRRI Dhan-49 could present a threat, as virulent pathogens can quickly overcome it [34].

In contrast, multiple defense layers, such as Xa21 and Xa7 in BRRI Dhan51, offer broad-spectrum immunity through numerous lines of defense and can provide increased immunity through various lines of attack. Xa21 confers resistance against various agroecological conditions [35], whereas BRRI Dhan 34’s Xa4 enhances cell wall integrity and improves stability in stressful environments [10]. It is crucial to use pyramiding techniques to maximize their effectiveness [36]. Expression profiling was not used to verify gene function; instead, a literature review and the presence of markers were performed. Gene pyramiding is a strategy that works by delaying pathogen adaptation processes and diversifying recognition routes. Marker-assisted pyramiding of Xa loci has been implemented in India (PR106, Samba Mahsuri) and deepwater rice [12, 14, 37] and Taiwan (Tainung 82) [11] and across Bangladesh with pathotype profiling and multilocation phenotyping [33, 38–40]; reviews emphasize stacking Xa4/Xa5/Xa7/Xa21 for durability [7, 9, 20, 34], with genome editing as a complementary route [16]. Our findings map resistance-specific (diagnostic) alleles at Xa loci in locally grown Bangladeshi materials, as well as their spatial distribution.

Regional studies conducted in Bangladesh confirmed the high level of efficacy of Xa21 among various rice cultivars in Bangladesh [3, 39, 40]. Rashid et al. [33] confirmed that pyramiding Xa4, Xa5, and Xa7 could also be a practical approach for treatment. This is based on previous reports, which highlighted its importance [14]. These results show how gene pyramiding can help combat genetic erosion caused by dynamic pathogen populations while combating pathogen-driven pathogen erosion. Alam et al. [39] and Haque et al. [40] reported the detection of two resistance loci, the Xa21 and Xa7 genes, but our absence of either Xa10 or Xa23 differs from earlier surveys conducted in southern Bangladesh. The Bangladesh germplasms presented lower overall Xa diversity than Indian [37] and Vietnamese studies [41] did, suggesting the need to introduce additional resistance loci into their genetic material. For five varieties (Table 4), field indicators (incidence, severity, yield) were directionally consistent with marker status in 5/5 cases: multiple-allele lines (BRRI Dhan-51: Xa21, Xa7, Xa5; BRRI Dhan-34: Xa4, Xa5, Xa7) presented lower disease/higher yield, whereas single/zero-allele lines (BRRI Dhan-49: Xa13 only; BRRI Dhan-28/29: none) presented higher disease/lower yield. These are observational associations (no causal claims); future work will use standardized inoculations and multi season trials.

The phylogenetic analysis revealed that the Bangladeshi rice varieties clustered tightly genetically. This suggests that a homogenous gene pool likely results from localized breeding for disease resistance [26, 29]. This homogeneity in genetic composition presents both opportunities and challenges. While offering stable, region-adapted genetic backgrounds to allow rapid trait introduction, it also highlights vulnerability to evolving pathogens and emerging diseases [36]. To increase genetic diversity and disease resistance, diverse germplasms from distinct genetic pools should be incorporated into breeding programs [42]. The spatial and varietal patterns revealed herein demonstrate the utility of correlating molecular data with geographic and phenotypic assessments. Through visualizations of gene presence in discrete areas (Figs. 1 and 2) and varietal-level disease performance (Tables 3 and 4), this investigation offers a nuanced perspective on the distribution and efficacy of BLB resistance genes. This application permits region-specific breeding priorities and determines site-specific implementation of resistant varieties in accordance with localized pathogen loads and agroecological conditions. This data linkage of molecular diagnostic-field data has recently been emphasized in studies as essential for precision breeding and for sustainable rice production. The clustering can be attributed to the phylogeny being constructed using gene sequences, not the whole genome. These findings indicate that the current Bangladeshi resistance breeding program is based on a narrow gene base and the risk of uniform susceptibility increases.

Breeders, farmers, and policymakers benefit from identifying and characterizing resistance genes among local rice varieties. Under local agroecological conditions, combining Xa21 with additional loci such as Xa7 and Xa5 is expected to increase the durability of resistance [34, 35, 43]. Advanced genomic tools and technology, such as genome editing or whole-genome sequence analysis, can accelerate the creation of rice varieties resistant to current production challenges. Because the study did not evaluate pathogen virulence or the effects of genes on Xoo pathotypes, the effectiveness of the genes may differ. The integration of molecular detection, phylogenetic clustering, and field performance shows that multigene varieties (e.g., BRRI Dhan-51, Dhan-34) offer stable resistance, whereas single-gene varieties (e.g., BRRI Dhan-49) remain vulnerable. These results confirm the importance of pyramiding resistance genes and broadening genetic diversity through the incorporation of novel germplasms.

Conclusions

Research into the genes responsible for conferring resistance to bacterial blight in rice (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7, Xa13, and Xa21) provides valuable insights into their genetic basis and distribution. This study emphasized that rice varieties are predominantly cultivated in the Mymensingh region of Bangladesh. BRRI Dhan-51 (Xa21, Xa7, Xa5) and BRRI Dhan-34 (Xa4, Xa5, Xa7) are promising donor parents for pyramiding, whereas BRRI Dhan-49 (Xa13) illustrates the weakness of monogenic resistance. This study advocates a dual breeding strategy: pyramiding key genes to increase durability and integrating diverse germplasms to reduce genetic vulnerability, thereby strengthening BLB resistance and ensuring food security. A phylogenetic analysis of Bangladeshi rice strains revealed their vulnerability to pathogen adaptation and genetic erosion. This research strongly advocates diversifying genetic backgrounds through the integration of international germplasm into regional breeding programs to address this issue effectively.

These findings have significant practical implications, establishing a robust foundation for breeding strategies that prioritize sustainable rice production. Furthermore, they enable farmers to adopt disease-resistant varieties. This study advocates the implementation of advanced breeding methods, such as gene pyramiding and marker-assisted selection, to develop rice varieties that are resistant to bacterial blight. Future research should rigorously test the identified resistance genes under both real-world conditions and controlled inoculation with local Xoo strains, examine host–pathogen interactions at the molecular level, and explore novel genomic technologies, including genome editing, to ensure sustainable rice cultivation and food security in Bangladesh and similar regions.

This study relied on naturally infected field samples, so we lacked controlled inoculations with local Xoo and a randomized comparison between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals [44]; together with admixture in farmer-saved seeds, this may explain the intravarietal variability and spatial patterns. Next, we pair standardized challenge tests to strengthen genotype–phenotype attribution and investigate gene–environment interactions, virulence dynamics, and pathogen population genomics using whole-genome sequencing (WGS). We will also pursue functional validation of resistance via CRISPR/Cas and integrate randomized surveillance with symptom-anchored surveys to guide durable deployment.
