Do school students study enough about the reproductive system? Data from Jordan
Authors: Soha Albeitawi, Zina M. Al-Alami, Dima Hattab, Wassan Jarrar, Ayham Alrbeiqi, Dema Jabata, Garavaglia Elisabetta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding reproductive anatomy and physiology is essential for safe wealthy sexual practices as well as reproductive attitudes. It has been advised that comprehensive sexual reproductive health (SRH) education should be provided in educational systems, colleges, and communities. We aimed to explore the knowledge about the reproductive system among first-year students in non-medical health schools.MethodA pre-validated questionnaire was distributed among first year non-medical health care school students in two universities.ResultsA number of 384 students were included. The average score of knowledge was 14.12 out of 42, Female students scored higher than males (15.06 versus 11.54).ConclusionFrom the results of the current study, it appears that Jordanian students do not study enough about reproductive systems. It is recommended that the school curricula in the fields about reproductive systems, need to be reassessed and reformed, teachers need to be trained for better teaching strategies about reproductive subjects as well.

Introduction

Sexual health knowledge is necessary for good health and well-being [1]. Therefore, understanding sexual reproductive health (SRH) is essential for safe wealthy sexual practices as well as reproductive attitudes. The education process starts at school. Family, peers, clinics, and technology are important sources for SRH education [2].

It has been advised that comprehensive sexual reproductive health (SRH) education should be provided in educational systems, colleges, and communities in order to enhance the understanding of human reproduction among various populations [3].

The implementation of sexual reproductive health (SRH) education is inconsistent worldwide and many young people miss out on school-based SRH education [4]. Accordingly, young people begin their early reproductive life with little understanding of SRH which has an impact on overall health and well-being [5, 6]. It has been reported that students are dissatisfied with how SRH was taught to them in school, which ultimately affects their SRH knowledge and behaviors [7, 8].

The components of SRH education programs differ among different areas. SRH education depends on several factors such as regional policies and the curricula supplied for a particular area [9]. In Jordan, school-based SRH education varies depending on the secondary school program. The most advanced SRH education is provided in the scientific programs and involves reproductive system anatomy and physiology.

Up to our knowledge, the outcome of school education regarding reproductive health in Jordan has not been evaluated previously. In this study, it is aimed to explore if school education provides adequate knowledge about reproductive system physiology and anatomy, by exploring the knowledge and beliefs about reproductive systems’ anatomy and physiology among first year university students.

Material and methods

Population

The target in the current study was first-year students in non-medical healthcare schools from two private universities in Jordan.

Ethical approval and informed consent

Al-Ahliyya Amman University Ethical Committee officially approved the study (No: AAU/1/4/2023–2024). The participants were asked to fill out the electronic google form of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was distributed among the students, through anatomy course lectures during the second term of the academic year 2023–2024. The questionnaire started with an informed consent to participate before answering the questions.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed by the authors after an extensive search of the scientific databases and reviewing high-school biology curricula. The questionnaire was both in Arabic and English languages. (English Version is uploaded with the manuscript).

The questionnaire consisted of five parts. The first part was about demographic information, the second part included 19 questions regarding the female reproductive system, the third section contained 16 questions about the male reproductive system, the fourth part involved 7 questions regarding fertilization, and the fifth part asked the respondents about their sources of information. Each correct answer was scored one point with an overall score of 42. Participants who scored ≥ 50% were considered to have good knowledge. Otherwise, they were considered to have poor knowledge [10].

The questionnaire has been validated by three consultants and academics in the field of reproduction. A pilot of 30 participants were asked for the intelligibility and the clarity of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was refined as recommended then the reliability of the questions was tested using Cronbach’s alpha using SPSS, and it was deemed to be reliable (Cronbach’s alpha value is 0.95).

Sample size

The number of first year students in both universities was 800, at a margin of error (5%) and confidence interval (95%) the required sample size was 280 [10]

Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 26.0; SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL) for windows. Data were expressed as Mean, Standard Deviation (SD), and frequencies. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical data two-tailed independent t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to detect the difference between two or more independent groups, respectively. Results were statistically significant once the observed significance level (p-value) was less than 0.05.

Results

The structured questionnaire was sent to the first-year students in the non-medical health schools. Out of 429 responses received, 384 participants signed the consent form and answered the questionnaire. Forty-five responses were excluded as they declined to participate.

Demographic data

Out of 384 respondents, 292 students were from the school of pharmacy, 85 were from the allied medical sciences school, and the other 7 participants were from either nursing or rehabilitation schools. The majority of the respondents were females aged about 20 years old, single, and residents in Jordan. Assessment of participants’ awareness of the female reproductive system

Students’ responses to each question about the female reproductive system were included in Table 5. A large proportion of the students have good knowledge about the questions concerned with the anatomy of the female reproductive system. In contrast, participants showed poor knowledge about its physiology. More than 60% of the students were able to name one of the hormones secreted from the ovaries and those that are responsible for developing female sexual signs, ovulations, and regulations of the ovarian and menstrual cycles. Almost 40% of them recognized the hormones that are secreted from the pituitary gland or corpus luteum; either Luteinizing hormone (LH) or follicle stimulating hormone (FSH). Assessment of participants’ knowledge about the fertilization

Students’ knowledge about the fertilization process was inadequate. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the knowledge of students about the anatomy and physiology of male and female reproductive systems. Our target was first-year university students in order to assess the outcome of high school education in this field. The overall knowledge about reproductive systems was unsatisfactory (overall score, 14.12 out of 42). However, the best knowledge was about the female reproductive tract (7.7 out of 19 (40.5%)), followed by the information about fertilization (2 out of 7 (28.6%)) then about the male reproductive tract (4.3 out of 16 (26.9%)). The knowledge of female students about the female reproductive system, the male reproductive system, and fertilization was significantly higher than male students. This could be attributed to that females are more likely to talk about, study, or explore reproductive-related issues because they have menstruation monthly, and this could be the reason for them being encouraged to learn more about reproductive health.

Numerous studies explored the sexual health knowledge, attitudes, needs, anatomy, and physiology of reproductive systems. However, none of them assessed the knowledge about anatomy and physiology in detail. This study primarily aimed to assess the educational outcome of the school curriculum among the students. The students in the secondary stages are taught about the anatomy and physiology of the reproductive systems. While contraception, sexual behavior, and needs are not included in the curriculum.

The current results were consistent with the Sydsjo G. et. al. study which revealed inappropriate knowledge among Sweden students [11]. As well, female participants showed higher awareness. In contrast to our results, older students scored higher than other students. This could be explained by the wide age distribution of the participants in the Sydsjo G. et. al. study which included students from grade 8, upper secondary school, and undergraduates. They found that 24% and 63% of male and female participants respectively answered correctly the time of ovulation, while 22.9% did in our study. Moreover, 20% knew that fertilization takes place in the fallopian tube which is much less than the findings of this study as almost half of the participants of our study (43%) knew that fertilization takes place in the fallopian tube. Additionally, the level of knowledge about sexual hormones was low, similar to the findings of our study. The most frequently used source of information was school similar to what we found however, friends were the second most common source of information in comparison to social media in our study [11].

Another study was conducted by Gaferi et. al. and assessed the knowledge and practice related to reproductive health among 350 female adolescents in secondary schools in Saudi Arabia. The Gaferi et al. study also showed an inaccurate knowledge regarding the female reproductive system among female participants [12].

Other studies showed contradictory results. The knowledge of ovulation, menstrual cycle, and associated reproductive changes was explored among 125 women in childbearing age (18–51 years) as a part of larger “Women Health Promotion Program”. Almost 80% of participants were aware of the anatomy of the reproductive system [13].

This study showed that the main source of students’ knowledge was school education followed by the social media. These findings were comparable to the Sydsjo study that reported that school education and friends are the main source of information [11]. As well, Maenzius M. and his colleagues explored the knowledge, behavior, and attitude toward sexual and reproductive health among 192 male adolescent (18 years old) in the third year attending three upper secondary school. Their study mentioned that the main source of knowledge was secondary school (grade6 to grade 9)- not upper secondary- followed by friends [14]. In contrast, Shahrazad Rahimi-Naghani et al. studied the sexual and reproductive health knowledge among 755 men and women aged 15–49 in Tehran, using the WHO endorsed questionnaire of sexual and reproductive health and their study revealed that the main source of knowledge was friends. [3]

The major drawback of this study is that it lacks assessing the knowledge, attitudes, and needs about sexual health. This could be our coming study to assess the impact of knowledge obtained from school and university on sexual beliefs, attitudes and behavior.

Because of the inappropriate knowledge of first-year students about the reproductive system, programs concerned with the continuous education of students should be implemented. This study highlights also the need for training lessons targeted to the teachers and academic staff in schools and universities. However, developing an evaluation strategy to ensure the effectiveness of these continuous education programs and training lessons is the most important step that has to be implemented. Moreover, family doctors should be encouraged to talk with adolescent patients about reproductive issues.
