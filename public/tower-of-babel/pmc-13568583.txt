Pretherapeutic radiological sarcopenia in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: a narrative review
Authors: Myriam Ayari, Sarah Ben Azouz, Amira Chehaider, Taieb Jomni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the deadliest solid malignancies despite advances in surgery, perioperative care, and systemic therapies. Pretherapeutic radiological sarcopenia has emerged as a clinically relevant body-composition phenotype associated with poor outcomes in oncology. This imaging-based concept differs from contemporary geriatric definitions of sarcopenia, which require impaired muscle strength and incorporate low muscle quantity and/or quality. This narrative review critically examines radiological assessment, biological context, prognostic implications, artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled quantification, and interventional evidence in PDAC. A structured narrative search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar was conducted from database inception through July 31, 2026. Computed tomography (CT)-based skeletal muscle index remains the most widely validated imaging biomarker, although substantial heterogeneity persists in methods and diagnostic thresholds. Low muscle mass is most consistently associated with reduced survival, whereas associations with postoperative morbidity and systemic treatment toxicity are more heterogeneous. Exercise, nutritional, and multimodal cachexia interventions appear feasible and may provide functional or nutritional benefits, but evidence remains insufficient to establish a survival benefit from improving muscle status. AI-based approaches enable scalable body-composition analysis, although external validation, methodological standardization, and prospective clinical integration remain necessary. Overall, radiological sarcopenia is a promising prognostic and potentially actionable phenotype in PDAC.Keywords: Pancreatic adenocarcinoma, sarcopenia, body composition, computed tomography, artificial intelligence, survival

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for the great majority of pancreatic malignancies and remains among the most lethal gastrointestinal cancers. Its incidence has increased steadily over recent decades, while prognosis remains poor because of late diagnosis, early metastatic dissemination, and limited long-term efficacy of available therapies. Only a minority of patients present with resectable disease, whereas most have locally advanced or metastatic tumors at diagnosis [1–4].

This clinical context has driven interest in biomarkers that capture not only tumor burden but also host vulnerability. Among these, sarcopenia has emerged as a clinically relevant marker in PDAC. An important terminology issue should therefore be made explicit. In the aging field, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) considers low muscle strength the primary feature of sarcopenia, with low muscle quantity or quality confirming the diagnosis and poor physical performance indicating severe disease [5]. In oncology, by contrast, many PDAC studies use the term ‘radiological sarcopenia’ for CT-defined low muscle quantity without a contemporaneous strength or performance assessment. We retain that terminology because it reflects the literature reviewed. Sarcopenia and low muscle mass also overlap with, but are not synonymous with, cancer cachexia [6–8].

The appeal of pretherapeutic radiological assessment lies in its availability on routine staging CT and in its potential to enrich risk stratification across treatment settings. Several reviews and meta-analyses already establish an association between low muscle mass and adverse outcomes in PDAC. The added value of the present review is therefore not to restate that association, but to reconcile apparently conflicting evidence across surgical and systemic-treatment settings, distinguish radiological low muscle mass from consensus sarcopenia, compare SMI with psoas-based metrics in the era of automated segmentation, quantify the current performance of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted tools, and critically appraise whether intervention trials justify translating body-composition screening into a clinical pathway.

Methods

This work was designed as a narrative, nonsystematic review intended to summarize and critically interpret the current evidence on pretherapeutic radiological sarcopenia in PDAC. A narrative approach was chosen as literature is highly heterogeneous regarding imaging definitions, cutoffs, treatment settings, patient populations, and study endpoints.

Literature search strategy

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar from database inception to July 31, 2026. The core search combined: (‘pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma’ OR PDAC OR ‘pancreatic adenocarcinoma’ OR ‘pancreatic cancer’) AND (sarcopenia OR ‘skeletal muscle’ OR ‘skeletal muscle index’ OR SMI OR ‘total psoas area’ OR TPAI OR myosteatosis OR ‘muscle attenuation’ OR ‘body composition’ OR cachexia) AND (CT OR ‘computed tomography’ OR MRI OR radiological OR imaging OR ‘artificial intelligence’ OR ‘deep learning’). Targeted supplementary searches combined the pancreatic-cancer terms with (inflammation OR cytokine OR IL-6 OR TNF OR metabolism OR ‘pancreatic exocrine insufficiency’ OR malnutrition) for biological mechanisms, and with (exercise OR prehabilitation OR nutrition OR ‘oral nutritional supplement’) for interventions.

Study selection

Studies were selected based on their clinical relevance and methodological contribution to the understanding of sarcopenia in PDAC. Eligible studies included observational cohorts, retrospective or prospective studies, clinical trials reporting body-composition outcomes, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses evaluating radiological sarcopenia or related imaging-based muscle parameters in PDAC.

Methodological considerations

As a narrative review, this study does not adhere to PRISMA guidelines and does not include formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analytic pooling. Nevertheless, priority was given to high-quality studies, large cohorts, and recent meta-analyses to ensure robustness and clinical relevance. Discrepancies between studies were highlighted and discussed to emphasize current knowledge gaps and the need for standardized sarcopenia definitions in pancreatic cancer research.

Biological context of pretherapeutic muscle depletion in PDAC

Inflammation and catabolic signaling

Pretherapeutic muscle depletion in PDAC is biologically plausible, yet it should not be presented as a single causal pathway. Tumor and host-derived inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1, interleukin-6, interleukin-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, can activate proteolytic programs, suppress anabolic signaling, and impair myogenic regeneration [9,10]. Human PDAC cachexia is heterogeneous and circulating inflammation, reduced intake, malabsorption, altered metabolism, and disease burden often coexist. Radiological low muscle mass should therefore be interpreted as an integrated host phenotype rather than a direct readout of one cytokine pathway [11].

Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency and maldigestion

Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI) is particularly relevant in PDAC because maldigestion can compound reduced intake and systemic catabolism. Shintakuya et al. reported a close association between pancreatic exocrine insufficiency and sarcopenia in patients with pancreatic disease [12]. This relationship provides a clinically actionable link: symptoms of steatorrhea, weight loss, or maldigestion should prompt assessment and pancreatic enzyme replacement when indicated.

Metabolic dysregulation and systemic host effects

PDAC is also associated with systemic metabolic dysregulation. Patterson et al. described a pancreatic cancer-induced metabolic dysregulation syndrome characterized by new-onset diabetes, insulin resistance, and altered substrate utilization involving the liver, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and pancreatic islets [13]. These systemic effects may accelerate muscle depletion and help explain why CT body composition captures a mixture of nutritional impairment, inflammatory burden, and tumor-host interaction rather than caloric intake alone.

Mechanical factors and reduced oral intake

Mechanical factors contribute as well. Depending on tumor location and stage, PDAC may lead to gastric outlet obstruction, pain, early satiety, nausea, vomiting, or biliary and duodenal compression, all of which can reduce oral intake and aggravate nutritional decline [14]. Surgical procedures may further accentuate these constraints in the perioperative period. These anatomical and functional limitations should therefore be regarded as part of the biological model of sarcopenia in pancreatic cancer rather than as mere epiphenomena.

Radiological assessment of sarcopenia

Skeletal muscle index

Radiological assessment of sarcopenia in PDAC is usually performed on baseline CT, which is already part of standard disease staging. This opportunistic use of routine imaging is a major strength of CT-based body-composition analysis. Among available methods, the skeletal muscle index (SMI) is the most widely used and remains the most comprehensive imaging-based marker. It is derived from total skeletal muscle area, usually measured at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) and normalized to height [8,15]. No single sex-specific cutoff has been universally adopted across pancreatic cancer studies, which complicates direct comparison between cohorts [16].

Total psoas area index

The total psoas area index (TPAI) was historically faster to obtain manually. It was therefore widely adopted in retrospective studies, particularly where dedicated body-composition software was unavailable. That workflow advantage is diminishing. TPAI samples only the psoas muscles and may not reflect whole-abdominal muscle quantity, whereas modern automated pipelines can quantify the complete L3 muscle compartment with minimal additional operator time [15,17]. TPAI should therefore be viewed as a pragmatic historical surrogate rather than a preferred contemporary metric when validated automated SMI analysis is available.

Total psoas volume, MRI, and emerging AI-based segmentation

Other imaging approaches have also been explored. Total psoas volume may reduce dependence on a single axial slice, but its additional processing burden historically limited use [17]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based body-composition analysis is feasible, yet it remains less standardized than CT in routine PDAC care. AI-assisted CT analysis is now more mature. Gunesch et al. validated the automated AutoMATiCA pipeline in 525 CT scans from patients with pancreatic cancer; automated and manual skeletal muscle cross-sectional area showed strong agreement (R2 = 0.94), although the automated method systematically overestimated muscle area by approximately 5.7% [18]. A correction factor was subsequently tested in an independent cohort of 36 patients with non-PDAC peripancreatic malignancies. Keyl et al. then applied deep-learning-derived volumetric body-composition measurements in a multicenter cohort of 601 patients with advanced PDAC from three German centers, showing that automated muscle and myosteatosis phenotypes retained prognostic information for overall survival [19].

More recent technical work further illustrates the achievable segmentation performance. Ahmed et al. developed the uncertainty-aware SMAART-AI pipeline; the segmentation model achieved a Dice similarity coefficient of 97.80% ± 0.93% in its gastroesophageal validation dataset, while across four cancer cohorts including pancreatic cancer, the median absolute deviation in skeletal muscle area from expert manual annotation was 2.48% [20]. Uncertainty metrics also tracked larger segmentation errors. These results support scalability, yet they do not establish interchangeability across scanners, contrast phases, reconstruction kernels, ethnic groups, or external PDAC populations. Automated output should therefore retain quality-control mechanisms and requires prospective validation before body-composition measurements are used to alter treatment decisions.

Muscle quality and myosteatosis

In addition to muscle quantity, muscle quality is increasingly recognized as clinically relevant. CT-derived muscle attenuation provides an indirect estimate of intramuscular fat infiltration and is commonly used as a marker of myosteatosis. This is important because some patients have relatively preserved muscle area but poor muscle quality and still experience unfavorable outcomes. Recent studies and meta-analytic data suggest that myosteatosis may complement muscle area measurements and refine prognostic stratification in pancreatic cancer [21,22].

Why study results differ: cutoffs, ethnicity, disease stage, and metrics

One of the main reasons the literature is difficult to interpret is the lack of universally accepted cutoffs. Reported prevalence varies according to sex, ethnicity, imaging metric, threshold choice, disease stage, and treatment setting [16,23]. This heterogeneity is not merely technical. It directly influences prevalence estimates, effect sizes, and apparent prognostic power. Accordingly, when studies report divergent findings, methodological nonequivalence may be as important as true biological differences.

Prevalence and clinical impact of pretherapeutic radiological sarcopenia across treatment settings

Prevalence of pretherapeutic radiological sarcopenia

Pretherapeutic CT-defined low muscle mass is common in PDAC. Across studies, estimates generally range from approximately 40% to 73%, with a recent meta-analysis reporting an overall pooled prevalence of about 45% [23,24]. It is consistently associated with poorer survival across treatment settings. Its relationship with postoperative morbidity is heterogeneous, although the most recent pancreaticoduodenectomy meta-analysis suggests increased major complication and mortality risks [25]. Prospective chemotherapy data and a 2026 systematic review also strengthen the association between adverse body composition and systemic-treatment vulnerability [26,27]. These findings support opportunistic body-composition assessment as a prognostic and supportive-care biomarker, not as a stand-alone reason to deny surgery, reduce chemotherapy, or alter oncologic treatment.

Postoperative morbidity and mortality

The association between pretherapeutic low muscle mass and postoperative morbidity after pancreatic surgery remains heterogeneous. Earlier systematic reviews found no consistent association with individual complications such as pancreatic fistula, delayed gastric emptying, or postoperative bleeding [28,29]. More recent pooled evidence is less neutral. He et al. included 30 studies and 5,323 patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy and reported higher risks of major complications, postoperative pancreatic fistula, biliary fistula, and postoperative mortality among patients classified as sarcopenic [25]. Recent studies have further refined the prognostic role of body composition after pancreatic resection. Siegel et al. found that preoperative sarcopenia and myosteatosis were mainly associated with early mortality after PDAC resection, whereas their prognostic influence became less apparent over longer follow-up [30]. Similarly, Dai et al., in a multicenter cohort, showed that low SMI contributed to the prediction of early recurrence or death after upfront surgery when combined with tumor-related factors [31]. These findings suggest that muscle depletion may be particularly useful for identifying patients with limited physiological reserve before surgery rather than acting as an isolated determinant of long-term outcome.

Neoadjuvant therapy

Neoadjuvant treatment is increasingly used in resectable and borderline resectable PDAC, which has focused attention on body-composition changes during therapy. Studies suggest that baseline sarcopenia remains common before neoadjuvant treatment and may be associated with poorer long-term outcomes [32–34]. At the same time, changes in muscle mass during neoadjuvant therapy seem to vary according to treatment response; patients ultimately resected may maintain or regain muscle, whereas those who progress or remain unresectable may lose further muscle mass [34]. This suggests that sarcopenia is not simply a static baseline variable, but part of a dynamic process shaped by both tumor biology and treatment response.

Adjuvant and palliative systemic therapy

The evidence linking low muscle quantity and quality to systemic-treatment vulnerability is evolving. Earlier retrospective cohorts reported higher rates of major, particularly hematologic, toxicity during gemcitabine-based or multi-agent chemotherapy [35–37]. Emori et al. prospectively studied 66 patients with unresectable PDAC receiving first-line gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel; 21 met the study definition of sarcopenia, which was associated with shorter progression-free and overall survival and a higher rate of grade 3–4 adverse events [26]. Zhang et al. reported that sarcopenia was independently associated with shorter progression-free and overall survival in patients with unresectable PDAC receiving chemotherapy, while myosteatosis was also independently related to overall survival [38]. Lee et al. showed that muscle attenuation and longitudinal body-composition changes provided prognostic information in metastatic pancreatic cancer, suggesting that muscle quality and its evolution during treatment may be as relevant as baseline muscle mass alone [39]. Chacón-Ovejero et al., in a 2026 systematic review of CT-derived body composition and chemotherapy outcomes in PDAC, concluded that SMI, muscle density, and adipose compartments may capture toxicity risk not reflected by body-surface-area dosing, while emphasizing the need for prospective trials before body-composition-guided dosing can be recommended [27]. In this review, ‘treatment vulnerability’ is used as an umbrella term encompassing toxicity, dose reduction or interruption, impaired treatment completion, and reduced functional reserve; systemic toxicity is one component of that broader construct.

Stereotactic body radiotherapy

In patients undergoing stereotactic body radiotherapy, limited but relevant evidence suggests that low TPAI may be associated with greater acute toxicity [40]. Although this evidence is less extensive than the chemotherapy literature, it fits with the broader concept that sarcopenia captures reduced physiologic resilience rather than treatment-specific vulnerability alone.

Survival across treatment settings

The most reproducible clinical association remains reduced survival. Láinez Ramos-Bossini et al. synthesized CT-defined sarcopenia across treatment settings and highlighted an adverse survival signal alongside substantial heterogeneity in definition and treatment context [23]. He et al. reported similarly adverse disease-free and overall survival after pancreaticoduodenectomy in their updated meta-analysis [25]. Emori et al. added prospective evidence in first-line gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel-treated unresectable PDAC [26]. Taken together, the survival signal is stronger than evidence for any single postoperative complication and supports radiological low muscle mass primarily as a prognostic biomarker rather than proof of a modifiable causal mechanism. Recent large cohorts further reinforce the view that radiological sarcopenia should be interpreted within a broader host phenotype. Wijsman et al., in 1,666 patients with PDAC, found that AI-derived muscle quality and sarcopenic obesity were associated with mortality across different treatment settings [41]. Hildebrand et al. similarly showed that combining low muscle mass, myosteatosis, and systemic inflammation identified patients with markedly poorer survival after pancreatic or periampullary cancer resection [42]. Together, these studies support moving beyond muscle area alone toward integrated assessment of muscle quantity, quality, and systemic inflammation.

Special considerations

Sarcopenic obesity

Sarcopenic obesity deserves specific attention because it may identify a subgroup at particularly high risk. Several studies suggest that the coexistence of low muscle mass and excess adiposity is more strongly associated with postoperative complications, pancreatic fistula, and mortality than sarcopenia alone [29,43,44]. This is biologically plausible because sarcopenic obesity combines impaired muscle reserve with a metabolically active and pro-inflammatory adipose compartment. It may also explain why muscle quantity alone is sometimes insufficient for risk prediction.

Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic implications

The relationship between sarcopenia and chemotherapy toxicity involves both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms. Most cytotoxic regimens are dosed according to body surface area, which does not accurately reflect lean body mass. In sarcopenic patients, the effective exposure to chemotherapy per unit of metabolically active tissue may therefore be higher. Reduced protein reserves, inflammation, altered hepatic metabolism, and lower functional reserve may further amplify toxicity [9,33,45]. From a pharmacodynamic perspective, sarcopenia may also indicate diminished capacity to tolerate hematologic, gastrointestinal, and constitutional adverse effects.

Clinical implications and intervention evidence

Supportive-care implications

The current clinical value of radiological low muscle mass lies less in excluding patients from treatment than in flagging potentially reversible contributors to vulnerability. Baseline CT information can be integrated with weight trajectory, nutritional screening, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, inflammatory markers, performance status, and treatment intent. This may trigger dietitian review, pancreatic enzyme replacement when clinically indicated, individualized exercise or rehabilitation, and closer toxicity monitoring. Importantly, these supportive actions are justified by their own clinical indications. The evidence does not yet show that a CT-defined sarcopenia threshold should automatically change chemotherapy dose or surgical candidacy. This supportive-care framework raises an important question: can interventions targeting muscle loss improve clinically meaningful outcomes? Several trials have explored exercise, nutritional, perioperative, and multimodal strategies.

Exercise interventions

Exercise evidence is encouraging but not definitive. Wiskemann et al. randomized 65 patients with pancreatic cancer to supervised progressive resistance training, home-based resistance training, or usual care; 43 patients were analyzed, and selected strength and workload measures improved without a clear effect on body weight [46]. Ngo-Huang et al., in the PancFit randomized trial of 151 patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, found that both enhanced usual care and prescribed aerobic-plus-resistance exercise groups improved six-minute walk distance, while quality of life and clinical outcomes did not clearly differ between randomized arms [47]. Sekino et al. pooled six pancreatic-cancer exercise intervention studies in 2025 and found improvements in physical quality of life and muscle strength, but emphasized high risk of bias and limited evidence for other endpoints [48].

Perioperative and nutritional interventions

Perioperative and nutritional trials provide similarly mixed evidence. Ausania et al. randomized 40 patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy to standard care or multimodal prehabilitation combining nutritional support, management of diabetes and pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, and physical/respiratory training. Overall and major complication rates were not reduced, although delayed gastric emptying was less frequent in the intervention arm [49]. Uemura et al. randomized 60 patients with advanced pancreatic cancer receiving chemotherapy to early elemental-diet supplementation or usual diet. The intervention attenuated body mass index decline and preserved handgrip strength, but did not clearly change SMI [50]. This single-center study also reported improved response and overall survival, findings that require independent confirmation.

Multimodal and pharmacological interventions

Multimodal cachexia trials provide important insights into anti-inflammatory strategies, although evidence specifically derived from patients with PDAC remains limited. Solheim et al. reported the phase III MENAC trial in patients with advanced lung or pancreatic cancer receiving systemic therapy. The program combined nutritional counseling and fish-oil-containing supplements, endurance and resistance exercise, and a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug. The intervention stabilized weight at six weeks but did not clearly improve muscle mass or physical activity [51]. Mitsunaga et al. randomized 131 older patients with pancreatic or non-small-cell lung cancer in NEXTAC-TWO to a 12-week nutrition-and-exercise program or control; adherence was high, but disability-free survival and secondary endpoints did not clearly differ [52]. Because these trials combine tumor types and interventions, they do not establish that anti-inflammatory medication reverses radiological sarcopenia in PDAC. At present, there is no validated pharmacological standard specifically for CT-defined sarcopenia in pancreatic cancer.

Discussion

Critical interpretation of the evidence

Several important conclusions emerge from the updated evidence. First, most prognostic studies remain retrospective and observational, which limits causal inference. Radiological low muscle mass may contribute biologically to vulnerability, but it may also serve as a surrogate for frailty, nutritional impairment, systemic inflammation, advanced tumor burden, or combinations of these factors. Second, the measurement itself is not uniform. SMI, TPAI, muscle attenuation, volumetric metrics, and population-specific cutoffs classify partially overlapping patient groups [16,23,24]. Third, the 2025–2026 literature strengthens rather than resolves these uncertainties: recent meta-analyses support adverse survival and perioperative associations [23,25], while prospective chemotherapy data and a new systematic review support treatment-vulnerability associations [26,27]. The consistency of association is therefore increasing, but evidence that the imaging phenotype is directly modifiable in a way that improves oncologic outcomes remains limited.

Finally, emerging metrics such as myosteatosis and AI-derived body-composition features may explain part of the residual heterogeneity. Keyl et al. showed prognostic value for fully automated volumetric body-composition phenotypes in patients with advanced PDAC [19]. Gunesch et al. and Ahmed et al. demonstrated high agreement between automated and expert muscle measurements, while also documenting systematic bias and the need for uncertainty-based quality control [18,20]. Automation makes comprehensive SMI and muscle-quality assessment increasingly feasible, but clinical deployment should be separated conceptually from evidence that acting on those measurements changes outcomes.

Limitations

This narrative review has limitations. The search was structured but nonsystematic and did not use formal PRISMA selection, duplicate screening, risk-of-bias scoring, or quantitative pooling. Relevant studies may therefore have been missed. The underlying literature is itself heterogeneous in disease stage, treatment era, ethnicity, CT acquisition, muscle metric, cutoff definition, and covariate adjustment. Many oncology studies define radiological ‘sarcopenia’ from muscle quantity alone and do not assess strength or physical performance, limiting comparability with EWGSOP2-defined sarcopenia. Publication bias and residual confounding remain likely, particularly because low muscle mass tracks disease severity, cachexia, inflammation, and malnutrition. Intervention trials are small, heterogeneous, and often include mixed cancer populations; few use reversal of CT-defined sarcopenia as a prespecified primary endpoint. Finally, AI segmentation studies report strong technical performance, but external validation, prospective workflow studies, and evidence that automated measurements improve clinical decisions remain insufficient.

Future directions

Several priorities emerge from the updated literature. First, studies should separate CT-defined low muscle mass from consensus sarcopenia and incorporate strength or performance when feasible. Second, harmonized sex- and population-specific thresholds and standardized CT acquisition/analysis are needed. Third, muscle quality should be integrated with muscle quantity, because myosteatosis may provide complementary prognostic information. Fourth, automated segmentation should be externally and prospectively validated with transparent reporting of Dice coefficients, measurement agreement, failure rates, and quality-control procedures. Finally, interventional trials should prespecify muscle quantity, muscle quality, strength, treatment completion, toxicity, and patient-centered outcomes, and should test whether CT-triggered multimodal care improves outcomes rather than merely whether sarcopenia predicts them.

Conclusion

Pretherapeutic radiological sarcopenia is common in PDAC and is consistently associated with poorer survival and greater treatment vulnerability across disease stages. Its relationship with postoperative complications is less consistent and appears to depend on the metric used, cutoff definition, patient population, and the coexistence of adverse body-composition phenotypes such as sarcopenic obesity and myosteatosis. The field is now moving from manual measurement toward scalable automated phenotyping, and the historical convenience advantage of psoas-only indices is becoming less persuasive. The next priority is prospective clinical validation: standardized whole-muscle and muscle-quality measurements, transparent AI quality control, randomized testing of nutrition, exercise, and multimodal interventions triggered by high-risk phenotypes. Current trials demonstrate the feasibility of sarcopenia-targeted interventions and suggest improvements in selected functional and nutritional outcomes. Nevertheless, whether reversal of radiologically defined sarcopenia leads to improved survival remains uncertain and requires confirmation in prospective trials.

Future perspective

Over the next 5–10 years, automated body-composition analysis is likely to become an opportunistic component of routine staging CT rather than a separate research procedure. Standardized whole-muscle and muscle-quality measures may be incorporated into radiology workflows and combined with nutritional, inflammatory, functional, and treatment-related variables to define clinically meaningful host-risk phenotypes. The key transition will be from prognostic association to actionability: prospective trials will need to determine whether CT-triggered multimodal supportive care improves treatment completion, toxicity, function, quality of life, and ultimately survival. Radiological low muscle mass is therefore unlikely to become a stand-alone criterion for treatment exclusion; its future value will depend on integration into validated, multidisciplinary decision-support pathways.
