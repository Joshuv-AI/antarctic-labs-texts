1.
Introduction

Given two points
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}
in
R^{t}
, we write
\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}\geq\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
if each component of
\mathbb{X}^{\prime\prime}
is not strictly below the corresponding component of
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
. This makes
R^{t}
a partially ordered set (poset). Consider a set
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{\mathbb{X}_{i}\}_{i\in[t]}
, where
\mathbb{X}_{i}
are chosen independently, and uniformly, in the
t
-dimensional unit cube
\{\mathbb{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{t}):x_{i}\in[0,1]\}
. Equivalently, the
nt
components of
\mathbb{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{n}
are chosen uniformly, and independently of each other, in the interval

[0,1]
. The partial order on
R^{t}
makes
\mathcal{S}_{n}
a random poset.

In this paper we study the length of the longest chain in
\mathcal{S}
, where a chain is defined as an ordered sequence

S=\{\mathbb{X}_{i_{1}},\mathbb{X}_{i_{2}},\cdots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{\ell}}\}
such that
\mathbb{X}_{i_{j}}\leq\mathbb{X}_{i_{j+1}}
,
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
;
\mathbb{X}_{i_{0}}:=\overrightarrow{\mathbb{0}}=(0,\dots,0)
.
In 1961, Ulam
[
12
]
posed a problem to determine an asymptotic length of the longest subsequence in the uniformly random
permutation of
[n]
. It is easy to see that Ulam’s problem is equivalent to the problem of the longest chain in the case
t=2
.

In 1977, the results of Logan and Shepp
[
4
]
and Vershik and Kerov
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
provided a solution: with high probability, Ulam’s parameter is asymptotic to
2n^{1/2}
. This breakthrough result was based on asymptotic analysis of
the shape of a Young diagram of size
n
, chosen according to Plancherel distribution: the probability of a generic diagram
\lambda
is
d^{2}(\lambda)/n!
, where
d(\lambda)
is
n!
divided by the product of
\lambda
’s hook lengths, which happens to be the number of the Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
. Combinatorially,
the distribution arises from a Robinson–Schensted bijection (see
[
10
]
, Knuth
[
9
]
, Vol. 3, Theorem A), between
n!
permutations and pairs of Young tableaux of the same shape
\lambda
, with a property that the size of the longest increasing subsequence in a permutation equals the size of the longest row in the associated
\lambda
. In 1991, Aldous and Diaconis
[
1
]
found an alternative proof of the
2n^{1/2}
result based on Hammersley’s interacting particles process.

Prior to
[
4
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
14
]
, Hammersley
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
was already able to prove that the parameter in question scaled by
n^{1/2}

converges, in probability, to a constant sandwiched between
\pi/8
and
e
. That
e
is an upper bound for the limit
in question followed from an observation that the expected number of chains of length
\ell
is
(n)_{\ell}/(\ell!)^{2}
. The lower bound followed from an asymptotic analysis of an auxiliary process of growing a chain of points via a two-dimensional Poisson process. Convergence of the scaled length of a longest subsequence to a constant limit followed from a general result for subadditive ergodic processes, due to Kingman
[
8
]
.

Ten years later, Bollobás and Winkler
[
3
]
studied the case
t>2
.
Extending Hammersley-Kingman’s techniques, they proved that the length of the longest chain scaled by
n^{1/t}
converges in probability to a constant in

\bigl[t^{2}/t!^{1/t}\Gamma(1/t),e\bigr)
, the left endpoint converging to the (excluded) right endpoint
e
as
t\to\infty
.
In this paper we replace the upper bound
e
with a smaller,
t
-dependent, constant.

Associated with a chain
S
there are
\ell

intervals
:

[\overrightarrow{\mathbb{0}},\mathbb{X}_{i_{1}}],\,[\mathbb{X}_{i_{1}},\mathbb{X}_{i_{2}}],\dots,[\mathbb{X}_{i_{\ell-1}},\mathbb{X}_{i_{\ell}}],

each interval consisting of the points
\mathbb{X}_{j}
, (
j\neq i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}
), comparable to the interval’s endpoints, and sandwiched strictly between them. We call a chain
\{\mathbb{X}_{i_{1}}\leq\mathbb{X}_{i_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbb{X}_{i_{\ell}}\}

maximal
if each of the
\ell
intervals is empty. Clearly, the longest chains are maximal. (Of course, for a longest chain
\mathbb{X}_{i_{1}}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbb{X}_{i_{\ell}}
, the extra interval
[\mathbb{X}_{i_{\ell}},\overrightarrow{\mathbb{1}}]
,

\overrightarrow{\mathbb{1}}:=(1,\dots,1)
, is empty too.)

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C_{\ell}
denote the total number of maximal chains of length
\ell=xn^{1/t}
,
x\leq e
.
(i)
For
a>\gamma t
, (
\gamma=-\Gamma^{\prime}(1)
), and
b>0
, introduce

q(x;a,b)\!=\!\max\!\left\{\!\min_{u\in(0,1)}\frac{\Gamma^{t}(1-u)}{e^{au}};\frac{1+b}{e^{b}};\exp\left(-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\right)\!\right\}\!<1.

q(x)=\min_{a,b}q(x;a,b)
is attained at a unique point
(a(x),b(x))
such that

\min_{u\in(0,1)}\frac{\Gamma^{t}(1-u)}{e^{a(x)u}}=\frac{1+b(x)}{e^{b(x)}}=\exp\left(-\frac{e^{-a(x)}}{x^{t}(1+b(x))^{t}}\right).

(ii)
Define
\bar{x}=\inf\bigl\{x\in[e^{-\gamma},e]:\bigl(\frac{e}{x}\bigr)^{t}q(x)<1\bigr\}
. Then
\bar{x}<e
, and

\mathbb{E}[C_{\ell}]=O\bigl[\exp(-\Theta(\varepsilon)n^{1/t})\bigr],\text{ if }\ell=(\bar{x}+\varepsilon)n^{1/t},\varepsilon>0.

Consequently, the length of the longest chain is below
(\bar{x}+\varepsilon)n^{1/t}
with probability
\geq 1-O\bigl[\exp(-\Theta(\varepsilon)n^{1/t})\bigr]
.

In conclusion, we refer the reader to Steele
[
11
]
for a highly readable, meticulously written survey of a rich collection of papers on lengths of increasing subsequences in a variety of combinatorial settings.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

By symmetry, with
\mathbb{x}_{0}=\overrightarrow{\mathbb{0}}
,

\mathbb{E}[C_{\ell}]=(n)_{\ell}\int\limits_{\mathbb{0}\leq\mathbb{x}(1)\leq\mathbb{x}_{2}\leq\dots\leq\mathbb{x}_{\ell}\leq\mathbb{1}}\!\!\left(\!1-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\bigl(x_{j}(i)-x_{j}(i-1)\bigr)\!\right)^{n-\ell}\!\!\prod_{i=1}^{l}d\mathbb{x}(i).

Explanation
:
(n)_{\ell}
is the total number of ways to select an ordered sequence of points
\{\mathbb{X}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell]}
in the cube
[0,1]^{t}
. The integral is the probability that this sequence is increasing
and
that all the
\ell
intervals
[\mathbb{X}(i-1),\mathbb{X}(i+1)]
are empty;
\mathbb{X}(0):=\overrightarrow{\mathbb{0}}
. Indeed, conditioned on

\{\mathbb{X}(i)=\mathbb{x}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell]}
, the integrand is the probability that none of the remaining
(n-\ell)
points
\mathbb{X}(k)
belongs to any of the
\ell
intervals in question.

Introduce the
\ell
increments
\mathbb{y}(i)=\mathbb{x}(i)-\mathbb{x}(i-1)
,
i\in[1,\ell]
, so that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{y}(i)\leq\mathbb{1}
.
Switching from the variables
\mathbb{x}(i)
,
i\in[\ell]
, to
\mathbb{y}(i)=\{y_{j}(i)\}_{j\in[t]}
,
i\in[\ell]
, we rewrite the above formula:

\mathbb{E}[C_{\ell}]=(n)_{\ell}\int\limits_{\mathbb{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{y}_{\ell}\geq\mathbb{0},\atop\mathbb{y}_{1}+\cdots+\mathbb{y}_{\ell}\leq\mathbb{1}}\biggl(1-\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\prod_{j\in[t]}y_{j}(i)\biggr)^{n-\ell}\,\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}d\mathbb{y}(i).

Our task is to upper bound this
t\ell
-dimensional integral. As the first step, we use the arithmetic/geometric means inequality in combination with
1-z\leq e^{-z}
,
(z\in[0,1])
, and bound the integrand by

\biggl(1-\ell\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\prod_{j\in[t]}y_{j}^{1/\ell}(i)\biggr)^{n-\ell}\leq\exp\biggl(\!\!-\ell(n-\ell)\prod_{j\in[t]}\prod_{i\in[\ell]}y_{j}^{1/\ell}(i)\biggr).

Probabilistically,

(2.1)

\mathbb{E}[C_{\ell}]\leq(n)_{\ell}\,\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-\ell(n-\ell)\prod_{j\in[t]}\prod_{i\in[\ell]}Y_{j}^{1/\ell}(i)\biggr)\cdot\mathbb{I}\biggl(\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\mathbb{Y}(i)\leq\overrightarrow{\mathbb{1}}\biggr)\biggr],

where
t\ell
random variables
\{Y_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell],j\in[t]}
are independent
(0,1)
-Uniforms, and
\mathbb{Y}(i)=\{Y_{j}(i)\}_{j\in[t]}
.

To proceed, for each
j\in[t]
switch to
s_{j}=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}y_{j}(i)
,
v_{j}(i)=y_{j}(i)/s_{j}
,
i<\ell
, and set
v_{j}(\ell)=1-\sum_{i<\ell}v_{j}(i)
.
In the new variables,

\prod_{j\in[t]}\prod_{i\in[\ell]}y_{j}^{1/\ell}(i)=\Pi(\mathbb{v})\cdot\prod_{j\in[t]}s_{j},\quad\Pi(\mathbb{v})=\prod_{j\in[t]}\prod_{i\in[\ell]}(v_{j}(i))^{1/\ell}.

An admissible
\{s_{j},v_{j}(i)\}_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell]}
satisfies the conditions
0\leq v_{j}(i)\leq\min\{1,1/s_{j}\}
,
s_{j}\leq 1
. The Jacobian of

\{y_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell],j\in[t]}
with respect to
\{s_{j},v_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell-1],\,j\in[t]}
is
\prod_{j\in[t]}s_{j}^{\ell-1}
, implying that the joint density of
\{S_{j}:=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}Y_{j}(i),V_{j}(i):=Y_{j}(i)/S_{j}\}_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell-1]}
is
at most

\prod_{j\in[t]}s_{j}^{\ell-1}\cdot\mathbb{I}\biggl(\sum_{i\in[\ell-1]}v_{j}(i)\leq 1\biggr)=\prod_{j\in[t]}\frac{s_{j}^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\cdot g\bigl(\{v_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell-1]}\bigr).

Here, for each
j\in[t]
,

g(\{v_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell-1]}):=(\ell-1)!\cdot\mathbb{I}\biggl(\sum_{i\in[\ell-1]}v_{j}(i)\leq 1\biggr),

is the joint density of the
first

(\ell-1)
components of
\mathbb{L}_{j}=\{L_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell]}
, where
L_{j}(i)
are the lengths of

\ell
consecutive subintervals of
[0,1]
obtained by selecting
(\ell-1)
points independently and uniformly at random in
[0,1]
. The product of these densities signifies that
\ell
-dimensional vectors
\mathbb{L}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{L}_{t}
are independent.
So, the expectation on the the RHS of (
2.1
) is at most

(2.2)

\frac{1}{((\ell-1)!)^{t}}\int\limits_{\{v_{j}(i)\}}\prod_{j\in[t]}g\bigl(\{v_{j}(i)\}_{i\in[\ell-1]}\bigr)\\
\times\left(\,\int\limits_{\{s_{j}\}}\exp\biggl(-\ell(n-\ell)\cdot\Pi(\mathbb{v})\prod_{j\in[t]}s_{j}\biggr)\prod_{j\in[t]}\!s_{j}^{\ell-1}\,\,d\mathbb{s}\right)\,\prod_{j\in[t]}\prod_{i\in[\ell-1]}d\mathbb{v}_{j}(i);

here
\sum_{i\in[\ell]}v_{j}(i)\leq 1
,
s_{j}\leq 1
, (
j\in[t]
).

To evaluate the innermost integral, switch to the variables
z_{1}=s_{1},z_{2}=s_{1}s_{2},\dots,z_{t-1}=s_{1}\cdots s_{t-1},z=s_{1}\cdots s_{t}
. The new variables meet the conditions
1\geq z_{1}\geq\cdots\geq z_{t-1}\geq z
. The Jacobian of the variables
s_{j}
, with respect to
z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1},z
is
1/z_{1}\times\dots\times 1/z_{t-1}
. So, the new integrand is

z^{\ell-1}\exp\biggl(-\ell(n-\ell)\cdot\Pi(\mathbb{v})z\biggr)\cdot\prod_{j\in[t-1]}z_{j}^{-1}.

Integrating this function with respect to
z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1}
, first over
z_{1}\in[z_{2},1]
, second over

z_{2}\in[z_{3},1]
,…, and last over
z_{t-1}\in[z,1]
, we see that the innermost integral simplifies to

\frac{1}{(t-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}z^{\ell-1}\exp\left[-\ell(n-\ell)\cdot\Pi(\mathbb{v})z\right]\log^{t-1}(1/z)\,dz.

Hence, recalling the definition of
g(\{v(i)\}_{i\in[\ell-1]})
, and setting
\mathbb{L}:=\{\mathbb{L}_{j}\}_{j\in[t]}
, we rewrite (
2.2
) this
way:

\frac{\ell^{t}}{((\ell)!)^{t}\,(t-1)!}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}z^{\ell-1}\exp\left[-\ell(n-\ell)\cdot\Pi(\mathbb{L})z\right]\log^{t-1}(1/z\,dz\right].

In summary, (
2.1
) becomes

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[C_{\ell}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(n)_{\ell}\,\ell^{t}}{(\ell!)^{t}(t-1)!}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}z^{\ell-1}\exp\left[-\ell(n-\ell)\cdot\Pi(\mathbb{L})z\right]\log^{t-1}(1/z\,dz\right].

To sharply estimate the RHS expectation, we use a classic property of the uniformly random partition of
[0,1]
,
(Karlin and Taylor
[
7
]
):
the lengths
L(1),\dots,L(\ell)
of the subintervals of
[0,1]
obtained by throwing
\ell-1
points into
[0,1]
uniformly and independently have the same joint distribution as

\bigl\{W(1)/S(\ell),\dots,W(\ell)/S(\ell)\bigr\}
, where
W(i)
are independent exponentials with mean
1
, and
S(\ell)=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}W(i)
. Then, introducing
t\ell
independent exponentials
W_{j}(i)
, and using

\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\equiv}
to indicate equality of two distributions in question, we have

\Pi(\mathbb{L})\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\equiv}\Pi(\mathbb{W})=\frac{\prod_{j,i}W^{1/\ell}_{j}(i)}{\prod_{j}S_{j}(\ell)},\quad S_{j}(\ell)=\sum_{i}W_{j}(i).

(1)
Using Chernoff-type bound for the i.i.d. summands
\log W_{j}(i)
, we have: for
a>0
,

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\ell^{-1}\!\!\!\!\sum_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell]}\!\!\!\log W_{j}(i)\leq-a\biggr)=\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell]}\!\!\!\log W_{j}(i)\leq-a\ell\biggr)\\
\leq\inf_{u\in(0,1)}\frac{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\exp\biggl(-u\sum_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell]}\log W_{j}(i)\biggr)\biggr]}{\exp(a\ell u)}=\inf_{u\in(0,1)}\frac{\bigl(\mathbb{E}[e^{-u\log W}]\bigr)^{t\ell}}{e^{a\ell u}};

here

\mathbb{E}[e^{-u\log W}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u\log w}e^{-w}\,dw=\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{-u}e^{-w}\,dw=\Gamma(1-u).

Therefore

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\ell^{-1}\!\!\!\!\sum_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell]}\!\!\!\log W_{j}(i)\leq-a\biggr)\leq\left(\inf_{u\in(0,1)}\frac{\Gamma^{t}(1-u)}{e^{au}}\right)^{\ell}.

Since
\Gamma^{\prime}(1)=-\gamma
, where
\gamma
(Euler-Mascheroni constant, is
0.57721\dots
) the infimum is strictly below
1
if
a>\gamma t
, which we assume from now. Since
\Gamma(x)
is log-convex (Andrews, Askey, and Roy
[
2
]
), and
\Gamma(+0)=\infty
, the infimum is attained at a single stationary point
u(a)
of
e^{-au}\,\Gamma^{t}(1-u)
in
(0,1)
, i.e. the
a
-dependent root of

\bigl(t\log\Gamma(1-u)-au\bigr)^{\prime}=-\left.t\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(v)}{\Gamma(v)}\right|_{v=1-u}-a=0.

Hence

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\ell^{-1}\!\!\!\!\sum_{j\in[t],i\in[\ell]}\!\!\!\log W_{j}(i)\leq-a\biggr)\leq\rho^{\ell}(a),\quad\rho(a):=\min_{u\in(0,1)}\frac{\Gamma^{t}(1-u)}{e^{au}}.

\rho(a)
decreases as a function of
a
,
\rho(\gamma t+)=1
, and
\rho(\infty)=0
.

(2)
Furthermore,
S_{j}(\ell)
,
j\in[t]
, are i.i.d., with density
e^{-y}y^{\ell-1}/\Gamma(\ell)!
. Picking
b>0
, we bound

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{j}\log S_{j}(\ell)\geq t\log(\ell(1+b))\biggr)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}^{t}\bigl[e^{u\log S_{1}(\ell)}\bigr]}{\exp\bigl[ut\log(\ell(1+b)\bigr])}\\
=(\ell(1+b))^{-ut}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-y}\frac{y^{\ell-1}}{\Gamma(\ell)}y^{u}\,dy\right)^{t}\\
=\left[(\ell(1+b))^{-u}\frac{\Gamma(u+\ell)}{\Gamma(\ell)}\right]^{t}.

By Stirling-type formula
\Gamma(\eta)=\Theta\bigl(\eta^{1/2}(\eta/e)^{\eta}\bigr)
,
u=\ell b
delivers qualitatively best bound,
namely

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{j}\log S_{j}(\ell)\geq t\log(\ell(1+b))\biggr)\leq\left(\frac{1+b}{e^{b}}\right)^{\ell}.

Combining (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we bound the expectation in (
2.3
) by

(2.6)

O\biggl[\rho^{\ell}(t)+\left(\frac{1+b}{e^{b}}\right)^{\ell}\biggr]\int_{0}^{1}z^{\ell-1}\log^{t-1}(1/z)\,dz\\
+\int_{0}^{1}z^{\ell-1}\exp\left(\!-e^{-a}\frac{\ell(n-\ell)}{\ell^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\,z\!\right)\cdot\log^{t-1}(1/z)\,dz.

Integrating the top integral in (
2.6
) by parts once, we obtain a recurrence equation, which implies that the integral equals
(t-1)!/\ell^{t}
. Since
t\geq 2
, the bottom integral is of order

\int_{0}^{1}z^{\ell-1}\exp\left(\!-e^{-a}\frac{\ell n}{\ell^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\,z\!\right)\cdot\log^{t-1}(1/z)\,dz\\
\leq\max_{z\in[0,1]}z^{\ell-1}\exp\left(\!-e^{-a}\frac{\ell n}{\ell^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\,z\!\right)\times\int_{0}^{1}\log^{t-1}(1/z)\,dz.

The second line integral equals
(t-1)!
, and the outside maximum is attained at
z=1
, provided that
\ell\geq e^{-\gamma}n^{1/t}
and
n
is large. Indeed, the function in question increases for
z\leq(1-1/\ell)e^{a}(1+b)^{t}(\ell^{t}/n)
; since
a\geq\gamma t
, the bound for
z
exceeds
1
for
\ell\geq e^{-\gamma}n^{1/t}
, and
n
is sufficiently large.
The resulting bound together with (
2.6
) imply that, for
x:=\ell/n^{1/t}\geq e^{-\gamma}
, the expectation in (
2.3
) is of order

\rho^{\ell}(a)+\left(\frac{1+b}{e^{b}}\right)^{\ell}+\exp^{\ell}\left(-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\right)\leq\rho^{\ell}(a)+2\left(\frac{1+b(a,x)}{e^{b(a,x)}}\right)^{\ell},

where
b(a,x)
is a unique root of

(2.7)

\frac{1+b}{e^{b}}=\exp\left(-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\right),\quad b>0.

And
b(a,x)
is the minimum point of
\max\left\{\frac{1+b}{e^{b}},\exp\left(-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\right)\right\}
,
since as functions of
b
,
\frac{1+b}{e^{b}}
decreases from
1
to
0
, while
\exp\left(-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\right)
increases to
1
.
Now, for

H(x,a,b):=\log(1+b)-b+\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}},

H^{\prime}_{b}<0
and
H^{\prime}_{a}=-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}<0
.
By implicit differentiation,
b(a,x)
decreases as a function of
a
.
Finally, we choose
a=a(x)>\gamma t
such that

(2.8)

\rho(a)=\frac{1+b(a,x)}{e^{b(a,x)}};

a(x)
exists since, as functions of
a\in(\gamma t,\infty)
,
\rho(a)
decreases from
1
to
0
, while
\frac{1+b(a,x)}{e^{b(a,x)}}
increases to
1
. And
a(x)
minimizes
\max\left\{\rho(a),\exp\left(-\frac{e^{-a(t)}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\right)\right\}
.
In summary, we proved that
(a(x),b(x))
, is a unique minimum point of

q(x;a,b):=\max\left\{\rho(a),\frac{1+b}{e^{b}},\exp\biggl(-\frac{e^{-a}}{x^{t}(1+b)^{t}}\biggr)\right\}

and

q(x):=q(x;a(x),b(x))=\rho(a(x))=\frac{1+b(x)}{e^{b(x)}}=\exp\biggl(-\frac{e^{-a(x)}}{x^{t}(1+b(x))^{t}}\biggr).

Now, in (
2.3
)
(n)_{\ell}\ell^{t}/(\ell!)^{t}
is of order
\bigl[n/(\ell/e)^{t}\bigr]^{\ell}\ell^{t/2}
. So, the previous discussion shows that for
\ell=xn^{1/t}
,
E[C_{\ell}]
is at most of order
n^{1/2}\left(\left(\frac{e}{x}\right)^{t}q(x)\right)^{\ell}
.
Introducing
\bar{x}=\inf\{x\in[e^{-\gamma},e]:(e/x)^{t}q(x)<1\}
, we see that
\bar{x}<e
, since
q(e)<1
.
And we conclude that
\mathbb{E}[C_{\ell}]=O\bigl[\exp(-\Theta(\varepsilon)n^{1/t})\bigr]
, if
\ell\geq(\bar{x}+\varepsilon)n^{1/t}
.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.
