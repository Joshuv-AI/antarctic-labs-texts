Comparative Effectiveness of Upadacitinib vs. Other JAK Inhibitors in Patients With Rheumatoid Arthritis in a Global Real-World Setting
Authors: Peter C Taylor, Aditi Kadakia, Jack Milligan, Ana Romero, Oliver Howell, Pankaj A Patel, Sophie Barlow, Roberto Caporali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to compare the real-world effectiveness of upadacitinib vs. other Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA).MethodsData were extracted from the Adelphi RA Disease Specific Programme™ (DSP), a cross-sectional survey administered to rheumatologists and their patients in the EU, UK, Japan, Canada, and the US from July 2021 to February 2022. Patients receiving upadacitinib 15 mg or other JAKis, including tofacitinib, baricitinib, or peficitinib, for ≥ 6 months were included in the study. Data captured at treatment initiation and follow-up (≥ 6 months) included physician-evaluated pain rating and Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28). Fatigue and medication adherence were recorded at follow-up only. Inverse-probability-weighted regression adjustment was used to compare clinical outcomes at follow-up between patients treated with upadacitinib vs. other JAKis.ResultsOf 1440 total patients with RA, 1205 patients received upadacitinib and 235 received other JAKis. At study initiation, the majority of patients in both treatment groups had moderate/high disease activity (upadacitinib: 63.5%, other JAKis: 51.3%). At the most recent follow-up, after adjusting for differences in clinical characteristics between groups, patients treated with upadacitinib were significantly more likely to have attained physician-reported remission (54.3% vs. 44.1%, P = 0.030), have no pain (43.4% vs. 32.8%, P = 0.016), and have complete medication adherence (59.5% vs. 49.0%, P = 0.025) compared with patients treated with other JAKis. Of patients treated with upadacitinib, 42.5% were reported as having no fatigue compared with 36.1% of patients receiving other JAKis (P = 0.173).ConclusionsThis real-world study of patients with RA showed that greater proportions of patients who received upadacitinib attained physician-reported DAS28 remission, absence of pain, and complete adherence to medication compared with patients receiving other JAKis.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Comparative effectiveness, JAK inhibitor, Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), Disease activity, Surveys

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) are clinically effective in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and currently recommended by the European Medicines Agency for patients with moderate-to-severe RA that cannot be controlled with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and by the Food and Drug Administration for patients who have had an inadequate response or intolerance to one or more tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors.Network meta-analysis of phase 3 clinical trial data showed that upadacitinib 15 mg had numerically higher efficacy relative to other approved JAKis. However, real-world data comparing the effectiveness of different JAKis has been limited.What was learned from the study?Patients with RA receiving upadacitinib 15 mg were significantly more likely to have achieved physician-reported outcomes of remission, no pain, and medication adherence compared with patients receiving other JAKis.This real-world data suggests differences in treatment effectiveness among JAKis and can help inform clinical practice in the treatment of RA.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease that leads to inflammation, swelling, and damage in joint tissues. The global prevalence of RA is estimated to be 0.46%, with the progressive nature of the condition potentially resulting in significant impairment in quality of life, disability, and increased risk of serious comorbidities if left untreated [1, 2]. Effective treatment for RA can lead to lower pain, better physical function, and higher quality of life [3]. Current recommendations for treatment targets from the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) suggest disease remission or low disease activity (LDA) for whom full remission is not possible [4, 5].

The current recommended treatment for RA consists of first-line conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), such as methotrexate [4, 5]. After experiencing an inadequate response (IR) or intolerance to csDMARDs, ACR and EULAR recommendations suggest patients to switch to or add a biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (bDMARD, which includes tumor necrosis factor-alpha inhibitors [TNFis], interleukin-[IL]-6/IL-1 inhibitors, and T-cell co-stimulation inhibitors) or targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (tsDMARD) to their treatment regimen [4, 5]. tsDMARDs are a class of therapeutics aimed at inhibiting Janus kinases (JAKs), a key signaling pathway involved in RA pathogenesis (reviewed in [6]). There are four members of the JAK enzyme family: JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2).

Several JAK inhibitors (JAKis), including tofacitinib, baricitinib, peficitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib, demonstrate clinical efficacy in randomized controlled trials in patients with moderate-to-severe RA and have been approved [7–10]. For treating RA, these medications currently vary in their approval in different countries (tofacitinib, baricitinib [2 mg and 4 mg], filgotinib, and upadacitinib are approved in the EU; tofacitinib, baricitinib [2 mg], and upadacitinib are approved in the US; tofacitinib, baricitinib [2 mg and 4 mg], upadacitinib [15 mg and 7.5 mg], filgotinib, and peficitinib are approved in Japan) [11]. These JAKis differ in their selectivity for specific JAKs, with peficitinib considered a pan-JAKi, tofacitinib a JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3 inhibitor, baricitinib a JAK1 and JAK2 inhibitor, while filgotinib and upadacitinib are relatively more selective inhibitors for JAK1 [12].

Previously published comparative analyses using clinical trial data have suggested potential differences in efficacy among the JAKi class. A network meta-analysis (NMA) of phase 3 clinical trial data previously reported that upadacitinib 15 mg demonstrated numerically, though not statistically, higher efficacy in achieving ACR20, 50, and 70 responses and DAS28 (CRP) (Disease Activity Score in 28 joints [C-reactive protein]) remission rates compared with baricitinib and tofacitinib in csDMARD-IR patients with moderate-to-severe RA, though this study did not include data from filgotinib or peficitinib [13]. A matching-adjusted indirect comparison approach between upadacitinib + csDMARD vs. tofacitinib + csDMARD reported higher DAS28(ESR [Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate]) remission at 6 months with upadacitinib + csDMARD; however, other comparisons, such as ACR20/50/70 were not significantly different with a stringent statistical cutoff [14]. Similarly, a broader NMA comparing five JAKis reported differences in certain efficacy outcomes at 12 and 24 weeks, with relatively higher surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) rankings for upadacitinib at 12 weeks (ACR20/50/70 and DAS28[CRP]) and baricitinib at 24 weeks (ACR50/70 and DAS28[CRP]) [15]. However, there are limited published studies on real-world comparative effectiveness among the JAKi class, with most focusing on medication adherence and persistence rates inferred from registry or insurance claims databases, with some studies reporting that patients taking upadacitinib are less likely to discontinue treatment within 12 months compared with baricitinib and tofacitinib [16, 17]. There have been fewer real-world studies that incorporate clinical outcome data, though a recent study based on a large Australian cohort found higher disease remission rates at 3 months for upadacitinib vs. other JAKis, as well as higher medication persistence rates over 33 months [18]. A previous analysis in this same cohort reported broadly similar persistence rates between tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib between different lines of therapy, though differences in the timing of treatment availability in Australia prevented specific comparisons from being made [19]. Studies from a smaller cohort of Japanese patients have not found differences in retention or remission rates among JAKis [20, 21], indicating further studies may be warranted. Here, we use a real-world cross-sectional survey with elements of retrospective data collection for patients with RA using advanced therapies to compare the effectiveness of upadacitinib vs. other JAKis (baricitinib, tofacitinib, or peficitinib) on physician-reported outcomes, including disease activity, pain, fatigue, and medication adherence.

Methods

Study Design, Patient Demographics, and Clinical Characteristics

Survey data were extracted from the Adelphi RA Disease Specific Programme™ (DSP), a cross-sectional study with elements of retrospective data collection [22]. Surveys were administered to rheumatologists and their patients in the EU-5, Japan, Canada, and the US from July 2021 to February 2022. For a given patient with RA, rheumatologists completed a patient record form (PRF), for the next 5–10 patients consulted in clinical practice.

Physicians were included if, at the time of the survey, they were practicing as a rheumatologist (or an orthopedist in Japan), were actively managing patients with RA, making management decisions for ≥ 5 patients a month, and had been qualified for longer than 1 year. Surveys were administered until a quota of 5–10 patients was reached. Once the main sample quota was reached, physicians were also invited to complete a further 2–3 PRFs for patients currently receiving upadacitinib to form an oversample. The oversample was carried out to ensure an adequate sample size for analysis, given the relatively recent approval of upadacitinib for RA (between 2019 and 2021 in the US [23], Canada [24], Japan [25], and Europe [26]). The population of interest for this study was patients ≥ 18 years of age with confirmed physician diagnosis of RA who received upadacitinib 15 mg or other JAKis (baricitinib, tofacitinib, or peficitinib) for ≥ 6 months and were not participating in a clinical trial at the time of the survey.

Patient demographics, disease activity, treatment history and management, symptoms, concomitant conditions, and baseline characteristics were collected. To ensure the most up-to-date medical information was included, physicians were instructed to utilize the patients’ medical records when completing their PRF. Physician-reported clinical outcomes assessed at treatment initiation and most recent follow-up visit (≥ 6 months from treatment initiation) included assessment of disease activity and pain (none, mild, moderate, or severe). Physician-reported clinical outcomes reported at the most recent follow-up included fatigue (none, mild, moderate, or severe) and medication adherence (completely adherent or not). Physician-reported disease activity was evaluated on the basis of formal measurements of the DAS28 score where available (performed at the initial consultation or conducted within 4 weeks prior and recorded in the medical records). As formal DAS28 is usually not measured at every consultation in routine clinical practice, where not available, the physicians used their own clinical judgement to assess disease activity, referring to information from patients’ medical records. Disease activity was categorized into remission (< 2.6), low disease activity (LDA; 2.6–3.2), moderate disease activity (MDA; 3.3–5.1), or high disease activity (HDA; > 5.1). To ensure the physician-estimated DAS28 scores reflected objective clinical measures of DAS28, Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between the two metrics for 30.83% of patients who had both measurements. Unadjusted physician-reported outcomes were reported as the change in disease activity from the initiation of current treatment to the most recent follow-up at ≥ 6 months.

Statistical Analysis

Summary statistics were computed for continuous variables, and frequency counts and percentages were computed for categorical variables. Patient demographics and clinical data were balanced using inverse-probability-weighted regression adjustment (IPWRA) methods [27]. IPWRA estimators use weighted regression coefficients to compute outcome averages, which are converted to percentages for binary outcomes by multiplying by 100. The covariates included in the IPWRA modeling were patient age, biological sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, DAS28 score at initiation of current JAKi treatment, number of prior advanced therapy (AT) lines, duration of diagnosis to initiation of current AT, and pain at initiation of current AT for the weighting and regression adjustment stage. Additionally, the current AT duration was used for the regression adjustment stage. Standardized mean differences were reported for the covariates before and after weighting. For the IPWRA analysis, only patients with data available for all covariates weighted in the modeling were included. No imputation of missing data was performed.

All physician-reported outcomes, including remission, pain, fatigue, and medication adherence, were modeled using this IPWRA approach and reported as estimated percentages along with P values for each treatment group, which were compared between upadacitinib vs. other JAKis. Analyses were run in Stata v18 (StataCorp 2023) and UNICOM Intelligence Reporter version 7.5.1 (UNICOM Systems 2021).

Ethics/Ethical Approval

Using a check box, patients provided informed consent for the use of their anonymized and aggregated data for research and publication in scientific journals. Data were collected in such a way that patients and physicians could not be identified directly; all data were aggregated and de-identified before receipt. Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association (EphMRA) guidelines [28], and therefore it does not require ethics committee approval. The research was submitted to the Institutional Review Board (IRB), study protocol number: Protocol #21-ADRW-104. Each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [29], Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [30] and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The US, Japan, and Canada are signatories to the EphMRA Code of Conduct.

Results

Baseline Clinical Characteristics

Baseline patient characteristics are found in Table 1. A total of 1440 patients were included (upadacitinib 15 mg, n = 1205; other JAKis, n = 235), with most patients in the other JAKis group receiving baricitinib (n = 120) or tofacitinib (n = 113) with the remainder receiving peficitinib (n = 2). Mean patient age was slightly lower for the upadacitinib group: 53.6 and 55.4 years for the upadacitinib and other JAKis groups, respectively (P = 0.046, t test). The upadacitinib group was 67.9% female (n = 818), and the other JAKis group was 70.6% female (n = 166; P = 0.444, Fisher’s exact test). The time since RA diagnosis was similar among the two groups, with 6.2 years for upadacitinib and 6.9 years for the other JAKis groups (P = 0.121, t test). In the upadacitinib group, 41.6% (n = 501) of patients were on their first AT line, 39.0% (n = 470) on their second, and 19.4% (n = 234) on their third + line. In the other JAKis group, 57.0% (n = 134) were on their first AT line, 29.8% (n = 70) on their second, and 13.2% (n = 31) on their third + line, indicating there are differences in the proportion of patients utilizing different lines of therapy between the upadacitinib vs. other JAKi group (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test). In the upadacitinib group, fewer patients did not have previous JAKi use (at any line previous to their current) compared with the other JAKi group (81.9% vs. 89.8%, respectively; P = 0.003, Fisher’s exact test).

Discussion

In this real-world study, we observed consistently higher clinical effectiveness across a range of physician-reported disease assessments in patients with RA treated with upadacitinib 15 mg relative to patients treated with other JAKis, including tofacitinib, baricitinib, and peficitinib. Patients on upadacitinib were significantly more likely to have attained disease remission (defined as DAS28 < 2.6), reduced pain, and have complete medication adherence compared with patients on other JAKis ≥ 6 months after treatment initiation. We observed no difference in patients reporting no fatigue at follow-up between the upadacitinib vs. the other JAKis group.

Previously published indirect treatment comparisons of clinical trial data suggest that upadacitinib may have numerically higher efficacy in some outcomes at certain timepoints relative to other JAKis either as monotherapy or in combination with csDMARDs [13–15]. A recent NMA (comprised of 36 randomized controlled trials of 16,713 patients) study of five JAKis found that upadacitinib 30 mg and upadacitinib 15 mg had the highest relative efficacy at 12 weeks for ACR20, ACR50, ACR70, and DAS28(CRP) < 2.6, and at 24 weeks for ACR20, though baricitinib + csDMARD had higher efficacy for ACR50, ACR70, and DAS28(CRP) < 2.6 at 24 weeks [15]. An additional NMA of 11 randomized controlled trials comparing upadacitinib, baricitinib, and tofacitinib reported similar findings, observing numerically higher ACR20/50/70 and DAS28 responses for upadacitinib at 12 and 24 weeks, though these differences were not statistically significant [13]. A matching-adjusted indirect comparison approach of two randomized controlled trials also reported higher ACR50 and remission (defined as SDAI [Simple Disease Activity Index] ≤ 3.3, CDAI [Clinical Disease Activity Index] ≤ 2.8, DAS28-ESR/CRP < 2.6) for upadacitinib + csDMARD compared with tofacitinib + csDMARD at 3 and 6 months, respectively, with ACR50 at 3 weeks and DAS28-ESR at 6 months remaining statistically significant after multiplicity correction [14].

Our real-world findings of patients treated with upadacitinib attaining higher rates of remission (DAS28 < 2.6) vs. other JAKis are consistent with results from a recent real-world study of the Optimising Patient outcomes in Australian rheumatoLogy (OPAL) dataset of 7089 patients with RA, which found higher DAS28(CRP) remission rates at 3 months for upadacitinib compared with baricitinib and tofacitinib [18]. It is worth noting, however, that not all studies utilizing real-world data have found differences in effectiveness when comparing JAKis. An analysis of The Kansai Consortium for the Well-Being of Rheumatic Disease Patients (ANSWER) cohort in Japan reported no difference in rates of retention, CDAI remission, or CDAI low disease activity at 6 months, although the overall study population was small (n = 361) [20].

A recent study of administrative claims in the Merative MarketScan Research Databases containing 6317 patients with RA found higher adherence rates for upadacitinib relative to baricitinib and tofacitinib within 12 months [16], which is parallel to our findings of a greater proportion of patients having physician-reported complete medication adherence for upadacitinib vs. other JAKis. Though not a focus of our study, several real-world studies have additionally found higher medication persistence for upadacitinib vs. other JAKis, which can potentially be used as a proxy for medication effectiveness and safety [16–18, 31–33].

In clinical practice, current recommendations suggest switching to or adding a bDMARD or tsDMARD/JAKi in patients who have failed first-line treatment on a csDMARD, and TNFis are a common first choice of AT [4, 5]. However, real-world data has shown that the prevalence of TNFi cycling is common, ranging from ~ 40–70% following initial TNFi failure [34–37]. Recent studies suggest patients experience beneficial treatment outcomes by switching to a JAKi [38, 39]. In particular, in an analysis using similar statistical methodology with the Adelphi RA DSP, Caporali et al. found that patients who switched from a TNFi to upadacitinib had better clinical outcomes of remission, no pain, and complete medication adherence than those who either cycled to another TNFi or switched to an AT with a different mechanism of action [27]. This was corroborated with a real-world dataset from Australia, where patients switching from another JAKi or TNFi to upadacitinib in the second line of AT showed longer persistence and greater DAS28(CRP) remission over 9 months [18].

Real-world data is important to assess alongside clinical trial data, as it can provide insights into treatment effectiveness in patient populations that may be excluded from participating in clinical trials. A strength of this study is that it represents an investigation of real-world data in a large patient cohort across the disease spectrum in RA, including clinically meaningful patient outcomes such as pain. Our results add to a growing body of literature supporting real-world beneficial clinical effectiveness of upadacitinib in the JAK inhibitor treatment landscape.

There are some limitations of the Adelphi RA DSP methodology, including that formal measures of disease activity (DAS28) were not available for all patients. In these cases, the physician’s own clinical judgement was used when the formal measure was not available (for 69% of patients). However, we observed a strong correlation between the physician-estimated and formal DAS28 scores, supporting that our inclusion of this criterion in the modeling is sound. Additionally, patients and physicians in the Adelphi RA DSP do not represent a true random sample of patients with RA and their clinicians, but are rather considered a pragmatic and pseudo-random sample [27]. We additionally did not examine differences in effectiveness between doses for JAKis with multiple approved dosages. Given the real-world nature of the dataset, patients in the DSP may differ in disease severity from the average RA patient, given those who consult more frequently are more likely to be included in the sample. However, to reduce selection bias, physicians were asked to include data from all consecutive patients who consulted and met the criteria for the DSP. Additionally, due to the real-world nature of the study, patients differed in aspects of their treatment that would otherwise be controlled for in a clinical trial setting (i.e., concomitant use of csDMARDs or corticosteroids), and therefore the effectiveness of these JAKis is not being compared in isolation. Differences in access to therapies (such as insurance barriers, cost, or patient assistance programs) may additionally impact medication adherence independent of effectiveness and were not controlled for in this real-world dataset. Lastly, adverse events have been shown to contribute to rates of switching among the JAKi class and may affect adherence [21]. We do not address safety using this dataset, though the long-term safety profile of JAKis has been previously reported [40–43].

Conclusions

The findings of this real-world study of patients with RA demonstrate that greater proportions of patients taking upadacitinib vs. other JAKis attained physician-reported DAS28 remission, absence of pain, and medication adherence.
