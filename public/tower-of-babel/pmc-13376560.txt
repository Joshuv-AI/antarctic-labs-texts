Emergence of periodic soliton patterns in the time-fractional Hodgkin–Huxley model arising in modern neuroscience
Authors: Yousef Jawarneh, Safyan Mukhtar, Safiqul Islam, Yaouba Amadou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe time-fractional Hodgkin-Huxley Model (tfHHM) with Katugampola Fractional Derivatives (KFDs) is the main subject of the current study. This model was developed in modern neuroscience that imitate the way action potentials move along the giant axon in squids. Explicit solutions and in-depth details of soliton patterns are obtained by using the Riccati Modified Extended Simple Equation Method (RMESEM). The suggested approach converts the intended model into a nonlinear algebraic system by providing traveling solutions in closed form. Using the symbolic computation tool Maple, the resultant algebraic system is analytically solved to yield a new set of solutions in terms of trigonometric, exponential, rational, hyperbolic and rational-hyperbolic functions. A two-dimensional visual investigation of the dynamical characteristics of the derived solutions for fractional parameters shows that periodic solitons, such as periodic lump, hump, dark, and bright solitons, are formed by the obtained soliton solutions. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed technique for solving complex nonlinear models and improve our knowledge of soliton behaviors in the context of tfHHM.Keywords: Nonlinear fractional partial differential equations, Time-fractional Hodgkin–Huxley model, Riccati modified extended simple equation method, Katugampola fractional derivatives, Periodic solitons.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Mathematics is broadly divided into two major fields, namely pure mathematics1–3 and applied mathematics. Pure mathematics mainly focuses on theories, principles, and rigorous foundations development, which is key in the modeling of physical phenomena. Conversely, applied mathematics gives the mathematical analysis and computation techniques necessary to analyze and solve the resulting models of problems in the real world. In the filed of applied mathematics, fractional calculus is an extension of classical integration and differentiation to fractional (non-integer) order, offering insightful computational tools which possess nonlocality and memory effects of dynamical frameworks4. Fractional derivatives are more appropriate for describing complicated processes like anomalous diffusion, long-range interaction systems and viscoelastic materials than typical integer-order derivatives because they make the current state of a system dependent on its complete history5. This expanded framework provides a more practical and adaptable tool for explaining biology, engineering, and physical phenomena in the real world systems, where the traditional models fall short. In the context of fractional calculus, the Nonlinear Fractional Partial Differential Equations (NFPDEs) are extension of ordinary Partial Differential Equations (NPDEs) which incorporates fractional derivatives. NFPDEs are becoming more and more common in a wide range of applications, from enhancing control systems and electrochemical processes to modeling viscoelastic behavior and anomalous diffusion6,7. As a result, in the modeling and comprehension of complex systems in a wide range of disciplines, including engineering, biology, finance and physics, NFPDEs are crucial. These equations provide a useful mathematical representation of complex behaviors, stability transitions, chaotic dynamics, wave propagation and pattern generation which cannot be sufficiently explained by linear models8–12.

Due to its significant relevance in nonlinear wave dynamics, the soliton has been the subject of a lot of research finding uses in a variety of fields, including quantum physics13, nonlinear optical fibers14, fluid dynamics15,16, and numerous others17,18. The remarkable stability and particle-like characteristics of these restricted waveforms make them special19. Unlike regular waves, soliton waves may pass through a medium without changing its profile. Analytical determination of soliton solutions is an important and constantly expanding subject of research. Several advanced strategies of analysis have been brought into use to get the soliton solutions of NFPDEs in recent years. These methods include the soliton ansatz method20, the extended tanh function method21, the Khater technique22, the sine-cosine method23, the Hirota bilinear method24, the Riccatti-Bernoulli sub-ODE method25, the modified simple equation method26, the Kudryashov method27, the Jacobi elliptic function method28, the extended sinh-Gordon equation expansion method29, the generalized Kudryashov auxiliary method30, the -expansion method31, the unified method32, the extended direct algebraic method (EDAM)33, the first integral approach34, the tanh-coth method35, the extended trial equation method36, the F-expansion method37, the Lie symmetry method38, the exp-function method39, the generalized exponential rational function method40, RMESEM41, and numerous other approaches42–44.

The Hodgkin–Huxley model (HHM)45, which was initially presented in 1952, continues to be a fundamental component of contemporary neuroscience. Even though the model was initially created to simulate how action potentials travel through the squid giant axon, it has since been used in a number of research domains, such as pattern recognition46, information representation47, small world networks modeling48 and noise-induced synchronization49. The intended model in both fractional and integer senses has been the subject of very few analytical and numerical works. For example, Telksnys et al. provided sufficient and necessary criteria for the existence of bright and dark solitary solutions in the HHM. Consequently, the generalized differential operator approach is used to obtain the second order analytic solitary solutions45. In another study Bilal et al. used the -expansion method to acquire N-soliton solutions for conformable HHM55. Rinzel and Miller numerically calculated stable and unstable periodic solutions to the HHMs56. Remarkably, the periodic soliton solutions of the intended model utilizing RMESEM were not reported in any literature. This work uses sophisticated RMESEM to establish and assess the soliton dynamics in the setting of tfHHM including KFD. By offering traveling solutions in closed form, the proposed method transforms the intended model into a nonlinear algebraic system57–61. A new set of solutions in terms of trigonometric, exponential, rational, hyperbolic, and rational-hyperbolic functions are obtained by analytically solving the resulting algebraic system using the symbolic computation tool Maple. Periodic soliton solutions, such as periodic lump, hump, dark, and bright solitons, are revealed according to a two-dimensional visual analysis of the dynamical properties of the obtained solutions for fractional parameters. These results enhance our understanding of soliton behaviors in the context of tfHHM and show how well the suggested method works for solving complicated nonlinear models62–66.

Graphs

The graphical dynamics of a few of the soliton solutions of the model under study are displayed in this section of the study. To comprehend the behavior of the interconnected physical processes, the comprehension of these soliton patterns is necessary. In the context of nonlinear NFPDEs, a soliton is a waveform that propagates while maintaining its shape and speed. Through 2D graphical depictions of the generated wave profiles, we reveal that periodic solitons, such as periodic lump, hump, dark, and bright solitons, are formed by the obtained soliton solutions. A periodic soliton is a soliton that oscillates with a fixed period. A dark periodic soliton is a periodic soliton which has dips in its periodic profile. In the filed of neuroscience, a dark soliton may models alternating excitation-inhibition dynamics or inhibitory neural rhythms. On the other hand, a bright periodic soliton is a periodic soliton which has peaks in its periodic profile. In the filed of neuroscience, a bright soliton may corresponds to oscillatory neural activity or rhythmic spiking. A periodic lump soliton is a periodic soliton which has localized lumps (decays) in its profile repeatedly. The periodic lump solitons may represents localized voltage packets physically. A periodic hump soliton is a periodic soliton which instead of sharp peaks has localized and smooth elevations in its amplitude. The periodic hump solitons may represents subthreshold oscillatory activity or smoother voltage oscillations.

Overall, the periodic soliton structures obtained such as periodic bright, dark, lump, and hump profiles enable an insightful understanding of the neural signal propagation in the tfHHM. Unlike the classical integer-order model, the use of the KFD brings in memory and nonlocal behavior, which is critical in the description of realistic neuronal behavior in which the current state is not only determined by current inputs but by previous activity. In contrast to the classical integer-order model where the signals are usually characterized by a standard pulse-like behavior, the fractional framework can have more interesting dynamics, such as repetitive or modulated firing patterns. A discussion of physically periodic bright solitons can be made of sustained or repetitive action potentials, i.e. rhythmic firing patterns in neurons, and dark solitons of localized suppression or inhibition in signal transmission. The development of periodic lump and hump features also indicates a possible existence of concentrated energy and clumped discharge that can be used to model the transient bursts or modulated firing patterns. These dynamics underscore the enrichment of the description of neural processes by the long-range temporal correlations and hereditary nature of these processes through the modeling in a fractional order, thus providing a more realistic description of the complex electrophysiological phenomena in biological neural systems. These trends also demonstrate that using fractional-order effects can be used to simulate complex biological processes, including rhythmic firing, signal transmission dependent on memory, and anomalous diffusion in neural tissues, thus improving the physiological applicability of the tfHHM. The obtained periodic soliton families of dark, bright, lump and hump patterns also suggest that the tfHHM allows persistent oscillating voltages patterns. These resolutions indicate that unlike the solitary single impulses, the effect of the memory-oriented ionic behavior results in the long-lasting transmission of rhythmic brain waves. Theoretically, these periodic solitons light up unnatural transmission of signals, cyclical brain functions and periodic firing activities in complicated tissue setups.

Conclusion

This work established and evaluated the soliton dynamics in the context of tfHHM incorporating KFD using advanced RMESEM. The suggested approach converted the desired model into a nonlinear algebraic system by providing traveling solutions in closed form. The resultant algebraic problem was solved analytically using the symbolic computation tool Maple, yielding a new set of solutions in terms of trigonometric, exponential, rational, hyperbolic, and rational-hyperbolic functions. A two-dimensional visual investigation of the dynamical features of the derived solutions for fractional parameters showed periodic soliton solutions, including periodic lump, hump, dark, and brilliant solitons. These findings improved our comprehension of soliton behaviors in the context of tfHHM and demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach for resolving complex nonlinear models. Additionally, the suggested approach has been carefully chosen because it continue to be straightforward algebraic tools, eliminating the need for intricate processes like the Darboux transformations, Lax pairs, Hirota bilinear forms, perturbation, linearization, and other transformation processes that would be required in other approaches. One of the main advantages of the suggested methods over more conventional methods is that they offer a range of solutions in different functions forms, which can enable a more thorough comprehension of the dynamics present in the intended model. However, it must be noted that when the greatest derivative terms and the most nonlinear term are not homogeneously balanced, the suggested approach cannot function. Because the approach is unable to balance the nonlinear term with the highest derivative, there is no case of soliton solutions formation. Despite this disadvantage, the present study demonstrates that the approach used in this work is very efficient for resolving nonlinear models in a variety of scientific domains. Future study will focus on the sensitivity of the soliton solutions produced as well as the addition and analysis of the impacts of fractional derivatives on soliton solutions in the context of the goal model.
