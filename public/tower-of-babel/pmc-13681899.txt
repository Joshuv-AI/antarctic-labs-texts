Analysis of barriers to self-monitoring of blood glucose among community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study integrating artificial intelligence-based risk prediction
Authors: Meiyan Wang, Yue Ying, Yuan Han, Jiaxiang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) is essential for diabetes management, yet elderly patients face multiple barriers that compromise adherence and glycaemic control. This study aimed to assess barriers to SMBG among community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes mellitus, identify associated factors, and develop an artificial intelligence (AI)-based predictive model for high-risk individuals.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at Chun’an First People’s Hospital from March to August 2025. Two hundred elderly patients (≥ 60 years) with diabetes were enrolled using convenience sampling. Data were collected using validated instruments: a sociodemographic questionnaire, SMBG Barriers Scale, Flourishing Scale (FS), and Digital Health Literacy Scale (DHLS). Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, and multiple linear regression. For predictive modelling, the dataset was split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets. Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine algorithms were developed and evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC. Model interpretability was assessed with SHAP values.ResultsThe mean SMBG Barriers score was 47.6 ± 9.8. Significant negative correlations were found between SMBG barriers and FS (r = − 0.42) and DHLS (r = − 0.51) scores. Multiple regression showed DHLS (β = −0.38), Flourishing score (β = −0.25), education, and diabetes education as protective factors, while complications and living alone were risk factors (R² = 0.462). The Random Forest model achieved the best performance (accuracy 88.5%, AUC 0.921). SHAP analysis identified digital health literacy, psychological well-being, and AI acceptance as the strongest predictors.ConclusionThis study highlights the importance of psychosocial and digital factors in SMBG barriers and demonstrates the effectiveness of explainable AI for risk prediction in elderly diabetic patients. Targeted interventions addressing modifiable factors could improve self-management.Clinical trial registrationThis study is a cross-sectional observational study and was not registered as a clinical trial.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus represents a significant global public health challenge, particularly among the elderly population. According to the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes in 2024, with projections indicating an increase to 853 million by 2050. One in four adults with diabetes is aged over 65, and prevalence is highest in those aged 75–79 years. In ageing societies, effective self-management is critical to prevent complications such as cardiovascular disease, retinopathy, and neuropathy [1–3].

Self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) is a cornerstone of diabetes self-management, enabling patients to track glycaemic levels, adjust treatments, and reduce complication risks. However, adherence to SMBG remains suboptimal among community-dwelling elderly patients. Multiple barriers contribute to this, including psychological resistance (e.g., fear of pain or anxiety), physical limitations (visual or motor impairments), economic burdens (cost of test strips), and knowledge/skill deficits. Elderly patients often face additional challenges due to cognitive decline, reduced dexterity, and comorbidities, which further complicate SMBG practices [4–7].

Psychological well-being plays a pivotal role in diabetes self-management. Higher levels of flourishing encompassing purpose, optimism, and positive relationships are associated with better adherence and fewer perceived barriers. Conversely, diabetes-related distress and lower psychological resources correlate with poorer glycaemic control and reduced self-care behaviors [8, 9].

Digital health literacy, defined as the ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply digital health information, has emerged as another critical determinant. Older adults with higher digital health literacy demonstrate improved diabetes knowledge, self-management behaviors, and engagement with technology-assisted monitoring. However, many elderly patients exhibit low-to-moderate digital health literacy, exacerbating barriers to SMBG, especially with the increasing integration of digital tools and continuous glucose monitoring systems [10–13].

Sociodemographic and clinical factors, such as lower education, living alone, diabetes duration, treatment modality (particularly insulin), and presence of complications, further influence SMBG adherence. Traditional epidemiological approaches have identified these associations, but they often lack predictive precision for individual risk stratification in community settings [6].

Artificial intelligence (AI) offers promising solutions by integrating multidimensional data for risk prediction. Machine learning models, including Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machines, combined with explainable AI techniques like SHAP, can identify key predictors and support targeted interventions. Despite growing interest in AI for diabetes care, studies specifically addressing SMBG barriers in elderly populations using integrated epidemiological and AI approaches remain limited [14–17].

This cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the status of SMBG barriers among community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes, identify associated sociodemographic, clinical, psychological, and digital health literacy factors, and explore the feasibility of an explainable AI-based predictive model for identifying elderly patients at increased risk of SMBG barriers. By combining traditional statistical analyses with advanced machine learning, this research seeks to provide actionable insights for personalized community-based diabetes management programs.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a cross-sectional, observational investigation to evaluate the status of barriers to SMBG and to identify their associated influencing factors among community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes mellitus [4, 5, 18]. The study was conducted at Chun’an First People’s Hospital, a comprehensive regional healthcare institution providing outpatient services and community-based chronic disease management programs. Data collection was carried out over six months from March 2025 to August 2025 under standardized conditions (Fig. 1).

Study population

A total of 200 elderly patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus were enrolled using a convenience sampling approach from outpatient clinics and affiliated community health service centers. Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥ 60 years, met established diagnostic criteria for diabetes mellitus, and were capable of effective verbal communication and comprehension of the study procedures. A total of 245 elderly individuals with diabetes mellitus were assessed for eligibility between March and August 2025. Following exclusion of 45 participants due to failure to meet inclusion criteria (n = 18), refusal to participate (n = 12), or incomplete baseline information (n = 15), 200 participants were enrolled in the study. All participants completed sociodemographic, psychological well-being, digital health literacy, and SMBG barrier assessments. The complete dataset (n = 200) was subsequently analyzed using descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression. For AI modeling, the dataset was randomly divided into training (80%, n = 160) and testing (20%, n = 40) subsets. Random Forest, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithms were developed and evaluated using multiple performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Model interpretability was assessed using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to identify the most influential predictors of SMBG barriers.

Exclusion criteria included the presence of severe comorbid physical diseases (such as advanced cardiovascular disease, end-stage renal disease, or malignant tumors) that could influence participation or outcomes. Additionally, individuals diagnosed with psychiatric or neurological disorders (including dementia, major depressive disorder, or Parkinson’s disease) that could impair cognition or response reliability were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size of 200 participants was determined based on statistical principles for multivariate regression modeling. According to standard methodological recommendations, a minimum of 10–15 observations per independent variable is required to ensure adequate statistical power and model stability. Considering the number of potential predictors included in the analysis (e.g., demographic, clinical, psychological, and digital literacy variables), a sample size of 200 was deemed sufficient to minimize overfitting and enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Data collection instruments and variables

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising four validated instruments, each targeting specific domains relevant to the study objectives.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables

A self-developed General Information Questionnaire was used to collect baseline characteristics, including age (years), gender (male/female), education level (primary, secondary, tertiary), marital status (married/single/widowed/divorced), living status (alone/with family), duration of diabetes (years), treatment modality (oral hypoglycemic agents, insulin, combination therapy), presence of diabetes-related complications (yes/no), frequency of outpatient visits, and history of diabetes education (yes/no).

Self-monitoring of blood glucose barriers scale

Barriers to self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) were assessed using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously published studies by Ong et al. and Munshi et al. Because no universally accepted or validated SMBG Barriers Scale specifically designed for community-dwelling Chinese elderly patients with diabetes is currently available, relevant items from these studies were synthesized and culturally adapted for the present study population.

The questionnaire consists of 16 items covering four conceptual domains:

Representative items include fear of finger-prick pain, difficulty operating the glucometer because of impaired vision or reduced hand dexterity, financial concerns regarding testing supplies, and insufficient knowledge regarding blood glucose monitoring procedures and interpretation of results. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Total scores range from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater perceived barriers to SMBG. Domain scores were calculated by summing responses within each domain, and the total score was obtained by summing all 16 items. Before formal data collection, the questionnaire underwent pilot testing in 20 elderly patients to evaluate clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility. In the present study, the instrument demonstrated good internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s Œ± of 0.87, indicating satisfactory reliability for research use. However, because the instrument has not yet undergone formal psychometric validation specifically in Chinese community-dwelling elderly patients, no validated clinical cutoff values currently exist. Accordingly, the SMBG barrier score was analysed primarily as a continuous outcome throughout the statistical analysis [4, 6].

Flourishing scale

Psychological well-being was measured using the Flourishing Scale (FS), consisting of 8 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 8 to 56, with higher scores reflecting greater psychological well-being, including aspects such as purpose in life, optimism, and social relationships [18].

Digital Health Literacy Scale (DHLS)

Digital health literacy was assessed using a validated multidimensional Digital Health Literacy Scale that measures an individual’s ability to obtain, understand, evaluate, and appropriately apply digital health information during health-related decision making. The questionnaire consists of multiple domains assessing information searching, critical evaluation of online health information, navigation of digital health resources, and application of digital information to health management. Each item is rated using a Likert-type response scale, with higher total scores indicating better digital health literacy. The total score ranges are consistent with previous validation studies of this multidimensional instrument. In the present study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80). The questionnaire was administered according to the published scoring instructions, and all statistical analyses were performed using the total score [19].

Artificial Intelligence Health Acceptance Questionnaire (AIHAQ)

Participants’ acceptance of AI-assisted healthcare technologies was assessed using the Artificial Intelligence Health Acceptance Questionnaire (AIHAQ), an existing questionnaire adapted from the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). The instrument was not developed specifically for this study and has previously been used to evaluate users’ acceptance of AI-enabled healthcare technologies.

The AIHAQ consists of 12 items distributed across four domains:

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted through face-to-face structured interviews by trained research personnel in outpatient clinics and community healthcare settings. Participants were first screened for eligibility, and those meeting the inclusion criteria were invited to participate. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection. For participants with limited literacy or visual impairment, questionnaires were administered verbally by trained investigators using standardized interviewing procedures to minimize interviewer bias. Each completed questionnaire was reviewed immediately by the research team to ensure completeness and consistency. When missing responses were identified during the interview, participants were asked to complete the omitted items whenever possible. Questionnaires with incomplete baseline information that could not be completed were excluded before final enrollment. Data were independently entered by two researchers using Microsoft Excel 2016, followed by double-entry verification to identify transcription errors. Additional data-cleaning procedures included range checks, logical consistency checks, and verification of implausible values against the original questionnaires. After completion of data cleaning, the final analytical dataset contained no missing values; therefore, complete-case analysis was performed, and no statistical imputation methods were required.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the overall SMBG barrier score measured using the structured 16-item SMBG Barrier Assessment Questionnaire. Possible scores ranged from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater perceived barriers to self-monitoring of blood glucose. This variable was treated as a continuous dependent variable in statistical analyses. In addition to the total score, domain-specific scores (psychological, physical, economic, and knowledge-related barriers) were analyzed to identify specific areas of difficulty.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included the association between SMBG barriers and psychological well-being as measured by the FS, and digital health literacy as measured by the DHLS [18].

Independent variables (Predictors)

Independent variables included sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education level, marital status), clinical characteristics (duration of diabetes, treatment modality, complications), and psychosocial factors (FS score, DHLS score).

Derived outcomes

Additional derived outcomes included subgroup comparisons of SMBG barrier scores across different categories (e.g., education levels, treatment types), and identification of independent predictors of SMBG barriers through regression modeling.

Artificial intelligence-based predictive modeling

To enhance the identification of elderly diabetic patients at risk of experiencing significant barriers to SMBG, an AI-driven predictive framework was developed. Machine learning algorithms were employed to analyze multidimensional data comprising sociodemographic characteristics, clinical factors, psychological well-being, digital health literacy, and AI readiness indicators. The predictor variables included age, sex, education level, marital status, living status, duration of diabetes, treatment modality, diabetes complications, diabetes education history, FS score, DHLS score, and Artificial Intelligence Health Acceptance Questionnaire (AIHAQ) score. The primary outcome variable was high SMBG barrier status. Because no clinically established threshold exists for defining “high SMBG barriers,” participants were classified into high- and low-barrier groups using the sample median as an exploratory threshold for binary prediction modeling. We acknowledge that dichotomization of continuous variables reduces statistical information and may decrease predictive efficiency. Therefore, this approach was adopted solely for exploratory machine-learning classification rather than clinical decision-making. Before model development, data preprocessing procedures were performed, including handling missing values, normalization of continuous variables, and encoding of categorical variables.

Categorical variables were transformed into numerical representations using one-hot encoding. The complete dataset was randomly divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. The machine-learning analyses were conducted as an exploratory proof-of-concept investigation intended to evaluate the feasibility of AI-assisted risk stratification rather than to establish a clinically validated prediction model. Therefore, the reported performance metrics should be interpreted as preliminary findings requiring confirmation in larger external datasets.

The primary outcome of this study was the continuous SMBG Barriers Scale score, which was analyzed using correlation analysis and multiple linear regression. For the exploratory machine-learning component only, a binary outcome was created using the sample median because no validated clinical cutoff values are currently available for the SMBG Barriers Scale. This dichotomization was performed solely to evaluate the feasibility of machine-learning classification models and should not be interpreted as representing a clinically meaningful threshold. We acknowledge that dichotomizing continuous variables results in loss of statistical information and may reduce predictive accuracy. Therefore, the AI analyses should be considered exploratory proof-of-concept models rather than clinically validated prediction tools. Future studies should establish validated clinical cutoff values or develop machine-learning models capable of directly predicting the continuous SMBG barrier score using regression-based approaches.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Independent-samples t-tests or one-way ANOVA were used for group comparisons of continuous variables, while chi-square (χ²) tests were used for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships between SMBG barriers, flourishing, and digital health literacy scores. Variables with a P-value < 0.10 in univariate analyses were included in a multiple linear regression model using the enter method to identify independent predictors of SMBG barriers. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF < 10 indicating acceptable levels. Model fit was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Digital health literacy scores were calculated according to the published scoring algorithm of the multidimensional instrument, and the total score was used in correlation analyses, multiple linear regression, and machine-learning model development. The internal consistency reliability of the SMBG Barrier Assessment Questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient before inferential analyses.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 200 community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes mellitus were included in the final analysis. The mean age of participants was 68.9 ± 6.7 years, with a range of 60 to 85 years. Among them, 112 (56.0%) were male and 88 (44.0%) were female. The majority of participants were married (72.5%) and lived with family members (78.0%). Regarding educational status, 38.0% had primary education or below, 42.5% had secondary education, and 19.5% had tertiary education. The mean duration of diabetes was 10.8 ± 5.6 years. Approximately 46.5% of participants were receiving oral hypoglycemic agents, 32.0% insulin therapy, and 21.5% combination therapy. Diabetes-related complications were present in 58.0% of patients, and 61.5% reported having received prior diabetes education (Table 1).

Education level and previous diabetes education also contributed significantly to the model, emphasizing the importance of health knowledge and self-management training in promoting effective blood glucose monitoring practices.

To enhance the interpretability of the AI model and understand how individual predictors influenced prediction outcomes, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis was performed (Fig. 6). As presented in Table 7, Digital Health Literacy (DHLS) demonstrated the highest mean absolute SHAP value (0.364), indicating that it exerted the strongest influence on the prediction of SMBG barriers. Higher DHLS scores were associated with a reduced likelihood of experiencing significant barriers, suggesting that digitally competent individuals are better equipped to access educational resources, operate glucose-monitoring devices, and interpret health information effectively. The FS score exhibited the second-highest SHAP value (0.292), confirming that psychological well-being plays a critical role in diabetes self-management. Participants with greater emotional resilience, life satisfaction, and social support were less likely to report difficulties with SMBG. Similarly, AI Acceptance Score showed a substantial contribution to model predictions (SHAP value = 0.254), indicating that positive attitudes toward AI-assisted healthcare technologies may facilitate engagement in regular blood glucose monitoring and self-care activities.

Educational attainment and prior diabetes education also demonstrated protective effects, with higher education levels and exposure to diabetes self-management training associated with lower SMBG barrier risk. Conversely, the presence of diabetes-related complications and living alone were identified as important risk factors that increased the likelihood of elevated SMBG barriers. Patients with complications may experience physical and emotional challenges that hinder effective glucose monitoring, while those living alone may lack the social support necessary to maintain consistent self-management behaviors.

Five-fold cross-validation results

To evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the AI model, five-fold cross-validation was performed using the Random Forest algorithm. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five equal subsets, with four subsets used for model training and the remaining subset used for validation during each iteration. This process was repeated five times so that each subset served as the validation set once (Fig. 7).

As presented in Table 8, the Random Forest model demonstrated consistently high predictive performance across all validation folds, with accuracy values ranging from 87.1% to 89.7%. The highest accuracy was observed in Fold 5 (89.7%), while the lowest accuracy was recorded in Fold 4 (87.1%). The mean cross-validation accuracy was 88.5 ± 1.1%, indicating minimal variation between folds and excellent model stability. The relatively small standard deviation suggests that the model maintained consistent predictive performance regardless of the specific training-validation split used. This finding indicates a low likelihood of overfitting and demonstrates that the model can generalize effectively to unseen patient data. The cross-validation results therefore support the reliability of the Random Forest algorithm as a predictive tool for identifying elderly diabetic patients at risk of experiencing significant barriers to self-monitoring of blood glucose.

Furthermore, the stable performance observed across all folds confirms the robustness of the selected predictor variables, including digital health literacy, psychological well-being, AI acceptance, educational level, diabetes education, and clinical characteristics. Collectively, these findings suggest that the AI-based predictive framework has substantial potential for application in community diabetes management programs, enabling healthcare professionals to identify high-risk individuals and implement targeted interventions before significant SMBG barriers develop (Table 8).

Discussion

The present study provides valuable insights into the multifaceted barriers to SMBG among community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes mellitus and demonstrates the utility of integrating traditional epidemiological methods with AI for risk prediction. The mean SMBG Barriers Scale score of 47.6 ± 9.8 indicated a moderate level of perceived barriers, with knowledge/skill deficits emerging as the most prominent domain. This finding aligns with previous research highlighting that elderly patients frequently struggle with technical aspects of glucometer use, result interpretation, and troubleshooting [20, 21].

A key observation was the significant negative correlation between SMBG barriers and both psychological well-being (FS, r = − 0.42) and digital health literacy (DHLS, r = − 0.51). These results underscore the critical role of psychosocial and technological competencies in diabetes self-management. Higher flourishing scores, reflecting greater purpose, optimism, and social connectedness, were associated with fewer barriers, consistent with evidence that positive psychological resources buffer against diabetes-related distress and promote adherence behaviors. Similarly, the strong inverse relationship with digital health literacy supports growing literature indicating that the ability to navigate digital health resources enhances engagement with glucose monitoring technologies, particularly amid the shift toward connected devices and telehealth.

Multiple linear regression analysis identified digital health literacy (β = −0.38), FS score (β = −0.25), higher education, and prior diabetes education as independent protective factors, while diabetes complications and living alone were significant risk factors. These findings extend prior studies by quantifying the relative contributions of these variables in a community elderly cohort. Living alone may exacerbate barriers through reduced social support and accountability, a pattern observed in geriatric diabetes research. The presence of complications likely compounds physical and psychological burdens, creating a vicious cycle that hinders consistent SMBG.

The AI component of this study represents a novel contribution. The Random Forest model achieved superior performance (accuracy 88.5%, AUC 0.921) compared to XGBoost and SVM, demonstrating robust predictive capability for identifying individuals with high SMBG barriers. Feature importance and SHAP analyses consistently ranked digital health literacy, psychological well-being, and AI acceptance as the top predictors. These results highlight the value of explainable AI in healthcare, allowing clinicians to understand “why” a patient is at risk rather than receiving black-box predictions. The inclusion of AI acceptance as a strong predictor is particularly timely, as trust in AI-assisted tools (e.g., smart glucometers or predictive alerts) may facilitate adoption among technology-hesitant elderly populations.

Five-fold cross-validation confirmed model stability (mean accuracy 88.5 ± 1.1%), suggesting good generalizability within similar community settings. This performance compares favourably with other ML studies in diabetes self-management, which typically report AUC values between 0.80 and 0.90. By combining sociodemographic, clinical, psychological, and digital variables, the model offers a practical tool for risk stratification in community chronic disease management programs.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships observed. Convenience sampling from a single regional hospital may limit generalizability to broader populations or different healthcare systems. Self-reported measures are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. Additionally, although the sample size was adequate for the analyses performed, a larger, multi-center study would enhance statistical power and external validity. The AI models were developed and validated on the same cohort without external testing, warranting future prospective validation. Limitations related to the machine-learning analyses should be acknowledged. First, the overall sample size (n = 200) and particularly the independent testing cohort (n = 40) were relatively small for predictive model development, limiting the precision and generalizability of the reported performance metrics. Second, although five-fold cross-validation was used to improve internal validation, no independent external validation cohort was available. Third, the prediction models should therefore be regarded as exploratory proof-of-concept models rather than clinically validated decision-support tools. Future multicenter studies involving substantially larger patient populations are required before clinical implementation can be considered.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the observed relationships. Second, this study employed a convenience sampling strategy from a single regional hospital in Chun’an County, China. Although this approach facilitated efficient participant recruitment, it may have introduced selection bias, as individuals who regularly attended outpatient clinics or participated in community diabetes management programs were more likely to be enrolled. These participants may have better healthcare access, higher health awareness, and greater motivation for diabetes self-management than elderly patients who seldom seek medical care. Consequently, vulnerable groups, such as individuals living in remote rural communities, those with limited healthcare access, severe functional impairment, lower socioeconomic status, or poor treatment adherence, may have been underrepresented. In addition, because all participants were recruited from one hospital within a single county, the sample may not adequately reflect the demographic, socioeconomic, cultural, and healthcare characteristics of the broader elderly diabetic population across other regions of China. Regional differences in healthcare resources, diabetes education programs, digital health infrastructure, and community support services may influence the barriers to self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG). Therefore, the external validity and generalizability of the present findings should be interpreted with caution. Third, self-reported questionnaires are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, which may have affected participants’ responses. Fourth, although the sample size was adequate for the planned statistical analyses, larger multicenter studies involving more heterogeneous populations are required to strengthen external validity. Finally, although internal validation using five-fold cross-validation was performed, the AI prediction models were developed using data from the same study population and were not externally validated. Therefore, the reported predictive performance should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive. Future studies should employ probability-based sampling methods, such as stratified or multistage random sampling, recruit participants from multiple hospitals and community healthcare settings across different geographic regions, and conduct external validation of the prediction models to improve representativeness, reduce sampling bias, and enhance the generalizability of the findings.

From a clinical perspective, these findings advocate for holistic, person-centred interventions. Targeted programs addressing digital health literacy through simplified training, enhancing psychological well-being via mindfulness or peer support groups, and providing tailored diabetes education could substantially reduce SMBG barriers. Integrating the Random Forest-based prediction tool into electronic health records may enable proactive identification of at-risk elderly patients and allocation of limited healthcare resources. Policymakers should consider subsidising glucose monitoring supplies and promoting age-friendly digital health solutions to alleviate economic and technological barriers.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs to examine how these barriers evolve over time and whether AI-guided interventions improve actual SMBG adherence and glycaemic outcomes. Incorporating additional variables such as health literacy (beyond digital), caregiver support, and cost-effectiveness analyses would further strengthen the evidence base. The integration of multimodal data, including wearable sensor outputs, could refine predictive accuracy even further. Future studies should employ longitudinal and multicenter designs involving participants from diverse geographic regions and healthcare settings to enhance the external validity and generalizability of the findings beyond Chun’an First People’s Hospital. External validation of the proposed AI model in larger and more heterogeneous populations is also recommended before widespread clinical implementation.

In conclusion, this study illuminates the complex interplay of factors influencing SMBG barriers among elderly diabetic patients and showcases the potential of explainable AI to translate research findings into actionable clinical tools. By addressing modifiable determinants particularly digital health literacy and psychological well-being, healthcare providers can empower community-dwelling older adults to achieve better diabetes self-management and improved quality of life.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study reveals that community-dwelling elderly patients with diabetes mellitus experience moderate barriers to SMBG, with a mean score of 47.6 ± 9.8. Knowledge/skill deficits and psychological resistance emerged as the most prominent domains. Digital health literacy (β = −0.38) and psychological well-being (FS, β = −0.25) were the strongest protective factors against SMBG barriers, while diabetes-related complications and living alone significantly increased risk. The integration of AI demonstrated substantial value, with the Random Forest model achieving superior predictive performance (accuracy 88.5%, AUC-ROC 0.921). SHAP analysis confirmed digital health literacy, psychological flourishing, and AI acceptance as the most influential predictors, offering explainable insights for clinical decision-making. These findings underscore the importance of addressing psychosocial, educational, and technological determinants in diabetes self-management among older adults. Implementing targeted interventions such as simplified digital training, psychological support programs, and personalized education could effectively reduce barriers and improve glycaemic control. The developed AI predictive tool holds promise for integration into community healthcare systems to enable early risk identification and resource optimization. Future longitudinal and multi-center studies are recommended to validate the model externally and evaluate the long-term impact of AI-guided interventions on actual SMBG adherence and clinical outcomes.
