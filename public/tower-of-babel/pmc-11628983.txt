Predictive validity of interleukin 6 (IL-6) for the mortality in critically ill COVID-19 patients with the B.1.617.2 (Delta) variant in Vietnam: a single-centre, cross-sectional study
Authors: Chi Van Nguyen, Chinh Quoc Luong, Co Xuan Dao, My Ha Nguyen, Dung Thi Pham, Nhung Hong Khuat, Quynh Thi Pham, Dat Tien Hoang, Anh Diep Nguyen, Phuong Minh Nguyen, Duong Dai Cao, Dung Thuy Pham, Thai Quoc Nguyen, Vuong Minh Nong, Dung Tuan Dang, Dat Tuan Nguyen, Vinh Duc Nguyen, Thuan Quang Le, Viet Khoi Nguyen, Hung Duc Ngo, Dung Van Nguyen, Thach The Pham, Dung Tien Nguyen, Nguyen Trung Nguyen, Tan Dang Do, Nhung Thi Huynh, Nga Thu Phan, Cuong Duy Nguyen, Khoi Hong Vo, Thom Thi Vu, Cuong Duy Do, Tuan Quoc Dang, Giap Van Vu, Tan Cong Nguyen, Son Ngoc Do
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the serum IL-6 levels and their rate of change in predicting the mortality of critically ill patients with COVID-19 in Vietnam.DesignA single-centre, cross-sectional study.SettingAn Intensive Care Centre for the Treatment of Critically Ill Patients with COVID-19 in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.ParticipantsWe included patients aged 18 years or older who were critically ill with COVID-19 and presented to the study centre from 30 July 2021 to 15 October 2021. We excluded patients who did not have serum IL-6 measurements between admission and the end of the first day.Primary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was hospital all-cause mortality.ResultsOf 90 patients, 41.1% were men, the median age was 60.5 years (Q1–Q3: 52.0–71.0), and 76.7% of patients died in the hospital. Elevated IL-6 levels were observed on admission (41.79 pg/mL; Q1–Q3: 20.68–106.27) and on the third day after admission (72.00 pg/mL; Q1–Q3: 26.98–186.50), along with a significant rate of change in IL-6 during that period (839.5%; SD: 2753.2). While admission IL-6 level (areas under the receiver operator characteristic curve (AUROC): 0.610 (95% CI: 0.459 to 0.761); cut-off value ≥15.8 pg/mL) and rate of change in IL-6 on the third day of admission (AUROC: 0.586 (95% CI: 0.420 to 0.751); cut-off value ≥−58.7%) demonstrated poor discriminatory ability in predicting hospital mortality, the third day IL-6 rate of change from admission ≥−58.7% (adjusted OR: 12.812; 95% CI: 2.104 to 78.005) emerged as an independent predictor of hospital mortality.ConclusionsThis study focused on a highly selected cohort of critically ill COVID-19 patients with a high IL-6 level and mortality rate. Despite the poor discriminatory value of admission IL-6 levels, the rate of change in IL-6 proved valuable in predicting mortality. To identify critically ill COVID-19 patients with the highest risk for mortality, monitoring the serial serum IL-6 measurements and observing the rate of change in serum IL-6 levels over time are needed.

Introduction

By the end of 2019, the SARS-CoV-2 emerged in Wuhan, China. Since then, it has swiftly disseminated globally, evolving into a global public health crisis. This disease manifests as COVID-19, causing pneumonia infections and posing a significant threat to global health.1 Notably, COVID-19 can incite a cytokine storm both locally and systemically due to overactive immune responses and the uncontrolled release of cytokines.2 3 The term ‘cytokine storm’ serves as a descriptive expression that encompasses a range of events, which can ultimately lead to multiorgan failure and fatal outcomes.4 Patients admitted to hospitals with poor prognostic features often face serious complications, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multiorgan failure and blood clotting, leading to significant mortality.5 Accumulating evidence indicates that the severity of COVID-19 is associated with elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines in the blood following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Of note, among these cytokines, serum interleukin 6 (IL-6) levels strongly correlate with disease mortality when comparing COVID-19 survivors and non-survivors.5 6 This finding suggests that fatal COVID-19 may involve a cytokine release syndrome triggered by a cytokine storm, with IL-6 playing a critical role.79

There remains an ongoing debate about the reliability of serum IL-6 levels in accurately assessing the severity of COVID-19 and predicting mortality risk.1016 According to a previous study, the initial level of serum IL-6 can effectively predict the risk of death in the hospital for patients with COVID-19.10 However, a meta-analysis suggests that although serum IL-6 is a useful diagnostic marker for predicting severe disease, it does not appear to be associated with COVID-19 mortality.11 Interestingly, a retrospective study reveals a significant difference in serum IL-6 levels between survivors and non-survivors over time, with the maximum serum IL-6 value serving as a significant predictor of intensive care unit (ICU) mortality in critically ill patients with COVID-19.12 As a result, we hypothesise that, in addition to highly elevated serum IL-6 levels, the rate of change of serum IL-6 over time could be a crucial factor in predicting mortality among COVID-19 patients, irrespective of illness severity or treatment protocols. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the serum IL-6 levels and their rate of change in predicting the mortality of critically ill COVID-19 patients with the B.1.617.2 (Delta) variant in Vietnam.

Methods

Study design and setting

From 30 July 2021 to 15 October 2021, we conducted a cross-sectional study on critically ill COVID-19 patients with the Delta variant at the Intensive Care Centre for the Treatment of Critically Ill Patients with COVID-19 in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This centre was a field hospital with 360 beds and was affiliated with the Bach Mai Hospital in Hanoi, Vietnam. Bach Mai Hospital is designated as the central hospital in northern Vietnam by the Ministry of Health of Vietnam.17 It is a large general hospital with 3200 beds.

In July 2021, Ho Chi Minh City faced a severe Delta variant COVID-19 outbreak, which led to a high demand for ICUs for critically ill patients.18 19 To address this situation, the Vietnam Ministry of Health decided to set up a special Intensive Care Centre for the Treatment of Critically Ill Patients with COVID-19 on 30 July 2021, where could only receive patients transferred from lower-level hospitals. The centre was under the management of Bach Mai Hospital and was called Field Hospital Number 16. The centre had a capacity of 500 beds at its peak and later reduced it to 360. The centre stopped operating on 30 April 2022, after the COVID-19 cases declined. The director of Bach Mai Hospital also served as the director of the Intensive Care Centre for the Treatment of Critically Ill Patients with COVID-19. Likewise, two deputy directors of the centre were from the Centre for Emergency Medicine of Bach Mai Hospital in Hanoi City and the Director Board of Hung Vuong Hospital in Ho Chi Minh City. The centre had 968 medical staff and volunteers in total, and more than half of them (542 medical staff) were from Bach Mai Hospital. They came from different departments and centres of Bach Mai Hospital, such as emergency medicine, critical care medicine, tropical diseases, respiratory, clinical nutrition, nephro-urology and dialysis, neurology, cardiovascular medicine, infection control, haematology and blood transfusion, radiology, biochemistry and microbiology.

Participants

This study included patients aged 18 years or older who were critically ill with COVID-19 and presented to the Intensive Care Centre for the Treatment of Critically Ill Patients with COVID-19. We defined a case of COVID-19 as a person with laboratory confirmation of COVID-19 infection, regardless of clinical or epidemiological criteria.20 Laboratory confirmation of COVID-19 infection was based on a positive real-time reverse transcription-PCR test using samples obtained from the respiratory tract by nasopharyngeal swab, throat or mouth swab, or tracheal lavage fluid.21 We excluded patients who did not have serum IL-6 measurements between admission and the end of the first day.

Data collection

The data for each study patient were recorded using unified case record forms (CRFs). Data entry into the study database was conducted via the KoboToolbox (Harvard Humanitarian Initiative, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA), an innovative open-source platform for collecting, managing and visualising data. This platform minimises data entry errors through real-time validation during collection. To protect patient confidentiality, patient identifiers were not entered into the database.

Variables

The CRF contained four sections, which included variables mainly based on the COVID-19 Treatment Guidelines of the National Institutes of Health22 and collected by fully trained clinicians, such as information on:

The first section focused on baseline characteristics, such as prehospital care (eg, prior hospitalisation, prehospital airway and prehospital oxygen), demographics (ie, age and gender), documented comorbidities (eg, cerebrovascular disease, chronic cardiac failure, coronary artery disease/myocardial infarction, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/asthma, other chronic pulmonary disease, tuberculosis, chronic renal failure and diabetes mellitus) and details of admission. We also used the 19 comorbidity categories to compute the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) Score, which measures the predicted mortality rate based on the presence of comorbidities.23 A score of zero indicates that no comorbidities were detected; the higher the score, the higher the expected mortality rate is.2325

The second section comprised characteristics on admission, such as vital signs (eg, heart rate, respiration rate, arterial blood pressure and body temperature), laboratory parameters (eg, IL-6), chest X-ray (CXR) findings (eg, bilateral opacities, number of involved quadrants and severity of COVID-19 based on lung involvement), gas exchange (eg, the partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) and carbon dioxide (PaCO2) in the arterial blood, the blood potential hydrogen (pH) and the ratio of partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood to the fraction of inspiratory oxygen concentration (PaO2/FiO2)) and severity scoring systems (eg, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) Score,26 Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) Score27 and Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory Rate ≥30 breaths/min, Blood Pressure <90 mm Hg (Systolic) or <60 mm Hg (Diastolic), Age ≥65 Years (CURB-65) Score.28 Serum IL-6 was measured using the Elecsys IL-6 immunoassay (Roche Cobas E411 analyser), an in vitro diagnostic test for the quantitative determination of IL-6 in human serum and plasma. The measuring range is from 1.5 to 5000 pg/mL. Data on the IL-6 levels were collected prospectively on a CRF from the time point of admission to the end of the first day and on each calendar day (eg, on the third day of admission). In this study, we defined IL-6 level as 5000 pg/mL when values were above the measuring range (>5000 pg/mL). We adapted and simplified the Radiographic Assessment of Lung Oedema (RALE) Score29 to create a simplified RALE score for assessing the severity of COVID-19 based on lung involvement. This approach has also been used in prior published studies to quantify the extent of pulmonary infection in COVID-19 cases.3033 In a frontal CXR, the simplified RALE scoring system assigns a score to each lung, ranging from 0 to 4. This score reflects the attendance of specific abnormalities. The system divides each lung into four zones, and abnormalities such as consolidation, ground glass opacification and reticular interstitial thickening are assigned a score of 1 in each zone. A score of 0 indicates no involvement, while a score of 1 corresponds to less than 25% involvement, a score of 2 represents 25% to less than 50% involvement, a score of 3 signifies 50% to less than 75% involvement and a score of 4 indicates 75% or greater involvement. The overall severity score is the sum of points from both lungs, with a maximum possible score of 8. Prospectively, we collected all necessary data elements for calculating the SOFA Score, APACHE II Score and CURB-65 Score within the time window from admission until 24 hours later and entered these data into an online CRF and stored them in a database for subsequent analysis.

HAP is defined as pneumonia that occurs 48 hours or more after admission and does not appear to be incubating at the time of admission.34 To diagnose and classify ARDS, we applied the Berlin criteria, which categorise ARDS severity based on the PaO2/FiO2 ratio as follows: mild (200 <PaO2/FiO2≤300 mm Hg), moderate (100 <PaO2/FiO2≤200 mm Hg) and severe (PaO2/FiO2≤100 mm Hg), with a minimum positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH2O applied to the lungs at the end of each breath.35 36 Additionally, septic shock is identified as a clinical construct of sepsis characterised by persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure ≥65 mm Hg, along with a serum lactate level >2 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) despite adequate volume resuscitation.37 Lastly, thromboembolism encompasses venous thromboembolism (ie, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), arterial events (ie, stroke, limb ischaemia and myocardial infarction) and microvascular thrombosis (eg, microvascular thrombosis in the lungs).22

We followed all patients till hospital discharge or death in the ICU/hospital, whichever was earliest.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was hospital mortality, which we defined as death from any cause during the hospitalisation. We also examined the following secondary outcomes: complications and hospital lengths of stay.

Sample size

In this cross-sectional study, the primary outcome was hospital mortality. Statistical analyses

We used IBM SPSS Statistics V.22.0 (IBM) and Analyse-it statistical software (Analyse-it Software, Leeds, UK) for data analysis. We report the data as numbers (no.) and percentages (%) for categorical variables and medians and IQRs (Q1–Q3) or means and SDs for continuous variables. Furthermore, comparisons were made between survival and death in the hospital for each variable using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test or one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables.

We calculated the rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission using the formula: rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission=100%×((serum IL-6 on the third day)−(admission serum IL-6))/(admission serum IL-6). To evaluate how well the CCI Score, simplified RALE score, admission serum IL-6 level, rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission, SOFA score, APACHE II score and CURB-65 score could differentiate between patients who survived and those who died in the hospital, we plotted receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated areas under the ROC curve (AUROCs). We used the ROC curve analysis to determine the best cut-off value for each level, rate or score, which was the point that gave the highest Youden’s index (ie, sensitivity+specificity−1). Then, we divided the patients into two groups based on their level, rate or score: one that was less than the cut-off value or another greater than or equal to the cut-off value. Additionally, we compared the AUROCs of serum IL-6 level and rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission with those of the CCI score, simplified RALE score, SOFA score APACHE II score and CURB-65 score to see which one was better at predicting deaths in the hospital using the Z-statistics. Finally, we calculated correlation coefficients (Rs) using Spearman’s r to explore the relationship between admission serum IL-6 level and each score.

We assessed the association of the serum IL-6 level and its rate of change with hospital mortality using logistic regression analysis. To reduce the number of predictors and the multicollinearity issue and resolve the overfitting, we used different methods to select variables as follows: (a) we put all variables of demographics, documented comorbidities, clinical characteristics, CXR findings, laboratory investigations, gas exchange, severity scoring system, first-day respiratory support, adjunctive therapies, complications into the univariable logistic regression model; (b) we selected variables if the p value was <0.05 in the univariable analysis between death and survival in the hospital, as well as those that are clinically crucial, to put in the multivariable logistic regression model. These variables included demographics (ie, age ≥60 years, gender (male)), documented comorbidities (ie, CCI score ≥cut off value), CXR findings (ie, simplified RALE score ≥cut off value), laboratory investigations (ie, admission serum IL-6 level ≥cut off value and rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission ≥cut off value), severity scoring system (ie, SOFA score ≥cut off value, APACHE II score ≥cut off value and CURB-65 score ≥cut off value). Using a stepwise backward elimination method, we started with the full multivariable logistic regression model that included the selected variables. This method then deleted the least statistically significant variables stepwise from the full model until all remaining variables were independently associated with hospital mortality in the final model. We presented the ORs and 95% CIs in the univariable logistic regression model and the adjusted ORs (AORs) and 95% CIs in the multivariable logistic regression model.

The significance levels were two tailed for all analyses, and we considered the p<0.05 as a statistically significant value.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

Of 90 patients, 41.1% were men, and the median age was 60.5 years (Q1–Q3: 52.0–71.0) (table 1). Among all patients, elevated serum IL-6 levels were observed both on admission (41.79 pg/mL; Q1–Q3: 20.68–106.27) and on the third day after admission (72.00 pg/mL; Q1–Q3: 26.98–186.50), along with a great rate of change in serum IL-6 during that period (839.5%; SD: 2753.2) (table 2). The median simplified RALE score was 8.0 (Q1–Q3: 6.0–8.0), the median SOFA Score was 4.0 (Q1–Q3: 2.0–5.0), the median APACHE II score was 8.0 (Q1–Q3: 4.0–12.0) and the median CURB-65 score was 1.0 (Q1–Q3: 1.0–2.0) (table 2). Overall, 76.7% (69/90) of patients died in the hospital (tables1 2). Overall prognostic performance of the serum IL-6 level and the severity scoring systems

The simplified RALE score (AUROC: 0.725 (95% CI: 0.588 to 0.863); cut-off value ≥5.5; sensitivity: 94.1%; specificity: 42.9%; PAUROC=0.002), SOFA score (AUROC: 0.733 (95% CI: 0.582 to 0.884); cut-off value ≥3.5; sensitivity: 61.7%; specificity: 78.6%; PAUROC=0.007) and CURB-65 score (AUROC: 0.772 (95% CI: 0.654 to 0.889); cut-off value ≥0.5; sensitivity: 91.2%; specificity: 47.4%; PAUROC<0.001) demonstrated the good discriminatory ability in predicting hospital mortality (table 3). However, the admission serum IL-6 level (AUROC: 0.610 (95% CI: 0.459 to 0.761); cut-off value ≥15.8 pg/mL; sensitivity: 84.1%; specificity: 42.9%; PAUROC=0.128) and rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day of admission (AUROC: 0.586 (95% CI: 0.420 to 0.751); cut-off value ≥−58.7%; sensitivity: 87.3%; specificity: 44.4%; PAUROC=0.270) showed the poor discrimination (table 3). Association of the serum IL-6 level and its rate of change with hospital mortality

In the univariable logistic regression analysis, the admission serum IL-6 level ≥15.8 pg/mL (OR: 3.955; 95% CI: 1.345 to 11.623; p=0.012) was significantly associated with an increased risk of hospital mortality (table 5). Discussion

In the present study, the admission serum IL-6 level and the rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day of admission demonstrated poor discriminatory ability in predicting hospital mortality (table 3). However, the admission serum IL-6 level remained weakly correlated with the simplified RALE score, CURB-65 score and SOFA score (table 4). Moreover, it was found that the serum IL-6 level ≥15.8 pg/mL on admission and the rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission ≥−58.7% were significantly associated with the higher risk of hospital mortality (table 5). Among these, the rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission ≥−58.7% was identified as an independent predictor of hospital mortality (table 5).

In this study, we found that over three-fourths of critically ill COVID-19 patients with the Delta variant died in the hospital (tables1 2). This rate is higher than the rates reported in other Vietnamese studies conducted in Ho Chi Minh City (52.2%; 263/504)19 and Binh Duong Province (64.2%; 97/151),39 as well as an Indian study (54.6%; 306/560).38 These disparities may arise from variations in the inclusion criteria across studies. For instance, our study only included critically ill patients with COVID-19 whose serum IL-6 levels were assessed on admission. Moreover, our hospital mortality rate greatly surpasses that reported in a Swedish study (38.2%; 190/498).40 The variation observed may stem from differences in the patients, pathogens and clinical ability to care for critically ill patients between low-income to middle-income and high-income country settings.4144 Additionally, the study centre almost only admitted critically ill patients with COVID-19 (91.5%, 75/90; Table S1 in online supplemental file 1) who encountered difficulties accessing treatment at lower-level hospitals, with a low rate of intubation (15.5%, 13/84; Table S1) in online supplemental file 1) and mechanical ventilation (13.5%, 12/89; Table S1) in online supplemental file 1) during transportation. Transferring critically ill patients with COVID-19 from a local to a central hospital may result in the worsening of their critical condition. Patient transfers in Vietnam may occur without intubation, ventilation, PEEP and other medical interventions.4547 Although data on transfer conditions are limited, a prior survey highlighted several risk factors associated with increased patient transfers and suboptimal prehospital care quality.48 Therefore, the cohort of the present study is likely to be overestimated in the hospital mortality rate.

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the SARS-CoV-2, presents a significant global health challenge. This virus primarily impacts the respiratory system, often resulting in pneumonia for most patients. In approximately 19.5% of cases, it leads to ARDS,49 a severe condition associated with elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, commonly known as a cytokine storm. Cytokine storm is defined as acute overproduction and uncontrolled release of proinflammatory markers, both locally and systemically. One of these markers is IL-6, which can reach serum levels as extremely high as 96 pg/mL (SD: 162).3 The present study reveals that elevated serum IL-6 levels were observed on admission and again on the third day after admission (table 2). Consequently, most of our critically ill patients with COVID-19 developed ARDS (94.1%, 80/85; Table S5 in online supplemental file 1). It is widely recognised that IL-6 serves as a biomarker for the severity of COVID-19.5053 It also predicts unfavourable clinical outcomes, including mortality, as well as for respiratory failure, and the need for mechanical ventilation.54 In a Thailandist study, serum IL-6 levels at ICU admission are associated with disease severity in COVID-19 and are predictive of worse respiratory outcomes and mortality in ICU.16 Although the present study did not demonstrate a correlation between the admission serum IL-6 level and the admission PaO2/FiO2 (table 4), it found that the admission serum IL-6 level remained weakly correlated with the simplified RALE score, SOFA score, APACHE II score and CURB-65 Score (table 4).

There is accumulating evidence that suggests an association between markedly elevated levels of inflammatory markers (such as D-dimer and ferritin) and proinflammatory cytokines (including IL-6) with critical and fatal cases of COVID-19.55 According to a Spanish study,10 the initial serum IL-6 level, with an AUROC of 0.74 and a 95% CI of 0.64–0.84, has a good discriminatory ability for predicting hospital mortality in COVID-19 patients. Specifically, an initial serum IL-6 level >86 pg/mL, with an OR of 7.67 and a 95% CI of 3.61 to 16.30, is significantly associated with an increased risk of hospital mortality.10 Furthermore, this elevated IL-6 level is also identified as an independent predictor of hospital mortality.10 In contrast, the present study reveals that the serum IL-6 level on admission and the rate of change in serum IL-6 on the third day after admission had a poor discriminatory ability to predict hospital mortality (table 3). This difference might be due to a different population between the two studies. However, the present study shows that the serum IL-6 level ≥15.8 pg/mL on admission and the rate of change in serum IL-6 on third day after admission ≥−58.7% were significantly associated with a higher risk of hospital mortality (table 5). Among these, the rate of change in serum IL-6 on third day after admission ≥−58.7% was identified as an independent predictor of hospital mortality (table 5). Our findings are supported by the previously published studies, which report that (1) there is a significant difference in serum IL-6 levels between survivors and non-survivors over time, with the maximum serum IL-6 value serving as a significant predictor of ICU mortality in critically ill COVID-19 patients12 and (2) a reduction in the serum IL-6 level of at least 86% within 24 hours from ICU admission is a survival predictor for patients with sepsis and septic shock.56 Hence, monitoring the serial measurements and observing the rate of change in serum IL-6 levels over time can help clinicians identify critically ill COVID-19 patients with the highest risk for mortality.

The present study has certain limitations. First, it was conducted at a single centre in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, focusing on a highly selected group of cases. The study site was urgently established in Ho Chi Minh City in response to the Delta variant epidemic, which posed significant challenges for medical providers dealing with a large influx of COVID-19 patients. Additionally, all COVID-19 patients were transferred from various hospitals within the Ho Chi Minh City area, and the treatments they received before admission influenced their disease severity on enrolment. As a result, these elements hinder the seamless integration of prehospital and hospital treatment procedures, as well as the collection of clinical data for surveillance, quality enhancement and research purposes. Additionally, they introduce an implicit selection bias and incomplete patient inclusion in the study database, which could potentially result in an overestimation of the mortality rate. Second, we conducted the present study during the Delta variant epidemic in Vietnam; however, no included patients with COVID-19 underwent genomic analysis to confirm infection with a SARS-CoV-2 mutant virus strain. However, according to sequencing data at the time of the study period in Ho Chi Minh City, COVID-19 was almost entirely caused by one lineage of the Delta variant (AY.57).57 In that sense, we evaluated a relatively homogeneous patient population in virologically. Finally, although the sample size was large enough, the CI was wide (±8.66%), which might influence the normal distribution of the collected sample. Further studies with larger sample sizes might be needed to consolidate the conclusions.

Conclusions

This study investigated a highly selected cohort of critically ill COVID-19 patients with the Delta variant, a high serum IL-6 level, and a high mortality rate presented to an Intensive Care Centre for the Treatment of Critically Ill Patients with COVID-19 in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Although the serum IL-6 levels on admission and their rate of change on the third day after admission both had a poor discriminatory value, they seem correlated to the severity of the illness and worthwhile in predicting mortality. To identify critically ill COVID-19 patients with the highest risk for mortality, monitoring the serial measurements and observing the rate of change in serum IL-6 levels over time are needed.
