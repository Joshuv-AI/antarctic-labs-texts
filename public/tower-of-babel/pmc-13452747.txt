Unmasking Behçet’s disease in the European population: a case series report
Authors: Paweł Głuszak, Mateusz Lucki, Natalia Kasprzyk, Vicky Chou, Aleksandra Dańczak-Pazdrowska, Bogna Grygiel-Górniak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Behçet’s disease (BD) is rare in European populations. Because clinical and immunological characteristics may differ between Caucasians and Middle Eastern patients, we analyzed BD patients from the Polish population. Data from eight patients with BD treated at a rheumatology clinic were analyzed and collected, including demographic data, clinical features, laboratory test results, comorbidities, and treatment options. The study included eight patients (mean age 38.2 ± 13.7 years; 75% female). None of the patients had a family history of the disease. The mean delay in diagnosis was 5.28 ± 4.6 years. The disease began with the development of mucosal ulcerations and a maculopapular rash, and some patients also developed anemia, alopecia, headaches, and arthritis. All patients had mucosal ulcerations and an erythematous or maculopapular rash; joint involvement occurred in 62.5%. Vascular complications (37.5%) included superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, ischemic stroke, and cephalic vein thrombosis. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (50%), dyslipidemia (37.5%), and depression (25%). Inflammatory markers were elevated in half of the patients and normalized after treatment. Antinuclear antibodies were positive in 62.5%, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies in 25%. Patients were treated with glucocorticosteroids (100%) in combination with azathioprine (62.5%), cyclosporine (25%), methotrexate (12.5%), and hydroxychloroquine (25%). Data from a single center indicate that BD in Caucasians is characterized by heterogeneity in clinical manifestations and develops predominantly in women. The presence of ANA and ANCA antibodiescan be misleading and requires differential diagnosis. The course is generally mild, but severe symptoms (especially thrombosis) require close monitoring by a multidisciplinary team.

Introduction

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a rare autoinflammatory disorder classified as a form of vasculitis. The disease typically develops in the third and fourth decades of life [1]. The highest incidence of BD is observed in southern Europe and Asia, particularly in Turkey [2], Iran [3], and other Middle Eastern and Asian countries, with a male predominance [4]. The disease can also occur in Caucasian patients, and in this group, it is more common in women.

BD is characterized by a variety of clinical manifestations, involving the mucous membranes and skin, eyes, musculoskeletal system, gastrointestinal tract, and central nervous system (CNS) [5]. Among these, mucosal ulcerations are the most common [6]. The development of individual symptoms may vary across populations, highlighting the heterogeneous clinical course of the disease.

The pathological background of the disease is characterized by excessive activity of innate immune cells, leading to hyperactivation of neutrophils and monocytes [7]. Excessive synthesis of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1beta and IL-23, leads to the recruitment of adaptive immune cells [7, 8]; therefore, BD is considered an autoinflammatory disease. An autoimmune T-cell response and features of spondyloarthropathy are also observed [9], and in some cases, antibodies may be synthesized.

Current therapeutic strategies for BD primarily include NSAIDs and immunosuppressive drugs, such as corticosteroids, colchicine, cytotoxic drugs, and biologics [10, 11]. Considering possible racial differences in symptom occurrence, the potential to induce ANA and ANCA antibody synthesis, and the response to conventional BD treatment, we decided to characterize the Polish population of patients who developed this disease.

Therefore, in this article, we plan to present the characteristics of eight Polish patients treated in a rheumatology department. Clinical and laboratory findings will be discussed in the context of epidemiological data, highlighting the characteristic features of BD in the Caucasian population. This approach will allow for the description of characteristic symptoms in the European population, serological analysis, and response to immunosuppressive treatment.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study included data from patients hospitalized at the Rheumatology Department (a tertiary referral unit). Medical records of eight patients diagnosed with BD between 2014 and 2025 were analyzed. Data included clinical symptoms, biochemical tests, serological tests (ANA and ANCA antibodies, titer, and profile), and, where available, imaging studies. Inflammatory markers were measured before and after treatment; CRP ≥ 4.9 mg/L and ESR ≥ 10 mm/g in men and ≥ 15 mm/h in women were considered elevated.

This study was designed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [12]. The Bioethics Committee of the Poznań University of Medical Sciences (protocol No. KB-854/23) approved the process of collecting anonymized patient data. Informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective design. All patients participating in the study have been managed in alignment with the recommendations and guidelines outlined in ‘Ethical Issues in Patient Safety Research: Interpreting Existing Guidance,’ approved by the World Health Organization (WHO) [13].Only patients diagnosed according to the 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease [14] were included in the study. Each patient with suspected BD at the outpatient unit was subsequently assessed by two rheumatologists at the clinic. Patients with alternative causes of the described symptoms (n = 6) or those previously diagnosed with another disease were excluded. Comorbidities, such as metabolic disorders and cardiovascular disease (primarily hypertension), were also assessed. Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis.

Results

The mean age of patients was 38.2 ± 13.7 years, ranging from 25 to 61 years at the time of hospitalization (Table 1). Patients were predominantly female, with a mean BMI of 25.1 ± 4.64 kg/m². The age at onset was 26 ± 13.9 years, with a mean delay in diagnosis of 5.28 ± 4.6 years. Initial symptoms consisted primarily of mucosal ulcerations; maculopapular rash, headaches, and myalgia were also reported. As the disease progressed, ophthalmological, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and neurological symptoms developed.

ESR was within the normal range in four patients, slightly elevated in three, and very high in Patient 8. Similarly, CRP levels were normal in three patients and elevated in five. White blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelets, electrolytes (Na, K, Ca), liver enzymes (ALT, AST), and creatinine were within the normal range. Only two patients had vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D) levels within the recommended range (30–50 ng/ml).

Five patients had positive ANA titers (ranging from 1/160 to 1/640), and only one had pANCA antibody detected. In most patients with positive ANA titers, these titers decreased over the course of the disease. All patients required glucocorticosteroids (GCs) at the onset of their disease. Immunosuppressive medications used by our patients included azathioprine, cyclosporine, hydroxychloroquine, and methotrexate. The patient with the most severe symptoms received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in combination with cyclophosphamide.

Discussion

Our study included eight patients diagnosed with BD (n = 6 women, n = 2 men) (Table 1).

The mean time from symptom onset to diagnosis was approximately 7 years (range: 0.5–25 years), which is similar to data reported in the available literature [15–17]. For example, in the study by Ideguchi et al., the mean time to diagnosis was approximately 7.5 ± 10.2 years in 412 Japanese patients [15]. Because BD is diagnosed solely on clinical symptoms, the final diagnosis is often made after several years, especially in populations where the disease is very rare. Furthermore, no biochemical markers commonly used in daily practice are known to confirm this condition [18]. The delay in our study may therefore be due to the rarity of BD in the Polish population, its insidious course, and benign onset, similarly reported in other studies [19]. None of the patients had a family history of BD, which could have helped in earlier diagnosis. Detailed patient characteristics are presented in Table 2.

In the study group, the most common initial symptoms were mucosal ulcerations (100%, n = 8), followed by skin manifestations, primarily a papulovesicular or erythematous rash (75%, n = 6) (Fig. 1).

Severe vascular complications were identified in 37.5% (n = 3), including superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, cephalic vein thrombosis, and ischemic stroke (ultimately leading to right hemiparesis). A similar incidence of vascular events has been reported in Middle Eastern populations. For example, data from the Palestinian population show that vascular complications, such as thrombosis or ischemic episodes, occur in 36.7% of patients and are more common in women [20]. In some individuals with BD, a vascular event may be the initial symptom, as reported in 26% of Egyptian patients [21] and 10.6% of Turkish patients [22]. The development of thrombosis in our patients suggests the possibility of developing serious vascular complications during the course of the disease, and therefore, these patients should be closely monitored.

Headache was the predominant neurological symptom (100%, n = 8), which could be caused by vascular changes in the central nervous system (such as a transient ischemic attack or minor ischemia), but could be independent of such changes. Because the headaches were mild, an MRI of the head was not performed in most patients. Severe neurological symptoms were observed in only one patient (right hemiparesis) (Table 2). The incidence of neurological symptoms, including extraparenchymal and parenchymal lesions, varies by country. Although reported in 25% of patients (Palestinian population) [20] and in one-third of BD cases worldwide [23], these complications are rare in some countries, for example, in 2.3% in Turkish [22] and 3.8% in Iranian patients [3]. Our study shows that neuro-Behçet’s disease (NBD) can develop in the Caucasian population and may have a severe course, leading to stroke.

Four patients reported gastrointestinal symptoms, most commonly transient abdominal pain, which responded well to gastrointestinal and antispasmodic treatment. Similar symptoms have been described in the literature and responded well to symptomatic treatment.

The most common ophthalmologic problem in BD is posterior uveitis. Ocular lesions may initially be asymptomatic and difficult to diagnose during a standard ophthalmologic examination [24, 25]. The incidence of ocular symptoms in our study is lower than that reported by other authors [26, 27]. Vision problems were diagnosed in only one patient, which may be due to the lower incidence of such disorders in Caucasian patients compared to Asians. Routine ophthalmological evaluation was not performed in our patients because they had no ophthalmic symptoms.

Our study did not reveal significant hematological or biochemical abnormalities typical of BD. The exceptions were inflammatory markers, including CRP and ESR, which were elevated in half of our patients. Similarly, other studies show that only some patients exhibit elevated inflammatory parameters, which typically normalize after appropriate treatment [28, 29].

In our study, the highest ESR (220 mm/h) and CRP (70 mg/dL) values were observed in Patient 8, who presented the most severe symptoms and required the most aggressive treatment. Data from the study by Parsaei et al. suggest that ESR ≥ 10.5 mg/dL may predict active BD (sensitivity 71%, accuracy 75%), and ESR values > 42.5 mm/h may predict active vascular symptoms (sensitivity 81%, specificity 83%) [28].

Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were detected in 62.5% of patients (n = 5), demonstrating heterogeneous antibody profiles, while antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were positive in 25% of patients (n = 2). Available data indicate that no antibodies specific for BD have been identified; however, both ANA and ANCA may be present in this vasculitis [30, 31]. In such cases, a broad differential diagnosis is necessary, as a positive ANA result is typical for other connective tissue diseases with similar symptoms and disease onset (e.g., oral ulcers also occur in systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE]). Therefore, patients 2, 4, and 6, who had a positive ANA profile with anti-PM75, anti-SS-B, anti-dsDNA, anti-PM-Scl100, and anti-histone antibodies, and who had mucosal ulcers, skin lesions, and arthralgia, required differentiation from other connective tissue diseases, especially SLE.

A positive ANCA test result in this study required differentiation from other types of vasculitides [31], which were excluded during the diagnostic process. Even a positive ANCA test result is not a strong marker against the diagnosis of BD and does not unequivocally exclude BD, as confirmed by numerous studies [32, 34]. ANCA are usually detected in patients with BD associated with vascular involvement. Therefore, a positive ANCA test result cannot be an absolute indicator distinguishing BD from AAV [31–33]. Not only ANCA but also ANA are detectable in approximately 10% of patients with BD [30]. Similarly, our data indicate that ANA and ANCA may be nonspecifically synthesized in BD, complicating diagnosis.

The most common comorbidities associated with Behçet’s disease included hypertension (50%, n = 4), dyslipidemia (37.5%, n = 3), and iron deficiency (37.5%, n = 3), all of which were adequately treated. Similar disorders have been described in the literature, highlighting the importance of routine screening for comorbidities in patients with BD. Assessing cardiovascular disease is particularly important, as these contribute to numerous complications [34, 35]. The coexistence of dyslipidemia or hypertension, especially with BD-related cardiovascular conditions, has significantly increased the morbidity risk [36].

Recently, vitamin D has been linked to the development of many diseases, underscoring its anti-inflammatory effects. Unfortunately, three of eight patients in our study were found to be vitamin D deficient (values ranging from 11.4 to 19 ng/mL, with a normal range of 30–50 ng/mL). Deficiencies may result from limited outdoor exposure, poor sunlight, and reduced vitamin D production. Epidemiological data show that deficiency is common among healthy individuals living in Central Europe, and the incidence of hypovitaminosis D is similar to that observed in Western Europe during the winter months (21–23 ng/ml, regardless of age group) [37, 38]. Therefore, adequate supplementation of this vitamin is crucial in the treatment and prevention of BD exacerbations, primarily because higher levels are associated with a reduced risk of active disease [38] and cardiovascular complications [34, 36]. Details of symptoms, laboratory results, and treatment strategy are presented in Table 1. In our study, patients were treated with GCS (100%, n = 8), AZA (62.5%, n = 5), MTX (25%, n = 2), CsA (25%, n = 2), HCQ (25%, n = 2), and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG, 12.5%, n = 1). One third of patients (n = 3) required treatment modification due to exacerbation or iatrogenic complications. Two patients required dual therapy with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs). All treatment strategies used were in accordance with current guidelines [10]. Patients 4 and 6 were administered hydroxychloroquine due to the initial suspicion of SLE, and treatment was modified after the correct diagnosis was established. Numerous studies have highlighted the beneficial effects of azathioprine on BD. This medication reduces the risk of new central organ involvement, has a good safety profile, and reduces cardiovascular complications [39, 40]. Therefore, our patients with recurrent oral ulcers were treated with azathioprine, as it may also reduce the risk of recurrent central organ involvement and has a good safety profile.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings. However, given the rarity of BD in Poland, the study includingeight patients still provides key clinical insights. The retrospective nature of data collection may introduce biases, including incomplete patient medical records and the lack of systematic laboratory, dermatological, and ophthalmological examinations at the onset of BD. Because patients were initially treated with NSAIDs by general practitioners or topical glucocorticosteroids by dermatologists before referral to the Rheumatology Clinic, inflammatory parameters at the time of initial evaluation may be lower than before outpatient treatment. Therefore, they may not fully reflect disease activity at baseline; however, these medications are recommended for the treatment of early mucocutaneous lesions in BD. On the other hand, low vitamin D levels, characteristic of our time zone, may contribute to the severity of inflammation in this disease. The lower incidence of ocular symptoms in our group of patients compared with other studies may suggest a different clinical course of the disease ; however, asymptomatic patients were not systematically examined ophthalmologically. The analyzed group includes case series that cannot be extrapolated to the Polish population. Furthermore, it appears that minor ocular symptoms may have been suppressed by initial anti-inflammatory treatment and may therefore be underestimated.

Benefits of the study

Despite the small sample size, our study provides valuable information on Behçet’s disease in a rarely reported Caucasian population in Central Europe. Detailed clinical and biochemical data are described, reflecting the spectrum of symptoms and comorbidities associated with active BD. Our analyses indicate a specific profile in the Caucasian population, characterized by initial symptoms of skin and mucosal lesions (primarily in the oral cavity, rarely in the genital tract), frequent joint pain, and a lack of visual involvement. This study demonstrates that, despite a mild onset, severe vascular symptoms (nervous system thrombosis) can develop over time.

Another value of this study is the analysis of ANA and ANCA antibodies (titers and profiles), which reflect an autoimmune basis. Although current diagnostic criteria for Behçet’s disease exclude these antibodies, low titers suggest immune stimulation. The presence of ANA and ANCA antibodies can complicate the diagnostic process and require consideration of other causes. This emphasizes the need for a thorough evaluation of each patient and a comprehensive differential diagnosis. Furthermore, immunological tests performed in these patients reveal immune dysregulation and may falsely suggest another autoimmune disease, complicating the diagnosis of BD.

Assessing vitamin D levels and BMI underscores the importance of modifiable risk factors for the patient’s health. Low vitamin D levels should be closely monitored, especially in areas with limited sunlight. Appropriate supplementation can help reduce inflammation in BD, enabling better patient management and providing key diagnostic clues. In addition to assessing and treating clinical symptoms typical of BD, our study highlights the need for weight monitoring, as patients tend to be overweight or obese (especially when co-administered with glucocorticoids).

Conclusion

In our study, we observed skin and mucosal involvement primarily, with some patients presenting with vascular, neurological, and gastrointestinal symptoms, confirming the systemic and variable nature of Behçet’s disease. Routine screening for comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and vitamin D deficiency is essential due to their modifiable impact on increased cardiovascular risk. A multidisciplinary approach is essential to ensure long-term disease control and improved treatment outcomes. Each patient should be evaluated by a team consisting of dermatologists, ophthalmologists, rheumatologists, and neurologists, as appropriate.

Our study demonstrates that Behçet’s disease is a rare, chronic, multisystemic vasculitis with a heterogeneous clinical presentation, often leading to misdiagnosis and delays, particularly in non-endemic regions such as Central Europe. This study adds to the limited data on Behçet’s disease in Caucasian populations, particularly in Poland. It highlights the diagnostic complexity associated with the presence of ANA and ANCA antibodies, as well as the overlap in symptoms with other autoimmune diseases. Further multicenter studies with larger cohorts of Caucasian patients are needed to better characterize the disease course, assess the efficacy of new medications, and identify reliable biomarkers of disease activity and prognosis that would facilitate diagnosis and treatment in non-endemic regions.
