Transporter‐Associated Therapeutic Efficacy of an SMVT‐Targeted Boron Carrier in Boron Neutron Capture Therapy
Authors: Kenichiro Eza, Kohei Tsujino, Rina Shidoh‐Kazuki, Yoshiki Fujikawa, Hideki Kashiwagi, Gen Futamura, Ryo Hiramatsu, Toshihiro Takami, Masahiko Wanibuchi, Naonori Hu, Hiroki Tanaka, Minoru Suzuki, Kai Nishimura, Shota Tanaka, Hiroyuki Nakamura, Shinji Kawabata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBoron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) requires selective delivery of boron compounds to tumor cells to achieve therapeutic efficacy. In this study, a BNCT strategy centered on the sodium‐dependent multivitamin transporter (SMVT)‐targeted biotin‐conjugated boron compound, BBCIP, was evaluated, with selective incorporation of p‐boronophenylalanine (BPA) according to tumor transporter characteristics. To examine transporter‐dependent effects, the CRL1666 metastatic brain tumor model in rats was compared with an F98 glioma model, which exhibits distinct transporter expression profiles. The F98 model is characterized by dominant L‐type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1) expression and low SMVT levels, whereas the CRL1666 model shows relatively high SMVT expression. BBCIP demonstrated stable and quantifiable intratumoral boron pharmacokinetics in both models. In BNCT experiments, BBCIP alone showed limited therapeutic efficacy in the LAT1‐dominant F98 model (median survival, 29 vs. 23 days in controls), whereas survival was further improved with the addition of BPA. In contrast, in the SMVT‐high CRL1666 model, BBCIP‐based BNCT significantly prolonged survival (30 vs. 15.5 days in controls), with only modest additional benefit from BPA. These findings indicate that the therapeutic contribution of BBCIP‐based BNCT depends on tumor transporter expression and that BPA may be advantageous in tumors with low SMVT expression. Rather than relying on a single boron carrier, these findings support a biology‐driven strategy in which boron agents are selected and combined according to tumor‐specific characteristics.Keywords: boron delivery, boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT), brain tumors, sodium‐dependent multivitamin transporter (SMVT), transporter‐guided therapy

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) is the most aggressive primary brain tumor in adults and remains associated with a dismal prognosis despite maximal surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Its infiltrative nature, therapeutic resistance, and limited options at recurrence underscore the need for novel strategies [1, 2, 3, 4].

Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a biologically targeted radiotherapy in which tumor‐accumulated boron‐10 (10B) undergoes a nuclear reaction upon thermal neutron irradiation, producing high–linear energy transfer (LET) α‐particles and 7Li nuclei. These particles induce lethal damage within a limited path length, allowing selective tumor cell destruction while sparing adjacent normal tissue [1, 3]. With the recent clinical implementation of accelerator‐based BNCT in Japan and accumulating clinical evidence supporting its efficacy in gliomas, optimization of BNCT based on tumor‐specific biology has become increasingly important [1, 2, 5, 6].

The therapeutic efficacy of BNCT critically depends on efficient and tumor‐selective boron delivery. The most widely used boron carrier, p‐boronophenylalanine (BPA), is transported into tumor cells via the L‐type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1) [7, 8]. However, heterogeneous or low LAT1 expression in certain tumors can limit BPA accumulation and compromise treatment efficacy. This limitation likely contributes to variable clinical outcomes in malignant glioma BNCT studies [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Recently, the sodium‐dependent multivitamin transporter (SMVT; SLC5A6), which mediates cellular uptake of biotin and pantothenic acid, has been reported to be upregulated in several malignancies [14]. BBCIP, a biotin‐conjugated boron compound designed to target SMVT, represents a promising alternative or complementary boron carrier, particularly for tumors exhibiting low or heterogeneous LAT1 expression.

Gliomas and metastatic brain tumors are known to differ substantially in their molecular features and transporter expression profiles; however, BNCT strategies that explicitly account for such biological diversity remain largely unexplored [15]. In the present study, a BBCIP‐centered BNCT strategy was evaluated using two rat brain tumor models with distinct transporter characteristics: the LAT1‐dominant, SMVT‐low F98 glioma model and the SMVT‐high CRL1666 metastatic brain tumor model.

The aim of this study was to determine whether transporter‐guided selection and combination of boron carriers could expand intratumoral boron coverage and improve the therapeutic potential of BNCT. By comparing BBCIP alone and in combination with BPA across biologically distinct models, we aimed to advance BNCT beyond single‐carrier optimization toward a biology‐driven strategy applicable to both glioma and metastatic brain tumors.

Material and Methods

Boron Carriers and Synthesis

In this study, two boron carriers, BBCIP and p‐boronophenylalanine (BPA), were used. BBCIP was synthesized as previously described in our earlier study, and its chemical structure has been reported by Nishimura et al. [16].

BBCIP has a molecular weight of 1115.74, and a boron‐10 enrichment rate of 99.8%. L‐4‐BPA was purchased from Interpharma Praha (Prague, Czech Republic) and converted into a fructose complex prior to use. Because BBCIP has a relatively high molecular weight and is unlikely to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), it was administered via convection‐enhanced delivery (CED).

Cell Culture

Two rat cell lines, F98 rat glioma cells and CRL1666 rat mammary carcinoma cells, were used in this study. F98 cells were kindly provided by Dr. Rolf Barth (The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH, USA), and CRL1666 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B, and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell culture reagents were purchased from Gibco (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA).

In Vitro Boron Uptake and Retention

Intracellular boron uptake was evaluated using F98 and CRL1666 cell lines. Cells (4 × 105) were seeded into 100‐mm culture dishes (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and cultured for 3 days until reaching near confluency. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh DMEM containing 5 μg B/mL of either BPA or BBCIP, followed by incubation at 37°C for 3, 6, or 24 h [17].

For boron retention analysis, F98 and CRL1666 cells were incubated with 5 μg B/mL of BPA or BBCIP for 24 h. The medium was subsequently replaced with boron‐free medium, and cells were further incubated for 1, 3, or 24 h. At each time point, cells were washed twice with cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and detached using a trypsin–EDTA solution. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 200× g for 5 min and washed twice with cold PBS to remove residual extracellular boron.

Cell pellets were digested overnight at room temperature in 1 N nitric acid (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan). Boron concentrations in the lysates were quantified by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‐AES) using an iCAP6300 spectrometer (Hitachi High‐Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Intracellular boron levels were expressed as micrograms of boron per 109 cells.

Colony‐Forming Assay for Evaluation of BNCT‐Induced Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxic effects of BPA and BBCIP in BNCT were evaluated using a colony‐formation assay. F98 and CRL1666 cells were assigned to three experimental groups: neutron‐only (cells incubated in boron‐free medium), BPA‐treated, and BBCIP‐treated groups. Cells were cultured in 150 cm2 flasks and incubated with 5 μg B/mL of either BPA or BBCIP for 24 h. Cells were then subjected to neutron irradiation at the Kyoto University Research Reactor Institute (KURNS) under a reactor power of 1 MW with a thermal neutron flux of 1.0 × 109 n/cm2/s for 10, 20, or 30 min.

Following irradiation, cells were seeded into 60‐mm dishes (Becton, Dickinson and Company), incubated for 7 days, after which they were fixed with 90% ethanol and stained with Giemsa solution. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted, and survival fractions (SF) were calculated by normalizing the colony numbers to those of the untreated control group.

Relative biological effectiveness (RBE) and compound biological effectiveness (CBE) for each boron compound were determined at a SF of 0.1 using the linear–quadratic model derived from X‐ray irradiation data (M‐150WE; SOFTEX) [18, 19].

Rat Glioma Model Using F98 Cells and Rat Metastatic Brain Tumor Model Using CRL1666 Cells

Male Fischer rats (10 weeks old, 200–250 g) were used in this study. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal administration of a combination of medetomidine (0.4 mg/kg; ZENOAQ, Fukushima, Japan), midazolam (2.0 mg/kg; SANDOZ, Yamagata, Japan), and butorphanol (5.0 mg/kg; Meiji Seika, Tokyo, Japan). Each animal was placed in a stereotactic frame (Model 900; David Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA, USA), and a midline scalp incision was made. A burr hole (1 mm in diameter) was drilled 1 mm posterior to the bregma and 4 mm lateral to the midline. F98 rat glioma cells and CRL1666 rat mammary carcinoma cells were implanted into the right cerebral hemisphere. For therapeutic experiments, 1 × 103 cells suspended in 10 μL of DMEM containing 1.4% agarose (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) were injected. For biodistribution studies, 1 × 105 cells were implanted. All injections were performed at a constant rate of 20 μL/min using an automated infusion pump.

The F98 glioma model was selected because of its highly invasive growth pattern within normal brain tissue and its resistance to conventional photon irradiation. These surgical procedures have been standardized and routinely employed in our laboratory, as described previously [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26].

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Use Review Board and Ethical Committee of Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University (No. AM23‐060) and the Institute for Integrated Radiation and Nuclear Science, Kyoto University (KURNS; Kumatori, Osaka, Japan; No. R7073).

Convection‐Enhanced Delivery in F98 and CRL1666 Rat Brain Tumor Models

An Alzet osmotic pump (model #2001D, Durect, Cupertino, CA, USA) was used to administer boron compounds to the Fischer rats via convection‐enhanced delivery (CED). A predetermined volume of 200 μL of BBCIP at the designated boron concentration was loaded into the pump, which was assembled by connecting the infusion cannula to a Brain Infusion Kit 2 (rigid stainless‐steel cannula, 28 gauge, 5 mm in length). Following anesthesia as described above, a skin incision was made on the dorsal surface of the rat, and the osmotic pump was implanted subcutaneously. The brain infusion cannula was then inserted into the preformed burr hole, and the wound was closed with 3–0 sutures, as described previously [20, 21, 23, 26, 27]. The Alzet osmotic pump delivered the boron carrier at a constant rate of 8 μL/h for a total infusion volume of 200 μL.

Biodistribution of Boron Carrier in F98 and CRL1666 Rat Brain Tumor Models

To evaluate the biodistribution of boron carriers, Fischer rats were intracranially implanted with 1 × 105 tumor cells. Approximately 12–14 days after implantation, an interval considered sufficient for tumor establishment [15], animals received BPA (1000 μg B/mL, equivalent to 12 mg BPA/kg body weight [b.w.]) via intravenous injection, whereas BBCIP (1000 μg B/mL, corresponding to 1.2 mg BBCIP/kg b.w.) was administered by CED.

At predetermined time points (3, 6, or 24 h post‐injection), animals were euthanized, and tissue samples, including tumor, normal brain, and blood, were collected and weighed. Each tissue sample was digested in 1 N nitric acid at room temperature for 7 days. Boron concentrations in the digested samples were quantified by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‐AES). Results are expressed as micrograms of boron (B) per gram of tissue (μg B/g tissue).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from F98 glioma and CRL1666 metastatic brain tumors using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using predesigned TaqMan fluorogenic probes and primer sets (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Relative mRNA expression levels of LAT1 and SMVT were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and expression ratios were compared between F98 and CRL1666 tumors.

Survival Analysis Following Neutron Irradiation in Rat Models

Survival studies were performed 14 days after intracranial implantation of 1 × 103 F98 glioma cells, whereas in rats bearing CRL1666 tumors, neutron irradiation was conducted on day 9 after implantation. These time points were selected based on biodistribution data indicating peak intratumoral boron accumulation. All animals were anesthetized before treatment. BPA was administered intravenously, whereas BBCIP was delivered by CED according to the experimental design [28, 29]. The timing of neutron irradiation in this study was determined based on the results of prior biodistribution experiments, specifically at time points corresponding to peak or near‐peak intratumoral boron concentrations.

To restrict neutron exposure to the cranial region, rats were shielded with a customized plate lined with 6LiF ceramic tiles. Neutron irradiation was performed at the Heavy Water Irradiation Facility at Kyoto University Research Reactor Institute (KURNS) at a reactor power of 5 MW, with a constant thermal neutron flux of 3.5 × 109 n/cm2/s for 20 min.

Statistical Analysis

Tumor cell boron concentrations obtained from the in vitro uptake assay, survival fractions (SF) following in vitro neutron irradiation, and data from the in vivo biodistribution study were analyzed using Student's t‐test. Survival outcomes from the in vivo neutron irradiation studies were compared using the log‐rank test based on Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro software, version 16.2.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Time‐dependent intracellular boron accumulation following exposure to BPA or BBCIP (5 μg B/mL) was evaluated in F98 glioma and CRL1666 cells. In F98 cells, BPA resulted in significantly higher intracellular boron concentrations than BBCIP at all time points (Figure 1) and increased over time, whereas BBCIP levels remained low. At 24 h, intracellular boron concentrations reached 14 ± 0.6 μg B/109 cells in the BPA‐treated group compared with 3.3 ± 1.0 μg B/109 cells in the BBCIP‐treated group (p < 0.05).

In Vivo Survival Following Neutron Irradiation

Neutron irradiation was performed 3 h after BBCIP administration, corresponding to peak intratumoral boron accumulation.

In the F98 glioma model, the median survival time (MST) was 23 days (95% confidence interval [CI], 19–26 days) in the untreated control group and 25.5 days (95% CI, 20–28 days) in the neutron‐only group. Treatment with BBCIP‐BNCT resulted in an MST of 29 days (95% CI, 25–31 days), whereas BPA‐BNCT further prolonged survival to 33.5 days (95% CI, 28–47 days). The longest survival was observed in the combination group receiving BBCIP plus BPA, with an MST of 39 days (95% CI, 29–53 days). BPA‐BNCT significantly prolonged survival compared with BBCIP‐BNCT. No statistically significant difference was observed between the BPA and combination groups. Correspondingly, the percent increase in life span (%ILS) was 10.9% for the neutron‐only group, 26.1% for the BBCIP group, 45.7% for the BPA group, and 69.4% for the BBCIP plus BPA group.

In contrast, in the CRL1666 metastatic brain tumor model, the untreated control group exhibited an MST of 15.5 days (95% CI, 15–18 days), which increased modestly to 17.5 days (95% CI, 17–20 days) in the neutron‐only group. Treatment with BBCIP‐BNCT markedly prolonged survival to an MST of 30 days (95% CI, 22–35 days), whereas BPA‐BNCT achieved an MST of 28 days (95% CI, 25–31 days). Notably, the addition of BPA to BBCIP did not confer further survival benefit, with an MST of 28 days (95% CI, 22–33 days) in the combination group. The %ILS values in this model were 12.9% for the neutron‐only group, 93.5% for the BBCIP group, 80.9% for the BPA group, and 80.6% for the BBCIP plus BPA group.

No treatment‐related toxicity was observed during the observation period (Table 2, Figure 6).

Estimated Photon‐Equivalent Dose

The physical absorbed doses and corresponding photon‐equivalent doses delivered to tumor and normal brain tissues during in vivo neutron irradiation were estimated using compound biological effectiveness (CBE) factors and mean boron concentrations derived from the biodistribution study.

In the F98 glioma model, the mean boron concentrations in tumor and normal brain tissue were 25.8 and 5.4 μg B/g, respectively. Based on these values, the estimated photon‐equivalent dose delivered to the tumor at 3 h after administration was 21.3 Gy‐Eq for BPA‐BNCT and 20.5 Gy‐Eq for BBCIP‐BNCT.

In contrast, in the CRL1666 metastatic brain tumor model, the mean boron concentrations in tumor and normal brain tissues were 24.7 and 5.16 μg B/g, respectively. Under these conditions, the estimated photon‐equivalent dose delivered to the tumor at 3 h was 16.3 Gy‐Eq for BPA‐BNCT, whereas BBCIP‐BNCT achieved a substantially higher photon‐equivalent dose of 33.4 Gy‐Eq.

Discussion

This study provides an experimental evaluation of a BNCT strategy centered on the SMVT‐targeted boron carrier BBCIP, with the conventional boron agent BPA serving as a reference boron carrier, using glioma and metastatic brain tumor models with distinct transporter expression profiles [30]. Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo analyses, we demonstrated that boron carrier efficacy is strongly influenced by tumor‐specific transporter expression and that the value of combination therapy depends on tumor biology [31].

In the in vitro cellular uptake experiments, BPA consistently showed higher boron accumulation than BBCIP in F98 glioma cells. In contrast, in CRL1666 mammary carcinoma cells, BBCIP exhibited greater boron accumulation than BPA at 24 h. These uptake patterns correspond to the molecular profiles of the two models—LAT1‐dominant with low SMVT expression in F98 cells versus relatively high SMVT expression in CRL1666 cells, supporting SMVT‐associated uptake of BBCIP. These uptake differences were reflected in therapeutic responses following in vitro neutron irradiation. In F98 cells, BPA produced greater cytotoxicity and higher CBE values than BBCIP, whereas in CRL1666 cells, the effects of the two carriers were comparable. Together, these findings indicate that transporter expression, rather than administered dose alone, is a key determinant of BNCT efficacy.

Consistent with these functional findings, mRNA expression analysis showed similar LAT1 expression in both models but significantly higher SMVT expression in CRL1666 tumors, providing mechanistic support for the preferential activity of BBCIP.

Although these findings suggest a transporter‐associated mechanism, direct functional validation of transporter dependency was not performed. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as correlative evidence rather than definitive proof of a causal transporter‐mediated therapeutic effect. Future studies using competitive inhibition assays, transporter‐specific inhibitors such as BCH for LAT1, and knockdown or overexpression models will be necessary to clarify the mechanistic contribution of SMVT and LAT1 to BBCIP and BPA uptake and therapeutic efficacy.

In vivo biodistribution further demonstrated marked tumor selectivity following CED [32]. Tumor‐to‐normal brain (T/N) and tumor‐to‐blood (T/B) ratios were 69 and 28 in F98 tumors and 166 and 493 in CRL1666 tumors, respectively, whereas BPA showed modest and similar selectivity in both models. Thus, BBCIP achieved substantially greater tumor selectivity, particularly in SMVT‐high tumors.

These biodistribution profiles were consistent with survival outcomes. In the F98 tumors, BPA‐BNCT conferred a greater survival benefit than BBCIP‐BNCT, although combination therapy showed a tendency toward further improvement. In contrast, in the CRL1666 tumors, BBCIP alone produced a marked survival extension, with no additive effect from BPA. These results indicate that BPA predominates in LAT1‐dominant tumors, whereas BBCIP is the principal contributor in SMVT‐dominant tumors.

In terms of safety, although no apparent adverse events, such as marked body weight loss or behavioral abnormalities, were observed during the experimental period, the safety evaluation in this study was based solely on general condition and body weight monitoring and did not include detailed assessments such as histopathological or neurological evaluations. Therefore, toxicity associated with local administration via CED remains an important issue to be addressed in future studies.

With respect to irradiation conditions, the timing was primarily aligned with the reported peak tumor concentration of BPA. However BBCIP delivered by CED may exhibit more sustained intratumoral retention and may not depend on a single peak concentration time point.

Dosimetric analysis further supported these observations. In the F98 tumors, estimated doses were comparable between carriers despite superior survival in BPA, suggesting that factors such as intracellular retention and CBE differences contribute to efficacy [19]. In CRL1666 tumors, BBCIP achieved nearly twice the tumor Gy‐Eq of BPA, consistent with its superior therapeutic performance.

An additional important limitation of this study is the assumption of compound biological effectiveness (CBE) values for BBCIP in photon‐equivalent dose (Gy‐Eq) estimation. Because compound‐specific CBE values for BBCIP have not yet been experimentally established, we used a conservative assumption based on previously reported BPA values, particularly for normal brain tissue, to avoid overestimation of treatment effects. However, this approach may not fully reflect differences in intracellular localization and microdistribution between boron carriers. Therefore, the Gy‐Eq values presented in this study should be interpreted primarily as a tool for relative comparison rather than as an absolute quantitative dosimetric evaluation.

In addition, the in vivo experiments were performed with relatively small group sizes as part of an exploratory preclinical study, which may limit statistical power. Therefore, observed differences, particularly in comparisons without statistical significance, should be interpreted cautiously, as insufficient power may have contributed to these results. Larger‐scale studies will be required to confirm reproducibility and strengthen the statistical robustness of the present findings.

Taken together, these findings suggest the importance of “transporter coverage” in BNCT. In tumors driven by a dominant transporter, a matched boron carrier may be sufficient, whereas tumors with heterogeneous expression may benefit from combining carriers with complementary transport mechanisms [15]. This transporter‐guided strategy provides a biologically rational framework for optimizing intratumoral boron distribution.

Because BBCIP and BPA are expected to utilize distinct transport pathways, their combination may provide complementary intratumoral boron distribution without direct competitive inhibition. However, pharmacokinetic interactions during combined administration remain to be clarified in future studies.

Finally, this study was conducted using only two tumor models with distinct transporter expression profiles and should therefore be regarded as a proof‐of‐concept study rather than a basis for broad generalization. Although further validation using additional tumor models and clinical specimens will be necessary, the present findings support the potential value of transporter‐oriented boron carrier selection as a biologically guided strategy for BNCT optimization.

In conclusion, this study supports a transporter‐oriented BNCT approach in which BBCIP serves as a central platform and BPA is selectively incorporated according to tumor biology, enabling more adaptive and tumor‐specific optimization of therapy [33].
