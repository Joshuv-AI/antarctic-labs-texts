Understanding the impact of extreme weather events on non-communicable diseases in sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Erika Canda, Aditya Mehta, Rima Pai, Shenita Peterson, Christine C Ekenga, Saria Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSub-Saharan Africa (sSA) is undergoing an epidemiological transition, with non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) rapidly becoming the leading cause of morbidity and mortality. Simultaneously, climate change is exacerbating the frequency, intensity and duration of extreme weather events, disproportionately affecting sSA. Extreme weather events strain healthcare, disrupting routine care for persons living with NCDs. The objective of this scoping review is to summarise the current evidence on the association of climate-related extreme weather events and NCDs in sSA.MethodsPubMed, Cochrane, Embase, PsycINFO (EBSCO), Web of Science, Global Health (CABI) and Scopus (Elsevier) were searched to identify peer-reviewed publications from 1995 to 2023, examining the effects of extreme weather events, including floods, droughts, cyclones, mud or landslides and extreme temperatures, on NCDs such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, respiratory conditions, cancer and mental health in sSA. Three reviewers conducted abstract screening of 903 articles, full-text screening of 108 articles and data extraction of 31 articles.ResultsOf the 31 included articles, 27 reported on the mental health burden associated with floods and droughts. Few studies reported the impact of cyclones (4), extreme temperatures (3) and mud or landslides (2) on NCDs. Furthermore, we found a lack of geographic diversity among cited countries, with only 16 sSA countries represented in the literature.ConclusionsThere is an urgent need for comprehensive research in under-represented sSA countries impacted by extreme weather events. Research needs to encompass a wider range of NCD outcomes, extreme weather events and countries. Lessons from other regions of the world can inform the development of cost-effective and climate-resilient strategies to reduce the impact of extreme weather events on NCD management in sSA.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Extreme weather events disrupt the provision of basic healthcare services and worsen health outcomes, particularly for persons living with non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) who require routine care. Limited data have been reported on the double burden of extreme weather events and NCDs in sub-Saharan Africa, as research mostly focused on infectious disease outcomes in the region.

Introduction

With the advent of climate change, human populations across the globe are experiencing an increasing frequency and severity of extreme weather events, including floods, heat waves and cyclones.1 These changes are poised to affect vulnerable populations worldwide, particularly those living in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). Countries in the sub-Saharan African (sSA) region have been affected by these extreme weather events. In 2022, more than 110 million people across Africa were directly affected by weather, climate or water-related hazardous events.1 Between January 2022 and May 2023, at least 4000 people on the continent were killed by extreme weather events, including cyclones, floods, heatwaves, wildfires and droughts.2 Additionally, compound extreme weather events, which are defined mainly as two or more extreme events occurring simultaneously or sequentially, are projected to become more frequent in sSA by the end of the century.3 Using modelling scenarios, compound extreme weather events (eg, coincident heat waves and drought) are projected to increase between 14-fold and 52-fold compared with frequencies before 2010, partly due to climate change and shifts in large-scale heat circulation systems.3 Suffering disproportionately from the impacts of climate change, the African continent is responsible for less than 10% of global greenhouse gas emissions that contribute to climate change and has the least climate-resilient infrastructure to deal with the negative ramifications of this process.1

The impact of climate-related extreme weather events on healthcare infrastructure in sSA is particularly troublesome, given global health disparities where 73% of mortality due to non-communicable diseases (NCDs) occurs in LMICs.4 NCDs include mental health disorders, respiratory conditions, cardiovascular conditions, diabetes and cancer. Countries in sSA have undergone an epidemiological transition over the past 60 years, with the increasing prevalence of NCDs compared with infectious diseases.5 Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to NCDs have increased 68% between 1990 and 2019 in the region.6 Morbidity and mortality from NCDs in sSA are projected to continue increasing and overtake infectious diseases by 2030.7

Literature from other parts of the world has shown a strong association between climate-related extreme weather events and NCDs. These extreme weather events have both direct and indirect impacts on NCDs. Direct effects include the impact of heat waves on cardiovascular disease (CVD), the cumulative impact of extreme heat and air quality on respiratory conditions, and the impact of cyclones and flooding on healthcare systems and healthcare delivery. Indirect effects include the impact on water quality and food safety and mental health.8 Mental health conditions, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression and psychological distress, are concerns for survivors of climate-related extreme weather events, as highlighted in observational studies and systematic reviews.8 Flooding as well as sustained droughts in sSA impacts access to food and agricultural production; this leads to food insecurity and malnutrition, which among children is associated with adult-onset cardiometabolic NCDs and among adults is a significant risk factor for NCDs.911 Air pollution impacts on health are exacerbated in the setting of extreme heat; this worsens respiratory and cardiovascular conditions, such as asthma or hypertension.12 Despite these critical associations, there is a paucity of literature examining the impact of extreme weather events on NCDs in sSA. For example, when Cyclone Idai struck southeast Africa in 2019, most of the research from Mozambique was focused on cholera and potential malaria outbreaks.13 There has been a noticeable lack of literature focused on NCD impacts from this cyclone in the 5 years since it made landfall, despite CVD being the second most common cause of death in the country. In Mozambique, Mugabe et al investigated the association of Cyclone Idai with a pellagra outbreak, a nutrition-related systemic disease linked to niacin deficiency.14 While pellagra is not an NCD, this study underscores the impact of extreme weather events on nutrition and the resultant high burden of non-infectious diseases among vulnerable groups in sSA.

This scoping review summarises the current literature on NCDs in the aftermath of climate-related extreme weather events across sSA. The primary objective is to understand how extreme weather events (eg, floods, cyclones, extreme heat, etc) impact the health of people with chronic NCDs in this region. Results from this scoping review will define gaps in the literature and inform the design and implementation of interventions and strategies to address the needs of persons living with NCDs in the setting of climate-related extreme weather events.

Methods

Protocol and eligibility criteria

The literature search included both peer-reviewed and grey literature to allow for a more expansive understanding of the association between the exposure and outcome of interest. A scoping review was selected for this process because it allowed for a more comprehensive search strategy to examine the extent of (and gaps within) prior research conducted in the region of interest, regardless of study design.15 The Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes and Study Design (PICOS) model was used to frame the overall literature search (table 1).

Search strategy and study selection

In collaboration with an information specialist, a comprehensive search strategy was developed through an iterative process. A protocol was developed for this review and used internally. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a draft search was created within the database PubMed to identify articles that contained information on the following concepts: sSA, climate-related weather events and NCDs, specifically mental health, cancer, CVD, respiratory diseases, diabetes and chronic kidney disease. Due to the variety of languages in sSA, studies published in English, Portuguese or French were all included in the search. All research included in this search had either extreme weather events or climate-sensitive events as the exposure of interest, with the primary outcome being the defined NCDs. The final search was executed in April 2023 within PubMed and was translated into Cochrane Database for Systematic reviews and CENTRAL, Embase.com, PsycINFO (EBSCO), Web of Science Core Collection, Global Health (CABI) and Scopus (Elsevier). One thousand four hundred and thirty-five citations were downloaded into Endnote V.20 for data collection. The time frame boundaries for the literature search were set as a publication date between 1995 and 2023, inclusive. The starting point was selected as 1995 because the first meeting of the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCC) Conference of Parties (COP 1) occurred that year in Berlin, Germany.16 The complete search strategy is found in online supplemental appendix A.

The results were uploaded into the web-based evidence synthesis tool Covidence for removal of duplicates and subsequent screening. The following inclusion and exclusion criteria were established for title/abstract screening as well as full-text screening: extreme weather events were designated as related to climatic processes, such as extreme heat, droughts, floods, cyclones, hurricanes, mudslides, tornadoes and monsoons. Included NCDs were related to mental health (PTSD, psychological distress, anxiety, depression), cancer, CVDs (heart disease, heart failure, hypertension, stroke), respiratory diseases (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(COPD), asthma), diabetes (type 1 and type 2) and chronic kidney disease (renal failure). Studies were excluded if set outside of sSA, were unrelated to the exposure or outcome of interest, were not in the specified target languages or were published before 1995. Animal studies were also excluded from this literature search. During the screening process, approximately 20 studies were used for piloting purposes to ensure the authors agreed about how to classify the included studies. Subsequently, two authors reviewed the studies independently. A third author resolved conflicts by serving as a tiebreaking vote. The same process was used for conflict resolution during the scoping review’s full-text review and abstraction phases.

The study was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines for reporting methods and findings.17

Data collection and synthesis

Data extraction was conducted in COVIDENCE by two independent reviewers using an extraction template created by the research team, with the following sections: article identifier, author names, publication date, geographic setting, aim of the study, study design, study dates, study population age (lower and upper age), study population sex, extreme weather events reported, NCDs reported, intervention details (if applicable), association between extreme weather event and NCDs, summary of main findings. Descriptive analysis was performed in Excel to quantify the number of studies reporting on each NCD outcome and extreme weather event association. A narrative synthesis of these data is presented, exploring the relationship between each NCD type by extreme weather events. A Sankey diagram was created to visualise the directional flow from each study to the extreme weather event it reported, and then the associated NCD outcome. The Sankey diagram was developed in Flourish, a data visualisation platform, using the Sankey diagram template published on the platform. A single association between an extreme weather event and an NCD was represented in two rows, where for each article, the first row linked the study (source) to the reported extreme weather event (target), and the second row linked the same extreme weather event (source) to its associated NCD outcome (target). Each association was assigned the value of one, and articles that reported multiple associations (eg, more than one NCD per event, or vice versa) were represented by multiple entries.

A descriptive map was developed using ArcMap V.10.8.2 to visualise the geographic distribution of extreme weather events across sSA, as reported in the reviewed studies. The base layer consisted of an African countries shapefile obtained from the ArcGIS platform (V.2023). Point data for each type of extreme weather event were extracted from the studies and organised into separate CSV files, each containing columns for country and frequency of reported events. These data were joined to the shapefile using the country attribute. Separate layers were created for each type of extreme weather event and styled using dot density symbology, with distinct colours to represent the distribution and intensity of each event type. A narrative synthesis of these data is presented, reporting on the most cited countries that experienced extreme weather events.

Results

We identified 1437 peer-reviewed and grey literature articles published between 1995 and 2023 (figure 1). Of those, 534 duplicates were removed, 524 automatically by Covidence and 10 manually by the reviewers, leaving 903 articles to go through title and abstract screening. After assessing the eligibility of those articles, we excluded 764 based on the following exclusion criteria: published before 1995, not written in English, Portuguese and French, were animal research, only reported infectious disease outcomes, and the setting was outside of sSA. After this thorough assessment, we removed 108 additional studies based on the criteria described above and any papers that were protocols, had the wrong outcomes, had no climate exposure, were not related to climate change and were non-extreme weather event related.

Study characteristics

Three categories of NCDs were reported in the 31 included articles: CVDs (n=3), cancer (n=2) and mental health disorders including anxiety (n=8), depression (n=6), post-traumatic stress (n=7) and psychological distress (n=14) (online supplemental table B1). Six of our studies looked at more than one NCD outcome and were, therefore, accounted for more than once (figure 2). Five types of extreme weather events were discussed in association with NCDs: cyclones, droughts, extreme temperatures, floods and mud or landslides associated with extreme rain. Studies took place in 16 sSA countries: Botswana, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Madagascar, Namibia, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Africa, Tanzania, Zimbabwe and Uganda (figure 3).

Countries most cited by extreme weather events

In the past 28 years (1995 to 2023), the literature cited the impacts of extreme weather events on NCDs in 16 out of 46 countries in sSA countries: Botswana, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Madagascar, Namibia, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Africa, Tanzania, Zimbabwe and Uganda. Water-related extreme weather events such as floods were the most cited type of extreme weather events (n=16), followed by droughts (n=8), cyclones (n=4), extreme temperatures (n=3) and mudslides (n=2). Figure 3 depicts the location and type of extreme weather event described in the included studies.

The three most cited countries were Ghana, South Africa and Zimbabwe. Ghana was the most cited country, with six studies highlighting the impact of flooding events on mental illnesses such as anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress and psychosocial distress. South Africa was the second most cited country, with articles primarily focusing on droughts (n=3), extreme temperatures (n=1) and floods (n=1). Droughts and floods in South Africa were associated with mental illnesses, while extreme temperatures were associated with CVDs. Zimbabwe was the third most cited country, with four studies reporting on cyclones (n=4), which led to an increase in mental illnesses.

NCD outcomes

Mental health definitions

Various diagnostic tools were used to measure mental health disorders in the included studies. Of the 27 articles discussing mental health, (n=17) used context-specific studies such as surveys (n=4)1821 or relied on symptom self-reports collected during interviews, photovoice and focus group discussions (n=13).2233 The remaining (n=10) studies used standardised screening tools. Anxiety was measured using the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI).34 Depression was assessed using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) and the Goldberg Depression Scale,35 the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9),36 the BSI34 and the 10 Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D10).37 In our reporting, we combined papers that discussed PTSD (n=4) and trauma (n=3) into the outcome of ‘post-traumatic stress’. PTSD was measured using the PTSD Symptom Scale Interview (PSS-I),36 the PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C-5),38 the Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ)39 and the Child Trauma Screening Questionnaire (CTSQ).40 The HQR was the only standardised tool used to measure trauma.39 41 Psychological distress was evaluated using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K-10)42 43 and the BSI.34

Mental health and floods

A total of 27 studies reported the effects of extreme weather events on mental health outcomes, with the majority 59% (n=16) reported in flood settings (online supplemental table B1). In Ghana, flood-exposed populations commonly experience anxiety, depression and psychological distress.34 Households that experienced flood-related property damage or loss suffered from a significant increase in depression.30 Factors such as the recurrence of floods, flood likelihood, experience with floods and climate variability contributed significantly to anxiety levels among individuals and households in South Africa and Ethiopia.18 21 A study in Ghana showed that fear of drowning during sleep contributed to feelings of anxiety.22 Individuals living in low-lying areas in Ghana were particularly vulnerable to anxiety that was triggered by signs of rain.23

The impact of floods on mental illnesses was reported up to 2 years following an extreme weather event.40 Psychological distress and PTSD had the longest-lasting effects. In northern Namibia, learners across all age groups exhibited trauma symptoms 2 years after the floods, with older learners (13 years and older) more severely affected than younger ones (12 years and younger).40 A 2019 study by Okaka and Odhiambo reported that it took more than 4 weeks for respondents in 6% of families in Mombasa, Kenya, to recover from mental stress and needed counselling.28 Another 2019 study by Okaka and Odhiambo highlighted that flood survivors needed psychological counselling due to the traumatic nature of property loss.29

In Ghana, Dziwornu et al found that survivors of flooding events scored higher for psychological distress than non-victims.34 Abass et al measured flood exposure using a scale with 15 items, covering experiences like property damage, injury and forced displacement; higher scores on this scale reflected greater exposure. They found that psychological distress increased as levels of flood exposure increased.43 Flood-related stressors, such as material and financial losses, personal or relative injuries, deaths or disruptions to family businesses, were significantly associated with increased risk of mental distress.42 Similarly, in Cameroon, floods had significant psychological impacts on survivors, leading to adaptive stress reactions such as emotional invasion and, in more extreme cases, panic, immobility and agitation.31

Three studies reported on interventions to address mental illnesses in flood settings.24 35 36 Specific interventions included Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT) for post-traumatic depression, Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET) for PTSD and depression, and Mental Health and Psychosocial Support for various psychological needs. In Nigeria, a 14-week post-treatment REBT had a significant effect on reducing depression for the treatment group, with sustained effects at follow-up 3 months after the post-test and lasted for 3 months (Ede 2022). In Burundi, flood survivors who participated in NET showed significantly greater improvement in depression and PTSD symptoms at the 9-month follow-up compared with those who did not receive treatment.36 Following cyclone-induced floods, programmes such as Mental Health and Psychosocial Support(MHPSS) have also been used in flood settings, specifically in Zimbabwe. In Zimbabwe, MHPSS was part of a holistic approach to address the psychosocial needs of young people and their communities.24

Mental health and droughts

The occurrence of mental health issues as a direct and indirect result of droughts was reported in 10 studies (online supplemental table B1). Experiences with chronic drought were associated with current trauma among individuals in Botswana,39 and determinants of depression among Tanzanian adolescents.37 In Botswana, a study by Zeligman et al found that loss of energy due to drought was a significant predictor of trauma symptoms.41

Anxiety was also prevalent among households facing recurrent droughts and floods in South Africa and drought-induced water scarcity in Zambia.19 21 In South Africa, farming communities are especially vulnerable to anxiety and psychosocial distress when facing drought-related environmental degradation.25 Psychological stress was among the leading indicators of social vulnerability to drought among communal farmers in South Africa.20 Although the effects of droughts are evident, the lack of mental health services is shared across multiple countries such as Somalia, Kenya and Ethiopia.27 In extreme cases, droughts ultimately drove men to migrate, causing intense anxiety over providing for their families, as seen in Tanzania.26

Only one study reported on current interventions aimed at mitigating poor mental health outcomes in drought settings.24 In the Great South of Madagascar, Christian Blind Mission Global Disability and Inclusion, an international nongovernmental organisation, has been addressing the adverse effects of climate change on the mental health and well-being of 1100 households with persons with disabilities. Their efforts include community awareness sessions on psychosocial disabilities, the distribution of mental health information and capacity building for advocacy on inclusive humanitarian response.24 These initiatives aim to support the mental health and resilience of vulnerable households during times of climate change-related crises. The paper discusses the case studies of the use of CGM in Madagascar, Nigeria, Sierra Leone and Zimbabwe, but no evaluation information is provided.

Mental health and cyclones

A total of four studies reported on the mental health outcomes in the aftermath of cyclones. In Zimbabwe, cyclones significantly impacted the mental health of survivors by causing psychological and emotional distress, PTSD and depression.33 44 Psychological distress is exacerbated by the loss of independence, control and personal belongings such as birth certificates, particularly affecting adolescent girls.44 Although mental illnesses such as depression are common in the aftermath of cyclones, the stigma surrounding mental illness hinders help-seeking behaviours.33

In the setting of cyclones, social cohesion and support networks can play crucial roles in providing security and hope to the affected communities.33 Mental health and psychosocial support interventions can help address community mental health needs and have been implemented in Zimbabwe following Cyclone Idai.24 In Chimanimani, Zimbabwe, social workers and psychologists played pivotal roles by offering trauma debriefing, distress relief, counselling and rehabilitation tailored for vulnerable populations such as orphaned children.32

Cardiovascular diseases and extreme temperatures

The impact of extreme temperatures, high (above the 95th percentile) and low (below the fifth percentile), on cardiovascular diseases was documented in three studies, two of which reported on cardiorespiratory outcomes.4547 The two studies that discussed respiratory outcomes noted that they did not differentiate or separate out chronic and infectious respiratory conditions. Scovronick et al found that low and high temperatures accounted for approximately 3.4% of the total mortality burden in South Africa over 17 years.46 Extreme temperatures, above the 97th percentile and below the 2.5th percentile as defined in the paper, accounted for 13% of the CVD-attributable mortality. Children and older adults are especially vulnerable to poor cardiorespiratory outcomes during cold and heat. Similarly, stakeholders, decision-makers and policymakers in Burkina Faso noted that extreme temperatures disproportionately impact vulnerable groups, such as children and the elderly population, who are prone to dehydration, cardiovascular diseases and respiratory disorders.47 During heatwaves, the elderly experience higher mortality rates due to their susceptibility to these conditions. The burden of NCD-related deaths due to extreme temperatures varied for men and women in Burkina Faso. Bunker et al highlighted that extreme heat increased NCD-related years of life lost more for men than women and that heat exposure notably impacted those aged 65 and older.45

Cancer, floods and extreme temperatures

Extreme weather events, such as floods and extreme temperatures, significantly impact cancer risks in two studies (online supplemental table B1). Iwegbue et al found that severe flooding in 2013 led to higher concentrations of 16 polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in floodplain soils, dramatically increasing cancer risks for exposed populations.48 In 2013, 6450 children and 4480 adults per million were at risk of developing cancer, compared with 443 children and 308 adults per million in the less severe 2014 flood.48 Bunker et al reported that extreme temperatures also contribute to premature NCD-related deaths, with malignant neoplasms accounting for 9% of NCD-related years of life lost in Nouna, Burkina Faso between 2000 and 2010.45

Discussion

This scoping review highlights the impact of extreme weather events on NCDs in sSA. Only 31 eligible articles were identified, spanning 16 countries in sSA, highlighting a significant gap in the literature. The paucity of identified articles does not reflect the rising prevalence of NCDs in sSA (67% increase in DALYs due to NCDs between 1990 and 2017) and the simultaneous increase in the frequency, magnitude and duration of climate-related extreme weather events.49 More research is urgently needed to further our understanding of the association of extreme weather events with NCDs in sSA to inform effective adaptation measures.

Gaps in NCD types

NCDs such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, mental health disorders and chronic respiratory diseases have become leading causes of morbidity and mortality in sSA.49 The DALYs due to diabetes have increased by 126.4% between 1990 and 2017. Similarly, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), driven predominantly by hypertension, account for 38% of all NCD-related deaths and 13% of all deaths in sSA.49 In recent years, chronic respiratory diseases such as COPD have also increased by at least 100%, significantly affecting the younger population.50 Despite the significant burden of chronic respiratory conditions in sSA, the two articles that mentioned respiratory disease did not differentiate infectious and chronic conditions, limiting our understanding of the impact of climate-related events on COPD and asthma. This scoping review highlights that the existing literature primarily reports on mental health outcomes in the context of extreme weather events.51 Although mental health outcomes were the most frequently reported in extreme weather event settings, there was still a lack of standardisation in how papers defined the same health outcome. While crucial to report on how mental illnesses (which account for 9% of DALYs in sSA) are affected by extreme weather events, solely focusing on one NCD leaves significant gaps in understanding how extreme weather events impact other major drivers of mortality.49 The identified studies also do not address the known impact of worsening mental health on NCDs such as CVD.51

In our review, we found that both low and high extreme temperatures affect CVD and that children and older adults are especially vulnerable to poor cardiovascular outcomes. The prevalence of CVD often increases following disasters due to a lack of access to healthcare centres, destruction of medication, loss of social networks and livelihood.51 While we did not find studies looking at the impact of extreme weather events on diabetes in sSA, individuals with diabetes face similar challenges during disasters.52 Disruptions in access to primary care and medications, compounded by psychological stress and lifestyle changes, negatively affect diabetes management.52 Extreme weather events can cause partial or complete destruction of medical infrastructure, thereby reducing access to medical equipment and damaging medication stocks. Access to medication is a particular concern for persons living with NCDs, which is challenged by healthcare infrastructure destruction as well as limitations to transportation. People living with insulin-dependent diabetes, for example, are particularly vulnerable to the impact of extreme weather events on medical infrastructure.52 Oncology patients are another vulnerable group whose outcomes worsen during natural disasters such as floods and tropical cyclones.53 This group requires highly individualised and continuous treatment, which becomes challenging with submerged medical records, destroyed in-patient units and power failures.53 54

Gaps in extreme weather event types

Disparities in reporting also exist for extreme weather event types. Most of the studies (52%) reported the effect of floods on NCDs, but only 13% reported cyclones, and 6% on land and mudslides. While floods are the most typical natural disaster type in the continent (60%), storms are the second most common type (17%), followed by droughts (16%).55 Since 1970, storms have more than doubled and are predicted to expand across the Southern African region.56 Though this is true, studies have focused more on the effects of droughts (32%) on NCDs than cyclones (13%). Additionally, climate-related floods and intense rainfall combined with deforestation and land degradation contribute to landslides, which account for 13% of extreme weather-related events on the continent.55

Gaps in cited countries

According to Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative (ND-GAIN Index), Chad, Central African Republic, Democratic Republic of Congo, Guinea-Bissau, Niger and Sudan were among the 10 countries most vulnerable and least resilient to climate change in 2022, none of which was represented in our study sample.57 Mozambique, which also was not cited in the literature, was the country most affected by climate change in the world in 2019, experiencing at least one severe extreme weather between 2019 and 2024, with cities such as Beira and Sofala losing over 100 healthcare facilities in the aftermath of cyclones Idai and Kenneth in 2019.58 One paper did address the association of these cyclone events with pellagra and did provide some evidence of the impact of cyclones on diet-related conditions.14 Mozambique has simultaneously been experiencing severe droughts, which were associated with mental illnesses in neighbouring countries such as South Africa, Zambia and Botswana.19 21 25 39 Over recent decades, Guinea-Bissau has also faced the compounding effects of severe droughts and floods, resulting in hundreds of deaths, property damage and economic losses.59 Chad, among the hottest countries in the world, now grapples with periodic droughts and floods, with the latter becoming a recurring hazard in Chad.60 It is estimated that in 2021, floods affected more than 300 000 people in Chad.61 The destruction of healthcare infrastructure in recurring and extreme weather events can negatively impact persons living with NCDs. Thus far, no studies have assessed the effect of climate-related extreme weather events on NCDs in these three highly climate-vulnerable countries (Mozambique, Chad, Guinea-Bissau), underscoring the urgent need for more research in this area.

Lessons learned from other parts of the world

The effects of extreme weather events such as hurricanes, heat waves and floods on persons living with NCDs are well documented in other parts of the world. Interventions such as population mapping, surveillance and early warning systems, telemedicine, stockpiling and predistributing medication, mobile clinics, psychosocial support and the use of community health workers have been employed or recommended outside of sSA to ensure that persons living with NCDs are prioritised and have their needs met during extreme weather events.6264 In India, for example, a combined intervention package that included community awareness, probabilistic forecasting and an early warning system as part of an action plan prevented an estimated 2380 deaths during heatwaves.65 Although interventions tend to be context-specific, lessons from other regions can be insightful to sSA countries. Piloting and testing these interventions in sSA is crucial for assessing their external validity and cost-effectiveness, particularly in contexts with low climate resilience. Addressing the needs of persons living with NCDs in the setting of extreme weather events requires strengthening the climate resilience of health systems by improving public health infrastructure, investing in financial, technological and human resources, and strengthening governance.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations that should be noted. The scoping review was conducted in 2023, with inclusion criteria spanning 1995 to 2023; no studies published since then were included. The second limitation is in the definition of an extreme weather event. There are climate-related events that, while impactful, do not necessarily meet the definition of extreme weather events in this review, such as unpredictable rainfall patterns and incremental temperature rises. This ambiguity may have limited the inclusion of relevant studies that explore the broader spectrum of climate-related impacts. In addition, our focus on NCDs means that chronic infectious diseases, such as HIV, were not included. This exclusion may have limited the inclusion of valuable insights in the literature. Finally, there was no standardised and uniform definition for NCD health outcomes. Many studies, for example, did not clearly define specific mental health conditions or employ different tools using self-reported data to diagnose the same mental health condition; this makes the comparison of findings across different studies challenging.

Recommendations

As extreme weather events intensify in sSA, it is critical to understand how these affect the burden of NCDs in the region. Addressing the identified research gaps is vital for developing effective public health strategies and interventions to strengthen the adaptive capacity of individuals living with NCDs to cope during extreme weather events. Multipronged approaches through policies, infrastructure and community engagement are needed to foster climate resilience among individuals living with NCDs.66 These approaches need to be context-specific and evidence-based. Therefore, it is critical to understand which sSA countries are most affected, what types of extreme weather events are most frequent, and which NCDs are exacerbated as a result of these extreme weather events.

Future studies should focus on:

Expanding geographic diversity to include more data from highly climate-vulnerable countries in sSA that are currently underrepresented in the literature.

Conclusion

The objective of this scoping review was to synthesise existing evidence on the association between climate-related extreme weather events and NCDs in sSA. We identified substantial gaps in the literature, with only 16 countries represented in the identified papers, with several highly vulnerable countries in sSA missing. Eligible studies focused mainly on flooding and drought, while the continent experiences several other weather extremes. The majority of studies addressed mental health, with fewer studies addressing other highly prevalent NCD conditions, such as cardiovascular disease. These findings underscore the need for a broader and more geographically representative evidence base for climate-related extremes and NCDs in sSA, with a particular focus on the most climate-vulnerable and least climate-resilient countries. In addition, future research should expand the range of NCD outcomes studied, consider a wider diversity of extreme weather event types and leverage lessons learnt from other parts of the world to inform sustainable, effective and cost-effective solutions to strengthen climate resilience in sSA.
