HPV11 targeting KDM4A regulates the polarization of macrophage M1 and promotes the development of nasal inverted papilloma
Authors: Liying Zheng, Baoji Hu, Wenhao Yao, Kaisai Tian, Gaohan Zhu, Mingming Jin, Shuixian Huang, Xiaoping Chen, Yi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of nasal inverted papilloma (NIP) is closely related to human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Previous studies indicated that HPV11 shows the highest expression in NIP tissues. However, the mechanisms following its integration into host DNA require further clarification. In this study, high-throughput sequencing was employed to identify the HPV integration site KDM4A in HPV-positive specimens. The HPV11E6/E7 overexpression model was established in human nasal mucosal epithelial cells (HNE-pC), and the KDM4A gene was knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Cell proliferation was assessed via CCK-8, colony formation, and EdU assays, while cell migration was evaluated through Transwell and wound healing assays. qRT-PCR and Western blot were used not only to analyze mRNA and protein expression in cells after HPV11E6/E7 overexpression and knockout of KDM4A but also to study the effect of the polarization of macrophages. A subcutaneous tumor model in nude mice validated the effects on proliferation and KDM4A knockout in vivo, with macrophage polarization types assessed via immunofluorescence staining. Results showed that HPV11E6/E7 overexpression significantly enhanced nasal epithelial cell proliferation and migration, along with promoting M1 macrophage polarization. Knockout of KDM4A inhibited these effects and delayed the progression of macrophages toward M1 polarization. Our findings suggest that low-risk HPV11 can drive the proliferation of nasal mucosa and regulate M1 macrophage polarization via KDM4A, potentially contributing to NIP pathogenesis. Targeting inhibition of KDM4A expression may represent a viable therapeutic strategy for HPV-positive NIP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01971-6.Keywords: Nasal inverted papilloma, HPV11, KDM4A, CRISPR/Cas9, Macrophage polarization

Introduction

Nasal inverted papilloma (NIP) is the most common benign tumor of epithelial origin in the nasal sinuses. It accounts for 0.5%-4% of all nasal tumors and 70% of benign tumors in the nasal sinuses, with an overall reported incidence of 0.03%-0.84% [1, 2]. NIP can involve the mucosa of the entire sinus system, including the maxillary sinus, ethmoid sinus, frontal sinus, and sphenoid sinus. Typical clinical manifestations comprise unilateral nasal congestion, epistaxis, sinusitis, and related symptoms. Although pathologically benign, NIP is highly locally invasive, has a recurrence rate as high as 34.09%, and has a potential for malignant transformation with a cancerous rate reaching up to 27% [3, 4]. Endoscopic resection is the main clinical treatment approach but often results in relapse; therefore, attention should be paid to its malignant characteristics. In the etiology of NIP, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is highly relevant. Therefore, further systematic exploration into molecular mechanisms related to the occurrence and development of NIP is essential for improving clinical efficacy through identifying important diagnostic markers and new treatment targets.

The human papillomavirus (HPV) is a double-stranded closed-loop DNA virus that infects the squamous epithelium of the human epidermis and mucous membranes, leading to a variety of diseases. Research shows that there are more than 200 types of HPV subtypes, which are classified according to their pathogenicity and carcinogenicity potential. These subtypes are divided into two categories: low-risk (such as HPV-6, 11, 40, 42, etc.) and high-risk (such as HPV-16, 18, 31, 33, 35, etc.) [5]. Moreover, HPV has been identified as an oncogenic virus that is not only closely associated with cervical cancer but also implicated in approximately 10%-15% of malignancies in other anatomical sites including the vagina, penis, and anus. Additionally, HPV has been linked to breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, bladder neoplasms, and so on [6]. Notably, the relationship between HPV and head and neck tumors, particularly with the NIP, has emerged as an area of intense research. In our previous study, Flow-through hybridization technology and gene microarray (HybridMax) were used to analyze the expression of HPV infection in the tissues of nasal sinus inverted papilloma (NIP) and nasal polyps(NP), and it was found that the subtype with the highest expression rate was HPV11.

From initial infection to tumorigenesis, it is a complex and protracted process encompassing persistent infection, virus-host interaction, and immune evasion. HPV acquires the capacity to proliferate and establish the foundation of infection by traumatizing the upper epithelial tissue and invading proliferating cells in the basal cell layer. During the process of keratinization, differentiation, and maturation, HPV undergoes transcriptional translation of early and late genes, aggregates to form viral particles, and sheds simultaneously with epithelial cells without eliciting a significant inflammatory response, thereby leading the immune system to overlook this viral infection [7]. HPV-DNA is integrated into host DNA through non-homologous recombination, which results in high expression of E6/E7. This inhibits apoptosis, interferes with cell cycle regulation, and accelerates cell proliferation. The E6/E7 proteins also interfere with specific aspects of the natural immune response, leading to persistent HPV infection, which immortalizes normal cells or causes precancerous lesions to progress to cancer, thereby contributing to the occurrence, recurrence, and carcinogenesis [8]. We employed high-throughput sequencing to detect the HPV high-frequency integration site KDM4A in the tissues of HPV-positive NIP patients. However, the specific mechanism of action in the occurrence and development of NIP remains incompletely understood.

KDM4A (histone lysine-specific demethylase 4a), a member of the JMJD family of histone lysine demethylases, plays a crucial role in epigenetic regulation. It has been found that the expression of KDM4A promotes the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells. Through the association between histone deacetylase (HDAC) and p53, KDM4A may induce p53 gene expression [9]. Abnormal expression of KDM4A is observed in lung cancer and it mediates tumorigenesis by regulating the transformation, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of cancer cells [10]. KDM4A has been found to accelerate the malignant progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by increasing the levels of miR-372 and Pim1 and inhibiting p21 expression [11]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), KDM4A promotes cell proliferation by interacting with p53 and reducing p21 transcription. Further studies have revealed that KDM4A sensitizes chemotherapy-induced cell death in a p53-dependent and p53-independent manner [12]. Therefore, KDM4A has become an important genetic target. Down-regulating KDM4A using molecular biological methods or inhibiting its catalytic activity with small molecule inhibitors may become novel anti-tumor targets.

In the study of HPV-induced NIP, the immune microenvironment also plays a crucial role. Macrophages, as efferent cells of the mononuclear macrophage system, are a class of phagocytic immune cells with heterogeneity and plasticity. Their phenotype is influenced by cytokines and other factors in different microenvironments and can be manifested as a classically activated pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and an alternately activated anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which is known as macrophage polarization [13]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) mediate tumor progression by regulating the tumor microenvironment (TME). The phenotypic and functional transformation of TAMs is a dynamic process, with the M1 phenotype present in the early stage of cancer. In the late stage, growth factors and anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β are expressed in TME, inducing the polarization of M2 macrophages [14]. Recent studies have shown that epigenetic mechanisms can also regulate the expression of genes in macrophages. Currently, there are more methylation modifications, acetylation modifications, and the like. As a demethylase of lysine site 27 (H3K27) of histone 3, KDM4s can catalyze the transformation of H3K27me3 into H3K27me1, thereby activating related genes [15]. KDM4A is an important epigenetic modulator that controls the reprogramming of macrophage M1 polarization [16]. In a study of atherosclerosis, it was found that KDM4A (JMJD2) promoted the activation of macrophages by oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL). Ox-LDL triggered the polarization of M1 in mouse and human macrophages, which was characterized by the expression of iNOS and the abundant production of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, MCP-1, and IL-1β. In contrast, the protein level of M2 marker Arg1 was significantly decreased after ox-LDL treatment [17]. However, there are currently no relevant studies on the mechanism of KDM4A in HPV-positive NIP and whether it can affect the tumor microenvironment in NIP.

Therefore, based on clinical phenomena, this study explored at the molecular, cellular, and animal levels whether HPV regulates the polarization of macrophages through the integration of human chromosome KDM4A gene locus and then promotes the occurrence of NIP caused by the proliferation of nasal mucosal epithelial cells, which became a breakthrough for the project team to explore the pathogenesis of NIP caused by HPV infection.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Cell line selection

The Human Nasal Epithelial Cells (HNEpC) were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Basal Media, Shanghai, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, New Zealand) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 95% air and 5% CO2. When the cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were harvested using 0.25%Trypsin–EDTA solution (Gibco, Shanghai, China). The reaction was terminated by adding the complete culture medium. After centrifugation at 1400 rpm for 5 min, the cells were prepared for experiments or resuspended in the new culture medium for passaging and future cultivation.

Construction of cell model

In this study, the early gene E6/E7 protein of the HPV was employed to simulate the infection process of HPV in host cells. By constructing the HPV11 lentivirus model, lentivirus with high expression of HPV11E6/E7 was stably transfected into HNEpC, followed by screening with puromycin (Pruo) at a concentration of 1.6 μg/ml for three days. Based on the overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 in HNEpC, the KDM4A gene was knocked out by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technique mediated by electroporation. A grouping model was established, with HNEpC as the negative control group, HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC as the positive control group, and koKDM4A-HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC as the experimental group, for the detection of changes in cell proliferation and migration.

Cell co-culture

The Transwell culture system was employed to simulate the cell environment. Human mononuclear leukemia cell (THP-1) cells were cultured in the medium of RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS in an incubator maintained at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2. A total of 1 × 106 cells/well were inoculated in a 6-well plate, and Phorboester (PMA) was added to each well to induce the differentiation of THP-1 cells into adherent M0-type macrophages for 24 h. HNEpC cells, HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells, and koKDM4A-HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells were added to the Transwell chamber and co-cultured with M0 macrophages for 48 h. Total RNA and protein were extracted from the cells in the lower compartment cells of each group, and the expression of typical M1 markers, such as CD86, and typical M2 markers, such as CD206, in the cells of the lower compartment of each group was detected by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell count Kit 8 (CCK8) determination

Cell viability was measured at 1, 2, and 3 days post-infection using CCK-8 (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Cells were inoculated into 96-well plates with 4000 cells/well with 5 replicate wells. After 5 h of pre-culture, 10 μl CCK-8 solution was added to each well at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Incubated at 37℃ in darkness for 1 h. Optical Density (OD) at 450 nm was measured by enzyme labeling. Relative proliferation rate analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

5-ethyl-2 '-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

Cell proliferation was detected using EdU test kit (UElandy, Suzhou, China). Normal control vector transduced HNEpC cells, HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells, and koKDM4A-HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells were inoculated into 24-well plates (5 × 104/well), and cultured overnight. 10 μmol/L EdU medium (500 μl) was added and incubated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 for 2 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.5% Triton X-100. EdU and DAPI staining solutions were added to the holes and the staining was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica microscope, Shanghai, China). We calculated the EdU incorporation rate as the ratio of the total number of EdU-positive cells (red signal) to the number of DAPI-positive cells (blue signal).

Colony formation assay

Three groups of cells were inoculated in a six-well plate at a density of 1000 cells/well and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium with 10% FBS in 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator for one week. Cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min. Colonies of ≥ 50 cells were counted and compared with untreated controls.

Migration assay

Wound healing assay

Logarithmically growing HNEpC cells, HNEpC-HPV11E6/E7 cells, and koKDM4A-HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells were seeded at 3.5 × 105 per well in 6-well plates and culture in DMEM/F12 medium with 10% FBS. Upon reaching 90–95% confluence, a ‘wound’ was created with a 200 μl pipet tip. Non-adherent cells were removed with PBS. Serum-free medium was added. Scratch distances were captured and wound areas were measured for 3 days. Wound closure percentage (% migration) was quantified using Image J compared to initial wound area (0 h). Photographs were taken at 100 × magnification under an inverted microscope. Cell scratch healing rate was calculated as (scratch area at 0 h—scratch area at timepoint) / scratch area at 0 h × 100%.

Transwell assay

The cells of each group were suspended in 200 μl serum-free medium with 2 × 104 cells/well and inoculated into the upper chamber of an 8 μm pore size Transwell plate. 600 μl of 15% FBS medium was placed in the lower chamber as a chemotactic agent. After 24 h, the Transwell membrane was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Three 100 × fields were randomly observed under an inverted microscope, and the number of cells passing through the aperture of each group was counted for comparison.

In vivo experiment

We constructed an HPV11-positive subcutaneous tumor model of NIP mice, to verify the effect of KDM4A gene knockout on tumor growth in vivo by injecting HNEpC cells, HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells, koKDM4A-HPV11E6/E7-HNEpC cells (2 × 106) into female BALB/c nude mice. We measured the weight of the mice and the volume of the tumors for 21 days. The extracted subcutaneous tissues were detected by qRT-PCR and stained by immunofluorescence. Among them, the expression levels of HPV11E6, HPV11E7, and KDM4A were detected by qRT-PCR, and the infiltration of M1 and M2 macrophages in subcutaneous tissue was detected by double staining of F4/80 + CD86 and F4/80 + CD206 by immunofluorescence method. Shanghai Gongli Hospital Animal Ethics Committee approved this animal experiment.

Immunofluorescence staining

The subcutaneous tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated dipped in wax, and then embedded and sliced. Then dewaxing, hydration, and antigen repair were performed, endogenous peroxidase was blocked, and then the tablets were rinsed with immunostaining blocking solution containing TritonX-100. The first type of primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 ℃, and the second antibody was incubated for 1 h and then developed and labeled. The antigen was repaired again and the film was closed. The second type of primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 ℃, and the second type of label was made. After DAPI restained the nucleus, the film was developed with a self-fluorescence quenching agent, and the anti-fluorescence quenching sealing agent was used to observe the images under the fluorescence microscope. Each sample was enlarged at 20 × and 3 fields of view were randomly selected for analysis. The immunofluorescence assay used F4/80 + CD86 and F4/80 + CD206 antibodies.

Quantitative Real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was used to detect the relative expression of mRNA in each part of cells and tissues. We used RNAfast2000 Kit (Fastagen) to separate total RNA, synthesized complementary DNA using cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Ozu, Japan), and designed Primer sequences using the Primer bank website. The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and measured using NanoDrop equipment (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The primers for qRT-PCR

We made qRT-PCR employing StepOnePlus RT-PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Thermal cycle conditions for qRT-PCR were: 40 cycles of 30 s at 95℃, 10 s at 95℃, and 30 s at 60℃. GAPDH and β-actin were utilized as endogenous controls. The 2−ΔΔCt method was utilized for calculation of relative gene expression. The value of each control sample was set to one and was used to normalize the fold change of the target.

Western blot analysis

We isolated total protein from transduced cells utilizing RIPA Lysis Buffer (RIPA Lysis Buffer Strong, Beyotime, China) through protease inhibitor (Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride, Beyotime, China). We gained protein concentration through BCA protein kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). We separated protein samples (20 μg) using electrophoresis with 10% and 15% SDS-PAGE gel to transfer them to the PVDF membrane. Next, we blocked membranes with skim milk (5%) for one hour. We incubated primary antibodies on blots at 4 °C overnight on a shaker. Primary antibodies used were: anti-KDM4A (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-HPV11E6 (1:1000, MyBioSource, San Diego, USA), anti-HPV11E7 (1:1000, Abcam, Shanghai, China), anti-CD206 (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-CD86 (1:2000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-NF-κB P65 (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-Phospho-NF-κB P65 (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-Phospho-IκB-α (1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and anti-GAPDH (1:20000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). GAPDH was applied as the internal reference. On the second day, we incubated blots through peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies on a shaker for one hour. Secondary antibodies used were: goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The protein bands were subsequently visualized by employing the ECL Western Blotting Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and the Tanon 5200 luminescent Imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Lastly, we analyzed bands utilizing Image J (bundled with Zulu OpenJDK 13.0.6).

Statistical analyses

Statisticians use one-way analysis of variance to assess differences between each group, with data expressed as averages. We considered p < 0.05 to be statistically significant. Our team used GraphPad Prism9.0 (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) for statistical analysis.

Overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 can promote the proliferation of HNEpC cells, and knockout of KDM4A can inhibit the proliferation of HNEpC cells caused by overexpression of HPV11E6/E7

HPV11E6/E7 virus vector was constructed and stably transfected into HNE-pC, and the HPV11E6/E7 infection-positive cell model was established. We used CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock out the KDM4A gene in an infection model. The expression of HPV11E6, HPV11E7, and KDM4A in cells of each group was verified by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. 1B-I). Interestingly, we found in the cell model that overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 also led to increased intracellular KDM4A expression.

CCK-8 assay, plate cloning assay, and EdU assay were used to verify the proliferation ability of cells in each group. The results showed that overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 promoted the proliferation of HNEpC cells, and knockout of the KDM4A gene significantly inhibited the proliferation of HPV11E6/E7 positive cells (Fig. 2A-E).

Overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 can promote the migration of HNEpC cells, and knockout of KDM4A can inhibit the migration of HNEpC cells caused by overexpression of HPV11E6/E7

Transwell experiments showed that overexpression of HPV11 E6/E7 resulted in increased cell migration, while knockout of KDM4A inhibited cell migration. Similar to the results in wound healing experiments, overexpression of HPV11 E6/E7 can promote cell migration, and knockout of KDM4A can inhibit cell migration (Fig. 2F-I).

Overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 in HNEpC cells can promote M1 polarization of macrophages, and knockout of KDM4A can inhibit the M1 polarization of macrophages caused by overexpression of HPV11E6/E7

To investigate the effect of HPV11 E6/E7 overexpression and KDM4A knockout on the polarization of macrophages in the HPV-positive nasal mucosa model. The typical markers of macrophage polarization (M1 and M2) after 24 h co-culture with M0 macrophages (THP-1 induced by PMA) were detected by qRT-PCR. At the cellular level, the expression of typical cell receptors of the M1 type (such as CD86, IL-6, IL-1β) was significantly increased in macrophages co-cultured with HPV11E6/E7 overexpressed cells and slightly increased in macrophages co-cultured with ko KDM4A cells. The expression levels of typical cell receptors (such as CD206, IL-10, Arg-1) of M2 macrophages were not significantly different in the co-cultured macrophages of all groups (Fig. 3A-F). In addition, we used Western blot to detect the expression levels of CD86 and CD206 proteins and the expressions of NF-κB P65, Phospho-NF-κB P65, and Phospho-IκB proteins in the M1 macrophage polarization pathway NF-kB (Fig. 3G-K). These all suggested that the overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 in nasal mucosal epithelial cells could promote the M1 polarization of macrophages, and the KDM4A downregulation could reduce the M1 polarization of macrophages caused by the overexpression of HPV11E6/E7.

At the animal level, subcutaneous tumor transplantation experiments in nude mice showed that HPV11E6/E7 positive cells grew faster in vivo, and knockout of the KDM4A gene significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 4A, B). It was suggested that overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 can recruit more macrophages in the tumor and induce the transformation of macrophages to the M1 type. Discussion

In the etiology research of NIP, although the etiology and pathogenesis of NIP have not been fully understood, the infection of HPV has a great correlation with the occurrence and development of NIP. Since the early 1980s, when evidence was provided to prove that HPV may be involved in the etiology of benign respiratory papilloma and squamous cell carcinoma [18], there have been international reports on the relationship between HPV infection and the occurrence and development of NIP. In view of HPV infection and expression in NIP, Xie Jia et al. [19] applied H&E staining, immunohistochemical staining, and Hybri Max, and the results showed that the total positive rate of HPV in all NIP paraffin specimens was 64.58%, much higher than that in normal nasal mucosa control tissue specimens. In 48 NIP samples, 49 strains of HPV virus were detected, including 12 subtypes (HPV-6, 11, 16, 18, 33, 39, 44, 44, 52, 53, 56, 58, 68), which suggested that HPV infection was closely related to the occurrence of NIP, especially HPV-11, 16, 44, 58 subtypes. A. Paehler vor der Holte et al. [20] analyzed tissue samples of 120 Schneider papillomas from 101 patients with non-recurrent and recurrent papillomas in the nasal cavity and sinuses, and used PCR for high-sensitivity screening of HPV. HPV infection rates for papillomas (46.7%) were higher than reported for healthy nasal mucosa, and in addition to common HPV genotypes (HPV-6, 11, 16), the team also detected infections with "rare" HPV subtypes (HPV-42, 58, 83, 91) in sinus papilloma tissue samples. Previous studies of our research group showed that the positive rate of HPV in NIP tissue specimens was 64.36%, and the single low-risk subtype HPV-11 was the main one (32.99%). The research data supported the hypothesis that HPV infection promoted the formation of NIP, and the clear distribution of HPV subtypes in NIP had a certain guiding significance for the prediction of clinical behavior of tumors [21].

E6/E7 is the main encoding protein of HPV pathogenesis, which inhibits apoptosis and interferes with cell cycle regulation by inactivating intracellular tumor suppressor genes p53 and pRB. HPV E1 is a DNA spiral box that is necessary for HPV genome replication assembly and replication, and HPV E2 is responsible for initiating DNA replication and regulating the transcription of HPV-coding genes [22]. The most important are the E6 and E7 oncogenes, HPV E6 is a powerful oncogene that acts by degrading or inactivating the oncosuppressor gene p53, E6-associated protein (E6AP) is a ubiquitin protein–ligand enzyme, also known as UBE3A, that has been identified to work with HPV E6 to degrade p53 through ubiquitination, Down-regulation of p53 indirectly promotes chromosomal instability, thereby increasing the likelihood of infected cells developing into malignant tumors [23]. HPV E7-associated proteins degrade another tumor suppressor protein, retinoblastoma protein (pRb), which is a central regulator of a variety of cellular processes including cell cycle, differentiation, and apoptosis. During the process of HPV replication, cancer suppressor genes are degraded or inactivated and host genes are altered to evade immune surveillance, resulting in persistent HPV infection, the immortalization of normal cells, or the development of precancerous lesions into cancer. Therefore, we simulated the persistent infection of HPV by overexpressing HPV11E6/E7 in HNEpC and then studied the role of HPV11E6/E7 in causing nasal hyperplasia.

As the disease progresses, the frequency of HPV integration with host genes increases, resulting in host chromosome instability, and the insertion of HPV genes causes changes in gene structure and function at nearby loci. Studies have shown that in tumor tissues, the expression level of HPV-integrated sites is higher than that of the same sites that are not integrated [24]. Unlike DNA sequence mutations, most epigenetic changes are reversible, which offers optimistic prospects for the treatment of diseases. Among them, histone modification has attracted more and more attention due to its rich diversity and important role in gene expression regulation. HPV11 E6/E7 gene overexpression resulted in 1,776 upregulated genes and 461 downregulated genes compared with the control cell lines of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [25]. Previous studies have confirmed that HPV31 E7 protein maintains the increased SETD2 levels in infected cells through an extension of protein half-life [26]. In precoated keratinocytes, Setd2 affects RNA m6A modification by increasing H3K36me3 modification, while KDM4A reduces H3K36me3 levels by demethylation, both of which play opposite but interdependent roles in regulating gene expression. In our clinical data, the majority of NIP patients were positive for HPV11, and the high-throughput sequencing results showed high expression of KDM4A in NIP tissues positive for HPV11. Similarly, overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 in cells can also lead to increased expression of KDM4A.

The immune system of the body is the most important means to defend against pathogen invasion. The fundamental reason for the occurrence, development, and recurrence of NIP is that the immune system of the body creates an inhibitory immune microenvironment at the most sensitive site of mucosal papillomavirus (the nasal mucosa and the transitional zone of the column epithelium and squamous epithelium in the healing wound margin area of the nasal mucosa) through relevant mechanisms. It mediates the immune escape after HPV infection, and thus cannot effectively clear the infected HPV [27]. The chromosome organization of the HPV genome is similar to the organization of cellular chromatin and is thought to be modulated by histone-based modifications [28]. Macrophages are highly plastic and they can change from one phenotype to another depending on different stimuli in the microenvironment. As a kind of immune regulatory cell, it plays a vital role in the process of immune prevention. In our study, overexpression of HPV11E6/E7 promoted M1 polarization of macrophages in vivo and in vitro, and by knocking out the KDM4A gene, we also observed a simultaneous reduction in M1 macrophages at the cellular and animal levels. Therefore, we hypothesized that in the early stage of HPV11 infection, the high expression of KDM4A stimulates the polarization of macrophage M1, which plays a role in the immune response against inflammation, which is also necessary for the initiation and development of benign tumors caused by HPV infection. Current observations suggest that KDM4A may be a new target for emerging epigenetic therapies for NIP diseases.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that low-risk HPV11 can promote the high expression of KDM4A and promote the proliferation and migration of nasal mucosal epithelial cells, and HPV11 can also target KDM4A to promote the polarization of macrophage M1 to regulate the local tumor microenvironment, and the elimination of KDM4A can inhibit cell proliferation and migration caused by HPV11 infection. It can also change the tumor microenvironment to regulate macrophages and reduce inflammation. Therefore, HPV11 can lead to the occurrence of NIP, and the infection of HPV11 may also be one of the reasons for the recurrence of NIP. Inhibiting the expression of KDM4A may be one of the treatment strategies for HPV-positive NIP.
