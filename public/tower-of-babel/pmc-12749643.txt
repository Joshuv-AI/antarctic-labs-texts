Stromal transcriptomics uncover LIF as a key effector in high tumor budding triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Pimchanok Phankeaw, Suparada Khanaruksombat, Warapan Numprasit, Pranisa Jamjuntra, Punn Augsornworawat, Malee Warnnissorn, Peti Thuwajit, Chanitra Thuwajit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The tumor budding (TB) and fibroblast activation protein (FAP)-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are associated with patient prognosis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). However, the impact between TB and FAP-positive CAFs on TNBC progression remains poorly understood. This study seeks to investigate the transcriptomic profile of FAP-positive CAFs in high TB TNBC to identify CAFs-derived factors contributing to disease aggressiveness. The 170 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded TNBC tissues were assessed for TB by pan-cytokeratin immunohistochemistry (IHC). Its clinicopathological correlations were examined through univariate and multivariate analyses, with overall survival (OS) evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis. CAFs whole transcriptomic profiles of 13 TNBCs was conducted using Templated Oligo-Sequencing and the differential expression was analyzed in R studio with the DESeq2 package. Pathways associated with differentially expressed genes were identified using the Enrichr package. The level of leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) was explored by IHC in 141 TNBCs, and its prognostic significance was determined. The induction of TNBC cell migration and expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) by a recombinant LIF, along with attenuation by EC359, a LIF inhibitor, were investigated using Transwell assay and flow cytometry. High TB was observed in 46.5% of TNBC patients and significantly associated with shorter OS, in which the upregulated MAPK cascade, ERK1/ERK2 signaling, and protein kinase B regulation signaling pathways were involved. In FAP-positive CAFs, pathways related to tyrosine phosphorylation, STAT protein tyrosine phosphorylation, and G-protein-coupled receptor regulation were also elevated. The overexpressed genes in FAP-positive CAFs from high TB-TNBCs included ZNF235, ANKRD30B, SLC26A2, SPDYC, CDKN1C, LIF, and FAM83A. The secreted LIF was found in 41.1% of TNBC cases with high levels detected in CAFs, while 26.2% of cases showed high LIF production in cancer cells. Recombinant LIF significantly promoted breast cancer cell migration and enhanced PD-L1 expression. These effects were attenuated by EC359. This study highlights the correlation of high TB in TNBC with poor prognosis and upregulation of key signaling pathways in CAFs. FAP-positive CAFs overexpress genes like LIF, which promotes cell migration and PD-L1 expression. LIF inhibition reduces these effects, suggesting LIF as a potential therapeutic target in TB-associated TNBC progression and immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28439-y.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer-related woman death worldwide1 with the estimation of 310,720 new cases and 42,250 deaths in 20242–5. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) subtype is classified by no expressions of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epithelial growth factor receptor 2 (HER2/ERBB2) and accounts for approximately 15% of invasive breast cancer6. TNBC has the high rate of recurrence and mortality due to its aggressive behavior7. Patients with advanced TNBC often exhibit resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs5. Although mechanisms such as miRNA dysregulation8–10 alterations, and Wnt signaling have been reported3,4, new approaches to better stratify TNBC are urgently needed.

Tumor budding (TB) is defined as an isolated single cancer cell or a cluster composed of less than four cancer cells scattered in the cancer stroma or the tumor microenvironment (TME), especially at the invasive tumor margin11,12. It has firstly reported as an important prognostic factor in patients with colorectal cancer13 and reflects the detachment of tumor cells driven by the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) representing an aggressive subpopulation of cancer cells with invasion and metastasis abilities14. According to the International Tumor Budding Consensus Conference, TB is considered an independent prognostic marker to aid clinical decision-making15. More recently, TB was found to be of prognostic significance in breast cancer including TNBC16–18.

The cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), the most abundant cells in TME, are indicated by an overexpressed fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and correlated with poor prognosis19. Moreover, CAFs secrete substances such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) which promote the cancer progression20–23. The association between TB and MMP/TIMP expression by CAFs was reported and suggested that breast cancer with high TB counts is associated with a more aggressive tumor phenotype, which combined with MMP/TIMP expression by stromal cells at the invasive front of the tumor, identifies patients with poor prognosis24. Moreover, half of TNBC cases had CAF-S1 and exhibited acquired resistance to immunotherapies25. According to a high resolution at single-cell level, a FAP-positive CAF-S1 subset mediated immunosuppression through accumulation of regulatory T cells and immunotherapy resistance in breast cancers26. However, the effect of FAP-positive CAF in high TB-TNBC on cancer progression and immunotherapy resistance are not explored yet.

In this study, the clinicopathological impact and prognostic value of TB in TNBC were explored. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) obtained from whole transcriptomic analysis in the high TB-CAFs compared to the low group, and in FAP-positive CAFs in comparison to those in CAFs with negative FAP were listed. The activated signaling pathways predicted by these DEGs identified the production of the potential key biological substances from CAFs related to high TB and cancer progression. In high TB-FAP-positive CAFs, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), a secreted cytokine, induced TNBC cell migration and the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). These findings highlight the prognostic value of TB and LIF secreted into the TME mainly from FAP-positive CAFs regulated cancer aggressive phenotype and PD-L1-mediated immunotherapy resistance.

Results

Tumor budding was a poor prognosis marker in TNBC patients

We designed a set of experiments to investigate the gene expression profile of high TBTNBCs compared to that of low TB cases (Fig. 1A). High TB was classified when at least one of the five areas had a TB count of ≥ 10 buds, as described by the International Tumor Budding Conference (ITBCC) guidelines15. From a total of 170 TNBC cases, 46.5% (79/170) had high TB (Fig. 1B; Table 1), while 53.5% (91/170) were low TB (Fig. 1C; Table 1). High TB was significantly associated with high pN stage (p = 0.006), high lymph node metastasis (p = 0.006), and high angiolymphatic invasion (p = 0.005) (Table 1). The OS of patients with high TB (n = 73) was significantly shorter than that of patients with low TB (n = 84) (95% CI 1.05–5.89, p = 0.041) (Fig. 1D). The univariate Cox regression analysis in these 157 TNBC cases revealed that high TB (HR 2.492, 95% CI 1.005–6.175, p = 0.049), tumor size > 2.8 cm (HR 2.603, 95% CI 1.010–6.711, p = 0.048), high pN stage (HR 2.453, 95% CI 1.041–5.778, p = 0.040), clinical stage III-IV (HR 2.837, 95% CI 1.195–6.737, p = 0.018), lymph node metastasis (HR 2.453, 95% CI 1.041–5.778, p = 0.040), and angiolymphatic invasion (HR 3.007, 95% CI 1.276–7.085, p = 0.012) were markedly correlated with poor patient survival (Table 2). While high TB, tumor size, lymph node stage, clinical stage, lymph node metastasis, and angiolymphatic invasion were considered significant risk factors of TNBC patients, the multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression model indicated high TB was not an independent poor prognostic factor (Table 2).

Gene expression profile of CAFs in high TB TNBCs indicated certain tumor-related signaling pathways

To explore the role of TB-driven CAFs in TNBC progression, spatial transcriptomics was performed on 13 TNBC tissue samples (11 high TB TNBCs and 2 low TB TNBCs) for which the demographic data were summarized (Supplementary Table S1, No. 1–13). Samples were compared using DESeq2 package. Hierarchical clustering analysis exhibited gene expression for the top 200 DEGs comparing the low TB cases to those with high TB (Fig. 2A). The volcano plot shows 139 significantly up-regulated genes in high TB CAFs (Log2fold change, Log2FC > 5 and p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S2) and 14 significantly down-regulated in high TB CAFs (Log2FC<-5 and p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S3). Among the 139 DEGs analyzed, the top five up-regulated genes were significantly associated with the phosphate ion homeostasis pathway (GO:0055062), monocyte chemotaxis pathway (GO:0090026), MAPK cascade pathway (GO:0043410), ERK1/ERK2 cascade (GO:0070374), and granulocyte chemotaxis regulation pathway (GO:0071622) (Fig. 2C). Gene enrichment analysis showed that the highest number of genes were involved in the regulation of the MAPK cascade, followed by the ERK1/ERK2 cascade.

Interestingly, the established tumor-related pathways, including regulation of MAPK cascade, ERK1 and ERK2 cascade, and protein kinase B, were upregulated in CAFs. The subnetwork between the pathways based on several databases including GO biological process 2021, KEGG 2021, and MGI mammalian phenotype level 4 2021 was generated (Fig. 2D). The network showed that fibroblast growth factor 4 (FGF4), insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2), CX3C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) interacted. FGF4 and IGF2 were involved in several cancer-related pathways including PI3K-Akt signaling, Ras signaling, and MAPK signaling (green line). CX3CR1 correlated with both FGF4 and IGF2 via protein kinase B signaling and regulation of protein phosphorylation (purple line). In addition, LIF is linked with all genes mentioned above (yellow line) through regulation of MAPK cascade (LIF-IGF2), regulation of protein phosphorylation and signal transduction (LIF-FGF4), and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (LIF-CX3CR1) (Fig. 2D). Moreover, MAPK cascade and regulation of protein kinase B pathway were upregulated in cancer cells of high TB (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These data suggest that the high-TB CAF gene signature may play a role in key signaling pathways, particularly the regulation of the MAPK cascade Supplementary Fig. S2B). Notably, several upregulated proteins associated with these pathways, including FGF4, IGF2, CX3CR1, and LIF, may contribute to their activation (Fig. 2D).

Up-regulated genes in FAP-positive CAFs revealed the aggressive phenotype of CAFs-related pathways

We investigated the level of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and FAP in all 34 TNBC tissues (Table S1). According to the level of FAP, these samples could be grouped to be FAP-positive (n = 19) and FAP-negative cases (n = 15) (Supplementary Table S1) with representative immunofluorescence staining (Supplementary Fig. S1). The KM plot revealed that FAP-positive CAFs (n = 18) were correlated with shorter OS compared to that of the FAP-negative group (n = 14) but with no statistical significance (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, the 712 TNBC cases from KM-plotter databases showed significantly shorter OS in patients with high FAP (p = 0.042) (Fig. 3B).

To further identify cancer-promoting genes in CAFs, transcriptomic analysis of 13 TNBC cases was reported in the heatmap clustering between 9 FAP-positive CAFs and 4 FAP-negative CAFs showing the up-regulated or down-regulated genes across the individual or groups of the patients. (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S1, No. 1–13). The data significantly demonstrated 45 up-regulated DEGs (log2FC > 5 and p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S4) and 3 down-regulated DEGs (log2FC<-5 and p < 0.05) in FAP-positive CAFs (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table S5), and 139 up-regulated and 14 down-regulated genes in high TB CAFs (Supplementary Table S2, S3). These up-regulated genes significantly correlate with a variety of cellular pathways such as Cellular response to hexose stimulus (GO:0071331), Peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation (GO:0050731), Tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT protein (GO:0042531), G Protein-Coupled receptor signaling pathway (GO:0008277) (Fig. 3E). For the subnetwork of the up-regulated genes in the FAP-positive group, the results showed the well-known gene, such as IL2RA correlated with Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, viral protein interaction, and hematopoietic cell lineage. Moreover, IL2RA was shown to be linked with LIF via cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and JAK-STAT signaling (blue line) (Fig. 3F). The interaction between LIF and CRLF1 was involved with the regulation of peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation and tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT protein (brown line). Interestingly, IL2RA was found to be linked with LIF and CRLF1 via cellular response to cytokine stimulus (pink line) (Fig. 3F). These findings revealed that FAP expression correlates with shorter TNBC survival and the gene signature from FAP-positive CAFs including IL2RA, LIF, and CRLF1 upregulation involve in the several mention pathways activation.

The seven up-regulated genes were found in FAP-positive CAFs in high TB TNBCs

The results revealed a significant association between FAP-positive CAFs and high TB CAFs in TNBC cases (Fig. 4A). High TB TNBC cases had a significant probability of having CAFs with FAP expression (p = 0.017). To identify the genes played a role in high TB TNBC cases with CAFs with FAP expression, we overlapped the 45 upregulated genes in FAP-positive CAFs and the 139 upregulated genes in high TB-CAFs, resulting in seven overlapped genes (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the volcano plot illustrated the differential expression of these seven genes including ZNF235, ANKRD30B, SLC26A2, SPDYC, CDKN1C, LIF, and FAM83A among the upregulated genes in high-TB group (Fig. 4C). Heatmap plots represented the expressions of these seven genes in CAFs from each sample categorized by TB (11 high and 2 low) (Fig. 4D) and FAP (9 positive and 4 negative) (Fig. 4E). The heatmaps demonstrated that at least three high TB patients exhibited upregulation of each gene compared to low TB patients (Fig. 4D). Similarly, FAP-positive cases also showed increased gene expression relative to FAP-negative cases (Fig. 4E). The cancer-related functions of these genes were reviewed and summarized (Supplementary Table S6). Among the seven genes, LIF showed the strongest correlation with the other upregulated genes in high-TB (Fig. 2D and Fig. 3F).

LIF expression in the TNBC tissue

LIF , is a cytokine playing roles in different systems27 including the regulation of breast cancer stemness28. To identify the major sources of cells for LIF, the expression of LIF in TNBC scRNA-seq using Single-cell Portal software was analyzed29. The data showed that LIF could be expressed in various cells in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 5A). LIF was found to be highly expressed in cancer cells and could be expressed in inflammatory CAFs and myofibroblast CAFs (Fig. 5A). Due to the effects of LIF via LIF receptor (LIFR) signaling on cancer progression30, LIFR expression was examined. The results indicated that the majority of cells expressing LIFR were CAFs, basal epithelial cancer cells, mature luminal epithelial cells, and endothelial cells (Fig. 5A).

We explored 141 TNBC cases showing expression of LIF from both CAFs and cancer cells (Fig. 5B). High LIF in CAFs was found in 41.1% (58/141) of TNBC cases, whereas only 26.2% (37/141) of cases showed high LIF in cancer cells (Fig. 5C). However, the univariate analysis suggested that LIF expression in both cancer cells and CAFs was correlated lymph node stage (p = 0.048), clinical stage (p = 0.33), and lymph node metastasis (p = 0.048) (Supplementary Table S8). The finding indicates that LIF can be expressed on both cancer cell and CAFs. However, LIF does not correlated with survival of the patients in our cohort. Therefore, the effects of LIF were further explored.

LIF could induce TNBC cell migration and PD-L1 expression

The human recombinant LIF was used to stimulate MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 TNBC cell lines. LIF in the range up to 50 ng/ml was found to be nontoxic to MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3). The recombinant LIF with nontoxic concentration (25 and 50 ng/ml) was used as a chemotaxis and induced TNBC cell migration in a dose-dependent manner compared to the untreated condition (Fig. 6A and B). The recombinant IL-6 was utilized in this case as a positive control of cancer cell migration. Only 50 ng/ml recombinant LIF induced both MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 TNBC cell migration with a significant cell migration induction only in MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 6B). The recombinant LIF (50 ng/ml) was found to significantly induce PD-L1 (Fig. 6C) but not B7-H3, Nectin-2, and CEACAM6 expressions (Supplementary Fig. S4). Furthermore, this PD-L1 induction effect of a recombinant LIF could be significantly attenuated by EC359 in MDA-MB-231 by a flow cytometry (Fig. 6D and E) and by an immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6F), but not significantly observed in MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 6D and E). In conclusion, the findings in this study highlight the crosstalk between cancer cells and CAFs in the context of TB-driven cancer progression affecting poor prognosis, which contributes to poor prognosis in TNBC. The data suggest that elevated TB levels may influence cancer progression through the upregulation of genes associated with MAPK, ERK1/ERK2, and protein kinase B signaling cascades in CAFs by cancer cell-derived substances (Fig. 6G). Additionally, CAFs-derived LIF secreted into the tumor microenvironment appears to play a pivotal role in TB-driven TNBC progression, both by directly promoting cancer cell migration and indirectly fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment through PD-L1 expression on cancer cells. The potential therapeutic use of anti-LIF antibodies in combination with chemotherapy or immunotherapy, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, in TNBC patients presents a promising yet challenging avenue.

Discussion

CAFs in the tumor microenvironment of TNBC have been strongly investigated and reviewed CAFs expressing FAP as poor prognostic markers by secreting substances from CAFs affect cancer cell progression31 and regulate immunosuppressive microenvironment26. TB has been approved to correlate with patient short survival in colorectal cancer and TNBC32,33, however, the roles of FAP-positive CAFs in high TB-mediated TNBC progression have been limited. The present findings revealed a notable decrease in survival among patients exhibiting high TB and FAP-positive CAFs. The spatial transcriptomic analysis of the CAFs in high TB TNBC exhibited upregulated expressions of a certain set of genes. LIF encoding is a secreted factor implicated in various tumorigenic induction mechanisms which may explain the association of TB-driven TNBC progression through induction of cancer cell migration and PD-L1 expression. This may support the important role of CAFs in facilitating tumor cell dissemination and evading the host immune response.

Our finding revealed the correlation between high TB TNBC with shorter survival, higher clinical stage, higher tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and angiolymphatic invasion. Similar findings were previously reported by Li X. et al. demonstrated that high TB was associated with larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and short survival time in TNBC34. Agrawal S. et al. revealed that TB count in TNBC was higher than the other types of breast cancer and correlated with shorter survival, larger tumor size, and vascular invasion35. Moreover, high TB was related to the androgen receptor as the potential candidate for prognostic markers in TNBC36. Interestingly, artificial intelligence and machine-learning analyses revealed the impact of TB on TNBC patients’ shorter survival33,37,38. Moreover, our findings showed a correlation between high TB and FAP⁺ACTA2⁺ CAFs or CAF-S1 subtype previously shown for its immunosuppressive microenvironment induction25. All these pieces of evidence support the fact that a high level of TB is a marker for poor prognosis in TNBC and has a role in the induction of cancer aggressiveness.

In this study, we employed TempO-Seq technology to profile FFPE samples, which offers a practical solution to the long-standing challenges of RNA analysis in tissues. Conventional RNA-seq methods are often limited by RNA degradation and chemical modifications introduced during fixation, resulting in poor data quality. TempO-Seq avoids these issues by using a direct hybridization and ligation strategy that eliminates the need for RNA extraction or reverse transcription, thereby allowing reproducible transcriptome profiling from fragmented RNA39. This feature makes TempO-Seq particularly valuable for retrospective studies, as it enables the use of clinically annotated FFPE repositories that have previously been difficult to analyze. By applying this approach to breast cancer FFPE specimens, our study demonstrates how TempO-Seq can generate reliable gene expression data and uncover clinically relevant molecular features.

The spatial RNA sequencing in CAFs of TNBC tissues exhibited the 7 upregulated genes detected in FAP-positive CAFs in high TB TNBCs including ZNF235, ANKRD30B, SLC26A2, SPDYC, CDKN1C, LIF, and FAM83A. Dimberg L. et al. identified the function of solute carrier family 26 member 2 (SLC26A2) on the downregulation of the TRAIL receptor, the tumor suppressor, to reduce breast cancer cell apoptosis40. Alsaleem M. et al. reported Speedy/RINGO cell cycle regulator family member C (SPDYC) as the novel prognostic signature for TNBC which is associated with shorter survival41. Moreover, the function of LIF on breast cancer was reported and suggested that secreting LIF enhanced breast cancer progression through the AKT-mTOR pathway42. Surprisingly, a Family with sequence similarity 83 (FAM83) member was reported as a breast cancer initiation biomarker43. On the other hand, cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 C (CDKN1C) was reported as a tumor suppressor gene in cancer cells, and its downregulation correlated with shorter survival of breast cancer patients44. There were no reports about the function of CDKN1C-encoding protein in CAFs, although its function in cancer cells as a negative regulator of cell proliferation in breast cancer has been reported44. There were no previous studies on the function of Zinc finger protein 235 (ZNF235) and Ankyrin repeat domain 30B (ANKRD30B) in cancers. Thus, further investigation of these genes’ function might lead to a novel approach to therapeutic targets.

The activated MAPK, ERK1/ERK2, and protein kinase B pathways were detected in FAP-positive CAFs in high TB TNBC. To support these observations in TNBC aggressiveness, these pathways may be involved in CAF-mediated cancer progression including acceleration of CAF generation, angiogenesis, and metastasis. The activated MAPK signaling through insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor (IGF2R) leads to CAF differentiation from normal fibroblasts45. Inhibiting of ERK signaling led to the reduction of IL-6 and VEGF produced from CAFs resulting in inhibition of angiogenesis46. In addition, the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a critical role in CAF differentiation, particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma, where exosomal miRNA-21 has been shown to drive CAF formation from hepatic stellate cells47. This suggests a broader role of PI3K/AKT signaling in CAF-driven tumorigenesis across different cancer types. Activation of this pathway not only enhances CAF differentiation but also supports tumor proliferation, survival, and resistance to therapy by modulating the tumor microenvironment47. Moreover, CAFs play a role in inducing an immunosuppressive environment by recruiting regulatory immune cells and modulating cytokine signaling which enhances immune evasion. Additionally, PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK activation have been associated with chemotherapy resistance and targeted therapies47.

Due to the limited number of TNBC cases, no significant correlation between patient survival time and FAP-positive CAFs. However, the published data showed the significantly poorer survival in TNBC patients with high numbers of FAP-positive CAFs. The activated signaling pathways of FAP-positive CAFs are peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation, STAT tyrosine phosphorylation, and G protein-coupled receptor-mediated cascade. The involvement of JAK/STAT in CAF conversion from normal fibroblasts was reported48. There is a report demonstrating that high expression of STAT3 in tumor stroma is associated with poor survival of breast cancer patients49. Moreover, G protein-coupled receptor 77 expressed on CAFs promoted tumor formation and chemoresistance in breast and lung cancer by providing a survival niche for cancer stem cells50. These findings suggest that the upregulated genes and signaling pathways in FAP-positive CAFs might promote cancer progression and chemoresistance.

LIF is a member of the IL-6 cytokine family but signals through a distinct receptor complex. While most IL-6 family cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-11, and oncostatin M (OSM), require their specific α-receptors to recruit gp130, LIF binds directly to LIFR and heterodimerizes with gp130 without trans-signaling51,52. Although all these cytokines activate JAK/STAT3, their biological roles differ: IL-6 and IL-11 mainly regulate immune and inflammatory responses, OSM modulates hematopoiesis and tissue remodeling, whereas LIF is involved in stem cell maintenance, embryonic development, and cancer progression, including epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). The increased levels of LIF and LIF receptor (LIFR) expression and its potential ability to promote breast cancer progression through a paracrine or autocrine pathway were reported53. Moreover, Li X. et al. found LIF expressed on cancer cells and associated with significantly shorter survival42. Our finding reported LIF expression was found in CAFs highlighting the tumor microenvironment’s influence on LIF signaling. LIF secreted from CAFs in more TNBC cases compared to that released from cancer cells. No matter the sources of LIF, the secreted LIF into the tumor microenvironment can activate cancer cells via the LIF receptor (LIFR) promoting EMT through STAT3 in breast cancer cells54. LIF/LIFR signaling drove the EMT and promoted breast cancer stem cells55. The CAFs-secreted LIF promoted cancer stemness through the dedifferentiation process and reacquisition of stem cell-like properties as evidenced by the upregulations of Nanog, Oct4, and CD44 + 28. LIF increased the glucose uptake and glycolysis via AKT/GLUT1 in breast tumorigenesis56. In addition, Li et al. defined the effects of LIF on breast cancer metastasis and tumorigenesis through the AKT-mTOR signaling pathways42.

According to our finding, LIFR is expressed in endothelial cells (Fig. 5A). This result suggested that LIF signaling might be associated with angiogenesis. Interestingly, Wu X. et al. showed the correlation between LIFR and IL-8 in increased tumor angiogenesis57. However, the effect of the role of LIF in breast cancer angiogenesis needs further investigation. For the effects of LIF on cancer migration induction, Zhou C. et al. have demonstrated that cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) secrete LIF which activates the STAT3 signaling pathway in breast cancer cells. This activation leads to increased secretion of CXCLs (C-X-C motif chemokines) which bind to their receptor CXCR2 on CAAs, initiating a positive feedback loop of LIF production that enhances the invasion and metastasis of breast cancer cells58. These collective findings suggest that LIF promotes breast cancer progression, migration, metastasis, and recurrence by increasing the properties of cancer stem cells.

It is accepted that PD-L1 expression was higher in breast and colon cancer stem cells59. Hence, PD-L1 expression may be detected in LIF-stimulated TNBC cells. Mittendorf E. A. et al. demonstrated that 20% of TNBC tumors express PD-L1 which is regulated by the loss of PTEN, a tumor suppressor gene. PTEN inhibits T-cell proliferation and enhances T-cell apoptosis, contributing to immune evasion60. Moreover, PD-L1 could influence the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages towards the M2 phenotype which is associated with immunosuppressive functions promoting tumor progression and metastasis61. Hence, LIF partly plays a crucial role in tumor evasion by the up-regulation of PD-L1 in TNBC cells.

Taken together, the present findings shed light on the effect of LIF from CAFs in high TB TNBC cases to induce cancer aggressiveness by induction of cancer cell migration and suppression of anti-tumor immune response via PD-L1. It is worth the value to propose the LIF/LIFR-mediated signaling pathway and its activated biological phenotypes as therapeutic targets in TNBC patients. This is evident by the previous report about EC359 inhibiting the LIF/LIFR oncogenic signaling pathway in TNBC62. Recently, the combination of chemotherapy, anti-LIF, and anti-PD-L1 targeting not only pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells but also augmenting the anti-tumor immune response has been demonstrated which strongly support the chemotherapy/anti-LIF/anti-PD-L1 combination therapy to clinical trials in PDAC63. This report showed the potential synergistic effect between EC359 and anti-PD-L1 by lowering tumor PD-L1 levels. Hence, EC359 enhanced T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity, providing a rationale for combining EC359 with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade as a potential treatment in TNBC. Future preclinical and clinical studies are warranted to explore the efficacy of such combination therapies in improving patient outcomes.

Methods

Patient cohort

The 170 TNBC cohort received treatment at Siriraj Hospital, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand between 2004 and 2017 were recruited in the study. The clinical data including patient age at diagnosis, histological grade, tumor size, lymph node status, tumor stage, clinical stage, and angiolymphatic invasion were completely available for analysis. Among the 170 FFPE cases with TB count data, 157 had complete survival information and were included in survival analysis. While, the FAP expression data were investigated in the other cohort containing 34 cases with no missing follow-up data. Hence, only 157 and 34 samples were used in Kaplan-Meier analysis for TB and FAP, respectively. These FFPE samples and data collection protocol were approved by the Siriraj Institution Review Board for human ethics standards based on the Declaration of Helsinki, the Belmont Report, CIOMS Guidelines, and the International Conference on Harmonization in Good Clinical Practice (COA No. si542/2022). informed consent was obtained from all patients and/or their legal guardians.

For FAP Kaplan-Meier analysis, the published cohorts were used for plotting survival curve64 using Kaplan-Meier plotter software (https://kmplot.com).

Spatial transcriptomic analysis of CAFs

The CAFs-enriched area in 13 FFPE TNBC (Additional file 1: Table S1) after H&E and panCK staining were identified by a high experienced pathologist under a light microscope and sent to perform the whole transcriptomic by Templated Oligo-Sequencing (TempO-Seq) analysis (BioClavis, Glasgow, UK). TempO-Seq was chosen for its suitability to profile transcriptomes from small FFPE tissue areas with high sensitivity, specificity, and compatibility with partially degraded RNA, enabling precise spatial transcriptomics. Briefly, CAFs-enriched area in FFPE tissues were deparaffinized using xylene and ethanol and then digested with TempO-Seq lysis buffer containing 0.5 mg/mL proteinase K. The digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate effective RNA release while preserving RNA integrity for downstream transcriptomic analysis65. The tissue lysate was incubated with detector oligos annealing to the targeted RNA template66. Amplification of the ligated oligos was performed using a unique primer set of each sample, introducing a specific premier set of each sample, introducing a sample-specific barcode and Illumina adaptors, and sequenced in an Illumina HiSeq 2500 High Output v4 flowcell and analyzed using BCL2FASTQ software (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The obtained FASTQ files were aligned to the Human Whole Transcriptome v2.0 panel consisting of 22,537 probes using STAR66. Further details can be found at https://www.BioClavis.co.uk and https://biospyder.com/technology/.

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

The raw data of gene expression were analyzed by comparing the gene expression profile of high TB versus low TB; and FAP-positive cases versus FAP-negative cases. The TempO-Seq data was analyzed in R version 4.3.1 using Seurat package (https://satijalab.org/seurat/) to normalize and cluster data. The differential gene expression between the patients with high and low budding was analyzed for statistical significance using DESeq2 packages (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) with the Wald test. Data visualization was performed using gplot and EnhancedVolcano (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/EnhancedVolcano.html) to generate a heatmap and volcano plot, respectively. For venn diagram, VennDiagram package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/VennDiagram/index.html) was used to generate the number of overlapping genes graph.

The related pathways and gene-gene interaction analysis

To explore the related pathways of the upregulated genes in both high TB and FAP-positive groups, the gene enrichment analysis was performed via R version 4.3.1 using Enrichr package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/enrichR/index.html) and the related pathways were reported as GO Biological process 2023 term (https://geneontology.org/). Then, the subnetwork that connects the 3 top enriched terms and the upregulated genes was performed using Enrichr-KG program67 (https://maayanlab.cloud/enrichr-kg) which include the data from GO Biological process 2021, KEGG database68 (https://www.kegg.jp/), and MGI mammalian phenotype (https://www.informatics.jax.org/vocab/mp_ontology ) databases.

Immunohistochemistry staining

For TB evaluation, the 170 FFPE sections were stained by an automatic staining machine at Department of Pathology as mentioned in our previous report (21). Briefly, the slides were strained with 1:350 dilution of anti-pan-cytokeratin (AE1/AE3, Dako). Then, the slides were subsequently incubated with anti-mouse EnVision + System HRP-labeled polymer (K4001) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (Dako). The slides were scanned using a 3DHISTECH PANNORAMIC 1000 microscope (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). To quantitate TB, five areas at invasive margins were selected within 200x original magnification and the number of budding cells was counted. According to the International Tumor Budding Conference (ITBCC) guidelines15, at least one TB count among the five areas was ≥ 10 buds was categorized as high TB.

For LIF expression level in the tissues, the 141 FFPE slides were baked at 60 °C for 2 h and then deparaffinized in xylene. The deparaffinized tissues were rehydrated sequentially in decreased ethanol concentrations and rinsed in running tap and distilled water according to a standard protocol. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in a 95 °C water bath for 1 h using Tris/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked by 1% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 30 min at room temperature, followed by blocking nonspecific-binding sites with 2.5% fetal bovine serum for 30 min. The sections were incubated with 1:400 rabbit anti-LIF (26757-1-AP; Proteintech®, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibody was the EnVision+ System-horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled polymer anti-rabbit (K4003; Dako, Carpinteria, CA). The immunoreactive signal was developed by Liquid DAB+ Substrate Chromogen System (K3467; Dako) and counterstained with hematoxylin. The tissue slides were dehydrated, mounted, and analyzed further using an Aperio ScanScope scanner (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL). To grade the IHC results, the LIF staining intensity (I) was recorded on a scale of 0 to 3 (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; and 3, strong). The proportion of positively stained cells (P) in relation to the whole tumor-involved area was scored as 0, 1 (1%-25%), 2 (26%-50%), 3 (51%-75%), and 4 (> 75%). The IHC score was calculated from P × I range from 0 to 12. The cutoff for the LIF score was set at the third quartile (Q3), with thresholds for the tumor area at 9 and the CAF area at 6.

LIF/LIFR expressions in the stromal cells of TNBC in the database

To validate the expressions of LIF and LIF receptor (LIFR) in the stromal cells of TNBC tissues in the established database, the tSNE plot was generated via the Single-cell portal (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell) using the data from the previous TNBC single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) study and the color in the plot defined the cluster of cells29. LIF and LIFR expressions in the various stromal cells were visualized using the tSNE plot.

Statistical analysis

The association between either TB or LIF expression and clinicopathological factors was assessed utilizing the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. Survival trends were examined via the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the Breslow test according to their non-parallel hazard functions. To assess this, we visually inspected Kaplan-Meier survival curves for crossing or divergence and performed formal tests of proportionality using Schoenfeld residuals. When non-proportional hazards were detected, the Breslow test was applied accordingly. Univariate and multivariate analysis of prognostic factors was conducted using a Cox proportional hazards model. All experiments were performed in triplicate as three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 29.0 statistics software (IBM, Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism, version 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA). The Student’s t-test was applied for pairwise comparisons of normally distributed data with equal variances, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among three or more groups after verifying homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. A statistically significant was considered when the p-value was less than 0.05.
