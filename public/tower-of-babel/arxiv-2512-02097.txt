Unitarizing non-relativistic scattering
Marcos M. Flores, Kalliopi Petraki

Abstract
Unitarity imposes coupled constraints on elastic and inelastic amplitudes. Satisfying them requires resummation of the self-energy contributions from both elastic and inelastic channels. Inelastic channels generate anti-Hermitian contributions that can be consistently deduced from the unitarity relation underlying the optical theorem, leading to non-local separable potentials and a compact unitarization scheme in the non-relativistic regime. We present two alternative derivations of the anti-Hermitian kernel, from the continuity equation and by integrating out inelastic channels. We further extend this framework to treat non-analytic and non-convergent behavior of inelastic amplitudes in the complex momentum plane and to incorporate bound states. For non-convergent amplitudes, we demonstrate a simple renormalization procedure in which anti-Hermitian separable potentials necessarily induce Hermitian separable counterterms, yielding finite cross sections consistent with unitarity. These results provide a general tool for non-relativistic scattering problems, with clear applications to dark-matter phenomenology.

Introduction

Unitarity plays a central role in particle physics. A direct consequence of it, the optical theorem imposes
coupled
constraints on elastic and inelastic partial-wave scattering amplitudes.
Because the optical theorem and the resulting bounds are non-linear in the amplitudes, they cannot be exactly satisfied in calculations truncated at any finite order in perturbation theory. Preserving unitarity therefore requires resummation of the relevant interactions. In the presence of elastic interactions only, the self-energy kernel of a (multiparticle) state is Hermitian, and its resummation ensures consistency with the unitarity constraint on elastic amplitudes. Inelastic channels, however, generate anti-Hermitian contributions to self-energy kernels that encode the probability flux into other states. Consistency with the coupled unitarity constraints on elastic and inelastic amplitudes then requires a resummation of these contributions as well.

The anti-Hermitian part of the self-energy kernel can be deduced directly from the unitarity condition underpinning the optical theorem. On this basis, Ref.
[
15
]
formulated a unitarization scheme in the non-relativistic regime for
both elastic and inelastic
amplitudes. The anti-Hermitian potential obtained in this way has a distinctive structure: it is a sum of non-local but separable potentials, a consequence of the fact that it originates from contracting inelastic amplitudes at different momenta. Remarkably, this structure permits compact analytic solutions that can be readily applied in phenomenological investigations, accommodating all partial waves and multiple inelastic channels. The form of the anti-Hermitian potential constitutes an important difference between Ref.
[
15
]
and other unitarization approaches that include imaginary self-energy contributions in the computation of inelastic amplitudes, either with
[

,
9

The goal of this work is twofold. First, we generalize aspects of the formalism introduced in Ref.
[

]
to establish a solid foundation for phenomenological applications. In particular, we develop a framework that consistently encodes possible non-analytic and non-convergent behavior of inelastic amplitudes in the complex momentum plane, which, as already recognized in Ref.
[

]
, affects unitarization. When such amplitudes generate UV-divergent separable optical potentials, we demonstrate a renormalization procedure and show that the absorptive (anti-Hermitian) separable interactions necessarily induce dispersive (Hermitian) separable counterterms. Including these Hermitian separable contributions is essential for renormalizability, and it extends the solution of Ref.
[
15
]
beyond the purely absorptive case; the reverse implication does not hold in general, i.e. Hermitian separable terms need not generate anti-Hermitian ones of the same form. Second, we expand on the theoretical underpinnings of the unitarization framework. We incorporate the possible presence of bound states, which introduce poles that contribute to the analytic structure, and present two alternative ways to derive the anti-Hermitian potential: one based on the continuity equation and LSZ reduction, and one based on the Feshbach formalism for integrating out states. Throughout the paper, we clarify various subtle points of the previous analysis.

The impetus for unitarizing inelastic cross sections arose in large part from renewed interest in long-range interactions in the non-relativistic regime in the context of dark matter. 2.3
Anti-Hermitian instantaneous self-energy kernel

To properly compute the self energy of a state, the irreducible kernel must be first identified. For a 2-particle state, this consists of all 4-point diagrams that cannot be separated into two 4-point sub-diagrams (contributing to the same 4-point function) by cutting two internal propagators, and are thus 2-particle irreducible (2PI).

3
Resummation

3.1
Schrödinger equation with a non-Hermitian non-local potential

Because unitarity implies a non-linear relation between scattering amplitudes, as seen directly from
Eq.

2.1
, it cannot be exactly satisfied by calculations truncated at a finite order in perturbation theory. Ensuring consistency of the scattering amplitudes with
Eq.

2.1
necessitates the appropriate resummation of all interaction kernels entering a calculation. The resummation consistently determines the self energies of the participating states, which in turn affect all scattering amplitudes. It is evident that elastic interactions contribute to the self-energy of a state. As discussed in the previous section, inelastic interactions contribute as well.

In the following, we shall denote by

{\cal K}^{\rm 2PI}(\mathbf{p^{\prime}},\mathbf{p})
the 2PI kernel of the state under consideration, with
\mathbf{p^{\prime}}
and

behavior of the unregulated inelastic amplitudes.

Two important implications of the
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
matrix are worth highlighting. First, the non-diagonal elements of

imply that an inelastic amplitude may be generated via loops that contain other inelastic channels, even if it vanishes at tree level.
Second, in the fully absorptive case (
\upeta_{\ell}^{2}\to\mathbbm{i}\upeta_{A,\ell}^{2}
) and for a single channel, the leading fractional corrections in the regulated cross sections of
Eq.

3.51
, with respect to the
\mathbbm{W}\to 0
limit of

are of order

\displaystyle\pm\dfrac{2y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}\sqrt{x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}(1-x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}})}}{x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}+(1-x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}})y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}^{2}}\eta_{A,\ell}^{2}\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}+\dfrac{y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}}{(1+y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}})^{2}}\dfrac{2x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}+y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}(2x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}-1)}{x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}+(1-x_{\ell,{\rm unreg}})y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}^{2}}\eta_{A,\ell}^{4}\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}^{2},

(3.58a)

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\eta_{A,\ell}^{4}\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}^{2}}{(1+y_{\ell,{\rm unreg}})^{2}},

(3.58b)

for the elastic and inelastic cases, respectively. This helps assess the importance of the corrections.

In summary,
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
collects contributions from non-analytic and high-momentum behavior of the inelastic amplitudes, and mixes different inelastic channels at loop level.

4
Renormalization

4.1
Setup

4.2
Divergence

The arc contribution to the

\displaystyle W_{\Lambda}(k)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2}{\pi{c}^{f}{c}_{\ell}\,m_{{T}}}\int_{C_{\Lambda}}\differential q\ q^{2}\ \dfrac{\hat{\cal M}_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}(q)\hat{\cal M}_{\ell,{\rm unreg}}^{\star}(q)}{q^{2}-k^{2}}=\dfrac{2}{\pi\,{c}^{f}{c}_{\ell}}\dfrac{1}{m_{{T}}\mu^{2\gamma}}\int_{C_{\Lambda}}\differential q\ \dfrac{q^{2+2\gamma}}{q^{2}-k^{2}},

4.3
Renormalizable theories:

4.4
Effective theories:
\gamma>0

Next, we consider
\gamma>0
. Equation

4.10
shows that the arc term

acquires cutoff
divergences multiplying successive powers of

, forming in particular a polynomial of degree
\gamma
.
Canceling these divergences requires contact counterterms with up to

\gamma
increases.
This UV growth also violates partial-wave unitarity at large momentum. In this framework, the loss of unitarity
tracks the loss of renormalizability: a contact interaction with
\gamma>0
cannot represent the true UV
behavior and must be replaced above some scale by dynamics that softens the high-momentum growth. When such
scaling appears in UV-complete theories, it is an artifact of the approximations employed.

Renormalization conditions at several momenta

To renormalize the polynomial UV sensitivity in
W_{\Lambda}(k)
, we may impose a set of conditions on both
w_{\ell}
and
\tilde{w}_{\ell}
at several reference momenta

,
4

and

\tilde{w}_{\diamond,n}\equiv\tilde{w}_{\ell}(k_{n})
,
which provides

renormalization conditions. The resulting renormalized parameters depend on the chosen set

, and the corresponding renormalization-group flow can be viewed as a multi-parameter (or multi-scale) generalization of the
\gamma=0

Low-momentum truncation

If one truncates

to leading order in

, i.e., keeps only the

becomes effectively momentum-independent. In such an approximation, the renormalization reduces to the
\gamma=0

k^{2}
divergences become relevant.

Eq.

5.10
,

\displaystyle\theta_{\ell}(-k)=-\theta_{\ell}(k),\qquad k\in\mathbbm{R},

(5.12)

up to integer multiples of 2
\pi
. From the above, we find the asymptotic behavior of the regular solutions at
r\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)

\displaystyle\overset{r\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\dfrac{\mathbbm{i}}{2k^{\ell+1}}\quantity[
\fs_\ell( k) e^{-\im(kr-\pi\ell/2)}
-\fs_\ell(-k) e^{\im(kr-\pi\ell/2)}
]

(5.13a)

\displaystyle\overset{k\in\mathbbm{R}}{\longrightarrow}\dfrac{|{f}_{\ell}(k)|}{k^{\ell+1}}\sin[kr-\ell\pi/2+\theta_{\ell}(k)].

(5.13b)

We may similarly determine the asymptotic behavior of the Jost solution,
H_{k,\ell}(r)
at
r\to 0
. In this limit, we expand in the irregular and regular solutions, as follows

\displaystyle H_{k,\ell}(r)\overset{r\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\alpha_{k,\ell}r^{-\ell}+\beta_{k,\ell}\,\varphi_{k,\ell}(r),

(5.14)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{k,\ell}=\lim_{r\to 0}\dfrac{{\cal W}\quantity{H_{k,\ell}(r),\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)}}{{\cal W}\quantity{r^{-\ell},\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)}}=\dfrac{(2\ell-1)!!\,{f}_{\ell}(k)}{(-k)^{\ell}}.

(5.15)

Because this is non-zero, except at the zeros of the Jost function (cf.
Section

), we need not consider the contribution from the regular solution, which is subdominant in the

limit. Thus,

\displaystyle H_{k,\ell}(r)\overset{r\to 0}{\longrightarrow}(2\ell-1)!!\,{f}_{\ell}(k)\ (-kr)^{-\ell}.

(5.16)

5.1.4
Orthonormality of regular and Jost solutions

For any two solutions,
u_{k,\ell}(r)
and
u_{k^{\prime},\ell}(r)
,
Schrödinger’s
Eq.

implies

\displaystyle\derivative{r}\ {\cal W}\quantity{u_{k,\ell}(r), u_{k',\ell}(r) }

\displaystyle=(k^{2}-k^{\prime 2})\,u_{k,\ell}(r)u_{k^{\prime},\ell}(r).

(5.17)

From this, and taking into account the asymptotic behavior of the regular and Jost solutions at the origin and at infinity,
Eqs.

5.3
,
5.13
,
5.16
and
5.5
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)\,\varphi_{k^{\prime},\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lim_{r\to\infty}{\cal W}\quantity{
\varphi_{k, \ell} (r),
\varphi_{k',\ell} (r) }}{k^{2}-k^{\prime 2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\mathbbm{i}}{4(kk^{\prime})^{\ell+1}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\dfrac{{f}_{\ell}(k){f}_{\ell}(-k^{\prime})\,e^{-\mathbbm{i}(k-k^{\prime})r}-{f}_{\ell}(-k){f}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\,e^{+\mathbbm{i}(k-k^{\prime})r}}{k-k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{\ell}\dfrac{{f}_{\ell}(-k){f}_{\ell}(-k^{\prime})\,e^{+\mathbbm{i}(k+k^{\prime})r}-{f}_{\ell}(k){f}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\,e^{-\mathbbm{i}(k+k^{\prime})r}}{k+k^{\prime}}\Bigg],

(5.18a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,H_{k,\ell}(r)\,H_{k^{\prime},\ell}^{*}(r)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\ \dfrac{\lim_{r\to\infty}{\cal W}\quantity{ H_{k, \ell} (r), H_{-k',\ell} (r) }}{k^{2}-k^{\prime 2}}=-\mathbbm{i}\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{e^{\mathbbm{i}(k-k^{\prime})r}}{k-k^{\prime}},

(5.18b)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)\,H_{k^{\prime},\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\lim_{r\to\infty}{\cal W}\quantity{\varphi_{k, \ell} (r), H_{k',\ell} (r)}+(-k^{\prime})^{-\ell}{f}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})}{k^{2}-k^{\prime 2}}

(5.18c)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2k^{\ell+1}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\quantity[
(-1)^\ell\dfrac{\fs_\ell(k) e^{-\im(k-k')r}}{k-k'}
+\dfrac{\fs_\ell(-k) e^{\im[(k+k')r]}}{k+k'}
]+\dfrac{{f}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})}{(-k^{\prime})^{\ell}(k^{2}-k^{\prime 2})}.

For real momenta,
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{R}

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to+\infty}\dfrac{e^{\pm\mathbbm{i}qr}}{q}=\pm\mathbbm{i}\pi\delta(q),\qquad q\in\mathbbm{R},

(5.19)

and

5.10

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)\,\varphi_{k^{\prime},\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{|{f}_{\ell}(k)|^{2}}{k^{2\ell+2}}\dfrac{\pi}{2}[\delta(k-k^{\prime})+\delta(k+k^{\prime})],

(5.20a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,H_{k,\ell}(r)\,H_{k^{\prime},\ell}^{*}(r)

\displaystyle=\pi\,\delta(k-k^{\prime}),

(5.20b)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)\,H_{k^{\prime},\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\mathbbm{i}\pi}{2(-k^{\prime})^{\ell+1}}\left[{f}_{\ell}(k)\,\delta(k-k^{\prime})+{f}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})\,\delta(k+k^{\prime})\right]+\dfrac{{f}_{\ell}(k^{\prime})}{(-k^{\prime})^{\ell}(k^{2}-k^{\prime 2})}.

(5.20c)

For momenta at which the Jost function vanishes, which, as we shall see, occurs for bound states,

5.2
Analytic properties of Jost functions

We now examine the zeros of the Jost function. We focus on solutions in the upper complex plane,

\displaystyle{f}(k_{0})=0,\quad k_{0}\in\mathbbm{C}_{+}.

(5.32)

From
Eq.

5.9
it follows that, when
k=k_{0}
, the regular and Jost solutions are linearly dependent,

\displaystyle H_{k_{0},\ell}(r)=c_{0}\varphi_{k_{0},\ell}(r).

(5.33)

In view of the asymptotic behavior (
5.5
) of
H_{k,\ell}
at
r\to\infty
, the left-hand side of
Eq.

5.33
vanishes at infinity for
\imaginary k_{0}>0
. The boundary conditions of the regular solution further imply that the right-hand side of
Eq.

5.33
vanish at the origin. Hence, both sides of
Eq.

5.33
are square integrable.
Since
k_{0}^{2}
is an eigenvalue of a Hermitian operator, as per
Eq.

5.1
, corresponding to a square-integrable function,
k_{0}^{2}
must be real.
This implies that
k_{0}
is purely imaginary.

We will now prove that the zeros of the Jost functions are simple zeros, i.e., that
{f}_{\ell}^{\prime}(k_{0})\neq 0
. Differentiating
Eq.

5.9
with respect to
k
and evaluating the subsequent expression at
k=k_{0}
, yields

\displaystyle{f}_{\ell}^{\prime}(k_{0})=c_{0}^{-1}(-k_{0})^{\ell}{\cal W}\quantity{H_{k_0,\ell}'(r),H_{k_0,\ell}(r)}+c_{0}(-k_{0})^{\ell}{\cal W}\quantity{\varphi_{k_0,\ell}(r), \varphi_{k_0,\ell}'(r)}

(5.34)

where
{}^{\prime}\equiv\differential/\differential k
. In order to evaluate the Wronskians, we consider
Eq.

5.17
for
\{\varphi_{k,\ell},\varphi_{k^{\prime},\ell}\}
and for
\{H_{k,\ell},H_{k^{\prime},\ell}\}
differentiate them with respect to
k^{\prime}
and set
k=k^{\prime}=k_{0}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\derivative{r}\ {\cal W}\quantity{\varphi_{k_0,\ell}(r), \varphi_{k_0,\ell}'(r)}

\displaystyle=-2k_{0}\quantity[\varphi_{k_0,\ell}(r)]^{2},

(5.35a)

\displaystyle\derivative{r}\ {\cal W}\quantity{H_{k_0,\ell}'(r), H_{k_0,\ell}(r)}

\displaystyle=+2k_{0}[H_{k_{0},\ell}(r)]^{2}.

(5.35b)

Integrating both sides, recalling that
\varphi_{k_{0},\ell}(0)=0
and
\lim_{r\to\infty}H_{k_{0},\ell}(r)=0
, we find

\displaystyle{\cal W}\quantity{\varphi_{k_0,\ell}(r), \varphi_{k_0,\ell}'(r)}

\displaystyle=-2k_{0}\int_{0}^{r}\differential r^{\prime}\ \quantity[\varphi_{k_0,\ell}(r')]^{2},

(5.36a)

\displaystyle{\cal W}\quantity{H_{k_0,\ell}'(r), H_{k_0,\ell}(r) }

\displaystyle=-2k_{0}\int_{r}^{\infty}\differential r^{\prime}\quantity[H_{k_0,\ell}(r')]^{2}.

(5.36b)

Combining
Eqs.

5.33
,
5.34
and
5.36
gives

\displaystyle{f}_{\ell}^{\prime}(k_{0})

\displaystyle=2c_{0}\,(-k_{0})^{\ell+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential r\ \quantity[\varphi_{k_0,\ell}(r)]^{2}.

(5.37)

Due to the boundary condition (
5.5
),
c_{0}\neq 0
. Furthermore, since the regular solution is a function of
k^{2}
,
\varphi_{k_{0},\ell}(r)
is real for purely imaginary
k_{0}
. This implies that
Eq.

5.37
cannot vanish. Since
{f}_{\ell}^{\prime}(k_{0})\neq 0
when
{f}_{\ell}(k_{0})=0
, we conclude that the zero at
k_{0}
is always simple.

The solutions (
5.33
), being square-integrable with energies
{\cal E}=k_{0}^{2}/(2\mu)<0
, correspond to bound states. Setting
k_{0}\to\mathbbm{i}\kappa_{n\ell}
, with
\kappa_{n\ell}>0
, and defining

\displaystyle|N_{n\ell}|^{2}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential r\ \quantity[\varphi_{\ii\kappa_{n\ell},\ell}(r)]^{2}=\dfrac{{f}_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mathbbm{i}\kappa_{n\ell})}{2c_{n\ell}(-\mathbbm{i}\kappa_{n\ell})^{\ell+1}}.

(5.38)

the bound-state wavefunctions normalized according to
Eq.

5.30
are simply

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{n\ell}(r)\equiv\dfrac{\sqrt{{c}_{\ell}}\ \varphi_{\mathbbm{i}\kappa_{n\ell},\ell}(r)}{N_{n\ell}}.

(5.39)

Note that the complex phase of
N_{n\ell}
is arbitrary, and can render the wavefunctions
{\cal B}_{n\ell}(r)
complex.

If the Jost function has no zeros, then there are no regular solutions that vanish at spatial infinity (cf.
Eq.

5.13
), that is, the potential does not support bound levels.

5.3
The Green’s Function

5.3.1
Position space

Having developed the above machinery, we will now use the regular solution, the Jost solution and the Jost function to construct the Green’s function corresponding to
Eq.

5.1
. In particular, we want to solve

\displaystyle[{\cal S}_{\ell}(r)-{\cal E}_{\mathbf{k}}]G_{k,\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\delta(r-r^{\prime}),

(5.40)

where

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{\ell}(r)\equiv-\dfrac{1}{2\mu}\derivative[2]{r}+\dfrac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^{2}}+V(r).

(5.41)

The Green’s function is a solution of Schrödinger’s equation for
r\neq r^{\prime}
, continuous at
r=r^{\prime}
with discontinuous derivative, as follows from
Eq.

5.40
,

\displaystyle\evaluated{
\dv{r} G_{k,\ell}(r,r')
}_{r = r'-\epsilon}^{r = r' + \epsilon}=-2\mu.

(5.42)

We are interested in a Green’s function that is regular at
r\to 0
and behaves as an outgoing (incoming) wave for
k>0
(
k<0
) at
r\to\infty
.
It follows that

\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\begin{cases}a_{k,\ell}(r^{\prime})\varphi_{k,\ell}(r),&r<r^{\prime},\\[4.30554pt]
b_{k,\ell}(r^{\prime})H_{k,\ell}(r),&r>r^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(5.43)

Continuity at
r=r^{\prime}
gives

a_{k,\ell}(r)=c_{k,\ell}H_{k,\ell}(r)
and

b_{k,\ell}(r)=c_{k,\ell}\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)
,
while the discontinuity (
5.42
) implies

c_{k,\ell}=2\mu/{\cal W}\quantity{H_{k,\ell}(r),\varphi_{k,\ell}(r)}=2\mu(-k)^{\ell}/{f}_{\ell}(k)
.
Collecting the above, the Green’s function can be concisely written as

\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2\mu(-k)^{\ell}}{{f}_{\ell}(k)}\varphi_{k,\ell}(r_{<})H_{k,\ell}(r_{>})\ \overset{k\in\mathbbm{R}}{\longrightarrow}\ \dfrac{\mathbbm{i}\,2\mu}{{c}_{\ell}\,k}{\cal F}_{k,\ell}^{*}(r_{<}){\cal H}_{k,\ell}^{(+)}(r_{>}),

(5.44)

where
r_{<}=\min\{r,r^{\prime}\}
,
r_{>}=\max\{r,r^{\prime}\}
, and in the second step, we used the solutions defined in
Eq.

5.21
.
This reproduces the Green’s function presented in (
3.15a
).

5.3.2
Spectral decomposition

For
k\in\mathbbm{R}
, the
r
dependence of the Green’s function can be expanded in terms of the eigenfunctions of the Hermitian operator
{\cal S}_{\ell}
that are consistent with the desired asymptotic scalings (
5.43
). Considering the small
r
behavior, we write

\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential q\ g_{k,\ell}(q,r^{\prime})\,\varphi_{q,\ell}(r)+\sum_{n}g_{k,\ell}(n,r^{\prime})\,{\cal B}_{n\ell}(r),

(5.45)

where we can take
g_{k,\ell}(-q,r)=g_{k,\ell}(q,r)
, since
\varphi_{q,\ell}(r)
is even in
q
(cf.
Eq.

5.4b
) and we integrate over a symmetric
q
interval. The functions
g_{k,\ell}(q,r^{\prime})
will determine the large
r
behavior of
Eq.

5.45
.
Inserting
Eq.

5.45
into
Eq.

5.40
gives

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential q\ g_{k,\ell}(q,r^{\prime})[{\cal E}_{\mathbf{q}}-{\cal E}_{\mathbf{k}}]\varphi_{q,\ell}(r)+\sum_{n}g_{k,\ell}(n,r^{\prime})[{\cal E}_{n\ell}-{\cal E}_{\mathbf{k}}]{\cal B}_{n\ell}(r)=\delta(r-r^{\prime}).

(5.46)

Acting with

\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential r\,\varphi_{\tilde{q},\ell}(r)

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential r\,{\cal B}_{\tilde{n}\ell}^{*}(r)

to project on scattering and bound states, and using the orthonormality
Eqs.

5.20a
,
5.30
and
5.31
, valid for real momenta, yields

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}[g_{k,\ell}(q,r)+g_{k,\ell}(-q,r)]=g_{k,\ell}(q,r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\,\dfrac{q^{2\ell+2}}{|{f}_{\ell}(q)|^{2}}\,\dfrac{\varphi_{q,\ell}(r)}{{\cal E}_{\mathbf{q}}-{\cal E}_{\mathbf{k}}}=\dfrac{2\mu}{\pi}\,\dfrac{q^{2\ell+2}}{|{f}_{\ell}(q)|^{2}}\,\dfrac{\varphi_{q,\ell}(r)}{q^{2}-k^{2}},

(5.47a)

\displaystyle g_{k,\ell}(n,r)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{{c}_{\ell}}\dfrac{{\cal B}_{n\ell}^{*}(r)}{{\cal E}_{n\ell}-{\cal E}_{\mathbf{k}}}=-\dfrac{2\mu}{{c}_{\ell}}\dfrac{{\cal B}_{n\ell}^{*}(r)}{\kappa_{n\ell}^{2}+k^{2}}.

(5.47b)

Combining the above, and exchanging
\varphi_{q,\ell}
for
{\cal F}_{q,\ell}
according to
Eq.

5.21a
, leads to the spectral decomposition of the Green’s function presented in
Eq.

3.15b
,

\displaystyle G_{k,\ell}(r,r^{\prime})=\dfrac{2\mu}{{c}_{\ell}}\times\quantity[
\frac{1}{\pi}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty\dd q\ \dfrac{{\cal F}_{q,\ell}(r){\cal F}_{q,\ell}^*(r')}
{q^2 - k^2 - \ii\epsilon}
-
\sum_n
\frac{
{\cal B}_{n\ell}(r)
{\cal B}_{n\ell}^*(r')
}{\kappa_{n\ell}^2 + k^2}
],\qquad k>0,

(5.48)

where the prescription
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
ensures the desired asymptotic behavior at large
r
given in
Eq.

5.43
, for the physical range,
k>0

6
Optical potential

In a Hermitian theory, the contribution of inelastic processes to the self-energy kernel can be obtained by isolating the state of interest and integrating out all other degrees of freedom, namely the final states of the inelastic interactions, using the Feshbach projection
[
12
,
13
]
. We review the standard derivation of the optical potential in this framework, and then extend it to express the anti-Hermitian kernel in terms of irreducible inelastic amplitudes. To our knowledge, this explicit representation has not previously appeared in the literature.

6.1
Feshbach projection

We consider the full Hermitian Hamiltonian of a system,
H_{{\rm full}}=H_{{\rm full}}^{\dagger}
, acting on a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
decomposed into orthogonal subspaces, with projection operators
P
and
Q
,

\displaystyle{\cal H}=P{\cal H}\oplus Q{\cal H},\qquad P+Q=1,\quad P^{2}=P,\quad Q^{2}=Q,\quad PQ=0,

(6.1)

i.e. a general state can be decomposed as

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=|\Psi_{P}\rangle+|\Psi_{Q}\rangle,\qquad\text{where}\quad|\Psi_{P}\rangle\equiv P|\Psi\rangle,\quad|\Psi_{Q}\rangle\equiv Q|\Psi\rangle.

(6.2)

Then, applying the two projection operators,
P
and
Q
, on the Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle H_{{\rm full}}\,|\Psi\rangle=E\,|\Psi\rangle,

(6.3a)

we obtain the projected equations

\displaystyle PH_{{\rm full}}P\,|\Psi_{P}\rangle+PH_{{\rm full}}Q\,|\Psi_{Q}\rangle

\displaystyle=E\,|\Psi_{P}\rangle,

(6.3b)

\displaystyle QH_{{\rm full}}P\,|\Psi_{P}\rangle+QH_{{\rm full}}Q\,|\Psi_{Q}\rangle

\displaystyle=E\,|\Psi_{Q}\rangle.

(6.3c)

We may solve
Eq.

6.3c
for
|\Psi_{Q}\rangle
,

\displaystyle|\Psi_{Q}\rangle=\dfrac{1}{E-QH_{{\rm full}}Q+\mathbbm{i}\epsilon}\,QH_{{\rm full}}P\,|\Psi_{P}\rangle,

(6.4)

with the prescription
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
ensuring an outgoing spherical wave. Substituting into
Eq.

6.3b
,

\displaystyle\left(PH_{{\rm full}}P+V_{\rm opt}\right)\,|\Psi_{P}\rangle=E\,|\Psi_{P}\rangle,

(6.5)

where
V^{\rm opt}
is the optical potential,

\displaystyle V^{\rm opt}\equiv PH_{{\rm full}}Q\dfrac{1}{E-QH_{{\rm full}}Q+\mathbbm{i}\epsilon}QH_{{\rm full}}P.

(6.6)

Hermitian and anti-Hermitian contributions

As any operator, the optical potential can be decomposed into Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts,

\displaystyle V^{\rm opt}=V_{H}^{\rm opt}+V_{A}^{\rm opt},\quad\text{where}\quad V_{H}^{\rm opt}\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}(V^{\rm opt}+V^{{\rm opt}\,\dagger}),\quad V_{A}^{\rm opt}\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}(V^{\rm opt}-V^{{\rm opt}\,\dagger}).

(6.7)

Considering
Eq.

6.6
, and using

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{x+\mathbbm{i}0^{+}}=\mathtt{P}\dfrac{1}{x}-\mathbbm{i}\pi\delta(x),

(6.8)

where
\mathtt{P}
denotes the Principal Value, we find

\displaystyle V_{H}^{\rm opt}

\displaystyle=PH_{{\rm full}}Q\ \mathtt{P}\left(\dfrac{1}{E-QH_{{\rm full}}Q}\right)QH_{{\rm full}}P,

(6.9a)

\displaystyle V_{A}^{\rm opt}

\displaystyle=-\mathbbm{i}\pi\,PH_{{\rm full}}Q\,\delta\Big(E-QH_{{\rm full}}Q\Big)\,QH_{{\rm full}}P.

(6.9b)

V_{A}^{\rm opt}
is the absorptive part of the optical potential. It is easy to see that
\mathbbm{i}V_{A}^{{\rm opt}}
is positive semi-definite for any state in
P
space,
\langle P\Psi|\mathbbm{i}V_{A}^{{\rm opt}}|P\Psi\rangle\geqslant 0
.

6.2
Absorptive potential in terms of irreducible inelastic amplitudes

Moving beyond the standard Feshbach formalism, we shall now project
V_{A}^{\rm opt}
into
P
-space states, and aim to express it in terms of
P
-state interaction amplitudes.

Let
\chi^{j}
be the eigenstates of
QH_{\rm full}Q

Q^{2}=Q

\displaystyle(QH_{\rm full}Q)\,\chi^{j}=E^{j}\,\chi^{j}\quad\overset{Q^{2}=Q}{\Rightarrow}\quad(QH_{\rm full}Q)\,(Q\chi^{j})=E^{j}\,(Q\chi^{j}).

(6.10)

If the

(no summation over
j
), i.e.
\chi^{j}
are eigenstates of
Q
as well, with
\lambda_{j}=1

and
Q

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{j}\dfrac{1}{{c}^{j}}\int\dfrac{\differential^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,|\chi^{j}(\mathbf{k})\rangle_{{{\rm NR}}}\ {}_{{{\rm NR}}}\langle\chi^{j}(\mathbf{k})|,

(6.11)

where we assumed for simplicity that

are 2-particle states, with
\pm{\mathbf{k}}

The index

{}_{{{\rm NR}}}\langle\chi^{i}(\mathbf{k})|\chi^{j}(\mathbf{\tilde{k}})\rangle_{{{\rm NR}}}={c}^{j}(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{\tilde{k}})\,\delta^{ij}

Inserting the above into

\displaystyle V_{A}^{\rm opt}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\mathbbm{i}}{2}\sum_{i,j}\dfrac{1}{{c}^{i}{c}^{j}}\!\int\!\dfrac{\differential^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\dfrac{\differential^{3}\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ {\cal T}^{\dagger}\,|\chi^{i}(\mathbf{k})\rangle_{{{\rm NR}}}\ {}_{{{\rm NR}}}\langle\chi^{i}(\mathbf{k})|\,(2\pi)\delta(E-QH_{{\rm full}}Q)\,|\chi^{j}(\mathbf{\tilde{k}})\rangle_{{{\rm NR}}}\ {}_{{{\rm NR}}}\langle\chi^{j}(\mathbf{\tilde{k}})|\,{\cal T}

where in the third step, setting
\mathbf{k}\to\mathbf{k}_{1}

Next, we project on incoming and outgoing

\mathbf{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle{}_{{{\rm NR}}}\langle\chi^{j}(\mathbf{k})|\,{\cal T}\,|\mathbf{p}\rangle_{{{\rm NR}}}

\displaystyle{}_{{{\rm NR}}}\langle\mathbf{p}|V_{A}^{\rm opt}|\mathbf{p^{\prime}}\rangle_{{{\rm NR}}}

7
Discussion

Methodology

The unitarization method developed in Ref.
[
15
]
ensures that scattering amplitudes satisfy the coupled unitarity constraints governing elastic and inelastic processes. By properly resumming the inelastic contributions to the self-energy of the incoming state, in addition to those from purely elastic interactions, this approach establishes a precise relation between the regulated and unregulated amplitudes, computed with and without inclusion of the inelastic effects, respectively.

The consistency of the method relies on the form of the anti-Hermitian potential, expressed in terms of the irreducible inelastic vertices. As argued in Ref.
[
15
]
, this specific form emanates from the unitarity relation underpinning the optical theorem. In the present work, we showed that it can also be derived from the continuity equation and from the Feshbach formalism. The anti-Hermitian potential – non-local but separable – gives rise to a Schrödinger equation that admits an analytic solution and yields a compact relation between the regulated and unregulated amplitudes, for both elastic and inelastic channels. Under suitable analyticity and convergence conditions, the regularization scheme depends solely on the unregulated inelastic amplitudes in a remarkably simple manner
[
15
]
.

In this work, we have employed the analytical solution previously obtained in Ref.
[
15
]
to systematically encode the effects of possible non-analytic or non-convergent behavior of the unregulated inelastic amplitudes, as well as the presence of bound states. This behavior is encapsulated in the
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
matrix defined in
Eq.

3.41
, which enters the unitarization prescription through
Eq.

3.51
.
The
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
matrix can be evaluated in three equivalent ways:

(i)

By (numerically) computing the integral involving the unregulated inelastic amplitudes, given in
Eq.

3.55
, along the real momentum axis.

(ii)

Through contour integration of the expression (
3.55
) on the complex momentum plane, as in
Eq.

3.57
, which makes manifest the contributions arising from their non-analyticities and from their divergent behavior at large momentum.

(iii)

Directly from the
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
definition (
3.41
), involving overlap integrals of the irreducible inelastic vertices with the regular and irregular wavefunctions of the
{\cal V}^{(0)}
potential.

For amplitudes that are non-convergent at large momentum, we demonstrated a renormalization procedure within the unitarization scheme. An important outcome is that absorptive (anti-Hermitian) separable interactions necessarily induce Hermitian separable counterterms of the same structure, and these must be included for renormalization to succeed. In doing so, we have generalized the solution of Ref.
[
15
]
, which, conversely, can now be utilized to handle Hermitian separable potentials, even in the absence of absorptive counterparts.

Phenomenology

Having developed this framework, it is now possible to apply the unitarization method to a broad range of cases. Let us return to the examples mentioned in the introduction, which have provided much of the phenomenological motivation for this and other related works
[
8
,
9
,
32
,
40
]
.

Bound-state-formation

amplitudes are ultra-soft and therefore fall rapidly at large momenta, giving a vanishing contribution from the large-momentum arc in
Eq.

3.57c
. They may nevertheless contain poles or branch cuts in the complex momentum plane, which yield finite contributions to the
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
matrix. These terms may be obtained either by contour integration, as in (
3.57
), or by evaluating (
3.55
) along the real momentum axis.

Regularizing bound-state formation is phenomenologically crucial when capture proceeds via emission of a scalar or vector boson that carries a conserved charge: without regularization, such processes violate unitarity severely at sufficiently low velocities, even for arbitrarily small couplings
[
29
,
31
,
30
,
16
,
7
,
5
]
. Even when this pathology is absent, e.g. for capture via emission of an Abelian gauge boson or a neutral scalar, unregulated cross sections can exceed the unitarity bound at large but still perturbative couplings
[
39
,
34
,
33
]
. The unitarization procedure developed here applies in both situations, and requires minimal input: the unregulated bound-state formation cross sections. (The unregulated elastic cross sections are also required to obtain the regulated ones, but do not enter the regularization of the inelastic cross sections.)

Contact-type inelastic interactions

(such as annihilation of non-relativistic particles into relativistic species), when calculated perturbatively, violate unitarity at large enough couplings that may still be well within the perturbative regime. The problem is ameliorated if the interacting particles couple via a long-range force (e.g. a Coulomb potential) that is integrated in the computation (see e.g.
[
39
]
), but persists nonetheless.

In these cases, resumming the irreducible inelastic interactions introduces divergences that can be absorbed into the
\mathbbm{W}_{\ell}
matrix and renormalized following the procedure described in
Section

4
. After renormalization, the resulting cross sections satisfy unitarity. The final expressions take a form analogous to
Eq.

4.23
, potentially extended to a multi-channel framework, with the necessary inputs consisting of the unregulated inelastic cross sections together with the dispersive couplings induced by the inelastic vertices.

Sommerfeld (parametric) resonances

arise when particles interact through a light but massive mediator and a bound level lies near threshold. For
s
-wave annihilation processes, these resonances drive the cross sections to
\sigma_{\ell=0}^{\rm ann}\propto v_{\rm rel}^{-3}
at low velocities, thereby violating unitarity even at small couplings. Equivalently, the unregulated partial-wave amplitudes behave as
{\cal M}_{\ell=0,\,{\rm unreg}}\sim 1/k
at small momenta. (For higher partial waves, the centrifugal barrier removes the infrared divergence at very low momenta.)

Upon regularization, this infrared divergence is canceled by phase-space suppression in the integral of
Eq.

3.55
and causes no problem. The ultraviolet issue due to the contact part of the interaction remains, and, as mentioned above, can be treated according to
Section

4
, with the result of unitarization and renormalization shown in
Eq.

4.23
for a single channel.

These examples have a wide range of phenomenological applications, including dark-matter production in the early universe and indirect-detection signals. We leave detailed phenomenological studies to future work, though we note Ref.
[
35
]
, where the simplest version of the prescription
[
15
]
has already been applied to dark-matter freeze-out. We anticipate that the unitarization framework developed in Ref.
[
15
]
, together with the extensions presented here, will prove useful across a range of physics beyond the Standard Model, and potentially also within the Standard Model, where long-standing interest in unitarization has motivated a variety of approaches, particularly in hadronic physics (see
[
