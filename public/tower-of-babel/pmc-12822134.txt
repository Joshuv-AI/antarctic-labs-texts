Self-efficacy and self-esteem are associated with health-promoting lifestyle in Iranian post-menopausal women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Majid Movahed Majd, Razieh Rayanpour, Farzaneh Mosahebfard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe number of postmenopausal women is expected to grow dramatically in the coming decades in Iran. Adopting a health-promoting lifestyle (HPL) is essential for improving health and quality of life of this population. This study pursues two objectives: (1) to assess the HPL levels among Iranian postmenopausal women and (2) to investigate the socio-demographic and psychological variables including self-efficacy and self-esteem associated with HPL.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study with correlational design, 400 married postmenopausal women in Shiraz (southwestern Iran) were selected via stratified random sampling. The data collection tools were the Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile-II (HPLP-II), General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE), the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, and a sociodemographic information questionnaire. Pearson’s correlation coefficient test, independent samples t-tests, and stepwise multiple linear regression were used for analyzing the data in SPSS v. 22.ResultsThe average age of natural menopause among participants was 49.33 ± 3.43 years, and they had relatively good HPLP (136.52 ± 21.46). The results highlighted that among all the HPLP subscales, the highest mean score was observed for health responsibility (33.62 ± 6.98), whereas the lowest scores were observed for stress management (12.26 ± 3.13) and physical activity (17.64 ± 6.52). Both self-efficacy (r = 0.445, p < 0.01) and self-esteem (r = 0.425, p < 0.01) demonstrated significant, moderate positive correlations with the total HPL score. Also, there was a significant association between the total HPLP score and two demographic variables: women’s education (r = 0.17, p < 0.01) and husband’s educational level (r = 0.113, p < 0.05). In addition, the HPLP total score had a significant negative relationship with the number of children (r=-0.114, p < 0.05). The stepwise multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the variables independently associated with the HPL of participants were self-efficacy (β = 0.32, p < 0.001), self-esteem (β = 0.27, p < 0.001), and occupation (β = 0.144, p < 0.001).ConclusionIt is suggested that health care professionals implement comprehensive planning to enhance the HPL of postmenopausal women. Interventions aimed at improving HPL in this population should prioritize strategies to enhance self-efficacy and self-esteem. Particular attention should be paid to women with lower education, unemployment, and a higher number of children.Keywords: Health behavior, Self efficacy, Self esteem, Postmenopause, Quality of life, Women, Iran

Introduction

Menopause, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), occurs when ovarian follicles cease to function and circulating estrogen levels decrease. Typically, it affects women between the ages of 45 and 55 as a natural part of aging [1]. As in many other parts of the world, the number of postmenopausal women is continually growing in Asian countries [2]. This trend has a faster pace in some countries such as Iran. In 2013, the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MOME) reported approximately 2,215,000 women were 45–60 years old in Iran [3, 4]. However, less than a decade later in 2022, close to 5 million women in Iran reached menopause [5, 6]. This rate is anticipated to rise significantly due to the aging of Iran’s baby boomer generation [7]. It is expected, therefore, that in near future a substantial number of Iranian women will reach middle age and experience menopause [7, 8].

Considering this demographic shift alongside the heightened focus of global health systems on the health of women [6, 9], it could be argued that prioritizing the health and well-being of postmenopausal women will be a significant concern for health system policymakers in Iran in the near future. Women undergoing menopause often endure a range of physical, psychosocial, and sexual concerns. Moreover, the primary health issues facing postmenopausal women include vasomotor instability, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, cancer, decreased cognitive function, and sexual problems [10–12]. These symptoms and changes may result in negative health outcomes [13] and worsen their quality of life [14, 15]. Therefore, adopting a health-promoting lifestyle (HPL) is essential for improving women’s health and quality of life while reducing the risk of diseases [11, 15–19].

HPL refers to a set of behaviors and practices that individuals adopt to enhance their overall health and well-being. This concept integrates various dimensions, including physical activity, nutrition, spiritual growth, interpersonal relationships, stress management, and health responsibility [11, 18, 20]. It is necessary to understand the factors that are associated with HPL in order to develop health-promoting behaviors among postmenopausal women.

Several studies have explored socio-demographic and psychological factors that are associated with health-promoting behaviors among women in different age groups [21, 22]. For instance, among women of the reproductive age, HPL is related to women’s education [9, 21, 22], husband’s educational level [22, 23], women’s employment status [22, 23], family income [21], and perceived social support [22].

In addition, there are significant relationships between HPL and demographic variables like age [24, 25], number of children [9, 14, 25, 26], higher education [9], and economic status [9, 27] among women beyond the reproductive age. Also, psychological factors like self-efficacy and self-esteem are associated with HPL in this population [28, 29]. Albert Bandura’s social cognitive theory defines self-efficacy as an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully perform the desired tasks in order to achieve specific goals or manage prospective challenges [30, 31]. This is considered the fundamental predictor of health-promoting behaviors for older adults [29, 32]. Self-esteem is another key factor affecting health promotion behaviors [33, 34]. It refers to an individual’s overall evaluation of their own worth and the judgments they make about themselves through socialization [35, 36].

Although a number of researchers have focused on HPL and its related factors among Iranian women in different age groups, a limited number of studies have examined HPL and investigated its relation with socio-demographic and psychological factors among menopausal women in the southern part of Iran. Therefore, this study aims pursues two objectives: to examine the level of HPL among postmenopausal women in Shiraz (southwestern Iran) and to investigate the socio-demographic and psychological variables including “self-efficacy” and “self-esteem” associated with HPL.

Methodology

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study with a correlational design conducted on postmenopausal women in Shiraz, southwestern Iran. The statistical population included postmenopausal women aged 40–59 years old. The minimum sample size was calculated as 384 using Cochran’s formula for infinite populations: (n = Z2 ×pq/E2) with a 95% confidence interval (Z = 1.96), 5% margin of error (E = 0.05), and an assumed prevalence (p) of 0.5. To account for anticipated non-response and potential incomplete questionnaires during data collection, a total of 433 women were approached. Of these, 33 questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete information, resulting in 400 complete questionnaires available for the final analysis.

To collect data by stratified random sampling, we divided Shiraz city into 10 municipal districts. Every district was divided into a number of neighborhoods, and every neighborhood was divided into blocks. Blocks were chosen through a random sampling method, and the quantity of blocks selected depended upon the population of each district. Ultimately, 40 blocks were selected for data collection. Data collection was carried out by a trained interviewer (FM) who visited the selected blocks. Once contact was established with the households in these blocks, married women who were in the menopause phase were identified and invited to participate in the study. Eligible women willing to take part in the study were given a self-administered questionnaire. The interviewer read the questions for respondents who could not complete the questionnaire independently due to illiteracy, poor literacy, or other limitations, and completed the questionnaire on their behalf. It should be noted that a number of questionnaires (n = 33) that had been filled independently by the participants were excluded from the final evaluation due to incomplete information.

The eligibility criteria included being a married woman in the menopause phase, passage of at least one year since the last menstrual period, normal menopause, and residence in Shiraz. The exclusion criteria included spotting in the past one year.

Study tools

The data were collected using a sociodemographic questionnaire and three standard questionnaires: The Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile II (HPLP-II), General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE), and Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale.

The sociodemographic questionnaire comprised variables such as age, age of onset of menopause, level of education, birthplace, ethnicity, occupation, husband’s age, and husband’s education.

The HPLP-II was utilized to evaluate HPL. The original version of this instrument was developed by Walker et al. [18], and its Persian version was developed and validated by Zidi et al. in 2011[37]. This instrument consists of 52 items divided into 6 subscales: health responsibility (HR, 9 items), physical activity (PA, 8 items), nutrition (N, 9 items), interpersonal relations (IR, 9 items), spiritual growth (SG, 9 items), and stress management (SM, 8 items). The responses are evaluated using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = routinely). The total HPLP score, ranging from 52 to 208, reflects the individual’s overall health-promoting lifestyle, categorized as poor (52–90), moderate (91–129), good (130–168), and excellent (169–208) [38]. Construct validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis, and reliability was 0.82 for this instrument, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha [37]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the entire HPL scale was 0.91. The reliability for each subscale was as follows: health responsibility (0.79), physical activity (0.89), nutrition (0.77), interpersonal relations (0.75), spiritual growth (0.75), and stress management (0.7).

The GSE, developed by Schwarzer and Jerusalem (1995) [39], was employed to assess the self-efficacy of postmenopausal women. This scale consists of 10 items and is scored using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The scale’s reliability coefficient is reported as approximately 0.7. The total score was between 10 and 40 points, and higher scores indicated stronger general self-efficacy. Narimani and Samadifard (2017) reported a reliability coefficient of 0.77 for this scale [40], and the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.84 in the present study.

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (1965) has been utilized to assess self-esteem levels of postmenopausal women [36]. This 10-item scale uses a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). The scale scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher self-esteem. Bohlool and Rajabi (2007) reported the internal consistency reliability of 0.80 for female students and 0.87 for male students in a sample from Shahid Chamran University of Ahvaz [41]. In this study, the internal consistency reliability of the scale was demonstrated by an alpha coefficient of 0.79.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS v. 22. In this study, quantitative variables were described using mean and standard deviation, while frequency and percentage were used for qualitative variables. To analyze the data and assess the relationships between variables, we applied Pearson’s correlation coefficient and independent samples t-tests, depending on the level of measurement of the respective variables. The theoretical model, grounded in the existing literature, proposed that a health-promoting lifestyle in postmenopausal women could be predicted by a linear combination of self-efficacy, self-esteem, and key socio-demographic variables [9, 24, 25, 28–34]. This study hypothesized that a health-promoting lifestyle in postmenopausal women is significantly associated with higher levels of self-efficacy and self-esteem, after controlling for socio-demographic factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Prior to analysis, we checked to ensure the data met all key assumptions of multiple linear regression. Scatterplots supported the linearity of the relationship between the independent variables and HPL. The normality of residuals was visually assessed using a histogram and a Normal Q-Q plot, and was formally confirmed by a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. The assumptions of homoscedasticity and independent errors were also met. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance statistics. As reported in Table 4, all VIF values were below 5 (range: 1.006 to 1.243), with corresponding Tolerance values well above 0.2, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern in the final model.

Pearson correlation analyses (Table 3) revealed that the total HPL score had a significant, moderate positive relationship with self-esteem (r = 0.425, p < 0.01) and self-efficacy (r = 0.445, p < 0.01). Significant, albeit weak, positive correlations were found with women’s education (r = 0.17, p < 0.01) and husband’s education (r = 0.113, p < 0.05), while a significant weak negative correlation was observed with the number of children (r=−0.114, p < 0.05). Analysis of the subscales showed that self-esteem and self-efficacy were significantly correlated with all HPLP-II domains. Furthermore, a higher number of children was significantly associated with lower scores in stress management and interpersonal relations.

The relationship between occupation and HPL was examined using independent t-test. The results indicated that employed women had a significantly higher total HPL score than homemakers (143.7 vs. 134.72, t = −3.39, p = 0.001). Significant differences were also found for all subscales except nutrition: physical activity (t = −2.37, p = 0.018), health responsibility (t = −2.73, p = 0.007), stress management (t = −4.14, p < 0.001), interpersonal relations (t = −2.48, p = 0.013), and spiritual growth (t = −2.08, p = 0.038).

A stepwise multiple linear regression was performed to determine the factors contributing to HPL. The initial model included the psychological variables of self-esteem and self-efficacy, alongside key socio-demographic variables (age, education, husband’s education, number of children, and occupation). The final model, presented in Table 4, retained three independent variables significantly associated with HPL: self-efficacy (β = 0.325, t = 6.85, p < 0.001), self-esteem (β = 0.266, t = 5.6, p < 0.001), and occupation (β = 0.144, t = 3.4, p = 0.001). Self-efficacy demonstrated the strongest effect on HPL (absolute value of standardized coefficient of beta was 0.325). Overall, self-efficacy, self-esteem, and occupation explained 28.3% of the variance in HPL scores.

Discussion

This study was aimed at evaluating the level of HPL among postmenopausal women in Shiraz. It also investigated the socio-demographic and psychological variables including self-efficacy and self-esteem associated with HPL. Another objective of this study was to determine the mean age of natural menopause among this population.

The mean age at natural menopause observed in this study (49.33 ± 3.43 years) aligns with the range reported in other studies conducted across Iran over the past two decades, which have generally reported means between 47.39 and 50.4 years [7, 43–49]. For instance, in 2004, the mean age at menopause in the total population of Iran was 50.4 years (S.D.=4.3) [43]. Also, studies in 2007 reported that the average age at menopause for Iranian women was 48.66 ± 3.79 in Isfahan [44] and 47.39 ± 5.089 in Yazd [45]. About one decade later, studies indicated that the average age of natural menopause among Iranian women was 48.57 years (CI: 47.96–49.18) [7]. Other researchers in different regions of Iran have reported the mean onset of menopause to be 49.6 ± 4.02 in Hamadan [46], 48.31 ± 6.34 in Bandare-Kong [47], 48.95 ± 6.13 in Kharameh [48], and 49.4 ± 3.3 years in Neyshabur [49]. This finding confirms that the mean age at menopause among women in Iran, like other Middle Eastern countries, is lower than North America [50], Europe [51], and China [52]. This difference could be explained by the socio-economic status, Iranian cultural norms, lifestyle choices, as well as genetics, nutrition, dietary habits, and health care access in Iran [46, 50, 53].

Furthermore, studies indicate a direct correlation between a high-carbohydrate diet, elevated consumption of vegetables, fiber, and grains, and a reduced age of menopause. Conversely, the consumption of protein, meat, and fat is associated with delayed menopause [46, 54]. Considering the prevailing unfavorable economic conditions impacting the nutritional status of Iranians [55], and the resulting suboptimal per capita consumption of meat and protein [56, 57], it is anticipated that the mean age of menopause will decline among Iranian women in the near future. Moreover, the rising rates of tobacco and hookah consumption among Iranian women [46, 54, 58, 59], alongside increases in overweight [60], inadequate physical activity [59], and socioeconomic status, pose a significant threat to women’s health by potentially lowering the mean age of menopause in Iran [46, 54]. Evidence indicates that the age of menopause is a crucial indicator of women’s health. Thus, a one-year increase in the age of menopause is associated with an approximate 2% reduction in overall mortality, while early menopause elevates the risk of various diseases [7]. To mitigate the potential decline in the age of menopause among Iranian women, policymakers in the health sector should prioritize comprehensive public health, educational, and cultural interventions.

The observation that our sample of Iranian postmenopausal women in Shiraz had relatively good health-promoting lifestyle profiles is consistent with the broader literature on menopausal women internationally [61, 62]. Also, studies have found that the health-promoting behaviors were at a moderate level among Iranian postmenopausal women [9, 17, 25, 63]. Therefore, the good scores of postmenopausal women in HPL may have been influenced by the relatively desirable level of the health culture of this population in Shiraz as well as relatively well-established access to health and psychological services for residents in this urban area.

This study highlights that health responsibility yielded the highest mean score within the assessed subscales of health-promoting lifestyle. Although Iran is known as a religious society, in this research the highest mean score of HPL was not related to spiritual growth. In this regard, previous studies in Iran have suggested that among the different subscales of health-promoting behaviors, spiritual growth received the highest score among postmenopausal women [9, 25, 62]. This discrepancy might be explained by the fact that religious beliefs and spiritual growth among this group have diminished within the socio-cultural context of this study. In this regard, studies have emphasized the necessity of focusing on strategies to enhance the spiritual health of postmenopausal women as a means of alleviating menopausal symptoms [64].

The notably low score in stress management among our sample presents a striking contrast to the high score among Iranian middle-aged women reported by Enjezab et al. over a decade ago [25]. Research indicates that postmenopausal women with higher levels of spiritual well-being experience lower levels of stress and depression [64]. Therefore, the insufficient level of spiritual well-being observed in the present study may partly explain the suboptimal stress management strategies employed by menopausal women. Consequently, interventions aimed at enhancing spiritual well-being and reducing stress are essential for promoting stress and anxiety management skills. Examples include deep breathing exercises, relaxation techniques, and the provision of counseling services to middle-aged women during the peri- and postmenopausal periods [65].

Globally, postmenopausal women consistently report lower scores in physical activity than in other HPL subscales. This finding is confirmed by our study and others [9, 25, 62, 65, 66]. Physical activity and exercise (whether regular or irregular) have a significant impact on reducing menopausal symptoms such as depression, anxiety, hot flashes, night sweats, and physical discomfort [63, 64, 67]. In this manner, they contribute to an improved quality of life for menopausal women [15].

Considering the low mean score of the physical activity sub-item observed among the postmenopausal women in this study, it is necessary to promote physical activity among this population through a combination of strategies. A potential strategy could involve community-based exercise programs offering socially engaging activities. Examples include walking clubs or low-impact aerobics classes, which could significantly improve the quality of life, strength, and balance in these individuals [68, 69]. Also, educational interventions such as those implemented by Iranian health houses and public health centers under the Ministry of Health and Medical Education could play a crucial role in promoting physical activity. These programs could emphasize the benefits of physical activity for menopausal health, including the reduction of hot flashes, improvement of bone density, and effective weight management [70, 71]. In addition, counseling services, including face-to-face meetings or group sessions, and phone calls can address barriers to physical activity such as lack of time or motivation and teach beneficial strategies to overcome them [72, 73]. Moreover, digital media-based health education interventions such as educational websites, channels, and mobile applications, could offer short exercise videos and educational films. These platforms could also provide online assessments of physical activity and BMI, potentially improving participation and consistency in physical activity routines among women [74]. Finally, implementing these strategies and training programs, alongside providing personalized exercise plans for postmenopausal women and expanding safe walking options in urban areas, may lead to a reduction in menopausal symptoms and improve health-promoting behaviors [62, 64].

In this study, demographic variables such as women’s education and husband’s educational level showed statistically significant but small positive relationships with HPL. Particularly, there was a significant relationship between husband’s educational level and the dimensions of stress management and interpersonal relationships. These findings are in agreement with other studies reporting a positive and significant relationship between education and health-promoting behaviors for postmenopausal women [25, 64, 75]. Also, husband’s educational level is recognized as a significant determinant of women’s HPL, with higher levels of spousal education potentially encouraging women toward healthier behaviors [16, 22, 23, 65].

A possible explanation for this might be that women with higher levels of education, particularly those living in households with educated spouses and high cultural capital, tend to be more health-conscious and display higher overall well-being [76–78]. Also, people with higher levels of education are more likely to engage in health-promoting behaviors, actively seek health information from various sources, and utilize medical consultations [79]. In addition, higher levels of education could enable women to navigate the menopausal transition more effectively and manage the associated changes [80, 81]. Regarding the importance of education for both women and their spouse as well as reinforcing familial cultural capital, it should be noted that women’s knowledge and information have a significant impact on increasing attention to healthcare in the pre- and postmenopausal periods. Furthermore, a more educated spouse can positively influence the wife’s focus on health and her engagement in health-promoting behaviors during menopause [19].

The strong association between occupation and HPL aligns with previous research [62, 82] and underscores the role of occupation as a potential source of empowerment for menopausal women. A possible explanation is that employment provides women with greater access to material resources and expands their social connections. As a result, it creates a more favorable context for engaging in health-promoting behaviors such as physical activity and interpersonal relationships [22, 23, 68, 83].

The observed inverse relationship between the number of children and HPL is in accordance with other studies indicating a negative correlation between the number of children and health-promoting behaviors among women [25, 83]. It seems possible that these results are due to the constrained time and financial resources available to women with larger families, limiting their capacity to prioritize health-related behaviors. However, this outcome is contrary to another study in Turkey which found no relationship between the number of children and women’s quality of life and health status in the pre- and postmenopausal periods [26]. This inconsistency could be due to differing socio-cultural contexts and economic status in Turkey and Iran.

The absence of a significant association between age and HPL (except spiritual growth dimension) in our sample aligns with another Iranian study [84] but contrasts with international trends [24]. This observation could be attributed to the provision of specialized social support and comprehensive health and welfare resources for middle-aged and older adults in these societies.

In this study, self-efficacy and self-esteem play a significant role in encouraging healthier lifestyle practices among the study population. In accordance with this result, previous studies have demonstrated that self-efficacy and self-esteem significantly influence health-promoting behaviors among women in different age groups [29, 61, 85]. In general, higher self-esteem and self-efficacy enhance an individual’s ability and competence in performing health-promoting behaviors. This leads to increased self-confidence, a greater sense of control over stressful events, reduced physical and psychological symptoms of menopause, and—ultimately—greater life satisfaction during middle age and beyond [19, 31, 86, 87]. Therefore, health policymakers can plan educational interventions and cognitive-behavioral therapies to enhance self-efficacy and self-esteem in postmenopausal women. Thus, they may empower them to adopt more effective health-promoting behaviors and experience a healthier, less challenging middle and old age [29, 31, 86, 88].

Limitations

The current study also had several limitations. First, the women included in this study were participants in a larger research project primarily aimed at examining the attitudes of married postmenopausal women in Shiraz toward menopause. Indeed, the sample in this study was limited to married women living in the city of Shiraz (southwestern Iran). Therefore, the results can only be generalized to the population of married postmenopausal women in this city and cannot represent the entire population of Iranian postmenopausal women. However, the general characteristics of our participants share some similarities with the broader Iranian female population. We strongly recommend that future research should investigate broader samples, including both married and unmarried women from various geographic regions and diverse socio-cultural backgrounds.

The second limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which identified associations between socio-psychological factors and health-promoting lifestyle among postmenopausal women at a single point in time. Conducting longitudinal studies over a longer time frame would provide a better understanding of the causal relationships between contributing variables and give a more dynamic understanding of how these factors evolve and influence one another over time.

Third, the relatively low explained variance (28%) in HPL activities observed in our study suggests that other socio-cultural and psychological factors, not accounted for in our model, may play a significant role in predicting HPL among postmenopausal women. These could include, but are not limited to, social support networks, cultural beliefs and practices, access to resources, economic status, and environmental factors. This suggestion aligns with the broader literature, which emphasizes the multifaceted nature of health-promoting behaviors and the need to consider a wide range of influences. Future studies could benefit from incorporating additional measures of these socio-cultural and psychological factors, as well as employing mixed-methods design to better understand the dynamics of HPL behaviors.

Fourth, although this study employed validated and reliable self-reported measures for assessing self-efficacy and self-esteem, we acknowledge the potential limitations of relying on self-reported data. Participants may have provided responses influenced by socially desirable norms, which could introduce bias. Future studies could complement self-reported data with objective or observational measures, thereby reducing the potential for bias and enhancing the validity of the results.

Finally, chronic comorbidities were not assessed despite their documented influence on menopausal health behaviors. It is promising if future studies explore this line of research.

Conclusion

Postmenopausal women in this study showed relatively good HPL profiles. Higher total HPLP scores were significantly associated with higher levels of women’s and husband’s education but negatively associated with a greater number of children. In addition, stepwise multiple linear regression identified self-efficacy, self-esteem, and occupation as factors independently associated with the HPL of postmenopausal women.

The results highlight the critical need for policymakers and health care professionals to prioritize comprehensive planning to improve HPL of postmenopausal women. This planning should include structured psychoeducation, lifestyle modification, supportive interactions, menopause self-management, physical and recreational activity programs, and empowerment-based workshops. These measures could enhance self-efficacy through skill mastery and bolster self-esteem via identity reframing and social support. These approaches establish psychological foundations that may support healthier lifestyle adoption in postmenopausal women. Moreover, the implementation of personalized self-care educational interventions has the potential to enhance their health-promoting behaviors. This is particularly rewarding if targeted at postmenopausal women with lower educational backgrounds, those who are unemployed and have less economic independence, and women with a greater number of children.

As the number of menopausal women in Iran is expected to grow dramatically in the coming decades, it is critical to promote health-promoting behaviors to enhance their quality of life throughout middle and old age. To inform these efforts, further research should be carried out to identify the factors influencing health-promoting behaviors as well as barriers to adopting HPL faced by Iranian postmenopausal women.
