On Randomized Algorithms in Online Strategic Classification
Chase Hutton, Adam Melrod, Han Shao

Abstract
Online strategic classification studies settings in which agents strategically modify their features to obtain favorable predictions ( 3 ) . For example, given a classifier that determines loan approval based on credit scores, applicants may open or close credit cards and bank accounts to obtain a positive prediction. The learning goal is to achieve low mistake or regret bounds despite such strategic behavior. While randomized algorithms have the potential to offer advantages to the learner in strategic settings, they have been largely underexplored. In the realizable setting, no lower bound is known for randomized algorithms, and existing lower bound constructions for deterministic learners can be circumvented by randomization. In the agnostic setting, the best known regret upper bound is O ⁡ ( T 3 / 4 ​ log 1 / 4 ⁡ T ​ | ℋ | ) O(T^{3/4}\log^{1/4}T|\mathcal{H}|) ( 3 ) , which is far from the standard online learning rate of O ⁡ ( T ​ log ⁡ | ℋ | ) O(\sqrt{T\log|\mathcal{H}|}) . In this work, we provide refined upper and lower bounds for online strategic classification in both the realizable and agnostic settings. In the realizable setting, using a new construction, we prove a lower bound that, for T > Ldim ⁡ ( ℋ ) ​ Δ 2 T>\ldim(\mathcal{H})\Delta^{2} , extends the existing lower bound Ω ⁡ ( Ldim ⁡ ( ℋ ) ​ Δ ) \Omega(\ldim(\mathcal{H})\Delta) , for deterministic algorithms to all algorithms. This yields the first lower bound that applies to randomized algorithms and resolves an open question posed by 3 . We also provide the first randomized algorithm that improves the known (deterministic) upper bound of O ⁡ ( Ldim ⁡ ( ℋ ) ⋅ Δ ​ log ⁡ Δ ) O(\ldim(\mathcal{H})\cdot\Delta\log\Delta) . Our algorithm achieves the bound O ⁡ ( T ⋅ Ldim ⁡ ( ℋ ) ​ log ​ Δ ) O(\sqrt{T\cdot\ldim(\mathcal{H})\log\Delta}) , which beats the known bound for T < log ⁡ Δ ​ Ldim ⁡ ( ℋ ) ​ Δ 2 T<\log\Delta\ldim(\mathcal{H})\Delta^{2} . In the agnostic setting, we give a proper learner using convex optimization techniques to improve the regret upper bound to O ⁡ ( T ​ log ⁡ | ℋ | + | ℋ | ​ log ⁡ ( T ​ | ℋ | ) ) O(\sqrt{T\log|\mathcal{H}|}+|\mathcal{H}|\log(T|\mathcal{H}|)) . We show a matching lower bound up to logarithmic factors for all proper learning rules, demonstrating the optimality of our learner among proper learners. As such, improper learning is necessary to further improve regret guarantees.

1
Introduction

Strategic classification lies at the intersection of machine learning and game theory and studies learning problems in which self-interested agents strategically modify their observable features to obtain favorable predictions
(
9
;
16
)
. Such behavior arises naturally in many high-stakes decision-making systems, including loan approval, hiring, and college admissions. For example, loan applicants may open or close credit cards to improve their credit scores, while students may retake standardized tests or adjust course selections to increase their chances of admission. This introduces a more complex learning environment, in which agents strategically adapt their features to influence the outcome of the deployed classifier while preserving their true underlying qualifications, such as being successful in college or repaying a loan.

These challenges are further amplified in online environments
(
3
)
, where a learner repeatedly interacts with a sequence of agents and updates its classifier over time with limited information. In particular, the learner perceives agents only through the lens of the currently deployed hypothesis and does not always have the ability to counterfactually infer how agents would have manipulated under a different hypothesis. As a result, the learner may not know the loss incurred by unimplemented hypotheses and thus cannot obtain full-information feedback.

While much prior work on online strategic classification focuses on deterministic algorithms
(
4
;
11
)
, randomized algorithms have been largely underexplored. As introduced by
3
, there are two models for randomized learning in strategic settings, depending on whether the learner’s random choice of classifier is revealed to the agent before responding. In the
fractional model
, the learner samples a classifier from a distribution but does not reveal the realization, so the agent best responds to the distribution itself. In the
randomized algorithms model
, the learner reveals the sampled classifier, and the agent best responds to this realization. As shown by
3
, revealing the realization is strictly more advantageous: when the learner does not reveal the sampled classifier, the learner may suffer linear regret, whereas revealing it allows for sublinear regret. Accordingly, in this work we focus on the randomized algorithms model.

While recent work has established mistake and regret guarantees in several settings
(
3
;
4
;
11
)
, several important questions remain open.

One question concerns the role of randomization in the realizable setting. In the non-strategic realizable setting, it is well known that randomization does not improve the mistake bound, and the optimal algorithm is deterministic, namely the Standard Optimal Algorithm (SOA). In contrast, in the strategic setting, prior work
(
3
;
11
)
has only established lower bounds for deterministic algorithms. Moreover, under the same lower bound construction,
4
shows that randomization can circumvent these lower bounds and significantly improve mistake guarantees. This leads to the following question:

Does randomization always improve mistake bounds in the realizable strategic setting?

A second question concerns regret bounds in the agnostic setting. In
3
, the authors provide regret upper bounds of
O(T^{3/4}\log^{1/4}T|\mathcal{H}|)
by using an all-positive classifier at randomly chosen time steps to obtain full information, and
O(\sqrt{T|\mathcal{H}|\log|\mathcal{H}|})
by applying standard bandit algorithms such as EXP3. Matching lower bounds, however, are left open. Since it is well known that a regret of
O(\sqrt{T\log|\mathcal{H}|})
is achievable in the standard online learning setting, this naturally raises the question:

Can we also achieve a regret guarantee of
O(\sqrt{T\log|\mathcal{H}|})
in the agnostic strategic setting?

In this work, we provide refined upper and lower bounds for online strategic classification in both the realizable and agnostic settings, thereby answering the two questions above. In the realizable setting, we show that every learner incurs at least
\Omega(\min\{\sqrt{T\ldim(\mathcal{H})},\ \ldim(\mathcal{H})\Delta\})
.
As a consequence, for
T>\ldim(\mathcal{H})\Delta^{2}
, randomized learners cannot improve upon the standard deterministic lower bound of
\Omega(\ldim(\mathcal{H})\Delta)
, thereby resolving the first question in the negative.
On the other hand, we construct a randomized algorithm that achieves a mistake bound of
O(\sqrt{T\cdot\ldim(\mathcal{H})\log\Delta})
, strictly improving upon the best known deterministic upper bounds when
T
is small, and providing a partial complementary answer to the first question.

In the agnostic setting, we establish a regret upper bound of
O(\sqrt{T\log|\mathcal{H}|}+|\mathcal{H}|\log(T|\mathcal{H}|))
. This bound provides a partially positive answer to the second question: such a guarantee is achievable when
T\geq|\mathcal{H}|^{2}
using a proper learning algorithm.

.

1.1
Related work

Our work is most closely related to online strategic learning.
The works of
11
;
4
also focus on improving the regret bounds established by
3
for online strategic classification. However, both primarily aim to extend results from finite hypothesis classes to infinite hypothesis classes and focus on deterministic algorithms. The only result concerning randomized algorithms in
4
shows that randomization can circumvent a lower bound for deterministic algorithms in a specific example.

There is also related work on strategic online learning that studies different learning problems. For example,
12
;
10
;
1
study strategic linear classification under various manipulation restrictions, such as allowing agents to manipulate their features only within a bounded-radius ball. In contrast, we consider general hypothesis classes and restrict manipulations through the degree of a manipulation graph. The work of
25
studies mistake bounds in realizable strategic classification with unknown manipulation structures and establishes a
\Theta(|\mathcal{H}|)
mistake bound, indicating limited scope for improvement.

Additional related work is deferred to the appendix due to space constraints.

Model

2.1
Strategic classification

We consider binary classification in the adversarial online setting. Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the feature vector space,
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,+1\}
denote the binary label space, and
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
denote the hypothesis class. In the strategic setting, an example
(x,y)
, referred to as an
agent
in this context, consists of a pair of feature vector and label.
An agent will try to receive a positive prediction by modifying their feature vector to some reachable feature vectors. Following prior work (e.g.
3
;
11
;
4
), we model the set of feasible manipulations using a
manipulation graph

G=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E})
. The vertices represent feature vectors, and an edge
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
exists if and only if an agent with feature vector
x
can manipulate to
x^{\prime}
. We let
N_{G}(x)=\{x^{\prime}:(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}\}
denote the neighborhood of
x
, and
N_{G}[x]=\{x\}\cup N_{G}(x)
denote the closed neighborhood.

Given a manipulation graph
G
, an agent with feature vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
best responds to a classifier
h
by moving to a reachable feature vector that is labeled positive, if one exists. More specifically, the agent remains at its original feature vector if it cannot obtain a positive prediction through manipulation or if it is already classified as positive. Otherwise, the agent moves to a reachable feature vector that yields a positive prediction.
Formally, we define the notion of best responses as follows.

Definition 1

(Best responses)
.

Given a manipulation graph
G
, a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
, and a feature vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the set of
best responses
of
x
under
h
is

\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x):=\{x^{\prime}\in N_{G}[x]:h(x^{\prime})=+1\}.

An agent with feature vector
x
selects a manipulated feature vector
z
as follows: if
\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x)=\emptyset
or
x\in\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x)
, then
z=x
; otherwise,
z\in\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x)
is chosen according to a fixed tie-breaking rule.

When implementing a hypothesis
h
, the loss incurred at the agent
(x,y)
is the misclassification error when the agent manipulates w.r.t.
h
. We define the strategic loss as follows.

Definition 2

(Strategic loss)
.

Given a manipulation graph
G
, a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
, and an agent
(x,y)
, let
z
denote the manipulated feature vector chosen by the agent in response to
h
. The
strategic loss
of
h
at
(x,y)
is defined as

\ell_{G}^{\mathrm{str}}(h,(x,y)):=\mathbf{1}\{h(z)\neq y\}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}\{h(x)\neq y\},&\text{if }\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x)=\emptyset\text{ or }x\in\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x),\\
\mathbf{1}\{y=-1\},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}.

Note that strategic behavior only harms the learner on negative instances (
y=-1
). If
y=+1
and the agent manipulates to a positive classification, the prediction is correct (loss 0). However, if
y=-1
and the agent successfully manipulates (i.e.,
\mathrm{BR}_{G,h}(x)\neq\emptyset
), the learner predicts positive while the true label is negative, incurring loss
1
.

2.2
Online learning with strategic agents

The learner classifies a sequence of strategic agents
{(x_{t},y_{t})}_{t=1}^{T}
selected by an adversary. At each round, the learner selects a distribution over hypotheses and samples a hypothesis from this distribution. As discussed in the introduction, the learner then reveals the sampled hypothesis to the agent, who best responds after observing it. At the end of each round, the learner observes the true label as well as only the manipulated features, rather than the agent’s original features (e.g., the learner observes post-manipulation credit scores in loan approval or the final SAT scores submitted by student applicants).

More formally, at each round
t
, the adversary selects an agent
(x_{t},y_{t})
, and simultaneously the learner selects a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{t}
over hypotheses. The learner then samples a hypothesis
h_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
and reveals
h_{t}
to the agent. After observing
h_{t}
, the agent best responds by manipulating her feature vector from
x_{t}
to
z_{t}
according to Definition
1
. At the end of the round, the learner observes the prediction on the manipulated feature,
\hat{y}_{t}=h_{t}(z_{t})
, as well as the true label
y_{t}
, and incurs a loss whenever
\hat{y}_{t}\neq y_{t}
. This interaction protocol, which repeats for
t=1,\ldots,T
, is summarized in Protocol
2.2
.

The learner’s goal is to minimize the expected
Stackelberg regret
against the best fixed strategy in hindsight:

\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{R}_{T}]\;=\;\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{G}^{\mathrm{str}}(h_{t},(x_{t},y_{t}))-\ell_{G}^{\mathrm{str}}(h,(x_{t},y_{t}))\right],

where the expectation is taken over the learner’s internal randomization. When the best hypothesis in
\mathcal{H}
has zero loss, i.e.,
\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\ell_{G}^{\mathrm{str}}(h,(x_{t},y_{t}))=0
, we call the sequence of agents
realizable
and otherwise call it
agnostic
.

Overview of Results

3.1
Realizable setting

In this section, we derive mistake upper and lower expected mistake bounds for the realizable setting in both the cases of finite and infinite hypothesis classes
\mathcal{H}
. For finite classes, we derive guarantees in terms of the size
n
of the hypothesis class, while for infinite classes, our bounds are given in terms of the Littlestone dimension
d
of
\mathcal{H}
and the max degree
\Delta
of the manipulation graph
G
.

3.1.1
Upper Bounds

In this section we give two algorithms, the first of which works for finite hypothesis classes and the second of which works for infinite hypothesis classes. Both algorithms work at a high level by maintaining a system of weighted experts. Each round, the algorithm decides to either choose an expert randomly by weight for use as a classifier, or to use an all-positive classifier to obtain full-information which is used to update the expert system.
This decision is made randomly by coin flip, biased to ensure the decrease in weight of the system is correlated to the chance of an expert making a mistake. By understanding how the weight changes, we may then upper bound the number of mistakes. Our algorithmic result for finite classes is the following.

Theorem 3
.

For any graph
G
and hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
of size
n
, and for any realizable sequence, we can achieve expected mistake bound
O(\sqrt{T\log n})
using Algorithm
3.1.1
. By combining this algorithm with the naive deterministic algorithm that tries every hypothesis until it fails, we obtain a learner with expected mistake bound
O(\min\{\sqrt{T\log n},n\})
.

Our algorithm is based on the following intuition. Say
(x_{t},y_{t})
is the agent for round
t
. Let us consider two strategies. In the first strategy the learner commits to sampling a hypothesis uniformly at random from the set of remaining consistent hypothesis
V_{t}
. Then the probability that a mistake occurs is exactly the proportion
\delta_{t}
of hypotheses in
V_{t}
which make a mistake.
Next consider the strategy where the learner commits to using an all positive classifier
h^{+}
which labels every vertex
+1
. Then since
x_{t}
is predicted as
+1
, the agent will never manipulate. By removing each hypothesis that made a mistake, we can ensure that
|V_{t+1}|=(1-\delta_{t})|V_{t}|
. The key observation is that when
\delta_{t}
is
small
, sampling a random
h\in V_{t}
leads to a small mistake probability. When
\delta_{t}
is
large
, always predicting
+1
leads to a large reduction of the hypothesis set. By playing an appropriate random mix of these two strategies we can ensure (in expectation) that we don’t suffer both a high mistake probability and poor learning progress on the same round. The pseudocode is given in Algorithm

Proof (sketch).

We split mistakes into two types: those incurred by the all positive strategy (exploration mistakes) and those incurred by the uniformly random strategy (expert mistakes). The exploration mistakes are upper bounded by the number of times the all positive strategy is selected, which in expectation is
pT
. We understand the expert mistakes by relating the mistake probability at round
t
to the change in valid hypotheses
V_{t}
. In particular we can show the following relationship.

\Pr[\text{expert mistake on round }t]=(1-p)\delta_{t}\leq\frac{1-p}{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{|V_{t}|}{|V_{t+1}|}\right].

After summing over all rounds
t
and combining with potential exploration mistakes, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[M(T)]\leq pT+\frac{1-p}{p}\log|V_{1}|
. Setting
p=\min\{1,\sqrt{{\log n}/{T}}\}
and using
V_{1}=\mathcal{H}
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[M(T)]=O\left(\sqrt{T\log n}\right)
.

Theorem 4

For any graph
G
and hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, and for any realizable sequence, we can achieve expected mistake bound

Our algorithm will take as input a standard online learning algorithm

As in the finite case, for each round, there are two possible procedures chosen by a biased coin flip. The first procedure is an exploration round, where an all-positive classifier is selected. As using such a classifier results in full information, we can identify all incorrect experts, and update our expert system accordingly (this update is identical to that used in Algorithm 2 from
11

w_{t}(E)/W_{t}

There are three fundamental behaviors that determine how we must update the expert system. First, the total weight associated to incorrect experts at round

Proof.

\mathbb{E}[M(T)]\leq pT+\frac{2(1-p)}{p}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{W_{1}}{W_{T+1}}\right].

, the adversary adaptively selects agents based on the learner’s
announced distribution
D_{t}
over classifiers.
We define three ‘bad’ sets of classifiers:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\{f:f(L)=+1\ \text{or}\ \exists i\in[n]\ \text{s.t. }f(u_{i})=+1\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\{f:f(R)=+1\ \text{or}\ f(z)=+1\},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle:=\{f:f(P\cup\{R,z\})=-1\}.

These sets are chosen so that if a classifier is a member, it makes a mistake on a particular choice of agent whereas a classifier not in any of these sets (including all the hypotheses in
\mathcal{H}
) do not. The agent witnessing this is
(L,-1)
for
A
,
(z,-1)
for
B
, and
(R,+1)
for
C
.

We call these sets ‘bad’ since the classifiers in the complement, the ‘good’ set, behave almost the same as hypotheses from
\mathcal{H}
. In the case that a learner plays a ’good’ hypothesis, the adversary should play
(u_{i^{*}},-1)
according to the proper learner strategy, and in the case that a ‘bad’ hypothesis is played, the adversary should punish the learner by choosing an agent according to the paragraph above. This informs the following strategy.
Let
\gamma=\Theta(1/\sqrt{T})
. On round
t
, the adversary plays according to the following cases.

(1)

If
\mathcal{D}_{t}(A)\geq\gamma
, play
(L,-1)
.

(2)

Else if
\mathcal{D}_{t}(B)\geq\gamma
, play
(z,-1)
.

(3)

Else if
\mathcal{D}_{t}(C)\geq\gamma
, play
(R,+1)
.

(4)

Otherwise, play
(u_{i^{*}},-1)
.

Any agent sequence resulting from this strategy is realizable by
h_{i^{*}}
.
The given adversary on the constructed graph witnesses Theorem
6
.

Proof (sketch).

Cases 1–3 are designed so that whenever the adversary triggers one of them, the learner
incurs mistake probability at least
\gamma
on that round (because
\mathcal{D}_{t}
places at least

\gamma
mass on classifiers that are forced to err in that case). Hence, if there are fewer
than
T/2
case 4 rounds, then there are at least
T/2
case 1–3 rounds and

\mathbb{E}[M(T)]\geq\frac{\gamma T}{2}=\Omega\left(\min\{n,\sqrt{T}\}\right)
.

Otherwise, analyze the first
\tau
case 4 rounds. If in each of these rounds, the hypothesis
h
sampled is good, so
h
is in
(A\cup B\cup C)^{c}
, then similar reasoning as in the proper case holds to conclude that the probability of incurring a mistake in all of those rounds is at least
(n-\tau)/n
. Now, in any case 4 round, the chance that the hypothesis sampled is good is at least
1-3\gamma
, so we determine that overall, the probability of incurring a mistake in each of the first
\tau
case 4 rounds is at least
(1-3\gamma)^{\tau}\cdot\frac{n-\tau}{n}\geq\frac{1-3\gamma\tau}{2}
.
The inequality follows from
\tau\leq n/2
and Bernoulli’s inequality. By taking
\tau=\Theta(\min\{n,\sqrt{T}\})
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[M(T)]=\Omega\left(\min\{n,\sqrt{T}\}\right).

3.2
Agnostic setting

In this section, we present our main results for the agnostic setting with finite hypothesis classes. Our methods for proving the upper and lower bounds share many similarities to those used in the sparse contextual bandit setting introduced by
13
.

3.2.1
Upper Bound

In this section, we present a
proper
online algorithm with expected regret
O\!\left(\sqrt{T\log|\mathcal{H}|}+\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{H}|)\right)
. We state the guarantee formally in the theorem below.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite hypothesis class of size
n
and
G
a manipulation graph. Then there exists an algorithm which guarantees an expected regret bound of at most
O\left(\sqrt{T\log n}+n\log nT\right)
against any adaptive adversary.

As observed by
3
, the feedback structure of our setting lies strictly between bandit and full-information feedback. Indeed, in addition to learning the true label
y_{t}
at the end of each round, the learner also observes the manipulated feature
z_{t}
in
\mathrm{BR}_{G,h_{t}}(x_{t})
. Importantly, whenever the agent does not manipulate (
z_{t}=x_{t}
) and the learner can
certify
this fact, the learner effectively gains full information, allowing for the explicit calculation of the loss for every hypothesis in
\mathcal{H}
. Prior work by
3
took advantage of this structure by employing an improper strategy. Their algorithm plays an all-positive classifier,
h^{+}(x)
at randomly chosen times. Since a positive classification is guaranteed, the agent has no incentive to manipulate, thereby certifying
z_{t}=x_{t}
.

We take a different approach. We define a shifted loss function that admits an estimator supported entirely on the subset of hypotheses where the learner effectively observes full information. As a consequence, we are able to show that the estimator has a constant second moment at the cost of introducing negative loss. To handle this, we employ the Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) framework with a log-barrier regularizer used by
13
. We establish the stability property of this FTRL algorithm in our setting and combine it with the constant second moment of our estimator to derive our desired regret bound. In the remainder of this section, we detail our algorithm and sketch a proof of Theorem
8
.

Write
\mathcal{H}
as
\{h^{1},h^{2},\dots,h^{n}\}
where
h^{i}
ranges over the
n
hypothesis of
\mathcal{H}
. Additionally define
R_{t}
to be the set of hypotheses that label the entire neighborhood of the true agent
x_{t}
as negative:
R_{t}:=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\forall x^{\prime}\in N_{G}[x_{t}],h(x^{\prime})=-1\}.

A key property of our model is that playing a hypothesis
h_{t}\in R_{t}
reveals the set
R_{t}
. The learner is able to recognize whether
h_{t}
is in
R_{t}
since this happens if and only if
h_{t}(z_{t})=-1
. In that case, the agent does not manipulate so
z_{t}=x_{t}
. This allows the learner to reconstruct
N_{G}[x_{t}]
and therefore
R_{t}
, so gains full-information. The strategic loss function can be written in terms of this set as:

\ell_{G}^{\mathrm{str}}(h,(x_{t},y_{t}))=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}\{h\in R_{t}\}&\text{if }y_{t}=+1,\\
1-\mathbf{1}\{h\in R_{t}\}&\text{if }y_{t}=-1.\end{cases}

This presents a difficulty: when
y_{t}=-1
, the loss is non-zero for hypotheses
outside

R_{t}
. However, we generally cannot determine if an unplayed hypothesis is outside
R_{t}
unless we know
R_{t}
. To fix this, we shift the loss by
\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}=-1\}
. This yields the shifted loss:

d_{t}(i):=\ell_{G}^{\mathrm{str}}(h^{i},(x_{t},y_{t}))-\mathbf{1}\{y_{t}=-1\}=y_{t}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{h^{i}\in R_{t}\}.

Importantly, shifting the losses of all hypotheses by the same constant does not affect the
regret. Moreover, shifted loss satisfies the identity
d_{t}(i)=y_{t}\mathbf{1}\{h^{i}\in R_{t}\}
which allows us to construct an importance-weighted estimator supported entirely on
R_{t}
:

\widehat{d}_{t}(i):=\mathbf{1}\{h_{t}\in R_{t}\}\mathbf{1}\{h^{i}\in R_{t}\}\dfrac{y_{t}}{q_{t}},

(1)

where
p_{t}(i)
is the probability of selecting
h^{i}
and
q_{t}:=\sum_{i\in R_{t}}p_{t}(i)
. This estimator is unbiased,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{d}_{t}(i)]=d_{t}(i)
, and enjoys a constant second moment:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{t}(i)\widehat{d}_{t}(i)^{2}\right]=\mathbf{1}\{h_{t}(z_{t})=-1\}\left(q_{t}\cdot\sum_{i\in R_{t}}p_{t}(i)\left(\frac{1}{q_{t}}\right)^{2}\right)\leq 1.

(2)

Shifting the losses in this manner, however, introduces negative losses. To handle this, we employ the Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) framework with a log-barrier regularizer, as introduced by
13
for sparse contextual bandits. The pseudocode for the procedure is detailed in Algorithm
3.2.1
. Following
13
, the algorithm performs FTRL updates over the
\epsilon
-shrunk
n
-dimensional probability simplex
\Delta_{n,\epsilon}
, defined by

\Delta_{n,\epsilon}:=\{p\in\Delta_{n}:p(h^{i})\geq\epsilon\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}

for
\epsilon=1/nT
, where
\Delta_{n}
is the probability simplex over
\mathcal{H}
. The regularizer we use is
R_{\eta,\nu}(p)=H_{\eta}(p)+\psi_{\nu}(p)
where
H_{\eta}(p)=\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(i)\log p(i)
and
\psi_{\nu}=-\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(i)
, for
\nu=1/16
and
\eta=\sqrt{\log n/T}.

p^{*}

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\left\langle d_{t},p_{t}-p^{*}\right\rangle]\leq\frac{n}{\nu}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{\eta}\log n+\eta T=O(\sqrt{T\log n}+n\log nT)

Corollary 9

3.2.2
Lower Bound

In this section, we show a matching lower bound (up to log factors) to Corollary
9
for proper learners. The lower bound is stated formally in the following Theorem.

Theorem 11
.

For any (possibly randomized) proper learning algorithm and all integers

n
, there exist a manipulation graph
G
and a hypothesis class

\mathcal{H}
with
|\mathcal{H}|=n
such that the algorithm must incur expected regret at least
\Omega\!\left(\min\!\left\{\sqrt{T\log n}+n,\sqrt{Tn\log n}\right\}\right).

Proof.

We combine two hard instances. Let
(G_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
be the realizable construction from
Section
3.1.2
with
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\{h_{1,1},\dots,h_{1,n}\}
, which yields regret
\Omega(\min\{n,T\})
for any proper learner in the realizable setting which is a special case of the agnostic setting. Let
(G_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})
be an instance with no effective manipulation (e.g.,
G_{2}
has no edges), together with a class
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\{h_{2,1},\dots,h_{2,n}\}
of Littlestone dimension
\Theta(\log n)
; by the standard agnostic online lower bound of
8
, any learner then incurs regret
\Omega(\sqrt{T\log n})
.

Define
G
as the disjoint union of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Define
\mathcal{H}=\{h^{1},\dots,h^{n}\}
by setting, for each
i\in[n]
,
h^{i}|_{V(G_{1})}=h_{1,i}
and
h^{i}|_{V(G_{2})}=h_{2,i}
, that is extend each hypothesis by matching
the
i
th hypothesis on the other component. At the start of the interaction, the adversary chooses which ever construction procedures larger regret (depending on
n
and
T
). Since the constructions are effectively disconnected, the interaction is reduced to the corresponding original instance. Therefore every proper learner satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{R}_{T}]\;=\;\Omega\!\left(\max\!\left\{\sqrt{T\log n},\min\{n,T\}\right\}\right).
Finally, a routine case analysis (given in Appendix
C.2
) shows that

\Omega\!\left(\max\!\left\{\sqrt{T\log n},\ \min\{n,T\}\right\}\right)=\Omega\!\left(\min\!\left\{\sqrt{T\log n}+n,\ \sqrt{Tn\log n}\right\}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

Strategic classification was first studied in a distributional model by
16
. Since then, researchers have considered different models of agents’ behavior, learning tasks, and analytical perspectives.

Agents’ manipulation restrictions.

Agents face restrictions when manipulating their features, as they cannot arbitrarily change to any feature vector.

16
modeled manipulation restrictions through a cost function, where agents manipulate to another feature vector if and only if the benefit of obtaining a positive prediction outweighs the cost of manipulation. This formulation naturally leads to two common ways of modeling manipulation structures.
The first is a geometric model (e.g.,
12
;
28
;
10
;
14
;
15
;
1
;
25
), where agents are allowed to manipulate their features within a bounded-radius ball under a known metric. In this work, following
29
;
19
;
18
;
3
, we adopt an alternative model based on manipulation graphs.
These two modeling approaches can be translated into each other. However, ball-based models typically restrict manipulation through bounded radii, whereas manipulation graphs restrict manipulation through the degree of the graph.

Distributional learning.

Beyond the online setting, there is a large body of work on strategic learning in the offline distributional setting.
Works such as
16
;
19
;
17
;
23
;
28
;
29
;
18
;
25
study settings in which agents are drawn i.i.d. from an underlying distribution. The learning objective in these works is to output a classifier with low strategic population loss using a minimal amount of training data.

Different analytical perspectives

While our work also focuses on prediction accuracy, many other works have explored additional perspectives on strategic classification, such as encouraging genuine improvements versus discouraging “gaming”
(
2
;
6
;
15
;
21
, e.g.)
, understanding causal implications
(
22
;
6
;
27
;
24
, e.g.)
, transparency in decision rules
(
7
;
26
, e.g.)
, and fairness considerations
(
17
;
21
;
23
, e.g.)
.

Assuming the above two Lemmas, we now complete the proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

If there are fewer than
\tau
case 4 rounds, then there are at least

case 1–3 rounds, and by item 1 in Lemma

the learner incurs at least

\mathbb{E}[M(T)]\;\geq\;\gamma\cdot\frac{T}{2}.

Otherwise, the first

case 4 rounds exist and by item 2 in Lemma

and Lemma

\mathbb{E}[M(T)]\;\geq\;\tau\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\tau}]\;\geq\;\tau\cdot(1-3\gamma)^{\tau}\cdot\frac{n-\tau}{n}.

and

Finally, plugging in for

To prove Lemma

\displaystyle:=\{\,k(f_{s})\in[n]:s<t,\ \text{round $s$ is case 4, }f_{s}\in\mathcal{G},\ \text{and }z_{s}=p_{k(f_{s})}\,\},

We say that a transcript

The following Lemma shows that if

has not been eliminated at round

Lemma 16
.

Fix

and let

be any realization with

\tau\in\mathrm{Fail}_{t}
.
Then

\Pr[i^{*}=j\mid\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau]\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{1}\{j\in S_{t}(\tau)\}}{|S_{t}(\tau)|}.

In particular, for every

\Pr[i^{*}=j\mid\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau]\;=\;\frac{1}{|S_{t}(\tau)|}.

Fix
\tau
with
\Pr[\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau]>0
and
\tau\in\mathrm{Fail}_{t}
.
Write

and

. For any

Step 1:
We show that
\Pr[\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau\mid i^{*}=j]
is the same for all

. Fix any

and consider generating the transcript round-by-round.
For each
s<t
, conditional on the prefix transcript
(\mathcal{D}_{r},f_{r},z_{r},y_{r})_{r=1}^{s-1}

the learner’s choice of
\mathcal{D}_{s}
and its sample

depend only on the learner’s internal randomness and the prefix, and therefore are conditionally independent of
i^{*}
.
Moreover, the adversary’s case choice at time

is a deterministic function of
\mathcal{D}_{s}
(hence independent of
i^{*}
given the prefix).

•

If round
s
is case 1–3, then
(x_{s},y_{s})\in\{(L,-1),(z,-1),(R,+1)\}
does not depend on
i^{*}
. Hence
z_{s}=\mathsf{BR}_{f_{s}}(x_{s})
and

are determined by
(f_{s},\mathcal{D}_{s})
and do not depend on
i^{*}
.

•

If round

is case 4, then
\tau\in\mathrm{Fail}_{t}
implies

and
z_{s}=p_{k(f_{s})}
in
\tau
. Let
k_{s}:=k(f_{s})
. Since

(by definition of

) we have

for every

. For such

Therefore, at every round

\Pr[\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau\mid i^{*}=j]=\lambda(\tau)\qquad\text{for all }j\in S

Step 2:

Using

\Pr[i^{*}=j\mid\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau]=\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau\mid i^{*}=j]\Pr[i^{*}=j]}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[n]}\Pr[\mathcal{T}_{t}=\tau\mid i^{*}=j^{\prime}]\Pr[i^{*}=j^{\prime}]}=\frac{\lambda(\tau)\cdot(1/n)}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in S}\lambda(\tau)\cdot(1/n)}=\frac{1}{|S|}

for

.

Next make a simple observation.

Lemma 17
.

If round

is case 4, then
\mathcal{D}_{t}(\mathcal{G})\geq 1-3\gamma

Equivalently,
\Pr[f_{t}\in\mathcal{G}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t}]\geq 1-3\gamma

Proof.

If round

is case 4, then by definition of the adversary we have

,
\mathcal{D}_{t}(B)<\gamma
, and

We are now ready to prove Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

Fix
r\in[\tau]
and condition on
\mathcal{E}_{r-1}
.
On this event, the transcript at the start of round

is a failure transcript in the sense
of
\mathrm{Fail}_{t_{r}}
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}}
denote the transcript at the start of round

.
By Lemma
17
,

\Pr[f_{t_{r}}\in\mathcal{G}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}}]\geq 1-3\gamma.

Next assume

and write
k_{r}:=k(f_{t_{r}})
.
In Case 4 the underlying vertex is
u_{i^{*}}
. If
i^{*}\neq k_{r}
, then by the definition of
\mathsf{BR}_{G,f_{t_{r}}}(\cdot)
we have

\mathrm{BR}_{G,f_{t_{r}}}(u_{i^{*}})=p_{k_{r}}
, i.e.

.
Thus

\Pr[z_{t_{r}}=p_{k_{r}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}},f_{t_{r}}]\;\geq\;\Pr[i^{*}\neq k_{r}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}}]=1-\Pr[i^{*}=k_{r}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}}].

Because we are conditioning on a failure transcript, Lemma

applies:

\Pr[i^{*}=k_{r}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}}]\leq\frac{1}{|S_{t_{r}}(\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}})|}.

Moreover, on
\mathcal{E}_{r-1}
the eliminated set has size at most

,
so
|S_{t_{r}}(\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}})|\geq n-(r-1)=n-r+1
.
Therefore,

\Pr[z_{t_{r}}=p_{k_{r}}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t_{r}},f_{t_{r}}]\;\geq\;1-\frac{1}{n-r+1}.

Combining the above two equations and using that the above bound holds for every realization of

\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{r}\mid\mathcal{E}_{r-1}]=\Pr\left[f_{t_{r}}\in\mathcal{G}\ \text{and}\ z_{t_{r}}=p_{k(f_{t_{r}})}\mid\mathcal{E}_{r-1}\right]\;\geq\;(1-3\gamma)\left(1-\frac{1}{n-r+1}\right).

Multiplying over

yields

\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\tau}]=\prod_{r=1}^{\tau}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{r}\mid\mathcal{E}_{r-1}]\;\geq\;(1-3\gamma)^{\tau}\cdot\prod_{r=1}^{\tau}\left(1-\frac{1}{n-r+1}\right).

Finally, the product telescopes:

\prod_{r=1}^{\tau}\left(1-\frac{1}{n-r+1}\right)=\prod_{m=n-\tau+1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)=\prod_{m=n-\tau+1}^{n}\frac{m-1}{m}=\frac{n-\tau}{n}.

∎

C.1
Proof of Theorem

We first establish that
\hat{d_{t}}(i)
is a conditionally unbiased estimator for

and a second moment bound for
\hat{d_{t}}
. Fix a round
t
and condition on the history up to time
t-1
, on
p_{t}
, and on the adversary’s choice

. Then
R_{t}
and

are fixed, and the only randomness is

Lemma 18
.

For every

\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{d}_{t}(i)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},p_{t},(x_{t},y_{t})\big]=d_{t}(i).

With probability

\widehat{d}_{t}(i)=0

Lemma 19
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{t}(i)\widehat{d}_{t}(i)^{2}\right]\leq T.

Proof.

It suffices to show

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{t}(i)\widehat{d}_{t}(i)^{2}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1},p_{t},(x_{t},y_{t})\right]\leq 1.

If

so the sum is

. Otherwise,
\widehat{d}_{t}(i)^{2}=\frac{1}{q_{t}^{2}}\mathbf{1}\{i\in R_{t}\}

\sum_{i}p_{t}(i)\widehat{d}_{t}(i)^{2}=\sum_{i\in R_{t}}p_{t}(i)\cdot\frac{1}{q_{t}^{2}}=\frac{q_{t}}{q_{t}^{2}}=\frac{1}{q_{t}}.

Since

with probability

, the expectation is simply
q_{t}\cdot\frac{1}{q_{t}}=1
.
∎

Next we recall a regret bound for FTRL with log-barrier regularization.

Lemma 20
.

Let

be a sequence of arbitrary loss vectors and

a convex regularization function defined as

Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{T}\in\Delta_{\epsilon}
be a sequence of distributions chosen via the rule:

p_{t}=\arg\min_{p\in\Delta_{\epsilon}}\left\{\left\langle p,\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}g_{s}\right\rangle+R_{\eta,\nu}(p)\right\}.

Then, for all

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle g_{t},(p_{t}-p^{*})\right\rangle\leq\frac{n}{\nu}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{\eta}\log n+\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{t}(i)g_{t}(i)^{2},

z_{t}\in[p_{t},p_{t+1}]
is some point on the line segment connecting
p_{t}
and
p_{t+1}

Proof.

The Theorem follows directly from Lemma 4 in Appendix A of

We also rely on the following lemma, which provides a multiplicative stability property of the FTRL iterates.

Lemma 21
.

Let

p_{t+1}(i)\leq 2p_{t}(i).

We prove Lemma

Before proving Lemma
21
, we prove two auxiliary Lemmas. Recall that
\psi_{\nu}(p):=-(1/\nu)\sum_{i}\log p(i)

\nabla^{2}\psi_{\nu}(p)=\frac{1}{\nu}\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{p(1)^{2}},\dots,\frac{1}{p(n)^{2}}\right).

Define the associated local norm

\|x\|_{\psi,p}^{2}:=x^{\top}\nabla^{2}\psi_{\nu}(p)\,x=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x(i)^{2}}{p(i)^{2}}.

For each
t

F_{t}(p):=\left\langle\widehat{D}_{t},p\right\rangle+R_{\eta,\nu}(p),\qquad F_{t+1}(p)=F_{t}(p)+\left\langle\widehat{d}_{t},p\right\rangle.

By definition,

\Delta_{n,\epsilon}

p_{t+1}

For any coordinate

\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(i)}{p_{t}(i)}-1\right)^{2}=\frac{(p^{\prime}(i)-p_{t}(i))^{2}}{p_{t}(i)^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(p^{\prime}(k)-p_{t}(k))^{2}}{p_{t}(k)^{2}}=\nu\|p^{\prime}-p_{t}\|_{\psi,p_{t}}^{2}\leq 1.

Lemma 23

Let

By Lemma

\tilde{p}

and
p^{\prime}

\tilde{p}(i)\leq 2p_{t}(i)

Since

Because

Since

-\frac{1}{q_{t}}(2q_{t}-q_{t})=-1&(y_{t}=-1).\end{cases}

In both cases this is at least

\left\langle\widehat{d}_{t},p^{\prime}-p_{t}\right\rangle\geq-2

Proof of Theorem

.

We prove that

\Omega\!\left(\max\!\left\{\sqrt{T\log n},\ \min\{n,T\}\right\}\right)=\Omega\!\left(\min\!\left\{\sqrt{T\log n}+n,\ \sqrt{Tn\log n}\right\}\right).

Proof.

Let
n\geq 2,\,T\geq 1,\,L:=\log n
, and
s:=\sqrt{TL}
. Define

A=\min\{T,\max\{\min\{n,T\},s\}\},\qquad B=\min\{T,\,n+s,\,\sqrt{n}\,s\}.

We show that
\frac{1}{2}A\leq B\leq 2A
which will imply the desired equality.
2
2

2

Note for every regret bound there is always an implicit
\min\{T,\cdot\}
.

Case 1:
s\geq T
.

In this case,
\max\{\min\{n,T\},s\}=s\geq T
, so
A=\min\{T,s\}=T
.
For
B
, we have
n+s\geq T
and
\sqrt{n}s\geq\sqrt{2}T>T
, so
B=T
.
Thus
A=B
, and the inequality holds.

Case 2:
s<T
.

Here
\max\{\min\{n,T\},s\}\leq T
, so the outer minimum in
A
is redundant.
Thus
A=\max\{\min\{n,T\},s\}
. We distinguish three subcases.

Case 2a:
n\geq T
.

Then
\min\{n,T\}=T
, so
A=\max\{T,s\}=T
(since
s<T
).
For
B
, we note
n+s\geq T
. Also,
\sqrt{TnL}\geq\sqrt{T^{2}L}=T\sqrt{L}
.
Since
n\geq 2
,
\sqrt{L}\geq\sqrt{\log 2}>\frac{1}{2}
, so
\sqrt{n}s\geq\frac{1}{2}T
.
Therefore,
B=\min(T,n+s,\sqrt{n}s)
satisfies
\frac{1}{2}T\leq B\leq T
, or
\frac{1}{2}A\leq B\leq A
.

Case 2b:
n<T
and
n\geq s
.

\min\{n,T\}=n
, so
A=\max\{n,s\}=n
.
Since
s\leq n
, we have
B\leq n+s\leq 2n=2A
.
For the lower bound,
n+s\geq n
