Impact of mental health disorders on problematic substance use and effectiveness of brief intervention among young people in foster care
Authors: Courtney B Dunn, Austin Blake, Katie Fox, Mary V Greiner, Sarah J Beal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsBrief interventions delivered in healthcare settings represent a promising substance use prevention and early intervention strategy for youth in foster care, among whom early life adversity and resulting psychological sequalae increase risk for problematic substance use. Limited research has examined the effectiveness of brief interventions in this subpopulation. This study aimed to examine whether psychological diagnoses were associated with screening positive for problematic substance use, and whether psychological diagnoses impacted effectiveness of a brief intervention for substance use.DesignLongitudinal observational study of adolescents (ages 10–20 years) seen for mandated health examinations in a foster care clinic. Youth who endorsed substance use and received a brief intervention were invited to complete baseline surveys and follow‐ups at 30 days, 60 days and 6 months. Logistic regressions evaluated associations of mental health disorders with endorsing problematic substance use. Generalized linear mixed models examined associations of mental health disorders with trajectories of post‐intervention change in substance use.SettingMidwest, United States.ParticipantsAdolescents seen for mandated health examinations in a foster care clinic who completed a substance use screening measure [CRAFFT2.1 + N; n = 1656; mean age (SD) = 14.4 (2.6); 53.4% female; 58.4% Black, 31.6% white]. A second subsample included only those adolescents who received a brief intervention for substance use during their clinic visit [n = 287; mean age (SD) = 15.9 (2.1); 59.9% female; 53.7% Black, 35.5% white].MeasurementsMental health diagnoses and CRAFFT2.1 + N were extracted from the electronic health record. Youth‐reported count of past 30‐day use of alcohol, cannabis and nicotine/tobacco at each wave.FindingsA minority (12%) of youth in foster care endorsed problematic substance use. Youth with a mental health diagnosis had 58% to 161% higher odds of reporting problematic substance use [odds ratios (ORs) = 1.58–2.61; 95% confidence intervals (CIs) = (1.16–2.13) – (1.93–3.56)]. Following the intervention, youth who had been diagnosed with internalizing disorders (i.e. anxiety, depressive and trauma disorders), and those with co‐occurring internalizing and externalizing disorders, reported smaller decreases or no decreases in their cannabis [ORs = 2.09, 7.03; 95% CIs = (1.17–3.71), (3.43–14.42)] and nicotine use [ORs = 1.95–7.68; 95% CIs = (1.08–3.53) – (4.12–14.32)]. Alcohol use did not vary as a function of mental health diagnoses [ORs = 0.31–13.10; 95% CIs = (0.00–19.37) – (0.19–915.80).ConclusionFoster youth in the United States, particularly those with mental health concerns, appear to be at high risk for problematic substance use.Keywords: brief intervention, foster care, mental health, prevention, screening, substance use

INTRODUCTION

Young people involved in the child welfare system represent an important population for the prevention and early intervention of substance use. Youth in foster care are more likely to initiate substance use at an early age [1], engage in problematic use [2] and develop substance use disorders [3]. They are also at higher risk of experiencing adverse consequences of substance use, including negative physical health outcomes [4] and juvenile and criminal justice involvement [5]. The high risk for substance use in this population is attributable to a family history of substance use [6], instability in family relationships and poor relationships with caregivers [7, 8], placements in congregate care and independent living settings [6, 9], children's experiences with maltreatment [9], and related psychological sequelae following maltreatment and adversity exposure [10, 11].

Compared with the general population, internalizing (e.g. anxiety, depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder) and externalizing (e.g. conduct disorder, oppositional defiant disorder) mental health disorders are more prevalent among youth in foster care [3, 12]. Substance use is linked to externalizing symptoms via behavioral dysregulation [13], whereas substance use is considered a method to regulate negative emotions associated with internalizing symptoms [14]. Indeed, research has identified positive associations between both externalizing and internalizing symptoms with substance use among youth in foster care [15, 16]. Although few longitudinal studies have focused on youth in foster care, one study found that early life stressors led to greater substance use via higher internalizing symptoms among adolescents adopted from foster care [11]. After accounting for internalizing symptoms, externalizing symptoms did not mediate associations between stressors and youth substance use. These findings suggest that youth in foster care who have experienced numerous early life stressors may be more likely to use substances to cope with negative mood. It is vital that practitioners consider the impact of comorbid mental health disorders when aiming to prevent and treat substance use among foster youth.

Medical providers often serve as first responders to mental health and substance use needs for young people in foster care, including through federal and state mandated health examinations [17], making medical appointments an ideal setting for substance use and mental health screening and intervention. Screening, brief intervention and referral to treatment (SBIRT) is a promising early intervention for substance use among young people that can be delivered in a variety of healthcare settings (e.g. emergency departments, primary care clinics) [18]. SBIRT has been shown to reduce alcohol use [18] and may also effectively reduce cannabis use [19] among adolescents; the effectiveness of SBIRT for other substances such as nicotine is less clear [20]. Research also suggests that SBIRT can prevent the progression to a substance use disorder among adolescents at high risk for problematic use [21], including among those with comorbid depressive symptoms [22]. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommendations emphasized the need for more research on the effectiveness of screening and brief intervention, including research examining its effectiveness for all types of substances and among high‐risk populations [23], such as youth in foster care.

The extent to which comorbid mental health symptoms or diagnoses impact the effectiveness of SBIRT has not been studied, and there have been inconsistent findings regarding their impact on other interventions for substance use. A review of the literature found that depressive symptoms may have a negative, positive or non‐significant effect on substance use outcomes across studies evaluating outpatient interventions [24]. Youth with externalizing disorders have been shown to experience greater reductions in substance use following intensive outpatient treatments (e.g. family‐based treatments), potentially because youth with greater symptoms have more potential room for improvement [25]. In contrast, studies have found poorer outcomes of in‐patient substance use treatment among adolescents with externalizing symptoms [26] and with comorbid internalizing and externalizing symptoms [27]. These symptom presentations may also interact. For example, an evaluation of a brief, five‐session intervention found that higher externalizing symptoms were associated with more frequent substance use when depressive symptoms were low [28]. Though it remains unclear how mental health symptoms may impact the effectiveness of SBIRT, research indicates that SBIRT for substance use does not demonstrate secondary effects on internalizing symptoms in youth [29]. Because internalizing and externalizing symptoms may contribute to and maintain youth substance use, brief interventions targeting substance use may be insufficient to reduce substance use among youth with mental health disorders. A better understanding of the effectiveness of SBIRT among youth in foster care with and without mental health disorders would help to inform the effectiveness of substance use screening and brief intervention in this high‐risk population.

The current study

This study examined the associations between mental health diagnoses and problematic substance use and change in substance use in a sample of youth in foster care presenting to a foster care clinic who received brief intervention for substance use and integrated behavioral health services for mental health concerns. First, we examined whether youth in foster care who have a mental health diagnosis are at greater risk for endorsing problematic substance use on a screening measure. Prior research has found that nearly 25% of youth in foster care screen positive for problematic substance use on the CRAFFT (car, relax, alone, forget, friends/family, trouble) [2], a substance use screening tool commonly used in pediatric settings. In addition, mental health disorders are associated with the endorsement of problematic substance use on the CRAFFT screening tool [30]. We thus hypothesized that youth in foster care with a mental health disorder would be more likely to endorse problematic substance use than those without a mental health diagnosis. Second, we examined whether the presence of a mental health disorder impacted any change in substance use in youth after receiving a substance use brief intervention and integrated behavioral health services. We hypothesized that the substance use brief intervention would be less effective in reducing substance use for youth with a mental health disorder.

METHODS

This longitudinal observational study took place at a foster care clinic within a free‐standing pediatric academic medical center in the Midwest USA. State law mandates that youth in foster care have health examinations upon entry into foster care and with each change of placement. The Institutional Review Board of the pediatric medical center and the regional county child welfare agencies approved this study. See Table S3 for reporting checklist for cohort studies.

Study setting, participants and procedures

Consistent with prior data demonstrating an increased risk for substance use onset by age 10 years [1], adolescents aged 10–20 years seen for mandated health examinations completed the CRAFFT 2.1+N screener via tablets as part of clinical care, with high completion rates [31]. All patients screened are without cognitive delay (i.e. intelligence quotient, IQ ≥ 70). All patients receive integrated behavioral health services that address mental and behavioral health via a clinical psychologist at mandated visits. Patients who endorsed substance use or the ‘car’ question on the CRAFFT (i.e. ‘ever ridden in a car with someone who was intoxicated’) or endorsed substance use during the foster care clinic visit also receive a brief intervention delivered by a psychologist or trained masters‐level social worker. The motivational interviewing‐based brief intervention follows the steps of the brief negotiated interview (BNI) [32], which are described in Table 1. If appropriate and the adolescent is receptive, a referral to treatment is discussed.

Participants could enroll at any mandated healthcare visit and could receive repeated doses of the brief intervention at subsequent mandated visits during the 6‐month enrollment period. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center (protocol #2021–0899). All participants in the longitudinal study provided electronic informed assent (if aged 10–17 years) or consent (if 18 years or older) via REDCap prior to completing any study measures. The IRB granted a waiver of parental consent.

All enrolled participants completed the verbally administered Government Performance and Results Act (GPRA) Client Outcome Measures for Discretionary Programs at baseline and at the 6‐month follow‐up as required for all projects funded by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA). The GPRA was administered verbally in person or via phone. To reduce participant burden, participants were asked to complete electronic surveys at baseline and at timepoints when the GPRA was not required (i.e. at the 30‐day follow‐up and 60‐day follow‐up). The measures administered at each timepoint are described in Table S1.

Measures

Substance use screening

At each foster care clinic visit, all adolescents aged 10 years or older complete electronic substance use screening via the CRAFFT 2.1+N [33]. Adolescents report the number of days in the past 12 months that they have used alcohol, tobacco or nicotine, cannabis and other substances. All adolescents are asked if they have ever ridden in a car driven by someone who was intoxicated, coded as ‘yes’ or ‘no’. Adolescents who reported past‐year substance use are also asked if they use substances to relax or feel better about themselves, use substances while alone, forget things while using substances, if family or friends have ever told them to reduce their substance use, and if they have ever gotten into trouble while using substances, coded as ‘yes’ or ‘no’. The CRAFFT score is the sum of ‘yes’ responses to all follow‐up items, with problematic substance use indicated by a score of 2 or greater (α = 0.76). Analyses examining problematic substance use thus include adolescents who presented to clinic who did or did not enroll in the SBIRT intervention evaluation study (Figure 1).

Past 30‐day substance use

Two measures assessed the past 30‐day count of substance use. Three survey items adapted from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) [34] assessed the use of ‘tobacco or nicotine’, ‘alcoholic beverage’ and ‘marijuana’ at the baseline, 30‐day follow‐up and 60‐day follow‐up. At baseline and at the 6‐month follow‐up, the GPRA measured the use of ‘alcohol’, ‘tobacco (not counting vaping products)’, ‘nicotine (vaping products)’, ‘other tobacco or nicotine (chewing tobacco, chaw, snuff, etc.)’ and ‘cannabis (marijuana)’. We used the highest count across the three GPRA tobacco and nicotine items (i.e. vaping, non‐vaping and chewing tobacco) to represent the past 30‐day use of nicotine. At baseline, the GPRA provided more complete data than the equivalent NSDUH items (i.e. 100% completion vs. 94%–97% across items). For all analyses, the GPRA items were used to represent substance use outcomes at baseline and 6‐month follow‐ups, and the NSDUH items were used for 30‐day and 60‐day follow‐ups (Table S1). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the NSDUH items at baseline (see Table S5).

Mental health diagnoses

Mental health diagnoses were extracted from the problem list section of the electronic health record (EHR) using the REDCap Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources module and were classified using The Child and Adolescent Mental Health Disorders Classification System [35]. Diagnoses on the problem list are documented by clinicians (i.e. MDs, PhD/PsyDs or Nurse Practitioners) during clinical encounters across the healthcare system. Three internalizing (anxiety disorders, depressive disorders, trauma and stressor‐related disorders) and two externalizing disorders (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and conduct or disruptive behavior disorders) were included in this study. The total number of mental health diagnoses summed these five diagnostic categories.

Covariates

At baseline, participant demographics, including date of birth, gender and race, were extracted from the EHR. Participant age in years was calculated using the date of birth and date of data collection. Participant gender was coded as male, female, transgender (male to female), transgender (female to male) or gender non‐conforming. Participant race was coded as White, Black or African American, Asian, other race, more than one race or unknown race. Length of time in state custody, placement type at the time of data collection (i.e. foster home, kinship home, group home or independent living), number of placements prior to enrollment and reasons for entry to care (i.e. abuse, neglect, parental substance use) were collected via chart review from IDENTITY [36]. In the county in which this study took place, children are mandated to receive a health exam within five business days of their initial placement into foster care and any subsequent change in placement. They receive a comprehensive exam 30 days after their initial exam. To control for the potential impact of a placement change on their baseline substance use, we extracted the reason for the child's clinic visit. Analyses compared a change of placement exam with an initial placement exam or comprehensive follow‐up.

Analyses

All analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2 [37]. Cross‐sectional analyses were completed using the sample of adolescents seen for mandated health examinations who completed pre‐enrollment screening (CRAFFT 2.1+N; n = 1656), whereas longitudinal analyses include the sample of youth who received the brief intervention and enrolled in the follow‐up study (n = 287; see Figure 1). In the cross‐sectional sample, logistic regressions examined baseline associations between each mental health diagnosis and problematic substance use in individual models. Among those enrolled in the longitudinal follow‐up study, independent samples t‐tests examined baseline mean differences in the number of days of substance use across mental health diagnosis status using Welch's t‐test to account for non‐normality in the dependent variables [38]. Next, generalized linear mixed models examined the relationship between covariates, mental health diagnoses and substance use over time using the ‘lme4’ package [39]. Participant age, sex, gender, race, length of time in state custody, number of prior placements, visit type and current placement type were included as covariates in all generalized linear mixed models because of their associations with baseline substance use (for details, see Table S2). Missing data were accounted for using maximum‐likelihood estimation. Adjusting for effects of covariates on intercepts (i.e. baseline use), we compared generalized linear mixed models with linear and non‐linear trajectories using log likelihood ratio tests to identify the optimal functional form for each type of substance use. Next, we examined the effects of each mental health variable on baseline levels and change in substance use over time. Simple slopes analyses probed significant interactions between the mental health variables and time. We converted estimates to event rate ratios (ERRs), which indicate the change in the count of substance use for each one‐unit increase in the predictor variable. The analyses were not pre‐registered, and the results should be considered exploratory.

A minority (12%) of youth in foster care endorsed problematic substance use (defined as a score of 2 or more on the CRAFFT 2.1+N). Youth significantly differed in their endorsement of problematic substance use based on their mental health diagnosis status (Table 3). Those who had been diagnosed with a trauma disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or a disruptive behavior disorder had between 56% and 85% higher odds of problematic substance use. Notably, youth with a depressive disorder had 155% higher odds of reporting problematic substance use. Anxiety disorders were not significantly associated with problematic substance use. Youth did not differ significantly in their baseline days of substance use based on their diagnosis status (Table 4).

Change in substance use

A non‐linear model provided an optimal fit to the data for change in each substance use outcome across the four waves of data (see Supporting information, Results). For each substance type, the number of days of use decreased linearly by 45%–96% through the 60‐day follow‐up. The quadratic slope accounted for a subsequent increase in days of use through the 6‐month follow‐up (Figure S1). Associations between covariates and substance use are reported in Table S2. Cannabis use

Diagnoses of ADHD, anxiety, depressive and trauma disorders significantly impacted the change in cannabis use over time. Figure 2(a) presents the model‐estimated days of cannabis use over time based on mental health diagnosis status and days of cannabis use at baseline (i.e. conditional upon hypothetical levels of the intercept). Simple slopes analyses revealed that youth with an anxiety disorder did not significantly change their substance use over time (ERR = 1.05, 95% CI = 0.55–1.99; Table S4), whereas those without an anxiety disorder showed a decrease in cannabis use over time (ERR = 0.15, 95% CI = 0.11–0.20). Those with a depressive or trauma disorder showed less of a decline in cannabis use (ERR = 0.33, 95% CI = 0.21–0.51, and ERR = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.18–0.35, respectively) than those without these diagnoses (ERR = 0.16, 95% CI = 0.11–0.23, and ERR = 0.15, 95% CI = 0.08–0.26, respectively). Youth with anxiety and trauma‐related disorders showed more stability over time. Youth with ADHD reported a greater initial linear decrease in use (ERR = 0.05, 95% CI = 0.03–0.08) relative to those without ADHD (ERR = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.35–0.72), followed by a steeper quadratic increase. The number of diagnoses was not associated with any change in cannabis use. As shown in Figure 2(a), these effects become more apparent among youth who reported more frequent baseline cannabis use (for variance around model‐estimated days of use, see Figure S2). Using the alternative baseline measure, depression did not significantly predict change in cannabis use (Table S5).

Moderating effects of mental health disorders on change in cannabis and nicotine use over time. ADHD = attention deficit hyperactivity disorder.

Nicotine use

The presence of ADHD, anxiety, depressive or trauma disorders and the number of diagnoses significantly impacted change in nicotine use. Figure 2(b) presents the model‐estimated days of nicotine use over time based on mental health diagnosis status and days of nicotine use at baseline. Youth in foster care with trauma and depressive disorders showed less of a decline in their nicotine use (ERR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.42–0.81, and ERR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.37–0.84, respectively), relative to those without these diagnoses (ERR = 0.10, 95% CI = 0.05–0.20, and ERR = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.19–0.43, respectively). Youth with an anxiety disorder did not change their nicotine use (ERR = 1.46, 95% CI = 0.89–2.42), whereas those without an anxiety diagnosis showed a linear decrease in their use (ERR = 0.19, 95% CI = 0.13–0.28). Similarly, those with ADHD did not significantly change their nicotine use (ERR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.50–1.16); however, those without ADHD reported a linear decrease (ERR = 0.21, 95% CI = 0.14–0.32). Youth with three or fewer of these diagnoses reported a decrease in use (ERR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.44–0.84), those with four diagnoses showed no change (ERR = 1.30, 95% CI = 0.82–2.05) and those with five diagnoses showed an increase in nicotine use over time (ERR = 2.76, 95% CI = 1.45–5.28). Youth with these specific diagnoses, and a greater number of diagnoses, showed more stability over time. As indicated in Figure 2(b), these differences are exacerbated for youth with higher baseline nicotine use.

Alcohol use

The number of mental health diagnoses and the specific diagnoses were not significantly related to days of alcohol use at baseline or change in alcohol use over time.

DISCUSSION

Young people in foster care are at an elevated risk for mental health and substance use concerns, and mandated healthcare appointments create an opportunity for prevention and early intervention. SBIRT is a potentially effective intervention for the prevention and early identification of substance use disorders, though its effectiveness among youth in foster care with comorbid mental health concerns has not been studied. In a sample of young people presenting to a foster care clinic in the Midwest USA, this study found that mental health diagnoses were associated with problematic substance use and diminished reductions in substance use following a brief intervention.

Our findings confirm a higher prevalence of substance use concerns among youth in foster care than in the general population [40]. Consistent with prior research [41], youth in foster care with a mental health diagnosis had 58%–161% higher odds of reporting problematic use, with the highest odds being reported for those with a depressive disorder. Diagnoses were unrelated to the number of days of substance use. This indicates that youth in foster care with mental health diagnoses reported a similar number of days of use as their peers without a given mental health diagnosis, but endorsed more consequences of their substance use (e.g. gotten in trouble) and risk factors for substance use disorders (e.g. use substances to relax). This may be because adolescents with mental health symptoms are using in a greater quantity or in riskier contexts (e.g. school). Prior research has established a greater risk of experiencing consequences of substance use among youth in foster care [4, 5] and the current results suggest that the presence of mental health concerns may exacerbate their risk for problematic use.

This study is the first to our knowledge to examine the impact of mental health disorders on the effectiveness of a brief intervention for substance use. Although mental health diagnoses were not associated with a change in alcohol use, our findings highlight the adverse impact of internalizing disorders on change in cannabis and nicotine use. As hypothesized, youth with anxiety, depressive and trauma disorders reported smaller decreases in their cannabis and nicotine use after receiving the brief intervention than youth without these diagnoses. In fact, youth with an anxiety disorder showed no post‐intervention change in their substance use. Despite strong associations between behavior problems and substance use in the general population [42], conduct and disruptive behavior disorders did not impact the response to SBIRT. These findings are consistent with prior research indicating that the internalizing pathway may be especially salient among youth in foster care [11]. Experiences of trauma may initiate an internalizing pathway to substance use through dysregulation of fronto‐limbic systems [43, 44]. Therefore, the internalizing pathway to substance use may be less common in the population at large, but more prevalent among youth with histories of trauma, such as those in foster care. It is perhaps unsurprising that internalizing symptoms led to diminished decreases in nicotine and cannabis use because adolescents perceive the use of these substances to be effective methods to cope with distress and to increase positive emotions [45, 46]. Together, these findings indicate that it is critical that substance use interventions targeting youth in foster care address concurrent internalizing symptoms to maximize treatment efficacy.

One exception to our hypotheses was that youth with ADHD showed a greater initial decline in their cannabis use. This may be in part explained by the neurocognitive pathways of nicotine. As a result of the stimulant‐like effects of nicotine, individuals with ADHD have been shown to be highly susceptible to nicotine use, in their effort to self‐medicate inattention and impulsivity symptoms [47]. Youth in foster care who are connected with medical treatment through mandatory examinations may be more likely to receive appropriate stimulant medication for ADHD, which has been shown to reduce the likelihood of substance use disorders in individuals with ADHD [48].

The total number of internalizing and externalizing disorders moderated the intervention effects on nicotine use. More specifically, youth in foster care with four or more diagnoses did not decrease their use and those with five diagnoses actually reported an increase in use after receiving the brief intervention. This suggests that despite receiving the intervention, nicotine use continued to escalate among youth presenting with co‐occurring internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Recent research has identified a combined internalizing and externalizing pathway leading to increased substance use risk [49]. Health screening may more effectively inform treatment needs if both internalizing and externalizing symptoms, in addition to substance use, are identified. For instance, clinicians may consider the presence of mental health diagnoses when determining whether a referral to substance use treatment is appropriate. Youth in foster care with comorbid psychological symptoms may need multifaceted interventions to address their substance use. This is particularly important in healthcare clinics serving youth in foster care owing to the high rates of comorbidity in this population [50]. Future research should continue to investigate the impact of co‐occurring symptom presentations on intervention effectiveness.

Several potential factors may account for the lack of an association between mental health symptoms and changes in alcohol use following the brief intervention. Alcohol use during adolescence is generally more episodic than in later life stages [51] and is driven by peers [52]. Social environments are often shifted for youth in foster care, potentially leading to fewer opportunities for alcohol use in peer contexts. This may have contributed to the overall low frequency of alcohol use in this sample (i.e. an average of less than 1 day of alcohol use in the past month). Owing to such low levels of alcohol use in this sample, there was limited opportunity to detect any change in youth alcohol use. Future research is needed to determine whether there are differential effects of SBIRT on alcohol use in other populations, including among youth with frequent alcohol use.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to our knowledge to examine the impact of mental health on the effectiveness of SBIRT. Its focus on youth in foster care has the potential to inform early identification and intervention for substance use in a high‐risk population. Several limitations, however, should be considered. The data for this study were collected from a foster clinic primarily serving one county within a free‐standing pediatric academic medical center in the Midwest USA, so these findings may not generalize to other systems. Recreational cannabis use was legalized in the state where this study took place over the course of data collection. Although cannabis use remained illegal for all participants in the present study (i.e. under the age of 21 years), the legalization status for cannabis in this state may contribute to a higher prevalence of cannabis use for adolescents and young adults, which may also impact the generalizability of these findings [53]. In addition, mental health diagnoses were based on those documented on the EHR problem list and symptom measures were not collected. Relevant diagnoses may have been omitted from the problem list. The use of secondary EHR data precluded our ability to examine symptom severity and diagnoses over the course of the study, or to explore potential secondary changes in symptoms resulting from the brief intervention.

Clinical implications and conclusions

To enhance our understanding of the effectiveness of SBIRT for youth with mental health concerns, future research evaluating SBIRT should include standardized measures assessing potential mechanisms of change, such as youth mental health symptoms. For example, the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment (ASEBA) offers Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)‐oriented scales, as well as broad measures of internalizing and externalizing symptoms. These measures are often used in clinical and research contexts to identify youth with clinically elevated symptoms [54]. Alternatively, HealthMeasures (www.healthmeasures.net) distributes freely available measures that were developed and evaluated with National Institutes of Health funding. These include normed self‐ and caregiver‐report measures of children's severity of mental health symptoms (e.g. anxiety, aggression) [55].

Youth in foster care are at high risk for problematic substance use due to the presence of mental health symptoms that result from early life adversity (e.g. maltreatment). Despite receiving a brief intervention for substance use, our findings suggest that youth in foster care with internalizing symptoms may continue to engage in substance use as a strategy to cope with negative emotions. Medical appointments provide an opportunity to implement additional brief interventions or connect youth with higher‐level services to address mechanisms contributing to their substance use. For instance, youth in foster care may benefit from additional brief sessions through integrated behavioral health services using evidence‐based approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy to reduce substance use and increase their use of adaptive coping skills [56]. Experts have also recommended incorporating caregiver interventions into primary care visits to improve the ability of caregivers to prevent and respond to youth substance use [57]. Regarding treatment referrals, SBIRT interventionists should consider mental health concerns when determining whether adolescents would benefit from a referral to treatment. Youth in foster care may need a higher level of evidence‐based mental health treatment (e.g. outpatient cognitive behavioral therapy) before they can effectively reduce their substance use.
