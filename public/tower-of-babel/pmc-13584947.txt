Chemotherapy-Induced Remodeling of the Gut Microbiota–Inflammation Axis: Implications for the Course of Bipolar Disorder
Authors: Sinuo Li, Siqi Wang, Yuxuan Ren, Jun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is growing evidence that bipolar disorder, BD and cancer share several biological features, including immune dysregulation, gut microbiota alterations, and chronic inflammation. Chemotherapy remains a mainstay of cancer treatment but can substantially alter the gut microbial ecosystem, impair intestinal barrier integrity, and contribute to systemic inflammation. However, little research has been done on whether these changes brought on by chemotherapy affect how BD develops clinically. This review summarizes the mechanisms through which chemotherapy may modify the gut microbiota–inflammation axis, along with how these changes may impact BD via the microbiota–gut–brain axis. We discuss how intestinal barrier failure, microbial metabolite production, microbial composition, and inflammatory signaling pathways—such as the LPS–TLR4/NF-κB axis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation—are affected by chemotherapeutic drugs. We also investigate the role of gut-derived inflammatory responses in neuroinflammation, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal,HPA axis, impaired neuroplasticity, neurotransmitter imbalance, and cognitive dysfunction, all of which may contribute to mood instability, treatment resistance, and neuroprogression in BD. We also evaluate developing microbiome-targeted therapies, such as probiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and dietary modification, and explore the possible therapeutic consequences of chemotherapy-induced microbiota remodeling for patients with concomitant cancer and BD. Taken together, the available evidence supports the possibility that chemotherapy-induced modification of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may contribute to the clinical course of BD. This integrated mechanistic paradigm offers a potential platform for future translational research and the creation of tailored treatment approaches for patients with comorbid cancer and BD, despite the lack of direct clinical proof. This narrative review summarizes the current evidence linking chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis and inflammatory responses to the clinical course of bipolar disorder and discusses their potential implications for integrated therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Bipolar Disorder and Cancer Comorbidity: Epidemiology and Clinical Challenges

Bipolar disorder, BD is a severe and recurrent psychiatric disorder associated with substantial functional disability, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare burden. Increasing epidemiological evidence suggests that BD and cancer may coexist more frequently than expected, although the strength and direction of these associations vary across cancer types and remain influenced by behavioral, metabolic, healthcare-access, and treatment-related confounders.1 Shared biological processes, including immune dysregulation, chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis abnormalities, may contribute to the biological overlap between psychiatric and oncological disorders. However, causal relationships between BD and specific malignancies remain incompletely established.

The coexistence of BD and cancer presents important clinical challenges. Mood instability, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and impaired treatment adherence may complicate cancer management, while chemotherapy and other cancer-related interventions may interact with psychiatric medications and potentially affect neuropsychiatric outcomes. Cancer-related cognitive impairment, CRCI, characterized by deficits in memory, attention, executive function, and processing speed, may overlap with the cognitive manifestations of BD, thereby complicating clinical assessment and longitudinal management.2

Several mechanisms may contribute to mood destabilization during cancer treatment, including direct neurotoxicity of anticancer agents, drug–drug interactions, cancer-associated systemic inflammation, and psychosocial stress. These mechanisms may coexist with alterations in the gut microbiota and intestinal barrier, making it important to distinguish the microbiota–inflammation pathway from other potential contributors to psychiatric outcomes.

The Gut Microbiota–Inflammation Axis in Bipolar Disorder

The microbiota–gut–brain axis provides a bidirectional communication network linking the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system through immune, neural, endocrine, and metabolic pathways. Increasing evidence indicates that this system may be involved in the pathophysiology of BD. Patients with BD have been reported to exhibit alterations in gut microbial composition, intestinal permeability, microbial metabolism, and systemic inflammatory activity, although findings vary across studies and may be influenced by medication, diet, disease state, and other clinical factors. Altered microbial communities may affect host physiology through changes in short-chain fatty acid production, epithelial barrier integrity, immune signaling, and microbial metabolite availability. In parallel, BD has been associated with abnormalities in inflammatory biomarkers, including TNF-α, IL-1-related mediators, and C-reactive protein, supporting the possibility that immune-inflammatory dysregulation may be involved in the pathophysiology of BD. However, the precise relationship between gut dysbiosis, systemic inflammation, and clinical manifestations of BD remains incompletely understood.3

Importantly, the existing evidence largely establishes an association between gut microbiota abnormalities and BD rather than demonstrating a direct causal pathway. Therefore, gut microbial alterations should be considered within a broader network of metabolic, immune, neuroendocrine, and environmental factors.

Chemotherapy as a Potential Modifier of the Gut Microbiota–Inflammation Axis

Chemotherapy may represent a clinically relevant but relatively underexplored environmental perturbation of this system. Although anticancer drugs primarily target rapidly proliferating tumor cells, many chemotherapeutic agents also affect intestinal epithelial cells and the resident microbiota. Chemotherapy-induced mucosal injury, disruption of epithelial barrier integrity, changes in microbial ecological niches, and altered microbial metabolism may collectively promote intestinal dysbiosis and facilitate the translocation of microbial products into the circulation.4 These alterations may increase systemic exposure to microbial-associated inflammatory signals, including lipopolysaccharide, and may also reduce the availability of microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids. Activation of innate immune pathways, including TLR4/NF-κB and inflammasome-related signaling, may subsequently amplify the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and contribute to persistent systemic inflammation.5 Thus, chemotherapy may not only produce direct toxicity but also reshape the gut microbial and immune environment in ways that could influence distant organs, including the brain.

From a neuropsychiatric perspective, this possibility is particularly relevant to BD. Peripheral inflammatory signals and microbiota-derived metabolites can influence neuroimmune activity, HPA-axis function, neurotransmitter metabolism, and synaptic plasticity. Therefore, chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis and inflammation may interact with pre-existing biological vulnerabilities in BD and contribute to mood instability, cognitive dysfunction, or altered treatment response.6

Rationale and Scope of This Narrative Review

The clinical course of BD has traditionally been attributed to interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental stressors. More recently, dysregulation of the gut microbiota–immune axis has emerged as another potential contributor to disease progression. Chemotherapy provides a distinct clinical context in which marked microbial and inflammatory changes may occur within a relatively short period. Agents such as doxorubicin and cisplatin can damage the intestinal epithelium and may disrupt microbial homeostasis and promote inflammatory mediator production. Through the gut–brain axis, these alterations may contribute to systemic inflammation and potentially influence brain function. Because both gut dysbiosis and chronic inflammation have been implicated in BD, chemotherapy-induced disruption of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may represent an underexplored factor affecting psychiatric outcomes in patients with cancer and comorbid BD.

Despite increasing recognition of the roles of the gut microbiota and inflammation in oncology and psychiatry, the mechanisms through which chemotherapy-induced intestinal alterations may influence the course of BD remain poorly understood, Clarifying these interconnections may improve risk assessment, symptom monitoring, and integrated management in patients with coexisting cancer and BD. This narrative review summarizes current evidence on the effects of chemotherapy on gut microbial composition, intestinal barrier integrity, and inflammatory signaling. It further discusses how chemotherapy-induced remodeling of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may contribute to neuroinflammation, cognitive dysfunction, and mood dysregulation, with particular emphasis on its potential relevance to the progression and management of BD.

A narrative literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus from database inception to June 2026. Search terms included combinations of “chemotherapy”, “gut microbiota”, “gut microbiome”, “intestinal dysbiosis”, “intestinal barrier”, “systemic inflammation”, “neuroinflammation”, “gut–brain axis”, “bipolar disorder”, “HPA axis”, “neurotransmitters”, and “neuroplasticity.” Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened. Peer-reviewed clinical studies, preclinical studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses addressing chemotherapy-induced alterations in the gut microbiota–inflammation axis and their potential neuropsychiatric consequences were considered. Priority was given to recent and mechanistically relevant evidence, whereas non-English publications, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, and studies unrelated to the scope of this review were excluded. As this was a narrative review, no formal meta-analysis or systematic risk-of-bias assessment was performed.

Alternative Explanations, Conflicting Evidence, and Limitations of the Microbiota–Inflammation Hypothesis

Although the microbiota–inflammation axis provides a biologically plausible framework linking chemotherapy to mood and cognitive outcomes in BD, the current evidence does not yet establish a direct causal relationship. Much of the supporting evidence is derived from preclinical models, observational studies, or indirect clinical observations, whereas prospective studies specifically evaluating patients with pre-existing BD remain scarce.7 In particular, it remains unknown whether chemotherapy-induced microbiota alterations precede and predict mood destabilization, treatment resistance, or cognitive decline in this population. Thus, the proposed pathway should be interpreted as a biologically plausible and testable hypothesis rather than an established causal mechanism.

Several alternative or complementary mechanisms may contribute to psychiatric outcomes during cancer treatment independently of, or in parallel with, gut microbiota alterations. First, chemotherapeutic agents may exert direct neurotoxic effects through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, altered neurogenesis, and neuroinflammatory signaling, potentially contributing to affective and cognitive symptoms without requiring microbiota-mediated pathways.8 Second, patients receiving chemotherapy are frequently exposed to concomitant medications, including glucocorticoids, antiemetics, opioids, antibiotics, and other supportive treatments, many of which can influence mood, cognition, immune signaling, or the gut microbiome. In patients with BD, these effects may further interact with psychotropic medications such as lithium, valproate, lamotrigine, or atypical antipsychotics. Third, cancer itself can generate systemic inflammation through tumor-derived mediators and host immune responses, which may promote neuroinflammation independently of intestinal barrier dysfunction. Fourth, cancer diagnosis and treatment are associated with substantial psychosocial stress, sleep disruption, fatigue, pain, and changes in physical activity, all of which may independently increase vulnerability to mood episodes. Therefore, microbiota-dependent and microbiota-independent pathways should not be regarded as mutually exclusive; rather, they may converge on shared neuroimmune, neuroendocrine, and metabolic processes.9

Importantly, the available literature does not support a uniform pattern of chemotherapy-associated microbiota remodeling. The direction and magnitude of microbial changes vary substantially according to cancer type, chemotherapy regimen and cumulative exposure, antibiotic use, dietary intake, gastrointestinal function, host characteristics, and baseline microbial composition. Such heterogeneity raises the possibility that chemotherapy-induced dysbiosis may represent not only a mediator of adverse psychiatric outcomes but also a consequence or biomarker of systemic treatment-related perturbations.10 Similarly, BD itself is characterized by considerable clinical and biological heterogeneity, and only a subset of patients may exhibit sufficient neuroimmune or metabolic vulnerability to develop inflammation-associated mood or cognitive deterioration. Baseline microbiome composition and host inflammatory status may therefore modify the relationship between chemotherapy exposure and psychiatric outcomes. Reverse causality and residual confounding further complicate causal interpretation. BD-related behavioral changes, including altered diet, sleep, physical activity, and medication exposure, may themselves reshape the gut microbiome. Psychotropic medications can also exert direct effects on microbial composition and host metabolism. Consequently, cross-sectional associations between microbial alterations, inflammatory biomarkers, and BD symptoms cannot readily determine whether microbiota changes precede psychiatric deterioration or emerge as a consequence of disease activity and its treatment. Longitudinal designs incorporating pre-treatment microbiome profiling are therefore essential for establishing temporal relationships.11

Taken together, current evidence supports the biological plausibility of a chemotherapy–microbiota–inflammation–brain axis but remains insufficient to demonstrate that chemotherapy-induced microbiota remodeling is a primary driver of BD progression. The proposed model should therefore be viewed primarily as a hypothesis-generating framework that integrates previously separate literatures on cancer treatment–associated microbiome alterations and BD-related gut–brain dysregulation. Future prospective studies should combine longitudinal microbiome profiling with inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers, neurocognitive assessments, detailed characterization of BD symptoms and treatment response, and systematic recording of chemotherapy exposure, concomitant medications, diet, sleep, and psychosocial factors. Such multidimensional approaches will be critical for determining whether chemotherapy-associated dysbiosis contributes causally to adverse psychiatric outcomes, identifying vulnerable patient subgroups, and distinguishing microbiota-dependent effects from parallel microbiota-independent mechanisms.

Mechanistic Underpinnings of Inflammation and Gut Microbiota Dysbiosis Caused by Chemotherapy

The gut microbiota has become a crucial connection between host immunological modulation, neuropsychiatric diseases, and cancer treatment in recent years. There is growing evidence that chemotherapeutic drugs affect intestinal barrier integrity, microbial metabolic processes, and host immunological homeostasis by directly and indirectly affecting the gut microbial environment in addition to tumor cells. Chemotherapy-induced remodeling of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may be a key biological link between cancer treatment and changes in psychiatric symptoms, as the resulting persistent low-grade inflammatory state closely resembles the chronic inflammatory phenotype commonly seen in patients with BD.12

Chemotherapy’s Impact on the Gut Microbiota

One of the commonly reported ecological changes associated with chemotherapy is gut dysbiosis. Metagenomic studies suggest that chemotherapy can alter microbial α-diversity, reduce the abundance of some commensal bacteria, and favor the expansion of opportunistic pathogens. Members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, Enterococcus species, and pathogenic strains of Escherichia coli are often enriched, whereas short-chain fatty acid, SCFA-producing bacteria, including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and Roseburia, are reduced.13 Chemotherapy-induced dysbiosis is primarily driven by two interconnected mechanisms: direct cytotoxic effects on microorganisms and reconfiguration of the intestinal ecological niche. In addition to direct antimicrobial effects, chemotherapy-induced mucosal injury can substantially alter the intestinal ecological niche. The active irinotecan metabolite SN-38 induces extensive apoptosis of intestinal crypt stem cells, impairing epithelial regeneration and disrupting the mucosal barrier.16 As villous atrophy, crypt loss, and epithelial shedding progress, the hypoxic environment that supports commensal anaerobes is progressively disrupted. In contrast, facultative anaerobic bacteria gain a competitive advantage by utilizing nitrate, iron, and nutrient-rich inflammatory exudates, thereby promoting pathobiont expansion.17

Similarly, methotrexate, MTX inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, DHFR, disrupting folate metabolism and nucleotide synthesis in rapidly proliferating crypt cells. Increased epithelial apoptosis and impaired regeneration progressively compromise mucosal integrity and reduce colonization resistance, facilitating the expansion of opportunistic microorganisms.18 Thus, despite differences in their primary molecular targets, multiple chemotherapeutic agents converge on a common pathological process involving crypt stem cell injury, ecological niche disruption, and gut microbiota dysbiosis.

Chemotherapy-associated dysbiosis also involves substantial functional remodeling of microbial communities beyond taxonomic changes. The expansion of LPS-producing bacteria may shift the intestinal environment toward a pro-inflammatory state, whereas depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria reduces SCFA availability.19 The mucus layer represents another critical barrier maintaining host–microbiota homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, goblet cells continuously secrete mucin 2, MUC2, which forms a two-layer mucus structure consisting of a dense inner layer and a looser outer layer that supports commensal colonization.20 Chemotherapy-induced goblet cell injury and reduced MUC2 expression can thin this protective layer and expose epithelial cells to luminal bacteria. Loss of this barrier promotes persistent activation of pattern recognition receptors, PRRs and facilitates colonization by opportunistic organisms such as Escherichia coli and Enterococcus, while reducing commensal anaerobes that depend on mucin-derived glycans.21

At the same time, inflammatory exudates enriched in iron, amino acids, and nitrate further favor the growth of facultative anaerobes and promote a microbiota associated with intestinal inflammation. Such ecological niche remodeling is considered an important component of chemotherapy-associated intestinal mucositis.22

Oxidative stress represents another important driver of chemotherapy-induced microbial dysregulation. Many chemotherapeutic agents increase reactive oxygen species, ROS in the intestinal microenvironment through inflammatory cell activation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and NADPH oxidase, NOX activation.23 Excessive ROS not only damages intestinal epithelial cells but also alters the luminal redox environment. Obligate anaerobes, which have limited antioxidant defenses, are particularly vulnerable, resulting in reduced survival of important SCFA-producing bacteria such as Roseburia, Bifidobacterium, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii.24 Conversely, facultative anaerobes, including Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus, possess greater oxidative stress resistance and can utilize inflammation-derived nitrate as an alternative electron acceptor, providing a competitive advantage.25 Thus, ROS-mediated changes in the intestinal redox environment create a selective ecological pressure that favors a shift from a symbiotic, anaerobe-dominated community toward a dysbiotic microbiota enriched in opportunistic pathogens.

Ultimately, chemotherapy-induced dysbiosis may promote the transition from local intestinal inflammation to systemic inflammation. Among the microbial inflammatory mediators involved, LPS is a potent inducer of innate immune activation. Notably, the resulting cascade of gut dysbiosis–intestinal permeability–systemic inflammation shares important features with the chronic low-grade inflammatory state observed in BD. Depletion of SCFA-producing bacteria and reduced microbial diversity may represent additional biological features shared by these conditions.26 Therefore, through the combined effects of direct antimicrobial activity, epithelial and mucus barrier injury, ecological niche remodeling, and oxidative stress, chemotherapy can profoundly disrupt beneficial microbial communities and establish a dysbiotic intestinal environment.

Routes Connecting Systemic Inflammation and Gut Dysbiosis

A crucial pathophysiological link between intestinal injury and systemic inflammation is gut dysbiosis. Increasing evidence indicates that microbial imbalance alters not only microbial metabolite profiles but also intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating the entry of pathogen-associated molecular patterns, PAMPs into the circulation. This process activates innate immune responses and promotes chronic low-grade systemic inflammation.27 Among the proposed mechanisms, toxin-mediated barrier disruption, microbial translocation, and activation of the LPS–Toll-like receptor 4, TLR4 signaling axis are particularly well characterized. Under physiological conditions, the intestinal barrier consists of biological, chemical, immunological, and mechanical components, with adherens junctions, AJs and tight junctions, TJs tightly regulating paracellular permeability. During dysbiosis, expansion of opportunistic pathogens and production of enterotoxins can directly disrupt epithelial junctional complexes. Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, ETBF, for example, produces Bacteroides fragilis toxin, BFT, a zinc-dependent metalloprotease that cleaves E-cadherin, a key component of AJs. This disrupts epithelial adhesion and promotes redistribution and dissociation of tight junction proteins, including occludin, ZO-1, and claudins, thereby increasing paracellular permeability and compromising barrier integrity.28 Similarly, Clostridium perfringens enterotoxin, CPE binds to claudin-3 and claudin-4, induces transmembrane pore formation, and promotes claudin internalization and degradation, resulting in increased epithelial permeability and, in severe cases, a “leaky gut” phenotype.29

In addition to bacterial toxins, depletion of SCFA-producing bacteria can further weaken the intestinal barrier. Reduced butyrate availability impairs epithelial energy metabolism and downregulates barrier-associated proteins such as ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1.30 Thus, toxin-mediated epithelial injury and loss of microbial metabolic support may act synergistically to promote barrier failure. Once the barrier is compromised, microbial components can translocate from the intestinal lumen into the systemic circulation. Major circulating PAMPs include LPS, peptidoglycan, PGN, flagellin, and bacterial DNA.31 Increased paracellular permeability facilitates the passage of smaller microbial products, whereas larger components may reach the circulation through transcellular transport, chylomicron-mediated lymphatic transport, outer membrane vesicles, OMVs, or immune-cell-mediated transport.32

Among these microbial products, LPS is one of the most potent and extensively characterized inducers of systemic inflammation. The lipid A moiety of LPS is recognized by the TLR4/MD-2 receptor complex after LPS is transferred from LPS-binding protein, LBP to CD14 and subsequently to TLR4/MD-2.33 TLR4 activation primarily recruits the MyD88-dependent pathway, in which MyD88 assembles the Myddosome and activates IRAK4, IRAK1, and TRAF6, leading to TAK1 activation and subsequent stimulation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways.34 NF-κB activation involves IKK-mediated phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, allowing p65/p50 to enter the nucleus and induce inflammatory genes, including those encoding TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and NLRP3.35 In parallel, TAK1 activates p38, JNK, and ERK, which stimulate AP-1 and further enhance inflammatory gene expression.

LPS can also amplify inflammation through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. In the canonical two-signal model, LPS–TLR4 signaling provides the priming signal by inducing NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β expression, whereas damage-associated molecular patterns, DAMPs, including extracellular ATP, ROS, and mitochondrial DNA, provide the activation signal.36 Activated NLRP3 recruits the adaptor ASC and promotes caspase-1 activation, which cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature forms and processes Gasdermin D, resulting in pore formation, pyroptosis, and further release of inflammatory mediators.37 In addition, LPS-stimulated macrophages can produce IL-12 and IL-18, activating ILC1s and natural killer, NK cells and increasing STAT4-dependent IFN-γ production. IFN-γ may further enhance TLR4 expression and macrophage inflammatory polarization, thereby establishing a self-sustaining positive-feedback loop.38 Collectively, intestinal barrier failure, microbial translocation, and activation of the LPS–TLR4/MyD88–NF-κB/MAPK axis and NLRP3 inflammasome provide interconnected mechanisms through which gut dysbiosis can promote chronic low-grade systemic inflammation. These pathways ultimately increase circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ, and may represent an important mechanistic bridge between gut microbiota abnormalities and neuropsychiatric disease, as well as a key basis of gut-derived metabolic endotoxemia.

Possible Effects of the Gut-Brain Axis on Bipolar Disorder Progression

BD is no longer just a mental illness marked by neurotransmitter imbalance, but rather a systemic disorder encompassing disruption of immunological function, metabolic equilibrium, and neuronal circuitry. The functional state of mood-regulating networks may be continually influenced by the gut microbiota and microbiota-mediated inflammatory responses, which may also contribute to the start, recurrence, and progression of BD, according to mounting evidence.39 Interestingly, the clinical changes observed in BD patients significantly coincide with intestinal barrier failure, systemic inflammatory activation, and gut dysbiosis brought on by chemotherapy. Therefore, one significant environmental trigger that affects the clinical course of BD may be the modification of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis brought on by chemotherapy. Growing evidence suggests that gut-derived inflammatory signals can cross the gut–brain interface through a variety of interconnected pathways, including immune, neuroendocrine, metabolic, and neural signaling mechanisms, defying the conventional belief that peripheral inflammation has limited access to the central nervous system, CNS. Neural circuits involved in emotional regulation are eventually impacted by these pathways, which allow bidirectional communication between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system.

Crucially, the extra inflammatory load brought on by chemotherapy-related changes in the gut microbiota may further disrupt neuronal homeostasis in BD patients, who frequently show pre-existing sensitivity to neuroinflammation and poor emotional control. Through gut-brain axis-mediated processes, this inflammatory challenge may cause the illness to transition from a reasonably stable state to an active mood episode, which might lead to symptom worsening, relapse, and disease progression.

Inflammation–Neural Interactions: Impaired Neuroplasticity as a Connection Between Bipolar Disorder and Gut-Derived Inflammation

Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a potential molecular link between peripheral immune dysregulation and neuropathological changes observed in BD. The detailed mechanisms are presented in Figure 3. Growing evidence indicates that BD is characterized by persistent immune activation, chronic low-grade inflammation, and disturbances in neurotransmitter homeostasis. Meta-analyses have shown elevated circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ, in patients with BD during manic, depressive, and euthymic phases, suggesting that inflammatory dysregulation may persist across the disease course rather than being restricted to acute mood episodes.40 Chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis may further exacerbate this inflammatory burden through intestinal barrier disruption and sustained systemic exposure to microbial-derived products such as LPS. These inflammatory signals may influence the central nervous system through vagal signaling, transport across the blood–brain barrier, BBB, or inflammation-induced changes in BBB permeability, thereby potentially promoting sustained activation of microglia and astrocytes.41 Thus, gut-derived inflammation may represent a potential environmental factor contributing to mood instability and recurrent mood episodes in BD. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, BDNF is a key regulator of neurogenesis, neuroplasticity, and synaptic remodeling. By binding to tropomyosin receptor kinase B, TrkB, BDNF promotes neuronal survival, dendritic spine formation, and long-term potentiation, LTP through signaling pathways including phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B, PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, and phospholipase C-γ, PLCγ.42 Persistent elevation of TNF-α and IL-1β may suppress BDNF transcription through NF-κB and p38-MAPK signaling while reducing TrkB expression, thereby potentially reducing neuronal resilience and neurotrophic support. Clinical studies have repeatedly reported lower peripheral BDNF levels in patients with BD than in healthy controls, with reduced BDNF associated with disease severity, progression, and cognitive impairment. Experimental evidence further suggests that chronic inflammation decreases BDNF expression in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, accompanied by impaired neurogenesis and mood-related behavioral abnormalities.43 Inflammation-induced BDNF deficiency may therefore represent an important molecular mechanism underlying impaired neuroplasticity in BD.

Synaptic plasticity is essential for the stable functioning of neural circuits involved in emotional regulation. Persistent increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines can disrupt the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. IL-1β may impair NMDA receptor-dependent LTP by interfering with key synaptic signaling molecules, including calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II, CaMKII and cAMP response element-binding protein, CREB, whereas TNF-α can enhance glutamatergic neurotransmission by promoting the membrane insertion and surface expression of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid, AMPA receptors.44 Chronic inflammation may also alter dendritic spine architecture and synaptic protein expression, thereby reducing neuronal adaptability to external stimuli.

In patients with BD, neuroimaging studies have demonstrated dysregulated activity within prefrontal–limbic circuits and reduced functional connectivity between the prefrontal cortex,PFC and amygdala.45 These structural and functional abnormalities are associated with elevated inflammatory biomarkers, supporting a potential link between inflammation-driven disruption of synaptic remodeling and impaired mood-regulation networks. Another important mechanism involves inflammation-induced alterations in tryptophan metabolism. Under physiological conditions, tryptophan is primarily metabolized toward serotonin 5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT synthesis; however, inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 can induce indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase, IDO, redirecting tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway.46

Kynurenine can enter the brain and undergo further metabolism to produce quinolinic acid, QUIN, a potent NMDA receptor agonist. Excessive QUIN production may promote oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and calcium overload, contributing to excitotoxic neuronal injury, while relatively reduced production of kynurenic acid, KYNA may further weaken neuroprotection. Abnormalities in kynurenine metabolism have been reported in BD and associated with disease activity, cognitive impairment, and relapse risk.47 The kynurenine pathway may therefore represent an important molecular interface linking inflammation, neurotransmitter imbalance, and impaired neuroplasticity.

Microglia are the principal effector cells of neuroinflammatory responses in the central nervous system and normally participate in synaptic surveillance and developmental pruning. Persistent inflammatory stimulation can shift microglia from a homeostatic toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, characterized by increased production of NO, ROS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6.48 Activated microglia may also promote excessive synaptic elimination through complement-dependent mechanisms. When functional synapses are aberrantly tagged by complement components such as C1q and C3, their recognition and engulfment by microglia can lead to excessive synaptic pruning.49 Such abnormal synaptic loss may contribute to reduced neural connectivity and cognitive impairment and has been implicated in the pathophysiology of several neuropsychiatric disorders, including BD.

The hippocampus, PFC, and amygdala are key regions involved in emotional regulation in which these inflammation-related pathways converge. Chronic inflammation may impair hippocampal structure and function by disrupting neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity. At the same time, increased amygdala reactivity to negative emotional stimuli and reduced dendritic complexity and cognitive control in the PFC may further compromise emotional regulation.50 The resulting dysfunction of prefrontal–limbic circuitry may impair emotional homeostasis and contribute to the alternating manic and depressive episodes characteristic of BD. Importantly, disruption of inflammation-dependent neuroplasticity may contribute to a positive-feedback loop involving inflammation, neuronal injury, emotional dysregulation, and further inflammatory activation. This self-sustaining process may contribute to the transition from an episodic disorder toward more persistent disease progression and cognitive impairment.

In conclusion, inflammation-mediated disruption of neuroplasticity represents an important pathway through which gut-derived inflammation may influence the course of BD. Dysregulated synaptic remodeling, aberrant microglial activation, kynurenine pathway activation, and impaired BDNF signaling may collectively disrupt mood-regulating neural circuits. These mechanisms provide a plausible biological basis for the potential contribution of chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis and inflammation to BD progression and support further investigation of microbiota- and inflammation-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Neuroendocrine Pathways: The Impact of Gut-Derived Inflammation on Bipolar Disorder is Mediated by HPA Axis Dysfunction

HPA axis dysregulation is a commonly reported biological abnormality in BD, according to mounting data. HPA axis disruption has been strongly linked to illness chronicity, cognitive decline, and a higher chance of recurrence in addition to its role in acute mood episodes.51 Notably, prolonged HPA axis hyperactivity may worsen inflammation and microbial imbalance, potentially creating a positive-feedback loop, whereas gut microbiota dysbiosis and the ensuing inflammatory responses can persistently stimulate the HPA axis. Under normal circumstances, the paraventricular nucleus, PVN of the hypothalamus secretes corticotropin-releasing hormone, CRH, which causes the anterior pituitary gland to produce adrenocorticotropic hormone, ACTH. ACTH then stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete glucocorticoids, mostly cortisol. Through glucocorticoid receptors, GRs in the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and hypothalamus, cortisol regulates negative feedback, preserving the homeostasis of the HPA axis.52 However, long-term increases in LPS and pro-inflammatory cytokines can directly activate hypothalamic neurons that produce CRH in the context of gut dysbiosis. It has been demonstrated that cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 can cross or affect the BBB and increase the production of CRH by triggering signaling pathways such nuclear NFκB and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3, STAT3. As a result, the HPA axis may become chronically activated. Concurrently, intestinal inflammatory signals can be directly sent by afferent vagal fibers to the brainstem’s nucleus tractus solitarius, NTS, which in turn activates hypothalamic stress-response regions.53 When compared to healthy individuals, clinical studies have consistently shown that patients with BD have significantly higher plasma cortisol levels, increased 24-hour urinary free cortisol excretion, and higher rates of abnormal dexamethasone suppression test results, all of which point to a persistently hyperresponsive HPA axis.54 Additionally, a pathogenic hypothesis in which inflammation leads to chronic HPA axis activation has been supported by the positive correlation seen between circulating inflammatory markers and cortisol concentrations.

The anti-inflammatory effects of glucocorticoids progressively wane despite persistent increases in cortisol levels during chronic inflammation; this process is known as glucocorticoid resistance. By triggering NF-κB and p38-MAPK signaling pathways, pro-inflammatory cytokines can degrade cortisol-mediated negative feedback control by decreasing Glucocorticoid Receptor, GR expression and impairing GR nuclear translocation. Furthermore, the methylation status of the GR gene NR3C1 promoter area may be changed by epigenetic alterations linked to oxidative stress and inflammation, which would further decrease GR sensitivity.55 The hippocampus is more susceptible to glucocorticoid toxicity and inflammatory assaults, and it plays a crucial role in the control of the HPA axis negative feedback. Long-term exposure to high cortisol reduces hippocampus volume and GR expression, decreases hippocampal neurogenesis, and causes dendritic atrophy.56 A vicious cycle marked by inflammation, hypercortisolemia, hippocampus damage, poor negative feedback, and further amplification of inflammation is created when hippocampal negative feedback capacity declines and HPA axis activation becomes more prolonged.

In addition to upsetting neuroendocrine balance, persistently high glucocorticoid levels also directly hinder neuroplasticity. Cortisol inhibits the development and maintenance of LTP, decreases the proliferative potential of neural stem cells in the hippocampal dentate gyrus, and lowers BDNF production, according to experimental research. Glucocorticoids also cause extracellular glutamate buildup and excitotoxic neuronal damage by downregulating the expression of the glutamate transporter 1, GLT-1.57 Chronic hypercortisolemia is linked to decreased neuronal connection, decreased dendritic spine density, and compromised executive function in the prefrontal cortex. On the other hand, the amygdala is more receptive to negative emotional stimuli and has higher neuronal excitability.58 The oscillation between manic and depressed states that defines BD has a neurobiological foundation due to the dysregulation of prefrontal–limbic circuitry caused by these structural and functional changes taken together.

When considered together, the HPA axis serves as a key amplifier of inflammation-induced impairment of neuroplasticity and emotional instability, as well as a vital route via which peripheral inflammatory signals impact the central nervous system. The HPA axis may be persistently activated by chemotherapy-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis, hastening the course of the disease and raising the chance of recurrence in BD patients.

By controlling the production, metabolism, and communication of neurotransmitters and their precursors, the gut microbiota can directly affect CNS function in addition to inflammatory and neuroendocrine pathways. According to the newly put forth microbiota–gut–brain axis theory, intestinal microbes are crucial modulators of neurochemical communication as well as a significant source of neuroactive chemicals. SCFAs are important microbial metabolites produced by the fermentation of dietary fiber, namely acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Butyrate is an important microbial metabolite involved in the regulation of gut–brain axis homeostasis.62

In addition to maintaining the integrity of the intestinal and blood-brain barriers, SCFAs can also modulate the expression of genes involved in neuroplasticity by blocking histone deacetylases, HDACs. This process may attenuate excessive microglial activation and support BDNF expression.63 As a result, SCFA deficiency may contribute to the pathogenesis of BD via a variety of pathways, such as increased neuroinflammation, decreased neuroplasticity, and compromised blood–brain barrier function. In addition to serotonin metabolism, the gut microbiota affects a number of other neurotransmitter systems, such as glutamate, acetylcholine, dopamine, and gamma-aminobutyric acid, GABA.

Glutamate decarboxylase, GAD, which catalyzes the transformation of glutamate into GABA, is expressed by species of the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. There is growing evidence that people with BD have anomalies in dopamine metabolism, an imbalance in the glutamate/GABA ratio, and decreased populations of GABA-producing bacteria. These changes have been associated with mood instability and cognitive impairment.64 Additionally, vagal afferent fibers can be stimulated by gut-derived neuroactive chemicals and microbial metabolites, facilitating the quick transfer of intestinal signals to the limbic system and brainstem. Gut microbial signals may influence neuronal networks involved in emotional regulation through this neural communication pathway. Crucially, changes in neurotransmitters are closely linked to inflammatory reactions and malfunctioning of the HPA axis. While HPA axis stimulation further modifies tryptophan usage and dopamine metabolism, inflammatory signals can hinder serotonin production by triggering the IDO pathway.65 On the other hand, disruptions in neurotransmitter systems can have a cascading effect on immunological regulation and stress response.

Taken together, these interactions may contribute to a self-reinforcing network linking gut dysbiosis, systemic inflammation, and neuropsychiatric dysfunction. On the one hand, by producing more LPS and pro-inflammatory cytokines, microbial imbalance encourages systemic inflammation and HPA axis activation. Conversely, mood-regulating neuronal circuits are directly impacted by SFA depletion, dysregulated tryptophan metabolism, and neurotransmitter imbalance. These processes may contribute to the development, recurrence, and long-term course of BD.

When considered together, the microbial metabolite–neurotransmitter pathway may represent one mechanism through which the gut–brain axis influences the pathophysiology of BD. Additionally, this pathway provides a rationale for further investigation of microbiota-targeted approaches, including as probiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and dietary modifications meant to improve neuropsychiatric outcomes and restore microbial equilibrium.

Clinical Repercussions: Possible Effect on Bipolar Disorder Progression

Preventive pharmacotherapy remains the cornerstone of bipolar disorder management. Mood stabilizers, particularly lithium and valproate, and certain atypical antipsychotics are recommended for long-term relapse prevention in patients with recurrent mood episodes. For many patients, continuous prophylactic treatment is necessary to reduce the risk of manic and depressive recurrences. This is especially relevant in the context of cancer, where chemotherapy and associated stressors may destabilize mood and increase vulnerability to relapse. However, the choice of prophylactic agents in cancer patients requires careful consideration of drug–drug interactions, organ function, and hematological status.66 Pathway provides a rationale for further investigation of microbiota-targeted approaches.67 Notably, there is significant molecular overlap between the chronic neuroinflammatory state that defines BD and the gut microbiota–inflammation remodeling brought on by chemotherapy. Chemotherapy’s increased inflammatory load may upset the neuroimmune balance in patients who are clinically stable or in remission, leading to mood swings, cognitive deterioration, and decreased treatment compliance. These alterations may eventually create a self-sustaining pathogenic cycle marked by deteriorating mental symptoms, decreased treatment effectiveness, and inflammatory activation.

Prolonged Inflammation May Increase Treatment Resistance and Decrease the Effectiveness of Mood Stabilizers

One of the main factors contributing to poor long-term results in BD is treatment resistance. Persistent inflammation has been implicated as a potential biological factor associated with reduced responsiveness to mood-stabilizing treatments in recent developments in psychoneuroimmunology. Patients with BD had consistently higher circulation levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CRP, according to several clinical investigations. Higher levels of these inflammatory biomarkers have been repeatedly linked to worse treatment outcomes, more frequent relapses, and quicker disease progression.68 Chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis may theoretically raise the risk of mood stabilizer medication failure since it increases the systemic inflammatory load. Pharmacokinetically speaking, inflammation can change how drugs are metabolized by inhibiting the activity of cytochrome P450, CYP450 enzymes. It has been demonstrated that pro-inflammatory cytokines, especially IL-6, downregulate the expression of CYP3A4, CYP2C19, and CYP1A2, which impacts the clearance of psychiatric drugs including risperidone, quetiapine, and olanzapine. These changes might raise the likelihood of negative medication responses and systemic drug exposure. Furthermore, blood-brain barrier transporters’ expression and activity can be altered by inflammatory mediators like TNF-α, which may have an impact on how well therapeutic medicines enter the central nervous system.69 More significantly, the neuroprotective processes that mood stabilizers need to achieve their therapeutic benefits are directly countered by chronic inflammation. Inhibition of GSK-3β, augmentation of BDNF expression, and restoration of synaptic plasticity are at least partially responsible for the therapeutic effectiveness of lithium, valproate, and lamotrigine.70 Persistent inflammatory activation, however, attenuates drug-induced recovery of neuroplasticity by promoting NFκB and GSK-3β signaling, suppressing BDNF production, and increasing metabolism of the kynurenine pathway.71

Interestingly, a number of mood stabilizers have inherent anti-inflammatory qualities. Therefore, a dynamic balance between the drug’s anti-inflammatory potential and the patient’s underlying inflammatory load may account for their therapeutic success. The endogenous anti-inflammatory effects of these drugs may not be adequate to restore neuroimmune homeostasis when systemic inflammation is continuously raised, which might lead to reduced clinical effectiveness and, in certain situations, treatment resistance.72 Therefore, in individuals with BD, chemotherapy-induced inflammatory activation may represent one environmental factor that influences treatment response.

Cognitive Decline May Be Accelerated by Synergistic Neuroinflammatory Effects

Cognitive impairment is increasingly recognized as an important long-term feature of BD. Patients often show chronic abnormalities in executive function, working memory, attention, and processing speed even during euthymic times. Chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment, CICI, sometimes known as “chemobrain”, is one of the most common neuropsychiatric side effects that cancer survivors encounter.73 Despite their different etiologies, CICI and BD share several overlapping neurobiological features. Neuroinflammation is a significant common pathogenic mechanism that underlies both disorders, according to mounting evidence.74 First, microglia are activated by chemotherapy-induced systemic inflammation, which increases the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. The confluence of chemotherapy-related and disease-associated inflammatory processes may synergistically increase central neuroinflammation since patients with BD already have a tendency toward microglial overactivation. Second, increased oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are linked to both chemotherapy and BD, which impairs brain energy metabolism. High metabolic demand areas of the brain, such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, seem to be particularly susceptible to these pathological insults.75 Moreover, both disorders are marked by diminished hippocampus neurogenesis and decreased BDNF production, which lead to long-lasting deficiencies in neuroplasticity. Both illnesses share glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity and dysregulation of the kynurenine pathway, which have been strongly linked to cognitive impairment. The idea of “inflammaging”, which has been put up more recently, postulates that long-term inflammation speeds up brain aging through processes including mitochondrial malfunction, telomere shortening, and epigenetic changes. The long-term cognitive impairment seen in both BD and CICI may have a similar biological foundation due to this process.76 Chemotherapy-induced neuroinflammation may work in concert with pre-existing disease-related neuronal damage in patients with concomitant BD and cancer, potentially increasing vulnerability to persistent cognitive decline.

Mood Deterioration May Impair Oncological Outcomes and Decrease Treatment Adherence

Chemotherapy-induced modification of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may have neurological consequences, but it can also negatively impact treatment behavior by upsetting mood, which can impact cancer treatment success. Mood state is one of the main factors influencing treatment adherence, and prior research has repeatedly shown that people with mental illnesses may have poorer treatment adherence than the general population. In contrast to depressive episodes, which are marked by emotions of hopelessness, decreased motivation for treatment, and limited capacity for self-management, manic episodes frequently result in impaired insight, increased impulsivity, and treatment rejection.77 Consequently, worsening mood symptoms can directly compromise adherence to anticancer therapeutic regimens. Fatigue, discomfort, and sleep issues brought on by chemotherapy may worsen psychological distress, resulting in a vicious circle of worsening symptoms, deteriorating mood, and decreased treatment compliance. 116 Multiple cohort studies have demonstrated that patients with psychiatric disorders are at significantly greater risk of medication nonadherence, treatment delays, and premature discontinuation of therapy, all of which have been associated with poorer oncological outcomes and increased mortality.78

Furthermore, the biology of tumors may be directly impacted by ongoing systemic inflammation. Angiogenesis, tumor cell migration, and immune evasion are all facilitated by pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-6. Furthermore, sustained stress-induced chronic activation of the HPA axis may inhibit anticancer immune responses.79 Thus, disruption of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may concurrently impact the pathogenic mechanisms underlying both mental illness and cancer in individuals with coexisting BD and cancer, eventually impacting overall clinical outcomes.

Summary and Clinical Implications

All things considered, the effects of chemotherapy-induced alteration of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may extend well beyond the traditional notion of gastrointestinal damage. Gut–brain axis-mediated inflammatory activation may be associated with reduced responsiveness to mood-stabilizing treatment, potentially contributing to reduced treatment responsiveness, cognitive impairment, and an increased risk of mood relapse, particularly in susceptible individuals. Cancer-related outcomes may also be influenced if worsening mood symptoms and cognitive impairment compromise treatment adherence.80 Nevertheless, these possibilities should not diminish the central role of standard preventive pharmacotherapy for bipolar disorder. Mood stabilizers and antipsychotics remain the cornerstone of relapse prevention, and current evidence does not support microbiota-targeted interventions, including probiotics, as substitutes for these agents. Whether comprehensive care that includes close assessment of systemic inflammatory state, cognitive function, and mental problems improves outcomes during cancer therapy remains to be determined and should be examined in prospective studies. In particular, the role of probiotics, prebiotics, and related strategies in preventing mood relapse remains uncertain, and their use in cancer patients requires additional caution because of potential risks in immunocompromised individuals and possible interactions with chemotherapy.

Therefore, it may be important to consider broadening treatment objectives beyond oncological therapy alone for patients with concomitant BD and cancer, although direct evidence to support this approach is still lacking. Future research is needed to evaluate whether integrating gut microbiota analysis, inflammatory biomarkers, and standardized mental health evaluations—alongside, rather than instead of, established mood-stabilizing treatment—may help inform more personalized and interdisciplinary approaches to clinical management.

Therapeutic Strategies Targeting the Gut Microbiota–Inflammation Axis: A Novel Perspective for Adjunctive Treatment of Bipolar Disorder

The gut microbiota has become an attractive, possibly changeable therapeutic target with the ongoing development of the microbiota–gut–brain axis idea. A growing body of research indicates that gut dysbiosis plays a role in the pathophysiology of BD by affecting neurotransmitter metabolism, causing systemic inflammation, and upsetting the homeostasis of the HPA axis. Growing interest in microbiota-targeted therapies, including as probiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, FMT, and dietary change, as possible supplemental therapeutic approaches, has been sparked by these findings. These methods have advanced from preclinical research to clinical assessment, showing promising ability to reduce inflammatory reactions and alleviate mood symptoms.81 Crucially, all therapies share a common therapeutic route focused on attenuating central neuroinflammation, lowering systemic inflammatory burden, and restoring intestinal barrier integrity despite having different modes of action. This new therapeutic paradigm offers a fresh scientific justification for BD adjunctive treatment approaches.

Probiotics and Synbiotics: Restoring Gut Microbial Homeostasis and Inflammatory Balance

The most researched microbiota-targeted therapies at the moment are probiotics and synbiotics. Their therapeutic effects are mainly mediated by inhibition of inflammatory responses, restoration of intestinal barrier function, and modification of gut microbial composition. A number of randomized controlled studies, RCTs have assessed their effectiveness in individuals with BD and associated mood disorders in recent years. Probiotic treatment, for instance, significantly reduced oxidative stress, improved many inflammatory biomarkers, and showed a trend toward improved mood outcomes, according to a clinical investigation including patients with first-episode BD. More recently, a 2023 clinical trial found that supplementing with a particular Bifidobacterium-based formulation dramatically improved depressed symptom scores while largely repairing gut microbiota balance in individuals with bipolar I illness going through a depressive episode.82 Additionally, a multispecies probiotic preparation significantly decreased intestinal permeability biomarkers, such as zonulin, as well as a number of inflammation-related indicators in a larger double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial, despite not significantly improving core psychiatric symptoms. These results imply that biological disease change, rather than direct clinical relief, may be the main therapeutic advantage of probiotics.83

These clinical observations are further supported by mechanistic data from animal research. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species have been shown in numerous stress-induced animal models to reduce impulsive and depressive behaviors by boosting SCFA synthesis, inhibiting NFκB signaling, and attenuating microglial activation.84 All things considered, the beneficial effects of probiotic supplementation in BD seem to be primarily mediated through reduction of peripheral inflammatory burden, including decreases in circulating IL-6 and TNFα, restoration of intestinal barrier integrity through tight junction protein recovery, and modulation of neurotransmitter precursor metabolism, especially those involved in the biosynthesis of GABA and serotonin 5-HT. However, there is still variation in the direct impact of probiotics on fundamental mood symptoms. Variations in probiotic strains, baseline gut microbial composition, host inflammatory condition, treatment duration, and patient heterogeneity are probably responsible for this variability. These findings highlight the significance of precision microbiome-based treatment approaches and imply that tailored probiotic treatments might be necessary to optimize clinical effectiveness in BD patients.

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation: A Potential Strategy for Restoring the Gut Microbial Ecosystem

FMT is thought to be a more all-encompassing approach to rebuilding the gut microbial community than probiotics, which mainly modulate the intestinal microbiota locally. Recently, FMT has been used to treat neuropsychiatric conditions in addition to gastrointestinal illnesses. FMT may reduce anxiety, gastrointestinal issues, and depressed symptoms in BD patients, according to preliminary clinical data; however, the total evidence is still small and clinical research is still in its early stages.85 Adjunctive FMT, when paired with standard pharmaceutical treatment, decreased depressed symptom scores, improved intestinal barrier function markers, and dramatically increased gut microbial diversity in a subset of patients, according to a recent double-blind randomized controlled experiment. Restoration of butyrate-producing bacteria like Faecalibacterium, reduction of LPS-producing microorganisms, reconstruction of SCFA metabolic networks, modulation of vagal afferent signaling, and attenuation of the peripheral inflammation–central immune activation axis are all likely multifactorial therapeutic mechanisms underlying these effects.86

These results are further supported mechanistically by preclinical research. FMT generated from healthy donors has been demonstrated to enhance hippocampus neurogenesis, restore hippocampal BDNF expression, and correct depression-like behaviors in chronic stress animal models. However, there are now a number of significant obstacles that prevent FMT from being widely used in treating mental illnesses. These include the absence of defined criteria for selecting donors, the ambiguity surrounding long-term safety, the significant interindividual variation in therapeutic response, and the comparatively little reductions in psychiatric symptoms that have been documented thus far.87 Consequently, FMT is more likely to become an important component of future precision microbiome-based therapies rather than a stand-alone treatment for BD.

Dietary Interventions: Modulating Gut Microbiota Through Metabolic Substrates

One of the most important environmental elements influencing the gut microbiota’s makeup and function is diet. Within weeks to months, different dietary habits can significantly change the inflammatory state and microbiota composition. The Mediterranean diet, high-fiber diets, low-refined sugar diets, and anti-inflammatory dietary patterns have drawn the most attention among dietary approaches studied in psychiatric diseases.88 Despite their variations, these dietary strategies have a number of things in common, such as consuming more dietary fiber to boost the production of SCFA, especially butyrate, and consuming less pro-inflammatory lipids and refined carbohydrates, which lowers metabolic endotoxemia, strengthens the intestinal barrier, and restricts the translocation of LPS throughout the body.

Dietary therapies have been shown in systematic reviews to greatly increase gut microbial α-diversity and, in multiple trials, to ameliorate the severity of depressed symptoms. SCFAs are important microbial metabolites that also support neuronal homeostasis by preventing HDAC activity, which increases BDNF expression and preserves microglial homeostasis. SCFAs may alter brain networks related to emotional regulation through several processes. These findings are further supported by experimental data from animal research. While high-fat diets cause gut dysbiosis, encourage microglial activation, and increase anxiety-like behaviors, high-fiber diets have been demonstrated to improve hippocampus neurogenesis and mitigate stress-induced behavioral abnormalities.89 Therefore, the ability of dietary interventions to reduce chronic low-grade inflammation, improve responsiveness to traditional pharmaceutical therapy, and sustainably promote the metabolism of beneficial microbes may be the source of their therapeutic potential in BD. According to available data, the intricate pathophysiological network underlying BD cannot be completely reversed by a single microbiota-targeted intervention. Rather, a tiered, integrated therapy model seems more biologically realistic. In this approach, probiotics replace functional bacterial populations, FMT helps rebuild the entire microbial ecosystem, and dietary interventions supply metabolic substrates that enable beneficial microbial metabolism. When combined, these complimentary strategies may stabilize neurotransmitter networks, lower systemic inflammation, and restore the homeostasis of the HPA axis. Therefore, rather than being isolated therapeutic modalities, microbiota-based interventions should be seen as upstream regulatory strategies that can simultaneously modulate several interconnected pathogenic pathways, such as inflammatory signaling IL-6/TNFα, neuroendocrine dysfunction, neurotransmitter systems 5-HT/DA/GABA, and neuroplasticity through regulation of BDNF expression and synaptic remodeling.

The precise stratification of BD subtypes based on gut microbial signatures, the identification of disease-specific microbial strains and metabolite targets, the development of individualized treatment approaches guided by inflammatory biomarkers, the establishment of standardized FMT protocols with long-term safety evaluation, and the optimization of combination strategies integrating microbiota-based therapies with mood stabilizers are all important priorities for future research. Establishing microbiome-based approaches as precision-medicine strategies for patients with BD will require these issues to be addressed.

Conclusion

A growing body of research in the fields of neuroimmunology, psycho-oncology, and gut microbiome suggests that the gut microbiota has functions that extend beyond the maintenance of gastrointestinal homeostasis. The intestinal microbiome influences host immune regulation, neurotransmitter metabolism, neuroendocrine function, and emotional processing through the intricate microbiota–gut–brain axis.

Genetic vulnerability and environmental variables interact to produce BD, a complex psychiatric illness marked by interwoven neurological, immunological, and metabolic problems. Among these variables, persistent low-grade inflammation and gut dysbiosis are increasingly recognized as potential contributors to neuroprogression and mood relapse. High-quality epidemiological data on the prevalence of bipolar disorder among cancer patients, or of cancer among patients with bipolar disorder, remain limited and inconsistent, highlighting the need for further population-based studies. This review suggests that chemotherapy-induced remodeling of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may represent an environmental factor that influences the clinical course of BD based on the evidence currently available. Chemotherapy-induced disruption of the intestinal microbial environment and epithelial integrity may contribute to reduced microbial diversity, impaired intestinal barrier function, and altered mucosal immune responses. Reduced generation of SCFAs, disruptions in bile acid metabolism, redirection of tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway, and disruption of T-cell immunological homeostasis are the results of these changes.

When taken as a whole, these modifications promote LPS translocation and the emergence of systemic inflammatory reactions. Through the systemic circulation, vagal afferent pathways, and immune cell trafficking, the resulting inflammatory mediators, microbial metabolites, and neuroendocrine signals may then influence the central nervous system, potentially contributing to neuroinflammatory signaling, microglial activation, HPA-axis dysregulation, and neurotransmitter imbalance. The prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus are key brain regions involved in emotional regulation that may be affected by these processes. This proposed mechanistic cascade overlaps with several neurobiological features reported in BD from a pathological standpoint. Manic and depressive episodes, as well as the neuroprogression of the disease, have been linked to elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, persistent microglial activation, circadian rhythm disruption, dysregulation of the tryptophan–kynurenine metabolic pathway, and abnormalities in dopaminergic reward circuitry. Therefore, the available evidence provides a biologically plausible rationale for investigating whether chemotherapy-induced remodeling of the microbiota–inflammation axis contributes to the clinical course of BD. Current evidence supports a biologically plausible mechanistic framework that provides a rationale for further clinical investigation, even though direct longitudinal data in patients with concomitant cancer and BD is still lacking.

Furthermore, through various biological processes, various kinds of chemotherapeutic drugs may contribute to this process. Alkylating agents mainly cause immunological remodeling and acute inflammatory amplification, which may increase vulnerability to manic episodes. With molecular characteristics that significantly overlap with bipolar depression and neuroprogression, antimetabolite agents are more strongly linked to neurotoxicity and cognitive impairment. On the other hand, chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy and prolonged neuroinflammation are two ways that plant alkaloids may affect emotional stability. These pharmacological differences suggest that future psychiatric care may benefit from risk assessment tailored to the specific chemotherapy regimen. From a clinical perspective, chemotherapy-induced microbiota–inflammation dysregulation may be associated with poorer adherence to anticancer treatment, cognitive decline, reduced effectiveness of mood-stabilizing therapy, and an increased risk of recurrent mood episodes. As a result, these impacts may have a negative impact on the prognosis for cancer as well as mental health consequences. Psychiatric care may need to be integrated into comprehensive cancer management for patients with comorbid BD and cancer, although the strength of this recommendation is limited by the current absence of direct longitudinal evidence.

Importantly, approaches targeting the gut microbiota–inflammation axis may represent a potential direction for future precision medicine research. Strategies such as probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, dietary modifications, and anti-inflammatory agents may modulate microbial composition and inflammatory activity, although current evidence derives largely from preclinical studies and small clinical trials. These methods may potentially be useful supplements to traditional mood-stabilizing medicines while also enhancing chemotherapy tolerance and psychiatric outcomes, even if the majority of the information now available originates from preclinical research and rather modest clinical trials.

The idea that chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis and inflammatory remodeling should not be viewed as solely gastrointestinal side effects of anticancer treatment is generally supported by the current data. Instead, through the microbiota–gut–brain axis, these changes may influence neurobiological processes relevant to BD and potentially contribute to its clinical course.

This concept is supported by a biologically plausible and increasingly coherent mechanistic framework, but its translational relevance remains to be established through prospective mechanistic and long-term clinical research. Future research should encourage greater interdisciplinary collaboration among microbiome science, psychiatry, oncology, gastroenterology, and nutrition. Incorporating routine evaluation of inflammatory biomarkers, gut microbiota composition, and psychiatric symptoms may be considered in future clinical protocols, but its utility remains to be established. If future studies confirm the clinical relevance of the microbiota–gut–brain axis in this context, establishing an integrated research and clinical framework may facilitate a transition from disease-centered treatment toward more comprehensive health management, with the potential to improve long-term outcomes and quality of life.
