Supporting sexual assault disclosure: a qualitative study of women's experiences and preferences in gynaecological and midwifery care in the Netherlands
Authors: Nienke S.W.M. Bosma, Hannah W. de Klerk, Iva A.E. Bicanic, Lisa Zuidema, Jaklien Leemans, Jacques W.M. Maas, Marieke Dewitte, Fleur Aben, Marlies Y. Bongers, Peggy M.A.J. Geomini, Janneke van 't Hooft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Sexual assault (SA) can have serious consequences for physical, mental, social, and reproductive health. Worldwide, about one in three women report experiencing physical SA. Despite this high prevalence, previous research shows only a minority of healthcare providers (HCPs) consistently inquire about SA during initial consultations.Objective: This study aims to establish how gynaecologists and midwives can best address SA by exploring women's preferences with disclosing their experience of SA.Method: An explorative qualitative research design was used with a web-based questionnaire among women who disclosed their SA to a gynaecologist or midwife. The questionnaire was distributed via the Dutch Sexual Assault Center, social media channels and outpatient clinics. A reflexive thematic analysis was conducted.Results: Based on 147 responses, we generated three themes: (1) women need empathy and understanding of trauma response in HCPs. This entailed a non-emotional stance, authenticity, validation after disclosure and sufficient time for a conversation. (2) Women need HCPs to take the lead in addressing SA. This entailed inquiring about SA proactively, informing women about possible connection between SA and symptoms, addressing needs and options, and proving follow-up care. (3) Women need control over the narrative and care pathway. This entailed control over the level of detail women wish to share, the terminology used, medical records, and who is present during consultations.Conclusions: The results can be used to inform education and trauma-informed guidelines on how gynaecologists and midwives can best support SA disclosures. This way, negative responses and re-traumatisation can be avoided and the ongoing recovery process of SA survivors can be supported in gynaecological and midwifery care.

Introduction

Approximately one in three women1 worldwide experience sexual assault (SA) (WHO, 2018). SA is defined as any form of sexually transgressive behaviour by witnessing or undergoing any form of sexually transgressive behaviour (this definition includes not only rape or sexual assault, but also other forms of sexual harassment)2 (Van Berlo & Van Beek, 2015).

SA can have both immediate and long-term physical and mental health consequences. In the immediate aftermath of SA, survivors are at risk for sexually transmitted infections and pregnancy, sustain injuries and can experience acute stress symptoms such as nightmares and hypervigilance (Basile et al., 2021; Jaffe et al., 2019). In the long-term, these acute stress symptoms can persist and develop into various mental health problems such as depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, anxiety, fear and suicidal ideation (Basile et al., 2021). Women with an SA experience often develop gynaecological symptoms, such as urinary incontinence, heavy menstrual bleeding and painful menstruations (Hassam et al., 2020) that lead them to seek gynaecological care. Somatic complications can arise during the perinatal period as well, such as antepartum bleeding and preterm birth (Brunton & Dryer, 2022; Gisladottir et al., 2016; Henriksen et al., 2013). Furthermore, a history of sexual violence is associated with mental health problems during the perinatal period such as fear of childbirth, dissociation during childbirth and prenatal and postpartum depression (Henriksen et al., 2013; Lukasse et al., 2011; Wosu et al., 2015). Moreover, in gynaecological, obstetric and midwifery care there is a risk of re-traumatisation when the effects of SA history are not acknowledged or taken into account (Polite et al., 2019; Ward, 2020).

While women's SA history is highly relevant to provide patient-centered and effective gynaecological and midwifery care that avoids misdiagnosis, unnecessary treatments, re-traumatisation, and referral to mental health services if necessary, healthcare providers (HCPs),3 are often unaware of patients’ SA history (Azizzada et al., 2023; Teunissen et al., 2016). It is therefore important that HCPs address SA with their patients (Ryan et al., 2016; Wijma et al., 2003). Previous research showed that there is large variation among gynaecologists and midwives in addressing SA history (Bosma et al.; de Klerk et al., 2022). Only a minority of HCPs consistently inquire about SA history during initial consultations (Bosma et al.). HCPs indicate that they need more training to improve communication and referral skills to confidently address SA in the consultation room (Bosma et al.; de Klerk et al., 2022).

When women disclose SA to a trusted individual who responds sensitively, it can have a positive impact on women by feeling less alone and (re)establishing trust in others (Draucker et al., 2009). In healthcare, delayed disclosure of SA history can have similar positive effects when HCPs respond sensitively to the disclosure (Lanthier et al., 2018). On the other hand, HCPs negative reactions after a disclosure, such as victim blaming, rejection and silencing can lead to increase in psychological and physical distress (Dworkin et al., 2019; Elliott et al., 2022; Lanthier et al., 2018; Swingle et al., 2016). Moreover, women value clear information and explanations when receiving healthcare after SA, and want a sense of control over the examinations conducted and how they are conducted (Fehler-Cabral et al., 2011; Rudolfsson et al., 2025).

Gynaecologists and midwives can play a crucial role in addressing women's negative sexual history by facilitating a safe and supportive environment. While the barriers and facilitators of gynaecologists and midwives in addressing SA are extensively described in literature (Bosma et al.; Aromaa et al., 2023; McCool et al., 2016), there is a noticeable lack of data regarding women's own preferences. This information is crucial to inform professionals how to best perform screening for SA in a trauma-sensitive manner. Therefore, the objective of this study is to establish how HCPs can best address SA by exploring women's preferences for disclosing SA to gynaecologists and midwives.

Method

Setting

In the Dutch healthcare system most gynaecological complaints are first assessed and managed within primary care. More complex cases are referred to a gynaecologist. Maternity care pathways are determined by assessment of women's pregnancy risk. Women with low-risk pregnancies and births are cared for by community midwives. Women with intermediate or high-risk pregnancies and births are cared for by hospital-based midwives and obstetricians. Gynaecological guidelines do not integrate screening for SA history. Obstetric and midwifery guidelines advise screening for SA history as part of general history taking, but do not offer guidance on the conditions or manner of screening. SA disclosures are noted in the medical file and remain confidential. Only when ongoing violence within a domestic situation is suspected, HCPs have an obligation to report this to the Dutch national advice and reporting centre for domestic violence and child abuse (Veiligthuis.nl, 2026). For pregnant women with psychiatric problems, including a history of trauma, integrated care is available where psychiatrists, obstetricians and pediatricians work together to form the best clinical care pathway for the patient and her child.

In the Netherlands, regional Sexual Assault Centers (SACs) provide coordinated, survivor-centred care through multidisciplinary collaboration between doctors, nurses, psychologists, social workers, lawyers, and police services (Covers et al., 2022). SACs provide acute care following SA, but also offer psychological support or refer patients with physical complaints persisting longer than 7 days. The medical team providing acute trauma care consists of at least one physician who is specialised in infectious disease or gynaecology, an emergency room physician, or a paediatrician. One day after admission to the SAC, (i.e. day one), victims are contacted by a casemanager via telephone or face-to-face. Casemanagers do not ask for details of the assault or for the victims to express their feelings, in order to refrain from psychological debriefing and its potentially harmful effects. Patients can also contact SACs anonymously by phone or chat for non-acute assistance.

Research design

We conducted an explorative qualitative study with an open-ended online questionnaire that was developed specifically for the current study. The questionnaire was developed through an iterative process of three cycles. The authors, with expertise in gynaecology (NB, JM, MB, PG, JH), midwifery (HK) trauma-psychology (IB), qualitative research (LZ) and sexology (JM, MD), developed a first draft, that was adjusted considering the feedback of a patient advisory group (five women with lived experience of SA). This questionnaire was pilot tested by four people including members of the patient advisory group resulting in modifications to the wording of some questions. In the final version of the questionnaire (Supplement 1), we used the term ‘sexual abuse’ which was defined for the respondents as: ‘all sexual acts that someone has to perform, undergo or witness without wanting to do so’ (Center DSA, 2025). The questionnaire began with a main question: You are participating in this study because you have previously discussed with a gynaecologist or midwife that you have experienced sexual abuse. Can you describe what this conversation was like for you? Everything you remember and how you feel about it is of importance.This approach allowed respondents to freely express what mattered most to them about their experience. This main question was followed by a series of six prompts about specific areas of interest: positives and negatives during the conservation, response of the HCP, terminology, reporting of SA in the medical record, opportunities for improvement, and consequences of disclosure. Respondents could indicate whether they wanted to elaborate on these topics and if so, describe their answer in an open text box. In order to ensure the anonymity of the respondents, the number of demographic questions was limited to age at the time of filling out the questionnaire and age at the time they disclosed sexual violence to an HCP. This way, the data could be contextualised in time. Furthermore, respondents were asked about migration background, in order to determine to what extent the data represents different cultural backgrounds. Finally, respondents were invited to provide feedback about the questionnaire. The questionnaire was available in Dutch and English. This research is presented following the Standards for reporting qualitative research (SRQR) checklist (Supplement 2) (O’Brien et al., 2014).

You are participating in this study because you have previously discussed with a gynaecologist or midwife that you have experienced sexual abuse. Can you describe what this conversation was like for you? Everything you remember and how you feel about it is of importance.

Population and data collection

The online questionnaire was available in Enalyzer (2000) and was distributed via the social media channels, Instagram and Facebook, of the Dutch Sexual Assault Center and through the LinkedIn pages of the project group members. Respondents were also recruited through flyers in the waiting room of Máxima Medical Center (MC) and during gynaecological consultations. The promotional text and introductory text of the questionnaire stated the inclusion criteria; (1) aged 18 years or older, (2) disclosed a form of SA to a gynaecologist or midwife and (3) ability to communicate in Dutch or English language. It was recommended at the start of the questionnaire that the questionnaire can be filled in by company of a trusted person, thus ensuring that assistance would be available should any unpleasant feelings or thoughts arise. Furthermore, advice to contact the general practitioner for necessary aftercare as well as links to the Dutch Sexual Assault Center were provided. Data collection took place from 1 October through 18 October 2024. In our protocol we initially expected sufficient information power (Malterud et al., 2016) with 50 completed questionnaires and aimed for a collection time of one month. However, due to an unexpectedly high response rate in the first days we decided to close the questionnaire after exceeding this intended number three-fold on 18 October.

Data analysis

The data was analysed following the six steps of reflexive thematic analysis as developed by Braun and Clarke (2021). We chose this method since it allowed us to explore, analyse and interpret patterns from the data in a systematic and critical way. We systematically documented the coding and theme development at each stage, ensuring the evolution of themes was both clear and traceable. This approach not only fostered research rigour, but also enabled the demonstration of process and dependability.

The first step of the analysis (familiarisation) was performed independently by NB, HK and JH, who read the responses several times while making notes and discussed their initial insights together. NB continued with the second step, coding the responses, using Atlas.it (Webversion 9.11) (Atlas.ti, 2025). Coding was done inductively. Codes represented factors that either facilitated or impeded a positive experience of disclosure of SA. Since we had expected that 50 responses would provide sufficient information power to answer the research question, HK and JH reviewed the coding after NB coded the first 50 responses. We agreed that the responses provided sufficient depth and complexity to answer the research question. We subsequently decided to analyse the full dataset to ensure a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the range and variation in participants’ experiences. At this stage, we also discussed discrepancies in the codes and the perspectives from the different researchers were incorporated in the coding framework before coding the remaining responses. During the third step (generating initial themes) NB developed candidate themes that transcended the distinction between positive and negative factors. These themes were reviewed and fine-tuned together with HK, JH and MD during the fourth step of analysis. The fifth step of the analysis (refining, defining and naming themes) was performed with all authors. Perspectives of their different academic disciplines were incorporated and a process of reflection was undertaken in which the authors considered their own views and existing biases. Writing was considered an integral part of the analysis during the sixth step and was performed by NB and HK. The boundaries and relationships between the themes were finalised during this last step. As the manuscript was being finalised, feedback and assistance with the interpretation of results was provided by three individuals from the patient advisory group.

Funding and ethical approval

This study is a part of the ‘My Sexual Health Matters’ project funded by ‘Stichting Achmea Slachtoffer en Samenleving’ (Caswell et al., 2019) and was approved by the local feasibility committee of Máxima Medical Center on 21st of August 2024 (N24.057). The Ethical Review Board confirmed that the Medical Research involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) does not apply to this study. Replying to the questionnaire implied written consent to participate in the study, which was made clear to the respondents at the start of the questionnaire. No personal data such as IP address or email address were collected to ensure anonymity of respondents. In addition, to ensure anonymity, one of the authors checked the transcripts for personally identifying data and verified whether it was necessary to pseudonymize information, but no personally identifying data were found.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 147 women responded to the questionnaire. The median age of the respondents was 40 years (IQR 33–51 years). The median number of years since SA was discussed with HCP was 5 years (IQR 2–14.75 years). Six respondents (4%) indicated a migration background (India, Lithuania, Greece, United States of America, or mixed including Togo, Lebanon, Ghana, and United Kingdom). Most respondents described a disclosure of a SA to a gynaecologist (57.8%), followed by a disclosure to a midwife (15.6%) or to both (12.9%) (see Table 1). Women's preferences with disclosing sexual assault to healthcare providers

Based on the open-ended responses, we generated three themes that contribute to a positive experience of disclosure of SA to a gynaecologist or midwife: (1) women need empathy and understanding of trauma response in HCPs, (2) women need HCPs to take the lead in addressing sexual assault and (3) women need control over the narrative and care pathway. Respondents described similar needs from gynaecologists and midwives and had both positive and negative experiences with both professions.

Theme 1: women need empathy and understanding of trauma response in healthcare providers

HCPs verbal and nonverbal demeanour, in terms of attitude, tone and manner of behaving, during consultations was fundamental to how the respondents experienced their disclosure of SA. Empathy and an understanding of internal trauma responses were considered to contribute to a safe space for disclosure.

Empathy

Many respondents simply stated that they required an HCP to be empathetic, while others elaborated more on what constitutes an empathetic attitude. First, an empathetic attitude required a warm but non-emotional stance, meaning that HCPs handled their own emotions in response to the disclosure in a balanced way while still showing understanding: ‘Stay more or less neutral - you really don't have to be a robot, a little compassion is nice, but strong emotions do not help your patient’ (R2) and ‘The questions were asked with empathy, but without emotions, which made it easier to talk about’ (R12).

Secondly, authenticity was considered an important aspect of empathy: ‘She dares to be vulnerable and say that something affects her and that she needs to let it sink in for a moment’ (R16) and I can't stress enough how important the first glance of the person sitting across from you is when you uncover the secret of sexual abuse. Her look was pure and sincere without judgement before she said anything, allowing her words to touch my heart. (R40)

I can't stress enough how important the first glance of the person sitting across from you is when you uncover the secret of sexual abuse. Her look was pure and sincere without judgement before she said anything, allowing her words to touch my heart. (R40)

The third important aspect of an empathetic attitude was acknowledgement or validation after a disclosure: ‘Again, the gynaecologist fell silent for a moment and complimented me on my openness, immediately believed what I said and let the expression linger for a moment’ (R133). Contrarily, descriptions of HCPs’ own interpretations, judgements or downplaying of experiences lacked empathy: ‘She thought that was the past, that I should let go and just undress. After all, it was her job and she is not surprised by anything’ (R106) or ‘I was abused several times as a child. And then he replied: there is not much difference in itself between receiving a punch or experiencing sexual abuse. Both are forms of violence’ (R3).

Lastly, taking sufficient time for a conversation about SA was considered crucial to an empathetic attitude: ‘Take time. That would make it easier for me as the care provider would then seem more approachable’ (R6). Respondents acknowledged that sufficient time was not always immediately available or realistic and appreciated the offer to make a new appointment for the examination or to discuss their needs further.

Knowledge of trauma response

HCPs often failed to observe or take into account respondents’ internal trauma responses. Many respondents were ashamed of their experience of SA and consequently had a negative self-image. This affected both the interaction with their HCP and their interpretation of the interaction with their HCP. Respondents were often unable to communicate their needs: ‘My own inability to communicate clearly and therefore making it as small as possible (shame) does not really help’ (R112). Respondents feared to become a burden to their HCP and attempted to make the experience easier for them, at the expense of their own needs: ‘I said I had experienced it, but I lied that it didn't bother me anymore because I didn't want to make her feel uncomfortable’ (R51). Signs of discomfort of the HCP reinforced the respondents’ negative thoughts and feelings: ‘Because there is no response, my head “turns on”. Thoughts like, “you see, I’m overreacting”’ (R20) and ‘Doctors often fail to realise that victims of sexual abuse can sense very clearly how the doctor feels about them, and certainly about their non-verbal behaviour. This often leads to mistrust, fear, aloofness, etc.’ (R119).

Respondents’ shame and negative self-image led them to minimise their own experience, limit their communication about their needs and be hypersensitive to the (non-verbal) communication of their HCP. ‘Because there is no response, my mind “switches on”. Thoughts like, “see, I'm just being dramatic”, “it's normal”, etc.’ (R20) Importantly, these trauma responses are invisible to HCPs. Another important invisible trauma response for HCPs, was the aftermath of the consultations for some respondents: As soon as I was out of the room, the impact came. Flashbacks, switching [A sudden shift from one dissociative state or identity to another.], etc. It took more than 2.5 h before I was okay enough to get into my car again. At home, a pseudo epileptic seizure also occurred. (R50)

As soon as I was out of the room, the impact came. Flashbacks, switching [A sudden shift from one dissociative state or identity to another.], etc. It took more than 2.5 h before I was okay enough to get into my car again. At home, a pseudo epileptic seizure also occurred. (R50)

Several respondents described noticeable reactions after disclosing SA, such as dissociations, physical or mental re-experiencing, tension and fears: ‘Tension, re-experiencing and dissociation are still there; before, during and after these examinations, check-ups and treatments’. (R109)

Theme 2: women need healthcare providers to take the lead in addressing sexual assault

Most respondents preferred HCPs to address SA proactively by taking the lead in inquiring about SA, explaining the potential association between gynaecological symptoms or obstetric complications and SA, offering specific treatment options and providing a follow-up.

Inquiring about SA proactively

Most respondents preferred HCPs to proactively address SA, thereby avoiding the need for them to do so themselves: ‘Especially that the healthcare provider asks a question about this as part of standard procedure, which is easier than bringing it up yourself’ (R6). Respondents who were able to reply to a question regarding SA in an advance questionnaire, often experienced this as positive: ‘It was already a question on the standard questionnaire I had to fill in. To this I answered yes, so it was a nice opening for that in the conservation’ (R73). Most respondents preferred to be asked about SA prior to commencing gynaecological examination: ‘A clear separation between conversation and examination: first the conversation, which includes asking about negative sexual experiences and reassuring me about the examination’ (R111). Subsequently, when HCPs screen for SA, they should not simply ‘tick a box’, but rather engage in a conversation after a disclosure: ‘So, there was no conversation really. Just a question. And that question, the way she asked it and the non-response, felt stigmatizing and even transgressive’ (R138), and ‘I indicated with both pregnancies that I had experienced sexual abuse ( … ) with the gynaecologist in the standard questionnaire. It was not really considered at the time and I did not dare to elaborate on it much further’ (R9).

Information about a possible connection between SA and somatic symptoms

After disclosure of SA, respondents found it beneficial when acknowledgement and explanations are provided regarding the association between somatic symptoms and their trauma: ‘It was nice to get confirmation that it was right that I named it. I felt relieved that my complaints were not weird, but logical after the abuse. That there was an explanation’ (R25). However, some respondents recounted that any correlation was dismissed: I have spoken to several gynaecologists because I have a lot of problems with my menstruation. But I am never taken seriously. Even when I tell them what happened, they shrug their shoulders and do not elaborate. I think that is a shame because I have the feeling that my problems are partly caused by the abuse. (R44)

I have spoken to several gynaecologists because I have a lot of problems with my menstruation. But I am never taken seriously. Even when I tell them what happened, they shrug their shoulders and do not elaborate. I think that is a shame because I have the feeling that my problems are partly caused by the abuse. (R44)

Addressing needs and options

After a disclosure, respondents wanted HCPs to inquire about their needs and offer a range of options for their care pathway or specific procedure: ‘She asked if there was anything she could take into account and together we thought it would be good if she would name what she was doing step by step’ (R48), and ‘And that if I say stop it will be stopped’ (R131). Other examples are the possibility of undergoing an examination with anaesthesia or scheduling a subsequent gynaecological examination at a more convenient time for the respondent. As many respondents did not know the specifics of care pathways, procedures or childbirth, or found it hard to formulate their needs, they needed the HCP to guide them through their options: It would have been a lot better -in retrospect- if she had been able to take a moment to consider the possible consequences of pregnancy and childbirth – after all, during your first pregnancy you have no idea what to expect. (R79)

It would have been a lot better -in retrospect- if she had been able to take a moment to consider the possible consequences of pregnancy and childbirth – after all, during your first pregnancy you have no idea what to expect. (R79)

Providing follow-up

Lastly, some respondents would have liked their HCP to follow-up after a disclosure, as many respondents were overwhelmed by the effects it had on them. They appreciated time to evaluate and discuss care afterwards: ‘For me, the post-delivery talk about the birth was good to handle the nightmares and re-experiencing. Maybe this should be a standard procedure if requested or deemed necessary’ (R6). Some respondents noted that in follow-up appointments, SA can become ‘an elephant in the room’ (R42) if it is left unmentioned.

Theme 3: women need control over the narrative and care pathway

Respondents emphasised the importance of empowering women by ensuring they have autonomy over their disclosure narrative and subsequent care. While they valued an initial guiding role from HCPs, it was essential that final decisions remained with the woman once they were fully informed about their options.

Control over level of detail

After disclosing SA, most respondents did not want to elaborate on the details of their experience. Follow-up questions about impact and care needs were experienced positively, but questions about details of the abuse were experienced as invasive: She asked how often and what happened. (…) I cannot and dare not give detailed information, because it sounds so intense to my ears. I cannot say it (…). I did feel guilty and ashamed after the conversation. Because I said it out loud and told it. I was shocked by myself and it felt as if I had acted too quickly and had not thought about it. With that I emotionally give away the control over my story. (R13)

She asked how often and what happened. (…) I cannot and dare not give detailed information, because it sounds so intense to my ears. I cannot say it (…). I did feel guilty and ashamed after the conversation. Because I said it out loud and told it. I was shocked by myself and it felt as if I had acted too quickly and had not thought about it. With that I emotionally give away the control over my story. (R13)

Control over terminology

Furthermore, respondents have differing, yet strong preferences for what words are used to describe their trauma. Some terms used by HCPs did not reflect their experience at all and were at times even considered offensive. Most respondents noted that ‘sexual abuse’ best encompassed their experience. Others considered this to be overly confrontational or associated it with childhood abuse: ‘I find the word abuse scary, because I'm afraid that they'll think it happened to me when I was a young child, and that's not the case’ (R4). For some, negative sexual experience or sexual harassment felt more suitable: ‘Unpleasant sexual experience is enough for me’ (R92). On the other hand, respondents often commented that the use of the word ‘experience’ suggested a subjective experience and dismissed their trauma: That word completely misses the point in my opinion. In my case, I was sexually abused by my father from the age of 4–13. It is not an ‘experience’ because that is subjective; it is abuse and in the highest category. (R113)Respondents also had different opinions about using ‘rape’ or ‘sexual violence’. Some thought it correctly described their trauma and should be named as such: ‘I notice that doctors often sugar coat the subject. This is counterproductive, because it reinforces the re-experiencing. Call a spade a spade (rape), that leaves my head no room to fill it with unnecessary images’ (R59). Others thought these terms are too confrontational and triggering in itself. Many respondents suggested that HCPs mirror the terminology used by their patients.

That word completely misses the point in my opinion. In my case, I was sexually abused by my father from the age of 4–13. It is not an ‘experience’ because that is subjective; it is abuse and in the highest category. (R113)

Control over medical record

Respondents wanted to know and be given a final say in who could access the sensitive information about SA in their medical record. Some were unpleasantly surprised about who was able to access their information: I don't really know what was reported. Also, I found out later that a medical record can be viewed by every department. That feels very unsafe afterwards (…). I might not have shared this experience if I had known that the ear, nose, throat specialist could also read it, so to speak. (R35)Others were frustrated or confused by the lack of documentation: When I was in the emergency room two years later and an internal echo had to be made, there were suddenly medical students present. I panicked, started to dissociate and that felt unsafe. In retrospect, that could have been prevented by adequate documentation. (R85)

I don't really know what was reported. Also, I found out later that a medical record can be viewed by every department. That feels very unsafe afterwards (…). I might not have shared this experience if I had known that the ear, nose, throat specialist could also read it, so to speak. (R35)

When I was in the emergency room two years later and an internal echo had to be made, there were suddenly medical students present. I panicked, started to dissociate and that felt unsafe. In retrospect, that could have been prevented by adequate documentation. (R85)

Control about who is present

Respondents expressed that they would like to decide who attends the consultation: ‘I indicated that I wanted as few people in the room as possible during delivery’ (R136). Respondents wanted a choice as to whether an intern is present at the consultation, the possibility to bring someone along with them, a choice regarding the gender of the HCP or the option to see the same HCP at follow-up consults. Respondents had good and bad experiences with both male and female HCPs. Some described that a good experience with a male HCP was particularly beneficial to their recovery: ‘I don't know if it changed the experience of giving birth (…) but the fact that a man treated me and my body so sensitively and respectfully was a huge corrective experience’ (R127).

Gaining confidence through control

While respondents were asked about the conversation about SA with their HCP, many also commented on the experiences they had during treatments or examinations. Respondents often indicated that their confidence in the HCP increased when they felt in control during the examinations (for example indicating when they are ready for the next step of an examination) and could rely on the agreements they had made with the HCPs beforehand: ‘Doing what they say they will do’ (R19).

Discussion

Summary

In this study, we aimed to establish how gynaecologists and midwives can best support disclosure of a history of SA. The accounts of 147 women who disclosed a history of SA to a gynaecologist or midwife, enabled us to formulate three themes that describe women's specific needs when they disclose SA in gynaecological and midwifery care that help foster a positive disclosure experience. First, respondents prefer an HCP to demonstrate empathy and understanding of trauma responses. Empathy entailed a non-emotional stance, authenticity, validation after disclosure and sufficient time for a conversation. Secondly, respondents prefer the HCP to proactively inquire about SA, giving explanations about possible connection between sexual assault and somatic symptoms, addressing needs and options, and follow-up care after a disclosure. Lastly, respondents prefer to be given autonomy and ownership over their narrative and subsequent care. This entailed control over the level of detail women wish to share, the terminology used, medical records, and who is present during consultations.

Interpretation

Our findings show that while many women have positive experiences with empathetic and knowledgeable HCPs, they also encountered negative responses and insufficient trauma expertise in HCPs after disclosing SA. For example, HCPs judged or downplayed aspects of the SA, leading to victim blaming and feeling unsafe. Literature shows that these negative reactions can lead to increase in psychological complaints (Dworkin et al., 2019; Elliott et al., 2022; Lanthier et al., 2018; Swingle et al., 2016). Also, the respondents indicate that the HCP did not consider the potential impact of disclosure after the consult. These examples indicate a need to improve trauma-informed care in Dutch gynaecology and midwifery practice. A trauma-informed approach in healthcare is distinct from trauma services that treat trauma. It refers to incorporating trauma-specific care pathways and key trauma principles in the organisation. This approach recognises the widespread impact of trauma and integrates trauma knowledge into policies, procedures and practices to avoid re-traumatisation (“Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (US)”, 2014).

Trauma-informed care frameworks emphasise the need to educate HCPs on trauma and have clear guidelines and care pathways available (Caswell et al., 2023; Law et al., 2021). This should prevent a blank recommendation to screen for SA from becoming a ‘tick box exercise’ without adequate follow-up. Balanced recommendations can be found in different resources that provide in-depth guidance on the road to implement a trauma-informed practice in perinatal care (Hutschemaekers et al., 2019; Law et al., 2021). One of their recommendations is that obstetricians-gynaecologist implement universal screening for current trauma and a history of trauma. Furthermore, the NHS recommends that the medical education system should provide balanced training on the risk of re-traumatisation,. However, these frameworks lack specifically tailored care pathways that HCPs and women can proceed with after disclosure. Our findings on the importance of robust referral pathways are in line with existing literature on trauma screening in healthcare (Callanan et al., 2025; Caswell et al., 2023) (1, 2) In addition, our findings show that even if screening is performed in a trauma-informed manner including referral pathways, when HCPs do not follow-up on the disclosure in subsequent consultations, this can also lead to feelings of stigma. Therefore, trauma-informed SA screening guidelines should include both referral pathways and caregiver follow-up to avoid further harm and re-traumatisation. HCPs should be aware that disclosure of SA is part of, and sometimes the first step in, an ongoing process of recovery (ACOG, 2021; Hutschemaekers et al., 2019).

Lastly, our findings showed some specific areas of attention for HCPs to address SA in a trauma-sensitive manner. These findings should be incorporated in guidelines and education. Reducing power imbalances between HCPs and women is a key aspect of trauma-informed care (“Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (US)”, 2014) and shared decision making should be adopted (Caswell et al., 2023). Our findings showed some important areas that women want control over. First, when they disclose SA, most women prefer not to share details and therefore HCPs should be careful with asking about specificities of their experience of SA. Trauma can be understood through the ‘three E's’; an event or period that is experienced as harmful or life-threatening and has lasting adverse effects (“Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (US)”, 2014). While a previous paper on integrating trauma-informed care into maternity care practice stated the importance of knowing as much as possible about all three E’s (Sperlich et al., 2017), our findings suggest that it is imperative that HCPs shift the focus of their follow-up questions from the details of the event to the women's personal experiences and the long-term effects on lives with psychological or somatic symptoms. This shift should give care providers the necessary information to assess care needs, while taking into account the triggering effect that talking about details of the traumatising event can have. Other examples are that HCPs should adopt women's terminology where possible and which HCPs (such as medical students) are present and the proceedings of the examination. When women are in control, they gain autonomy and trust in the HCP and the care they receive (Caswell et al., 2019, 2025; van Rosmalen-Nooijens et al., 2017).

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is the large number of respondents who recounted both positive and negative disclosure experiences as well as experiences with both gynaecologists and midwives, which provided a wide range of insights into the topic. Our study design, which used an anonymous online questionnaire, provided an accessible way for a large number of respondents to answer. It was possible to answer openly regarding all positive and negative aspects of the disclosure in an accessible manner, without speaking face-to-face to a researcher or a possible tracing back to the place or person of their disclosure. While this method did not allow us to ask further questions, the broad open question combined with the large number of respondents provided sufficiently rich data. Furthermore, women with lived experience of SA were involved in the study design, enhancing the study's relevance and sensitivity.

This study also has some limitations. First, the sample included very few participants with a migration background and lacked any of the largest ethnic minority groups in the Netherlands, which limits the generalizability of findings, as different dynamics could potentially play a role. For example, people from minority groups often experience discrimination and experience disbelief, and are consequently not believed about SA more often as a result. This may require a different way of addressing SA with people from minority groups, especially when the HCP is from the majority group (ACOG, 2021). In addition, the transferability of our findings may be influenced by differences between healthcare systems across countries. For example, a transfer of care between GP and gynaecologist or midwife and obstetrician may cause particular problems with documentation and building relationships that would not occur in countries with direct access to specialised care. Future research could further explore disclosure preferences in different healthcare contexts and among diverse populations. Second, the median time between the disclosure conversation and completing the questionnaire was 5 years, which may have introduced recall bias. However, this study focused on women's preferences based on their lived experiences. In this context, the details of what happened are less important; instead, the focus lies on how women experienced the disclosure of SA and what they perceived as helpful or unhelpful during these interactions. However, we did not ask participants how much time passed between the SA and the conversation with the HCP. This time gap may have affected the way the HCP responded and what help was offered and should be taken into account in future research. Third, researcher's background, assumptions, and interaction style may influence how questions are asked. We tried to minimise this impact by including several different perspective from healthcare professionals in various fields (gynaecology, obstetrics, midwifery, sexology, psychology, and methodology) and also including several patients with a history of SA reviewing the questions asked and the final report. Lastly, respondents were mainly recruited through social media, which might have led to selection bias. Followers of social media about SA are potentially more aware of the impact of SA and have, in a way, started their processing or recovering. It is therefore possible that we have missed a group of women that are in a different phase of recovery. For this group, their preferences for disclosure of SA may be different, but this is difficult to investigate.

Conclusion

Our qualitative study showed that while many women have positive experiences with empathetic and knowledgeable HCPs, some encountered negative responses and insufficient trauma expertise after disclosing SA. To help HCPs with addressing SA in a trauma-sensitive manner and avoid further harm or re-traumatisation, trauma-informed guidelines with specific care pathways and training should be developed, tailored and evaluated. Particular attention should be given to a proactive role of HCPs in addressing SA and offering control to women over their SA narrative and care pathway. There should be concrete guidance on follow-up after disclosure, with clear options on care pathways alongside the needs of the individual women. This study offers a concrete contribution to how HCPs and systems can support the ongoing recovery process following SA.
