Biologics for Children and Adolescents with Asthma: A Systematic Review and Exploratory Indirect Comparative Analysis
Authors: Mengwei Wu, Haoyuan Li, He Geng, Peize Liu, Chao Wang, Ling Zhou, Na Liu, Xu Li, Lili Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiologics targeting type 2 inflammation have expanded treatment options for children and adolescents with uncontrolled asthma, but pediatric randomized evidence is limited and direct head-to-head comparisons are unavailable. We reappraised comparative efficacy and safety evidence and the certainty of current indirect estimates.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and public trial sources through May 25, 2026, for randomized controlled trials of biologics in children or adolescents with asthma. The primary efficacy outcome was annualized exacerbation rate (AER); safety outcomes were adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs). Between-biologic effects were explored using Bucher adjusted indirect comparisons through placebo as the common comparator. Transitivity was assessed qualitatively, and evidence certainty was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.ResultsThree placebo-controlled pediatric trials evaluating omalizumab, dupilumab, and mepolizumab contributed to the primary analysis. Each biologic reduced AER versus placebo. The adjusted indirect estimate for dupilumab versus mepolizumab was below 1, but this finding was derived from a sparse, clinically heterogeneous evidence structure and was considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of superiority. Omalizumab and dupilumab were not associated with a clear increase in overall AEs, whereas mepolizumab had higher broad AE reporting in MUPPITS-2. SAE estimates were imprecise. Certainty was moderate for biologics versus placebo on AER, low for between-biologic comparisons, and low to very low for safety outcomes.ConclusionRandomized pediatric evidence supports biologics for reducing exacerbation burden versus placebo. Current adjusted indirect estimates do not establish a biologic hierarchy. Phenotype-stratified head-to-head pediatric trials remain necessary.

Introduction

Asthma is one of the most common chronic inflammatory airway diseases in children and adolescents and remains a major contributor to global morbidity, with current wheeze reported in 9.1% of children and 11.0% of adolescents worldwide.1,2 Childhood asthma is heterogeneous, and allergic sensitization, inflammatory biomarkers, environmental exposures, and respiratory infections can contribute to variable symptom expression and exacerbation risk.3 Allergic rhinitis and rhinosinusitis commonly coexist with pediatric asthma and may affect disease control, clinical course, and treatment decisions.4,5 Inhaled corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of controller treatment, although parental concerns about corticosteroids can undermine adherence in some families.3,6,7 Despite optimized guideline-based treatment, a clinically important subgroup continues to experience recurrent exacerbations, impaired quality of life, emergency healthcare use, and substantial treatment burden.3,6 Severe or exacerbation-prone asthma during childhood may also adversely affect lung-function development and long-term respiratory health.8 Advances in type 2 airway biology have expanded phenotype-directed biologic options for selected children and adolescents with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe asthma.3,6,8 Omalizumab targets immunoglobulin E (IgE), dupilumab inhibits interleukin-4/interleukin-13 signaling, and mepolizumab targets interleukin-5.9–11

However, the pediatric randomized evidence base remains substantially more limited than that for adults. Trials are few, enroll biologically distinct or phenotype-selected populations, and differ in age range, background therapy, outcome definitions, and follow-up duration.12–15 Most compare a single biologic with placebo rather than another biologic, making direct comparative interpretation difficult.12–15 Adjusted indirect comparison through a common placebo can estimate between-biologic effects while preserving within-trial randomization, but sparse star-shaped evidence without head-to-head trials or closed loops does not permit loop-based consistency assessment and remains vulnerable to intransitivity.15–17 We therefore performed a systematic review with an exploratory adjusted indirect comparative analysis. Our objective was not to establish a biologic hierarchy, but to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety signals that can reasonably be inferred from current pediatric randomized evidence and to assess the certainty and limitations of those inferences.

Material and Methods

Search Strategy and Reporting Framework

This systematic review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.18 Relevant reporting items for indirect comparisons were considered where applicable. The review was not prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), and no registration number was available. This unregistered status is acknowledged as a reporting limitation. PubMed, Embase, the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), ClinicalTrials.gov, and public trial or regulatory sources were searched through May 25, 2026. Search terms included combinations of asthma-related terms, child, pediatric, adolescent, biologic, monoclonal antibody, omalizumab, dupilumab, mepolizumab, lebrikizumab, benralizumab, and tezepelumab. Randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, or clinical-trial terms were applied where appropriate. Complete search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Eligibility Criteria and Evidence Tiers

Eligible studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) enrolling children or adolescents with asthma, evaluating a biologic intervention against placebo or another eligible comparator, and reporting at least one prespecified efficacy or safety outcome. The primary analysis set was restricted to core pediatric/adolescent randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials with extractable AER, AE, or SAE data. Open-label extension studies, real-world studies, label summaries, and prescribing information were not included in the primary analysis. These sources were considered only as supportive background evidence when clinically necessary. ACOUSTICS was retained only as sensitivity or supportive evidence because it enrolled adolescents, included two lebrikizumab dose arms sharing placebo, and was prematurely terminated.19 ICATA was used as supportive evidence for omalizumab because it included participants up to 20 years of age.20

Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was AER, expressed as a rate ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Primary safety outcomes were patient-level AEs and SAEs, defined as participants experiencing at least one event and analyzed as risk ratios (RRs). SAEs were extracted according to each trial’s investigator-reported regulatory definition and were not reclassified across trials. Secondary outcomes included percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (ppFEV1), symptom scores, patients experiencing at least one exacerbation, injection-site reactions, viral upper respiratory tract infection (vURTI) or upper respiratory tract infection (URTI), skin-related events, and hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (MW and HL) independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed potentially eligible full texts. The same reviewers independently extracted study identifiers, participant age range and phenotype, intervention and comparator, sample size, follow-up duration, outcome definitions, analysis population, and efficacy and safety results from published articles, public trial reports, and publicly available trial sources. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with consultation of a senior reviewer (XL) when required. Extracted data were checked against the original sources before analysis. No unpublished data were obtained through direct contact with investigators.

Risk of Bias and Certainty of Evidence

Two reviewers (MW and HL) independently assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.21 Disagreements were resolved by discussion, with consultation of XL when necessary. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.22 For adjusted indirect estimates, additional attention was given to evidence sparsity, lack of direct head-to-head evidence, absence of closed loops, imprecision, and limited transitivity.23

Statistical Analysis and Transitivity Assessment

A placebo-connected evidence structure was constructed for each eligible outcome. Between-biologic rate ratios were calculated using the Bucher adjusted indirect comparison with placebo (P) as the common comparator: log(RR_AB) = log(RR_AP) - log(RR_BP), and SE_AB = sqrt(SE_AP^2 + SE_BP^2). The 95% CI was calculated as exp[log(RR_AB) ± 1.96 × SE_AB].17 Standard errors were derived from the reported 95% CIs on the log scale. Indirect-comparison estimates and 95% CIs were calculated using custom Python scripts. No fixed- or random-effects pooling was required because each core biologic-placebo contrast was represented by a single eligible trial; conventional pairwise meta-analysis was therefore not feasible. Because the evidence structure was star-shaped and contained no direct head-to-head pediatric trials or closed loops, formal inconsistency testing was not performed. Potential effect modifiers were prespecified and assessed qualitatively, including age range, inflammatory phenotype, baseline exacerbation risk, background controller therapy, follow-up duration, and outcome definition. Treatment ranking and the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) were not used to infer a hierarchy. The small number of eligible trials precluded formal funnel-plot or Egger-test assessment of publication bias and small-study effects.

Risk of Bias Results

VOYAGE and MUPPITS-2 were judged at low risk of bias in all five RoB 2 domains. IA05 was judged at low risk for the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, and outcome measurement, with some concerns for missing outcome data and selection of the reported result. ICATA, used as supportive evidence, had low risk in the first four domains and some concerns for selection of the reported result. According to RoB 2, the overall risk of bias among core trials ranged from low risk to “some concerns”, and no core trial was judged at high risk overall. Detailed domain-level judgments are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Primary Efficacy Outcome: Annualized Exacerbation Rate

All included biologics reduced AER compared with placebo (Figure 3). Omalizumab reduced AER from 1.36 to 0.78 events per year in IA05 (RR 0.57, 95% CI 0.45–0.72).12 Dupilumab reduced AER from 0.748 to 0.305 in the type 2 inflammatory asthma population in VOYAGE (RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.27–0.61).13 Mepolizumab reduced AER from 1.30 to 0.96 in MUPPITS-2 (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.56–0.96).14 Bucher adjusted indirect comparisons yielded RR 0.71 (95% CI 0.45–1.14) for dupilumab versus omalizumab, RR 0.56 (95% CI 0.35–0.90) for dupilumab versus mepolizumab, and RR 0.78 (95% CI 0.54–1.12) for omalizumab versus mepolizumab (Table 2). Primary Safety Outcomes: Adverse Events and Serious Adverse Events

AEs and SAEs were interpreted using treatment-specific risk ratios versus placebo, not ranking metrics. Overall AE risk was not clearly increased with omalizumab or dupilumab. Mepolizumab showed higher broad AE reporting in MUPPITS-2, but this endpoint included heterogeneous and non-specific events (Table 3; Figure 4). No consistent increase in SAEs was observed. IA05 reported fewer participants with SAEs with omalizumab than with placebo; however, asthma exacerbations requiring hospitalization contributed to serious-event reporting in this pediatric program.12,24 The apparent reduction may therefore reflect efficacy against asthma-related serious events and should not be interpreted as generalized protection from non-asthma harms. Secondary Outcomes

ppFEV1 and symptom scores were considered exploratory secondary outcomes and were not used for treatment ranking. Dupilumab improved pre-bronchodilator ppFEV1 at week 12 in VOYAGE.13 Omalizumab also showed ppFEV1 improvement in a post hoc pediatric exacerbator analysis.25 The adjusted indirect comparison between dupilumab and omalizumab was imprecise and did not establish a clear difference. The corresponding ppFEV1 evidence network and forest plot are shown in Supplementary Figures S4 and S5, respectively. Mepolizumab did not show a clear ppFEV1 or FEV1/FVC difference in MUPPITS-2.14 Symptom control was summarized narratively because trials used different measures. ICATA reported asthma symptom days during the previous 2 weeks, VOYAGE used the Asthma Control Questionnaire-7 Interviewer Administered (ACQ-7-IA), and MUPPITS-2 used the Composite Asthma Severity Index (CASI) (Supplementary Table S1). Reported exacerbation outcomes and specific safety events, including injection-site reactions, vURTI or URTI, skin-related events, and hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis, were summarized descriptively because definitions and reporting formats were inconsistent (Supplementary Table S2).

GRADE Certainty of Evidence

GRADE certainty varied across outcomes (Table 4). Certainty was moderate for biologics versus placebo on AER, mainly because randomized evidence was directionally consistent but affected by indirectness related to phenotype and baseline-risk differences. Certainty was low for between-biologic AER comparisons because all estimates were indirect and the evidence structure contained no head-to-head trials or closed loops. AE evidence was rated low because of broad definitions and reporting heterogeneity. Discussion

This systematic review found that biologics reduce exacerbation burden versus placebo in children and adolescents with asthma. The adjusted indirect estimate for dupilumab versus mepolizumab was below 1, but this finding is hypothesis-generating because every biologic-placebo contrast was informed by one trial and the evidence was clinically heterogeneous. The main contribution of this review is therefore not another ranking of biologics. A previously published pediatric network meta-analysis (NMA) by Lin et al reported favorable ranking results for dupilumab in efficacy outcomes and omalizumab in safety outcomes.26 Our findings are directionally consistent with that review, but we deliberately avoid interpreting ranking or a nominally significant indirect estimate as confirmatory evidence. The absence of head-to-head trials and closed loops prevents consistency assessment and reduces confidence in any definitive treatment hierarchy.15,16

Transitivity is a central concern. Omalizumab was studied mainly in allergic IgE-mediated asthma, dupilumab in type 2 inflammatory asthma, and mepolizumab in eosinophilic or exacerbation-prone asthma.9–14 Differences in age range, baseline exacerbation risk, background therapy, and outcome definitions may modify treatment effects.15,16 The dupilumab estimate should therefore not be described as confirmed superiority. Current RCT evidence did not show a consistent increase in SAE risk with biologic therapy,12–14 but events were uncommon in VOYAGE and MUPPITS-2 and estimates were imprecise.13,14 The lower SAE risk observed with omalizumab in IA05 requires particular caution: serious asthma exacerbations and hospitalization contributed to serious-event reporting, so improved disease control may have reduced some SAEs without implying a broad protective effect against unrelated serious harms.12,24 Because overall AE definitions also varied, safety outcomes should not be used to establish a ranking.

From a clinical perspective, biologic selection in pediatric asthma should remain phenotype-driven rather than ranking-driven.3,6,8 Omalizumab is most relevant for allergic IgE-mediated asthma, dupilumab for type 2 inflammatory asthma, and mepolizumab for eosinophilic exacerbation-prone asthma.3,9,11 Adjusted real-world and target-trial emulation studies in adolescent or adult-mixed populations have been directionally consistent with a lower exacerbation rate with dupilumab in some comparisons, but residual confounding prevents these studies from replacing pediatric randomized evidence.27,28 This study has several limitations. First, the evidence was sparse, placebo-connected, and contained no direct head-to-head pediatric trial. Second, transitivity could not be fully verified because trials differed in age range, inflammatory phenotype, baseline exacerbation risk, background therapy, and outcome definitions.15,16,23 Third, safety outcomes, especially SAEs, were limited by low event numbers and imprecision. Fourth, secondary outcomes were inconsistently reported and were unsuitable for definitive comparison. Fifth, real-world and open-label evidence was excluded from the primary analysis and provides only external context. Sixth, the small number of trials precluded formal assessment of publication bias or small-study effects. Finally, the review was not prospectively registered, which remains a reporting limitation and increases the importance of transparent reporting and cautious interpretation.

Conclusions

Current randomized pediatric evidence supports biologics for reducing exacerbation burden compared with placebo. An adjusted indirect estimate suggested a lower AER with dupilumab than with mepolizumab, but the sparse, clinically heterogeneous, placebo-connected evidence does not establish comparative superiority or a definitive biologic hierarchy. Safety analyses do not show a consistent increase in SAEs, although certainty is limited by sparse events and imprecision. Phenotype-stratified head-to-head pediatric trials with standardized outcome definitions and longer follow-up are needed.
