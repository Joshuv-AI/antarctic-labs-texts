Balancing lung and brain: physiological strategies for ARDS management in acute brain injury
Authors: Chiara Robba, Nekane Romero-García, Shaurya Taran, Rafael Badenes, Luigi Camporota, Patricia R. M. Rocco, Mariangela Pellegrini, Lise Piquilloud, Antoine Roquilly, Matthieu Schmidt, Fabio Silvio Taccone, Sarah Wahlster, Nicolò Antonino Patroniti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a common and clinically significant complication in patients with acute brain injury (ABI), affecting up to one-third of critically ill individuals and contributing to increased mortality, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and worse neurological outcomes. The coexistence of ARDS and ABI creates a fundamental therapeutic dilemma: strategies that protect the lung may adversely affect cerebral physiology, whilst neuroprotective targets may compromise respiratory management. This narrative review examined the pathophysiological interactions between the injured lung and brain, highlighting the competing effects of key ventilatory variables. Lung-protective ventilation, including low tidal volume and higher positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), reduces ventilator-induced lung injury but may increase arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2), resulting in intracranial hypertension and impaired cerebral perfusion. Conversely, strict control of PaCO₂ and optimisation of cerebral perfusion may necessitate deviations from conventional ARDS strategies. Oxygenation targets further illustrate this tension, as both hypoxaemia and hyperoxaemia can exacerbate secondary brain injury. We synthesised current evidence on respiratory support, including non-invasive strategies, invasive mechanical ventilation, rescue therapies such as prone positioning and extracorporeal support, and pharmacological interventions, with emphasis on their differential effects on pulmonary and cerebral physiology. Attention was also given to the role of multimodal neuromonitoring, including intracranial pressure and brain tissue oxygenation, as tools to individualise ventilatory management and reconcile competing organ priorities. Overall, available data support a shift from protocolised approaches towards physiology-driven, patient-specific strategies that integrate lung mechanics, gas exchange and cerebral haemodynamics in patients with concomitant ARDS and ABI. Future studies should incorporate combined lung and brain endpoints to define strategies that simultaneously minimise ventilator-induced lung injury and secondary brain injury.Visual abstract

Take-home message

The coexistence of acute respiratory distress syndrome and acute brain injury is a fundamental clinical challenge, as strategies that protect the lung may adversely affect cerebral physiology. Evidence to guide ventilatory strategies is limited in this population, and a physiology-driven, patient-specific approach that integrates lung and cerebral physiology is warranted. Future studies should incorporate combined lung and brain endpoints to define the optimal mechanical ventilation strategies in this population.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a common and clinically important complication in critically ill patients with acute brain injury (ABI) (defined as traumatic or non-traumatic vascular brain injuries) [1]. The incidence of ARDS in ABI varies according to the type and severity of brain injury, as well as ventilatory management, with particularly high rates reported in traumatic brain injury (TBI, 30%) [2, 3] and non-traumatic intracranial haemorrhage (ICH, 20%) [4, 5]. Its occurrence is independently associated with increased mortality, prolonged mechanical ventilation and longer intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay [2, 6].

The coexistence of ARDS and ABI presents a complex clinical challenge. Ventilatory strategies that are protective for the lung may adversely affect cerebral physiology, whereas approaches aimed at preserving intracranial homeostasis may compromise respiratory management [1, 7]. Consequently, clinicians are frequently required to navigate competing physiological priorities in the absence of clear, evidence-based guidance (Table 1). Patients with ABI are particularly vulnerable to lung injury owing to multiple, interrelated mechanisms triggered by the primary neurological insult [8]. Impaired consciousness with an increased risk of aspiration, coupled with early immunosuppression, excessive sympathetic activation, neuroinflammation and a systemic inflammatory response, contributes to lung injury and dysfunction from the earliest phases of ABI [1, 9, 10]. In addition, exposure to non-protective ventilatory strategies may further compound lung injury, although this relationship remains incompletely defined [4, 5, 11]. These processes reflect a bidirectional interaction between the injured brain and the lung, in which neurological injury predisposes to respiratory complications that, in turn, may exacerbate secondary brain injury [4, 5, 11].

Despite the clinical relevance of this interaction, current approaches to mechanical ventilation remain largely derived from single-organ paradigms. This limitation underscores the need for an integrated framework that accounts for both pulmonary and cerebral physiology. In this narrative review, we synthesise the current evidence on ARDS in ABI, focusing on the physiological interplay between lung and brain. We also examine how key ventilatory variables influence both organ systems and discuss the role of monitoring strategies in guiding individualised management. By integrating perspectives from pulmonary and neurocritical care, we aim to provide a clinically grounded approach to respiratory support in this complex and high-risk population.

Physiological challenges in ARDS with acute brain injury

Amongst patients with ARDS, lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes (i.e. 4–8 ml/kg predicted body weight [PBW]) is recommended to reduce ventilator-induced lung injury and improve the probability of survival [12]. However, in the setting of ABI, reductions in tidal volume may also lead to increased arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO₂), particularly when the functional aerated lung volume is limited (the so-called “baby lung”). Increases in PaCO₂ may cause cerebral vasodilation, increased cerebral blood volume (CBV), and raised intracranial pressure (ICP) [13]; these effects are amplified in patients with impaired intracranial compliance or pre-existing intracranial hypertension [13, 14]. As a result, the degree of tolerable hypercapnia is often constrained, limiting the straightforward application of lung-protective strategies.

Adequate titration of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is another cornerstone of ARDS management. Whilst PEEP improves oxygenation, prevents alveolar collapse, improves respiratory system compliance and reduces cyclic atelectrauma [15], it also may increase intrathoracic pressure, with potential consequences for both systemic and cerebral haemodynamics. High PEEP-mediated increases in pleural pressure may reduce venous return and cardiac output, resulting in a decrease in mean arterial pressure (MAP) and, consequently, cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) [16].

High PEEP levels may increase pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) by compressing intra-alveolar vessels, thus elevating right ventricular (RV) afterload and potentially impairing cardiac output (Fig. 1). Oxygen targets

Both hypoxaemia and hyperoxaemia are associated with secondary brain injury, with low arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO₂) compromising cerebral oxygen delivery, and supraphysiological PaO₂ causing cerebral vasoconstriction and oxidative stress-mediated tissue damage [17]. However, the association with adverse outcomes appears to be stronger for hypoxaemia than hyperoxaemia, consistent with the well-established pathophysiological role of hypoxaemia in secondary brain injury [18].

Current consensus guidelines suggest maintaining PaO₂ within a higher-than-usual range of 80–120 mmHg, whilst recognising that optimal targets are patient-specific [7]. Secondary analyses of the ENIO studies suggest a U-shaped relationship between PaO₂ and outcomes in ABI, with the lowest mortality observed at PaO2 values between 92 and 156 mmHg, and increased mortality at both lower and higher levels [18]. However, existing evidence is largely observational and does not imply a causal relationship. Notably, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing conservative (i.e. SpO₂ 88–92% or PaO₂ 55–70 mmHg) versus liberal oxygenation strategies have been described in mixed ICU populations [19], but are lacking in ABI populations. Few data support pulmonary oxygen toxicity, which would allow clinicians to use higher FiO2 without major concerns when considering brain oxygen delivery targets in normal ranges [20].

The pilot COMACARE trial found no changes in neuron-specific enolase (NSE) or outcomes with mild hyperoxia in patients with anoxic brain injury [21]; also, the BOX [22] and ICONIC [23] trials in different populations of non-ABI mechanically ventilated patients and the LOCO2 [24] and HOT-ICU [25] trials in acute respiratory failure or ARDS could not detect significant differences in clinical outcomes amongst the two strategies. In the CENTER-TBI cohort, higher PaO₂ or FiO₂ exposure was independently associated with increased 6-month mortality, although residual confounding from higher FiO₂ use could not be excluded [26]. Whilst recent meta-analyses in ABI link hyperoxaemia to higher mortality and worse neurological outcomes, heterogeneity between studies is substantial [27]. Brain tissue oxygenation (PbtO₂)-guided strategies may refine oxygen therapy by escalating interventions (such as blood pressure increase, carbon dioxide manipulation) in patients with persistently low PbtO2, whilst PaO2 targets of up 150 mmHg in tier 2 and higher targets in tier 3 are reserved for persistently low PbtO₂ and normal ICP [28]. Advanced neuromonitoring could help individualise oxygen thresholds in patients with concomitant ARDS, where preventing cerebral hypoxia must be balanced against lung injury and secondary brain damage [29].

Carbon dioxide targets

PaCO₂ is a potent and rapidly acting regulator of CBF and intracranial dynamics. Hypercapnia induces cerebral vasodilation, increasing cerebral blood volume and ICP, whereas hypocapnia reduces CBF through vasoconstriction [13]; these effects are mediated through CO2-induced changes in the perivascular pH. Even small deviations in PaCO₂ may have clinically significant neurological consequences.

High-quality randomised evidence defining optimal PaCO₂ targets in ABI remains lacking. Current recommendations are mainly supported by physiological rationale and observational studies showing associations, rather than causal relationships, between PaCO₂ ranges and outcomes.

Secondary analyses of large cohorts, such as ENIO and MIMIC, have shown that a physiological PaCO₂ range of 35–45 mmHg is associated with the lowest mortality, supporting normocapnia as a default target in patients without ICP elevation [18, 30]. When intracranial hypertension is present, expert consensus recommends a tiered strategy in which low-normal PaCO₂ (35–38 mmHg) is used as the first-line approach, with mild hypocapnia (32–35 mmHg) reserved as a second-tier intervention and moderate hypocapnia (30–32 mmHg) considered as a time-limited measure in tier 3; PaCO₂ reductions below 30 mmHg are discouraged [28]. This ABI-centric strategy contrasts with lung-protective ventilation in ARDS, where permissive hypercapnia is frequently accepted [12, 31]. In the context of TBI, carefully titrated increases in PaCO₂ (45–50 mmHg) may be considered when ICP is controlled but brain tissue hypoxia persists [28], an approach currently under investigation in ongoing PbtO₂-guided trials such as BONANZA [32] and BOOST-3 [33]. Randomised studies are needed specifically in patients with non-traumatic ABI conditions and amongst those with concomitant ARDS.

Overall, the available data suggest that PaCO₂ targets cannot be standardised. They require continuous reassessment based on intracranial dynamics, cerebral oxygenation and respiratory mechanics, underscoring the need for an individualised, physiology-guided approach based on a balance between CBF, PbtO2 and ICP optimisation. Continuous capnography monitoring and relative, rather than absolute, end-tidal CO2 changes may help to guide PaCO₂ adjustments in patients with ARDS and ABI. When permissive hypercapnia is considered according to ARDS strategies, its use should be individualised and guided by neuromonitoring evaluating intracranial compliance and, if possible, tissue oxygenation.

Non-invasive respiratory support and indications for intubation

Non-invasive respiratory support, including high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO), continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) and non-invasive ventilation (NIV) [34], may be used in selected patients without contraindications (such as impaired airway protection, absent cough reflex, haemodynamic instability, and severe hypoxaemia or hypercapnia) to improve gas exchange whilst avoiding invasive mechanical ventilation and its associated complications. However, evidence supporting use of these modalities in ABI remains limited, and current guidelines do not provide firm recommendations [7]. In patients with ARDS, a secondary analysis of the LUNG SAFE study demonstrated higher hospital mortality with NIV (compared with invasive ventilation) amongst those with PaO2/FiO2<150, highlighting the need for caution when using NIV in this subgroup [35].

Each modality presents specific advantages and limitations. HFNO does not require a tight-fitting interface, making it well-suited for patients with decompressive craniectomy or agitation, and may reduce the risk of aspiration in patients with impaired consciousness. CPAP and NIV provide positive airway pressure and may be particularly beneficial in neurogenic pulmonary oedema by reducing preload and afterload. However, increased intrathoracic pressure may impair cerebral venous outflow, potentially raising ICP in vulnerable patients [36].

Avoiding intubation may facilitate neurological assessment by limiting the need for sedation. Nonetheless, this strategy requires careful patient selection and close monitoring. Delayed intubation is associated with worse outcomes in ARDS and should be avoided [37]. Indications for prompt intubation include neurological deterioration, impaired airway protection, ineffective clearance of secretions, haemodynamic instability, worsening gas exchange despite non-invasive support or the need for sedation to tolerate the interface [7].

In this setting, the decision to initiate or escalate respiratory support should be guided by continuous reassessment of both respiratory and neurological status, balancing the benefits of non-invasive strategies against the risks of delayed invasive ventilation.

Optimising mechanical ventilation settings in ABI patients: preventing lung injury whilst preserving cerebral homeostasis

Although clinical priorities have traditionally focussed on preventing secondary brain injury, ARDS is consistently associated with worse neurological outcomes after ABI [3, 6 observations identify ventilatory management as a modifiable determinant of both pulmonary and neurological outcome.

Ventilatory modes and patient–ventilator interaction

Ventilation practices vary widely across neuro-ICUs, with no single mode consistently associated with improved outcomes [38]. Controlled modes are typically preferred in the acute phase to ensure stability and minimise metabolic demand, especially when ensuring lung-protective ventilation is a priority. As neurological status improves, assisted modes such as pressure support ventilation may be introduced. However, patient–ventilator asynchrony is frequent in ABI and may increase work of breathing, promote hypercapnia and adversely affect ICP [40]. Close monitoring is essential when transitioning to spontaneous breathing to avoid deleterious swings in both respiratory and cerebral physiology, due to changes in haemodynamics, oxygenation, carbon dioxide and therefore ICP.

Tidal volume: balancing lung protection and CO₂ control

Low tidal volume ventilation is central to lung-protective strategies but raises specific concerns in ABI. Reductions in tidal volume may increase PaCO₂, leading to cerebral vasodilation, augmentation of cerebral blood volume and elevated ICP. Consequently, patients with ABI have often been excluded from major ARDS trials using low tidal volume [38, 41]. Recent observational data suggest a graded relationship: tidal volumes below 9 ml/kg predicted body weight are associated with a lower incidence of ARDS, whereas volumes above 11 ml/kg increase pulmonary risk [16]. However, outcome benefits remain inconsistent. In a trial amongst 524 trauma patients with risk factors for developing ARDS, sigh breaths added to usual care did not increase ventilator-free days but were associated with improvement in some secondary outcomes, including mortality [42]. These findings support a strategy in which tidal volume is titrated not in isolation but in conjunction with plateau pressure and driving pressure, allowing modest adjustments when necessary to maintain PaCO₂ within acceptable cerebral targets [13].

PEEP: recruitment versus cerebral haemodynamics

PEEP improves oxygenation and prevents alveolar collapse but may increase intrathoracic pressure, impair cerebral venous return and elevate ICP. In practice, moderate PEEP levels (5–10 cmH₂O) are generally well tolerated, provided that PaCO₂ is controlled and haemodynamics are preserved. Higher PEEP levels (e.g. >12 cmH₂O) should be applied cautiously, as they may further increase ICP and reduce CPP, particularly in patients with severe brain injury or impaired autoregulation [43]. Lung recruitability is an important marker of whether PEEP will be well tolerated or not by the brain [16, 44, 45], but cardiovascular stability is also important to avoid CPP reduction when increasing PEEP [46]. Integrated monitoring of ICP, CPP, and, when available, PbtO₂ is therefore essential to guide individualised PEEP titration [47].

Protective ventilation: evidence and limitations

Evidence supporting lung-protective ventilation in ABI remains limited and partly conflicting. Two before-and-after studies involving more than one thousand two hundred patients reported increased ventilator-free days and reduced mortality with low tidal volume and moderate PEEP strategies when PaCO₂ was carefully controlled [47, 48]. In contrast, a recent meta-analysis found no significant reduction in mortality or ARDS incidence with protective ventilation in severe ABI patients [11]. The PROLABI found no differences in outcomes between protective ventilation strategies (higher PEEP and strict tidal volume) vs conventional treatment in neurocritically ill patients, and the Ventibrain study suggested that higher tidal volumes are associated with better outcomes, suggesting that selected patients may tolerate higher tidal volumes when plateau and driving pressures remain within protective ranges [38, 49].

Notably, protective strategies were associated with improved oxygenation in the early phase of critical illness, suggesting a potential role in ARDS prevention [14]. Overall, these data support the cautious application of lung-protective principles, provided that carbon dioxide levels and cerebral physiology are closely monitored.

Driving pressure and mechanical power: towards individualised ventilation

Driving pressure (ΔP) integrates tidal volume and respiratory system compliance (ΔP = tidal volume/respiratory system compliance) and has emerged as a key determinant of outcomes in critically ill patients. Mechanical power is a measure of the total energy transferred from a ventilator to the respiratory system over time and integrates the effects of tidal volume, airway pressures, respiratory rate and airflow. Observational data indicate that higher ΔP and mechanical power are associated with increased mortality, including in ABI populations. In the VENTIBRAIN cohort, lower ΔP, plateau pressure and mechanical power were independently associated with improved survival, whereas tidal volume alone showed an inverse association with outcome [38]. Pending further confirmation from adequately powered trials, these findings suggest that, in selected patients, slightly higher tidal volumes may be acceptable if ΔP and plateau pressure remain within protective ranges, thereby avoiding excessive increases in respiratory rate to control PaCO₂ (Table 3, Fig. 1) [38]. Rescue therapies in patients with concomitant ARDS and acute brain injury

Neuromuscular blocking agents

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) have been used in both ARDS and ABI, typically in the context of severe patient–ventilator asynchrony and to control ICP. However, their role remains uncertain, with inconsistent evidence regarding their impact on outcomes and no clear consensus on routine use. In ARDS, current guidelines diverge: the American Thoracic Society suggests early use in severe cases (conditional recommendation, low certainty of evidence) [31], whereas the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine advises against routine administration owing to the absence of clear benefit in randomised trials [50, 51], as also reflected in current ESICM guidance [12].

In ABI, NMBAs may reduce ICP by lowering systemic metabolic demand and improving ventilatory control. They are recommended as a tier 2 strategy for intracranial hypertension in traumatic brain injury [52, 53], although their effects on ICP are inconsistent [54]. Observational data indicate wide variability in clinical practice and suggest that NMBAs are preferentially administered in patients with more severe respiratory failure and in those undergoing ICP monitoring. Their use has been associated with lower mortality but longer duration of mechanical ventilation, without a clear effect on tracheostomy rates [55]. These observations, however, derive from a general acute brain-injured population rather than from the specific subgroup of patients with concurrent ARDS, in whom no dedicated large data are available; they should therefore be interpreted with caution.

A key limitation is the loss of the neurological examination, which may delay detection of clinical deterioration. For this reason, NMBAs should be reserved for selected patients with refractory asynchrony or uncontrolled ICP, used for the shortest duration possible, and accompanied by close neuromonitoring. The interaction between sedation depth, neuromuscular blockade and outcomes remains insufficiently defined and warrants further study.

Prone positioning

Prone positioning is one of the most effective interventions for moderate-to-severe ARDS [56]; however, its use in ABI patients has been limited by concerns regarding intracranial hypertension. Increased intrathoracic and abdominal pressures, together with impaired cerebral venous drainage, may theoretically elevate ICP. As a result, patients with ABI have largely been excluded from randomised trials of prone positioning [57].

Survey data from 2018–2019 indicate that a substantial proportion of clinicians do not routinely use prone positioning in ABI patients with refractory respiratory failure [58]. Current consensus recommendations support its use in patients with concomitant ARDS and ABI in the absence of significant intracranial hypertension, whilst acknowledging the lack of evidence in patients with elevated ICP [7].

More recent data are reassuring. Although prone positioning may increase ICP [59], reported changes are generally modest, and cerebral perfusion pressure is often preserved, particularly with appropriate haemodynamic support [60–63]. Consistent improvements in arterial oxygenation, PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio and brain tissue oxygenation have been observed [57, 62, 63]. Interruption of prone sessions owing to refractory intracranial hypertension appears uncommon and is more likely in patients with elevated baseline ICP or impaired intracranial compliance [64].

Taken together, these studies indicate that prone positioning is feasible and probably safe in selected patients with ABI. Its physiological benefits may extend beyond gas exchange by improving cerebral oxygen delivery. Implementation should be guided by continuous neuromonitoring, with attention to optimising venous drainage (e.g. head-up positioning, or keeping the dominant jugular vein up), minimising intra-abdominal pressure (e.g. shoulder and abdominal padding) and maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion pressure [65]. Also, the potential impact of ventilatory adjustments on ICP, particularly PEEP and tidal volume [66, 67], warrants special attention during prone positioning. The evidence now supports treating prone positioning not as a relative contraindication in ABI but as a monitored, individualised intervention that integrates respiratory and cerebral physiology [64, 68, 69].

Recruitment manoeuvres

Current ARDS guidelines from the American Thoracic Society and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine advise against the routine use of recruitment manoeuvres (RMs), particularly prolonged high-pressure strategies (PEEP ≥35 cmH₂O for ≥1 min), given signals of increased mortality, barotrauma and cardiovascular complications in recent trials [12, 31] and meta-analyses [70]. Brief high-pressure RMs (PEEP ≥35 cmH₂O for <1 min) are also discouraged owing to the absence of demonstrated benefit and potential safety concerns [71, 72].

In ABI patients, these risks are amplified [73, 74]. Elevations in intrathoracic pressure during RMs may impair cerebral venous outflow, increase ICP and reduce CPP [75]. Evidence in this population is limited, but available data suggest unfavourable cerebral haemodynamic effects. In a prospective study, RMs were associated with increases in ICP, reductions in CPP and MAP, and impaired cerebral autoregulation. Given these considerations, RMs should be avoided in most patients with ABI and reserved, if at all, for carefully selected cases of refractory hypoxaemia. Any use requires strict monitoring of ICP, CPP and systemic haemodynamics. The transient benefits in alveolar recruitment must be weighed against the risk of secondary brain injury, pulmonary overdistension and cardiovascular instability. Decisions should be individualised, integrating both lung and brain mechanics.

Extracorporeal support

Venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) enables ultra-protective ventilation and is an established therapy for severe ARDS with refractory hypoxaemia or hypercapnia. Its use in ABI has historically been limited by concerns regarding anticoagulation and the risk of intracranial haemorrhage [76–78].

ECMO profoundly alters haemostasis through systemic anticoagulation, platelet dysfunction and acquired coagulopathy, with intracranial bleeding representing the most feared complication. Nonetheless, advances in circuit technology and growing clinical experience have led to a reassessment of its role in selected patients with ABI [79].

Observational studies suggest that ECMO can be applied without excess mortality in carefully chosen patients, particularly younger individuals without active bleeding or absolute contraindications to anticoagulation. Strategies that minimise anticoagulation exposure, including heparin-sparing approaches, have been reported, although they may increase the risk of circuit thrombosis [80, 81]. Therefore, ABI patients should not be denied ECMO solely because of their brain injury, as prognosis is frequently uncertain in the acute phase.

Careful control of arterial carbon dioxide is essential during ECMO initiation, as rapid reductions in PaCO₂ may precipitate cerebral vasoconstriction and have been associated with intracranial haemorrhage. Close monitoring of coagulation parameters and neurological status is mandatory throughout support [82].

In contrast, extracorporeal carbon dioxide removal (ECCO₂R) for moderate ARDS remains experimental in ABI. Although it may facilitate PaCO₂ control, the requirement for anticoagulation and the absence of demonstrated clinical benefit preclude routine use outside research settings [83].

Weaning, extubation and tracheostomy

Weaning from mechanical ventilation in patients with concomitant ARDS and ABI requires balancing pulmonary recovery with neurological safety [84]. In ARDS, spontaneous breathing may promote derecruitment and increase the risk of patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI), particularly in the presence of high respiratory drive [85]. These factors complicate conventional weaning strategies and necessitate careful control of ventilatory support during the transition phase. In ABI, weaning is typically preceded by a neurological wake-up test (during which sedation is reduced or discontinued, and the patient is carefully monitored) [84]. Neurological stability during a wake-up test is inferred from preserved brainstem reflexes, stable pupillary reactivity without asymmetry, absence of a Cushing response, and tolerable ICP dynamics, whilst respiratory stability is gauged by work of breathing, absence of excessive effort (e.g. assessed using P0.1 and end-expiratory occlusion), and stable blood gases [86].

Patients meeting these criteria are evaluated with a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT). General preconditions for an SBT include low-to-moderate levels of PEEP (≤ 10 cmH₂O), acceptable gas exchange (PaO₂/FiO₂ ≥150 with FiO₂ ≤0.5), stable ICP and noradrenaline equivalents ≤0.2 mcg/kg/min [87], although protocols are highly variable. In ARDS, clinicians often balance minimising PEEP against the risk of lung derecruitment and increased work of breathing. SBTs are conducted via T-piece, CPAP, or pressure support with or without PEEP [88]. In the largest prospective observational study of extubation and tracheostomy practices in ABI, the most common SBT methods worldwide were pressure support (n = 513; 43.0%) and T-piece (n = 445; 37.3%), with a median SBT duration of 60 (IQR 30–120) min [89]. A recent post hoc analysis of this study found no difference in extubation success according to SBT method or duration [90].

A successful SBT should generally prompt the decision to extubate, although caution is warranted in ABI patients given that extubation failure is higher in this population (20–25%) than in general ICU patients (10–20%) [90, 91]. Risk factors for extubation failure include impaired airway protective reflexes, lack of visual pursuit, older age and longer duration of mechanical ventilation, whereas the usefulness of the Glasgow Coma Scale for predicting extubation outcome is less certain [90–94]. A multicentre randomised trial testing a protocolised approach to extubation in ABI, based on assessment of cough, gag, swallowing, and visual pursuit compared with usual care, is currently ongoing [95]. In general ICU patients, post-extubation use of high-flow oxygen or non-invasive ventilation reduces extubation failure following planned extubation [96]; however, in ABI patients, a recent observational study found no difference compared with conventional oxygen therapy [97], highlighting the need for further data.

Rates of tracheostomy following ABI are high, occurring in approximately 20–30% of patients [89, 98]. Tracheostomy improves patient comfort, reduces sedation requirements, and facilitates pulmonary hygiene and rehabilitation, but optimal timing remains uncertain. Tracheostomy is generally recommended in patients with concerns regarding airway protection, persistently reduced level of consciousness, or after one or more failed extubations; however, decisions must frequently be individualised on the basis of neurological trajectory, ventilatory requirements and the anticipated duration of support [7].

Pharmacological treatment

Pharmacological management of ARDS in ABI patients illustrates the close interaction between pulmonary and cerebral physiology in critical illness. In this setting, drugs prescribed to improve respiratory function may directly or indirectly alter cerebral perfusion, intracranial pressure and cerebral metabolism, necessitating therapeutic strategies that simultaneously protect the lung and the brain [67].

Amongst available pharmacological interventions, corticosteroids remain one of the few therapies shown to improve clinically relevant outcomes in selected cases of ARDS, and their use is now conditionally recommended in critically ill adults [99]. However, recent data suggest that treatment response may depend on the underlying biological subphenotype, with potential benefit in hyperinflammatory ARDS and possible harm in hypoinflammatory forms [100]. In patients with ABI, the risk–benefit profile of corticosteroids is particularly complex. Whilst attenuation of lung inflammation and fibroproliferation may be advantageous [101, 102], systemic adverse effects, including hyperglycaemia, immunosuppression, delirium and ICU-acquired weakness, may negatively influence neurological recovery [67]. The HYPOLYTE trial showed that stress-dose hydrocortisone reduced hospital-acquired pneumonia and increased ventilator-free days in patients with severe trauma [103]. However, corticosteroids have failed to improve outcomes in various subtypes of stroke and may even increase mortality in traumatic brain injury as demonstrated in the CRASH trial [104]. Accordingly, in patients with concomitant ARDS and ABI, corticosteroids should be restricted to compelling pulmonary indications, administered for the shortest effective duration, and accompanied by close metabolic and neurological surveillance.

Inhaled nitric oxide provides selective pulmonary vasodilation and may transiently improve oxygenation but has consistently failed to improve survival in ARDS, and may also be associated with increased renal dysfunction [105]. In patients with ABI, its cerebral effects remain uncertain. Improvements in arterial oxygenation do not necessarily translate into enhanced cerebral oxygen delivery, particularly in the presence of impaired cerebral autoregulation [106].

Fluid and haemodynamic management represent a critical interface between lung and brain in patients with concurrent ARDS and ABI. Conservative fluid strategies improve respiratory outcomes in ARDS [107], but hypovolaemia and hypotension are strongly associated with secondary brain injury and increased mortality in ABI [108, 109]. Because cerebral autoregulation is frequently impaired, arterial pressure targets must be individualised using clinical context and neuromonitoring data, whilst always avoiding fluid overload [110]. Vasopressors, most commonly noradrenaline [111, 112], are often required, although their effects on pulmonary vascular load and right ventricular function must be considered in severe ARDS.

Overall, pharmacological treatment of ARDS in patients with ABI requires an integrated, physiology-driven approach in which pulmonary, cerebral, and haemodynamic effects are considered simultaneously. Several strategies are under investigation, including inhaled volatile sedation [113].

No intervention should be regarded as lung-specific or brain-neutral. Future trials should incorporate combined lung and brain endpoints and multimodal neuromonitoring to define safer and more effective therapeutic strategies for this particularly vulnerable population [38].

PbtO₂ depends not only on PaO₂ but also on CBF, microvascular function and regional perfusion [114]. Therefore, when PbtO₂ is preserved, lower PaO₂ levels may be acceptable. Conversely, in selected patients with brain tissue hypoxia, higher PaO₂ targets (e.g. >120 mmHg) may meaningfully increase PbtO₂ and have been incorporated into therapeutic strategies aimed at correcting brain tissue hypoxia [32, 115]. A low PbtO₂/PaO₂ ratio should prompt consideration of impaired cerebral perfusion rather than inadequate arterial oxygen content, favouring haemodynamic optimisation (e.g. CPP, cardiac output and/or PaCO₂ manipulation) over simply increasing FiO₂ or transfusion [116].

Maintenance of normocapnia is appropriate in patients with stable ICP and adequate PbtO₂. In refractory intracranial hypertension, short periods of controlled mild hypocapnia may be used to reduce cerebral blood volume and lower ICP, provided that PbtO₂ remains stable [28]. Conversely, in patients with normal ICP but persistent brain tissue hypoxia despite adequate oxygenation and haemoglobin levels, carefully titrated moderate hypercapnia may increase CBF and improve PbtO₂ [117], whilst facilitating oxygen unloading at the tissue level. This approach requires invasive monitoring, as increases in PaCO₂ may precipitate deleterious elevations in ICP that would otherwise go undetected. Because PaCO₂ strongly influences cerebrovascular tone, moderate hypocapnia may enhance microvascular responsiveness and potentially improve autoregulation indices, independently of MAP manipulation [118].

Low tidal volume ventilation remains central to ARDS management; however, its routine application in ABI patients may lead to hypercapnia or hypoxaemia, which have been associated, in the absence of ARDS, with worse neurological outcomes [49]. When respiratory mechanics allow plateau and driving pressures remain within safe limits, modestly higher tidal volumes can be considered in selected ABI patients with ARDS. This approach may be indicated in the presence of refractory intracranial hypertension or persistent brain tissue hypoxia, to avoid hypercapnia and optimise brain oxygenation.

Although higher PEEP levels have traditionally been used cautiously in patients with ABI, incremental PEEP adjustments may be considered in hypoxaemic patients, with continuous assessment of ICP, CPP and PbtO₂ together with respiratory mechanics. This integrated approach helps distinguish harmful effects, such as ICP elevation related to hypercapnia or impaired venous return [74], from beneficial improvements in oxygenation and lung recruitment [119]. If PEEP escalation leads to a clinically relevant rise in ICP, levels should be reduced and underlying mechanisms should be explored. Conversely, when PEEP results in minimal ICP variation but significant improvement in PbtO₂ and oxygenation, it may represent an acceptable target with favourable brain–lung interaction. Over time, clinicians tend to apply more frequently lung-protective strategies (Table 3), but with a contemporary utilisation of more neuromonitoring tools to balance the risks and benefits for the brain and the lung.

Conclusion

The coexistence of ARDS and ABI presents a complex therapeutic challenge in which lung- and brain-protective strategies may conflict. Lung-protective ventilation can be applied safely in many patients with ABI and may improve respiratory outcomes, but its impact on neurological outcomes remains uncertain. Optimal management requires an individualised approach that integrates pulmonary and cerebral physiology to minimise both ventilator-induced lung injury and secondary brain injury. Future randomised trials incorporating combined lung and brain endpoints and multimodal neuromonitoring are needed to define optimal ventilatory strategies in this high-risk population.
