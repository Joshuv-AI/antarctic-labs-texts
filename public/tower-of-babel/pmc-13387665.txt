Impact of seven plus one initiative on prevention and control of dengue infection at tertiary care institute – A pre- and post-education intervention
Authors: Santosh Kumar, S Teja Rama Krishna, Abhishek Sadasivan, Pathak E. D. S. Dutt, Poojashree Rajaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Dengue continues to pose a major public health challenge in India, particularly during monsoon seasons. Frontline workers play a crucial role in surveillance and prevention, yet gaps in their knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) often limit effective control. The Seven-Plus-One (7 + 1) initiative emphasises weekly source reduction and early fever management through community engagement.Objectives:To assess the impact of a 7 + 1-based educational intervention on dengue-related KAP among frontline workers in a tertiary care teaching institute in Rishikesh.Methods:A pre–post interventional study was conducted among 131 frontline workers, including security personnel, sanitation staff, engineering staff, and students. A structured, pre-tested KAP questionnaire was administered before and after a 7 + 1-focused training session. Data were analysed using SPSS v25, and pre–post differences were assessed using McNemar’s test.Results:Significant improvement was observed across KAP domains. Recognition of Aedes aegypti as the dengue vector increased from 14.5% to 85.5% (P = 0.001), and awareness of clean stagnant water as a breeding site increased from 61.8% to 90.8% (P = 0.001). Positive care-seeking attitudes (consulting a doctor immediately upon fever) increased from 94.7% to 99.2%. Preventive practices improved markedly, with daily cleaning increasing from 14.5% to 45% and weekly cleaning from 32.1% to 74% (P = 0.028).Conclusion:The Seven-Plus-One educational intervention effectively enhanced dengue-related knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices among frontline workers. Routine incorporation of weekly source-reduction activities and structured awareness modules may improve institutional dengue preparedness and community resilience.

Introduction

Dengue fever is one of the fastest spreading mosquito-borne viral infections globally, particularly endemic in tropical and subtropical regions.[1] India continues to report recurrent seasonal outbreaks, especially during and after the monsoon period, influenced by climatic variations, rapid urbanisation, water storage practices, and inconsistent vector control efforts.[23] According to the National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme (NVBDCP), over 160,000 dengue cases were documented across India in 2023, reflecting a persistent public health challenge.[4] Apart from morbidity and mortality, dengue imposes a substantial economic burden due to hospitalisation costs, outpatient care, and loss of work productivity.[5]

The disease is transmitted primarily through the bite of Aedes aegypti, a day-biting mosquito that breeds in artificial containers holding clean stagnant water commonly present in households, institutional campuses, and construction zones.[1] Effective prevention, therefore, requires systematic elimination of potential breeding sites and timely recognition of febrile cases.[6] Frontline workers − including sanitation staff, security personnel, engineering and maintenance workers, and student volunteers − play a critical operational role in implementing vector control measures within institutional environments.[7] However, gaps in their knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices (KAP) can significantly reduce the effectiveness of dengue prevention programs.[8]

In response to recurring outbreaks, a community-based strategy known as the Seven-Plus-One (7 + 1) Dengue Prevention Initiative was developed and implemented in the Rishikesh-Dehradun region of Uttarakhand starting in 2019.[9] The approach focuses on weekly inspection and elimination of mosquito breeding sources over seven consecutive days, followed by a sustained weekly maintenance cycle (“+1”). Core components include environmental source reduction, cleaning and covering water storage containers, clearing drains and wet waste sites, promoting early fever reporting, and improving institutional and community involvement in vector surveillance.[9] Although promising, this initiative is not a formally issued national MoHFW guideline, but rather a regionally developed operational model supported through institutional adoption and academic documentation.[9] Evidence assessing its effectiveness in improving dengue-related KAP among frontline workers in institutional settings remains limited.

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a structured Seven-Plus-One (7 + 1) educational intervention on dengue-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among frontline workers in a tertiary care teaching institute in Rishikesh, Uttarakhand. Specifically, the study sought to:

Methodology

Study design and setting

A pre–post interventional study was conducted at a tertiary care teaching institute in Rishikesh, Uttarakhand, during August–September 2023. The campus accommodates students, healthcare workers, patients, and visitors, making dengue prevention a continuous operational priority.

Study participants

The study included frontline workers involved in daily campus operations, categorised as sanitation staff, security personnel, engineering and maintenance workers, and student volunteers.

Sample size

Complete enumeration was used to include all eligible frontline workers available during the intervention period, yielding a final sample of 131 participants. A minimum required sample size was also verified using a pre–post proportion comparison model, assuming a 20% improvement in key KAP indicators, 80% power, and α = 0.05, which indicated a minimum sample size of 110 participants. The achieved sample, therefore, provided adequate statistical power.

Description of the intervention: Seven-Plus-One (7 + 1) Model

The educational intervention was based on the Seven-Plus-One (7 + 1) Dengue Prevention Strategy, a community-based operational model developed and implemented in the Rishikesh–Dehradun region to strengthen weekly source-reduction and fever surveillance activities.[9]

The training emphasised:

identification and elimination of mosquito breeding sites;cleaning, drying, and covering water storage containers;clearing drainage and wet waste accumulation points;personal protection and early fever recognition;immediate medical consultation upon developing a fever;repetition of source reduction on a weekly cycle (“+1” maintenance).

A standardised 45-minute audiovisual and demonstration-based training module was delivered in small groups by public health faculty and trained field workers.

Data collection and analysis

Baseline (pre-test) data were collected immediately before the training, and the post-test assessment was conducted 1 week later [Figure 1]. Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analysed using SPSS version 25.

Study phases and operational procedures

Potential bias and mitigation

Social desirability bias in self-reported practices was minimised by ensuring anonymity and clarifying that participation would not affect employment. Selection bias was reduced by including all eligible available staff through complete enumeration. Recall bias was limited by standardising the interval between intervention and post-test to 1 week.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to study initiation. All participants provided written informed consent. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study.

Knowledge regarding dengue prevention and control

A significant increase in knowledge was observed regarding dengue re-infection, with 80.9% of participants recognising the possibility in the pre-test, rising to 99.2% in the post-test, and only a small percentage (19.1% in the pre-test and 0.8% in the post-test) believed that dengue re-infection was not possible, which was found to be statistically significant (P = 0.001). Followed by a significant increase in knowledge was observed regarding the transmission of dengue by mosquitoes, with 83.2% recognising mosquitoes as the vector in the pre-test, rising to 98.5% in the post-test. The understanding of alternative transmission methods (Others) decreased from 16.8% in the pre-test to 1.5% in the post-test, respectively, and it was found to be statistically significant with a P value (P = 0.001). A significant improvement in knowledge was noted regarding the vector responsible for dengue transmission, with 85.5% recognising Aedes aegypti in the post-test compared to 14.5% in the pre-test and the knowledge of Anopheles as a vector decreased from 3.1% in the pre-test to 0% in the post-test, which was found to be statistically significant (P = 0.001) [Table 2].

Discussion

In our study encompassing 131 participants, 24.4% were male and 75.6% were female, reflecting a notable gender disparity. This contrasts with the studies by Gupta et al. and Pandey et al. (2025), which did not specify detailed gender distributions. Our study also detailed educational backgrounds: 32.1% had completed high school, and 45.8% had attained higher secondary education, with smaller percentages in diploma (1.5%), undergraduate (16.8%), and postgraduate (3.8%) categories. Gupta et al. provided a comprehensive review of dengue in India, emphasising epidemiological aspects without detailed demographic breakdowns. In contrast, Pandey et al. (2025)[10] explored knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to dengue among residents of South Delhi, focusing on behavioural aspects rather than sociodemographic characteristics. Our study contributes by highlighting the intersection of gender and educational backgrounds with dengue-related behaviours, which are critical for understanding community responses and guiding targeted public health interventions. The educational diversity observed underscores varying levels of knowledge acquisition and health literacy, influencing local responses to dengue outbreaks. These sociodemographic insights are crucial for designing targeted strategies to enhance dengue prevention and control efforts. Future research could further explore these factors’ nuanced impacts on health outcomes, facilitating more effective public health policies and interventions tailored to diverse populations affected by dengue fever.

In our study, significant advancements in knowledge regarding dengue prevention and control were evident among participants. Post-intervention, 99.2% recognised the possibility of dengue re-infection compared to 80.9% in the pre-test, showcasing substantial improvement. Similarly, awareness of mosquitoes as the primary vector rose from 83.2% to 98.5%, while understanding of alternative transmission methods decreased significantly from 16.8% to 1.5%.

Comparatively, Gupta et al. provided a comprehensive epidemiological overview of dengue in India, laying foundational data but lacking specific insights into knowledge shifts among populations. Pandey et al. (2025) explored knowledge, attitudes, and practices in dengue among residents of South Delhi, offering community response insights without detailed knowledge enhancement data. Wong and AbuBakar focused on health beliefs in Malaysia, complementing our findings by broadening our understanding of community perceptions. Munasinghe et al. studied dengue knowledge in Sri Lanka, providing a descriptive analysis, while Alves et al. (2016)[11] assessed dengue awareness in São Paulo, Brazil. Our study’s contributions lie in quantifying specific knowledge gains post-intervention, particularly in vector identification and transmission pathways, crucial for targeted public health strategies. These findings underscore the efficacy of educational interventions in enhancing dengue-related knowledge. Future research should explore the sustainability and broader applicability of such interventions across diverse populations to optimize dengue prevention and control efforts globally.

In our study, significant improvements were observed in recognising dengue vector breeding sites. Pre-intervention, 61.8% correctly identified clean stagnant water as a breeding site, increasing to 90.8% post-intervention. Conversely, awareness of dirty stagnant water decreased from 32.8% to 5.3%. These changes were statistically significant (P = 0.001), highlighting effective knowledge enhancement. Comparatively, studies by Gupta et al., Pandey et al., (2025), Wong and AbuBakar, Munasinghe et al., and Alves et al. (2016) provided broader insights into dengue epidemiology and community perceptions but did not focus specifically on detailed shifts in knowledge regarding breeding sites. Our study’s findings contribute by quantifying specific improvements in identifying critical breeding sites, crucial for targeted vector control strategies and enhancing community-based dengue prevention efforts.

Our study demonstrated a significant improvement in attitudes towards managing dengue fever, with 99.2% of participants post-intervention expressing readiness to seek professional medical help promptly, up from 94.7% in the pre-test. Concurrently, there was a notable decrease in the use of traditional remedies like giloy and papaya juice, from 3.1% pre-intervention to 0.8% post-intervention. Comparatively, Gupta et al. provided a broad overview of dengue in India, touching on healthcare-seeking behaviours but without specific focus on attitude shifts towards medical help. Similarly, while Pandey et al., (2025) explored community responses in Delhi, our study’s emphasis on reduced reliance on traditional remedies adds specific insights into evolving healthcare attitudes. Wong and AbuBakar, Munasinghe et al., and Alves et al. (2016) provided context on health beliefs and practices related to dengue but did not detail shifts in attitudes towards medical management as our study did. Our findings underscore the importance of promoting evidence-based healthcare-seeking behaviours for effective dengue management. Our study revealed significant changes in dengue prevention practices among participants, with an increase in daily cleaning from 14.5% pre-intervention to 45% post-intervention, and an increase in weekly cleaning from 32.1% to 74%, demonstrating statistical significance (P = 0.028). These findings indicate the effectiveness of educational interventions in promoting more sustainable community practices.

Comparatively, Gupta et al. provided a comprehensive overview of dengue in India without a specific focus on community-level practices like cleaning routines. Pandey et al., (2025)explored knowledge and attitudes in community residents in Delhi but did not delve into changes in cleaning practices. Wong and AbuBakar discussed health beliefs in Malaysia without quantitative data on practice changes. Munasinghe et al. described dengue-related practices among Sri Lankan adults, contributing insights but not specifically on cleaning routines. Alves et al. (2016) assessed dengue awareness in São Paulo, Brazil, highlighting community practices without detailed emphasis on observed changes in cleaning behaviours. Our study’s findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions to sustainably enhance dengue prevention practices, crucial for reducing disease transmission and improving community health outcomes.

This study demonstrated a significant improvement in dengue-related knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices among frontline workers following the Seven-Plus-One (7 + 1) educational intervention. The substantial increase in identification of Aedes aegypti as the dengue vector (from 14.5% to 85.5%) and recognition of clean stagnant water as a mosquito breeding site (from 61.8% to 90.8%) indicates that structured, context-specific training can effectively enhance critical knowledge required for vector control. Similar findings have been reported in institutional and community settings, where targeted awareness programs have been shown to improve understanding of dengue transmission and prevention measures among frontline personnel.[56]

The results of this study closely align with the institution-based intervention reported from Uttarakhand by Kumar et al.,[9] where the 7+1 model significantly improved KAP levels among frontline workers. The consistency of outcomes reinforces the suitability of the 7+1 model as an operational, feasible, and context-appropriate approach for dengue prevention in institutional environments. Unlike traditional awareness campaigns, the 7+1 model emphasises repeated weekly action cycles, which may explain the moderate but meaningful improvements observed in cleaning and source-reduction practices in this study.

Attitudes towards seeking timely medical care for fever were already favourable at baseline but improved further following the intervention. This pattern reflects a known ceiling effect, commonly observed when baseline attitude scores are high.[7] However, the decline in reliance on unverified home remedies suggests a shift towards evidence-based health behaviours, which is essential for reducing delays in diagnosis and treatment.

Behavioural improvements, although significant, were less pronounced than knowledge gains, which is expected because behaviour change often requires sustained reinforcement, supervisory support, and environmental facilitators.[8] The increase in daily and weekly cleaning practices (14.5% to 45% and 32.1% to 74%, respectively) indicates an early transition towards habitual preventive action. Continued institutional reinforcement and monitoring will be important to maintain and strengthen these gains over time.

From a public health standpoint, frontline workers occupy a critical position in vector control within high-density institutional campuses. Empowering this workforce with structured, repeatable prevention routines aligns with national vector-borne disease control priorities and may enhance institutional resilience to seasonal dengue transmission.[12] The simplicity, low cost, and scalability of the 7+1 model further support its integration into routine campus health and sanitation operations.

Limitations

The study used a pre–post design without a comparison group, which limits causal inference. Post-intervention assessment was short-term; therefore, sustained behavioural change could not be evaluated. Self-reported practices may have been influenced by social desirability bias, although anonymity and non-evaluation assurances were provided to mitigate this.

Recommendations

To maintain and scale the observed improvements, the 7+1 routine should be incorporated into institutional standard operating procedures. Periodic refresher sessions, supervisory monitoring, provision of necessary cleaning resources, and visible accountability measures (e.g., weekly checklists) are recommended. Future studies should examine long-term follow-up and multi-site implementation to assess sustainability and generalizability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study underscores significant improvements in dengue-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices following targeted educational interventions. These interventions led to enhanced understanding of vector breeding sites, increased awareness of dengue transmission modes, and improved healthcare-seeking behaviours. The findings highlight the effectiveness of tailored educational strategies in empowering communities to adopt proactive measures against dengue. Moving forward, sustained efforts are crucial to maintain these positive outcomes and further strengthen community resilience to dengue outbreaks through continued education and public health interventions.
