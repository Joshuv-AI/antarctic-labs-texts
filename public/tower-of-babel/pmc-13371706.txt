OGDH primes macrophage for M1-like polarization and ferroptosis in sepsis associated acute lung injury
Authors: Lingli Chen, Ruijia Li, Hongqian Qin, Yu Jiang, Qiaoling Jiang, Yantong Li, Yuanyuan Li, Juan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis-associated acute lung injury (S-ALI) is a clinical syndrome characterized by dysregulated inflammation and overwhelming oxidative stress, and is associated with a poor prognosis. Alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (OGDH) is a key enzyme in the tricarboxylic acid cycle that catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) to succinyl-CoA, a critical step linking mitochondrial energy metabolism to immune response. However, the mechanism by which OGDH participates in S-ALI remains unclear.MethodsThe mouse model of sepsis was established via intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and CPI-613 was administered intraperitoneally prior to LPS challenge. Peripheral blood samples and baseline characteristics were collected from septic patients. In vitro, BMDMs were used to evaluate the therapeutic effects of CPI-613. Using untargeted metabolomics (LC-MS), RNA sequencing transcriptomics (RNA-seq), macrophage-specific small interfering Nrf2 RNA (si-Nrf2), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), we assessed lung tissue pathology, inflammatory cytokine levels, ferroptosis markers, OGDH enzyme activity, and clinical correlation.ResultsIn LPS-induced septic mice, we observed systemic metabolic changes characterized by decreased α-KG levels and increased OGDH enzyme activity. Intraperitoneal administration of the OGDH inhibitor CPI-613 attenuated acute lung injury and systemic inflammation, thereby improving survival. Flow cytometry revealed that CPI-613 suppressed M1-like polarization of alveolar macrophages in septic mice, a finding corroborated in vitro. Transcriptomic profiling indicated that CPI-613 treatment preferentially modulated ferroptosis and glutathione metabolic pathways in LPS-treated bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). In both LPS-challenged mice and BMDMs, CPI-613 reduced ferroptosis biomarkers and activated the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant axis. Critically, genetic silencing of Nrf2 via siRNA or pharmacological inhibition with ML385 eliminated the anti-ferroptotic effects of CPI-613, positioning Nrf2 as a central mediator of this protection. Serum from patients with sepsis-associated ARDS exhibited significantly elevated OGDH enzyme activity relative to healthy controls. Moreover, OGDH activity correlated strongly with disease severity and clinical outcomes, including septic shock and death.ConclusionOverall, our study reveals that OGDH is a key immunometabolism regulator and a novel biomarker in S-ALI, and its inhibitor CPI-613 may represent a potential therapeutic target for this condition.Graphical Abstract In a murine LPS-induced sepsis model, overactivation of OGDH in macrophages depletes α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and reduces the α-KG/succinate ratio. This metabolic shift promotes M1-like macrophage polarization while simultaneously inducing ferroptosis through suppression of the Nrf2/GPX4 axis and subsequent lipid peroxidation. Inhibiting OGDH with CPI-613 reversed these pathological changes and attenuated sepsis-associated acute lung injury. Introduction

Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) with high mortality risk driven by dysregulated host–pathogen interactions. Global epidemiological data estimate approximately 49 million sepsis cases annually, with about 1.1 million deaths [1]. Acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are major contributors to sepsis-related mortality, with case fatality rates ranging from 40% to 50% [2]. Despite substantial progress in early fluid resuscitation, antibiotic use, and supportive care, specific therapies targeting maladaptive immune–metabolic interactions in sepsis-associated organ injury—particularly ALI—remain lacking [3]. Hence, mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies are urgently needed.

Alveolar macrophages (AMs) play a central role in driving immunometabolic dysregulation and the aberrant immune response in S-ALI [4, 5]. Upon pathogen recognition, AMs shift toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype and release large quantities of inflammatory mediators, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), thereby initiating the inflammatory cascade [6]. Beyond disrupting macrophage polarization, ferroptosis has also been identified as a key pathogenic mechanism in ALI [7, 8]. First described in 2012 as an iron-dependent form of cell death [9], ferroptosis is marked by lipid peroxidation accumulation and loss of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity [10]. M1 macrophage polarization and ferroptosis may engage in bidirectional crosstalk, synergistically accelerating ALI progression. Whether these targets can be effectively translated into novel therapeutic strategies for S-ALI, however, remains unknown.

M1-like polarization and alterations in macrophage immune function are accompanied by metabolic reprogramming [11]. M1-like polarized macrophages require sustained energy and metabolic cues to rapidly and continuously produce inflammatory mediators, with rewiring of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle being a key component of this reprogramming. In M1 macrophages, the TCA cycle undergoes adaptive changes characterized by decreased α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and accumulation of succinate [12]. Specifically, α-KG has been shown to suppress inflammation by downregulating proinflammatory genes [13], whereas succinate serves as a critical metabolite that sustains the proinflammatory state of M1 macrophages [14]. Tannahill et al. reported that intracellular succinate levels increased 30-fold in mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) 24 h after LPS stimulation. Succinate stabilizes HIF-1α, which in turn promotes the secretion of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Moreover, succinate accumulated in macrophages undergoes oxidation by succinate dehydrogenase, directly driving mitochondrial membrane potential hyperpolarization and ROS generation, thereby further amplifying proinflammatory mediator expression [15]. Liu et al. exposed LPS-challenged macrophages to varying α-KG-to-succinate ratios and found that a reduced α-KG/succinate ratio enhanced M1-like polarization, whereas an increased ratio suppressed it, underscoring the antagonistic interplay between these two TCA cycle metabolites in regulating macrophage function [16].

Alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (OGDH) is a key regulatory enzyme in the TCA cycle, catalysing the irreversible oxidative decarboxylation of α-KG to produce succinyl-CoA and NADH, the former containing a high-energy thioester bond. Succinyl-CoA is then hydrolysed to succinate, releasing CoA and its high-energy bond. OGDH activity is functionally upregulated in M1 macrophages, driving succinyl-CoA synthesis from α-KG and reducing intracellular α-KG while increasing succinate levels. Mechanistically, succinate accumulation serves as a metabolic checkpoint for M1 polarization. It stabilizes HIF-1α, which promotes transcription of pro-inflammatory genes such as IL-1β and stimulates mitochondrial ROS production, thereby reinforcing the inflammatory response [16]. Additionally, OGDH regulates macrophage inflammatory responses through activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, enhancing the expression of M1 polarization markers [17]. Inhibition of OGDH or its gene knockout elevates the intracellular α-KG/succinate ratio, reducing the risk of pancreatic and breast cancer metastasis [18, 19]. CPI-613 (Devimistat) is a novel lipoate analog that selectively disrupts mitochondrial metabolism, specifically and potently inhibiting two key enzymes in the TCA cycle: pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and OGDH [20]. These enzymes play crucial roles in cellular metabolism, particularly in cancer cells. A Phase I study (NCT01835041) [21] reported that patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer treated with CPI-613 in combination with modified FOLFIRINOX achieved an objective response rate of 61%, including a complete response rate of 17%. Another Phase I/II clinical trial (NCT02882321) [22] demonstrated that the combination of CPI-613 with modified cytarabine and mitoxantrone achieved an ORR of 46% and a median overall survival of 8.7 months in patients with relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia, representing a statistically significant improvement over historical control. Beyond oncology, given its regulatory effects on energy metabolism and oxidative stress, CPI-613 may hold therapeutic potential for non-oncological diseases. Notably, Young et al. demonstrated that CPI-613 suppresses formate-induced mitochondrial ROS generation when pyruvate is used as the substrate [23]. Since OGDH has been reported as a more potent generator of mitochondrial ROS than PDH in liver and cardiac tissues [24], we hypothesized that CPI-613 might exert therapeutic effects in S-ALI by specifically inhibiting OGDH. However, whether OGDH promotes M1-like polarization of alveolar macrophages and S-ALI inflammation through regulation of intracellular α-KG and succinate levels remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

Previous studies have indicated that ferroptosis is subject to complex regulation by cellular metabolic remodelling. Notably, M1 macrophage polarization is accompanied by robust ROS production and lipid peroxidation, which are also central features of ferroptosis, suggesting a potential link between these two processes. For instance, inhibiting ferroptosis in macrophages with metabolites such as uridine [25] and itaconate [26] has been shown to alleviate S-ALI. Additionally, glutamine metabolism influences cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis by generating α-KG and reducing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) [27]. To date, the specific role of OGDH in regulating ferroptosis has not been fully defined.

This study investigated the role of OGDH in driving excessive M1 macrophage polarization and ferroptosis during S-ALI. OGDH enzyme activity was elevated in M1-like polarized macrophages from septic patients and mice. Inhibition of OGDH with CPI-613 significantly attenuated ALI and systemic inflammation, improving survival in septic mice. Mechanistically, CPI-613 enhanced the cellular antioxidant defence system via Nrf2/GPX4 activation and reduced M1-like polarization by increasing the α-KG/succinate ratio, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and excessive inflammatory responses in S-ALI. Our findings indicate that CPI-613, a metabolic inhibitor currently under investigation in oncology, shows preclinical potential as an adjunctive therapy for S-ALI.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

In accordance with the guidelines of Sepsis-3 [28] and the new global definition of ARDS [29], this study included 31 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of sepsis complicated by ARDS. Between March and October 2024, admission-day peripheral blood was collected from every patient enrolled upon entry to the Respiratory Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of Xiangya Hospital. The demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients, including age, sex, oxygenation index, type of infection, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, serum procalcitonin, C-reactive protein and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-10), and length of hospital and ICU stay, were retrieved from the electronic medical records system. The 28-day mortality of patients was obtained through the electronic medical records system and post discharge follow-up. Detailed information on the individual patients is provided in Supplementary Table S1. In addition, twenty age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers served as controls. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (202103115), and all participants provided written informed consent before sampling.

Cell culture and treatment

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were harvested from WT C57BL/6 mice, cultured in petri dishes, and differentiated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and recombinant mouse macrophage colony-stimulating factor (40 ng/ml, cat# 315-02, Peprotech, NJ, USA). The medium was replaced with fresh medium on days 3 and 5. On day 6, 1 × 106 cells were plated into 6-well plates for subsequent in vitro assays.

The RAW264.7 macrophage line (ATCC) was cultured in DMEM–10% FBS plus 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The cell lines were regularly authenticated via Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling and tested for mycoplasma, all of which were confirmed to be free of contamination.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 300 ng/mL, cat# L3024, Sigma, MO, USA) and interferon-γ (20 ng/mL, cat# 315-05, Peprotech, NJ, USA) were added to the cell cultures for 24 h to induce M1-like polarization. IL-4 (40 ng/mL, cat# 214 − 14, Peprotech, NJ, USA) was added to the cell cultures for 24 h to induce M2-like polarization. For separate experiments, BMDMs or RAW264.7 cells were pretreated for 2 h with 5 µM ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, cat# T6500, TargetMOI, Shanghai, China), an OGDH inhibitor (CPI-613, dissolved in DMSO, 50 µM, cat# T6157, TargetMOI, Shanghai, China), or an Nrf2 inhibitor (ML385, dissolved in DMSO, 5 µM, cat# T4360 TargetMOI, Shanghai, China) for 1 h, followed by LPS and interferon‐γ challenge for the indicated times.

Animal models and treatment

C57BL/6 mice (male and female, 6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Central South University (Changsha, China). The mice were housed under SPF conditions, with five animals per cage, under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The temperature was maintained at 20 °C to 25 °C, with a relative humidity of 40% to 60%. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Experiments were conducted using randomly selected littermates of the same sex matched by age and body weight. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics and Use Committee (IACUC) of Central South University (CSU-2023–0307). All procedures adhered to Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC) International standards for laboratory animal welfare.

For the non-lethal sepsis-induced lung injury model, 8–10-week-old mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 15 mg/kg of lipopolysaccharide (LPS, from E. coli O111:B4, cat# L2880; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). LPS was freshly dissolved in sterile, pyrogen-free saline (0.9% NaCl) and administered in a total volume of 200µL per mouse based on body weight. Control mice received an equal volume of sterile saline alone. Lung tissues were collected at 24 h post-LPS injection for histological and biochemical analyses.

For the lethal sepsis model, a separate cohort of mice received a higher dose of LPS (30 mg/kg, i.p., same LPS source and vehicle). After LPS injection, survival was monitored every 4 h for up to 72 h post-treatment. Mice that survived until the 72 h endpoint were euthanized, and no further tissue analyses were performed unless otherwise specified. The survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method.

To determine the optimal therapeutic dose of CPI-613 (cat# T6157, TargetMOI, Shanghai, China) and evaluate its off-target toxicity, C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to the following groups (n = 4 mice per group): vehicle control, LPS alone, LPS + CPI-613 (25 mg/kg), LPS + CPI-613 (50 mg/kg), and LPS + CPI-613 (100 mg/kg). CPI-613 or vehicle was administered intraperitoneally 2 h before LPS challenge (15 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). For long-term safety assessment, mice were treated with vehicle control or CPI-613 (50 mg/kg) alone (without LPS challenge) and euthanized at 24 h, 72 h, or 7 days post-treatment (n = 4 mice per group per time point). To investigate the effect of the OGDH inhibitor on S-ALI, CPI-613 (50 mg/kg) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) 2 h before LPS or PBS injection to suppress OGDH activity (n = 5 mice per group). To investigate the effect of ferroptosis on S-ALI, Fer-1 (5 mg/kg, cat# T6500, TargetMOI, Shanghai, China) or vehicle (0.1% DMSO) was injected intraperitoneally for 2 h before LPS injection (n = 5 mice per group).

Histological and immunostaining analyses

Lung samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm sections. For histological evaluation, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The stained sections were blindly examined under a microscope to calculate the lung injury score as described previously [30].

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), after deparaffinization and heat-mediated antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies, including 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE, 1:100; cat# bs-6313R; Bioss, Beijing, China), F4/80 (1:100; cat# 14-4801-82; Invitrogen, CA, USA), CD11c (1:100; cat# 11-0114-82; Thermofisher, MA, USA), SiglecF (1:50; cat# 12-4801-82; Thermofisher, MA, USA) and OGDH (1:200; cat# EPR-27181-78; Abcam, MA, USA). The sections were subsequently exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, developed with DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, and mounted in neutral balsam; positive staining was semiquantified via light microscopy.

For tissue immunofluorescence, paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked with bovine serum albumin, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following specific primary antibodies: anti-F4/80 (1:100, cat#14-4801-82, Invitrogen, CA, USA) and OGDH (1:200, cat#EPR-27181-78, Abcam, MA, USA). The sections were incubated with Alexa-488- or Alexa-594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; CST cat#4408 and #8889, respectively), and the nuclei were labelled with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired via a confocal microscope (Nikon Ni-U, Tokyo, Japan) and quantitatively analysed for fluorescence intensity with ImageJ software.

For cellular immunofluorescence, the cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 2 min. Then, the cells were blocked with PBS containing 5% goat serum. The cells were incubated with primary antibodies, specifically anti-iNOS (1:100; cat# 18985-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-CD206 (1:100; cat# GB113497; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and anti-GPX4 (1:100; cat# 67763-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) antibodies. The subsequent labelling steps mirrored the tissue immunofluorescence protocol, and images were captured on a Nikon Ni-U microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Plasma metabolomic profiling by LC‒MS/MS

Metabolomic profiling was performed following an established pipeline, which involved rigorous sample preparation, periodic quality control (QC) injections, LC–MS/MS acquisition, and downstream data processing, as previously described [31]. Briefly, 500 µL of mouse blood was obtained via eyeball enucleation and centrifuged at 3000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The serum samples from the mice were collected and immediately stored at -80 °C. A pooled QC sample was prepared by combining equal aliquots of every serum sample to monitor analytical drift.

Metabolite profiling was conducted on a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap MS (Thermo Fisher) at Novogene (Beijing) operating in positive/negative dual-ion mode. Separation was achieved on a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) using a 17-min linear gradient delivered at 0.2 mL/min. Distinct mobile phase systems were used for different ionization modes: in positive mode, 0.1% aqueous formic acid (A) and methanol (B); in negative mode, 5 mM ammonium acetate, pH 9.0 (A), and methanol (B) were used. Peak picking, alignment, and quantification were performed in Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher) to generate the metabolite matrix. Metabolite identities were assigned by matching accurate mass and MS² spectra to mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList libraries and then annotating against KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID MAPS before statistical testing. Three samples with nucleic acid detection times > 24 h were subsequently excluded to maintain temporal consistency across the cohort. After preprocessing, 255 plasma metabolites passed quality control filters and were log2-transformed; the coefficient of variation (CV) was then assessed across the QC samples to validate the analytical precision.

Measurement of OGDH enzyme activity

Blood was collected from both septic patients and septic mice, and serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. OGDH enzymatic activity was subsequently quantified with a commercial colorimetric assay kit (cat# NBP3-25918; Novus Biologicals, MO, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of lactate levels

Mouse serum lactate concentrations were measured via a specific assay kit (Boxbio, cat# AKAC014M) according to the provided instructions. Briefly, 100µL of diluted serum was reacted with the assay mixture at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated, and the plate was read at 450 nm.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), lung tissues were fixed overnight in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The samples were then infiltrated with a graded acetone–epoxy resin series, embedded in pure resin, and polymerized. Sixty- to eighty-nanometre ultrathin slices were obtained with an ultramicrotome, poststained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and viewed via a JEOL JEM-1400Flash transmission electron microscope at 80 kV.

Flow cytometry

After anaesthesia, each lung was gently lavaged eight times with 0.8 mL of ice-cold PBS containing 10nM EDTA; the pooled bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was centrifuged and processed for further analysis. The cellular component of the BALF was first filtered through a 40 μm strainer and then centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. After counting, the cell pellets were resuspended in 100µL of FACS buffer, and the following fluorochrome-conjugated primary antibodies were added for myeloid immunophenotyping: APC/CY7-labelled CD45, PE/CY7-labelled CD11C, PE-labelled SiglecF, PerCP/Cy5.5-labelled Ly6G, FITC-labelled CD11b, and APC-labelled CD54 (BioLegend, CA, USA). The samples were stained for 30 min on ice in the dark. After centrifugation (350 × g, 4 °C, 5 min), the final cell pellet was resuspended in 500µL of FACS buffer and analysed immediately on a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The cell populations were identified via sequential gating. CD45 was used as a marker for leukocytes. Alveolar macrophages (AMs) were gated as CD45⁺Siglec-F⁺CD11c⁺ cells. M1-like AMs were distinguished by elevated coexpression of CD54 and CD11c. Finally, neutrophils were defined as CD45⁺CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ cells.

After treatment, the BMDMs were washed with PBS, trypsinized (0.25% trypsin-EDTA), and neutralized with serum-containing medium. The cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 350 × g for 5 min and processed for downstream analysis. After the supernatant was discarded, the cell pellet was collected and resuspended in precooled PBS at 4 °C without serum. The cells were counted and processed for further analysis. Immunophenotyping was performed via the following monoclonal antibodies: PE-labelled F4/80, FITC-labelled CD11b, and PE/CY7-labelled CD86 (BioLegend, CA, USA). F4/80 and CD11b are pan-BMDM markers, whereas CD86 is a marker for M1-like macrophages. All data analyses were performed with FlowJo X software.

Serum biochemical analysis

Blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital sinus and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE) were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 510 nm (ALT/AST) or 540 nm (BUN/CRE) using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1).

Lung tissue ATP measurement

Lung tissue samples (approximately 50 mg) were homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer provided with the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, #S0026, China). After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. ATP levels were measured using a luciferase-based bioluminescence assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence was detected using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Envision). ATP concentrations were normalized to total protein content measured by BCA assay and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Lung tissue Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) measurement

Lung tissue homogenates (10% w/v) were prepared in ice-cold PBS and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for LDH activity measurement using a commercially available kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, # P0395S, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. LDH activity was normalized to total protein content and expressed as mU/mL protein.

Assessment of serum TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β

According to the manufacturer’s protocols, we measured the following serum concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in both patients and mice via commercial ELISA kits from NeoBioscience (Shenzhen, China): EMC102 (TNF-α), EMC004 (IL-6), and EMC001b (IL-1β).

Measurement of MDA, Fe2+ and the GSH/GSSG ratio

In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and Fe2+ and the ratio of oxidized to reduced glutathione (GSH/GSSG) in the lung samples were measured via the MDA Assay Kit (catalogue no. M496, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan), Iron Assay Kit (catalogue no. I291, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan), and Glutathione (GSH/GSSG) Assay Kit (catalogue no. G263, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan), respectively.

Cell viability assay

We assessed cell viability via a CCK-8 kit (MedChemExpress, HY-K0301). The cells were plated in 96-well plates, treated as needed, and then incubated with CCK-8 solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The OD450 was read after 2 h, and the viability was normalized to that of the control wells.

ROS assay

The total intracellular ROS content was quantified with a commercially available ROS detection kit (Cat#R252, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). After the cells were treated as instructed, 10 µmol/L 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was diluted in serum-free medium and incubated with the cells at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were washed three times with Hank’s balanced salt solution. DCFH-DA is hydrolysed by an intracellular esterase to generate DCFH. Once inside cells, DCFH-DA is deacetylated to DCFH, which is subsequently oxidized by ROS to form the fluorescent product DCF, allowing for quantification. A fluorescence microscope (Nikon Ni-U, Tokyo, Japan) was used to analyse the green fluorescence of DCF.

Nrf2 siRNA transfection

RAW264.7 cells seeded in 6-well plates were transiently transfected with siRNA. Nrf2 siRNA (si-Nrf2) and a negative control (si-NC) were purchased from Hanbio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). The detailed sequences are as follows: forward, 5’-GUAUGAGACUGAGGUUCAUTT-3’ and reverse, 5’-AUGAACCUCAGUCUCA.

UACTT-3’. Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Hanbio) was used as the transfection reagent. The cells were harvested after 72 h of treatment for further immunoblotting analysis.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from primary BMDMs from mice with TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan) for subsequent RNA-Seq analysis. Poly(A) + RNA was enriched with oligo(dT) beads, fragmented, and converted into strand-specific cDNA libraries. Paired-end 150-bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten. Differentially expressed genes were defined by |log₂FC| > 1.5 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. The results were visualized via heatmaps and further subjected to functional annotation and pathway analysis, including functional terms (Gene Ontology [GO]), biological pathways (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG]), and predefined gene sets (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis [GSEA]).

Western blotting

The quantification of total proteins extracted from cells or tissues was performed. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer, heated for 10 min, and then loaded into an SDS‒polyacrylamide gel containing molecular weight markers. Following SDS‒PAGE, the separated proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was subsequently incubated with blocking buffer to prevent nonspecific binding of the primary antibody. Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with antibodies specific for iNOS (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Nrf2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), SLC7A11 (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), HO-1 (1:1000, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), GPX4 (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), OGDH (1:1000, Abcam, MA, USA) and GAPDH (1:2000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After primary antibody incubation and washing, the membrane was subsequently incubated with an HRP (horseradish peroxidase)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and then washed three times for 10 min each with TBST to remove unbound antibody. We detected the protein bands by applying a hypersensitive ECL substrate (Zzenbio, #17046) to the membrane. The resulting chemiluminescence was captured with an imaging system (ImageLab, Bio-Rad), and band intensities were analysed via ImageJ software.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the tissues and cells via the TRIzol Reagent (Accurate, Changsha, China). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA via the Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Kit (Accurate, AG11617) with random primers. Subsequently, quantitative PCR was carried out via the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS One-Step RT‒qPCR Kit (Accurate, AG11701) on a real-time PCR system. Gene expression was normalized to that of β-actin or GAPDH as internal controls, and relative fold changes were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

C11-BODIPY 581/591 staining

Alveolar macrophages were collected from each group and stained with C11-BODIPY 581/591 (5 µM, Thermo Fisher, USA) in PBS at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing, cells were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan) or flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA). The ratio of oxidized (green) to reduced (red) fluorescence was quantified using ImageJ software.

LDH release assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and treated with LPS (1 µg/mL) with or without CPI-613 (50 µM) or Fer-1 (1 µM) for 24 h. LDH release in the culture supernatant was measured using a commercial LDH cytotoxicity detection kit (cat#C0016, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of LDH release was calculated as: (experimental release – spontaneous release) / (maximum release – spontaneous release) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 29.0 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.0 (CA, USA) were used for statistical analysis. All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3), unless otherwise stated. For in vivo studies, each biological replicate represents one individual mouse, and the sample size (n) per group is indicated in the figure legends. For in vitro studies, each biological replicate represents an independent cell culture preparation from different passages or different mice. All the data are presented as the means ± SD. Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted via two-tailed Student’s t tests, while comparisons involving three or more independent groups were performed, and statistical significance was assessed via one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The log-rank test was used to analyse survival rates. To assess the linear relationship between serum OGDH enzyme activity and several clinical variables, we conducted Pearson correlation analysis. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance. All experiments were repeated independently with similar results, and representative data are shown.

Results

Untargeted metabolomics reveals significant metabolic alterations characterized by a lower α-KG/succinate ratio in mice with sepsis

To identify metabolic drivers in mice with sepsis, we conducted an LC-MS-based untargeted metabolomics study. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that quality control (QC) samples from each group clustered well under both positive and negative ion modes, indicating reproducibility and method stability throughout the acquisition period (Fig. 1A, B). Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) models generated from both positive- and negative-ion datasets revealed distinct clustering that completely separated control from septic group (Fig. 1C, D), indicating substantial metabolic alterations in septic mice compared to control group. The R2 and Q2 values of the PLS-DA score plots (R2Y = 0.99, Q2Y = 0.98 in positive ion mode; R2Y = 1, Q2Y = 0.97 in negative ion mode) indicated high model fit and reliability, making it suitable for the analysis and screening of differentially abundant metabolites. Volcano plots were employed to visualize the overall distribution of all detected serum metabolites (Fig. 1E, F). Applying the criteria of variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 and P < 0.05, we screened 255 differentially expressed endogenous metabolites in the model group compared to the control group. Profiling against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) identified 255 metabolites in our cohort.180 were upregulated and 75 were downregulated (Supplementary Tables S3, S4). The ten most significantly altered metabolites in each direction were prioritized as potential biomarkers. Upregulated metabolites with diagnostic potential included N-valeric acid-3-carboxyindole metabolite, 6-(4-methoxyphenyl) pyrimidine-2,4-diamine, glutaric acid, N-valeric acid metabolite, β-nicotinamide mononucleotide, 5’-deoxy-5’-(methylthio) adenosine, prostaglandin E2, γ-glutamyl/methionine, taurodeoxycholic acid, and L-asparagine. Downregulated metabolites included 4-methoxycinnamic acid, α-KG, D-ribose-1-phosphate, ethyl-β-D-glucuronide, ribitol-5-phosphate, 8-hydroxyguanine, N-acetyl-L-histidine, 2-oxoglutarate, acetylcysteine, and N-acetylcysteine, which may provide a basis for developing novel biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets (Fig. 1G, H). To screen for metabolites with potential therapeutic effects on sepsis, we selected the hydrophilic metabolite α-KG from the downregulated metabolites. Previous studies [32, 33] suggest that α-KG, together with succinate, regulates macrophage polarization and may be involved in ARDS-related oxidative stress (Fig. 1I). Specifically, a decreased α-KG/succinate ratio promotes M1 polarization, while an increased ratio inhibits it. Notably, we observed that the serum α-KG/succinate ratio in the LPS group was lower than that in the control group (Fig. 1J). Based on these findings, the reduced α-KG/succinate ratio may be closely associated with sepsis, warranting further investigation into its regulatory mechanisms.

Pharmacological inhibition of OGDH by CPI-613 attenuates LPS-induced inflammation and ALI in septic mice

OGDH is responsible for catalyzing the oxidative decarboxylation of α-KG to generate succinyl-CoA, which is subsequently converted to succinate through substrate-level phosphorylation. Therefore, we investigated the potential role of OGDH in LPS-induced septic mice. To determine the optimal dose of CPI-613 and assess potential off-target effects, we compared 25, 50, and 100 mg/kg CPI-613 in LPS-challenged mice. H&E staining showed that the 50 mg/kg dose markedly alleviated LPS-induced lung pathological changes compared to the 25 mg/kg group (Supplementary Figure S1A). Serum ALT, AST, BUN, and Cr levels were significantly lower in the 50 mg/kg group compared to LPS alone and 25 mg/kg groups, while the 100 mg/kg group exhibited marked elevations indicating hepatotoxicity (Supplementary Figure S1B). Lung ATP and LDH levels (Supplementary Figure S1B) and plasma IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Supplementary Figure S1C) showed similar trends. These data establish 50 mg/kg as the optimal safe dose. Furthermore, to evaluate long-term effects, mice treated with vehicle or CPI-613 (50 mg/kg) were assessed at 24 h, 72 h, and 7d post-treatment. No significant differences were observed in serum ALT, AST, BUN, Cr, lung ATP, LDH, or plasma cytokines between groups at any time point (Supplementary Figure S1D-I), indicating no detectable long-term adverse effects.

Quantitative analysis of serum OGDH enzyme activity demonstrated a marked elevation in the LPS-induced septic mice relative to control mice (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2A). In addition, we found that LPS treatment did not decreased the serum PDH activity compared to the control group (Supplementary Figure S2). Immunofluorescence staining and immunohistochemistry of lung tissue sections further confirmed the upregulation of OGDH expression in alveolar macrophages (AMs) of LPS-induced septic mice. Using fluorescence microscopy, we observed that a considerable proportion of OGDH-positive cells colocalized with CD11c-positive and SiglecF- positive AMs (macrophage markers) in the lung tissues of LPS-induced septic mice (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, immunohistochemical analysis identified predominant OGDH+ cells displaying spherical to ovoid configurations with short membrane protrusions and lobulated nuclei (Fig. 2C), features that are pathognomonic of tissue-resident macrophages. Subsequently, we pretreated mice with the OGDH inhibitor CPI-613 before intraperitoneal injection of LPS. Histopathological analysis of lung tissues showed that CPI-613 markedly alleviated LPS-induced alveolar hemorrhage, alveolar septal thickening, and leukocyte infiltration compared to the untreated group (Fig. 2D). CPI-613 also reduced the inflammatory damage to hepatocytes and the destruction of renal tubules caused by LPS (Supplementary Figure S2A). Compared to the untreated group, CPI-613-treated septic mice exhibited significantly reduced lung injury scores, lung wet/dry weight ratios, total protein and total cell counts in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (Fig. 2E, F, G, and H). To assess the impact of CPI-613 treatment on systemic and pulmonary inflammatory responses, we quantified lactate, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels in serum and BALF using ELISA. The results demonstrated that intraperitoneal injection of LPS significantly increased the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in BALF and plasma compared to the control group; notably, CPI-613 administration attenuated these cytokine surges stimulated by LPS, demonstrating potent immunomodulatory efficacy against sepsis associated cytokine storms (Fig. 2I, J, and K). Furthermore, in the LPS-induced lethal sepsis model, CPI-613 treatment conferred significant survival advantage with 72-hour survival rates improving from 30% to 80% (log-rank P = 0.0115), demonstrating therapeutic potential against fulminant sepsis (Fig. 2L). These findings indicate that OGDH expression and enzyme activity are predominantly elevated in alveolar macrophages, and the inhibition of OGDH by CPI-613 mitigates sepsis associated lung injury and systemic inflammation, thus improving the mortality of septic mice.

The OGDH inhibitor CPI-613 alleviates M1-like polarization of alveolar macrophages in LPS-induced septic mice

Polarization imbalance of AMs plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of S-ALI. Previous studies have shown that OGDH enzyme activity is enhanced in M1 macrophages, promoting the conversion of α-KG to succinyl-CoA, leading to a decrease in intracellular α-KG and an accumulation of succinate [16]. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the effect of OGDH inhibition and macrophage polarization both in vivo and in vitro. The results of flow cytometry analysis presented AMs polarization in BALF from mice (Fig. 3A-C). CD45+Siglec-F+CD11c+ was used as a marker for AMs, while CD54 and CD11C were used as markers for M1 macrophages. After labeling AMs with CD45+Siglec-F+CD11c+, the expression of polarization markers was compared. AMs of LPS-induced septic mice exhibited the strongest rightward shift in CD54 and CD11C expression; pharmacological inhibition of OGDH with CPI-613 significantly attenuated these M1-associated surface markers compared to the LPS group but higher than the control group, indicating partial reversal of pathological macrophage activation (Fig. 3A and C). These results suggest that OGDH inhibition by CPI-613 suppresses M1-like polarization of AMs in septic mice.

Next, we evaluated the effect of the OGDH inhibitor on M1-like polarization in vitro. As expected, the protein abundance of iNOS was increased in M1 (LPS + IFN-γ) group, but dramatically decreased after CPI-613 co-treatment in BMDMs and RAW264.7 cell lines (Fig. 3D, E and F). Similarly, the mRNA levels of iNOS and CD86, two surface markers of M1-like macrophages, were significantly increased in the M1 group compared to the control group in two cell lines, while CPI-613 treatment reduced their mRNA levels (Fig. 3G, H). Quantitative fluorescence microscopy demonstrated that LPS/IFN-γ-induced M1 polarization significantly amplified iNOS expression, which was attenuated by CPI-613 treatment (Fig. 3I). The fluorescence intensity of CD206, a classical M2 marker, was markedly reduced in M1 macrophages, but no significant enhancement was observed after CPI-613 treatment (Figure S3A). These findings were supported by similar results from qPCR analysis (Figure S3B).

Flow cytometry analysis of BMDMs was performed to further validate these results. F4/80+CD11b+ was used as a pan-BMDM marker, and CD86 was used as a marker for M1 macrophages. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) demonstrated that CD86 expression was significantly higher in the M1 group compared to the M0 group, while CPI-613 treatment reduced CD86 expression compared to the M1 group (Fig. 3J). CPI-613 activates the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway to suppress ferroptosis in mouse lungs

To investigate the downstream molecular mechanisms regulated by the OGDH inhibitor CPI-613, we conducted RNA-seq analysis to compare the transcriptional profiles of LPS-activated BMDMs treated with CPI-613 or not. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed that ferroptosis and glutathione metabolism were among the top pathways affected by CPI-613 treatment, suggesting that the inhibition of OGDH may exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing ferroptosis (Fig. 4A). We further investigated whether OGDH regulates ferroptosis in S-ALI. The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed the activation or inhibition of inflammatory response pathways and genes in both the control group or the CPI-613 co-treatment group compared to LPS treatment alone. In the case of CPI-613 treatment, glutathione transferase activity and nuclear events mediated by NFE2L2 (Nrf2) pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. 4B, C). To validate the impact of the OGDH inhibitor on ferroptosis in vivo, we conducted Western blot and RT-qPCR analyzes. Western blot analysis confirmed that protein levels of Nrf2, SLC7A11, HO-1, and GPX4 were significantly reduced in the LPS group, while treatment with the OGDH inhibitor or Fer-1 reversed these changes (Fig. 4D). In the LPS-treated mice group, mRNA levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11, two key ferroptosis antioxidant markers, were reduced in lung tissues, while CPI-613 reversed this effect. Although mRNA levels of GCLC, HMOX-1, NQO1, and Nrf2 were compensatory elevated in the LPS group, CPI-613 further increased their expression, achieving a similar effect to Fer-1 (Fig. 4E). As expected, the injured lungs of septic mice exhibited typical ferroptosis morphology. Immunohistochemical staining for 4-HNE, a highly reactive aldehyde produced during lipid peroxidation and a marker of oxidative damage, showed a significant increase in 4-HNE-positive cells in the LPS group compared to the control group. CPI-613 treatment markedly reduced the number of 4-HNE-positive cells (Fig. 4F). Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed characteristic ferroptosis-related mitochondrial changes in the lung tissues of septic mice, including mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced size, and diminished cristae (Fig. 4G). These alterations were alleviated by treatment with CPI-613 or Fer-1. Furthermore, increased levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and Fe2+ were observed in the LPS group than control group, along with reduced GSH/GSSG ratios. CPI-613 treatment reversed these changes, with Fer-1 serving as a positive control for ferroptosis inhibition (Fig. 4H, I, and J). To further validate the involvement of ferroptosis, we directly assessed lipid peroxidation and cell death in macrophages. C11-BODIPY 581/591 staining, a specific probe for lipid ROS, was performed in alveolar macrophages. As shown in Fig. 4K, LPS stimulation significantly increased the MFI of oxidized C11-BODIPY compared with the control group, indicating elevated lipid peroxidation. Notably, pretreatment with CPI-613 markedly reduced the MFI to levels comparable with the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1, suggesting that CPI-613 suppresses lipid ROS accumulation. We next quantified cell death using the LDH release assay in RAW264.7 cells. As shown in Fig. 4L, exposure to LPS/IFN-γ for 24 h resulted in a marked increase in LDH release (P < 0.001). Pretreatment with CPI-613 or Fer-1 reduced LDH release (both P < 0.001). These findings demonstrate that CPI-613 activates the Nrf2/GPX4 axis and inhibits ferroptosis in vivo, which may be the mechanism of attenuating sepsis.

CPI-613 suppresses LPS-induced ferroptosis by upregulating Nrf2

Next, we investigated whether the OGDH inhibitor CPI-613 attenuates ferroptosis in vitro. Cell viability assay revealed that CPI-613, similar to Fer-1, reduced LPS/IFN-γ-induced cell death in BMDMs (Fig. 5A). A hallmark of ferroptosis, besides the accumulation of ferrous ions, is excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS). Using the DCFH-DA probe to measure ROS levels in BMDMs, we found that LPS/IFN-γ increased intracellular ROS levels, which were reversed by treatment with CPI-613 or Fer-1 (Fig. 5B). Additionally, either CPI-613 or Fer-1 decreased the mRNA levels of LPS/IFN-γ-induced inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) in cells (Fig. 5C). Next, we examined the expression changes of ferroptosis antioxidant markers (SLC7A11 and GPX4) and the glutathione metabolism pathway (Nrf2 and HO-1) at both protein and mRNA levels. Western blot results showed that the protein levels of Nrf2, HO-1, SLC7A11, and GPX4 were significantly reduced in the M1 group but upregulated after treatment with CPI-613 or Fer-1 (Fig. 5D). RT-qPCR results were consistent, except for HMOX-1, which showed compensatory upregulation in the M1 group (Fig. 5E). Fluorescence microscopy revealed that LPS/IFN-γ stimulation decreased GPX4 expression in BMDMs, while treatment with CPI-613 or Fer-1 significantly increased GPX4 levels (Fig. 5F). Previous studies have indicated that the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway is not only a key regulator of ferroptosis, but also plays a critical role in regulating cellular resistance to oxidative stress and eliminating ROS. Indeed, we found that CPI-613 treatment also significantly reduced the protein and mRNA levels of ferroptosis antioxidant markers (SLC7A11 and GPX4) and glutathione metabolism pathway components (Nrf2 and HO-1) in LPS/IFN-γ-induced RAW264.7 cells (Figure S5). To further explore the underlying mechanism, we transfected RAW264.7 cells with small interfering RNA targeting Nrf2 (si-Nrf2). Nrf2 siRNA was transfected into RAW264.7 cells 48 h before LPS/IFN-γ stimulation, followed by assessment of ferroptosis and inflammation. Western blot results and quantitative real-time PCR analysis confirmed that Nrf2 protein level was significantly downregulated in RAW264.7 cells after transfection, indicating successful Nrf2 knockdown with si-Nrf2-3 (Fig. 5G). Further, immunoblot analysis and qPCR analysis demonstrated that LPS/IFN-γ pretreatment downregulated the expression of antioxidant markers (SLC7A11 and GPX4) and glutathione metabolism pathway components (Nrf2 and HO-1) compared to the control group, CPI-613 treatment promoted the levels of these anti-ferroptosis proteins. However, the anti-ferroptosis effects of CPI-613 treatment were abolished by siNrf2. si-NRF2 pretreatment further reduced the expression of anti-ferroptosis proteins relative to the LPS/IFN-γ group, and under this condition, CPI-613 could not exert the anti-ferroptosis effects (Fig. 5H, I). Similarly, using ML385, a specific Nrf2 inhibitor, we found that the anti-ferroptosis effects of the CPI-613 were also eliminated (Fig. 5J, K). After genetic or pharmacological inhibition of Nrf2, the anti-ferroptosis effects could not be rescued by CPI-613 treatment. These data suggest that CPI-613 suppresses LPS-induced septic lung injury and ferroptosis via upregulating the Nrf2/GPX4 axis. This mechanism highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting OGDH to mitigate ferroptosis and inflammation in sepsis.

OGDH enzyme activity is closely associated with the severity and prognosis of sepsis in patients

In accordance with the Sepsis-3 guidelines [28], we investigated whether OGDH enzyme activity is altered in the plasma of patients with bacterial sepsis. The demographic and clinical characteristics of a cohort of 31 patients diagnosed with bacterial sepsis were analyzed. Compared to healthy controls, plasma samples from septic patients exhibited significantly higher OGDH enzyme activity (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, plasma samples from the non-survivor group or septic shock group showed higher levels of OGDH enzyme activity compared to the survivor group or non-septic shock group (Fig. 6B). OGDH enzyme activity levels were positively correlated with SOFA scores, arterial blood lactate levels, and LDH levels (Fig. 6C, D, and F). In contrast, PaO2/FiO2 ratios were negatively correlated with OGDH enzyme activity (Fig. 6E). Additionally, a positive correlation was observed between OGDH enzyme activity levels and serum concentrations of inflammatory markers, including TNF, IL-1β, PCT, and CRP (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the role of OGDH in macrophage polarization and ferroptosis during S-ALI. OGDH activity was significantly elevated in both LPS-challenged mice and septic patients, correlating with disease severity. Pharmacological inhibition of OGDH with CPI-613 attenuated M1 macrophage polarization and ferroptosis, thereby alleviating lung injury. Mechanistically, CPI-613 increased the α-KG/succinate ratio, reduced M1 polarization, and activated the Nrf2/GPX4 antioxidant pathway. These findings identify OGDH as a therapeutic target for S-ALI and support repurposing CPI-613 for non-oncological inflammatory diseases.

The imbalance in alveolar macrophage polarization, particularly the overactivation of M1 macrophages, is a primary driver of multi-organ dysfunction and mortality in sepsis. Without reliable biomarkers or targeted therapies, the survival and prognosis of sepsis patients remain uncertain. Emerging evidence indicates that TCA cycle metabolites—including α-KG, fumarate, and succinate—play a significant role in regulating macrophage polarization and function. Changes in macrophage polarization status and immune function are closely linked to cellular metabolic reprogramming [34]. In M1 inflammatory macrophages, glycolysis is significantly enhanced, while the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle is disrupted at multiple critical nodes, leading to substantial accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates [11]. In the present study, we observed reduced M1 macrophage polarization following OGDH inhibition, suggesting that metabolic intermediates such as α-KG and succinate serve as key regulators of immune cell function. This finding aligns with earlier reports that succinate accumulation promotes pro-inflammatory responses [32, 35], whereas α-KG exerts anti-inflammatory effects [33, 36].

OGDH is a key regulatory enzyme in the TCA cycle, catalyzing the rate-limiting step of α-KG oxidative decarboxylation. Enhanced OGDH activity in M1 macrophages has been shown to promote the conversion of α-KG to succinyl-CoA, reducing intracellular α-KG and leading to succinate accumulation [16, 37]. Consistent with these findings, our data show that OGDH activity is markedly upregulated in inflammatory macrophages, and its inhibition significantly increases the α-KG/succinate ratio and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion.

Ferroptosis, driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, plays a key role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including sepsis [38]. Studies have shown that ferroptosis is a major mode of cell death in macrophages, with M1-like polarized macrophages being particularly susceptible [39]. We therefore hypothesized that OGDH may increase ferroptosis susceptibility by promoting inflammatory macrophage polarization. In this study, RNA-seq analysis revealed that the ferroptosis and glutathione metabolism pathways were among the most significantly enriched. Following measurement of several ferroptosis-related indicators, we found that an OGDH inhibitor reduced intracellular ROS, MDA, and Fe²⁺ levels while increasing the GSH/GSSG ratio; moreover, it enhanced the mRNA and protein levels of Nrf2, HO-1, SLC7A11, and GPX4, indicating that OGDH inhibition effectively mitigates ferroptosis. We further determined that CPI-613 suppresses ferroptosis in macrophages through an Nrf2-dependent mechanism. Both genetic silencing of Nrf2 via siRNA and pharmacological inhibition with ML385 reversed CPI-613-mediated ferroptosis suppression, confirming Nrf2 as the central effector in this protective pathway. Nrf2 is a critical regulator of antioxidant defense, transcriptionally controlling multiple genes involved in iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation [40–42]. Nrf2 upregulation promotes transcription of target genes such as GPX4, SLC7A11, NQO1, Gclc, Gclm, and HO-1. Collectively, these findings suggest that Nrf2-mediated metabolic regulation and iron homeostasis are crucial for shaping the inflammatory response in sepsis.

Recent publications have established that glutamine acts as a primary substrate for replenishing the TCA cycle and sustaining bioenergetic demands when key mitochondrial enzymes in this cycle are dysfunctional [43, 44]. Moreover, glutamine serves as an essential metabolic precursor in the synthesis of both GSH and NADPH, thereby supporting the NADPH-dependent glutathione antioxidant system [27]. Dai et al. [45] reported that OGDHL-negative hepatoma cells exhibit higher concentrations of NADPH and GSH than OGDHL-reconstituted cells, indicating activation of a robust antioxidant response. These observations are consistent with our data and further substantiate the present findings. We therefore hypothesized that metabolic reprogramming of macrophages following OGDH inhibition supplies abundant NADPH and GSH, which may represent a key mechanism by which OGDH inhibitors attenuate ferroptosis and inflammatory responses.

More importantly, we confirmed that OGDH enzyme activity was higher in patients with septic shock and in non-survivors, and that its levels were positively correlated with SOFA scores, arterial blood lactate, LDH, and several inflammatory cytokines, but negatively correlated with the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio. Consistent with our findings, Hao et al. [17] reported elevated OGDH expression in both a CLP-induced pulmonary injury cell model and lung tissues from ALI mice. Their study further showed that OGDH downregulation alleviated sepsis-associated ALI. Notably, OGDH expression was positively correlated with levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α). Collectively, these preliminary data suggest that serum OGDH activity correlates with disease severity; larger, serially sampled cohorts are needed to evaluate its independent prognostic value and to support formal biomarker qualification.

The critical role of AMs in S-ALI is supported by independent studies demonstrating that clodronate liposome-mediated AM depletion attenuates LPS-induced lung injury [46–48]. Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although our data confirm OGDH expression in alveolar macrophages during S-ALI, we cannot exclude the contribution of other lung cell types to OGDH-mediated inflammation. The precise role of OGDH in modulating macrophage polarization and ferroptosis in sepsis-associated lung injury warrants further investigation using clodronate liposome‑induced AM depletion or myeloid‑specific OGDH knockout mice. Second, given the essential role of the TCA cycle in cell survival and function, in vivo interventions targeting excessive TCA cycle activity in immune cells must not only avoid disrupting the energy metabolism necessary for life but also maintain metabolic homeostasis through appropriate dose control. Finally, the specific signaling pathway linking ferroptosis and OGDH regulation remains unidentified. CPI-613 may exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects via additional pathways, and the molecular mechanism by which it activates Nrf2 signaling requires further elucidation.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies OGDH as a key metabolic regulator in sepsis pathophysiology. OGDH enzyme activity is markedly elevated in sepsis and closely correlates with disease severity and poor clinical outcomes, supporting its potential as both a biomarker and therapeutic target. Pharmacological inhibition of OGDH with CPI-613 provides dual therapeutic benefits by reprogramming macrophage immunometabolism—normalizing the α-KG/succinate ratio to reduce M1 polarization markers—and activating the Nrf2/GPX4 axis to suppress ferroptosis. Given its established clinical safety profile in oncology trials, CPI-613 represents a translational-ready candidate for sepsis-associated ARDS therapy, meriting further trials to assess its efficacy in reversing immunometabolic paralysis in human sepsis.
