Loneliness and rumination serially mediate the relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction among Turkish university students
Authors: Gülçin Güler Öztekin, Hurizat Hande Turp, Nouf Abdullah Alshehri, Abdulmohsen Mohammed Abdullah Alkhulayfi, Abdullah Alkhulayfi, Murat Yıldırım
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread use of the internet brings with it both advantages and disadvantages. The current research sought to investigate the serial mediating role of loneliness and rumination in the link between social anxiety and internet addiction. This study consisted of 1,717 Turkish university students. The participants’ mean age was 21.70 (SD = 1.90). The findings indicated that social anxiety was positively related to internet addiction. Loneliness served as a mediator in the link between social anxiety and internet addiction. Rumination mediated the effect of social anxiety on internet addiction. The results also supported the serial mediation model, specifically revealing that individuals with social anxiety may have high levels of loneliness, which may be associated with high levels of rumination, and this was related to high levels of internet addiction. The results of this study indicate the need for psychoeducational programs for university students to raise awareness about the negative consequences of social anxiety, loneliness, rumination, and excessive internet use.Keywords: Social anxiety, Loneliness, Rumination, Internet addiction, University studentsSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

With the rapid development of technology, the internet has unquestionably transformed various aspects of life, such as communication, access to information and entertainment. This transformation, alongside its numerous advantages, has also brought it potential dangers. One of these dangers is the risk of internet addiction. Internet addiction can be considered a non-chemical dependency on internet use. It is characterized by an obsessive desire to access the internet when offline and an inability to get offline when online1. This situation harms the functionality of people in their daily lives. For example, internet-addicted youth were more likely to report psychological distress, and their school engagement was affected in many ways2. The degree of addiction to the Internet was effective in reducing responsibility and increasing procrastination3. Individuals with internet addiction often experience symptoms of distress, social withdrawal, loss of control, feelings of anger, and family conflicts, indicating impairments in psychological, social, and family functioning4. In addition, a systematic review showed that internet addiction was increasing in new generations, and variables such as an increase in individuality, a decrease in socialization and a decrease in cultural adaptation played an important role in this increase5. Internet addiction was negatively associated with resilience and self-control6, psychological well-being, sleep quality7, quality of life and mental health8,9. Internet addiction reduces a person’s self-confidence, self-esteem, academic self-efficacy, and social self-efficacy and leads to loneliness10. Internet-related problems were found to heighten negative emotional problems (depression, loneliness) and reduce positive emotional problems (happiness, life satisfaction)11. Focusing on internet addiction and identifying influencing factors to prevent or reduce these negative consequences will pave the way for healthy functioning and mental health.

Social anxiety refers to the emotional experience of fear, nervousness, and dread caused by real or imagined scenarios in interpersonal interactions12. Social anxiety was related to peer functioning, including peer rejection, friendship quality, and peer victimization13, and social satisfaction14. Social anxiety was a factor that negatively impacted the youth’s academic engagement15 and mental health16. Social anxiety has also been included in previous literature as a psychological variable that leads to behavioral addictions regarding internet use. Socially anxious individuals were susceptible to mobile phone addiction17. , YouTube addiction18, problematic smartphone use19, and problematic internet use20. One systematic review showed that individuals with social anxiety perceived online video games as safer places than face-to-face interactions, which predisposed individuals to problematic online gaming21. In addition, social anxiety was a powerful factor that could trigger internet addiction22, and internet addiction symptoms were predicted by social anxiety23,24. These studies emphasize the potential adverse impact of social anxiety on internet-related problems and the need to mitigate this negativity.

The mediating effects of loneliness and rumination

Humans are inherently social. A lack of understanding and insufficient social support play a critical role in the development of loneliness. Loneliness is the emotional distress that arises when an individual perceives their social relationships as insufficient or less fulfilling than desired25. Loneliness is not only associated with mental health challenges such as depression and anxiety, but also poses a risk factor for the positive mental health of individuals, which is important for healthy maturation26. Furthermore, previous studies have provided evidence of the adverse impact of social anxiety on loneliness. Individuals with social anxiety were at elevated risk for loneliness27. Individuals suffering from social anxiety were more likely to experience loneliness because they limited their resources to make meaningful social connections by avoiding social situations28. Social anxiety symptoms play an important role as a potential precursor to feelings of loneliness and depression29,30. Socially anxious and lonely people may prefer online communication and activities on the internet. Internet use enables individuals to meet essential needs for self-expression, social interaction, and identity exploration31. Indeed, lonely people avoided face-to-face social interactions and turned to the internet. Previous literature has also demonstrated the mediating role of loneliness. Social anxiety was positively associated with loneliness, which had a relationship with mobile phone addiction32, low coping skills and internet addiction33. The association between psychological satisfaction and smartphone addiction was mediated through loneliness34. Social anxiety was linked with smartphone addiction, and loneliness mediated their relationship35. Individuals with social anxiety and loneliness engaged more problematically online, potentially seeking social support on internet-related platforms such as social media to compensate for the lack of face-to-face support36. These studies reveal that social anxiety affects individuals’ loneliness, which increases the likelihood of internet addiction. Therefore, we assume that loneliness may mediate the impact of social anxiety on internet addiction.

Rumination is related to repetitive thoughts about personal problems and is experienced as involuntary and unintended. Rumination is a state that elevates, perpetuates and exacerbates depressive mood37. Rumination undermines the well-being of individuals38. Socially anxious individuals engaged in post-event evaluation that sustained anxiety, and this process involved rumination39. Rumination was influenced by the degree to which socially anxious individuals perceived their performance negatively and allocated attentional resources in response to this unfavorable self-image40. In addition, rumination was positively associated with internet addiction41 and digital addiction42. Rumination was positively linked to the severity of various internet use disorders, a pattern that remained consistent across multiple technology-related problematic behaviors. These included excessive smartphone use, internet addiction, compulsive social media engagement, problematic online gaming, online gambling addiction, and compulsive online shopping43. Moreover, a growing body of literature has demonstrated the mediating role of rumination. Rumination mediated the relationship between social anxiety and problematic Facebook use44. Social anxiety explained variability in rumination, which was associated with problematic smartphone use levels45. Because rumination is related to social anxiety and problematic internet use, rumination may also be an important mechanism that explains the relationships between social anxiety and internet addiction.

Previous studies have also shown that loneliness and rumination had a positive association and acted as a chain mediator. In stressful situations, greater loneliness was related to higher levels of rumination46. Frequently feeling lonely and then ruminating on the feeling of loneliness activated and maintained the close relationship between loneliness, rumination, and depressive symptoms47. Social anxiety was positively related to the problematic use of conversational artificial intelligence, and loneliness and rumination serially mediated this relationship48. Based on these results, we determine that loneliness and rumination may act as serial mediators.

Present study

Recent data in Türkiye show that internet usage is at its highest level among individuals between the ages of 16 and 3449. This age range coincides with university years. It is known that the risk of addiction increases with excessive internet use50. Therefore, university students in the risk group were determined as the sample of the study. Additionally, the above-mentioned studies have provided evidence that social anxiety has a positive impact on loneliness, rumination, and internet addiction. However, to our knowledge, there is no study investigating the serial mediation effect of loneliness and rumination on the social anxiety-internet addiction link. Therefore, we aimed to examine the following hypotheses:

H1: Social anxiety is expected to have a positive correlation with internet addiction.

H2: Loneliness is expected to serve as a mediator in the relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction.

H3: Rumination is expected to serve as a mediator in the relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction.

H4: Loneliness and rumination would serially mediate the association between social anxiety and internet addiction.

Methods

Participants

A total of 1717 Turkish university students between the ages of 18–29 years were recruited for the study. The mean age of students was 21.70 (SD = 1.90). 1409 (82.1%) of the participants were female, and 308 (17.9%) were male. 527 (30.7%) of the participants were students at the Faculty of Education, 255 (14.9%) were at the Faculty of Science and Letters, 369 (21.4%) were at the Faculty of Health, and 566 (33%) were at the Faculty of Islamic Studies. 301 (17.5%) participants were freshmen, 440 (25.6%) were sophomores, 590 (34.4%) were juniors, and 386 (22.5%) were seniors. 1045 (60.9%) participants were staying in dormitories, 358 (20.8%) participants were staying at home, and 314 (18.3%) participants were residing with their families.

Measures

Social anxiety scale

This measure was developed to determine the social anxiety levels of individuals51 and adapted to Turkish culture by Can and Bozgün52. The scale comprises 12 items, which are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = I completely disagree, 5 = I completely agree). The scale includes three sub-dimensions: social avoidance and distress in new situations, fear of negative evaluation, and social avoidance and distress in general situations. A sample item is “I worry that others won’t like me”. High scores indicate high levels of social anxiety. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91.

UCLA loneliness scale

This measure was developed to determine the loneliness levels of individuals53 and adapted to the Turkish language by Doğan, Çötok54. The scale comprises 8 items and is a 4-point Likert-type scale (1 = not suitable at all, 4 = completely suitable). The sample item is “I feel excluded”. High scores show high levels of loneliness. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.81.

Ruminative response scale

This measure was developed to identify the rumination levels of individuals55. Nolen-Hoeksema and Morrow56 designed the short version and Erdur Baker and Bugay57 adapted it to Turkish culture. The scale consists of 10 items scored on a 4-point Likert-type scale (1 = never, 4 = always). The scale includes two sub-dimensions: brooding and reflection. The sample item is “Within the past 2 weeks, how often do you think ‘Why do I have problems and other people don’t?” High scores demonstrate that individuals engage in high levels of ruminative thought. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was .81.

Young’s internet addiction test

This scale was developed to measure the internet addiction levels of young people58 and adapted to Turkish culture by Kutlu et al.59. This measure reflects a generalized form of problematic internet use rather than any specific subtype. The scale consists of 12 items and is a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = never, 5 = always). The sample item is “How often do you stay online longer than you planned?”. High scores show high levels of internet addiction. For this study, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as 0.89.

Procedure

Before conducting the study, the ethical protocol was obtained by the Ağrı İbrahim Çeçen University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Ethic Code: 124704). The data of this study were collected through an online survey with Google Forms. Participants were recruited using the convenience sampling method. The criteria for inclusion in the study were being an internet user, being a university student, and being willing to participate. The first page of the survey form contained the consent form and information about the research and participant rights. Participants who approved the consent form could answer the research questions.

Data analyses

Preliminary analyses were performed to describe sample characteristics and study variables. Skewness and kurtosis scores were used to assess normality by considering the acceptable threshold between + 1.0 and − 1.0 suggested by Tabachnick and Fidell60. An Independent Samples T-test was performed to determine whether there is a gender difference in the study variables. PROCESS-Macro v4.2 was utilized to test the serial mediation effect with Model 6. The 5,000 bootstrapping method was employed with a 95% confidence interval to assess the indirect effects of serial mediators. According to Hayes61, the findings of mediation analysis should be interpreted with squared multiple correlations (R2) values and unstandardized path estimates (β). Therefore, the results were interpreted by referring to these values. All analyses were performed with SPSS version 27.

Correlation analysis

Social anxiety was positively and significantly correlated with loneliness (r = .46, p < .001), rumination (r = .35, p < .001), and internet addiction (r = .42, p < .001). Loneliness (r = .36, p < .001) and rumination (r = .40, p < .001) were positively correlated with internet addiction.

Gender differences

The growing literature on internet addiction highlights gender differences62,63. To address this, the Independent Samples T-test was conducted. The results are presented in Table 2. The gender difference in social anxiety, loneliness and internet addiction was found to be statistically insignificant (p > .05). This indicated that there was no statistically significant difference between the social anxiety, loneliness and internet addiction levels of male and female participants. The gender difference in rumination was found to be statistically significant (p < .05). Females had higher levels of rumination than males.

Loneliness significantly predicted rumination (β = 0.32, p < .001). Loneliness (β = 0.31, p < .001) and rumination (β = 0.44, p < .001) significantly predicted internet addiction. Social anxiety, rumination, and loneliness explained 27% of the variance in internet addiction (F = 211.65; p < .001). Discussion

Excessive internet use among university students poses risks that can lead to addiction. This study reveals significant results in reducing internet addiction. The results of the present study indicated that social anxiety was positively related to internet addiction. This result supports previous literature on the positive relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction. A study found that social anxiety potentially predisposed individuals to internet addiction64. Socially anxious individuals exhibited heightened interpersonal sensitivity, making them particularly reactive to external criticism and rejection65. Individuals with heightened social anxiety tend to favor online interactions over in-person communication66. In this regard, the internet is an alternative means of relieving social anxiety, but the timing and frequency of use may be difficult to control. Low time management may enhance internet addiction67.

This research found that loneliness served as a mediator in the link between social anxiety and internet addiction. The participants who experienced social anxiety reported high levels of loneliness, which was associated with high internet addiction. Similar to our results, social anxiety was positively associated with loneliness, which was related to internet addiction, suggesting an urgent need to reduce the feeling of loneliness and social anxiety levels of the participants68. The relationship between social anxiety and mobile phone addiction was mediated by loneliness32. Higher social anxiety had a relationship with internet addiction through greater loneliness and an increased tendency to use negative coping styles33. Social anxiety had a significant relationship with loneliness69 and diminished more in online interaction70. However, spending too much time on the internet may lead to addiction10.

The results also revealed that rumination partially mediated the link between social anxiety and internet addiction. Socially anxious university students stated higher rumination levels and, subsequently, higher internet addiction. Consistent with our results, social anxiety heightened the likelihood of fixating on negative emotions, resulting in rumination, which was related to problematic smartphone use71. Individuals with social anxiety repeatedly focus on negative emotions and corresponding events, which are the characteristics of rumination, and this process was linked with problematic smartphone use72.

The most valuable result of this study was that the relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction was serially mediated by rumination and loneliness. University students with social anxiety reported greater loneliness and rumination, which was related to higher internet addiction. There is only one study in the literature on the serial mediation of loneliness and rumination. In Hu, Mao48 study, social anxiety was positively related to the problematic use of conversational artificial intelligence, and rumination and loneliness had a serial mediating effect on this relationship. Humans are a fundamentally social species with a need to belong and a strong drive for closeness and companionship73. Socially anxious individuals struggle to form and sustain relationships due to their heightened anxiety, which often results in avoidance of interpersonal interactions and social withdrawal, ultimately increasing their sense of loneliness. These people may try to meet their social needs on the internet. Lonely individuals who continuously dwell on their negative experiences may develop an excessive reliance on the internet as an unhealthy coping mechanism.

The present study provides important theoretical implications and practical applications. In terms of theoretical implications, the present research extends previous research on internet addiction by confirming that socially anxious people are more likely to develop high loneliness, high rumination, and a high probability of internet addiction. From a practical perspective, the present study contributes to clarifying a new effect pathway on internet addiction. This study confirmed that individuals with social anxiety may have high levels of loneliness, which may be associated with high levels of rumination, and this was linked with high levels of internet addiction. The study results suggest that loneliness and rumination may be potential targets for future intervention research, which is needed to establish causality. University-based psychological providers can develop psychoeducation programs for students regarding the negative consequences of social anxiety, loneliness, rumination, and excessive internet use. They can screen students with social anxiety and implement group activities or cognitive-behavioural strategies to help students manage these psychological processes.

The findings of this study should be considered in light of certain limitations. First, self-report measures carry a risk of bias. Future studies may utilize alternative data collection methods, such as qualitative or mixed methods. Second, this cross-sectional study fails to investigate causal relationships. In future studies, researchers should focus on longitudinal and experimental studies. Third, the convenience sampling method affects the validity of the study. Future studies can examine the relationships between study variables using a random sampling method. Fourth, the sample of this study consisted largely of female students, indicating a gender imbalance. Future studies should focus on gender balance to address this. Finally, internet addiction was measured with a single-factor scale, and we used the term in a generalized sense in this study. Problematic internet use is associated with Internet Gaming Disorder, Social Media Addiction, online gambling, and compulsive information seeking, encompassing different psychological underpinnings, risk factors, and demographic profiles. Therefore, researchers can replicate this study using multifactorial scales and scales measuring these problematic behaviors.

In conclusion, the internet offers a vast array of opportunities, including gaming, work, social interaction, entertainment, shopping, and numerous other activities and services. As a highly diverse and multidimensional space, it provides an extensive range of experiences within a unique virtual environment. However, excessive engagement with the internet can increase the risk of addiction74. Therefore, it is necessary to gain awareness about this issue and to address ways to reduce the risk. Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the serial mediating impacts of loneliness and rumination in the relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction. The findings showed a positive relationship between social anxiety and internet addiction. Loneliness and rumination mediated this relationship both separately and serially. These results suggest that students should be informed about the negative effects of social anxiety, loneliness, rumination and internet use. These programs can raise awareness by teaching healthy coping strategies.
