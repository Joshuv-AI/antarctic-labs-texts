Stable magnetic soft structures
Authors: Hong Wang, Yunming Zhang, Xu Liu, Hongde Li, Xi Chen, Zhuoqun Cao, Qiang Luo, Ziyu Ren, Wenqi Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Magnetic soft structures are highly versatile in unstructured environments, making them attractive for minimally invasive medical devices. However, this versatility also renders them susceptible to unintended deformations under fluctuating magnetic fields. The challenge is further compounded by medical imaging limitations that hinder accurate estimation of device pose and shape and by anatomical boundaries that enforce misalignment with the field. Collectively, these factors can cause uncontrolled shape change and functional failure. Here, we focus on slender magnetic soft structures to investigate their stability under misaligned fields. Our anisotropic design, achieved through flexural joints, yields ~52-fold higher bending stiffness in the preferred direction and ~18-fold higher torsional stiffness than isotropic beams. This structure serves as a building block for stable spiral tentacles, helices, planar sheets, expandable devices, cubes, and three-dimensional tessellation. We validate robustness through wormlike self-propulsion, guidewire navigation with 30-fold lower insertion force, and capsule peristaltic locomotion, advancing safer, more controllable devices.

INTRODUCTION

Functional soft materials are extensively used in robotic setups due to their versatility and adaptability in unstructured environments (1–3). Notably, magnetic soft materials can be remotely actuated by external energy sources, facilitating multimodal motion by programming their magnetization profiles (4–7). Their untethered actuation and high degree of control render them well suited for miniature magnetic robots, which have notable potential applications in medical devices (8–11), including minimally invasive surgery (12, 13), target drug delivery, and diagnostic procedures (14, 15).

To achieve these diverse functionalities, various structures of magnetic soft materials have been developed, such as slender (16, 17) or sheetlike configurations (4, 18–20), origami-inspired architectures (21, 22), bistable mechanisms (23, 24), cilia-based designs (19, 25), and modular structures (26). Among them, magnetic continuum beam is one of the popular designs. Their minimalist geometry and ease of modeling enable the development of precise control using external magnetic fields (27–29). Such magnetic beam structures have demonstrated notable potential in minimally invasive biomedical applications, including guidewires (8, 9), catheters (30, 31), and other medical devices (14, 32–34) at a small scale.

Nevertheless, the translation of these innovations into clinical practice has not yet been realized. One potential reason concerns the functional robustness of these devices. From a mechanical perspective, achieving desired control of these devices requires accurate alignment of the external magnetic field under cameras (10, 28, 35, 36). If the B field is disturbed, they are highly susceptible to unintended deformations, leading to structural instability, as detailed in movie S1 and note S1. When the magnetization of the structure is antiparallel with the external B field, the interaction is even intrinsically unstable (see note S1.2). Therefore, conventional magnetic soft beams or sheets are prone to functional failures or reduced operational efficiency due to kinking, twisting, and undesired bending (Fig. 1A). These instabilities can escalate into catastrophic structural behaviors in a more complicated design (Fig. 1B). Such functional failure would raise concerns for clinical applications.

Aside from the internal factors discussed above, two external conditions further compromise the stability of the structures made by conventional magnetic materials. First, the tortuous physiological environment—combined with cardiac, respiratory, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract motions—induces continuous tissue deformations that dynamically alter the material’s pose, shape, and relative position with respect to the external B field [Fig. 1C (a)]. Second, the limited resolution and restricted imaging planes of current clinically available modalities, along with constraints on plane orientation, often hinder accurate estimation of the pose and deformation of micro- or millimeter-scale devices [Fig. 1C (b) and note S2]. The above two conditions can lead to a critical feedback deficit, in which initial misinterpretations of deformation and pose propagate through field misalignment, ultimately escalating into severe structural instabilities that jeopardize clinical safety. Therefore, although some studies have demonstrated precise closed-loop control in free-space or ex vivo environments (10, 37, 38), clinical implementation remains considerably more challenging.

Although modifying external clinical equipment and setups is notoriously challenging, few studies have investigated the intrinsic instability of magnetic soft structures themselves, even in basic forms such as beams and sheets. A relatively related reported approach involves incorporating a notch in the soft material (39), enabling directional folding or bending under magnetic actuation. However, as the thinned region is made of soft elastomer, the notch often becomes the weakest point in the structure, more likely resulting in twisting and buckling under magnetic field misalignment. Some research also explored the use of intrinsic mechanical anisotropy in materials, such as liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs), to achieve diverse behaviors under multiple external stimuli (40). In these studies, the LCE can enhance stability to some extent, but it exhibits a modest stiffness anisotropy ratio through programming its mesogen alignment (41, 42). This value is typically less than 5:1 (parallel : perpendicular to the mesogen alignment), which is insufficient to maintain structural stability under a misaligned magnetic field.

In this study, we first focus on the magnetic soft slender structure, a fundamental and widely adopted structure in minimally invasive interventions. By introducing anisotropic stiffness via compliant joints, the design enables desirable mechanical responses while suppressing undesired ones. This structure then serves as a versatile building block for more complex structural variants—including spiral tentacles, helices, planar sheet, expandable devices, cubes, and three-dimensional (3D) tessellation—spanning from 1D to 3D. These variants respond reliably to intended magnetic inputs while maintaining robustness against external perturbations. To demonstrate the robustness of the proposed structure, we constructed a wormlike, undulating magnetic beam that exhibited improved stability under deliberately misaligned magnetic fields with over twofold speed increase. This enhanced performance further supports the creation of longer, multiperiod worms capable of dragging a guidewire through tortuous tubular phantoms, a task where conventional magnetic soft materials often fail due to deformation or kinematic breakdown. These advancements culminate in a harmony locomotion mode driven by a rotating magnetic field: intestine-like peristalsis. This locomotion strategy, previously unattainable using conventional magnetic materials, is particularly well suited for harmonic and gentle navigation within the human GI tract.

RESULTS

Design of the proposed structure and its anisotropic stiffness

Although totally soft creatures do widely exist, more biomechanical structures, such as snake, seahorse tail, beetle tarsus, and human spine, are composed of a combination of both soft and rigid components (43–45). These structures typically comprise repeating rigid units interspersed with compliant tissue, enabling stable, directionally controlled morphing. Such mixed-material strategies are a widespread evolutionary solution for balancing strength, flexibility, and precise control (46). With such inspiration, we developed a magnetic soft composite structure consisting of multiple backbones. Each backbone is composed of 5-μm NdFeB particles embedded in an aluminum-filled epoxy resin matrix (EpoxAcast 655) at 33.3% weight ratio. As a result, the backbone has an elastic modulus of 49.6 GPa. In addition, a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) spine was manufactured to run through the center of the backbone array. The overall design was fabricated using a two-step molding process detailed in fig. S2A. The first positive mold, fabricated via stereolithography (SLA) and microArch S230 printing, achieves a resolution down to 2 μm, ensuring sufficient precision for millimeter-scale devices, particularly the spacing between the two backbones [Fig. 1D (b)], to maintain stable deformation.

Figure 2 (A to D) and movie S2 compare the kinematics of a conventional cylindrical magnetic beam with that of the proposed design. Both beams were axially magnetized along its length and clamped at one end. They were tested under a B field provided by a rotating permanent magnet. As shown in Fig. 2 (A and C), the conventional beam tends to rotate freely in all directions, whereas the proposed beam suppresses undesired deformation, enabling bending strictly within the desired plane. The proposed structure can also maintain its stability when wrapped with extra magnetic soft materials to form a more continuous cylindrical beam that is visually identical to the beam in Fig. 2A, as demonstrated in fig. S3.

We quantitatively measured the bending stiffness in two orthogonal directions, as well as the twisting stiffness, of the two beams (Fig. 2, E to G). Beams made by the proposed structure shows strong anisotropy in bending stiffness. It has a high stiffness of 1320 N/m in the out-of-plane direction and a low stiffness of 25.4 N/m in the in-plane direction. In contrast, beams made by the conventional material have nearly equal bending stiffness in both directions, measured at 39.1 and 40.8 N/m. The proposed structure also substantially enhances the twisting stiffness, increasing it from 8.74 μN·m in the conventional material to 165.32 μN·m. This anisotropic stiffness allows the beam to freely bend in the desired direction while suppressing both undesired bending and twisting. This behavior underpins the stable deformation of more complex magnetic soft structures discussed in later sections.

Design justification and geometric parameter characterization

As the most straightforward approach, directional stiffness might be modified by adjusting the aspect ratio or cross-sectional shape of a beam made solely by magnetic soft material, without resorting to the backbone–soft spine structure proposed here. However, under the conditions examined in this study, such adjustments were proven insufficient. In confined spaces, geometric constraints limit the largest dimension (e.g., beam width), leaving only thickness or height adjustable. Within this constraint, fig. S6 illustrates the effect of aspect ratio variation. The results indicate that modifying the aspect ratio of a rectangular cross section alone cannot suppress undesired bending and distortion under the same external B field.

In addition, Fig. 3 (A to C) examines the effects of changing the cross-sectional shape and the inclusion of the soft spine, i.e., the thin film. To be more specific, the first three beams in Fig. 3A were fabricated from the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) matrix embedded with NdFeB magnetic particles. Therefore, they are labeled as PDMS-I, PDMS-II, and PDMS-III. PDMS-I featured a rectangular cross section (1.5 mm by 1.0 mm) and represents the conventional magnetic beam widely used in the previous literature. PDMS-II cut chisel shapes based on the first sample. PDMS-III further embedded a PET spine in the center.

The experimental setup for bending characterization is shown in Fig. 3B. The magnet was positioned 15 mm below the beam, oriented parallel to the xy plane. The B field was rotated 45° counterclockwise away from the y axis. Under this configuration, the beam experienced a dominant magnetic torque and a magnetic gradient force pulling it downward. Details of the magnetic field and its spatial gradient are provided in fig. S5. Under this setup, in-plane deflection, out-of-plane deflection, and twisting angles are clearly defined in Fig. 3B. They are visually distinguished in corresponding projection planes using green, blue, and orange; detailed snapshots on these projection planes can be found in fig. S7.

Figure 3C and fig. S7 show that the proposed design demonstrates a superior ability to suppress both out-of-plane deflection and twisting. The out-of-plane deflection angle and twisting angle were only 2.3° and 3.2°. In comparison, the other three samples showed values >15°. Despite effectively restricting undesired deformations, the proposed design maintained a high in-plane deflection. It achieved an in-plane angle greater than 40°, with little difference with PDMS-II, which only contained magnetic soft materials and did not have the proposed design. However, the large out-of-plane bending and twisting for PDMS-II further indicate that the presence of notches or chisel alone (39) is insufficient to suppress undesired deformation, highlighting its limitations in maintaining structural stability. Furthermore, a comparison of PDMS-II and PDMS-III shows that the insertion of a spine film can reduce out-of-plane bending due to increased stiffness in undesired direction. However, this modification also allows undesired twisting to be larger. Consequently, the presence of an inserted film alone is inadequate for achieving stable deformation. Therefore, an inserted film is not enough for stable deformation.

The above findings indicate that a proper combination of rigid backbone and soft spine is essential for achieving stable deformation. To further evaluate its long-term robustness, a durability test of the proposed beam material was conducted. As illustrated in Fig. 3D, the sample was placed in a 35-mT B field rotating at 120 rpm, causing it to swing left and right continuously for over 18 hours at 2 Hz. The material maintained stable performance throughout the test, with no observable degradation in bending amplitude after more than 130,000 actuation cycles. Such a result can be attributed to the superiority of PET (47). At the beginning of the study, various materials were evaluated as potential spine candidates, including foil paper (brittle), PDMS film (superelastic), and parafilm (plastic) (Fig. 3E). However, materials incorporating foil paper and PDMS were too fragile to be demolded successfully. Parafilm, on the other hand, exhibited substantial elongation during demolding and detached from the rigid blocks due to poor interfacial adhesion. In contrast, PET provided the necessary mechanical strength, stability, and robustness during the manufacturing. Its durability under repeated loading conditions makes it well suited for the current design and was therefore selected as the spine material for all subsequent experiments. Other similar linear elastic materials with comparable modulus and properties, such as polypropylene (PP) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), could also be potentially used as the spine.

To further optimize the performance of the proposed structure, we focused on three key variables: block distance (d), film thickness (t), and block angle (α). Although block length (l) and block height (h) were also analyzed, their effects were found to be negligible. Guided by the above relationship, the characterization was conducted as shown in Fig. 3 (F to H). Additional details of the characterization parameters are provided in table S1.

The key design parameter for the proposed structure is the block distance, d, which strongly influences undesired twisting (Fig. 3F). We characterized d from 10 to 600 μm. We found that a measured distance of 10 μm provides the best performance, yielding 44° in-plane deflection while keeping undesired out-of-plane deflection below 4°. As d increases from 10 to 90 μm, the in-plane deflection decreases, primarily because accumulation of small residual resin particles on the film suppresses the effective block distance and consequently the bending of the compliant joint (details shown in fig. S9). Beyond 90 μm, the influence of residual resin particles diminishes. Once d exceeds 150 μm, further increases reduce the advantages of the proposed design. At d = 0.2 mm, the out-of-plane deflection angle rises to 19.3°, and at d = 0.6 mm, it increases further to 40.4°. Correspondingly, undesired twisting grows from 8.43° to 37.7° over this range.

The second parameter characterized is the film thickness, t. As shown in Eq. 3, the in-plane deflection angle is inversely proportional to the cube of the thickness, a trend supported by the experimental results in Fig. 3G. However, as t decreases, a slight increase in undesired out-of-plane bending and twisting is also observed. Specifically, reducing t from 25 to 12.5 μm increases the out-of-plane and twisting angles from 3° to 10°. Notably, the reduction in desired, in-plane deflection between 12.5 and 25 μm is relatively minor and does not strictly follow the cubic trend predicted by theory. This deviation may be attributed to heat-induced stiffening of the 12.5-μm film edge during the laser-cutting process. Therefore, to achieve a trade-off between desired bending and suppression of undesired deformation, a film thickness of 25 μm was selected for all subsequent demonstrations.

The third parameter characterized is the block angle, α. In this study, the block length is kept constant. From the perspective of maximal bending angle at each joint, a larger α is preferred. However, a larger α also means in a smaller block size, as illustrated in the inset at the top right of Fig. 3H. This, in turn, reduces the magnetic moment of each backbone. Therefore, if the maximum bending angle is not fully achieved, the in-plane deflection angle decreases as α increases. Last, it has to be mentioned that the choice of α has little effect on undesired deflection and twisting. Across all tested angles, these undesired deformations can be kept below 5° by minimizing t.

The observed experimental trends related to the design parameters d, t, and α in Fig. 3 (F to H) are further supported by finite element analyses (FEAs), as shown in Fig. 3 (I and J). More details about the simulation in Fig. 3 (I and J) can be found in figs. S10 to S12, including the comparison of simulation and experimental result (fig. S11). Moreover, it is important to note that, as the external magnetic field becomes increasingly misaligned, its projection onto the desired deformation plane of the beam eventually vanishes, and the material stops deforming. However, with the proposed design, undesired deformations remain minimal during the process. In other words, the beam either deforms as intended or remains stationary—an especially desirable feature for applications requiring stringent safety. This behavior is illustrated by the simulation in Fig. 3K, which confirms the above phenomenon by showing the relationship between the deflection angles and the misalignment angle of the magnetic field, β.

1D to 3D design variants

Magnetic soft materials and structures have been widely proposed for tasks such as precise grasping, effective locomotion, and adaptive morphing configurations (48, 49). The proposed structures can be extended to construct diverse configurations to realize these functionalities while offering greater robustness.

First, the beam structure can be diversified into various forms to address different application needs. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, fig. S13, and movie S3, by logarithmically varying the size and shape of each backbone, the 1D structure can be transformed into a miniature octopus-like tentacle capable of morphing into a spiral configuration. This enables effective object manipulation (Fig. 4B), with potential applications in microsurgery (50).

In addition, the beam can be engineered to bend helically, like a vine (Fig. 4C). This is achieved by introducing an inclination angle across the height of the backbone (fig. S14A). The inclination angle can determine the minimum radius of the helix. Both single and double helices were demonstrated. Mounted on the tip of the catheter, such configurations could be applied in minimally invasive thrombectomy procedures (51), enabling stable clot encapsulation and extraction. The single helix can be used as a wireless magnetic robot too (Fig. 4D and movie S4). The experimental setup was divided into two sections: one with a normal rotating B field and the other with a tilted rotating magnetic field. The whole helical propulsion is smooth and fast. The performance of the helical-shaped robot remained unaffected by the misalignment, which may alter the deformation of the conventional magnetic soft materials (33). When the tube was blocked, the helical structure was able to propel toward the blockage and either push it (Fig. 4E, top) or drag it away (Fig. 4E, bottom).

Although the proposed material could be woven into 2D surfaces (fig. S16), its potential for constructing complex 3D architecture is particularly compelling. As shown in Fig. 4 (F to H), fig. S15, and movie S5, we fabricated an expandable device based on the proposed structure that mimics the function of a balloon catheter (52). The device demonstrated steerability (0 to 6 s, Fig. 4G), reliable expandability within a phantom (8 to 10 s, Fig. 4G), and effective object retrieval (Fig. 4H). These results highlight its potential applications in angioplasty and other interventional tasks such as removing kidney stones. In contrast, a similar device made of conventional magnetic soft materials [8 to 14 s, Fig. 4F (b)] fail to achieve comparable functionality as their four individual beams randomly bent inward, outward, or twist, preventing proper expansion.

In addition, Fig. 5 (A and B) and movie S6 also show two cubes. These two were fabricated and magnetized with identical magnetization profiles (fig. S17): one using the proposed structure and the other using the conventional magnetic soft material. Upon applying a 100-mT B field in various directions in 3D space, their deformation behaviors were distinct with each other. The cube based on the proposed structure exhibited a desired deformation, responding only when the B field aligned with the positive or negative z axis while remaining stationary when the B field aligned with the x or y axes. In contrast, the cube made from conventional magnetic soft materials showed twisted and undesired deformations under all conditions.

Building on the above results, four types of cubes were further engineered, as shown in Fig. 5C. Their corresponding magnetization profiles and design details are provided in fig. S18. Among them, type I and type II cubes exhibit expansion or contraction only when the B field is aligned with the z axis and y axis. Type III cubes bend around the y axis in response to a B field directed along the x axis (forward and backward), whereas type IV cubes bend around the x axis when the field is aligned with the y axis (leftward and rightward).

Using these four types of cubes, two assemblies were constructed, as shown in Fig. 5 (D and E) and movie S6. The first assembly consists of five units from type I and type II cubes. This configuration enables simultaneous contraction along all three axes when exposed to a uniform B field along the z axis. The second assembly is a serial structure composed of three distinct cubes (type I, type III, and type IV cubes). This setup enables independent actuation of each cube by the corresponding component of the B field, allowing for controlled functionalities such as unidirectional bending and axial contraction without causing undesired twisting. Together, these two assemblies highlight the potential of the proposed structural design for constructing reliable, programmable magnetic field–controlled systems.

Last, a tessellation was constructed to demonstrate that the proposed beams can serve as building blocks for more complex 3D architectures. As shown in Fig. 5 (F to H) and movie S7, a 3×3 tessellation exhibited diverse deformation modes under different constraints and magnetic field directions. When an external magnetic field was applied, the structure deformed as intended, whereas nontargeted beams—those aligned with other field components—remained unaffected, ensuring reliable and programmable actuation. This observation is consistent with a prior work showing that motions in three orthogonal planes can be decoupled by compliant joints (53), and our magnetic design extends this principle by enabling selective, field-controlled deformation. This finding highlights the potential of the tessellated beam structure as a versatile building block for constructing complex, robust, and programmable 3D magnetic soft systems.

Robust undulatory locomotion under misaligned magnetic fields

Beam-shaped or sheet-shaped magnetic soft materials with sinusoidal magnetization profiles have been widely studied for their diverse deformation modes and adaptability to various environments (4, 18, 54, 55). However, existing works have primarily focused on deformation behaviors under perfectly aligned magnetic fields. In contrast, we intentionally introduced a challenging scenario in this study: Two wormlike beams were evaluated under a deliberately misaligned magnetic field.

As shown in Fig. 6A, the rotation plane of the magnetic field was tilted by 20° relative to the ideal alignment. The “conventional beam” was fabricated using a standard soft magnetic material, whereas the “proposed beam” incorporated the proposed structures. Both beams were magnetized with identical sinusoidal profiles, contained the same volume fraction of magnetic particles, and shared identical dimensions. During characterization, the tilted rotating magnetic field—generated by a permanent magnet mounted on a xy translation stage—was centered at the midpoint of each beam. An overhead camera was fixed in place to record the motion of the beam and give feedback to control the stages.

Figure 6B, fig. S19, and movie S8 compare the performance of the two beams in straight soft channels of different widths. Under a tilted magnetic field, the conventional beam exhibited noticeable twisting, as evidenced by the alternating visibility of its top and bottom edges, marked by green and light blue dashed lines. In contrast, the proposed beam maintained a smooth, continuous undulatory wave pattern with no observable twisting. This difference in locomotion becomes more pronounced as the channel width increases. As shown in fig. S19, at a channel width of 3.8 mm and a magnetic field frequency of 4.17 Hz, the proposed design achieved nearly three times the speed of the conventional beam. This outcome is intuitive: Narrow channels constrain undesired beam deformations, resulting in only minor differences between the conventional and proposed designs. In wider channels, however, the conventional beam undergoes substantial undesired deformation, which greatly reduces its speed. This observation is clinically relevant as devices typically encounter channels of varying widths before reaching their final target. A more detailed discussion is provided in note S9.

Although undesired deformation may only reduce locomotion speed in straight channels, it becomes a critical factor in determining whether the beam can move at all in winding channels. As shown in Fig. 6 (C and D) and movie S8, a winding channel was constructed to replicate the anatomical route from the radial artery to the common carotid artery, characterized by sharp and, continuous turns. Under the misaligned B field, the conventional beam became stuck at the first bend and failed to progress further. In contrast, the proposed beam moved through the entire channel smoothly. Its undulatory motion was further visualized using an ultrasound, with the setup detailed in fig. S20. Ultrasound snapshots in Fig. 6E clearly capture the full body length throughout the sequence, indicating stable deformation behavior and offering potential advantages for real-time localization and pose estimation of the robot during clinical use.

Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the flexibility of the proposed magnetic soft material is not compromised in achieving its enhanced stability. This is demonstrated by its successful locomotion through a hairpin turn with a 10° bend, as shown in Fig. 6F, fig. S21, and movie S9. This retention on flexibility would be further supported by the result in the next section.

Last, owing to its stable locomotion behavior and smooth contact within confined spaces, we found that extracting the envelope of the traveling wave from the beam with the proposed structure can be used to morphologically reconstruct the shape of the surrounding channel. As shown in Fig. 6 (G to I), we used digital subtraction angiography (DSA) to record the motion of the proposed structure through a serpentine channel with irregular morphology (Fig. 6I). Although the channel itself was difficult to visualize under DSA (Fig. 6G), extracting the envelope of the traveling wave from the proposed structure successfully reconstructed its morphology (Fig. 6H). This capability could be highly valuable for diagnosing vascular narrowing and tracking the path of advancement without the need for contrast agents.

Self-advancing guidewire in tortuous phantoms

The guidewire is a thin, flexible filament used by interventionists to navigate the vascular system during minimally invasive procedures for conditions such as strokes and aneurysms (56). Commercially available guidewires are commonly made from nickel-titanium alloys and are coated and wrapped with polymers to reduce the friction (57). Regardless of the guidewire’s shape, safely maneuvering it through tortuous vessels, remains a great challenge (58). Figure 7A shows a commercially available guidewire (diameter: 800 μm, Terumo Medical Corporation) navigating in a tortuous phantom but becoming stuck even when a large insertion force of about 5 N was applied.

Comparison of the navigation capability between a commercial guidewire, a guidewire with tip made of conventional magnetic soft materials, and a guidewire with proposed tip in tortuous phantoms.

(A) Commercial guidewire without a magnetic tip fails to navigate tortuous phantom due to its high stiffness. (B) The guidewire has a tip made of conventional magnetic soft materials with sinusoidal magnetization profile. It can implement undulation under a well-aligned rotating B field. However, the instability, largely due to the torturous geometry, limits the ability of this magnetic tip to provide sufficient traction to pull the guidewire, even with a distal insertion force larger than 5 N. (C) The guidewire has a tip made of stable magnetic soft structures with a sinusoidal magnetization profile. The four-period magnetic tip can advance reliably and pull the guidewire smoothly into different branches of a tortuous phantom. The required force at the distal end is less than 1 N. (D) The guidewire has a tip made of conventional magnetic soft material with axial magnetization profile. It can react and align with the direction of B field. However, the mechanical locking of guidewire causes the guidewire to buckle in the middle. Scale bar, 5 mm. (E) The guidewire has a tip made of stable magnetic soft structures with a sinusoidal magnetization profile. The nine-period magnetic tip can advance reliably and pull the guidewire smoothly to the target. Scale bar, 5 mm. (F) Insertion force comparison of three kinds of guidewire. The data about inserting a commercial guidewire and details of the insertion force can be found in fig. S22. Each group is repeated five times. (G) Success rate and efficiency comparison of guidewire made by conventional magnetic materials and the proposed structure. Each group is repeated seven times.

To address this challenge, researchers have developed various magnetic soft guidewire tips (8, 59, 60). These innovations enable guidewire steering via external B fields, facilitating easier navigation. However, it is noteworthy that the magnetization profile of most reported magnetic guidewire tips to date remains limited to the simplest form—a uniform axial profile along the length of the magnetic tip (9, 31, 34, 35, 38, 56, 61). Although this basic profile merely allows the B field to steer the direction of the tip, it would be promising if the beam structure shown in Fig. 6 could be adapted as a guidewire tip as its undulatory motion enables self-advancement. It would both make the navigation much easier and reduce mechanical risks to the vasculature (62). However, a guidewire with a conventional, sinusoidally magnetized soft tip easily became stuck in the phantom model too (Fig. 7B). Although the tip attempted to undulate and move forward, the tortuous geometry of the phantom tube forced it to misalign with the external B field, severely impairing the undulation performance. This outcome is consistent with the behavior previously observed in Fig. 6 (B and C). In contrast, Fig. 7C demonstrates that the magnetic tip made by the proposed material could advance reliably and smoothly pull the guidewire into different branches of the phantom. The required insertion force at the distal end is less than 1 N. Experiments of Fig. 7 (A to C) are also shown in movie S10.

The above results suggest that, regardless of how smooth the guidewire is or how precisely its tip can be steered, mechanical locking inevitably occurs after multiple turns due to the large, accumulated tension, as shown in Fig. 7B. This phenomenon is well known as the Capstan effect (63). To further examine this, we constructed a serpentine phantom with multiple turns and tested three cases: a commercial guidewire (fig. S22A), a guidewire with an axially magnetized conventional magnetic soft tip (Fig. 7D), and a guidewire incorporating the proposed structure (Fig. 7E). These results are also shown in movie S11, with quantitative summary in Fig. 7 (F and G). Both the commercial guidewire and the conventional magnetic soft-tipped guidewire became stuck after the first two turns, whereas the proposed structure enabled smooth passage through the phantom, requiring as little as 0.1 N of insertion force—representing a 30-fold reduction compared with the conventional magnetic guidewire (Fig. 7F)—and achieving markedly higher success rates and passing efficiency (Fig. 7G).

Stable intestine-like locomotion

Within the GI tract, capsule robots have been extensively studied for applications such as imaging, pathological sampling (15, 64), and drug delivery (65, 66). These robots are particularly useful for navigating the small bowl, which remains difficult to access using conventional gastroenteroscopy (67). To enhance locomotion within the intestinal environment and reduce the size of the robot, magnetic fields have been leveraged to control the capsule’s motion. The methods adopted include anchoring-based (68, 69) and rotational (70, 71) approaches. However, these locomotion modes often rely on exerting substantial force against the intestinal lining to generate sufficient friction, which can pose safety risks such as volvulus, intussusception, and even intestinal obstruction or perforation (72–74). Therefore, it is imperative to develop a locomotion strategy that ensures both effective propulsion and improved safety for clinical use.

To address this challenge, the design inspiration can be drawn directly from the intestine itself. As shown in Fig. 8A (a), the intestine relies on peristalsis to transport fluids and food. Mimicking this behavior, a magnetic robot capable of executing peristaltic motion could interact in harmony with the intestinal walls, enabling smooth and safe locomotion through the complex GI environment. Several studies have attempted to replicate peristalsis using conventional magnetic soft materials (48, 75, 76). For example, our group developed a miniature soft pump before to transport liquid and solid. Similarly, Meng et al. introduced a similar device to pump yogurt. However, a closer check of the deformation kinematics reveals that the cross-sectional shape of these two examples collapses along one radial axis while expanding along the orthogonal direction. In contrast, peristaltic motion features a symmetric radial contraction and expansion, producing a time-varying cross-sectional area that propagates longitudinally as a traveling wave. Therefore, authentic peristaltic motion has not been achieved by the magnetic soft material yet.

Reflecting on the undulatory locomotion demonstrated in Fig. 6, it is conceptually straightforward to devise a design capable of achieving peristalsis. As illustrated in Fig. 8A (b), this can be accomplished using four magnetic soft beams positioned at the four corners of the device. By programming the phase difference, the beams on the diagonal plane (e.g., beams 1 and 3) can be actuated to deform in opposite directions, whereas those on the same side (e.g., beams 1 and 2) deform to the same side. However, when a robot of this design is constructed using conventional magnetic soft beams, the result is catastrophic: The entire robot collapses and rolls into a ball under the rotating magnetic field (Fig. 8B and movie S12). Although the detailed underlying reasons are discussed in notes S1 and S10, the fundamental issue is geometric. It is impossible to find a single rotating magnetic field plane that simultaneously satisfies the actuation requirements of all four conventional magnetic soft beams.

In sharp contrast, Fig. 8C and movie S12 demonstrate that a robot constructed with the proposed structures can reliably propagate a peristaltic wave along its body, enabling controlled forward and backward crawling. Under a rotating B field, its crawling speed can be easily tuned by adjusting the rotation speed and the distance of the permanent magnet. In the current setup, the robot can achieve a forward crawling speed of ~9.4 mm/s at a rotation speed of 240 rpm and a magnetic field strength of 250 mT [Fig. 8, C (a) and C (b)]. The backward crawling speed saturates once the field strength exceeds 100 mT, likely due to the anisotropic bending direction of the supporting soft joints (see Fig. 8A and fig. S23). The stable deformation of the robot with proposed structures is also confirmed by the FEA simulation shown in Fig. 8D, fig. S24, and movie S12, with detailed simulation setup discussed in note S11.

In addition to straight channels, this robot is capable of maneuvering through torturous paths by roughly following a rotating magnet. Figure 8E and movie S13 present an S-shaped tube with a 16-mm diameter in the straight segments and a 14-mm diameter in the curved segments. The tube features a turning radius of 28 mm and a total length of ~800 mm. Before entering the curved section, it can be observed that the robot adaptively adjusts its pose, bending one of its beams to accommodate the reduced diameter. Similar crawling performances in two other tubes with different geometries are provided in fig. S25.

This robot also exhibits strong resistance to damage. As shown in Fig. 8F, one of the beams on the robot was intentionally broken. Despite this damage, the robot continues to crawl reliably, albeit at a reduced speed (~3.8 mm/s). This damage tolerance further underscores the robot’s robustness and operational reliability.

We further evaluated the robot’s load-carrying capability. As shown in Fig. 8G and movie S14, the robot successfully carried a 1.5-g payload, ~1.5 times of its own weight, while maintaining reliable locomotion at a speed of about 0.8 mm/s. To demonstrate practical application, an omeprazole-filled capsule, a common medication for GI conditions, was loaded into the robot (Fig. 8H and movie S15). The robot first transported the capsule to a designated target location within a phantom model. Upon arrival, the ambient temperature was raised to body temperature (37°C), triggering the release of the drug. After completing the delivery, the robot exited the site.

In addition to this drug-carrying demonstration, the robot can also carry a camera for medical imaging. As shown in Fig. 8 (I and J) and movie S16, an endoscope camera (OV6946, OmniVision Technologies Inc.) was mounted on the robot, which navigated through a soft channel containing marked symbols. The robot successfully captures clear images of the “U,” “S,” and “T” markers, ultimately arriving at and visualizing the HKUST badge.

Last, an experiment with an ex vivo experiment was also conducted. The robot traveled well on the porcine colon surface (Fig. 8K and movie S17). Together, these demonstrations validate the potential of the proposed robotic locomotion module for future medical applications inside the GI tract.

DISCUSSION

Although existing research has addressed similar challenges in large-scale robotics through the use of tensegrity structures or specific mechanical metamaterials to stabilize behavior and facilitate efficient torque transmission (77–79), the stability of magnetic soft materials have often been overlooked. In this study, we present a robust magnetic soft structure that enhances the functional stability of magnetic devices for future clinical use. This structure demonstrates effective operation even under conditions where the magnetic field is tilted or misaligned, showcasing its versatility for potential applications in wormlike beams, octopus-like miniature tentacle, helical robot, self-advancing guidewires, and capsule robot with peristaltic motion.

Our proposed structures, although currently discussed at a millimeter scale, can be further minimized by adopting microfabrication with higher resolution, such as micromolding (80), jig-assisted assembly (76), and two-photon polymerization microprinting (81). These efforts can further shrink the proposed beam down below 300 μm, smaller than the finest clinical guidewire. Furthermore, we can use the technique reported in our previous study to wrap the entire beam in biocompatible materials, such as Parylene-C (80), to make it biocompatible for medical applications. Preliminary biocompatible results can be found in figs. S27 and S28. In the current study, the highest achievable weight ratio of NdFeB particles to EpoxyAcast 655 is 1:0.5 and the remanence is shown in fig. S29. Further increasing the particle concentration results in an inhomogeneous mixture. Future work could explore alternative matrix materials capable of accommodating a higher concentration of larger magnetic particles with more substantial magnetic domains, thereby increasing the overall magnetization. In the most extreme scenario, custom sintered magnets could be used to replace the rigid blocks, enabling maximal magnetic torque output.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication and materials

Basic proposed beam structure

As shown in fig. S2, the beam structure was fabricated using a two-step molding process. First, a positive mold was 3D printed with an SLA printer (Halot-MAGE S, Shenzhen Creality Technology Co. Ltd.) using its Standard Resin, achieving a printing resolution of 0.05 mm. The MicroArch S230 printer was also used for a more precise printing with a printing resolution of 2 μm. A negative mold was then cast from this positive using Mold Max 30 (Smooth-On). To ensure dimensional consistency for subsequent applications, each negative mold was carefully selected to minimize deviations under a handheld microscope (AM7915MZT, Dino-Lite). The magnetic composite was prepared by mixing EpoxyAcast 655 (Smooth-On) with NdFeB particles (5 μm) at a weight ratio of 1:0.5 and pouring the mixture into the selected negative mold. Notably, higher particle loading ratios may result in uneven dispersion and therefore not used. A PET film, laser-cut to specific dimensions by a UV (ultraviolet) laser cutting machine (General Laser Tech.), was inserted into the mold to serve as the spine. The composite was cured at 60°C for 2 hours before being demolded.

Other beam structures

For the conventional magnetic soft materials used in the control experiments, the fabrication process followed a similar procedure described above, replacing the ExpoxyAcast 655 with PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning; the base and curing agent mixed at a 10:1 mass ratio). For the wrapped beam material (fig. S3C), the fabricated beams were further encapsulated in a matrix composed of Ecoflex 00-30 (Smooth-On) and magnetic particles (NdFeB), mixed at a weight ratio of 1:0.8.

Cubes shown in Fig. 5

Cubes were assembled by bonding four beam materials to two square frames (15 mm by 15 mm, 1 mm thickness) using glue. The square frames were 3D printed via SLA (Halot-MAGE S, Creality) using its Standard Resin, with dimensions of 15 mm by 15 mm and a thickness of 1 mm. Different types of cubes were realized by altering the magnetization profiles of the beams and their orientations relative to the frame. For cubes with contraction ability (type I and type II), each beam was fixed in a jig and bent to 30° before being magnetized in a 1.5-T magnetic field generated by a Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (EZ7, Microsense). More detailed information can be found in fig. S18.

Wormlike beam and guidewire tip

Each wormlike beam consisted of two beams (dimensions: 1 mm by 1 mm by 15 mm; block length: 1.5 mm) with identical single-period sinusoidal magnetization profiles. The two beams were bonded together using adhesive. For conventional ones, two identical magnetic soft beams were similarly joined. The four-period or nine-period wormlike beam was directly glued to the tip of a commercial guidewire (diameter: 800 μm; Terumo Medical Corp. or Abbott Cardiovascular Corp.), forming the guidewire tip.

Capsule robot

The capsule robot (dimensions: 12 mm by 12 mm by 36 mm) was composed of four magnetized soft beam structures and three soft joints (Mold Max 30). The beams featured a two-period sinusoidal magnetization profile. Its outward surface was attached with a 1-mm-thick soft film made from Ecoflex 00-30. It is used to enhanced friction with the environment. Soft joints with notches were fixed at both ends and the center of the capsule robot to provide structural support to the beams. These joints also played a critical role in determining the radial dimensions of the robot.

Control method of the magnetic field

All experiments were conducted using permanent magnets (size: 50 mm by 50 mm by 50 mm), with the magnetic field profile illustrated in fig. S5. For experiments that required a rotating B field along a specific path, a motor mounted on a two-stage translation system was used to implement the programmed route and speed, with a top-view camera as feedback. The stage is controlled by an Arduino Mega 2560. The setup is illustrated in fig. S20.

FEA of the magnetic field

The FEA results were numerically simulated in the commercial FEA software ABAQUS/Standard 2023, following the same FEA methodology proposed by Zhao and colleagues (82). The simulation models, which featured varying dimensions, were created using SolidWorks 2024 and subsequently imported into Abaqus for mesh processing. Initially, the model was imported as a single part. Then, it was divided into two distinct parts, the backbone and the spine, each assigned different material properties within Abaqus. All FEA simulations were conducted in static analysis mode, with the model subjected to a uniform magnetic field applied in various directions to evaluate its response under different loading conditions. Details of the simulation in this paper can be found in note S11.

Statistical analysis

The results in the figures are presented as the mean, with an error bar showing the SD. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were conducted independently and repeated at least three times to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the data.
