An electronic review of clinical outcomes after return of actionable genetic research results from a health system research biobank
Authors: Giorgio Cocchella, Lillian Phung, Elisabeth Wood, Brian Egleston, Lily Hoffman-Andrews, Sarah Brown, Demetrios Ofidis, Rajia Mim, Hannah Griffin, Dominique Fetzer, Anjali Owens, Susan Domchek, Reed Pyeritz, Bryson W Katona, Staci Kallish, Giorgio Sirugo, JoEllen Weaver, Katherine L Nathanson, Daniel J Rader, Angela R Bradbury
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile research participants report interest in receiving genetic research results, how best to return results to ensure medical benefits remains unclear. In the Penn Medicine Biobank Return of Results Study, participants receive results through a digital intervention or a genetic counselor and are recommended to complete clinical confirmation testing and a visit in clinical genetics. We reviewed EHR encounters to understand longitudinal clinical outcomes in 16 patients who completed all steps (Group A), 8 patients who did not complete the clinical genetics visit (Group B), and 21 patients who did not complete confirmation testing or the clinical genetics visit (Group C). Most participants in Group A (69 %) had evidence of adherence to medical recommendations. Some patients faced usual barriers to care and related to comorbid conditions. In contrast, most participants in Group B (75 %) did not have evidence of discussing their confirmed result with a provider or adherence to medical recommendations. Most participants in Group C (71 %) did not have evidence of discussing their research results with providers, although one discussed their unconfirmed result. Men were less likely to complete all steps (Group A), and participants with cardiovascular results were less likely to complete the medical visit (Group B). There was no documentation regarding testing in relatives in any non-genetics encounters for any groups. These data suggest that the steps following return of research results may be critical to realizing the medical benefits of returning actionable genetic research results and highlight the importance of collecting systematic longitudinal outcomes.Clinical trial registration: NCT04242667.

Introduction

Genetic discovery research conducted in biobanks can identify actionable genetic mutations that could impact the health care of research participants and raises questions about the obligation to share individual research results (Evans and Rothschild, 2012; Wolf, 2013; Bledsoe et al., 2012, 2013; Jarvik et al., 2014; Knoppers et al., 2015; West et al., 2020). There is increasing consensus that programs should consider returning results that could impact a participant’s health (Wolf, 2013; Jarvik et al., 2014; Burke et al., 2013; Anticipate and Communicate Ethical Management, 2013; Griffin et al., 2007; Clift et al., 2015; Shahmirzadi et al., 2014; Dressler et al., 2012; Sciences NAo, 2018; Fossey et al., 2018) However, how best to implement these recommendations and communicate genetic research results to research participants remains unclear. Unlike patients with clinical genetic testing, research participants may not be prepared for actionable results, particularly if the results are unrelated to the reason they enrolled in research, or if they enrolled as healthy unselected participants (e.g. large biobanks). Programs have used genetic counselors (GCs) to share genetic research results (Jarvik et al., 2014; Forrest and Young, 2016; Graves et al., 2014; Christensen et al., 2011; Roberts et al., 2010; Jouni et al., 2017), while others have provided results to local health care providers (Shaibi et al., 2018; Kullo et al., 2018) who may lack the necessary expertise or resources, resulting in suboptimal patient outcomes (Plon et al., 2011). Limited data exist on patient outcomes, clinical outcomes, and costs associated with various approaches, and empirical data are needed to inform best approaches for pre-disclosure education and returning results to realize benefits for participants.

Although research participants have reported high interest in receiving research results (Shaibi et al., 2018; Edwards et al., 2018; Fernandez et al., 2007; Facio et al., 2013; Halverson et al., 2020a; Karlson et al., 2016; Hoell et al., 2020; Joffe et al., 2019; Carey et al., 2016; Bollinger et al., 2012), uptake has been lower when results have been offered (48–86 %), particularly in biobank studies where return of results was not emphasized during enrollment and uptake has generally been around 50 % (Blout et al., 2021; Beil et al., 2021; Leitsalu et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2012; Keogh et al., 2004, 2014; Fiallos et al., 2017; Crook et al., 2015; Wynn et al., 2018). Available patient-reported outcomes in research settings suggest limited negative impact, (Blout et al., 2021; Beil et al., 2021; Leitsalu et al., 2021; Graves et al., 2013). (Bradbury et al., 2018; Kilbride et al., 2023) but, there are limited available longitudinal data to inform how frequently research participants adhere to screening or risk reducing interventions after return of genetic research results (Mitchell et al., 2024).

In a randomized pilot study returning actionable results from the Penn Medicine Biobank, we assessed the feasibility of returning actionable research results through a two-step process including a patient-centered digital intervention as compared to a GC in the Penn Medicine biobank (Phung et al., 2024). In Step 1, participants with an actionable result according to ACMG SF v.3.0) (Miller et al., 2021) and procedural controls (no actionable result) were invited to digital pre-disclosure education and provided options for opting out of results. In Step 2, those with actionable results who had not opted out were randomized to receive results via a digital disclosure intervention or with a GC. All participants who received actionable research results were informed that clinical confirmation testing, covered by the study, is needed before taking any medical actions based on their results. In addition, all those with confirmed results were recommended to follow-up in the corresponding genetics clinic to receive medical recommendations for screening and prevention of disease. As previously reported, we had low uptake of genetic research results and only 57 % of participants completed confirmation testing, which means that their genetic research results should not directly inform medical decision making. Their results were shared with them but not included in the EHR, as this only occurs after clinical confirmation testing. Further, even among those who confirmed their result, only 67 % completed the recommended clinical genetics appointment to receive expert recommendations for clinical care.

This pilot study was not designed to assess longitudinal outcomes, including adherence to screening recommendations, communication to relatives and communication with their other health care providers. Thus, the impact of not completing confirmation testing or the clinical genetics visit was not formally assessed. It is possible that participants who complete confirmation testing successfully share their results with their health care providers and receive and complete appropriate gene-specific recommendations. Similarly, it is possible that participants who declined confirmation testing may change their mind and get clinical testing through their regular health care providers, or they are ascertained for clinical testing based on their personal and family history. To better understand the possible longitudinal clinical outcomes in participants who do and don’t complete all the recommended steps after return of genetic research results, we sought to review encounters in the EHR to explore these longitudinal outcomes.

Methods

Participants

For this study, we sought to evaluate outcomes in three groups of patients who received actionable results, as previously reported (Phung et al., 2024). Briefly, participants were English-speaking adults who enrolled in the Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB), which links genomic data to electronic health record (EHR) phenotype data at the University of Pennsylvania (Verma et al., 2022). We reviewed whole exome sequencing (WES) for actionable variants (according to ACMG SF v.3.0) (Miller et al., 2021). Variants included those with consensus of pathogenicity or likely pathogenic in ClinVar and protein truncating variants (PTVs) (nonsense, frameshift), excluding the last exon and the last 50 bp of the penultimate exon, in genes where PTVs are a disease mechanism. We excluded participants who were deceased or had clinical testing identifying the actionable variant. From the remaining participants with actionable variants, we selected 130 ‘cases’ and matched them on age, sex, and race/ethnicity to 130 ‘procedural controls’ with no actionable variants. Procedural controls were enrolled in the biobank similar to cases and only differed in that they did not have an actionable finding in sequencing to date. This allowed participants to learn about the return of results program and opt out before notification of an actionable result. This step was felt to be particularly important for this study as the return of results was not a primary focus at the time of consent to the PMBB, and the types of results being returned and procedures for return were not included at the time of original consent. The University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board approved the study and return of result procedures.

The three groups evaluated for this analysis include: 1) Group A included biobank participants who received their research results, underwent clinical confirmation testing, and completed the recommended clinical genetics visit (n = 16). 2) Group B included biobank participants who received their research results, underwent clinical confirmation, but did not complete the recommended clinical genetics visit (n = 8). In these two groups, notes documenting the receipt of the research result and the clinical testing confirmation report were documented in the EHR. 3) Group C included biobank participants who received their research results but did not undergo clinical confirmation (n = 21). For this group, no result was documented in the EHR because the only result available was a research result and clinical confirmation did not occur.

For each of these groups, we identified ideal longitudinal outcomes of returning actionable genetic research results (e.g. benefits of returning results), as well as clinical outcomes that would raise concerns about the risks or limitations of returning actionable results and would represent non-ideal clinical longitudinal outcomes (see Table 1).

Electronic health record review

Research results were returned between June 2020 and July 2022, and the EHR review took place in March of 2023 and a second review in July and August of 2024. Qualitative data were collected through retrospective review of patient charts (including but not limited to health care provider encounter notes, phone call documentation, messaging via the EHR, etc.) to identify: a) adherence to gene specific guidelines or potential barriers to adherence, b) communication of actionable research results with their health care providers, c) any unresolved questions regarding disease risks and recommendations, and d) any other findings suggesting risks or benefits to return of actionable genetic research results as outlined in Table 1. Records from Penn Medicine and from other institutions linked through Care Everywhere (a feature in EPIC which permits review medical records from other health care centers) were reviewed. While all provider notes were queried, the analysis primarily focused on visits most pertinent to the participant’s actionable gene or condition (e.g., oncology visits for BRCA variants, cardiology visits for cardiovascular variants), in addition to primary care visits where discussions of genetic results and recommendations would likely occur.

First, two student researchers (LP, GC) reviewed the EHR and created summarized notes and observations from the EHR. Next, a student researcher (GC) and investigator (AB) identified thematic patterns for each subgroup using inductive coding, constant comparison and consensus (Glaser, 1967; Bingham, 2023).

Results

Participant characteristics

Fifty-two biobank participants from the randomized PMBB return of results pilot study (NCT04242667) received actionable research results. Seven biobank participants who we learned already knew of their mutation (e.g. already had clinical testing through usual clinical care) were excluded, leaving 45 included in the EHR review (Fig. 1). This included 16 biobank participants in Group A (those who completed confirmation testing and the clinical genetics visit), 8 in Group B (those who completed confirmation testing but not the clinical genetics visit), and 21 in Group C (those who did not complete confirmation or genetics follow-up). Sixty three percent of participants were male, 63 % white and actionable mutations included both cancer and cardiovascular conditions (Table 2).

Thematic patterns in group A

Among biobank participants who completed confirmation testing and the clinical genetics visit, most (n = 11, 69 %) had evidence in the EHR of adherence to recommended screening and/or risk reducing recommendations (Table 3). Yet, some patients (n = 3) faced barriers to care leading to non-adherence, such as insurance challenges or logistical issues related to comorbid conditions. Additionally, for two biobank participants in Group A, there was limited information in the EHR making it difficult to determine adherence. In all cases, there was no documentation referencing communication to relatives or cascade testing in relatives in non-genetics visit encounters.

Thematic patterns in group B

Among biobank participants who received their genetics results and underwent confirmation testing but did not complete the clinical genetics visit follow-up, most (n = 6, 75 %) did not have evidence of any communication about their clinically confirmed actionable mutations with their health care providers (Table 4). Additionally, they did not have evidence of completing the expected preventive measures or screening recommendations related to their confirmed genetic mutation. There was evidence in the EHR that two patients did discuss their confirmed genetic test results with a health care provider. In one, there was no evidence that the expected screening or preventative care was completed. For the other, there was evidence of strong recommendations from their primary health care provider to complete appropriate gene-specific cancer screening, but the patient was unable to pursue these recommendations due to financial constraints. In all cases, there was no documentation referencing communication to relatives or cascade testing in relatives.

Thematic patterns in group C

Among individuals who received their genetic research results but did not complete clinical confirmation testing, the majority (n = 15, 71 %) did not have evidence that they discussed their research results with their health care providers (Table 5). In one of these, the patient had already had a heart transplant and was seeing cardiology. In a few biobank participants (n = 3), there was evidence of appropriate screening or preventive care, but it was not clear if the result had been discussed with their provider and if these actions could have been related to existing diagnoses or family history and not impacted by the genetic research results. Only one participant had documentation in the EHR of sharing their unconfirmed genetic research result with their health care provider. While there was a change to their medical management, it again was not clear that it was related to this discussion. In all cases, there was no documentation referencing communication to relatives or cascade testing in relatives.

Discussion

This study sought to investigate the potential longitudinal clinical outcomes among biobank participants who received actionable genetic research results through an EHR review focusing on patients who did and did not complete recommended clinical confirmation testing and the clinical genetics visit for expert recommendations. We found that the majority of biobank participants who completed all steps, including the clinical genetics visit, appeared to be following screening and prevention as would be expected for their genetic finding. In contrast, the groups that did not have clinical follow-up or did not complete confirmation testing had less evidence in the EHR of adherence to clinical screening, suggesting that these steps may be critical to realizing the benefits of returning actionable results and highlighting the need for longitudinal prospective real-world and comprehensive outcomes of returning actionable genetic research results.

A central benefit of returning genetic research results is the provision of results that can both change medical management and result in early detection of disease or disease reduction for the individual research participant. This requires not only providing the results but also communicating them in a way that leads to health behavior changes. Further, a critical component of this may be a hand-off to a clinical team or provider who can initiate and support patient adherence to disease reduction recommendations. There are relatively few studies describing longitudinal screening and health behavior outcomes after return of genetic research results (Mitchell et al., 2024). We were encouraged to find in this analysis that most participants who received an actionable research result, completed confirmation testing and met with the recommended clinical team had evidence of appropriate screening and risk reduction, suggesting the potential for these long-term benefits. However, there were a subset of participants in this group who faced usual barriers to optimal care including costs, burden of frequent visits and competing co-morbid illness. These are not surprising in a real-world return of results study where the primary intent of the biobank is research rather than return of genetic results and participants are reflective of real-world clinical patients. Among recently published studies returning secondary findings, adherence to screening varied from 42 to 78 % (Mitchell et al., 2024). Additionally, more research is needed to understand if men face greater barriers to completing confirmation testing and medical follow-up or interventions to support completion of these steps in men. This also raises the possibility that the benefits of returning actionable results may be more limited in some patients and highlights the need for more rigorous and systematic longitudinal studies evaluating adherence to medical recommendations as well as barriers to adherence. Such research could identify some populations where benefits of returning actionable results may be more limited or where benefits will be greater for relatives than the research participant. It could also identify key barriers and inform new strategies or interventions to increase the likelihood that the benefits of returning actionable results are realized.

Equally important, we found that most participants who clinically confirmed their results but did not complete the recommended follow-up with the genetics team did not have evidence of following recommended screening and risk-reduction measures. While a small number of studies have reported recommending specialist referral, these did not report if specialist referral occurred (Mitchell et al., 2024). While it is possible in our study that appropriate screening and risk reduction measures were completed elsewhere, it does raise concerns that simply returning actionable genetic research results will not be sufficient without this critical clinical follow-up. Further, lower completion with cardiovascular results should be confirmed and could identify a need for greater awareness of the importance of actionable cardiovascular mutations. We acknowledged when embarking on this analysis that it was possible that participants could take their confirmed results to their established health care providers and that these providers could provide appropriate recommendations and initiate recommended care, suggesting that referral to a genetics expert is not necessary to realize the benefits of returning actionable results. These data don’t exclude this possibility, but they further support the need for systematic longitudinal evaluation of health behaviors and clinical care, particularly in this subset of participants. It is also possible that participants brought their results to their health care providers and discussions regarding these results were not recorded in the EHR. The limited available data suggest that primary care providers may not feel comfortable addressing actionable genetic research results that their patients bring to them to address, with these interactions serving to potentially strain the provider-patient relationship (Kudron et al., 2023; Halverson et al., 2020b; Lemke et al., 2020).

There has been an increasing literature reporting on barriers to routine clinical genetic testing and some early studies and reports identifying barriers specific to return of genetic research results (Raymond et al., 2021; Shaibi et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2021; Raspa et al., 2021). Recommended strategies have included integrating genetics expertise directly into primary care, expanding telehealth-based genetic counseling, and using EHR alerts to prompt follow-up care (Raspa et al., 2021; Mills et al., 2021; Gasteiger et al., 2023). Programs serving low-resource populations have also suggested an emphasis on enhancing logistic supports, such as transportation assistance, insurance navigation, and flexible scheduling, to facilitate adherence to recommended screening and preventive measures (Raymond et al., 2021; Shaibi et al., 2020) These interventions have aimed to increase the likelihood that participants complete recommended screening and risk-reducing actions after receiving actionable results. However, such organizational supports may be limited due to constrained resources and funding limitations. Our data support the need for both system-level and individual-provider interventions to address barriers, as well as further research to understand how best to support providers in delivering appropriate follow-up care.

A significant limitation of returning individual genetic research results that were not identified in CLIA approved research labs is the requirement for subsequent CLIA confirmation testing prior to using results for medical management. Yet, restricting return of results to only those labs with CLIA approval would result in many missed opportunities of benefit as previously demonstrated—particularly given that studies to date suggest that 47 % of participants would not have met clinical criteria for testing in usual care (Blout et al., 2021; Buchanan et al., 2020). Understanding barriers to confirmation testing and the outcomes in participants who elect not to complete confirmation testing is critical. In our EHR review, there was little evidence of discussions with providers and further research is needed to understand if this communication is occurring, its impact, and its outcomes. As noted above, discussions could lead providers to refer patients back to the program to complete confirmation testing or refer patients to complete clinical testing based on their personal or family history. Less favorable outcomes could occur if patients or providers do not understand the limitations of research results and choose to initiate changes to medical care based on research results. While we believe this risk to be low, it is critical to obtain longitudinal data to confirm this.

A key benefit to returning actionable genetic research results (and clinical genetic testing in general) are the benefits to relatives, even beyond the individual research participant. This benefit is a critical component of our communication and process and is a recommended step following confirmation testing. Our EHR review revealed limited evidence of cascade testing being actively pursued or documented in non-genetics notes. While it remains possible that these conversations and cascade testing occurred, the absence provides additional support for including assessment of familial cascade testing in longitudinal research assessments. Many studies have identified low uptake of cascade testing in clinical testing and barriers could be similar or even greater after return of actionable research results, particularly in research programs where participants were not expecting to receive genetic findings (Mitchell et al., 2024; Wynn et al., 2022; Passero and Roberts, 2024). Thus, assessing communication to relatives and uptake of testing in relatives are important components to be included in longitudinal research.

This study has several limitations that may impact the interpretation and generalizability of its findings. First, this report includes a small sample size, and the results are exploratory. Additionally, lack of documentation in the EHR does not mean that genetic test results were not discussed with providers or that there were no discussions regarding cascade testing outside of genetic visits. Similarly, not identifying completion of recommended screening or risk reduction activities in the EHR does not mean that they did not occur, as they could have been completed at other clinics or health systems. It is possible that some patients already had a clinical diagnosis or had their condition treated (e.g. cancer had already occurred, or heart transplant had already occurred), but the frequency of these was low in this analysis. These data are considered exploratory and designed to highlight the importance of, and inform data collection for, longitudinal studies evaluating the outcomes of returning actionable genetic research results in biobank participants where sequencing is not the primary goal. This highlights the critical need for a systematic, longitudinal approach to follow-up to capture these behaviors fully.

In conclusion, this study suggests that patients who receive genetic research results and complete the recommended confirmation testing, and follow-up may engage in appropriate screening and risk reduction, although usual barriers to adherence to medical recommendations may exist for some. This study also highlights gaps in our understanding of longitudinal outcomes in research participants who receive results, but don’t complete confirmation testing or follow-up with genetic experts, emphasizing the need for systematic longitudinal studies to understand the risks and benefits of returning actionable research results.
