Occurrence, source identification, and human health risk assessment of potentially toxic elements in freshwater sources used for aquaculture in the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river basins
Authors: Safiétou Mbaye, Dani Khoury, Gaspard Conseil, Jean Fall, Pamela Hartmeyer, Frédéric Pierlot, Damien Banas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFreshwater resources in Senegal play a crucial role in drinking water (DW) supply, agriculture, and the rapidly expanding aquaculture sector promoted under the National Sustainable Aquaculture Development Strategy. However, no nationwide assessment has previously investigated potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in freshwater resources supporting aquaculture. This study provides the first integrated evaluation of dissolved arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), nickel (Ni), lead (Pb), and zinc (Zn) in surface water and groundwater collected from 17 aquaculture farms distributed across the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river basins. Median PTE concentrations followed the order Cu > Zn > Ni > Cr > Pb ≈ As > Cd, and all measured concentrations remained below the corresponding World Health Organization DW guideline values. Human health risk assessment indicated that ingestion was the dominant exposure pathway, producing substantially higher chronic daily intake than dermal exposure for both adults and children. Nevertheless, hazard quotient, hazard index, and carcinogenic risk values remained below the United States Environmental Protection Agency acceptable thresholds, indicating no significant non-carcinogenic or carcinogenic health risks associated with the investigated PTEs. Principal component analysis identified distinct river-specific contamination signatures, with Zn, Cu, and Cd predominantly associated with the Casamance basin, whereas Cr and Pb were more strongly related to the Senegal River, while the Gambia River exhibited consistently low PTE concentrations. Overall, these findings indicate that freshwater resources supporting aquaculture in Senegal are currently of good chemical quality with respect to the investigated PTEs. This study provides the first national baseline for environmental monitoring and supports sustainable aquaculture development and water-resource management in Senegal.Keywords: Aquaculture, Environmental monitoring, Freshwater, Trace elements, Risk assessment, Water quality

Introduction

Freshwater pollution by potentially toxic elements (PTEs) has become a major global environmental and public health concern because of their persistence, toxicity, non-biodegradability, and tendency to bioaccumulate in aquatic ecosystems and food webs [1]. PTEs originate from both natural processes, including rock weathering and volcanic activity, and anthropogenic sources such as mining, industrial discharges, agricultural runoff, urbanization, and wastewater effluents [2]. Once introduced into freshwater environments, these contaminants can accumulate in sediments, aquatic organisms, and drinking water resources, posing serious ecological and human health risks through chronic exposure. Consequently, monitoring the occurrence, distribution, sources, and health implications of PTEs has become a global priority for sustainable water-resource management and environmental protection [3,4].

Across Africa, increasing population growth, rapid urbanization, agricultural intensification, mining activities, and climate change are placing unprecedented pressure on freshwater resources while environmental monitoring remains insufficient in many regions [5]. Rivers, lakes, and groundwater are essential sources of drinking water, irrigation, fisheries, and aquaculture, yet they are increasingly threatened by PTE contamination. Recent studies from Ghana, Ethiopia, Nigeria, and other African countries have reported elevated concentrations of arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), nickel (Ni), lead (Pb), and zinc (Zn), together with associated ecological and human health risks [3,4,6,7]. These investigations have demonstrated the importance of integrating contamination assessment, source identification, pollution indices, multivariate statistical analyses, and human health risk assessment to provide a comprehensive understanding of freshwater quality and to support evidence-based environmental management.

In Senegal, freshwater resources are increasingly important for domestic consumption, agriculture, livestock production, and the rapidly expanding aquaculture sector. Previous investigations have primarily focused on coastal ecosystems, estuaries, marine sediments, and localized mining areas [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. These studies demonstrated that both anthropogenic activities and natural geological processes contribute to PTE occurrence in aquatic environments. However, most available information concerns sediments or coastal environments, whereas dissolved PTEs in inland freshwater systems remain poorly characterized. Consequently, knowledge regarding the occurrence, spatial distribution, and potential health implications of PTEs in freshwater resources supporting inland aquaculture is still limited.

This information gap is particularly important because Senegal has identified aquaculture as a strategic sector for improving food security, employment, and economic development through the National Sustainable Aquaculture Development Strategy [13]. The success of this strategy relies heavily on the availability of high-quality freshwater resources supplied by the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river systems, as well as associated groundwater. Although these waters constitute the primary source for fish farming activities, no comprehensive study has simultaneously investigated PTE occurrence, spatial distribution, pollution sources, and potential human health risks across the principal freshwater systems supporting aquaculture in Senegal. Furthermore, existing studies rarely integrate source identification techniques such as principal component analysis with quantitative human health risk assessment, limiting the understanding of contamination pathways and their implications for sustainable aquaculture development.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study provides the first integrated assessment of PTEs (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn) in freshwater systems supporting aquaculture across the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river basins. Specifically, the objectives were (i) to determine the concentrations and spatial distribution of dissolved PTEs in surface water (SW) and groundwater (GW) used for aquaculture, (ii) to identify potential natural and anthropogenic sources, (iii) to evaluate non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic human health risks associated with ingestion and dermal exposure for adults and children following United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) methodologies, and (iv) to provide baseline scientific evidence to support sustainable aquaculture development, environmental monitoring, and water resource management in Senegal.

Materials and methods

Study Area

The present study was conducted in Senegal (Figure S1), a West African country located between latitudes 12°18′ and 16°41′ N and longitudes 11°21′ and 17°32′ W, with a total area of approximately 196,700 km2. Senegal experiences a tropical climate characterized by two distinct seasons: a rainy season extending from June to October and a dry season from November to May. Mean annual precipitation ranges from less than 200 mm in the semi-arid northern regions to more than 1250 mm in the humid southern part of the country, while average annual temperatures generally vary between 24 and 30 °C. The country's hydrographic network is dominated by the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance rivers, which constitute the principal freshwater resources supporting agriculture, domestic water supply, fisheries, livestock production, and the rapidly expanding aquaculture sector [14,15].

Consequently, maintaining good water quality is essential not only for fish health and productivity but also for safeguarding public health, as these water resources are frequently used simultaneously for domestic consumption, irrigation, livestock watering, and recreational purposes.

The Senegal River is the largest river basin in the country, extending over approximately 1800 km from the Fouta Djallon Highlands in Guinea through Mali and Mauritania before reaching the Atlantic Ocean at Saint-Louis. The river is regulated by the Manantali and Diama dams, which provide water for irrigation, hydropower generation, navigation, and aquaculture. Intensive agricultural activities, urban development, and localized industrial operations occurring within the basin represent potential sources of PTEs, particularly through agricultural runoff, domestic wastewater, and industrial effluents. The Gambia River originates in the Fouta Djallon massif of Guinea and flows through southeastern Senegal before entering The Gambia and discharging into the Atlantic Ocean. Compared with the Senegal River, this basin is less densely populated and experiences lower industrial activity. Nevertheless, artisanal gold mining, expanding agriculture, livestock production, and increasing aquaculture development constitute potential anthropogenic sources of PTEs. In addition, natural weathering of mineralized geological formations may contribute to the release of PTEsinto the aquatic environment. The Casamance River, located in southern Senegal, drains a humid tropical region characterized by abundant rainfall, mangrove ecosystems, floodplains, and extensive agricultural activities. The river supports numerous freshwater aquaculture farms owing to its relatively stable hydrological conditions throughout much of the year. However, agricultural runoff, urban expansion, navigation, domestic wastewater discharges, and natural geochemical processes may influence water quality and increase the concentrations of dissolved PTEs within the basin. These three river systems were selected because they represent the principal freshwater environments currently supporting inland aquaculture development in Senegal while encompassing contrasting climatic, geological, hydrological, and socio-economic conditions.

From a public health perspective, assessing PTE contamination in these freshwater systems is particularly important because local communities frequently rely on these waters for drinking, food preparation, bathing, irrigation, and fish production.

Sampling activities were conducted with the collaboration and logistical support of the National Aquaculture Agency (ANA) within the framework of the national aquaculture development program. Access to aquaculture facilities was granted by farm owners and local authorities prior to sampling.

Sampling strategy and analysis

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between June and August 2022 to characterize the occurrence, spatial distribution, and potential sources of dissolved PTEs in freshwater resources supporting aquaculture production in Senegal. A cross-sectional design was selected because it provides a representative assessment of water quality during the principal aquaculture production period while allowing comparison among geographically distinct river systems under similar hydrological conditions. This approach has been widely adopted in environmental monitoring studies as a baseline framework for contamination profiling, source identification, and probabilistic human health risk assessment [3,4].

The sampling campaign targeted freshwater resources used directly for aquaculture production, including both SW and GW. Including both water sources was considered essential because Senegalese aquaculture systems rely on rivers, reservoirs, wells, and boreholes depending on seasonal water availability. During the dry season, GW frequently becomes the principal source of water for fish farming, whereas SW predominates during periods of higher discharge. Consequently, evaluating both sources provide a more comprehensive assessment of potential human and environmental exposure to PTEs.

Sampling locations were selected from the national inventory of commercial aquaculture farms established during the implementation of the SNDAq [13]. Site selection was based on four principal criteria: (i) active aquaculture production at the time of sampling, (ii) dependence on freshwater resources for pond supply, (iii) geographical representation of the three principal river basins supporting aquaculture development, and (iv) logistical accessibility during the sampling campaign. Seventeen farms fulfilled these criteria and were retained for the study. Because one farm utilized both SW and GW (river and borehole), a total of eighteen water samples were collected.

The sampling network included seven farms located within the Senegal River basin (Farms 1–7), two farms supplied by the Gambia River (Farms 8 and 9), and eight farms distributed throughout the Casamance River basin (Farms 10–17). This spatial distribution ensured broad geographical coverage across Senegal while encompassing diverse climatic, geological, hydrological, and land-use conditions. Sampling sites were selected to represent different sections of the rivers whenever possible. Upstream locations were chosen to characterize areas experiencing relatively limited anthropogenic influence and to provide reference conditions representative of background water quality. Midstream sites were selected in zones influenced by agricultural production, rural settlements, and aquaculture activities, where cumulative watershed inputs were expected to increase. Downstream sites represented areas potentially affected by integrated upstream discharges, urban activities, irrigation return flows, and estuarine processes.

SW samples were taken from the outlet of the supply pipe delivering river water to the aquaculture ponds. Before collection, the pumping system was operated for several minutes to flush stagnant water and ensure that the sample reflected freshly pumped river water. GW samples were collected from the borehole or well outlet supplying the ponds, after similarly flushing the system for several minutes to obtain representative sample. In both cases, water was collected directly into pre-cleaned 1 L glass bottles conforming to ISO 4796–1:2016 standards. At each location, sampling containers were rinsed three times with ambient water before final collection to reduce the possibility of cross-contamination.

All sampling equipment was thoroughly cleaned before field deployment using laboratory detergent, rinsed with ultrapure water, soaked for at least 24 h in 10% nitric acid (HNO3), and finally rinsed several times with ultrapure water. This cleaning protocol minimized the risk of contamination during sampling and transportation.

Immediately after collection, water samples were filtered through pre-cleaned 0.45 μm glass microfiber (GF/A) filters to obtain the dissolved fraction of PTEs. Filtered samples were transferred into acid-washed polypropylene Falcon tubes and stored in insulated coolers containing ice packs throughout transportation to the laboratory. Within 24 h of collection, samples were preserved by acidification with ultrapure HNO3 to a final concentration of approximately 3% (v/v), thereby preventing PTE precipitation, adsorption onto container walls, and microbial alteration prior to chemical analysis. Samples were subsequently stored at −18 °C until laboratory analysis. Concentrations of Cd, Cu, Pb, Ni, Cr, Zn, and As in the dissolved phase were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) at the Centre de Recherches Pétrographiques et Géochimiques (CRPG, Nancy, France). The limits of quantification (LOQ) were 0.005, 0.002, 0.01, 0.01, 0.04, 0.015, and 0.025 μg L−1 for Cd, Cu, Pb, Ni, Cr, Zn, and As, respectively.

To ensure sample integrity throughout the sampling campaign, each container was assigned a unique identification code indicating the river basin, farm number, sampling date, and water source. Samples were transported under refrigerated conditions and handled according to internationally accepted protocols for trace element analysis to minimize contamination and preserve analytical quality.

Human health risk assessment (HHRA)

The potential human health implications associated with exposure to PTEs were evaluated using the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) human health risk assessment (HHRA) methodology. HHRA provides a quantitative framework for estimating both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks resulting from long-term exposure to environmental contaminants and has been widely applied in water quality assessments to identify priority contaminants, vulnerable populations, and potential public health concerns [3,4,6,7].

The assessment considered two primary exposure pathways: ingestion, representing the direct consumption of contaminated water, and dermal absorption, representing skin contact during activities such as bathing, swimming, washing, and aquaculture-related practices. The dermal pathway was included because direct contact with river water is common among communities living along the Senegal and Gambia rivers [[16], [17], [18]]. In contrast, the inhalation pathway was excluded because dissolved PTEs exhibit negligible volatility and limited potential for aerosolization under normal environmental conditions.

Human health risks were assessed separately for adults and children by calculating the chronic daily intake (CDI), hazard quotient (HQ), hazard index (HI), and carcinogenic risk (CR) for each exposure pathway. All exposure assumptions, toxicity parameters, and calculation procedures were selected according to standard USEPA guidelines and the published literature to ensure consistency with internationally accepted HHRA methodologies.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to characterize the distribution of PTE concentrations in aquaculture waters. For each element, the median, minimum, maximum, first quartile (Q1), third quartile (Q3), and interquartile range (IQR) were calculated. Because concentrations exhibited right-skewed distributions and spanned several orders of magnitude, results were summarized using medians and quartiles and visualized on logarithmic (log10) scales where appropriate.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to explore multivariate relationships among PTEs and to identify spatial patterns across the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river basins. Prior to PCA, concentration data were standardized using z-score normalization (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) to eliminate differences in measurement scales among PTEs. PCA was performed using the correlation matrix, and the first two principal components were retained for interpretation. Loading vectors were used to identify the PTEs contributing most strongly to each principal component, while sampling sites were projected onto the PCA space to visualize similarities and basin-specific clustering.

All statistical analyses, calculations, and graphical visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.1) within the RStudio environment.

Quality assurance / quality control

Quality assurance and quality control procedures were implemented throughout sample preparation and instrumental analysis to ensure the accuracy, precision, and reliability of the analytical results. Calibration curves were prepared using multi-element standard solutions (Agilent Technologies, USA; Ref. Nos. 8500–6940, 8500–6942, and 8500–6948) at concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 500 µg L⁻1. Five calibration points were generated by serial dilution with ultrapure water, and only calibration curves with correlation coefficients (R² > 0.99) were accepted for quantification.

Instrument performance was optimized before each analytical batch using a 1 µg L⁻1 multi-element tuning solution (Agilent Technologies, USA) containing a broad mass range of elements. Analytical precision was evaluated through duplicate analyses and repeated measurements, yielding relative standard deviations (RSD) below 5%. Method accuracy was verified using the certified reference material NIST SRM 1643 f (PTEs in Water), with recoveries ranging from 90% to 110%.

Quality control during analysis included procedural blanks, instrument blanks, field blanks, and replicate samples to monitor potential contamination and analytical variability. Blank corrections were applied where appropriate, and no detectable contamination was observed in any of the field blanks. All water samples were analyzed in triplicate to ensure analytical reproducibility.

Results and discussion

PTE concentration

The summary of PTE concentrations (Fig. 1) highlighted significant contrasts in both magnitude and variability among the analyzed elements. Median concentrations followed the decreasing order Cu > Zn > Ni > Cr > Pb ~ As > Cd. indicating that Cu (1.94 µg L⁻1) and Zn (1.67 µg L⁻1) were the dominant PTEs in the Senegal aquaculture waters. Both Zn and Cu displayed wide concentration ranges, particularly in one sample (F16) collected from Casamance River, with maxima of 52.45 µg L⁻1 and 12.71 µg L⁻1, respectively. These elevated upper values, combined with large interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3), reflected substantial spatial heterogeneity, most likely reflect combined lithogenic contributions from mineral weathering [33] and localized anthropogenic inputs, including metal release from corroded metal structures [34], runoff from metal roofing [35] and urban areas [36], and agricultural runoff [37,38]. Nickel (median of 0.62 µg L⁻1) and chromium (median of 0.38 µg L⁻1) displayed moderate variability, with upper quartiles around 1.00 µg L⁻1 and 0.54 µg L⁻1, respectively. This pattern pointed to mixed lithogenic and anthropogenic sources. Natural mineral weathering or groundwater-rock interactions may contribute to background levels [33], which is consistent with the presence of Ni and Cr formations documented in West Africa [39], while local human activities could occasionally enhance concentrations. As concentrations ranged from 0.05 to 1.80 µg L⁻1, showing a moderately skewed distribution suggestive of geogenic origin, possibly influenced by sediment redox processes typical of tropical aquifer systems [40]. In contrast, Pb and Cd showed narrow ranges (Pb: 0.14–0.44 µg L⁻1; Cd: 0.01–0.03 µg L⁻1) with very low dispersion, indicating uniformly low background concentrations and limited anthropogenic impact. Their medians (Pb 0.25 µg L⁻1; Cd 0.01 µg L⁻1) were well below typical guideline thresholds for SW used in aquaculture, suggesting negligible contamination risk for these two elements. Overall, the data indicated that Cu, Zn, and Ni were the most variable and potentially site-influenced PTEs, whereas Pb and Cd remained consistently low. The relatively moderate levels observed for all PTEs were within or below international guideline limits [41] implying that there was no widespread PTE pollution in Senegalese aquaculture waters. However, the occurrence of isolated high Zn and Cu concentrations highlighted the need for targeted monitoring to prevent potential bioaccumulation risks in farmed fish and sediments.

Dissolved PTE concentrations (µg L⁻1) in Senegal water samples. Values are presented on a logarithmic (log10) scale.

The spatial distribution of the investigated PTEs across the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance rivers is illustrated in Fig. 2. In the Senegal River, median concentrations of most PTEs were slightly higher upstream than downstream, showed minimal variation, indicating overall low spatial heterogeneity. In contrast, the Gambia River displayed a different pattern, with slightly higher concentrations downstream for certain elements, suggesting localized enrichment in the lower reaches. Upstream-downstream increases were evident for Cu (1.40–2.44 µg L⁻1), Zn (0.45–2.16 µg L⁻1), and Ni (0.27–0.68 µg L⁻1). The geological basement of eastern Senegal belongs to the Paleoproterozoic Birimian formations [42]. This unit, known to host copper and zinc [43], may progressively release these elements through meteoric weathering along the upstream–downstream gradient of the Gambia River. This trend may also reflect the downstream transfer of fine particles derived from artisanal gold-mining areas in the upper basin, a process known to enhance PTE loads in rivers draining the Kédougou region [11]. An upstream-downstream contrast occurred in the Casamance River, where median concentrations of Zn and Cr increased markedly from 2.02 to 3.68 µg L⁻1 and from 0.38 to 0.54 µg L⁻1, respectively. Cu remained high throughout (2.18–2.22 µg L⁻1), while As and Ni showed moderate reductions downstream (0.09–0.12 µg L⁻1 and 1.08–0.62 µg L⁻1, respectively). These downstream enrichments, particularly for Zn and Cu, point to cumulative anthropogenic influence, likely arise from multiple anthropogenic sources. Possible contributions include lithogenic release from tropical lateritic soils under seasonal redox changes [40], dissolution of PTEs from corroded metallic infrastructures such as pumps or galvanized surfaces [35], diffuse inputs from populated areas with untreated effluents [36], and agricultural runoff linked to fertilizer and organic amendments [37]. Given this combination of processes, it remains difficult to attribute the observed downstream increases to a single dominant driver.

Human health risk assessment

Chronic daily intake (CDI)

The estimated CDI values revealed that ingestion was the dominant exposure pathway for all investigated PTEs, exceeding dermal exposure by approximately two to four orders of magnitude in both adults and children (Fig. 3). This pattern is expected because the gastrointestinal tract absorbs dissolved PTEs much more efficiently than intact human skin, whose low permeability and modest dermal absorption coefficients substantially limit transdermal uptake [44]. Similar observations have been reported in freshwater risk assessments from Africa and elsewhere, where ingestion consistently represents the principal contributor to human exposure [29,45]. Consequently, dermal exposure associated with routine activities such as bathing, washing, swimming, and aquaculture-related work is expected to represent only a secondary exposure pathway.

Mean ± SD of chronic daily intake (CDI, mg kg⁻1 day⁻1) for adults and children via ingestion and dermal exposure, categorized by carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic pathways. Values are presented on a logarithmic (log10) scale.

Among the investigated PTEs, Zn and Cu produced the highest CDI values owing to their comparatively higher dissolved concentrations in aquaculture waters, whereas Cd consistently exhibited the lowest exposure levels. Although CDI values varied among the three river basins, these differences primarily reflected the spatial distribution of PTE concentrations rather than differences in exposure pathways. In particular, the relatively elevated Zn and Cu concentrations measured in the Casamance basin resulted in higher estimated intake compared with the Senegal and Gambia river systems. Children generally exhibited higher body-weight-normalized CDI values than adults, with a child-to-adult ratio of approximately 2.3, owing to their lower body mass, greater water intake relative to body weight, and their increased hand-to-mouth activity, which enhances incidental ingestion [46]. Nevertheless, all estimated CDI values remained low and primarily served as input parameters for subsequent non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risk assessment rather than indicating adverse health effects.

Hazard quotient (HQ) and hazard index (HI)

The calculated HQ and cumulative HI demonstrated that exposure to the investigated PTEs through both ingestion and dermal contact did not pose significant non-carcinogenic health risks (Fig. 4). For both adults and children, all individual HQ values and cumulative HI values remained well below the USEPA safety threshold of 1, indicating that long-term exposure to the investigated freshwater resources is unlikely to produce adverse non-cancer health effects under the assessed exposure scenarios.

HQ via ingestion and dermal exposure routes for adults and children across studied PTEs. The dashed red line represents the safety threshold (HQ = 1). Values are presented on a logarithmic (log10) scale.

Among the investigated PTEs, As and Cr were the principal contributors to the cumulative HI, accounting for approximately 25% and 40%, respectively, of the overall non-carcinogenic risk. Consequently, the overall trend in mean HI values (ingestion + dermal) for both adults and children followed the order As > Cr > Pb > Cu > Ni > Zn > Cd. Despite their relatively low environmental concentrations, As and Cr exerted the greatest influence on the cumulative HI because of their comparatively low toxicological reference doses, which increase their relative contribution to risk estimates. These observations emphasize that non-carcinogenic risk depends not only on the environmental concentration of individual PTEs but also on their intrinsic toxicological characteristics and absorbed dose [2]. Similar findings have been reported in freshwater systems where As and Cr dominated cumulative non-carcinogenic risk despite concentrations remaining below DW guideline values [4,6,30,47]. The relatively greater contribution of both PTEs (As and Cr) suggests that they warrant continued attention in future monitoring and risk management programs. This is particularly relevant for As, which ranks first on the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) 2022 Substance Priority List because of its toxicity, frequency of occurrence, and potential for human exposure [48].

Children consistently exhibited higher HQ and HI values than adults. Nevertheless, the cumulative HI remained below unity for both age groups, indicating that neither adults nor children are expected to experience adverse non-carcinogenic health effects under current exposure conditions. Similar child-dominated exposure patterns have been widely documented in drinking-water risk assessments worldwide [28,[49], [50], [51]].

Consistent with the CDI results, ingestion represented the principal contributor to non-carcinogenic risk, whereas dermal exposure accounted for only a negligible fraction of the total HI. Similar studies have reported that dermal exposure contributes less than 1% of the total non-carcinogenic risk in freshwater systems [30,52]. Overall, these findings indicate that freshwater resources supporting aquaculture in the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river basins currently present low non-carcinogenic health risks for both adults and children.

Carcinogenic risk (CR)

The estimated CR associated with As, Cd, Cr, Ni, and Pb remained within the acceptable risk range (10−6–10−4) recommended by the USEPA for both adults and children (Fig. 5). Among the investigated PTEs, Ni contributed the greatest proportion of the cumulative CR, followed by Cr and As. However, all calculated CR values remained within the USEPA acceptable risk range, indicating that the investigated waters are unlikely to pose significant CRs under current exposure conditions. Similar findings have been reported in recent studies, where Ni emerged as the principal contributor to CR despite overall risks remaining within acceptable limits [30,[49], [50], [51],53]. Adults exhibited slightly higher cumulative CR values than children, reflecting the exposure assumptions adopted in the USEPA lifetime risk model and the longer cumulative duration of exposure through bathing, washing, and household activities. Dermal exposure represented only a small proportion of the total CR, contributing approximately 6% of the total CR for most PTEs, while ingestion accounted for more than 80% of the cumulative risk in both adults and children. Overall, the HHRA indicates that freshwater resources supporting aquaculture in the investigated river basins currently pose a low carcinogenic risk to exposed populations. Nevertheless, continued environmental monitoring remains essential because increasing agricultural activities, urbanization, and aquaculture development may alter future PTE inputs and associated health risks.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear spatial structuring of PTE contamination across the three river systems, with the first two components explaining 64% of the total variance (PC1 = 40.9%, PC2 = 22.9%) (Fig. 6). PC1 represented a major contamination gradient strongly influenced by Zn, Cu, and Cd, which exhibited high negative loadings (–0.551, –0.540, and –0.521, respectively) and together explained more than 86% of the variance within this axis. The coherent orientation of these loadings suggests a tightly correlated Zn–Cu–Cd assemblage that likely arises from common anthropogenic pressures, such as agricultural practices or stormwater runoff from metal roofs [35]. Farms located in the Casamance basin projected strongly along the negative side of PC1, highlighting their relatively elevated concentrations of these PTEs and forming a clearly identifiable cluster. PC2 captured a second and more complex gradient dominated by Cr and Pb, which displayed contrasting loadings; Cr with a strong negative loading (–0.648, 42% contribution) and Pb with a strong positive loading (0.570, 32% contribution). This opposition suggests the influence of two distinct geochemical or contamination processes. Cr enrichment may reflect lithogenic sources or sediment resuspension, whereas Pb enrichment is more typically associated with anthropogenic activities such as traffic emissions, industrial discharge, or domestic waste inputs. Farms from the Senegal River aligned more closely with this Cr–Pb axis, forming a compact and distinct group relative to those in Casamance. In contrast, the farms located in the Gambia River (F8–F9) clustered near the origin of the PCA space. Their proximity to the center reflects uniformly low concentrations of all analyzed PTEs, consistent with the comparatively low variability and weaker contamination gradients measured in this system. Collectively, the PCA highlighted clear and robust river-specific PTE signatures. The Casamance system is characterized by elevated concentrations of Zn, Cu, and Cd, the Senegal system by enrichment in Cr and Pb, and the Gambia system by consistently low PTE levels.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides one of the first comprehensive assessments of PTEs in freshwater resources supporting aquaculture across Senegal's three major river basins. It offers a holistic evaluation of water quality, potential contamination sources, and associated public health implications. The simultaneous assessment of both SW and GW further strengthens the study by providing a more representative overview of freshwater resources used for aquaculture under contrasting hydrological conditions. In addition, the evaluation of both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks for adults and children through ingestion and dermal exposure pathways provides valuable baseline information for environmental monitoring, sustainable aquaculture management, and evidence-based decision-making in Senegal.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the study quantified total dissolved concentrations of seven PTEs but did not determine their chemical speciation. Since the toxicity, mobility, and bioavailability of elements such as Cr and As strongly depend on their oxidation state and chemical form, the actual health risks may differ from those estimated using total concentrations. Second, the investigation represents a cross-sectional assessment conducted during a single sampling campaign and therefore does not capture seasonal or interannual variations associated with river discharge, rainfall, or changing land-use practices. Long-term monitoring is required to evaluate temporal trends and the effects of climatic variability on PTE dynamics.

Additional limitations relate to the spatial and analytical scope of the study. Although GW was included because of its importance for aquaculture during the dry season, their smaller number compared with SW samples may reduce the representativeness of GW contamination patterns across Senegal. Furthermore, the investigation focused on seven priority PTEs (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, and Zn). Other inorganic contaminants (such as Hg, Mn, Fe) were not investigated and could contribute to cumulative environmental and human health risks.

The HHRA relied on deterministic USEPA exposure models and internationally accepted toxicity reference values. While these approaches are widely adopted in environmental toxicology, they do not explicitly account for mixture toxicity, cumulative low-dose effects, or inter-individual variability in exposure and susceptibility. Moreover, the assessment was limited to dissolved PTE concentrations in water and did not incorporate bioaccumulation in fish, internal biomarkers of exposure, dietary intake, or trophic transfer through aquatic food webs. Previous studies have demonstrated that these additional exposure pathways may substantially increase overall health risks in contaminated aquatic environments [[54], [55], [56], [57]].

Conclusion

This study provides the first integrated assessment of dissolved potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in freshwater resources supporting aquaculture across the Senegal, Gambia, and Casamance river basins. It provides a comprehensive evaluation of contamination patterns, potential sources, and associated public health implications. Overall, the investigated waters exhibited relatively low concentrations of the seven analyzed PTEs, with most values remaining below international drinking-water guideline values. Nevertheless, distinct spatial differences were observed among river basins. The Casamance basin exhibited comparatively higher concentrations of zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), cadmium (Cd), and nickel (Ni), whereas the Senegal River was characterized by relatively greater chromium (Cr) and lead (Pb) enrichment. In contrast, the Gambia River displayed the lowest concentrations and the least spatial variability, suggesting comparatively limited anthropogenic influence.

Multivariate analysis confirmed these basin-specific contamination patterns. The first two principal components (PCs) explained 64% of the total variance and successfully discriminated the three river systems according to their characteristic PTE signatures. The first PC was primarily associated with the combined influence of Zn, Cu, and Cd, whereas the second PC reflected contrasting contributions of Cr and Pb. In agreement with these findings, the overall concentration patterns indicated that dissolved PTEs were predominantly controlled by natural lithogenic processes, with only isolated evidence of localized anthropogenic enrichment.

The HHRA demonstrated that ingestion was the dominant exposure pathway. Children consistently experienced higher exposure than adults because of their lower body weight and higher water intake relative to body mass. Despite these differences, all hazard quotient (HQ), hazard index (HI), and carcinogenic risk (CR) values remained below the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) acceptable thresholds, indicating that the investigated waters currently pose low non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks to exposed populations under the assessed exposure scenarios.

Although these findings indicate that freshwater resources supporting aquaculture in Senegal remain of generally good chemical quality with respect to the investigated PTEs, several limitations should be acknowledged. Consequently, the results should be considered as an important baseline rather than a definitive assessment of long-term environmental quality.

Future research should prioritize long-term seasonal monitoring, chemical speciation, probabilistic HHRA, bioaccumulation studies in aquaculture species, and advanced source apportionment techniques, including isotopic and multivariate approaches. Integrating these complementary methodologies with ecological and epidemiological investigations will improve understanding of contaminant dynamics and provide a stronger scientific basis for sustainable aquaculture development, freshwater resource management, and environmental health protection in Senegal and throughout West Africa.
