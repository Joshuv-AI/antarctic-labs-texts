Factors associated with compliance among urban street food vendors with the food safety and standards authority of India (FSSAI) checklist: evidence from multinomial logistic and gamma regression models
Authors: Rupeshkumar Deshmukh, Pooja Sohil, Sudhanshu Mahajan, Saibal Adhya, Prakash Doke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionStreet food is an integral part in India’s culinary landscape, providing affordable and delicious food options to millions. To regulate food quality and ensure public health, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) has established guidelines and statutory requirements for street food vendors. This study aimed to assess compliance with the FSSAI checklist and examined the sociodemographic and employment-related factors associated with food safety compliance among urban street food vendors.MethodsIn this community-based cross-sectional study, 170 street food vendors from the Urban Health Training Centre’s field practice area in Pune, India, were evaluated through face-to-face interviews. The compliance of street food vendors was systematically evaluated across four key domains. Multinomial logistic, gamma regression model and factor analysis were used to examine the association between the independent variables and compliance among food vendors.ResultsIn multinomial logistic regression, we found that being male was associated with higher odds of better personal hygiene compliance (OR = 1.31, 95% CI 1.10–1.55, p = 0.002), and being unmarried was associated with higher odds of better Control of Operations compliance (OR = 1.55, 95% CI 1.12–2.16, p = 0.009). Conversely, we found that having no pre-placement examination was associated with lower odds of better Maintenance & Sanitation compliance (OR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.47–0.79, p < 0.001), working as a cook was associated with lower odds of better Design and Facilities compliance (OR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.65–0.95, p = 0.013), and lower income was associated with lower odds for the total audit score (OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.61–0.91, p = 0.005). Using the Gamma GLM (log link), we found positive associations with higher domain scores for males in Personal Hygiene (β = 0.27, 95% CI 0.10–0.44, p = 0.002) and for unmarried vendors in Control of Operations (β = 0.44, 95% CI 0.11–0.77, p = 0.009), and negative associations with lower domain scores for no pre-placement examination in Maintenance & Sanitation (β=−0.49, 95% CI − 0.75 to − 0.23, p < 0.001), cooks in Design and Facilities (β=−0.24, 95% CI − 0.42 to − 0.05, p = 0.013), and lower income for the total audit score (β=−0.29, 95% CI − 0.49 to − 0.09, p = 0.005).ConclusionThe study highlights significant hygiene and sanitation non-compliance among street food vendors in Pune, India which poses a serious public health risk to the large population relying on street food. Although the FSSAI checklist is a valuable monitoring tool, its effectiveness relies on consistent implementations, regular inspections and enforcement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28942-5.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), it is estimated that each year around 600 million cases of foodborne illnesses occur, resulting in approximately 420,000 fatalities. Among these deaths, 125,000 involve children under five, primarily due to the consumption of unsafe food [1]. The WHO also estimates that every day, one in 10 people suffers illness due to contaminated food [2]. The rapid urbanization and an alarming increase in urban lifestyles create a demand for fast food. In addition, individuals, mainly those without formal employment, have recognized the potential of entrepreneurship in meeting market demand for readily cooked food. Thus, street food vending is experiencing rapid growth on a global scale. The global street vendors market, which includes food, is projected to grow from \$2.68 billion in 2024 to \$4.73 billion by 2029, at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of about 12.2% [3].

Street food vendors are those who sell ready-to-eat food to the public in streets and other public places for immediate or later consumption without any further processing or preparation [4]. Research in developing countries indicates that 20–25% of household food expenditures occur outside the home, with some population segments relying entirely on street foods [5]. Based on many observations, many people prefer to buy pre-prepared foods rather than prepare meals at home themselves. Street foods are popular because they are affordable, convenient, and easily accessible, while street food vending also provides an important source of income for individuals from low-income households [6, 7]. In many countries, vendors are poorly educated and even untrained in food safety behavior., resulting in poor hygiene practices and food handling behaviour [8]. Street food vendors often operate in unsanitary environments with inadequate waste disposal, limited access to safe water, presence of rodents and vermin and poor sanitation, increasing the risk of food contamination and foodborne diseases [9]. Street food handlers are essential for ensuring food safety throughout the supply chain, from production and processing to storage and preparation [10]. Restaurants and food service establishments play a major role in spreading foodborne diseases, highlighting the critical need for strict food safety measures in these environments [11]. Foodborne diseases are caused from infectious agents like viruses, bacteria, parasites, or harmful chemicals contaminating food or water. These agents are often virulent. In India, the projected burden of foodborne diseases is estimated to rise to 100 to 170 million people annually by 2030 in a business-as-usual scenario, increasing the average incidence from one out of 12 to one out of 9 individuals falling ill [12].

Street food is a weak link in the food safety surveillance system [13]. Street food not only provides convenience for many people but is also a livelihood for low-income people and particularly for women [13]. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) is a statutory regulatory authority under the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India [12]. Regulation of the food industry in Pune district, India, is not well established, yet it is crucial to monitor and control street food vending for the health and safety of consumers. The findings of this study could significantly contribute to the development of health promotion programs focused on safe food-handling practices in street food vending. Most existing studies in India have focused on the microbial safety of various food types and the bacteriological safety of water [14–16]. This is the first community-based study conducted in Pune, western India, to assess food handling practices using the standardised Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) checklist. The present study was undertaken to assess compliance among urban street food vendors with the FSSAI checklist, identify the sociodemographic and employment-related factors associated with compliance using multinomial logistic regression, and examine the association of sociodemographic, employment-related risk factors and domain-wise as well as total audit scores by estimating beta coefficients using a generalised linear model with a gamma distribution and log link function.

Method

Study design, population and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted on 170 street food vendors from May 2025- December 2025. The study was conducted in the field practice area of the Urban Health Training Centre (UHTC) at a private medical college in Pune, Maharashtra, India. An inspection checklist was utilized under Project Clean Street Food Hub by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) [17]. Pune is a city in the state of Maharashtra in the Deccan plateau in Western India. Since Pune is one of the metropolitan cities and the educational capital of India [18]. It has many street food vendors operating in busy markets, near various educational universities and in other public places. The present study included all local street food vendors who had individual stalls and were present at the vending site during data collection, provided they had been working for at least one month. Vendors who only sold packaged food items (such as chips and biscuits), those operating from permanent establishments, and those who did not give consent to participate were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on a previously reported prevalence of 37% for washing fruits or vegetables after cutting (good hygiene practice) among street food vendors in urban South Delhi [19]. Assuming a 95% confidence level and an allowable error of 7.4 (20% of the estimated prevalence), the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 164 street food vendors. A total of 170 street food vendors were included in the study.

Sampling technique

A two-stage sampling method was implemented. Pune city is home to many prestigious universities and research institutions. The Pune Municipal Corporation is the civic body that governs the inner areas of Pune, which is divided into 15 administrative wards [20]. Among these 15 wards, the Urban Health Training Centre attached to a private medical college provides health-related services to 3 of the wards. In the first stage, one of the three UHTC wards was selected by simple random sampling using a random number table. Within the selected ward, a major public landmark (such as a market area, bus stand, educational institution, or other prominent public location with a high concentration of street food vendors) was identified based on local administrative maps and field reconnaissance. In the second stage, one of the four cardinal directions (north, south, east, or west) from the selected landmark was chosen by simple random sampling. The survey team then proceeded systematically along the selected route and enumerated all eligible street food vendors operating on both sides of the road. Every vendor meeting the inclusion criteria was approached consecutively and invited to participate until the required sample size was achieved; therefore, no sampling interval was applied. This sampling approach may have introduced selection bias by excluding vendors located outside the selected direction or those absent during the survey period. However, the ward and survey direction were randomly selected to minimise selection bias and improve the representativeness of the sample (Fig. 1).

Data collection method

Street food vendors were informed about the study’s purpose and written consent for their voluntary participation was obtained. In the first step, each vendor and their vending site were observed for approximately 10 min to evaluate their design and facilities, operational control, maintenance and sanitation and personal hygiene. The observed information was recorded immediately on a designated form. In the second step, the principal investigator administered a questionnaire to the street vendors through a face-to-face interview. The interviews were conducted in Marathi, the local language, to ensure comprehension. Each interview took approximately 20–30 min.

Data collection tool

A predesigned, pretested and validated semi-structured questionnaire was utilised to collect the data. The questionnaire was developed in English based on the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) inspection checklist and an extensive review of the published literature. Face and content validity were established through an expert review that assessed the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was subsequently pretested among approximately 10% of the calculated sample size in a non-study area with similar characteristics. A pilot study was conducted to assess the feasibility, clarity, and reliability of the study instrument before the main survey. Data obtained from the pilot study were not included in the final analysis. Internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80, indicating acceptable reliability for use in the main study. The questionnaire comprised three main sections (Supplementary appendix 1). After being originally written in English, the questionnaire was translated into the local language, which is Marathi, and then back-translated to English to ensure the accuracy of the translation. The data collection tool was created using the KOBO tool, a free survey administrative software. The questionnaire included Section A, which covered socio-demographic factors of street food vendors, such as age, gender, income, and duration of employment. Section B focused on employment factors for street food vendors, including pre-placement examinations, typhoid vaccination and deworming. Section C has an inspection checklist under the Project Clean Street Food Hub by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) [17]. This section covered four major domains: Design and Facilities (DF), Control of Operations (CO), Maintenance and Sanitation (MS), and Personal Hygiene (PH). The DF domain consists of 6 questions, with a maximum score of 16. The CO domain has 20 questions, with a maximum score of 54. The MS domain includes 5 questions, with a maximum score of 14. The PH domain contains 7 questions, with a maximum score of 16. Therefore, a total score of 100 is calculated for each street food vendor. Since the FSSAI audit checklist does not provide domain-specific compliance categories, domain-wise scores were categorised into poor, adequate, and excellent and defined a priori by the investigators before statistical analysis, based on the maximum obtainable score for each domain, to facilitate statistical analysis. The cut-offs were: DF (poor: ≤5, adequate:6–10, excellent:>10), CO (poor: ≤17, adequate:18–34, excellent:>34), MS (poor: < 5, adequate:5–9, excellent:>9), and PH (poor: < 5, adequate:6–10, excellent:>10). The compliance categories are like a total audit score above 90% is classified as Compliance-Exemplar. A score between 76% and 89% is classified as Compliance-Satisfactory. A score between 66% and 75% is classified as needs improvement. A score below 66% is classified as non-compliance.

Ethical consideration and quality control

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Bharati Vidyapeeth Deemed to be University, Medical College (BVDUMC), Pune, under certificate number BVDUMC/IEC/72/25–26. Participants were informed about the study’s objectives and the details of the questionnaire. Any concerns raised by the participants were addressed by providing information about the study data. Informed written consent was obtained from each participant. No personal identifiers were used in the datasets and no personal information was collected or shared during the research. All the forms were rechecked by the investigator to ensure that they were complete and error-free.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 29. Quantitative variables result were summarized by mean ± SD and categorical variables result were summarized by frequencies and percentages. At the initial stage, associations between the independent variables (sociodemographic factors and employment factors) and categorical outcomes were examined using the Chi-square test of independence. Variables not showing evidence of association at the bivariate stage were excluded from subsequent modeling. Multinomial logistic regression was then performed to identify factors associated with total audit ratings within each domain, namely Design and Facilities (DF), Control of Operations (CO), Maintenance and Sanitation (MS), and Personal Hygiene (PH). For each domain, outcomes were categorized as poor, adequate, and excellent, with excellent as the reference category, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated for poor versus excellent and adequate versus excellent. Multinomial logistic regression was also conducted for the total audit score category, comparing need improvement and non-compliance against the reference category Satisfactory and reporting Crude ORs with 95% CI. Multinomial models were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation and evaluated using standard model fit statistics. To assess the association of multiple risk factors on domain scores analysed as continuous outcomes, multivariable generalized linear models (GLMs) were fitted for each domain using a Gamma distribution with a log link. This approach was selected instead of multiple linear regression because the domain scores were strictly positive and right-skewed, making the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions of linear regression less appropriate; the Gamma model better accommodates skewed, positive outcomes with variance increasing with the mean, while the log link supports multiplicative interpretation. For the Gamma GLMs, regression coefficients (B or β) were reported with 95% Wald confidence intervals, and statistical significance of predictors was evaluated using the Wald Chi-square test (df and p-values). Model assumptions were assessed for each analysis. For Chi-square tests, assumptions included independence of observations, mutually exclusive categories and adequate expected cell counts. For multinomial logistic regression, assumptions assessed included independence of observations, adequate sample size across outcome categories, absence of multicollinearity, linearity in the logit for continuous predictors, absence of influential outliers/high-leverage observations, correct model specification and the independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA). For Gamma GLMs, assumptions assessed included independence of observations, strictly positive outcomes, appropriateness of the Gamma mean–variance relationship and log link specification, absence of undue association from outliers/high-leverage observations, and acceptable goodness-of-fit based on deviance/residual diagnostics. Exploratory factor analysis was additionally conducted using the four domain scores (DF, CO, MS, PH) together with the total audit score to examine the underlying factor structure; eigenvalues were estimated and used to guide factor retention and factor loadings were interpreted to describe contributions of the domains and total score to the extracted factors. Model assumptions were assessed before fitting the regression models. For the Chi-square test, assumptions of independence of observations and mutually exclusive categories were satisfied, and more than 80% of the expected cell frequencies were ≥ 5, with no expected cell count less than 1. For multinomial logistic regression, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and no evidence of multicollinearity was observed (all VIF values < 5 and tolerance > 0.20). The assumption of Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) was assessed using the Hausman–McFadden test and was satisfied (p > 0.05). Model fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test, Pearson chi-square and deviance goodness-of-fit statistics, which indicated an adequate fit to the data (p > 0.05). Residual diagnostics, including standardized residuals and influential observations assessed using Cook’s distance, indicated no substantial outliers or influential observations (standardized residuals within ± 3 and Cook’s distance < 1). For generalized linear models (Gamma distribution with log link), the outcome variables were strictly positive and right-skewed, supporting the use of the Gamma distribution. Model adequacy was assessed using deviance and Pearson residuals, which demonstrated acceptable model fit, and no influential observations were identified. Throughout, results p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 170 street food vendors participated in the study. Half of the participants (50.0%) were aged > 30 years, while 43.5% were aged 21–30 years. The mean age of the study participants was 30.32 ± 7.03 years, with ages ranging from 18 to 52 years. Females constituted 63.5% of the study population, and 63.5% were married. Nearly half of the participants were Hindu (48.2%), followed by Buddhist (34.1%) and Muslim (17.6%). Most vendors (72.4%) had an educational qualification up to secondary school (≤ SSC), and 60.6% reported a monthly income of less than ₹20,000. Approximately two-thirds (63.5%) owned their food stall, 61.2% had undergone a pre-placement medical examination, and 58.2% had been engaged in street food vending for ≤ 5 years. Food safety training had been received by only 21.8% of the vendors, whereas 77.6% reported receiving typhoid vaccination and 54.7% had taken deworming medication. The total score indicates that 68% of street food vendors were non-compliant across all four domains. Unexpectedly, no one was from the Compliance-Exemplar category. Across the individual domains, the highest proportion of poor performance was observed in Maintenance and Sanitation (64.1%), followed by Personal Hygiene (61.2%), Control of Operations (53.5%), and Design and Facilities (42.9%).

An assessment of food vendors in the DF domain showed mixed compliance levels. While 100% of vendors had working surfaces above 60–70 cm, only 32.9% provided lidded waste bins, indicating poor waste management. No vendors operated in enclosed premises, so structural safety and pest-proofing were not applicable. When it comes to stall surfaces, 35.9% were made of corrosion-resistant materials, while the same percentage showed partial compliance with the relevant standards. Only 32.9% of people used food-grade utensils and equipment, while 52.9% were non-compliant. Only 32.9% of street food vendors had access to adequate handwashing facilities, and more than half lacked basic hygiene infrastructure. Findings from the CO domain indicate significant gaps in food safety practices in street food vendors. Only 32.9% of the vendors used fresh, FSSAI-licensed raw materials, while 55.9% were found to be non-compliant. Compliance with hygiene storage practices was limited to 23.5% and only 37.1% of the food items were appropriately segregated between vegetarian and non-vegetarian categories. Although all street vendors kept food containers 15 cm above the ground, only 14% washed their ingredients with potable water before cooking. Compliance with hygiene thawing was 37.1% and just 28.8% adhered to adequate water usage and storage practices. Alarmingly, only 17% of vendors monitored oil quality. Although thorough cooking was observed in 83.5% of cases, only 23.5% maintained appropriate food temperatures, and only 27.1% stored perishable foods correctly. Ready-to-eat food was covered in only 32,4% of cases, while service area cleanliness was maintained by 42.9% of vendors. Moreover, 23.5% of establishments practiced safe disposal of leftover food and a significant 67.1% did not manage waste properly. Additionally, only 23.5% used thermal or refrigerated transport for food held for more than 2 h. An assessment of the MS domain revealed a mixed pattern of compliance among vendors. Only 35.9% of vendors maintained their working surfaces in good condition, free from defects such as chipping, loose nails and surface damage, 28.2% of vendors were non-compliant, while a further 35.9% exhibited partial compliance. Only 35.9% of vendors used clean clothes for cleaning carts, utensils, crockery and wiping hands and the remaining 64.1% were non-compliant. Food preparation areas were well maintained, but pest control measures were implemented by 35.9% of vendors. Proper cleaning of utensils and equipment after each operation was ensured by 48.2% of vendors. In terms of general cleanliness, 50.6% of vendors cleaned floors, walls and other surfaces daily and maintained them in good condition. In the public health domain, compliance with key hygiene indicators varied significantly. Over half of the street food handlers (54.1%) were observed washing their hands before starting work or after engaging in non-food-related activities, such as using the restroom or emptying dustbins. The cleanliness of street food handlers, which includes wearing clean clothing and maintaining trimmed nails and facial hair, was seen in only 48.8% of cases. Furthermore, only 48.8% of street food handlers covered their heads and mouths and wore appropriate protective gear, such as aprons and gloves, while the remaining street food handlers did not adhere to these essential hygienic practices. The wearing of false nails or loose jewellery was observed in 51.2% of street food handlers, meaning that 48.8% complied with this safety requirement. Alarmingly, only 29.4% of street food handlers refrained from smoking, chewing, or spitting while preparing or serving food, leaving 70.6% in violation of proper hygiene standards. Health checks showed that 36.5% of street food handlers were free of infectious or communicable diseases, while 63.5% posed a potential health risk. Lastly, 64.1% of street food handlers had no open wounds or burns.

Association of sociodemographic and employment variables with individual domain categories and total audit score (n = 170)

The Chi-square analysis revealed significant associations between various sociodemographic and employment factors and the key domains of food safety total audit checklist, which include DF, CO, MS, PH, and the total audit score. In the DF category, gender was found to be significantly associated (p = 0.02), with females achieving adequate and excellent scores as compared to males. Furthermore, educational attainment exceeding the Secondary School Certificate (SSC) exhibited a robust association with elevated adequate scores (p = 0.004). An income level of ₹20,000 or more per month also demonstrated a significant link to improved DF outcomes (p < 0.001). Ownership of the establishment was positively associated with DF adequate scores (p < 0.001), as was participation in pre-placement health examinations (p < 0.001). Additionally, work duration was significantly associated with Design and Facilities (DF) scores, with vendors having five years or less of work experience attaining higher DF scores (p = 0.01). Regular consumption of deworming medication was significantly associated with higher DF scores (p = 0.001). In the CO domain, gender remained a significant factor (p = 0.02), along with shop ownership (p < 0.001), completion of pre-placement examinations (p < 0.001), absence of any co-morbid conditions (p < 0.001) and taking of both the typhoid vaccine and deworming medication (p < 0.001). Regarding MS, a monthly income of rupees 20,000 or more was significantly associated with improved outcomes (p = 0.004) as of pre-placement examinations (p < 0.001). Additionally, a larger family size of more than 4 members was associated with higher MS scores (p = 0.002), and occupational roles were also associated with outcomes, with multipurpose workers performing better in MS than cooks and dishwashers (p = 0.03). In the PH domain, religious affiliation showed a statistically significant relationship (p = 0.02), with Buddhists demonstrating superior hygiene standards compared to Muslims and Hindus. Typhoid vaccination was significantly associated with better Personal Hygiene (PH) compliance (p = 0.03). The analysis of the total audit score revealed gender as a significant factor (p < 0.001). However, contrary to the findings in individual key domains, females were more likely to exhibit non-compliance compared to males. Additionally, lower educational levels (below SSC) were associated with higher rates of non-compliance (p = 0.02), and larger family sizes were again linked to increased non-compliance (p < 0.001). Regular deworming proved to be consistently associated with higher compliance across the various domains (p = 0.001).

Discussion

In this community-based cross-sectional study, we examined the relationship between sociodemographic and employment factors associated with compliance among 170 street food vendors. We employed multinomial logistic regression for our analysis. This study is unique as it is one of the first to be conducted on street food vendors in India, utilising the FSSAI audit checklist to evaluate overall hygiene and sanitation compliance.

A majority of 68.8% of street food handlers were found to be non-compliance with expected standards, indicating a significant gap in adherence across all four assessed domains. Only 17.6% of the street food handlers achieved a satisfactory level of compliance, and none reached the exemplar compliance category, highlighting the lack of ideal hygiene and operational standards in Pune, western India. Additionally, 13.5% of study participants were classified as needing improvement, suggesting that there is potential for positive change through effective targeted interventions. These findings highlight the urgent need for structured training programs, strict policy enforcement, and regular monitoring to improve hygiene practices in food establishments, especially in urban slums. A study of street food vendors in the urban slums of Pune, India, using Bureau of Indian Standards criteria, found that 64% of vendors achieved satisfactory scores, while 36% were rated unsatisfactory scores [21]. The reasons cited for the poor scores are primarily attributed to inadequate waste disposal methods, such as the failure to use covered rubbish bins and disposable plastic bags. Additional challenges included limited water accessibility, including the absence of handwashing facilities, washing utensils under running water and insufficient availability of drinking water. Other factors associated with lower scores included inadequate refrigeration facilities and improper glove use, such as not discarding gloves during interruptions, failing to wash hands before donning gloves, and handling money while wearing gloves. A study conducted at street food vending sites in Nigeria [22] revealed that a significant majority (75.7%) exhibited low compliance with food safety regulations, while 12.5% had moderate compliance and 11.8% demonstrated high compliance. These findings align closely with the results of our own study.

The DF domain highlighted significant gaps in the structural and hygiene infrastructure of street food vendors. While basic requirements, such as the height of working surfaces, were generally met, there were critical deficiencies, including a lack of lidded waste bins, food-grade utensils and handwashing facilities. These findings are consistent with those reported by Reddy et al., who observed that only about one-third of street food vendors were registered with FSSAI and that adherence to basic food safety practices, including the use of aprons, handwashing facilities, soap, and refrigeration, remained poor despite the existence of comprehensive national food safety standards [23]. These findings emphasize the urgent need for infrastructure upgrades, increased financial support, and stricter enforcement of hygiene standards at food vending sites in urban slums. The study conducted in Puducherry on urban street food vending units found that only 28% had dedicated handwashing facilities [24]. The CO domain revealed significant shortcomings in key food safety practices, especially regarding raw material sourcing, storage, and waste management. While 83.5% of vendors adhere to proper cooking protocols, there are compliance issues in critical areas such as the use of potable water, monitoring oil quality and maintaining temperature control. The MS domain showed moderate compliance, with many vendors failing to adequately follow key hygiene practices. This highlights the need for improved sanitation and regular inspections to ensure food safety standards. In the PH domain, compliance with key hygiene practices was inadequate, with fewer than half of individuals adhering to important guidelines such as proper handwashing, use of protective gear, and personal cleanliness. Alarmingly, risky behaviours were observed, with 70.6% of participants smoking or chewing tobacco while handling food, and over 60% lacking proper health screenings. These findings highlight the necessity of behaviour-change training and regular health monitoring through comprehensive screening programs. A comparable study conducted in China found that both consumers and street food vendors had a good understanding of food safety. However, safe food-handling practices among street vendors were lacking, with only 26.7% using hand-washing facilities or being fully equipped with them. Despite this, more than 60% of the vendors wore clean and tidy clothing and masks [13].

The present study demonstrated that several sociodemographic and employment-related factors were significantly associated with food safety compliance among street food vendors. The multivariable analyses indicated that male vendors had poorer compliance across several food safety domains, including Design and Facilities and Control of Operations, and lower total audit scores in the generalised linear model, suggesting that gender-related differences in work practices, infrastructure investment, or adherence to hygiene recommendations may be associated with compliance. Additionally, vendors with lower education levels (≤ SSC) and larger families (more than four members) showed a significant association with non-compliance. This may indicate a lack of awareness about proper hygiene practices or resource constraints that hinder their ability to maintain adequate standards. Overall, poor compliance across multiple food safety domains indicates that food safety deficiencies are multidimensional rather than confined to a single aspect of food handling. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive interventions that combine food safety training, infrastructure improvements, regular health screening, and strengthened regulatory monitoring to enhance compliance among street food vendors. A study [25] conducted in Vietnam, involving 400 street food vendors, revealed a significant association between compliance with food safety regulations and several factors, including age, education level, total business capital, the area used for business, type of mobile vending, and training in food safety (p < 0.01). However, gender and the duration of business operations were not significantly associated with respondents’ compliance status regarding food safety and hygiene practices. The study conducted in Ghana indicated that factors such as education, main occupation, business ownership, business location, business registration, permits, food handler certificates, inspection frequency, and training significantly associated the level of food safety compliance. The study conducted in Somaliland [26] among 306 street food handlers found that a lack of food safety training, limited work experience, lack of appropriate water storage equipment, lack of regular sanitary inspections, and poor knowledge were statistically associated with poor food safety practices. The current study reveals some unexpected findings, notably that cooks are statistically more likely to fall into the non-compliance category. This is counterintuitive because cooks, being directly involved in food preparation, are expected to maintain high hygiene standards. However, the observed lack of compliance among them highlights the need for further investigation into their specific practices and the underlying causes. The observation that vendors who had not received deworming medication demonstrated poorer compliance and lower total audit scores in the adjusted analyses suggests that preventive health practices may reflect greater awareness and adherence to food safety measures. Although vendors without food safety training generally demonstrated poorer compliance, strengthening food safety training programmes remains an important public health strategy to improve hygiene practices and regulatory compliance among street food vendors in Pune. Multinomial logistic regression analysis indicated that male vendors had significantly higher odds of poor compliance in the Design and Facilities domain compared with female vendors. This may reflect differences in the type of vending operations, access to infrastructure or adherence to hygiene practices among male vendors. Conversely, lower income is associated with poorer designs and facilities, likely reflecting financial barriers that hinder the acquisition of appropriate equipment and infrastructure. Additionally, vendors who do not own their shops are also linked to subpar designs and facilities, as employees may have limited control over the vending environment and less motivation to invest in improvements. There is a strong association between the absence of pre-placement examinations and poor designs and facilities, suggesting inadequate regulatory oversight and monitoring. Moreover, the correlation between not receiving a typhoid vaccine and poor designs and facilities may indicate a general lack of awareness regarding health and hygiene. Similarly, the association between not receiving deworming medication and poor designs and facilities further reinforces the conclusion that some vendors lack sufficient knowledge of health and hygiene practices. The use of a standardised FSSAI inspection checklist in the present study provides a systematic framework for documenting food safety practices and identifying critical gaps in compliance among street food vendors. Such standardised documentation and monitoring systems can generate evidence to support targeted regulatory actions, capacity-building initiatives, and continuous quality improvement. Furthermore, integrating systematic monitoring with risk-based public health interventions may contribute to safer food environments and improved nutritional outcomes, particularly among vulnerable populations who rely heavily on street foods for daily meals [27, 28].

Study limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, this study employs a cross-sectional design, which captures associations at a single point in time and cannot establish causal relationships between risk factors and compliance across various domains. Additionally, the research is based on data collected from a field practice area within an urban slum associated with a single medical college, which may not be representative of the rural population or more diverse urban settings. Future studies with a longitudinal, multicentric design and mixed-method approaches are recommended to gain deeper insights.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated a high level of non-compliance with the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) food safety standards among street food vendors in Pune, particularly across the domains of design and facilities, control of operations, maintenance and sanitation, and personal hygiene. Several sociodemographic and employment-related factors were independently associated with poor compliance, highlighting the need for targeted rather than uniform interventions. The findings have important public health and policy implications. Regular, mandatory food safety training should be integrated into vendor licensing and licence renewal processes to improve knowledge and promote safe food-handling practices. Municipal authorities and food safety regulators should strengthen routine inspections, supportive supervision, periodic medical examinations, vaccination of street food handlers, and access to essential hygiene infrastructure, including safe water, handwashing facilities, and appropriate waste disposal systems. Food safety certification initiatives and targeted support for socioeconomically disadvantaged vendors may further enhance compliance with FSSAI standards. Collectively, these evidence-based measures have the potential to improve food safety, reduce the burden of foodborne diseases, protect consumer health, and support the long-term sustainability of the street food sector. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are warranted to evaluate the effectiveness of these strategies in improving compliance over time.
