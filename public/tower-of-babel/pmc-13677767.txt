Sperm-TLR2 activation enhances bovine embryo developmental competence via modulation of intracellular calcium and autophagic flux
Authors: Taiyo HASHIMOTO, Ihshan AKTHAR, Yu MATSUZAWA, Alireza MANSOURI, Ren UCHIYAMA, Fumie MAGATA, Akio MIYAMOTO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe optimization of in vitro embryo production (IVP) is critical for bovine biotechnology, particularly for maximizing the yield of high-quality embryos suitable for transfer. This study investigated how Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) activation in sperm, using the agonist Pam3CSK4 (Pam), influences fertilization outcomes, metabolic activity, and cryotolerance. Japanese Black sperm were treated with 50 ng/ml Pam for 30 min before fertilizing Holstein oocytes. Embryos were cultured for 7 days, with quality assessed via total cell counts, apoptosis, calcium signaling, and autophagy markers. For cryopreservation trials, only high-quality (grade A) blastocysts were selected for slow-freezing to evaluate re-expansion and hatching rates. Our results demonstrate that TLR2 activation in sperm significantly enhanced cleavage and blastocyst rates, increasing the overall efficiency of embryo production by approximately 10% compared to the control. Embryos derived from Pam-treated sperm exhibited superior biological quality, characterized by increased total cell numbers in blastocysts and reduced apoptosis in expanded blastocysts. Mechanistically, TLR2 activation elevated basal calcium levels and modulated autophagic flux, indicating a beneficial shift in metabolic programming. Notably, while the absolute number of embryos increased, the proportion of grade A blastocysts and their post-thaw survival metrics remained comparable to the control. This suggests that while TLR2 signaling effectively scales up IVP efficiency, the resulting developmental gains represent a hidden physiological strength that conventional morphological grading may not fully capture. These findings indicate that pre-IVF sperm treatment with Pam effectively primes the embryo for superior cellular development while maintaining the essential cryo-survival standards required for commercial applications.Keywords: Cattle, Embryo development, Japanese Black, Sperm, Toll-like receptor 2

The efficiency of in vitro embryo production (IVP) is a basis of cattle breeding, facilitating the distribution of superior genetics and the production of high-value crossbred cattle [1]. However, achieving consistent developmental competence remains a significant challenge [2]. In vitro-produced embryos often exhibit lower quality compared to in vivo embryos, characterized by altered metabolic profiles and a reduced ability to survive the physical stresses of cryopreservation [3, 4]. Cryotolerance, the capacity of a blastocyst to survive freezing and thawing, is essential for the commercial viability of IVP, yet the molecular pathways that can be targeted to enhance this survival potential remain under investigation [5].

Toll-like receptors (TLRs), traditionally recognized as primary sensors of the innate immune response, have recently emerged as vital modulators of reproductive success [6]. Among these, TLR2 is of particular interest as it is expressed throughout the bovine reproductive tract and on gametes [7,8,9,10,11]. Our previous work revealed that TLR2 activation in Holstein sperm triggers critical physiological transitions, including hyperactivation and the acrosome reaction, which ultimately improve fertilization and subsequent embryonic development [10, 12].

Building on these findings, it is evident that the beneficial effects of TLR2 activation are mediated through the calibration of essential metabolic pathways, most notably cytoplasmic calcium (Ca2+) signaling and autophagy. As a versatile second messenger, Ca2+ serves as a major signalling molecule during the transition from the fertilized ovum to the blastocyst stage; indeed, even a single Ca2+ transient is sufficient to accelerate developmental speed in mammalian embryos [13,14,15]. In parallel, intracellular Ca2+ dynamics are fundamentally linked to the induction of autophagy, a homeostatic process required for the degradation of damaged organelles and the recycling of amino acids for protein neo-synthesis during preimplantation [16,17,18]. In murine models, the absence of functional autophagy leads to developmental arrest beyond the 4- to 8-cell stage [19], underscoring the necessity of these cytoprotective pathways for sustained embryonic viability.

Furthermore, we recently identified robust TLR2 expression during the early embryonic stages [20]. In our study on 4-cell stage embryos, we demonstrated that TLR2 activation at 36 h post-insemination significantly enhances blastocyst rates and developmental speed. This improvement was linked to a specific metabolic signature: a slight elevation in basal cytosolic Ca2+ levels and the induction of autophagy, which collectively suppressed cathepsin B activity and DNA damage, thereby reducing apoptosis [20].

While these findings establish TLR2 as a potent regulator of both gamete function and early embryogenesis, it remains unknown whether these metabolic benefits can be initiated even earlier, at the point of fertilization. Specifically, it is unclear if priming sperm with a TLR2 agonist can exert a prolonged influence that carries these metabolic advantages, such as modulated calcium signaling and induced autophagy, through to the blastocyst stage. Determining whether sperm-TLR2 signaling can improve the internal machinery, embryo viability, and subsequent cryotolerance of the resulting embryo is essential for advancing both clinical and commercial applications.

In this study, we investigated how treating Japanese Black (JB) sperm with the TLR2 agonist Pam3CSK4 (Pam) impacts the developmental potential, viability, morphological quality and cryotolerance of crossbred embryos produced with Holstein oocytes. Beyond evaluating standard cleavage and blastocyst rates, we focused on deeper physiological shifts in embryonic metabolism, specifically basal Ca2+ signaling and autophagy, to determine if pre-IVF sperm modulation can serve as a targeted approach for producing high-quality embryos. To assess the commercial relevance of this treatment, selected embryos were cryopreserved and subsequently thawed to evaluate their post-thaw recovery. By monitoring re-expansion and hatching rates, this research seeks to establish whether sperm priming offers an effective method for enhancing both the production efficiency and the cryo-survival of high-value crossbred cattle embryos.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

Animal experiments described in this article were conducted following the Guiding Principles for the Care and Use of Research Animals of Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine, Japan. The protocol was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (Permit number 22–222).

Experimental design

The experimental design was structured to evaluate the influence of sperm-TLR2 activation on the developmental competence and cryotolerance of crossbred bovine embryos. To account for individual variability, cryopreserved sperm from three different JB bulls (bulls A, B, and C) were utilized. Given the high economic value of JB cattle due to their superior meat quality, evaluating the consistency of the treatment at the individual bull level is essential for practical application. Sperm were thawed and subjected to a 30-min incubation with 50 ng/ml of the TLR2 agonist (Pam group) or without supplementation (control group), followed by the fertilization of Holstein cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs). The resulting embryos were cultured for 7 days to quantify cleavage and blastocyst rates. Based on the superior and consistent developmental performance observed in bull A during these initial trials, embryos derived from this bull were utilized for all subsequent in-depth physiological analyses. These assessments aimed to reveal the underlying biological strength of the embryos beyond morphology, specifically through the measurement of basal cytosolic Ca2+ levels and autophagic flux across early developmental stages. Furthermore, embryonic quality was rigorously evaluated through total cell counts and apoptosis assays, alongside standard morphological grading on days 6.5 and 7. Finally, to assess the impact on cryosurvival, high-quality (grade A) blastocysts from day 6.5 and day 7 were selected for slow-freezing. Post-thaw recovery was monitored through re-expansion and hatching rates over a 72-h culture period. The comprehensive experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Sperm sample preparation and TLR2 activation

The synthetic TLR2 agonist Pam3CSK4 (ab142085, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; referred to as Pam) was utilized for the specific activation of sperm-TLR2. Pam3CSK4 is a potent TLR2 agonist that mimics the triacylated amino terminus of bacterial lipoproteins; it is recognized for its high selectivity, as it does not cross-react with other TLRs [10, 12, 20]. While our previous investigations established effective parameters for Holstein sperm (100 ng/ml for 30 min) [12], preliminary dose-response trials were conducted in this study to optimize the treatment for JB sperm. Based on these trials, an optimal Pam concentration of 50 ng/ml was identified for JB sperm, while the incubation duration was maintained at 30 min to align with previously established protocols for bovine species [12].

Frozen semen straws were obtained from three highly fertile JB bulls (bulls A, B, and C) provided by Genetics Hokkaido Association (Shimizu, Hokkaido, Japan) and AG Embryo Support Co., Ltd. (Obihiro, Hokkaido, Japan). For each experimental replicate, a single straw was thawed at 37ᵒC for 30 sec and diluted in modified sperm-Tyrode’s albumin lactate pyruvate (SP-TALP) medium [8, 12]. The suspension was washed twice via centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. Following the wash steps, the sperm pellet was resuspended in SP-TALP to a final concentration of 20 × 106 sperm/ml. The agonist Pam was dissolved in 50% ethanol to prepare a 100 µg/ml stock solution. The suspension was then divided into two experimental groups: the Pam group (supplemented with 0.5 µl of stock solution per 1000 µl of sperm suspension to achieve a concentration of 50 ng/ml Pam and 0.025% ethanol) and the control group (untreated). Both groups were incubated for 30 min at 38.5ᵒC under 5% CO2. After incubation, the sperm suspension was washed once in SP-TALP (300 × g for 5 min) and finally resuspended in IVF100 fertilization medium (Research Institute for the Functional Peptides Co., Ltd., Yamagata, Japan) for subsequent in vitro fertilization (IVF).

COCs recovery and in vitro maturation (IVM)

COCs collection and IVM were conducted based on the previous reports with minor modifications [12, 20]. Briefly, Holstein ovaries were obtained from a local slaughterhouse (Obihiro, Hokkaido, Japan) and transported to the laboratory within 1–2 h in a physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). COCs were aspirated from follicles ranging from 2 to 8 mm in diameter using an 18-G needle attached to a 10 ml syringe. The aspirated COCs were washed three times in oocyte collection medium (OCM; Research Institute for the Functional Peptides Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest, Nuaillé, France). Only COCs exhibiting a homogeneous cytoplasm and at least three layers of compact cumulus cells were selected for maturation. Groups of 30–60 COCs were transferred into high-performance modified 199 medium (HP-M199; Research Institute for the Functional Peptides Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 10 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (EGF; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 10% FBS. The oocytes were incubated for 22 h at 38.5ᵒC in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air.

IVF and in vitro culture (IVC)

Following Pam treatment, the sperm concentration was adjusted to 0.5 × 106 sperm/ml in IVF100 fertilization medium. Groups of 10–20 matured COCs were co-incubated with the prepared sperm for 6 h at 38.5ᵒC in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air.

At 6 h post-insemination (hpi), the remaining cumulus cells were removed from the presumptive zygotes by gentle, repeated pipetting. Groups of 30–60 zygotes were then transferred into 4-well culture dishes containing BO-IVC medium (IVF Bioscience, Falenty, Poland) under a mineral oil overlay. Unfertilized or degenerate oocytes were not discarded at this stage and remained in the culture throughout the entire incubation period. The embryos were cultured at 38.5ᵒC in a humidified triple-gas incubator (5% O2, 5% CO2, and 90% N2). The cleavage rate was recorded at 36 hpi (day 1.5), and the blastocyst development rate was evaluated on day 7. The blastocyst development rate was calculated as the percentage of total blastocysts relative to the total number of presumptive zygotes subjected to IVC.

Measuring cytosolic Ca2+ levels

Cytosolic Ca2+ levels were assessed using the cell-permeable fluorescent indicator Fluo-4 AM (F311; Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc., Kumamoto, Japan) as previously described with minor modifications [20, 21]. Day 7 blastocysts were washed three times in 0.1% polyvinyl alcohol in phosphate-buffered saline (PVA-PBS) and subsequently loaded with 5 µM Fluo-4 AM for 40 min at 38.5ᵒC in the dark. Following incubation, embryos were washed three times in 0.1% PVA-PBS to ensure the removal of extracellular (unbound) dye. The stained embryos were then transferred to 0.1% PVA-PBS droplets on a glass-bottom imaging dish (Iwaki, Shizuoka, Japan) and covered with mineral oil. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted microscope (BZ-X800; Keyence, Osaka, Japan) equipped with a BZ-X FITC filter (OP-87763) for green wavelength detection. To allow for accurate quantitative comparison, all images were acquired using identical exposure and gain settings. The mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software based on a previous report [20]. Briefly, the area of each blastocyst was defined as a region of interest, and a consistent threshold was applied across all images to exclude background noise and calculate the relative fluorescence intensity.

Detection of autophagy activity

Autophagic activity was analyzed using the Cyto-ID Autophagy Detection Kit (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) according to previously established protocols with minor modifications [20, 22]. Developmental stages assessed included 4-, 8-, and 16-cell embryos (day 1.5), morulae (day 5), and both early and expanded blastocysts (day 7). Embryos were washed three times in 0.1% PVA-PBS and subsequently incubated in a reaction mixture (1 µl Cyto-ID Green Detection Reagent in 500 µl 0.1% PVA-PBS) for 30 min at 38.5ᵒC. Following incubation, the embryos were transferred to 0.1% PVA-PBS imaging drops on a glass-bottom dish (Iwaki) under a mineral oil overlay.

Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800; Keyence) equipped with a BZ-X FITC (OP-87763) filter for green (autophagy) wavelength. To ensure comparability, all images were acquired using identical exposure settings. The relative fluorescence intensity of the autophagic signal was quantified using ImageJ software as previously described [20]. Briefly, individual embryos were defined as regions of interest, and the background was excluded by setting a consistent threshold across all analyzed images to calculate the mean fluorescence intensity.

Detection of apoptotic activity

Apoptotic activity in day 7 blastocysts was evaluated using the TUNEL-based In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Fluorescein; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) according to previously described methods with minor modifications [20, 23]. Briefly, blastocysts (early, and expanded) were washed three times in 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS (BSA-PBS). The embryos were then fixed and nuclear-stained by incubation in 4% paraformaldehyde containing 30 µg/ml Hoechst 33342 for 20 min in the dark. Following fixation, the embryos were washed three times in 2% BSA-PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. After three subsequent washes, the embryos were incubated in 50 µl of the TUNEL reaction mixture for 45 min at 38.5ᵒC in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in the dark. After a final series of washes in 0.2% BSA-PBS, the embryos were mounted on glass slides using Vectashield mounting medium (H-1000; Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) and examined under a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800; Keyence). The BZ-X DAPI (OP-87762) and BZ-X FITC (OP-87763) filters were used to visualize total nuclei (blue) and apoptotic nuclei (green), respectively. The apoptotic index was calculated as the ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei to the total number of nuclei (total cell count) per embryo.

Embryo morphological quality evaluation

Embryo morphological quality was assessed on day 6.5 and day 7 post-insemination according to the International Embryo Technology Society (IETS) guidelines [24] and previously established morphological criteria [25]. For this study, embryos were categorized into two primary quality groups: Grade A, comprising code 1 (excellent) and code 2 (good) embryos, and grade B, comprising code 3 (fair) embryos. Code 4 embryos (dead or degenerating) were excluded from all analyses. This standardized grading served as the primary selection criterion for identifying high-quality blastocysts for cryopreservation. The proportion of high-quality embryos was calculated as the percentage of grade A embryos relative to the total number of transferable embryos (grades A and B).

Slow freezing and thawing

High-quality expanded blastocysts at 156 h (day 6.5) and both blastocysts and expanded blastocysts at 168 h (day 7) post-insemination were selected for cryopreservation. Following IETS guidelines, only grade A embryos [24] were utilized for the freezing procedures as previously described protocols with modifications [26, 27]. The selected embryos were washed twice in BO-IVC medium and subsequently washed three times in a commercial bovine embryo cryopreservation solution (Research Institute for the Functional Peptides), followed by equilibration for 20–30 min at room temperature (20–25ᵒC). During equilibration, groups of 1 to 6 embryos were loaded into 0.25 ml straws (Fujihira Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The straws were then placed into a programmable dry-freezing device (Yamanetec, Nagano, Japan) pre-chilled to –7ᵒC. After 2 min of equilibration at –7ᵒC, manual seeding was performed using a pre-cooled L-shaped metal rod to induce controlled ice crystal formation and prevent supercooling. Following an additional 15 min of equilibration at –7ᵒC, the straws were cooled to –32ᵒC at a constant rate of –0.5ᵒC/min. Upon reaching the target temperature, the straws were immediately plunged into and stored in liquid nitrogen for at least one week.

For thawing, straws were held in ambient air for 10 sec and then immersed in a 30ᵒC water bath for 30 sec. The embryos were expelled into PBS supplemented with 5% FBS and washed twice in BO-IVC medium. The thawed embryos were then transferred to 4-well dishes containing BO-IVC medium under a mineral oil overlay and cultured for 72 h at 38.5ᵒC in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2, 5% O2, and 90% N2). Cryotolerance was assessed by monitoring the re-expansion and hatching rates at 24, 48, and 72 h post-thaw.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 5 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The data in all experiments were normally distributed. For the embryonic development data, the individual bull was analyzed as random variable. Equality of variances was assessed using the F-test. For data with equal variances, the standard Student’s t-test was applied to compare the mean differences between two groups; in cases where variances were significantly different, Welch’s correction was applied. All values are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data were considered to be statistically significant at * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, **** P < 0.0001.

For initial developmental trials, 5 replicates per bull were conducted using presumptive zygotes from Bull A (control: n = 538, Pam: n = 488), Bull B (control: n = 309, Pam: n = 306), and Bull C (control: n = 376, Pam: n = 361). Cytosolic Ca2+ levels were quantified across 4 replicates in day 7 blastocysts (control: n = 23, Pam: n = 28) and expanded blastocysts (control: n = 21, Pam: n = 26). Stage-specific autophagic flux was monitored across 3 replicates, evaluating 4-cell, 8-cell, 16-cell, morula, day 7 blastocyst, and expanded blastocyst stages (exact group sizes are detailed in the respective figure legends). Finally, cellularity and apoptotic indices were determined across 4 replicates in day 7 blastocysts (control: n = 36, Pam: n = 25) and expanded blastocysts (control: n = 37, Pam: n = 37). To ensure data integrity, relative fluorescence intensity data sets were screened for extreme statistical outliers, which were removed before final analysis.

Results

Sperm-TLR2 activation increased the cleavage and blastocyst rates of cross-bred embryos

Sperm-TLR2 activation significantly enhanced the cleavage rate in bull A compared to the control group (85.27 ± 1.63% vs. 91.72 ± 0.81%, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Pam treatment did not significantly modulate cleavage rates in bulls B and C (Figs. 2B and 2C). Notably, however, Pam treatment significantly increased day 7 blastocyst rates across all three bulls, with an average increase of approximately 10%: bull A (33.77 ± 2.19% vs. 44.70 ± 1.61%, P < 0.01), bull B (34.45 ± 2.00% vs. 44.04 ± 0.75%, P < 0.01), and bull C (33.07 ± 3.24% vs. 47.12 ± 3.88%, P < 0.05) (Figs. 2A, 2B, and 2C). These results indicate that while the effect on early cleavage may be bull-dependent, TLR2 activation consistently improves overall blastocyst development and IVP efficiency in crossbred embryos.

Sperm-TLR2 activation elevates the intracellular Ca2+ levels in cross-bred blastocysts

Sperm-TLR2 activation significantly elevated the basal intracellular Ca2+ levels in resulting day 7 blastocysts (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A). This stimulatory effect persisted into the expanded blastocyst stage, which also exhibited significantly higher Ca2+ concentrations compared to the control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that the initial activation of TLR2 in sperm triggers a sustained modulation of calcium signaling that is maintained throughout preimplantation development.

Stage-specific modulation of autophagic activity by sperm-TLR2 activation

Sperm-TLR2 activation exerted a dynamic, stage-specific influence on autophagic activity during early embryonic development. In the initial cleavage stages, Pam treatment resulted in a significant reduction of autophagic activity at the 4-cell stage compared to the control group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A), whereas no significant differences were observed at the 8-cell stage (Fig. 4B). Conversely, autophagic activity was significantly enhanced in the Pam group during the 16-cell (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4C) and morula stages (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4D). By day 7, however, this effect decreased, with no significant differences in autophagic activity detected in either the blastocyst or expanded blastocyst stages (Figs. 4E and 4F). These results suggest that TLR2-mediated signaling selectively regulates autophagic flux during the critical transitions of early cleavage and compaction.

Sperm-TLR2 activation increases total cell number in blastocysts and suppresses apoptosis during blastocyst expansion

Sperm-TLR2 activation significantly increased the total cell number in day 7 blastocysts compared to the control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A); however, this effect was not observed in embryos that had already reached the expanded blastocyst stage (Fig. 5B). Conversely, while the apoptotic rate remained unchanged in day 7 blastocysts (Fig. 5A), Pam treatment significantly reduced the apoptotic index in expanded blastocysts (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that TLR2 signaling promotes cell proliferation in early blastocysts and subsequently enhances cellular survival by suppressing programmed cell death during the transition to the expanded stage.

Impact of sperm-TLR2 activation on embryo morphological quality

Sperm-TLR2 activation did not significantly alter the morphological quality of the resulting embryos. Specifically, the proportion of grade A embryos remained consistent between the Pam-treated and control groups across all assessed stages, including day 6.5 expanded blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 1A), day 7 blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 1B), and day 7 expanded blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 1C). These results indicate that the physiological and molecular enhancements triggered by TLR2 activation occur without inducing visible changes in the gross morphological characteristics of crossbred bovine embryos.

Impact of sperm-TLR2 activation on post-thaw survival and cryotolerance

Sperm-TLR2 activation did not significantly alter the post-thaw recovery metrics of grade A crossbred embryos. Specifically, re-expansion rates remained comparable between the Pam-treated and control groups across all assessed stages, day 6.5 expanded blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 2A), day 7 blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 2B ), and day 7 expanded blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 2C), at 24, 48, and 72 h post-thaw. Similarly, Pam treatment did not significantly modulate the hatching rates for day 6.5 expanded blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 2D), day 7 blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 2E), or day 7 expanded blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. 2F) at any of the observed time points. These results indicate that while TLR2 activation enhances certain physiological parameters during pre-implantation development, it does not exert a visible influence on the gross cryotolerance or hatching ability of morphologically superior embryos following slow-freezing.

Discussion

This study investigated how activating TLR2 on sperm influences the developmental competence and physiological status of crossbred bovine embryos. By treating JB sperm with the TLR2 agonist Pam before fertilization, we observed significant increases in both early cleavage rates and blastocyst yields, specifically resulting in a 10% increase in total embryo production. Beyond these growth parameters, our results demonstrate that TLR2 signaling in sperm triggers a sustained elevation in internal embryo Ca2+ levels and modulates autophagic activity in a stage-specific manner. Furthermore, while TLR2 activation did not alter the gross physical appearance of the embryos, it enhanced their quality by increasing total cell numbers and reducing programmed cell death during the expansion stage. Although the ability of the embryos to survive the slow-freezing and thawing process remained comparable to the control group, these findings demonstrate that sperm-TLR2 activation significantly promotes the physiological development without compromising the survival of crossbred bovine embryos. The schematic illustrates the signaling cascade initiated by Pam3CSK4 (Pam)-treated sperm during fertilization and its subsequent impact on embryonic development. (Left) Upon fertilization, the activation of the TLR2/MyD88 signaling complex by sperm factors triggers a downstream regulatory axis within the zygote. (Middle) During early embryonic reprogramming (days 2–5), this activation leads to a synergistic modulation of two verified pathways: (A) Modulated cytosolic Ca2+ flux, which serves as a secondary messenger for downstream development; and (B) Elevated autophagic flux, particularly during the 16-cell and morula stages, facilitating cellular remodeling and metabolic support. (Right) Collectively, these physiological enhancements translate into superior day 7 developmental outcomes, characterized by significantly increased total cell counts and a marked reduction in apoptotic indices. A key finding of our study is that sperm-TLR2 activation by Pam significantly enhanced early cleavage and subsequent blastocyst development. While the improvement in cleavage rates varied among the three bulls used, the final yield of day 7 blastocysts was consistently higher in the Pam-treated groups. This suggests that TLR2 signaling on the sperm surface may play a vital role in the early events of fertilization or the initial stages of embryonic genome activation [12, 28]. Previous research has shown that TLR2 is involved in recognizing molecular patterns and triggering pathways that support cell survival and metabolism [20]. In this context, the activation of TLR2 likely primes the sperm or the resulting zygote to better handle the stresses of in vitro culture. The fact that blastocyst yields improved even in cases where cleavage rates were similar to the control suggests that the benefits of TLR2 activation are not limited to the moment of fertilization but continue to support the embryo throughout its pre-implantation development [29, 30].

The observed increase in intracellular Ca2+ levels in embryos following sperm-TLR2 activation likely serves as a primary signal for the downstream physiological changes seen in the developing embryos [20]. Calcium is well-known to act as a secondary messenger [31] that regulates several cellular processes, including the initiation of autophagy [16, 32]. Our data revealed a dynamic shift in autophagic activity: a reduction at the 4-cell stage followed by a significant increase during the 16-cell and morula stages. This response may reflect the embryo's changing metabolic needs during the transition from maternal to embryonic genomic control [33,34,35]. Early in development (4-cell stage), lower autophagy may preserve maternal resources, while the later increase at the morula stage could facilitate the intensive cellular remodeling required for compaction and blastocyst formation [33, 34, 36]. By maintaining elevated Ca2+ levels, TLR2 signaling may provide the necessary regulatory environment to fine-tune these autophagic transitions [20, 37]. This molecular priming translates into a higher intrinsic biological strength, ensuring the embryo has the metabolic strength required for successful development and cellular survival [19].

Furthermore, our results demonstrated that sperm-TLR2 activation significantly increased the total cell number in day 7 blastocysts and reduced the apoptotic rate specifically within expanded blastocysts. In bovine embryo production, total cell count is a critical indicator of developmental competence, as a higher number of cells is often associated with improved implantation potential and better post-transfer outcomes [38, 39]. The observed reduction in apoptosis in expanded blastocysts is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests that TLR2 signaling helps the embryo better manage cellular stress during the rapid expansion phase [20, 39]. By suppressing programmed cell death and increasing the overall cell population, sperm-TLR2 activation produces embryos with enhanced cellular survival and structural integrity [12, 20]. These findings indicate that the physiological benefits of activating TLR2 on the sperm carry forward into the final stages of in vitro development, resulting in blastocysts of superior biological quality [20].

Interestingly, despite improvements in cellularity and reduced apoptosis, the percentage of grade A embryos and their subsequent cryotolerance were not modulated by Pam treatment. This observation highlights a critical distinction between morphological appearance and physiological status. While embryos in both groups were morphologically indistinguishable and met the same high-quality standards for cryopreservation, the true impact of sperm-TLR2 activation was only revealed through deeper molecular and cellular analysis, including Ca2+ signaling dynamics, stage-specific autophagic flux, and programmed cell death markers. These findings suggest that conventional morphological evaluation alone may not be sufficient to fully assess the developmental competence of in vitro-produced embryos [40, 41]. Instead, our results demonstrate that sperm-TLR2 activation enhances the underlying biological strength and structural integrity of crossbred embryos. This implies that while these developmental gains do not translate into visible changes in cryotolerance under current slow-freezing protocols, they represent a significant improvement in hidden embryonic quality that warrants further investigation in post-transfer studies [42].

Beyond the cellular improvements observed, a primary finding of this study is the significant enhancement in IVP efficiency. By increasing blastocyst production by ~10%, we show that Pam-treatment allows more embryos to reach the final stage while still maintaining the same high morphological standards. Critically, because the percentage of grade A embryos remained steady while the total number of embryos increased, the absolute yield of transferable, high-quality embryos is higher. In a commercial setting, this translates to a more productive system where a higher number of embryos meets the rigorous standards for cryopreservation, even if the individual freezing survival of those embryos remains at the current physiological level.

We consider embryonic quality to be a multifaceted concept encompassing the embryo's ability to reach advanced stages with appropriate timing, maintain cellular health, and possess efficient regulatory mechanisms, such as modulated Ca2+ signaling and autophagic flux, necessary for successful implantation [20]. Our data collectively indicate a significant improvement in these quality indicators following sperm-TLR2 agonist treatment. Notably, these improvements in developmental competence and cellular strength align with our previous findings where TLR2 was activated directly at the 4-cell stage [20]. This suggests that the beneficial effects of this pathway are conserved, whether initiated during the earliest events of fertilization or during the transition to embryonic genomic control [12, 28], further validating the TLR2 signaling axis as a primary driver of bovine embryonic quality.

While our study focuses on the embryonic outcomes of sperm-TLR2 activation, the immediate effects on sperm physiology before fertilization likely contribute to this carry-over phenomenon. Although not directly measured in these experiments, previous research has indicated that TLR2 activation can modulate sperm kinetic parameters [10]. Pam3CSK4 treatment may prime sperm for efficient gamete interaction or trigger the release of paternal signaling factors that remain active through the first mitotic divisions. By enhancing the initial fertilization event, sperm-TLR2 activation could establish a more vigorous developmental trajectory before embryonic genome activation.

Although this study demonstrates the benefits of sperm-TLR2 activation on bovine IVP efficiency and embryo health, several limitations remain. First, while highly controlled, our in vitro system does not fully replicate the physiological environment of the bovine oviduct and uterus; thus, the long-term impact on implantation and full-term pregnancy must be validated via embryo transfer trials. Second, although three JB bulls were used to account for individual variability, expanding future studies to a larger, cross-breed sire population would confirm universal applicability. Third, while consistency in blastocyst yield was verified across all three bulls, detailed mechanistic analyses were limited to a single representative sire. Investigating potential individual variations in these specific intracellular signals is warranted. Finally, the exact distribution of lower-grade, degenerate embryos (such as code 4) was not quantified. Characterizing the complete morphological grading ratios across the entire embryo pool will further elucidate how paternal priming rescues poor-quality embryos from developmental arrest.

In conclusion, pre-IVF treatment of sperm with Pam3CSK4 provides a dual benefit: it significantly boosts the quantitative efficiency of bovine IVP by approximately 10% and simultaneously improves the qualitative hidden health of the resulting blastocysts. Although these internal improvements such as modulated Ca2+ levels, autophagic flux and reduced apoptosis, are not evident as visible morphological changes, the increased yield of high-quality embryos offers a practical and vital tool for the livestock industry. These findings suggest that sperm-TLR2 priming is a viable method for maximizing the output of superior-quality embryos in assisted reproductive technologies.
