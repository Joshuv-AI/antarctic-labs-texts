Structure Elucidation and Initial Characterization of a N-glucuronide Metabolite (M1) of Tuvusertib
Authors: Benedikt Lang, Hanno Schieferstein, Kai Thede, Matthias Bader, Tianjing Ren, Jatinder Kaur Mukker, Holger Scheible
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveThe investigational DNA-damage response inhibitor tuvusertib showed the formation of a direct glucuronide in hepatocyte incubations. Early metabolite identification workflows were unable to assign the site of modification. Thus, a synthesis approach was established to facilitate structure elucidation as well as an initial characterization regarding drug–drug interaction and safety.MethodsDifferent methods for generating sufficient amounts of the glucuronide were assessed and after purification structure elucidation of the glucuronide was performed via nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR). A series of in vitro experiments were conducted to determine its potential risk for drug–drug interaction. Confirmation of its identity was achieved by co-chromatography with hepatic incubations, which initially showed the presence of the metabolite.ResultsChemical synthesis showed the most promising route for the formation of the proposed glucuronide and NMR determined the modification to be at the primary amino group attached to the pyrazolo-pyrimidine moiety. The retention time as well as the mass spectra in the co-chromatography aligned with the glucuronide of interest in the hepatocyte incubation, confirming the synthesized metabolite to be M1. Subsequent in vitro experiments showed that M1 did not inhibit any major CYP enzyme.ConclusionsThe tuvusertib metabolite M1 was identified as an N-glucuronide demonstrating no direct liabilities associated with drug–drug interactions (DDI) or safety implications. Furthermore, M1 can be used in bioanalyses as retention time marker or reference material in further clinical evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13318-026-01006-0.

Introduction

Tuvusertib (see Fig. 1) is an investigational, highly selective ATR inhibitor that is under clinical development both as monotherapy and in combination with other DNA damage response (DDR) inhibitors. Since many tumors exhibit elevated levels of replication stress, which can occur endogenously due to oncogene expression or as a result of treatment with DNA-damaging agents commonly used in cancer therapies, ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related (ATR) kinase, a critical component of DNA damage response (DDR) that coordinates cellular responses to replication stress during DNA replication, is triggered [1, 2].Fig. 1Chemical structure of tuvusertib

Chemical structure of tuvusertib

Once activated, ATR phosphorylates key downstream targets including checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1), triggering cell cycle arrest, stabilizing replication forks, and promoting DNA repair pathways [2, 3]. ATR inhibition, on the contrary, disrupts the protective replication stress response leading to the accumulation of genomic instability and ultimately cancer cell death [4].

Tuvusertib demonstrated pronounced activity as monotherapy in preclinical tumor models with gene mutations that result in a reliance on ATR activity, including an ATM-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) xenograft model and an ARID1A-mutated gastric cancer xenograft model [5]. Tuvusertib also exhibited antitumor activity as monotherapy and in combination with the poly-ADP Ribose Polymerase (PARP) inhibitor niraparib and the ATM inhibitor lartesertib in xenograft models [5, 6]. The safety, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and preliminary efficacy of tuvusertib monotherapy has been assessed in a phase 1 trial (NCT04170153) in patients with advanced solid tumors [7, 8]. Additionally, the combination of tuvusertib with other DDR inhibitors, including the PARP inhibitor niraparib and the ATM inhibitor lartesertib, is currently being evaluated in phase 1 clinical trials [9, 10] and a phase 2 study in patients with epithelial ovarian cancer [11].

An integrated approach for early characterization of the ADME characteristics, especially metabolism and clearance pathways, is essential for small molecule drugs in drug development [12]. This includes glucuronidation being a major route for the clearance of xenobiotics facilitated by the enzyme family of UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGT) via conjugation of endogenous uridine diphosphate glucuronic acid (UDPGA) to various functional groups: e.g., hydroxyl groups forming O-glucuronides, carboxylic acid groups forming acyl-glucuronides, amines forming N-glucuronides, and thiols and sulfoxides forming S-glucuronides [13].

During the preclinical development of tuvusertib, a direct glucuronide (mass shift +176 amu) was identified in in vitro biotransformation studies in human hepatocyte incubations, which are essential studies for early understanding of the drug disposition as well as to identify potential safety concerns [14]. Direct glucuronides are of great interest since they can lead to hepatotoxic, idiosyncratic effects if they are reactive, such as acyl-glucuronides, with the potential to interact with drug-metabolizing enzymes under physiological conditions [15, 16]. In contrast to acyl-glucuronides, to our knowledge no N-glucuronide has been reported to directly cause toxicity. However, we acknowledge that there could be a potential risk when N-glucuronides (being often Mrp-2 substrates) reach tissues or organs where the physiological conditions (acidic conditions) lead to a cleavage of the glucuronic acid, and therefore conversion back to parent drug. This back conversion can ultimately cause toxicities in target tissues [17], and has been studied in early days in detail for N-hydroxy amines [18, 19].

Another potential concern about glucuronide metabolites is the pharmacological on target activity, which could have an impact on the PK/PD relationship, if not fully understood. That fact has been described in detail for morphine, where the morphine-6-glucuronide is 20-fold more active as an analgesic than morphine itself [20–22]. Similar potent activity has been noted for glucuronide conjugates of digitoxin, digoxin [23], and fluorouracil [24], as well as recent drug candidates [25]. Interestingly, for fluorouracil, the O-glucuronide was active on tumor growth in an in vivo animal experiment, but the N-glucuronide was not [26].

In some cases glucuronides have been reported to cause drug–drug interactions (DDIs), such as gemfibrozil or clopidogrel, which inhibit CYP2C8 resulting in an increase in exposure of co-medicated drugs metabolized by CYP2C8 [27, 28]. Besides acyl-glucuronides, N-glucuronides have also been reported to decrease enzyme activity via time-dependent inhibition (TDI), e.g., mirtazapine N-glucuronide on CYP2C9 or regorafenib N-β-D-glucuronide on CYP2C8.

Guidelines from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or European Medicines Agency (EMA) advise that major metabolites be characterized toward their inhibitory potential, but this has often been neglected for drugs approved a long time ago, especially when it comes to glucuronide conjugates [29, 30]. To be able to perform an initial characterization of the direct glucuronide M1 of tuvusertib (tuvusertib+176 amu), microbial and chemical synthesis approaches were employed. Initially, sufficient amounts of M1 were generated for nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy investigations to confirm the structure proposal. Furthermore, in vitro DDI studies were performed to generate a comprehensive preliminary package of the characteristics of M1.

Our approach depicts how an integrated metabolite assessment strategy can create synergies to initially de-risk potential DDIs or safety concerns of a metabolite and further utilize the synthesized compound for an efficient chromatographic method development for upcoming metabolite investigation studies.

As a side note, the synthesized M1 is also intended to support and facilitate the metabolite identification process in future clinical evaluations, e.g., as reference or retention time standard [31].

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Water purified by the Milli-Q IQ 7000 purification system, Supelco LicChrosolv HPLC-grade acetonitrile (ACN), Suprapur formic acid, LiChropur LC-MS grade ammonium formate, phosphate buffered saline, Uvasol dimethylsulfoxide, and Trypan blue staining solution were all provided by Merck.

Substrates amodiaquine, dextromethorphan, diclofenac, midazolam, phenacetin, and S-mephenytoin, their respective metabolites α-hydroxy-midazolam, 4’-hydroxy-diclofenac, as well as the isotopologues [2H]-α-hydroxy-midazolam, [13C]-4’-hydroxy-diclofenac, and [2H]-dextrorphan, were all purchased from Sigma Aldrich Chemie GmbH (Taufkirchen, Germany). A deuterated analogue of tuvusertib used as internal standard was provided by the local compound logistics department of Merck Healthcare KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany.

Human liver microsomes, human hepatocytes, and Invitrogro HT medium and Invitrogro Krebs-Henseleit buffer (KHB) were all purchased from BioIVT LLC (West Sussex, UK). Nicotine amide adenine dinucleotide (NADPH) tetrasodium salt was obtained from AppliChem GmbH (Darmstadt, Germany).

Pooled female CD1-mouse serum was purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Sulzfeld, Germany), while human pooled serum was available from healthy voluntary donors, which was sampled in the Merck occupational health department (Darmstadt, Germany).

All other chemicals were obtained from chemical suppliers and used without further purification, if not otherwise specified.

Incubation and Metabolite Identification in Human Hepatocytes

Incubation of tuvusertib in human hepatocytes for the identification and allocation of metabolites were conducted at a final incubation concentration of 10 µM. Thus, a 10 mM stock solution was serially diluted to 20 µM in KHB buffer and prewarmed at 37 °C, as well as the buffer used in hepatocyte preparation. Human hepatocytes were carefully thawed in HT medium and centrifuged at 50 × g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in KHB buffer to a final cell concentration of 4 × 106 cells mL−1, which was confirmed by cell counting. After transferring the cells to a 24-well plate the incubation was started by addition of the 20 µM compound solution 1+1 (V/V) to the cell suspension. Incubation was conducted at 37 °C at a CO2 concentration of 5% for 5 h with a shaking speed of 150 rpm. The incubation was stopped by protein precipitation using acetonitrile (ACN) with twofold of the volume, and subsequent centrifugation at 16,100 × g. The supernatant was diluted 1+4 (V/V) with 0.1% formic acid (FA) and subjected to HRMS. Detailed instrumental parameters regarding chromatographic conditions and MS data acquisition are listed in the supplemental information (see Supplementary Information 1.1).

Upscaling of Chemical Screening Route and Structural Elucidation

Synthesis of (2S,3S,4S,5R)-6-((6-fluoro-3-((5-fluoro-4-(1-methyl-1H-imidazol-5-yl)pyridin-3-yl)carbamoyl)pyrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidin-2-yl)amino)-3,4,5-trihydroxytetrahydro-2H-pyran-2-carboxylic acid (M1): 1 (100 mg, 270 µmol) was suspended in a mixture of acetone (10 mL) and water (10 mL). 2 (1.31 g, 6.75 mmol, 25 eq.) was added and the solution was stirred for 96 h at 40 °C. The mixture was concentrated and purified by preparative HPLC. Two identical preparations as described above were run in parallel and the purified materials were combined to give M1 (133 mg, 30% of theory) as a pale-yellow solid.

The analytical data package (NMR and HRMS) for the final upscaled product (M1) can be found in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S1–S5).

NMR Spectroscopy

The full structure elucidation of M1 was determined by NMR spectra including 1H NMR, 13C NMR, Distortionless Enhancement by Polarization Transfer (DEPT-135), Heteronuclear Single Quantum Correlation (HSQC), correlation spectroscopy (COSY), and Heteronuclear Multiple Bond Correlation (HMBC). NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance IIITM HD spectrometer (1H NMR 600 MHz, 13C NMR 150 MHz, Bruker BioSpin GmbH & Co. KG, Ettlingen, Germany) at room temperature for solutions in DMSO-d6. Chemical shifts are expressed in ppm and are reported relative to the solvent signal (2.50 ppm for 1H NMR and 39.5 ppm for 13C NMR).

Elemental Composition by High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry

High resolution precursor mass spectra were obtained by infusion of 1 µM synthesized M1 at 5 µL min−1 on Orbitrap IQ-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany) equipped with a OptaMax NG heated-ESI source (H-ESI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany) using the internal calibration configuration (EASY-IC) with fluoranthene as a lock mass, which compensates for scan-to-scan variations and temperature changes. Spectra were recorded at positive ionization for 0.5 min at a resolution 500,000 using a scan range of m/z 150–1000 with 1 microscan, a maximum injection time of 100 ms, and a radio frequency (RF) level of 70%. An average spectrum of ten scans was used for the determination of the elemental composition.

Caco-2 Cell Permeability Assay

Caco-2 cell (TC7) monolayers, which had been cultured for 14 days and prepared by Site Management Lab Services department (SM-L) of Merck KGaA, were obtained in 24-transwell plates. Apical as well as the basolateral compartments were washed twice with HBSS buffer (0.5% (V/V), pH 7.4) prior to the assay. M1 was prepared at a final concentration by a two-step dilution with DMSO and HBSS to a final incubation concentration of 1 µM (0.5% (V/V) DMSO, pH 7.4). The incubation was initiated by addition of M1 to the apical and basolateral compartments and the Caco-2 monolayer was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Samples being drawn after this time were quenched with acetonitrile containing pruvanserine as internal standard. For the LC-MS/MS analysis, the samples were diluted 1+9 (V/V) with ACN/HBSS (1+1, V/V). Quenched samples were analyzed via LC-MS/MS and apparent permeabilities as well as the efflux ratio (ER) were calculated according to the equations in the supplements (see Supplementary Information 1.3).

Direct Inhibition in Human Liver Microsomes

Two incubations with different sets of probe substrates were used to evaluate the inhibition potential of M1 at 1 µM in human liver microsomes. The first set included probe substrates amodiaquine for CYP2C8, diclofenac for CYP2C9, and midazolam for CYP3A4/5, which were incubated for 10 min while the second set contained phenacetin for CYP1A2, S-mephenytoin for CYP2C19, and dextromethorphan for CYP2D6 for a 45 min incubation.

The different CYP probe substrate mixes (see Supplementary Information 1.4) were preincubated at 37 °C at 800 × rpm for 5 min with M1 and the reactions were started with the addition of the human liver microsomes (HLM) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) at final incubation concentrations of 0.1 mg/mL and 1 mM, respectively. The incubations were terminated by addition of twofold volume of ACN containing an internal standard for normalization. Additionally, a positive control was performed by incubating the probe substrates without M1 in the incubation. The quenched incubations were further diluted 1+9 (V/V) with 66% ACN prior to the LC-MS/MS analysis.

Time-Dependent Inhibition of Cytochrome P450 Enzymes

Time-dependent inhibition (TDI) of midazolam, diclofenac, and dextromethorphan serving as reference substrates for CYP isoforms 3A4, 2C9, and 2D6 was evaluated in duplicates in human microsomes in the presence of M1 at 10 µM. Therefore, a preincubation solution containing HLM (0.5 mg/mL), NADPH (1 mM), magnesium chloride (MgCl2, 3.3 mM), and M1 (10 µM) in PBS (100 mM, pH 7.4) was incubated at 37 °C, and aliquots thereof were drawn after the following timepoints: 0, 7, 13.5, 20, 26.5, and 33 min. Subsequently, each aliquot was subjected to a 5-min second incubation containing midazolam, diclofenac, and dextromethorphan as well as their respective internal standards of their respective metabolites. After quenching via protein precipitation and centrifugation, the supernatant was used for analysis via LC-MS/MS (for further details see Supplementary Information 1.5).

Protein Binding in Serum

Serum protein binding was tested in 50% human and mouse serum, which was prediluted with 70 mM phosphate buffered solution pH 7.4 (PBS) at a final compound concentration of 0.5 µM using the rapid equilibrium dialysis device (RED, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Langenselbold, Germany). Stock solutions were prepared 100-fold concentrated in 1+1 (V/V) H2O/ACN and mixed with the diluted serum, which was preheated at 37 °C. The incubation was directly initiated by transferring the spiked serum samples into the sample chambers (donor) and PBS into the buffer chamber (acceptor) of the device and equilibrium dialysis was carried out for 4 h at 37 °C and 800 rpm. The reaction was stopped by quenching an aliquot of each chamber with twofold acetonitrile containing an internal standard at 200 nM. To balance out matrix differences between the two sample types, the quench solutions were matrix-matched with the respective missing matrix (i.e., addition of PBS to quench solution inheriting diluted serum from the sample chamber and vice versa for samples of the buffer chamber). Quenched samples were stored on ice for 30 min followed by centrifugation for 30 min at 4 °C and 3500 rpm. An aliquot of the resulting supernatant was diluted 2+1 (V/V) with PBS and analyzed with LC-MS/MS.

Considering the dilution factor (DF) of the serum, it was then possible to assess the fraction unbound in serum fu,p according to equations detailed in the Supplementary Section (see Supplementary Information 1.6).

Results

Incubation of Tuvusertib in Human Hepatocytes

Tuvusertib was incubated in a suspension of human hepatocytes at 10 µM for 5 h. Metabolite identification by LC-HRMS showed turnover of parent leading to the formation of several oxidation products as well as glucuronidations and combinations thereof. The focus was on the detection and identification of direct glucuronides where we were able to detect only one direct glucuronide. A detailed characterization of the other metabolites is beyond the scope of this manuscript.

Microbial, Chemical Glucuronidation Screening, and Co-Chromatography of M1

The microbial screen, employing 55 strains that are known to produce glucuronides, did not lead to the desired glucuronide. Subsequently, a chemical screening of reaction conditions was performed, and glucuronide formations were monitored via HPLC-MS. Parameters such as solvent, reaction temperature, and pH were investigated. The condition with best yield according to HPLC-MS was achieved with acetone as organic solvent without adjustment of the pH value, forming two products with the desired molecular mass with 42% peak area (retention time 0.50 min) and 8% (retention time 0.53 min). In addition, 48% (retention time 0.77 min) were left as remaining starting material tuvusertib.

Discussion

The importance of an integrated metabolism strategy is manifold and can help to develop the understanding of compound properties early, supporting lean and fast candidate development from DMPK perspective. Here we report the utilization of early in vitro MetID data to synthesize the N-glucuronide of tuvusertib to enable an initial in vitro characterization with respect to drug–drug interactions as well as the usage of M1 as retention time standard for metabolite profiling for future clinical investigation of tuvusertib (NCT06308263) [31].

M1 was first seen in vitro using hepatocytes incubated with 14C-tuvusertib as a direct glucuronide, displaying a mass shift of +176 amu, and could later been confirmed by co-chromatography in plasma samples of the first-in-human trial. Since the glucuronide conjugation site was unclear based on the HRMS fragmentation data, we decided to perform a structural elucidation and to further characterize M1. As known from literature, direct glucuronides can pose concerns in drug development due to drug–drug interactions via direct/time-dependent inhibitors and safety issues, as well as being active against the target [20–22, 34, 35].

To this end, tuvusertib was subjected to a microbial screening including 55 fungi and bacteria strains, which are known to catalyze glucuronidations. However, this screening approach did not lead to the desired glucuronide. As an alternative route, a chemical screen was initiated by reacting tuvusertib directly with an excess of glucuronic acid in a mixture of organic solvent and water and under different reaction conditions. The best screening result was obtained with acetone as organic solvent without adjustment of the pH value and led to two direct glucuronides 3 and 4 with conversion yields of 42% and 8%, respectively, and co-chromatography with human hepatocyte incubations showed that 3 co-elutes with the desired M1 (+176 amu) metabolite. Subsequently, an upscaling campaign was initiated to obtain enough material for further characterization, which included three runs with a combined total yield 133 mg (30%) of M1. For the full structural elucidation, NMR experiments were performed, and connectivities in the HMBC spectrum confirmed the structure of a 6-N-glucuronide, as depicted in Fig. 2, with the glucuronide moiety connected to the amino group at the pyrazolopyrimidine.

The initially synthesized amount of M1 was used to get a first understanding on potential DDI liabilities and safety risks of M1. Thus, M1 has been tested for its in vitro serum protein binding to relate the total levels to free levels of the metabolite in plasma, which was ~54% for both human and mouse. As expected from the chemical structure, a permeability assay confirmed that M1 had a low passive permeability (0.24 cm s−1) and an efflux ratio of 2.6 indicating active transport out of the cell. Ultimately, M1 has been tested for direct and time-dependent inhibition. No direct inhibition toward CYPs 1A2, 2D6, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, and 3A4 was observed for M1. In the time-dependent assay, M1 showed kobs values of 0.005, 0.003, and 0.01 min−1 for CYP 2D6, 2C9, and 3A4, respectively. Since kobs values correlate well with the ratio of Kinact to KI, we neither expect an inhibition potential nor a DDI for kobs values below 0.02 min−1 [33].

On the basis of the in vitro characterization mentioned above, M1 did not demonstrate any direct liability concerning drug–drug interactions (DDI) or safety considerations. Further investigations with regard to metabolic pathway profiling via UGT phenotyping, MiST assessment, and pharmacologic activity testing will be carried out when the data of the ongoing human mass balance and absolute bioavailability study of tuvusertib (NCT06308263) [31] become available.
