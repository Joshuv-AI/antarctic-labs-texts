Impact of Regulatory Risk Communication on Thrombosis With Thrombocytopenia Syndrome for COVID‐19 Adenovirus Vector Vaccines on European Healthcare Professionals
Authors: Ella van Vliet, Nanča Čebron Lipovec, Marloes van der Goot, Shahab Abtahi, Inês Ribeiro ‐Vaz, Elita Poplavska, Foteini Dermiki‐Gkana, Chara Oikonomou, Elena Deligianni, Christos Kontogiorgis, Ana Marta Silva, Paula Barão Sousa Ferreira, Mitja Kos, Anna Birna Almarsdóttir, Ramune Jacobsen, Caroline Buhl, Adrie de Bruijn, Ingrid Hegger, Teresa Leonardo Alves
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe European Medicines Agency (EMA) issued regulatory actions and communications in 2021 on thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) associated with adenovirus vector vaccines Vaxzevria or Jcovden. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of these actions on awareness, knowledge and implementation in practises of healthcare professionals (HCP).MethodsWeb‐based cross‐sectional surveys were conducted on HCPs engaged in the vaccination, monitoring or counselling about the vaccines. We measured awareness and knowledge of the risk of TTS and their implementation of recommendations in practise. Descriptive and qualitative analyses were conducted. This study took place in Greece, Latvia, Netherlands, Portugal and Slovenia.ResultsWe surveyed 1659 HCPs. From these, 914 were included in the analysis. Most were aware about the reports of TTS associated with COVID‐19 adenovirus vector vaccines, with countries reporting percentages between 85% and 97%. Mainstream media, health authorities and peers were the main sources of TTS risk information. Most HCPs were able to identify key symptoms from TTS but were less familiar with minor symptoms. Guidelines from health authorities on COVID‐19 vaccination impacted on professional practise of 55%–77% of HCPs. The reported use of product information across countries was moderate, with some variations.ConclusionsAwareness about and knowledge of TTS risk from COVID‐19 adenoviral vector vaccines were high among HCPs. HCPs reported a clear preference for national guidelines as source of risk information and the implementation of product information remained moderate across countries.Keywords: COVID‐19 vaccines, healthcare professionals, regulatory actions, risk communication, SARS‐CoV‐2 adenovirus vector vaccines, thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome

Purpose

Reports of the very rare (< 1/10.000 vaccinated people) but potentially serious thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) emerged during 2021 as an adverse drug reaction (ADR) to the adenoviral vector vaccines Vaxzevria (AstraZeneca) and Jcovden (Janssen‐Cilag) [1]. These vaccines were developed to immunise against SARS‐CoV‐2. Vaxzevria (INN: COVID‐19 Vaccine (ChAdOx1‐S [recombinant])) was granted conditional marketing authorisation by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) at the end of January 2021 [2]. Jcovden (INN: COVID‐19 vaccine (Ad26.COV2‐S [recombinant])) was authorised conditionally by the EMA in March 2021 [3]. The first reports on TTS after vaccination with Vaxzevria were published in early April 2021 about patients in Germany and Austria [4]. A Danish‐Norwegian study proved causality between the use of Vaxzevria and TTS in early May 2021 [5]. Reports on thrombotic events with thrombocytopenia after vaccination with Jcovden were published in April 2021 in the United States [6]. Symptoms of TTS include severed headache, shortness of breath, chest pain or discomfort, swelling or pain in the legs, abdominal pain, a rash that looks like a bruise and nausea [7].

Throughout 2021, the EMA issued multiple safety warnings, adapted the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) and Package Leaflet (PL), provided recommendations and disseminated Direct Healthcare Professional Communications (DHPC) on TTS (Figure 1) [8, 9]. During a crisis, it is essential that decisive information reaches its target audience as quickly as possible. Throughout the COVID‐19 pandemic, a wealth of information and knowledge was disseminated [10, 11]. The information made available was essential for healthcare professionals (HCP) and used as a resource when informing patients or altering practises as to vaccination guidance, TTS monitoring and diagnosis. Little is known about the impact of official EMA communication on HCPs and their experiences with risk communication during the COVID‐19 pandemic [12]. The emergence and prominence of TTS during the COVID‐19 vaccine roll out makes it a pertinent case‐study. Therefore, the study aimed to assess how EMA regulatory actions and communications have impacted HCPs' awareness and knowledge of TTS, and how that information was implemented in practise.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

To study the effect of EMA risk communication about TTS during a pandemic on HCPs, a cross‐sectional design with online surveys was performed. The study was performed in the following five EU member states: Greece (GR), Latvia (LV), Netherlands (NL), Portugal (PT) and Slovenia (SI), which have different healthcare systems and COVID‐19 national strategies.

An online survey consisting of approximately 30 closed and open questions was developed and covered the following topics:

HCPs' awareness and knowledge about (the risk of) TTS (risk, symptoms, sources of information)

HCPs' awareness and knowledge about (the risk of) TTS (risk, symptoms, sources of information)

HCPs' awareness and knowledge about the materials for Vaxzevria and Jcovden and/or the recommendations on the prevention of TTS

HCPs' eventual implementation in their practise

HCPs' eventual implementation in their practise

To measure the awareness of TTS, questions were asked on if they were familiar with the ADR and if so, via which channels they had received that information. To study their knowledge, HCPs were asked to identify from a list its signs and symptoms, and its risk frequency and their sources of information on its treatment. To assess their changes in practise, statements were provided. HCPs were then asked to select those applicable to their practise. Questions focused on actions before and after the regulatory review.

The questionnaire was first developed in English and translated to Dutch for pilot‐testing. The pilot‐testing was conducted among four Dutch HCPs with varied professional experience and roles during the pandemic. Participants received the survey via e‐mail and were asked to complete it and note any comments. They were also asked to reflect on the questionnaire on (1) readability, response options and language use, (2) question order and pathway, (3) reading comprehension, (4) likelihood of odd or sensitive topics, (5) completeness and (6) length. Their comments and suggestions were adopted before finalising the survey. The final version was then translated into the languages of the participating countries following a translation protocol. The full survey and translation protocol are available as Supporting Information S2 and S4.

The surveys were distributed between August and September 2022. Data collection lasted 1–2 months depending on the country response. Data collection were extended in some countries to increase respondents or to recruit other respondents from specific professional groups that were underrepresented. Surveys were hosted on the platforms 1KA (University of Ljubljana) in Slovenia and Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) for the remaining countries.

The protocol of this study is publicly accessible in the RWD Catalogues (under EUPAS 44970) and bears an European Network of Centres for Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacovigilance (ENCePP) seal [13]. This seal means that the study has been performed in accordance with the standards set by ENCePP and international guidelines [14].

Inclusion Criteria

Only those professionals who declared to have been involved in vaccination (administration or management), or in the provision of information about its benefits and risks, or in the monitoring and treatment of vaccine ADRs were eligible to participate.

Recruitment

The study was designed to have a descriptive, qualitative approach. The maximum variation (purposive) sampling applied was intended to reflect a spectrum of knowledge, awareness about and attitudes towards the TTS risk. We expected that data from 500 completed surveys would be sufficient to reach saturation and to display variety among participating member states. The minimal target of HCPs surveyed per country was based on its number of inhabitants. Countries with less inhabitants have a lower number of HCPs and therefore reach saturation at an earlier stage. It was assumed that 50–150 HCPs per country would be sufficient to reach saturation. Given that the professions involved in vaccination activities varied greatly, each country developed its own inventory of professionals eligible for recruitment. Professional societies and networks were used to disseminate the survey. Each country devised a recruitment strategy fitting their own healthcare system and COVID‐19 vaccination policy. Details are listed in Supporting Information S1.

Data Analysis

Responses were considered valid when they gave informed consent, provided all baseline information (e.g., gender, age, occupation) and answered at least one TTS‐related question.

Only valid entries were included in the descriptive analyses. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages were reported. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28) and R version 4.2.0.

Coding trees were created to analyse each open question in the survey using inductive coding by two researchers per country. The Dutch data were used as a sample to create the initial codes, but other countries were able to add codes based on their own (national) data. The coding trees were reviewed and adjusted by research teams as necessary.

Ethical Considerations

Each participating country team was responsible for obtaining approval from local ethical boards. Approvals were waived for Latvia and The Netherlands. Greece, Portugal and Slovenia were granted ethical permission to conduct the study.

Results

In total, 1659 HCPs participated in the survey, of which 1555 provided informed consent. From these, 914 HCPs were considered valid and included in the final analysis. 565 HCPs filled in all the questions of the survey. All countries except Greece, reached recruitment targets. Awareness

A large majority of HCPs were aware about the reports of TTS associated with COVID‐19 adenovirus vector vaccines, with all countries reporting percentages between 85% (PT) and 97% (GR).

When asked about their sources of TTS risk information, most HCPs mentioned mainstream media (ranging from 24% (LV) to 63% (PT)), government authorities (ranging from 30% (GR/SI) to 73% (LV)) and colleagues (ranging from 18% (LV) to 45% (GR)) (see Table 3). Little information was gained from vaccine manufacturers, academic training, social media and pharmacovigilance centres. In the open questions respondents listed family and their patients as additional sources of information. They also often referred to using ‘common sense’.

HCPs were asked if they were aware of product information and official documentation on the vaccines such as the SmPC, the package leaflet, the distributed DHPCs, as well as national vaccination guidelines or protocols. Most HCPs were aware that the SmPC existed, except for the Netherlands where approximately 23% declare to be unaware (percentages in other countries varied between 0% (LV) and 10% (PT)). About one third of HCPs were aware about the SmPC but had not studied it (ranging from 3.3% (LV) to 31.4% (SI)). Comparable results were found for the package leaflet with most HCPs being aware and roughly 20% reporting not having studied it. The Netherlands were an exception with 31% of the HCPs reporting not being aware of its existence. The questions on the distributed DHPCs yielded varying results. Roughly half of the respondents were aware and studied the DHPCs. However, about a third of the HCPs in Greece (27%) and the Netherlands (30%) were unaware of their existence. HCPs were very aware of national guidelines from health authorities on the vaccines, with 80% (GR) to 95% (LV) reporting to have studied them.

When asked about TTS treatment guidelines, half to two thirds of the HCPs were unsure whether they were available or unaware about any clinical guidelines or recommendations from learned societies. This was particularly striking for the Netherlands (88%), Portugal (93%) and Slovenia (87%). Results on the awareness of TTS reports and their sources are available per country in Table 3.

Knowledge

The most frequently identified signs and symptoms of TTS were an unusual headache accompanied with other signs (weakness, blurred vision, sickness, etc), a rash that looked like bruises, and shortness of breath with chest pain, leg swelling and abdominal pain. The least commonly identified symptoms were headache that felt worse when lying down/bending over and nausea. Only an extremely small numbers of HCPs were able to identify all TTS symptoms correctly (ranging from 0% (LV) to 5.2% (SI), data not shown). In particular, less severe symptoms were often not recognised.

Approximately half of the HCPs were able to identify the correct risk frequency of developing TTS (i.e., very rare side effect), with the highest percentage of correct answers in The Netherlands (63%) and the lowest percentage in Portugal (38%). About two thirds of the HCPs felt confident in knowing what to do when faced with a (suspected) case of TTS (ranging from 47% (PT) to 67% (LV)). About a quarter of the HCPs was unsure. When asked where they had obtained that information, they mainly mentioned government authorities, colleagues or employers and guidelines. Results are shown per country in Table 4.

Implementation in Practice

Overall, only one third to half of HCPs admitted having changed their professional practise due to the risk minimisation measures, with the highest percentages reported in Greece (50%) and the lowest in Slovenia (34%).

Prior to the regulatory review, half of the HCPs stated that they usually or always informed their patients about the importance of monitoring health symptoms after vaccination. This number grew to two thirds after the recommendations and changes to the guidelines. The practise of informing patients about TTS symptoms was reported to have grown from one third to two thirds after the changes.

There was also an increase in the number of professionals who advised patients to seek further assistance when experiencing signs of TTS. Around half to two thirds of HCPs did so prior to the regulatory review. That number grew thereafter to a range of 53% (NL) to 86% (GR).

Before the changes, about one third to half of HCPs monitored for signs of TTS after vaccination with Vaxzevria or Jcovden. Afterwards, 60%–80% of the HCPs reported to actively look for signs.

In the open answers HCPs stated that, due to the TTS risk safety warnings, they had become more alert about TTS signs and symptoms and lowered their thresholds for testing on TTS. Additional Remarks

HCPs were also invited to share any remarks about the COVID‐19 vaccines and their risk communication. Many found that the information dissemination was dispersed and felt overwhelmed both by the amount of information available and its frequent changes. They would have preferred to receive information from one or a few reliable sources. HCPs were pressed for time and thus unable to review all information properly. Some would rather receive new risk information beforehand, to prepare for eventual questions and adapt practise accordingly. Some HCPs also stated that the information was not user‐friendly, too extensive and complex to grasp for professionals and patients.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of the regulatory actions for Vaxzevria and Jcovden following the 2021 safety review on awareness and knowledge about TTS risk and subsequent changes in practise among HCPs. An overwhelming majority of HCPs were aware about the reports of TTS associated with COVID‐19 adenovirus vector vaccines. Most had received this information via mainstream media, health authorities or colleagues. Many were able to recognise major signs and symptoms of TTS, but minor signs (such as nausea) were not as well‐known. Vaccination guidelines were the most prominently used and best‐known documents to obtain further TTS risk information. Only a third up to half of the HCPs implemented changes to their practises after the regulatory actions. HCPs reported to have become more alert about monitoring health symptoms after vaccination, after the TTS risk minimisation recommendations. This also involved proactively informing patients about TTS symptoms and about the need to seek further assistance should warning signs occur.

The role played by the information provided by the EMA and/or national drug regulatory agencies seems relatively modest. There is moderate to high awareness about the regulatory measures established by the EMA in 2021. The SmPC and the package leaflet were not amply used. However, about a third of the HCPs surveyed stated not to have studied most documents. The DHPC yielded varying results with one third of HCPs in some countries reporting not being aware of its existence. This is striking as a small survey, carried out by the EMA, indicated that HCPs did prefer receiving information on COVID‐19 vaccines directly from (inter)national health authorities [15]. Documents such as the SmPC and PL serve many other purposes and might therefore not be considered as suitable resources for HCPs who quickly had to adapt to the latest information, make changes to their practises, and answer questions from patients and colleagues, and so forth. Previous research on the effectiveness of the SmPC and the DHPC showcased that information was often hard to implement in practise and did not meet the needs of HCPs [16, 17]. This is reverberated in our findings which indicate that HCPs gravitate towards guidelines and made but minor changes to their practise based on regulatory documents. This underscores the need for practical, applicable and tailored information, particularly in the context of a future pandemic or major health threat, where more knowledge needs to be gained on how to quickly disseminate vital information among HCPs.

Limitations

The overall limitation of our study is recall bias, given that data collection tools were implemented once the COVID‐19 vaccination campaign was near completion. To help respondents remember the accurate timing of events or changes to decisions about vaccination policies, figures describing national timelines were included in the survey (Supporting Information S3).

As for most survey studies, selection or non‐response bias must be considered. This bias is also likely to have occurred during recruitment, that is, the most interested HCPs were more prone to respond to the survey, and more likely to be aware about events, which would then have boosted their awareness about TTS risks and the recognition of the risk communication measures implemented in their country.

As the results presented in this manuscript were part of a larger qualitative study [13, 18], the goal was to obtain a range of responses that reflected the diversity in attitudes and behaviours that are to be found in each country. As sampling methodologies differed, overall representativeness cannot be guaranteed. While we strived to obtain data saturation, there is no way to prove it quantitatively nor confirm it formally [19].

The questions on usage of product information were specific to adenoviral vector vaccines. HCPs were only invited to complete these sections if they had reported to be involved in the administration, counselling or monitoring of these specific vaccines. As Vaxzevria and Jcovden were less used than mRNA vaccines, professionals might have felt less need to keep updated.

Since a baseline measurement was lacking, the extent of change in attitudes cannot be ascertained, not since the implementation of the risk communication measures for adenovirus vector vaccines, nor since the changes to vaccination policies. Our results may be an overestimation of the awareness and knowledge about TTS risk among HCPs. We are limited in our ability to evaluate whether there have been, de facto, alterations to the behaviour of HCPs and we would therefore would recommend the use of baseline measurements in future studies investigating the impact of regulatory measures.

Conclusion

Our study showed that there was large variability through which channels regulatory measures from the EMA reached HCPs. In general, there was a high awareness about TTS risk associated with adenoviral vector vaccines across the five countries surveyed, but this information was distributed through channels other than the regulatory network. HCPs were able to recognise most signs and symptoms of TTS and altered their practise based on the information received from national guidelines.

Overall, information about the TTS risk associated with adenoviral vector vaccines was reported to have reached most HCPs. In contrast, information sources used by HCPs varied and were often other than the official EMA communications.
