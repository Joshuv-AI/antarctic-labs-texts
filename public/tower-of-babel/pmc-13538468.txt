Associations of fluid intake patterns, hydration status, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD): a cross-sectional study in older adults
Authors: Guotian Lin, Yingzi Liang, Qi Wang, Miaomiao Cui, Qingqing Wang, Lingyun Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined associations between fluid intake characteristics, consumption patterns, hydration status, and MASLD among community-dwelling older adults, exploring whether hydration status shows statistical associations consistent with an indirect pathway in cross-sectional analyses. This cross-sectional study enrolled 876 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years recruited from community health centers. Fluid intake was assessed using 3-day beverage records, including two weekdays and one weekend day. First-morning urine osmolality was used as the overnight hydration indicator. MASLD was diagnosed based on ultrasound-detected hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction criteria. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to identify beverage patterns. Bootstrap-based exploratory analyses with 5000 resamples were performed as secondary descriptive decompositions to examine whether first-morning urine osmolality showed statistical associations consistent with an indirect association in cross-sectional data. These analyses are descriptive and hypothesis-generating only, and do not imply causality or temporal ordering. Mean total fluid intake was 1385 ± 355 mL/day, with plain water comprising 85.2% (1180 ± 285 mL/day) of consumption. Overall MASLD prevalence was 36.1% (316/876). In the fully adjusted model (adjusted for age, sex, education, physical activity, total energy intake, smoking, DII, red meat, vegetables, whole grains, fruit, season, and diuretic use), lower fluid intake (< 1500 mL/day) was associated with higher odds of MASLD (OR 2.31, 95% CI 1.74–3.08, P < 0.001), and dehydration (urine osmolality > 800 mOsm/kg) was also associated with higher odds (OR 2.55, 95% CI 1.89–3.43, P < 0.001). PCA identified three beverage patterns that cumulatively explained 49.9% of the total variance. In fully adjusted models, the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern was positively associated with MASLD (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.52–2.63, P < 0.001); after additional adjustment for total added sugar intake, this OR attenuated to 1.76 (95% CI 1.31–2.36). The Basic Hydration pattern was inversely associated with MASLD (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89, P = 0.005). The Basic Hydration pattern was inversely associated with MASLD (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89, P = 0.005). The fully adjusted model showed good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.420) and acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.782). The Sweet Beverage Preference pattern should be interpreted primarily as a marker of broader unhealthy dietary behavior and cardiometabolic risk rather than as evidence that minimal SSB intake directly increases MASLD risk. In older adults with plain water-dominant reported beverage intake, lower reported beverage intake, higher first-morning urine osmolality, and a Sweet Beverage Preference pattern were associated with MASLD. The Sweet Beverage Preference pattern should be interpreted primarily as a marker of broader dietary and metabolic risk rather than as a beverage-specific risk signal. Introduction

MASLD has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver condition globally. Epidemiological surveys indicate that MASLD affects approximately 38% of adults worldwide, with prevalence increasing markedly with advancing age1,2. Beyond hepatic steatosis, MASLD is intimately linked to type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease3,4. Current prevention strategies focus predominantly on weight management, dietary modification, and physical activity, with dietary interventions typically targeting caloric restriction and macronutrient composition of solid foods5. Fluid intake, however, remains a fundamental yet understudied component of dietary structure6—a research gap particularly pronounced in older populations.

Mounting evidence supports the independent metabolic benefits of plain water consumption7–9. Adequate water intake maintains glucose homeostasis and improves insulin sensitivity9. Conversely, sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption is positively associated with MASLD through mechanisms involving fructose-induced hepatic de novo lipogenesis and elevated uric acid. However, most available evidence comes from Western dietary settings. These settings are often characterized by diverse beverage choices and relatively balanced consumption across beverage categories. Chinese older adults have a different fluid intake structure. Plain water usually accounts for the largest proportion of total fluid intake, and traditional low-sugar beverages, such as tea and clear soup, are also commonly consumed. Although SSB intake remains lower than in many Western populations, it is increasing in China7,8. The metabolic implications of this “plain water-dominant, traditional beverage-supplemented” pattern remain unclear. Specifically, does minimal SSB consumption retain pathological significance when plain water intake is adequate? What role do traditional beverages play? These questions cannot be answered through Western-derived analytical frameworks.

The physiological particularities of aging further necessitate investigation of these relationships. Aging is accompanied by diminished thirst sensitivity and reduced renal concentrating capacity. These changes may increase susceptibility to chronic subclinical dehydration11. Different beverages may also differ in hydration effects. Hypertonic beverages, including SSBs, may worsen hydration status through osmotic diuresis10. Dehydration may activate the renin-angiotensin system and increase antidiuretic hormone activity, which could contribute to insulin resistance and hepatic fat accumulation12–14. This suggests a plausible indirect association linking beverage patterns to MASLD via hydration status, though empirical evidence remains scarce in older populations.

Methodological limitations of existing research also constrain our understanding. Prior studies typically apply balanced analytical frameworks weighting all beverage types equally—plain water, SSBs, tea, and dairy—failing to accommodate the Chinese older adult pattern in which plain water may exceed 85% of total fluid intake. Conventional regression models may have limited sensitivity to detect the health relevance of low-volume beverages and beverage combinations. Therefore, PCA was employed, using absolute plain water intake together with proportional non-plain water beverage variables, to address this dominant-component analytical challenge.

The hypotheses were that: (1) insufficient total fluid intake and poorer hydration status are associated with MASLD in populations consuming plain water as the primary beverage; (2) beverage patterns incorporating plain water volume and non-plain water beverage composition are associated with MASLD; and (3) hydration status may show statistical associations consistent with an indirect pathway in cross-sectional analyses, though causal mediation cannot be tested.

Research objects and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study enrolled community-dwelling residents aged ≥ 65 years recruited from multiple community health centers.Preparatory work, including investigator training, questionnaire refinement, and fieldwork coordination, was completed between January and May 2025. Formal recruitment and data collection began after ethical approval was obtained on 4 June 2025 and continued from June 2025 to April 2026.Inclusion criteria required local residency ≥ 1 year. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) other known liver diseases (viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, drug-induced liver injury); (2) severe cardiopulmonary or renal insufficiency, or advanced malignancy; (3) cognitive impairment precluding informed consent (Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] < 24); (4) habitual alcohol consumption (> 20 g ethanol/day)15.

Fluid intake and pattern assessment

Fluid intake was assessed using a standardized 3-day fluid record, including two weekdays and one weekend day. In this study, fluid intake refers to all recorded beverage volumes (including plain water, tea, coffee, coconut water, soups, congee water, and all other liquid beverages), but does not include water derived from solid foods. Before recording, trained investigators provided face-to-face instructions to participants and, when necessary, to family members or caregivers. Participants recorded all beverages consumed during each 24-h period, including beverage type, time, estimated volume, preparation method, and whether sugar or sweetener was added.

Fluid categories included plain water, tea, coffee, coconut water, SSBs, sweetened dairy beverages, artificially sweetened beverages, clear soup, nourishing soup, and congee water. Plain water included boiled water, bottled water, and filtered water without added sugar or other ingredients. SSBs included carbonated drinks, sweetened tea drinks, fruit-flavored drinks, sweetened herbal drinks, and commercially sweetened beverages. Sweetened dairy beverages included yogurt drinks and milk-based drinks with added sugar. Clear soup referred to lightly seasoned soup without visible meat or medicinal ingredients, whereas nourishing soup referred to soup containing meat, bones, seafood, medicinal herbs, or other nutrient-dense ingredients. Congee water referred to the liquid layer from rice porridge.

Total fluid intake was calculated as the sum of all recorded beverage volumes over the 3 recording days and expressed as mL/day. Water derived from solid foods was not included in total fluid intake. Alcoholic beverages were not included because participants with habitual alcohol consumption above the predefined threshold were excluded. Lower fluid intake was defined as total beverage-recorded fluid intake < 1500 mL/day as a descriptive cut-off for comparison, noting that this value does not represent total water intake from all dietary sources.

Given the dominance of plain water (> 85% of total reported beverage intake in this cohort), conventional regression models that weight all beverage types equally may fail to capture the structural role of plain water and the relative composition of other beverages. To address this analytical challenge, we employed a two-stage variable entry strategy for PCA. First, plain water intake was retained as an absolute volume variable (mL/day) to preserve its dominant volume effect. Second, all other beverages (tea, coconut water, coffee, SSBs, sweetened dairy, artificially sweetened beverages, clear soup, nourishing soup, and congee water) were expressed as percentage contributions to total reported beverage intake (%), rather than absolute volumes. PCA was executed using a standardized correlation matrix (z-scores) to reduce scale-induced variance weighting. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.68, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 821.5, P < 0.001), indicating marginal but acceptable sampling adequacy for exploratory factor analysis. Principal components were extracted based on eigenvalues > 1.0 and scree plot inflection, followed by Varimax orthogonal rotation. Because proportional variables are subject to compositional constraints (they sum to 100%), the identified factor structure should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Sensitivity analyses using absolute volumes only (without proportional variables) yielded similar patterns, supporting robustness.

Hydration status assessment

Hydration status was assessed using first-morning urine osmolality. First-morning urine osmolality was used as the hydration biomarker. While a single morning sample does not capture 24-h hydration dynamics, it has been widely used in population-based studies as a practical and objective indicator of overnight hydration status2,12. However, this measurement may be influenced by renal function, evening fluid intake, and medication use, particularly in older adults with diabetes or diuretic use.

Participants were instructed to collect a midstream urine sample from the first void after waking on the morning of the field examination. Participants were instructed to avoid excessive fluid intake or vigorous exercise before bedtime. Those with acute illness, fever, vomiting, or diarrhea in the preceding 48 h were rescheduled. Urine collection timing was standardized to first morning void between 07:00 and 08:00. Overnight fasting (≥ 8 h) was confirmed by interview.Urine samples were collected in sterile containers and transported to the laboratory under cold-chain conditions.Urine osmolality was measured using a freezing-point depression osmometer, CM-10, manufactured by Beijing Beiyan Keyi Instrument Co., Ltd. The measurement principle was based on freezing-point depression: the decrease in the freezing point of the urine sample was detected and converted into osmolality, expressed as mOsm/kg. The instrument was calibrated before measurement according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality-control procedures were performed during testing to ensure measurement stability and reliability.Hydration status was classified according to urine osmolality as follows: adequate hydration, ≤ 500 mOsm/kg; intermediate hydration, > 500 to 800 mOsm/kg; and dehydration, > 800 mOsm/kg17. In regression analyses, urine osmolality was also analyzed as a continuous variable per 100 mOsm/kg increase to preserve statistical information and assess dose-response associations.

MASLD diagnosis

MASLD was diagnosed according to international consensus criteria18. Diagnosis required ultrasound-detected hepatic steatosis, exclusion of alternative causes of steatosis, and the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk criterion. These criteria included elevated waist circumference using Asian cutoffs (men ≥ 90 cm, women ≥ 80 cm), fasting glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L or diabetes treatment, blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or antihypertensive therapy, triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L, or reduced HDL-C levels (men < 1.0 mmol/L, women < 1.3 mmol/L). Two ultrasonographers blinded to participant characteristics independently reviewed ultrasound images, and discordant readings were adjudicated by a senior physician.Ultrasound examinations were performed by trained radiologists using a standardized protocol with a 3.5-MHz convex probe (Mindray DC-8). Examiners were blinded to participants’ fluid intake data, laboratory results, and clinical history. Hepatic steatosis grading followed standard criteria: mild—slight increase in echogenicity with normal visualization of the diaphragm and intrahepatic vessels; moderate—moderate increase in echogenicity with slightly impaired visualization; severe—marked increase in echogenicity with poor or absent visualization of the diaphragm and vessels. All images were independently reviewed by two ultrasonographers who were also blinded to participant characteristics. Inter-reader agreement was high (κ = 0.89; 95% CI 0.84–0.94), and discordant cases (42 cases, 4.8%) were adjudicated by a senior hepatologist who was also blinded to clinical data.

Dietary quality and covariates

Standardized questionnaires captured demographic data (age, sex, education, residence), lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking), and clinical history. Measurements included height, weight, waist circumference, and calculated BMI. Fasting venous blood samples provided glucose, insulin, lipids, and renal function markers19. We assessed habitual dietary intake over the preceding 3 months using a simplified food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). We standardized each dietary component against global means and standard deviations, converting these to inflammatory scores (pro-inflammatory components positive, anti-inflammatory negative), then weighted and summed these to generate total DII scores. Higher DII scores indicate greater inflammatory potential20. We recorded survey month. Based on the actual data collection period (June 2025 to April 2026), the warm season was defined as June to September (mean temperature 28.5 °C, mean relative humidity 82%), and the cool season as October to April (mean temperature 22.3 °C, mean relative humidity 75%). Fresh coconut water is available year-round in Hainan, but consumption may increase during warm months. Season was included as a covariate in all multivariable models to account for potential differences in fluid intake, physical activity, and beverage preferences related to temperature and humidity.Current diuretic use, including loop and thiazide diuretics, was recorded because these medications may influence urine concentration.Current diuretic use, including loop and thiazide diuretics, was recorded and included as a fixed covariate in all multivariable regression models to control for the direct pharmacological effect of these medications on urine concentration. In addition, based on the simplified food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) data, we calculated total added sugar intake (g/day) and included it as an additional adjustment variable in sensitivity analyses to partially separate the effect of sugar intake on MASLD from the effect of fluid volume.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and R 4.2.1. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR]; categorical variables as n (%). Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-normal distributions) for continuous variables, and χ2 tests for categorical variables.PCA extracted beverage patterns as described. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between fluid exposure variables and MASLD odds. Bootstrap-based exploratory analyses with 5000 resamples were used to examine whether hydration status showed statistical associations consistent with an indirect pathway in cross-sectional data. Because the study was cross-sectional, these analyses cannot establish causality, temporal ordering, or mechanistic mediation. They should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating only.Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding diuretic users and stratifying participants by season. Because product-specific sugar concentrations and free-fructose contents were not available in the beverage records, we could not directly adjust for beverage-derived free fructose or sugar calories. Therefore, the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern was interpreted as a mixed behavioral pattern reflecting both lower plain water intake and greater consumption of sugar-containing beverages. Where possible, absolute SSB intake and sweetened dairy beverage intake were considered as proxy indicators of beverage-derived sugar exposure in sensitivity analyses.Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with P > 0.05 indicating adequate model fit. Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Sensitivity, specificity, and overall classification accuracy were calculated at the optimal cut-off point determined by the Youden index.Two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 876 participants were enrolled (mean age 68.2 ± 6.2 years; 45.1% male [n = 395]). MASLD prevalence was 36.1% (316 cases). 58.2% (510/876) surveyed during warm months 41.8% (366/876) during cool months (P = 0.342). MASLD participants were older (70.6 ± 6.1 vs. 66.8 ± 6.0 years, P < 0.001) and exhibited higher BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and triglycerides, with lower HDL cholesterol (all P < 0.001). Diabetes (35.8% vs. 15.2%) and hypertension (63.3% vs. 50.9%) were more prevalent in the MASLD group. Diuretic use was marginally higher among MASLD participants (12.3% vs. 8.8%, P = 0.082). MASLD participants had higher DII scores (1.85 ± 1.02 vs. 0.92 ± 0.89, P < 0.001), higher total added sugar intake (36.1 ± 20.8 vs. 24.2 ± 15.6 g/day, P < 0.001) greater red meat consumption (65.5 ± 25.3 vs. 52.3 ± 22.1 g/day, P < 0.001), and lower vegetable intake (185.6 ± 65.2 vs. 215.3 ± 58.4 g/day, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Fluid intake characteristics

The cohort demonstrated typical plain water-dominant intake: mean total fluid consumption was 1383.8 mL/day, with plain water contributing 1182 ± 285 mL (85.2%). Other beverage categories showed markedly skewed distributions. Seasonal analysis revealed higher total fluid intake during warm months (1450 ± 365 vs. 1295 ± 320 mL/day, t = 5.12, P < 0.001), though plain water proportion (84.8% vs. 85.6%, P = 0.698) and MASLD prevalence (35.3% vs. 37.2%, P = 0.562) did not differ seasonally.

MASLD participants consumed less plain water (1060 ± 275 vs. 1250 ± 270 mL, P < 0.001). Among other beverages, MASLD participants reported higher intake of SSBs (median: 20 vs. 0 mL, P < 0.001), sweetened dairy (10 vs. 0 mL, P < 0.001), and nourishing soups (40 vs. 25 mL, P = 0.002), but lower tea (35 vs. 50 mL, P = 0.001), coconut water (15 vs. 25 mL, P = 0.004), and congee water (40 vs. 55 mL, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Beverage pattern extraction

PCA yielded three components with eigenvalues of 2.40, 1.82, and 1.28. These components explained 21.8%, 16.5%, and 11.6% of the total variance, respectively, with a cumulative explained variance of 49.9% (Table 3; Fig. 1). Pattern 1 was labeled Basic Hydration. It had high loadings for plain water intake (0.88), tea (0.62), and congee water (0.40). Pattern 2 was labeled Sweet Beverage Preference and had high loadings for SSBs (0.76) and sweetened dairy beverages (0.70). Pattern 3 was labeled Traditional Supplement and had high loadings for coconut water (0.55) and nourishing soup (0.65). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure was 0.68, indicating marginal sampling adequacy. Therefore, the pattern structure should be interpreted as exploratory (Table 4).

Associations between fluid intake and MASLD odds

Multivariable logistic regression results appear in Table 5. In Model 2, insufficient total fluid intake (< 1500 mL/day) was associated with higher odds of MASLD (OR 2.31, 95% CI 1.74–3.08, P < 0.001). Each 100 mL/day increase in plain water consumption was associated with lower odds of MASLD (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.96, P < 0.001). Each 100 mOsm/kg increase in urine osmolality was associated with higher odds of MASLD (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.31–2.38, P < 0.001; results not tabulated). Compared with adequate hydration, dehydration was associated with higher odds of MASLD (OR 2.55, 95% CI 1.89–3.43, P < 0.001).

Regarding beverage patterns, each 1-SD increase in the Basic Hydration pattern score was associated with lower odds of MASLD (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.89, P = 0.005). Conversely, each 1-SD increase in the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern score was associated with higher odds of MASLD (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.52–2.63, P < 0.001). The Traditional Supplement pattern showed no significant association (OR 1.10, P = 0.435). In sensitivity analyses, we additionally adjusted for total added sugar intake (from FFQ) to assess whether dietary sugar confounded the association between the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern and MASLD. After this additional adjustment, the OR for the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern attenuated from 2.00 (95% CI 1.52–2.63) to 1.76 (95% CI 1.31–2.36), suggesting that sugar partially, but not entirely, explained the association. Results for the Basic Hydration pattern (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.90) and traditional supplement pattern (OR 1.09, 95% CI 0.83–1.44) remained materially unchanged indicated good calibration for both models: Model 1 (adjusted for age and sex): χ2 = 6.82,P = 0.556; Model 2 (fully adjusted, including diuretic use): χ2 = 8.14, P = 0.420. The AUC for Model 2 was 0.782 (95% CI 0.748–0.816), indicating acceptable discrimination. At the optimal cut-off, Model 2 achieved an overall classification accuracy of 74.6%, with sensitivity of 68.2% and specificity of 78.5%.

Exploratory assessment of indirect associations of hydration status

In exploratory analyses, we examined whether first-morning urine osmolality showed statistical associations consistent with an indirect role in the associations between beverage patterns and MASLD using bootstrap resampling with 5000 iterations (detailed results presented in Table S1 in the supplementary material). For the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern, the total association with MASLD from the fully adjusted logistic regression model was positive (β = 0.703, 95% CI 0.475–0.931). In this descriptive decomposition, first-morning urine osmolality accounted for approximately 12.2% of the statistical association. These analyses are secondary and hypothesis-generating only; they do not establish causality, temporal ordering, or mechanistic mediation.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that, in a population where plain water constituted > 85% of fluid intake, inadequate total fluid volume and poorer hydration status were associated with MASLD. However, because of the cross-sectional design, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that hydration status independently predicts future MASLD. This aligns with previous observations linking hydration deterioration with metabolic syndrome components12.

The Sweet Beverage Preference pattern was associated with higher odds of MASLD. However, the absolute SSB intake in the MASLD group was low, with a median intake of only 20 mL/day. This amount is unlikely to represent a direct hepatotoxic exposure by itself. Therefore, the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern should be interpreted primarily as a marker of broader unhealthy dietary behavior, cardiometabolic risk, socioeconomic context, or health literacy, rather than as evidence that minimal SSB intake directly increases MASLD risk. This interpretation is supported by the observed correlations between the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern and higher DII scores, greater red meat intake, and lower vegetable intake.

To further quantify the confounding effect of sugar intake, we additionally adjusted for total added sugar intake (from solid foods) in a sensitivity analysis. The OR for the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern attenuated from 2.02 (95% CI 1.53–2.66) to 1.76 (95% CI 1.31–2.36). This attenuation suggests that dietary sugar partially, but not entirely, explains the association between this pattern and MASLD. The remaining effect may still be related to inadequate plain water intake, overall poor dietary quality, and other behavioral factors. Therefore, this pattern should be viewed as a composite behavioral marker rather than a pure sugar-exposure metric.

The exploratory cross-sectional decomposition (Table S1) suggested that first-morning urine osmolality may account for a proportion of the statistical association between the Sweet Beverage Preference pattern and MASLD. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal mediation pathway. Beverage intake, urine osmolality, and MASLD status were measured at the same time point; therefore, temporal ordering cannot be established. Reverse causality is a plausible alternative explanation: participants with MASLD, diabetes, or insulin resistance may have altered thirst perception, dietary preferences, or renal concentrating ability, which could influence both beverage selection and urine osmolality. These analyses are therefore secondary and hypothesis-generating, requiring validation in longitudinal or interventional studies.

The null association between the Traditional Supplement pattern and MASLD may reflect low absolute intake volumes and heterogeneity in soup composition. Traditional beverages may be reasonable alternatives to sweetened beverages when plain water intake is adequate, but the current data do not demonstrate a clear inverse association or harmful association.The interpretation of traditional beverages should be placed in the cultural context of Southern Chinese older adults. In this population, tea, congee water, clear soup, and coconut water are commonly consumed as part of daily meals or routine hydration rather than as discretionary sugary drinks. Unsweetened tea may provide fluid together with polyphenols and usually contributes little energy. Congee water is typically a diluted rice-based liquid consumed with meals, whereas clear soup may contribute fluid but can vary substantially in sodium content. Coconut water contains naturally occurring sugars and electrolytes, and nourishing soups may contain meat, fat, or medicinal herbs. Therefore, traditional beverages differ metabolically from Western beverage patterns dominated by carbonated SSBs, fruit drinks, and high-energy dairy beverages. This heterogeneity may partly explain why the Traditional Supplement pattern was not significantly associated with MASLD in the present study.

In this cross-sectional study of older adults with plain water-dominant reported beverage intake, lower reported beverage intake, higher first-morning urine osmolality, and a higher Sweet Beverage Preference pattern score were associated with higher odds of MASLD. The Sweet Beverage Preference pattern should be understood primarily as a composite marker of unhealthy dietary behavior and cardiometabolic risk rather than as evidence that minimal SSB intake directly increases MASLD risk. These findings should not be interpreted as causal. While the results are consistent with current general dietary recommendations to maintain adequate beverage intake and limit sugar-sweetened beverages, this cross-sectional study does not directly test intervention efficacy. Any practical recommendations derived from these findings should be confirmed in prospective cohort studies or randomized trials. Individualized advice is needed for those with renal insufficiency, heart failure, or medical indications for fluid restriction.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the focus on plain water-dominant dietary structures and the attempt to separate beverage volume from beverage composition. Adjustment for DII, seasonal variation, diuretic use, and added sugar intake improves confounding control, although causal inference remains limited by the cross-sectional design.

Key limitations merit consideration: (1) The cross-sectional design precludes temporal sequencing, and reverse causality cannot be excluded. (2) Single-point first-morning urine osmolality reflects overnight hydration status and may not represent daily hydration dynamics; however, it remains a widely used objective biomarker in cross-sectional population studies. (3) Self-reported 3-day beverage records may underestimate intake, especially during outdoor dining. (4) Residual confounding remains possible despite adjustment for DII, food groups, added sugar, and diuretic use; unmeasured socioeconomic variables, health literacy, medication use, and comorbidity severity may influence both beverage choices and MASLD. (5) PCA based on beverage percentages is subject to compositional-data constraints (variables sum to 100%), and the KMO of 0.68 indicates marginal sampling adequacy; therefore, the pattern structure should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies should consider isometric log-ratio transformation for compositional data. (6) Ultrasound has limited sensitivity for detecting mild hepatic steatosis, particularly when liver fat content is below approximately 20–30%; therefore, some participants with mild steatosis may have been misclassified. Such misclassification may have affected the observed associations. (7) PCA-derived patterns reflect Southern Chinese older adults and may not generalize to other regions or age groups.
