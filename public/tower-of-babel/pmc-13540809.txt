Neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and prevalent cardiometabolic multimorbidity in older adults: an exploratory cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Yan Chen, Haonan Ju, Qingzhi Zhou, Xiangpeng Kong, Xin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR), a composite surrogate marker derived from neutrophil count and HDL-C, has been reported to be associated with various metabolic and cardiovascular diseases. This study aimed to investigate the association between NHR and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) in older adults and to explore whether insulin resistance and uric acid metabolism may statistically account for part of this association.MethodsThis study was based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and included a total of 5,987 older adults. CMM was defined as the coexistence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases within the same individual, including diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, and stroke. Weighted logistic regression, restricted cubic splines (RCS), subgroup analyses, and exploratory mediation analyses were performed to assess the association between NHR and CMM. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the discriminatory ability of NHR for CMM.ResultsMultivariable weighted logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, compared with older adults in the lowest quartile (Q1) of NHR, those in the highest quartile (Q4) had 2.33-fold higher odds of CMM (95% CI: 1.56-3.48, P for trend < 0.001). Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that TyG, as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, and uric acid statistically accounted for 23.78% (95% CI 16.08%-54.60%) and 13.69% (95% CI 9.31%-56.83%) of the NHR–CMM association, respectively. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal mediation. Moreover, RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between NHR and CMM risk (P for non-linearity < 0.0001). ROC analysis showed that NHR had modest but statistically higher discriminatory ability for CMM than the comparison indices.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that NHR is closely and positively associated with the CMM prevalence in the elderly population. The mediation patterns were consistent with the potential involvement of insulin resistance and uric acid–related metabolic-inflammatory pathways. Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) has recently become a major public health concern worldwide. It is defined as the coexistence of two or more cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs), including diabetes mellitus (DM), coronary heart disease (CHD), and stroke [1, 2]. Compared with a single CMD, CMM often confers synergistic health risks. Previous studies have shown that individuals with two coexisting CMDs have a 3.5- to 3.8-fold higher risk of all-cause mortality, while those with all three conditions face approximately a sevenfold higher risk of death, and life expectancy at age 60 is reduced by up to 12–15 years [3]. The burden of metabolic diseases is particularly pronounced in older adults; for instance, the prevalence of diabetes among US adults aged ≥ 65 years is as high as 22–33%, and the incidence of CMDs such as CHD and stroke increases significantly with age [4, 5]. Previous studies based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) have further shown that the age-standardized prevalence of CMM among US adults increased from 9.4% in 1999–2000 to 14.4% in 2017–2018, with an even greater burden observed in the elderly population [6]. With the acceleration of global aging, older adults are increasingly affected by CMM, highlighting the need to identify shared biological processes that link inflammation, lipid metabolism, and cardiometabolic disease clustering [7, 8].

In older adults, cellular senescence and immune aging may contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation, while dyslipidemia is also common [9–11]. The neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) is a novel composite laboratory index that combines neutrophil count with HDL-C concentration [12]. A key feature of NHR is that it incorporates inflammation-related information from neutrophil count and lipid-related information from HDL-C within a single index. Notably, NHR is calculated by dividing neutrophil count by HDL-C concentration and may increase because of a higher neutrophil count, a lower HDL-C concentration, or both. This mathematical combination does not establish mechanistic or clinical superiority over either component considered separately. It should therefore be interpreted as a routinely derived, unit-dependent composite laboratory index rather than as a direct measure of inflammatory activity, HDL function, or biological interaction between its components.

Neutrophils are innate immune effector cells involved in inflammatory and oxidative responses [13], whereas HDL-C is a concentration measure related to lipid transport and is not equivalent to HDL functional capacity [14]. Previous studies have associated NHR with prognosis after acute myocardial infarction [15], cardiovascular outcomes in pre-diabetes [16], mortality in the general population [17], diabetes prevalence and mortality [18], and acute coronary syndrome in patients with type 2 diabetes [19]. These studies primarily addressed individual diseases or mortality and did not determine whether NHR is associated with the coexistence of multiple cardiometabolic conditions.

Prospective evidence has linked insulin resistance-related indices to CMM incidence and progression [1], and triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) is commonly used as an accessible surrogate index of insulin resistance in population studies [20–22]. Uric acid (UA) has also been associated with metabolic syndrome, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and cardiometabolic disorders [23–25]. These prospective and mechanistic observations provide background for examining the potential roles of TyG and UA in the association between NHR and CMM, but they cannot validate temporal mediation in the present cross-sectional dataset. TyG and UA were therefore evaluated only as exploratory statistical correlates that might contribute to the observed cross-sectional association, not as confirmed biological intermediates.

Using NHANES 1999–2018 data, we aimed primarily to examine the cross-sectional association between NHR and prevalent CMM in adults aged ≥ 60 years. Secondary analyses characterized nonlinearity, subgroup variation, and same-sample discriminatory performance. We also estimated exploratory cross-sectional indirect effects involving TyG and UA without assuming temporal ordering or testing a causal mediation hypothesis.

Methods

Study population and data sources

This study was based on data from the NHANES, a program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized US population. NHANES employs a stratified, multistage, clustered probability sampling design to ensure national representativeness. Data were obtained from NHANES 1999–2018. A total of 101,316 participants were initially identified. We first excluded 82,229 participants aged < 60 years, leaving 19,087 older adults aged ≥ 60 years. We further excluded 13,100 participants with missing data on key variables required for the present analysis, including marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), educational level, alcohol consumption, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), white blood cell count, neutrophil count, UA, fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, triglycerides (TG), LDL-C, coronary heart disease, stroke, diabetes mellitus, and weights. Finally, 5,987 older adults were included in the analysis, including 644 (9.92%) participants with CMM (Fig. 1).

Assessment of CMM

Based on the definition of CMM used by the Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, the diagnostic criterion for CMM was the coexistence of two or more CMDs within the same individual, including DM, myocardial infarction, and stroke [3]. Considering the characteristics of the NHANES study population, we used CHD as a broader category in place of myocardial infarction, which may help identify more individuals with potential CMM. Similar definitions have been used in previous studies [26, 27]. Therefore, CMM in the present study was defined as the coexistence of at least two of the following conditions: DM, CHD, and stroke. DM was defined as having a self-reported physician diagnosis, FBG levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels ≥ 6.5%, random blood glucose levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, 2-hour plasma glucose levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and/or current use of antidiabetic medications. CHD was defined according to standardized NHANES questionnaire responses. Participants were classified as having CHD if they answered “yes” to any of the following questions: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had coronary heart disease?” (MCQ160C), “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had angina, also called angina pectoris?” (MCQ160D), or “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had a heart attack, also called myocardial infarction?” (MCQ160E). Stroke was defined based on an affirmative response to the question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had a stroke?” (MCQ160F). The NHANES team implemented rigorous quality control procedures to enhance the reliability of questionnaire-based data, including standardized interviewer training, structured questionnaires, and the use of the Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI) system with built-in consistency checks [28]. Although standardized interviews improve data consistency, these outcomes remain partly dependent on self-report and may be subject to recall or reporting bias.

Calculation of NHR and TyG

NHR, an integrated marker reflecting both inflammatory and lipid metabolic status, has been applied in several studies [29]. NHR was calculated as neutrophil count divided by HDL-C. TyG, a reliable surrogate marker of IR, was calculated as Ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL) / 2] [20, 30].

Covariates

The covariates included in this study encompassed demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and laboratory measurements. All information was obtained from NHANES questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory test results. Demographic characteristics consisted of age, sex, race, education level, marital status, and PIR. Lifestyle factors included smoking status and alcohol consumption. Clinical variables included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and use of lipid-lowering medications. Laboratory measurements included HbA1c, FBG, UA, WBC count, neutrophil count, TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C. The definitions of the main categorical covariates were as follows. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current smoking. Never smokers were defined as individuals who had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime; former smokers were defined as those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime but were not currently smoking; and current smokers were defined as those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and were still smoking at the time of the survey. Alcohol consumption was categorized as never, mild, moderate, or heavy drinking. Never drinking was defined as consuming fewer than 12 drinks in one’s lifetime. Heavy drinking was defined as consuming ≥ 3 drinks per day for women, ≥ 4 drinks per day for men, or binge drinking on ≥ 5 days per month; moderate drinking was defined as consuming two drinks per day for women, three drinks per day for men, or binge drinking on ≥ 2 days per month. Mild drinking was defined as alcohol consumption that did not meet the criteria for heavy or moderate drinking. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported history of hypertension, current use of antihypertensive medications, or an average systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or average diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Hyperlipidemia was defined as LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL (3.37 mmol/L), total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL (5.18 mmol/L), triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL (1.70 mmol/L), HDL-C < 40 mg/dL (1.04 mmol/L) in men or < 50 mg/dL (1.30 mmol/L) in women, or current use of lipid-lowering medications. Lipid-lowering medications mainly included statins, fibrates, ezetimibe, bile acid sequestrants, and niacin.

Statistical analysis

All primary analyses in this study were conducted using the complex survey design of NHANES, incorporating the appropriate sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units to account for the survey design and to produce nationally representative estimates of the US population. Because fasting measures were used in the present analysis, NHANES fasting subsample weights were applied. WTSAF4YR was used for participants from the 1999–2002 cycles, and WTSAF2YR was used for participants from the 2003–2018 cycles. To construct the combined fasting subsample weight for the pooled 1999–2018 analysis, WTSAF4YR for the 1999–2002 cycles was multiplied by 2/10, whereas WTSAF2YR for each subsequent cycle was multiplied by 1/10. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with weighted percentages, and continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Group differences were analyzed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. To evaluate the association between NHR and the odds of CMM, we constructed three sequentially adjusted weighted logistic regression models. Specifically, Model 1 was an unadjusted model; Model 2 was adjusted for sex and age; and Model 3 was further adjusted for race, PIR, marital status, education level, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, LDL-C, hyperlipidemia, and use of lipid-lowering medications. P values for trend were calculated using the median value of each NHR quartile as a continuous variable in the corresponding weighted logistic regression model. The fully adjusted model included approximately 14 variables and 644 CMM events; therefore, the corresponding events-per-variable (EPV) ratio was above the commonly recommended threshold. To further explore potential dose-response relationships, RCS analyses were performed using four knots for NHR, with the P value for nonlinearity derived from the nonlinear spline terms. As a sensitivity analysis, unweighted logistic regression was performed to assess the consistency of the main findings. Exploratory mediation analyses were conducted to assess whether TyG and UA statistically accounted for part of the association between NHR and CMM. Both crude and age- and sex-adjusted mediation models were constructed. Nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 500 simulations was used to estimate confidence intervals for the indirect effect, direct effect, total effect, and proportion mediated. The proportion mediated was calculated as the estimated indirect effect divided by the estimated total effect and expressed as a percentage. Given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, these analyses were considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal mediation. Finally, stratified and interaction analyses were conducted to examine whether these associations varied across subgroups defined by sex, age, BMI, PIR, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and hypertension history. P values for interaction were calculated by adding multiplicative interaction terms between NHR and each subgroup variable to the corresponding adjusted models. ROC curve analyses were used as exploratory, unweighted comparisons to evaluate the discriminatory ability of NHR and other markers (NHHR, NLR, and WBC) for discriminating CMM, with the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated. Differences between AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test. Given that these markers may share biological or mathematical components, the ROC comparisons were interpreted as exploratory comparisons of discriminatory performance rather than evidence of biological independence or superiority. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 5,987 participants were included in the final analysis (median age: 68.0 years; 54.68% were female), among whom 644 (9.92%) were diagnosed with CMM. Baseline characteristics according to NHR quartiles are shown in Table 1. In descriptive comparisons across NHR quartiles, participants in the higher NHR groups were more likely to be male, older, non-Hispanic White, have lower educational attainment and PIR, be current smokers or former drinkers, and present with higher levels of BMI, HbA1c, FBG, UA, WBC, neutrophils, TG, and TyG. In addition, the prevalence of CMM was higher among participants with higher NHR compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1). Associations between NHR and prevalent CMM among older adults

To further investigate the association between NHR and CMM, multivariable weighted logistic regression analyses were performed (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, participants in the highest quartile of NHR (Q4) had a 5.29-fold higher odds of CMM compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (OR = 5.29, 95% CI: 3.67–7.64, P for trend < 0.0001). This association remained robust in the fully adjusted model. Specifically, compared with participants in the lowest quartile of NHR (Q1), those with higher NHR had increased odds of CMM by 63% (Q2: OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.11–2.39), 75% (Q3: OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.22–2.53), and 133% (Q4: OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.56–3.48), respectively. When NHR was analyzed as a continuous variable, it showed a generally consistent positive trend with the categorical analysis; however, the association did not reach statistical significance in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.99–1.23, P = 0.058).

These findings further supported the robustness of the positive association between NHR and CMM.

Nonlinear association between NHR and CMM

RCS analysis was performed to examine the nonlinear association between NHR (as a continuous variable) and CMM. The RCS results indicated a nonlinear dose-response relationship between NHR and CMM (P for non-linearity < 0.0001). Specifically, the odds of CMM increased with higher NHR levels. However, the upward trend markedly attenuated once NHR exceeded a certain threshold (Fig. 2A). In addition, we further explored the dose-response relationships between NHR and CMM across different age (Fig. 2B) and sex (Fig. 2C) subgroups, and the trends were consistent with those observed in the overall population.

Exploratory mediation analysis of TyG and UA in the association between NHR and CMM

Exploratory mediation analyses were performed to examine whether TyG and UA statistically accounted for part of the association between NHR and CMM (Fig. 3). TyG was interpreted as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, whereas UA was interpreted as a metabolic factor. The exploratory analysis suggested that TyG and UA were estimated to statistically account for 23.78% (95% CI 16.08%-54.60%) and 13.69% (95% CI 9.31%-56.83%) of the NHR–CMM association (Table S2), respectively. After adjustment for sex and age, these statistical mediation patterns remained. Given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal mediation.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore whether the association between NHR and CMM varied across different populations and to assess potential interaction effects (Fig. 4). The results showed that, in most subgroups, including sex (female, male), age (60–69 years, 70–79 years, and ≥ 80 years), PIR (≤ 1, 1–3, > 3), educational level (< high school, high school, > high school), smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (never, former, mild, moderate, heavy), and hypertension status (no, yes), no significant interactions were observed between NHR and the stratified variables. However, an exploratory subgroup analysis showed a significant interaction between NHR and BMI (P for interaction = 0.013). Specifically, the association between NHR and CMM was significant and most pronounced among participants with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²: OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.10–1.38), whereas no statistically significant association was observed among participants with BMI < 25 kg/m² (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.94–1.23) or BMI 25–30 kg/m² (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.99–1.05).

The discriminatory abilities of NHR and other individual lipid or inflammatory markers for CMM were evaluated using ROC curves (Fig. 5). The results demonstrated that NHR (AUC = 0.640, 95% CI: 0.628–0.653), as a surrogate marker of inflammatory–lipid imbalance, showed modest discriminatory ability but had a statistically higher AUC than the comparison lipid or inflammatory markers. Discussion

In this cross-sectional study based on NHANES 1999–2018 data, elevated NHR was significantly and positively associated with the prevalence of CMM in older adults. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest quartile of NHR had 2.33 times the odds of CMM compared with those in the lowest quartile. Exploratory mediation analyses suggested that TyG and UA statistically accounted for part of the observed association; however, these findings cannot establish temporal ordering, biological pathways, or causal mediation and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. ROC analysis indicated that NHR had modest but statistically higher discriminatory ability than the comparison indices. However, its absolute discriminatory ability was limited (AUC = 0.640). Therefore, NHR may provide epidemiological insight into the inflammatory–lipid profile associated with CMM, but its discriminatory value is insufficient for individual-level clinical diagnosis or decision-making.

Previous studies have associated NHR with prognosis after acute myocardial infarction, cardiovascular outcomes in pre-diabetes, mortality, diabetes, and acute coronary syndrome [15–19]. The principal contribution of the present study is the extension of this literature to CMM, defined here by the coexistence of diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke, rather than another single-disease endpoint. This distinction is clinically and epidemiologically relevant because CMM represents accumulated disease burden and is associated with substantial mortality and loss of life expectancy [3]. The clustering of these conditions may reflect shared demographic, behavioral, metabolic, and vascular determinants, making multimorbidity a conceptually distinct phenotype for investigation. Studies of hsCRP/HDL-C and NHHR have likewise supported interest in composite inflammation- or lipid-related indices in CMM [31, 32]. However, the present study did not directly compare the strength of the association between NHR and CMM with the corresponding associations of other indices or the individual components of NHR; therefore, its contribution lies in extending the epidemiologic setting rather than establishing superiority.

The difference between the quartile-based and continuous estimates is informative rather than necessarily contradictory. A per-unit odds ratio assumes a constant log-linear change in the odds of CMM across the entire NHR distribution, whereas the RCS results indicated that this assumption may not hold. The stronger contrast between the upper and lower quartiles may therefore reflect a concentration of adverse hematologic-lipid characteristics within higher NHR ranges rather than a uniform dose-response relationship. The flatter slope at higher values should not be interpreted as a confirmed biological threshold or saturation effect, because the shape of the curve may also be influenced by the exposure distribution, heterogeneity in disease duration and treatment, and smaller numbers of participants at the extremes. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether the nonlinear pattern is reproducible and whether particular NHR ranges have prognostic relevance.

The biological interpretation of NHR should distinguish plausibility from direct mechanistic measurement. Neutrophils participate in innate immune responses, oxidative activity, protease release, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation [15], whereas HDL particles are involved in lipid transport and may exert antioxidant and endothelial anti-inflammatory functions [13, 14]. These domains are relevant to cardiometabolic disease, providing biological coherence for examining their numerical combination. Nevertheless, the numerator in NHR is neutrophil count rather than a measure of neutrophil activation, and its denominator is HDL-C concentration rather than cholesterol efflux capacity or another functional HDL assay. A higher NHR may therefore characterize an adverse hematologic-lipid profile arising from a higher neutrophil count, a lower HDL-C concentration, or both, but it does not demonstrate a specific interaction between these components or activation of an inflammatory-lipid pathway. Given this context, the observed association should be interpreted as biologically plausible epidemiologic evidence rather than mechanistic confirmation.

The indirect-effect estimates involving TyG and UA are more appropriately interpreted as evidence of statistical overlap across related metabolic domains than as confirmation of intermediary pathways. TyG incorporates FBG and TG concentrations and is commonly used as an accessible surrogate index of insulin resistance [20–22]. Prospective evidence has linked insulin resistance-related indices with the incidence and progression of CMM [1], while insulin resistance is biologically associated with free fatty acid overflow, endothelial stress, altered lipoprotein metabolism, and pro-atherogenic signaling [33, 34]. UA has been associated with metabolic syndrome, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammasome-related activation, and cardiometabolic disorders [23–25, 35]. These observations provide a theoretical basis for considering TyG and UA as markers of metabolic contexts shared by NHR and CMM. However, the estimated proportions varied after age and sex adjustment and had wide confidence intervals. They may also reflect reverse causation, residual confounding, treatment effects, existing diabetes or cardiovascular disease, and clinical or mathematical overlap between TyG components and the diabetes component of CMM. Accordingly, these findings generate longitudinal hypotheses about insulin-resistance-related and uric-acid-related metabolic phenotypes but do not identify confirmed mediators.

The potential heterogeneity observed across BMI strata provides an additional, although exploratory, perspective. The association between continuous NHR and prevalent CMM was most apparent among participants with obesity. Obesity commonly coexists with insulin resistance, adverse lipid profiles, low-grade inflammatory activity, and altered UA metabolism [33–36]; this broader metabolic context may increase the degree to which NHR clusters statistically with CMM. The finding is therefore compatible with the possibility that NHR is more informative in the presence of an adverse metabolic phenotype. However, this finding requires replication in independent cohorts using prespecified subgroup hypotheses.

The immediate clinical utility of NHR should not be overstated, but the findings retain epidemiologic and research relevance. Neutrophil count and HDL-C are routinely measured, making NHR easy to derive, yet an AUC of 0.640 is insufficient for standalone diagnosis, prospective prediction, or treatment decision-making. The reported cutoff, sensitivity, and specificity were derived and evaluated in the same cross-sectional sample and were not internally optimism-corrected or externally validated. The present results also do not establish incremental value beyond established demographic and cardiometabolic variables. A more appropriate next step is to evaluate NHR as a candidate component of multivariable assessment in prospective cohorts, with direct comparison against neutrophil count, HDL-C, established inflammatory markers, and other composite indices, together with calibration, internal and external validation, and assessment of clinical utility. Thus, the current study supports further investigation of NHR as a population-level correlate of cardiometabolic disease clustering, not its adoption as a standalone screening or risk-stratification tool.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes determination of whether NHR preceded CMM and does not satisfy the temporal assumptions required for causal mediation. The TyG and UA analyses should therefore be interpreted only as model-dependent indirect-effect estimates. Second, residual confounding may remain because the models did not include all potentially relevant factors, including physical activity, dietary patterns, socioeconomic conditions beyond PIR, acute infection or inflammatory disease status, renal function-related influences, and detailed medication classes. Third, some participants were excluded from the analysis because of missing data on key study variables, which may have introduced selection bias. Fourth, NHR does not directly measure inflammatory activity or HDL function, TyG is not equivalent to a direct measure of insulin sensitivity, and mathematical and clinical overlap among NHR, TyG, their components, and the diabetes component of CMM limits mechanistic interpretation. Fifth, the ROC analyses were unweighted, derived in the same analytic sample, not optimism-corrected, and not externally validated. Sixth, NHANES represents the non-institutionalized US population, and the findings may not generalize to younger adults, institutionalized older adults, or populations with different ancestry, healthcare access, dietary patterns, and cardiometabolic profiles. Seventh, the CMM definition was restricted to diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke and may not apply to broader definitions that include additional cardiometabolic conditions. Eighth, Model 3 simultaneously included LDL-C, hyperlipidemia, and lipid-lowering medication use. Because hyperlipidemia was partly defined by lipid concentrations and treatment, residual collinearity or overadjustment cannot be excluded despite the reported correlation matrix. Finally, lipid-lowering medication classes were grouped even though their associations with lipid concentrations and inflammatory measures may differ.

Conclusion

Overall, in this study, we found a positive association between NHR and CMM. Exploratory analyses conducted within a conventional mediation framework further suggested that TyG and UA statistically accounted for part of this association. However, given the cross-sectional design, the findings from these exploratory mediation analyses cannot establish temporal ordering and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mediation.
