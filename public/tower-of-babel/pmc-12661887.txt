Immune checkpoint inhibitors for the treatment of solid tumors and lymphoma in the past 26 years (2000–2025)
Authors: Liling Huang, Haohua Zhu, Yuankai Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer immunotherapy originated from the use of Coley’s toxins at the end of the nineteenth century. However, immunotherapy had not made great strides in cancer treatment until the discovery of immune checkpoints among which cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death 1 (PD-1), and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) are the most representative ones. Since the first CTLA-4 inhibitor ipilimumab started the first clinical trial in 2000, immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has gradually emerged as the most successful and widely applied strategy in the field of cancer immunotherapy, revolutionizing treatment paradigms across a broad spectrum of malignancies. Recently, the approvals of monoclonal antibodies targeting lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3) and novel bispecific antibodies targeting immune checkpoints may indicate the next wave of ICI agents development in cancer immunotherapy. In this review, we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview and in-depth discussion covering the current ICI treatment landscape in the past 26 years (2000-2025), indications and limitations of efficacy-predicting biomarkers, immune-related adverse events, resistance mechanisms and overcoming strategies, as well as future directions of ICI therapy.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor, CTLA-4, PD-1/PD-L1, LAG-3, Immunotherapy

Introduction

The earliest attempt to mobilize human immune system against cancer dated back to the late nineteenth century with William Coley’s use of bacterial mixtures, later called Coley’s toxins [1]. Cancer immunotherapy stalled for decades until the discovery and characterization of negative regulators of immune activation—immune checkpoints, which were found to be hijacked by tumor cells to escape immune surveillance. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) ushered in a new era of immunotherapy, shifting the paradigm from immune enhancement to immune normalization [2]. To date, the blockade of four immune checkpoint targets have realized both their successful translation into therapeutic agents and approval for clinical use in cancer treatment, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) [3, 4], programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) [5], programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) [6, 7] and lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) [8]. While CTLA-4 primarily downregulates the early priming of T cells in lymph nodes by counteracting CD28-mediated co-stimulation, the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction and LAG-3 mainly function in the effector phase of T cells [9]. The expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells suppress anti-tumor immunity of T cells by interacting with PD-1 [10]. LAG-3 exerts its suppressive function through the combination with major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecular, contributing to T cell exhaustion [11]. Since the first CTLA-4 mAb ipilimumab started the first clinical trial in 2000 [12, 13] and was approved for the treatment of melanoma by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on March 25, 2011 [14], a total of 31 ICIs have been approved for a wide spectrum of cancers across all lines of treatment setting by the data cutoff date on June 1, 2025, including 2 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting CTLA-4, 14 mAbs targeting PD-1, 11 mAbs targeting PD-L1, 1 premixed combination of fixed-dose LAG-3 and PD-1 mAbs (relatlimab and nivolumab), 1 bifunctional combination of PD-1 and CTLA-4 mAbs (iparomlimab and tuvonralimab), as well as 2 bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) cadonilimab (targeting PD-1 and CTLA-4) and ivonescimab (targeting PD-1 and vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF]). In this review, we comprehensively summarized the 26-year overview of ICIs for the treatment of solid tumors and lymphoma, through a thorough search of PubMed and published abstracts from international congress from January 1, 2000, to June 1, 2025. We focused on key registration trials and studies with greatest clinical or scientific relevance to ICIs in these malignancies, while also illuminating critical themes and unresolved challenges that merit further exploration, including biomarker discovery, the development and management of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance and corresponding strategies, as well as future directions of ICI therapy.

The overview of ICIs in current cancer treatment

Melanoma

The success of ICIs originated with their use in the treatment of metastatic melanoma which serves as a gateway to the broader application of ICI therapy across various cancer types. Apart from targeted therapies, the advent of ICIs has profoundly revolutionized the treatment landscape of melanoma [15].

Later-line treatment

Following the approval of the CTLA-4 mAb ipilimumab for the treatment of previously treated, unresectable stage III/IV melanoma with an objective response rate (ORR) of 10.9% (95% confidence interval [CI] 6.3–17.4) and a median overall survival (OS) of 10.1 months (95% CI 8.0–13.8) [14], the PD-1 mAb nivolumab or pembrolizumab monotherapy demonstrated superior ORRs over chemotherapy in patients with ipilimumab-refractory melanoma (nivolumab vs chemotherapy: 27% vs 10%; pembrolizumab vs chemotherapy: 21.1% vs 4.5%) [16, 17]. However, both nivolumab and pembrolizumab resulted in a non-statistically significant improvement in OS compared to chemotherapy, which may be related to crossover to nivolumab or pembrolizumab following progression on chemotherapy [18, 19]. Toripalimab [20] and pucotenlimab [21] monotherapy demonstrated a median OS of 20.0 months (95% CI 14.8–29.3) and 16.6 months (95% CI 14.0–27.0) respectively in patients with melanoma who had failed prior systemic treatments.

First-line treatment

Subsequently, nivolumab [22, 23] and pembrolizumab [24–26] monotherapy established their place as first-line treatment options over ipilimumab for previously untreated, unresectable stage III/IV melanoma. Later toripalimab monotherapy improved progression-free survival (PFS) compared to chemotherapy [27]. In addition to monotherapy, combination strategies have further advanced the field. Dual checkpoint blockade—anti-PD-1 plus CTLA-4, or anti-PD-1 plus LAG-3—has shown enhanced and durable clinical benefits in the first-line setting. The 10-year outcome of the CHECKMATE 067 study demonstrated that nivolumab plus ipilimumab significantly improved PFS (median 11.5 vs 2.9 months; HR 0.42, 95% CI 0.35–0.51) and OS (median 71.9 vs 19.9 months; HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.44–0.65) compared to ipilimumab monotherapy in patients with previously untreated unresectable stage III/IV melanoma [28–30]. However, there was a higher risk of adverse events (AEs) with the combination treatment, as grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurred in 55.0% of the patients in the nivolumab plus ipilimumab group, 16.3% in the nivolumab group, and 27.3% in the ipilimumab group [28]. In the RELATIVITY-047 study, the fixed-dose combination of LAG-3 mAb relatlimab (160mg) and PD-1 mAb nivolumab (480mg) significantly improved PFS compared to nivolumab monotherapy (median 10.1 vs 4.6 months; HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.62–0.92), the safety profile was favorable with grade 3–4 TRAEs of 18.9% in the relatlimab and nivolumab group and 9.7% in the nivolumab group [31].

The studies aforementioned were all conducted in patients without B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) mutation. While for patients with BRAF V600-mutated unresectable stage IIIC/IV melanoma, the addition of the PD-L1 mAb atezolizumab to vemurafenib and cobimetinib significantly prolonged PFS (median 15.1 vs 10.6 months; HR 0.78; 95% CI 0.63–0.97) in patients with unresectable stage IIIC/IV BRAF V600-mutated melanoma in the IMspire150 study, while no statistical difference in OS was observed [32, 33]. While two other similar studies of combining the inhibitors of PD-1 plus BRAF and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) including the KEYNOTE 022 study and the Combi-I study failed to meet their primary endpoint of PFS [34, 35]. To date, the dual BRAF and MEK inhibition remains the standard of care in BRAF-mutated melanoma. Current evidence does not support the widespread use of combining BRAF and MEK inhibition plus ICI for the first-line treatment of BRAF-mutated melanoma. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal sequencing strategy and to identify robust molecular biomarkers capable of selecting patients most likely to benefit from this triplet therapeutic approach.

Neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and perioperative treatment

In recent years, the success of ICI monotherapy in melanoma has extended beyond advanced disease to the early disease setting. In the adjuvant setting, for patients with resected stage III cutaneous melanoma at high risk of recurrence, agents such as ipilimumab [36, 37], nivolumab [38, 39], and pembrolizumab [40, 41] have significantly prolonged recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared to placebo or standard comparators. These results have led to expanded indications, including patients with resected stage IIB/IIC melanoma [42–44]. Emerging evidence suggests that neoadjuvant using of ICIs may offer superior clinical efficacy compared to their use in the adjuvant setting. The NADINA study, as the first phase 3 study investigating neoadjuvant ICI therapy in melanoma, demonstrated that neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab followed by nivolumab adjuvant therapy (only for patients with a partial response or non-response) significantly improved event-free survival (EFS) compared to adjuvant nivolumab alone (12-month EFS: 83.7% vs 57.2%; HR 0.32; 99.9% CI: 0.15–0.66) [45]. Similarly, the phase 2 SWOG S1801 study which evaluated the efficacy of neoadjuvant-adjuvant pembrolizumab vs adjuvant pembrolizumab alone in resectable stage III melanoma achieved positive results [46]. These findings highlighted the promising potential of 2–3 cycles of neoadjuvant ICI therapy prior to surgery, particularly in patients at high risk of recurrence. Longer follow-up and OS results will be essential to determine whether neoadjuvant or perioperative immunotherapy could be established as a new standard of care in resectable melanoma.

Lung cancer

Non-small-cell lung cancer

Among all cancer types, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) stands out as one of the most successful examples of the clinical use of ICI therapy. Multiple ICI agents have been approved for the treatment of NSCLC, the indications now span across multiple therapeutic settings, including later-line, first-line, consolidation, and perioperative settings.

Later-line treatment

In the later-line setting, nivolumab [47–49], pembrolizumab [50, 51], atezolizumab [52], and tislelizumab [53] monotherapy have demonstrated significantly improved OS compared to docetaxel in several phase 3 studies, extending the median OS from 8.1–11.9 months with docetaxel to 11.1–16.9 months with PD-(L)1 mAbs (HR of 0.66–0.73) in patients with previously treated NSCLC. Sintilimab was also investigated in the second-line treatment of squamous (sq)-NSCLC [54]. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated NSCLC usually show poor response to ICI therapy. Nevertheless, in patients with EGFR-mutant non-squamous (nsq)-NSCLC who had progressed on previous EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), sintilimab plus bevacizumab biosimilar and chemotherapy [55] or the PD-1/VEGF BsAb ivonescimab plus chemotherapy [56] presented with positive results.

First-line treatment

In the first-line setting, pembrolizumab [57–60], atezolizumab [61], and cemiplimab [62] monotherapy have established their roles in the treatment of patients with advanced NSCLC with high PD-L1 expression and without sensitizing EGFR mutations or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) translocations, offering an effective chemotherapy-free option for this patient population. In the HARMONi-2 study, ivonescimab significantly improved PFS compared to pembrolizumab (median 11.1 vs 5.8 months; HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.38–0.69) in patients with locally advanced or metastatic PD-L1 positive NSCLC without sensitizing EGFR/ALK alterations [63]. Apart from monotherapy, the combination of PD-(L)1 mAbs and chemotherapy established their place in the first-line treatment of nsq-NSCLC [64–73] or sq-NSCLC [74–80] or both [81–84] without sensitizing EGFR/ALK alterations. Furthermore, the combination of PD-(L)1 plus CTLA-4 mAbs with or without chemotherapy (including nivolumab plus ipilimumab [85, 86], nivolumab plus ipilimumab and chemotherapy [87, 88], durvalumab plus tremelimumab and chemotherapy [89]) have also achieved approvals in the first-line setting.

The attempt of PD-(L)1 mAbs plus anti-angiogenesis therapy and chemotherapy in the first-line treatment of NSCLC were not satisfying. Positive results were only shown in the IMpower150 study [90]. However, the IMpower151 study failed to demonstrate a superior PFS in atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and chemotherapy compared to bevacizumab plus chemotherapy in Chinese patients with nsq-NSCLC [91]. The ASTRUM-002 study also showed that serplulimab plus chemotherapy was associated with significantly improved PFS compared to chemotherapy alone (median 11.0 vs 5.6 months; HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.43–0.69), however the addition of HLX04 (a bevacizumab biosimilar) to serplulimab plus chemotherapy failed to bring further benefit in PFS [73]. Apart from the concurrent combination, the TQB2450-III-12 study demonstrated that benmelstobart plus chemotherapy followed by sequential benmelstobart plus anlotinib significantly improved PFS compared to tislelizumab plus chemotherapy followed by sequential tislelizumab treatment (median 10.12 vs 7.79 months; HR 0.64, 98.35% CI 0.45–0.93) in the first-line treatment of sq-NSCLC [92]. The optimal use and sequence of PD-(L)1 inhibitors plus anti-angiogenesis therapy and chemotherapy in the treatment of NSCLC merit future investigation. Beyond that, the combination of anti-PD-L1 plus T cell immune receptor with immunoglobulin and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif domain (TIGIT) (atezolizumab plus tiragolumab) was also investigated as a first-line treatment for PD-L1 positive NSCLC, but failed to meet its primary endpoints of PFS and OS in its phase 3 study [93].

Consolidation therapy

Furthermore, two PD-L1 mAbs have established their roles in the consolidation setting, durvalumab first gained widespread recognition for its role in the consolidation therapy for stage III unresectable NSCLC after chemoradiation based on the results of the PACIFIC study, which demonstrated that durvalumab significantly prolonged PFS (median 16.9 vs 5.6 months; HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.45–0.68) and OS (median 47.5 vs 29.1 months; HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.59–0.89) compared to placebo [94]. The GEMSTONE-301 study demonstrated that sugemalimab significantly prolonged PFS (median 9.0 vs 5.8; HR 0.64, 95%CI 0.48–0.85) and OS (median NR vs 24.1; HR 0.44, 95%CI 0.27–0.73) in the same patient population [95].

Neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and perioperative treatment

Growing studies have been exploring the value of adding ICIs in the treatment of early-stage NSCLC. Atezolizumab or pembrolizumab monotherapy have established their roles as adjuvant therapy in patients with completely resected stage IB-IIIA NSCLC [96, 97]. Five phase 3 studies including the AEGEAN study of durvalumab [98], the Neotorch study of toripalimab [99], the CheckMate-77 T study of nivolumab [100], the KEYNOTE-671 study of pembrolizumab [101], and the RATIONALE-315 study of tislelizumab [102] confirmed the survival benefit of incorporating ICIs plus chemotherapy in the perioperative setting. However, a concern about the risk–benefit balance of adjuvant immunotherapy was raised as we observed some controversial results. The CheckMate 816 study only allowed neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy in the interventional arm, which significantly improved EFS (median 31.6 vs 20.8 months; HR 0.63, 97.38% CI 0.43–0.91) and 5-year OS rates (65.4% vs 55.0%, HR 0.72, 95%CI 0.523-0.998) compared to chemotherapy alone in patients with resectable stage IB-IIIA NSCLC [103]. On the other hand, the adjuvant use of durvalumab failed to meet the primary endpoint of DFS in the ADJUVANT BR.31 study [104]. Additionally, a recent debate on whether adjuvant immunotherapy should be used in patients who achieved pathological complete response (pCR) has drawn attention [105, 106]. More rigorous study designs are needed to generate more robust and convincing evidence regarding the necessity and indication of adjuvant immunotherapy in NSCLC.

Small cell lung cancer

Compared to NSCLC, therapeutic advancements in small cell lung cancer (SCLC) have been relatively limited. In the later-line setting of SCLC treatment, nivolumab and pembrolizumab monotherapy were once approved [107, 108], however their approvals were both withdrawn by the U.S. FDA as their respective phase 3 studies failed to meet the primary endpoints of OS [109, 110]. Nevertheless, the addition of PD-(L)1 mAbs to chemotherapy has now supplanted chemotherapy alone as the standard first-line treatment for extensive stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC). Currently, 6 ICIs, including 3 PD-L1 mAbs (atezolizumab [111], durvalumab [112], and adebrelimab [113]) and 3 PD-1 mAbs (serplulimab [114], toripalimab [115], and tislelizumab [116]), in combination with platinum and etoposide for the first-line treatment of ES-SCLC significantly extended the median OS to 12.3–15.4 months with HRs of 0.63–0.80, as well as prolonged the median PFS to 4.7–5.8 months with HRs of 0.48–0.78. Later benmelstobart combined with anlotinib and chemotherapy presented with an OS improvement (median 19.3 vs 11.9 months; HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.47–0.79) compared to chemotherapy alone [117]. In limited stage SCLC, durvalumab as a consolidation therapy after chemoradiotherapy significantly prolonged both OS (median 55.9 vs 33.4 months; HR 0.73; 98.321% CI 0.54–0.98) and PFS (median 16.6 vs 9.2 months; HR 0.76, 97.195% CI 0.59–0.98) compared to chemoradiotherapy alone [118].

Head and Neck malignancies

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma

In the later-line setting, pembrolizumab [119, 120] or nivolumab [121] monotherapy in patients with r/m head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) whose disease progression on or after platinum-containing chemotherapy. In the first-line setting, the combination of pembrolizumab or finotonlimab with chemotherapy established their roles for the treatment of patients with recurrent or metastatic (r/m) HNSCC with significantly improved OS (pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy vs chemotherapy: median 13.0 vs 10.7 months, HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.93 [122]; finotonlimab plus chemotherapy vs chemotherapy: median 14.1 vs 10.5 months; HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.57–0.95 [123]). Pembrolizumab monotherapy also has been approved for patients with previously untreated r/m HNSCC with a PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 1. Increasing PD-L1 expression levels in patients with HNSCC have been correlated with enhanced therapeutic efficacy [122, 123]. In the KEYNOTE-048 study, neither pembrolizumab alone nor pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy improved PFS compared to cetuximab plus chemotherapy, despite demonstrating confirmed OS benefit [122]. Several factors may contribute to this phenomenon: first, unlike chemotherapy which exerts rapid cytotoxic effects on fast-dividing cells, immunotherapy typically has a delayed onset of action. This “slow-burning” characteristic may not be adequately captured by PFS which primarily relies on radiographic assessments. Second, a subset of patients derives long-term benefit from PD-1 blockade, leading to the so-called “tail of curve” benefit and contributed to a prolonged OS in the long-term.

In the adjuvant setting, the addition of adjuvant nivolumab to chemoradiotherapy significantly improved disease-free survival (DFS) compared to chemoradiotherapy (3-year DFS: 63.1% vs 52.5%, HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.60–0.98) in resected patients with locally advanced HNSCC at high risk of relapse [124]. Of note, no approval in the adjuvant setting of HNSCC has been received, more mature results are awaited.

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma

In the later-line setting, for patients with r/m nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) refractory to standard chemotherapy, ICI monotherapy (including toripalimab [125], camrelizumab [126], tagitanlimab [127], and penpulimab [128] demonstrated ORRs of 20.5–28.2%, median PFS of 1.9–3.7 months, and median OS of 16.2–22.8 months. In the first-line setting, several PD-(L)1 mAbs in combination with chemotherapy demonstrated significantly improved PFS compared to chemotherapy, including toripalimab (median 21.4 vs 8.2 months; HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.37–0.73) in the JUPITER-02 study [129], camrelizumab (median 10.8 vs 6.9 months; HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.37–0.69) in the CAPTAIN-1st study [130], tislelizumab (median 9.6 vs 7.4 months; HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.37–0.68) in the RATIONALE-309 study [131], penpulimab (median 9.6 vs 7.0 months; HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.33–0.62) in the AK105-304 study [132], and tagitanlimab (median not reached [NR] vs 7.9 months; HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.33–0.66) in the KL167-Ⅲ−08 study [133].

Gastrointestinal malignancies

Esophageal cancer

In the esophageal cancer, beyond ICIs’ applications in the later-line setting regardless of PD-L1 expression [134–136], anti-PD-(L)1 plus chemotherapy have emerged as the standard of care in the first-line setting. Compared to chemotherapy alone, camrelizumab [137], nivolumab [138], sintilimab [139], toripalimab [140], tislelizumab [141], serplulimab [142], and sugemalimab [143] in combination with chemotherapy significantly prolonged the median PFS to 5.7–8.4 months with HRs of 0.56–0.81 as well as the median OS to 13.2–17.2 months with HRs of 0.58–0.74 in the first-line treatment of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), regardless of PD-L1 status. Besides, the combination of nivolumab plus ipilimumab received positive results in the same indication [138]. Pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy established its place in the first-line setting of esophageal cancer or gastro-esophageal junction (E/GEJ) cancer [144].

Adjuvant nivolumab has shown definitive clinical benefits in patients who failed to achieve a pCR following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. In the CheckMate 577 study, nivolumab following surgery significantly improved DFS compared to placebo (median 22.4 vs 11.0 months; HR 0.69, 96.4% CI 0.56–0.86) in patients with completely resected stage II/III E/GEJ cancer who had received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and had residual pathological disease [145]. However, the role of ICIs in the neoadjuvant setting remains under investigation, with a lack of high-quality evidence to support its routine use.

Gastric cancer

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) overexpression and/or amplification is observed in approximately 20% of gastric cancers, with the prevalence even higher in GEJ tumors. In the later-line setting, pembrolizumab was firstly approved for gastric or gastro-esophageal junction (G/GEJ) cancer whose disease has progressed after 2 or more lines of therapy [146], however its indication was withdrawn by the U.S. FDA, as its phase 3 study failed to meet its primary endpoint of OS [147]. The treatment landscape of patients with advanced gastric cancer is now stratified based on HER2 status. For HER2-negative G/GEJ cancer, the combination of a PD-(L)1 mAb [148–152] or the PD-1/CTLA-4 BsAb cadonilimab [153] with chemotherapy has become the standard first-line treatment, particularly in patients with higher PD-L1 expression. For HER2-positive G/GEJ cancer, the combination of pembrolizumab plus trastuzumab and chemotherapy represented a significant advancement in the first-line treatment of this molecular subtype, as proved in the KEYNOTE-811 study [154].

Colorectal cancer

Colorectal cancer (CRC) can be categorized into two parts based on tumor’s mismatch-repair status and microsatellite stability, including deficient mismatch repair (dMMR)/microsatellite instability high (MSI-H) CRC (~ 15%) and proficient mismatch repair (pMMR)/microsatellite instability low (MSI-L) CRC (~ 85%). ICI therapy have made huge success in the treatment of dMMR/MSI-H CRC, but not in pMMR/MSI-L CRC [155].

In the later-line setting, nivolumab monotherapy, nivolumab plus ipilimumab, and pembrolizumab monotherapy have established their placein the treatment of patients with previously treated MSI-H/dMMR CRC [156–158]. In the first-line setting, pembrolizumab monotherapy or the combination of nivolumab plus ipilimumab has become the standards of care for patients with untreated r/m MSI-H/dMMR CRC. In the KEYNOTE-177 study, pembrolizumab monotherapy significantly improved PFS compared to chemotherapy in the first-line treatment of MSI-H/dMMR CRC (median 16.5 vs 8.2 months; HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.45–0.80) [159], while the prespecified superiority significance level of OS was not met (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.53–1.03) [160]. In the CheckMate-8HW study, nivolumab plus ipilimumab not only significantly improved PFS compared to chemotherapy (median NR vs 5.9 months, p <0.001) in the first-line setting [161], but also improved PFS compared to nivolumab monotherapy (NR vs 39.3 months; HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.48–0.81) in all-lines of setting [162].

Till now, the approvals of ICI agents only limited in the later-line and first-line treatment of dMMR/MSI-H CRC. In the ATOMIC study, the combination of atezolizumab plus chemotherapy was investigated in the adjuvant therapy of stage III dMMR colon cancer, which significantly improved DFS compared to chemotherapy alone (36-month DFS rate: 86.4% vs 76.6%; HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.72) [163]. Whether it could become the new standard adjuvant treatment of stage III dMMR colon cancer need more studies to confirm.

Hepatobiliary malignancies

Hepatocellular carcinoma

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, and even after surgical resection, it carries a high risk of recurrence. Systemic therapy has become a critical component in the overall management of HCC. ICIs, either monotherapy, or in combination with anti-angiogenic agents, or dual ICIs regimens, has replaced oral small-molecular anti-angiogenic targeted agents like sorafenib, lenvatinib, or donafenib monotherapy in the first-line treatment of advanced HCC [166].

In their respective phase 3 studies, durvalumab [167, 168] and tislelizumab [169] monotherapy met their non-inferiority threshold of OS compared to sorafenib. While nivolumab monotherapy was once approved for HCC who were previously treated with sorafenib [170], but the indication was withdrawn by the U.S. FDA as its phase 3 CheckMate 459 study failed to meet the primary endpoint of OS [171]. Besides, similar to renal cell carcinoma (RCC), HCC represents a successful example of harnessing the synergistic potential of combining anti-PD-(L)1 agents with anti-angiogenic therapies. Atezolizumab plus bevacizumab [172, 173], sintilimab plus IBI305 (a bevacizumab biosimilar) [174], camrelizumab plus apatinib [175], toripalimab plus bevacizumab [176], and finotonlimab plus SCT510 (a bevacizumab biosimilar) [177] met their dual primary endpoints of OS and PFS, and established their place in the first-line setting of HCC treatment. Durvalumab plus tremelimumab (median OS: 16.43 vs 13.77 months; HR 0.78, 96.02% CI 0.65–0.93) [167, 168] and nivolumab plus ipilimumab (median OS: 23.7 vs 20.6 months; HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.65–0.96) [178] also won their place over sorafenib monotherapy among patient with previously untreated HCC not eligible for curative surgical or locoregional therapies.

Biliary tract cancer

In the treatment of biliary tract cancer (BTC), the ORRs of pembrolizumab monotherapy in patients with previously treated advanced BTC were 5.8–13.0% [179]. In the first-line setting, the addition of durvalumab [180, 181] or pembrolizumab [182] to gemcitabine and cisplatin chemotherapy brought about an addition OS benefit of 1–2 months compared to chemotherapy alone.

Urologic malignancies

Renal cell carcinoma

Since 2016, the survival outcomes of patients with RCC have continued to be improved, largely attributed to the advances in combination therapies of ICIs and anti-angiogenic targeted agents. The integration of a PD-(L)1 mAb with a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor‐associated multi‐targeted TKI has set a new standard in the first-line setting of advanced clear-cell RCC treatment, achieving ORRs of 55.2–71.0% and extending PFS by 6–15 months than sunitinib monotherapy [189–197]. In the latest update of the CheckMate 9ER study, the median OS continued to favor nivolumab plus cabozantinib compared to sunitinib (49.5 vs 35.5 months; HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.56–0.87) [193, 194]. In the final prespecified OS analysis of KEYNOTE-581/CLEAR, pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib still presented OS benefit (median 53.7 vs 54.3 months, HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.63–0.99; 3-years OS rate: 66.4% vs 60.2%) [195, 196]. Theoretically, the rapid proliferation of tumor cells often outpace their vascular supply, resulting in a hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) that fosters immunosuppression. The combination of anti-angiogenic therapy with ICIs serves to normalize aberrant vascular-immune crosstalk, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses and restoring immune surveillance [198]. This combination of ICI with anti-angiogenic agents in the treatment of RCC stands as a paradigm of success in the field of immuno-targeted therapy.

Concurrently, the role of immunotherapy in the perioperative and adjuvant setting of RCC has been actively explored, though with mixed results. The KEYNOTE-564 study ignited hope for adjuvant immunotherapy in RCC, which demonstrated that adjuvant pembrolizumab significantly improved both 48-month DFS rate (64.9% vs 56.6%; HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.53–0.87) and 48-month OS rate (91.2% vs 86.0%; HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.44–0.87) compared to placebo in patients with clear-cell RCC who were at intermediate-high risk for recurrence after nephrectomy [199, 200]. However, three other studies including the IMmotion010 study [201] (atezolizumab, adjuvant setting), the CheckMate 914 study [202] (nivolumab plus ipilimumab, adjuvant setting), and the PROSPER study [203] (nivolumab, perioperative setting) failed to meet their primary endpoints of DFS, casting doubt on the consistency of ICI efficacy in the early-stage RCC. The failure of the CheckMate 914 study was primarily attributed to the substantial AEs associated with the nivolumab plus ipilimumab combination, with grade 3–5 TRAEs occurring in 38% of patients, and a treatment discontinuation rate of 32.0% due to TRAEs [202]. The failure of the PROSPER study might be partly due to its design limitations and excessively long enrollment period [203]. Further studies are warranted to optimize study design and enrollment criteria, refine therapeutic strategies, and optimize pharmacologic dosing for early-stage RCC.

Urothelial cancer

In the later-line setting, nivolumab [204], pembrolizumab [205, 206], avelumab [207], tislelizumab [208], and toripalimab [209] monotherapy demonstrated ORRs of 17–26%, median PFS of 2.0–6.3 months, and median OS of 6.5–10.3 months in patients with unresectable urothelial carcinoma who had progressed after previous platinum-based chemotherapy. While two PD-L1 mAbs, atezolizumab and durvalumab, were once approved in the same indication [210, 211], but were further withdrawn by the U.S. FDA as their respective phase 3 studies failed to subsequently meet their primary endpoints of OS [212].

In the first-line setting, patients with advanced urothelial carcinoma who are ineligible to platinum-based chemotherapy have a poor prognosis historically. Pembrolizumab monotherapy yielded an ORR of 28.9% (95%CI 24.3–33.8) and a median PFS of 2.5 months (95%CI 2.1–3.4) [206]. The approval of atezolizumab monotherapy for the treatment of same patient population [213] was withdrawn by the U.S. FDA, as its phase 3 study failed to meet its primary endpoint of OS [214]. In the combination therapy, the outcomes observed with ICI plus antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) appear markedly superior to those achieved with ICI plus standard chemotherapy. In the CheckMate 901 study, nivolumab plus chemotherapy prolonged OS by 2.8 months (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.63–0.96) and PFS by 0.3 months (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.59–0.88) compared to chemotherapy [215]. But the combination of pembrolizumab plus enfortumab vedotin (an ADC targeting Nectin-4) significantly prolonged PFS (median 12.5 vs 6.3 months; HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.38–0.54) and OS (median 31.5 vs 16.1 months; HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.38–0.58) compared to chemotherapy [216]. For those who had not progressed with first-line chemotherapy, avelumab monotherapy as maintenance treatment demonstrated significantly prolonged OS (median 23.8 vs 15.0 months; HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.63–0.91) compared to best standard of care [217, 218]. Additionally, in the KEYNOTE-057 study, for patients with bladder cancer, pembrolizumab monotherapy demonstrated a complete response rate of 41% (95% CI 30.7–51.1) in patients with Bacille Calmette-Guérin-unresponsive carcinoma in situ of the bladder with or without papillary tumours who declined or were ineligible for radical cystectomy [219], and a 12-month DFS rate of 43.5% (95% CI 34.9–51.9) in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer with papillary tumors without carcinoma in situ [220].

Muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma is an aggressive disease with high risks of relapse even after surgery [221]. In the adjuvant setting, in the CheckMate 274 study, nivolumab monotherapy significantly improved DFS compared to placebo (median 22.0 vs 10.9 months; HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.58–0.86) in patients with muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma [222, 223]. The AMBASSADOR study of pembrolizumab provided additional evidence supporting the use of ICIs in the adjuvant setting for muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma by demonstrating a 15.4-month improvement in DFS over placebo [224]. Besides, the addition of perioperative durvalumab to neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved the pCR rate (33.8% vs 25.8%) and EFS (24-month EFS rate: 67.8% vs 59.8%; HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.56–0.82) compared to chemotherapy alone in patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer who received radical cystectomy [225].

Female-specific malignancies

Triple-negative breast cancer

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for 15–25% of newly diagnosed breast cancer cases [230]. Among all subtypes of breast cancer, TNBC demonstrated the highest responsiveness to ICI therapy. The enhanced sensitivity is attributed to more enriched tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [231], higher neoantigen presentation driven by a higher mutational burden and genomic instability [232], as well as elevated PD-L1 expression, with approximately 50% of patients exhibiting a CPS ≥ 10 [233]. Importantly, the efficacy of ICIs in TNBC varies across different disease stages. In the metastatic setting of TNBC, PD-L1 expression level serves as a critical predictive biomarker for ICI response. The KEYNOTE-355 study demonstrated that pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy significantly improved PFS (median 9.7 vs 5.6 months; HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.88) and OS (median 23.0 vs 16.1 months; HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.55–0.95) compared to chemotherapy alone in the first-line treatment of TNBC with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 10, while in PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 subgroup and the whole population, the difference of OS between the two groups were not significant [234, 235]. The TORCHLIGHT study demonstrated that toripalimab plus chemotherapy significantly prolonged PFS compared to chemotherapy alone as the first-line treatment for patients with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 TNBC [236]. However, atezolizumab plus chemotherapy was once approved for the first-line treatment of TNBC [237], however it was withdrawn by the U.S. FDA as its phase 3 study failed to meet its primary endpoint of OS [238]. Of note, as TNBC progresses, increasing intratumoral heterogeneity, exhaustion of immune effector cells such as CD8 + T cells and TILs, and declining PD-L1 expression contribute to diminished efficacy of ICIs [239]. In patients with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 TNBC, pembrolizumab demonstrated an ORR of 21.4% in the first-line setting [240], and 5.7-12.3% in later-line therapies [241, 242].

In contrast, the perioperative setting of early-stage TNBC offers greater immune plasticity and responsiveness to immunomodulation, enabling broader clinical benefit. In the KEYNOTE-522 study, the addition of perioperative pembrolizumab to neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly increased the pCR rate (64.8% vs 51.2%; estimated treatment difference 13.6%, 95% CI 5.4–21.8), the 5-year EFS rate (81.2% vs 72.2%; HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.51–0.83) and 5-year OS rate (86.6% vs 81.7%; p = 0.002) in patients with previously untreated stage II/III TNBC, clinical benefits were observed irrespective of PD-L1 expression levels [243–245].

Cervical cancer

The three most common gynecologic cancers in women worldwide included cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, and ovarian cancer [246]. In patients with r/m cervical cancer who have progressed after previous systemic therapy, apart from the applications of PD-(L)1 mAbs pembrolizumab [247], socazolimab [248], zimberelimab [249], enlonstobart [250] monotherapy with ORRs of 15.4–29.0% and PFS of 2.1–3.7 months, two novel agents including cadonilimab with an ORR of 32.3% (95%CI 23.3–42.5) and a median PFS of 3.7 months (95%CI 1.9–6.3) [251], as well as iparomlimab and tuvonralimab (a bifunctional combination of PD-1/CTLA-4 mAbs at a ratio of 2:1) with an ORR of 33.8% (95%CI 26.2–42.0) and a median PFS of 5.4 months (95%CI 3.9–6.9) [252, 253] brought new options for the second-line treatment of cervical cancer.

In the first-line treatment of r/m cervical cancer, pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab [254, 255], or pembrolizumab in combination with chemoradiotherapy [256, 257], have become standard first-line treatment options. However, in the KEYNOTE-826 study, the PFS and OS benefit of pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab were not observed in the PD-L1 CPS < 1 subgroup [254, 255]. The dual inhibition of PD-1 and CTLA-4 might bring more benefit for the overall patient population and negative PD-L1patient population. Both cadonilimab and iparomlimab and tuvonralimab were also explored with promising efficacy in the first-line setting of r/m cervical cancer treatment. In the COMPASSION-16 study, the first-line treatment of cadonilimab plus chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab significantly improved PFS (median 12.7 vs 8.1 months; HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.49–0.80) and OS (median NR vs 22.8 months; HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.48–0.86) compared to chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab in the first-line setting, regardless of PD-L1 expression [258]. In the phase 2 DUBHE-C-204 study, the first-line treatment of iparomlimab and tuvonralimab plus chemotherapy with or without bevacizumab demonstrated an ORR of 81.0%, a median PFS of 15.1 months, with ≥ grade 3 TRAEs of 15.0% [259].

Endometrial cancer

For endometrial cancer, driven by the findings of the GARNET study of dostarlimab [260, 261] with an ORR of 45.5% (95%CI 37.1-54.0) and the KEYNOTE-158 study of pembrolizumab with an ORR of 48% (95%CI 37-60) [262], ICI therapy has garnered significant attention in the treatment of previously treated r/m dMMR endometrial cancer. In contrast, pMMR/MSI-L endometrial cancer—accounted for approximately 70% of endometrial cancer cases—responded poorly to ICI monotherapy with ORRs of 3–14% [263]. In recent years, the combination of ICI and anti-angiogenic agents including pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib [264], benmelstobart plus anlotinib [265], and sintilimab plus fruquintinib [266] has notably reshaped the later-line therapeutic paradigm of patients with pMMR endometrial cancer with ORRs of 31.8–35.6% and median PFS of 6.7–9.5 months.

In the first-line setting, the combination of a PD-1 mAb (dostarlimab [267, 268] or pembrolizumab [269, 270]) and chemotherapy have established their roles in the treatment of advanced endometrial cancer, irrespective of MMR status. While the indication of durvalumab plus chemotherapy is limited in dMMR endometrial cancer based on the results of the DUO-E study, which investigated durvalumab plus chemotherapy followed by maintenance durvalumab with or without olaparib in patients with newly diagnosed stage III/IV or recurrent endometrial cancer. In dMMR subpopulation, durvalumab plus chemotherapy followed by durvalumab maintenance significantly improved PFS than chemotherapy alone (median NR vs 7.0 months; HR 0.42, 95% CI 0.22–0.80), while the addition of olaparib maintanance failed to further improve PFS than durvalumab maintenance alone (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.49–1.98) [271].

Lymphoma

Lymphoma comprises a wide range of subtypes, among which relapsed or refractory (r/r) classical Hodgkin’s lymphoma (cHL) demonstrates the most impressive response to PD-(L)1 monotherapy with ORRs of 65.0%−90.6% [272–285]. This exceptional sensitivity is likely due to its unique biological features and the immune hot microenvironment. High PD-L1 expression is observed on Hodgkin Reed–Sternberg (R-S) cells, which might be due to frequent Epstein–Barr virus infection and genetic alterations such as 9p24.1 amplification [286, 287]. Besides, cHL is characterized by R-S cells being surrounded by an inflamed milieu with CD8 + T cells [288]. Additionally, nivolumab plus doxorubicin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (AVD) chemotherapy in the first-line treatment of stage III/IV Hodgkin’s lymphoma showed superior efficacy than brentuximab vedotin plus AVD treatment (2-year PFS rate 92% vs 83%; HR 0.48, 99% CI 0.27–0.87) [289], which might be setting as a new standard therapy for the first-line treatment of this patient population.

The sensitivity of B-cell non-Hodgkin's lymphoma to ICI therapy is generally lower than that of cHL, with only one approval respectively in r/r primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) and r/r (NK)/T cell lymphoma. In the KEYNOTE-170 study, pembrolizumab monotherapy demostrated an ORR of 41.5% (90% CI 30.0–53.7) in r/r PMBCL [290]. In the GESTONE-201 study, sugemalimab monotherapy demonstrated an ORR of 44.9% (95%CI 33.6–56.6) and a 12-month duration of response rate of 82.5% (95%CI 62.0–92.6) in r/r (NK)/T cell lymphoma [291]. Besides, PD-1 mAb plus rituximab was investigated in r/r DLBCL [292]. Geptanolimab monotherapy was investigated in r/r peripheral T cell lymphoma with an ORR of 40.4% (95% CI 30.2–51.4) [293]. Geptanolimab was also investigated in alveolar soft part sarcoma and PMBCL with promising results [294, 295].

Malignancies not suitable for ICI therapy

While the extensive use of ICIs across various malignancies, there are certain types of cancer such as estrogen receptor (ER) positive breast cancer [317], prostate cancer [318], pMMR CRC [319], ovarian cancers [320] exhibit poor response to ICI therapy, which are due to their inherently immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironments. ER-positive breast cancer, which accounts for around 70% of breast cancer, is typically microsatellite stable (MSS), along with a low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and limited neoantigen generation as well as fewer TILs presentation in the TME [231]. Unlike the robust effector T-cell expansion observed in melanoma or lung cancer, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that prostate cancer is predominantly characterized by T-cell exhaustion and enrichment of immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [318, 321–323], so does ovarian cancer [320, 324]. Apart from the stable microsatellite status, pMMR/MSI-L CRC is also characterized by sparse TILs and low numbers of PD-1 positive lymphocytes [155].

The rationales behind the neoadjuvant treatment

Growing studies are exploring the value of integrating ICIs in the management of early-stage cancers. Neoadjuvant ICI therapy is a diverse and robust arena of clinical research, with particularly striking outcomes observed in NSCLC, TNBC, and melanoma. This paradigm shift is driven by both clinical benefits and biological rationales. The early disease setting before surgery is probably a favorable context for ICI therapy, supported by several underlying biological rationales: i) compared to adjuvant immunotherapy when the tumor burden is minimal, neoadjuvant PD-(L)1 blockade allows the immune system to be exposed to the intact, antigen-rich TME, leverages higher levels of endogenous tumor antigen presentation, enabling optimal T-cell priming and expansion as well as inducing a more profound systemic immune response [325, 326]; ii) allowing for the elimination of micro-metastatic tumor deposits in earlier condition and reducing the risk of postoperative relapse [327]; iii) allowing for early assessment of treatment response to the regimens used and enabling timely adjustment to the therapeutic strategy when necessary.

Efficacy biomarkers

PD-L1

Indications

PD-L1 expression detected by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in tumor tissue was the first U.S. FDA-approved companion diagnostic biomarker for ICI response based on the result of the KEYNOTE-024 study [57, 58], where pembrolizumab significantly improved PFS (median 7.7 vs 5.5 months; HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.39–0.65) over platinum-based chemotherapy in the first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with PD-L1 tumor proportional score (TPS) ≥ 50%, marking a significant milestone in the implementation of biomarker-guided precision immunotherapy. Subsequently, the KEYNOTE-042 study established PD-L1 TPS ≥ 1% as a valid criterion for selecting patients with NSCLC receiving first-line pembrolizumab monotherapy [59]. Later the predictive value of PD-L1 TPS has been verified in several cancer types including HNSCC, NSCLC, TNBC, and gastric cancer but under different cutoff value and different measurement method. Taking pembrolizumab as an example, apart from TPS, PD-L1 CPS has also been approved for guidence of pembrolizumab treatment in different cancers with different thresholds: CPS ≥ 1 in HNSCC [122], CPS ≥ 1 in G/GEJ cancer [154], CPS ≥ 10 in TNBC [234], and CPS ≥ 10 in esophageal cancer [135], etc. Atezolizumab monotherapy has been approved for use based on tumor cell (TC) or immune cell (IC) criterion: i.e. first-line use in NSCLC with PD-L1 TC ≥ 50% or IC ≥ 10% [61], adjuvant use in NSCLC with PD-L1 TC ≥ 1%[96].

HNSCC serves as a representative example in which patients with higher PD-L1 expression derive greater clinical benefit from ICI therapy. In a phase 3 study, finotonlimab plus chemotherapy improved OS especially in the CPS ≥ 20 subgroup of patients with r/m HNSCC compared to chemotherapy (CPS ≥ 20: HR 0.5, 95% CI 0.35–0.72; CPS ≥ 1: HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.56–0.96; CPS < 1: HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.22–1.32) [123]. While a recent study of NSCLC discovered that the predictive significance of PD-L1 expression varied between sq- and nsq-NSCLC. Increasing PD-L1 expression was associated with a stepwise survival improvement in nsq-NSCLC, whereas in sq-NSCLC, PD-L1 TPS functioned more as a dichotomous predictor (< 1% vs ≥ 1%) rather than a continuous predictor [328]. These findings carried important implications for clinical decision-making and for the design and interpretation of ICI-related clinical studies.

Limitations

However, based on the results of multiple clinical studies and real-world clinical practice across various types of cancers, we cannot ignore that the single predictive value of PD-L1 is still limited. Several challenges of PD-L1 should be noted: i) the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of PD-L1 can vary between different tumor sites (primary vs metastatic). ii) the variability among PD-L1 IHC assays—including different antibody clones (22C3, SP142, 28–8, SP263), scoring algorithms for quantifying PD-L1 expression (TPS, CPS, TC, IC), and threshold values for defining PD-L1 positivity—poses a significant challenge to establishing a standardized cutoff for real world clinical decision-making. iii) in cancers such as melanoma, lymphoma, colorectal cancer, and cervical cancer, the correlation between PD-L1 expression and response to immunotherapy were not observed, which suggests that the tumor context or microenvironment matters in deciding the predictive value of PD-L1 expression level.

TMB

Indications

TMB refers to the number of somatic non-synonymous mutations or the total number of mutations per megabase (mut/Mb) of the coding genome. A high TMB is associated with increased neoantigen load and tumor heterogeneity, which enhances the likelihood of tumor cell recognition and elimination by T cells [329]. In 2020, the U.S. FDA approved TMB as a companion diagnostic biomarker for pembrolizumab based on the KEYNOTE-158 study which included 790 patients with advanced solid tumors progressed on or intolerance to at least one line of standard treatment, and defined TMB-high status as TMB ≥ 10 mut/Mb, the results showed that pembrolizumab monotherapy yielded an ORR of 29.4% (95% CI 20.8–39.3) in the TMB-high subgroup and 6.3% (95% CI 4.6–8.3) in the non-TMB-high group [311].

Limitations

Similar to PD-L1, the threshold used to define TMB-high status varied across studies. For instance, in NSCLC, reported cutoffs range from 10 to 16 mut/Mb when using next-generation sequencing (NGS), and from 175 to 245 mutations per exome using whole-exome sequencing (WES) [330]. These discrepancies are further compounded by variations across cancer types [331, 332]. Moreover, the different methodology used to assess TMB—including NGS and WES—as well as differences in sequencing depth and the number of genes covered, can significantly influence TMB quantification, posing challenges to standardization and clinical interpretation.

MMR/MSI

Indications

The MMR system plays a critical role in preserving genomic stability by correcting base mismatches that occur during DNA replication. Deficiency in the MMR system leads to the persistence of replication errors, ultimately disrupting chromosomal integrity and giving rise to MSI [333]. MSI-H/dMMR is the first U.S. FDA-approved tissue-agnostic biomarker, guiding treatment decisions based solely on tumor biology rather than histological origin. In 2017, pembrolizumab was granted accelerated approval for dMMR/MSI-H solid tumors. Among all cancer types, the MSI-H/dMMR phenotype is most prevalent in endometrial cancers (30%), CRC (10%−15%) and gastroesophageal cancers (9%), where it also demonstrated the strongest predictive value for response to ICI therapy [334]. In the RUBY study, dostarlimab plus chemotherapy demonstrated a 24-months PFS rate of 61.4% (95% CI 46.3–73.4) in patients with MSI-H/dMMR endometrial cancer and 28.4% (95% CI 21.2–36.0) in patients with pMMR/MSS endometrial cancer [267, 268]. The results of KEYNOTE-868/NRG-GY018 study also supported the predictive value of MMR/MSI status in endometrial cancer [269, 270]. In the pilot study investigating the efficacy of PD-1 blockade in MSI-H/dMMR solid tumors, pembrolizumab demonstrated an ORR of 40% (95% CI 12–74) in patients with dMMR CRC, 0% (95% CI 0–19) in patient with pMMR CRC, and 71% (95% CI 29–96) in patients with dMMR non-CRC [335, 336].

Limitations

However, dMMR/MSI-H status was only observed in a small number of cancer patients, the pooled overall prevalence of dMMR and MSI in all cancer patients was reported as 2.9% and 2.7%, respectively, which limited its broad applicability [334].

Other biomarkers

However, currently available biomarkers remain insufficient for precisely predicting patient response to ICI therapy, patients without favorable aforementioned biomarkers presentation might still respond to immunotherapy. Not a single biomarker would be expected to encapsulate the complexity of tumor biological process as well as tumor immune context and potent. Apart from the four approved biomarkers, other indicators such as TILs, CD8 + T cells, transcriptomic signatures and microbiome, though currently not approved, have been identified as being associated with immune responses and are emerging as potential predictive biomarkers for ICI therapy.

In the TME, elevated levels of TILs, CD8 + T cells have been associated with improved survival outcomes [337–340]. Unfortunately, different studies have applied their own scoring methods and cutoffs for CD8 + T cells, making the results inconsistent [330]. Importantly, the mere presence of CD8 + T cells does not necessarily imply functional anti-tumor activity. As such, there is growing interest in more precise evaluation of CD8 + T cells function through the incorporation of additional biomarkers. Assessing activation or exhaustion states—such as co-expression of PD-1 or CD39—has been proposed to better characterize the cytotoxic potential of CD8 + T cells and improve their utility as predictive biomarkers [341, 342]. Moreover, the TME is also enriched with immunosuppressive cell populations, such as MDSCs, Tregs, cancer-associated fibroblasts, M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages, and various immunosuppressive cytokines, including tumor growth factor-β, interleukin-6, et al. These components contribute to an adaptive immune resistance phenotype and were found to be associated with poor response to ICI therapy [330, 343].

Transcriptomic signatures, including tumor antigenicity, T-cell priming and activation, and machine-learning based biomarkers, have shown predictive significance in cancer immunotherapy [344–347]. Apart from utilizing tissue biopsies for detecting tumor mutation and transcriptomic profiles, liquid biopsy has emerged as a significant research focus due to its non-invasive, dynamic, and highly sensitive nature, with increasingly broad applications [348–351]. Additionally, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of the human microbiome which reside across various niches throughout the human body in modulating tumor development and influencing responses to immunotherapy [352–354].

Overall, rather than striving to identify a single “perfect biomarker”, efforts should focus on establishing a comprehensive predictive framework that includes key biomarkers and captures the multifaceted nature of the immune response. Additionally, dynamic monitoring of biomarker fluctuations over time may offer deeper insights into the evolving immunological landscape within the host.

IrAEs development and management

During the treatment of ICIs, the recognition and management of irAEs comprises an essential component of clinic care and a key determinant of final success. These toxicities are closely associated with dysregulated or excessive activation of the immune system and may, in part, result from bystander effects of T cell activation. IrAEs can involve virtually any organ system [355]. Although most irAEs are mild to moderate in severity, fatal toxicities occur in up to 2% of patients in clinical trials [356]. Notably, these fatal irAEs tend to develop early, with a median onset of approximately 15 days following ICI initiation [356]. A particularly high risk of severe and potentially lethal irAEs was observed in thymoma patients treated with anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy, with rates ranging from 38–71.4%, compared to 9–21% in NSCLC and melanoma [357–359]. Grade 3–4 myocarditis and myositis were reported in 42.9% (3/7) [358] and 57.1% (4/7) of thymoma patients [360].

Distinct irAE profiles between PD-(L)1 and CTLA-4 blockade were also revealed. PD-1 mAbs more frequently led to pneumonitis, hypothyroidism, arthralgia, and vitiligo, while CTLA-4 mAbs were more commonly associated with colitis, hypophysitis, and rash [361]. Besides, compared to PD-(L)1 mAbs, CTLA-4 inhibitors were associated with higher rates of grade 3–4 irAEs (31% vs 10%). This discrepancy is thought to stem from the broader T-cell activation induced by CTLA-4 blockade, which affects multiple T-cell subsets [361]. In the dual ICIs treatment, although CTLA-4 plus PD-(L)1 inhibition does not appear to introduce new unexpected irAEs, clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that the incidence and severity of irAEs were significantly higher with anti CTLA-4 plus PD-1 combination therapy compared to either monotherapy [28, 362]. While the safety profile of anti-PD-1 plus LAG-3 seems more favorable [31].

IrAEs are typically managed and guided by severity [363]. For most grade 1 irAEs, ICIs are generally continued with close clinical monitoring. Most grade 2 irAEs warrant ICI treatment suspension and the initiation of glucocorticoid therapy, with potential resumption when symptoms revert ≤ grade 1. Grade 3 irAEs usually require ICI discontinuation and high dose corticosteroid administration which should be tapered over the course of at least 4–6 weeks. Grade 4 irAEs typically recommend permanent discontinuation of ICI treatment. Once irAEs resolve, careful consideration must be given to the potential risks and benefits of ICI rechallenge. Additionally, chronic irAEs and long-term cardiovascular sequelae following ICI therapy as well as the potential impact of ICI therapy on fertility are emerging as clinical challenges [356, 364–368]. These long-lasting toxicities underscore the need for prolonged surveillance and management beyond active treatment periods.

Mechanisms driving ICI resistance and anti-resistance strategies

To date, ICIs remain a cornerstone of modern cancer immunotherapy, with the broadest clinical applications across malignancies. However, despite the remarkable advancements of ICI therapy in recent years, only approximately 20–25% of patients experience long-term benefits [369–372]. Challenges such as the presence of “cold” TMEs in certain cancer types, intrinsic and acquired resistance to ICIs, and the occurrence of irAEs limit the efficacy of ICI therapy [369]. The primary resistance mechanisms include low PD-L1 expression [122, 123], low neoantigen load [311], tumor-intrinsic mutations that disrupt signaling in the interferon-γ or antigen presentation pathway [373–375], the epigenetic properties of tumors [376], et al. Secondary resistance mechanisms include loss of neoantigens, T-cell exhaustion, defects of antigen progressing machinery, increase in immunosuppressive cells, aberrant genomic signaling, changes in PD-L1 expression, co-inhibitory checkpoints, abnormal glycolysis/lipid metabolism, microbiota, DNA methylation, et al. [377, 378].

Apart from the combination of ICIs with other common strategies including chemotherapy [379]/radiotherapy [380]/targeted therapy [381], the challenges above have also driven the development of novel therapeutic approaches, including agents targeting new immune checkpoints as well as innovative modalities like bispecific/trispecific antibodies, ADCs, novel small molecular inhibitors, and noval fusion proteins to enhance T-cell infiltration and activation within the TME [382, 383]. For instance, BPI-371153, an orally bioavailable small molecule that disrupts PD-1/PD-L1 interaction, has demonstrated promising anti-tumor activity in advanced solid tumors and r/r lymphoma [384]. This novel oral agent may offer a more convenient and potentially safer alternative to traditional PD-(L)1 inhibitors. The response of B cell lymphoma to ICI therapy remains limited, recently amulirafusp alfa, a novel fusion protein of CD20 and CD47 binding domain of signal-regulatory protein alpha as well as enhancing the activity of both macrophages and NK cells, demonstrated an ORR of 41.2% (95% CI 18.4–67.1) in patients with follicular lymphoma and 33.3% (95% CI 3.7–71.0) in patients with marginal zone lymphoma in a phase 1/2 study [385], meriting further investigation. Additionally, other strategies such as TILs [386], CAR-T [387], cancer vaccine [388], fecal microbiota transplantation [389] are also under investigation.

Future directions of ICI therapy

The discovery of immune checkpoints and the development of ICIs have revolutionized cancer treatment and substantially improved clinical outcomes across a broad range of malignancies. For the time being, despite extensive research and multiple possible theoretical targets, no ICIs other than anti-PD-(L)1, anti-CTLA-4 and anti-LAG-3 agents have entered clinical practice. The clinical indications for ICIs have progressively expanded from later-line to front-line settings, the next focus of ICI-based clinical research would be the widespread implementation in the perioperative management of early-stage cancers. Besides, another critical mission will be the identification and validation of more reliable predictive biomarkers. Rather than relying on a single indicator, future efforts must prioritize the development of a comprehensive, multi-dimensional biomarker system to improve stratification, optimize patient selection as well as minimize unnecessary exposure to potential toxicities. Furthermore, the emergence of next-generation immunotherapies—such as BsAbs, trispecific antibodies, and ICI therapy plus ADCs—has opened new avenues for enhancing anti-tumor immunity and overcoming primary or acquired resistance to ICIs-based therapy. The future of ICIs lies in their rational integration with these novel agents, forming synergistic combinations that expand therapeutic application, particularly in ICI-refractory or biomarker-negative populations. Of note, as combination regimens may increase the risk of irAEs, real-time monitoring and proactive management of irAEs will be essential to ensure both safety and treatment efficacy.

In summary, the future of ICI therapy will be defined by three pillars: expanding indications into early-stage disease, integrating with next-generation immunotherapies, and advancing biomarker-driven precision medicine, all while maintaining vigilant safety monitoring. These strategic directions not only reflect the current trajectory of immuno-oncology but also point toward a more personalized, effective, and sustainable era of cancer immunotherapy.
