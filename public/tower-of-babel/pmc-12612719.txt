Rapid sequential mixed-method study to identify barriers and explore solutions for improving equitable access to community-based eye care services in Uttar Pradesh, India
Authors: Shalinder Sabherwal, Luke Allen, Mohd Javed, Atanu Mazumdar, Nam Thaker, Malebogo Tlhajoane, Divyanshi Rathore, Aman Singh, Ishaana Sood, Andrew Bastawrous
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn low-income and middle-income countries, significant geographical and socioeconomic inequalities affect access to eye care. This study explores an equity-focused approach to improve access to eye care services provided by a community-based eye care organisation in northern India.DesignA sequential exploratory mixed-method approach.SettingA high-volume eye screening programme in north Indian villages. Individuals identified with eye care needs during the screening were referred to the six nearby primary eye care centres.Participants7578 individuals identified with eye needs through a community-based eye screening programme. Of these, 4431 (58.6%) were women and 3137 (41.4%) were men.InterventionSocioeconomic questions, developed by experts and lay representatives, were integrated into an ongoing digitally supported (Peek Vision) eye screening programme in north Indian villages. Data from referred individuals identified with eye needs were analysed using logistic regression with a mixed-effect model to identify socioeconomic characteristics most strongly associated with poor access to care after referral. A sequential exploratory mixed-method approach, including in-person interviews and follow-up telephonic surveys of individuals with these characteristics, was used.OutcomeTo identify barriers and gather suggestions for improving attendance from groups least likely to attend services.ResultsOf 7627 individuals referred for eye care, 7578 (99.3%) participated in the study. Of those, 2937 (38.5%) attended the Vision Centre, to which they were referred. The least likely to attend were individuals aged >16, those with dependents, and those referred for non-cataract conditions. Among the 3939 individuals with all three characteristics, the attendance rate was 35.3% compared with 42.5% (p<0.001) among the rest. Interviews with this group highlighted transport, personalised counselling, information dissemination, subsidised spectacles and village leader involvement as key suggestions. A telephonic survey with 400 individuals confirmed that the top priorities were free transport, personalised counselling and subsidised spectacles.ConclusionSuggested programme improvements, including better counselling, reminder calls and transportation, could increase access among those least likely to access services after referral. This study demonstrates a two-step approach for identifying solutions from individuals facing the most significant barriers to care. We will go on to conduct trials of these suggested interventions.

Introduction

More than one billion people around the world are currently living with visual impairment, and over 90% of these cases are easily treatable with inexpensive interventions like spectacles and cataract surgery.1 Poor vision impacts quality of life, independence, economic well-being and mobility.13 As one of the most populous countries in the world, India, has an estimated 150 million people with vision loss.4

There are marked geographic and socioeconomic inequalities in the burden of visual impairment and eye health needs within and between countries, and the most disadvantaged groups in society often experience the worst health outcomes.5 A recent nationally representative rapid assessment of avoidable blindness (RAAB) survey in India found over a quarter of those over 50 years were visually impaired, and 2% were blind; women, rural residents and illiterate individuals faced higher risks.6 The UN urges routine data disaggregation by population groups to identify and address inequalities.7 8 Operation guidance notes that many face multiple disadvantages, with a Sri Lanka study showing intersectionality in cataract blindness: female sex, rurality and widowed status are all associated.9

A recent analysis by Allen and colleagues of the principles of Universal Health Coverage and ‘health for all’ noted that it has been challenging to operationalise real-world models that routinely collect and disaggregate health outcomes to identify left behind groups.10 Dr Shroff’s Charity Eye Hospital has been working with a collaboration of partners, including the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine and Peek Vision. They have sought to fill this gap by developing an equity-focused continuous improvement model. This approach can be embedded into routine health programmes to identify population groups left behind, engage with them to identify barriers and potential solutions, and then test these potential solutions using embedded randomised controlled trials (RCT) (figure 1).11 The model was originally developed for community-based eye screening programmes in Kenya, Botswana and Nepal.

The Improvement Studies for Equitable and Evidence-based and Innovation (IM-SEEN model)11 (web only).

This study aimed to embed two stages of the improvement studies for equitable and evidence-based innovation (IM-SEEN) approach into community-based eye screening programmes at Dr Shroff’s Charity Eye Hospital—‘Gather’ stage by incorporating routine sociodemographic data collection into the screening phase to identify which population groups are being left behind, and ‘Engage’ stage by interacting with them to explore potential interventions. The expected outcome was to identify service improvement strategies to be tested in a series of follow-up trials.

In these ongoing programmes, screeners go house-to-house and use smartphones to perform ‘tumbling E’ visual acuity testing with the Peek smartphone visual acuity test (online supplemental figure 1).12 Those who screen positive are referred within the Peek system to the local community vision centre for further assessment and care and tracked to see if they attend. Additionally, automated reminder messages are sent to the patient or carer. However, programme data from the Peek data platform indicate that less than half of those referred attend clinics. By collecting routine sociodemographic data during screening, we aim to identify and engage population groups that are being left behind for possible interventions.

Methods

Setting and context

We focused on the general population served by the door-to-door community-based eye screening programme operated by Shroff’s Charity Eye Hospital (SCEH) in rural central Uttar Pradesh. Agriculture is the primary source of employment, and this area has higher poverty and illiteracy rates than the national average.13 According to an RAAB survey conducted in this region in 2021,14 around 23% of people above 50 years were blind or moderately to severely visually impaired. More than 80% of blindness or severe vision loss was due to cataract. About 35% of moderate visual impairment was due to uncorrected refractive errors. We focused on the populations of administrative blocks served by six vision centres surrounding Mohammadi town—Sahabad, Bhawal Khera, Behjam, Phardhan, Sunderban and Mitauli (figure 2). These were the administrative blocks where the screening programme was being implemented. Screening and referral data from all these blocks were included.

Location of the programme within which this study was incorporated (web only). The original maps were sourced from the website https://www.mapsofindia.com/ and subsequently modified.

Each centre has a catchment population of approximately 50 000 to 70 000 people. Each centre employs a community health worker who conducts door-to-door screening within the community. These screeners reach approximately 1500 individuals each month aged over 5 years. Guided screening, which includes basic visual eye inspection, is performed using a torch light or phone light, and visual acuity is assessed using the ‘Peek smartphone visual acuity test’. Screening is free, and all individuals with eye problems are referred to their local vision centre for further free assessment. The only exclusion criterion for screening is being under 5 years of age. Approximately 15% of individuals screen positive and are referred to their local vision centre. The furthest any person would have to travel to reach their nearest facility is about 10 km, representing an auto rickshaw journey with a cost of approximately INR 100 (US$ 1.2). Cataract operations are performed at no cost, and spectacles and eye drops are sold at a subsidised rate. Vision centres also offer private services for wealthier clients that cross-subsidise care for poorer clients.

Gathering sociodemographic data

Peek processed the data included in this study according to the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Peek stores the data on a secure server within India, and transmission and processing are carried out via encrypted devices.

Establishing which domains to use

In the standard programme, when a person is screened using the Peek app and found to have an eye care need, their name, age, sex, language (Hindi/English), village, contact number and visual acuity is entered into and recorded in the app, along with referral status (yes/no). The participants are given an appointment date and the location of the local vision centre. When they arrive, the receptionist checks them in using the same Peek software. This means that the Peek platform holds a complete record of every person who has/has not attended, along with their basic demographic data. These data are currently used to assess whether attendance varies according to age, sex or village. Before our study, no data were collected on income, education, occupation or other important sociodemographic domains.

The details of the methodology followed are included in a published protocol.15 For the first phase of this study, we used an iterative multistakeholder deliberative process to identify the most appropriate set of additional socioeconomic questions to embed within the screening programme to identify population groups that are being ‘left behind’. Our review group included lay representatives, community screeners, a health economist, a programme manager, the director of public health at Dr Shroff’s Charity Eye Hospital, the head of continuous improvement at Peek Vision and a public health and primary care expert from LSHTM. Meetings were conducted virtually, with further asynchronous iterative revision conducted via email.

We started with a long list of 16 variables recommended by WHO, World Bank, the Cochrane Equity Group and the Lancet Global Health Global Eye Health Commission, which were identified in a previous literature review.16 After two rounds of iteration, our first draft variable list included age, sex, education, occupation (including farm ownership and farm size), assets (ownership of smart/basic phone and types of transportation), religion, household monthly income, number of dependents, disability status and marital status (online supplemental table S1).

Piloting the new domains

We piloted the new domains with a draft survey incorporated into an existing screening programme in Uttar Pradesh. Over 2 weeks, we gathered data from a convenience sample of 337 consenting adults. Then, we convened a multistakeholder meeting to review the data and feedback from the survey administrators.

We agreed to supplement an item on personal education with an additional question on the highest level of education in the household—and the same for occupation. This was based on the observation that some housewives and other groups can have relatively low levels of personal education/low status occupation but live in affluent households with high levels of power, privilege and access. We also agreed to change the income question to use three broad household income categories, which would be easier for people to answer. Also, as most respondents were in the INR 5000–10 000 category of monthly income, we decided to include a subthreshold of INR 7000 to provide more granular information. The group felt that asking about religion was too culturally contentious, even though this was likely to be an important predictor of access to care. Given that religion can be inferred from family names with a high degree of accuracy, we decided to document likely religion using this information rather than asking directly, accepting the reliability limitation. The full details of refining the survey questions are provided in the online supplemental tables S1 and S2. The final items are presented in table 1. This additional information was collected only from people referred after being screened.

Identifying groups with the worst access to care

We used logistic regression to calculate the odds of attendance for each sociodemographic subgroup, for example, men, women, married, unmarried, etc. Our sample size calculation was driven by discussions around the acceptable margin of error while estimating our primary outcome variable-access rate. Estimating the access rate with a 95% CI of ±5% yields a sample size of 385 under the assumption of maximum variance. We also wanted to estimate the access rate for each socioeconomic stratum independently with the same accuracy; hence, our actual sample size was much larger to ensure this minimum number of observations within each stratum to make it statistically valid for estimation. No statistical software was used; the calculation was done manually in MS-Excel using the standard formula for estimating a population proportion.

The most recent Indian Demographic and Health Survey (2019–2021)17 presents urban/rural data on domains such as religion, caste/tribe, wealth quintile and education level. The smallest subgroup for wealth and education contained approximately 8% of the total sampled rural population. Assuming that our smallest subdomain includes 385 people and represents 8% of the total sampled population, we would have needed an overall sample size of 385/0.08=4813 people. We used a minimum sample size of 5000, representing approximately 10% of the catchment population.

This enabled us to estimate the access rates for any socioeconomic stratum in the entire population with a 95% CI of ±5% if the stratum contained at least 7.7% of the total population. For smaller subgroups, the sample size was logistically challenging, and we would have to accept a wider CI.

Once we obtained sociodemographic data, we analysed each variable included in the study without any adjustment first, to assess if any of these factors apparently influenced the attendance compliance following the ocular diagnosis. All the factor variables were categorical, and we reported their frequency and percentage distributions across two categories: those who attended and those who did not. We used the χ2 test to check the significance of the difference between the two groups.

Regression was carried out including all factors as independent variables and attendance as the outcome. We ran a mixed effect model including the region variable (screening site) as a random effect. The education, income, distance and vision test result categories were declared ordinal in the multivariate regression. Statistical analysis has been performed using R V.4.3.2.

In the second stage, we aimed to collect barriers and suggestions from individuals across the greatest number of subgroups who were less likely to attend services after being referred. All variables significant at the 5% level in the multivariable model were naturally included in the second-stage stratification. However, we also considered including additional variables with p values <0.15 to slightly expand the sampling base in the second stage, allowing potentially relevant variables to be retained for further exploration.

Rapidly exploring barriers and potential solutions

Once we had identified the sociodemographic group that was least likely to access community eye services (those considered to be the ‘left-behind’ group), we engaged with representatives from this group to explore their perceptions of the barriers and what could be done to improve equitable access. We used sequential exploratory mixed-methods approach (figure 3) to move from rich experiential data to generalisable findings that could be applied at scale.15

Sequential mixed-method approach followed15 (web only).

First, we generated a list of non-attenders from the left-behind group. We used this list to identify a maximum variation sample for qualitative interviews. Two early-career researchers visited each household and performed semistructured interviews in Hindi, which were audio recorded. A senior qualitative researcher provided initial training, supervised the first five interviews and debriefed the interviewers.

After providing informed consent, each participant was asked about the issues that had prevented them from attending and how they thought the programme could change to enable them and people like them to access care. We continued recruiting participants until no new barriers or suggestions emerged from three interviews in a row, with a minimum of 18 interviews in total.

Analysis of the interviews

Each day, the interviewers listened back to the interview recordings and entered direct quotes into a deductive framework matrix, using 10 broad themes derived from the literature.16 The matrix also included room for inductive identification of new themes that did not fit with a prior heading. A senior qualitative researcher reviewed the matrix daily to check the data entry quality and assess thematic saturation.

Feasibility review

Once no new themes emerged from three interviews in a row, all barriers and solutions were extracted from the matrix and entered into a summary document. The programme lead reviewed this document to identify any suggestions that were wildly unfeasible. The chief executive officer and lead researcher at Peek Vision independently reviewed these decisions.

Ranking the suggested solutions

Using the same master list, we conducted telephone surveys with a representative sample of 400 non-attenders from the left-behind group. We used computer-generated random numbers to sequence the calls. After recorded verbal consent had been provided, respondents were asked to rank each potential from 1 to 3 in terms of its likely impact. Calls were conducted in Hindi.

The sample size was calculated using a 95% CI, 5% margin of error and a population of 1 million. The research team used a script to complete the surveys and entered scores into an online data entry sheet. We calculated the mean score for each suggestion and then produced a ranked list.

Selection of interventions to implement and test

As the final step, we conducted a hybrid multistakeholder workshop following the protocol.15 The workshop was attended by the implementing team, a representative from the implementing organisation, a member of our community advisory board, elected leaders from three villages and two representatives from the left-behind group. After our team presented the ranked list, the workshop participants discussed the likely impact, cost, feasibility and risk of each suggestion. The discussion of a particular suggestion was only concluded once consensus was reached through open discussion.

Patients and public involvement

Patients and the public were involved in this study while designing the socioeconomic questions included in the screening. The results were disseminated to stakeholders from the same community, and they were also involved in planning the interventions for the next stage of this work.

Results

Timeline and referral cascade

Data collection started on 9 October 2023 and concluded on 18 January 2024. Forty-five thousand two hundred eighty-six people were screened in 50 villages; 7627 (16.8%) were referred. Of those referred, 2937 (38.5%) attended their local clinic.

Identifying the left-behind group

Faster-than-expected screening meant that screeners collected sociodemographic data from 7578 people rather than the originally planned 5000. Of these people, 58.6% were female, 61% were below the age of 55 and 74.3% had a primary-grade education. We obtained around 99% responses for each question. For the vision test results, data were missing for 16 (0.2%) of the referred individuals, and for the income categories, data were missing for 108 (1.4%) individuals. No data were missing from other sociodemographic or clinical groups. For further analyses of individuals in these sociodemographic or clinical groups, those with missing data were excluded.

Association between attendance and different sociodemographic and clinical factors after logistic regression

We were pleasantly surprised to find that there were no significant disparities in access between many groups, with broadly equivalent access rates across sex, education and occupation, that is, the existing programme is already reaching people from different backgrounds relatively equally. The downside is that access was <50% for every subgroup.

In the adjusted analysis, we found trends suggesting that those aged >16 years, middle-earners, phone owners and those with at least one dependent may be less likely to access care. Given our equity focus on minoritised and vulnerable groups, we decided to focus our qualitative work on adults (>16 years) with one or more dependants, rather than people who owned their own phones and were not on low incomes, reasoning that people from these more privileged groups were less likely to miss their appointments due to structural barriers or lack of resources. Given the clinical findings, we also focused our analysis on those with non-cataract conditions and normal-looking eyes on visual inspection, as these people were 36% less likely to attend. Among those referred for cataract, 46.8% attended, compared with 35.7% of the rest. There were 3939 individuals (54.9%) aged over 16 with at least one dependent in the family and non-cataract ocular morbidities, of whom 35.3% (1392) attended. In contrast, among the remaining 3639 individuals, the attendance rate was higher at 42.5% (1545). The difference in attendance rates between the two groups was statistically significant (p<0.001).

Interview findings

We identified 2547 people aged >16 years who had one or more dependents and had been referred with normal-looking eyes for non-cataract conditions. We selected the people to be interviewed using random sampling. We interviewed 37 of these people before reaching thematic saturation. Only one person out of 38 people called declined to participate. Five of the 19 suggestions were identified as being unfeasible by the programme manager and independent reviewer: offering gifts to people at vision centres for attending, providing free glasses to everyone, provide entire treatment at the doorstep, calls only by male candidates and conducting rescreening for those who did not attend. The 14 remaining suggestions were taken forward to the telephone survey.

Telephone survey findings

We used the same list of non-attenders from the left behind group to complete the telephone survey. We completed 400 telephone surveys, having attempted to contact 1270 people in total. These participants were also selected using random sampling. (854 people could not be reached and 16 people declined to participate.) Of all the participants interviewed, in-person or telephonically, 57% had their own phone and 53% had an income between 7 and 10 000. Thus, there was an adequate representation of people from these categories, which we initially decided not to focus on while shortlisting categories for interviewing. The 400 participants ranked each of the 14 suggestions from 1 to 3 on a Likert scale. Only one of the suggestions received a mean score of below 2, and 71% of the suggestions received mean scores >2.5, indicating that participants felt that all but one of the suggestions were fairly-highly likely to improve access to care. Suggested interventions with scores and feedback from the final stakeholder workshop

After the survey had been completed, each of the ranked suggestions was discussed at the multistakeholder workshop in terms of likely impact, harm, costs and feasibility. No obvious risks of harm were identified for any of the suggestions. Two of the suggested solutions were already being implemented (providing high-quality treatment and ensuring that vision centres are not closed unannounced). Three potential solutions were felt to be too expensive or infeasible (providing subsidised glasses, following up non-attenders in-person for counselling and reminding each person before the day of appointment)

Community representatives felt that transport interventions should only be offered to take non-attenders to catch-up appointments, rather than being offered to everyone. Community representatives felt that reminders and follow-up calls would only work if they were relatively lengthy and personalised conversations, rather than short and generic calls.

Having identified eight potentially feasible interventions, we ended by holding a second workshop focused on identifying the best means of testing the most promising interventions in future trials. This workshop was led by the statisticians and the Peek team. We agreed to start by focusing on the two most highly ranked solutions.

Discussion

Our analysis of the community eye health programme data showed that of approximately 7500 people referred to the local vision centre after screening less than 40% of people attended. This is similar to the attendance rate shown in a similar eye screening programme in Kenya, where the mean attendance rate is around 40%,18 and not very different from the attendance of 48% reported in a US vision screening programme.19 In our study, we analysed the sociodemographic factors of those who were least likely to attend, and found that people with one or more dependent, non-cataract conditions with normal distance vision, not having a mobile phone and with an income between 7000 and 10 000 INR were the least likely to attend. In the national RAAB survey conducted in India, multivariate logistic regression found that people who were illiterate were over five times more likely to be blind than those who had completed 10 years of education (adjusted OR: 5.6, 95% CI 3.6 to 8.8). Women and those living in rural areas were also found to have higher rates of blindness.6 However, our entire study population was rural, and literacy and gender were not found to influence attendance significantly.

A previous equity analysis conducted in Sri Lanka and Nigeria found that rural female widows were the least likely to access cataract services.9 A previous IM-SEEN analysis conducted in peri-urban central Kenya found that younger working men were less likely to attend after referral from a community-based screening programme.19 In our study also, children and adults older than 55 years of age had better attendance than the working age group. However, gender was not found to be a factor associated with non-attendance in our study population.

In a previous study from south India, it was found that people who were literate or had cataract and/or severe vision loss were more likely to attend.20 However, in another study by Padhy et al, people with visual impairment due to causes other than cataract were found to be more likely to attend.21 In our context, we believe that individuals detected with cataract could be explained a very clear-cut referral and management pathway by the screeners as compared with those referred with other issues where the exact diagnosis could be made only at triage and screeners would have offered limited awareness regarding the probable condition and future course of management. This could have led to lower attendance among individuals referred for conditions other than cataract.

Having more dependents could have been an issue due to availability of time or economic constraint. Those with income <INR 7000 (around $85) may not have had many alternatives to the free treatment being offered as compared with those with the next levels of income who may have had alternatives and paucity of time due to their involvement in their occupational work.

Barriers included difficulty in arranging transport to the vision centres, lack of clear messaging about reason for referral and the referral pathway, being busy with other activities (low felt need) and cost and perceived cost of glasses and other treatment. Among these, lack of felt need and cost of treatment align with previous research from India.20 21 Although lack of an escort did not emerge directly as an important barrier in these studies, this was found to be an important issue in a southern Indian study led by Marmamula.22 Another study conducted in residential care homes for the elderly in south India found that lack of family consent was a major issue, but again, that did not emerge from our community-based population.23 Lack of affordable and safe public transport came out as a barrier even in an urban context when the elderly residing in the periphery of a metropolitan city in south India were interviewed.24

One of the strengths of our study is that the IM-SEEN model11 has been implemented in India for the first time. Unlike most earlier studies conducted to identify barriers to access in India,2024 the barriers and suggestions were gathered from people with documented clinical needs who had not been able to access care. We focused our interviews and surveys on groups most likely to face structural barriers to care. We also engaged with a wide range of local stakeholders throughout the project and concluded with specific recommendations for follow-up trials that could test the patient-derived solutions in future work.

All the possible solutions which were rated by the telephonic interviews, except for one regarding conveying the operational time of the vision centres via SMS, scored highly (2.24–2.95 on a scale of 1 to 3). These high scores conveyed that most of the suggestions collected through in-person interviews were thought to be very likely to improve uptake of services. This participatory approach for generating solution is a strength of our study; however, the specific solutions shortlisted may have been very regional in their relevance.

The two themes that we have shortlisted for testing are personalised reminder calls along with counselling for those who miss their appointment and organised transport from villages. While the first can be useful to tackle the barrier of frequently highlighted low ‘felt need’2123 the latter can be useful for those who experience lack of transport as a barrier.21 22 In one of the arms for the trial for transport, we plan to provide free organised transport, which could be useful for reducing the indirect cost, which has also been mentioned as a barrier previously.24 The suggestion for organised transport (not necessarily free) implies that companionship is perceived as important by the rural community in the study region.

While in the first phase of the study, we received approximately 99% response rate to all our socio-demographic and clinical questions, one limitation of our study is that we achieved a sample size of 400 individuals to be interviewed by phone after attempting to contact 1270 randomly selected individuals from a list of 2547 people in our target group who were less likely to access services. This resulted in a low response rate. However, since all individuals on the list shared characteristics that made them less likely to attend, this cohort of 400 respondents should still be representative.

After gathering and scoring the suggestions, a consensus was reached in a workshop to identify the top-ranked solutions for the next set of trials. This process followed the study protocol.15 However, group dynamics influenced by the diversity of stakeholders present in the workshop may have affected the outcome. We believe that such influence was kept reasonably in check by discussing each suggestion strictly within the agreed themes of impact, cost, feasibility and risk.

One potential criticism of this methodology is that service providers are made completely responsible for improving access and this may bring about interventions that may not be sustainable and may hamper health-seeking behaviour in long term. The issue of sustainability of the intervention was taken care by including the decision-makers from the ongoing programme in the final stakeholder workshop. Some potentially effective suggestions may have been overlooked due to low rating by the study participants. However, we believe that the two-step approach of gathering solutions was effective in gathering perceptions of a representative population among those who faced difficulty in attending.

Conclusion

The in-person interviews, followed by telephonic surveys conducted among people belonging to groups least likely to attend, revealed 14 potential solutions to improve the uptake of referrals in that group. These solutions mainly focused on facilitating transport from villages, providing better counselling at the time of referral and sending reminders after missed appointments, either in person or through a personalised call. We involved laypeople, local leaders, service providers and funders in the final decision-making for shortlisting interventions for trials. A similar two-step approach to identifying solutions from those facing the most significant barriers to care can be implemented in other community-based health screening programmes
