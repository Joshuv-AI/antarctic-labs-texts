Biomechanical analysis of a novel, triangular spinal fixation system
Authors: Jacob Mazza, Jill Serbousek, Richard G. Fessler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPedicle screw fixation is a mainstay surgical technique for providing posterior stability to the spinal column, and consists of screws, rods and set caps. These components are prone to failure in various ways, but developing fixation systems with fewer components at risk of malfunction may limit construct failures. Utilizing a rod-less, triangular design, the novel system is intended to optimize construct strength while minimizing points of failure. This study aims to characterize the biomechanical performance of a novel posterior spinal instrumentation construct compared to traditional pedicle screw fixation.MethodsThe ASTM (American Society for Testing and Materials) F1717 protocol for static compression bending, static torsion, and dynamic compression bending was followed. Biomechanical data for six (6) novel constructs were collected and compared to three (3) predicate traditional pedicle screw and rod constructs. Testing was performed to failure for each biomechanical test, and all mechanisms of failure were recorded.ResultsIn static compression bending, the average stiffness and displacement across all novel systems tested were 111±9.2 N/m and 4.2±0.5 mm, respectively as compared to predicate constructs which were 50±2 N/m and 18±6 mm, respectively. Static torsion testing showed that the novel systems, on average, required 21±1.7 N/m to rotate 1°—four times greater force than predicate systems. In dynamic compression bending, four novel constructs successfully reached runout at 5,000,000 cycles with a maximum applied force of 250 N. Predicate constructs were not able to tolerate loads greater than 160 N.ConclusionsThe novel spine fixation system demonstrated superior biomechanical performance compared with predicate constructs in all testing completed. The novel construct was stiffer, displaced less, and required approximately four times as much torque to rotate the construct by a single degree compared with the predicate pedicle screw construct. The applied forces achieved at runout were substantially greater than predicate systems with less intraconstruct motion.

Introduction

Pedicle screw and rod constructs remain foundational in posterior lumbar fixation, providing immediate segmental stability and promoting arthrodesis across fused levels [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite their widespread use, these systems are susceptible to several hardware-related failure mechanisms [4,5]. Analysis of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database reveals that among more than 17,000 reported thoracolumbar instrumentation failures, nearly half involved pedicle screws or set caps. In a random sample of 500 cases, 48% of failures were attributed to fracture or breakage, 45% to migration or detachment (including set cap disengagement), and 5% to deformation of the construct components. These findings underscore the need for spinal fixation systems that reduce the number of vulnerable interfaces and improve overall construct reliability.

Construct performance is also influenced by patient-specific factors. Osteoporosis remains one of the strongest predictors of hardware loosening and mechanical failure, as diminished bone mineral density compromises screw purchase and load transfer [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Additional contributors include the extent of degenerative disease, tobacco use and systemic conditions that impair bone quality or healing [3,[8], [9], [10]]. Technical factors, particularly pedicle screw trajectory, also influence fixation strength. Trajectories designed to maximize contact with dense cortical bone, such as the cortical bone trajectory (CBT), have demonstrated enhanced pull-out strength and improved overall construct stiffness compared with traditional transpedicular screws [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. These biological and technical considerations highlight the importance of both optimizing screw-bone interface mechanics and minimizing construct complexity.

The novel construct is a newly developed lumbar fixation system designed to address these limitations through geometric and component-level simplification. The system uses a compact, rod-less, three-piece configuration consisting of a cranial “halo screw" (a screw with an integrated plate), a caudal “sphere screw” and a single locking set cap (Fig. 1). The sphere screw seats within the halo screw head, forming a rigid triangular construct between adjacent pedicles. Both screws utilize a CBT-like trajectory, with the caudal sphere screw taking a trans-facet trajectory to the caudal pedicle (Fig. 2). By eliminating the longitudinal rod and its corresponding set cap interfaces, the novel system removes common sites of mechanical failure and reduces opportunities for stress concentration and loosening [4,5,13]. The “neck” of the halo screw is modified to be thicker due to the lack of the need to accommodate a universal tulip, allowing for increased strength. The triangular configuration, paired with cortical bone-engaging screw trajectories, is intended to enhance stiffness and load distribution while reducing the number of components required for stabilization. In concept, the novel construct leverages fundamental geometric principles—namely, the inherent rigidity of triangles—to maximize strength while minimizing failure-prone interfaces. Methods

Construct assembly

Each novel construct consisted of three (3) components: one (1) 5.5×40 mm halo screw, one (1) 5.5×45 mm sphere screw and one (1) set screw. Each tested construct consisted of bilateral device placement, for a total of six (6) components; two (2) each of the aforementioned constructs. The constructs were assembled at Empirical Technologies, Colorado Springs, CO (part of Applied Technical Services [ATS]) for biomechanical laboratory testing.

Biomechanical testing of the novel construct was conducted following ASTM F1717, a protocol describing various test methods using a vertebrectomy model to assess the biomechanical characteristics of spinal constructs. The assembled novel constructs were placed in ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) test blocks for static compression bending testing, static torsion testing and dynamic compression bending testing (to estimate the maximum runout force value at 5,000,000 cycles). Set screws were torqued to 80 in-lb using a calibrated Empirical Technologies provided torque wrench (TW008). Predicate systems were constructed in UHMWPE test blocks for static torsion and dynamic compression bending testing, or in stainless steel test blocks for static compression bending testing (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Untested novel constructs in UHMWPE blocks in the top-down (left panel, top row), side (left panel, middle row) and front-back (left panel, bottom row) views; untested predicate constructs in both UHMWPE (right panel, top) and stainless steel (right panel, bottom).Fig 3: dummy alt text

Untested novel constructs in UHMWPE blocks in the top-down (left panel, top row), side (left panel, middle row) and front-back (left panel, bottom row) views; untested predicate constructs in both UHMWPE (right panel, top) and stainless steel (right panel, bottom).

The sphere screw was inserted into the inferior test block such that a 12 mm gap between the test block and surface of the underside of the screw head existed. The halo screw was inserted into the superior test block such that there was a 5 mm gap from the test block surface to the underside of the screw head (Fig. 3). The gap between the UHMWPE blocks simulates a vertebrectomy model, considered the worst-case scenario [16]. Compared with cadaveric models, these test blocks control for variability in bone density and provide a reproducible way to measure biomechanical properties of the fixation systems.

Surgical placement included AP fluoroscopy using a bulls-eye trajectory. A Jamshidi needle is positioned in the pedicle and a K-wire is advanced into the vertebral body under lateral fluoroscopy. The pedicle is tapped over the wire, then the halo screw is placed to approximately one-half to two-thirds of vertebral body depth. The sphere screw trajectory is confirmed on AP and lateral fluoroscopy, a K-wire is advanced into the caudal pedicle without violating the disc space, the tract is tapped, and the sphere screw is inserted over the wire. The set screw is then inserted and tightened; the K-wire is removed before final seating. Fig. 1, Fig. 2 illustrate the novel construct components and CBT/trans-facet trajectories.

Deviations from ASTM F1717

Section 6.2 of ASTM F1717 states that a mounting plate for one set of side support plates should be free to rotate about the Z axis for the compression bending and fatigue tests. Empirical’s default is to have both mounting plates constrained in rotation about the Z-axis. Section 6.6, 6.11 and Fig. 2 of ASTM F1717 states that the L, X, and Y dimensions for a lumbar device should be 76 mm, 40 mm, and 20 mm, respectively. Due to the nature of the device, the longitudinal element was 10.93 mm, xsuperior = 37.3 mm, xinferior = 38.4 mm, ysuperior = 21.3 mm, yinferior = 20.4 mm. The predicate construct test implants were modified from the design in ASTM F1717 to approximate a single-level implantation. The longitudinal distance between the central axes of the implant screws was 38±0.5 mm, which is a deviation from the recommended 76±0.5 mm outlined in the ASTM F1717 protocol. The ASTM F1717 protocol outlined the following tests, all of which were completed with the novel and predicate system constructs.

Testing modalities

Six (6) novel and three (3) predicate constructs were tested in static compression bending. Outcome data for all constructs included linear fit offset (millimeter [mm]), displacement at 2% offset yield (mm), compressive bending yield load (Newton [N]), elastic displacement (mm), compressive bending stiffness (N/mm), compressive bending ultimate displacement (mm), compressive bending ultimate load (N), compressive bending yield moment (Newton-meter [N-m]), and compressive bending ultimate moment (N-m). Failure mode was recorded. Static compression bending testing was performed in displacement control at a rate of 12 mm/minute for novel constructs and at 25 mm/minute for predicate constructs. Load and displacement data were recorded by the test system software. The ramp waveform was performed until test block contract occurred. An example testing configuration is shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Example testing configuration for static compression bending.Fig 4: dummy alt text

Example testing configuration for static compression bending.

Six (6) novel and three (3) predicate constructs were tested in static torsion. Outcome data for all constructs included linear fit offset (°), elastic angular displacement (°), angular displacement at 2% offset yield (°), yield torque (N-m), and torsional stiffness (N-m/°). Failure mode was recorded. Static torsion tests were performed in angular displacement control at a rate of 60°/minute, with a static axial compression preload of 0 N (control). Torque and angular displacement data were recorded by the test system software. The test was performed in the clockwise direction when looking down on the test setup. Testing for all specimens was stopped once the construct reached at least 90 N-m.

Six (6) novel and three (3) predicate constructs were tested in dynamic compression bending. A cyclic load at a constant frequency of 5 Hz was applied to each specimen. Data was recorded by system software. Loads were maintained with a constant sinusoidal load amplitude and a load ratio (R=min/max) of 10. Testing was terminated when the specimen reached the endurance value of 5,000,000 cycles or experienced failure, defined as a fracture visible under a microscope at 10x magnification or a maximum displacement of 10 mm from the initial displacement at the minimum desired load level. The distance between hinge-pin centerlines was measured on the left and right sides pretesting for all specimens and post-testing for runout specimens only. Failure mode and the corresponding cycle count were recorded.

Statistical analysis included calculation of mean and standard deviation for all variables.

Discussion

Failure of pedicle screw instrumentation remains a major challenge in spinal surgery, particularly in patients with compromised bone quality and in long-segment constructs [4,6,9]. Traditional rod-and-screw systems, while the clinical standard, are mechanically vulnerable at the screw-rod interface and tulip-set cap interfaces and rely heavily on cancellous bone—the first to degrade in osteoporosis—for purchase [6,9]. Instrumentation complications such as screw loosening, rod fracture and set cap disengagement are well documented, with rates ranging from <1% in short constructs to >20% in complex deformity cases [4,17]. Adjuncts such as cement augmentation, fenestrated screws and multirod or cross-link constructs can improve strength but also increase complexity and cost while maintaining the same rectangular geometry. The novel system was designed to address these limitations by replacing the traditional “rectangular” rod construct with a compact, rod-less triangular frame using CBT-like screw paths. In this study, the novel construct showed superior performance in static compression bending, static torsion and dynamic compression bending compared with the predicate (rod-and-screw) system, suggesting a mechanically robust alternative for posterior lumbar fixation. While the novel construct reduces component interfaces, hardware-related complications such as loosening or implant failure can still occur and should be considered in clinical use.

Static compression bending

Lumbar spinal instrumentation must tolerate complex multiplanar loads, with axial compression as a predominant vector generated by body weight and muscle forces [18]. The ASTM F1717 vertebrectomy model isolates posterior instrumentation under worst-case conditions where the construct alone spans a defect, allowing standardized comparison of stiffness, ultimate load, and displacement at failure [18,19]. In this configuration, the novel system exhibited markedly greater static compression bending stiffness (111 N/m) than the predicate construct (Fig. 10). The novel system also had substantially less displacement under comparable loads, displacing less than one quarter as much as the predicate system (Fig. 11).Fig. 10Comparison of static compression bending stiffness between various spinal instrumentation systems.Fig 10: dummy alt textFig. 11Comparison of static compression bending ultimate displacement between various spinal instrumentation systems.Fig 11: dummy alt text

These findings highlight the importance of construct geometry. Conventional bilateral pedicle screw systems function as a four-bar linkage (a rectangle) that is prone to “racking” unless cross-linked [20]. The novel construct, in contrast, forms a closed triangular frame between superior and inferior pedicles. Because the internal angles of a triangle cannot change without altering side length, this configuration is inherently more resistant to deformation. The resulting increase in stiffness and reduction in micromotion at the fusion or fracture site may improve the mechanical environment for arthrodesis, provided stiffness does not become so high as to induce stress shielding [21].

Static torsion

Static torsion testing simulates rotational loads during twisting and combined motions, which are clinically relevant mechanisms of hardware loosening and failure [18,19]. The novel construct demonstrated a torsional stiffness of 20 (N-m/°)– approximately four times that of the predicate rod construct [5 (N-m/°)]—and higher yield torque (Fig. 12, Fig. 13). This means it takes four times the force to rotate the novel construct by one degree, as compared to traditional pedicle screw-and-rod geometries. Two design features likely account for this. First, the novel system’s CBT-like trajectories engage the pars interarticularis and dense cortical bone along the pedicle walls and posterior vertebral body. CBT has been shown to produce higher insertion torque, greater pull-out strength, and improved resistance to toggling compared with traditional trajectories that rely more on cancellous bone, including in osteoporotic conditions [2,11,13,22]. Second, eliminating the rod and tulip removes a common rotational weak point and stress concentrator. In conventional systems, rotation and early deformation often originate at the rod–tulip interface, where the rod can move before full set-cap engagement and where stress is concentrated [[23], [24], [25]]. Dynamic compression bending

Fatigue failure is a central concern for spinal implants, which must remain stable over the months required for solid fusion. This has often been approximated as one million cycles of physiologic loading [26,27]. In ASTM F1717, dynamic compression bending at high cycle counts (up to 5,000,000 cycles) is used to interrogate implant endurance under repetitive axial loading [18,19]. In our study, four novel constructs reached 5,000,000 cycles at maximum loads of 250 N without failure (Table 3). The predicate system experienced more frequent rod-screw interface failures at lower loads and cycle counts, reaching runout at 5,000,000 cycles only once and at a load of 160 N.

These findings suggest that the novel construct has a higher fatigue endurance limit under worst-case conditions. The results are consistent with prior failure analyses showing that rod-based constructs suffer from fatigue fractures commonly initiated at the rod-set cap interface, often described as a “notch effect” occurring at the tulip-rod junction [[23], [24], [25]]. Eliminating the rod and using a compression-locked ball-and-socket-style joint allows the novel construct to reduce these notch-driven stress points, shifting fatigue limits toward the intrinsic strength of the screws and overall construct geometry.

Wolff’s law and clinical implications

Wolff’s law and bone physiology demonstrate that bone remodels in response to mechanical loading and that physiologic strain promotes bone formation. Under-loading or excessive stress may impair bone quality and fusion, and can lead to unwanted bone resorption [28,29]. Spinal instrumentation must therefore balance sufficient rigidity to prevent pseudoarthrosis with enough load sharing to avoid stress shielding and promote adequate arthrodesis [21,[28], [29], [30]].

Although the novel construct is stiffer than the predicate construct, its CBT-like trajectories may help preserve favorable load conditions. Engaging the vertebral bone cortex directs load through high-density bone rather than the cancellous core [2,[11], [12], [13],22]. Finite element analyses (FEA) have suggested that CBT-based instrumentation can achieve higher rigidity while maintaining physiologic strain patterns in the adjacent bone, reducing unwanted resorption seen with overly rigid constructs [12]. The novel system’s triangular frame and cortical screw trajectories may provide strong stabilization while permitting physiologic load sharing through interbody grafts and the vertebral bodies, potentially providing an optimal “middle ground” between construct strength and fusion potential. While the present study cannot directly address fusion biology specifically, its findings are consistent with prior work emphasizing that successful spinal arthrodesis depends on a construct’s ability to stabilize without completely shielding bone from load or inducing supra-physiologic loads [12,21,[28], [29], [30]].

Limitations

All testing was performed according to the ASTM F1717 vertebrectomy model using UHMWPE blocks to represent the vertebra. This model is intentionally a worst-case scenario without anterior column support and lacks representative anisotropy, heterogeneity and variability in physiologic parameters such as bone mineral density. Similarly, it does not model the bone-screw interface well, which is an important consideration when thinking about clinically relevant implant loosening that often is attributed to microfractures at the bone screw interface [10]. The biomechanical testing was performed on a single-level construct model with a single screw size for the novel system tested. In practice, varying screw size (length and diameter), multilevel constructs, and patient-specific alignment all factor into the mechanical picture, with stress distributions varying from patient to patient [18,19,21]. The human spine is subject to more complex loading than represented by the tests performed, and as such these experiments do not fully characterize the in-vivo behavior. Lastly, this is purely a biomechanical study. Factors such as preoperative considerations, operative technique and clinical outcomes—including rates of fusion, adjacent segment degeneration and patient-reported outcomes are of paramount importance when evaluating spinal fixation and fusion. Future work including dynamic cadaveric testing with supporting FEA models and prospective clinical studies would be helpful adjuncts in understanding the benefits and limitations of the novel spinal system.

Traditional pedicle screw and rod constructs have remained the backbone of spinal fixation for decades because of their versatility across cervical, thoracic, and lumbar levels, proven efficacy in multilevel constructs, and extensive long-term clinical data. The purpose of this novel system is not to replace them, but to serve as an additional option in the surgical armamentarium. Where the novel system may offer advantage is in patients with single-level lumbar pathology requiring fixation and fusion, where its rod-less triangular design provides enhanced biomechanical performance across multiple metrics compared to the traditional construct. Its compact, three-piece configuration is compatible with both open and minimally invasive surgical approaches, and its simplified design may lend itself to expanded use in ambulatory surgical settings, though prospective clinical data will be needed to validate these potential benefits.

Conclusion

Within the ASTM F1717 vertebrectomy model, the novel spinal fixation system demonstrated superior static compression, torsional and dynamic fatigue performance compared with a traditional pedicle screw-and-rod construct. The rod-less, triangular design and CBT-like trajectories of the screws allow the novel construct to achieve high stiffness and fatigue resistance in a construct that minimizes common failure points by reducing system components. This data supports the novel system as a robust spinal fixation system for posterior lumbar instrumentation with the potential to provide increased longevity and fewer instrumentation-related complications.
