Pre-pregnancy body mass index range and prevalence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy: the Japan environment and children’s study
Authors: Tsuyoshi Murata, Karin Imaizumi, Hirotaka Isogami, Toma Fukuda, Hyo Kyozuka, Shun Yasuda, Akiko Yamaguchi, Akiko Sato, Yuka Ogata, Kosei Shinoki, Mitsuaki Hosoya, Koichi Hashimoto, Hidekazu Nishigori, Seiji Yasumura, Keiya Fujimori, Michihiro Kamijima, Michihiro Kamijima, Shin Yamazaki, Yukihiro Ohya, Reiko Kishi, Nobuo Yaegashi, Chisato Mori, Shuichi Ito, Zentaro Yamagata, Hidekuni Inadera, Takeo Nakayama, Hiroyasu Iso, Masayuki Shima, Hiroshige Nakamura, Narufumi Suganuma, Koichi Kusuhara, Takahiko Katoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNo consensus has been established for the optimal pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) required to reduce the incidence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) in Japanese women. This study investigated the association between the pre-pregnancy BMI range and the prevalence of HDP.MethodsData from 96,796 women with singleton pregnancies at ≥ 22 weeks of gestation from the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (enrolled between 2011 and 2014)—a nationwide birth cohort study—were analyzed. The participants were categorized according to pre-pregnancy BMI, as follows: ≤ 16.00, 16.01–17.00, 17.01–18.00, 18.01–19.00, 19.01–20.00, 20.01–21.00, 21.01–22.00, 22.01–23.00, 23.01–24.00, 24.01–25.00, 25.01–26.00, 26.01–27.00, 27.01–28.00, 28.01–29.00, 29.01–30.00, and > 30.00 kg/m2. Odds ratios (ORs) for HDP were calculated using multivariable regression analysis in each group, with the pre-pregnancy BMI 20.01–21.00 kg/m2 group as the reference group, adjusted for maternal characteristics, followed by the Bonferroni correction. Pre-pregnancy BMI cut-off value for predicting the incidence of HDP was calculated through a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsThe median pre-pregnancy BMI was 20.52 kg/m2 (interquartile range: 19.07‒22.48 kg/m2). Adjusted ORs for HDP and severe HDP increased significantly in women with pre-pregnancy BMI > 22.00 kg/m2, and in those with pre-pregnancy BMI of 24.01–25.00 kg/m2 and BMI > 26.00 kg/m2, respectively. No changes were observed in those with pre-pregnancy BMI ≤ 20.00 kg/m2. A pre-pregnancy BMI cut-off value of 22.31 kg/m2 and 22.37 kg/m2 predicted HDP and severe HDP with Youden’s index (sensitivity: 47.1% and 44.0%, specificity: 73.9% and 74.2%) with AUC of 0.63 and 0.62, respectively. Another cut-off value of 32.47 kg/m2 and 32.89 kg/m2 was identified for predicting HDP and severe HDP, respectively, with a specificity > 99%. A similar trend was observed when the analysis was restricted to individuals of Japanese nationality only.ConclusionsPre-pregnancy BMI > 22.00 kg/m2 was associated with an increased prevalence of HDP compared with the pre-pregnancy BMI 20.01–21.00 kg/m2. Due to the low AUC, we propose that the cut-off BMI values for predicting the incidence of HDP may be used as a supplementary data to identify high-risk populations.

Background

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) directly cause approximately 30,000 maternal deaths per year, accounting for 14% of maternal deaths worldwide [1, 2]. Moreover, HDP often leads to medically induced preterm births, which affect perinatal mortality and long-term morbidity [3–5]. HDP mainly consists of pre-eclampsia and gestational hypertension, which are conventionally defined as new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation, with (in cases of pre-eclampsia) or without (in cases of gestational hypertension) signs of dysfunction in organs such as the kidneys, liver, and placenta [6]. Currently, no consensus regarding the optimal strategy for clinical screening for HDP has been reached despite numerous studies on predictive factors and screening tests for HDP [7]. Thus, clarifying the association of maternal background characteristics with HDP may aid in predicting the potential risks for HDP, as the clinical assessment of HDP risks based on maternal history is currently limited to multiparous women [8].

HDP is related to maternal demographic and socioeconomic status, as well as lifestyle factors [9–17]. High maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) is a major risk factor for HDP [15, 17]. Asian women tend to be leaner than women from Western countries [18]; several studies have reported a pre-pregnancy BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2 and appropriate gestational weight gain as factors for better obstetric outcomes in Japanese women, with reduced obstetric complications [19, 20]. Although a previous study recommended a revision of the standard BMI cut-offs [21], the optimal pre-pregnancy BMI that is associated with a lower incidence of HDP in Japanese women remains unestablished. Therefore, in this study, we analyzed the pre-pregnancy BMI range that is associated with a lower prevalence of HDP based on data from a nationwide birth cohort study. We also calculated the cut-off value of pre-pregnancy BMI for predicting the incidence of HDP.

Methods

Study design

We analyzed data from the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), which was a nationwide, government-funded, prospective birth cohort study that started in January 2011 (participants were enrolled between 2011–2014) to investigate the effects of environmental factors on children’s health [22, 23]. Briefly, the JECS was funded directly by the Ministry of the Environment of Japan and involved a collaboration among the Programme Office (National Institute for Environmental Studies), Medical Support Centre (National Center for Child Health and Development), and 15 Regional Centres (Hokkaido, Miyagi, Fukushima, Chiba, Kanagawa, Koshin, Toyama, Aichi, Kyoto, Osaka, Hyogo, Tottori, Kochi, Fukuoka, and South Kyushu/Okinawa) [22, 23]. For inclusion, expectant mothers had to meet the following criteria: (1) residence within the study area at the time of recruitment, with an expectation to continue residing in Japan for the foreseeable future; (2) expected due date between August 1, 2011, and mid-2014; and (3) ability to participate in the study without difficulty (i.e., ability to comprehend the Japanese language and complete a self-administered questionnaire). In the JECS, 88.1% of the participants were of Japanese nationality, 0.4% were of a foreign nationality, and 11.4% had missing information regarding their nationality.

There were two modes of recruitment: (1) at the first prenatal examination at co-operating healthcare providers and (2) at local government offices that issued a pregnancy journal—the Maternal and Child Health Handbook—to all expecting mothers in Japan before they received municipal services for pregnancy, delivery, and childcare. Pregnant women were contacted through the cooperating healthcare providers and/or local government offices that issued the Maternal and Child Health Handbooks. Those who were willing to participate in this study were registered. Self-administered questionnaires, which the women completed during the first and second/third trimesters, were used to collect information on demographic factors, medical history, physical and mental health, lifestyle, occupation, environmental exposures at home and in the workplace, housing conditions, and socioeconomic status [22, 23].

Data collection

The current analysis used data released in October 2019 (dataset: jecs-ta-20190930). Participants with singleton pregnancies were included in the present study. Women who experienced abortions, experienced stillbirths, and had missing data for these outcomes, as well as those with missing information on pre-pregnancy BMI and HDP, or the presence of hypertension before pregnancy (chronic hypertension), were not included in the analysis. This enabled the specific examination of the association between pre-pregnancy BMI and HDP in singleton live births at ≥ 22 weeks of gestation.

Exposure variables

Pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated as the ratio of body weight (kg) to height squared (m2). Pre-pregnancy maternal height was extracted from medical records, typically recorded during pregnancy, although occasionally measured at other time points. Pre-pregnancy maternal body weight was determined based on the self-reported questionnaire administered at the first visit; however, in some cases, the measurements were performed by the cooperating healthcare providers during the first visit. The participants were categorized into 16 groups, as follows: BMI ≤ 16.00, 16.01–17.00, 17.01–18.00, 18.01–19.00, 19.01–20.00, 20.01–21.00, 21.01–22.00, 22.01–23.00, 23.01–24.00, 24.01–25.00, 25.01–26.00, 26.01–27.00, 27.01–28.00, 28.01–29.00, 29.01–30.00, and > 30.00 kg/m2.

Main outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the prevalence of HDP, initially diagnosed as pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH) by the respective institutions and as derived from the medical records. The term PIH was reclassified as HDP in Japan in 2018 with the same definition for hypertension; however, PIH did not include chronic hypertension and HDP included chronic hypertension. To prevent confusion, we excluded chronic hypertension from the analysis. HDP, akin to PIH, was defined as persistently high blood pressure (≥ 140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of gestation in an otherwise normotensive woman [6]. Moreover, information about severe HDP, defined as maternal blood pressure exceeding 160/110 mmHg, was collected [6]. Detailed diagnostic evidence for each case in the JECS dataset was unavailable. Therefore, HDP could have included various types, including gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome.

Confounding factors

Maternal age, parity, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, educational status, and annual household income were identified as confounding factors based on previous studies [9–17].

Maternal age was categorized as follows: ≤ 20, 21–30, 31–40, and ≥ 41 years. Parity was dichotomized as nulliparous or multiparous. The participants were requested to provide information regarding their smoking status by choosing one of the following: “currently smoking,” “never,” “previously did, but quit before realizing current pregnancy,” and “previously did, but quit after realizing current pregnancy.” Participants who responded “currently smoking” were included in the “smoking” category, and the others were included in the “non-smoking” category. Maternal participants were requested to provide information about their alcohol consumption status by choosing one of the following: “never drank,” “quit drinking before pregnancy,” “quit drinking during early pregnancy,” and “continued drinking during pregnancy” [24]. Maternal participants who chose “continued drinking during pregnancy” comprised the “drinking” category; all other participants were included in the “non-drinking” category. Maternal educational status was categorized into four groups based on the number of years of education completed (junior high school, < 10 years; high school, 10–12 years; technical junior college, technical/vocational college, associate degree, or bachelor’s degree, 13–16 years; and graduate degree (masters/doctorate), ≥ 17 years) [25]. Annual household income was categorized into four levels: < 2,000,000; 2,000,000–5,999,999; 6,000,000–9,999,999; and ≥ 10,000,000 JPY [25]. Missing answers in each questionnaire were considered a single item.

Statistical analysis

The participants were stratified into 16 groups based on the pre-pregnancy BMI category, and the maternal characteristics and obstetric outcomes of the total participants were summarized.

Additionally, multivariable logistic regression models were used to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for HDP and severe HDP among participants in each pre-pregnancy BMI category, with the pre-pregnancy BMI 20.01–21.00 kg/m2 group as the reference group (based on the median pre-pregnancy BMI in the study participants). The odds ratios were adjusted for confounding factors.

The cut-off value of pre-pregnancy BMI for predicting the incidence of HDP was calculated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The cut-off value was calculated (1) with Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) to be at the maximum value to provide the best trade-off between sensitivity and specificity and (2) with specificity > 99%. The pre-pregnancy BMI was treated as a continuous variable.

A sensitivity analysis was performed to limit the study population to Japanese nationals because participants of other nationalities (0.4%) or those with missing information regarding their nationality (11.4%) were included in the study. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons. In multiple analyses (N times), p-values < 0.05/N were considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The JECS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ministry of the Environment Institutional Review Board on Epidemiological Studies (No. 100910001) and the Ethics Committees of all the participating institutions. The JECS study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and other national regulations and guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Discussion

We found several lines of evidence on the association between pre-pregnancy BMI range and the prevalence of HDP. First, the aORs for HDP significantly increased in women with pre-pregnancy BMI > 22.00 kg/m2 both in the total participants and in participants of Japanese nationality only. Second, the aORs for severe HDP significantly increased in women with pre-pregnancy BMI 24.01–25.00 kg/m2 and BMI > 26.00 kg/m2, excluding BMI 27.01–28.00 in participants of Japanese nationality only. Third, the aORs for HDP and severe HDP did not change significantly in women with pre-pregnancy BMI ≤ 20.00 kg/m2 both among all participants and participants of Japanese nationality only. Lastly, the cut-off values calculated in this study may raise questions owing to the low AUC in the ROC curve, low sensitivity, and low specificity.

Maternal obesity increases the risk of pregnancy complications such as preterm births, gestational diabetes, stillbirths, fetal acidosis, and HDP [26–30]. However, women in Asian countries, including Japan, tend to have lower pre-pregnancy BMI than those in Western countries [18]. Consequently, a different categorization is used to define obesity in Japanese women (pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) [19]. Therefore, a unique optimal pre-pregnancy BMI analysis is required for a Japanese cohort. Based on the results of this study, pre-pregnancy BMI > 22.00 kg/m2 was associated with an increased prevalence of HDP compared with the overall median pre-pregnancy BMI of the participants. Hypertension may be induced by multifactorial conditions. Among obese women, excessive visceral fat distribution may trigger various hormonal, inflammatory, and endothelial responses, which stimulate mechanisms that precipitate hypertensive conditions [31, 32]. Obstetricians can counsel women about the potential risks of being obese before pregnancy in terms of the increased prevalence of HDP. However, although diet and physical activity as pre-pregnancy interventions in obese women were effective in modifying diet quality and physical activity and in reducing gestational weight gain, these did not prevent the development of obstetric complications [33]. Further studies evaluating the effects of lifestyle modifications for bodyweight control on the prevention of HDP are required. Moreover, low pre-pregnancy BMI was not significantly associated with a decreased prevalence of HDP. Lower pre-pregnancy BMI has been related to several adverse obstetric outcomes, particularly preterm births and fetal growth restriction [34, 35]; thus, we do not suggest that pregnant women should be overly lean. Overall, in addition to the recent reports for proper gestational weight gain in Japanese pregnant women [36–38], the results of this study may aid in suggesting the optimal pre-pregnancy BMI for reducing the incidence of HDP in Japanese cohorts. It should be noted that the aOR results were slightly different between the total participants and those of the Japanese nationality. There may have been several participants of Japanese nationality among the excluded participants because of missing information on nationality, potentially leading to biased results. Therefore, these results should be interpreted based on the characteristics of the target population.

The cut-off values calculated using Youden’s index for predicting the incidence of HDP and severe HDP were 22.31 kg/m2 and 22.37 kg/m2, similar to that recommended for healthy adults in Japan [39]. However, these cut-off values did not have sufficiently high sensitivity and specificity; the AUC in the ROC curve was also relatively small [40]. The cut-off values calculated with specificity > 99% for predicting the incidence of HDP and severe HDP were above 32.00 kg/m2, which is within the obesity range in both Japanese women and women worldwide. Based on the acronym SPIN (i.e., Specific test when Positive rules IN the disease) [41], women with pre-pregnancy BMI above cut-off values would carry a markedly higher risk for HDP, considering the results of the highest aOR for HDP in those with pre-pregnancy BMI > 30.00 kg/m2. The utility of the cut-off values calculated in this study may be questionable; however, obese pregnant women may be considered high-risk pregnancies for HDP based on the ORs from our study. Further analyses to evaluate the proper cut-off values for predicting HDP with stratified analyses based on detailed clinical data are required to establish clinical implications. Estimation of the risk of HDP before it occurs might warn obstetricians to closely observe the patient’s condition with blood tests and to consider aspirin prophylaxis, which has been shown to reduce the risk of pre-eclampsia safely in high-risk women [8], and possibly even in low-risk women [42].

This study had some limitations. First, we did not unify the methods used for measuring pre-pregnancy BMI and the definition of HDP in the JECS. Pre-pregnancy BMI was primarily based on self-reported questionnaires, while HDP diagnoses were made by the respective institutions. However, the mean pre-pregnancy BMIs reported in four previous Japanese cohort studies (21.1, 21.3, 21.0, and 21.7 kg/m2) were close to that reported in our study [43]; furthermore, 2.6% of participants in another Japanese cohort had HDP [44], which was close to the prevalence of HDP in our study. Second, detailed information on several maternal characteristics that have previously been associated with HDP was lacking in the JECS and was not considered (i.e., presence of abnormal laboratory data including soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1/placental growth factor ratio, level of gestational proteinuria, intervals between pregnancies, maternal blood pressure at each stage of pregnancy, abnormal behaviors, comorbidities, and detailed previous pregnancy history with HDP). Moreover, we did not take maternal diet into account because we judged that diet could serve as the mediator rather than the confounding factor. These factors should be considered in future studies to clarify the true association of pre-pregnancy BMI with the prevalence of HDP. Third, careful interpretation is needed because several participants were excluded owing to missing data on exposure and outcome, leading to potential bias; however, similar characteristics between the included and whole participants could address these limitations (data not shown). Finally, the generalizability of the results of this study may be limited because the study population was extracted from a nationwide Japanese cohort, predominantly composed of Japanese nationals, and Japanese women tend to be leaner than women in Western countries [18]. Therefore, the study results can be carefully generalized to relatively lean populations.

Conclusions

A higher pre-pregnancy BMI than pre-pregnancy BMI 20.01–21.00 kg/m2 was associated with an increased prevalence of HDP and severe HDP in a Japanese nationwide cohort. The cut-off BMI values, calculated using different methods to predict the incidence of HDP, yielded low AUC values on the ROC curve, suggesting they may serve merely as supplementary information for identifying high-risk populations. Combining higher HDP and severe HDP prevalence based on pre-pregnancy BMI would help in preconception counseling and formulating government and guideline decisions. Further studies are required to clarify the optimal pre-pregnancy BMI for better obstetric outcomes with stratification of the etiology of complications and maternal background characteristics.
