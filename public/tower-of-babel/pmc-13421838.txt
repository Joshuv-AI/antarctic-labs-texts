Emphysema in an 11‐month‐old boy with alpha‐1 antitrypsin deficiency
Authors: Irvin Yi, Rachel Williams, Leela Chandrasekar, Saif Al‐Qatarneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAlpha‐1 antitrypsin deficiency (A1ATD) is characterized by reduced levels of protease inhibitor alpha‐1 antitrypsin, most commonly manifesting with liver disease in childhood, and/or pulmonary disease starting in the 3rd decade of life. Childhood‐onset lung disease in A1ATD has almost never been reported.Case presentationAn 11‐month‐old boy with a history of neonatal cholestasis and A1ATD phenotype PiZZ presented with prolonged fever of unknown origin. A computed tomography chest was obtained, given continued diagnostic uncertainty and the history of A1ATD, showing paramediastinal right upper lobe consolidation, as well as basilar centrilobular emphysema in the left lower lobe, likely secondary to the patient's A1ATD. The patient had no respiratory symptoms and clinically recovered with antibiotics.ConclusionPediatric development of emphysema has been rarely described, and this is the earliest documented case to date at 11 months of age. This is also the first finding of asymptomatic emphysema in childhood A1ATD, demonstrating that disease progression may be chronic and may transition from asymptomatic to symptomatic disease.

INTRODUCTION

Alpha‐1 antitrypsin deficiency (A1ATD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations to the SERPINA1 gene, leading to reduced levels of alpha‐1 antitrypsin (AAT), an antiprotease and natural inhibitor of serine proteases released by activated neutrophils. The “M” allele for SERPINA1 is considered the normal allele – variations are observed in the “S” allele with moderately reduced function and the “Z” allele with the most severe dysfunction. 1 Individuals with the PiZZ allele combination have the most severe phenotype of A1ATD – this is observed in approximately 1 in 2000–5000 live births. 2 This deficiency of AAT most commonly manifests with liver disease in childhood, and/or pulmonary disease starting in the 3rd decade of life.

Liver disease is caused by pathologic polymerization of the variant AAT, known as a “toxic gain of function” abnormality, leading to intrahepatocyte accumulation of misfolded AAT proteins. 3 Manifestations of liver disease in childhood extend the spectrum of asymptomatic healthy children to fatal liver failure in infancy. Cumulatively, approximately 10%–15% of newborns with the PiZZ genotype will present with neonatal hepatitis. 4 Of these patients, up to 80% experience resolution of their disease by 1 year, 10%–20% develop chronic liver disease such as portal hypertension, and 5% will require liver transplantation within the first 4 years of life. 5 , 6

On the other hand, pulmonary disease is due to “toxic loss of function” abnormality, to which AAT deficiency disrupts the protease‐antiprotease balance in the lungs, leading to destruction of elastin by elastase and other proteases in the lung. 3 Between 60% and 80% of patients with A1ATD will develop emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in their lifetime. 3 Emphysema in A1ATD classically presents in a basilar‐predominant pattern; in one study, patients exhibited a reduced mean forced expiratory volume in 1 s of 43% of predicted. 7 Dyspnea, chronic cough, and wheezing are all classically seen in A1ATD. Smoking is a major risk factor in the early onset of pulmonary disease, with smokers experiencing symptoms in the 3rd and 4th decades, while non‐smokers experience symptoms in the 4th and 5th decades of life. 3 Childhood‐onset lung disease has almost never been reported. A Swedish study following 200 000 infants with A1ATD reported no cases of lung disease in the first two decades of life. 8 Case reports have described lung disease in children aged 10 and 13, with the earliest identifiable onset at 18 months in a case report from 1971. 9 , 10 , 11 In all such cases, the patients presented with respiratory symptoms and faced respiratory insult such as smoke exposure or bronchopneumonia. 9 , 10 , 11

We present a patient presentation of fever of unknown origin in an 11‐month‐old boy with A1ATD, incidentally found to have basilar centrilobular emphysema, likely in the setting of A1ATD. This case report details the earliest documented case to date of pediatric development of emphysema in A1ATD.

CASE REPORT

An 11‐month‐old boy presented with a prolonged fever of unknown origin. He was born at 36 weeks' gestational age (GA) via emergency cesarean section for breech presentation with nuchal cord (wrapped twice), with a birth weight of 1.81 kg, classifying him as low birth weight. Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) was identified by routine ultrasound at 32 weeks GA, with no interval growth between weeks 32 and 36 GA. The mother received two doses of antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity. Maternal health and nutritional status were unremarkable, with no maternal smoking history or substance use during pregnancy. On repeat ultrasound at 36 weeks GA, fetal heart rate decelerations prompted emergency delivery, and the patient was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for 4 days. The patient received supportive care only, and no mechanical ventilation was required. He was discharged home without respiratory support. At 8 weeks of age, he received an extensive workup for neonatal cholestasis and was diagnosed with A1ATD with an A1AT level of 390 mg/L and phenotype PiZZ. He was subsequently managed with nutritional support, ursodiol, and vitamin supplementation, followed by hepatology. Initial formula transition was complicated by irritability, gassiness, and projectile vomiting, which resolved after caloric density was decreased to 0.68 kcal/mL. He was later transitioned to standard formula (Similac 360) at 828–887 mL per day, as well as pureed fruits, vegetables, and yogurt, with an estimated 355 mL of solid food intake daily. Growth curve by height has improved from the 0.03% percentile at 3 months to the 1.88% percentile at presentation, and from the 0.11% percentile to 0.80% percentile by weight.

There was no history of passive smoke exposure, indoor wood smoke, or other significant environmental pollutant exposures. Both parents had no history of emphysema, early‐onset COPD, liver disease, or other respiratory conditions. The patient had been fully asymptomatic and taking no medications prior to this illness.

Two weeks prior, the patient was measured to have a fever of 39.4°C. He was recommended supportive care by his pediatrician. The fevers continued for 4 days, upon which he tested positive for group A streptococcus. The patient was started on amoxicillin without improvement over the following 6 days, prompting presentation to an outside hospital emergency department for further evaluation.

The patient presented as fatigued but irritable. He weighed 7.78 kg (12th percentile) with a height of 67 cm (<3rd percentile). He was febrile with a temperature of 39.3°C. Physical exam demonstrated no rash, conjunctival injection, tongue swelling, or lymphadenopathy. Heart, lung, and abdominal exams are unremarkable. Initial laboratory evaluation was notable for marked leukocytosis with a white blood cell (WBC) count of 44 × 109/L and an absolute neutrophil count of 25 × 109/L, accompanied by an elevated C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 40 mg/L and thrombocytosis with a platelet count of 1210 × 109/L. Serum ferritin was elevated at 347 ng/mL (347 µg/L), consistent with an acute‐phase response. Further diagnostic evaluation demonstrated the patient was rhinovirus positive; however, blood and urine cultures were without growth, urinalysis was clear, and chest X‐ray demonstrated “streaky perihilar opacities bilaterally, suggestive of bronchiolitis. No evidence of consolidation to suggest pneumonia.” He was treated with intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone 75 mg/(kg·day) over the course of 3 days without improvement in his fever curve. At this point, he was transferred to our inpatient service for a higher level of care.

Upon presentation to our service, repeat examination and laboratory evaluation were consistent with the outside hospital. A repeat chest X‐ray demonstrated “slightly increased perihilar interstitial markings, nonspecific, may be seen with small airways disease or atypical pneumonia/pneumonitis. More confluent right apical suprahilar opacity suggests atelectasis and/or developing pneumonia with associated pleural thickening or loculated right apical effusion.” The patient was started on IV ceftriaxone 50 mg/(kg·day) and IV vancomycin 15 mg/(kg·day). Infectious disease was consulted and recommended a computed tomography (CT) chest given continued diagnostic uncertainty and the history of A1ATD.

CT chest from the coronal view demonstrated “paramediastinal right upper lobe consolidation and right hilar and possible mediastinal lymphadenopathy, most likely representing an infectious etiology/pneumonia” (Figure 1). At the same time, CT results best seen in the axial view noted an incidental finding of “subtle basilar centrilobular emphysema. More prominent areas of air trapping, including possible early bullous formation in the left lower lobe, the largest measuring 1.4 cm. Both findings likely secondary to known AAT deficiency” (Figure 2).

The patient experienced a significant clinical and laboratory response to the addition of vancomycin, evidenced by improvement in activity, energy, and excellent oral intake. His WBC decreased from 41 × 109/L to 17 × 109/L and CRP from 168.8 mg/L to 124.2 mg/L over the course of 2 days. Subsequently, the patient was transitioned to oral amoxicillin‐clavulanate 85 mg/(kg·day) and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim 10 mg/(kg·day). He was discharged on these medications and completed the remaining 1‐week course without complications. At his 1‐month follow‐up visit, the patient was fully asymptomatic and a thriving 13‐month‐old boy.

DISCUSSION

In this report, we present an 11‐month‐old boy with a history of A1ATD presenting with a fever of unknown origin, found to have a right upper lobe pneumonia that was successfully treated with antibiotics. In the process of an extensive respiratory workup, CT imaging identified basilar centrilobular emphysema in the left lower lobe and areas of prominent air trapping measuring up to 1.4 cm. The patient had no respiratory symptoms during the disease course; respiratory exams were clear to auscultation bilaterally, with oxygen saturation continuously above 95% during hospitalization. The patient had no documented household smoke exposure or history of respiratory disease.

This case is an important addition to our understanding of A1ATD. First, while pediatric development of emphysema has been described previously, this is the earliest documented case to date at 11 months of age. Second, our patient differs from the previous reports of emphysema in childhood A1ATD, where all patients presented with symptomatic pulmonary disease, including cough, dyspnea, and wheeze. 9 , 10 , 11 Third, the pattern of emphysema is partially consistent with adult‐onset A1ATD. Basilar predominance has been reported in adult‐onset emphysema, which is also seen in our patient. 1 , 2 , 3 There has been evidence that suggests many patients present with upper zone predominant emphysema, which was found in this pattern, especially in PiSZ patients compared to PiZZ patients. 12 In our PiZZ patient, the basilar emphysema is more consistent with the general pattern in adults. On the other hand, the centrilobular pattern is an atypical finding compared to the classic, panacinar pattern. 1 , 2 The most straightforward explanation for this discrepancy could be challenges in radiologist interpretation of centrilobular versus panacinar emphysema in an immature lung. Interrater reliability, even in adult cohorts, is only moderate (k = 0.63–0.70), and challenges of identifying landmarks in an infant lung could further complicate this distinction. 13 , 14 A mechanistic explanation could relate to IUGR in the perinatal period, which may have influenced the development of these findings. IUGR has been shown to impair alveolarization through elevated proteolytic activity of MMP2 and MMP9, with a 50% reduction in lung fibers postnatally. 15 This mechanism could further the protease‐antiprotease imbalance in A1ATD and may represent a synergistic mechanism, accelerating the emphysematous changes seen in early life. Given the difficulty of subtype classification in an immature lung, the findings may not be classified simply to establish adult patterns and could represent a morphologically distinct pattern in the developing lung. Alternatively, atypical distribution from the classic lower‐lobe, panacinar pattern has been well documented in A1ATD and could be seen in this patient. 16 Finally, this case adds to our understanding of A1ATD as the changes were only identified because the fever of unknown origin necessitated an extensive respiratory workup, including CT imaging. Without which, the structural changes would have remained undetected. It is possible that respiratory changes in A1ATD may commonly present asymptomatically earlier in life but go unrecognized and undiagnosed until respiratory symptoms develop. Pulmonary insults early in life, such as smoke exposure, bronchopneumonia, or developmental delays such as IUGR, may trigger early development of emphysema in A1ATD. Further elucidation into asymptomatic patients or patients at high‐risk may provide a deeper understanding of the disease process and progression.

Differential diagnoses for the CT findings must be considered for this patient case. A potential explanation for the findings could be due to post‐infectious small airway disease, especially in the context of suspected pneumonia. Bronchiolar inflammation secondary to infection has been shown to manifest as mosaic attenuation with air trapping on CT, which closely mimics centrilobular emphysema. 17 However, this would be less likely as there was demonstrated right upper lobe consolidation seen by both chest X‐ray and CT, which is geographically distinct from the left lower lobe changes. Furthermore, the patient had no prior severe respiratory infections and no respiratory symptoms during the infection, which makes severe, post‐infectious pulmonary changes less likely. Congenital lobar emphysema can manifest as lung lesions found early in life, but typically present with respiratory distress in the neonatal period and demonstrate massive imaging changes. 18 Our patient had limited focal changes and no evidence of neonatal respiratory distress, making this unlikely. Other differential causes for the observed changes include interstitial lung diseases or chronic lung damage secondary to recurrent infections, which are unlikely given the patient's unremarkable respiratory history.

The findings of this report are intrinsically limited by the nature of the case report and the co‐occurrence of multiple risk factors, including PiZZ genotype, IUGR, prematurity, and acute pneumonia, making the report unable to attribute the findings to one root etiology. While both parents are not known to have A1ATD, parental genotyping is unavailable. Technical limitations include acquisition of the CT image, as CT imaging in children who cannot breath‐hold increases motion artifact and reduces test sensitivity. 19 In addition, the interpretation of structural changes in an immature lung complicates the characterization of the imaging findings. Furthermore, the findings are confounded by the acute infectious presentation, which may mimic emphysema. Follow‐up CT imaging would strengthen evidence that the findings are emphysematous as compared to transient and post‐infectious, but this was not performed due to radiation exposure considerations.

Overall, this case provides an important addition to our understanding of A1ATD and its respiratory manifestations. Clinicians should be aware that emphysematous changes may occur early in childhood, and the absence of respiratory symptoms does not exclude early structural lung disease. A history of IUGR may be a risk‐modifier for patients with A1ATD, and clinicians should have increased vigilance for emphysema in settings of acute infections in pediatric patients with A1ATD. There is rapid growth in the therapeutic landscape for A1ATD. RNA interference therapies (fazirsiran and belcesiran), gene editing, and RNA editing therapies are in various stages of preclinical and clinical development. 20 , 21 , 22 By targeting the underlying genetic defect, these therapies aim to reduce hepatotoxic protein accumulation and restore functional AAT secretion. This case raises important questions regarding patient selection and timing of intervention for these new therapies. If structural changes can develop in early infancy for high‐risk patients, then early identification and testing may help select patients who can benefit from early use of disease‐modifying therapy, before irreversible or symptomatic damage occurs. While current trials are limited to adults and the safety and efficacy in pediatric populations is still unknown, pediatric clinicians should be aware of these ongoing advances, as their patients may be among those most likely to benefit from disease‐modifying therapies. A continued focus on early‐onset emphysema and respiratory risk factors may further elucidate disease pathogenesis and provide a basis for selecting patients for these therapeutic interventions.
