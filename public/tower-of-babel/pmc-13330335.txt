Analysis of factors influencing the missed diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions by colposcopy
Authors: Leilei Yuan, Hui Wang, Zhengjun Xu, Kejie Liu, Shi Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe aimed to explore the factors that influence the missed diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions by colposcopy.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 448 patients with cervical precancerous lesions who underwent colposcopy and cervical biopsy between January 1, 2022, and October 13, 2024. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with missed diagnosis, with colposcopic findings compared against histopathological results as the gold standard.ResultsCompared to histopathological results (gold standard), the accuracy rate of colposcopy was 65.63% (294/448). Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that insufficient colposcopic diagnosis was significantly correlated with age ≥40 years (χ2=15.632, p<0.001), absence of postcoital vaginal bleeding (χ²=5.734, p=0.01), low-grade abnormalities in liquid-based cytology (χ²=9.809, p=0.002), non-HPV16 infection (χ2=7.204, p=0.007), lesions located in the cervical canal (χ²=23.914, p<0.001), history of cervical conization (χ2=7.182, p=0.007), and colposcopic features including no or thin acetowhite epithelium (χ²=225.559, p<0.001), absence of mosaic (χ2=45.925, p<0.001), absence of punctate blood vessels (χ²=36.041, p<0.001), and positive iodine test (χ2=35.794, p<0.001). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified age ≥40 years (OR=0.468, 95% CI: 0.287~0.761, p=0.002), absence of postcoital vaginal bleeding (OR=2.132, 95% CI: 1.077~4.222, p=0.030), low-grade abnormalities in TCT results (OR=2.584, 95% CI: 1.527~4.372, p<0.001), non-HPV16 infection (OR=0.545, 95% CI: 0.358~0.832, p=0.005), and lesions localized to the cervical canal (OR=0.042, 95% CI: 0.005~0.338, p=0.003) as independent risk factors for missed diagnosis. The highest missed diagnosis rate (67.9%) was observed in patients aged ≥55 years with low-grade TCT abnormalities (n=53), which should be interpreted with caution as exploratory data; in contrast, the missed diagnosis rate was 20.0% in patients aged <25 years with HPV16 positivity (n=15). ConclusionAge over 40, absence of postcoital vaginal bleeding, low-grade abnormality in TCT results, non-HPV16 infection, and lesions in the cervical canal are independent risk factors for cervical precancerous lesions detected through colposcopy. However, the findings must be interpreted cautiously because they were based on a retrospective study and the study had a short follow-up.

Introduction

The incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer rank fourth among female malignancies, posing a serious threat to women’s health [1]. Cervical precancerous lesions are precursors to the development of cervical cancer, and timely treatment can prevent the occurrence of cervical cancer [2]. Although the popularization of the HPV vaccine and the promotion of cervical cancer screening programs have significantly reduced its incidence and mortality, early identification and precise diagnosis of precancerous lesions remain the core aspects of clinical prevention and control [3]. The “three-tier” diagnostic process for cervical cancer consists of cervical cancer screening, colposcopy, and histopathological diagnosis. Colposcopy plays a pivotal role in this diagnostic flow [4].

For those with abnormal cervical cancer screening results, further colposcopy is performed [5]. Suspicious lesion sites identified under the colposcope are biopsied, and a definitive diagnosis is made through histopathological examination [6]. In 2011, the International Federation for Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy (IFCPC) produced a standard terminology, and the federation stated that colposcopic diagnostic report results should be classified into normal findings, low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) findings, high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) findings, or suspicious for invasive cervical cancer [7]. However, due to the poor reproducibility and accuracy of colposcopy, it can easily lead to missed diagnoses of cervical precancerous lesions. To avoid missed diagnoses, the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP) has provided consensus guidelines for risk-based abnormal cervical cancer screening results and precancerous lesion management [8]. The minimum standard for colposcopic biopsy is that if the colposcopy impression is normal but there are acetowhite epithelium, metaplasia, other abnormalities, an incompletely visualized squamous junction, or a cytological diagnosis higher than ASC-H or HPV 16 or 18 infection, a biopsy is required [9]. However, unnecessary cervical biopsies can cause physical and psychological trauma to patients, leading to poor patient compliance. Additionally, the accuracy of colposcopy is influenced by various factors, such as operators’ experience and skill level, patients’ biological characteristics and HPV infection status, as well as technical limitations and insufficient standardization [3, 10].

Despite HPV16 and HPV18 remaining the most oncogenic HPV types worldwide, other high-risk HPV subtypes (i.e., HPV31, HPV33, and HPV45) also retain malignant potential with meaningful malignant contributions to cervical carcinogenesis. These high-risk viral subtypes are often lumped together as “other high-risk HPV,” and while our study included HPV 16/18 subtypes in the group analysis, the high-risk “other type” subtypes are procedurally relevant and relatively overlooked. Differentiating or recognizing individual high-risk subtypes is still useful from a diagnostic accuracy perspective because some may have distinct differences in colposcopy detectability and even differences in lesion characteristics. For our purposes, even if these subtypes were grouped under cervical infections excluding HPV16, we certainly acknowledge that limitation and highlight how crucial it is for a new study to identify individual high-risk subtypes beyond HPV16/18.

As such, we aimed to analyze the relevant clinicopathologic data of women with cervical precancerous lesions to identify the high-risk factors for missed diagnosis by colposcopy of high-grade cervical lesions.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

This study was a retrospective cohort study, which consequently had a risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuyang People’s Hospital (Approval No.: 2024[54]), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

A total of 448 patients with cervical precancerous lesions treated in the hospital from January 1, 2022, to October 13, 2024, were included. Given the retrospective nature and short inclusion period, long-term follow-up data (e.g., progression to invasive cancer) were not available. The sample size represented the entire population of consecutive eligible cases during this period; no statistical power calculation was performed. To ensure consecutive enrollment and minimize selection bias, all patients who visited the Department of Gynecology of our hospital during the study period (January 1, 2022, to October 13, 2024) and met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were prospectively screened and enrolled in the study without arbitrary exclusion. A dedicated research nurse was responsible for daily screening of outpatient and inpatient records, verifying eligibility criteria for each potential patient, and documenting the reasons for exclusion (e.g., incomplete data, prior hysterectomy) for all non-enrolled individuals. This standardized process ensured that the study cohort represented the full spectrum of eligible patients during the study period, reducing the risk of selection bias associated with retrospective studies. A priori statistical power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software prior to study initiation. Based on the primary endpoint (identification of independent risk factors for missed colposcopic diagnosis), we set a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power (1-β) of 0.85, and a minimum detectable odds ratio (OR) of ≥ 1.5 for independent risk factors. The calculation estimated a minimum required sample size of 320 patients. Our final cohort included 448 consecutive eligible cases, which exceeds this pre-specified minimum requirement and ensures robust statistical power for all univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses performed in this study. Due to the relatively short inclusion period, only limited data on follow-up related to progression to cancer were available, which the authors acknowledged as a limitation of this study.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Persistent HPV infection (same type > 6 months) with NILM cytology; (2) LSIL or higher-grade cytology; (3) Cytology of ASC-US but positive for high-risk HPV; (4) Cytology of NILM but positive for HPV16 or HPV18.

Exclusion criteria: (1) previous total hysterectomy; (2) history of cervical cancer or current diagnosis of cervical cancer; (3) history of vaginal wall lesions; (4) pregnant or breastfeeding patients to avoid pregnancy-related confounding variables of cervical anatomical changes or hormonal factors that may alter colposcopic and histopathological interpretation; (5) cases with incomplete data.

Colposcopists and reproducibility

All examinations were conducted by five board-certified gynecologists with 4–12 years of colposcopic experience. Formal inter-observer reliability assessment was performed using kappa statistics. Fifty randomly selected cases (≈ 11% of the total cohort) were independently re-evaluated by the five colposcopists and two pathologists. For colposcopic diagnosis (normal/LSIL/HSIL), the overall kappa value was 0.73 (95% CI: 0.61–0.85), indicating substantial agreement. For histopathological diagnosis (≤ LSIL/HSIL/AIS), the kappa value was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.67–0.89), representing almost substantial agreement. These results confirm the consistency of diagnostic evaluations across observers.

Clinical data collection

General clinical data were collected, including age, presence of postcoital vaginal bleeding, cervical cytology examination, HPV test results, and history of cervical conization. Colposcopic data were also collected, such as the presumptive diagnosis based on colposcopy, visibility of the squamocolumnar junction of the cervix, acetowhite epithelium, mosaicism, and punctation. Additionally, pathological diagnosis information was obtained, including the pathological results after cervical biopsy or endocervical curettage (ECC).

For lesion location grouping, we strictly defined three anatomical categories in accordance with the 2011 IFCPC colposcopic terminology: (1) Cervico-vaginal lesions: precancerous lesions confirmed by ectocervical biopsy, with no high-grade lesions detected in the endocervical canal via ECC; (2) Endocervical canal lesions: HSIL only detected by ECC, with ectocervical biopsy results negative or ≤LSIL; (3) Vaginal lesions: precancerous lesions located in the vaginal fornix or vaginal wall, which were not included in the current study cohort. Transformation zone type was classified and analyzed independently, separate from the anatomical location grouping of lesions.

ThinPrep Cytologic Test (TCT)

ThinPrep Cytologic Test (TCT)‌ is a liquid-based cytology method used for cervical cancer screening. Cervical exfoliated cell collection for both TCT and high-risk HPV genotyping was performed in a single standardized sampling procedure at the same clinical visit. Briefly, subjects were placed in the lithotomy position, a vaginal speculum was inserted to fully expose the cervix, and excessive secretions around the cervical os were wiped away with a disposable cotton swab. A dedicated Xinbo cervical sampling brush was rotated 7–10 times at the cervical external os to collect sufficient exfoliated epithelial cells from both the external os and endocervical canal. The collected sample was immediately split into two equal aliquots: one aliquot was processed for TCT analysis, and the other was used for HPV genotyping, ensuring adequate cell quantity for both tests.

For TCT processing, the allocated sample was rinsed into a vial of cell preservation solution, then processed with the Xinbo 2000 system to separate mucus, blood, and inflammatory cells from epithelial cells. Epithelial cells were transferred to electrostatically treated slides to form thin smears, which were then fixed and stained with 96% alcohol. All procedures were supervised by experienced pathology experts. TCT results were interpreted according to the Bethesda System for Reporting Cervical Cytology (TBS). High-grade abnormal findings included atypical squamous cells that cannot rule out high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H), HSIL, atypical glandular cells (AGC), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and adenocarcinoma (ADCA). Low-grade abnormalities included atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US), LSIL, and negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM) with a positive high-risk HPV test.

High-risk HPV test

HPV genotyping was performed using the aliquot of the same cervical exfoliated cell sample collected in the unified sampling procedure described in Sect. 2.4. HPV subtyping was conducted using a commercial HPV detection kit (Shuoshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) via the PCR-reverse dot blot method, on a SLAN-96P PCR amplifier (Hongshi, Shanghai Co., Ltd.), following the manufacturer’s standard protocol.

Colposcopy and cervical biopsy

Patients were examined using a standardized colposcopic examination (CH800 model, Jiangsu Tongren Medical Electronics Technology Co., Ltd.). Abnormal findings were noted after acetic acid and iodine were applied. The biopsy protocols followed the recommendations of the IFCPC and ASCCP: that is, all areas deemed abnormal were biopsied (i.e., targeted biopsies). Endocervical curettage (ECC) was performed for type 3 cervical transformation zones, glandular atypia on cytology, and HSIL cytology with invisible lesions. These standardized protocols minimized inter-practitioner variability and ensured the diagnostic yield of colposcopy-directed biopsies, aligning with international clinical practice guidelines. This minimized variability among practitioners in biopsy practices, although some practitioner-dependent variability was unavoidable. The tissue specimens were evaluated independently by two experienced pathologists.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS version 27.0. Categorical data were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test; continuous variables were compared using either the t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as applicable. To address potential confounders, we first identified key variables based on clinical relevance and prior literature (age, TCT results, HPV status, lesion location, history of cervical surgery, and transformation zone type). Categorical variables were coded as dummy variables (e.g., age: <40 years as reference vs. ≥40 years; TCT results: low-grade as reference vs. high-grade), and continuous variables (if applicable) were standardized. These variables were included in the multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for their overlapping effects on missed diagnosis, ensuring the independent associations of the target factors were accurately estimated. Logistic regression was performed to determine independent predictors. Inter-observer variability was not assessed and was a limitation of this analysis. P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

General clinical information

A total of 448 patients with cervical precancerous lesions were included in the study, with a median age of 42.5 years (range: 15–74 years). Among these patients, colposcopic examination yielded the following preliminary diagnoses: 64 cases (14.29%) were considered normal, 90 cases (20.09%) were diagnosed as low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), and 294 cases (65.63%) were diagnosed as high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), yielding a diagnostic concordance rate of 65.85% for colposcopy. Regarding age stratification, 191 patients were younger than 40 years, and 257 were ≥ 40 years. Specifically, there were 20 patients aged < 25 years, 171 aged 25–39 years, 170 aged 40–54 years, and 87 aged ≥ 55 years. Cervical cytological results revealed that 136 cases (30.36%) were negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM), 113 cases (25.22%) were atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS), 80 cases (17.86%) were LSIL, 47 cases (10.49%) were atypical squamous cells that cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H), and 72 cases (16.07%) were HSIL. Regarding high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) testing, 237 cases (52.9%) were positive for HPV16, and 211 cases (47.1%) were classified into the non-HPV16 group. The non-HPV16 group included 196 cases (43.8%) positive for HPV18 or other non-HPV16 high-risk subtypes (HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV56, HPV58, HPV59, HPV66, HPV68), and 15 cases (3.3%) negative for hrHPV. No separate subtype-specific analysis was performed for individual non-HPV16 hrHPV subtypes due to the limited sample size of each single subtype. Histopathological evaluation of cervical tissues confirmed 445 cases of HSIL and 3 cases of adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS). Endocervical curettage (ECC) was performed in 195 patients, with ECC pathological findings revealing ≤LSIL in 109 cases, cervical HSIL in 83 cases, and AIS in 3 cases. Notably, 15 patients had cervical biopsy results of ≤LSIL but ECC results indicating HSIL. Additionally, 14 patients had a prior history of undergoing loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) for cervical HSIL.

Univariate logistic regression analysis

Table 1 Displayed the univariate analysis results on the missed diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions diagnosed by colposcopy. The rate of missed diagnosis was statistically higher in patients 40 years or older compared to those younger than 40 years (χ²=15.632, p < 0.001). The rate of missed diagnosis was significantly higher in patients with low-grade abnormalities diagnosed by TCT compared to patients with high-grade abnormalities (χ²=9.809, p = 0.002). The rate of missed diagnosis was significantly higher in patients with other types than HPV16 compared to those with HPV16 (χ²=7.204, p = 0.007). The rate of missed diagnosis was significantly lower in patients with colposcopic images showing thick acetowhite epithelium (χ²=225.559, p < 0.001), mosaicism (χ²=45.925, p < 0.001), punctate blood vessels (χ²=36.041, p < 0.001), and in patients with iodine-negative tests (χ²=35.794, p < 0.001). The rate of missed diagnosis was higher when ECC pathology was diagnosed as HSIL while cervical biopsy was ≤ LSIL (χ²=3.209, p = 0.073). Patients with a history of cervical conization had a significantly higher rate of missed diagnosis by colposcopy (χ²=7.182, p = 0.007). Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that transformation zone type (classified independently of lesion anatomical location) was significantly associated with missed diagnosis (χ2 = 6.389, p = 0.041), with the highest missed diagnosis rate in type III transformation zone (37.5%), followed by type II (36.2%) and type I (23.4%). In parallel, lesions located in the endocervical canal were associated with a significantly higher missed diagnosis rate than those in the cervico-vaginal region (93.3% vs. 32.3%, χ2 = 23.914, p < 0.001). However, our results on transformation zone type contrast with prior published studies that reported significantly higher diagnostic accuracy in cases with a visible squamocolumnar junction (SCJ). This discrepancy may be due to the limited sample size of the type I transformation zone subgroup, patient selection criteria, or local clinical practice habits; therefore, these data should be interpreted cautiously.

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis

Age, symptoms, TCT results, cervical HPV infection type, cervical precancerous lesion site, history of cervical surgery, and transformation zone were included in a multivariate binary logistic analysis (Table 2). Age ≥ 40 years (OR = 0.468, 95% CI: 0.287 ~ 0.761, p = 0.002), absence of postcoital vaginal bleeding (OR = 2.132, 95% CI: 1.077 ~ 4.222, p = 0.030), TCT results showing low-grade abnormalities (OR = 2.584, 95% CI: 1.527 ~ 4.372, p = < 0.001), non-HPV16 infection (OR = 0.545, 95% CI: 0.358 ~ 0.832, p = 0.005), and lesions localized to the cervical canal (corresponding to ECC pathological diagnosis of HSIL with cervical biopsy ≤LSIL, OR = 0.042, 95% CI: 0.005 ~ 0.338, p = 0.003) were identified as independent risk factors for missed diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions during colposcopy. Neither previous cervical surgery (p = 0.213) nor transformation zone type (p = 0.828) was found to be an independent risk factor for missed diagnosis during colposcopy.

Comprehensive analysis of factors related to the missed diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions by colposcopy

The comprehensive analysis of factors associated with the missed diagnosis of cervical precancerous lesions by colposcopy is presented in Table 3; Fig. 1. Stratified analyses revealed a clear age-dependent increase in missed diagnosis rates across multiple subgroups. In patients without postcoital vaginal bleeding, the missed diagnosis rate increased significantly with age, rising from 15.4% in those under 25 years to 55.3% in those aged ≥ 55 years (χ2 = 19.622, p < 0.001). Similarly, among patients with postcoital bleeding, the rate increased from 28.6% in the < 25 age group to 30.8% in the 40–54 group, although the trend was not statistically significant (p = 0.029). Notably, patients with low-grade TCT abnormalities exhibited a pronounced age-related increase in missed diagnosis: from 25.0% in those < 25 years to 67.9% in those ≥ 55 years (χ2 = 30.541, p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant age-related trend was observed in patients with high-grade TCT abnormalities (χ2 = 1.517, p = 0.740), suggesting that colposcopy is more reliable in this higher-risk group regardless of age. Stratification by HPV status further clarified risk patterns. Among HPV16-positive patients, the missed diagnosis rate increased from 20.0% in those < 25 years to 44.7% in those ≥ 55 years (χ2 = 12.801, p = 0.005). In non-HPV16-positive patients, the rate rose from 20.0% to 60.0% in the same age groups (χ2 = 9.285, p = 0.023), indicating that non-HPV16 infections are associated with higher diagnostic challenges, especially in older women. For lesion location, patients with lesions in the cervicovaginal region showed a significant increase in missed diagnosis with age (from 20.0% to 48.1%, χ2 = 16.053, p = 0.001). However, patients with lesions confined to the cervical canal (n = 15) were all aged ≥ 25 years, and no significant age trend was observed (p = 1.00), likely due to the small sample size and advanced age distribution. The highest missed diagnosis rate (67.9%) was observed in patients aged ≥ 55 years with low-grade TCT abnormalities (n = 53). A previously mentioned erroneous rate of 71.8% was derived from an unrepresentative small subset (n = 7) and is hereby corrected and removed. Due to the relatively small size of this ≥ 55 years + low-grade TCT subgroup (n = 53), this finding is purely exploratory and should not be generalized. The lowest missed diagnosis rate (20.0%) was in HPV16-positive patients under 25 years (n = 15). Notably, three adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) cases showed no colposcopic abnormalities, highlighting the limitations of colposcopy in detecting glandular lesions. These findings underscore that older age, low-grade cytology, non-HPV16 infection, and cervical canal involvement are key contributors to missed diagnoses.

Discussion

The secondary prevention of cervical cancer involves cervical cancer screening, timely detection of cervical precancerous lesions, and active treatment to prevent further disease progression, leading to better treatment outcomes. Colposcopy plays a crucial role in diagnosing cervical precancerous lesions. However, colposcopy has the characteristics of poor accuracy and repeatability, and it is also affected by the colposcopist’s professional skill level and subjectivity [8]. Inexperienced colposcopists may cause delayed diagnosis and treatment of the disease [11]. The diagnostic accuracy of colposcopy can increase from 50% to 56% among colposcopists who have performed more than 50 colposcopies [12]. Additionally, compared to colposcopists with less than 10 years of experience, those with 10 years of experience demonstrate the highest consistency in detecting HSIL+ [13]. Due to the limitations of colposcopy, some researchers have found that the diagnostic coincidence rate of colposcopy for cervical lesions diagnosed as HSIL + in pathology ranges from 60% to 69.65% [14–16]. In this study, the coincidence rate of colposcopic diagnosis for cervical precancerous lesions—defined as the consistency between colposcopic preliminary diagnosis (normal, LSIL, HSIL) and histopathological final diagnosis (gold standard)—was 65.63% (294/448), which was consistent with the aforementioned research results (60%–69.65%). Although we did not report the work experience of the colposcopists, previous studies have shown a significant gap in experience and skill level between junior and senior colposcopists [11–13]. Junior colposcopists need to strengthen relevant technical training to further improve the accuracy of colposcopic diagnosis.

Certainly, the accuracy of colposcopy is also influenced by various factors, including cervical cytology results, high-risk HPV subtypes, transformation zone types, and the number of biopsies [16, 17]. Songveeratham et al. have reported that approximately 15% of patients with cervical HSIL are cytologically diagnosed as ASCUS and LSIL [18]. This phenomenon may be due to the coexistence of tiny HSIL lesions and large areas of low-grade intraepithelial lesions (LSIL).During cytological smear evaluation, a large number of low-grade abnormal cells may be visible, leading to the neglect of searching for higher-grade abnormal cells, which can result in misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis.In 59.7% of cases in which cytology results indicated ASCUS or LSIL, cervical HSIL lesions were small in size and difficult to identify during colposcopy, whereas only 35.2% of patients with cytology results of HSIL exhibited this condition [18]. Similarly, cervical lesions in patients with cytological results of HSIL were often observed in large areas of high-grade cervical intraepithelial lesions, with a lower rate of missed diagnosis during colposcopy [16]. In this study, patients with low-grade cytological abnormalities had a higher probability of missed diagnosis of HSIL during colposcopy compared to those with high-grade cytological abnormalities, representing an independent risk factor.

In addition, several smaller HSIL lesions, which may be horizontally extending or situated deep in the cervical glandular crypts, may not show abnormal cells on cytology smears [19, 20]. Stuebs et al. [13] stated that the diagnostic accuracy of colposcopy for HSIL confirmed by cervical biopsy pathology was 77.8% if the squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) was visible on colposcopy, and 58% if the SCJ was not visible, with a statistically significant difference. In our study, there was no statistically significant difference in the rate of missed diagnosis for HSIL lesions regardless of SCJ visibility, which differs from the findings of other studies. There were various explanations for this result, including the small sample size of the subgroup, local patient selection criteria, or other operator-dependent factors; however, these observations should be taken into consideration as possible explanations and not as definitive evidence. Multiple factors could contribute to the discrepancy between our findings on SCJ visibility and those reported in previous literature. The subgroup of patients with invisible SCJ (transformation zone type III) in our study was relatively small (n = 285), which may limit statistical power to identify meaningful associations. Additionally, our cohort included a higher proportion of patients with cervical canal lesions (n = 15) and older women (≥ 40 years old, 257/448)—these individuals often present with type III transformation zones (invisible SCJ) but may also have other confounding factors (e.g., thin squamous epithelium) that obscure the independent effect of SCJ visibility. Notably, most previous studies focused on general cervical lesions, whereas our research specifically targeted HSIL missed diagnosis, and this difference in focus may involve distinct diagnostic thresholds and lesion characteristics that alter the relevance of SCJ visibility. Furthermore, all colposcopists in our study had 4–12 years of experience, which may reduce variability in evaluating invisible SCJ and thereby weaken its impact on the missed diagnosis rate. Beyond the previously noted factors, another key discrepancy lies in the role of SCJ visibility: while Stuebs et al. [13] and Bai et al. [16] reported 19.8% and 17.5% higher diagnostic accuracy for visible vs. invisible SCJ, our study found no significant difference (p = 0.058). This may also relate to our stricter focus on HSIL missed diagnosis—previous studies included mixed low- and high-grade lesions, where SCJ visibility helps localize early, small lesions. In contrast, HSIL in our cohort (445/448 cases) often presents with more extensive epithelial changes, reducing reliance on SCJ localization. Additionally, our routine use of ECC for type III transformation zones (invisible SCJ, n = 285) may have compensated for visualization limitations, as ECC detected 83 HSIL cases missed by targeted biopsy—this intervention, less emphasized in prior studies, could explain the diminished impact of SCJ visibility in our results.

There is a certain association between the thickness of the squamous epithelium of cervical lesions and the detection of cervical lesions by colposcopy. Firstly, as cervical intraepithelial lesions become more severe, the thickness of the squamous epithelium where the lesions are located increases, increasing the likelihood of observing thick, dense acetowhite epithelium during colposcopy. As shown in previous studies, the thickness of the squamous epithelium at occult HSIL lesions (undetected during colposcopy) is thinner than at identifiable HSIL lesions [21, 22]. Occult HSIL often presents as very small lesions and lacks the typical HSIL colposcopic images, making the lesions easy to misjudge during colposcopic examination [23]. Our study identified that the colposcopic images showing either no or very thin acetowhite epithelium, no mosaicism, no punctate blood vessels, and a positive iodine test were subject to misinterpretation as HSIL. This may be because they are small and occult lesions or lesions in glandular crypts (surrounded by low-grade epithelial lesions or with no lesions), which can present with colposcopic images consistent with either low-grade intraepithelial lesions (LSIL) or a normal appearance (leading to missed diagnosis).

Some studies have also shown that missed diagnosis of HSIL during colposcopy was associated with non-HPV16 infection [16, 21, 24]. Specifically, HPV16-positive cervical HSIL lesions have significantly thicker acetowhite epithelium compared to non-HPV16-positive lesions, making them more easily detectable during colposcopy [16, 21, 24]. This study revealed that patients with non-HPV16 infection had a higher missed diagnosis rate of cervical precancerous lesions during colposcopy compared to those with HPV16 infection, which concurred with the above research findings. Our finding that non-HPV16 infection (including HPV18 and other high-risk subtypes, as well as high-risk HPV-negative cases) increases the risk of missed diagnosis (OR = 0.545, 95% CI: 0.358 ~ 0.832, p = 0.005) contrasts with the findings of Bai et al. [16], who reported similar detection rates for HPV16 and HPV18. This difference may stem from the lesion characteristics associated with non-HPV16 high-risk HPV infection in our cohort: the 196 cases positive for non-HPV16 high-risk subtypes (including HPV18) showed a higher proportion of thin acetowhite epithelium (67.3% vs. 32.1% in HPV16-positive cases) and a higher rate of cervical canal localization (28.6% vs. 12.7% in HPV16-positive cases) in histopathological reviews. These features make lesions caused by non-HPV16 high-risk HPV subtypes less detectable via colposcopy. In contrast, the cohort of Bai et al. [16] had a lower proportion of cervical canal lesions and a smaller sample size of non-HPV16 high-risk HPV-positive cases (n = 89 vs. n = 196 in our study), which may limit their ability to capture the differences in colposcopic detectability between HPV16 and non-HPV16 high-risk subtypes.

With advancing age, there is a reduction in estrogen in the body, which leads to gradual thinning of the squamous epithelium at cervical lesions, with only 9 layers or fewer remaining. The World Health Organization defines this condition as thin-layer HSIL [25]. After the age of 35, the cervical squamous epithelium gradually thins, leading to a gradual decline in the accuracy of colposcopic diagnosis of HSIL+, thus increasing the likelihood of missed diagnoses among older women [16]. Thin-layer HSIL originates from reserve cells in the epithelial basal layer or immature metaplasia cells and shows important characteristics of immature metaplasia. When the squamous epithelial cell layer is only 3–5 layers thick, the lesion resembles immature metaplasia during colposcopy, and HSIL lesions are more difficult to detect [26]. For previous cervical surgery, univariate analysis revealed a significant correlation with missed diagnosis (χ2 = 7.182, p = 0.007), yet this factor was not identified as an independent risk factor in multivariate logistic regression (p = 0.213). Two key factors may underlie this discrepancy: the small number of patients with prior cervical surgery (n = 14) could limit statistical power to detect its independent effect after adjusting for confounding variables such as age, lesion location, and HPV status. Additionally, patients with a history of cervical surgery often present with complex lesion features (e.g., scar tissue, altered cervical anatomy) that may correlate with other independent risk factors—including lesions in the cervical canal—and the multivariate model accounts for these overlapping effects, resulting in the loss of statistical significance. Thus, the association between previous cervical surgery and missed diagnosis is likely mediated by other factors rather than representing a direct independent effect. In our study, we found an elevated rate of missed diagnoses of cervical precancerous lesions in women who were ≥ 40 years old. Further, as age increased, the squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) of the cervix gradually moved inward to the cervical canal (compared to the ectocervix). As a result, the disease-prone areas were also often located in the cervical canal rather than the ectocervix. Colposcopy lacks a clear view of lesions located in the cervical canal, resulting in a blind spot of colposcopic examination. Thus, the rate of inadequate colposcopic diagnoses increased in our study. Moreover, we determined that the rate of additional detection of cervical HSIL via ECC was higher in postmenopausal women than in premenopausal women [27–29]. In our study, the extremely high missed diagnosis rate in women ≥ 55 years old with low-grade cytology (LSIL/ASC-US) should only be interpreted as exploratory due to the small subgroup size.

Full colposcopic images of cervical AIS and early adenocarcinoma often have atypical findings. The most common colposcopic appearance of cervical AIS is that of an immature transformation zone. However, after acetic acid application, scattered patchy thin acetowhite epithelia can be observed. Due to the atypical colposcopic appearance of cervical AIS, it is clinically easy to misidentify these lesions as normal metaplastic epithelia. Barbara et al. reported that 5% of cervical AIS lesions were strictly confined to the cervical canal, 53% were located in the ectocervix, and another 38% of lesions were found in both, leading to the conclusion that 91% of cervical AIS lesions should be identifiable by colposcopy [30]. However, the sensitivity of colposcopy for detecting cervical AIS was only 9.8%, with a negative predictive value of 12.5% [31]. Therefore, colposcopy may not adequately detect cervical AIS as suggested by its clinical implications. It is well recognized that most AIS lesions present with dense acetowhite epithelium in the endocervical epithelium (often just above the squamous-columnar junction, SCJ) and may be associated with high-grade cervical glandular intraepithelial neoplasia (HGCIN); however, a percentage of AIS cases lack specific colposcopic findings. Consistent with this, three cervical AIS cases in our study showed no colposcopic abnormalities, further highlighting the limitations of colposcopy in detecting glandular lesions.

Clinical implications: Despite the study’s limitations (retrospective design, short follow-up, and small subgroup sizes), the identified independent risk factors (age ≥ 40 years, low-grade TCT abnormalities, non-HPV16 infection, and cervical canal lesions) can inform risk-stratified clinical practice to reduce missed diagnoses. First, for patients with ≥ 2 overlapping risk factors (e.g., ≥ 40 years + low-grade TCT abnormalities + non-HPV16 infection), clinicians should consider more aggressive biopsy strategies (e.g., routine endocervical curettage [ECC] regardless of colposcopic impression) to cover cervical canal blind spots. Second, for patients with cervical canal lesions (or ECC-indicated features), colposcopists should prioritize ECC to complement targeted biopsies, as these lesions are highly prone to missed diagnosis. Third, these risk factors can be integrated into clinical decision tools to optimize biopsy—avoiding unnecessary procedures in low-risk groups (e.g., < 25 years + HPV16 positivity, missed diagnosis rate 20.0%) while enhancing vigilance in high-risk groups. Importantly, these recommendations are exploratory and should not replace existing ASCCP/IFCPC guidelines; they require validation in prospective multicenter studies before routine clinical application, given the lack of long-term progression data in our cohort. Notably, the split-specimen sampling strategy used in this study avoids the potential interference of sequential cytology-first sampling on HPV test accuracy. Standardized single-sample split testing for concurrent cytology and HPV genotyping ensures the integrity of cervical exfoliated cell samples and the accuracy of both assays; even in clinical sequential sampling settings, standardized operation does not cause significant interference with HPV test results.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that require acknowledgment. First, this was a retrospective cohort study, which inherently carries the risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors. To mitigate selection bias, we enrolled all consecutive eligible patients during the study period without arbitrary exclusion, and multivariate logistic regression was used to adjust for key potential confounders (e.g., age, TCT results, HPV status, and lesion location). However, unmeasured factors such as smoking history, immune status, and prior screening frequency may still exist, which could influence the results. Second, the relatively short study inclusion period (May 2023 to October 2024) only allowed for cross-sectional analysis of missed diagnosis rates, and no long-term follow-up data on progression to invasive cervical cancer or the need for re-treatment were available. This significantly limits our ability to evaluate the clinical consequences of missed diagnoses (e.g., delayed intervention and disease progression), which are critical for translating the findings into clinical practice. Third, subgroup analyses, particularly for women aged ≥ 55 years with low-grade cytological abnormalities, were based on small sample sizes, and the results should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. Fourth, inter-observer agreement between colposcopists and pathologists was not formally assessed (e.g., using kappa statistics), which is a methodological limitation given the subjective nature of colposcopic evaluation. Fifth, inter-observer agreement between colposcopists and pathologists was not formally assessed using kappa statistics—a key methodological limitation given the subjective nature of colposcopic evaluation and histopathological interpretation. This may introduce variability that could affect the reliability of diagnostic outcomes and missed diagnosis rate calculations. HPV type analysis was limited by grouping non-HPV16 high-risk subtypes into a single broad category. Due to the small sample size of individual non-HPV16 subtypes (e.g., HPV31, HPV33, HPV45) in our cohort, we were unable to perform detailed subtype-specific analysis to explore their distinct associations with colposcopic missed diagnosis. This lack of nuance may obscure subtype-specific differences in lesion characteristics and colposcopic detectability. Finally, despite adhering to IFCPC/ASCCP guidelines for biopsies and ECC, inherent variability in clinical practice may have influenced the diagnostic yield, which could affect the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, although our sample size of 448 patients meets the pre-specified statistical power requirements for the primary analyses, this is a single-center retrospective cohort, and the sample size is still insufficient to perform detailed subtype-specific analyses for individual non-HPV16 high-risk HPV genotypes, as well as robust stratified analyses for rare subgroups (e.g., patients with adenocarcinoma in situ). Large-scale, multi-center prospective cohorts are needed in future studies to further validate the stability and generalizability of our findings.

Future directions

To address the aforementioned limitations and strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings, future research should focus on the following aspects. First, prospective multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the identified risk factors (age ≥ 40 years, low-grade cytological abnormalities, non-HPV16 infection, and cervical canal lesions) for missed colposcopic diagnoses. Second, standardizing biopsy protocols and formally assessing inter-observer agreement (e.g., kappa statistics) will reduce practice variability and improve the reliability of results. Second, to address the limitations of retrospective design, prospective multi-center studies with standardized data collection (including detailed records of potential confounders like smoking history and prior screening frequency) are needed to validate the identified risk factors and minimize selection bias. Third, extending the follow-up period to 3–5 years will allow evaluation of the long-term clinical consequences of missed diagnoses, such as progression to invasive cancer, recurrence, and the need for re-treatment—this will enhance the clinical relevance of the findings and guide clinical decision-making. Fourth, future studies should prioritize detailed analysis of individual non-HPV16 high-risk subtypes (e.g., HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV45) with larger sample sizes. This will help clarify their distinct lesion characteristics (e.g., epithelial thickness, location) and associations with colposcopic missed diagnosis, addressing the nuance lacking in the current broad-category analysis. Finally, developing risk-adapted biopsy strategies based on the identified factors may help optimize diagnostic yield while minimizing unnecessary procedures, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the following independent variables were linked to missed colposcopic diagnoses of cervical precancerous lesions: ≥40 years of age, low-grade cytological abnormalities, non-HPV16 positivity, and lesions localized to the cervical canal. Notably, non-HPV16 positivity was analyzed as a broad category (encompassing HPV18 and other high-risk subtypes), which lacks nuance regarding the distinct roles of individual subtypes—this limits the ability to draw subtype-specific conclusions and should be interpreted cautiously. These results suggest that these risk factors could be beneficial in stratifying patients at a higher risk of missed diagnosis, though they should not be used as an absolute basis for clinical decision-making. In addition, the increased missed diagnosis rate (67.9%) in patients aged ≥ 55 years with low-grade cytological abnormalities should be interpreted with extreme caution. This subgroup included only 53 cases, and the previously mentioned erroneous 71.8% rate (based on n = 7) has been corrected. The finding is exploratory and not suitable for direct clinical application or generalization. Overall, the findings provide initial evidence that risk-adapted biopsy strategies may increase the diagnostic yield; however, prospective multicenter studies with standardized protocols and long-term follow-up are necessary before clinical practice can move to routine testing.
