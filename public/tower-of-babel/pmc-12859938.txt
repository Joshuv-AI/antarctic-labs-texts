HMGB1-enhanced autophagic degradation of E-cadherin facilitates mesothelial trans-differentiation and postoperative peritoneal adhesion
Authors: Xuetao Yuan, Shuting Yang, Chao Qu, Rui Zhang, Yaru Ren, Yanjun Sun, Enmeng Li, Gan Li, Junxiang Zhang, Jiayin Du, Tianli Shen, Zhengdong Jiang, Xuqi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Postoperative peritoneal adhesion (PA), a prevalent complication of abdominal surgery, arises from aberrant healing of the injured peritoneum. This process involves mesothelial cell trans-differentiation into myofibroblast-like cells, driving peritoneal matrix remodeling and pathological fibrous band formation between organs. Existing therapeutic strategies to prevent PA exhibit limited efficacy, underscoring the need for previously unrecognized mechanistic insights into PA pathogenesis. Here, we elucidate the role of autophagy in mesothelial cell transition and PA development. Utilizing a murine PA model coupled with comprehensive in vitro analyses, we demonstrate that HMGB1-mediated autophagy facilitates TGF-β mediated mesothelial cell trans-differentiation. Specifically, autophagic degradation of E-cadherin counteracts TGF-β-driven mesothelial transition. Pharmacological activation of autophagy via rapamycin or recombinant HMGB1 potentiated this transition, whereas HMGB1 inhibitor (glycyrrhizin) or autophagy suppressor (spautin-1) markedly attenuated it. Crucially, early postoperative administration of glycyrrhizin or spautin-1 significantly reduced PA severity. Our findings unveil autophagy and HMGB1 as pivotal regulator targets of mesothelial plasticity during PA formation, provide mechanistic insights into peritoneum repair, and propose novel therapeutic avenues for PA prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04572-w.

Introduction

Peritoneal adhesion (PA) after abdominal surgery remains a highly challenging complication of abdominal surgery. It serves as the major etiological factor for postoperative intestinal obstruction, female reproductive dysfunction, and chronic abdominal discomfort, which has imposed a substantial burden on socioeconomic and personal well-being [1–3]. PAs originate from aberrant peritoneal healing processes, which involves mesothelial cells trans-differentiation into myofibroblast-like cells, driving peritoneal matrix remodeling and pathological fibrous band formation between organs [4, 5]. This mesothelial plasticity is mechanistically governed by TGF-β, ROS, et, al. within the peritoneal inflammatory microenvironment. Targeting the dysregulated repair, emerging anti-adhesion strategies focus on engineering materials for physical segregation of injured peritoneum and regulation of inflammatory responses [6, 7]. However, the effects of these remain unsatisfactory, and a more detailed elucidation of the mechanisms related to PAs pathogenesis is required.

High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), an evolutionarily conserved chromatin-associated protein, exerts biphasic nuclear functions through dynamic DNA bending and histone interaction, and coordinates transcriptional activation [8]. Under physiological conditions, HMGB1 exhibits strict nuclear confinement through continuous nuclear localization signal-mediated import; however, pathological stimuli such as oxidative stress or cytokine signaling induce its cytoplasmic translocation, followed by active secretion through non-classical vesicular pathways [8]. This typical damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) orchestrates sterile inflammation and chemoattractant signaling (such as CXCL12/CXCR4 cascade) in tissue damage microenvironments [8–10]. In recent years, HMGB1 has garnered increasing attention from scholars and clinicians as a therapeutic target in diseases such as muscular dystrophy [11], liver fibrosis [12, 13], mesothelioma [14], and cognitive impairment [15], indicating its important role in disorders as inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) disruption.

Autophagy is an intracellular degradation process, it function as a cellular homeostat to counteract stress through clearance of senescent organelles and proteotoxic aggregates as well as maintaining stabilized transcription and proliferation [16]. Pathological perturbations, such as oncogenic insults and oxidative stress, could induce autophagy flux reprogramming to change the cellular functional status. For example, tumor cells degrade major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-1) via autophagy to evade the immune response [17]. Therefore, the regulation of cellular autophagy has significant therapeutic potential [18]. Multiple studies have reported the dual role of autophagy in promoting or inhibiting extracellular matrix remodeling and accumulation [19, 20], with these effects being highly cell- and tissue-specific. For example, autophagy helps promote the repair of damaged tendons [21], the process of chronic pancreatic fibrosis [22] and peritoneal fibrosis [23], whereas impaired autophagic activity accelerates the progression of renal fibrosis and intrauterine adhesions [24, 25]. Collectively, elucidating the context-dependent functions of autophagy across different disease settings will deepen our understanding of autophagy in tissue homeostasis and pathology.

In this study, we aim to deepen the existing knowledge of mechanism of PA formation by elucidating whether autophagy of peritoneal mesothelial cells is involved as well as how it affects postoperative PA pathogenesis. The research showed that elevated HMGB1 levels in the inflammatory peritoneal microenvironment enhanced the autophagic activity of injured peritoneum during the early stages of postoperative PA formation. This increased autophagy flux could promote the activity of TGF-β/Smad signaling in mesothelial cells by degrading the cell membrane protein E-cadherin, accelerating the trans-differentiation process and promoting the pathogenesis of PAs, indicating mesothelial autophagy is vital for the formation of PAs after surgery. Our research provides novel biological and theoretical references for the pathogenesis of PAs and contributes new ideas for strategies to reduce and prevent the pathogenesis of postoperative PAs.

Materials and methods

Adhesion models and treatment

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks (N = 60) were acquired from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Xi’an, China). All mice underwent a minimum 7-day acclimation period in stable chambers (room temperature, 50% relative humidity, programmed 12-hour light/dark cycles) to ensure environmental adaptation.

The adhesion model was prepared using the cecum–abdominal wall friction method, as described in our previous studies [26, 27]. Briefly, isoflurane was used to induce anesthesia by inhalation at a concentration of 4% (1 L/min), after mice entered the surgical deep anesthesia period (unconscious, steady breathing and heart rate, weak corneal and pedal reflex), the concentration of isoflurane was set to 2% (1 L/min) to maintain anesthesia. Mice were placed on a heating pad (37℃) to maintain body temperature. An incision of approximately 15 mm was created along the abdominal midline to expose the cecum. A small surgical brush was used to rub the adjacent abdominal wall 5–20 times at the root of the cecum. The same method was used to rub the adjacent abdominal wall until punctate bleeding appeared on the tissue surface; then, the anatomical position of the cecum was restored. Subsequently, a sterilized small silicone hose was placed on the opposite side of the friction area and approximately 0.7–1 cm away from the midline incision as a drainage tube. The inner opening of the drainage tube was placed in the friction area, and the outer opening was closed and buried subcutaneously. The outer opening of the drainage tube was opened 8–24 h after surgery, and approximately 50 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution was injected into it. The solution was slowly sucked back using a syringe, and approximately 50–100 µL of fluid could be obtained, and the drainage tube was immediately removed. An image of drainage tube placement is shown in Figure S1.

For treatment, 50 mg/kg glycyrrhizin (SM6092, Beyotime) or 1 mg/kg spautin-1 (SC5498, Beyotime) was slowly injected into the friction area before suturing the incision, and a constant intraperitoneal injection was administered daily in the following 3 days. The injection site was maintained far away from the friction area of the abdominal wall to avoid potential interference caused by injection injury. The solvent of the drug (i.e., PBS) was used as a control. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation 8 h, 24 h, or 7 days after surgery to collect the specimens. For the negative control group, the cecal wall was used as a specimen.

Evaluation of adhesion scores

Following euthanasia, a U-shaped incision was created along the abdominal midline of each mouse, resulting in sufficient exposure of peritoneal cavity. The adhesions formed on the specimens were blindly evaluated. The scoring criteria are based on the PA adhesion scoring system shown in Table 1 [28, 29].

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining and Masson trichrome

We used 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) to fix the tissue samples (BL539A, BioSharp, Beijing, China) for at least 24 h, which were then subjected to gradient dehydration and paraffin embedding. Next, the tissue samples were sliced into 5-micron thickness, which were then processed with H&E or Masson staining. These samples were microscopically examined using bright-field optics.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry (IHC) of the tissue samples

Paraffin slices were deparaffinized in anhydrous ethanol and hydrated in a PBS buffer. After antigen repair (P0081, Beyotime), the slices were soaked in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at room temperature under light-shielding conditions for 25 min, rinsed with PBS three times for 3 min each, incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin (A8020, Solarbio) for a duration of 30 min. Slices were then overnight incubated (12 h/4°C) with primary antibodies. After washing with PBS, Alexa Fluor 594 conjugate (for immunofluorescence) or biotin-labeled (for IHC) secondary antibodies were used for another incubation. For immunofluorescence, DAPI staining solution (G1012, Servicebio) was administered to the tissue slices and incubated for 10 min, followed by sealing with anti-fluorescence quenching tablets. For IHC, 3,3’-diaminobenzidine staining solution was administrated dropwise to the slices, and the positive staining presented a visible color from yellow to brown. H&E re-staining of cell nuclei was performed for 3 min. After dehydration and sealing, the slices were microscopically examined. ImageJ software was used for image analysis.

The primary antibodies involved in the experiment include: anti-TGF-β1 antibody (21898-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-collagen I antibody (14695-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-HMGB1 antibody (380710, Zenbio). The secondary antibodies used were the HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin (Ig)G antibody (GB23303, Servicebio) and CoraLite594—conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody (SA00013-3, Proteintech).

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Body fluids were collected as described above. The collected fluids were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant—devoid of cells and tissue debris—was collected. Subsequently, the human/mouse/rat HMGB1 ELISA Kit (PH406, Beyotime) was used to determine the amount of HMGB1 following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell culture and treatment

Human peritoneal mesothelial cells (HMrSV5, acquired from Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences), were maintained in the Minimum Essential Medium (C11095500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (16140071, Gibco) and 1% penicillin - streptomycin antibiotic solution (15070063, Gibco) under controlled environmental conditions (37 ℃ and 5% CO2).

The cells were induced to mesothelial trans-differentiation under the treatment of TGF-β1 (HY-P7118, MedChemExpress) at a concentration of 5 ng/mL. For the intervention of autophagy, 50 µM of glycyrrhizin (SM6092, Beyotime), 1 µM of spautin-1 (SC5498, Beyotime), 50 nM of rapamycin (HY-10219, MedChemExpress), 10 µM of hydroxychloroquine (HCQ; HY-W031727, MedChemExpress), or 100 ng/mL of recombinant HMGB1 (rHMGB1) was added to the medium 2 h before TGF-β1 treatment. The aforementioned treatments slightly affected cell viability. (Figure S2C)

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed through CCK-8 assays. HMrSV5 cells were plated at 2 × 10³ per well in a 96-well plate, cultured for at least 24 h to ensure proper cell adhesion, and then maintained in fresh medium supplemented with varying concentrations of glycyrrhizin, spautin-1, rapamycin, or HCQ for 24 h. The optical density values at 450-nm (OD450) were measured after incubation with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (BS350B, Biosharp) for 100 min.

Immunofluorescence of cells

The cell samples were soaked in 4% PFA at room temperature, fixed for at least 30 min, blocked with TWEEN-20 tris base buffer containing 1% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 30 min, and punched with 0.3% TritonX-100 for 30 min. For cytoskeletal staining (phalloidin), the cells were incubated with phalloidin rhodamine (C2207S, Beyotime) at room temperature in the dark for 60 min. For cell immunofluorescence staining, the primary antibody was added and incubated at 4 ℃ for 12 h; then, the secondary antibody was introduced and incubated at room temperature in a dark environment for a duration of 1 h, following with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime) incubation for 5 min. Images were acquired utilizing an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DM2500; Wetzlar, Germany).

The primary antibodies include: anti-HMGB1 antibody (380710, Zenbio), anti-E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-vimentin (10366-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-P120 catenin (p120ctn; 66208-1-Ig, Proteintech), and anti–α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (14395-1-AP, Proteintech) antibodies.

The secondary antibodies include: coraLite594—conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody (SA00013-3, Proteintech)—and CoraLite488—goat anti-rabbit recombinant secondary antibody (H + L) (SA00013-2, Proteintech).

mCherry-EGFP-LC3 plasmid transfection

The mCherry-EGFP-LC3 plasmid (P0446) was obtained from MiaoLingBio, China. The HMrSV5 was transfected using the Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L30000008, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 12 h following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cells were cultured in a fresh medium for 48 h for subsequent experiments.

Confocal Immunofluorescence

The cells were cultivated using a 35-mm confocal specialized cell culture dish (801002, NEST CHINA), and plasmid transfection, drug treatment, and immunofluorescence staining was performed following the steps described above. Images were captured with a laser confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP5) subsequently analyzed with LAS AF Lite 2.6.3 software.

Monodansylcadaverine (MDC) assay

MDC was used as an acidophilic fluorescent probe to specifically label autophagosomes by ion trapping, allowing cellular autophagy to be detected. An autophagy staining assay kit with MDC (C3018S, Beyotime) was applied for staining the cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescent imaging was conducted on an inverted microscope system (Leica DM2500, Wetzlar, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy

Autophagosomes were visualized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The cells were fixed using glutaraldehyde, washed with a dimethyl arsenate buffer, fixed in 1% OsO4, dehydrated using a gradient concentration of ethanol, and embedded in Epon resin. Ultrathin slices were prepared using an ultramicrotome, mounted on TEM - specific copper mesh, and then contrasted with agent of 8% uranyl acetate and lead citrate solutions. Autophagosomes were observed using the Hitachi HT 7800 TEM.

PCR analysis of mRNA

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed for the evaluation of the gene transcription levels. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times. The total RNA extraction were conducted with RNAiso Plus (9108; TaKaRa). The final RNA concentration is controlled at 200-1000ng/ml. The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized according to the steps outlined in the manual of the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, ThermoFisher). In short, cDNA synthesizing was performed using a 20ul reaction system, and the temperature cycling parameters were set at 25 ℃ for 10 min, 42 ℃ for 15 min, 85 ℃, for 5 min, and 4 ℃ for cooling and storage. iQ™ Supermix (1708882AP, Bio-Rad) was used to conduct quantitative PCR (SYBR Green). iQ™ Supermix (1708882AP, Bio-Rad) was used to conduct quantitative PCR. In short, reverse transcription reactions were performed using a 10ul reaction system, and the temperature cycling parameters were set at 95 ℃ for 30 s in the initial denaturation; 95 ℃ for 10 s, 30 ℃ for 30 s and 72 ℃, for 30 s in PCR cycling (40 cycles); for melt curve, every 0.5 ° C lasted 5 s. Western blot

Western blot analysis was conducted to assess the expression levels of proteins. For the extraction of the total protein, RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (ST506-2 Beyotime) and phosphatase inhibitor (P1081, Beyotime) were used for lysing the cells or tissues. Nuclear and Cytodynamic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027, Beyotime) were used to obtain Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. The extracted protein lysate was electrophoretized on a sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel, and the resolved proteins were electrotransferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. After nonspecific binding sites were blocked by the protein-free rapid blocking buffer (PS108P, Epizyme, China), the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody at 4 ℃ for 12 h. Next, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h.

The primary antibodies used were the anti-smad2/3 (382472,Zenbio), anti p-smad2/3(Thr8) (251795, Zenbio), anti-MAPLC3B (381544, Zenbio), anti-SQSTM1/P62 (R25788, Zenbio), anti-HMGB1 (380710, Zenbio), anti-vimentin (10366-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-P120 catenin (66208-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-α-SMA (14395-1-AP, Proteintech). anti-laminB1 (YT5180, Immunoway), anti-β-tubulin (YM3030, Immunoway), anti-GAPDH antibodies (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), and anti-H3 antibodies (R381432, Zenbio). The secondary antibodies used were HRP–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (GB23303, Servicebio) and HRP–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibodies (GB23301, Servicebio).

Analysis of RNA sequencing data

For the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, subsets of the adhesion tissue transcriptome dataset GSE123413 at 0 and 8 h were selected, and differentially expressed genes were analyzed using the Limma package in R (v.4.4.2). KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analysis results were obtained through an online analysis website (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), which were visualized in R the using ggplot2 package. For single-cell sequencing data, the adhesion tissue single-cell sequencing dataset GSE186658 was selected, and dimensionality reduction clustering was performed using Seraut 5.2.1 and Harmony 1.2.3 packages. Subgroup annotation was performed using CellMaker 2.0 (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/index.html). Cellchat analysis, geneset enrichment analysis and pseudo-time analysis were carried out based on the following R packages: AUCell 1.26, clusterProfiler 4.12.6, msigdbr 10.0.2, CellChat 1.6.1 and monocle3 1.3.7.

Statistical analysis

All measurement data are represented as means ± standard deviation. The correlation results were analyzed through linear regression, and the comparison results for multiple groups were analyzed using One-Way analysis of variance for data of normal distribution, with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc multiple testing; and Kruskal-Wallis test were used for data of non-normal distribution, which was described in the figure legend. Statistical significance was deemed to be achieved when P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Results

HMGB1 levels elevate within peritoneal inflammation microenvironment during the early stage of postoperative PA formation

The first 24 h after surgical injury is the inflammatory exudative phase of PA formation, during which acute inflammatory reactions and cell damages occur [26]. To investigate early changes in the peritoneal tissue, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of early-phase PA formation was conducted on the sequencing dataset of the mouse PA tissue (data points up to 0 h and12 h after the operation were selected for the analysis) (GSE123413) [30]. The results revealed activation of inflammatory cascades, and the pathway named advanced glycation end-product (AGE)–receptor for AGE (RAGE) signaling pathway in diabetic complications, emerging among the top 10 enriched pathways (Fig. 1A) was found activated, which is considered HMGB1-related for RAGE being a high-affinity receptor of HMGB1. Additionally, the TNF-alpha signaling pathway, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction were reported to be HMGB1 involved pathways as well [31–34]. (Fig. 1A) Therefore, an investigation of HMGB1 was performed by western blot (Fig. 1B) to quantitatively detect the HMGB1 levels in peritoneal adhesion tissue, and Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) to detect the amount of HMGB1 in collected mouse postoperative peritoneal fluid (Fig. 1C and figure S1A), the results indicated a peak expression of HMGB1 around PA tissues 8 h after surgery. Furthermore, by changing the friction scale on the cecum and abdominal wall to form different degrees of peritoneal injury, we obtained mice with different adhesion scores (Table 1), and the quantitative results of HMGB1 in the peritoneal fluid approximately 8 h after surgery showed a positive correlation (R2 = 0.9017, P < 0.0001) with the PA scores. (Fig. 1D) Additionally, tissue IHC staining showed areas of high abundance of HMGB1 surrounding the PA interface (8 h after surgery) (Fig. 1E, F), and tissue immunofluorescent showed HMGB1 cytoplasmic translocation within such areas. (Fig. 1G, H) The above results confirmed that HMGB1 was significantly enriched in the peritoneal inflammation microenvironment during the early stage of PA formation.

Elevated HMGB1s facilitates TGF-β1 mediated trans-differentiation of peritoneal mesothelial cells

To further explore the role of HMGB1 in the early formation of PAs, we analyzed single-cell sequencing data from mouse PA tissues (public dataset: GSE186658) [29]. Mesothelial cells emerged as pivotal contributor of ECM production and remodeling [35], which were successfully identified. (Fig. 2A-B) [36] KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of mesothelial cells revealed activation of extracellular matrix remodeling pathways (Figure S1B-C), consistent with previous reports that mesothelial cells undergo trans-differentiation into myofibroblasts to mediate PA formation [27, 37]. Notably, advanced glycation end-product (AGE)–receptor for AGE (RAGE) signaling pathway in diabetic complications, which is HMGB1 associated, was enriched as well, indicating that HMGB1 is involved in the trans-differentiation process of mesothelial cells.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis demonstrated transient upregulation of HMGB1 expression during the transition from mesothelial cells to fibroblasts, (Figure S1 D-E, Fig. 2C-D) accompanied by dynamic changes in extracellular matrix production. (Fig. 2D) Hence, we treated the human peritoneal mesothelial HMrSV5 with recombined HMGB1 (rHMGB1, 100ng/ml). The result showed no significant differences, except for a decreased cell size. (Fig. 2E) However, co-treatment with TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) and rHMGB1 (100 ng/mL) accelerated trans-differentiation compared to TGF-β1 alone, evidenced by morphological changes in the cell shape from pebble-shaped to fusiform-shaped (Figs. 2E) [38] and altered expression of differentiation markers (α-SMA↑, Vimentin↑, E-cadherin↓) [27, 37] (Figs. 2F) Significant changes in the cellular morphology and increased amount of cytoskeleton could be observed as well. (Figure S2A) Notably, TGF-β1 - a principal mediator of mesothelial trans-differentiation [27, 36, 38, 39], showed predominant expression in macrophages and neutrophils, (Fig. 2G) with spatial enrichment at injured peritoneal interfaces, as shown in IHC images. (Fig. 2H) These findings collectively demonstrate that HMGB1 facilitates TGF-β1-mediated mesothelial trans-differentiation during PA pathogenesis.

HMGB1 enhances TGF-β1 induced autophagic flux of peritoneal mesothelial cell

Building upon the finding that HMGB1 potentiates TGF-β1-induced mesothelial trans-differentiation, we investigated the underlying mechanism. The observed HMGB1-mediated reduction in HMrSV5 cell size (Fig. 2E) prompted us to explore potential autophagy involvement. To verify this hypothesis, we examined autophagy activity of PA tissue, the result of LC3B-II and SQSTM1/P62 showed an enhanced autophagy activity that was attenuated by glycyrrhizin (HMGB1 inhibitor) [40]. (Fig. 3A) Hence, in vitro assessment of monodansylcadaverine (MDC) assays were performed, and the results demonstrated that both TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) and rHMGB1 (100 ng/mL) significantly increased autophagic activity in HMrSV5 cells (Fig. 3B and G). To further confirm this, plasmids containing mCherry-EGFP-LC3 were transfected into HMrSV5 cells, and elevated autophagic flux could be observed after treatment with TGF-β1 (5 ng/ml) or rHMGB1 (100 ng/ml) for 8 h under confocal fluorescence microscopy, (Fig. 3C and H) and the autophagosome was visualized through transmission electron microscopy (TEM). (Fig. 3D) Notably, co-treatment with TGF-β1 and rHMGB1 produced synergistic effects on autophagy activation compared to TGF-β1 monotherapy for 8 h (Figure S2B). Both glycyrrhizin and spautin-1 (autophagy inhibitor) effectively reversed these effects (Figure S2B). Therefore, we then explored HMGB1-TGF-β1 interplay. Confocal fluorescence microscopy imaging revealed TGF-β1-induced cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1 in HMrSV5 cells (H2O2 was set as positive control). (Fig. 3E) This was also confirmed in protein levels through the nuclear–cytoplasmic protein separation of HMrSV5 cells. (Fig. 3F) These in vitro findings suggest that HMGB1 is involved in TGF-β1-mediated autophagy, and exogenous HMGB1 amplifies TGF-β1’s autophagic effects.

Pharmacological autophagy Inhibition alleviates TGF-β1-mediated mesothelial trans-differentiation

The observed synergy between rHMGB1-enhanced autophagy and TGF-β1-driven mesothelial trans-differentiation prompted our further investigation of their inner relationship. Pharmacological inhibition of autophagy using spautin-1 (1 µM), a specific autophagy inhibitor targeting USP10/USP13-mediated stabilization of the Vps34/Beclin1 complex [41], attenuated TGF-β1-induced trans-differentiation in HMrSV5 cells, whereas treatment with rapamycin to activate autophagy significantly enhanced this process of trans-differentiation. Notably, the Vps34/Beclin1 complex serves as a critical autophagy initiator known to interact with HMGB1 and mediates formation of autophagosomes [8, 16, 41, 42]. Similarly, rHMGB1 (100 ng/mL) co-treatment enhanced TGF-β1 effects, while glycyrrhizin (50 µM) suppressed differentiation. Interestingly, both inhibitors (spautin-1 and glycyrrhizin) partially reversed the synergistic effects of TGF-β1 + rHMGB1 co-treatment. The above results were observed through cell immunofluorescence, (Figs. 4A-C) validated at the mRNA (Figs. 4D-E) and protein levels, (Figs. 4F) indicating that pharmacological intervention at either node (autophagy/HMGB1) can mitigate TGF-β1-driven mesothelial trans-differentiation.

Autophagy induced E-Cadherin loss promotes TGF-β/Smad mediated mesothelial trans-differentiation

Building on the finding that facilitate TGF-β1 mediated mesothelial trans-differentiation by enhancing autophagy, we evaluated the TGF-β/Smad signaling. Immunoblotting revealed significant phosphorylation of SMAD2/3 (p-SMAD2/3), indicating significant activation of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. (Fig. 5A) To elucidate how autophagy modulates this signaling, we focused on E-cadherin - a known molecule for cells to resist TGF-β1 signaling [43], The accelerated E-cadherin downregulation observed with rHMGB1 + TGF-β1 co-treatment versus TGF-β1 alone (Fig. 2F) suggested autophagy-dependent degradation. To confirm this, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) was used to inhibit autophagosome-lysosome fusion [44, 45], thus the substances in the autophagic vesicles to be degraded could be intercepted and gradually accumulated in the cytoplasm. Based on this, we observed that E-cadherin in HMrSV5 cells treated with TGF-β1 and HCQ exhibited intracellular dot-like (or vesicular-like) accumulation, and this cumulation was more significant with rapamycin or rHMGB1 treatment, (Fig. 5B) suggesting that E-cadherin is degraded in an autophagy-dependent manner.

Discussion

In this study, we established a novel pathological mechanistic link between HMGB1-mediated autophagy potentiation and TGF-β/Smad-driven peritoneal adhesion (PA) pathogenesis. (Figs. 8)

Fig. 8HMGB1-enhanced autophagic degradation of E-Cadherin facilitates mesothelial trans-differentiation and postoperative peritoneal adhesion

HMGB1-enhanced autophagic degradation of E-Cadherin facilitates mesothelial trans-differentiation and postoperative peritoneal adhesion

PA is considered an inevitable postoperative complication, and its incidence is attributed to diseases such as chronic abdominal pain, female infertility, and intestinal obstruction [1–3]. The presence of PAs is the result of abnormal healing of the damaged peritoneum, manifested as the fibrous tissue between the visceral peritoneum and the visceral/parietal peritoneum, or adhesion bands [4]. Adhesion bands are essentially made up of the abnormal deposition of the extracellular matrix that mostly produced by mesothelial cells [35]. During the formation of PA, mesothelial cells undergoes myofibroblast-like transformation, which exhibits changes in cellular morphology, cytoskeleton, and production in ECM, and are regulated by reactive oxygen species, TGF-β/Smad signaling axis, et al. [36] Among these factors, TGF-β1-induced mesothelial trans-differentiation could be stably simulated in vitro [38]. Treatment for inhibiting TGF-β signaling can alleviate the mesothelial trans-differentiation and reduce the formation of adhesion bands, which is considered a potential effective approach to prevent the occurrence of postoperative PAs [27].

In this study, we found that HMGB1 was highly enriched in the inflammatory microenvironment of the peritoneum after abdominal surgery, showing positive correlation with the severity of PA. Pseudo-time analysis based on monocle 3 showed the involvement of HMGB1 in the mesothelial cell transition, as evidenced by changes in the cellular production of ECM and transient HMGB1 elevation, revealing the positive role of HMGB1 in mesothelial cell mediated ECM remolding after peritoneum damage.

HMGB1 is a type of DAMP and an autophagy activator [8, 9], which can activate autophagy through extracellular AGER binding or intracellular Beclin1 interaction [8, 9]. High levels of autophagy in the adhesion tissue can be alleviated by glycyrrhizin, the inhibitor of HMGB1, indicating regulatory role of autophagy activity in HMGB1. Glycyrrhizin also alleviated the formation of PAs, indicating the involvement of HMGB1 in the formation of PAs. Notably, though HMGB1 functions as a chemokine to attract inflammatory cells and promote tissue inflammatory infiltration [48, 49], treatment with glycyrrhizin slightly reducing the infiltration of inflammatory cells lacked statistical significance. Our research also demonstrates that TGF-β1 can induce HMGB1 cytoplasmic translocation and promote autophagy. In addition, we simulated the enrichment of HMGB1 in the inflammatory microenvironment after peritoneal injury by exogenous administration of TG-β1 and rHMGB1. We found that extracellular rHMGB1 can also promote autophagy, enhance TGF-β1-induced autophagy, and further accelerate TGF-β1-induced cell phenotype transformation. Here, future research can further elucidate whether endogenous and exogenous HMGB1 promote mesothelial cell autophagy through the same mechanism. Interestingly, the autophagy inhibitor spautin-1 achieved therapeutic effects similar to those of glycyrrhizin, demonstrating the positive role of HMGB1-mediated autophagy within mesothelial cells in the formation of PAs.

Autophagy is a conserved cellular clearance mechanism, and serves dual roles in stress adaptation and pathological progression [16]. However, the cell demands for certain functional status are autophagy dependent. For example, nucleophagy provides the basis for chemotherapy resistance in tumor cells [50], tumor cells degrade MHC-I through autophagy to achieve immune evasion [17], and the autophagy-dependent degradation of N-cadherin reduces cell membrane tension, which provides a driving force for HPCAL1-mediated iron death of cells [51]. In diseases characterized by extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and remodeling, the role of autophagy remains highly dependent on the specific organ and pathological context. Distinct autophagy-related regulatory patterns have been observed in the uterus, kidney, pancreas, and tendons, leading to markedly different disease outcomes [21, 22, 24, 25]. Therefore, autophagy cannot be regarded as exerting a uniform effect during fibrosis; rather, its function should be interpreted in a context-dependent manner. Interestingly, autophagy has been reported to be involved in TGF-β1-induced chronic peritoneal fibrosis, and using 3-methyladenine to inhibit autophagy shows therapeutic efficacy [23]. In our study, elevated autophagy flux was observed the during TGF-β1 induced mesothelial trans-differentiation, autophagy inhibitor (spautin-1) or HMGB1 inhibitor (glycyrrhizin) exhibited efficacy of blocking this process, indicating TGF-β1-induced mesothelial trans-differentiation is autophagy- and HMGB1-dependent. Notably, spautin-1 is specific autophagy inhibitor targeting USP10/USP13-mediated stabilization of the Vps34/Beclin1 complex [41], which serve as a critical autophagy initiator known to interact with HMGB1 and mediate formation of autophagosomes [8, 16, 41, 42]. In addition, loss of E-cadherin in mesothelial cell during the trans-differentiation process was faster under the additional treatment of rHMGB1. Lysosomal blockade by hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) revealing autophagosome-accumulated E-cadherin degradation indicating that E-cadherin undergoes autophagy-dependent degradation under TGF-β1 signaling.

E-cadherin, one of the mediators of intercellular junctions, is a known molecule for cells to resist TGF-β1 signaling. Mechanistically, E-cadherin forms a stable complex with P120ctn in the cell membrane and further recruits RhoA to prevent TGF-β1 from increasing RhoA-mediated SMAD3 phosphorylation and maintain the epithelial phenotype of cells [43, 46, 47]. Notably, our study showed that rHMGB1 or rapamycin significantly promote the decrease in P120ctn abundance caused by TGF-β1. Consistent with the loss of E-cadherin, inhibiting autophagy alleviated P120ctn decrease, demonstrating the necessity of autophagic degradation during TGF-β-mediated mesothelial trans-differentiation. The absence of intracellular dot-/vesicle-like accumulation of P120ctn when HCQ blocks the late stage of autophagy may be attributed to its different degradation way (through ubiquitination rather than in an autophagy-dependent manner) [46]. In addition, autophagy activation in the mesothelial cells coinciding with HMGB1 elevation in the peritoneal microenvironment in the early stage after peritoneum injury may contribute to the driving force behind aberrant healing.

As a redox-sensitive molecule, HMGB1’s multiple redoxes within the postoperative peritoneal microenvironment may have different effects on process of peritoneum process [11, 13, 49]. Future research should focus on exploring the impact of HMGB1 redox balance on the formation of PAs. This study is also limited by some technical shortcomings, such as the inability to use gene knockdown/knockout techniques including peritoneal mesothelial cell–specific HMGB1 knockout mice. Future studies to address these limitations are needed.

In summary, our work deepens the pathological mechanism in the interaction between autophagy activity and cellular transition, emphasizing the significant role of autophagy in the trans-differentiation of peritoneal mesothelial cells during the formation of PAs. Importantly, we also provided new ideas for the pathogenesis of PA and contribute to the development of therapeutic strategies to prevent and alleviate the development of postoperative PAs.
