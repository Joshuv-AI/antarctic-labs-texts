∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B} , outer boundary, and Biot–Savart in magnetosphere MHD simulations


Abstract
We examine the size of ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B} and outer surface boundary integrals in estimating the surface magnetic field from magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations. Maxwell’s equations tell us ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 = 0 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}=0 , which may be violated due to numerical error. MHD models such as the Space Weather Modeling Framework (SWMF) and the Open Geospace General Circulation Model (OpenGGCM) use different techniques to limit ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B} . Analyses of MHD simulations typically assume ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B} errors are small. Similarly, analyses commonly use the Biot–Savart Law and magnetospheric current density estimates from MHD simulations to determine the magnetic field at a specific point on Earth. This calculation frequently omits the surface integral over the outer boundary of the simulation volume that the Helmholtz decomposition theorem requires. This paper uses SWMF and OpenGGCM simulations to estimate the magnitudes of the ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B} and outer boundary integrals compared to Biot–Savart estimates of the magnetic field on Earth. In the simulations considered, the ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B} and outer surface integrals are up to 30 30 % of Biot–Savart estimates when the Biot–Savart estimates are large. These integrals are largest, relative to Biot–Savart magnetospheric contributions, when the Biot–Savart estimates are small. The integrals are correlated; locations on the Earth’s surface near one another have integrals of similar magnitudes. The conclusions are the same for a simulation involving a simple change in the interplanetary magnetic field and a more complex superstorm simulation.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we examine two integrals – the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
volume integral and the surface integral over the outer boundary of the simulation volume – that affect estimates of the magnetic field on the Earth’s surface using magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulation results.

According to Maxwell’s equations,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}=0

Baumjohann & Treumann (2012)
; however, this condition may be violated due to numerical error in MHD simulations
Tóth (2000)
. To combat this issue, MHD models use numerical techniques to limit
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
. Consequently, researchers typically ignore
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
errors
Rastätter et al. (2014)
.

It is also common to apply the Biot–Savart Law to MHD magnetospheric current density estimates,
\mathbf{j}
, to determine the magnetic field at a specific point, such as a magnetometer site on the Earth’s surface
Rastätter et al. (2014)
. Consistency with the Helmholtz decomposition theorem requires that the surface integral over the outer boundary of the simulation volume be included, but it is frequently omitted in these calculations.

There are other concerns with applying the Biot–Savart Law.
Parker (1996)
notes that this approach is ineffective when treating strong, non-symmetric, or time-dependent situations. And
Ferdousi & Raeder (2016)
saw substantial signal propagation times in MHD results (e.g., 72 seconds for propagation over 30 Earth radii from the tail to Earth). All of these factors affect the analysis of MHD results but are beyond the scope of this paper.

This paper examines
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
and the outer boundary integrals using two MHD models to estimate their magnitude compared to other magnetospheric contributions to the magnetic field. There are differences between the two models examined due to differing methods of handling
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
, solving the MHD equations, and modeling the ionosphere. While the two models provide similar results, they are not identical.

The integrals depend on the size of the Biot–Savart estimates of magnetospheric contributions to the magnetic field on Earth. In the simulations considered, the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
and outer surface integrals are up to
30
% of Biot–Savart estimates when the Biot–Savart estimates are large. The percentages increase when the Biot–Savart estimates are small. The contributions from the outer surface boundary term are typically larger than contributions from the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
term. The integrals are spatially correlated – locations on the Earth’s surface near one another exhibit contributions from these integrals of similar magnitudes. The conclusions are the same for a simple simulation involving a change in the interplanetary magnetic field and a more complex simulation representing a superstorm.

Arfken & Weber (2005)
. We examine two cases, applying the theorem to volumes
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{G}
to determine the magnetospheric contributions to the magnetic field inside
\mathcal{G}
, such as a point on Earth’s surface.

2.1
Helmholtz Decomposition Theorem Applied to
\mathcal{M}

The Helmholtz decomposition theorem states that a vector field, in this case the magnetic field
\mathbf{B}
, can be written as the sum of irrotational and solenoidal components. For
\mathbf{B}
defined over a finite–volume,
\mathcal{M}
, and with
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, the theorem states that for sufficiently smooth
\mathbf{B}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}_{0})&=\phantom{+}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{M}}[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})]\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\\
&\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\big[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\big]\times\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\\
&\phantom{=}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\bigg(\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}}]+\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\times[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}}]\bigg)dS\end{split}

(1)

This equation involves volume integrals over
\mathcal{M}
and surface integrals over the boundary
\partial\mathcal{M}
. The
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}
volume integral is the Biot–Savart Law contribution:

\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\big[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\big]\times\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x

(2)

A complimentary result is valid for
\mathbf{x}_{0}\not\in\mathcal{M}
, for example,
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
. In this case, the left–hand side of equation
1
is 0. One way to derive the Helmholtz decomposition theorem starts with the delta function:
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})d^{3}x
. From this, it follows that
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is 0 for
\mathbf{x}_{0}\not\in\mathcal{M}
.

With the left–hand side 0, equation
1
can be rearranged to isolate the
\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}
term. This version is useful for examining the Biot–Savart Law applied at a point inside
\mathcal{G}
, such as a magnetometer site on the Earth’s surface:

\begin{split}\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})&=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{M}}[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})]\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\\
&\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\bigg(\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}}]+\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\times[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}}]\bigg)dS\end{split}

(3)

\partial\mathcal{M}
can be split into outer and inner boundaries with
\partial\mathcal{M}=\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}+\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{I}}
.
\partial\mathcal{G}
coincides with
\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{I}}
with the opposite normal direction. Thus, equation
3
can be written as:

\begin{split}\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})&=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{M}}[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})]\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\\
&\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}}\bigg(\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}}]+\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\times[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}}]\bigg)dS\\
&\phantom{=}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{G}}\bigg(\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{G}}]+\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\times[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{G}}]\bigg)dS\end{split}

(4)

This equation is equivalent to equation
2
.

2.2
Helmholtz Decomposition Theorem Applied to
\mathcal{G}

We arrive at a different equation when the Helmholtz decomposition theorem is applied to volume
\mathcal{G}
with
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
. Inside of
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathbf{B}
approximates a dipole field, with field–aligned and ionospheric currents in
\mathcal{G}
generating deviations from a dipole. Thus, in MHD simulations, inside
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathbf{B}
is commonly treated as a dipole magnetic field with
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}=0
and
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}=0

Yu et al. (2010)
. The deviations due to field–aligned and ionospheric currents typically are handled by applying the Biot-Savart Law to these currents
Rastätter et al. (2014)
. Ignoring these deviations for the moment, for
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
, the Helmholtz decomposition theorem gives:

\begin{split}\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})&=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{G}}\bigg(\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{G}}]+\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\times[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{G}}]\bigg)dS\end{split}

(5)

where
\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}
is the integral over the inner surface of the magnetosphere, and along with equation
4
, is a second way of computing the magnetospheric current contribution to
\mathbf{B}
on Earth.

2.3
Deviations in Gap Region

The deviations to the dipole field described in section
2.2
typically are handled by applying the Biot-Savart Law to the field–aligned and ionospheric currents in
\mathcal{G}
. The field–aligned current density,
\mathbf{j}_{\text{FAC}}
, creates a deviation to
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
of the form:

\mathbf{B}_{\text{FAC}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\frac{\mu_{o}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{j}_{\text{FAC}}(\mathbf{x})\times\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x

There is a similar equation,
\mathbf{B}_{\text{iono}}
, due to ionospheric currents that also are handled via the Biot–Savart Law
Rastätter et al. (2014)
.

\mathbf{B}_{\text{FAC}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{iono}}
are divergence-free and do not introduce
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
integrals. This fact is easily demonstrated. For
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}_{\text{FAC}}
, the divergence derivatives are over
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, and the Leibniz rule allows the divergence to be moved inside the integral. We then apply the vector identity:

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})=(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B})

to the integrand.
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{j}_{\text{FAC}}=0
because the divergence derivatives are over
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\mathbf{j}_{\text{FAC}}
only depends on
\mathbf{x}
. Expanding the derivatives shows

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{3}}=0

With both terms vanishing,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}_{\text{FAC}}=0
. The same argument demonstrates
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}_{\text{iono}}=0
.

To determine the total change in
\mathbf{B}
on Earth due to magnetospheric, field–aligned, and ionospheric currents, the deviations are added to either equation
4
or
5
. The
\mathbf{B}_{\text{FAC}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{iono}}
deviations are identical for either equation.

2.4
Comparing Equations

The relationship between the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
and outer surface boundary integrals and the application of the Biot–Savart Law to magnetospheric currents is understood if we compare equations
4
and
5
. We begin by defining divergence and outer boundary integrals:

\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal{M}}[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})]\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}x

(6)

and

\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=+\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}}\bigg(\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}}]+\frac{\phantom{|}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\phantom{|^{3}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}}\times[\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial\mathcal{M}_{\text{O}}}]\bigg)dS

(7)

With these definitions, the difference between equations
4
and
5
is:

\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})+\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})

(8)

We see that
\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
reduces to
\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
when
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
are small.

Gombosi et al. (2021)
derived a similar equation (C.43 in their paper). However, their derivation assumes the outer boundary is at infinity, the magnetic field decays sufficiently rapidly at large distances, and
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
is zero. Thus, their equation only includes
\mathbf{B}_{\text{in}}
with
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}=0
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}=0
.

3
Models and Solar Wind Conditions

To estimate the magnitude of the
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
integrals, we use two MHD models for the analysis: Space Weather Modeling Framework (SWMF)
Gombosi et al. (2003)
and Open Geospace General Circulation Model (OpenGGCM)
Raeder et al. (2008)
. They were chosen because they use different approaches to solve the MHD equations and to control
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
numerical errors.

For SWMF, the Block–Adaptive–Tree–Solarwind–Roe–Upwind–Scheme (BATS–R–US) model is used to simulate the magnetosphere. It is a generalized MHD code that utilizes adaptive mesh refinement on a three–dimensional Cartesian grid. To solve the MHD equations, it employs Roe’s approximate Riemann solver. For controlling
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
terms, BATS–R–US uses the eight–wave scheme based on a symmetric form of the MHD equations. While computationally inexpensive to implement, the eight-wave scheme has a known limitation,
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
may grow in regions where the source is large
Gombosi et al. (2003)
;
Powell et al. (1999)
.

OpenGGCM is a global magnetosphere model that uses a semi–conservative form of the MHD equations on a three-dimensional, stretched Cartesian grid. The equations conserve plasma energy and use a second-order predictor–corrector finite difference scheme for solving the MHD equations. To control
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
terms, OpenGGCM uses the Constrained Transport (CT) method that employs a staggered grid that maintains
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}=0
to round–off error. Historically, the primary disadvantage of CT is that it is not easy to extend the method to generalized grids
Evans & Hawley (1988)
;
Gombosi et al. (2003)
. However, there has been work on this limitation
Mignone & Del Zanna (2021)
.

We use the same solar wind conditions in both models. Because the integrals in equations
4
and
5
depend on the magnetic field in the magnetosphere, we use solar wind conditions that produce significant changes to it. Consequently, all solar wind conditions except the
Z
–component of the interplanetary magnetic field,
B_{Z}^{\text{\tiny{IMF}}}
, are held constant and near their long–term average values
Curtis (2014)
. The simulation runs from 00:00 to 20:00 UTC, and the dipole tilt is zero and constant. Ion number density,
n
, is 5
/\text{cm}^{3}
. Temperature,
T
, is
10^{5}\text{ K}
. The
X
–component of solar wind velocity,
V_{X}
, is
-400\text{ km/s}
.
B_{Z}^{\text{\tiny{IMF}}}
is
+5\text{ nT}
from 00:00 until 06:00. At 06:00,
B_{Z}^{\text{\tiny{IMF}}}
flips to
-10\text{ nT}
and remains at this value until 20:00. All other parameters (
V_{Y}
,
V_{Z}
,
B_{X}^{\text{\tiny{IMF}}}
, and
B_{Y}^{\text{\tiny{IMF}}}
) are 0. All solar wind conditions are in Geocentric Solar Magnetospheric (GSM) coordinates at 33 Earth radii (
R_{E}
) sunward of Earth.

The model runs were executed at NASA’s Community Coordinated Modeling Center (CCMC;
Hesse et al. (2001)

Conclusions

Our analysis examined MHD simulations, where we observed two different results for
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
. For large
|\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}|
computed using equation
2
, the surface-averaged:

•

\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
is
20-30
% of
\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}
for BATS–R–US and
{\sim 20}
% for OpenGGCM.

•

\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
is
5-20
% of
\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}
for BATS–R–US and
<1
% for OpenGGCM.

These values are derived from the heatmap surface averages at 10:00 and 16:00 in Figures
6
and
6
. For small
|\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}|
, the percentages are larger, with BATS–R–US having the largest percentages. This conclusion is from the heatmap surface averages before 07:00. These conclusions are consistent with those seen in a simulation of a superstorm
Thomas et al. (2024)
.

This result implies that using only the Biot-Savart volume integral to compute the magnetic field on Earth’s surface due to magnetospheric currents has two uncertainties that should be acknowledged (1) An approximation uncertainty due to the omission of the contribution of currents outside of the magnetospheric volume, which are captured by
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
and (2) A self–consistency uncertainty associated with
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
. Even if
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
is accounted for, to be consistent with the Helmholtz decomposition theorem, the contribution from
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
must be included.

There is a third “model uncertainty” that should be acknowledged when interpreting the magnetic field on Earth’s surface estimated using MHD simulation results. We note the substantial differences in the results from different MHD models. Different simulations provide similar
\mathbf{B}
trends (i.e., similarly shaped curves), but the specific values of
\mathbf{B}
substantially differ. In the conditions we examined, differences of up to a factor of
\approx 2
occur. These differences are expected because different MHD models use different methods for handling
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
numerical errors, solving the MHD equations, and modeling the ionosphere.

These conclusions indicate that caution should be used in interpreting MHD results based on Biot–Savart analysis. An analysis that uses the Biot–Savart Law to compute
\mathbf{B}
at a point using magnetospheric current density estimates may have substantial uncertainty. We should not assert that the actual values based on an MHD simulation are accurate, but we can trust similar trends across models. Concern is warranted when
|\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}|
is small and the contributions from
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
and outer surface boundary integrals are large compared to the magnetospheric contributions.

If
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
is a concern, the analysis can include the outer surface integral in the Biot–Savart analysis. Or the analysis can use the inner surface integral through equation
5
.

Regarding
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
, we see differences between the simulations examined. It is already minimized in OpenGGCM. For BATS–R–US, the eight-wave method has a known limitation:
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
may grow when the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
source is large
Gombosi et al. (2003)
. As with
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
, analyses can employ the inner surface integral through equation
5
to account for
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
.

We note that the
\mathbf{B}_{\text{div}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\text{out}}
are spatially correlated. This observation is consistent with the known behavior of
\mathbf{B}
on the Earth’s surface.

Overall, we believe the uncertainties described above are reasonable bounds for contributions from the
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{B}
and outer boundary integrals. As space weather researchers, we are interested in what happens in an evolving scenario when
|\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}|
is large, not a stagnant one when it is small. And as
Ngwira et al. (2014)
observed, solar wind conditions employed in MHD simulations require realistic variability at the temporal scales observed in real events. Solar wind variability is needed to balance fluctuations in the magnetospheric structures under extreme driving conditions. Uncertainties in the range
5-30
% of
\mathbf{B}_{\text{BS}}
are expected.

Comparing these to the inherent variability between MHD simulations, we conclude that using the Biot–Savart Law to estimate the magnetic field at magnetometer sites is reasonable. However, we also acknowledge the known limitations of the Biot–Savart approach. It is ineffective when treating strong, non-symmetric, or time-dependent situations
Parker (1996)
. And MHD results demonstrate substantial signal propagation times
Ferdousi & Raeder (2016)
.
