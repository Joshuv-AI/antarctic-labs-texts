Development of a neoadjuvant chemotherapy efficacy prediction model for nasopharyngeal carcinoma integrating magnetic resonance radiomics and pathomics: a multi-center retrospective study
Authors: Yiren Wang, Huaiwen Zhang, Huan Wang, Yiheng Hu, Zhongjian Wen, Hairui Deng, Delong Huang, Li Xiang, Yun Zheng, Lu Yang, Lei Su, Yunfei Li, Fang Liu, Peng Wang, Shengmin Guo, Haowen Pang, Ping Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a predictive model for assessing the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) by integrating radiomics and pathomics features using a particle swarm optimization-supported support vector machine (PSO-SVM).MethodsA retrospective multi-center study was conducted, which included 389 NPC patients who received NACT from three institutions. Radiomics features were extracted from magnetic resonance imaging scans, while pathomics features were derived from histopathological images. A total of 2,667 radiomics features and 254 pathomics features were initially extracted. Feature selection involved intra-class correlation coefficient evaluation, Mann-Whitney U test, Spearman correlation analysis, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression. The PSO-SVM model was constructed and validated using 10-fold cross-validation on the training set and further evaluated using an external validation set. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic curve, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis.ResultsEight significant predictive features (five radiomics and three pathomics) were identified. The PSO-SVM radiopathomics model achieved superior performance compared to models based solely on radiomics or pathomics features. The AUCs for the PSO-SVM radiopathomics model were 0.917 (95% CI: 0.887–0.948) in internal validation and 0.814 (95% CI: 0.742–0.887) in external validation. Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Decision curve analysis showed that the PSO-SVM radiopathomics model provided higher clinical net benefit over a wider range of risk thresholds compared to other models.ConclusionThe PSO-SVM radiopathomics model effectively integrates radiomics and pathomics features, offering enhanced predictive accuracy and clinical utility for assessing NACT efficacy in NPC. The multi-center approach and robust validation underscore its potential for personalized treatment planning, supporting improved clinical decision-making for NPC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13235-0.Keywords: Multimodal, Radiomics, Machine learning, Pathomics, Prediction model, Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma, Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a type of head and neck cancer with unique epidemiological and clinical characteristics, primarily affecting populations in East and Southeast Asia [1, 2]. Despite advances in radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the overall prognosis for NPC patients, especially those with advanced disease, remains challenging. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is widely used clinically to shrink tumors before radiotherapy, improve local control, and reduce distant metastasis especially in stage III and IV locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma cases. For metastatic cases, depending on the extent of metastasis and overall patient condition, NACT may reduce the tumor burden prior to local or palliative treatment [3]. However, the response to NACT varies from patient to patient, so reliable predictive models are needed to identify the patients most likely to benefit from this treatment strategy prior to treatment [4]. Additionally, identifying patients for whom neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is unlikely to be effective is critical to avoid unnecessary exposure to chemotherapy-related toxicities and adverse effects, which can compromise patients’ quality of life and delay potentially more effective treatments [5]. By accurately identifying patients who are unlikely to benefit from NACT, clinicians can avoid these disadvantages, personalize treatment strategies, and focus on alternative therapies that are more likely to be effective, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

Radiomics is a rapidly developing technology that extracts high-dimensional quantitative features from medical images to reflect tumor heterogeneity and microstructural characteristics [6]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with its excellent soft tissue contrast and multi-modal imaging capabilities, provides a rich source of radiomic features for NPC [7]. These features can capture subtle changes within the tumor, thus, predicting treatment outcomes. On the other hand, pathomics involves analyzing histopathological images to extract quantitative features that reflect the tumor microenvironment and cellular architecture [8]. Pathomics offers an in-depth understanding of tumor biological behavior, complementing the macroscopic perspective of radiomics [9]. Combining radiomics and pathomics could provide a more comprehensive and accurate characterization of tumor features, thereby better prediction of treatment responses.

Although studies have shown that radiomics and pathomics have great potential in predicting treatment outcomes for various cancers, there is still a gap in research combining these two modalities to predict NACT efficacy in NPC [10]. Additionally, most existing studies on NACT efficacy prediction for NPC are single-center studies focused primarily on radiomics, limiting their generalizability and clinical application value [11, 12]. To address these shortcomings, we conducted a multi-center retrospective study aimed at developing and validating a predictive model that integrates MRI-based radiomics and pathomics features to assess the efficacy of NACT in NPC patients.

In this study, we utilized a particle swarm optimization-supported support vector machine (PSO-SVM) to integrate radiomics and pathomics features and develop a robust predictive model. We hypothesize that this integrated model will outperform traditional support vector machine models based solely on radiomics or pathomics features, providing a valuable tool for more accurately predicting NACT efficacy and supporting personalized treatment plans for NPC patients. Methods

Patients

This retrospective study included 389 NPC patients who received NACT between January 2017 and January 2023. The MRI used to construct the predictive models were all obtained prior to the performance of the NACT treatment. The patients were from three institutions: the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (n = 311), Qingyang People’s Hospital (n = 36), and Xinzhou People’s Hospital (n = 42). The inclusion criteria were as follows: ①: histopathologically confirmed NPC patients; ②: treatment-naïve patients; ③: patients who underwent MRI after two independent cycles of NACT; ④: patients with complete MRI, pathological images, clinical baseline data, and treatment response records. The exclusion criteria were: ①: patients with any missing MRI, pathological images, clinical baseline data, or treatment response records; ②: patients with poor quality images due to motion or metal artifacts.

In this study, the patients included primarily received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) using either the docetaxel, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil (DCF) regimens or the gemcitabine and cisplatin (GP) regimens. These chemotherapy protocols are widely used in the treatment of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) to maximize tumor shrinkage prior to definitive therapy. All NACT regimens included two cycles, each lasting 21 days. Nasopharyngeal and neck MRI scans were performed three months after the completion of NACT. The response to NACT was evaluated by three oncologists with more than 10 years of experience, according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria [13]. Complete response (CR) and partial response (PR) were classified as NACT effective, while stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD) were classified as NACT ineffective.

In this study, the cohort from the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (n = 311) served as the training set for feature selection, model construction, and training. The cohorts from Qingyang People’s Hospital (n = 36) and Xinzhou People’s Hospital (n = 42) were combined into a single dataset and used as an external validation set to evaluate model performance. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, Qingyang People’s Hospital and Xinzhou People’s Hospital (KY2023041; LC-202427; XZRY-2024030) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all the patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Magnetic resonance scanning parameters

The contrast-enhanced MRI scans from the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University were obtained using an Achieva 1.5T MR scanner (Philips, Netherlands). The scanning sequences and parameters were as follows: T2 weighted imaging (T2WI) sequence (TR/TE = 2975 ms/90 ms), T1WI sequence (TR/TE = 429 ms/18 ms), T1-weighted high-resolution isotropic volume examination (Thrive) sequence (TR/TE = 2500 ms/3.2 ms), with a field of view (FOV) of 240 mm × 240 mm, axial slice thickness of 1 mm, and sagittal and coronal slice thickness of 2 mm.

The contrast-enhanced MRI scans from Qingyang People’s Hospital were obtained using a GE Discovery 750 W 3.0T MRI system with a head and neck combined coil. The scanning sequences and parameters were as follows: T1WI sequence (TR/TE = 836 ms/10.4 ms), T2WI sequence (TR/TE = 6198 ms/85 ms), Thrive sequence (TR/TE = 3.9 ms/1.9 ms), with an axial slice thickness of 1 mm, sagittal and coronal slice thickness of 2 mm, and a FOV of 230 mm × 230 mm.

The contrast-enhanced MRI scans from Xinzhou People’s Hospital were obtained using a GE Discovery MR750 3.0T scanner. The scanning sequences and parameters were as follows: T1WI sequence (TR/TE = 1750 ms/24 ms), T2WI sequence (TR/TE = 8500 ms/155 ms), Thrive sequence (TR/TE = 4183 ms/102 ms), with a FOV of 240 mm × 240 mm, axial slice thickness of 1 mm, and sagittal and coronal slice thickness of 2 mm.

Radiomics feature extraction

All MRI images were sourced from DICOM format medical image archiving and communication systems and transferred to the 3D Slicer software (version 4.21). Two oncologists with more than 5 years of clinical experience manually segmented the regions of interest (ROIs) on each MRI sequence using the 3D Slicer software. In the case of disagreement, a third oncologist with over 10 years of experience made the final decision. Voxel size normalization was performed by resampling the voxels to 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm. Finally, radiomics features, including shape features, first-order statistics, and high-order texture features, were extracted from each ROI using the Pyradiomics package [14].

Pathomics feature extraction

Pathologists collected biopsy samples from all NPC patients before diagnosing NPC. First, the biopsy tissue was soaked in 10% formalin solution for 4 h and then embedded in immunohistochemistry paraffin blocks. The biopsy tissue was then cut at 4-micron intervals and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) for pathological assessment. A pathologist with 10 years of diagnostic experience scanned all H&E-stained pathology slides at 10x magnification using a digital slide scanner (KFBio KF-PRO-020). A pathologist with more than 5 years of experience selected sample areas containing tumor cells and obtained five non-overlapping typical area screenshots, each with a FOV of 1024 × 1024 pixels. Another pathologist with more than 5 years of experience confirmed the selection. In the case of disagreement, a third pathologist with over 10 years of experience made the final decision. All screenshots were cropped into small patches (512 × 512 pixels) for subsequent analysis through non-overlapping sampling. We used CellProfiler software (version 4.0.7) to extract quantitative pathomics features from the selected patches. The “Unmix Colors” module was used to separate and convert the H&E-stained images into grayscale images of H&E stains. The “ColorToGray” module was also used to convert H&E-stained images into grayscale images. The feature extraction process consisted of three steps. First, general primary pathomics features were extracted from the original grayscale images. Then, the primary and secondary objects were identified and extracted from the separated H&E stained images. Finally, the features extracted from the five patches of each sample’s whole slide image (WSI) were averaged for subsequent analysis [15].

Feature screening

After feature extraction, all radiomics and pathomics feature values were normalized to a range between 0 and 1 using the min-max normalization method, to account for differences in scanning protocols across multiple centers. First, the reproducibility of the features was evaluated using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), and only features with good reproducibility (ICCs > 0.75) were retained [16]. Next, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to identify outcome-related features for each sequence, and only factors with a significance level of P < 0.05 were included for further screening [17]. For highly reproducible features, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to calculate the correlation between features. Among any two features with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.9, one feature was retained [18]. To maximize the descriptive ability of the features, a greedy recursive elimination strategy was employed for feature filtering. Finally, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model was used to construct the radiomics features. The LASSO method shrinks all regression coefficients to zero and precisely sets the coefficients of many irrelevant features to zero based on the tuning weight λ [19]. To determine the optimal λ, lamda.min and 7-fold cross-validation were used, where the final value of λ produced the smallest cross-validation error. Features with non-zero coefficients retained through this process were considered important and included in the subsequent predictive model construction.

Construction of the predictive model

In this study, a PSO-SVM was employed for predictive model construction. Radiomics and pathomics data typically have high dimensionality and complex inter-feature correlations. PSO-SVM can automatically adjust parameters, reducing manual intervention and enhancing the model’s automation. This is particularly important for handling complex, high-dimensional data, effectively reducing the complexity and error rate of manual parameter tuning [20]. In this study, PSO-SVM models and cross-validation methods were used to train radiomics models, pathomics models, and radiopathomics models separately. The models trained on the training set were applied to the external validation set to ultimately verify the performance of the PSO-SVM model proposed in this study and the performance of models based on different ‘omics’ data. The area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the models, and calibration curves were used to compare the predicted probabilities of events with the actual event frequencies, thereby assessing the model’s calibration. Additionally, decision curves were used to evaluate the clinical net benefit of all models.

Model interpretation

The radiopathomics model was interpreted using the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) algorithm to understand the prediction process and the contribution of individual features to the risk of radiation-induced dermatitis. First, the global mean absolute SHAP values were calculated to assess the overall importance of the features in the model. Subsequently, the impact of each variable’s SHAP values on the model output was evaluated. Finally, the SHAP values were used to calculate the influence of each important feature on the prediction model output for each individual sample.

Feature screening

In this study, a total of 2,667 radiomics features and 254 pathomics features were extracted from each sample. All radiomics and pathomics features were evaluated using ICC. A total of 2,368 radiomics features and 201 pathomics features had ICCs greater than 0.75. These features were then subjected to the Mann-Whitney U test, with 903 radiomics features and 178 pathomics features found to be statistically significant (P < 0.05). Subsequently, Spearman correlation analysis and a greedy recursive elimination strategy were employed to further screen 324 radiomics features and 86 pathomics features, which were then included in LASSO regression for additional selection. The LASSO regression for feature selection was performed using 7-fold cross-validation, selecting features at the minimum penalty parameter (lambda.min). Finally, 5 radiomics features and 3 pathomics features were identified for model construction (Fig. 3) (Supplementary File 1).

Construction and evaluation of the predictive model

In this study, we utilized both PSO-SVM and SVM to construct models based on radiomics, pathomics, and combined radiopathomics features. Internal validation in the training set was performed using 10-fold cross-validation. The hyperparameters for training the SVM model were determined through a grid search (Supplementary File 2). For the PSO-SVM model, parameters were optimized using the particle swarm optimization algorithm to obtain the optimal particle swarm (Supplementary File 3), and internal validation was also conducted using 10-fold cross-validation. The internal validation results, as shown in Fig. 4, indicate that the PSO-SVM models generally outperformed the SVM models. Specifically, the PSO-SVM radiomics model achieved an AUC of 0.749 (95% CI: 0.692–0.805), the PSO-SVM pathomics model achieved an AUC of 0.762 (95% CI: 0.708–0.816), and the PSO-SVM radiopathomics model achieved an AUC of 0.917 (95% CI: 0.887–0.948). In contrast, the SVM radiomics model had an AUC of 0.699 (95% CI: 0.638–0.760), the SVM pathomics model had an AUC of 0.772 (95% CI: 0.717–0.827), and the SVM radiopathomics model had an AUC of 0.831 (95% CI: 0.790–0.871). These results demonstrate that the PSO-SVM models, especially those combining radiomics and pathomics features, have superior predictive capabilities.

Internal validation results of models on the training set

For external validation, the SVM and PSO-SVM models trained on the training set were applied to an external dataset to assess their generalization ability. As shown in Fig. 5, the PSO-SVM models continued to outperform the SVM models in external validation. Specifically, the PSO-SVM radiomics model achieved an AUC of 0.708 (95% CI: 0.632–0.783), the PSO-SVM pathomics model achieved an AUC of 0.727 (95% CI: 0.630–0.823), and the PSO-SVM radiopathomics model achieved an AUC of 0.814 (95% CI: 0.742–0.887). In contrast, the SVM radiomics model had an AUC of 0.676 (95% CI: 0.577–0.776), the SVM pathomics model had an AUC of 0.686 (95% CI: 0.593–0.780), and the SVM radiopathomics model had an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.691–0.860). These results further validate the robust predictive performance and generalization ability of the PSO-SVM models, particularly when integrating radiomics and pathomics features. The consistent improvement in AUCs in both internal and external validations emphasizes the reliability and effectiveness of the PSO-SVM approach in radiopathomic prediction. In addition, we constructed a prediction model based on TNM staging as predictors using PSO-SVM algorithm, and the prediction model based on TNM staging alone showed moderate prediction performance (Supplementary File 4).

Model performance in the external validation set

Further validation of the model in the external validation dataset

This study evaluated the consistency between the predicted probabilities and actual outcomes using calibration curves (Fig. 6). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed that both the PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics and SVM-Radiopathomics models demonstrated good calibration (P > 0.05). This indicates that the predicted probabilities closely match the actual probabilities. Specifically, compared to the SVM-Radiopathomics model, the PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics model more closely aligns with the ideal diagonal line, suggesting that the PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics model provides more accurate probability predictions.

The study also assessed the clinical net benefit of the models across different risk thresholds using decision curve analysis (Fig. 7). The decision curves for the PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics and SVM-Radiopathomics models are shown in Fig. 5C and F, respectively. The PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics model demonstrated clinical net benefit over a broader range of risk thresholds compared to the SVM-Radiopathomics model. The PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics model showed clinical net benefit at risk thresholds between 9%–45% and 62–90%, while the SVM-Radiopathomics model only showed clinical net benefit at risk thresholds between 8%–38%. This indicates that the PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics model has superior clinical utility and provides greater net benefit in identifying patients at risk for the target disease. The higher net benefit of the PSO-SVM-Radiopathomics model highlights its enhanced ability for clinically relevant predictions, thereby supporting its potential application in clinical decision-making.

To gain an in-depth understanding of the prediction process and the contribution of individual features to the radiopathomics model, we utilized the SHAP algorithm. SHAP provides a unified measure of feature importance and allows detailed analysis of how each feature influences the model’s output. Figure 8A presents the global feature importance ranking based on mean absolute SHAP values. The top feature is wavelet-HLL.glszm.SizeZoneNonUniformityNormalized_Thrive (Feature 4), whose mean absolute SHAP value indicates a significant impact on model predictions. Other important features include H_Mean_IdentifySecondaryObjects_AreaShape_Zernike_2_4 (Feature 7) and H_Mean_IdentifyPrimaryObjects_AreaShape_Zernike_3_7 (Feature 6), which assess the area shape of secondary and primary objects, respectively. Wavelet-LHH.glcm.ClusterShade_Thrive (Feature 0) and wavelet-LHL.glszm.SmallAreaEmphasis_T1w (Feature 1) are also key features, with the former measuring the cluster shade of the gray-level co-occurrence matrix and the latter capturing the proportion of small areas in the image. These features play a crucial role in the model’s decision-making process.

These analyses using SHAP demonstrate the algorithm’s powerful capability to interpret individual predictions by highlighting the contribution of each feature. Discussion

The radiopathomics prediction model constructed in this study for predicting the efficacy of NACT in NPC demonstrates significant clinical value. In this study, we identified three radiomics features from the Thrive sequence, one from the T1WI sequence, and one from the T2WI sequence, along with three pathomics features, as important predictors of NACT efficacy in NPC. These eight predictive factors were used to construct radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics models using both SVM and PSO-SVM. The results showed that the combined radiopathomics model (AUC = 0.917) outperformed the models built with either radiomics (AUC = 0.749) or pathomics features (AUC = 0.762) alone, achieving better predictive performance with both SVM (AUC = 0.831) and PSO-SVM (AUC = 0.917) algorithms. Furthermore, external validation of the models confirmed these findings, demonstrating the robustness and generalizability of the PSO-SVM radiopathomics model.

Currently, only a few published studies on NACT prediction models have utilized multi-sequence MRI and radiomics. Most studies are single-center and involve small sample sizes. For instance, the earliest study by Zheng et al. [22]. included 63 patients from a single center, using serial dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI to evaluate early NACT efficacy in NPC, with kinetic parameters from the Tofts model achieving an AUC of 0.703. Hu et al. [23]. conducted a study with 284 patients using T1WI and T2WI sequences, developing a radiomics prediction model for NACT efficacy in NPC. They found that the T1WI-based model had an AUC of 0.79 and the T2WI-based model had an AUC of 0.81, showcasing the predictive potential of radiomics. Compared to these studies, our model showed a 10.7% improvement in AUC (AUC = 0.917) in internal validation. Fan et al. [24]. developed a radiomics model using MRI for predicting NACT efficacy in NPC (C-index = 0.816), which outperformed models based solely on clinical data (C-index = 0.746), highlighting the advantages of radiomics. Piao et al. [25]. conducted a single-center study with 108 patients, using logistic regression to build a prediction model, which showed promising results in internal validation (AUC = 0.904). However, these studies were single-center study with a small sample size, and may have suffered from sampling bias and overfitting, potentially leading to overly optimistic performance. Our study, being multi-center with a larger sample size (n = 311) for model training, effectively mitigates the risk of overfitting due to small sample sizes.

Radiomics relies on high-dimensional features extracted from medical images, which can reflect tumor heterogeneity and structural changes [26]. However, predictions based solely on radiomics features might overlook important biological and pathological information crucial for tumor treatment response. For example, radiomics features may not fully capture the tumor microenvironment, cellular composition, and gene expression characteristics, all of which play critical roles in tumor growth and treatment response [27]. Therefore, a single radiomics model may not provide sufficient predictive accuracy and reliability in certain cases. To address the limitations of radiomics, we introduced pathomics features in this study. Pathomics extracts quantitative features reflecting the tumor microenvironment and cellular structure from histopathological images, complementing the limitations of radiomics features and providing richer information for comprehensive tumor characterization [28]. For instance, pathomics can reveal tumor cell density, distribution, and morphological changes, which are essential for assessing tumor biological behavior and treatment response [29]. Additionally, pathomics features can reflect the composition of the tumor stroma and immune cell infiltration, closely related to treatment sensitivity [30].

By integrating radiomics and pathomics features, we constructed a more comprehensive and accurate radiopathomics prediction model. The results showed that the combined model exhibited higher accuracy and stability in predicting NACT efficacy in NPC compared to models based solely on radiomics or pathomics features. This finding validates the complementary nature of radiomics and pathomics features and emphasizes the importance of multi-modal data integration in tumor prediction. The advantage of this radiopathomics prediction model is consistent with the findings by Zhang et al. [15]., where a radiopathomics model was used to predict the efficacy of NACT in breast cancer. The use of multi-center data further enhances the model’s generalizability and clinical applicability, offering robust support for personalized treatment strategies. By combining imaging and pathological information, we can more comprehensively assess tumor characteristics, thereby improving the precision of the predictive model and providing a more scientific and reliable basis for treatment decisions for NPC patients.

Despite the significant progress made in predicting NACT efficacy in NPC, there are still some limitations in this study. First, although multi-center data were used to enhance the model’s generalizability and reliability, the external validation dataset was relatively small. The limited sample size may affect the model’s generalizability to larger populations. Second, the radiomics and pathomics features used in this study were based on existing extraction methods; future research may need to develop more advanced feature extraction algorithms to capture richer and more useful information. Additionally, this study did not explore the effect of Epstein-Barr virus load on NACT efficacy of patients before NACT, which could be a potential aspect affecting the efficacy of NACT. Future studies could explore integrating more multi-modal data, including genomics, metabolomics and EBV data, to build more comprehensive efficacy prediction models.

Conclusion

In this multi-center retrospective study, we developed and validated a predictive model for NACT efficacy in NPC by integrating MRI-based radiomics and pathomics features using the PSO-SVM algorithm. The combined radiopathomics model significantly outperformed models based solely on radiomics or pathomics features, achieving the highest AUC values in both internal and external validations. The SHAP analysis provided insights into the key contributing features, enhancing model interpretability and clinical applicability. Despite the promising results, further validation with larger, diverse cohorts and advanced feature extraction techniques is needed. This integrated approach offers a robust tool for personalized treatment planning, improving the precision and reliability of predicting NACT efficacy in NPC.
