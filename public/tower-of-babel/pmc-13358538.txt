Pulmonary cavitation as a dominant prognostic indicator in tuberculosis complicated by chronic pulmonary aspergillosis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Fen Gao, Fuming Zhao, Zhuo Zhang, Qian Li, Xingming Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) complicated by chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality; however, its prognostic determinants remain incompletely characterized. This study aimed to identify factors associated with prognosis and unfavorable outcomes in patients with PTB complicated by CPA.MethodsBetween January 2023 and June 2024, consecutive adult patients diagnosed with PTB complicated by CPA were retrospectively enrolled. Unfavorable outcomes were defined as massive hemoptysis requiring surgical intervention or death during follow-up. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify independent prognostic factors. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to quantify the relative contribution of each predictor. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to compare event-free survival across risk strata.ResultsAmong 440 patients (median follow-up, 6.0 months), 88 (20.0%) experienced unfavorable outcomes. Multivariable analysis identified older age [hazard ratio (HR) =1.042 per year], diabetes mellitus (HR =1.888), pretreatment hemoptysis (HR =1.652), pulmonary cavitation (HR =2.069), and irregular medication adherence (HR =2.599) as independent predictors of poor prognosis. SHAP analysis ranked pulmonary cavitation as the strongest contributor to unfavorable outcomes. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated significantly lower event-free survival among patients with these risk factors (all P<0.05).ConclusionsIn patients with PTB complicated by CPA, unfavorable outcomes were independently associated with older age, diabetes mellitus, pretreatment hemoptysis, pulmonary cavitation, and irregular medication adherence, with pulmonary cavitation representing the dominant prognostic indicator. These findings support risk-stratified management strategies and highlight the value of interpretable machine learning for prognostic assessment.Keywords: Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA), outcome, prognosis, factors

Highlight box

Key findings

• This retrospective cohort study identified age, diabetes mellitus, pretreatment hemoptysis, pulmonary cavitation, and medication adherence as independent prognostic factors for unfavorable outcomes in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis complicated by chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (PTB-CPA). Among these, pulmonary cavitation emerged as the most influential determinant, contributing most strongly to adverse outcomes.

What is known and what is new?

• PTB complicated by CPA is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly in patients with residual structural lung disease. Several clinical factors have been linked to poor outcomes in PTB or CPA individually; however, prognostic determinants in patients with combined PTB-CPA remain poorly defined.

• This study provides real-world evidence demonstrating that pulmonary cavitation is the dominant prognostic indicator in PTB-CPA. By integrating multivariable Cox regression with SHapley Additive exPlanations-based interpretability, the study quantitatively ranks prognostic factors and clarifies their relative contributions to adverse outcomes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Patients with PTB complicated by CPA, especially those with pulmonary cavitation, should be recognized as a high-risk subgroup. Clinical management should emphasize early identification of cavitary disease, intensified radiological surveillance, proactive hemoptysis control, strict glycemic management in patients with diabetes, and reinforcement of treatment adherence. Incorporating interpretable prognostic models into clinical practice may facilitate risk stratification and support individualized management strategies in this high-risk population.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health challenge. An estimated 10.8 million new TB cases occurred worldwide in 2024, resulting in approximately 1.25 million deaths (1). Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) accounts for more than 84% of all TB cases and represents the predominant clinical manifestation of the disease (2). Patients with PTB are particularly susceptible to pulmonary aspergillosis owing to a combination of disease-related and treatment-related factors. Progressive pulmonary parenchymal destruction, prolonged exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, and use of immunosuppressive agents may impair host immune defenses, thereby increasing vulnerability to opportunistic fungal infections (3-5). Structural lung damage secondary to TB, particularly residual pulmonary cavities, is recognized as the principal predisposing factor for pulmonary aspergillosis (6). In parallel, the growing global burden of immunodeficiency states, most notably human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, further amplifies the risk of TB-fungal co-infection. Compared with HIV-negative individuals, people living with HIV are more than 10 times more likely to develop TB (7). However, the frequency of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) in this population remains uncertain. Available evidence is limited and largely derived from studies of invasive aspergillosis rather than CPA specifically, and there is substantial diagnostic overlap with subacute invasive aspergillosis and smear-negative PTB, which may lead to misclassification and under-recognition. Recent analyses highlight the lack of robust epidemiological data on CPA in HIV-infected populations (8).

The coexistence of PTB and pulmonary aspergillosis poses substantial clinical challenges and is likely associated with adverse outcomes. Co-infection with Aspergillus species and Mycobacterium tuberculosis increases diagnostic complexity, complicates clinical management, and heightens the risk of severe complications (9). In the setting of impaired host immunity, chronic Aspergillus infection may accelerate respiratory deterioration and is associated with substantial long-term mortality (10). Unlike invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, which predominantly affects profoundly immunocompromised hosts, CPA typically develops in individuals with pre-existing structural lung disease, most commonly as a sequela of PTB. As a complication of PTB, CPA affects an estimated 3 million individuals worldwide, with a global prevalence of approximately 42 per 100,000 population, based on model-derived estimates (11). Reported 5-year mortality rates for CPA range from 50% to 85% (12), highlighting its substantial global disease burden.

Despite the recognized clinical importance of TB complicated by CPA, evidence regarding prognostic determinants in this population remains limited, particularly in real-world clinical settings. Most previous studies have examined PTB or CPA as isolated disease entities. Although several prognostic risk factors, including malnutrition, HIV-associated immunosuppression, and corticosteroid exposure, are shared between the two conditions (13-16), important differences in disease progression and outcome determinants remain. A clearer understanding of factors associated with poor prognosis is therefore essential to optimize the clinical management of patients with TB-Aspergillus co-infection.

Accordingly, this study aimed to identify clinical and laboratory factors associated with prognosis in patients with PTB complicated by CPA using a retrospective cohort design, to inform risk stratification and improve clinical outcomes in this high-risk population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0393/rc).

Methods

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Chest Hospital (No. LS2026020501). Owing to the retrospective design, informed consent was waived. All data were collected, processed, and analyzed in an anonymized manner.

Study population

Between January 2023 and June 2024, consecutive adult patients diagnosed with PTB complicated by CPA were retrospectively identified from hospital medical records. Patients were classified into favorable-prognosis and unfavorable-prognosis groups. Unfavorable prognosis was defined as massive hemoptysis requiring surgical intervention or death during follow-up.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) a diagnosis of PTB in accordance with the Diagnostic Criteria for Pulmonary Tuberculosis (WS 288–2017), including both microbiologically confirmed cases (positive smear microscopy, culture, or nucleic acid amplification tests) and clinically diagnosed cases based on compatible clinical, radiological, and/or treatment response criteria (17); and (III) a diagnosis of CPA occurring concurrently with active PTB, including cases identified during anti-TB therapy, defined according to the consensus case definition proposed by Denning et al. [2018], requiring all of the following: (i) compatible clinical symptoms persisting for ≥3 months; (ii) progressive radiological abnormalities on chest imaging, including cavities with or without fungal balls, pleural thickening, or pericavitary infiltrates; and (iii) microbiological and/or immunological evidence of Aspergillus infection (18). Compatible clinical manifestations included chronic cough, recurrent or persistent hemoptysis, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue, or progressively worsening dyspnea. Characteristic computed tomography (CT) features comprised progressive pulmonary parenchymal lesions, including single or multiple cavities with or without aspergilloma, pulmonary nodules with or without a halo sign, consolidation or infiltrative changes, pleural effusion (particularly in chronic necrotizing CPA), thickening of pre-existing cavity walls (≥3 mm), or new infiltrates arising in areas of prior fibrosis or bronchiectasis. Pulmonary cavitation was defined on CT as a gas-filled space within a pulmonary consolidation, mass, or nodule with a definable wall, typically ≥3 mm in thickness. Cavities were distinguished from bullae, which were defined as air-filled spaces with thin or imperceptible walls (<1 mm) and without associated parenchymal abnormalities such as consolidation, fibrosis, or pericavitary infiltrates. Microbiological or immunological evidence required at least one of the following: positive serum Aspergillus-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies, isolation of Aspergillus species from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or lung tissue, positive Aspergillus polymerase chain reaction (PCR), or histopathological confirmation of Aspergillus hyphae with associated tissue inflammation or necrosis.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (I) presence of other active pulmonary infections, malignant tumors, or severe immunodeficiency disorders; (II) severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; (III) pregnancy, lactation, significant psychiatric disorders, or impaired consciousness; or (IV) massive hemoptysis at admission or prior to the initiation of standardized treatment, or coexisting pulmonary diseases associated with a high risk of massive hemoptysis. In addition, cases in which CPA developed after completion of anti-TB treatment were excluded.

All patients received standardized anti-TB and antifungal therapy, along with supportive care as clinically indicated. Standard first-line anti-TB treatment consisted of rifampicin (0.6 g once daily), isoniazid (0.3 g once daily), ethambutol (0.75 g once daily), and pyrazinamide (0.5 g three times daily) for a total duration of 6 months. Antifungal therapy was based on voriconazole, administered intravenously at a loading dose of 6 mg/kg twice daily on day 1, followed by 4 mg/kg twice daily for 2 weeks, and subsequently transitioned to oral voriconazole at 0.2 g twice daily for an additional 6 weeks.

Patients were monitored through outpatient visits, telephone interviews, and review of records from the hospital information system. Routine follow-up assessments were scheduled at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after treatment initiation and continued until an endpoint event, loss to follow-up, or the study end date (October 31, 2025).

Data collection

Demographic characteristics, clinical information, laboratory parameters at admission, and follow-up data were extracted from electronic medical records. Demographic variables included age, sex, height, weight, disease duration, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus and hypertension), and exposure history (smoking and alcohol consumption). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2).

Clinical characteristics included lesion distribution, hemoptysis, chest pain, dyspnea, the presence of pulmonary cavitation on imaging, and medication adherence. Mild hemoptysis was defined as less than 30 mL per episode or blood-streaked sputum. Regular medication adherence was defined as uninterrupted, concurrent use of both anti-TB and antifungal agents throughout the 6-month treatment period, according to prescribed dosages and 24-hour dosing intervals.

Fasting peripheral venous blood samples were collected at admission to measure serum levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and albumin (ALB). Follow-up variables included duration of follow-up, occurrence of endpoint events, time to endpoint, and loss to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For continuous variables with a sample size ≥50, normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Age, BMI, disease duration, CRP, IL-6, and ALB did not follow a normal distribution and are therefore presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR; P25, P75). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ2 test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify factors associated with adverse prognosis in patients with PTB complicated by CPA. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed, and the log-rank test was used to compare prognoses across categorical variables identified as independent predictors.

To further quantify the relative contribution of each independent predictor and improve model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied based on the Cox regression results. Variables were ranked according to their absolute mean SHAP values, and a SHAP importance bar plot was generated to visualize the contribution of each factor to adverse outcomes.

During follow-up, 88 patients experienced unfavorable outcomes, yielding an overall incidence of 20.0%. Among these, 64 patients (14.5%) underwent surgical intervention for massive hemoptysis without subsequent death, whereas 24 patients (5.5%) died due to massive hemoptysis. Patients with unfavorable outcomes were older (median age, 62.0 vs. 59.0 years; P<0.001) and had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus (31.8% vs. 15.9%; P=0.001), hypertension (23.9% vs. 12.2%; P=0.006), pulmonary cavitation (54.6% vs. 31.3%; P<0.001), and irregular medication adherence (26.1% vs. 11.4%; P<0.001). No significant differences were observed in sex, BMI, disease duration, smoking or alcohol history, lesion distribution, chest pain, dyspnea, or baseline inflammatory markers (all P>0.05).

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression identified age, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hemoptysis before treatment, pulmonary cavitation, regular medication adherence, and baseline IL-6 levels as factors significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes in patients with PTB and CPA (all P<0.05; Table 2). Variables meeting this threshold were included in the multivariable Cox model.

In the multivariable analysis, age, diabetes mellitus, pretreatment hemoptysis, pulmonary cavitation, and regular medication adherence remained independently associated with unfavorable outcomes (Table 2). Each 1-year increase in age was associated with a 4.2% increase in risk of adverse prognosis [hazard ratio (HR) =1.042; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.009–1.077]. The presence of diabetes mellitus significantly increased the risk compared with its absence (HR =1.888; 95% CI: 1.189–3.000). Patients presenting with hemoptysis before treatment had a higher risk of unfavorable outcomes (HR =1.652; 95% CI: 1.039–2.626). Pulmonary cavitation conferred more than a twofold increased risk (HR =2.069; 95% CI: 1.314–3.258). Additionally, irregular medication adherence was strongly associated with adverse prognosis compared with regular adherence (HR =2.599; 95% CI: 1.588–4.254).

SHAP-based feature importance analysis

To quantify the relative contribution of independent prognostic factors, SHAP analysis was applied to the multivariable Cox regression model. The resulting feature importance ranking is shown in Figure 2. Pulmonary cavitation was the most influential contributor to adverse prognosis (mean absolute SHAP value =0.372), followed by hemoptysis before treatment (0.242), regular medication adherence (0.236), diabetes mellitus (0.219), and age (0.215).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to estimate cumulative event-free survival probabilities by key prognostic factors, with group differences assessed using the log-rank test (Figure 3). From month 2 of follow-up, patients with diabetes mellitus had a significantly lower event-free survival rate than those without diabetes (χ2=8.398, P=0.004). A similar separation was observed between patients presenting with hemoptysis before treatment and those without hemoptysis (χ2=5.709, P=0.02). Patients with pulmonary cavitation exhibited a markedly reduced event-free survival probability relative to those without cavitation (χ2=24.262, P<0.001). Additionally, from month 1 onward, patients with irregular medication adherence had a significantly lower event-free survival probability than those with regular adherence (χ2=14.244, P<0.001).

Discussion

CPA constitutes a substantial and often underrecognized complication of PTB, contributing significantly to global morbidity and mortality. Epidemiological data suggest that approximately 5–35% of patients with PTB may develop CPA, with TB accounting for nearly half of the global CPA burden (19). In this retrospective cohort study, age, diabetes mellitus, hemoptysis before treatment, pulmonary cavitation, and regular medication adherence were identified as independent predictors of unfavorable outcomes in patients with PTB complicated by CPA. Among these factors, pulmonary cavitation emerged as the most influential prognostic indicator, highlighting its central role in disease progression and clinical risk stratification.

Advancing age was independently associated with an increased risk of unfavorable outcomes, aligning with previous observations in populations with TB and post-TB lung disease. Among patients receiving modified TB treatment regimens, age greater than 64 years has been identified as a strong predictor of treatment failure (19). Age-related immunosenescence, particularly the impairment of cell-mediated immunity and mucosal defense mechanisms, compromises host control of both Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Aspergillus spp., thereby increasing susceptibility to CPA and its complications (20,21). In addition, elderly patients often exhibit reduced pulmonary elasticity, impaired tissue repair capacity, and a higher burden of comorbidities, which collectively limit physiological reserve and hinder recovery from infection (22). The increased vulnerability of elderly individuals to severe complications, including respiratory failure and septic shock, further contributes to excess mortality (23). Consistent with these findings, previous surgical cohorts with TB-destroyed lung have demonstrated that increasing age (≥40 years), along with male sex, independently predicts severe postoperative complications (24), potentially reflecting the heightened susceptibility of older patients to CPA-related structural and infectious sequelae. Taken together, these findings highlight the need for intensified surveillance and proactive multidisciplinary management in older patients with PTB complicated by CPA.

Diabetes mellitus was another independent predictor of adverse prognosis, conferring an approximately twofold increased risk. Chronic hyperglycemia induces metabolic dysregulation and immune dysfunction, including impaired neutrophil phagocytosis, blunted cellular immune responses, and exaggerated release of inflammatory mediators, thereby facilitating persistent mycobacterial and fungal infections (25-28). Furthermore, pharmacokinetic interactions between anti-TB agents (such as rifampicin and isoniazid) and glucose-lowering medications may destabilize glycemic control and reduce antimicrobial efficacy (29). These mechanisms collectively account for the poorer outcomes observed in patients with diabetes and highlight the importance of strict glycemic control and close monitoring of drug-drug interactions throughout the treatment course.

Chest imaging plays a pivotal role in evaluating disease severity and prognosis in PTB complicated by CPA. In this study, both pretreatment hemoptysis and pulmonary cavitation were independently associated with unfavorable outcomes. Hemoptysis is a hallmark symptom of both PTB and CPA (30,31), reflecting active disease and vascular involvement. Even mild hemoptysis (<30 mL per episode) was associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, suggesting that limited bleeding should not be regarded as clinically insignificant. Recurrent or persistent hemoptysis often indicates ongoing tissue destruction, inadequate infection control, and an elevated risk of respiratory failure or secondary infection (32).

Pulmonary cavitation is a defining radiological feature of CPA and emerged as the strongest predictor of adverse prognosis in both Cox regression and SHAP analyses. Cavitary lesions indicate advanced parenchymal destruction and create a permissive microenvironment for Aspergillus colonization and persistence (33,34). Fragile neovascularization within the cavity walls predisposes to vascular erosion and life-threatening massive hemoptysis, directly linking cavitation to increased mortality risk (35). Previous studies have also indicated that cavity formation is closely associated with impaired host immunity, perpetuating a vicious cycle of structural damage, immune suppression, and chronic infection (36). The absence of cavitation on imaging has been reported as a negative predictive marker for CPA, further emphasizing its prognostic significance (37). These findings support prioritizing cavitary disease in clinical risk stratification, with intensified imaging surveillance, early antifungal treatment optimization, and proactive preparedness for hemoptysis.

Regular medication adherence emerged as a major modifiable determinant of prognosis. In this cohort, fewer than three-quarters of patients with unfavorable outcomes maintained regular, uninterrupted therapy, and irregular adherence was associated with more than a twofold increased risk of adverse events. Inadequate anti-TB treatment—whether due to insufficient dosing, premature discontinuation, or unsupervised interruption—facilitates disease persistence, the emergence of drug resistance, and progressive lung damage. Similarly, inconsistent antifungal therapy compromises fungal clearance, allowing continued colonization of cavitary lesions and increasing the risk of hemoptysis and disease progression (38). These findings highlight the importance of structured adherence support, including patient education, supervised treatment, and regular follow-up, as integral components of comprehensive care for PTB complicated by CPA.

A key strength of this study lies in integrating conventional Cox regression with interpretable machine learning techniques. By applying SHAP analysis, the relative contribution of each prognostic factor was quantified, establishing a clear hierarchy of risk, with pulmonary cavitation, hemoptysis, diabetes mellitus, and medication adherence emerging as dominant drivers of poor outcomes. This approach provides clinicians with an intuitive and quantitative framework for individualized risk assessment and stratified management.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design is inherently susceptible to selection and information bias and precludes causal inference. Second, the moderate sample size and region-specific population may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings with differing disease characteristics and resource availability. Third, the relatively short follow-up period primarily captured early severe outcomes, such as massive hemoptysis and death, and may not fully represent the long-term clinical trajectory of CPA. Fourth, inclusion of clinically diagnosed PTB, particularly in smear-negative cases, raises the possibility of misclassification between PTB and CPA, which may mimic or coexist. Fifth, although aspergilloma has been reported as an adverse prognostic factor (39), it was not systematically recorded and thus not analyzed; its effect may have been partially captured by pulmonary cavitation, but its independent impact requires further evaluation with detailed radiological phenotyping. Sixth, non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection was not systematically excluded; given overlapping clinical and radiological features with PTB, misclassification cannot be excluded, and the coexistence of non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) disease and CPA may have influenced outcomes. Seventh, drug-resistance data for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, including multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant strains, were not consistently available and were not incorporated, despite their potential influence on disease severity and prognosis. In addition, the definition of unfavorable outcomes focused on major clinical events, potentially underestimating milder but clinically meaningful disease progression. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including nutritional status, socioeconomic conditions, and host susceptibility, cannot be excluded. Finally, although SHAP analysis enhances interpretability, the feature importance it identifies reflects predictive relevance rather than causal effects.

Future multicenter, prospective studies with extended follow-up, standardized data collection, and refined phenotyping are warranted to validate these findings and further optimize prognostic models. The integration of multidimensional biomarkers and social determinants of health may additionally enhance predictive accuracy and clinical applicability.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, age, diabetes mellitus, hemoptysis before treatment, pulmonary cavitation, and medication adherence were identified as independent determinants of unfavorable outcomes in patients with PTB complicated by CPA. Among these factors, pulmonary cavitation emerged as the most influential prognostic indicator, highlighting the central role of structural lung damage in disease progression and adverse clinical events. The integration of conventional Cox regression with SHAP-based interpretability enabled quantitative ranking of prognostic contributors, offering a transparent and clinically applicable framework for risk stratification. These findings emphasize the importance of early identification of high-risk patients and support a stratified management approach that prioritizes close monitoring of cavitary disease, proactive hemoptysis control, optimized glycemic management in patients with diabetes, and reinforcement of treatment adherence. Collectively, this study provides real-world evidence to guide individualized prognostic assessment and targeted intervention in PTB complicated by CPA, and establishes a foundation for future prospective, multicenter studies aimed at improving outcomes in this high-risk population.
