Reachability in Vector Addition System with States Parameterized by Geometric Dimension
Yangluo Zheng

Abstract
The geometric dimension of a Vector Addition System with States (VASS), emerged in Leroux and Schmitz (2019) and formalized by Fu, Yang, and Zheng (2024), quantifies the dimension of the vector space spanned by cycle effects in the system. This paper explores the VASS reachability problem through the lens of geometric dimension, revealing key differences from the traditional dimensional parameterization. Notably, we establish that the reachability problem for both geometrically 1-dimensional and 2-dimensional VASS is PSPACE -complete, achieved by extending the pumping technique originally proposed by Czerwiński et al. (2019).

Introduction

Vector Addition Systems with States (VASSes), equivalent to Petri nets, serve as a fundamental model for concurrency

[
7
]
. A VASS extends finite automata with integer counters that cannot be zero-tested but
must be kept non-negative. Central to the algorithmic theory of VASS is the
reachability problem
: determining if
a run exists from one configuration to another. Due to its generic nature, numerous practical problems can be modeled
via the reachability problem
[
15
]
. After decades of study, the computational complexity
of the VASS reachability problem was settled to be Ackermann-complete

[
12
,
5
,
11
]
. However, when fixing the

dimension
—the number of counters, a gap remains in complexity bounds. For
d
-dimensional VASS where
d>2
,
reachability lies in
\mathsf{F}_{d}

[
9
]
, the
d
th level of the Grzegorczyk hierarchy of
complexity classes
[
13
]
, while
\mathsf{F}_{d}
-hardness is achieved with

(2d+3)
-dimensional VASS
[
3
]
. This gap is known to be closed only in low
dimensions.
PSPACE
-completeness holds for 2-dimensional VASS under binary encoding

[
2
,
4
]
, and
NL
-completeness under unary encoding

[
6
]
.
NP
-completeness holds for 1-dimensional VASS under binary encoding

[
10
]
, and again
NL
-completeness under unary encoding

[
6
]
. Dimension has traditionally served as the standard parameterization for reachability.

On the other hand, the structure of
cycles
was identified as a pivotal factor controlling the
complexity of the VASS reachability problem
[
12
]
. The notion of
geometric dimension
,
formalized in
[
9
]
, measures the dimension of the
cycle space
—vector space spanned by all cycle effects. Insights from
[
12
,
9
]
suggest that the
\mathsf{F}_{d}

upper bound of the famous KMLST algorithm applies to geometric dimension
d
, not only to dimension
d
. In this work we
propose geometric dimension as an alternative parameterization for the VASS reachability problem. In theory the
geometric dimension is closer to the nature of the reachability problem, as suggested by
[
12
]

and
[
9
]
, and the cycles provides a rich structure for analysis. In practice, as the system
parameters may not necessarily be independent, fixing geometric dimension rather than dimension allows one to introduce
certain types of interconnections in system parameters for free, which could possibly make the model more expressive.

.

for their inner product. The
support
of a vector
\bm{v}
is
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v}):=\{i\in[d],\bm{v}(i)\neq 0\}
. The support of a set
S
of vectors is
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(S):=\bigcup_{\bm{v}\in S}\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})
. A vector
\bm{v}
is

2.1
Vector Addition System with States

Let
d\geq 0
be an integer. A
d
-dimensional vector addition system with states (
d
-VASS)
is a pair
G=(Q,T)
where
Q
is a finite set of
states
and
T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
is a finite set of

transitions
. Clearly a VASS can also be viewed as a directed graph with edges labelled by integer vectors. Given
a word
\pi=(p_{1},\bm{a}_{1},q_{1})(p_{2},\bm{a}_{2},q_{2})\ldots(p_{n},\bm{a}_{n},q_{n})\in T^{*}
over transitions, we say
that
\pi
is a
path from
p_{1}
to
q_{n}
if
q_{i}=p_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. It is a

cycle
if we further have
p_{1}=q_{n}
. Such a path is usually presented in the following form:

\pi=p_{1}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}q_{1}\xrightarrow{t_{2}}q_{2}\xrightarrow{t_{3}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}q_{n}

(1)

where
t_{i}=(p_{i},\bm{a}_{i},q_{i})
. The
effect
of
\pi
is defined to be
\Delta(\pi):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{a}_{i}
.

Size, traversal number and characteristic

The norm of a transition
t=(p,\bm{a},q)
is defined by
\left\|{t}\right\|:=\left\|{\bm{a}}\right\|
. For a
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)

we write
\left\|{T}\right\|:=\max\{\left\|{t}\right\|:t\in T\}
. We shall mainly consider VASS under binary encoding, so the

size
of
G
is given by
|G|:=|Q|+d\cdot|T|\cdot\lceil\log(\left\|{T}\right\|+1)\rceil+1
.

We define the
traversal number
of
G
to be the maximal number of distinct states that can be visited
(traversed) by a path in
G
, denoted by
\varsigma(G)
. We remark that
\varsigma(G)
is an upper bound of (i) length
of any simple path/cycle, and (ii) number of connected components visited by any path. Note also the trivial fact

\varsigma(G)\leq|Q|
.

The
characteristic
of
G
, denoted by
\chi(G)
, is defined to be
\chi(G):=\varsigma(G)\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
. So

\chi(G)
upper bounds the norm of effect any simple path/cycle in
G
.

Semantics of VASSes

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS. A
configuration

c
of
G
is a pair of a state
p\in Q
and a vector

\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, written as
c=p(\bm{v})
. We will often confuse a configuration with its vector. So we
shall write, for example,
\left\|{c}\right\|
for
\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|
, and
c(i)
for
\bm{v}(i)
. The semantics of
G
is defined
as follows. For each transition
t=(p,\bm{a},q)\in T
, the one-step transition relation
\xrightarrow{t}
relates
all pairs of configurations of the form
(p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v}))
where
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+\bm{a}
. Then for a word
\pi=t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n}\in T^{*}
, the relation
\xrightarrow{\pi}
is the
composition
\xrightarrow{\pi}{}:={}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\xrightarrow{t_{n}}
. So
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\pi}q(\bm{v})
if and only if there are configurations
p_{0}(\bm{u}_{0}),\ldots,p_{n}(\bm{u}_{n})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that

p(\bm{u})=p_{0}(\bm{u}_{0})\xrightarrow{t_{1}}p_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})\xrightarrow{t_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}p_{n}(\bm{u}_{n})=q(\bm{v}).

(2)

Also, when
\pi=\epsilon
is the empty word, the relation
\xrightarrow{\epsilon}
is the identity relation over

Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Note that
\xrightarrow{\pi}
is non-empty only if
\pi
is a path. When
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\pi}q(\bm{v})
we also say that
\pi
induces (or is) a
run
from
p(\bm{u})
to
q(\bm{v})
. We
emphasize that all configurations on this run lie in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, and that they are uniquely determined by

p(\bm{u})
and
\pi
. Finally, the
reachability relation
of
G
is defined to be
\xrightarrow{*}{}:={}\bigcup_{\pi\in T^{*}}\xrightarrow{\pi}
.

Does

hold in

It is a folklore that this problem can be reduced to the following one in polynomial time:

, two states

.

Does

Thus, in this paper we mainly focus on the runs starting from

and ending at
\bm{0}
. Such a run is called a

Reverse of VASS

For a VASS
G=(Q,T)
we define its

as the VASS
G^{\text{rev}}=(Q,T^{\text{rev}})
where

. The
reverse

of a path (or a

and negating their effects. We note that

Geometric Dimension

Definition 3
.

Let
G
be a
d
-VASS. The
cycle space
of
G
is the vector space
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}

spanned by the effects of all cycles in
G
, that is:
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G):=\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{\Delta(\theta):\theta\text{ is a
cycle in }G\}
.

The dimension of the cycle space of
G
is called the
geometric dimension
of
G
, denoted by
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G):=\dim(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G))
. We say
G
is
geometrically
k
-dimensional
if
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)\leq k
.

It should be noticed that the cycle space of a VASS is indeed spanned by the effects of all simple cycles in it.

Lemma 4
.

Let
G
be a
d
-VASS, then
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
equals to the vector space spanned by the effects of all simple cycles
in
G
, that is,
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)=\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{\Delta(\beta):\beta\text{ is a simple cycle in }G\}
.

Proof.

Just note that the effect of every cycle is a finite sum of effects of simple cycles.
∎

A naive algorithm computes the geometric dimension of a VASS by enumerating all simple cycles in it, which requires
PSPACE
. Indeed, we show here a more efficient algorithm which computes
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)
in polynomial time.

3.1
Computing Geometric Dimension

We shall actually design a stronger algorithm that given a VASS
G
as input, computes a basis for
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
.
Observe that every cycle lies within some maximal strongly connected component (SCC) of
G
. Once we have an algorithm
that computes a basis for the cycle spaces of every SCC of
G
, a basis for
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
is just a maximal linearly
independent subset of the union of these bases, which can be computed by Gaussian elimination in polynomial time. So the
problem reduces to computing a basis for
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
in case
G
is strongly connected.

Fix a strongly connected
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)
. We introduce an operation called
cycle shrinking
. Let
\theta
be
a simple cycle in
G
that is not a self-loop and has the form

\theta=p_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}p_{1}\xrightarrow{t_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}p_{n}=p_{0}.

(3)

First we define a “shift function”
s:Q\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as follows. If
q=p_{k}
for some
k\in[n]
, we set

s(q):=\Delta(\theta[1..k])=\Delta(t_{1})+\ldots+\Delta(t_{k})
; otherwise we set
s(q):=\bm{0}
. Note that

s(p_{0})=s(p_{n})=\Delta(\theta)
. Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
be the set of states that occurs on
\theta
. Now we
define a new VASS
G/\theta=(Q^{\theta},T^{\theta})
that “shrinks”
\theta
into a single states as follows.

•

Q^{\theta}:=(Q\setminus P)\cup\{\theta\}
.

•

Let
h:Q\to Q^{\theta}
be defined as
h(q)=\theta
if
q\in P
and
h(q)=q
otherwise. The
transitions are given by
T^{\theta}:=\{(h(p),s(p)+\bm{a}-s(q),h(q)):(p,\bm{a},q)\in T\}
.

It should be clear that
|Q^{\theta}|<|Q|
as
\theta
is not a self-loop, and that
G/\theta
can be constructed in
polynomial time given
G
and
\theta
. Observe that
h
is a graph homomorphism from
G
to
G/\theta
. Thus

G/\theta
is strongly connected as long as
G
is. We can show that
G/\theta
preserves the cycle space of
G
.

Proposition 5
.

\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G/\theta)=\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
.

Now the algorithm for computing a basis of
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
where
G
is strongly connected should be clear. As listed
in
algorithm 1
, we repeatedly shrink a cycle in
G
until there remains only one state. Then its cycle
space is the span of effects of self-loops in it. A basis of
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
can be computed using Gaussian
elimination. Since a cycle shrinking reduces the number of states by at least one, after at most
|Q|
rounds we must
stop with a single state remained. Thus the algorithm runs in polynomial time.

3.2
Geometry of Reachability Sets and Runs

Given a VASS
G=(Q,T)
and a configuration
p(\bm{u})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we write
\mathop{\operatorname{Reach}}\nolimits_{G}(p(\bm{u}))
for
all configurations that is reachable from
p(\bm{u})
:
\mathop{\operatorname{Reach}}\nolimits_{G}(p(\bm{u}))=\{q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}:p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{v})\}
. The next lemma shows that the “dimension” of any reachable set is bounded by

\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)
, in the sense that it is contained in a finite union of affine copies of
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
. Here the sum of a
vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
and a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
is defined as
\bm{v}+S:=\{\bm{v}+\bm{s}:\bm{s}\in S\}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS,
p(\bm{u})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
be a configuration of
G
. Then

\mathop{\operatorname{Reach}}\nolimits_{G}(p(\bm{u}))\subseteq Q\times\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
\left\|{\bm{z}}\right\|\leq\chi(G)\end{subarray}}\bm{u}+\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)+\bm{z}.

(4)

In other words, for any configuration
q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{v})
, we have
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+\bm{c}+\bm{z}
for some
\bm{c}\in\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
and
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
\left\|{\bm{z}}\right\|\leq\chi(G)
.

Note that one may need exponentially many (roughly
O(\chi(G)^{d})
) affine copies of
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
to cover

\mathop{\operatorname{Reach}}\nolimits_{G}(p(\bm{u}))
. The next lemma shows that any fixed run from
p(\bm{u})
is confined in, however, at most
|Q|

affine copies of
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS. For any run
\pi
in
G
with source
p(\bm{u})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
, there is
a function
f_{\pi}:Q\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that for every configuration
q(\bm{v})
occurring on
\pi
, we have

\bm{v}\in\bm{u}+\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)+f_{\pi}(q)
. Moreover,
\left\|{f_{\pi}(q)}\right\|\leq\chi(G)
for every
q\in Q
.

These two lemmas follows easily from the fact that we can view a run as a simple path interleaved with many cycles.
Their proofs are moved into the appendix.

4
Geometrically 2-Dimensional VASS

In this section we focus exclusively on geometrically 2-dimensional VASSes. We prove that reachability in geometrically
2-dimensional VASSes is
PSPACE
-complete. The lower bound is a simple corollary of
[
2
, Lemma
20]
, so most effort will be devoted to the upper bound. Our proof is based on the pumping
technique proposed in
[
4
]
for 2-VASSes, where they showed that every run in a 2-VASS
is either
thin
— confined in some belt-shaped regions, or
thick
— enjoying good pumping properties
that can be exploited to shrink long runs. We extend this technique and prove a similar thin-thick classification for
geometrically 2-dimensional VASSes. This enables us to obtain an exponential length bound for reachability witnesses,
from which the
PSPACE
upper bound follows easily.

At first, we introduce some projection tools in
subsection 4.1
and
subsection 4.2
, which enable us to create
a suitable 2-dimensinoal coordinate system in the cycle space.

4.1
Sign Reflecting Projection

An
orthant
is one of the
2^{d}
regions in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
split by the
d
axes. Formally, given a vector
t\in\{+1,-1\}^{d}
, the orthant
Z_{t}
defined by
t
is the set
Z_{t}:=\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}:\bm{u}(i)\cdot t(i)\geq 0\text{ for all }i\in[d]\}
. The non-negative orthant
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{d}=Z_{(+1,+1,\ldots,+1)}
is a
major concern in this paper.

Let
I\subseteq[d]
be a subset of indices. For a vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, we define its
projection
onto indices in
I
as a function
\bm{u}|_{I}\in\mathbb{Q}^{I}
given by
(\bm{u}|_{I})(i)=\bm{u}(i)\text{ for all
}i\in I
. We tacitly identify the function
\bm{u}|_{I}\in\mathbb{Q}^{I}
as a vector in
\mathbb{Q}^{|I|}
. For a set
of vectors
V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, we define
V|_{I}:=\bigl\{\bm{v}|_{I}:\bm{v}\in V\bigr\}
. It should be clear
that the projection of a vector space onto indices in
I
is again a vector space in
\mathbb{Q}^{|I|}
, and the
projection of an orthant
Z_{t}
onto
I
is an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{|I|}
defined by
t|_{I}
.

Definition 8

(
[
9
, Definition A.7]
)
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a vector space and

Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. A set of indices
I\subseteq[d]
is called a

for

\bm{v}\in P

\bm{v}|_{I}\in Z|_{I}

Sign-reflecting projection helps us project the vectors in a vector space to some of its components so that the
pre-image of a certain orthant still belongs to one orthant. Moreover, we know that such a projection is one-to-one when
restricted to that orthant.

Lemma 9

(
[
9
, Lemma A.8]
)
.

Let

be a vector space and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Let

be a
sign-reflecting projection for

w.r.t.
Z
. Then every vector
\bm{v}\in P
is uniquely determined by

\bm{v}|_{I}
. In other words, for any
\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\in P

\bm{v}|_{I}=\bm{v}^{\prime}|_{I}
implies
\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{\prime}
.

We mainly care about sign-reflecting projections for a plane, i.e. a

Lemma 10

9

)

4.2
Support Projection

12
gives bounds on the components in the support of the plane. So we would like the cycle
space of a VASS
G
to have full support
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G))=[d]
. In this section we develop a technique called

support projection
to transform an arbitrary geometrically 2-dimensional VASS to one with such good property,
without increasing its traversal number and the characteristic.

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a geometrically 2-dimensional
d
-VASS. Let
S=\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G))
and
\overline{S}=[d]\setminus S
. For vectors
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{S}
and
\overline{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\overline{S}}
, we define
their composition
\bm{v}\circ_{S}\overline{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
naturally by
\bm{v}\circ_{S}\overline{\bm{v}})(i)=\bm{v}(i)
if
i\in S
and
\bm{v}\circ_{S}\overline{\bm{v}})(i)=\overline{\bm{v}}(i)

if
i\in\overline{S}
. Since
S
is always clear from the context, we will simply write
\bm{v}\circ\overline{\bm{v}}
for this composition.

The
support projection
of
G
is the
|S|
-dimensional VASS
G^{S}=(Q^{S},T^{S})
where

\displaystyle Q^{S}

\displaystyle:=\{(q,{\bm{v}})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{\overline{S}}:\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|\leq 2\chi(G)\},

(6)

\displaystyle T^{S}

\displaystyle:=\{((p,{\bm{u}}),\bm{a}|_{S},(q,{\bm{v}}))\in Q^{S}\times\mathbb{N}^{S}\times Q^{S}:(p,\bm{a},q)\in T,\bm{u}+\bm{a}|_{\overline{S}}=\bm{v}\}.

(7)

A state of the form
(q,\bm{v})
in
G^{S}
is denoted
q^{\bm{v}}
for conciseness.

There is a huge expansion in the size of
G^{S}
, as
|Q^{S}|=|Q|\cdot(2\chi(G))^{|\overline{S}|}
. On the other hand, we
can show that support projection does not increase traversal number and characteristic, and the projected VASS has full
support as we expected.

Proposition 13
.

\varsigma(G^{S})\leq\varsigma(G)
,
\chi(G^{S})\leq\chi(G)
, and
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G^{S}))=S
.

Proof.

Let’s denote by
\varsigma(\pi)
the number of distinct states in the path
\pi
. Consider any path
\pi^{S}
in
G^{S}

of the form
\pi^{S}=p_{0}^{\bm{v}_{0}}\xrightarrow{t_{1}^{S}}p_{1}^{\bm{v_{1}}}\xrightarrow{t_{2}^{S}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}^{S}}p_{n}^{\bm{v}_{n}}
where
t_{i}^{S}\in T^{S}
. We define a corresponding path
\pi
in
G
as
\pi:=p_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}p_{1}\xrightarrow{t_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}p_{n}
where
t_{i}=(p_{i-1},\bm{a}_{i},p_{i})

and
\bm{a}_{i}:=\Delta(t_{i}^{S})\circ(\bm{v}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i-1})
. Verify that
t_{i}
is indeed a transition in
T

by the definition of
T^{S}
. We claim that for any
i,j\in[0,n]
,
p_{i}=p_{j}
implies
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{v}_{j}
,
then it follows that
\varsigma(\pi^{S})=\varsigma(\pi)
. Indeed, suppose
p_{i}=p_{j}
, then the sub path in
\pi

from
p_{i}
to
p_{j}
is a cycle with effect
\Delta(t_{i+1}t_{i+2}\ldots t_{j})\in\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
. So
\bm{v}_{j}=\bm{v}_{i}+\Delta(t_{i+1}t_{i+2}\ldots t_{j})|_{\overline{S}}=\bm{v}_{i}
, which proves the claim. As the choice of

\pi^{S}
is arbitrary, for any path in
G^{S}
, there exists a path in
G
visits the same number of distinct states.
This proves
\varsigma(G)\geq\varsigma(G^{S})
.

Since it is clear that
\left\|{T^{S}}\right\|\leq\left\|{T}\right\|
, we immediately have
\chi(G^{S})=\varsigma(G^{S})\cdot\left\|{T^{S}}\right\|\leq\varsigma(G)\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|=\chi(G)
.

Finally, we show
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G^{S}))=S
. Observe that it suffices to prove
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)|_{S}\subseteq\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G^{S})
. By
4
,
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
is spanned by effects of
all simple cycles in
G
. So consider any cycle
\theta
in
G
of the form
\theta:=p_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}p_{1}\xrightarrow{t_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}p_{n}=p_{0}
where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in T
. We define vectors
\bm{v}_{0},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{\overline{S}}
by

\bm{v}_{0}:=\chi(G)\cdot\bm{1},\qquad\bm{v}_{i+1}:=\bm{v}_{i}+\Delta(t_{i+1})|_{\overline{S}}.

(8)

Since
\theta
is simple, we have
n\leq\varsigma(G)
. It follows that
\bm{0}\leq\bm{v}_{i}\leq 2\chi(G)\cdot\bm{1}
for all
i\in[0,n]
. So
p_{i}^{\bm{v}_{i}}
is a state in
G^{S}
. Also note that
\bm{v}_{n}=\bm{v}_{0}+\Delta(\theta)|_{\overline{S}}=\bm{v}_{0}
. We can define a corresponding cycle
\theta^{S}:=p_{0}^{\bm{v}_{0}}\xrightarrow{t_{1}^{S}}p_{1}^{\bm{v_{1}}}\xrightarrow{t_{2}^{S}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}^{S}}p_{n}^{\bm{v}_{n}}
,
where
t_{i}=(p_{i-1}^{\bm{v}_{i-1}},\Delta(t_{i})|_{S},p_{i}^{\bm{v}_{i}})
. Verify that each
t_{i}
is a transition in

T^{S}
by definition. So
\theta^{S}
is a cycle in
G^{S}
. In particular, we have
\Delta(\theta)|_{S}=\Delta(\theta^{S})\in\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G^{S})
. As the choice of
\theta
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)|_{S}=(\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{\Delta(\theta):\theta\text{ is a simple cycle in }G\})|_{S}\subseteq\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G^{S}),

(9)

which is the desired result.
∎

Main results

Our major goal is to exhibit an exponential length bound of reachability witnesses for geometrically 2-dimensinoal VASS,
from which the

-completeness follows immediately.

Theorem 14
.

For any
\bm{0}
-run
\tau

-VASS

in

Reachability in geometrically 2-dimensional VASS is

Proof.

The lower bound is inherited from the

[

after reducing to the
\bm{0}
-reachability problem, for which

is
enough.
∎

Indeed, we will focus on the support projection of

Lemma 16
.

For any
\bm{0}
-run
\tau
in a geometrically 2-dimensional

-VASS

4.4
Degenerate VASS and Thin Runs

Depending on whether the cycle space of a VASS can be sign-reflectively projected onto an axes-plane with respect to the
non-negative orthant
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, we classify geometrically 2-dimensional VASSes into the following two
classes.

Definition 17
.

A geometrically 2-dimensional VASS

is
proper
if
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)\cap\mathbb{Q}^{d}_{\geq 0}
contains two
linearly independent vectors; it is

otherwise.

In this subsection we focus on degenerate VASSes. We show that every run from
\bm{0}
in a degenerate VASS is

thin
in the sense of the following definitions.

Definition 18
.

Let
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and

. The

\mathcal{B}_{\bm{v},W}

\mathcal{B}_{\bm{v},W}:=\left\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0},\left\|{\bm{u}-\alpha\bm{v}}\right\|\leq W\right\}.

(10)

The beam
\mathcal{B}_{\bm{v},W}
is said to be an
A

where
A\in\mathbb{N}
if
\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|\leq A
and
W\leq A
.

Definition 19
.

Let
G
be a
d
-VASS. A run
\pi
in
G
is said to be
A
-thin
if for every configuration
p(\bm{u})

occurring in
\pi

\bm{u}
belongs to some
A
-beam.

Indeed, we can relax the definition of beams by letting the direction
\bm{v}
range over all integer vectors

\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Let
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}
. The
generalized beam

\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{v},W}
is defined by

\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{v},W}:=\left\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{Q},\left\|{\bm{u}-\alpha\bm{v}}\right\|\leq W\right\}.

(11)

Lemma 20
.

For any
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
W\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
\bm{v}^{+}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
\bm{v}^{-}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{v},W}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\bm{v}^{+},W}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\bm{v}^{-},W}
and that
\left\|{\bm{v}^{+}}\right\|,\left\|{\bm{v}^{-}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|
.

With
20
,

is equivalent to say that each configuration is located in
some generalized
A
-beam. This makes it easy to argue if a run is thin. We remark that the following result do not
depend on whether the VASS has full support.

Lemma 21
.

Let
G
be a geometrically 2-dimensional VASS that is degenerate. Then every
\bm{0}
-run in
G
is

\chi(G)^{O(d)}
-thin.

Proof sketch.

A degenerate VASS
G
falls into one of the following 3 cases: (i)
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)<2
; (ii)
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)=2
and
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)\cap\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{d}=\{\bm{0}\}
, or (iii)
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)=2
and
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)\cap\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{d}=\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{\bm{u}\}
for some

42

Suppose
\mathop{\operatorname{gdim}}\nolimits(G)<2

\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)=\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{\bm{c}\}
where
\bm{c}
is the effect of a (possibly empty)

G
. So
\left\|{\bm{c}}\right\|\leq\chi(G)

every

Geometrically 1-Dimensional and 0-Dimensional VASS

We have shown that the reachability problem in geometrically 2-dimensional VASS is
PSPACE
-complete, which is same as the
complexity of reachability in 2-VASS. The situation becomes different for lower geometric dimensions. In this section we
study the reachability problems for geometrically 1-dimensional and 0-dimensional VASSes. Indeed, these results turn out
to be just rephrase of existing results for 2-VASS and 1-VASS.

Theorem 37
.

Reachability in geometrically 1-dimensional VASS is
PSPACE
-complete.

Proof.

The upper bound is implied by
Theorem 15
. For the lower bound, we refer the readers to
the reduction from bounded one-counter automata to 2-VASS
[
2
, Lemma 20]
. We remark
that as the reduced VASS uses 2 counters to simulate a bounded counter, every transition have effect of the form

(z,-z)
for some
z\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, the cycle space of the reduced VASS is contained in
\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{(1,-1)\}
.
So this reduction indeed establishes the
PSPACE
-hardness of reachability in geometrically 1-dimensional VASS.
∎

Theorem 38
.

Reachability in geometrically 0-dimensional VASS is
NP
-complete.

Proof.

We first show the upper bound. Let
G=(Q,T)
be a geometrically
0
-dimensional VASS. Then any cycle in
G
has
effect
\bm{0}
. So for any run
\tau
in
G
we can safely remove all cycles from
\tau
and obtain a run

\overline{\tau}
whose length is bounded by
|Q|
. Now a nondeterministic algorithm can decide reachability in
G

by simply guessing a run of length at most
|Q|
.

For the lower bound, we recall the folklore reduction from
Subset-Sum
to the reachability problem in
1-VASS. Given an instance
\langle S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\},s\rangle
of
Subset-Sum
, where
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and the goal is to find a subset of
S
whose sum is
s
, we construct a 1-VASS
G=(Q,T)
as follows.
The states are
Q:=\{q_{0},\ldots,q_{n}\}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,n
we add two transitions
q_{i-1}\xrightarrow{a_{i}}q_{i}
and
q_{i-1}\xrightarrow{0}q_{i}
. One can easily observe that there exists a subset of
S

with sum
s
if and only if
q_{0}(0)\to^{*}q_{n}(s)
in
G
. Note that there are no cycles in
G
, thus
G
is indeed
geometrically 0-dimensional.
∎

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have proposed and studied the reachability problem in vector addition systems with states (VASS)
parameterized by geometric dimension. We introduced an efficient algorithm for computing the geometric dimension of a
VASS and demonstrated some simple geometrical properties of reachable sets and runs. The primary focus is on VASS with
low geometric dimensions, particularly those that are geometrically 2-dimensional. By generalizing existing pumping
techniques for 2-VASS, we have shown that the reachability problem in geometrically 2-dimensional VASS is

PSPACE
-complete. The techniques of sign-reflecting projection and support projection also provide insights into how
results for
d
-VASS can be adapted to geometrically
d
-dimensional VASS.

The results for geometrically 1- and 0-dimensional VASS are both interesting and technically straightforward. By
re-examining existing results, we have shown that reachability in geometrically 1-dimensional VASS is

PSPACE
-complete, while in geometrically 0-dimensional VASS it is
NP
-complete. It is worth noting that reachability
is known to be
NP
-complete in 1-VASS and
NL
-complete in 0-VASS. Our findings highlight a distinction in
expressiveness and computational power between geometrically
d
-dimensional VASS and
d
-VASS. We suggest that
comparing these two models could be a possible direction for future research.
