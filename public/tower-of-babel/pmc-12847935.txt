A comparison of sperm parameters DNA fragmentation and telomere length in testicular versus ejaculated spermatozoa
Authors: Malihe Afsari, Haniyeh Nikkhah, Mohsen Ahrari, Ali Nabi, Ehsan Farashahi-Yazd, Azam Agha-rahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile previous studies reported conflicting findings on DNA fragmentation in testicular versus ejaculated spermatozoa, this study aimed to perform a detailed analysis of testicular spermatozoa, evaluating sperm parameters and detailed morphology, DNA fragmentation, and relative telomere length across testicular and ejaculated spermatozoa. The study included 60 men in four groups: normozoospermic men (control), men with oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT), men with a history of sperm presence in the semen who were candidates for conventional testicular sperm extraction (TESE), and non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) patients undergoing microdissection testicular sperm extraction (M-TESE). Spermatozoa were evaluated for vitality (eosin staining), teratozoospermia Index (TZI) and detailed morphology (Diff-Quik staining), DNA fragmentation (sperm chromatin dispersion assay), and relative telomere length (q-PCR). The results demonstrated that sperm vitality was significantly lower in the OAT group. The control group showed markedly higher rate of normal morphology and lower TZI compared to the OAT, TESE, and M-TESE groups. The TESE and M-TESE groups showed higher rates of head-midpiece abnormalities and residual bodies compared to the control group (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, respectively). Sperm DNA fragmentation in the control group showed a significant decrease relative to all experimental groups (P < 0.0001). The control group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of sperm with intact acrosomes than all experimental groups (P < 0.0001). Moreover, the relative sperm telomere length was markedly lower in the M-TESE group compared to the control (P = 0.0006), OAT, and TESE groups (P < 0.0001). Our results demonstrated that testicular sperm have significantly elevated DNA damage levels. Moreover, these spermatozoa exhibit increased frequencies of morphological abnormalities in the head and midpiece regions, reacted acrosomes, and residual bodies.Keywords: Testicular spermatozoa, DNA fragmentation, Morphology, Sperm telomere lengthSubject terms: Cell biology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Urology

Introduction

Male factor alone accounts for about 30% of infertility cases, as the primary cause, and contributes around 20% more when combined with female factor infertility1. Since the introduction of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) in 1992, sperm retrieval techniques have become standard practice for managing male infertility related to azoospermia. In cases of obstructive azoospermia (OA), retrieval of sperm is commonly achieved through percutaneous and open retrieval methods. Conversely, in non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA), sperm is usually limited to specific localized areas within the testicular tissue, making extraction more challenging2,3.

In addition to azoospermia, testicular sperm retrieval is also utilized in various other conditions, such as severe or complete asthenozoospermia4, anejaculation related to spinal cord injury5, high levels of DNA fragmentation in spermatozoa, cryptozoospermia, and other cases6. Recently, sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) and its underlying mechanisms have attracted considerable research attention, particularly regarding the use of testicular sperm retrieval in patients with high levels of DNA damage. It is predicated on the hypothesis that testicular spermatozoa exhibit significantly lower levels of DNA fragmentation than ejaculated spermatozoa7–10, primarily due to the absence of post-testicular oxidative stress occurring during epididymal transit. However, clinical evidence confirming the efficacy of using testicular sperm remains limited11, and some of these studies exhibit methodological defects, such as the use of inconsistent techniques for evaluating DNA integrity7, or delayed assessment of DNA damage, up to four hours post-retrieval8. It has also been demonstrated that much of the DNA damage originates from the testes12.

Besides DNA fragmentation, current evidence indicates that telomere length has emerged as a novel biomarker for evaluating sperm quality in male infertility, complementing routine semen analysis13,14. Telomeres, which are nucleoprotein structures found at the chromosomal termini, contain non-coding hexameric sequences made up of tandem repeats of a well-conserved genomic sequence (TTAGGG)15. This unique configuration protects telomeres from improper DNA repair pathways, prevents gene damage caused by incomplete DNA replication, safeguards chromosome ends from loss, maintains chromosomal stability, and preserves critical biological functions of DNA16. Telomeres can be damaged by double-stranded DNA breaks or by the degradation of telomere-binding proteins. Conversely, sperm DNA damage may result from telomere dysfunction17.

A review by Vasilopoulos et al. highlighted that telomere length is shorter in infertile men and is associated with sperm DNA damage18. Several meta-analyses have demonstrated that elevated levels of SDF negatively affect fertilization rates, embryonic development, and overall pregnancy outcomes19,20. Moreover, increased DNA fragmentation has been observed in the spermatozoa of men exhibiting shorter telomere lengths21. However, no studies have yet investigated sperm telomere length (STL) in testicular samples.

Given the contradictory findings regarding testicular versus ejaculated spermatozoa in non-azoospermic cases and the lack of comprehensive studies on the characteristics of testicular sperm, this study aimed to perform a detailed analysis of testicular sperm. The evaluation included sperm parameters, detailed morphology, vitality, acrosome reaction, DNA fragmentation, and relative STL across different testicular sperm groups, including those retrieved via conventional testicular sperm extraction (TESE) and microdissection testicular sperm extraction (M-TESE). These findings were compared with two ejaculated sperm groups: one with oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT), representing severely abnormal sperm parameters22, and a control group with normal semen parameters. This comparison is intended to provide more accurate insights into the nature of testicular sperm, thereby paving the way for potential advances in future treatments.

Materials and methods

Approval for this cross-sectional study was obtained from our institutional ethics committee (IR.SSU.MEDICINE.REC.1403.006), and all experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study involved 60 male participants referred to the Yazd Reproductive Sciences Institute from June 2024 to February 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion.

Samples

The ejaculate samples comprised two groups: 15 normozoospermic semen as the control group, consisting of couples undergoing assisted reproduction using donated oocytes due to female factor infertility, and 15 semen samples with OAT, representing severely abnormal sperm parameters22.

The testicular samples also included two groups. 15 men with a history of sperm presence in the semen were candidates for conventional TESE. Approximately half of the TESE cases were performed for therapeutic purposes, either due to profoundly reduced sperm vitality in their ejaculate in 3 cases or the patient’s failure to provide a semen sample on the day of puncture in 5 cases. Other etiologies included vasectomy in 3 cases and a history of epididymal inflammation in 4 cases. Since these patients had sperm in their semen in previous records, we considered this group as having active spermatogenesis.

The fourth group included 15 men with NOA who underwent M-TESE with defective spermatogenesis. The diagnosis of NOA with impaired spermatogenesis was established based on semen analysis, endocrine evaluation, clinical examination, and genetic testing. All patients exhibited a normal karyotype, and none demonstrated hormonal profiles consistent with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Patients who were heavy smokers or had substance addiction were excluded from the study.

TESE and M-TESE procedure

Conventional TESE was usually carried out under general or local anesthesia. After opening the scrotal skin and tunica vaginalis, a small vertical incision was made through the tunica albuginea to excise small pieces of testicular tissue from different areas of each testis. M-TESE was also performed under general anesthesia. The tunica albuginea was widely opened along the antimesenteric border, allowing direct inspection of the testicular tissue under an operating microscope23.

Laboratory handling of testicular specimens

Testicular tissue samples were washed in a Petri dish with 1–2 ml of sperm washing medium (Ravan saze Co., Tehran, Iran). Mechanical dissection was subsequently performed using two sterile glass slides, and any associated blood clots were removed. Sperm presence was assessed at ×400 magnification using an inverted microscope (Olympus IX70). Cell suspensions were collected into sterile centrifuge tubes and diluted with 3 ml of culture medium. The suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. Following the removal of the supernatant, the pellet was mixed with 0.5–1.0 ml of culture medium24.

At each experimental stage, to enhance the accuracy of the analysis, testicular sperm were separated from surrounding tissue cells and red blood cells (RBCs). For this purpose, each sperm observed in the droplet was carefully aspirated into an injection needle under an inverted microscope. Subsequently, the sperm were either placed on a slide for staining assessment or transferred into a sterile microtube for molecular evaluation. Given the limited number of sperm in testicular samples25 and the small volume of the patient samples available for analysis, a total of 100 spermatozoa were collected for each analysis.

Assessment of sperm parameters and detailed morphology

Ejaculated samples were obtained after a period of 2 to 7 days of sexual abstinence and were allowed to liquefy at 37 °C for 30 to 60 min. The sum of the percentages of progressive and non-progressive motility in ejaculated samples, as well as any twitching motility in the testicular samples, was evaluated according to the WHO 2021 guidelines. Morphological analysis was performed using Diff-Quick staining (Ideh Varzan Farda Co., Tehran, Iran), following the manufacturer’s protocol26.

For morphological assessment, abnormalities in the sperm head, midpiece, tail, and residual body were recorded separately for each spermatozoon at ×1,000 magnification. Sperm exhibiting multiple defects were classified as having multiple abnormalities. The percentage of spermatozoa with morphological abnormalities was recorded.

We also calculated the Teratozoospermia Index (TZI), defined as the ratio of the total number of morphological defects (head, midpiece, tail, and residual body) to the number of abnormal spermatozoa. A higher TZI indicates a greater number of morphological abnormalities per spermatozoon26.

Assessment of sperm vitality

For assessing sperm vitality, eosin staining was performed. Based on WHO 2021 protocol, 0.67 g of eosin Y (Merck, Germany) and 0.9 g of sodium chloride were dissolved in 100 ml of purified water. Ten microliters of the sperm suspension were mixed with an equal volume of the eosin solution. After 30 s, 10 µl of the mixture were examined under a light microscope at ×1,000 magnification. Viable spermatozoa remained colorless, while dead spermatozoa appeared pink or red. The percentage of viable sperm was then calculated26.

Sperm chromatin dispersion (SCD) assay

DNA fragmentation index (DFI) was evaluated using the SCD method with the SDFA kit (Ideh Varzan Farda Co., Tehran, Iran). This analysis was performed immediately after sampling to prevent DNA damage caused by prolonged incubation27,28.

For testicular samples29, single selected spermatozoa were aspirated under an inverted microscope at ×200 magnification and 37 °C. Subsequently, they were embedded in agarose on a precoated slide and covered with a pre-warmed coverslip. The agarose was then allowed to solidify at 4 °C, after which the coverslip was carefully detached. The sample was subsequently treated with an acid denaturation solution followed by a lysis buffer. After rehydration, staining, washing, and drying, at least 100 spermatozoa were evaluated. Spermatozoa exhibiting large or medium-sized halos were classified as having intact DNA, while those with small or absent halos were considered to have fragmented DNA. The percentage of sperm with DNA fragmentation was then reported.

Assessment of acrosome reaction

Smears were initially air-dried and then fixed in 95% ethanol for 30 min. After fixation, each slide was incubated at 4 °C for over 1 h with 25 µL of PSA-FITC staining solution. Following washing with distilled water, the slides were examined under a fluorescence microscope at ×1,000 magnification using a wavelength of 450–490 nm. Spermatozoa that underwent an acrosome reaction either displayed a fluorescent band only at the equatorial segment or showed no fluorescence in the acrosomal region. Spermatozoa exhibiting bright and uniform fluorescence across more than half of the head were classified as having an intact acrosome. The percentage of spermatozoa with intact acrosomes was recorded30.

Relative sperm telomere length assessment

Genomic DNA was extracted using the BehPrep DNA extraction kit (BehGene, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative STL was determined by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), following Joglekar’s protocol with minor modifications, in which 36B4 was used as the reference gene31. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Based on the normality test, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test for pairwise comparisons was applied. Only statistically significant results from these pairwise comparisons (p < 0.05) are reported in the Results section. The absence of a reported comparison for any specific pair of groups indicates a non-significant finding. For correlation analysis, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used.

Sperm motility

The sum of the percentages of progressive and non-progressive motility for the control and OAT groups was 54.20 ± 5.95 and 27.80 ± 9.77, respectively. For the testicular group, any twitching motility was considered as motile sperm. These values were 26.27 ± 11.80 and 37.53 ± 13.43 for the TESE and M-TESE groups, respectively.

Discussion

As previously mentioned, in current assisted reproductive technologies (ART), testicular sperm retrieval is not limited to cases of azoospermia but is also employed in other clinical scenarios4–6. The rationale behind this approach is to bypass potential damage incurred during post-testicular sperm transport7. Given the limited number of studies on the characteristics of testicular sperm, we were prompted to investigate this issue in greater detail, with a specific focus on DNA fragmentation and telomere length. To address the limitations of previous studies7,8, DNA fragmentation was assessed immediately after sperm retrieval to avoid time-related DNA damage27,28, using the same technique across all groups. Additionally, spermatozoa in the testicular groups were separated from surrounding tissue and RBCs to minimize potential measurement errors.

Some studies suggest using testicular sperm in cases of high DNA fragmentation7–10. However, the use of testicular sperm instead of ejaculated sperm for ICSI remains a subject of debate among experts, citing insufficient evidence, biased study designs, small sample sizes, concerns about unnecessary testicular surgery, sperm retrieval failure32,33, intratesticular bleeding34, and, more importantly, reduced testosterone production after repeated procedures35.

In our study, in addition to the high level of DNA damage observed in the OAT group, which was associated with the presence of the most severe abnormalities22, it was also elevated in both testicular groups, consistent with our previous studies36–38. To our knowledge, only one study conducted by Meseguer et al. has compared DNA fragmentation levels between OA and NOA groups. Their results, in alignment with our findings, demonstrated elevated DNA fragmentation in testicular sperm, with significantly higher levels in sperm from NOA patients compared to those with OA. These findings contradict the hypothesis of post-testicular DNA damage, indicating that impaired spermatogenesis is the main cause of SDF39.

We also found that DNA damage remained elevated in the TESE group. This finding is in agreement with the study by Ramos et al., which found that the DNA fragmentation level was significantly higher in spermatozoa from men with obstructive azoospermia compared to those from fertile donors40.

Three main mechanisms have been identified for SDF: abortive apoptosis, defective chromatin remodeling within the testis, and oxidative stress, which may occur after spermiation41. Therefore, the retrieval of sperm from testicular tissue does not always prevent DNA damage, as such damage may already originate during spermiogenesis within the seminiferous epithelium42. In addition, spermatozoa acquire some of the essential maturation features during epididymal transit43, and defective or dead spermatozoa are tagged for elimination through interactions with epididymosomes44–46. When sperm are retrieved directly from the testis, these physiological selection processes are bypassed. Moreover, after retrieval, spermatozoa are surrounded by collateral cells such as white blood cells, RBCs, and epithelial cells, which can contribute to reactive oxygen species production47.

In addition to these factors, consistent with the findings of Steele et al., morphological analysis across the experimental groups revealed a significantly higher prevalence of residual bodies, as well as head and midpiece abnormalities, in testicular sperm compared to ejaculated sperm48. This suggests incomplete maturation and impaired spermatogenesis, which may cause increased DNA fragmentation, aligning with the reported inverse relationship between sperm morphology and DNA stability49.

The mean of normal sperm morphology was 1.2% in the OAT group and 0.2% in both TESE and M-TESE groups, indicating a lower morphological quality in testicular sperm relative to the OAT group. However, based on our study, testicular sperm exhibited higher vitality than the OAT group, suggesting their potential use in cases of necrozoospermia or severely reduced vitality. Overall, the benefit of using testicular sperm instead of ejaculated sperm for reducing SDF appears to be limited to cases of post-testicular DNA damage11, which can be evaluated using staining methods such as aniline blue to detect defects in histone-to-protamine replacement, and chromomycin A3 to detect protamine deficiency12.

Another factor associated with sperm DNA damage is telomere length50. Studies have shown that telomere length in spermatozoa or leukocytes may predict reproductive outcomes13. Telomere shortening may be both a consequence and a cause of DNA damage, although some previous studies have suggested that telomere shortening leads to increased DNA damage21. In our study, the NOA group exhibited a comparatively shorter relative STL among the examined groups. To date, a few studies have assessed telomere length in testicular sperm. Anna et al. reported that measuring telomere length in spermatogenic cells could provide a prognostic indicator of the developmental competence of embryos originating from testicular sperm, at both pre- and post-implantation stages51. Also, consistent with our findings, Qingling Yang et al. reported that NOA patients exhibited shorter leukocyte telomeres compared to OA patients and normospermic controls, highlighting the critical role of telomere maintenance in genomic stability52.

It is reported that 75% of sperm samples with abnormal telomere length exhibited increased levels of DNA fragmentation and sperm aneuploidy. Moreover, both abnormally short and long telomeres were associated with significantly lower pregnancy rates following ART53. Similarly, the research team led by Moskovtsev reported that although testicular spermatozoa tend to show lower levels of DNA damage, this potential advantage for ICSI may be counteracted by the higher aneuploidy rates observed in these cells9. Telomeres are essential for the attachment of chromosomes to the nuclear envelope, and their shortening disrupts this process, leading to increased aneuploidy, failure of pronuclear formation, and impaired cell cycle progression after ICSI54. Therefore, the low birth rates reported in NOA cases55,56 may be associated with both shorter telomere length and increased DNA fragmentation.

Besides lower morphology and higher TZI, acrosome analysis revealed a reduced percentage of spermatozoa with intact acrosomes in both testicular and OAT groups. Unstable acrosomal status, poor responsiveness, or insufficient enzymatic activity can adversely affect male fertility57. In addition to maturation defects during spermatogenesis, the epididymis plays a crucial role in maintaining acrosomal integrity and preventing premature reactions, which further underscores the challenges of using testicular sperm45,58. However, it remains unclear whether the low proportion of intact acrosomes observed in testicular groups results from maturation defects, insufficient acrosomal enzymatic activity, lack of epididymal protection, or a combination of these factors.

Despite the loss of the acrosome’s penetrating role in testicular sperm due to direct injection into the oocyte via ICSI, recent studies have demonstrated a significant association between acrosomal defects and Phospholipase C zeta) PLCζ(deficiency. Young et al. identified a novel population of PLCζ in both the acrosomal and post-acrosomal regions, suggesting its roles beyond oocyte activation, including involvement in the acrosome reaction59. Given the high proportion of testicular sperm with low levels of intact acrosomes, the reduced fertilization rates observed in azoospermia patients may be attributed to acrosomal defects and epigenetic abnormalities involving specific forms of PLCζ60.

Overall, our results suggest that in non-azoospermic cases, the use of testicular sperm should be considered when the origin of damage is post-testicular. For azoospermic patients undergoing testicular sperm retrieval, employing antioxidant-enriched culture media and appropriate sperm selection techniques may enhance ART outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. The primary constraint was the low sperm count in some testicular samples. On the other hand, the ideal design of obtaining paired ejaculated and testicular samples from the same individual was not ethically feasible. Consequently, patients with a prior documented history of sperm presence were identified and classified as having active spermatogenesis in the TESE group. Furthermore, the testicular group comprised a heterogeneous patient population with diverse etiologies. Future studies employing animal models could provide a controlled setting to overcome this limitation and validate our findings. Further research is warranted to validate these findings and to optimize clinical protocols.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results demonstrated that testicular sperm have significantly elevated DNA damage levels. The bypass of epididymal oxidative stress by testicular sperm does not guarantee intact DNA. The persistence of other mechanisms, notably abortive apoptosis, means that the risk of sperm with DNA damage remains. Moreover, these spermatozoa exhibit increased frequencies of morphological abnormalities in the head and midpiece regions, reacted acrosomes, and residual bodies.
