The association between sleep duration and mental health among pregnant women in rural northwest China
Authors: Nan Wang, Jianmin Ai, Chengjie Hao, Yi Yuan, Yiman Gu, Jie Yang, Jingchun Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal mental health during pregnancy is a significant public health concern in rural China. Given the limited availability of professional psychological services in rural areas, understanding the factors that influence mental health and identifying simple, effective family support strategies are crucial. This study investigates the association between sleep duration and maternal mental health among pregnant women in rural northwest China.MethodsThis study was conducted among 1035 pregnant women from 10 counties in rural areas. Sleep duration was measured by self-reported, which was categorized as short (≤ 8 h), medium (8–10 h), or long (≥ 10 h). Mental health was measured using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21). Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were employed to elucidate the prevalence of sleep duration issues and explored their association with mental health. Heterogeneity analyses were performed by family support and informal social support.Results21.06% of pregnant women reported short sleep duration, 56.72% had medium sleep duration, and 22.22% had long sleep duration. In addition, 34.40%, 38.26%, and 31.88% of participants showed moderate-severe depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively. Both short and long sleep durations were significantly associated with higher risks of moderate-severe stress (short: AOR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.14–2.49, p = 0.009; long: AOR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.05–2.13, p = 0.024). The associations were stronger among pregnant women without husband accompany them to prenatal check-ups or with poor relationships with their mothers-in-law. Negative attitudes toward psychological counseling and lower nutrition and health knowledge scores also reinforced these effects.ConclusionsBoth short and long sleep durations were associated with higher risks of moderate-to-severe stress among pregnant women, with stronger effects observed among those lacking family or informal social support. Helping pregnant women maintain a healthy sleep duration through household involvement represents a feasible and low-cost approach, especially in rural areas where professional psychological services are limited. Strengthening family and informal social support may therefore offer practical strategies to improve pregnant women mental health.Keywords: Sleep duration, Mental health, Pregnant women, Rural China

Background

Pregnant women mental health is an increasingly serious issue in China. A meta-study based in mainland China found that the combined prevalence of maternal depression in China was 16.3%, including 19.7% for prenatal depression and 14.8% for postnatal depression, which is roughly 1.5–2.0 times the incidence of depression in the general population [1]. Additionally, geographic, group, and urban–rural disparities exist in maternal mental health. Rural areas consistently show higher prevalence rates than urban centers, with studies reporting antenatal mental health problems in 23–32% of rural pregnant women [2, 3] versus approximately 15.5% in urban counterparts [4]. In addition, study has shown that approximately 9% of women experience moderate-severe depression during each stage of pregnancy [5], while another study indicates that 11.9% of pregnant women screen for severe depression [6]. Evidence from rural China indicates that 10.7%, 15.3%, and 7.4% of pregnant women experience moderate to severe levels of depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively [7].

Sleep is fundamental to human well-being and plays a critical role in maintaining mental health. A growing body of evidence indicates that deviations in sleep duration are important risk factors for mental health problems [8, 9]. Especially during pregnancy, women experience dramatic hormonal changes, including fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone levels, as well as physical discomfort caused by uterine enlargement and anticipatory anxiety regarding childbirth and parenting [8, 10]. These physiological and psychological changes contribute to a remarkably high prevalence of sleep disturbances, ranging from 40% to 75%, far exceeding the rates observed among non-pregnant women [11–13]. The recommended sleep duration during pregnancy is 7–9 h per night [14, 15]. However, some studies have suggested that hormonal changes such as elevated progesterone may increase fatigue, warranting an extension of the target range to 8–10 h. In addition, both insufficient and excessive sleep can disrupt the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and alter neurotransmitter metabolism, thereby increasing the risk of depression, anxiety, and other psychological problems [16].

Sleep duration during pregnancy and its association with mental health have been mainly studied in urban populations of high-income countries, especially in North America and Western Europe. For instance, researchers have noted that poor sleep quality (including duration, efficiency, disturbances) predicted increased emotional distress among pregnant women in samples from the western developed countries [17, 18]. A longitudinal study of pregnant and postpartum women at the University Hospital of Ferrara and Udine, Italy, found that those with insufficient sleep at baseline were more likely to experience anxiety and depression, and showed poorer mental functioning during the perinatal period [19]. Meanwhile, a survey of 12 maternity hospitals in Japan found that pregnant women who sleep < 6 h had an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 2.08 (95% CI: 1.60–2.70) for high Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) scores, compared to those with 7–8 h of sleep. For those sleeping 6–7 h, the AOR was 1.41 (95% CI: 1.18–1.68). Each additional hour of sleep was linked to a roughly 14% lower risk of high EPDS scores [20].Although these body of evidences highlight the importance of sleep for maternal mental health, much less is known about these associations in low- and middle-income settings, particularly among pregnant women in rural China.

Research on pregnant women in developing countries, particularly in rural China, remains limited. Compared with their urban counterparts, rural pregnant women face multiple disadvantages in terms of access to medical resources, professional consultation, and social support [21–23], which may exacerbate the negative effects of sleep duration on mental health. Some rural pregnant women often perceive excessively short or long sleep as a “normal pregnancy reaction,” leading them to underestimate potential mental health risks, and consequently, few of them actively seek professional medical assistance. Such cognitive bias may result in delayed interventions and consequently elevate the risk of depression, anxiety, and other mental health problems [24, 25]. Given the limited availability of specialized psychological services in rural settings, enhancing household support to promote appropriate sleep duration could represent a practical and accessible pathway for promoting pregnant women mental health. Therefore, examining sleep duration among rural pregnant women is crucial and carries substantial practical significance for improving mental health.

This study focused on pregnant women in rural western China to examine the association between sleep duration and moderate-severe mental health problems during pregnancy. It first describes the distribution of sleep duration among pregnant women and then investigates its relationship with moderate-to-severe depression, anxiety, and stress. In addition, the study explores the heterogeneity of these associations across different levels of family and informal social support.

Methods

Sampling

A repeated cross-sectional study design was adopted. Data were collected from households located in rural areas of Shaanxi Province, northwestern China. A multilevel cluster random sampling approach was applied in several stages. First, five prefectures in Shaanxi Province were selected. Within each prefecture, two counties were randomly chosen, resulting in a total of ten counties. Second, within these counties, all townships excluding county seats were included in the sampling frame. For counties with more than ten townships, ten were randomly selected, whereas for counties with fewer than ten, all were included. Third, with the assistance of local health bureaus, we obtained a list of all pregnant women residing in each selected township. Because pregnant women were generally required to register with local maternal and child health systems to access antenatal care and hospital delivery services, the registration lists provided a near-complete sampling frame in the study areas. Although a small number of unregistered pregnancies may not have been captured, previous national data indicate that hospital delivery rates in rural China reached approximately 99.8% in recent years, suggesting that the proportion of unregistered pregnant women is likely to be very small [26]. Townships with fewer than three eligible pregnant women were excluded. To further ensure accuracy, the sampling lists were cross-validated with primary healthcare providers responsible for maternal health management in each village. Finally, ten pregnant women were randomly selected from each township.

The sample size was calculated based on the desired precision for estimating the prevalence of depression using the standard formula for single-population proportion estimation: n=Z2p(1 − p)/d2, where n is the required sample size, Z is the standard normal value corresponding to the desired confidence level, p is the expected prevalence, and d is the margin of error. Assuming an expected depression prevalence of 35%, a 95% confidence level, and a margin of error of ± 3.5% points, the minimum required sample size under simple random sampling was estimated to be 713 participants. To account for clustering associated with the multistage sampling design, a design effect of 1.4 was applied, based on an average cluster size of approximately six participants and an assumed intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.07. In addition, a 10% inflation was applied to account for potential non-response and missing data. The final target sample size was therefore estimated at 1,109 participants.

It is worth noting that data were collected in three rounds: March 2021, December 2021, and March 2023, enrolling 592, 229, and 332 participants, respectively. Data were collected across three independent survey waves to improve sample representativeness, capture temporal variations in prenatal status, and enhance the robustness of the findings. To ensure the representativeness of the population and to address ethical and practical considerations, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18–49 years, corresponding to the typical reproductive age range and optimal physical condition; (2) residency in the local area for at least one year, to minimize potential attrition due to migration and mobility; and (3) currently pregnant at the time of the survey.

Across the three survey rounds, 118 participants were excluded due to missing or incomplete responses. Specifically, 41 participants were excluded from wave 1 (March 2021), 24 from wave 2 (December 2021), and 53 from wave 3 (March 2023). These exclusions primarily involved missing data on the DASS-21 psychological scale (n = 62) and key sociodemographic variables, including family income and employment status (n = 56). A total of 1,153 participants were surveyed across the three waves, exceeding the target sample size. After exclusions, the final sample comprised 1,035 pregnant women from 10 counties in Shaanxi Province.

Setting

This study was conducted in ten randomly selected counties in Shaanxi Province, located in northwest China, a region with relatively low levels of economic development. First, according to the Shaanxi Provincial Bureau of Statistics, the province had a total population of approximately 39.52 million in 2021, of whom 14.75 million lived in rural areas, accounting for about 37.0% of the total population. Among the total population, females accounted for 49.0% (19.36 million), and women of reproductive age (15–49 years) constituted approximately 24.3% of the provincial population, reflecting a considerable population of maternal health service users in rural areas [27]. Second, data from the National Bureau of Statistics of China indicate that in 2020, the annual per capita disposable income of rural residents nationwide was 17,131 yuan (US$2,483), whereas that of rural residents in Shaanxi Province was only 13,317 yuan (US$1,930), ranking 27th among the 31 provinces in China [28]. Pregnant women in these areas are routinely registered and followed through the local maternal and child health management system, primarily supported by township health centers and village clinics. However, access to specialized mental health and social support services remains limited in many rural areas. Third, Shaanxi Province is characterized by remarkable geographical diversity, encompassing mountains, plateaus, and plains. Such topographic variety makes it an exemplary region for studying the social issues of rural northwestern China. Therefore, the selected sample counties can be considered broadly representative of rural areas in China.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with pregnant women, and responses were recorded and verified item-by-item using tablet-based questionnaires. All interviewers participated in a three-day centralized training program prior to the surveys, which included detailed instructions on questionnaire administration and extensive mock interview practice.

Before the interview began, participants were informed that all personal information would be kept strictly confidential, and informed consent was obtained. Interviews were conducted in a private and quiet location within the participant’s home whenever possible, with efforts made to minimize interruptions and ensure that only the participant was present during the interview, thereby ensuring both visual and auditory privacy during data collection. Participants were also informed that their responses would be used solely for research purposes.

With participants’ permission, the entire interview process was audio-recorded for quality control purposes. After data collection, each interviewer was assigned a remote quality-control supervisor, who reviewed screen recordings and questionnaire responses to verify data accuracy and consistency. Feedback and correction suggestions were provided when necessary, including whether interview questions had been administered appropriately and consistently. In addition, all collected data were anonymized prior to analysis by removing personal identifiers, and access to the data was restricted to authorized research staff only.

Measures

Mental health

We assessed the mental health of pregnant women, including symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21), a validated instrument developed by Lovibond and Lovibond [29]. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the high reliability and validity of the DASS-21 across diverse cultural contexts [30, 31]. The Chinese version of the DASS-21 has also been validated, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients exceeding 0.80 for all subscales [32]. We have calculated Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the DASS-21 based on our data. The results indicate good internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92, and subscale coefficients of 0.82 for depression, 0.72 for anxiety, and 0.83 for stress.

The DASS-21 consists of 21 items divided into three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress, each containing seven items. Participants rated the extent to which each statement applied to them over the past week on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3 where 0 refers to “did not apply to me at all”, 1 refers to “applied to me to some degree”, 2 refers to “applied to me to a considerable degree”, and 3 refers to “applied to me very much”. Scores for each subscale range from 0 to 21 and are multiplied by 2 to obtain the final scores, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive, anxious, or stress. Following the DASS-21 scoring guidelines, each subscale score was further converted into a binary variable indicating the presence of a moderate-severe tendency toward depression (score > 13), anxiety (score > 9), or stress (score > 18), coded as 1 if present and 0 otherwise.

Sleep duration

To assess self-reported sleep duration, participants were asked: “Over the past week, on average, how many hours per day did you sleep?” Sleep duration was assessed using a single self-reported question regarding the average number of hours slept per day. It has been widely used in epidemiological studies and demonstrates good agreement with objective physiological assessments [33].

For sleep duration categorization, we referred to pregnancy-specific recommendations and prior epidemiological evidence. The National Sleep Foundation recommends 7–9 h of sleep per night for adults [34]. We adopted a modified categorization to capture clinically meaningful patterns in pregnancy: short sleep (≤ 8 h), medium sleep (8–10 h; reference group), and long sleep (≥ 10 h). This classification aligns with previous pregnancy studies that used ≥ 10 h to define long sleep [35] and ≤ 8 h to capture sleep below the recommended threshold [36], while allowing for natural variation during pregnancy.

Heterogeneity variables

To explore heterogeneity in the association between sleep duration and mental health, this study included family support and informal social support variables. Family support refers to help within the household, including husbands’ accompaniment to prenatal check-ups and relationships with mothers-in-law. Family support was assessed using two indicators: (1) husband’s accompaniment to prenatal check-ups, based on the survey question “Who has mainly accompanied you to prenatal check-ups during this pregnancy?” Responses were coded as 1 for “husband” and 0 for “others.” (2) Satisfaction with the relationship with the mother-in-law, measured by the question “Are you satisfied with your relationship with your mother-in-law?” Responses of “very satisfied” or “relatively satisfied” were coded as 1, while “neutral,” “relatively dissatisfied,” or “very dissatisfied” were coded as 0.

Social support refers to informal resources available in social environments, such as attitude towards psychological counseling or maternal nutritional and health knowledge, which remain available where professional mental health services are limited. Informal social support was assessed using two indicators: attitude toward psychological counseling and maternal nutrition and health knowledge score. The attitude toward psychological counseling was measured by the question “What do you think if someone seeks professional psychological counseling?” Responses considering it “normal” were coded as 1, and those considering it “abnormal” were coded as 0. The maternal nutrition and health knowledge score was derived from a questionnaire based on the Basic Knowledge and Skills of Maternal and Child Health Literacy. This scale assessed pregnant women’s knowledge of pregnancy health, nutritional intake, breastfeeding, and so on. It consisted of 25 items, with each correct answer scored as 1 and each incorrect or missing answer scored as 0. This distinction allows this study to explore practical intervention pathways that do not rely on specialized mental health services.

Covariates

We collected demographic information on mother, father, and family characteristics. Mother and father characteristics include their age, education level, employment status, and health status. Family characteristics covered annual family income, co-residence with the husband, and the number of children. All variables are dummy variables.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using STATA 18.0 software. Pregnant women were categorized according to their sleep duration, and characteristics variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests were conducted to compare these variables across different sleep duration groups. In addition, chi-square tests were used to examine differences in the prevalence of moderate-severe depressive, anxious, and stress tendencies across sleep duration categories. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to examine the associations between sleep duration and mental health, including moderate-severe depression, anxiety, and stress tendencies.

Variables associated with the outcomes in the univariate analyses at p < 0.20, together with variables considered theoretically relevant based on previous literature, were included in the multivariable logistic regression models. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), the mean VIF for the main variables was 1.24, and all individual VIF values were below 2, indicating no evidence of significant multicollinearity. In order to improve comparability across mental health outcomes, a consistent set of socio-demographic and family-related covariates was retained in all multivariable models [37, 38]. All analyses additionally controlled for county and survey wave fixed effects. Furthermore, subgroup logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the heterogeneity of these associations by whether the husband accompanied prenatal check-ups, the relationship with the mother-in-law, attitude towards psychological counseling and scores on nutritional and health knowledge. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Ethics, consent and permissions

This research followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Normal University (Xi’an, China) and Xi’an Jiaotong University (Xi’an, China, No.2020 − 1240). The study was conducted in accordance with established research ethics guidelines. Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Similarly, nutritional and health knowledge influenced the relationship between sleep duration and mental health. Discussion

Utilizing survey data from rural pregnant women in northwestern China, we examined the prevalence of sleep duration challenges encompassing short, medium, and long. Our findings highlighted sleep duration as a potential contributing factor to moderate-severe mental health. Our findings show that perceived family support and informal social support can buffer the adverse effects of short or long sleep duration on pregnant women’s moderate-severe mental health. In rural settings where professional psychological services are scarce, such informal support offers a practical and scalable avenue for intervention.

Our data showed that approximately 20% of pregnant women experienced either short or long sleep duration, which is consistent with findings from previous studies conducted in China. A cross-sectional study conducted in 2015 across 16 hospitals in five Chinese provinces similarly reported that 23.9% of pregnant women had short sleep and 20.9% had long sleep [15], indicating that abnormal sleep duration is a common issue among pregnant women in China. In addition, our data revealed that 34.40% of participants experienced moderate-severe depressive, 38.26% experienced moderate-severe anxiety, and 31.88% reported moderate-severe stress. These prevalence rates are comparable to those observed in other cultural contexts. For instance, a study conducted in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia, found that 27.2% of pregnant women experienced moderate stress and 4% experienced severe stress, while 32.9% reported moderate anxiety and 2.6% reported severe anxiety [39].

The result revealed that sleep duration was associated with an increased risk of moderate-severe stress. These findings coincide with prospective studies showing that sleep duration could influence mental health [17, 19, 40]. Several potential mechanisms could account for this observed association. First, insufficient sleep can activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and increase cortisol secretion, leading to heightened physiological arousal and stress reactivity [41, 42]. Chronic activation of the stress response may impair emotional regulation, reduce cognitive control, and increase vulnerability to stress symptoms. Moreover, short sleep is often accompanied by fragmented sleep patterns and reduced slow-wave sleep, which compromise neural recovery and emotional stability. Second, prolonged sleep duration may also indicate underlying psychological or physiological dysregulation. Long sleep can be a compensatory behavior for fatigue, subclinical depression, or inflammatory responses, all of which are associated with poor mental health outcomes [40, 43]. Long sleep has also been linked to low physical activity, metabolic imbalance, and social withdrawal, which can further exacerbate feelings of stress or depressive mood. Third, hormonal fluctuations during pregnancy, particularly changes in estrogen and progesterone levels, may increase the sensitivity of both short and long sleepers to psychological stress [44]. Overall, abnormal sleep duration reflects an imbalance in the body’s stress and sleep regulation system, thereby increasing the risk of mental health.

Regarding heterogeneity analysis, we found that when pregnant women feel stronger family support, it can weaken the negative effects brought by short or long sleep duration. Two forms of family support were particularly important. First, pregnant women whose husbands did not accompany them to prenatal check-ups showed significantly higher risks of anxiety and stress when their sleep duration was short or long. Pregnant women often face multiple sources of stress, including adaptation to parental roles, concerns about maternal and infant health and safety, changes in body shape and physical activity, and other stressors [45]. These factors may exacerbate the psychological burden caused by sleep problems. In contrast, when husbands are present during prenatal check-ups, they can provide emotional reassurance and practical assistance, thereby alleviating sleep-related mental stress. Second, poor relationship with mother-in-law was associated with greater stress risks among women with short sleep duration. In the unique cultural context of China, intergenerational conflicts between mothers-in-law and daughters-in-law are common and often stem from differences in beliefs, values, and household practices [46]. During pregnancy, when emotional sensitivity heightens, a harmonious mother-in-law and daughter-in-law relationship can foster a stable, low-conflict household environment that buffers the adverse mental health consequences of abnormal sleep duration. These results highlight the value of family-based, non-professional support, which is especially important in rural areas where access to formal psychological services is limited.

We also found that greater perceived informal social support reduces the negative impact of short and long sleep duration on pregnant women’s mental health. Specifically, pregnant women who held negative attitudes toward psychological counseling, both short and long sleep durations were strongly associated with increased risks of depression, anxiety, and stress. Prior studies indicate that mental health stigma is common among Chinese pregnant women, and psychological help is often perceived as a sign of “weakness” or “abnormality,” discouraging early help-seeking [47]. This may leave women who are already vulnerable due to sleep problems without adequate coping resources. Promoting positive perceptions of counseling through public health education and expanding access to basic psychological services could help mitigate sleep-related mental distress. Meanwhile, among women with lower knowledge scores, both short and long sleep durations were linked to higher risks of anxiety and stress. Adequate maternal health knowledge has been linked to better nutrition, improved quality of life, healthier gestational weight management, and favorable birth outcomes [48, 49]. Conversely, women with limited maternal knowledge of nutrition and health may lack awareness of the causes of sleep disturbances and effective self-care strategies, making them more vulnerable to the mental health problems of abnormal sleep duration.

Overall, this study has several strengths. First, it focused on pregnant women in rural China, a relatively understudied population in maternal mental health research. Second, the study simultaneously examined depression, anxiety, and stress, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between sleep duration and mental health during pregnancy. Third, the relatively large sample size enhanced the robustness of the study findings.

Our findings offer valuable insights for policymakers. First, strengthening home-based support can help pregnant women maintain a healthy sleep duration, which is essential for preventing mental health problems. Because sleep duration is shaped within the household, family members can directly assist in improving sleep without relying on professional psychological institutions. Such home-based efforts are highly operational, low cost, and readily scalable in rural settings. Second, policies should strengthen both family involvement and informal social support to mitigate the mental health risks associated with sleep duration. Encouraging husbands’ participation in prenatal care and promoting harmonious family relationships can provide emotional reassurance and practical assistance that help stabilize sleep duration. At the same time, public health programs should improve informal social support by fostering positive attitudes toward help-seeking and enhancing maternal health and nutrition knowledge. These approaches are practical in rural areas, making them valuable components of pregnant women mental health strategies.

Limitations

However, the limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, sleep duration was obtained through pregnant women’s self-reports instead of a standardized scale, it may introduce measurement bias and hinder the comparability of results across different studies. Second, the sample of this study was exclusively drawn from rural areas in northwestern China, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other demographic groups. Third, this study employed repeated cross-sectional design, which makes it impossible to evaluate the causal relationship between sleep duration and the mental health of pregnant women.

Conclusions

In summary, this study shows that both short and long sleep durations are associated with higher risks of stress among pregnant women in rural northwestern China. Strengthening family and informal social support may help women maintain healthy sleep duration and reduce the risk of mental health problems in resource-limited rural settings. These findings highlight the importance of husband participation, harmonious family relationships, positive attitudes toward psychological counseling, and adequate maternal nutrition and health knowledge in promoting maternal mental health. Maintaining medium sleep duration and enhancing family and informal social support may provide practical and scalable strategies to improve pregnant women mental health in resource-limited rural settings.
