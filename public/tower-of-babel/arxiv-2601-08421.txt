Coverage Improvement and Fast Convergence of On-policy Preference Learning
Juno Kim, Jihun Yun, Jason D. Lee, Kwang-Sung Jun

Abstract
Online on-policy preference learning algorithms for language model alignment such as online direct policy optimization (DPO) can significantly outperform their offline counterparts. We provide a theoretical explanation for this phenomenon by analyzing how the sampling policy’s coverage evolves throughout on-policy training. We propose and rigorously justify the coverage improvement principle : with sufficient batch size, each update moves into a region around the target where coverage is uniformly better, making subsequent data increasingly informative and enabling rapid convergence. In the contextual bandit setting with Bradley-Terry preferences and linear softmax policy class, we show that on-policy DPO converges exponentially in the number of iterations for batch size exceeding a generalized coverage threshold. In contrast, any learner restricted to offline samples from the initial policy suffers a slower minimax rate, leading to a sharp separation in total sample complexity. Motivated by this analysis, we further propose a simple hybrid sampler based on a novel preferential G-optimal design, which removes dependence on coverage and guarantees convergence in just two rounds. Finally, we develop principled on-policy schemes for reward distillation in the general function class setting, and show faster noiseless rates under an alternative deviation-based notion of coverage. Experimentally, we confirm that on-policy DPO and our proposed reward distillation algorithms outperform their off-policy counterparts and enjoy stable, monotonic performance gains across iterations.

Formalization of Alignment

2.1
Problem Setup

Notation.

We adopt a contextual bandit formalism for language modeling
(
57
;
85
;
17
;
32
)
. Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}
be finite prompt and response spaces. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a fixed probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}
and
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
denote the space of distributions over
\mathcal{A}
. For any divergence measure
D
, we adopt the shorthand
D(\pi\|\pi^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[D(\pi(x)\|\pi^{\prime}(x))]
. We model the
policy
induced by a generative model as a function
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
from prompts to a distribution over responses; we will also consider
joint
policies
\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A})
. We are given a
base
or reference policy
\pi_{0}
, typically an LLM obtained by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to be aligned. We denote by
B_{p}(\theta_{0},r)=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert\theta-\theta_{0}\rVert_{p}\leq r\}
the closed
d
-dimensional
L^{p}
-ball of radius
r
centered at
\theta_{0}
. The sigmoid function is
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
.

Learning from preferences.

We study the problem of alignment from pairwise preference data. As is standard, we model preferences using the Bradley-Terry assumption
(
13
;
21
;
57
)
.

Assumption 1

(Bradley-Terry model)
.

There exists a reward function
r^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
such that preferences between
a^{1},a^{2}\in\mathcal{A}
are determined according to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)=\sigma(r^{*}(x,a^{1})-r^{*}(x,a^{2})).

(1)

In the basic formulation of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
21
;
94
;
7
;
52
)
, we learn the reward function
r^{*}
from human-labeled preference data using the negative log-likelihood loss and optimize the KL-regularized reward:
\argmax_{\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})}J_{\gamma}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\pi(x)}[r^{*}(x,a)]-\gamma\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{0})
, where
\gamma>0
is a regularization parameter. We define its solution as the
target policy

\pi^{*}
:

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(a\mid x)\propto\pi_{0}(a\mid x)\exp(\gamma^{-1}r^{*}(x,a)).

We also define the
induced binary preference distribution
of a policy
\pi
relative to
\pi^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x):=\sigma\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\pi(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi^{\prime}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi^{\prime}(a^{2}\mid x)}\right).

(2)

Since
\mathbb{P}_{\pi^{*},\pi_{0}}=\mathbb{P}_{*}
under Assumption
1
, learning the target policy
\pi^{*}
is equivalent to learning the ground-truth preferences
\mathbb{P}_{*}

(
57
)
. Taking this a step further,
90
reframe the alignment problem as explicitly learning
\pi^{*}
, and we build upon this viewpoint, deriving our results in terms of convergence of the learned policy
\hat{\pi}
to
\pi^{*}
. This
distribution learning
perspective offers a stricter but arguably more natural notion of alignment; simple reward maximization may hide undesirable behavior such as model collapse (Section
5.1
). Nonetheless, our results can be straightforwardly converted into regret bounds for
J_{\gamma}
as in
85
;
82
.

Direct preference optimization (DPO).

DPO is an classification-based alternative to RLHF which finetunes a model directly from pairwise preferences without the need for a separate reward model
(
57
)
. Given a dataset of prompts and preferred/dispreferred response pairs
D=\{(x,a^{+},a^{-})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the DPO objective is defined for policy
\pi
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi;D):=\sum_{(x,a^{+},a^{-})\in D}-\ln\sigma\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\pi(a^{+}\mid x)}{\pi(a^{-}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi_{0}(a^{+}\mid x)}{\pi_{0}(a^{-}\mid x)}\right).

(3)

Hence, the DPO objective can also be seen as the maximum likelihood objective w.r.t. the induced binary preference distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\pi_{0}}(\succ\mid x)
defined in Eq. (
2
).

Policy class.

To develop our theory in a concrete manner, we first focus on the linear softmax policy class

;

Let

(Realizability)

\pi^{*}=\pi_{\theta^{*}}
for some

While our analysis can be applied to any geometry with the corresponding coverage, we will see that the lower bound for offline learning is most naturally presented w.r.t.

Generalized coverage.

We require a notion of feature-level coverage for our analysis, which is an instance of often-studied

(

We define the (single) coverage of

by

with radius

\displaystyle C_{\pi\shortrightarrow\pi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{C>0\mid\mathbb{V}(\pi^{\prime})\preceq C\cdot\mathbb{V}(\pi)\},\quad C_{*}^{p}(r):=\sup\{C_{\pi_{\theta}\shortrightarrow\pi^{*}}\mid\theta\in B_{p}(\theta^{*},r)\}.

(4)

A naive bound shows that
C_{\pi\to\pi^{\prime}}\leq\lVert\pi^{\prime}/\pi\rVert_{\infty}
and
C_{*}^{p}(r)\leq e^{2r}
(Lemma
B.8
), but

may be much smaller than the maximum density ratio and possibly polynomial in

(

;
39
;
1
;
38
)
. Our results hold regardless of the magnitude of

as we show a separation between on-policy and offline DPO in the total sample complexity
overhead

n/C_{*}^{p}(R)
.

Assumption 3
.

\mathbb{V}(\pi^{*})\succeq\lambda{\mathbf{I}}_{d}
for some
\lambda>0
.

For this condition to hold, we must have
\lambda\lesssim d^{-1}
. This may be weakened to
\mathbb{V}(\pi^{*})|_{S}\succeq\lambda{\mathbf{I}}_{S}
on the subspace

by always requiring the identifiability condition

; all subsequent analyses will still hold when restricting to

In order to study the dynamics of coverage in Section
3
, we assume a mild growth condition for

Assumption 4
.

The map
r\mapsto\sqrt{C_{*}^{p}(r)}
is convex on
[0,R]
. More generally, for some
\kappa\geq 1
, the set
\{r\in[0,R]:C_{*}^{p}(r)/r^{2}\leq\kappa\cdot C_{*}^{p}(R)/R^{2}\}

This assumption is weak in the sense that if it does not hold, we may simply replace

by any upper bound
\bar{C}_{*}^{p}\geq C_{*}^{p}
with
\bar{C}_{*}^{p}(0)=1

always suffices.

2.2
Minimax Lower Bound for Offline Preference Learning

In this section, we show a information-theoretic lower bound for general offline preference learning methods, including vanilla DPO. Under the Bradley-Terry model,
32
as well as
82
give a worst-case proof that to learn a reward-maximizing policy, any (online or offline) learner starting from
\pi_{0}
requires an exponentially large (w.r.t. inverse temperature) number
n_{0}
of samples, roughly equal to the target coverage
C_{\pi_{0}\to\pi^{*}}
. Their argument considers a skewed two-armed bandit policy
\pi_{0}
from which it is unlikely to sample the optimal action even once. However, these results do not quantify the
convergence
of error once
n>n_{0}
, and hence cannot capture the difference between online and offline DPO in the practical regime when DPO is actually effective. Here, we obtain a worst-case convergence rate in
n
which applies to any purely offline method, and clarify the dependency on coverage and dimension.

Consider the bandit setting where
\mathcal{X}=\{\perp\}
,
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\cdots,d\}
and features
\phi(i)=e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will construct a target hypothesis class
\Theta^{*}\subset B_{p}(0,R)
consisting of near-uniform
\pi^{*}
candidates, and an adversarial base policy
\pi_{0}
from which worst-case coverage sample complexity is required to obtain information on
\pi^{*}
. For such
\pi^{*}
, we verify Assumptions
3
,
4
(with
\lambda\asymp 1/d
) and tightly bound the local
L^{p}
-coverage via the following result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\pi_{\theta}=\softmax(\theta)
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then if
\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert_{\infty}=O(1)
and
d=O(\operatorname{poly}(R))
, it holds that
\mathbb{V}(\pi^{*})|_{S}\gtrsim\frac{1}{d}{\mathbf{I}}_{S}
and
C_{*}^{p}(R)\asymp e^{R}
when
p=1
, or
C_{*}^{p}(R)\asymp\frac{1}{d}e^{2^{1-1/p}R}
when
p>1
.

We now show the following bound, which applies to all estimators which rely on pairwise preferences sampled from the base policy
\pi_{0}
. If the sampling policy is different, for example if the responses in the dataset were collected ahead of time from a different LLM, the coverage term should be replaced by the corresponding coverage. The proof is an application of the Yang-Barron method
(
89
)
to the near-uniform hypothesis class
\Theta^{*}
equipped with the
L^{p}
-norm.

Theorem 2.2

(Lower bound for offline preference learning)
.

Let the dataset
D
consist of
n
i.i.d. pairs of actions
a^{1},a^{2}\sim\pi_{0}
labeled by
\mathbb{P}_{*}
. Then for
p>1
, over all estimators
\hat{\pi}(D):=\pi_{\hat{\theta}(D)}
of
\pi^{*}
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{*}\in\Theta^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\pi^{*}}\left[\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{1}\right]\gtrsim d\sqrt{\frac{C_{*}^{1}(R)}{n}},

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\sup_{\theta^{*}\in\Theta^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\pi^{*}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{*}\|\hat{\pi}(D))\right]\gtrsim\frac{C_{*}^{1}(R)}{n}\wedge\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

For
p>1
, we instead have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{*}\in\Theta^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\pi^{*}}\left[\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\right]\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{dC_{*}^{p}(R)}{n}}.

When
p=1
, we recover the ordinary parametric
L^{1}
minimax risk but multiplied with the corresponding coverage
C_{*}^{1}(R)
, reflecting the difficulty of receiving a positive signal when sampling offline from
\pi_{0}
. Here the KL bound is the ‘correct’ rate since an extra
d
factor is incurred when converting from
L^{2}
to
L^{1}
-norm, and another from KL curvature (Lemma
E.1
). This result is tight:

\frac{d}{n}\times
(coverage) for their PMLE method, which is equivalent to offline DPO without the base policy

In contrast, when

is roughly uniform, the largest density ratio with

such that

Remark 3.4
.

The decay factor

Remark 3.5
.

The recurring factor of

is a crude bound for the curvature of the Bradley-Terry model, often appearing in analyses of logistic models
(

;
60
;

. We choose to hide this dependency in the sequel by assuming

In terms of dimension, the upper and lower bounds have a

A substantial body of work has focused on developing online preference learning algorithms based on principles such as optimism or active learning to remove the dependence on coverage

Fast Convergence of On-policy Reward Distillation

While direct preference learning methods are attractive due to their simplicity and stability, they still suffer from out-of-distribution or degeneracy issues
(
87
)
. A recent line of work has instead proposed a class of methods termed
reward model distillation

(
33
;
30
;
90
)
, which trains the implicit reward model of the policy to fit reward differences in paired responses. These methods retain much of the simplicity of DPO as they only need an already-trained reward model
\tilde{r}
and do not require policy gradient estimation, and can match or outperform DPO baselines efficiently
(
33
;
30
)
.

In this section, we extend our coverage improvement analysis to understand the benefits of on-policy reward distillation. Our key insight is that distillation is fundamentally noiseless since the learner accesses relative rewards directly instead of sampling stochastic preferences from Eq. (
1
), and thus should achieve fast rates in the sense of
72
, i.e.,
O(n^{-1})
instead of
O(n^{-1/2})
. We provide a brief overview of existing methods in Section
5.1
and propose a general formulation of designing iterative reward distillation methods, given in Algorithm
3
. We then describe a more general function class approach for the theoretical analysis and provide the improved convergence result in Section
5.2
.

5.1
Distilling Rewards: REBEL, VCB, PD

REBEL and VCB.

The REBEL algorithm
(
33
)
aims to iteratively solve the KL-constrained reward maximization problem
\argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\pi(x)}[\tilde{r}(x,a)]-\gamma\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\hat{\pi}_{k})
, where
\tilde{r}
is a queryable reward model and
\hat{\pi}_{k}
is the previous round’s learned policy. Given access to paired samples
(x,a^{1},a^{2})
, REBEL regresses the log-likelihood ratio of
a^{1},a^{2}
(regularized with the previous round) on the difference in rewards by minimizing the squared loss

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\bigg[\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\pi(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\hat{\pi}_{k}(a^{2}\mid x)}-(\tilde{r}(x,a^{1})-\tilde{r}(x,a^{2}))\right)^{2}\bigg].

(9)

47
have studied VCB, the offline version of Eq. (
9
), where
\hat{\pi}_{k}
is replaced with the SFT policy; a pessimistic variant has also been developed by
30
. REBEL can be interpreted as an adaptive policy gradient method approximating mirror descent, and the expected reward
J(\hat{\pi}_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}[r^{*}(x,a)]
is shown to converge at a
O(K^{-1/2})
rate to its maximum
(
33
)
.

However, we claim that this iteration is flawed from a distribution learning perspective. Assuming each step is solved perfectly, the model learns the RLHF solution
\hat{\pi}_{k+1}\propto\hat{\pi}_{t}\exp(\gamma^{-1}\tilde{r})
; running this update
K
times will compound the tilt to yield
\hat{\pi}_{K}\propto\pi_{0}\exp(K\gamma^{-1}\tilde{r})
, collapsing onto a one-hot distribution supported only on the maximum reward response as
K\to\infty
. Each preference distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)
will also collapse on whichever of
a^{1},a^{2}
has higher reward. Even though this indeed maximizes expected reward, the model fails to learn the target
distributions

\pi^{*}
and
\mathbb{P}_{*}
, resulting in undesirable behavior.
3
3

3

This is different from the degeneracy issue described in
30
for offline DPO, which applies only to the tabular and unconstrained policy class setting.
We also should not regularize on-policy DPO with the previous policy instead of the base policy, as done in
33
, for the same reason.

Preference distillation (PD).

90

preference distillation
(PD), motivated by the observation that
\tilde{r}
can be used to simulate the preference distribution as
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)=\sigma(\tilde{r}(x,a^{1})-\tilde{r}(x,a^{2}))

\Bern(\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
and running

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
as soft preference labels:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\pi_{0}(x)}\left[-\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\ln\sigma\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\pi(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi(a^{2}\mid x)}\right)-\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)\ln\sigma\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\pi(a^{2}\mid x)}{\pi(a^{1}\mid x)}\right)\right].

Designing on-policy reward distillation methods.

Combining the above works, we can give a general recipe for designing (pairwise relative) reward distillation algorithms. Let
D

for simplicity of presentation.
We consider the associated general policy class

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}:=\left\{\pi_{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})\mid\pi_{f}(a\mid x)\propto\pi_{0}(a\mid x)\exp f(x,a)\text{ for some }f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}.

We assume both the target policy and reward model are realizable. The analysis can be adapted in a straightforward manner to non-realizable
\tilde{r}
but must incur a slower
1/\sqrt{n}
rate.

Assumption 5
.

It holds that
\gamma^{-1}\tilde{r}\in\mathcal{F}
and
\pi^{*}=\pi_{f^{*}}
for some
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
.

We also require a different notion of coverage from before. Let

\displaystyle\MAD_{\pi}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\pi(x)}\left[\left\lvert f(x,a)-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(x)}\left[f(x,a)\right]\right\rvert\right]

denote the mean absolute deviation, and the single and local MAD-based coverages as

\displaystyle C_{\pi\to\pi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{C>0\mid\MAD_{\pi^{\prime}}(f)\leq C\cdot\MAD_{\pi}(f),\;\forall f\in\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}\},

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{F}}(r)

\displaystyle:=\sup\{C_{\pi_{f}\to\pi^{*}}\mid\MAD_{\pi^{*}}(f-f^{*})\leq r\}.

Intuitively,
C_{\mathcal{F}}
measures an
L^{1}
-based coverage over a local
L^{1}
-ball, compared to
C_{*}^{p}
in Section
2
which measures an
L^{2}
-based coverage over a local
L^{p}
-ball.
C_{\mathcal{F}}
is also uniformly upper bounded by
e^{2R}
over the class
\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}
similarly to Lemma
B.8
. However, in the general function class setting, there is no guaranteed
convex
upper bound for
C_{\mathcal{F}}
(respecting
C_{\mathcal{F}}(0)=1
) since we may have large density ratio even with small absolute deviation, unlike Section
2
where we could just invoke
C_{*}^{p}(r)\leq e^{2r}
. Hence we postulate:

Assumption 6
.

C_{\mathcal{F}}
admits a convex upper bound
\bar{C}_{\mathcal{F}}
on
[0,R]
such that
\bar{C}_{\mathcal{F}}(0)=\Theta(1)
.

As with Assumption
4
, Assumption
6
still holds in the linear softmax setting (Lemma
D.3
). More broadly, a sufficient condition is for functions in
\mathcal{F}
to satisfy a bound of the form
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim\lVert f\rVert_{1}^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
, so that
\MAD_{\pi^{*}}(f-f^{*})\leq r
implies an
O(r^{\alpha})
density ratio bound, and
C_{\mathcal{F}}(r)=\exp(O(r^{\alpha}))
indeed admits a convex upper bound by truncating at the unique inflection point. Such inequalities hold in broad generality given a metric-measure structure on the domain for function classes of generalized smoothness such as Lipschitz functions and fall under the umbrella of Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequalities
(
62
;
8
;
58
)
.

With these ingredients in place, we now state the analogous result to Theorem
3.2
for on-policy reward distillation with improved noiseless rates.

Theorem 5.1

(Fast convergence of on-policy reward distillation)
.

Fix any
\eta,\delta\in(0,1)
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\tilde{r}\in\gamma\mathcal{F}
be a reward model with error
\varepsilon_{\RM}:=\MAD_{\pi^{*}}(\gamma^{-1}\tilde{r}-f^{*})
. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
n
such that

\displaystyle\xi_{n}:=\frac{R\Pdim(\Delta\mathcal{F})}{\eta\cdot n}\ln\frac{Kn}{\delta}\lesssim\frac{R}{C_{\mathcal{F}}(R)}\wedge\Theta(1),

the output of running
K
iterations of on-policy reward distillation (Algorithm
3
) satisfies

\displaystyle\MAD_{\pi^{*}}(\hat{f}_{k}-f^{*})\lesssim\xi_{n}\vee R\eta^{K}\vee\varepsilon_{\RM}.

The left-hand side is a linear measure of error, proportional to
\lVert\theta-\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}
in the linear softmax setting (rather than quadratic as for KL; see Lemma
D.3
). Hence reward distillation indeed achieves a faster
n^{-1}\vee e^{-\Omega(K)}
rate than the
n^{-1/2}\vee e^{-\Omega(K)}
rate of on-policy DPO, but with an irreducible error
\varepsilon_{\RM}
arising from the quality of the reward model. Of course, this issue is not entirely absent for on-policy DPO as the feedback oracle may also not be well-aligned with
\mathbb{P}_{*}
or even compatible with the Bradley-Terry assumption, e.g., when using LLMs to annotate preferences
(
34
)
.

Conclusion

Our work provides a theoretical explanation for the strong performance of on-policy preference learning based on the coverage improvement principle: on-policy updates can rapidly improve their own data quality through better coverage. We show linear convergence of on-policy DPO in the number of iterations and establish a sharp separation in sample complexity compared to offline DPO. We also describe a simple hybrid sampler, based on a novel preferential G-optimal design, which eliminates dependence on coverage and enables two-step convergence. Finally, we develop principled on-policy schemes for reward distillation methods, and clarify how on-policy distillation can outperform learning directly from preferences by viewing each round as noiseless relative regression.

Appendix A
Related Work

A.1
On-policy preference learning methods

We first provide a brief and non-exhaustive overview of on-policy DPO-style preference learning methods for language model alignment. One of the simplest and most widely used versions of on-policy DPO is introduced by
34
;
86
, where each batch is sampled purely on-policy from the previous policy and used to iteratively update the current policy, with online preference feedback obtained from either an LLM annotator
(
34
)
or a classifier model
(
86
)
.
45
add length regularization to address verbosity and find that on-policy DPO can improve a 7B model to perform on par with GPT-4.
15
propose an online preference learning algorithm which combines identity preference optimization
(
6
)
and Nash mirror descent
(
49
)
.

An alternative approach to on-policy DPO is proposed by
56
, which simulates intraspecific competition between fast and slow chasing DPO-updated models to drive learning. Another line of work by
67
;
59
reformulates preference optimization as two-player zero-sum games with the preference function as the payoff, and develops iterative single-play algorithms to solve for the Nash equilibrium; these works guarantee average-iterate convergence to the minimax winner policy.
55
extend iterative DPO from instruction fine-tuning to chain-of-thought reasoning tasks
(
78
)
.

A.2
Theory of on-policy preference learning

We now describe existing theoretical studies of on-policy preference learning most relevant to our work.

82
introduce XPO, an optimistic variant of DPO with online exploration, and show a regret bound in the MDP setting with sample complexity dependent on the trajectory-level coverability coefficient (best-case coverage) rather than the potentially poor coverage of the fixed reference policy. This is similar to online RL, where the existence of a distribution with good concentrability is sufficient for sample-efficient exploration
(
83
)
. The XPO algorithm is further studied by
37
from the viewpoint of self-improvement via distribution sharpening.

63
study convergence of gradient descent for DPO and propose an online sampler which achieves quadratic convergence. However, their analysis crucially relies on a tabular softmax parametrization, which is unrealistic for large prompt/response spaces, as well as access to exact population gradients. In contrast, our approach studies the
statistical
convergence or sample complexity of on-policy DPO in the finite-sample regime, and under a log-linear feature model (Section
3
) or general function class (Section
5
) setting.

85
obtain statistical guarantees for offline RLHF/DPO with pessimism as well as online exploration methods in the contextual bandit setting with linear softmax policies. Their iterative GSHF algorithm utilizes active exploration to guarantee
\varepsilon
suboptimality with
O(d/\varepsilon^{2})
batch size independent of data coverage (Theorem 3). However, their algorithm requires
\widetilde{\Omega}(d)
iterations and needs to re-compute the exploration policy
\hat{\pi}_{k}^{2}
based on the collected prompts and current (exploitation) policy
\hat{\pi}_{k}^{1}
. In contrast, our preferential G-optimal design (Section
4
) achieves convergence with a comparable batch size but only two iterations, and only requires computing a fixed design
\pi^{g}
beforehand, resulting in a much simpler algorithm.

Also in the linear contextual bandit setting,
32
study the computational-statistical tradeoff for online alignment methods by distinguishing data efficiency, or reward model queries, and computational efficiency, or sampling oracle queries; the complexity of the latter is shown to be lower bounded by coverage. They propose an inference-time exploration algorithm based on active learning principles which achieves near-optimal (i.e., up to polynomial dependence) query complexity for both oracles. Under the same setting,
17
propose value-incentivized preference optimization, the online version of which is an optimistic variant of per-sample online DPO, and obtain regret bounds. The algorithm encourages exploration by effectively adding a negative KL regularizer to the DPO loss. Earlier works by
92
;
79
also utilize experimental design or active learning for preference-based RL.

Focusing specifically on the effects of coverage,
65
show that a global coverage condition is necessary to convert small loss under the reference policy to a performance guarantee for offline DPO, while only local coverage is needed for online RLHF. Moreover, they propose a hybrid preference learning algorithm which combines offline DPO with reverse KL regularization from online samples. Our results also leverage a notion of local coverage, however we provide end-to-end statistical learning guarantees.

Besides direct alignment from preferences, there is also a wide literature on on-policy or online preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL), which we mention here without going into detail
(
88
;
50
;
20
;
53
;
76
;
27
;
92
;
79
;
25
)
.

A.3
Reward distillation methods

Given access to a (typically frozen) reward model and a dataset consisting of unlabeled response pairs, reward model distillation aims to match the pairwise differences of the ‘rewards’ implicit in the policy
(
57
)
with those of the explicit rewards. The value-based calibration algorithm proposed by
47
and REBEL algorithm independently proposed by
33
implement this distillation process with squared loss in an offline and online manner, respectively, and demonstrate similar or stronger performance as PPO and DPO baselines.
30
study a pessimistic version of this objective, leading to improved robustness. Moreover,
90
propose the preference distillation algorithm which acts as a soft version of DPO with synthetic preference labels computed from the relative rewards using the Bradley-Terry model. This can also be seen as reward distillation with the binary KL loss as we show in Section
5.1
. Finally, the
A^{*}
-PO algorithm by
14
bypasses the need for pairwise responses by directly estimating the optimal value function to use in the distillation objective.

Appendix B

B.1
Proof of Proposition
3.1

We will prove the following extended proposition for completeness. The tighter bound
(2)
comes from constraining the update to
\Theta_{k}:=B_{p}(\hat{\theta}_{k},r_{k})
assuming knowledge of
r_{k}
. This allows us to slightly improve the fixed-point analysis of Theorem
3.2
, however we omit this for brevity. In addition, we can also allow choosing any user-defined radius
\bar{R}\geq R
for the policy class if
R
is unknown; the dependence on
\bar{R}
(as opposed to
R
) will be only logarithmic.

Proposition B.1

(Full version of Proposition
3.1
)
.

Assume
\hat{\pi}_{k}\in\Pi
satisfies
\hat{\theta}_{k}\in B_{p}(\theta^{*},r_{k})
with radius
r_{k}\in[0,\bar{R}]
. Suppose
D_{k}
consists of
n_{k}
i.i.d. samples of prompts
x\sim\mathcal{D}
and pairs of responses
a^{+},a^{-}
sampled from
\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)
and labeled using
\mathbb{P}_{*}
. Let
\Theta_{k}:=B_{p}(\hat{\theta}_{k},r_{k})
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
that:

(1)

The update
\hat{\theta}_{k+1}=\argmin_{\theta\in B_{p}(0,\bar{R})}\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\theta;D_{k})
satisfies
\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\in B_{p}(\theta^{*},r_{k+1})
where

\displaystyle r_{k+1}^{2}:=\frac{52e^{4\gamma R}d^{2/p}}{\lambda\gamma^{2}n_{k}}C_{*}^{p}(r_{k})\ln\frac{9\gamma\bar{R}n_{k}}{\delta},\quad\text{as long as}\quad r_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\wedge\bar{R}.

(2)

The update
\hat{\theta}_{k+1}=\argmin_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\theta\in\Theta_{k}}}\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\theta;D_{k})
satisfies
\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\in B_{p}(\theta^{*},r_{k+1})
where

\displaystyle r_{k+1}^{2}:=\frac{52e^{4\gamma R}d^{2/p}}{\lambda\gamma^{2}n_{k}}C_{*}^{p}(r_{k})\ln\frac{9\gamma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r_{k}}n_{k}}{\delta},\quad\text{as long as}\quad r_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\wedge{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2r_{k}}.

The proof proceeds in several steps. We will give the proof for Proposition
B.1
(2)
and provide the necessary modifications to obtain Proposition
B.1
(1)
at the end.

Step 1: Bounding learned preferences.

For each preference pair
(a^{+},a^{-})
, denote the corresponding pair of unlabeled responses as
(a^{1},a^{2})
and the indicator
y=1\{a^{+}=a^{1}\}
so that
\mathbb{P}(y=1)=\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)
by Assumption
1
. We adopt the following shorthand:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x):=\sigma\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\pi(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi^{\prime}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi^{\prime}(a^{2}\mid x)}\right),

(12)

so that
\mathbb{P}_{*}=\mathbb{P}_{\pi^{*},\pi_{0}}
. Then the objective at the
k
th step can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi;D_{k})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{(x,a^{1},a^{2})\in D_{k}}y\ln\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)+(1-y)\ln\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\pi_{0}}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x).

Construct an
\varepsilon
-cover
\mathcal{M}
of the
L^{p}
-ball
B_{p}(\hat{\theta}_{k},r_{k})
so that
\left\lvert\mathcal{M}\right\rvert\leq(3r_{k}/\varepsilon)^{d}
. Define the policy estimator
\check{\theta}_{k+1}
as the closest element to
\hat{\theta}_{k+1}
in
\mathcal{M}
, with ties broken arbitrarily, and set
\check{\pi}_{k+1}:=\pi_{\check{\theta}_{k+1}}
. Let
\tilde{D}_{k}
be an independent copy of
D_{k}
. From the symmetrization inequality (Lemma
B.4
), it holds with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle-\ln\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{D}_{k}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi^{*};\tilde{D}_{k})-\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\check{\pi}_{k+1};\tilde{D}_{k}))\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq-\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi^{*};D_{k})-\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\check{\pi}_{k+1};D_{k}))+\ln\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{\delta}.

The left-hand side further satisfies

\displaystyle-\ln\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{D}_{k}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi^{*};\tilde{D}_{k})-\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\check{\pi}_{k+1};\tilde{D}_{k}))\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\ln\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{D}_{k}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(x,a^{1},a^{2})\in\tilde{D}_{k}}y\ln\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)}{\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)}+(1-y)\ln\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)}{\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-n_{k}\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}

\displaystyle\qquad\left[\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)}{\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)}\right)^{1/2}+\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)}{\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)}\right)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle=-n_{k}\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}

\displaystyle\qquad\left[\sqrt{\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)}+\sqrt{\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{2}\succ a^{1}\mid x)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n_{k}}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right],

by Lemma
B.5
.

For the right-hand side, since
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\check{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\check{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{p}\leq\varepsilon
, by Lemma
B.7
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\check{\pi}_{k+1};D_{k})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\hat{\pi}_{k+1};D_{k})+2\gamma\varepsilon n_{k}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi^{*};D_{k})+2\gamma\varepsilon n_{k}

and so

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\pi^{*};D_{k})-\mathcal{L}_{\DPO}(\check{\pi}_{k+1};D_{k}))+\ln\frac{|\mathcal{M}|}{\delta}\leq\gamma\varepsilon n_{k}+d\ln\frac{3r_{k}}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{1}{\delta}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\check{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{4}{n_{k}}\left(\gamma\varepsilon n_{k}+d\ln\frac{3r_{k}}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right),

again applying Lemma
B.7
. Choosing
\varepsilon=1/\gamma n_{k}
and noting
1+\frac{1}{16n_{k}}\leq\frac{17}{16}<\ln 3
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{n_{k}}\left(d\ln(3\gamma r_{k}n_{k})+\ln\frac{3}{\delta}\right).

(13)

Step 2: Initial improvement of radius.

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)=\sigma\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi_{0}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{0}(a^{2}\mid x)}\right)

where

\displaystyle\left\lvert\gamma\ln\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi_{0}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{0}(a^{2}\mid x)}\right\rvert=\gamma\left\lvert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}^{\top}(\phi(x,a^{1})-\phi(x,a^{2}))\right\rvert\leq 2\gamma\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{2}.

Since
\hat{\theta}_{k}\in B_{p}(\theta^{*},r_{k})
and
\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\in B_{p}(\hat{\theta}_{k},r_{k})
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{p}\leq\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k}\rVert_{p}+r_{k}\leq\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}+2r_{k}\leq R+2r_{k}.

(14)

Moreover by Assumption
1
,
\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)=\sigma(r^{*}(x,a^{1})-r^{*}(x,a^{2}))
where

\displaystyle\left\lvert r^{*}(x,a^{1})-r^{*}(x,a^{2})\right\rvert=\gamma|\theta^{*\top}(\phi(x,a^{1})-\phi(x,a^{2}))|\leq 2\gamma R

since
\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\leq R
. Hence by Lemma
B.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{5.09(4\gamma r_{k}\vee 1)e^{2\gamma R}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi_{0}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{0}(a^{2}\mid x)}-r^{*}(x,a^{1})+r^{*}(x,a^{2})\right)^{2}\right].

Now, owing to the fact that
\mathbb{E}[(X-X^{\prime})^{2}]=2\Var(X)
for i.i.d.
X,X^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a^{2}\mid x)}-\gamma\ln\frac{\pi_{0}(a^{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{0}(a^{2}\mid x)}-r^{*}(x,a^{1})+r^{*}(x,a^{2})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\Var_{a\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left(\gamma\ln\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k+1}(a\mid x)}{\pi_{0}(a\mid x)}-r^{*}(x,a)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\Var_{a\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left((\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\phi(x,a)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\gamma^{2}(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\mathbb{V}(\hat{\pi}_{k})(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}).

Moreover,

\displaystyle(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\mathbb{V}(\hat{\pi}_{k})(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})\geq\frac{1}{C_{\hat{\pi}_{k}\shortrightarrow\pi^{*}}}(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\mathbb{V}(\pi^{*})(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})\geq\frac{\lambda}{C_{\hat{\pi}_{k}\shortrightarrow\pi^{*}}}\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}

by Assumption
3
. Since
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\leq r_{k}
, we also have
C_{\hat{\pi}_{k}\shortrightarrow\pi^{*}}\leq C_{*}^{p}(r_{k})
by definition. Putting everything together, we have in conjunction with Eq. (
13
):

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{2/p-1}}{\lambda}C_{\hat{\pi}_{k}\shortrightarrow\pi^{*}}(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\mathbb{V}(\hat{\pi}_{k})(\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{2/p-1}}{2\lambda\gamma^{2}}C_{\hat{\pi}_{k}\shortrightarrow\pi^{*}}5.09^{2}(4\gamma r_{k}\vee 1)^{2}e^{4\gamma R}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a^{1},a^{2}\sim\hat{\pi}_{k}(x)}\left[\left(\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\pi}_{k+1},\pi_{0}}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)-\mathbb{P}_{*}(a^{1}\succ a^{2}\mid x)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{52e^{4\gamma R}d^{2/p}}{\lambda\gamma^{2}n_{k}}C_{*}^{p}(r_{k})\ln\frac{9\gamma r_{k}n_{k}}{\delta}}_{=:\Phi_{k}}(16\gamma^{2}r_{k}^{2}\vee 1).

Denote this initial upper bound as
\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}^{2}
. This is an improvement over the trivial bound

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\leq\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\hat{\theta}_{k}\rVert_{p}+\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\leq 2r_{k},

if and only if

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}^{2}=\Phi_{k}(16\gamma^{2}r_{k}^{2}\vee 1)\leq 4r_{k}^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Phi_{k}\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma^{2}}\wedge 4r_{k}^{2}.

(15)

Step 3: Iterative tightening.

The following trick allows us to shave off a factor of at most
O(\bar{R})
from the initial bound
\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}
when
n_{k}
is sufficiently large. With the newfound knowledge that
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\leq\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}
, we can replace Eq. (
14
) with
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{p}\leq R+\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}
and repeat the following steps to show that

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}^{2}\leq\Phi_{k}(4\gamma^{2}\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}^{2}\vee 1)=:\tilde{r}_{k+1,2}^{2}.

Assuming
\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}\leq 2r_{k}
as in Eq. (
15
), this gives an improved bound
\tilde{r}_{k+1,2}\leq\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}
. Repeating this argument yields a sequence of increasingly tighter radii
(\tilde{r}_{k+1,i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
defined recursively as

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{k+1,i+1}^{2}=\Phi_{k}(4\gamma^{2}\tilde{r}_{k+1,i}^{2}\vee 1).

(16)

By the monotone convergence theorem, we may define the limiting radius
r_{k+1}:=\lim_{i\to\infty}\tilde{r}_{k+1,i}
so that
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq r_{k+1}
. Taking
i\to\infty
on both sides of Eq. (
16
) yields

\displaystyle r_{k+1}^{2}=\Phi_{k}(4\gamma^{2}r_{k+1}^{2}\vee 1)\quad\Rightarrow\quad r_{k+1}^{2}=\Phi_{k}

since
r_{k+1}^{2}=\Phi_{k}\cdot 4\gamma^{2}r_{k+1}^{2}
cannot be true under Eq. (
15
). Hence we have proven Proposition
B.1
(2)
.

Finally, for the radius-agnostic update described in Proposition
B.1
(1)
, we must replace the policy class radius
r_{k}
in the covering number analysis of Step 1 with the worst-case radius
\bar{R}
, as well as Eq. (
14
) in Step 2 with the trivial bound
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}\rVert_{2}\leq\bar{R}
. This yields the initial bound

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\underbrace{\frac{52e^{4\gamma R}d^{2/p}}{\lambda\gamma^{2}n_{k}}C_{*}^{p}(r_{k})\ln\frac{9\gamma\bar{R}n_{k}}{\delta}}_{=:\Phi_{k}}(4\gamma^{2}\bar{R}^{2}\vee 1)=:\tilde{r}_{k+1,1}^{2}.

Then as long as

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(4\gamma^{2}\bar{R}^{2}\vee 1)\leq\bar{R}^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Phi_{k}\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma^{2}}\wedge\bar{R}^{2},

we can again construct a tightening sequence as
\tilde{r}_{k+1,i+1}^{2}=\Phi_{k}(4\gamma^{2}\tilde{r}_{k+1,i}^{2}\vee 1)
and repeat the limiting argument to get rid of the
4\gamma^{2}\bar{R}^{2}
factor and obtain the desired guarantee. ∎

Remark B.2
.

The use of the symmetrization inequality and Lemma
B.5
to bound the squared differences of the preference distributions borrow from Theorem 6 of
31

. Moreover, using the tighter sigmoid difference bound Lemma

Proof of Theorem
3.2

Define the quantity

\displaystyle\xi:=\frac{\eta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\xi_{n}=\frac{52e^{4\gamma R}d^{2/p}}{\lambda\gamma^{2}n}\ln\frac{9\gamma RKn}{\delta}.

From the assumed condition
\xi_{n}\lesssim 1/C_{*}^{p}(R)
, we can guarantee

\displaystyle\xi\leq\frac{\eta^{2}R^{2}}{C_{*}^{p}(R)}\wedge\frac{1}{4\gamma^{2}C_{*}^{p}(R)}\wedge\frac{\eta^{2}}{e^{2}}

(17)

where the second comparison follows from
\sqrt{C_{*}^{p}(R)}\wedge R\gtrsim 1/\gamma
.

By Proposition
3.1
and Assumption
2
, union bounding over all
K
iterations, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
that
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\leq r_{k}
for all
k\leq K
for the sequence of radii
(r_{k})_{k\geq 0}
defined recursively as

\displaystyle r_{k+1}^{2}=\xi C_{*}^{p}(r_{k}),\quad r_{0}=R,

(18)

as long as
r_{k}\leq(1/2\gamma)\wedge R
for each
k\geq 1
(we verify this below).

We now analyze the convergence of this iteration. For
z>0
, define the set

\displaystyle S_{z}:=\{r>0\mid\xi C_{*}^{p}(r)\leq z^{2}r^{2}\}.

If
C_{*}^{p}
is square root-convex, the mapping
r\mapsto\sqrt{\xi C_{*}^{p}(r)}-zr
is convex, so its level set
S_{z}
is a closed convex subset of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. More generally, under Assumption
4
, we may choose
\eta^{\prime}\leq\eta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{(\eta^{\prime})^{2}}{\xi}=\kappa\frac{C_{*}^{p}(R)}{R^{2}}

satisfying Eq. (
17
), so
S_{\eta^{\prime}}
is a closed interval, and proceed with the remainder of the proof with
\eta
replaced by
\eta^{\prime}
. Now under the first condition of Eq. (
17
), it holds that
R\in S_{\eta}
, so
S_{\eta},S_{1}
are nonempty and we may write

\displaystyle S_{1}=[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}],\quad S_{\eta}=[\alpha_{\eta},\beta_{\eta}],\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{\eta}\leq R\leq\beta_{\eta}\leq\beta_{1}.

Note that
\eta^{2}\alpha_{\eta}^{2}\geq\xi C_{*}^{p}(\alpha_{\eta})\geq\xi C_{*}^{p}(0)=\xi
so that
\alpha_{\eta}\geq\sqrt{\xi}/\eta
. This bound is nearly tight: since

\displaystyle\xi C_{*}^{p}\left(\frac{e\sqrt{\xi}}{\eta}\right)\leq\xi\exp\left(\frac{2e\sqrt{\xi}}{\eta}\right)\leq e^{2}\xi=\eta^{2}\left(\frac{e\sqrt{\xi}}{\eta}\right)^{2}

due to Lemma

\alpha_{\eta}\leq e\sqrt{\xi}/\eta
. Moreover, the set
S_{1}

(r_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is monotone decreasing and
r_{1}=\sqrt{\xi C_{*}^{p}(R)}\leq(1/2\gamma)\wedge R
under the second condition of Eq. (

).

We now claim that

\displaystyle r_{K}\leq\alpha_{\eta}\vee\eta^{K}r_{0}\leq\frac{e\sqrt{\xi}}{\eta}\vee R\eta^{K}.

Indeed, if
r_{K}>\alpha_{\eta}
, then
r_{k}\in S_{\eta}
for all

and

\displaystyle r_{k+1}^{2}=\xi C_{*}^{p}(r_{k})\leq\eta^{2}r_{k}^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad r_{K}\leq\eta r_{K-1}\leq\cdots\leq\eta^{K}r_{0}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}_{K}-\theta_{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert\hat{\theta}_{K}-\theta_{*}\rVert_{p}^{2}\leq\frac{e^{2}\xi}{\eta^{2}}\vee R^{2}\eta^{2K}=\frac{e^{2}\xi_{n}}{\gamma^{2}}\vee R^{2}\eta^{2K}

and

upper bounds both
\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\pi}_{K}\|\pi^{*}),\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{*}\|\hat{\pi}_{K})
by Proposition
B.9
. We also have

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\hat{\pi}_{K},\pi^{*})\lesssim\ln\left(1+\chi^{2}(\hat{\pi}_{K},\pi^{*})\right)\leq\frac{e^{2}\xi_{n}}{\gamma^{2}}\vee R^{2}\eta^{2K}

due to the inequality
z\lesssim\ln(1+z)
, valid for positive

.
∎

B.3
Auxiliary Results

Lemma B.3

(Tight sigmoid differences)
.

Let
a,b>0
be fixed radii. For all
\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq a
,
\left\lvert w\right\rvert\leq b
it holds

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)\right\rvert\geq\frac{\left\lvert z-w\right\rvert}{5.09(\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert\vee 1)e^{a\wedge b}}.

This improves upon the
e^{-(a\vee b)}
constant in Lemma 8 of
90
by relaxing the exponential dependency on the larger radius
b
to linear. A similar result has been shown in Lemma 9 of
29
, however our bound can be slightly tighter and will prove easier to manipulate.

Proof.

By symmetry we may assume
a\leq b
and
w>0
. Fix an auxiliary constant
\tau>0
. First suppose
w<a+\tau
and note that
\sigma^{\prime}
is even and decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Then by the mean value theorem, there exists
t\in(-a,a+\tau)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)}{z-w}=\sigma^{\prime}(t)\geq\sigma^{\prime}(a+\tau)=\frac{1}{1+e^{a+\tau}}\frac{1}{1+e^{-(a+\tau)}}\geq\frac{1}{(1+e^{\tau})(1+e^{-\tau})e^{a}}.

Now suppose
a+\tau\leq w\leq b
. Differentiating w.r.t.
z
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}\frac{\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)}{z-w}=\frac{1}{z-w}\left(\sigma^{\prime}(z)-\frac{\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)}{z-w}\right),

so the value of the slope at any critical value
\left\lvert z\right\rvert<a
(excluding the endpoints) is equal to
\sigma^{\prime}(z)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\min_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq a}\frac{\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)}{z-w}\geq\min\left\{\frac{\sigma(w)-\sigma(a)}{w-a},\frac{\sigma(w)-\sigma(-a)}{w+a},\sigma^{\prime}(a)\right\}.

The first and third terms are bounded below as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(a)\geq\frac{\sigma(w)-\sigma(a)}{w-a}\geq\frac{\sigma(a+\tau)-\sigma(a)}{b-a}\geq\frac{\tau\sigma^{\prime}(a+\tau)}{b-a}

again by the mean value theorem, and we can reuse the above lower bound for
\sigma^{\prime}(a+\tau)
. Moreover, the second term is larger than the first since

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(w)-\sigma(a)}{w-a}\leq\frac{\sigma(w)-1+\sigma(a)}{w+a}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{w}\left(\sigma(w)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{a}\left(\sigma(a)-\frac{1}{2}\right)

and the function
w\mapsto(\sigma(w)-1/2)/w
is decreasing. Hence, we have shown

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq a,\left\lvert w\right\rvert\leq b}\left\lvert\frac{\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)}{z-w}\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{\left\lvert b-a\right\rvert\vee\tau}\cdot\frac{\tau}{(2+e^{\tau}+e^{-\tau})e^{a\wedge b}}

for all
a,b,\tau>0
; the statement follows by taking
\tau=1
.
∎

The following proposition gives a tight upper bound on the KL and Rényi divergence between linear softmax policies. This is tighter than the naive density ratio approach in Lemma

Proposition B.9
.

Given
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\theta^{\prime}})\leq\ln\left(1+\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta},\pi_{\theta^{\prime}})\right)\leq\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The first inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality (applied to the hidden expectation over
x\sim\mathcal{D}
) and monotonicity of Rényi divergence
(
73
)
. We proceed to show the second inequality.

Define the partition and log-partition functions

\displaystyle Z_{x}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{0}(x)}\left[\exp(\theta^{\top}\phi(x,a))\right],\quad A_{x}(\theta):=\ln Z_{x}(\theta).

It holds that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}A_{x}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla^{2}Z_{x}(\theta)}{Z_{x}(\theta)}-\frac{\nabla Z_{x}(\theta)\nabla Z_{x}(\theta)^{\top}}{Z_{x}(\theta)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(x)}\left[\phi(x,a)\phi(x,a)^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(x)}[\phi(x,a)]\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(x)}[\phi(x,a)]^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Var_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(x)}(\phi(x,a))

and
\lVert\phi(x,a)\rVert_{2}\leq 1
, hence
\nabla^{2}A_{x}(\theta)\preceq{\mathbf{I}}_{d}
for all
x,\theta
. It follows for all
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that the second order finite difference

\displaystyle A_{x}(\theta+\nu)+A_{x}(\theta-\nu)-2A_{x}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s^{2}}\bigg|_{s=t^{\prime}}A_{x}(\theta+s\nu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}\int_{t}^{t+1}\nu^{\top}\nabla^{2}A_{x}(\theta+t^{\prime}\nu)\nu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\nu\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Now set
\nu=\theta^{\prime}-\theta
. The Rényi divergence of order two can be expressed as

\displaystyle\ln\left(1+\chi^{2}(\pi_{\theta},\pi_{\theta^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle=\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}\left[\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(a\mid x)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}\left[\frac{Z_{x}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}}{Z_{x}(\theta)^{2}}\exp(-2\nu^{\top}\phi(x,a))\right]

\displaystyle=\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},a\sim\pi_{0}(x)}\left[\frac{Z_{x}(\theta^{\prime})}{Z_{x}(\theta)^{2}}\exp((\theta^{\prime}-2\nu)^{\top}\phi(x,a))\right]

\displaystyle=\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{Z_{x}(\theta+\nu)Z_{x}(\theta-\nu)}{Z_{x}(\theta)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\ln\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\exp\left(A_{x}(\theta+\nu)+A_{x}(\theta-\nu)-2A_{x}(\theta)\right)\right],

which is upper bounded by
\lVert\nu\rVert_{2}^{2}
as shown.
∎

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-4-4\tau}}{2(d+1)}\left(\lVert\Pi_{S}(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{J})\rVert_{2}^{2}\wedge 1\right).

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{J}\|\hat{\pi})\geq\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{J}\|\pi_{\zeta})
due to convexity of the map
\theta\mapsto\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{J}\|\pi_{\theta})
along the segment between
\Pi_{S}\theta_{J}
,
\Pi_{S}\hat{\theta}
(Lemma
E.1
). We conclude from Eq. (
E.2
):

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\sup_{\theta^{*}\in\Theta^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\pi^{*}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{*}\|\hat{\pi})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}_{J}\left[\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\pi_{J}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{J}\|\hat{\pi})\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\frac{e^{-4-4\tau}}{2(d+1)}\mathbb{E}_{J}\left[1_{\{\hat{J}\neq J\}}\left(\lVert\Pi_{S}(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{J})\rVert_{2}^{2}\wedge 1\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{-4-4\tau}}{2(d+1)}\Pr(\hat{J}\neq J)\tau^{2}c_{1}d

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{C_{*}^{1}(R)}{n}\wedge\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

∎

Case II:
p>1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\varnothing
,
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\cdots,d\}
and features
\phi(i)=e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set

\displaystyle\pi_{0}=\softmax(-\varphi),\quad\varphi=(-2^{-1/p}(R-1),2^{-1/p}(R-1),0,\cdots,0).

Set the target class
\Theta^{*}=B_{p}(\varphi,1)\subset B_{p}(0,R)
and define
\theta_{j}:=j\tau e_{2}+\varphi
for
j\in\{0,\pm 1,\cdots,\pm M\}
for some sufficiently large
M=\Theta(1)
. It holds that all
\theta_{j}\in\Theta^{*}
as long as
M\tau\leq 1
. Again defining the sample distribution
(a^{1},a^{2},y)\sim\mathcal{P}_{j}
as Eq. (
27
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{P}_{j}\|\mathcal{P}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{0}}\left[(\theta_{j,a}-\theta_{k,a})^{2}\right]\leq\pi_{0}(2)M^{2}\tau^{2}\asymp e^{-2^{1-1/p}R}M^{2}\tau^{2}

and so
I(D,J),\ln 2\leq\frac{1}{4}\ln(2M+1)
by taking
M
sufficiently large and

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\asymp\frac{e^{2^{1-1/p}R}\ln(2M+1)}{M^{2}n}\wedge\frac{1}{M}.

Hence Fano’s inequality applies and the estimation error of the following estimator is at least
1/2
:

\displaystyle\hat{J}:=\argmin_{1\leq j\leq M}\,\lVert\Pi_{S}(\theta_{j}-\hat{\theta})\rVert_{p}.

Note that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\lVert\Pi_{S}x\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\lvert x_{i}-\bar{x}\right\rvert\leq 2\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}
so that
\lVert\Pi_{S}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}\leq 2
, moreover
\lVert\Pi_{S}\rVert_{1\to 1}<2
by Eq. (
29
). By the Riesz–Thorin interpolation theorem, it follows that
\lVert\Pi_{S}\rVert_{p\to p}\leq 2
for all
p>1
, so

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta_{J}\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\Pi_{S}(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{J})\rVert_{p}\geq\frac{1}{4}\lVert\theta_{J}-\theta_{\hat{J}}\rVert_{p}\gtrsim 1_{\{\hat{J}\neq J\}}\tau.

Since
C_{*}^{p}(R)\asymp e^{2^{1-1/p}R}/d
by Proposition
2.1
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta^{*}\in\Theta^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\pi^{*}}\left[\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{p}\right]

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{e^{2^{-1/p}R}}{\sqrt{n}}\wedge 1\asymp\sqrt{\frac{dC_{*}^{p}(R)}{n}}\wedge 1.

∎

E.3
Auxiliary Results

Lemma E.1

(Positive local curvature of KL)
.

Let
\tau>0
and consider the distribution
\pi_{\theta}:=\softmax(\theta)
defined over the domain

\displaystyle S\cap B_{\infty}(0,\tau)=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\theta^{\top}\bm{1}=0,\;\;\lVert\theta\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau\right\}.

(32)

Then for arbitrary
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in S\cap B_{\infty}(0,\tau)
, the map
\theta\mapsto\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}\|\pi_{\theta})
is convex and

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-4\tau}}{2d}\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}\|\pi_{\theta})\leq\frac{e^{4\tau}}{2d}\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The domain
S\cap B_{\infty}(0,\tau)
is clearly convex. Identify
\pi_{\theta}
with
(\pi_{\theta}(1),\cdots,\pi_{\theta}(d))\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define the map
h:S\cap B_{\infty}(0,\tau)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
as
h(\theta):=\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}\|\pi_{\theta})
. A standard computation shows

\displaystyle\nabla h(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}-\pi_{\theta^{\prime}},\quad\nabla^{2}h(\theta)=\diag\pi_{\theta}-\pi_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}^{\top}

when restricted to
S
, and
h(\theta^{\prime})=0
,
\nabla h(\theta^{\prime})=0
. Then for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle u^{\top}\nabla^{2}h(\theta)u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{\theta}(i)u_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{\theta}(i)u_{i}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\pi_{\theta}(i)\pi_{\theta}(j)(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}.

Moreover as in Proposition
2.1
, it follows from
\lVert\theta\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau
that

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-2\tau}}{d}\leq\pi_{\theta}(i)\leq\frac{e^{2\tau}}{d},\quad i=0,\cdots,d,

and so for any
u\in S
,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-4\tau}}{d}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{e^{-2\tau}}{d}\right)^{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d}(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}\leq u^{\top}\nabla^{2}h(\theta)u\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{e^{2\tau}}{d}\right)^{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d}(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}=\frac{e^{4\tau}}{d}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The claim now follows from convexity of
\Theta_{\infty}(\tau)
and the identity

\displaystyle h(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)^{\top}\nabla^{2}h(\theta+t(\theta^{\prime}-\theta))(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

∎

Lemma E.2
.

For
z,w\in(0,1)
, it holds that
\mathrm{KL}(\Bern(\sigma(z))\|\Bern(\sigma(w)))\leq\frac{1}{8}\left\lvert z-w\right\rvert^{2}
.

Proof.

Define
f_{z}(w):=\mathrm{KL}(\Bern(\sigma(z))\|\Bern(\sigma(w)))
so that

\displaystyle f_{z}(w)

\displaystyle=\sigma(z)\ln\frac{\sigma(z)}{\sigma(w)}+(1-\sigma(z))\ln\frac{1-\sigma(z)}{1-\sigma(w)}=\ln\frac{\sigma(z)}{\sigma(w)}+(1-\sigma(z))(w-z).

A simple computation gives
f_{z}(z)=f_{z}^{\prime}(z)=0
and
f_{z}^{\prime\prime}(w)=\sigma(w)(1-\sigma(w))\in[0,\frac{1}{4}]
. It follows from Taylor’s theorem that
f_{z}(w)\leq\frac{1}{8}\left\lvert z-w\right\rvert^{2}
.
∎
