What Do Agents Think Others Would Do? Level-2 Inverse Games for Inferring Agents’ Estimates of Others’ Objectives
Hamzah I. Khan, Jingqi Li, David Fridovich-Keil

Abstract
Effectively interpreting strategic interactions among multiple agents requires us to infer each agent’s objective from limited information. Existing inverse game-theoretic approaches frame this challenge in terms of a “level-1” inference problem, in which we take the perspective of a third-party observer and assume that individual agents share complete knowledge of one another’s objectives. However, this assumption breaks down in decentralized, real-world decision scenarios like urban driving and bargaining, in which agents may act based on conflicting views of one another’s objectives. We demonstrate the necessity of inferring agents’ heterogeneous estimates of each other’s objectives through empirical examples, and by theoretically characterizing the prediction error of level-1 inference on fictitious gameplay data from linear-quadratic games. To address this fundamental issue, we propose a framework for level- 2 inference to address the question: “What does each agent believe about all agents’ objectives?” We prove that the level-2 inference problem is non-convex even in benign settings like linear-quadratic games, and we develop an efficient gradient-based approach for identifying local solutions. Experiments on a synthetic urban driving example show that our approach uncovers nuanced misalignments that level-1 methods miss.



Introduction

Figure 1:

Schematic of the proposed approach for level-2 game-theoretic model inference.

Given observations of a multi-agent interaction, our method infers each agent’s objective
and
its estimate of the others’ objectives,
thus accounting for potential mismatch in each agent’s understanding of the interaction.
By contrast, existing “level-1” methods assume that each agent knows the others’ objectives, and lack the flexibility to explain nuanced behavior that results from that mismatch.

Autonomous agents in interactive environments must develop an understanding of other decision-makers’ objectives in order to model their behavior.
In urban driving, for example, failure to understand the intentions of another driver can easily lead to unsafe and/or inefficient behavior.
Consider
fig.

1
, in which the blue car wants to change into the lane currently occupied by the red car.
Initially, the two vehicles deadlock because both agents incorrectly estimate the other agent’s target lane.
A third-party observer
seeking to understand the interaction might conclude that both agents wish to stay in their lanes, cf. the “level-1 observer” in
fig.

1
. However, that incorrect conclusion would lead to substantial error in predicting the agents’ future behavior.
The level-2 observer in
fig.

1
, by contrast, infers each agent’s objective and their estimate of the other agent’s objective, concluding that
both agents incorrectly estimate the other’s target lane, and so they drive overcautiously and deadlock.
Thus, misunderstandings between agents in these complex interactions can significantly influence outcomes.

This work aims to develop a formal mathematical model which can explain these interactions, and an algorithm that a third party observer—such as a city regulator seeking to optimize traffic patterns—can use to identify agents’ estimates of one another’s objectives.
To this end, we take a game-theoretic perspective and model the behavior of each agent as a rational best response to what it believes others will do.
Given such an interaction model, a third party with access to (possibly noisy, partial) observations of the agents’ actions can solve an “inverse game” problem to identify parameters of each agent’s objective function that best explain the data.
However, existing methods
(
Awasthi and Lamperski 2020
;
Le Cleac’h, Schwager, and Manchester 2021
;
Peters et al. 2023
;
Mehr et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
assume that, although the agents’ objectives are unknown to the observer, they
are
fully known to the interacting agents.
This “level-1” assumption readily breaks down in practice, per the discussion about the lane change example in
fig.

1
.

Instead of assuming that all observed agents share knowledge of their objectives, our work uses the “level-2” reasoning concept from the theory of mind literature
(
Harbers, Van Den Bosch, and Meyer 2009
)
to model each agent as acting rationally based on its own objectives and estimates of others’ objectives.

Specifically, we make
three contributions
.

1.

We formalize a framework, based in theory of mind, for modeling level-2 dynamic and inverse games using sets of coupled Nash equilibrium problems.
We motivate the need for inferring agents’ objectives and their estimates of other agents’ objectives through empirical studies.

2.

We prove that the level-2 inference problem is non-convex and derive prediction error bounds for level-1 inference applied to linear-quadratic (LQ) game data generated by agents operating under a level-2 model, theoretically confirming the advantage of level-2 inference over level-1 methods.

3.

We propose an efficient gradient-based algorithm for identifying locally optimal solutions to the level-2 inverse game problem. Our method outperforms level-1 inference in empirical studies on LQ games and a synthetic urban driving scenario by identifying agents’ misaligned estimates of others’ objectives.

Related Work

Dynamic Games and Computational Methods

Dynamic games
(
Başar and Olsder 1998
)
model interactions among a group of agents; each strategically acts to minimize its individual objective over time.
To ensure computational tractability, a majority of existing methods for computing game-theoretic equilibria commonly assume complete knowledge of all agents’ dynamics and objectives.
Under these assumptions, Nash equilibria, where each agent’s decision is unilaterally optimal, are computed using model-based optimization

(
Busoniu, Babuska, and De Schutter 2006
;
Lowe et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Daskalakis, Foster, and Golowich 2020
;
Lu et al. 2021
;
Giannou et al. 2022
)
.

In particular, under certain regularity conditions, (local) Nash equilibria
(
Ratliff, Burden, and Sastry 2016
)
can be efficiently computed by formulating them as variational inequalities
(
Kinderlehrer and Stampacchia 2000
;
Scutari et al. 2010
)
or mixed complementarity problems (MCP)
(
Dirkse and Ferris 1995a
)
.
In our proposed level-2 inference framework, we utilize PATH
(
Dirkse and Ferris 1995b
)
, an MCP solver, to compute Nash equilibria given agents’ specified objective functions.
This facilitates efficient inference of maximum likelihood estimates of each agent’s belief regarding other agents’ objectives.

Inverse Dynamic Games

The assumption of complete information in dynamic games often fails in realistic multi-agent interactions, motivating the development of inverse game methods to infer missing information from observed behaviors
(
Köpf et al. 2017
;
Molloy, Ford, and Perez 2017
;
Rothfuß et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2022
)
.
Recent approaches include leveraging extended Kalman filters
(
Le Cleac’h, Schwager, and Manchester 2021
)
, maximum entropy-based inference

(
Mehr et al. 2023
)
, and considering constrained cases
(
Awasthi and Lamperski 2020
)
.
Other studies
(
Peters et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Khan and Fridovich-Keil 2024
;
Soltanian and Zhang 2025
)
utilize differentiable game solvers and propose efficient gradient-based inverse game solutions.
Despite the diversity of methods, most prior work studies the level-1 inference problem, which assumes that observed agents know each other’s objectives.
Inspired by literature on the
theory of mind
(see below), our study demonstrates the limitations of level-1 inference when observed agents have misaligned estimates of each other’s objectives, and proposes a level-2 inference technique to address this challenge.

Theory of Mind and Level-k Thinking

The theory of mind
(
Rabinowitz et al. 2018
;
Pynadath and Marsella 2005
)
provides a framework for modeling how agents reason about each other’s beliefs and objectives, making it valuable in multi-agent scenarios with misaligned information.
Building upon recursive reasoning
(
Gmytrasiewicz and Durfee 1995
)
, many prior studies
(
Karimi and Vahidi 2020
;
Fotiadis and Vamvoudakis 2021
;
Tian, Tomizuka, and Sun 2021
;
Tian et al. 2021
;
Levin and Zhang 2022
;
Ma et al. 2022
;
Jin et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2025
;
Jara-Ettinger 2019
)

leverage level-k reasoning methods to influence or infer hierarchical beliefs for strategic coordination.
However, these approaches often face challenges in efficient optimization within continuous state and action spaces and typically lack theoretical and empirical characterization demonstrating the advantages of level-2 inference over level-1 methods.

In contrast, our work focuses on level-2 inference, i.e., inferring each agent’s estimate of others’ objectives.
To infer these parameters efficiently in continuous state and action space, we model level-2 games as differentiable MCPs and derive a gradient-based solver for the level-2 inverse games.
We show empirically and theoretically that level-2 inference outperforms level-1 inference in settings where agents differ in their understandings about each other’s goals.

Nash Equilibria in Dynamic Games.

Let each agent

\theta^{i}\in\mathcal{P}^{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}^{i}}

\theta{}\equiv\{\theta^{1},\theta^{2},\cdots,\theta^{N{}}\}\in\mathcal{\mathbf{P}}{}\equiv\mathcal{P}^{1}\times\mathcal{P}^{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{P}^{N{}}.

(2)

Each agent

optimizes a stagewise objective
\ell_{t}^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t};\theta^{i})

J^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u};\theta{})\equiv\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t};\theta^{i})

(3)

Given an initial state

\Gamma(\theta{},\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}},T)\equiv(N{},\{J^{i},f^{i},h^{i},g^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}),

\forall i\in[N{}]\left\{\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}^{i}}\ &J^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u};\theta{})\\
\text{s.t. }0&=h^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}),0\leq g^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{x}^{i}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}^{i}\\

\displaystyle~~(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in\text{LGNE of }\Gamma(\hat{\theta}{}).

Formulating Level-2 Inverse Games

In this section, we extend the classical (level-1) inverse game formulation to the level-2 setting. Specifically, from the standard viewpoint of a third-party observer, we aim to infer each agent
i
’s objective as well as its estimates of other agents’ objectives using historical behavior data
\mathbf{y}

Before presenting the detailed formulation, we first define a level-2 game as one in which each agent
i
knows its own true objective parameter
\theta^{i}
and maintains estimates of the other agents’ objective parameters, denoted by

\theta^{i,-i}\equiv\{\theta^{i,1},\cdots,\theta^{i,i-1},\theta^{i,i+1},\cdots,\theta^{i,N}\}

i

\Theta^{i}\equiv\{\theta^{i},\theta^{i,-i}\}\in\mathcal{\mathbf{P}}{}.

(7)

i

\Theta^{i}

\mathbf{X}^{i}\equiv\{\mathbf{x}^{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{N}

\Theta^{i}

In the corresponding inverse problem, the

\Theta^{i}

\hat{\Theta}^{i{}}\equiv\{\hat{\theta}^{i{}},\hat{\theta}^{i{},-i}\}\in\mathcal{\mathbf{P}}{}.

(8)

N{}

\{\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{1}),\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{2}),\cdots,\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{N})\}

In an

\hat{\Theta}\equiv\{\hat{\Theta}^{1},\hat{\Theta}^{2},\cdots,\hat{\Theta}^{N}\}\in\mathcal{\mathbf{P}}{}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{\mathbf{P}}{},

(9)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{X}^{i},\mathbf{U}^{i})\in\text{LGNE of }\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{i}),

(10b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\{\textbf{u}^{1,1},\textbf{u}^{2,2},\cdots,\textbf{u}^{N{},N{}}\},

Theoretical Characterization of Level-2 Inverse Games

In this section, we focus on LQ games, a class of dynamic games with linear dynamics and quadratic objectives that allow for analytical tractability. We show that level-2 inference is inherently non-convex. However, we demonstrate its necessity by deriving upper and lower bounds on the minimal prediction error of level-1 inference when the data involves agents with misaligned estimates of others’ objectives. All proofs are provided in the Appendix.

In an
N
-agent,
T
-horizon LQ game, the
i
-th agent considers the quadratic stage cost

\ell_{t}^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t};\theta^{i})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{x}_{t}}^{\top}Q({\theta^{i}})\mathbf{x}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{u}^{i}_{t}}^{\top}R^{i}\mathbf{u}^{i}_{t}

(12)

are parameterized by the vector
\theta^{i}
, and
R_{t}^{i}
is a fixed positive definite matrix.
For each agent
i
, its dynamics
f^{i}
is

\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t+1}=A^{i}\mathbf{x}^{i}_{t}+B^{i}\mathbf{u}^{i}_{t}.

(13)

To formulate the Lagrangian for each agent
i
, we associate the initial condition constraint
0=\mathbf{x}^{i}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}^{i}
with a Lagrange multiplier
\bm{\eta}^{i}

with dynamics constraint

13
.
For clarity, we denote
\bm{\eta}\equiv\{\bm{\eta}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
\bm{\lambda}_{t}\equiv\{\bm{\lambda}^{i}_{t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and
\bm{\lambda}\equiv\{\bm{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}
.

0&\bar{R}&\bar{\textbf{{B}}}^{\top}&0\\
\bar{A}&\bar{B}&0&0\\
\bar{C}&0&0&0\end{array}\right]}_{M(\theta{})}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{u}\\
\bm{\lambda}\\
\bm{\eta}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\

(14)

where variables
\{\bar{A},\bar{B},\bar{C},\bar{\textbf{{A}}},\bar{\textbf{{B}}},\bar{\textbf{{C}}},\bar{Q}({\theta{}}),\bar{R}\}

Let

We show in the following proposition that level-2 inference is a non-convex problem, even in this benign setting.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\{\Theta^{i*}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be the ground truth parameters of a level-2 inverse game. Suppose that, in each agent

\Gamma(\Theta^{i*})

is a non-convex function.

MCP Transcription for Level-2 Inverse Games

Next, we consider a more general setting: nonlinear dynamics and non-quadratic costs.
Within each agent
i
’s hypothesized game
\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{i})
, for each agent
j\in[N]
, we associate inequality constraints
0\leq g^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
with multipliers
\bm{\mu}^{j}\geq 0
, and equality constraints
0=h^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
with multipliers
\bm{\phi}^{j}
. Similar to the LQ settings, at each stage
t
, a dynamics constraint multiplier
\bm{\lambda}^{j}_{t}
is introduced. A multiplier
\bm{\eta}^{j}
is also introduced for the initial state constraint
0=\mathbf{x}^{j}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\textrm{init}}
. As before, we define
\bm{\mu}\equiv\{\bm{\mu}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
,
\bm{\phi}\equiv\{\bm{\phi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}{}}
, and
\bm{\eta}\equiv\{\bm{\eta}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{g}{}}
.
We compactly store all variables as

\mathbf{z}\equiv[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\eta},\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T{}(n_{x}+n_{u}+n_{x})+n_{h}{}+n_{g}{}}.

(19)

In the agent
i
’s hypothesized game
\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{i})
, the Lagrangian for agent
j
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{j}(\mathbf{z};\hat{\Theta}^{i})=~

\displaystyle J^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u};\hat{\Theta}^{i})

(20a)

\displaystyle\quad+{\bm{\phi}^{j}}^{\top}h^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

(20b)

\displaystyle\quad+{\bm{\eta}^{j}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\textrm{init}})

(20c)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}{\bm{\lambda}^{j}_{t}}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t+1}-f^{j}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t},\mathbf{u}^{j}_{t}))

(20d)

\displaystyle\quad-{\bm{\mu}^{j}}^{\top}g^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}).

(20e)

Under the linear independent constraint qualification, an LGNE satisfies the following KKT conditions

\forall j\in[N]\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=\nabla_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}^{j})}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\mathbf{z};\hat{\Theta}^{i})\\
&0=h^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\\
&0=\mathbf{x}^{j}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\textrm{init}}\\
&0=\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t+1}-f^{j}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t},\mathbf{u}^{j}_{t}),\ t\in[T-1]\\
&0\leq g^{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\perp\bm{\mu}^{j}\geq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

Motivated by the numerical efficiency of existing solvers such as PATH
(
Dirkse and Ferris 1995b
)
for solving such mixed complementarity problems
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, §9.4.2)
, we can transform the above KKT conditions into mixed complementarity conditions by following a similar process as in

Fridovich-Keil 2024
.
We define two vector functions
F_{\textrm{ineq}}(\mathbf{z};\hat{\Theta}^{i})\equiv[g^{1\top},\cdots,g^{N\top}]^{\top}
and

F_{\textrm{eq}}(\mathbf{z};\hat{\Theta}^{i})\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}^{i})}\mathcal{L}^{j}\\
h^{j}\\
\mathbf{x}^{j}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{j}_{\textrm{init}}\\
\left[\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t+1}-f^{j}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t},\mathbf{u}^{j}_{t})\right]_{t=1}^{T-1}\end{bmatrix}_{j=1}^{N}\end{bmatrix},

and we aim to find a solution
\mathbf{z}^{*}
satisfying the mixed complementary conditions:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0=F_{\textrm{eq}}(\mathbf{z};\hat{\Theta}^{i})\\
&0\leq\bm{\mu}\ \ \perp\ \ F_{\textrm{ineq}}(\mathbf{z};\hat{\Theta}^{i})\geq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

Leveraging the PATH solver, we can efficiently compute an LGNE. Moreover, under certain regularity conditions such as strict complementarity, we can apply the implicit function theorem
(
Krantz and Parks 2002
)
to the above mixed complementarity conditions and use the chain rule to obtain the gradient of
\mathbf{z}^{*}
with respect to
\hat{\Theta}^{i}
, i.e.,
\nabla\mathbf{z}^{*}(\hat{\Theta}^{i})
. After reshaping
\hat{\Theta}
as a vector
\bm{\Theta}
, we obtain the gradient of
L(\hat{\Theta})
with respect to an arbitrary element

via the chain rule:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{\Theta}_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial(G(\mathbf{x}^{i,i*}_{t}))}\cdot\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{x}^{i,i*}_{t})}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{i,i*}_{t}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}^{i,i*}_{t}}{\partial\bm{\Theta}_{j}}

(22)

where
\{\mathbf{x}^{i,i*}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
represents agent
i
’s LGNE state trajectory in its hypothesized game
\Gamma(\hat{\Theta}^{i})
.
The additive structure of
eq.

22
enables efficient parallel computation over each agent.

We propose a first-order gradient-based method (

Next, we run level-1 (

is available to an observer, recorded from receding horizon rollouts of the interaction.

A third-party observer infers the parameters that describe each agent’s objective and its estimate of the other agent’s objective.
Both level-1 and level-2 inference initially converge to similar incorrect estimates about each agent
i{}
,
\hat{\theta}^{i{}}\approx\hat{\theta}^{i{},i{}}\approx\hat{\theta}^{j{},i{}}

However, as the methods see the lane change observations, we find they correct their inferences

However, level-2 inference provides additional information:
\hat{\theta}^{1,2}

These level-2 estimates for each agent indicate that it believes the other agent wishes to move towards the center of the road,

that the level-2 inference problem is generally non-convex and the presence of multiple local minimizers (to which

However, when agent 2 begins to make an effective lane change, these estimates shift quickly.
The third-party inference of agent 2’s estimate of agent 1’s objective,

While agent 1’s estimate of agent 2’s objective,

Overall, our results indicate that level-1 inference is not always sufficient to understand agents’ behavior, particularly when their decisions are based upon mismatched estimates of one another’s objectives.
However, the proposed level-2 interaction model can effectively capture this nuance because it is strictly more expressive than the level-1 model.
As the level-2 model can generally admit multiple hypotheses to explain the observation data, it remains an exciting, open challenge to develop algorithms capable of reasoning about the likelihood of each hypothesis in the context of extended strategic interactions.

Conclusion

We introduce a level-2 inference framework to overcome limitations in existing inverse game-theoretic approaches, which typically assume that agents share complete knowledge of each other’s objectives.
Our theoretical and empirical analyses highlight the necessity of level-2 inference by identifying significant prediction errors in level-1 inference in settings where agents have heterogeneous estimates of one another’s objectives.
We develop an efficient, gradient-based algorithm to infer these misaligned estimates by
formulating misaligned-information, “level-2” dynamic games as differentiable mixed complementarity problems.
Through experiments on LQ games and a synthetic urban driving scenario, we showed that our method effectively identifies critical misalignments that simpler level-1 inference approaches fail to detect.

Future directions include exploring level-2 inference for strategic planning of interacting agents, extending the framework to more complex nonlinear stochastic games, and characterizing the observability of level-2 parameters. Ultimately, this would enable autonomous agents to manage information asymmetry and improve coordination.

\displaystyle L(\bar{\Theta}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq L(\check{\Theta})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\delta\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(41)

Note that

\displaystyle\delta\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}

\displaystyle=M(\check{\Theta})^{-1}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}

(42)

\displaystyle=M(\check{\Theta})^{-1}M(\Theta^{j*})\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}

\displaystyle=M(\check{\Theta})^{-1}(M(\check{\Theta})+M(\Theta^{j*})-M(\check{\Theta}))\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}

\displaystyle=-M(\check{\Theta})^{-1}\Delta^{j}\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}

and we have

\displaystyle L(\check{\Theta})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|M(\check{\Theta})^{-1}\Delta^{j}\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\max}(M(\Theta^{j*})-M(\check{\Theta}))}{\sigma_{\min}(M(\check{\Theta}))}\|\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}(\Theta^{j*})\|_{2}\right)^{2}

Next, we show the lower bound. We observe that the level-1 inference loss function can be simplified as

L(\bar{\Theta})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|E^{j}\left(M(\Theta^{j*})^{-1}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}-M(\bar{\Theta})^{-1}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(44)

Assuming
Y=M(\bar{\Theta})^{-1}
, and setting the gradient of the above loss over
Y
to be zero, we have

0=\sum_{j=1}^{N}M(\Theta^{j*})^{-1}-NY^{*}

(45)

Solving this equation with respect to
Y
, we can obtain an optimal
Y^{*}

Y^{*}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M(\Theta^{j*})^{-1}

(46)

and we have

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(M(\Theta^{j*})^{-1}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}-Y^{*}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}\right)=0

(47)

Observe
\check{M}=(Y^{*})^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle L(\bar{\Theta})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|E^{j}\left(\check{M}^{-1}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}-M(\Theta^{j*})^{-1}S\mathbf{x}_{\textrm{init}}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|E^{j}\left(\check{M}^{-1}(\check{M}+M(\Theta^{j*})-\check{M})\bar{\mathbf{z}}-\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{j}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|E^{j}\check{M}^{-1}\left(M(\Theta^{j*})-\check{M}\right)\bar{\mathbf{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\|E^{j}(M(\Theta^{j*})-\check{M})\bar{\mathbf{z}}(\Theta^{j*})\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\max}(\check{M})}\right)^{2}

∎
