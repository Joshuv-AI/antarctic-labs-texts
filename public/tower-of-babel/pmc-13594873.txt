Sex-dependent responses to posthatch protein-reduced feeding in broilers derived from low-protein-fed hens
Authors: Li Zhang, Yaqin Li, Yurou Pan, Yue Xuan, Keying Zhang, Xuemei Ding, Qiufeng Zeng, Shiping Bai, Jingbo Liu, Yan Liu, Jianping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the sex-dependent responses of broiler offspring derived from low-protein-fed hens to posthatch low-protein feeding, with emphasis on growth efficiency, carcass traits, amino acid metabolism, breast meat quality, and breast muscle transcriptomic profiles. A total of 312 broiler offspring were assigned to a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement with 2 posthatch dietary protein levels and 2 sexes and reared to 84 d. The low-protein diet represented a practical protein-reduced formulation in which Lys, Met, Thr, and Trp were supplemented, whereas Arg, Leu, Ile, and Val were not fully equalized with the normal-protein diet. Posthatch low-protein feeding reduced final body weight and increased feed-to-gain ratio. A significant posthatch dietary protein level × sex interaction was observed for average daily gain (P = 0.031), with the reduction occurring in males but not in females. Low-protein feeding decreased breast muscle yield (P = 0.005) and increased abdominal fat yield (P = 0.002). Serum triglyceride (P = 0.022), uric acid (P = 0.001), and several free amino acids, including Lys (P = 0.026), Thr (P < 0.001), Val (P = 0.009), Leu (P = 0.006), Ile (P = 0.030), Phe (P < 0.001), and Met (P = 0.016), were increased, whereas intestinal morphology was largely unaffected. In breast muscle, low-protein feeding increased Val (P = 0.005) and Pro (P = 0.016) concentrations and altered meat quality traits, including pH45min (P = 0.001), pH24h (P = 0.030), and b* (P < 0.001), with significant posthatch dietary protein level × sex interactions for L* (P = 0.006), a* (P = 0.003), and cooking loss (P < 0.001). Transcriptomic analysis showed sex-specific breast muscle responses, with females showing broader changes in signaling- and stress-related pathways, whereas males showed pathway changes more closely related to amino acid metabolism, protein turnover, and metabolic regulation. In conclusion, among broilers derived from low-protein-fed hens, the posthatch protein-reduced dietary strategy altered growth efficiency, carcass nutrient partitioning, amino acid profiles, breast meat quality, and breast muscle transcriptomic responses in a sex-dependent manner. These findings indicate that sex should be considered when evaluating responses to posthatch protein-reduced feeding in broilers derived from low-protein-fed hens.

Introduction

Dietary protein is one of the most expensive components of poultry feeds and is a major source of nitrogen (N) excretion. Therefore, reducing dietary crude protein (CP) has become an important strategy to improve feed cost efficiency and reduce N output in broiler production (Cho et al., 2024; Siegert et al., 2025). Previous studies indicate that moderate CP reduction can maintain growth performance when limiting amino acids (AA) are properly supplemented, while improving N utilization and reducing N excretion (Woyengo et al., 2023). However, responses to protein reduction are not determined by CP level alone. They also depend on AA balance, bird age, genotype, sex, and management conditions (Brink et al., 2022; Kop Bozbay et al., 2023; Asiamah et al., 2025). Therefore, the biological consequences of protein-reduced diets should be interpreted in relation to both the extent of CP reduction and the adequacy and balance of indispensable AA supply.

Maternal nutrition is also an important determinant of offspring development in poultry. In broiler breeders, changes in maternal energy or protein supply during rearing or laying can affect offspring growth, carcass traits, and metabolic status (Moraes et al., 2014, 2019). Our recent study showed that maternal low-protein (LP) feeding did not markedly impair overall offspring growth performance but induced sex-dependent changes in several physiological and tissue traits, including intestinal morphology, breast meat quality, and muscle fiber characteristics (Li et al., 2025). Because the offspring in that study were subsequently fed a standard diet, an unresolved question was whether broilers derived from LP-fed hens would also show sex-dependent responses when exposed to a protein-reduced diet after hatch. The present study was designed as a follow-up experiment to address this specific question.

Sex is a major biological factor in broiler production because males and females differ in growth potential, nutrient partitioning, carcass composition, and meat quality (Shim et al., 2012; Maynard et al., 2023). These physiological differences may also influence their responses to dietary protein reduction, as reduced-CP diets place greater emphasis on the adequacy and balance of amino acid supply and utilization (Hernández et al., 2012; Woyengo et al., 2023). Altered amino acid availability and metabolism can affect lean tissue deposition and muscle metabolic status, thereby contributing to changes in growth efficiency, carcass composition, and meat quality (Joo et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024c). Accordingly, evaluating phenotypic, metabolic, and molecular responses together may provide a more integrated view of how males and females adapt to posthatch protein reduction. However, it remains unclear whether broilers derived from LP-fed hens respond differently to posthatch dietary protein reduction according to sex. Clarifying this response is important for understanding the consequences of posthatch protein reduction within this specific maternal nutritional background.

Therefore, building on our previous maternal nutrition study and the established sex differences in broiler growth and nutrient utilization, the present experiment evaluated how male and female broilers derived from LP-fed hens respond to posthatch dietary protein reduction. We hypothesized that the posthatch protein-reduced dietary strategy would alter growth efficiency, nutrient partitioning, and breast muscle-related traits, and that the magnitude or pattern of these responses would differ between sexes. We further expected these phenotypic responses to be accompanied by changes in AA metabolism and breast muscle transcriptional profiles. Because offspring from normal-protein-fed hens were not included in the present experiment, maternal diet was not an experimental factor, and the study was not designed to assess maternal diet effects or maternal diet × posthatch diet interactions. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted specifically as responses to posthatch protein reduction in broilers derived from LP-fed hens.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No. 2023111007). The experiment was conducted in accordance with the Welfare and Ethical Guidelines for Laboratory Animals of Sichuan Agricultural University (Chengdu, Sichuan, China).

Birds and experimental design

Fertilized eggs used in the present study were obtained from a previous broiler breeder experiment involving maternal dietary protein manipulation, as described by Li et al. (2025). Briefly, the maternal LP diets contained 30 g/kg less CP than the corresponding maternal NP diets, with CP levels of 120 vs. 150 g/kg during 10 to 18 wk and 130 vs. 160 g/kg during 19 to 33 wk. Eggs were collected from LP-fed hens at 30 wk of age and incubated at 37.8 ± 0.1°C and 55 % relative humidity. Only offspring derived from LP-fed hens were used in the present study. Therefore, maternal diet was not included as an experimental factor and maternal diet effects or maternal diet × posthatch diet interactions could not be evaluated in the present experiment.

At hatch, chicks were vaccinated subcutaneously in the neck against Marek’s disease (HVT strain FC-126, ≥2,000 PFU/dose; Ceva Santé Animale, Libourne, France) and sexed by cloacal examination. A total of 312 one-day-old Tianfu broilers, including 156 males and 156 females, were selected within ±5 % of the mean body weight. The initial body weight of the selected chicks was 33.61 ± 0.40 g. Birds were assigned to a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement with posthatch dietary protein level and sex as the two experimental factors. The four treatments were ONP-male, ONP-female, OLP-male, and OLP-female. Each treatment comprised 6 replicate units with 13 birds per replicate. Each replicate consisted of 2 adjacent wire cages (100 × 45 × 60 cm, L × W × H) managed as a single experimental unit. The poultry house was preheated to 34–36°C for 24 h before chick placement. The ambient temperature was maintained at approximately 36°C during the first 3 d and was gradually reduced to 32°C by the end of wk 1, 30°C by the end of wk 2, 28°C by the end of wk 3, and 26°C by the end of wk 4. Thereafter, the temperature was maintained at 21–25°C. Relative humidity was maintained at 60 %–70 %. Continuous lighting was provided during the first 3 d, followed by a 23L:1D photoperiod thereafter.

The posthatch diets were formulated according to NY/T 3645-2020 recommendations for medium-growing yellow-feathered broilers. The NP diets were formulated to meet the recommended CP levels, whereas the LP diets contained 40 g/kg less CP. The LP diets were supplemented with crystalline Lys, Met, Thr, and Trp to meet their recommended levels, whereas Arg, Leu, Ile, and Val, were not fully equalized with the NP diets. Accordingly, the LP treatment represented a practical protein-reduced dietary formulation rather than a formulation in which all indispensable amino acids were fully equalized between protein levels. Growth performance

Growth performance was evaluated on a replicate-unit basis. All birds in each replicate were weighed at 1 and 84 d of age, and the mean body weight per bird within each replicate was used to determine initial body weight (IBW) and final body weight (FBW), respectively. Feed offered and feed residues were recorded for each replicate throughout the trial using a calibrated electronic scale (OHAUS Corporation, Shanghai, China). Average daily gain (ADG) was determined from the change in body weight over the experimental period divided by the corresponding number of days. Average daily feed intake (ADFI) was calculated from total feed consumption per replicate over the same period on a bird-day basis. Feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) was calculated as total feed intake divided by total body weight gain. Mortality was recorded daily, and performance data were corrected accordingly.

Carcass traits, organ indices, and sample collection

At 84 d of age, one broiler with a body weight close to the replicate mean was selected from each experimental unit. The selected bird was used to represent its replicate in the determination of carcass traits, organ indices, serum biochemical indices, tissue composition, meat quality, histomorphology, and free amino acid analyses. Following a 4-h feed withdrawal, with water available ad libitum, birds were individually identified and weighed, and then humanely euthanized and exsanguinated according to approved institutional procedures. Blood samples were collected during exsanguination into sterile serum tubes (BD Vacutainer, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 to 60 min. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C using a refrigerated centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Serum was separated, transferred into microcentrifuge tubes (Axygen Scientific, Union City, CA, USA), and stored at −20°C until analysis. After exsanguination, feathers, beak, and feet were removed, and carcass weight was recorded using a calibrated electronic balance (OHAUS Corporation, Shanghai, China). Semi-eviscerated weight was recorded after removal of the esophagus, crop, trachea, pancreas, and spleen. Eviscerated carcass weight was then recorded after removal of the remaining visceral organs, including the heart, liver, gizzard, and intestines. Breast muscles, including the pectoralis major and pectoralis minor, leg muscles, including the thigh and drumstick, and abdominal fat were dissected and weighed individually. Dressing percentage, semi-eviscerated yield, and eviscerated yield were calculated as carcass weight, semi-eviscerated weight, and eviscerated carcass weight relative to live body weight, respectively. Breast muscle yield, leg muscle yield, and abdominal fat yield were calculated relative to carcass weight. The liver and immune organs, including the spleen, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius, were excised and weighed immediately. Organ indices were calculated as organ weight relative to live body weight. The left pectoralis major was trimmed of visible connective tissue and stored at 4°C for subsequent meat quality analysis. A portion of the pectoralis major was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde solution (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for muscle fiber histomorphological analysis. Additional portions were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for nutrient composition, free amino acid, and transcriptomic analyses. Segments approximately 3 cm in length from the mid-duodenum and mid-jejunum were collected, gently flushed with physiological saline, and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde solution for intestinal histomorphological evaluation.

Meat quality

Breast muscle quality traits were evaluated using the left pectoralis major collected at slaughter. Muscle pH was measured at 45 min and 24 h postmortem using a portable penetration-type pH meter with a resolution of 0.01 pH units (Li-Chen Technology, Shanghai, China). For each sample, pH was recorded at three different locations, and the mean value was used. Meat color parameters, including lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*), were determined using a portable colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) on the freshly exposed muscle surface after blooming for 30 min at room temperature. Measurements were taken at three different locations and averaged. Drip loss was determined gravimetrically according to Gao et al. (2022) with minor modifications and expressed as the percentage of weight loss after refrigerated storage. For cooking loss determination, breast muscle samples were weighed, individually sealed in heat-resistant plastic bags, and heated in a thermostatically controlled water bath at 80°C until the internal temperature reached 70°C. The cooked samples were cooled to room temperature, gently blotted dry, and reweighed, and cooking loss was calculated as the percentage of weight loss relative to the initial sample weight. For shear force determination, cooked breast muscle samples were cut into uniform strips (approximately 1 × 1 × 3 cm) aligned parallel to the muscle fiber orientation. Shear force was measured perpendicular to the muscle fiber orientation using a texture analyzer (TA.XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK) equipped with a Warner–Bratzler shear blade. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the average value was used for analysis. Muscle fiber characteristics were evaluated according to the histological procedures described by Weng et al. (2022).

Serum biochemistry

Serum biochemical parameters were determined using commercial colorimetric assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The measured indices included alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), total protein (TP), uric acid (UA), and urea. All assay kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Serum samples stored at −20°C were thawed on ice and gently mixed. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (ELx800, BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) at the wavelengths specified for each kit. All measurements were performed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Free amino acid analysis in serum and breast muscle

Free amino acid (FAA) profiles were determined in serum and breast muscle samples. Serum samples stored at −20°C and breast muscle samples stored at −80°C were thawed on ice before preparation. For serum FAA analysis, 400 μL of serum was mixed with 800 μL of 10 % sulfosalicylic acid and vortexed thoroughly. The mixture was incubated at 4°C for 30 min to precipitate proteins and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before analysis. For breast muscle FAA analysis, approximately 0.2–0.3 g of freeze-dried breast muscle was mixed with 1 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl and homogenized with steel beads using a tissue homogenizer (XOYM-24DL, Sichuan Century Shunhe Technology Co., Ltd., Sichuan, China). The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 400 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 800 μL of 10 % sulfosalicylic acid, incubated at 4°C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Serum and breast muscle FAAs were quantified using an LA8080 High-Speed Amino Acid Analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Intestinal morphology

Morphological analysis of the small intestine was conducted using duodenal and jejunal samples collected at slaughter. Tissue segments were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared, and embedded in paraffin according to standard histological procedures. Paraffin blocks were sectioned perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the intestine at 4–6 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2155, Leica Biosystems GmbH, Germany). Sections were mounted onto glass slides, dried at room temperature, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Multiple sections were prepared from each intestinal segment. Histological images were captured using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at 40 ×, with the mucosal layer occupying the full field of view. Images were captured from randomly selected, well-oriented areas. Villus height and crypt depth were measured using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Several intact villus–crypt units were analyzed per image, and measurements from multiple fields were averaged for each sample. The villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was calculated.

Muscle fiber histomorphology

Breast muscle fiber morphometrics were quantified in the pectoralis major collected at slaughter. Muscle samples were trimmed to remove visible connective tissue, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, and processed for routine paraffin histology. After dehydration and paraffin embedding, tissue blocks were sectioned perpendicular to the muscle fiber axis (4–6 μm) using a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2155, Leica Biosystems GmbH, Germany). Sections were mounted on glass slides and stained with HE. Muscle fiber measurements were performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The quantified variables included muscle fiber cross-sectional area (CSA, μm²), fiber diameter (μm), and fiber area density (%). For each sample, measurements were obtained from multiple non-overlapping fields with well-oriented transverse fibers, and only intact fibers with clearly defined boundaries were included. Fiber area density was calculated as the ratio of total muscle fiber area to the analyzed field area and expressed as a percentage. Values were averaged across fields to obtain one value per bird for analysis.

RNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis

Transcriptome sequencing and preliminary bioinformatic analyses were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Three biological replicates of pectoralis major were selected from each treatment group, yielding 12 RNA-seq libraries. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA samples with A260/A280 1.8 to 2.2, an A260/A230 greater than 2.0, and RNA integrity number greater than 7.0 were used for library construction. Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, size selection, and PCR amplification. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using a paired-end 150 bp sequencing. Raw reads were filtered by removing adapter-containing, poly-N-containing, and low-quality reads, yielding clean reads. Q20, Q30, and GC content were calculated to assess sequencing quality. Clean reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome using HISAT2, and gene read counts were obtained for differential expression analysis. Gene expression abundance was normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) for descriptive analysis and visualization. Principal component analysis (PCA) and expressed-gene distribution analysis were performed to evaluate overall transcriptomic patterns among groups. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 in R based on raw read counts. Pairwise comparisons were conducted separately within each sex: OLP-F vs. ONP-F and OLP-M vs. ONP-M. Genes with a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 2 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs. KEGG-based Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted separately in females and males to identify coordinated pathway-level responses to posthatch dietary protein reduction.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships among growth performance, carcass traits, serum biochemical indices, serum and breast muscle free amino acid profiles, breast meat quality traits, and breast muscle fiber characteristics. This rank-based approach was selected to evaluate monotonic associations among these heterogeneous biological variables. Variables were selected based on their biological relevance to growth efficiency, nutrient partitioning, amino acid metabolism, and meat quality responses to posthatch dietary protein reduction. These variables comprised F/G, ADG, FBW, breast muscle yield, abdominal fat yield, serum TG, serum UA, selected serum free amino acids, selected breast muscle free amino acids, pH45min, pH24h, L*, a*, b*, drip loss, cooking loss, shear force, and fiber area density. Spearman correlation coefficients and corresponding P values were calculated using the Hmisc package in R. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. The correlation matrix was visualized as a lower-triangular heatmap using the pheatmap package in R. Significant correlations were indicated as *FDR < 0.05, **FDR < 0.01, and ***FDR < 0.001.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the general linear model procedure of SAS software (version 9.2, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA for the effects of posthatch dietary crude protein level (normal protein vs. low protein), sex (male vs. female), and their interaction. The statistical model was:yijk=μ+Ai+Tj+(A×T)ij+εijkwhere Yijk is the observed value of the dependent variable for the k-th replicate under the i th dietary protein level and the j-th sex; μ is the overall mean; Ai represents the fixed effect of dietary protein level; Tj is the fixed effect of sex; (A × T)ij is the interaction effect between dietary protein level and sex; and εijk is the random error term, assumed to be normally distributed with zero mean and constant variance.

Normality of residuals and homogeneity of variances were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s test, respectively. Least squares means were used for treatment comparisons. When a significant interaction effect was detected (P < 0.05), the four treatment groups were compared using Tukey’s test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05, and a tendency was defined as 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10.

Results

Growth performance

Discussion

Maternal nutrition can influence posthatch growth, metabolism, and tissue development in poultry through nutrient supply during egg formation and embryogenesis (Andrieux et al., 2022; Roura et al., 2025). Maternal low-protein nutrition can alter developmental and metabolic traits in chicken offspring (Rao et al., 2009). In our previous study, maternal LP feeding induced sex-dependent physiological and tissue phenotypes without markedly impairing overall growth performance (Li et al., 2025). Based on these findings, the present study was designed as a follow-up experiment to determine how broilers derived from LP-fed hens respond to posthatch dietary protein reduction, with emphasis on growth performance, amino acid metabolism, breast muscle traits, and meat quality. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as posthatch dietary protein responses within a maternal LP background. The present design therefore addresses responses to posthatch dietary protein reduction rather than maternal dietary effects or maternal diet × posthatch diet interactions. In the present study, posthatch LP feeding reduced FBW and increased F/G, whereas ADG showed a significant posthatch dietary protein level × sex interaction, with the reduction occurring in males but not in females. Similar reductions in weight gain and feed efficiency have been reported when amino acid supply or amino acid balance is insufficient to support optimal protein deposition (El-Far et al., 2024). Because the LP diet was supplemented with major limiting amino acids but was not fully amino acid-equivalent to the NP diet, the observed effects represent a whole-diet response to a practical protein-reduced formulation. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that growth responses to reduced-CP diets depend not only on the extent of protein reduction, but also on the adequacy and balance of indispensable amino acids (An et al., 2022).

The carcass results showed that, within the maternal LP background, posthatch LP feeding shifted carcass nutrient partitioning, as indicated by lower breast muscle yield and higher abdominal fat yield. This is consistent with previous studies showing that low-protein diets can reduce breast muscle development and alter carcass composition in broilers, especially when amino acid supply or amino acid balance is insufficient to support optimal lean tissue growth (Majdeddin et al., 2019; El-Far et al., 2024). Because breast muscle is a major site of amino acid deposition, an inadequate or imbalanced amino acid supply may limit muscle accretion and favor lipid retention (Wang et al., 2024a). In the present diets, Arg, Leu, Ile, and Val were not fully equalized between the NP and LP treatments. In particular, the lower dietary concentrations of Leu, Ile, and Val altered both BCAA supply and their relative balance with Lys and with one another, including the Leu:Lys, Val:Lys, Ile:Lys, Leu:Val, Leu:Ile, and Val:Ile ratios. Because BCAA balance can influence skeletal muscle amino acid metabolism and protein deposition, these differences may have contributed to the lower breast muscle yield and altered nutrient partitioning observed under the LP feeding condition (Zeitz et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024a). Thus, these carcass responses reflect the overall protein-reduced dietary formulation rather than the isolated effect of reduced dietary CP. The higher abdominal fat yield in females is also biologically plausible, given the well-established sex differences in nutrient partitioning and body composition in broilers (van der Waaij et al., 2011). Thus, posthatch LP feeding altered carcass nutrient partitioning, while sex independently influenced abdominal fat deposition.

In contrast to the evident changes in growth performance and carcass traits, immune organ indices were comparatively stable. Although posthatch LP feeding tended to reduce the spleen index, the other immune organ indices were not markedly affected, suggesting that productive traits and carcass composition were more responsive than lymphoid organ development. Similar findings have been reported in broilers, in which dietary interventions influenced carcass or muscle-related traits more clearly than selected organ indices (Sigolo et al., 2017). The higher spleen index in males may reflect sex-related physiological variation, as male and female broilers differ in growth, fat deposition, and immune-related responses under different nutritional conditions (Khatibjoo et al., 2011).

The increased serum free amino acid concentrations observed in the present study provide important information on postabsorptive amino acid metabolism, and are best interpreted together with the concurrent changes in growth performance and nitrogen-related metabolites. Posthatch LP feeding increased the serum concentrations of several amino acids, and this pattern, together with reduced FBW, increased F/G, the male-specific reduction in ADG, and elevated serum UA, suggests a shift in postabsorptive amino acid handling and nitrogen metabolism under the posthatch LP feeding condition. Previous studies indicate that the response of broilers to reduced-CP diets depends not only on total amino acid supply but also on the balance among indispensable amino acids and their postabsorptive utilization (Wang et al., 2024a; Woyengo et al., 2023). Similar changes in free amino acid profiles and nitrogen-related metabolites have also been reported in broilers fed reduced-protein diets (Elahi et al., 2020). The correlation analysis further supports this interpretation by showing that serum amino acid profiles were closely linked with growth efficiency and carcass nutrient partitioning. Thus, the elevation of circulating free amino acids appears to be part of a broader metabolic response involving reduced growth efficiency, altered nitrogen metabolism, and modified carcass composition, rather than an isolated biochemical change.

The sex effects on several serum amino acids indicate sex-related differences in amino acid metabolism under the present nutritional conditions. Male broilers showed higher concentrations of Thr, Met, His, Ala, and Gly, whereas Tyr showed a significant posthatch dietary protein level × sex interaction. These findings are consistent with previous reports that male and female broilers differ in their metabolic and growth responses to reduced-protein diets (Hernández et al., 2012). Together with the higher abdominal fat yield in females and the sex-dependent meat quality responses, these differences suggest distinct patterns of nutrient utilization and partitioning between males and females.

By contrast, intestinal morphology was relatively stable under the present experimental conditions. Villus height, crypt depth, and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio in the duodenum and jejunum were not significantly altered, suggesting that the changes in growth performance, carcass composition, and breast muscle traits were not primarily associated with marked structural alterations of the small intestine at 84 d of age. Previous studies have likewise reported variable effects of reduced-CP feeding on intestinal morphology. For example, moderate protein reduction supplemented with limiting amino acids improved duodenal and jejunal villus height in some studies (Shazali et al., 2019), whereas CP reduction negatively affected villus and crypt traits even when essential amino acid requirements were met in others (Kuritza et al., 2022). Segment-specific responses have also been reported, indicating that low-protein feeding does not necessarily induce a uniform morphological response throughout the small intestine (Naeem et al., 2024). Therefore, the present phenotypic changes appear to be more closely associated with postabsorptive metabolism and carcass nutrient partitioning than with major remodeling of small-intestinal villus architecture.

The breast meat quality results showed that posthatch LP feeding increased pH at 45 min and 24 h postmortem and altered b*, whereas L*, a*, and cooking loss showed significant posthatch dietary protein level × sex interactions in broilers derived from LP-fed hens. The higher pH at 45 min and 24 h postmortem, together with changes in color traits, suggest altered muscle metabolic status and postmortem biochemical processes that determine water distribution and visual appearance. In broiler meat, postmortem pH decline is closely related to muscle energy metabolism and to color and water-holding capacity (Bowker and Zhuang, 2013). Previous studies have reported variable effects of reduced-CP diets on breast meat quality, depending on the degree of CP reduction and the adequacy of amino acid supplementation. Balanced reduced- CP diets may have little or no detrimental effect on meat quality in some broiler systems (Benahmed et al., 2023), whereas other formulations may alter pH, color, water loss, or texture-related traits (Ma et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2018). These interactions indicate that the magnitude and direction of the meat-quality response differed between males and females. These changes occurred without significant effects on muscle fiber cross-sectional area, diameter, or fiber area density, suggesting that overt muscle fiber remodeling was not the primary driver. This is consistent with previous evidence showing that meat quality can be influenced by muscle biochemical status and postmortem metabolism even when histomorphological changes are limited (Marchewka et al., 2023). The interaction observed for breast muscle dry matter, together with the absence of a dietary effect on crude protein content, further supports an interpretation centered on muscle biochemical status, water-holding properties, and postmortem metabolism rather than muscle fiber morphology alone (Bowker and Zhuang, 2013). The breast muscle free amino acid profile provided additional metabolic context. Posthatch LP feeding increased Val and Pro concentrations and tended to increase Ile and UAA, indicating altered intramuscular amino acid metabolism. Previous studies have shown that dietary interventions can modify the amino acid profile of broiler breast muscle together with changes in meat quality or metabolic status (Wang et al., 2024b; Yu et al., 2021). Under the present conditions, the increased free amino acid pool in muscle, together with the lower breast muscle yield, suggests that posthatch LP feeding altered intramuscular amino acid metabolism and breast muscle accretion. These intramuscular responses should also be considered in the context of the differences in dietary BCAA supply and balance between the NP and LP formulations. This pattern supports the view that intramuscular amino acid profiles were linked with breast muscle development and meat quality responses under the present protein-reduced feeding condition.

The transcriptomic data provide molecular context for the phenotypic responses. Notably, females exhibited a larger number of differentially expressed genes than males, indicating a broader transcriptional response to posthatch LP feeding. This corresponded with the sex-dependent phenotypic differences observed for growth performance, fat deposition, circulating amino acid profile, and meat quality. Previous studies have similarly associated sex differences in the breast muscle transcriptome with divergent developmental and metabolic programs, including pathways related to extracellular matrix organization, focal adhesion, muscle structure, and metabolic regulation (Brothers et al., 2019; Bošković Cabrol et al., 2025). Pathway analysis further revealed distinct responses between sexes. In males, the enriched pathways were mainly related to amino acid metabolism, extracellular matrix interaction, and metabolic regulation, including glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, ECM-receptor interaction, and PPAR signaling. In females, the enriched pathways were broader and involved calcium signaling, MAPK signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, focal adhesion, arachidonic acid metabolism, and cell adhesion-related processes, consistent with broader signaling-, adhesion-, and stress-related transcriptional programs. Transcriptomic studies in chicken breast muscle have linked ECM-related, focal adhesion, calcium signaling, and metabolic pathways to muscle development, structural remodeling, and meat quality variation (San et al., 2021; Choi et al., 2025). The GSEA results highlighted representative sex-specific pathways. In females, mTOR signaling and Toll-like receptor signaling were positively enriched, indicating coordinated transcriptional changes in nutrient-sensing, adaptive signaling, and stress- or immune-related regulatory pathways. Given the central role of mTOR in broiler skeletal muscle protein synthesis and breast muscle growth, enrichment of this pathway may reflect an adaptive nutrient-sensing response of breast muscle to posthatch protein reduction (Wen et al., 2014). The enrichment of Toll-like receptor signaling is also consistent with immune- and stress-related signaling pathways in the female transcriptomic response (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2023). In males, the representative negatively enriched pathways were ribosome and proteasome, suggesting that posthatch LP feeding was associated with changes in protein synthesis-, degradation-, and proteostasis-related transcriptional programs in breast muscle. This interpretation is biologically plausible because protein deposition in broiler Pectoralis major depends on the balance between protein synthesis and degradation, and this balance is sensitive to nutritional conditions and growth environment (Achour et al., 2025). This transcriptomic pattern is consistent with the reduced growth performance and lower breast muscle yield observed in LP-fed broilers, supporting an association between protein turnover-related transcriptional programs and the male response to posthatch protein reduction (Zeitz et al., 2019; Nakashima and Ishida, 2020).

The correlation analysis indicated interconnected changes in growth efficiency, nutrient partitioning, amino acid profiles, and breast meat quality under posthatch LP feeding. The association of poorer growth efficiency with greater abdominal fat deposition and serum TG supports the interpretation that protein-reduced feeding altered nutrient partitioning, with reduced lean tissue accretion and increased lipid retention (Chrystal et al., 2020). The correlations involving serum and muscle free amino acids suggest that amino acid-related metabolic changes were linked with carcass composition and breast meat quality, connecting systemic and tissue-level responses (Watanabe et al., 2020). In the context of reduced growth performance and altered carcass traits, the elevation of free amino acids is more consistent with altered amino acid handling and nitrogen metabolism than with improved amino acid availability for tissue growth (El-far et al., 2024). The associations among pH, color, water loss, shear force, and metabolic traits indicate that meat quality variation was more closely related to muscle biochemical status and postmortem metabolism than to marked muscle fiber remodeling, which was limited in the present study (Mir et al., 2017).

Taken together, posthatch LP feeding altered growth performance, carcass nutrient partitioning, amino acid metabolism, breast meat quality, and breast muscle transcriptomic profiles in a sex-dependent manner. The phenotypic data, correlation analysis, and transcriptomic results collectively indicate that reduced growth efficiency, increased abdominal fat deposition, altered free amino acid profiles, and meat-quality changes were interconnected under the present feeding condition. These findings extend our previous maternal LP study by showing that broilers derived from LP-fed hens exhibit distinct responses to a subsequent posthatch protein-reduced feeding strategy. Because the present study focused on offspring derived from LP-fed hens, the results are best interpreted as posthatch dietary protein responses within this maternal nutritional background. In addition, although Lys, Met, Thr, and Trp were supplemented to their recommended levels, Arg, Leu, Ile, and Val were not fully equalized between the NP and LP diets. The observed responses therefore reflect the overall practical protein-reduced dietary formulation rather than an amino acid-equivalent reduction in CP. Given that RNA-seq was conducted with 3 biological replicates per group, the statistical power for gene-level inference was limited; accordingly, the transcriptomic findings are most appropriately interpreted in terms of overall response patterns and pathway enrichment. However, RNA-seq primarily reflects the pre-slaughter transcriptional state of muscle and does not directly capture the postmortem biochemical processes that determine meat quality. Moreover, because proteomic, metabolomic, and functional validation of relevant proteins or enzyme activities were not performed, the transcriptomic associations observed here should not be interpreted as direct causal mechanisms underlying the meat-quality differences. Therefore, the RNA-seq results are best regarded as supportive molecular context for the observed phenotypic responses rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, within a maternal low-protein background, posthatch low-protein feeding reduced growth efficiency, altered carcass nutrient partitioning, modified serum and breast muscle free amino acid profiles, and affected breast meat quality, with several responses differing between males and females. These responses were accompanied by sex-specific transcriptomic changes in breast muscle, involving pathways related to amino acid metabolism, nutrient sensing, extracellular matrix interaction, immune- and stress-related signaling, and protein turnover. Overall, the present findings indicate that broilers derived from LP-fed hens respond distinctly to a posthatch protein-reduced feeding strategy, and that the pattern of these responses differs between males and females. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex when evaluating responses to posthatch protein-reduced feeding in broilers derived from LP-fed hens.
