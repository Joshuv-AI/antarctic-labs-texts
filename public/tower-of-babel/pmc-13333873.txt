Latent profile analysis of physical activity and mental health among chinese high school adolescents
Authors: Siyu Zhang, Jingsong Liu, Jianan Gong, Shoujiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent mental health is an increasingly urgent public health issue, and physical activity is often promoted as a practical way to support psychological well-being. Yet the benefits of physical activity may not increase linearly, and average associations can mask meaningful differences between subgroups. Using questionnaire data from a nationally archived Chinese adolescent health database, we analyzed 13,494 students aged 14–18 years. Physical activity was assessed with the Physical Activity Questionnaire for Adolescents, and psychological symptoms were measured with the Symptom Checklist-90, including nine symptom domains and a total index. We evaluated the shape of the activity–symptom association across the full activity range and used latent profile analysis to identify subgroups defined by the joint distribution of activity and symptom burden. A four-profile model was supported by fit and classification indices, with AIC = 876,522, BIC = 892,341, and entropy = 0.89; the Lo–Mendell–Rubin test favored four over five profiles (p = 0.012), and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test was significant (p < 0.001). The profiles captured substantial heterogeneity, spanning a high-activity/low-symptom pattern, a low-activity/high-symptom pattern, and two intermediate profiles in which similar activity levels co-occurred with different levels of distress. At the population level, symptom burden was lowest at moderate activity, while both lower and higher activity levels were associated with higher symptom burden than the moderate group. These findings indicate that activity volume alone does not fully explain adolescent psychological distress and support profile-informed approaches that prioritize an attainable moderate-activity target and targeted support for higher-risk subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51176-9.Keywords: Adolescent mental health, Physical activity, Dose–response, Sport participation, Latent profilesSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Adolescent mental health issues have become one of the core challenges in global public health and education systems, with the burden of these disorders continuing to rise in recent years. These issues are characterized by earlier onset, higher comorbidities, and greater functional impairments1. Simultaneously, insufficient physical activity (PA) remains highly prevalent among adolescents globally, forming an intervention-amenable and scalable risk factor2. In this context, physical activity (PA) is seen as a low-cost, low-barrier, and potentially protective behavior that can be integrated into school and community environments. Its association with depression, anxiety, overall psychological distress, and subjective well-being has been repeatedly reported in numerous observational and intervention studies3,4.

However, the prevailing consensus has shifted from the simplistic “more activity is better” approach to a more nuanced framework. On one hand, average effects are evident; on the other hand, the relationship between PA and mental health is highly context-dependent and marked by individual heterogeneity. This association may be moderated by factors including activity dose, exercise intensity, exercise type, organizational involvement, gender, and baseline symptom levels5. Notably, several recent large-scale studies and reviews have proposed a potential nonlinear dose-response relationship, where mental health benefits peak at moderate doses of PA, with diminishing returns or even adverse effects observed at very low or very high doses. This provides a robust theoretical foundation and defensible evidence for your current findings regarding “U-shaped or inverted U-shaped” patterns6,7.

Research on the relationship between sports and adolescent mental health has developed in three distinct stages. The first stage marked the initiation of evidence and the establishment of associations, with a predominance of cross-sectional and early cohort studies focused on validating the statistical links between sports and mental health indicators such as depression, anxiety, and self-esteem, culminating in early integrative review frameworks3. An explosion of systematic reviews and causal discussions characterized the second stage. Research expanded significantly, with increasing differentiation between observational and intervention studies. This stage also marked the recognition that “correlation does not equate to causality,” while highlighting risks such as measurement error, confounding, and reverse causality, and emphasizing the concept of “evidence strength”4,8.

The third stage represented a paradigm shift in methodology and research questions, with the focus transitioning from “Is there an association?” to “Who benefits, at what dose, and in which exercise contexts?” During this period, three significant developments occurred: First, there was a rapid expansion of dose-response and nonlinear models, with evidence for inverted U-shaped or U-shaped relationships repeatedly observed in large datasets5,7; second, there was an increase in the use of objective measures and prospective cohorts, which reduced the biases associated with self-reports and enhanced temporal explanatory power6; research began to move beyond total PA volume to consider the type of exercise and the organizational context in which it occurs, with systematic summaries of differences between team sports, structured environments, and psychological outcomes, pointing to mechanisms such as social support and sense of belonging9,10.

This research direction evaluates the causal effects of PA on depression, anxiety, and overall mental health through school- or community-based exercise programs. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that exercise interventions yield small to moderate effects on adolescent depression and overall mental health, but with high heterogeneity. Adherence rates and program differences significantly influence outcomes11–13. The main strength of this approach lies in stronger causal inference. However, it is constrained by inconsistent intervention designs, short follow-up periods, and blinding challenges, resulting in moderate- to low-quality evidence.

The direction aims to address the question of “What is the optimal amount?” by emphasizing nonlinear rather than monotonic relationships. Representative studies have shown that mental health benefits are concentrated at moderate PA doses, with diminishing returns or even inverse associations at higher doses, which challenges traditional linear explanations5,7. Its strength lies in its ability to output actionable dosage windows directly. However, the results are highly sensitive to grouping strategies, model settings, extreme sample sizes, and residual confounding, necessitating additional cohort and experimental support for cross-sample replicability6.

The focus is on total PA volume in participation contexts, examining the differences in psychological outcomes between team sports, individual sports, and organized exercise environments. Research based on large samples suggests that team sports are more consistently associated with lower levels of psychological distress. In contrast, individual sports may show more complex or even negative associations with certain indicators. This discrepancy is often explained by differences in social support, sense of belonging, identity, and stress structures14. Simultaneously, qualitative systematic reviews emphasize the critical role of “setting factors,” such as coaching environment, peer relationships, and a sense of safety, in mediating the psychological benefits of PA15. The main strength of this direction lies in its closer alignment with practical intervention. However, it is limited by inconsistent classification of types, greater selection bias, and the intertwining of PA participation with family resources and academic pressures9,10. The issue of causality and reverse causality remains a core controversy. Proponents argue that intervention studies and prospective cohorts provide directional evidence. At the same time, critics highlight the persistent risk of residual confounding and the influence of psychological states on PA participation, suggesting that “association” cannot be simplistically equated with “protective effect”4,8.

The most significant gap currently lies in the insufficient causal identification and long-term follow-up evidence. Future research should combine high-quality cohort and experimental designs, employing multiple-wave tracking, cross-lagged models, or quasi-experimental strategies, while incorporating more comprehensive measurements of key confounders to more clearly distinguish between “PA affecting mental health” and “mental health affecting PA”6,8. The replicability of dosage windows and type combinations remains to be validated. Nonlinear findings are sensitive to modeling strategies, and future research should conduct preregistered analyses across different regions and measurement tools, incorporating key covariates such as training load, sleep, academic pressure, and competitive stress, to verify the robustness of the dose-response relationship4,16. More detailed mechanistic evidence is required to clarify contextual mechanisms. Organized exercise settings, coaching atmosphere, peer relationships, and sense of belonging may be critical mediators in transforming “exercise participation” into “psychological benefits.” Future research should integrate psychological and environmental processes through longitudinal mediation models, mixed-methods designs, and ecological momentary assessments10,15.

Although a substantial body of research has linked physical activity to adolescent mental health, an important limitation of the existing literature is that it has tended to treat this association as broadly uniform across individuals. Most prior studies have relied on variable-centered approaches, which are useful for estimating overall relationships but less well suited to capturing the heterogeneity that may exist within adolescent populations. This issue is especially relevant in the context of high school students, whose experiences of physical activity and mental health are likely to vary in meaningful ways across subgroups. If such heterogeneity is overlooked, the resulting evidence may offer only a partial account of the physical activity–mental health link and provide limited guidance for more targeted intervention. Against this background, the present study adopts a person-centered perspective to examine whether distinct latent profiles can be identified in the association between physical activity and mental health among Chinese high school students. By doing so, the study aims not only to extend current understanding of this relationship beyond average effects but also to provide a more refined empirical basis for identifying groups that may differ in their psychological vulnerability and behavioral patterns.

Methods

Data collection

The present study used data from the Adolescent Health Database (DYH) hosted by the National Population Health Data Center (PHDA). The dataset corresponds to the 2024–2025 adolescent health data collection cycle, with fieldwork conducted from September 2024 to September 2025, and annual updates (most recent revision: 1 September 2025). The sampling frame covers multiple provincial-level regions in China, including Shandong, Tianjin, Jiangxi, Hubei, Hainan, Chongqing, and Xinjiang, and was designed to support multi-regional surveillance through an expanded sampling network. According to the dataset description, it includes a large base sample from Shandong and additional samples from the expanded regions. For the current analysis, eligible participants were high school students aged 14–18 years who were able to complete the questionnaire and required assessments; individuals with missing core questionnaire/fitness-test data, or with serious chronic disease, motor impairment, or other conditions likely to bias physical performance or psychological measurement materially were excluded17.

PAQ scale and SCL-90 scale

Physical activity was measured using the Physical Activity Questionnaire for Adolescents (PAQ-A), a self-administered 7-day recall instrument derived from the PAQ-C and designed to capture habitual moderate-to-vigorous physical activity during the school year18. The PAQ-A yields a composite score ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater physical activity19. In addition to the standard weekly activity items, the questionnaire includes a global item assessing overall activity frequency in the past week, with response anchors progressing from “almost all free time spent in non-physical activities” to “very often engaging in physical activity (≥ 7 times/week).” Evidence supports the psychometric performance of the Chinese PAQ-A in adolescent samples; for example, a recent validation study reported acceptable internal consistency and construct adequacy, supporting its use for population-based research in Chinese adolescents20. To evaluate the reliability and validity of the Physical Activity Questionnaire (PAQ), we conducted an exploratory factor analysis to assess construct adequacy (KMO = 0.868; Bartlett’s test of sphericity p < 0.001) and calculated Cronbach’s alpha to examine internal consistency (α = 0.89).

Mental health outcomes were assessed using the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90; revised Chinese version), a self-administered instrument designed to screen a broad spectrum of psychological symptoms experienced over the past week. The scale comprises 90 items covering nine symptom dimensions—somatization, obsessive–compulsive symptoms, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism—and yields a Global Severity Index (GSI) that summarizes overall distress (higher mean scores indicate greater symptom burden). Prior psychometric evaluations in Chinese samples have supported the scale’s reliability and construct validity, while acknowledging ongoing debates regarding its factor structure and optimal use across populations21–23.

To assess measurement quality in the present dataset, we evaluated construct validity via exploratory factor analysis and internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha. The adequacy of the correlation matrix for factor analysis was supported (KMO = 0.948, p < 0.001), and internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91), indicating that the SCL-90 demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity for use in the current sample.

Statistical analysis

Before analysis, the dataset was cleaned and quality checked. Records were screened for eligibility and completeness, and cases with missing key measures (PAQ-A or SCL-90) were excluded. We further examined value ranges and internal consistency to identify implausible entries and, where applicable, removed duplicate records. Descriptive diagnostics were then used to verify the distributions of all study variables before conducting inferential analyses.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and Python 3.9. Descriptive statistics and independent samples t-tests were computed for all variables. (BH-corrected). Latent profile analysis (LPA) identified subgroups; optimal class solutions were determined by Bayesian information criterion (BIC), Akaike information criterion (AIC), entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted LRT (LMR-LRT), and bootstrap LRT (BLRT). Bootstrap 10,000 resamples provided robust confidence intervals24,25.

As shown in Table 3, to move beyond average associations and capture potential heterogeneity in the co-occurrence of physical activity (PAQ) and psychological symptoms (SCL-90), we conducted latent profile analysis (LPA) and compared solutions with increasing numbers of profiles. Model selection was guided by information criteria (AIC, BIC), classification quality (entropy), and likelihood-based tests (LMR-LRT and BLRT), while also considering parsimony and substantive interpretability. The four-profile solution provided the most defensible balance between model fit and complexity: it achieved the lowest AIC/BIC among the candidate models, maintained high classification precision (entropy = 0.89), and demonstrated a statistically meaningful improvement over the three-profile model (LMR-LRT p = 0.012; BLRT p < 0.001). Figure 1. visualizes the four-profile solution, which reveals clear heterogeneity in how physical activity co-occurred with psychological symptoms. The Healthy Active group (n = 3,318) showed relatively higher PA alongside uniformly low SCL-90 scores, whereas the High-Risk group (n = 2,723) clustered at low PA with consistently elevated symptoms, forming the most pronounced risk-gradient pattern. Two additional profiles did not fit a simple linear ordering: the Resilient profile (n = 4,817) spanned moderate-to-high PA while maintaining low-to-moderate symptom levels, and the Compensation profile (n = 2,633) displayed comparatively low symptoms despite only low-to-moderate PA. Taken together, these patterns indicate that PA volume alone does not uniquely predict symptom burden—especially in the low-to-mid PA range, where profiles overlap in PA but diverge in symptoms. This motivates the subsequent analyses, which quantify between-profile differences and formally test the shape of the PA–symptom association, including potential nonlinearity.

Four PA–mental health profiles.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted to identify heterogeneous subgroups in adolescents’ physical activity and mental health. The model included 12 observed indicators across two domains. Physical activity indicators comprised PAQ total score, frequency of low-, moderate-, and vigorous-intensity activity, total weekly exercise duration, and activity-type diversity. Mental health indicators included the SCL-90 total score and symptom dimensions: somatization, anxiety, depression, hostility, and interpersonal sensitivity. Together, these indicators were used to capture joint variation in activity patterns and psychological symptoms.

PAQ was entered as a standardized continuous variable to improve comparability across indicators. As a sensitivity check, an alternative model using quartile-based PAQ categories was also estimated, but this yielded lower classification accuracy than the continuous specification (entropy = 0.82 vs. 0.89). The continuous-score model was therefore retained.

Model selection was based on both statistical fit and substantive interpretability. The four-profile solution showed the best overall performance, with lower BIC and AIC values than the three- and five-profile models, high classification quality (entropy = 0.89), and significant LMR-LRT (p = 0.012) and BLRT (p < 0.001) results. The four profiles were also readily interpretable: a Deficiency profile marked by low physical activity and high symptoms, a Resilient profile with moderate activity and moderate symptoms, an Optimal profile characterized by high activity and low symptoms, and an Elite profile marked by very high activity but only moderate symptom levels. Taken together, the four-profile solution provided the most convincing balance between statistical adequacy and substantive meaning.

Given the central role of latent profile analysis in this study, the final profile solution was determined on both statistical and substantive grounds. The four-profile model was retained because it provided the most appropriate balance between fit, parsimony, class size, and interpretability. In addition to showing favorable fit indices, the model yielded profiles that were sufficiently distinct, adequately sized, and conceptually meaningful. Lower-profile solutions appeared to mask important heterogeneity, whereas more complex solutions offered limited additional insight and produced smaller, less stable classes. The four-profile solution was therefore considered the most appropriate representation of the underlying heterogeneity in the physical activity–mental health patterns of the sample.

Discussion

The four-profile solution identified in the present study is broadly consistent with earlier person-centered research. Brown et al., for example, also reported a four-profile solution in adolescents and found meaningful differences in depressive symptoms across profiles. Although their indicators were not identical to ours, the overall implication is similar: physical activity and mental health do not cluster uniformly but instead form distinct subgroup patterns. This parallel is important because it suggests that the heterogeneity observed in the present study is unlikely to be an artifact of model fitting alone. Rather, it supports the view that adolescent physical activity is linked to mental health in qualitatively different ways across subgroups. In this sense, the four-profile solution retained here is not only statistically acceptable but also substantively plausible and consistent with the broader person-centered literature26.

A plausible interpretation of the present four-profile solution is that it reflects meaningful heterogeneity in how physical activity and psychological functioning cluster during adolescence, rather than a single uniform association across the whole sample. This interpretation is consistent with earlier person-centered research in adolescent populations. Brown et al., for example, identified four movement-behavior profiles and found clear differences in depressive symptoms across profiles, with the most favorable profile showing the lowest symptom levels. Similarly, Sánchez-Oliva et al. reported four profiles of physical activity and sedentary behavior and showed that more active profiles were associated with better positive affect and health-related quality of life27.

Beyond model fit, the four-profile solution was retained because it provided the clearest and most meaningful account of heterogeneity in the relationship between physical activity and mental health. It was the only solution that clearly separated a resilient group with low activity and low symptoms from an elite group with very high activity and elevated symptoms, a distinction that is important both conceptually and practically. All four profiles were also of adequate size, which supports the stability of the solution. Most importantly, this model revealed a pattern that simpler solutions did not capture clearly: better mental health was associated with moderate rather than maximal activity levels. Taken together, the four-profile solution was retained not only because it fit the data well but also because it offered the most interpretable representation of subgroup differences in this sample.

Evidence increasingly suggests that the mental health benefits of adolescent physical activity are not strictly “more is better” but are often maximized at moderate intensity, yielding the most consistent reductions in depressive symptoms, with benefits attenuating outside an “effective window”28. Converging meta-regression evidence similarly highlights moderate-intensity prescriptions as a pragmatic regimen associated with stronger symptom improvement in youth29. These findings provide a direct rationale to discuss endurance-oriented activity as a plausible “high-yield” pathway for mental health gain, and they offer a defensible interpretive anchor when your results suggest a non-monotonic (U-shaped/inverted-U) pattern rather than a linear gradient. Importantly, endurance activity is also reflected upstream in cardiorespiratory fitness, and large-scale longitudinal evidence shows that lower fitness predicts higher subsequent risk of clinically diagnosed mental disorders in youth30, consistent with broader quantitative reviews linking higher physical fitness to more favorable youth mental-health profiles31.

The U-shaped pattern may partly reflect differences in the context of physical activity. At higher levels, activity may not simply represent healthy or recreational exercise but also involve competitive sport or intensive training, in which performance pressure, fatigue, and injury-related stress may become more salient. In this sense, the relatively higher symptom levels observed among highly active adolescents may not contradict the broader benefits of physical activity. Still, they may instead suggest that its mental health implications depend, in part, on the setting in which it occurs32.

The study’s limitation is the narrow sampling frame, which comprised high school students from selected provinces in China. While this cohort provides valuable insights into adolescent behavior in those regions, it may not represent the broader youth population. Adolescent mental health and physical activity patterns can vary markedly across different locales and developmental stages. For instance, a national survey in China found significant regional disparities in youth mental well-being33. Such geographic variability underscores that findings from a limited provincial sample may not readily extend to all Chinese adolescents or to youth in other cultural contexts. Moreover, our focus on upper-middle school ages (approximately 14–18 years) limits generalizability to other age groups and educational stages. Developmental differences mean that relationships between physical activity and mental health observed in mid-to-late adolescence might differ in early adolescence or in college-aged young adults. Caution is therefore warranted in extrapolating these results beyond the sampled demographic. Future studies should aim for more diverse and representative samples, including students from varied regions, rural and urban settings, and different grade levels, to enhance external validity. Broadening the sample in this way would help determine whether the protective associations observed here hold across China’s heterogeneous populations and beyond.

An additional limitation concerns the use of self-report measures for both physical activity and psychological symptoms. Although questionnaire-based assessment is practical in large adolescent samples, it is also susceptible to recall error and socially desirable responding, particularly when the behavior being reported is viewed positively. Prior research has shown that self-reported physical activity can be biased upward by social desirability and may not align closely with objective indicators, suggesting that some adolescents may overstate their activity levels when responding to survey items. At the same time, self-reported psychosocial measures can be shaped by similar response tendencies, which raises the possibility that part of the association observed in the present study reflects shared method variance rather than the underlying relationship alone. In this context, the profile structure identified here should be interpreted with some caution, as misreporting may have influenced both the classification of activity levels and the estimated link between physical activity and symptom burden. Future studies would benefit from combining self-report data with more objective measures of physical activity, such as accelerometry, and from incorporating multi-informant assessments of mental health to strengthen measurement validity. This study relied on self-report instruments, notably the Physical Activity Questionnaire for Adolescents (PAQ-A) for activity and the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) for mental health symptoms, which introduce well-known measurement challenges. Self-reported physical activity is susceptible to recall bias and social desirability effects. Evidence suggests that adolescents often overestimate their moderate-to-vigorous exercise when using recall questionnaires, as compared to objective monitors34. Social desirability can further skew reports; in one validation study, participants with higher tendencies to “look good” consistently over-reported their physical activity levels on week-long recalls35.

Such biases likely influenced our PAQ-A data, potentially inflating activity estimates. Indeed, studies comparing self-reports with accelerometry have found poor concordance; for example, only ~ 4% of youth met recommended activity levels by accelerometer versus ~ 44% by questionnaire in one sample36. highlighting the limitations of subjective tools. Similarly, the SCL-90 captures psychological distress via self-rating of symptoms, which can be affected by respondent mood, honesty, and understanding of the items. The use of a single informant means we lacked corroborating perspectives that might identify issues the student underreports. Furthermore, although the Chinese versions of these instruments have documented reliability and validity, some psychometric concerns remain. Recent evaluations of the PAQ-A in Chinese adolescents, for instance, indicate that its factor structure may differ from the original and that certain items perform weakly, necessitating refinement.

For the SCL-90, a large-scale study in China found generally acceptable reliability and convergent validity but poor discriminant validity between some symptom dimensions37. This suggests overlapping constructs that could blur the interpretation of subscale scores in our context. Additionally, the SCL-90 emphasizes psychopathological symptoms and omits positive aspects of mental health, giving an incomplete picture of students’ psychological state. Taken together, these measurement issues may introduce bias and constrict the scope of our findings. Future work should consider employing objective activity monitors alongside self-reports to more accurately quantify exercise behavior38. Likewise, incorporating multi-informant assessments or broader mental health scales would provide a more robust and nuanced assessment of adolescents’ mental status.

The findings should also be interpreted with the recognition that several potentially important confounding factors were not included in the present analysis. Sleep, academic stress, screen time, and socioeconomic conditions are all likely to shape both physical activity and mental health during adolescence. This is particularly relevant in the Chinese context, where academic stress has been shown to relate to anxiety and depression both directly and indirectly through physical activity and sleep. Recent review evidence likewise suggests that greater screen time is associated with poorer mental health in adolescents, while socioeconomic conditions can influence health-related behaviors and access to physical activity opportunities more broadly. In light of these factors, the association observed in this study may partly reflect a wider network of behavioral and contextual influences that were not explicitly modeled. Future research would benefit from incorporating these variables into more comprehensive analytic frameworks so that the unique contribution of physical activity can be estimated more precisely39.

Applying such models in future studies would clarify how much of students’ mental health is shaped by individual activity levels relative to the school environment or community context. Network analysis offers another novel lens, treating mental health not as a single outcome but as a web of interrelated symptoms and behaviors. A recent network analysis of Chinese youth identified how symptoms like anxiety and depression clustered and linked with external factors, revealing “bridge” symptoms that connected different problem domains. Similarly, one could construct a network model that includes various SCL-90 symptom dimensions, physical activity, academic stress, and other relevant factors to visualize and analyze their interconnections. Such an approach might uncover, for example, that low energy or sleep problems mediate between physical inactivity and depression, insights that traditional regressions might overlook. Embracing these advanced analytical methods will yield a more comprehensive understanding of the student experience, capturing the multifaceted and interdependent nature of physical and mental health40,41.

From a cognitive science viewpoint, physical activity can be linked to enhancements in brain function and executive control, which in turn relate to better mental health. Interventional studies have shown that regular exercise, especially cognitively engaging or high-intensity activities, can improve executive functions like attention and cognitive flexibility in youth42. These improvements may bolster psychological resilience and academic performance, suggesting a pathway whereby exercise benefits mental well-being via cognitive enhancements. Developmental psychology perspectives remind us that adolescents’ motivational and psychosocial needs differ from those of adults; thus, frameworks such as self-determination theory or stage-of-life-appropriate behavior change models can inform how we engage students in sustained physical activity. Incorporating family and peer contexts is also crucial, as developmental research emphasizes the social milieu of adolescence. Meanwhile, insights from behavioral medicine and physiology can deepen the mechanistic understanding of the PA–mental health link. For example, physical activity is known to modulate stress physiology by regulating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and lowering chronic cortisol levels associated with distress43,44.

The present findings are best understood as descriptive of association rather than evidence of causation. Because physical activity and psychological symptoms were assessed at the same time, the data do not establish which comes first. It is therefore not possible to conclude that physical activity shaped symptom levels in one direction only. The reverse interpretation is equally plausible: adolescents with greater psychological distress may be less likely to remain physically active, while those with fewer symptoms may find it easier to sustain regular activity. This is an inherent limitation of cross-sectional work, and it sets a clear boundary on how the results should be interpreted45.

The generalizability of the present findings also requires caution. Although the sample size was large, participants were drawn from a specific region of China, and recent national evidence suggests that adolescent mental well-being is not evenly distributed across the country. Regional differences in educational pressure, family resources, and local social environments may shape both mental health and opportunities for physical activity. For this reason, the profile structure identified in this study should not be assumed to apply uniformly to adolescents in other parts of China or in other cultural settings. Replication in more geographically diverse and nationally representative samples is needed before stronger claims about broader applicability can be made.

In conclusion, while our cross-sectional findings highlight a link between physical activity and mental health in Chinese high school students, they also point to several limitations that temper strong causal claims. By broadening sampling strategies, improving measurement rigor, and adopting longitudinal, multi-level, and interdisciplinary research designs, future studies can address these shortcomings. These efforts will yield more definitive evidence on how, why, and for whom physical activity confers mental health benefits. Such knowledge is essential for informing policies and interventions. The “Healthy China 2030” initiative aimed at fostering the well-being of adolescents in school settings and beyond. Continued research along these lines will enhance our ability to generalize findings across populations, accurately gauge youth experiences, and ultimately develop tailored, science-based programs that improve both the physical and mental health of young people.
