The relationship between self-reported adherence and virological outcomes among adolescents on antiretroviral therapy in Ghana
Authors: Doris Hagan, Victor Luckboy Dzramado, Cynthia Yaba Kumah, Charles Domfeh, Joana Edem Koto, Samuel Antwi, Thomas Hormenu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescents living with HIV demonstrate suboptimal adherence to antiretroviral therapy compared with adults. This significantly impedes progress toward achieving the UNAIDS 95—95—95 targets. Understanding the relationship between self-reported adherence and virological outcomes among this population is essential for guiding treatment monitoring strategies and intervention development in resource-limited settings.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 515 adolescents aged 10 to 19 years living with HIV and receiving ART at seven health facilities in the Ashanti Region of Ghana between August and November 2023. Self-reported adherence was measured using the adapted AIDS Clinical Trials Group 4-day recall questionnaire combined with items from the LifeWindows Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills adherence questionnaire. Viral load data were extracted from medical records. Spearman's rank correlation was used to examine the relationship between adherence rates and viral load outcomes.ResultsThe mean adherence rate was 92% (SD = 18.93). Males comprised 49.5% of participants and females 50.5%, with a mean age of 15.44 years (SD = 2.62). Most participants (67.9%) lived with parents, and 98% had low to middle economic status. The mean number of missed doses showed an ascending pattern across the four-day recall period: 0.056 (Day 1), 0.075 (Day 2), 0.077 (Day 3), and 0.093 (Day 4). Spearman's rank correlation revealed a weak negative correlation between adherence rate and viral load (rs = -0.052, p = 0.235, 95% CI: -0.141 to 0.037), which was not statistically significant. Among participants, 323 (62.7%) achieved viral suppression, while 192 (37.3%) did not.ConclusionsDespite high self-reported adherence levels, the weak and non-significant correlation with virological outcomes raises important validity concerns regarding self-reported adherence as a proxy measure for viral suppression among adolescents in this setting. The temporal mismatch between four-day adherence recall and longer-term viral load measurements may partly explain this discordance. Findings underscore the need for objective adherence measures, routine viral load monitoring, and adolescent-specific interventions that address psychosocial barriers affecting both adherence behavior and its biological impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27647-z.

Background

The global HIV epidemic continues to pose significant public health challenges, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where the disease burden remains disproportionately high. As of 2024, an estimated 40.8 million people were living with HIV worldwide, with 1.3 million new infections and 630,000 AIDS-related deaths occurring that year [19, 23]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for approximately two-thirds of the global HIV burden, with adolescents representing a particularly vulnerable population [19]. In 2024, over 210,000 adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 years acquired HIV globally, averaging 570 new infections daily [19]. This persistent incidence among adolescents highlights critical gaps in prevention, testing, and treatment adherence within this demographic.

Antiretroviral therapy has transformed HIV from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition. With proper adherence, people living with HIV can achieve viral suppression, maintain immune function, and prevent HIV transmission to others [10]. The UNAIDS 95—95—95 targets established for 2025 aim for 95% of all people living with HIV to know their status, 95% of those diagnosed to receive sustained ART, and 95% of those on ART to achieve viral suppression [18]. These ambitious targets recognize that optimal adherence to ART is fundamental to achieving viral suppression and preventing disease progression, opportunistic infections, and onward transmission [11].

Adolescents living with HIV face unique developmental, psychosocial, and structural challenges that often compromise their adherence to ART. Research consistently shows that adolescents have lower adherence rates and poorer virological outcomes compared with adults [10, 13]. A systematic review and meta-analysis across sub-Saharan Africa reported an average ART adherence rate of only 65% among adolescents [11]. Similarly, another meta-analysis study in Ghana reported a pooled adherence rate of 70% (95% CI: 58—81), with adolescents and young people showing even lower rates of approximately 66% (95% CI: 46—84) [3]. These figures fall substantially short of the levels necessary to achieve and maintain viral suppression.

Several factors contribute to suboptimal adherence among adolescents. Developmental characteristics during adolescence, including incomplete cognitive maturation, risk-taking behaviors, and limited future orientation, can interfere with consistent medication-taking [17]. Psychosocial challenges such as stigma, fear of disclosure, depression, and lack of social support further compound adherence difficulties [10]. Structural barriers including poverty, food insecurity, transportation challenges, and healthcare system factors also play significant roles [13]. Moreover, adolescents often have less control over their daily routines and medication schedules compared with adults, particularly when living with caregivers or in institutional settings.

Accurate measurement of adherence is essential for clinical management, research, and program evaluation. Self-reported adherence measures are widely used in resource-limited settings due to their low cost, ease of administration, and non-invasive nature [6]. However, self-report methods are susceptible to social desirability bias, recall inaccuracy, and overestimation of actual adherence behavior [24]. Adolescents may be particularly prone to these biases due to developmental factors, fear of disappointing caregivers or healthcare providers, and cognitive challenges with accurate recall. Understanding the validity of self-reported adherence in predicting virological outcomes is therefore critical for interpreting adherence data and guiding clinical decisions.

Viral load testing serves as the gold standard biomarker for treatment success and adherence assessment. According to current treatment guidelines, viral suppression is typically defined as HIV RNA levels below 50 to 200 copies per milliliter, depending on assay sensitivity [22]. Consistent ART adherence is theoretically expected to result in viral suppression through sustained inhibition of HIV replication. However, the relationship between self-reported adherence and viral load outcomes may be influenced by numerous factors, including measurement validity, timing discordance between adherence assessment and viral load testing, individual pharmacokinetic variability, drug resistance, and other biological factors [4].

In Ghana, where this study was conducted, progress has been made in expanding ART coverage and improving HIV care services. However, challenges persist in achieving optimal adherence and viral suppression among adolescents. The Ashanti Region, Ghana's most populous region, hosts multiple ART service delivery points serving significant numbers of adolescents living with HIV. Understanding the relationship between self-reported adherence and virological outcomes in this context can inform both clinical practice and policy decisions regarding adherence monitoring and support interventions.

This study aimed to examine the correlation between self-reported ART adherence and viral load outcomes among adolescents living with HIV in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. Specifically, the study sought to determine adherence levels using validated self-report instruments, examine the pattern of missed doses over a four-day recall period, and assess the strength and direction of the relationship between adherence rates and virological suppression. The findings contribute to ongoing discussions about the validity and utility of self-reported adherence measures in adolescent HIV care settings and provide insights relevant to achieving the UNAIDS 95—95—95 targets in resource-limited contexts.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between August and November 2023 among adolescents living with HIV who were receiving antiretroviral therapy in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. The Ashanti Region is the most populous region in Ghana, with an estimated population of over 5 million people. Seven health facilities providing ART services were purposively selected based on their patient volume and geographic distribution across the region. These facilities included Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH), the regional referral center, as well as district hospitals and health centers that serve as primary ART delivery points for adolescent populations.

Study population

The target population comprised adolescents aged 10 to 19 years who were living with HIV and receiving ART at the selected health facilities. Inclusion criteria required participants to be within the specified age range, enrolled in ART care for at least six months, and willing to provide informed consent or assent with parental/guardian consent. Adolescents who were critically ill, had cognitive impairments affecting their ability to respond to the questionnaire, or who had not yet been disclosed to regarding their HIV status were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling

The sample size was calculated using the formula for correlation studies, with assumptions of a minimum detectable correlation coefficient of 0.15, alpha level of 0.05, and power of 80%. This calculation, accounting for an estimated 10% non-response rate, yielded a required sample size of 515 participants. A stratified random sampling approach was employed, with facilities stratified by level of care (tertiary, district, health center) and participants randomly selected from eligible adolescents attending each facility during the study period. Sampling was proportional to the adolescent HIV patient load at each facility.

Data collection instruments

Self-reported adherence was assessed using an adapted questionnaire that combined validated items from two established instruments: the AIDS Clinical Trials Group (ACTG) 4-day adherence questionnaire [5] and the LifeWindows Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills (IMB) ART adherence questionnaire [8, 16]. The ACTG questionnaire measures adherence over a four-day recall period by assessing the number of prescribed doses taken versus missed for each antiretroviral medication. The LifeWindows IMB questionnaire assesses factors influencing adherence based on the Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills model.

The combined questionnaire was adapted through a systematic process involving literature review, expert consultation with HIV clinicians and researchers, and pilot testing with 30 adolescents not included in the main study. Adaptations ensured cultural appropriateness, age-appropriate language, and alignment with local ART service delivery contexts while maintaining the psychometric integrity of the source instruments. The complete adapted questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Demographic and clinical data including age, sex, education level, relationship status, living arrangements, religion, and economic status were collected using a structured questionnaire. Viral load data were extracted from participants' medical records. Viral load measurements had been conducted within the six months preceding data collection as part of routine clinical care at the participating facilities.

Data collection procedures

Trained research assistants with experience in adolescent HIV research conducted face-to-face interviews with participants in private settings at the health facilities. Participants were assured of confidentiality, and responses were recorded electronically using tablet computers with secure data entry systems. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 45 min. Viral load results were abstracted from medical records by the research team with permission from facility authorities and participants.

Measurement of variables

Adherence rate

Self-reported adherence was calculated as a percentage based on the number of doses taken versus prescribed over the four-day recall period. Participants reported the number of doses missed on each of the four days prior to the interview. The adherence rate was calculated as: [(Total prescribed doses—Total missed doses)/Total prescribed doses] × 100. Adherence rates were classified as optimal (≥ 95%) or suboptimal (< 95%) based on established thresholds.

Viral load

Viral load was measured in copies per milliliter using standard laboratory assays at certified testing facilities. For analysis, viral load was categorized as suppressed (viral load < 1000 copies/mL) or not suppressed (viral load ≥ 1000 copies/mL), consistent with WHO guidelines [22]. This threshold reflects the level at which viral replication poses significant risk for disease progression and transmission.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were calculated for demographic characteristics, adherence patterns, and viral load outcomes. The relationship between adherence rate and viral load was examined using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rs). This non-parametric statistical test was selected because both variables were categorized into ordinal classifications (optimal vs. suboptimal adherence; suppressed vs. unsuppressed viral load), and the relationship between variables was assessed for monotonic trends rather than assuming linear associations. Spearman's correlation is particularly appropriate when dealing with ordinal data or when underlying assumptions of normality may not be met, as is common in self-reported adherence data [7].

Fisher's r—to—z transformation was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals for the correlation coefficient, with standard error estimation based on the Fieller, Hartley, and Pearson formula. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The strength of correlations was interpreted as follows: 0.00 to 0.19 (very weak), 0.20 to 0.39 (weak), 0.40 to 0.59 (moderate), 0.60 to 0.79 (strong), and 0.80 to 1.00 (very strong).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable national and international guidelines and regulations for research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from three institutional review boards: the University of Cape Coast Institutional Review Board (approval number: ET/HPT/20/0006, approved on June 15, 2023), the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (approval number: GHS/ASH/AD-1, Vol. 6, approved on July 20, 2023), and the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital Committee on Human Research, Publication and Ethics (approval number: KATH IRB/AP/093/23, approved on August 5, 2023).

All study procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Ghana Health Service Research Ethics Guidelines and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) International Ethical Guidelines for Health-related Research Involving Humans. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians of all participants aged 10 to 17 years, with assent from the adolescents themselves. Participants aged 18 to 19 years provided their own written informed consent. All participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences to their medical care. Data confidentiality and participant anonymity were maintained throughout the study in accordance with applicable data protection regulations.

Viral load outcomes

Analysis of viral load data extracted from medical records showed that 323 participants (62.7%) achieved viral suppression (viral load < 1000 copies/mL), while 192 participants (37.3%) had unsuppressed viral loads (≥ 1000 copies/mL). This proportion of viral suppression falls short of the UNAIDS third 95 target, which stipulates that 95% of those receiving ART should achieve viral suppression.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between self-reported ART adherence and virological outcomes among adolescents living with HIV in Ghana's Ashanti Region. The findings revealed high self-reported adherence rates (mean = 92%) but a weak and non-significant correlation with viral load suppression. These results raise important questions about the validity of self-reported adherence as a proxy measure for viral suppression in adolescent populations and highlight the complexity of achieving optimal treatment outcomes in this vulnerable group.

Adherence levels among adolescents

The observed mean adherence rate of 92% in this study substantially exceeds both regional and national averages previously reported for adolescents in sub-Saharan Africa and Ghana specifically. While encouraging, this finding must be interpreted cautiously given the reliance on self-report methodology. The reported adherence level approaches, but does not quite reach, the 95% threshold historically considered necessary for optimal viral suppression with older protease inhibitor-based regimens [15].

It is important to acknowledge that the 95% adherence threshold was established during an era when first-line ART regimens consisted primarily of protease inhibitors with lower genetic barriers to resistance and less forgiving pharmacokinetic profiles [1]. Modern first-line regimens, particularly those based on integrase strand transfer inhibitors such as dolutegravir, have demonstrated sustained viral suppression at lower adherence levels, often between 80 and 90% [12, 20]. Studies have shown that adherence to INSTI-based regimens as low as 69% to 75% can achieve viral suppression in 90% of treated populations [14]. However, the present study did not document specific ART regimen compositions for participants, making it difficult to determine whether the 95% threshold remains appropriate for this population. This represents a limitation that future studies should address by collecting detailed medication data.

The ascending pattern of missed doses observed across the four-day recall period warrants attention. Participants reported progressively more missed doses on days more distant from the interview date (Day 4: 0.093 vs. Day 1: 0.056). This pattern could indicate recall bias, with participants having clearer memory of more recent medication-taking behavior. Alternatively, it may reflect "white coat adherence," a phenomenon where patients improve their medication-taking in anticipation of clinical encounters or research interviews [2]. A third possibility is that this pattern represents genuine variability in day-to-day adherence influenced by routine disruptions, weekend schedules, or other contextual factors.

Weak correlation between adherence and viral load

The central finding of this study, a weak and non-significant correlation between self-reported adherence and viral load (rs = −0.052, p = 0.235), contrasts sharply with the well-established biological relationship between consistent medication intake and viral suppression. According to HIV treatment guidelines and pharmacological principles, sustained ART inhibits HIV viral replication, and adequate adherence is expected to result in viral suppression [22]. The discordance observed in this study requires careful interpretation.

Several plausible explanations exist for the weak relationship observed. First, and perhaps most importantly, is the temporal mismatch between the adherence measure and viral load assessment. Self-reported adherence covered only the four days preceding the interview, providing a brief snapshot of recent medication-taking behavior. In contrast, viral load reflects the cumulative effect of adherence over weeks to months, as viral load levels integrate adherence patterns across much longer time horizons [4]. A four-day adherence assessment is unlikely to meaningfully predict viral load results that reflect sustained adherence behavior over extended periods. This temporal discordance represents a fundamental limitation of using short-term self-report measures to predict long-term biological outcomes and may substantially explain the weak correlation observed.

Second, measurement validity concerns related to self-reported adherence may have contributed to the weak correlation. Self-reported measures are highly susceptible to social desirability bias, particularly among adolescents who may wish to avoid disappointing caregivers, healthcare providers, or researchers [6]. Adolescents in this study may have over-reported their adherence, leading to inflated adherence estimates that do not accurately reflect true medication-taking behavior. This social desirability bias could mask the true relationship between adherence and viral outcomes. Zhou et al. [24] demonstrated that discrepancies between self-reported and electronically monitored adherence are particularly pronounced in adolescent populations.

Third, individual variability in response to ART may influence the adherence-viral load relationship. Factors such as pre-existing drug resistance mutations, pharmacokinetic variability, absorption differences, drug-drug interactions, and individual immunological responses can all affect treatment efficacy independent of adherence levels [4]. Some adolescents may achieve viral suppression despite lower adherence due to favorable pharmacokinetics or drug potency, while others may experience viral rebound despite good adherence due to resistant viral strains or poor drug absorption.

Fourth, patterns of adherence may matter as much as overall adherence rates. Strategic timing of doses relative to viral replication cycles, treatment interruptions versus consistent low-level non-adherence, and the pharmacological forgiveness of specific drug regimens all influence virological outcomes in complex ways not captured by aggregate adherence percentages [21]. Adolescents may exhibit intermittent adherence patterns with periods of excellent adherence alternating with treatment interruptions, resulting in acceptable overall adherence rates but insufficient sustained drug levels for viral suppression.

Viral suppression outcomes

The finding that 62.7% of participants achieved viral suppression, while 37.3% had unsuppressed viral loads, falls substantially short of the UNAIDS 95—95—95 target. This gap is concerning and highlights the challenges of achieving optimal treatment outcomes among adolescents even in the context of reported high adherence. The discordance between high self-reported adherence (92%) and suboptimal viral suppression (62.7%) further reinforces concerns about the validity of self-report measures and suggests that other factors beyond medication-taking behavior are affecting virological outcomes.

Research from other sub-Saharan African settings has documented similar challenges. Gill et al. [9] found that adolescents in Kenya struggled with viral suppression despite reported ART adherence, attributing difficulties to psychosocial challenges, stigma, and treatment regimen complexity. Haas et al. [10] demonstrated that mental health conditions, including depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders, were associated with higher rates of viral non-suppression among adolescents and young adults in South Africa, even among those with good self-reported adherence.

Implications for the UNAIDS 95—95—95 targets

The findings of this study have important implications for achieving the UNAIDS third 95 target, which aims for 95% of all people receiving ART to achieve viral suppression. The weak correlation between self-reported adherence and viral outcomes suggests that monitoring adherence through self-report alone may be insufficient for identifying adolescents at risk for treatment failure. While the observed 92% adherence rate is encouraging, the substantially lower viral suppression rate (62.7%) indicates that good self-reported adherence does not guarantee virological success in all cases.

This disconnect highlights the critical importance of routine viral load monitoring as the definitive measure of treatment success. Clinical programs should not rely solely on adherence self-reports for treatment monitoring but must implement regular viral load testing to identify adolescents with virological failure who require enhanced adherence support, regimen optimization, or resistance testing. The weak predictive value of self-reported adherence for viral outcomes in this study suggests that viral load testing cannot be replaced or delayed based on favorable adherence reports.

Methodological considerations

The choice of Spearman's rank correlation for this analysis was appropriate given that both variables (adherence rate and viral load) were ultimately categorized into ordinal classifications for clinical interpretation. Spearman's correlation is particularly suitable for ordinal data and makes no assumptions about the linearity of the relationship, instead assessing monotonic trends [7]. This non-parametric approach is robust to outliers and does not require normally distributed variables, making it well-suited to self-reported adherence data which often exhibits non-normal distributions.

However, dichotomization of continuous variables (adherence percentage and actual viral load copies/mL) into binary categories (optimal/suboptimal and suppressed/unsuppressed) does result in loss of statistical information and reduced power to detect relationships. Future studies could benefit from examining these variables in their continuous forms using complementary analytical approaches such as logistic regression, which would allow for adjustment of potential confounding factors including age, sex, education level, economic status, time since ART initiation, and ART regimen type. Multivariate analyses could help clarify whether the weak correlation observed persists after accounting for these demographic and clinical factors.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis could also provide valuable insights by examining the discriminative ability of continuous adherence scores to predict viral suppression. Such analysis would quantify how well self-reported adherence distinguishes between suppressed and unsuppressed individuals through metrics including area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value at various adherence cut-off points. An AUC approaching 0.5 would confirm poor discriminative value of self-reported adherence, while a higher AUC with identification of optimal threshold values could guide clinical decision-making regarding when to intensify monitoring or intervention.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits understanding of adherence patterns over time. Longitudinal studies would better capture the dynamic nature of adolescent adherence and its relationship with viral load trajectories.

Second, the temporal mismatch between four-day adherence recall and viral load measurements (taken within the preceding six months) represents a fundamental limitation. Viral load integrates adherence behavior over weeks to months, while the adherence measure captured only recent days. This temporal discordance likely contributes substantially to the weak correlation observed and highlights the need for adherence measurement approaches that better align with the biological timeframe relevant to viral suppression.

Third, reliance on self-reported adherence introduces social desirability bias and recall inaccuracy. More objective adherence measures, such as pharmacy refill data, pill counts, or electronic monitoring devices, would provide more accurate adherence estimates. However, these approaches have their own limitations and may not be feasible in resource-limited settings.

Fourth, the study did not collect data on specific ART regimens participants were receiving, limiting the ability to assess whether observed adherence-viral load relationships vary by medication class or to determine the appropriateness of the 95% adherence threshold for the regimens in use. Contemporary regimens based on integrase inhibitors may achieve viral suppression at lower adherence levels, making the 95% benchmark potentially outdated for this population.

Fifth, no multivariate or adjusted analyses were conducted. The absence of regression modeling means that potential confounding factors were not statistically controlled. Demographic variables, clinical factors, and adherence determinants may modify the adherence-viral load relationship in ways not captured by bivariate correlation alone.

Sixth, the study did not assess for drug resistance mutations, which could explain viral non-suppression in some adherent individuals. Resistance testing would have provided important context for interpreting discordance between adherence and viral outcomes.

Intensive adherence support with problem-solving approaches, treatment interruption management, and resistance testing should be considered before changing regimens.

Conclusions

This study found that despite high self-reported ART adherence rates among adolescents living with HIV in Ghana's Ashanti Region, there was only a weak and non-significant correlation with virological suppression. These findings raise critical concerns about the validity of self-reported adherence as a proxy measure for viral suppression in this population and highlight the complexity of factors influencing treatment outcomes among adolescents.

The temporal mismatch between the four-day adherence assessment and longer-term viral load measurements represents a fundamental limitation that may substantially explain the weak relationship observed. Additionally, social desirability bias inherent in self-report methods, individual variability in treatment response, and unmeasured factors such as drug resistance and adherence patterns likely contribute to the discordance between reported adherence and virological outcomes.

These findings underscore the indispensable role of routine viral load monitoring as the gold standard for assessing treatment success. Self-reported adherence measures, while useful for identifying potential adherence challenges and guiding counseling, should not replace biological outcome monitoring. Achieving the UNAIDS 95—95—95 targets among adolescent populations will require comprehensive approaches that combine accurate viral load monitoring, valid adherence assessment methods, and multi-faceted interventions addressing the unique developmental, psychosocial, and structural challenges affecting this vulnerable population.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs, objective adherence measures, multivariate analyses, and regimen-specific evaluations to advance understanding of the adherence-viral load relationship among adolescents. Clinical programs must prioritize viral load testing infrastructure, enhance adherence support services, and adopt holistic, adolescent-centered care models to improve both adherence behaviors and their biological impact on treatment outcomes.
