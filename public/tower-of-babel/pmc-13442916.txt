Delayed Recognition of Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura Following Platelet Transfusion: A Clinical Pitfall
Authors: Abdulrahman Al‐Dawoudi, Mujahed Dalain, Daniil Varlamov, Shaghaf Alhallak, Dāvis Freimanis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare, life‐threatening thrombotic microangiopathy caused by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, usually due to autoantibody‐mediated inhibition. Its presentation is heterogeneous and often lacks the classic pentad, leading to diagnostic delays. Neurological symptoms may predominate, masking the underlying hematologic emergency. In our case, a 37‐year‐old previously healthy woman presented with acute headache, speech disturbance, and altered mental status. Despite normal neuroimaging, laboratory tests revealed severe thrombocytopenia and normocytic anemia. Lack of response to erythrocyte and platelet transfusions prompted further evaluation, revealing microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with elevated lactate dehydrogenase, undetectable haptoglobin, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, reticulocytosis, and normal coagulation parameters. A PLASMIC score of 6/7 indicated a high probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. Treatment with high‐dose corticosteroids was initiated immediately, followed by plasma exchange and rituximab. ADAMTS13 activity confirmed severe deficiency at 0.3%, supporting the diagnosis of acquired TTP in the clinical context. The patient achieved rapid remission and complete neurological recovery. This case highlights the importance of early recognition of microangiopathic hemolysis and prompt treatment without waiting for confirmatory ADAMTS13 results to reduce morbidity and mortality.Keywords: ADAMTS13, neurological manifestations, plasma exchange, thrombotic microangiopathy, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

Introduction

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare, life‐threatening thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by severe thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and organ ischemia caused by widespread platelet‐rich microthrombi. The acquired form of TTP is most commonly mediated by autoantibodies against ADAMTS13, a von Willebrand factor–cleaving protease, leading to the accumulation of ultra‐large von Willebrand factor multimers and subsequent microvascular thrombosis [1]. TTP is a clinical emergency with high mortality if treatment is delayed [2].

Clinical presentation is often heterogeneous and incomplete, and neurological manifestations may predominate despite normal neuroimaging [3, 4]. Untreated TTP carries mortality exceeding 90%, whereas early plasma exchange dramatically improves survival [5]. Clinical prediction tools, such as the PLASMIC score, have been developed to assist in early risk stratification and decision‐making [6]. Current guidelines recommend initiating treatment based on clinical suspicion without awaiting ADAMTS13 confirmation [7, 8].

This case emphasizes early recognition, bedside risk stratification, and immediate therapy prior to laboratory confirmation in undifferentiated neurological presentations.

Case Presentation

Presentation

A 37‐year‐old previously healthy woman presented with sudden headache, speech disturbance, and altered mental status. On arrival, she had limited verbal contact and could not clearly explain her symptoms. She had no significant past medical history. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed severe thrombocytopenia and normocytic anemia, and she was admitted with a working diagnosis of anemia and thrombocytopenia. She received erythrocyte and platelet transfusions due to severe cytopenias. No clinical or laboratory improvement followed transfusion.

Investigations and Differential Diagnosis

A neurology consultation was obtained due to the patient's confusion and speech disturbance. Neurological examination revealed impaired orientation and reduced verbal responsiveness, without focal motor deficits, cranial nerve abnormalities, or signs of meningeal irritation. The Glasgow Coma Scale score was 13 (E4 V3 M6), indicating spontaneous eye opening, inappropriate verbal responses, and preserved motor command following. Computed tomography of the brain with angiography showed no evidence of acute ischemia, intracranial hemorrhage, or large‐vessel occlusion. In the absence of focal neurological findings and normal neuroimaging, the altered mental status was considered unlikely to be due to a primary structural neurological disorder and was interpreted as potentially secondary to a systemic condition. Further neurological investigations were deferred due to clinical instability.

On re‐evaluation, she was confused and jaundiced. Laboratory findings demonstrated microangiopathic hemolysis, including elevated lactate dehydrogenase of 934 U/L (reference interval, 100–205 U/L), undetectable haptoglobin of < 0.03 g/L (reference interval, 0.4–1.6 g/L), indirect hyperbilirubinemia with indirect bilirubin of 39 μmol/L (reference interval, 0–16 μmol/L), and reticulocytosis of 16.88% (reference interval, 0.5%–2.5%). Key early laboratory findings included hemoglobin of 67 g/L (reference interval, 120–160 g/L), platelet count of 13 × 109/L (reference interval, 150–400 × 109/L), creatinine of 100 μmol/L (reference interval, 49–90 μmol/L), and coagulation parameters within normal limits.

Given the combination of severe thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, neurological impairment, and normal coagulation studies, a thrombotic microangiopathy was strongly suspected. A PLASMIC score was calculated and indicated a high probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (6/7 points). Based on this high clinical suspicion, high‐dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g) was administered immediately, and urgent hematology and nephrology consultations were requested. Blood samples for ADAMTS13 activity testing were obtained, although results were not available overnight.

Therapeutic plasma exchange with fresh frozen plasma was initiated the following day. ADAMTS13 activity subsequently returned severely reduced at 0.3%, consistent with immune‐mediated thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. Secondary causes of thrombotic microangiopathy were investigated. Complement levels were within normal limits, antineutrophil cytoplasmic and antiphospholipid antibodies were negative, and rheumatologic evaluation did not identify evidence of a systemic autoimmune disorder. The absence of a lifelong history of thrombocytopenia or hemolytic anemia, together with recovery of ADAMTS13 activity following immunosuppressive therapy, supported the diagnosis of acquired rather than congenital TTP. Outcome and Follow‐Up

Daily plasma exchange and corticosteroid therapy were continued, and rituximab was added to the treatment regimen. The patient demonstrated rapid clinical improvement, with complete resolution of neurological symptoms and progressive normalization of laboratory parameters. Platelet counts increased from a nadir of 13 × 109/L to 173 × 109/L by day 4 and exceeded 450 × 109/L at discharge. Hemoglobin levels improved from 67 g/L on presentation to > 100 g/L, with normalization of lactate dehydrogenase and bilirubin levels, indicating resolution of hemolysis. Renal function recovered fully, with serum creatinine returning to baseline values.

ADAMTS13 activity, which was 0.3% at diagnosis, increased to 51.8% by day 7 and 61.1% prior to discharge, confirming effective treatment of immune‐mediated thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, performed after clinical stabilization, revealed no acute ischemic or structural abnormalities. The patient completed five plasma exchange sessions and was treated with rituximab in addition to corticosteroids, and was discharged in stable condition on a tapering course of oral corticosteroids. At follow‐up, she remained asymptomatic, with normalized blood counts, recovery of ADAMTS13 activity, and no residual neurological deficits.

Discussion

This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of acquired TTP when neurological manifestations predominate. The patient presented with predominant neuropsychiatric manifestations, including acute confusion and speech disturbance, in the absence of focal neurological deficits or abnormalities on initial neuroimaging. Such presentations delay recognition, as symptoms often fluctuate and may not correlate with neuroimaging.

The pivotal diagnostic moment was recognition of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia as the mechanism of anemia rather than hemorrhage. Failure to respond to transfusion prompted reassessment and confirmed ongoing hemolysis, evidenced by markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase, undetectable haptoglobin, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and reticulocytosis.

The initial administration of platelet transfusion prior to recognition of TTP represents an important learning point. In patients with TTP, platelet transfusion is generally avoided in the absence of life‐threatening bleeding, in line with established guideline‐based management, due to concern for exacerbation of microvascular thrombosis [7]. This strategy aligns with contemporary guidelines recommending that treatment in suspected TTP be initiated on clinical probability alone, without awaiting confirmatory ADAMTS13 testing.

The PLASMIC score proved instrumental in guiding early management. A score of 6/7 indicated a high likelihood of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and justified prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy and plasma exchange prior to laboratory confirmation. This approach aligns with current recommendations emphasizing that treatment decisions in suspected TTP should be driven by clinical probability rather than delayed awaiting confirmatory testing, which is often unavailable in the acute setting [8].

The diagnosis of acquired TTP was supported by severely reduced ADAMTS13 activity, exclusion of secondary causes of thrombotic microangiopathy, absence of a lifelong history of cytopenias, and recovery of ADAMTS13 activity following immunosuppressive therapy. The rapid and sustained response to plasma exchange, corticosteroids, and rituximab further supports an immune‐mediated mechanism [9]. Early use of rituximab may have contributed to durable remission and normalization of laboratory parameters.

Close coordination across emergency medicine, neurology, hematology, and nephrology was essential to achieving a favorable outcome. Awareness of TTP in patients with unexplained cytopenias and neurological symptoms remains essential. The reporting of this study conforms to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines [10].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates how acquired TTP may present with predominant neurological symptoms, leading to diagnostic delay. Early recognition of microangiopathic hemolysis, application of bedside risk stratification tools, and prompt initiation of plasma exchange and immunosuppressive therapy prior to laboratory confirmation were critical to achieving a favorable outcome. Clinicians should maintain high suspicion for TTP in patients with neurological symptoms and cytopenias.
