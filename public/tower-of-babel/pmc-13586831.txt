Pathogen surveillance in cave-dwelling phlebotomine sand flies from Chiang Rai, northern Thailand, with the first detection of Hepatocystis
Authors: Sakone Sunantaraporn, Pathamet Khositharattanakool, Kanyarat Kraivichian, Pranyu Leemingsawat, Rungfar Boonserm, Puckavadee Somwang, Padet Siriyasatien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sand flies are important vectors of several pathogens of medical and veterinary significance, including Leishmania spp., Trypanosoma spp., Bartonella spp., and various arboviruses. Cave ecosystems provide suitable habitats for sand flies and often harbor large bat populations that serve as reservoirs for diverse microorganisms, potentially facilitating pathogen circulation within cave environments. The present study aimed to investigate the presence of sand fly-borne pathogens in cave-dwelling sand flies from three caves in Chiang Rai Province, northern Thailand. A total of 616 sand fly specimens were collected, morphologically identified, and pooled into 95 pools for nucleic acid extraction. Molecular screening was performed to detect RNA viruses (Phlebovirus, Orbivirus, and members of the family Rhabdoviridae), blood parasites (Leishmania spp., Trypanosoma spp., and haemosporidian parasites), and Bartonella spp. bacteria. Positive samples were subsequently confirmed by sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. One pool of Phlebotomus shadenae tested positive for Rhabdoviridae and clustered within the Vesiculovirus. PCR screening also detected Leishmania martiniquensis (2 pools), Trypanosoma spp. (5 pools), and Hepatocystis sp. (2 pools), while Bartonella DNA was not detected. Importantly, this study provides the first molecular detection of a species of Hepatocystis in cave-dwelling sand flies from Thailand. The findings expand current knowledge of pathogen diversity in cave ecosystems and provide further insight into the range of pathogens molecularly detected in sand flies.

Introduction

Sand flies are tiny hematophagous insects of significant medical and veterinary importance due to their role in transmitting a range of emerging and re-emerging pathogens (Cecílio et al., 2022; Jancarova et al., 2023). Various sand fly species have been found to harbor pathogens such as Leishmania spp., Trypanosoma spp., Bartonella bacilliformis, and viruses within the genera Phlebovirus, Vesiculovirus, and Orbivirus, posing health risks to both humans and animals (Cecílio et al., 2022; Jancarova et al., 2023). Increasing numbers of autochthonous leishmaniasis cases have been reported in both southern and northern Thailand, caused by two Leishmania species of the subgenus Mundinia: Leishmania martiniquensis (Pothirat et al., 2014; Chiewchanvit et al., 2015) and Leishmania orientalis (Jariyapan et al., 2018; Anugulruengkitt et al., 2022). Investigations in endemic areas have detected DNA of Leishmania spp. in several sand fly species, including Phlebotomus stantoni (Sriwongpan et al., 2021), Grassomyia indica (Preativatanyou et al., 2023), and several species of the genus Sergentomyia (Chusri et al., 2014; Srisuton et al., 2019; Sriwongpan et al., 2021; Phumee et al., 2024), suggesting their potential role as vectors.

Several sand fly-borne pathogens have been reported in Thailand (Soe et al., 2026). Orbiviruses and viruses belonging to the family Rhabdoviridae have been detected in sand flies from the southern part of the country (Phumee et al., 2021a, 2021b). Additionally, Trypanosoma parasites of anuran trypanosomes and Trypanosoma noyesi within the Trypanosoma cruzi clade have been identified in sand flies collected from both endemic and non-endemic areas of leishmaniasis in southern and northern Thailand (Srisuton et al., 2019; Khositharattanakool et al., 2024; Sunantaraporn et al., 2024). Bat-associated Bartonella spp. have also been detected in several cave-dwelling sand fly species (Sunantaraporn et al., 2024). Meanwhile, in northern Thailand, multiple cases of leishmaniasis caused by L. martiniquensis and/or L. orientalis have been documented across several provinces, including Nan (Jariyapan et al., 2018), Lamphun, Chiang Mai (Chiewchanvit et al., 2015), Lampang (Promrangsee et al., 2024; Sunantaraporn et al., 2026a) and particularly Chiang Rai (Sriwongpan et al., 2021; Srivarasat et al., 2022; Sunantaraporn et al., 2026a), suggesting increasing recognition of the disease in this region.

Many sand fly species inhabit cave ecosystems where they frequently coexist with bats, which serve as important vertebrate hosts and reservoirs for diverse microorganisms (Soomro et al., 2025). Bats (Order Chiroptera) are recognized as natural reservoirs for several pathogens of public health relevance, including viruses, bacteria, and protozoan parasites (Mühldorfer, 2013; Han et al., 2015; Lutz et al., 2016). Several bat-associated pathogens, such as Bartonella spp., Trypanosoma spp., and haemosporidian parasites including Plasmodium spp., Nycteria spp., Polychromophilus spp., and Hepatocystis spp., have been reported in bat populations worldwide (Stuckey et al., 2017; Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Riana et al., 2022; Ceballos-Pérez et al., 2024). The ecological overlap between bats and sand flies may facilitate the circulation and maintenance of these pathogens within cave environments. Therefore, investigating pathogen diversity in cave-dwelling sand flies is important for understanding potential transmission dynamics and the role of these insects in the ecology of bat-associated pathogens. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the diversity of sand fly-borne pathogens in cave-dwelling phlebotomine sand flies from Chiang Rai Province, northern Thailand.

Materials and methods

Sand fly collection and morphological identification

Sand flies were collected from three limestone caves located in Chiang Rai Province, northern Thailand, including Phra Cave (19°55′3.1″N, 99°47′19.8″E), Pha Tong Cave (20°05′31″N, 99°54′12″E), and Mae Khrua Cave (20°05′13.9″N, 99°54′9.8″E) (Fig. 1). The capture sites were selected based on their proximity to tourist attractions and were inhabited by numerous bats. The flies were captured using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) light traps equipped with a 25 W ultraviolet light bulb. Five traps were installed both outside and inside each cave at a height of approximately 50 cm above the ground and operated continuously from 18:00 to 6:00 h for three consecutive nights during each sampling period (2–4 July 2025 and 22–24 August 2025). The same trapping protocol was applied consistently across all caves during both sampling periods. Sand flies collected in the bags were placed in a Petri dish, anesthetized in an ice box for 30 min, and then sorted from other insects. The head and genitalia of female sand flies were cut off in a sterilized 0.9% normal saline under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and mounted on slides with a drop of Marc Andre solution, after the specimens were clarified and immediately identified based on cibarium and spermathecae according to the morphological keys by Lewis (1978), Phumee et al. (2017) and Depaquit et al. (2019). The remaining thorax and abdomen of the sand flies were pooled in groups of 1–15 individuals by species in sterile 1.5-ml tubes containing an RNA stabilization solution for subsequent nucleic acid extraction. Sand flies were sorted according to sex; however, separate pooling by sex was not performed. Nucleic acid extraction

Nucleic acids (DNA/RNA) were extracted from each sand fly pool following digestion in 200 μl of lysis buffer, followed by the addition of 20 μl each of proteinase K and carrier RNA solution. The specimens were then ground using a sterile plastic pestle, gently mixed, and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min. Nucleic acid extraction was subsequently performed using the GENTi32-viral DNA/RNA Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions with the Genti™ 32 automated nucleic acid extraction instrument, which employs a magnetic bead-based system (GeneAll, Seoul, South Korea). The extracted nucleic acids were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Viral RNA detection in sand flies

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed to detect RNA viruses from the genera Phlebovirus (Sánchez-Seco et al., 2003), Orbivirus (Palacios et al., 2011), and the family Rhabdoviridae (Bourhy et al., 2005). Amplification was performed using the SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The expected amplicon sizes were 244 bp for Phlebovirus, 188 bp for Orbivirus, and 260 bp for family Rhabdoviridae. A synthetic plasmid was used as a positive control to confirm the performance of the PCR assay. A non-template control (NTC) was included in each PCR run, with deionized distilled water used instead of template DNA to monitor for potential contamination. RNA extracted from male sand flies was used as a negative control for viral detection. Detailed protocols and primer sequences used for viral detection are provided in Supplementary Text S1 and Supplementary Table S1, respectively.

Detection of blood parasites and Bartonella spp. bacteria in sand flies

Conventional PCR was conducted to detect Leishmania spp. by targeting the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region of ribosomal RNA, using the primers LeF and LeR (Spanakos et al., 2008). Additionally, a nested PCR was performed to amplify Trypanosoma spp., targeting the small subunit (SSU) rRNA region. This involved the use of outer primers TRY927F and TRY927R, followed by inner primers SSU561F and SSU561R (Noyes et al., 1999). During nested PCR, aerosol-resistant filter tips were used to minimize carry-over contamination when transferring first-round PCR products to the second-round reaction. The thermal cycling conditions for both PCR assays were based on protocols previously described for Leishmania spp. and Trypanosoma spp. (Srisuton et al., 2019; Sunantaraporn et al., 2021), yielding amplification products of approximately 379 bp for Leishmania spp. and 560 bp for Trypanosoma spp.

To detect haemosporidian parasites in sand flies, PCR screening was conducted using degenerate primers targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) gene. The primers (Pan_Haemos_CytbF (5′-GGG TCA AAT GAG TTW YTG GGG-3′) and Pan_Haemos_CytbR (5′-GAA TTA ATG TTG TTA AYR TTC TTT GTT CTG C-3′)) were specifically designed to amplify cytb gene fragments from a broad range of bat-infecting haemosporidian parasites, including Plasmodium spp., Polychromophilus spp., Nycteria spp., and Hepatocystis spp., as previously reported by Arnuphapprasert et al. (2020). The expected amplicon size for a positive specimen was 524 bp.

All DNA specimens from sand flies were screened for the presence of Bartonella spp. DNA by targeting the citrate synthase (gltA) gene, using the primers BhCS871p and BhCS1137n (Norman et al., 1995). Conventional PCR was carried out according to the protocol described by Promrangsee et al. (2019) and Sunantaraporn et al. (2026b). A successful amplification was indicated by the presence of a 379 bp fragment. All PCR detection assays were conducted using a reaction mixture containing 12.5 μl of 2× Green Hot-Start PCR Master Mix (Biotechrabbit, Berlin, Germany), 0.4 μl of each 10 μM forward and reverse primer, and 4 μl of nucleic acid specimen, with nuclease-free water added to adjust the final volume to 25 μl. DNA extracted from a Leishmania martiniquensis culture (MHOM/TH/2012/CULE1), DNA from blood samples of dogs infected with Trypanosoma evansi, DNA from cattle lice positive for Bartonella sp., and plasmid DNA containing the cytb gene of a haemosporidian were used as positive controls. DNA extracted from male sand flies served as a negative control, whereas sterilized deionized water was used as a non-template control (NTC). Appropriate controls were included in each PCR run to verify the validity and reliability of the results.

Nucleotide analysis and phylogenetic reconstruction of pathogen-positive sand flies

Positive PCR products from pathogen detection were ligated into the pGEM® T-Easy vector (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). Plasmid DNA was then extracted and sent to Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) for DNA Sanger sequencing. A BLAST search was performed to compare our sequences with available sequence data in the GenBank database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The final datasets used for phylogenetic analyses comprised 42 Rhabdoviridae RdRp sequences ranging from 243 to 267 bp, 24 ITS1 sequences of Leishmania subgenus Mundinia ranging from 259 to 308 bp, 40 haemosporidian cytb sequences ranging from 260 to 378 bp, and 65 Trypanosoma SSU rRNA sequences ranging from 560 to 650 bp. Sequence alignments were performed and manually inspected and trimmed using BioEdit sequence alignment editor v.7.2.5 (Hall, 1999) to retain homologous nucleotide regions. Phylogenetic analyses were subsequently conducted using the maximum-likelihood (ML) method implemented in MEGA 11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The best-fitting nucleotide substitution models were selected using the model-testing function in MEGA 11. The Tamura 3-parameter model with gamma distribution (T92+G), Kimura 2-parameter model (K2), Kimura 2-parameter model with gamma distribution (K2P+G), and Tamura-Nei model with gamma distribution (TN93+G) were used for the Rhabdoviridae, Leishmania, Trypanosoma, and haemosporidian datasets, respectively. Gaps and missing data were handled using the complete-deletion option, whereby sites containing gaps or missing data were excluded from the analysis. The reliability of the resulting phylogenetic trees was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replicates, and bootstrap support values ≥ 50% were displayed at the corresponding nodes. The resulting ML tree was visualized and edited using FigTree version 1.4.4 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/FigTree/). Genetic network analysis for Hepatocystis spp. was generated from cytb sequences using DnaSP v.6 (http://www.ub.edu/dnasp/) (Rozas et al., 2017) and evaluated using the TCS method in PopART v.1.7 (https://popart.maths.otago.ac.nz/). Pairwise comparisons of Hepatocystis spp. isolates from this study, along with reference isolates from other hosts and global sources, were conducted using Sequence Demarcation Tool v.1.3 (SDTv1.3) (Muhire et al., 2014). The final dataset included 58 and 60 Hepatocystis cytb sequences, each 378 bp in length, for TCS network and pairwise identity analyses, respectively.

Molecular identification of sand fly species

Molecular identification of sand fly species was performed on 36 pools, including pathogen-positive pools and randomly selected pools representing each sand fly species. Approximately 500 bp of the mitochondrial cytb gene was amplified using the PhleF/PhleR primers as previously described (Latrofa et al., 2011). The PCR products were directly sequenced using the corresponding forward and reverse primers by Macrogen Inc., South Korea. Consensus nucleotide sequences were compared with previously published sequences in the GenBank database using the online BLAST tool to confirm species identity.

Data analysis

Pathogen prevalence was estimated using the minimum infection rate (MIR), calculated as the number of positive pools divided by the total number of individual sand flies tested multiplied by 1000. Because the number of pathogen-positive pools was small and pool sizes varied among species, MIR was calculated at the overall study level using all 616 specimens as the denominator. This provides a consistent and conservative estimate, assuming one infected insect per positive pool, while avoiding unstable species-specific estimates based on small sample sizes and few positive pools. Sand fly populations were characterized by relative abundance, calculated as the percentage of specimens of each species among all specimens collected (number of specimens of each species/total number of specimens × 100), and species richness, defined as the number of species identified across the surveyed areas.

Results

Sand fly species composition

A total of 616 female sand fly specimens were collected from three caves in Chiang Rai province, northern Thailand: Phra Cave (n = 399), Pha Tong Cave (n = 184), and Mae Khrua Cave (n = 33). Phra and Pha Tong caves exhibited the highest species richness, each with seven and eight identified species, respectively, while Mae Khrua Cave harbored five species. Regarding the distribution of specimens among sampling sites, the largest proportion was recorded in Phra Cave (64.77%), followed by Pha Tong Cave (29.87%) and Mae Khrua Cave (5.36%). Sergentomyia anodontis had the highest relative abundance, comprising 68.92% of the specimens collected from Phra Cave and 48.48% from Mae Khrua Cave. In contrast, Sergentomyia siamensis had the highest relative abundance in Pha Tong Cave, representing 27.17% of the specimens collected from that cave (Table 1). Sand fly species were identified based on cytb gene sequences from 36 sand fly pools. BLAST analysis showed 93.88–100% sequence identity with the closest reference sequences in GenBank. The molecular identifications were consistent with the morphological identifications for most pools. Four pools morphologically identified as Idiophlebotomus sp. showed 99.16–100% sequence identity with Idiophlebotomus longiforceps. The highest sequence identity (100%) was observed for Phlebotomus shadenae and Sergentomyia khawi, whereas the lowest identity (93.88%) was observed for Phlebotomus barguesae. Detection of blood parasites and Bartonella bacteria in sand flies

PCR screening detected Leishmania spp., Trypanosoma spp., and haemosporidian parasite DNA in the examined sand fly pools, whereas no Bartonella spp. DNA was detected in any of the specimens tested. Leishmania spp. DNA was detected in two pools, both collected from Phra Cave, including one pool of Se. anodontis and one pool of Ph. barguesae. Trypanosoma spp. DNA was detected in five pools, all from Pha Tong Cave, comprising three pools of Se. siamensis, one pool of Se. khawi, and one pool of Phlebotomus mascomai. In addition, haemosporidian DNA was detected in two pools from Pha Tong Cave, including one pool of Se. khawi and one pool of Ph. mascomai. Haemosporidian and Leishmania spp. DNA were detected in two positive pools each, with an overall MIR of 3.25 (95% CI: 0.39–9.01). Trypanosoma spp. DNA was detected in five positive pools, corresponding to an overall MIR of 8.12 (95% CI: 2.64–16.54) (Table 3). No detections of these blood parasites were observed in sand fly pools collected from Mae Khrua Cave (Table 2).

BLAST similarity and phylogenetic analyses

BLAST analysis of the obtained ITS1 sequences demonstrated the presence of Leishmania martiniquensis in sand fly specimens. The sequence derived from Se. anodontis (TAP2) exhibited 99.61% nucleotide identity with L. martiniquensis isolate LP374, previously detected in Culicoides mahasarakhamense from Lampang (GenBank: OR917783) in northern Thailand. Similarly, the L. martiniquensis sequence obtained from Ph. barguesae (TAP31) showed 100% nucleotide similarity with the same L. martiniquensis isolate LP374 (GenBank: OR917783).

BLAST analysis of three SSU rRNA sequences of trypanosomes obtained from Se. siamensis (PT4, PT35, and PT65) showed similarity to Trypanosoma rotatorium, previously detected in the amphibian host (Rana catesbeiana) (GenBank: U39583), with nucleotide identity of 92.59–92.75%. Another sequence derived from Se. khawi (PT87) showed 95.22% nucleotide identity with Trypanosoma sp. isolate AF02_RS0742, previously reported from Mastomys natalensis (Natal multimammate rat) in Zambia (GenBank: MZ703214). Additionally, a Trypanosoma SSU rRNA sequence obtained from Ph. mascomai (PT61) showed 100% nucleotide identity with T. noyesi, previously detected in Ph. mascomai from Lampang (GenBank: OP861666). Furthermore, BLAST analysis of the Hepatocystis sp. sequence obtained from Se. khawi (PT60) showed 95.62% nucleotide identity, whereas the sequence from Ph. mascomai (PT61) showed 96.16% nucleotide identity with Hepatocystis sp. (GenBank: MK640503), previously detected in the bat Pteropus scapulatus (little red flying fox) from Australia. Co-detection of T. noyesi and Hepatocystis sp. was recorded in Ph. mascomai (PT61).

The phylogenetic tree based on ITS1 sequences showed that two sequences (TAP2 and TAP31) obtained in the present study clustered within the well-supported Leishmania martiniquensis clade (Fig. 3). Phylogenetic analysis based on Hepatocystis cytb sequences revealed that the sequences obtained from sand fly pools clustered within the Hepatocystis lineage. The Hepatocystis cytb sequences isolated from Ph. mascomai and Se. khawi formed a well-supported clade with previously reported Hepatocystis cytb sequences detected in bat hosts. This lineage was clearly separated from other haemosporidian genera, including Plasmodium, Nycteria, Polychromophilus, and Leucocytozoon (Fig. 5). These findings represent the first molecular detection of Hepatocystis sp. in cave-dwelling sand flies from Thailand, with the detected sequences showing similarity to Hepatocystis lineages previously reported from bats. Genetic network analysis based on Hepatocystis cytb sequences using the TCS method revealed geographical structuring among the available sequences, with distinct haplotype groups corresponding broadly to isolates from Africa, Thailand, and Australia and including sequences obtained from different vertebrate hosts. The sequences obtained in this study clustered within the Thailand haplotype group and formed two closely related haplotypes that were connected to previously reported bat-derived Hepatocystis haplotypes from Thailand by a small number of mutational steps (Fig. 6). Discussion

The present study investigated the diversity of pathogens in cave-dwelling phlebotomine sand flies from three different tourist caves in Chiang Rai Province, northern Thailand. Our findings demonstrate that cave ecosystems can support diverse sand fly populations and may serve as ecological interfaces for the circulation of vector-borne pathogens associated with wildlife hosts. More than 36 species of phlebotomine sand flies have been recorded in Thailand (Polseela et al., 2016a, 2016b; Phumee et al., 2017; Depaquit et al., 2019; Renaux Torres et al., 2023). In the present study, eight species were identified using both morphological and molecular approaches, namely Se. anodontis, Se. siamensis, Se. khawi, Se. hivernus, Ph. barguesae, Ph. stantoni, Ph. mascomai, and Ph. shadenae, while a group of specimens was morphologically identified at the genus level as Idiophlebotomus sp. but was identified as Idiophlebotomus longiforceps based on cytb sequences. Among these, Se. anodontis and Se. siamensis were the most abundant species across the surveyed caves. Sergentomyia anodontis is recognized as a cavernicolous species and has been widely reported as highly abundant in caves across central Thailand (Uthai Thani) (Polseela et al., 2015), northern (Lampang and Chiang Rai) (Sunantaraporn et al., 2024), and southern provinces (Satun and Chumphon) (Srisuton et al., 2019; Thammapalo et al., 2020). In contrast, Se. siamensis represents a more recent record, previously reported from Chiang Rai in northern Thailand (Renaux Torres et al., 2023) and from Laos (Vongphayloth et al., 2024).

In the present study, only a single pool of Phlebotomus shadenae tested positive for a sequence belonging to the family Rhabdoviridae. Molecular characterization showed that the partial sequence shared 90% amino-acid identity with Vesiculovirus bogdanovac. Phylogenetic analysis placed the sequence within a Vesiculovirus-related clade. However, given the short length of the partial sequence and the available sequence similarity, the detected sequence could not be confidently assigned to a specific Vesiculovirus species. To date, at least eleven Vesiculovirus species have been reported from sand flies (Jancarova et al., 2023), highlighting the diversity of Vesiculovirus-related sequences detected in these insects. Vesiculovirus bogdanovac (Yug Bogdanovac virus) was originally isolated from Phlebotomus perfiliewi in Serbia (Gligić et al., 1983). Although this virus has been shown to infect vertebrate hosts, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to the sequence detected in the present study. Members of the family Rhabdoviridae have been detected in various hematophagous insects, including sand flies, mosquitoes, and biting midges, and some members are known to infect vertebrates (Tesh et al., 1987; Letchworth et al., 1999; Jancarova et al., 2023). A previous study identified a novel cluster of rhabdoviruses closely related to Bactrocera dorsalis sigmavirus strain BDSV.abc5 in Phlebotomus papatasi from southern Thailand (Phumee et al., 2021b). Together with the present finding, these reports indicate that rhabdovirus-related sequences have been detected in sand flies from different regions of Thailand and contribute to the growing knowledge of rhabdovirus diversity associated with sand flies. Further sequencing of longer genomic regions and virus isolation will be required to determine the taxonomic identity and biological significance of the sequence detected in this study.

Despite these findings, the detection of viral RNA alone is insufficient to incriminate sand flies as vectors of viruses. According to the World Health Organization, four principal criteria are required to establish an arthropod as a competent viral vector: (i) the virus must be isolated from field-collected arthropods that show no evidence of a recent blood meal; (ii) the arthropod must be capable of acquiring infection through feeding on a viraemic vertebrate host or via experimental exposure; (iii) the arthropod must be able to biologically transmit the virus through its bite; and (iv) there must be field-based evidence demonstrating an ecological association between the arthropod and a suitable vertebrate host (WHO, 1967). Therefore, the virus identified in this study should be regarded as a putative Vesiculovirus-like Rhabdoviridae, and its biological and epidemiological significance remains to be determined.

Molecular screening using ITS1-PCR followed by sequencing revealed the presence of Leishmania martiniquensis in two sand fly pools collected from Phra Cave: Se. anodontis and Ph. barguesae. Previous studies in Thailand have also reported the detection of L. martiniquensis DNA in several sand fly species (Kanjanopas et al., 2013; Chusri et al., 2014; Srisuton et al., 2019; Preativatanyou et al., 2023; Phumee et al., 2024), indicating a potentially broad distribution. However, only one study has demonstrated the presence of L. martiniquensis DNA in Phlebotomus stantoni, with a low prevalence reported in Chiang Rai Province of northern Thailand (Sriwongpan et al., 2021). Recent studies have suggested that non-sand fly insects, particularly biting midges of the genus Culicoides, may serve as potential vectors of L. martiniquensis and L. orientalis in northern Thailand (Sunantaraporn et al., 2021; Promrangsee et al., 2024). Notably, a study by Ampol et al. (2024) reported a relatively high prevalence (34.8%) of co-infection with L. martiniquensis and L. orientalis in Culicoides guttifer and Culicoides mahasarakhamense collected from Phra Cave, the same location as the present study. This finding is particularly important as it suggests that the transmission dynamics of Leishmania spp. in this area may be more complex than previously recognized, potentially involving multiple vector species.

In the present study, we detected five positive pools for Trypanosoma DNA based on SSU rRNA-PCR in Ph. mascomai, Se. siamensis, and Se. khawi from Pha Tong Cave. Phylogenetic analysis revealed the presence of multiple Trypanosoma lineages associated with different vertebrate hosts, including (i) amphibian-associated trypanosomes (An04/Frog1), (ii) lizard-associated trypanosomes, and (iii) Trypanosoma noyesi within the T. cruzi clade. The anuran Trypanosoma has been recorded in several hosts, including toads, frogs, leeches, geckos, and invertebrates, such as sand flies (Ferreira et al., 2008; Spodareva et al., 2018; Toontong et al., 2022). Sand flies have been reported as vectors of anuran trypanosomes, which are classified into the lineages An04/Frog1, An01+An02/Frog2, and An03+An06/Frog3 (Spodareva et al., 2018). Several studies have documented An04/Frog1 trypanosomes in multiple sand fly species collected from different habitats, including caves and residential areas. For example, Srisuton et al. (2019) reported An04/Frog1 trypanosomes in sand flies collected from caves (Se. anodontis, Idiophlebotomus asperulus, and Ph. bettisi) as well as from areas surrounding houses of leishmaniasis patients (Se. khawi and Grassomyia indica). Khositharattanakool et al. (2024) also detected An04/Frog1 trypanosomes in Se. khawi, Gr. indica, and Se. hivernus from three regions of Thailand. Similarly, Preativatanyou et al. (2023) detected amphibian-associated An04/Frog1 trypanosomes in four sand fly species, including Ph. stantoni, Se. khawi, Se. barraudi, and Gr. indica. In addition, Sunantaraporn et al. (2024) and Klaiklueng et al. (2025) reported An04/Frog1 trypanosomes in Se. khawi collected from rural and cave sampling sites in Narathiwat and Songkhla provinces, southern Thailand. A previous study isolated Trypanosoma spp. parasites from Ph. kazeruni in leishmaniasis-endemic areas of Pakistan, and phylogenetic analysis grouped these parasites within the An04/Frog1 lineage, which had previously been described as amphibian-associated Trypanosoma spp. (Kato et al., 2010). Other trypanosome lineages, such as An01+An02/Frog2, have also been reported in Thailand (Preativatanyou et al., 2023; Khositharattanakool et al., 2024; Sunantaraporn et al., 2024). In the present study, phylogenetic analysis of Trypanosoma spp. detected in three Se. siamensis specimens showed a close relationship to T. rotatorium within the An04/Frog1 clade. In addition, another Trypanosoma lineage belonging to the lizard-associated clade was detected in Se. khawi. However, blood-meal analysis was not conducted in this study, therefore, host feeding preferences could not be determined. Several species within the genus Sergentomyia are known to feed predominantly on cold-blooded vertebrates, including amphibians and reptiles. A previous report detected frog blood in Se. khawi and sun skink blood in Se. anodontis (Khositharattanakool et al., 2024) and Se. iyengari (Siripattanapipong et al., 2018), suggesting that frogs and sun skinks may represent common blood-meal sources for these sand flies, which is consistent with the herpetophilic behavior of many Sergentomyia species (Maia and Depaquit, 2016). It is also noteworthy that these hosts may contribute to the maintenance and possible transmission of anuran trypanosomes when infected synanthropic reptiles or amphibians are available as blood-meal sources.

In the present study, Trypanosoma noyesi was detected in a single pool of Ph. mascomai collected from Pha Tong Cave, consistent with a previous report of T. noyesi in Ph. mascomai from Lampang Province, northern Thailand (Sunantaraporn et al., 2024). This parasite has also been detected in other sand fly species, including Se. anodontis and Idiophlebotomus longiforceps from Chiang Rai Province (Khositharattanakool et al., 2024), and Id. asperulus from Chumphon, Trang, and Phatthalung provinces (Srisuton et al., 2019). The detection of T. noyesi in Ph. mascomai is noteworthy because sequences of this species have shown phylogenetic relationships with Trypanosoma isolates previously detected in bats in Thailand (Riana et al., 2022; Soomro et al., 2025) and in tabanid flies in Australia (Botero et al., 2016).

However, the ecological relationships among T. noyesi, sand flies, and vertebrate hosts remain unclear. In the present study, detection was based solely on parasite DNA using a PCR-based approach and therefore does not establish a host-parasite or vector-parasite relationship. In particular, host blood-meal analysis, parasite isolation and characterization from sand flies, and investigation of T. noyesi in potential vertebrate hosts, including bats, were not performed. Further studies incorporating these approaches are needed to determine the source of infection in sand flies and to clarify the potential role of Ph. mascomai and other sand fly species in the transmission cycle of T. noyesi. The present findings nevertheless provide molecular evidence of T. noyesi in cave-dwelling sand flies and contribute to the growing knowledge of Trypanosoma spp. diversity in cave-associated ecosystems.

In addition to Leishmania and Trypanosoma, this study detected Hepatocystis-related sequences in Ph. mascomai and Se. khawi. Phylogenetic analysis based on mitochondrial cytb sequences showed that the sand fly-derived sequences clustered with previously reported Hepatocystis lineages from chiropteran hosts in Thailand (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020). Consistently, genetic network analysis placed the sequences close to previously reported Hepatocystis haplotypes from Thai bats. To our knowledge, this represents the first molecular detection of Hepatocystis-related sequences in sand flies in Thailand.

Hepatocystis has been reported from a range of vertebrate hosts, including bats and primates (Arnuphapprasert et al., 2020; Ejotre et al., 2021; Tsague et al., 2022; Putaporntip et al., 2025), but its transmission cycle and vectors remain poorly understood. Biting midges (Culicoides spp.) have been suggested as potential vectors, although evidence for other hematophagous dipterans is limited (Ejotre et al., 2021). The occurrence of Hepatocystis-related sequences in sand flies collected from bat-inhabited caves raises the possibility that these insects may acquire parasite DNA during blood-feeding. However, the present molecular data alone cannot determine the source of the detected sequences or demonstrate active parasite development within the sand flies. Blood-meal analysis was not performed, bats and other potential vertebrate hosts from the sampled caves were not examined, and parasite developmental stages were not assessed. Therefore, a specific host association or vector role for sand flies cannot be inferred from the present findings.

Interpretation of the phylogenetic and haplotype relationships is further limited by the geographical and host sampling available for comparison. The sand flies examined in this study were collected from Chiang Rai Province, whereas available Hepatocystis sequences from vertebrate hosts, including bats and non-human primates, originate from other geographical regions. Consequently, the clustering of sand fly-derived sequences with bat-derived sequences from Thailand may reflect geographical structure rather than a specific association with bats. Broader sampling of Hepatocystis spp. from sand flies and potential vertebrate hosts across Thailand, together with blood-meal analysis, characterization of parasite developmental stages, and experimental vector competence studies, will be necessary to clarify the transmission cycle, host associations, and biological significance of Hepatocystis in sand flies.

Unfortunately, no Bartonella DNA was detected in the sand fly specimens examined in this study. This finding contrasts with previous reports of bat-associated Bartonella spp. detected in cave-dwelling sand flies in Thailand (Sunantaraporn et al., 2024). The negative result may be related to differences in seasonality, host availability, or temporal variation in pathogen prevalence; however, these explanations remain speculative and require further investigation. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of pooled specimens with variable pool sizes may have influenced prevalence estimates, and positive pools could not be used to determine which individual sand flies were infected. Secondly, the lack of blood-meal data limited the ability to identify the vertebrate sources of the detected pathogens. Thirdly, sampling was conducted over a limited period and therefore may not represent seasonal or temporal variation in pathogen occurrence. Additionally, pathogen isolation and examination for developmental stages were not performed. Consequently, molecular detection alone cannot distinguish between the presence of pathogen nucleic acids and viable or actively developing infections and does not provide evidence of vector competence. Finally, the relatively limited sample size may have reduced the probability of detecting pathogens occurring at low prevalence. Despite these limitations, the study expands current knowledge of pathogen diversity in cave-dwelling sand flies in northern Thailand. The detection of Leishmania martiniquensis, multiple Trypanosoma lineages, a Vesiculovirus-like virus belonging to the family Rhabdoviridae, and Hepatocystis-related sequences demonstrates the diversity of microorganisms detected in sand flies inhabiting cave environments. These findings provide a basis for further investigation of pathogen circulation at cave-associated wildlife-vector interfaces and highlight the value of continued surveillance in such environments.

Conclusions

This study provides molecular evidence of diverse microorganisms in cave-dwelling phlebotomine sand flies from Chiang Rai Province, northern Thailand, including Leishmania martiniquensis, multiple Trypanosoma lineages, a Vesiculovirus-like rhabdovirus, and Hepatocystis-related sequences. Notably, the detection of Hepatocystis-related sequences represents the first molecular evidence of this parasite group in sand flies in Thailand. Although the findings demonstrate the presence and diversity of pathogen-associated sequences in cave-dwelling sand flies, their epidemiological significance and potential roles in transmission remain to be determined. Further studies integrating longitudinal sampling, blood-meal analysis, investigation of vertebrate hosts, parasite isolation, and assessment of pathogen development and vector competence are warranted to clarify pathogen transmission dynamics at cave-associated wildlife-vector interfaces.
