Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) in a patient with post streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN): A case report
Authors: Aftab Ahmed, Anum Akbar, Digbijay Kunwar, Fena Mehta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey Clinical MessageConcurrent recurrence of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) and poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN) in a thalassemia intermedia patient is rare and underscores the complexity of autoimmune disorders. This case emphasizes the importance of considering ADEM in the differential diagnosis of children presenting with PSGN accompanied by neurological symptoms.AbstractPost‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN) is a common group A streptococcal (GAS) infection sequela. The pathophysiology of PSGN involves immune complex deposition, with type 3 hypersensitivity reaction triggered by GAS. Certain neurological conditions may also arise following a GAS infection, possibly due to molecular mimicry in the brain, a pathophysiology similar to rheumatic fever, another common sequel of GAS infection. We present the case of a child with β‐thalassemia intermedia who exhibited the classic triad (edema, hypertension, hematuria) of PSGN along with neurological manifestations, including a low glasgow coma scale (GCS) score and seizures. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain indicated changes consistent with acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM). Initially treated with methylprednisolone, the patient eventually received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) due to lack of response. The patient had a good outcome, with complete resolution of all symptoms and no residual neurological deficits. This case underscores the importance of considering ADEM in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with neurological signs and symptoms following a recent throat infection with GAS. Furthermore, given the increased risk of infection in thalassemia, patients with thalassemia who have a throat infection and neurological symptoms should be evaluated for the possible presence of ADEM.Keywords: acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, case report, glomerulonephritis, group A streptococcal, MRI, thalassemia

INTRODUCTION

Thalassemia intermedia is a clinical category for conditions with an α/β chain imbalance and symptoms that are more severe than thalassemia minor but less severe than thalassemia major. 1 Thalassemia intermedia is non‐transfusion‐dependent, and its management in the pediatric population involves close monitoring and observation. 2 Acute post‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN) stands as a significant immune‐complex‐mediated glomerular nephritic disease, predominantly triggered by group A streptococcus (GAS) infections. 3 The condition manifests with gross hematuria, generalized edema, and hypertension, often following episodes of streptococcal pharyngitis or pyoderma.

Various neurological conditions may occur in patients with post‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis. The three common neurological conditions are (1) hypertensive encephalopathy, (2) Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy, and (3) Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM). 4 , 5 , 6 However, very few cases of patients with APSGN and ADEM have been reported, including a case report in 1998 and 2002 and a case series in 2001 with 10 pediatric patients. 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, the convergence of thalassemia intermedia, APSGN, and ADEM within a single clinical entity is rare. We report a case of a child who was collectively diagnosed with all three conditions, shedding light on the complex interplay between hematological, renal, and neurological pathologies in pediatric patients.

CASE PRESENTATION

An 8‐year‐old boy with β‐thalassemia intermedia presented to the emergency department with complaints of generalized body swelling for 6 days, fever for 3 days, and seizures for 1 day. Vital signs assessment showed a temperature of 39°C, blood pressure (BP) of 145/76 mmHg, oxygen saturation of 98% at room air, and a heart rate of 115 beats/min. On general physical examination, the boy appeared pale, with obvious chipmunk facies and generalized body swelling, including periorbital edema. His chest and abdomen examinations were normal, with a clear chest on auscultation and normal heart sounds with no murmurs. Neurological examination showed a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 12/15 (E4V3M5) with no neck stiffness. Motor examination showed equal and normal tone in all four limbs with equal strength of 4/5 and 2+ reflexes. His weight was three standard deviations (SD) lower, and his height was 2 SD lower on the growth chart. Past medical history was notable for sore throat, for which he took no medications. He did not have any significant surgical or family history. The patient's vaccination was up to date, except he did not receive the vaccine against coronavirus.

While in the emergency department, the patient had an episode of brief tonic‐colonic seizure with urinary incontinence (coca‐colored urine) and had postictal drowsiness for which IV diazepam and levetiracetam were given stat.

Given the patient's symptoms and signs, a differential diagnosis of thalassemia with hypertensive encephalopathy, renal artery stenosis, meningoencephalitis, and acute post‐streptococcal glomerulonephritis (APSGN) was established.

Diagnostic assessment

His urine and blood analysis (Table 1) revealed findings of elevated antistreptococcal titer in blood, low complement levels in serum, and a significant presence of RBCs in urine, ultimately confirming the diagnosis of APSGN. To screen for rheumatic heart disease (RHD), another sequel of group A streptococcal infection, echocardiography was performed, which was normal.

Therapeutic interventions

The patient was admitted to the pediatric floor, and the management plan included intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone (1.5 g once daily) for 15 days, IV levetiracetam (20 mg/kg/day), intramuscular (IM) furosemide (1 mg/kg every 8 h) for 2 weeks, and oral ramipril (5 mg/day) for 2 months. Following consultation with the neurology department for the management of ADEM, once the BP was controlled, IV methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/day) was initiated. The patient did not show any improvement with methylprednisolone; hence, the patient subsequently received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (2 g/kg/day) for 3 days and was discharged after showing complete recovery. Follow‐up and outcome of Interventions

With the above‐mentioned treatment, our patient demonstrated significant improvement in generalized body swelling and in blood pressure. His GCS was also improved by the third day of treatment with IVIG, and he remained seizure‐free for at least 72 h before getting discharged. The patient came for a follow‐up after a week of getting discharged, and at the follow‐up visit patient, he had no residual neurological deficits. The patient was advised to continue regular follow‐ups to observe and monitor his β‐thalassemia intermedia; however, the patient was then lost to follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

PSGN is a common complication of GAS infections, typically presenting with a triad of hypertension, hematuria, and edema. Our patient exhibited all three symptoms, necessitating further confirmation through serum and urine analysis. The pathophysiology of PSGN involves a Type 3 hypersensitivity reaction triggered by GAS, leading to the deposition of immune complexes in the glomerulus. Currently, there is no vaccine for GAS, and thus no preventive measures for PSGN, including antibiotic treatment for GAS infection, which may not reduce the risk of developing PSGN. 9 The treatment for PSGN is symptomatic, focusing on controlling blood pressure and edema, which was also the approach taken for our patient.

The relationship between GAS and ADEM is complex and still not fully understood. While GAS has been proposed as a trigger for ADEM, the exact pathogenesis is not fully understood. Still, it is believed to arise from an aberrant immune response, where the immune system mistakenly identifies components of the CNS, particularly myelin, as foreign or antigenic. 6 One proposed mechanism underlying the autoimmune response in ADEM is molecular mimicry. As a result, the immune system may produce antibodies or T cells that cross‐react with the microbial antigens and self‐antigens in the CNS, leading to inflammation and tissue damage. Another proposed mechanism involves the non‐specific activation of pre‐existing auto‐reactive T cells or B cells within the immune system. Under normal circumstances, these auto‐reactive lymphocytes are kept in check by mechanisms of immune tolerance. However, in conditions such as ADEM, immune tolerance may be compromised, leading to the activation and proliferation of auto‐reactive clones that target myelin or other self‐antigens in the CNS. The pathophysiology involves an inflammatory cascade leading to demyelination, gliosis, necrosis, peri‐venous hemorrhage, edema, and infiltration of inflammatory cells, which are critical components of this inflammatory cascade. Many infectious diseases, including GAS, are known to be a risk for ADEM. Our patient had a recent history of throat infection for which he did not receive any treatment. Given his positive ASO titer now, it is mostly likely that his throat infection was due to GAS, which eventually led to ADEM. The recommended guidelines for the treatment of ADEM in children include corticosteroids as the first line treatment with additional options of IVIG and plasma exchange in case the patient does not respond to steroids. 10 Likewise, our patient received methylprednisolone initially but did not show any clinical neurological improvement with steroids, and therefore, he eventually received IVIG. Approximately 20% of pediatric patients may experience residual neurological deficits following an episode of ADEM, though the majority do not experience further neurological deterioration beyond the initial 3 months after presentation. Fortunately, our patient did not exhibit any neurological deficits during the initial follow‐up, similar to a previously reported case. 11

CONCLUSION

In summary, while PSGN and rheumatic fever are two common sequelae of GAS, our patient also developed ADEM, a rare complication of GAS. Furthermore, patients with thalassemia have an increased susceptibility to various infections, including GAS infections. This suggests that the patient diagnosed with thalassemia intermedia may be at risk of developing a GAS infection. It is essential to provide parents with guidelines on recognizing the symptoms of GAS. Promptly bringing their child to the hospital for timely antibiotic treatment may reduce the risk of developing ADEM. Finally, a differential diagnosis of ADEM should be considered in children who present with neurological signs and symptoms with a recent history of throat infection.
