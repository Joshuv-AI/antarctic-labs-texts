More than Meets the Eye: Understanding the Effect of Individual Objects on Perceived Visual Privacy
Mete Harun Akcay, Siddharth Prakash Rao, Alexandros Bakas, Buse Gul Atli

Abstract
. User-generated content, such as photos, comprises the majority of online media content and drives engagement due to the human ability to process visual information quickly. Consequently, many online platforms are designed for sharing visual content, with billions of photos posted daily. However, photos often reveal more than they intended through visible and contextual cues, leading to privacy risks. Previous studies typically treat privacy as a property of the entire image, overlooking individual objects that may carry varying privacy risks and influence how users perceive it. We address this gap with a mixed-methods study (n = 92) to understand how users evaluate the privacy of images containing multiple sensitive objects. Our results reveal mental models and nuanced patterns that uncover how granular details, such as photo-capturing context and co-presence of other objects, affect privacy perceptions. These novel insights could enable personalized, context-aware privacy protection designs on social media and future technologies.

Contributions:
Our main contributions from this work are as follows. First, we offer novel insights into how end-users evaluate visual privacy and the factors that shape their privacy perceptions.
Secondly, our work provides empirical evidence on users’ privacy heuristics, derived from a mixed-methods analysis of qualitative and quantitative data gathered through an online user survey (n = 92).
Finally, we demonstrate the feasibility of using synthetic images in user studies, highlighting their value as an alternative when obtaining real-world data is challenging.

Overview of results:
Our results are drawn by fixing the individual visual element (referred to as
objects
) in the foreground and studying its perceived privacy by the user using varying combinations of objects in the background. In such a setup, we make the following observations.

•

We found that end-users demonstrate a latent cognitive model for evaluating the intricate visual and contextual cues of the images they publish online. Users pay attention to granular details about the objects, such as what or who is in the photo and the situational context in which the photo is captured. This finding supports the well-established theories of contextual anchors of privacy decisions in the prior art
(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
, but also extends the knowledge by providing evidence about micro-details of context.

•

We found that the presence of specific categories of sensitive objects in the background dictates the privacy perception of those in the foreground. Especially when the background objects are unrelated to each other, the presence of other objects has a negligible impact on the users’ foreground privacy perception due to the
dominance effect
. While previous studies have discussed the impact of similar salient features on privacy perception using anecdotal examples, we provide empirical evidence for the dominance effect.

•

Certain combinations of semantically related sensitive objects in the background also contribute to the perceived privacy of those in the foreground. While such individual background objects may still influence users’ privacy judgments to some extent, their co-presence can trigger stronger concerns than what each would cause in isolation. Prior research has extensively examined inference risks from the combination of related elements from different datasets. However, a similar effect emerging from a single data item (such as individual images) is less explored to the best of our knowledge. In this direction, our findings offer novel insights into
pair-wise co-presence effect
and its potential to trigger privacy concerns.

Our work contributes towards a comprehensive understanding of human visual privacy perception that can potentially be utilized for developing personalized privacy preference options for sharing visual content online. In particular, the insights from this study can be leveraged for designing assistive technologies that share the entire content while removing, blurring, or masking privacy-sensitive objects, but still function effectively. Such technologies can be integrated into various AI applications, including privacy-sensitive visual aid devices, smart home assistants, wearable cameras, augmented reality platforms, surveillance systems, and live classroom environments.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Privacy Risks in Visual Content

Online platforms increasingly rely on AI-driven personalization and pervasive data collection practices that amplify privacy risks. Through everyday interactions, and often without full awareness, users reveal personal and behavioral data that can be profiled to infer sensitive attributes
(
Goyeneche et al. 2023
;
Shrestha et al. 2024
)
such as health status, political beliefs, or identity. Visual media, such as photos and videos, are particularly information-rich, with shared content potentially exposing location, social ties, or sensitive activities, leaving users vulnerable to reputational damage, discrimination, or harassment. Restricting privacy settings does not fully protect users, as both the platform and third-party algorithms can still access and repurpose these images in ways beyond the user’s intent, thereby increasing privacy risks
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Amil et al. 2024
)
. Certain user groups, such as children and people with visual impairments, face unique challenges in recognizing and controlling these risks
(
Stangl et al. 2022
;
Akter et al. 2020b
;
Goyeneche et al. 2023
)
. Moreover, advances in computer vision amplify these risks, as increasingly powerful models can analyze and recognize visual content with high accuracy, enabling new forms of information that adversaries can easily exploit
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
.

2.2.
Perceived Privacy, Norms and Behavior

Perceptions of visual privacy are shaped not only by what is visible in an image, but also by expectations of who will see it, how the platform mediates sharing, and the broader social norms surrounding disclosure. Contextual integrity theory
(
Nissenbaum 2004
)
also supports this: privacy is maintained when information flows conform to the norms and expectations of a given context. Empirical work demonstrates that situational and normative factors, such as intention, relationships, or setting of sharing, often matter more than the presence of objects in an image
(
Hoyle et al. 2020
)
. Building on this perspective, previous research has also applied contextual properties to guide the design of privacy protections for online visual content
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
. Our work extends these insights by examining how object-level cues and their co-occurrence interact with contextual factors to influence perceived privacy.

2.3.
Visual Privacy Protection

To mitigate the risks mentioned above, prior work has explored privacy-enhancing technologies for visual content. Technical solutions include automatic detection and obfuscation of sensitive regions, blurring or masking private content, facial de-identification, redaction before sharing
(
Ravi et al. 2024
)
, or developing AI-assisted tools that guide users in making informed decisions
(
Orekondy et al. 2017
)
. Other studies emphasized user-centered controls, such as allowing people to set sharing preferences on upload or delegate privacy decisions to trusted assistants
(
Stangl et al. 2022
)
. However, many existing tools do not offer robust protection mechanisms or often overlook how specific objects and contextual cues shape perceptions of privacy.

Despite current limitations, visual privacy protection efforts and understanding user privacy practices align with broader regulatory principles, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union, which treats visual data as personal data, granting individuals rights over how it is collected, processed, and shared. More recently, the EU AI Act has highlighted that AI systems using personal and biometric data (the majority of them are extracted from visual content) should be labeled as high-risk systems, requiring stronger safeguards and oversight. These frameworks highlight the need for privacy-aware systems that can detect, interpret, and protect sensitive visual cues in ways that adhere to legal obligations and respect users’ preferences.

2.4.
Preliminaries

In line with previous research
(
Akter et al. 2020b
;
Niu et al. 2025
;
Orekondy et al. 2018
)
, we define the key concepts used in our research and explain the relationships between them.

•

Object
denotes to a bounded, semantically meaningful entity that belongs to a specific category and can be distinguished from other objects and the background. In a visual content, visual objects (e.g., face, person, tree) are entities that can be recognized based on their visual appearance such as shape, texture, color, while textual objects (e.g., name, date) consist of symbolic representations (characters, words, phrases).

•

Privacy-sensitive object (PSO)
refers to an object that contains personally identifiable or sensitive information that owners may feel uncomfortable sharing on public platforms. A visual content can include PSOs and non-PSOs (objects that do not carry sensitive information) and background. Referring again to Figure
4
, person in the center of the photo and the bystanders are labeled PSO, the trees are labeled non-PSO, and the sky and floor belong to the background.

•

Foreground PSO
is the primary subject of interest and is typically located in the center of a visual content, while the background PSO contains sensitive information but is not treated as the main subject. In Figure
4
, person in the center is the foreground PSO and the bystanders are the background PSO.

•

Perceived privacy level (PPL)
is the degree to which individuals believe their personal information, activities, or beliefs are protected from unwanted observation, access, or misuse. It is subjective as the same setting might be judged differently by various individuals.

•

Comfortability level
is the degree of ease and safety experienced by an individual in a given setting. PPL and comfortability level are conceptually aligned, but they are inversely related: as the perceived privacy level increases, the comfortability level decreases.

Building upon this terminology, we construct the following hypotheses:

H1
The perceived privacy level of a foreground PSO increases when it is accompanied by a background PSO.

H2
The surrounding visual scene affects the perceived privacy level of the same PSO present in the image.

H3
The co-presence of multiple background PSOs further elevates the perceived privacy level of a foreground PSO compared to when only a single background PSO is present.

3.
Methodology

3.1.
Study Design

3.1.1.
Selection of Environments

We consider two different environments for the survey: the café and the office. These environments represent distinct contexts, one social and one professional, in which photography is taken and might also be shared. Privacy expectations are usually lower in café due to the co-presence of strangers and a sense of privacy through anonymity. On the other hand, people in office environment has clear roles, often handle professional or sensitive information, leading discomfort to take photos and share in social media.

3.1.2.
Selection of Foreground and Background PSOs

To determine both foreground and background PSOs, we examined the subset of the VISPR dataset designed for image redaction (VISPR-Redacted
(
Orekondy et al. 2018
)
) and the VizWiz-Priv dataset
(
Gurari et al. 2019
)
. VISPR-Redacted contains user uploaded, publicly available Flickr images and is specially curated for automatic detection of PSOs and redacting them by masking. VISPR-Redacted contains 24 labels, all of which are considered PSO. VizWiz-Priv is a large-scale collection of real-world images captured by blind photographers and accompanied by questions and crowd-sourced answers. VizWiz-Priv includes 23 categories that annotators label as PSO. Our analysis revealed that both datasets include several identical labels. This provided a perspective on which PSOs are commonly shared with others and online. We chose the three most common object categories from VizWiz-Priv to serve as our foreground PSOs:
face, miscellaneous paper and computer screen
. Each foreground PSO carry inherent sensitive information while also offering potential information for users:

(1)

Face
: Visual identification of a person. Sharing a face can support social connection (sending it to friends), identity verification (online applications), or professional representation (LinkedIn profile, news).

(2)

Miscellaneous Paper
: Documents such as tickets, forms, printouts, or receipts. These may contain private information (e.g., names, addresses, birthdates, signatures), but are often shared for practical purposes such as proof of purchase, reimbursement, or customer support.

(3)

Computer/phone screen
: Digital content displayed on electronic devices. Screens may reveal sensitive information (e.g., usernames, phone numbers, emails), but screenshots are frequently shared for collaboration or troubleshooting purposes.

For each foreground PSO, we identified five background PSOs through an internal expert discussion process on both datasets, considering diversity in modality (visual vs. textual), potential sensitivity, and variation in real-world presence. These background PSOs can change user comfortability and can be present with the associated foreground PSO. Our set of foreground/background PSO combinations is presented in Table

3.1.4.
Branch Logic Design

Requiring participants to evaluate 48 images could lead to participant fatigue, reduced engagement, and unreliable responses due to rushed decision making. To mitigate this, we used branch logic to direct participants to different flows based on their responses to ranking comfortability levels when the image contains background PSOs. Instead of rating all possible combinations, participants first ranked the five background PSOs by sensitivity.
Only the combinations involving the background PSO with the lowest effect on the foreground PSO’s perceived privacy level (PPL) level were then displayed. As a result, participants rated
1+5+4=10
images per foreground PSO and
30
images in total. This approach also avoided redundant evaluation: If the PPL of a foreground PSO is highest when paired with a certain background PSO, then requesting participants to reassess it alongside other background PSOs becomes redundant, since the foreground PSO is already perceived as the most sensitive across all pairings. Appendix
A.2
shows how the branch logic is implemented when the environment is café, the foreground PSO is face, and the landmark is chosen by the background PSO with the lowest effect.

3.2.
Study Practicalities

3.2.1.
Survey Organization

The first page of the survey contains the informed consent form, explaining the general objective of the study, how the data will be collected and processed, and compliance with GDPR. Proceeding the survey requires the confirmation of all mandatory consent statements, and an optional checkbox allows entry into a lottery for a movie ticket. Once consent is obtained, participants respond to demographic questions and questions regarding their social media habits and privacy awareness. We constructed privacy awareness questions to understand individual privacy preferences in general. Following this, participants complete three sections, each focusing on a different foreground PSO. In total, the survey contains 50 questions, all of which require a response to proceed, except for the optional open-text fields. We should note that we do not evaluate whether individuals with stronger privacy preferences are more likely to perceive PSOs as private (i.e., less comfortable sharing them online). Our focus is on the
effect of context
rather than individual privacy awareness. Moreover, prior work
(
Hoyle et al. 2020
)
has shown that higher privacy awareness is often associated with perceiving visual content, and attributes shape information sharing behaviors
(
Bélanger and Crossler 2011
)
. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the reported comfortability levels may vary depending on the privacy awareness of the participants. Individuals with stronger privacy concerns might rate PSOs as less comfortable to share, while those with lower awareness may perceive the same objects as less sensitive.

In our user study, each individual section starts with the image containing a single PSO: the foreground PSO, and participants are asked about their comfortability level of sharing this object. The initial question is necessary to obtain a baseline perceived privacy level (PPL). The participants then view a row of images showing the same foreground PSO, paired with a single background PSO different for each image, and a ranking question that asks for the change in their comfortability. They are also asked about the rationale behind their ranking choice (optional open-text answer). The branch logic design is utilized according to their ranking. In the final stage, participants view a row of images including the same foreground PSO, the background PSO ranked with the lowest decrease in their comfortability level (lowest PPL effect) paired with the remaining background PSOs. The final stage also includes an optional question asking to describe whether and in what way the lowest-ranked background PSO influenced the comfortability level of the foreground PSO when combined with other background PSOs.

All ratings were provided on a Likert scale (1-5) where 1 indicated "not comfortable at all" and 5 "very comfortable." We deliberately asked for the comfortability level instead of PPL since it is easier for participants to interpret while still capturing the level of privacy in a more natural form. Table
10
in Appendix
A.3
shows the inverse mapping between comfortability level and PPL. The complete set of survey questions is provided in the Appendix
A.2
.

Participants were recruited using a chain-referral (snowball) sampling approach. We initially distributed the survey link through our personal contacts and social media channels, reaching 73 individuals, and encouraged them to share it further within their own networks. In total, 109 participants completed the survey, with 61 assigned to the café version and 48 to the office version. After excluding responses that showed inconsistencies between ratings and rankings, 92 valid responses remained (51 café and 41 office). As compensation, participants who opted to participate in a raffle were eligible to win one of the 24 movie tickets.

3.3.
Analysis overview

3.3.1.
Quantitative Analysis

To evaluate
H1
, we used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as our design compares paired measurements of the same foreground PSO with and without background PSO. The null hypothesis
H1
is “There is
no
difference between perceived privacy level (PPL) of a foreground PSO when it appears alone versus when it is accompanied by a background PSO”.

To test
H2
, we compared the comfortability scores between different environments using the Mann-Whitney U test, as different participants rate the same foreground PSO in the café vs. office and the variable of comfortability level is ordinal. The null hypothesis for
H2
is “The distributions of perceived privacy levels are the same for café and office”.

For
H3
, we did not perform statistical testing, as the subset of participants who answered each combination question varied according to their individual rankings, making group-level inference unreliable. Instead, we measured the mean change in comfortability level and the proportion of participants who change their comfortability level when there are two background PSOs.

In addition to the above tests, we used the Mann-Whitney U test (with single categories) to understand whether demographics influence perceived privacy levels. To test factors with more than one category, including age group, profession, and educational level, we used the Kruskal–Wallis H test.

3.3.2.
Qualitative Analysis

We conducted qualitative analysis of the open-text responses from the survey questionnaire based on Braun and Clarke’s framework for inductive thematic coding
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
;
Braun et al. 2023
)
. First, the author with an HCI background performed an independent review of the responses to get acquainted with the data using memoing and to generate the initial list of in-vivo codes
(
Blandford et al. 2016
)
. Then, the author iteratively reviewed the initial codes along with the memos to deduce themes that could represent users’ cognition or behaviour while dealing with visual privacy. This process was repeated until saturation was reached, with no further codes and themes or sub-themes emerging. Finally, the author drafted a codebook along with the definitions, inclusion, and exclusion criteria. The codebook was then reviewed to refine the wording of codes, their definitions, and thematic categories. The final codebook was reviewed once again to ensure that it resonated with the research questions and represented the survey data. Two authors then used the codebook to code all individual responses independently.
Since the participants provided their responses as a summary of their cognitive walkthrough during our study, it is possible to tag the responses with multiple codes. Due to this overlap, the coders were allowed to use up to three codes per response. To assess reliability at the theme level, we collapsed the codes into their respective themes. We quantified agreement among coders using Krippendorff’s alpha as the inter-rating reliability (IRR) measure due to its suitability
(
McDonald et al. 2019
)
. We found the IRR to be 0.891, which indicates that coders consistently identified the same overarching themes and a strong agreement at the theme level, supporting the reliability of our thematic analysis.

3.4.
Study Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with regional privacy compliance and well-established best practice guidelines for user studies
(
Redmiles et al. 2017
;
Rea and Parker 2014
;
Egelman et al. 2007
)
. Before the actual survey began, the participants were presented with information about the use of study results and the participation reward terms. Also, the participation was voluntary, and we sought explicit consent from each participant who attempted the survey. The consent and privacy policy included in our study was drafted and provided by the data privacy regulation unit of our institution. A high-level privacy impact assessment was also conducted to review the study methodology and data to be collected to ensure ethical treatment of sensitive data. We ensured that personally identifiable information was solely used for contacting the participants and excluded from the analysis. We used an open-source, GDPR-compliant tool to build and host our study. All the data is entirely under our control on a server in the EU region, and it will be discarded after the project finishes. The visual images used in our study were purely illustrative and did not depict any real human subjects.

4.
Results

Before analyzing the results, the responses that showed clear inconsistencies between the rankings and ratings were excluded from the data set. Inconsistency refers to instances where a participant’s assessment of the comfortability level concerning a foreground privacy-sensitive object (PSO), when there is a single background PSO, conflicts with the subsequent ranking question. This method also enabled us to filter out inattentive participants, thereby improving the quality of the remaining data.

5.
Discussion

One of the key observations from our work is that users’ privacy perceptions do not always translate into privacy behaviour or user actions. For example, the results from the privacy awareness questions (Table
3
) show that the participants expressed general concern about online privacy, but not all examined the photos for personal information before sharing. Furthermore, the qualitative data from the survey displays several instances of privacy awareness among the participants, which appears to have been formed through public news media debates around privacy concerns and mandatory training at the workplace and educational institutions. Despite the awareness, users often fail to assess the privacy risks or control their behavior while sharing visual content on online platforms. The lack of proactive practices highlights that the current systems fail to bridge the gap between users’ mental models and actions.

The complexity of privacy–utility tradeoffs further contributes to this gap. Usually, the foreground privacy-sensitive objects (PSOs) carry inherent sensitivity. Still, people may choose to share them due to the tangible benefits they provide, such as identity verification, professional collaboration, or social connection. Our results show that the perceived privacy risks associated with these PSOs are not fixed, but dynamically reshaped by the presence of background PSOs. For example, a selfie becomes more sensitive when medicine is visible, a document becomes more risky to share when a signature is present, and a screenshot becomes problematic when the username or phone number is exposed.
Such contextual amplification indicates that privacy–utility tradeoffs, especially in the case of visual contents, are a rather complicated process beyond a simple binary choice. The complexity in privacy decision-making arises from users’ attempts to weigh multiple contextual factors and engage in multidimensional self-negotiation for the tradeoffs.

These observations emphasize the need for novel technical interventions and research opportunities to explore the notion of context-aware privacy. In this realm, the hypothesis evaluation and results from our study provide insights into design principles and required characteristics of a context-aware privacy system. From a system design point of view, such a system should avoid uniform treatment of visual elements or objects and adapt privacy sensitivity based on contextual cues. Moreover, the design should consider different types of contextual cues in the visual content. One of the types includes the subtler and latent cues captured in the visual scenes, such as religious symbols, political expressions, or social affiliations. These are not easily identifiable as discrete objects but are often regarded as very sensitive. The other type of contextual cues are visual elements (e.g., medicine) that have a dominance effect, overriding other cues. The dominant cues disproportionately influence user perceptions, as users often anchor their privacy judgments on the most dominant cue rather than considering the full context. Moreover, we also observed that the co-presence of certain visual elements can lead to even higher perceived privacy risks. Towards this end, we argue that privacy-aware system design benefits from integrating object-level detection with contextual inference while also accounting for the dominance and co-presence of visual elements.

From a HCI design perspective, privacy-aware systems should provide automated assistance and fine-grained control to users. The assistance can be provided in the form of nudges, via visual highlighting and prompts, that guide users towards privacy-oriented actions without requiring extra effort. Alternatively, assistance can also be provided through the automatic detection of a highly sensitive object or contextual cue in the background that could amplify the privacy perception. Furthermore, the automated assistance should be suggesting appropriate privacy controls. The controls should be designed in such a way that the users can selectively obfuscate specific sensitive objects of the image in a way that it still retains utility and maintains users’ privacy when shared in the redacted form. In this realm, we argue that a human-centered design approach can assist users in translating their privacy perceptions into concrete actions, reducing cognitive load and improving decision-making.

The existing online social media and instant communication platforms, which deal with user-generated visual content, largely lack user assistance and nuanced controls for privacy. The security and privacy settings for the user accounts offered by some of these platforms fail to address the privacy risks highlighted in our work. The results and discussions from our work can provide actionable insights to integrate visual privacy protection components. These insights can also guide usable system design themes of research works and development of future technologies that deliver personalized privacy protection.

5.1.
Limitations and Future Work

In this section, we discuss the limitations of our work in terms of methodological trade-offs and factors affecting generalizability, which may constrain the external validity of our results. We also outline directions for future research to address these limitations.

Most of the participants in our user study identify as White/Caucasian, highly educated, younger, and mid-career adults. Such a skewed demographic sample could limit the generalizability of our results for cultural and personal background diversity, as these factors tend to shape users’ privacy awareness and perceptions. Similarly, our study involved a representative set of privacy-sensitive objects. Although these represent a good range of sensitive objects that are widely available in visual content, such a finite set may not fully capture the diversity and combinations of objects encountered in the wild. Due to this, our findings may apply to real-world scenarios only to a certain extent. Future work should include more diverse participants and broaden the scope of objects for the user study to capture and understand the complexity of everyday contexts better.

From a methodological point of view, we relied mainly on synthetically generated images to eliminate possible other effects on the perceived privacy level. Although it is a good use case for generative AI in HCI research, and we instructed the participants to imagine themselves as the subject or the photographer/owner, artificial images may not evoke the same kind of cognitive responses as authentic photos or those of the participants themselves. On the other hand, we studied the influence on privacy perception of at most two objects. Real-world visual contents have higher-order combinations, and studying them could open up opportunities to make more detailed observations about co-presence and dominance effects. Furthermore, we relied on conditional branching logic in our user study to personalize the questionnaire and avoid survey fatigue for the participants. Some branches received very few participants and responses, thus limiting statistical comparisons for specific contexts. Future work may consider complementing synthetic stimuli with carefully curated real-world images (with appropriate ethical considerations) and increasing the sample size to ensure sufficient coverage of all conditional branches to validate our results.

6.
Conclusion

This paper advances the study of visual privacy by moving beyond a whole-image approach to a fine-grained, object-level perspective. Using a mixed-methods study with 92 participants, we uncovered how specific objects, their co-presence, and contextual cues shape people’s privacy perceptions. Our results highlight the hidden dynamics that guide privacy judgments and demonstrate that small contextual details can substantially influence perceived privacy concerns.
We reemphasize the importance of human-centric design approaches that can help simplify the cognitive and technical complexity of visual privacy. Towards this end, we believe our work lays the groundwork for developing adaptive, context-aware systems by providing empirical and practical insights.
