Shiraz Khan, Jikai Ye, Gregory S. Chirikjian

Abstract
The concepts of mean (i.e., average) and covariance of a random variable are fundamental in statistics, and are used to solve real-world problems such as those that arise in robotics, computer vision, and medical imaging. On matrix Lie groups, multiple competing definitions of the mean arise, including the Euclidean, projected, distance-based (i.e., Fréchet and Karcher), group-theoretic, and parametric means. This article provides a comprehensive review of these definitions, investigates their relationships to each other, and determines the conditions under which the group-theoretic means minimize a least-squares type cost function. We also highlight the dependence of these definitions on the choice of inner product on the Lie algebra. The goal of this article is to guide practitioners in selecting an appropriate notion of the mean in applications involving matrix Lie groups.

Introduction

1.1
Background and Motivation

The concept of the
mean
(i.e., arithmetic average) of a random vector has been used for statistical applications since the works of Legendre and Gauss, who observed that the mean of a set of vectors minimizes the sum of its squared Euclidean distances to the vectors.
A modern and more sophisticated notion of the mean was studied by Élie Cartan
[
6
,
3
]
, who was interested in defining the mean of a set of samples in a Riemannian manifold. As there is generally no canonical choice of Riemannian metric for a given manifold, there is no canonical definition of the mean on an arbitrary manifold. Hence, for statistical applications involving non-Euclidean manifolds, one must choose between the various definitions of means based on additional considerations. One such consideration is the existence of a group structure on the manifold, which leads to the notion of a Lie group
[
16
,
7
,
27
]
.
The Lie groups that are most commonly encountered in applications are
matrix Lie groups
,
and are represented using a set of invertible matrices that is closed under the multiplication and inversion of matrices (which are analytic maps).
The concept of the mean in matrix Lie groups is used in state estimation
[
44
,
14
,
12
]
, generative modeling
[
34
,
36
]
, and imaging
[
72
]
, among other applications.
One expects the mean of a random variable in a matrix Lie group to transform in a natural way (as will be made precise in this article) when the underlying random variable is transformed. This ensures that when the choice of units or coordinate system is changed, the mean of a random variable transforms in accordance.
There are other considerations that are taken into account when choosing a definition of the mean for a given application, such as existence and uniqueness, ease of computation, and the ability to quantify uncertainty (e.g., via a generalized notion of the covariance matrix).

1.2
Literature Review

In the past, several works have investigated the problem of defining the
mean
of a random variable that assumes values in a non-Euclidean space, such as a Riemannian manifold or a Lie group
[
53
]
.
Means on homogeneous spaces such as spheres and symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices have also been studied
[
6
,
13
,
51
]
. However, the aforementioned works restrict their discussion to the case where a discrete set of samples are obtained from a random variable, failing to discuss the extension to continuous random variables on Lie groups, and the ensuing details related to the Haar measure of the group.
Continuous random variables on Lie groups can be represented using parametric probability density functions (pdfs)
[
44
,
61
,
37
]
or by using Monte Carlo methods such as particle filtering
[
77
]
.
With the advent of deep learning tools like normalizing flows, Moser flows, and diffusion models, it is now possible to represent fairly complicated pdfs on Lie groups

[
24
,
21
]
. Given a continuous random variable on a Lie group, one is often interested in summarizing the random variable using a mean and covariance, e.g., for downstream control and decision-making tasks
[
44
,
77
,
10
,
8
]
. Hence, the problem of defining and computing the mean of a continuous random variable on a Lie group is of practical relevance.

A secondary limitation of the existing works is that they do not explain how the various definitions of means relate to one another. For instance, the usual Euclidean notion of the mean as well as the mean computed using the
exponential parametrization
of the group (which we call the
\log
-Euclidean mean) are both commonly-used by practitioners
[
73
,
11
,
28
]
. However, the literature that discusses the intrinsic (e.g., Riemannian and group-theoretic) notions of the mean often omits the extrinsic (e.g., Euclidean and parametric) means in its discussion, as the latter are coordinate-dependent. Nevertheless, a clear and careful exposition of the differences between the various competing
definitions of means – without extensively relying on differential geometric concepts – would benefit practitioners in disciplines like robotics and computer science who are already acquainted with the language of Lie groups.

A third point which this article aims to address is that of the dependence of the definitions of means on the choice of basis and/or inner product on the Lie algebra. It is well-known that an
\Ad
-invariant inner product on the Lie algebra (if one exists) makes the group-theoretic definition of the mean coincide with the Riemannian ones. However, there is little discussion of what happens when one works with an inner product that is not
\Ad
-invariant. For instance, the geodesics corresponding to an
\Ad
-invariant inner product on
\mathfrak{so}(3)
(i.e., the Lie algebra of
SO(3)
) are rotations about a fixed axis, but the same is not true when one works with a weighted inner product on
\mathfrak{so}(3)
.
There is a similar gap in the literature when it comes to the mean of a random variable in
SE(3)
(i.e., the Special Euclidean group, which comprises all the rigid transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
); while the works in
[
76
,
49
,
23
]
have studied the Riemannian exponential map of
SE(3)
, the implications of their findings to the problem of defining a mean for random variable in
SE(3)
have not been explored.

1.3
Contributions and Overview

In this article, we review, compare, and contrast the existing definitions of means and covariances for a random variable that takes values in a matrix Lie group. The dependence of each of these definitions on additional structure (e.g., a choice of inner product and/or basis on the Lie algebra) is clarified. In particular,
Theorems

2
and
4
address the question of whether the group-theoretic definition of mean (which is defined using the Lie-theoretic log map) minimizes a cost function similar to the Euclidean, Fréchet, and Karcher means. The examples of
SO(d)
and
SE(d)
(where
d=2
or
3
) are used to illustrate the main ideas. Finally, the appendices explain the technical aspects of defining and working with pdfs on Lie groups, keeping the discussion accessible to practitioners.

The organization of this article is as follows.
Section

2
introduces the various definitions of means on Lie groups.
In
Section

3
, we clarify the relationship between the group-theoretic and Karcher means, which coincide under certain (sometimes unsatisfiable) conditions.
Section

4

Finally,
Section

5
discusses the examples of
SO(d)
and

in detail. To keep the main text accessible and easy to follow, the technical details of calculus and Riemannian geometry on Lie groups are deferred to the appendices.

Definitions of Means

2.1
Euclidean Mean

Given a set of
N
vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
its Euclidean mean is defined as the vector
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, which is also known as the arithmetic average.
Let
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{W}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}}
denote the weighted Euclidean norm, where
\mathbf{W}
is any symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix.
It is known that the Euclidean mean has the following characterization:

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}\big(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\big)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\underset{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\arg\min}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{y}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{W}}

(1)

in which the second equality holds irrespective of the choice of (positive definite) weighting matrix,
\mathbf{W}
.
That the second equality of
eq.

1

holds irrespective of the choice of weighting matrix
\mathbf{W}
is a property that, as we will show in
Section

3
, does not extend to Lie groups.
Suppose we have a random variable
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
in

whose probability density function (pdf) is

via an integral:

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\underset{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\arg\min}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{W}}f(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}.

(2)

Observe that (
1
) arises as a special case of (
2
) for the case where
f
is given by a sum of Dirac delta functions:
f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
1
1

1

This can be made rigorous by considering instead of

, a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By replacing

1
)

Throughout this article, we use Dirac delta functions as a notational shorthand for converting between probability measures that are absolutely continuous w.r.t. the integration measure (whose pdfs are continuous functions on

) and counting measures (whose pdf is technically not a function, but a

The Euclidean mean is alternatively referred to as the

,

It is clear that the Euclidean mean generalizes to vector spaces other than

denote the space on

If

is a matrix-valued random variable in

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}}\mathbf{A}f(\mathbf{A})d\mathbf{A}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{A}f(\mathbf{A})\,dA_{11}\,dA_{12}\cdots dA_{mm},

where

To see the relationship between

\displaystyle\vecn:\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m^{2}}

Furthermore, we have

of the matrix

Using

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})=\underset{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}}{\arg\min}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}}\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\rVert^{2}\,f(\mathbf{A})d\mathbf{A}.

(4)

The above can be generalized to other weighted variants of the Frobenius norm. For instance, one can consider the weighted Frobenius norm,
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})}\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\rVert

\mathbf{Q}

Finally, we point out that the discussion above readily extends to the case where

is a random variable in

Distance-Based Means

The Euclidean mean of a random vector, defined via (

\mathcal{D}:G\times G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

5

5

2.4
Group-Theoretic Means

The group-theoretic exponential map of

\exp

\mathfrak{g}

In general, this map is neither injective nor surjective.

\displaystyle\int_{G^{\prime}}\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}_{m},

1.

\int_{G^{\prime}}f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=1

2.

\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})

\mathbf{g}\in G^{\prime}

Hereafter, we write

to denote a subset of

8
8

8

The set

\bm{\upmu}\textup{S}_{G}\coloneqq\{\bm{\upmu}\mathbf{g}\,|\,\mathbf{g}\in\textup{S}_{G}\}
is a neighborhood of
\bm{\upmu}
, and
\textup{S}_{G}
is the domain of the
\log

Using the identity

3
Left, Right, and Bi-Invariant Distance Functions

The group-theoretic means
\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
are defined without reference to an inner product or basis, while the Fréchet and Karcher means depend on the choice of distance function or Riemannian metric.
While there is a great degree of freedom when defining distances or metrics on
G
, one typically works with distances or metrics that are
invariant
w.r.t. the group operations. Invariant distances and metrics manifest as physically meaningful quantities in the applications of Lie groups to rigid-body dynamics
[
75
,
76
,
17
]
, which motivates their use.
A distance function is said to be
left-invariant
(resp.,
right-invariant
) if, for all
\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\mathbf{h}\in G
,

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2})

=

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}_{2})
(resp.,
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2})=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{g}_{1}\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g}_{2}\mathbf{h})
). For example, a
bi-invariant
(i.e., both left and right-invariant) distance function for
SO(d)
is
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{Q}\rVert
, where
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q}\in SO(d)
and
d
is a positive integer. Similarly, a left-invariant Riemannian metric is one whose geodesic distance function satisfies
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}_{2})
.
11
11

11

See Appendix
C
for an equivalent characterization of left-invariant Riemannian metrics, which is stated in terms of the differential of the left multiplication map,
L_{\mathbf{h}}:\mathbf{g}\mapsto\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}
.

While left- and right-invariant Riemannian metrics always exist on a given Lie group, there may not exist any bi-invariant Riemannian metrics;
for example,
SE(d)
does not admit any bi-invariant Riemannian metrics
[
58
, Prop. 7.2]
.

To define an invariant Riemannian metric on
G
, one first defines an
inner product
on the Lie algebra,
\mathfrak{g}
.
An inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is a symmetric bilinear map
\langle{\,\cdot\,},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the axioms in
[
32
, Sec. 5.1]
.
A choice of inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
induces a
norm
on
\mathfrak{g}
, given by
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{\mathfrak{g}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{X}}\rangle}
. The inner product is said to be
\Ad
-invariant
if

\displaystyle\langle{\Ad_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{X}},{\Ad_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{Y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\rangle

(14)

for all
\mathbf{g}\in G
and
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathfrak{g}
.
As an example, consider the
Frobenius inner product
for
\mathfrak{so}(d)
defined as
\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(d)}\coloneqq\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Y})
, which is
\Ad
-invariant.
However, if we consider a different inner product for
\mathfrak{so}(d)
, defined as
\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(d)}^{\prime}\coloneqq\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{S})
(where
\mathbf{S}
is an arbitrary SPD matrix), such an inner product is
not

\Ad
-invariant in general. Similarly, if we extend the Frobenius inner product to all of
\mathfrak{gl}(d,\mathbb{R})
as
\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{gl}(d,\mathbb{R})}\coloneqq\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Y})
, then this is not an
\Ad
-invariant inner product, since

\langle{\Ad_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{X}},{\Ad_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{Y}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{gl}(d,\mathbb{R})}=\tr\left(({\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}^{-1})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{g}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{g}^{-1}\right)\neq\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Y})

in general, where
\mathbf{g}\in GL(d,\mathbb{R})
.
We remark that the induced norm of the Frobenius inner product coincides with the Frobenius norm:
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{\mathfrak{gl}(d,\mathbb{R})}=\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert
.

Once an inner product is defined on
\mathfrak{g}
, it can be uniquely extended to define a left-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
, as detailed in
Appendix

C
. If the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\Ad
-invariant, then the corresponding left-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
will be bi-invariant.
The converse direction also holds, so that bi-invariant Riemannian metrics on
G
and
\Ad
-invariant inner products on
\mathfrak{g}
are in one-to-one correspondence.
Given a bi-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
(equivalently, an
\Ad
-invariant inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
), the Riemannian log map is related to the Lie-theoretic
\log
map as follows (cf. Lemma
9
):

\displaystyle\textup{Log}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{g}\log(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{h})=\log(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}^{-1})\mathbf{g}\quad\forall\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in G,

(15)

where a capital ‘
L
’ is used to distinguish the Riemannian log map from the Lie-theoretic one. Consequently, the geodesic distance function of a bi-invariant metric is
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=\lVert\log(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{h})\rVert_{\mathfrak{g}}
. For instance, the Frobenius inner product for
\mathfrak{so}(d)
defines a bi-invariant Riemannian metric for
SO(d)
whose geodesic distance function is
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})=\lVert\log(\mathbf{R}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q})\rVert
, where
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q}\in SO(d)
.
12
12

12

In the literature, the inner product on
\mathfrak{so}(d)
is sometimes defined as
\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(d)}^{\prime}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Y})
, such that the geodesic distance function incurs an additional factor of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}

[
50
]
.

In the absence of a bi-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
, the equality in (
15
) generally does not hold.
13
13

13

It is possible to endow the Lie group with its canonical
Cartan-Schouten
connection (also called the
0
-connection), which defines a notion of a ‘geodesic’ that satisfies a property analogous to
eq.

15
. This is the approach taken in
[
57
,
59
]
. However, these ‘geodesics’ do not come with a corresponding notion of
geodesic distance
unless the aforementioned connection is the Levi-Civita connection of some underlying Riemannian metric. Thus, the means defined in
[
57
,
59
]
, although stated using differential geometric concepts, are the same as the group-theoretic means considered in this article.

For Lie groups like
SE(d)
, there do not exist
any
Riemannian metrics for which (
15
) holds
[
75
]
,
[
22
, Lem. 3.1]
. Even if
G
does admit a bi-invariant metric, the application at hand may demand the use of a Riemannian metric for
G
that is not bi-invariant.
14
14

14

For instance,
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\in SO(3)
can be used to describe the (uncertain) orientation of a rigid-body. In this case, a weighted inner product (i.e., one that differs from the Frobenius inner product) can be used to model the different moments of inertia of the rigid-body along its principal axes. The geodesics of the corresponding left-invariant Riemannian metric are not (in general) rotations about a fixed axis. Rather, they are the solutions to Euler’s equations of rigid-body motion
[
47
, Sec. 1.2]
.

In what follows, we illustrate the relevance of bi-invariant Riemannian metrics to the problem of defining means on Lie groups.

Remark 1
(Bi-Invariant Riemannian Metrics).

A complete characterization of the connected Lie groups for which bi-invariant metrics exist is given in
[
48
, Lemma 7.5]
: the Lie groups that admit bi-invariant metrics are those that are compact, Abelian (i.e., commutative), or a direct product of a compact Lie group and an Abelian Lie group.
If
G
is a simple Lie group, then the bi-invariant metric is unique up to scaling
[
48
, Lemma 7.6]
. More generally, there are as many bi-invariant metrics as there are
\Ad
-invariant inner products on
\mathfrak{g}
(cf. Lemma
9
).

Let
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis for
\mathfrak{g}
.
Any vector
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
can be uniquely expressed as
\mathbf{X}=x^{i}\mathbf{E}_{i}
, where
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and the summation convention is used. The inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
can be described using a SPD matrix
\mathbf{W}
, defined such that
\mathbf{W}_{ij}=\langle\mathbf{E}_{i},\mathbf{E}_{j}\rangle
.
15
15

15

Alternatively, one way of
defining
an inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is by choosing the basis
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
first, and then specifying the SPD matrix,
\mathbf{W}
.
Recalling the definition of the map
(\,\cdot\,)^{\vee}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined in
section

2.5
,
we observe that

\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sqrt{\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{X}}\rangle}_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sqrt{\langle{x^{i}\mathbf{E}_{i}},{x^{j}\mathbf{E}_{j}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}}=\sqrt{\,{x^{i}}x^{j}\mathbf{W}_{ij}}=\lVert\mathbf{X}^{\vee}\rVert_{\mathbf{W}}.

The function
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\coloneqq\lVert\log(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{h})\rVert_{\mathfrak{g}}=\lVert\log(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{h})^{\vee}\rVert_{\mathbf{W}}
satisfies some of the axioms of a distance function. In particular, we have
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{g})
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})=0
if and only if
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{h}
.
Given these observations, we ask whether the group-theoretic means are critical points (i.e., local minimizers) of the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{h}\in G}{\textrm{minimize}}\left(\int_{G^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})^{2}f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}\right).

(16)

That is, we ask whether a group-theoretic mean, like a Karcher mean, locally minimizes a cost function similar to
eq.

9
. If the inner product is
\Ad
-invariant, then
\mathcal{L}
is nothing but the geodesic distance function
\mathcal{R}
, so that (

\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
(i.e.,
\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})=\mu_{K}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}},\mathcal{R})
).
It turns out that
\Ad

\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
to be critical points of (
16

Theorem 2
.

Let
\langle{\,\cdot\,},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
be an inner product on
\mathfrak{g}

•

If the inner product is
\Ad

is the set of critical points of (

•

If the inner product is not
\Ad

\displaystyle{X}\mathcal{C}(\bm{\upmu})

Consider the random variable

\log(\mathbf{h})
appears an odd number of times can be canceled to yield

\displaystyle{\log(\mathbf{h}^{-1})^{\vee}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\frac{1}{2}\bm{\ad}_{\log(\mathbf{h}^{-1})}\mathbf{X}^{\vee}+{\log(\mathbf{h})^{\vee}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\frac{1}{2}\bm{\ad}_{\log(\mathbf{h})}\mathbf{X}^{\vee}={\log(\mathbf{h})^{\vee}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\bm{\ad}_{\log(\mathbf{h})}\mathbf{X}^{\vee}=0.

Since the preceding equation should hold true for all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
, the condition for the point
\mathbf{I}_{m}
to minimize (
16
) is
\bm{\ad}_{\log(\mathbf{h})}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\log(\mathbf{h})^{\vee}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times 1}
. Lemma
9
shows that this holds for an arbitrary element
\mathbf{h}\in G
if and only if the inner product is
\Ad
-invariant.

In addition to offering an alternative interpretation to the group-theoretic means and clarifying their relationship to the distance-based (i.e., Karcher and Fréchet) means, Theorem
2
also offers a strategy to choose between the group-theoretic means in
\mu_{G}
. If
\mu_{G}(\mathbf{g})
has more than one element, then one can choose the element(s) of
\mu_{G}(\mathbf{g})
that minimize (
16
) and discard the remaining elements (i.e., those that locally, but not globally minimize (
16
)). For example, it is shown in
[
50
, Fig. 4.1]
that, while a random variable on
SO(2)
can have multiple group-theoretic means, it is often the case that there is a
unique
group-theoretic mean
that minimizes (
16
).

4
Properties of Means

Each of the definitions of a
mean
that were introduced in
Section

2
has distinctive properties that determine its suitability for a given statistical problem or application.
In this section, we discuss some of these properties, as well as the main considerations that are taken into account when choosing the most appropriate definition for a given application.

4.1
Compatibility with Group Operations

Given a (fixed) group element
\mathbf{h}\in G
, we can define the left-shifted random variable,
\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
. The pdf of
\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
is the function
\mathbf{g}\mapsto f(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\mathbf{g})
.
16
16

16

Here, it is assumed that the pdfs of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
and
\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
are each defined w.r.t. the left Haar measure (c.f.
Appendix

B
).

If
\bm{\upmu}
is a mean of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
, one can ask whether
\mathbf{h}\bm{\upmu}
is a mean of
\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
. This property is called
left-invariance
, and it holds for group-theoretic means. To see this, observe that

\displaystyle\int_{G^{\prime}}\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\int_{G^{\prime}}\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{h}^{-1}\mathbf{g})f(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\int_{G^{\prime}}\log((\mathbf{h}\bm{\upmu})^{-1}\mathbf{g})f(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}.

Hence, if
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
, then
\mathbf{h}\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
. Succinctly, we can write
\mu_{G}(\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}})=\mathbf{h}\bm{\upmu}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
for all
\mathbf{h}\in G
.
When
G=(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued random variable, left-invariance of
\mu_{G}
reduces to the well-known property:
\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{h})=\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{h}
, where
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The Lie group
G
is said to be
unimodular
if the left Haar measure is (in addition to being left-invariant) right-invariant. Unimodularity (i.e., the existence of a bi-invariant Haar measure) is a weaker condition than the existence of a bi-invariant metric. In particular,
SE(d)
and
GL(d,\mathbb{R})
are unimodular, which can be verified using the conditions for unimodularity that are presented in
Appendix

B
. When
G
is unimodular, the pdf of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{h}
is
f(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h}^{-1})
. From (
12
) and the invariance properties of the Haar measure, it follows that the group-theoretic means are right-invariant as well.
For this reason,
[
58
,
59
]
refers to the group-theoretic means as
bi-invariant means
.

The extrinsic Euclidean mean satisfies

\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{g}})=\mathbf{A}\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
and
\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{A})=\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\mathbf{A}

for any matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, which implies that it is bi-invariant.
The projected mean is left- (resp., right-) equivariant if
\mathcal{P}
commutes with the left- (resp., right-) multiplication map of
G
. For example, if
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{h}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{A})
for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\mathbf{h}\in G
, then we can show that the projected mean is left-invariant:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\mathbf{h}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}))=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{h}\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}))=\mathbf{h}\mathcal{P}(\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})).

(20)

For example, it is readily verified that the map
\mathcal{P}_{SO}
introduced in
Section

2.2

is bi-invariant, and therefore defines a bi-invariant projected mean for
SO(d)
.
A set of Fréchet means (defined with respect to a particular choice of distance function) is left-, right-, or bi-invariant if the corresponding distance function has the desired invariance property.
Similarly, Karcher means are left-, right-, or bi-invariant if the Riemannian metric used to define them has the corresponding invariance
[
59
]
, but the same does not hold for a general Riemannian metric.

4.2
Defining a Notion of Covariance

One of the main applications of the concept of a mean is to summarize the random variable
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
. In this context, it is called a
first-order statistic
. While a first-order statistic describes the ‘center of mass’ of a pdf, a
second-order statistic
characterizes its dispersion/spread about the center of mass. In the case of the Euclidean mean, the most commonly used second-order statistics are the variance and the covariance.

The
\vecn(\,\cdot\,)
operation introduced in
Section

2.1
, can be used to define an
m^{2}\times m^{2}
matrix which we refer to as the
extrinsic Euclidean covariance
matrix:

\displaystyle\Cov_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\coloneqq\int_{G}{\vecn\big(\,\mathbf{g}-\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\big)\,\vecn\big(\,\mathbf{g}-\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\big)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}.

(21)

An extrinsic covariance for the projected mean can be defined similarly, by replacing
\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
with
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}))
. In either case, the dimension of the covariance matrix may far exceed the intrinsic dimension of the Lie group.
The trace of the covariance can be used to define the
Euclidean variance
:

\displaystyle\textrm{var}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\tr\big(\Cov_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\big)}={\int_{G}\left\lVert\vecn\big(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}-\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\big)\right\rVert^{2}f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}}={\int_{G}\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{g}}-\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\rVert^{2}f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}}.

(22)

If
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{D})
is a Fréchet mean, one can define the
Fréchet variance
of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
as the minimal value of the cost function (
9
), which is attained at
\bm{\upmu}
:

\displaystyle\textrm{var}_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\int_{G^{\prime}}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\upmu},\mathbf{g})^{2}f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}},

(23)

where the choice of
\bm{\upmu}
in
\mu_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{D})
does not matter.
However, since the definition of a Fréchet mean does not (in general) involve vector operations, there is no natural notion of a
covariance
; one can describe the spread of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
about its mean using
\textrm{var}_{F}
, but the directional aspects of this spread cannot be captured using
\textrm{var}_{F}
.

Given the basis
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the group-theoretic covariance and variance are defined as
[
8
,
69
]

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{G^{\prime}}\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})^{\vee}{\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})^{\vee}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}

(24)

\displaystyle\textrm{var}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\tr\big(\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})\big)}={\int_{G^{\prime}}\big\lVert\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})^{\vee}\big\rVert^{2}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}},

(25)

where
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
.
While the definition of
\mu_{G}
did not depend on the choice of basis for
\mathfrak{g}
, the definitions in
eq.

24
and
eq.

25
do depend on the choice of basis. In particular, if we choose an orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{g}
(such that
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
), then we have

\displaystyle\textrm{var}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})={\int_{G^{\prime}}\lVert\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})\rVert_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}},

(26)

which is precisely the cost function that was considered in
Section

3
. Thus, if the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\Ad
-invariant, and if one defines the covariance of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
using an orthonormal basis, then the group-theoretic means are minimizers of the map
\bm{\upmu}\mapsto\textrm{var}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})
.

The Karcher covariance and variance can be defined similarly, by letting
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{K}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{R})
and using
\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\textup{Log}_{\bm{\upmu}}(\mathbf{g})
in place of
\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})
in (
24
).
If the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\Ad
-invariant and
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is orthonormal, then the Karcher covariance (defined w.r.t. the corresponding bi-invariant metric) is the same as the group-theoretic covariance.
When using a parametric mean (as defined in
Section

2.5
), the Euclidean covariance of the vector-valued random variable,
\psi^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
, can be used as a measure of dispersion.

4.3
Compatibility with Symmetries

Given a point
\bm{\upmu}\in G
, we say that the pdf
f
is
symmetric about
\bm{\upmu}
if it has the property
f(\bm{\upmu}\mathbf{g})=f(\bm{\upmu}\mathbf{g}^{-1})
(equivalently,
f(\mathbf{g}\bm{\upmu})=f(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\bm{\upmu})
) for all
\mathbf{g}\in G
. Let
\rho
denote the pdf of the shifted random variable
\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
, such that
\rho(\mathbf{g})=f(\bm{\upmu}\mathbf{g})
. It follows that
f
is symmetric about
\bm{\upmu}
if and only if
\rho
is symmetric about the identity element, i.e.,
\rho(\mathbf{g})=\rho(\mathbf{g}^{-1})
.
As the forthcoming theorem shows, if
G
is unimodular, then the symmetry of
f
about
\bm{\upmu}
implies that
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
. Furthermore, the group-theoretic means minimize a cost function similar to (
16
) wherein the norm on
\mathfrak{g}
is defined in terms of the group-theoretic covariance. Unlike the analysis in
Section

3
, the analysis here does not assume that the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\Ad
-invariant.
To state the result, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\bm{\upeta}
be a vector-valued function on
G
that satisfies
\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g})=-\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}^{-1})
for all
\mathbf{g}\in G
, where
\mathbf{h}\in G
is some (fixed) point. If
G
is unimodular, then

\int_{G}\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proof.

Since
G
is unimodular, the Haar integral is invariant under left multiplication as well as the inversion map
\mathbf{g}\mapsto\mathbf{g}^{-1}
. Using these facts, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{G}\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\int_{G}\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=-\int_{G}\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}^{-1})d\mathbf{g}=-\int_{G}\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=-\int_{G}\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}.

Theorem 4
.

If
G
is unimodular and
f
is symmetric about
\bm{\upmu}
, then
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
. Furthermore, if
\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})
is invertible, then
\bm{\upmu}
is a critical point of the problem

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{h}\in G}{\textrm{minimize}}\left(\int_{G^{\prime}}\lVert\log(\mathbf{h}^{-1}\mathbf{g})^{\vee}\rVert^{2}_{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}\right),

(27)

where
\bm{\Sigma}\coloneqq{\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})}
.

Proof.

First, we observe that the function
\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})\coloneqq\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})
satisfies
\bm{\upeta}(\bm{\upmu}\mathbf{g})=-\bm{\upeta}(\bm{\upmu}\mathbf{g}^{-1})
(since
\log(\mathbf{g})=-\log(\mathbf{g}^{-1})
),
whereupon
lemma

3
is used to conclude that
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
.
To show the second part of the theorem, we proceed similarly to the proof of
theorem

2
. The condition for minimization of (
27
) is

\displaystyle\int_{G^{\prime}}\,{\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})^{\vee}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{\log}({\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})})\mathbf{X}^{\vee}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{G^{\prime}}\,{\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})^{\vee}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\ad}_{\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})}\mathbf{X}^{\vee}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}=0

\forall\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
, where we used the fact that each of the terms of
\mathbf{J}_{\log}({\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})})
in which
\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})
appears an odd number of times vanish due to
lemma

3
.
Expressing
\bm{\upeta}(\mathbf{g})
as
\eta^{i}(\mathbf{g})\mathbf{E}_{i}
,
the condition for minimization can be written using components, as

\displaystyle\int_{G^{\prime}}\,\eta^{i}(\mathbf{g})\Sigma^{ij}\eta^{k}(\mathbf{g})C_{k\ell}^{j}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}=0\quad\forall\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\},

(28)

where
\Sigma^{ij}
is the
(i,j)^{th}
element of
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
and
\{C_{ij}^{k}\}_{i,j,k=1}^{n}
are the
structure constants
of
\mathfrak{g}
; see
Appendix

A
for details. Let
\Sigma_{ij}=\int_{G^{\prime}}\eta^{i}(\mathbf{g})\eta^{j}(\mathbf{g})f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}
be the
(i,j)^{th}
component of
\bm{\Sigma}
. We can rewrite
eq.

28
as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{ij}C_{k\ell}^{j}\int_{G^{\prime}}\,\eta^{i}(\mathbf{g})\eta^{k}(\mathbf{g})\,f(\mathbf{g})\,d\mathbf{g}=\Sigma^{ij}C_{k\ell}^{j}\Sigma_{ki}=C_{k\ell}^{j}\delta_{jk}=C_{k\ell}^{k}=0

(29)

\forall\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Here,
\delta_{ij}
is the
(i,j)^{th}
element of
\mathbf{I}_{n}
.
The last equality in (
29
) is precisely the condition for the unimodularity of
G
(c.f. App.
B
). Hence, the conditions for
\bm{\upmu}
to be a critical point of (
27
) are that
G
is unimodular and
\bm{\Sigma}
is invertible.

The following corollary follows readily from the proof of
theorem

4
.

Corollary 5
.

If
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
and if the pdf
f
is sufficiently concentrated (so that terms on the order of
O(\lVert\log(\bm{\upmu}^{-1}\mathbf{g})^{\vee}\rVert^{3})
may be neglected), then
\bm{\upmu}
is a critical point of (
27
).

Therefore, a group-theoretic mean
\bm{\upmu}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
will locally minimize the cost function given in
eq.

16
(which is defined w.r.t. an inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
) if either of the following conditions hold:

1.

(
theorem

2
) the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\Ad
-invariant, or

2.

(
theorem

4
)
\bm{\Sigma}
is invertible,
f
is symmetric about
\bm{\upmu}
, and the inner product is defined such that
\mathbf{W}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
,

where
\bm{\Sigma}\coloneqq\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})
.

4.4
Compatibility with Convolution

Given a pair of independent random variables
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
in
G
, we consider the problem of characterizing their group-theoretic product,
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
.
Let the pdfs of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
(as defined w.r.t. the left Haar measure) be
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively. When
G
is unimodular, the pdf of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
is given by the group-theoretic
convolution
of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, defined as follows
[
18
,
19
]
:

\displaystyle\big(f_{1}\ast f_{2}\big)(\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{G}f_{1}(\mathbf{g})f_{2}(\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{h})d\mathbf{g}.

(30)

Due to the computational difficulty of evaluating the integral in (
30
), one is often interested in computing the mean and covariance of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
as a function of the means and covariances of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
. This bypasses the computationally difficult step of evaluating
f_{1}\ast f_{2}
, and is amenable to real-time applications where the mean and covariance of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
may be needed for time-sensitive tasks.

In the case of
G=(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
, the group-theoretic product of random variables
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
is their vector sum
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
, whose mean and covariance can be described in terms of the mean and covariance of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})=\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1})+\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}),\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})=\bm{\Sigma}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1})+\bm{\Sigma}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}),

respectively.
The group-theoretic means of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
satisfy this property approximately, in the following sense. Let
\bm{\upmu}_{1}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1})
and
\bm{\upmu}_{2}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2})
. If the pdf is sufficiently concentrated (such that third-order terms can be neglected), then it holds that
\bm{\upmu}_{1}\bm{\upmu}_{2}\in\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2})
. The group-theoretic covariance of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
about this mean is

\bm{\Sigma}_{12}\approx\bm{\Ad}_{\bm{\upmu}_{2}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}{\bm{\Ad}_{\bm{\upmu}_{2}}^{-1}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+\bm{\Sigma}_{2},

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\coloneqq\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1};\bm{\upmu}_{1})
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{2}\coloneqq\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2};\bm{\upmu}_{2})
, and
\bm{\Sigma}_{12}\coloneqq\bm{\Sigma}_{G}(\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}\,;\bm{\upmu}_{1}\bm{\upmu}_{2})
.
Higher-order correction terms can be introduced when a greater degree of accuracy is required
[
69
,
56
]
.

Remark 6
.

One can also consider a stochastic process on
G
, which can be used to describe the continuous-time evolution of uncertainty in a random variable. Suppose a stochastic process
(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
G
is defined via a stochastic differential equation (SDE). The corresponding family of pdfs
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(where
f_{t}
is the pdf of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t}
) satisfies a partial differential equation, known as the Fokker-Planck equation
[
16
]
. While it is generally infeasible to solve the Fokker-Planck equation explicitly, it is possible to update the group-theoretic mean and covariance of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{t}
using ordinary differential equations
[
56
,
74
]
.

Analogous properties hold for the Euclidean mean as well. Using the invariance properties of the Haar integral, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\int_{G}\mathbf{g}_{1}\mathbf{g}_{2}\,f_{1}(\mathbf{g}_{1})f_{2}(\mathbf{g}_{2})\,d\mathbf{g}_{1}\,d\mathbf{g}_{2}=\bm{\upmu}_{E}({\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}})\,\bm{\upmu}_{E}({\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}}).

(31)

It is clear that the projected mean does not have an analogous property, since
\mathcal{P}
is generally a nonlinear map that does not commute with integration. In general, the parametric, Fréchet, and Karcher means (defined w.r.t. an arbitrary Riemannian metric) of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
are also unrelated to the corresponding means of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}
.

4.5
Computational Feasability

For some Lie groups, such as
SO(d)
and
SE(d)
(
d=2
or
3
), the group-theoretic
\exp
and
\log
maps have closed-form expressions
[
16
,
7
]
. More generally, the matrix exponential and logarithm functions must be used to evaluate
\exp
and
\log
, respectively; these functions are implemented in most linear algebra toolboxes. On the other hand, the Riemannian exponential map is generally computed by solving ordinary differential equations (ODEs). While the geodesics of an arbitrary Riemannian metric are solutions to a system of second-order ODEs known as the
geodesic equation
, the geodesics of a left-invariant Riemannian metric are solutions of first-order ODEs (c.f.
Section

C.2
), which simplifies their implementation. In either case, computational tools such as
geomstats
(for Python)
[
30
]
and
Manifolds.jl
(for Julia)
[
5
]
can be used to evaluate the Riemannian exponential and log maps on Lie groups. Nevertheless, if a bi-invariant metric for
G
exists, then one prefers to work with the bi-invariant metric due to the computational ease of implementing the Lie-theoretic
\exp
and
\log
maps.

Consider the map
\bm{\upmu}\mapsto\bm{\upmu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{LE}}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu})
, where
\bm{\upmu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{LE}}
is the log-Euclidean mean. As discussed in
Section

2.5
, a fixed point of this map (i.e., a point
\bm{\upmu}^{\star}
that satisfies
\bm{\upmu}^{\star}=\bm{\upmu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{LE}}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu}^{\star})
)
is a group-theoretic mean. Hence, the fixed point iteration
\bm{\upmu}_{k+1}=\bm{\upmu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{LE}}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\bm{\upmu}_{k})
can be used for computing the group-theoretic means, as noted in
[
57
,
69
]
.
A slight modification of this algorithm (that replaces the Lie-theoretic exponential and log maps with the Riemannian ones) yields a procedure for computing Karcher means, albeit with the added computational cost of solving the geodesic equation.
Fréchet means can be computed using optimization algorithms on Lie groups, such as gradient descent.
There also exist second-order algorithms for computing group-theoretic and Fréchet means
[
54
,
2
]
.

5
Examples of Lie Groups

In this section, we consider some of the prototypical examples of finite-dimensional Lie groups that typically arise in engineering and scientific applications.

5.1
SO(2)
and
U(1)

The
Orthogonal
group
O(2)
is the set of
2\times 2
orthogonal matrices, such that
\mathbf{R}\in O(2)\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{R}^{-1}=\mathbf{R}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
. However, when viewed as a manifold,
O(2)
has two disconnected components – there exist pairs of elements
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q}\in O(2)
with
\det(\mathbf{R})=+1
and
\det(\mathbf{Q})=-1
that lie in different connected components of
O(2)
, such that there is no geodesic in
O(2)
connecting
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Since
\det(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{Q})=-1
, the matrix
\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}
is outside the domain of the group-theoretic
\log
map as well. Due to these difficulties, one typically restricts to the
Special Orthogonal
group
SO(2)
, which comprises the elements of
O(2)
whose determinant is
+1
, to ensure that the group-theoretic and Karcher means are well-defined.

SO(2)
is isomorphic to the
Unitary
group
U(1)
, which can be viewed as the set of unitary matrices in
{GL}(1,\mathbb{C})
, i.e., the unit complex numbers. That is, the anti-clockwise rotation by
\theta
radians can be represented as the unit complex number
e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\in U(1)
, or as the
2\times 2
rotation matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\in SO(2).

(32)

In this sense,
SO(2)
and
U(1)
can each be identified with the unit circle of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, denoted as
S^{1}
(i.e., the one-dimensional sphere).
For this reason, the theory of random variables on these Lie groups is often referred to as
circular
statistics.
Since points on the circle correspond bijectively to the
Direction of Arrival (DoA)
of a wireless signal being received at a sensor array, the uncertainty in the DOA can be modeled using circular statistics
[
33
,
71
]
.
The identification of
SO(2)
with the unit circle is also useful for defining pdfs on the circle, such as the wrapped Gaussian, wrapped Cauchy, von Mises, Fisher, and Bingham distributions
[
37
,
35
,
45
]
.

Given a random variable
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
in
SO(2)
, the matrix
\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
is generally not an element of
SO(2)
. Nevertheless, we can assume that it is invertible (if not, an arbitrarily small amount of noise can be added to the data to ensure its invertibility). Given an invertible matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we can consider the map
\mathcal{P}_{SO}(\mathbf{A})\coloneqq(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
that extracts the rotation matrix in the polar decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
. This defines a bi-invariant projected mean for
SO(2)
that is frequently used in the literature
[
50
,
62
]
. Additional care needs to be taken to ensure that the projected Euclidean mean has a positive determinant, e.g., by multiplying
\mathcal{P}_{SO}(\mathbf{A})
with
\mathrm{sign}(\det(\mathbf{A}))
.

To define the Fréchet mean on
SO(2)
, one can consider the Euclidean distance between points in
SO(2)
, which is the function
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{Q}\rVert
.
The resulting Fréchet mean is called the
induced arithmetic mean
in
[
62
]
, and is shown to be the same as the projected mean defined via
\mathcal{P}_{SO}
above. The Riemannian Fréchet mean is defined using the geodesic distance between points in
SO(2)
, which (for the Frobenius inner product introduced in
Section

3
)
is
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})=\lVert\log(\mathbf{R}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q})\rVert
.
17
17

17

In fact, since
SO(2)
is Abelian,
\Ad_{\mathbf{R}}
is the identity map on
\mathfrak{g}
for all
\mathbf{R}\in SO(2)
. It follows that any inner product on
\mathfrak{so}(2)
is
\Ad

\mathfrak{so}(2)
amounts to a choice of units for measuring angles; since
\mathfrak{so}(2)
is one-dimensional, the weighting “matrix”
\mathbf{W}
is in this case a positive number.
It follows that the group-theoretic means are critical points of the Riemannian Fréchet mean defined by
\mathcal{R}
. They are not unique; if the pdf of
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
is symmetric about the identity element (i.e.,
f(\mathbf{R})=f(\mathbf{R}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})
), it can be shown that
\mathbf{I}_{2}

-\mathbf{I}_{2}
are both in
\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})

Remark 7
.

If we identify
SO(2)
with
U(1)
via the map
\mathbf{R}(\theta)\mapsto e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
, then
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})
is nothing but the chordal distance (i.e., the length of the chord) between

is the arclength between
\mathbf{R}

5.2
SO(3)
and
SU(2)

The
Special Unitary
Lie group
SU(2)
is isomorphic to the group of unit quaternions. The unit quaternions (and therefore,
SU(2)
) can be visualized as the unit sphere
S^{3}
of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
Each unit quaternion can be mapped to a matrix in
SO(3)
, due to which quaternions are used extensively in fields like computer graphics and spaceflight dynamics
[
60
,
46
,
67
]
to encode orientations of rigid bodies.
Since the quaternions
\mathbf{q}
and
-\mathbf{q}
both map to the same rotation, we say that
SU(2)
is a
double cover
of
SO(3)
. While
SO(3)
and
SU(2)
have isomorphic Lie algebras (i.e.,
\mathfrak{so}(3)\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)
), they are not isomorphic as Lie groups.

Due to the identification of
SU(2)
with
S^{3}
, the existing literature on
spherical statistics

[
13
]
, which deals with pdfs on spheres, can be applied to the study of statistical distributions on
SU(2)
. In particular, the function
\mathcal{P}_{S^{3}}:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{x}/\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert
can be used to project points from
\mathbb{R}^{4}\backslash\{0\}
onto
S^{3}
. Similar to circular statistics, in spherical statistics, the Fisher and Bingham distributions can be defined as pdfs on
S^{3}
.
However, when describing
SO(3)
-valued random variables, one should ensure that the pdf thus defined on
S^{3}
is antipodally symmetric (as in the case of the Bingham distribution). That is, the pdf on
S^{3}
should have the same value at
\mathbf{q}
and
-\mathbf{q}
, as these points (viewed as quaternions) correspond to the same element in
SO(3)
.

The projection function also provides a way to define the mean of quaternions. However, the projected mean of quaternions has undesirable features when the quaternions are meant to represent spatial rotations (i.e., elements of
SO(3)
). While points on the quaternion sphere that are (almost exactly) antipodal correspond to the same rotation, their Euclidean mean is close to the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
the projected mean is highly sensitive to perturbations of either point
[
46
]
.
Nevertheless, the quaternion-based method of averaging rotations remains popular in the state estimation literature, which is typically focussed on concentrated distributions (i.e., pdfs of random variables that have a small variance)
[
60
]
.

In
[
46
]
,
the authors propose to use a Fréchet mean on
SO(3)
rather than the projected mean of quaternions. Specifically, let
\mu_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{D})
be the Fréchet mean defined by the Euclidean distance function
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{Q}\rVert
. The Fréchet mean cost function
eq.

9
is then the same as the cost function in
eq.

4
, which is minimized by the Euclidean mean
\mu_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
. However, unlike
\mu_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
, the Fréchet means in
\mu_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{D})
are constrained to lie in
SO(3)
. It is shown in
[
50
, Propositions 3.3 and 3.5]
that
\mu_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}};\mathcal{D})
can also be viewed as a projected mean and is readily computed as
\mathcal{P}_{SO}(\mu_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}))
, where
\mathcal{P}_{SO}
is defined identically to the
SO(2)
case. Therefore, the Fréchet mean is almost surely (i.e., except in the measure-zero event that
\mu_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
is singular) unique and computable in closed-form.

As explained in
Section

3
, the Frobenius inner product
\langle{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{so}(3)}\coloneqq\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Y})
can be uniquely extended to define a bi-invariant Riemannian metric
\mathcal{R}
on
SO(3)
, whose geodesic distance function is
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{Q})=\lVert\log(\mathbf{R}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q})\rVert
.
Any point
\bm{\upmu}\in SO(3)
that satisfies

\int_{SO(3)}\log(\bm{\upmu}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R})f(\mathbf{R})d\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{0}_{3}

is an element of
\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
. Moreover, since
\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})=\mu_{K}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}};\mathcal{R})
, it follows that
\mu_{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}};\mathcal{R})\subseteq\mu_{G}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
.

5.3
SE(2)
and
SE(3)

In this section, we consider the
Special Euclidean
group
SE(d)
, focusing on the cases of
d=2
and

.
As a manifold,

is diffeomorphic to the direct product group,
SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is of the form
(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t})
(i.e., a pair of rotation matrix and translation vector). The map
\mathbf{H}
introduced in
Section

2.2
is a smooth, invertible map from

to
SE(d)
. As a Lie group,

is a

of the constituent groups (written as
SE(d)=SO(d)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t})(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{s})=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{s}),

(33)

which is different from the group operation of
SE(d)
that was illustrated in

7
.
Given an element
\mathbf{g}\in SE(3)
, we will write
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{g}}
and
\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{g}}
to refer to its rotation and translation parts, so that
\mathbf{H}\left(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{g}}\right)=\mathbf{g}
.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
be a random variable on
SE(d)
. Observe that
\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}}
is a random variable on
SO(d)
whereas
\mathbf{t}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}}
is a random variable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Hence, the Euclidean mean of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
is a function of the Euclidean means of
\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}}
and
\mathbf{t}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}}

\displaystyle\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})

\displaystyle=\int_{SE(d)}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{g}}&\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{g}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times d}&1\end{bmatrix}f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\mathbf{R}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}})&\bm{\upmu}_{E}(\mathbf{t}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}})\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times d}&1\end{bmatrix}.

(34)

Define
{\mathbf{g}}\cdot\mathbf{x}\coloneqq\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{g}}

\mathbf{g}\in SE(d)
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

a

\mathbf{g}

As discussed in
Section

,

is the mean of the random vector

. This means that the Euclidean mean is a suitable definition of mean when one is interested in the action of
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
on the vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\tilde{\mathbf{g}}

\mathbf{x}

Remark 8

The adjoint representation of

6
Conclusion

In this article, we investigated the problem of defining notions of
mean
and
covariance
for a random variable that takes values in a matrix Lie group.
The existing definitions of means on Lie groups were reviewed, and their relationships to each other were clarified. We also discussed the various properties of these means, including their dependence on additional geometric structure on the Lie algebra (such as an inner product and/or choice of basis).
The
Special Orthogonal
and
Special Euclidean
Lie groups were considered as motivating examples, as these Lie groups are frequently encountered in engineering applications.
By highlighting the subtleties involved in defining and computing means on noncommutative Lie groups, this article lays the foundation for principled statistical inference on Lie groups and serves as a guide for practitioners faced with the task of selecting an appropriate definition of mean for a given application.

A.1
Matrix Lie Groups

A
matrix Lie group
refers to an
n
-dimensional Lie group equipped with an
m
-dimensional matrix representation. For instance,
{GL}(m,\mathbb{C})
is the group of all
m\times m
invertible matrices with complex entries, and has the dimension
n=m^{2}
. The Lie algebra of
{GL}(m,\mathbb{C})
, denoted as
\mathfrak{gl}(m,\mathbb{C})
, is the space
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
(viewed as a vector space under addition) equipped with the Lie bracket
[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]\coloneqq\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}
.
We write
G\leq{GL}(m,\mathbb{C})
to indicate that
G
is a Lie subgroup of
{GL}(m,\mathbb{C})
. It can be shown that
\mathfrak{g}
is a subset of
\mathfrak{gl}(m,\mathbb{C})
that is closed under the Lie bracket. We say that
\mathfrak{g}
is
real
if it is real as a vector space; equivalently, if it is a subset of
\mathfrak{gl}(m,\mathbb{C})
that is closed under vector addition, scalar multiplication by real numbers, and the Lie bracket
[
31
]
.

The
exponential map

\exp:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow G
relates each vector
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
to a group element
\exp(\mathbf{X})\in G
. Since we have assumed that
G
is a matrix Lie group,
\exp
coincides with the matrix exponential

\exp(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i!}\mathbf{X}^{i}
whenever this sum converges. There exists an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{0}_{m}\in\mathfrak{g}
, denoted as
\textup{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, where the exponential map has a well-defined inverse, which we call the (Lie-theoretic) log map of
G

[
27
,
16
]
, also known as the
principal logarithm

[
31
]
.
The set
\textup{S}_{G}\coloneqq\exp(\textup{S}_{\mathfrak{g}})
represents the domain of the log map;
for
\mathbf{X}\in\textup{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, it holds that
\log\exp(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}
, and for
\mathbf{g}\in\textup{S}_{G}
, it holds that
\exp\log(\mathbf{g})=\mathbf{g}
.
The
\exp
and
\log
maps (in general) are not the same as the Riemannian
\textup{Exp}_{\mathbf{g}}
and
\textup{Log}_{\mathbf{g}}
maps introduced in Appendix
C
.
The exponential and log maps for some Lie groups (including
SO(d)
and
SE(d)
) can be computed using closed-form formulae
[
16
,
7
]

a basis for
\mathfrak{g}
. Given a vector
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}

\mathbf{X}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
18
18

18

G

\mathfrak{g}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{g}}\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{h}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\ad}_{[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]}=[\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{Y}}]

(39)

for all
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}\in G

A.2
Inner Products on the Lie Algebra

Let
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a basis and
\langle{\,\cdot\,},{\,\cdot\,}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
an arbitrary
inner product
for
\mathfrak{g}
.
The inner product can be described using an
n\times n
positive definite matrix
\mathbf{W}
, whose components are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle{\mathbf{E}_{i}},{\mathbf{E}_{j}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\quad\forall i,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}.

(40)

Let
\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}
be the symmetric square root of
\mathbf{W}
(whose existence and uniqueness follows from
[
32
, Thm. 7.2.6]
), i.e.,
\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{2}=\mathbf{W}
and
\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}
. Then, we can define the basis
\{{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
via

\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{ij}\mathbf{E}_{j},

where
\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{ij}\coloneqq\big(\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}\big)_{ij}
. It is straightforward to verify that
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is orthonormal w.r.t. the foregoing inner product, i.e.,
\langle{\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{E}_{j}^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\delta_{ij}
. Therefore, no loss of generality is caused by choosing to work with an orthonormal basis.
However, one often knows the structure constants and their properties in the original basis
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, in which they take a simpler form than they do in the orthonormal basis. For instance, the structure constants of
\mathfrak{so}(3)
, in the choice of basis for
\mathfrak{so}(3)
considered in
Section

5.2
, are the well-known permutation symbols (also known as Levi-Civita symbols) from multivariable calculus
[
31
, Prop. C7]
. Therefore, it is worthwhile to discuss how various quantities (e.g., structure constants) transform under the change of basis,
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\mapsto\{\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Given a vector
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
, it can be expressed in either basis as
\mathbf{X}=X^{i}\mathbf{E}_{i}={X^{\prime}}^{i}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\prime}
.
To the basis
\{\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a corresponding
{(\,\cdot\,)^{\vee}}^{\prime}
map, defined analogously to (
37
).
Similarly, let
C^{\prime k}_{ij}
denote the structure constants in the
\{\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
basis. The coefficients in either basis are related via the following formulae:

\displaystyle{X^{\prime}}^{i}=\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}_{ij}{X}^{j}\quad\text{and}\quad{C^{\prime k}_{ij}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{is}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{j\ell}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}_{kr}C^{r}_{s\ell}.

(41)

Let
\bm{\ad}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}}
be the matrix of
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}
in the
\{E^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
basis. Using the expression for
C^{\prime i}_{kj}
, we have

\displaystyle({\bm{\ad}}_{\mathbf{X}})^{\prime}_{ij}={X^{\prime}}^{k}C^{\prime i}_{kj}=\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{j\ell}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}_{ir}C^{r}_{s\ell}={X}^{s}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{j\ell}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}_{ir}(\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}})_{r\ell}.

(42)

Equation (
42
) can be rewritten as
\bm{\ad}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{X}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}
, which shows that
\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}
undergoes a similarity transformation under the change of basis. In fact, it can be shown that the matrix of
any
linear transformation from
\mathfrak{g}
to
\mathfrak{g}
changes by a similarity transformation under a change of basis, so we have
\bm{\Ad}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{g}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{X}}\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}
as well.

Appendix B
Calculus on Lie groups

Given a vector
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathfrak{g}
and a group element
\mathbf{g}\in G

\bm{\upgamma}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow G
defined by
\bm{\upgamma}(t)=\mathbf{g}\exp(t\mathbf{Z})
. At

\bm{\upgamma}(t)
has the value
\bm{\upgamma}(0)=\mathbf{g}
and the derivative (i.e., tangent vector)
\dot{\bm{\upgamma}}(0)=\mathbf{g}\mathbf{Z}
. The space
T_{\mathbf{g}}G\coloneqq\{\mathbf{g}\mathbf{Z}\,|\,\mathbf{Z}\in\mathfrak{g}\}
can be given a vector space structure, at which point it is called the tangent space of
G
at
\mathbf{g}
. Note that

is the same as
\mathfrak{g}
(except that
\mathfrak{g}
has, in addition, the Lie bracket operation).

B.1
Parametrization

A

of

refers to a diffeomorphism (i.e., a smooth invertible map)
\psi:\textup{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rightarrow S_{G}
, where
\textup{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
S_{G}\subseteq G
,
n
being the dimension of

as a manifold.
The map
\psi^{-1}
is then a system of coordinates (i.e., a chart) for
S_{G}
.
For example, the parametrization for
SO(2)
described in
eq.

32
is defined as the map
\mathbf{R}:(-\pi,\pi)\rightarrow SO(2)\backslash\{-\mathbf{I}_{2}\}
. Letting
\mathbf{x}\in\textup{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
be a point in the parameter space,
we define the
Jacobian
of

as the matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\psi}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\left(\psi(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x^{1}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\vee}&\left(\psi(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x^{2}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\vee}&\cdots&\left(\psi(\mathbf{x})^{-1}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x^{n}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\vee}\end{bmatrix}.

(43)

This matrix is the differential (i.e., the pushforward map) of
\psi
at
{\mathbf{x}}
, as expressed w.r.t. the standard basis for
T_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the basis
\{{\psi(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{E}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}

The Jacobian of the inverse map

is given by
\mathbf{J}_{\psi^{-1}}(\mathbf{g})=\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\big(\psi^{-1}{\small(\mathbf{g})}\big)^{-1}

\exp
and
\log

\mathbf{J}_{\psi}(\mathbf{x})
and

can be computed using closed-form expressions in the cases of

or

[

]

B.2
Differentiation

We can extend
\mathbf{Z}
to a unique
left-invariant vector field (LIVF)

{Z}
on
G
, which assigns to each point
\mathbf{g}\in G
the tangent vector

{Z}_{\mathbf{g}}\coloneqq\mathbf{g}\mathbf{Z}
. It has the property

for all

\dot{\bm{\upgamma}}(t)={Z}_{\bm{\upgamma}(t)}
, due to which

is called the
integral curve
of

starting at
\mathbf{g}
. Given a smooth function

where
V
is a vector space, the
Lie derivative
of

along

is defined as

\displaystyle\big({Z}f\big)(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{d}{dt}f\big(\mathbf{g}\exp(t\mathbf{Z})\big)\Big|_{t=0}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{f\big(\mathbf{g}\exp(\tau\mathbf{Z})\big)-f(\mathbf{g})}{\tau}.

(44)

In this sense,
{Z}
is a
differential operator
that maps
f
to another function on
G
; the latter function represents the derivative of

along
\bm{\upgamma}
.
Similarly, one can define a right-invariant vector field (RIVF) and a corresponding differential operator. We remark that
{Z}
is the same as the
right
Lie derivative in
[
16
]
(where it is denoted as
\mathbf{Z}^{(r)}

\exp(\,\cdot\,)
appears to the right of
\mathbf{g}
in (
44
).

B.3
Integration

The concept of integration on
G
can be defined w.r.t. the
left Haar measure
. It is characterized (up to scaling) by the following
left-invariance
property.
If
S\subseteq G
and
\mathbf{h}S\coloneqq\{\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}\,|\,\mathbf{g}\in S\}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{S}d\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{h}S}d\mathbf{g}\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{h}\in G,

(45)

where ‘

If
G
is compact, then the integral in

45

The volume of
S\mathbf{h}
is not the same as that of

, and we say that the left Haar measure is
bi-invariant
. An important consequence of bi-invariance of the Haar measure is that given
any (measurable) function

, where
V
is a vector space, we have

\displaystyle\int_{G}f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\int_{G}f(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}=\int_{G}f(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h})d\mathbf{g}=\int_{G}f(\mathbf{g}^{-1})d\mathbf{g},

(46)

for all
\mathbf{h}\in G
. The condition for

is
\det(\Ad_{\mathbf{g}})=1
for all
\mathbf{g}\in G

\mathbf{g}
as

\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
, we have that
[
25
, Ch. 4]

\displaystyle\det(\Ad_{\exp(\mathbf{X})})=\det\big(\exp(\ad_{\mathbf{X}})\big)=e^{\tr(\ad_{\mathbf{X}})}=1.

(47)

Thus,

\tr(\ad_{\mathbf{X}})=0
for all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
. The conditions in

47

\mathfrak{g}
, since the determinant and trace of a linear operator are both basis-independent concepts.
In terms of the structure constants (w.r.t. an arbitrary basis for
\mathfrak{g}
), the condition for unimodularity is that

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

To compute the Haar integral numerically, one typically uses the parametrization

\textup{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\textup{S}_{G}\subseteq G
.
The function

under

coincide, giving us

\displaystyle\int_{\textup{S}_{G}}f(\mathbf{g})d\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\int_{\textup{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}f\left(\psi(\mathbf{x})\right)\,|\det\left(\mathbf{J}_{\psi}(\mathbf{x})\right)|\,d\mathbf{x},

(48)

where

represents the absolute value of a real number.
Examples of Haar integrals for commonly-encountered Lie groups can be found in
[
16
]
.

B.4

19

19

This can be shown by using the fact that

Let

be the space of smooth functions on

Given a set of

Appendix C
Riemannian Geometry on Lie Groups

The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathfrak{g}
induces a unique
left-invariant Riemannian metric

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}
on
G
, defined via the relation

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}\coloneqq\langle\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{V},\mathbf{g}^{-1}\mathbf{W}\rangle

(52)

for all
\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}\in T_{\mathbf{g}}G
. Roughly speaking,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}
is an inner product for the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{g}}G
that is also
smooth
as a function of
\mathbf{g}
. If one inserts a pair of smooth vector fields into
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{L}
, then the result is a smooth function on
G
. The term
left-invariance
refers to the property
\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}=\langle\mathbf{h}\mathbf{V},\mathbf{h}\mathbf{W}\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{h}\mathbf{g}}
.

Each choice of Riemannian metric on
G
offers a way to differentiate tangent vectors, via the
Levi-Civita connection

[
39
,
15
]
. One says that a curve on
G
is a
geodesic
if the derivative of its velocity (as computed using the Levi-Civita connection) is zero. In the special case of
G=(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
, the geodesics of a left-invariant Riemannian metric are straight lines, and vice versa. An alternative characterization of geodesics on Lie groups is given in
Section

C.2
, which circumvents the use of a connection.

C.1
Bi-Invariant Riemannian Metrics

We say that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}
is
right-invariant
if it holds that

\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}=\langle\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h},\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}\rangle_{\mathbf{g}\mathbf{h}}^{L}
,
in which case it is said to be
bi-invariant
.
The following lemma lists the conditions under which the left-invariant metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{L}_{\mathbf{g}}
is bi-invariant.

Lemma 9
.

Let an inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
be extended to define a left-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
. Assume that
G
is connected. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

The inner product is
\Ad
-invariant, i.e., for all
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathbf{g}\in G
,

\langle\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\rangle=\langle\Ad_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{X},\,\Ad_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{Y}\rangle.

2.

The map
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}
is a skew-isometry, i.e., for all
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\in\mathfrak{g}
,

\langle\mathbf{Y},\ad_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{Z}\rangle=-\langle\ad_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\rangle.

3.

The left-invariant Riemannian metric is also right-invariant, i.e., it is bi-invariant.

4.

Any curve of the form
\bm{\upgamma}(t)=\mathbf{g}\exp(t\mathbf{X})
, where
\mathbf{g}\in G
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
, is a geodesic.

5.

It holds that
\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{x}^{\wedge}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times 1}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

6.

The matrix
{\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}
whose
(i,j)^{th}
component is defined by
({\mathbf{C}_{\ell}})_{ij}=C_{i\ell}^{k}\mathbf{W}_{kj}
is skew-symmetric.

Proof.

It was shown by Milnor in
[
48
]
that
\Ad
-invariance of the inner product,
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}
being a skew-isometry, and bi-invariance of the corresponding left-invariant metric are equivalent conditions; this is (a), (b), and (c), respectively.
The equivalence of (d) and (e) is the content of
[
15
, Prop. 3.18]
. The remaining implications will be shown here.

First, we show that
\textup{(b)}\Rightarrow\textup{(e)}
. The
adjoint
of the operator
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}
(with respect to the inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
) is the unique operator
\ad^{\ast}_{\mathbf{X}}
that satisfies
\langle{\mathbf{Y}},{\ad_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{Z}}\rangle=\langle{\ad^{\ast}_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{Y}},{\mathbf{Z}}\rangle
.
It is readily shown that the matrix of
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}^{\ast}
satisfies
\mathbf{W}\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}=(\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\ast})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}
. Since
\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\ast}=-\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}
(using
(\textup{b})
), we have
\mathbf{W}\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}=-\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}
. Condition
(\textup{e})
then follows from the fact that
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}]=\mathbf{0}_{m}
.

Next, we show that
\textup{(e)}\Rightarrow\textup{(f)}
. Using the linearity of the adjoint representation, we can rewrite (e) as
X^{i}(\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{E}_{i}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})_{\ell j}\mathbf{W}_{jk}X^{k}=0

\forall\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
In terms of the structure constants, we have

\displaystyle X^{i}C_{i\ell}^{j}\mathbf{W}_{jk}X^{k}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}=0.

(53)

It follows that
\mathbf{x}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}({\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}+{\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})\,\mathbf{x}=0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
({\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}+{\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})
is symmetric, the preceding condition reduces to
{\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}+{\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{0}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{C}_{\ell}}
is skew-symmetric; this shows
\textup{(e)}\Rightarrow\textup{(f)}
.
Finally, the condition for
\ad_{\mathbf{X}}
to be a skew-isometry for all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
is

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{E}_{j},\ad_{\mathbf{E}_{\ell}}\mathbf{E}_{i}\rangle=-\langle\ad_{\mathbf{E}_{\ell}}\mathbf{E}_{j},\mathbf{E}_{i}\rangle\ \Leftrightarrow\ C_{\ell i}^{k}\mathbf{W}_{kj}=-C_{\ell j}^{k}\mathbf{W}_{ki}

(54)

for all
i,j,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, which shows
\textup{(f)}\Leftrightarrow\textup{(b)}
and completes the proof.

If one chooses to work with an orthonormal basis, then the conditions in
lemma

9
take a simpler form, as presented in the following corollary.

Corollary 10
.

If the basis
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is orthonormal (i.e.,
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
), then conditions
(a)
and
(b)
of Lemma
9
are equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{g}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{g}}=\mathbf{I}_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}=-\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

(55)

for all
g\in G
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{g}
, respectively. That is,
\bm{\Ad}_{\mathbf{g}}
is orthogonal and
\bm{\ad}_{\mathbf{X}}
is skew-symmetric. Equivalently, the structure constants satisfy
C_{\ell i}^{j}=-C_{\ell j}^{i}
for all
i,j,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Unlike the geodesic equation on general Riemannian manifolds, which is a system of second-order differential equations, the variational approach (based on the Euler-Poincaré equations) yields first-order differential equations.
