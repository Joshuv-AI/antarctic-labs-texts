Where shame meets vulnerability: unintended adolescent pregnancy within the cultural context of the rural district of Rwandan Eastern Province
Authors: Jean Pierre Ndayisenga, Abe Oudshoorn, Michaela Hynie, Donatilla Mukamana, Yolanda Babenko-Mould, Kimberley T. Jackson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnintended pregnancy has been a longstanding public health, social, and political issue in Rwanda, with a significant impact on the perinatal and maternal health outcomes of childbearing women, especially in the case of adolescents. Although studies have been conducted in Rwanda to address the issue of unintended pregnancies among adolescent girls, the sociocultural factors that contribute to the complexity of the risks have not been explored. Thus, this study aimed to understand the cultural and personal meanings of unintended adolescent pregnancy in the Rwandan context and examine the contextual factors that shape this issue from a multidimensional perspective.MethodA critical ethnographical study was conducted in Rwanda framed by both critical and intersectionality theoretical lenses. By using purposive criteria and maximum variation sampling, a total of eight focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with 74 society members from the selected rural district of the Eastern Province of Rwanda, including adolescent mothers, parents of adolescents, men of varying ages, nurses, midwives, religious leaders, and staff members from non-government organizations (NGOs) that assist adolescent mothers. The data collected from FGDs were transcribed verbatim and translated into English, imported into Dedoose, and analyzed according to Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six steps for thematic analysis.ResultsThe findings suggested that unintended adolescent pregnancy is associated with shame felt personally by young mothers, shame enacted interpersonally by family members, and societal cultural positioning of adolescent pregnancy as shameful. Additionally, the issue of adolescent pregnancies was associated with the broader sense of youth as deviant, the existence of poverty and vulnerability, exposing adolescents to trading sex for money, advancement, or opportunity, as well as violence and abuse.ConclusionThis critical ethnographic study sheds light on the complex cultural and personal meanings of unintended adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda, which goes beyond individual behaviors and involves social, cultural, and structural factors. Therefore, all efforts to prevent and address unintended adolescent pregnancy should incorporate the promotion of positive social norms and the reduction of stigma associated with it.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09133-x.

Introduction

Adolescent pregnancy refers to any pregnancy that occurs between the ages of 10 and 19 years old [1]. Across the globe, unintended pregnancy is a public health and social concern in both high-income countries (HICs) and low-income countries (LICs) and is contributing to extremely high levels of perinatal and maternal complications for childbearing women. These complications are especially prevalent within adolescent pregnancies [1–3]. According to Save the Children [4], every year, more than 17 million adolescent girls give birth around the world, and 95% of these births occur in LICs, while only 5% are in HICs [5–7]. These births have been noted as a factor that slows the economic development of LICs [3, 4].

In Rwanda, adolescent pregnancy is not a new phenomenon. For example, the National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda [NISR] [8] indicated that the number of teenage pregnancies increased from 6.1% to 7.3% in 2014. Compared with data from 2014 to 15, the rate of adolescent pregnancy has remained relatively stable at 7% [9]. The recent data from the 2019–2020 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) [10] reported that, in Rwanda, the prevalence of unintended pregnancies among girls aged 15–19 had decreased to 5% nationally, with significant variations by province. It is noteworthy that the Eastern Province had the highest percentage at 6.4%, followed by the Southern Province (5.5%), Northern Province (4.6%), Kigali City (4.4%), and Western Province with the lowest rate at 4.1% [10]. Though the national prevalence of unwanted adolescent pregnancy appears relatively low compared to neighboring countries such as Kenya (18.1%), Uganda (24.8%), Tanzania (26.7%), and Burundi (8.3%) [3, 11], what is particularly concerning is the recent increase in adolescent pregnancies among older teens, especially those aged 17–19 years. For example, the NISR et al. [10] highlighted that, in Rwanda, unintended pregnancies among girls age 15–19 continue to rise. Moreover, unintended adolescent pregnancies are rising in Rwanda in spite of the fact that, globally, the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) indicate that women of reproductive age (15–49 years old), including teens, have increased access to sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHRs) services, including contraceptives, which have been reported to be at 62.9% [12, 13]; however, unmet need for contraceptives is still significant at 18% [14]. These unmet needs, coupled with socioeconomic challenges such as poverty and limited access to SRHR services in rural areas, contribute to the rise in unintended adolescent pregnancies. The rise in adolescent pregnancies, particularly in rural areas like the Eastern Province, where resources are scarce and cultural norms are strong, underscores the urgent need for exploring the multifaceted nature of adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda.

The problem of unintended adolescent pregnancy is a socially complex issue that is highly associated with cultural perspectives, psycho-social stigma, health inequities, and inequity based on gender and social-relational norms [15–19]. The health inequities around the issue of unintended pregnancies among adolescent girls in the Rwandan context are rooted in the history of Rwanda. Historically, in the context of a paternalistic society, the issue of adolescent pregnancies, particularly outside of marriage, is inconsistent with Rwandan values, beliefs, and social norms. For example, in the independent kingdom of Rwanda, before colonization in 1899 by Germany, when young girls became pregnant unintentionally before marriage, they were viewed and labeled as sex workers or criminals, and they would be banished to exile on an island on Lake Kivu [19–21]. It is told that those who were too far from Lake Kivu for this banishment were at times put to death by drowning [20, 21]. Therefore, it is important to note that, historically, single mothers had no fundamental human rights in Rwandan society. These historical perspectives of blaming girls still find a place within the beliefs, norms, and values that are shaping Rwandan culture today and help us situate barriers to health equity. Though the GoR and policymakers are advancing gender equality, equity, and the rights of all women, including adolescent girls, Rwandan society and some policymakers are still transitioning from seeing unintended pregnancy as a personal flaw rather than as a societal issue [20–23].

Currently, many adolescent girls who become pregnant unintentionally experience a poor relationship with their parents and their relatives, often including being banished from the family home [9, 24]. Additionally, while not being actually banished from their family home, other young women in Rwanda may move away or out of their family home as an alternative way of escaping violence, stigma, harassment, and other types of abuse experienced from their family members, most often their parents [19–21]. Adolescent pregnancies are, therefore, a frequent cause of family breakdown and conflict. What is notable in these experiences is the gendered nature of centering blame on the young woman versus considering the role of boys and men in pregnancies.

It is important to recognize that, in Rwanda, the Catholic church manages approximately 40% of healthcare service delivery points [25]. Due to Rwanda’s cultural tradition of considering sexuality taboo, as well as religious beliefs, the prevailing perception of youth sexuality as sinful is deeply embedded within the sociocultural structure of the country, affecting a wide variety of groups. This is especially true when it comes to addressing social determinants of reproductive health for young women, for whom abstinence is seen as the only priority. This perception can have significant implications for adolescents seeking information and support on SRHR. Although religious faith-based health facilities provide spiritual guidance and healthcare services in a significant way, the manner in which these sociocultural and religious beliefs shape organizations’ policies and practices in regard to reproductive and sexual health can vary considerably based on the beliefs and values of specific leaders, which may have an impact on the quality or variety of sexual reproductive services that youth and adolescent mothers may be able to access. Furthermore, in spite of efforts to address the complexity of adolescent pregnancy issues, there is still a consistent taboo surrounding discussions regarding adolescent sexuality. Due to this silence and stigma, adolescents facing unintended pregnancies have limited access to comprehensive sexual education and healthcare services, as well as effective support mechanisms.

In light of these circumstances, this critical ethnographic study is highly necessary to address the complex sociocultural dynamics that arise in the context of religious beliefs and sociocultural perceptions of adolescent sexuality. These concerns extend beyond healthcare and religious institutions; some people within the community and society in Rwanda continue to perpetuate a wide range of misconceptions, stigma, and negative reactions toward adolescent pregnancy and motherhood. According to Mpetshwa [26], parents tend to place the blame for pregnancies on their young daughters without considering the role of boys or men in this issue, and consequently, these girls face mistreatment in the family home. Outside the home, many adolescents experiencing pregnancy in youth face rejection by their peers, extended family members, and friends. This rejection may be compounded by being rejected by their sexual partner, and thus being forced to carry the stigma alone. Understandably, mental illnesses such as depression have been identified as a secondary effect of familial rejection related to unintended pregnancies [27].

In Rwanda, adolescent pregnancies remain a priority concern, as over 45% of the population of Rwanda is under 18 years old. This represents approximately 5.4 million people out of an estimated 11.8 million people [10, 28]. More than three quarters of the population (78%) is under the age of 35, placing the country fifth on a list of countries with the fastest-growing adolescent population [28]. Even though adolescents make up an increasingly large proportion of the population in LMICs, when it comes to public interventions such as access to SRHRs, they remain a relatively neglected group, even though they are vital to the development of a society that is socially and economically productive [29, 30]. In countries like Rwanda with a growing adolescent population, adolescents may also provide a demographic dividend. However, this dividend is best obtained if adolescents are supported to have a safe and empowered developmental stage. It is believed that adolescents with promoted social determinants of health (SDoHs), free from unintended pregnancies and their consequences, may contribute to achieving several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) if they are adequately supported. Conversely, unmanaged adolescent SRHRs may increase the number of unintended adolescent pregnancies, contribute to uncontrollable population growth, and negatively impact the progress toward the achievement of SDGs [30].

To respond to the problem of unintended adolescent pregnancies, Rwandan government ministries have collaborated with local government leaders, community members, non-governmental agencies, and religious bodies to address various identified factors associated with adolescent pregnancy. Nonetheless, the rate of unintended pregnancies among Rwandan adolescents has seen a sustained uptick. Although studies have been conducted with a focus on reducing unintended pregnancies among adolescent girls, there is a lack of exploration of the socio-cultural elements that contribute to the complex set of risks. Therefore, the aim in this critical ethnographic study was to determine and understand the cultural and personal meanings of unintended adolescent pregnancy in the Rwandan context and examine the perceived consequences and management approaches. In this study, diverse perspectives were taken into consideration, including those of adolescent mothers, girls, boys, men, parents, nurses and midwives, education leaders, NGO staff, and local leaders. In order to understand this highly nuanced and multidimensional problem, we addressed the following two research questions, which helped the research team to identify the interpersonal, intrapersonal, social-structural, and community factors that contribute to the meaning of unintended adolescent pregnancies: (1) What meanings, perspectives, and experiences do Rwandan single adolescent mothers and community members bring to the issue of unintended adolescent pregnancy? (2) What are the contextual and cultural factors that are shaping the meaning of adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda? The findings from this study advance health knowledge and provide a context for considering future interventions based on the culturally-rooted perspectives of those closest to the issue of concern within this particular socio-cultural context.

Methodology and methods

This study is grounded in and shaped by the critical paradigm, which includes critical theory along with feminist perspectives and intersectionality as theoretical frameworks. As stated by Anderson [31], critical ethnography helps researchers to explore interactions within culture, including structural and power relations, with the purpose of transforming inequities. Therefore, the use of critical ethnography design in this study helped the research team to understand the issue of unintended adolescent pregnancy as a public concern in Rwandan society while also pushing towards better reproductive rights and social justice for young women. In addition, critical theory helps scholars to conduct studies that include a reflective assessment and critique of culture and society in order to reveal and challenge health inequities and power structures [32–34]. This study utilized a feminist perspective to highlight the persistent gender-based power imbalances embedded within societal norms and structures, ensuring that young women’s experiences, voices, and agency were highlighted and prioritized. In the context of unintended pregnancies, many adolescent mothers experience oppressions rooted in such social locations as gender and age [35–37]. Therefore, the use of intersectionality as a theoretical perspective in this study supports us in understanding the complex socially-rooted vulnerabilities that may be at play and helped to acknowledge the complexity and breadth of Rwandan society members’ experiences and perspectives regarding the issue of adolescent pregnancies.

Research setting and study population

This study was conducted in one rural district located in the Eastern Province of Rwanda as this province was ranked to have the highest percentage of adolescent pregnancy with 6.4% [10]. This provided the research team access to a region where the phenomenon of interest was most prevalent. The targeted population of this study included a diversity of groups (Table 1) both directly and indirectly influenced by and influencing adolescent pregnancies. Therefore, we involved adolescent girls and boys aged 16–24 years, including both those who have been involved with an unintended pregnancy and those who have not, men who were potential partners aged 25 years and above, as well as parents of adolescents. Additionally, as recommended by Polit and Beck [34], to capture the diverse perspectives around the public and social problem of adolescent pregnancies, we included nurses and midwives working at community health centers, religious leaders, school leaders, Social-Economic Development Officers (SEDO) from regions within the district, and stakeholders like non-governmental organization (NGO) staff working in this domain.

Recruitment and sampling strategies

Upon receiving ethical approval from [Western University Research Ethics Board (ID:119527)] and [the University of Rwanda, College of Medicine and Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (Approval Notice: No 303/CMHS IRB/2021)], fieldwork for this study started. Because of the gender and age differences and given that adolescent pregnancy is a sensitive subject like other sexually related topics, it was anticipated that some participants would not feel comfortable in being open with a male researcher, especially one who was significantly older than then. As discussed in the published methodological paper from this study [38], to gain the trust from participants and key informants, our research team strategically recruited four research assistants (RAs)—two female and two male faculty members from the University of Rwanda—to assist with recruitment and data collection. These RAs were trained by the Principal Investigator (PI) to ensure consistency, safety, and clarity in their responsibilities during the fieldwork process. Engaging with the study setting’s community members, including gatekeepers, participants, and key informants, allowed the RAs to share detailed background information about this study using a pre-developed letter of information and to respond to any concerns or questions about this study [38]. Recruitment was facilitated through flyers distributed in key locations, including community health centers, local government offices, and health care offices, as well as by gatekeepers who circulated the flyers within their communities [38]. Snowball sampling was also used to distribute recruitment flyers, helping the research team recruit more participants from individuals who had already participated in this study. A purposive criterion sampling strategy was used, along with maximal variation [39, 40]. As supported by Palinkas [40], the use of criterion sampling in this inquiry helped the research team to select and identify participants who had a clear understanding of the meaning and consequences of adolescent pregnancies in the Rwandan context. In terms of maximum variation [34, 41], this strategy helped the research team to ensure that different community members with different viewpoints about the issue of adolescent pregnancies were included in the study. As was clearly described on our recruitment flyers, eligible participants were invited to contact the research team if they were interested in taking part in either individual interviews (IDIs) or focus group discussions (FGDs), depending on their personal preferences.

While both interviews and FGDs were offered, all participants felt comfortable participating in FGDs and therefore chose FGDs [38]. Our FGDs were designed to be homogeneous, to ensure participants felt comfortable discussing sensitive subjects with peers who had similar experiences and closer perspectives. A further reason why participants preferred FGDs may be that these groups fostered a supportive environment that encouraged open dialogue and validated participants’ perspectives. It was apparent that the FGD dynamic not only resulted in richer discussions but also made the process seem less formal and less intimidating compared to individual interviews, resulting in a more relaxed and engaging experience. Additionally, the use of FGDs allowed the research team to benefit from the group effect, which contributed to the detailed exploration from both diversity and areas of consensus in the experiences shared by FGD members [42, 43].

To be included in this study, the following common criteria were applied to all categories of our participants: (a) lived in the selected district/setting of this study, (b) presented the willingness to share their knowledge, perceptions, beliefs, values, or experiences about adolescent pregnancy. Beyond these common inclusion criteria mentioned above, additional specific recruitment enhanced the likelihood of quality participation and rich data. Inclusion criteria for adolescent mothers included: (1) having experienced unintended pregnancy within the past five years from the time of data collection, (2) adolescent girls and young women who were partnered or not without necessary disclosing their status age between 16 and 24 years.

Included adolescent girls and boys were aged between 16 and 24 years. In addition to the above-mentioned common inclusion criteria for all participants, adolescent girls and boys who had not experienced unintended pregnancy were included in this study as they are among the at-risk group for unintended adolescent pregnancy. Involving the latter groups in this study helped the research team to understand their perspectives about different cultural factors and sexual behaviors that are influencing Rwandan adolescents to engage in early sexual practice and what is contributing to the increased rate of adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda. Not all participants required parental consent depending on whether they were still living with parents or were emancipated minors [38].

Additional inclusion criteria for parents were being a parent (father or mother) of an adolescent girl aged 16–24 years who has (or has not) experienced an unintended pregnancy. For men of varying ages, an additional inclusion requirement was being aged 25 years and above. For nurses and midwives, additional inclusion considerations included: (a) working as a nurse or midwife in reproductive health service and/or perinatal services (Antenatal Consultation [ANC], Prevention of Mother to Child Transmission [PMTCT], and maternity), (b) having provided the mentioned services for at least one year at a community health center level located in selected district/setting of this study. Religious leaders were included after meeting the above-mentioned common criteria and were also required to (a) be legally or officially heading a religion or church registered in Rwanda Development Board, (b) have served at their leadership position for at least one year, (c) heading or leading a church or religious entity located in selected district/setting of this study. Leaders of 12-years basic education schools were included if they were: (a) leading a public or private 12 years of basic education school located in a selected district/ setting of this study, (b) serving at this position for at least one year, Additional inclusion criteria for Non-Governmental Organizations (NGO) staff: (a) working with officially recognized NGO, (b) being involved or working with adolescent girls who experienced unintended pregnancies in their NGO for at least six months in the selected district/setting, Additional inclusion criteria for the Social Economic Development Officer (SEDO) was working at this position in one of the cells of sectors located in selected district, b) serving at this position at least one year.

Data collection

A total of eight FGDs were conducted with 74 society members in the selected rural district of the Eastern Province of Rwanda (Table 1). Four RAs facilitated the FGDs mentioned below (Table 1), where two male RAs moderated FGDs with male participants and two female RAs moderated FGDs with female participants.

FGD guides were developed by our multidisciplinary research team, which included experts in adolescent sexual reproductive health, mental health, social sciences, psychology, and qualitative and ethnographic designs experts, and with a clear understanding of the Rwandan cultural context. These FGD guides were designed with open-ended questions to facilitate in-depth discussions and were carefully reviewed and approved by the ethical review boards from two institutions. The development process involved multiple iterations to ensure that our research purpose and questions were clearly captured in our FGD guides, and we ensured cultural relevance and appropriateness for the Rwandan context, drawing on both research team expertise and existing literature. Moreover, fieldnotes were taken to capture unspoken observations during FGDs. The FGDs involved the use of a hypothetical vignette to elicit beliefs, attitudes, religious, structural, and political related norms about specific situations with unintended pregnancies in their living community. Opening with a vignette allowed the researchers to elicit participants’ responses about difficult and sensitive topics such as violence, stigmatization, gender, and practices of induced abortion [38, 44]. The FGDs with participants took place at a convenient location like the nearest school or at the local cell [an administrative structure in the Rwandan Context which is an entity that provides basic services and helps the population to achieve sustainable development], village, or sector office in a room designated for group use. Conducted FGDs lasted approximately 90–120 min. The FGD guide was translated into Kinyarwanda to facilitate easy communication between the FGD moderator and FGD members/participants. The FGDs were digitally audio-recorded to facilitate the capture of all information from the participants.

Observation of participants and cultural immersion

Our research team comprised six members, including two Rwandans with extensive healthcare experience and two Canadians with prior experience conducting research projects in the Rwandan context. The firsthand observations of participants and cultural immersion were ideal; COVID-19 travel restrictions prevented the PI (JPN) from returning to Rwanda, posing challenges to firsthand immersion. Before starting the field work-related activities, RAs received clear guidance of how cultural immersions and debriefing should be done regularly before and after each activity and how to access any support if needed. As described in our methodological article published from this study [38], to address these challenges, well-trained research assistants (RAs) conducted fieldwork, and the team held regular virtual debriefing sessions with RAs via Zoom to enhance cultural immersion and facilitate dynamic information exchange. These debriefings had several advantages: (1) they facilitated collective reflection and shared insights among team members, (2) allowed the RAs to contextualize cultural practices related to adolescent pregnancy, (3) helped in the validation of field observations and refined research analysis, (4) uncovered hidden patterns in the social dynamics of unintended adolescent pregnancy, and (5) enabled reflexivity and adaptability throughout the research process [38].

Data analysis and rigour

A web-based application, Dedoose, was used to assist in the organization and analysis of transcribed data from the participants [45, 46]. This study used inductive analysis, and the research team followed Braun and Clarke’s [47] six steps of thematic data analysis, including familiarization, generating initial codes, developing themes, viewing themes, defining and naming the themes, and writing reports. Through the data familiarization, the Principal Investigator (PI) used reflective fieldnotes taken during data collection to complement the transcripts’ information. Following familiarization with collected data, the PI imported all FGD transcripts into Dedoose. The PI then engaged in an open coding process by organizing data into meaningful and interesting units used as preliminary codes. The PI then collaborated with the research supervisor to apply these codes to the transcripts. After coding and collating all data, the PI and the research supervisor combined all similar codes with similar meaning under an overarching theme or sub-themes that were created inductively. Using the structure of created codes and initial themes helped the researcher to visualize the relationship between different themes and their codes that was used to determine the conceptual level of created themes. Together with the research team, all themes were checked and verified against the data, the study purpose, and the literature review to consider relevance and suitability. Some themes were added, removed, or combined at this phase. After getting a satisfactory thematic map of analyzed data, in collaboration with the research team, the researcher defined, and further refined themes based on the overall story developing of the data. Finally, after having a well-defined set of themes, the researcher wrote up a final analysis and dissertation draft describing the findings from this study. This provided the team a final opportunity for refinement.

To ensure the quality of the research findings from this ethnographic study, the researcher used criteria of catalytic validity [48], credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [49], disclosure and empowerment, awareness of identity and power differentials, and contextuality [11] within an overall process on reflexivity [38]. These particular criteria were used because they are aligned with the critical paradigm as well as critical ethnographic methodology.

The credibility of a qualitative study is related to the extent to which the findings of the qualitative inquiry accurately maintain the participants’ perspectives [50]. Credibility was sought by creating a safe context for participants to express themselves, and by linking participant quotes to the codes, sub-themes, and themes so that the voices of participants would not be lost through the analysis process. The concept of dependability refers to the ability to explain how decisions are made during the analytical process [49, 51]. In this study, dependability was ensured by keeping records of all decisions made from the beginning of the research process to the end of data analysis using an audit trail. The transferability of a qualitative study pertains to its applicability to other contexts [52]. Taking into account that this is a critical ethnographic study, it was assumed that its findings would not easily translate to other cultural contexts. It was assumed, however, that the findings would at least be useful to other Rwandan regions, and further consideration could be given to applying them to other countries where culture has some similarities. To achieve other rigor criteria per Meleis [11], such as disclosure and empowerment, awareness of identity and power differentials, and contextuality, most of the correspondence and FGDs with potential participants were in the Kinyarwanda language, which is the primary language in the area of study. Through this, language barriers were overcome, and their perceptions and experiences were captured more deeply by considering the cultural, contextual, and ethical sensitivity of participants’ language; the researcher captured participants’ meanings. The researcher and RAs also used other communication strategies, such as asking the meaning of used expressions or terminologies, to guarantee the accuracy of the interpretation. Quotations from participants’ stories are used to preserve and privilege their voices. Whenever necessary, researchers asked participants to elaborate on their shared experiences, which for them represented an opportunity for personal development and empowerment [11].

Findings

To explore culturally rooted and personal meanings of unintended adolescent pregnancy in the Rwandan context, this study involved 74 Rwandan society members from various categories (Table 1). By combining critical theory with a feminist perspective and by incorporating intersectionality theory, the forthcoming findings section represents the culmination of a rigorous thematic analysis conducted within the context of a critical ethnographic study. This analysis demonstrates the profound interplay between gender dynamics, power structures, and diverse identities in this setting that deeply influences perceptions, experiences, and implications of unintended adolescent pregnancy. By applying intersectionality theory to this exploration, further light is shed on the intersecting dimensions of gender, socio-economic status, and cultural intricacies that contribute to the complex narrative of adolescent pregnancy within the Rwandan context. Following our thematic analysis, the meaning and sociocultural factors shaping the meaning of adolescent pregnancy can be summarized into three themes (Table 2): shame, fallen youth, and vulnerability.

The findings (Table 2) focus more on culturally rooted meaning and contextual factors shaping the issue of unintended adolescent pregnancies in Rwanda from multi-dimensional perspectives. These findings mainly describe meaning, thoughts, assumptions, perceptions, beliefs, and feelings about the meaning and various cultural, social, and contextual factors. Based on the thematic analysis process, the three interconnected themes are described here (Table 2). Theme One: Shame describes the overarching culturally rooted meaning of adolescent pregnancy. Theme Two: Fallen Youth discusses how the issue of adolescent pregnancy is considered to be deviance that is rooted in a broader sense of youths as “fallen” or at least constantly on the boundaries of deviance. Theme Three: Vulnerability describes how adolescent pregnancy is linked to individual vulnerability in various ways, mostly connected to being raised in disadvantaged environments that may include violence and poverty-related stress.

Theme one: shame

In answering the first research question: “What meanings, perspectives, and experiences do Rwandan single adolescent mothers and community members bring to the issues of unintended adolescent pregnancy?”, we learned that in the meaning of adolescent pregnancy, the concept of ‘shame’ was ever-present. The theme of shame has three sub-themes that discuss how shame is experienced personally, enacted interpersonally, and reflects ‘the cultural perception that adolescent pregnancy is shameful.’

Shame felt personally

When asked to describe the meaning, their perspective, and their experiences related to the issue of unintended adolescent pregnancy, multiple participants described adolescent pregnancy as “a shame felt personally by pregnant adolescent mothers” immediately after realizing that she had become pregnant unintentionally as one adolescent mother described it:As an adolescent girl, it was hard for me to accept such a shameful pregnancy… I spent sleepless nights pondering on how I am going to be viewed by my peers or neighbors, in my family, and at school community, wondering if my boyfriend will accept to hear this bad news and help me [or not help me] to raise a child as I am still a child too. It was and it is still a shame to me and my family (FGD-AML).

Adolescent pregnancy was defined as ‘shame’ felt by the teenage mother, including being culturally internalized and felt directly and adolescent girls feel shame in relation to pregnancy. One nurse-midwife commented: “… Most of the time adolescent mothers are not happy about her pregnancy; feel ashamed and isolate themselves…., assuming that colleagues treat them as someone who is not respecting cultural values and norms like other well-educated Rwandan girls” (FGD-NMF).

Shame enacted interpersonally

Many participants viewed and described adolescent pregnancies as shameful and noted that social shame was enacted on adolescent mothers and their families. Therefore, adolescent mothers typically use a variety of approaches and attitudes to mask pregnancy-related shame within the family, according to one SEDO: “to keep the family from embarrassment, wealthy parents prefers sending a pregnant adolescent [their daughter] far away [at her aunt] to distance herself from neighbors who would comment on her pregnancy and stigmatize her and her family: [In Kinyarwanda means Guhunga uruvugo]” (FGD-VSP-H). In addition, Rwandan men similarly viewed impregnating an adolescent girl as a socially, culturally, and illegally shameful, immoral act, which is also viewed as disappointing the society, in which the sexual partner felt embarrassed both within his family and toward the adolescent’s family as one man stated: “In our society, men who unexpectedly impregnate teenage girls are mistreated and live with shame as …, they will be treated or viewed as immoral persons who enacted sexual and unethical behavior” (FGD-MVAC).

Various participants noted that the shame enacted on mothers is also experienced by children born of unintended pregnancies. As highlighted by participants, these children are given names, such as ‘bastard’, ‘illegitimate child’, ‘fatherless children’, ‘cursed children’, ‘children of caressed mothers,’ or an ‘unwelcome infant’ and some others. One man expressed: “sometimes, both parents decide banishing their daughter and her baby or repeatedly tell her hurtful words like ‘you brought the unwelcome child [bastard] to the family; both of you are a curse to us and other harassing words” (FGD-MVAB). Similarly, one local leader and service provider mentioned: “in our culture, adolescent pregnant is not welcomed… even a child born at her/his grandmother’s home is considered illegal and grows up feeling embarrassed and stigmatized…that child is not called like other children in family” (FGD-VSP-F).

The cultural positioning of adolescent pregnancy as shameful

Shame felt by young women and shame enacted on these women, their partners, their families, and their children, is connected to an overall conceptualization of adolescent pregnancy as a cultural shame. In Rwandan society, adolescent pregnancy is largely viewed from the perspective of social and religious beliefs, where different religious groups see adolescent pregnancy as a scandalous sin that should not be tolerated. One religious leader commented:adolescent pregnancy is taken like shame or an abomination to the family or her community…., it is an embarrassing situation being on their parent’s side, and to her generation, as they say, [the failure of one girl in the family or a village could be attributed to her peers in Kinyarwanda means “umukobwa aba umwe agatukisha bose]” (FGD-RLD).

Participants further highlighted that adolescent pregnancy is shameful as it is considered as not conforming to accepted Rwandan sexual morality. To answer the second research question of this study, our findings highlighted that the contextual and cultural factors that are shaping the meaning of adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda, which were captured through the following overarching and interconnected themes of “Fallen Youth” and “Vulnerability,” as discussed below:

Theme two: fallen youth

The theme of ‘fallen youth’ situates adolescent pregnancy in a broader social narrative of youth as ‘fallen’ or at least constantly on the boundaries of deviance. This global phenomenon of perceiving youth/young people/adolescents as ‘fallen’ or inherently or potentially problematic is also present in Rwandan culture. This theme describes how participants revealed that the conceptualization of adolescent pregnancy as reflecting youth deviance is connected with a broader perception of the growing deviance of Rwandan youth, including adolescents being out of control, adolescent sex as sinful or deviant, and youth being driven by temptation, lust, and deviant desires.

Adolescents being out of control

Participants shared a social perception that “adolescents are out of control”, and adolescent pregnancy is one manifestation of this. Participants felt that, for example, the increase in access to and use of sexuality-related information among adolescents has put them out of parental control. One parent pointed out that parents are no longer able to control which information their children receive, and they believed that this contributes to the growing adolescent birth rate: “today’s social living context have changed with advancement in use of social platforms like Facebook, Twitter, etc., and many parents don’t have technology literacy at all, which increase difficulties of controlling how children use online information safely… (FGD-RLJ). Some participants believed that perceived adolescent deviance resulted from poor parenting or failed parenting, especially when it comes to educating their children on sexual and reproductive health and how to access safe information through technology. Participants reflected on how some approaches to managing youth behaviours simply created the impetus for youth to mask their activities:… If a child does not receive a friendly approach to using the smartphone/other technology, the child prefers to own and use the phone in a hidden manner… If the phone is privately owned [maybe from a boyfriend or sugar dad or she trades it for sex], then the child will misuse it …. visiting inappropriate websites like pornography and nude photos…. end up practicing what they learned from these online platforms without parental guidance. This also returns to the out-of-control nature of our society regarding guiding and educating children about sexual reproductive health core values and the effective use of technology (FGD-RLJ).

Adolescent sex as sinful or deviant

It is a culturally rooted belief that adolescent sex before marriage is sinful or deviant and as such, unintended adolescent pregnancy has a negative cultural connotation. Several participants commented that once they become pregnant, their pregnancy is immediately viewed as a sin or abomination, and these adolescent mothers are viewed as fallen to the sin of adultery: “they [some Rwandan society members] often think of adolescent mothers as misbehaving girls without parental respect who begin having sex too early or as children without cultural values who violate biblical guidelines and commit adultery and sins with boys (FGD-NMA). In Rwanda, like other predominantly Christian communities, premarital sex is considered wrong or sinful, according to one religious’ leader: “in our society, sexual relations before marriage are considered sinful, immoral, or evil… it as a taboo, while most of us [religious leaders] define it as stepping outside the boundaries of faith” (FGD-RLK).

Youth are driven by temptation, lust, and deviant desires

In constructing the idea of adolescent deviance, participants framed the root motivations of youth as selfish, sinful, or deviant. Participants individualized the motivations of young girls, highlighting the perceived benefits of engaging in sex particularly with older or wealthier men. On this concern, one adolescent girl stated: “in many cases, girls become pregnant unintentionally as a result of their deviant desires to purchase new shoes, live expansive lives beyond their families’ financial means, and engage in adultery with naughty men.” (FGD-AGD). Another young mother started: “Girls often need many things to match their friends’ lifestyles; if we don’t get what we’ve seen in movies, we may slide into adultery (prostitution), and through prostitution, we may get pregnant (FGD-AMB). Parents of adolescent girls emphasized that a high number of teenage pregnancies in Rwandan society illustrates how sexually deviant some adolescent girls may be. Parents discussed a belief that it is becoming a social pathology where many youths’ goals are to live like a foreign singer or star portrayed in movies instead of living like a Rwandan girl, and for that reason, they engage in transactional sex with men, so they can afford modern clothing or dressing like famous youth without relying on their parents’ finances.

Adolescent pregnancy was an issue where blame was added to shame for young women. From the perspectives of several participants, Rwandan youth today have both deviant desires and high lust for things, putting them at an increased risk of living a life filled with temptation from wealthy older men who lavish gifts or cash upon them for sexual favors, leading to an increased teenage pregnancy rate. One man put it:As girls’ families sometimes cannot afford all the things their need [due to their behavior sometimes or their high demand for items that parents thought were unnecessary or beyond their means], wealthy men or sugar daddies tempt them with lovely and expensive gifts such as money, smartphones, fancy clothes, or shoes, which convinces teenage girls, and they give into sexual relations with him; she finds herself faced with the consequences of an unintended pregnancy afterward.(FGD-MVAD).

Theme three: vulnerability

Sitting in tension with the preceding theme is that of vulnerability. Participants discussed how being vulnerable in multiple ways increases the chances of young girls becoming pregnant unintentionally. From their perspectives, beliefs, opinions, and experiences, participants highlighted that Rwandan adolescent girls’ ‘vulnerability’ is positively related to violence and abuse within their families or unhealthy relationships (sexual violence or rape), poverty within their families, and can make them more likely to trade sex for money, career advancement, survival, or power.

Violence and abuse within families or from partners

Participants pointed out that adolescent pregnancies may be higher in Rwanda due to the high vulnerability of adolescent girls. Several participants explained that young girls are still vulnerable to sexual violence by older men and boys who engage them in unconsented, unprotected sex due to their immaturity in decision-making and inability to consent, and any pregnancy by an underage girl constitutes sexual-based violence. One man described that: “due to the fact that girls are usually impregnated by older boys or men, adolescent pregnancies are often caused by sex-based violence” (FGD-MVAA). From their perspectives, men pointed out that some men or boys’ prey on adolescent girls’ vulnerability and weakness and sometimes give them empty promises that attract these young girls to believe in them and end up having unhealthy relationships that lead to unintentional pregnancies.… males who are highly sexually lustful approach teenage girls with empty or unrealistic promises and pressurize them into sex acts because they’re easy to approach and don’t complicate things like adult women or rape them because of their physical weakness, … when she becomes pregnant, she is neglected by their sexual partners (FGD-MVAE).

During FGD with adolescent mothers, some of them mentioned that they become pregnant unintentionally because of sexual violence or rape; one adolescent mother noted: “I was raped by an old man, and I didn’t know what to do; later, my period stopped as a sign I was pregnant” (FGD-AMJ). This sexual violence included non-consensual sex with romantic partners. One single mother started: “…when I visited my so-called boyfriend, and there was no one around like before, he asked me to make love; I refused; he forced me to sleep with him, so after two months, I discovered I was pregnant” (FGD-AMK).

Due to their vulnerability within their families and society, young girls who become pregnant after rape, sexual violence, or other such circumstances face double violence within their families and society. First, they are impregnated from violence and then they face the violence of exclusion, isolation, or even physical violence when their pregnancy becomes known. The double violence makes them live in shame and increases motivation to hide their pregnancy. A single adolescent mother said: “your parents mistreat you and your baby, and you are often expelled from the house, claiming that two women cannot live under the same roof, that there is no support, and that you must work to survive” (FGD-AMM). After learning that she was unintentionally pregnant, another adolescent mother described how, instead of being heard and supported, her parents physically abused and harassed her: “after searching for days in town for the boy who impregnated me, I returned home, where my father verbally abused me, chasing me away with a stick and telling me your pregnancy is shameful, and you are not welcome” (FGD-AMJ).

Poverty

Participants also spoke to poverty as a form of vulnerability where young women face barriers to accessing basic necessities or to social advancement. A school leader pointed out that: “many adolescent girls who grew up in poor or financially vulnerable families are more likely to be taken advantage of by married men who give them gifts or money to escape poverty-related hardships” (FGD-VSP-A). In a FGD with parents, it was noted that poverty remains a major factor that forces some adolescents to engage in unprotected sexual activities just to survive or to obtain basic needs or gifts that their parents do not have. For example, one parent of an adolescent girl said: “poverty in some families leads young girls to be impregnated by men and boys older than they are, luring them into sex in exchange for money or gifts”. Continuously, poverty or being raised in a low-income family further exacerbates their vulnerability and dictates how these adolescent mothers and their families handle unintended pregnancies. As an example, poverty within families continues to disadvantage adolescent mothers’ rights and choices because they are manipulated by their sexual abusers who negotiate with their families to give them hidden financial support [bribes], subsequent to which adolescent mothers and their family members at times involuntarily accept to keep perpetrators’ names hidden without reporting them. “Recently, we were informed about an adolescent pregnancy case, …the perpetrator [sugar daddy] asked guardians to be silent, promised to pay for the family’s health insurance and asked both the girl and parents not to disclose his name” (FGD-VSPC).

Related to the issue of poverty above, vulnerable girls trade sex for money to meet their basic needs or to survive, to gain opportunities or to advance toward their goals, or for a stable relationship. As an example, one adolescent mother noted:I don’t have parents…., as a young girl, I needed pants, shoes, and trousers like others, but I did not have access to these things…, if someone buys these items for me and asks to sleep with him… and having sex once or twice won’t harm you; [that is how I unintentionally become pregnant] (FGD-AMC).

Several participants agreed that there is an increase in adolescent pregnancies because some young people exchange sexual services to cover or obtain items their families cannot afford, such as school fees or basic needs; “there is no public disclosure of the practice, but some girls without money sometimes turn to men who can give them money and give them sex rather than losing face in front of their classmates or not finishing school” (FGD-PAJ). During the FGD, another adolescent girl described how some of her peers who engaged in unplanned sex [sexual activity that occurs due to pressure without prior planning, preparation, intentions, or if one party feels coerced or pressured into the act] were taken advantage of by men who promised them opportunities, such as school fees, scholarships, or financial assistance from a local NGO, one adolescent girl shared: “my schoolmates… [one is from a single-parent household who cannot afford school fees] both were promised financial support by NGO staff through local leaders;…, she accepted to sleep with some staff [local leader]who impregnated her unintentionally” (FGD-AGC). Discussion

From the multidimensional perspectives of various Rwandan society members, the overarching culturally rooted meaning of unintended adolescent pregnancy and factors shaping its meaning were best captured through three interconnected themes, specifically, shame, a perception of youth as ‘fallen’, and vulnerability.

Shame as ubiquitous

Although many countries strive to ensure that young women’s SRHRs are optimized, sociocultural shame around adolescent pregnancy remains an issue to a greater or lesser extent in most countries, including Rwanda [3, 53–55]. We found that culturally rooted shame around adolescent pregnancy within Rwandan society may (in)directly leads to many SDoHs not being met for many adolescent mothers, particularly those pertaining to SRHRs, including physical health, gender equality, mental health, and human rights. For instance, SRHR approaches promote timely access to healthcare services; however, because of the internalized stigma, shame and embarrassment associated with unintended adolescent pregnancy, which often leads to stigma— many adolescent mothers may fail to receive appropriate healthcare, including prenatal care, contraception, and reproductive health information [54, 56–58]. Since it is culturally shameful to become pregnant before marriage, many teenage mothers still use various mechanisms and harmful responses, such as social isolation, self-blame, avoiding others, not attending antenatal care, and dropping out of school, to avoid social humiliation, stigma, and discrimination [18, 56, 58–60]. Conceivably, there is, therefore, a connection between the cultural construction of adolescent pregnancy and limitations on achieving national progress around adolescent pregnancy outcomes. Thus, this study suggests that joint efforts should be made to assist adolescents in navigating the challenges associated with unintended pregnancy and reducing the negative impact of sociocultural shame by fostering community mobilizations and campaigns and engaging society members at all levels. These efforts may help to address intersecting stigma— personal, internalized, and societal enacted stigma and prioritizing sociocultural support, ensuring that adolescent mothers are well supported and assisted to access comprehensive healthcare services, including mental health services and social support.

Individualizing the blame on ‘fallen youth’

Based on the narratives shared by our participants, it is evident that due to shame around adolescent pregnancy that sometimes intersects with religious beliefs and societal norms around sex before marriage, consequently many adolescent mothers prefer to attribute their unintended pregnancies to violence or coercion. We note that it would not have been acceptable for young women to say that they had sex by choice within a sociocultural context that considers sex before marriage as being sexually deviant or immoral, thereby making the focus almost exclusively on sexual assault and coercion. Due to the power differentials in adolescent sexual relations, these norms further exacerbate vulnerabilities that result from socioeconomic and gender inequities, increasing the risk of unintended pregnancies and GBV. By failing to address such social beliefs that hide gender inequities, adolescents, particularly girls, may be coerced or exploited as an indirect consequence of these noted Rwandan societal and religious beliefs, especially in religious contexts that emphasize abstinence and moral responsibility, while overlooking coercive circumstances. As an example, in Rwandan society, where adolescent sexual activity is most often seen as a sin or morally deviant, or as being outside of religious values, confessing to an unintended pregnancy resulting from consensual sexual relations during adolescence would be difficult or impossible. It is for this reason that many adolescent mothers might appear to contradict prevailing societal norms if they acknowledge their own agency in seeking out sexual experiences. Therefore, while many pregnancies presumably resulted from violence, framing all their pregnancies within narratives of violence or sexual assault may offer a more socially acceptable explanation. The power imbalance, combined with cultural norms, silences adolescent girls and may continue to perpetuate cycles of shame and stigma, further limiting their SRHRs. The reason for this phenomenon may be attributed to cultural norms which dictate that adolescents, especially females, should refrain from engaging in sexual activity during their teenage years. Furthermore, preconceived expectations surrounding adolescent sexuality contribute to the reluctance to admit or disclose if some of the cases of adolescent pregnancy are the result of consensual choices, leading them instead to emphasize violence. Therefore, many adolescent mothers may not seek comprehensive SRHR services, which further marginalizes them and limits their access to essential healthcare, mental health, and post-violence services.

Considering previous studies around adolescent pregnancies in Rwanda [9, 56, 61], what has been shared by our participants also reflects multidimensional reality around the assumptions and beliefs of many other society members about the problem of adolescent pregnancies, and it appears that there is a noticeable sociocultural bias that places blame on adolescent girls when it comes to unintended teenage pregnancies in Rwandan society. This is evident from the use of blame-centered language, such as referring to adolescents as “fallen youths” who spend considerable time online, or nowadays, youth are not following family norms and boundaries, misbehaving girls do not wear modest clothing, and girls who expose themselves to toxic environments, girls without parental respect, etc. Other individuals attribute this to poor parenting. It is important to note that this is a global phenomenon and each subsequent generation tends to view “today’s youth” as more deviant than their own. Secondly, it is important to note that promoting SDoHs for adolescents and preventing unintended pregnancies is not solely the responsibility of youth and their parents. Thus, it is not only harmful to assign sole blame to adolescents as “fallen youth,” but it also fails to take into account the larger systemic and upstream factors that are often deeply rooted in social and cultural structures, such as the social and cultural norms regarding sexuality and gender inequality, poverty, economic inequalities, and limited access to ASRHRs [62–66]. As an alternative to presuming unintended adolescent pregnancy can be reduced by simply disciplining youth more severely or reducing access to the internet, a joint effort is needed to build a socioeconomic and cultural environment that is supportive and inclusive so adolescents can make informed sexual health decisions as well as meet their broader determinants of health.

Vulnerability

Adolescent girls worldwide are vulnerable in multiple ways, particularly those raised in rural areas with limited incomes, which ultimately leads to higher rates of adolescent pregnancy [56, 67–69]. Similar to previous studies [58, 70–73], our study confirms that some Rwandan adolescent girls’ vulnerability is connected with violence and abuse in their families, unhealthy relationships that expose them to rape and sexual violence, poverty within their families, and a tendency to trade sex for money, school completion, career advancement, survival, or power. Unintended adolescent pregnancies in Rwanda represent a significant public health challenge, particularly in the context of transactional sex involving authority figures and older men, as well as pervasive societal power imbalances that increase girls’ vulnerability [74, 75]. The dynamic whereby young girls engage in relationships with older men in order to obtain financial support reflects entrenched gender inequities within a patriarchal society, resulting in young girls being dependent and incapable of negotiating safe sexual practices [75]. The involvement of authority figures in transactional sex, such as local leaders and staff, exacerbates the problem by exploiting the trust they have been given to protect young people, perpetuating social hierarchies and gender inequity [7, 17, 18, 76, 77]. In accordance with the literature, any form of violence, particularly that which is linked to unintended pregnancy, which was defined by most of our participants as a shameful phenomenon when it occurs during adolescence, has a significant impact on their physical and mental well-being. For example, because of this cycle of violence, adolescent mothers are often excluded, isolated, or physically and psychologically abused [9, 24, 70], which makes them feel ashamed and motivates them to hide or terminate their pregnancies [78–80], while others continue to endure that exacerbated vulnerability, which limits their ability to seek appropriate assistance, such as health care, social support, and justice. Therefore, nurses, midwives, and other service providers working at different levels can use knowledge from this study and advocate for both confronting sociocultural norms and facilitating system changes and health policies and advance efforts to promote health equity for adolescent mothers experiencing shame, stigma, violence, and discrimination.

Having experienced both violence that led to an unintended pregnancy as well as double sociocultural and systemic violence, adolescents may find it difficult to access SRH services because of a lack of support from family, sexual partners, or healthcare providers, as well as limited access to social services [81–83]. It is even more difficult for them to access SRHRs and support during and after pregnancy as a result [84]. In order to effectively address the issue of adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda, there is a need for a cultural shift that better acknowledges how vulnerable these adolescent mothers are, moves away from harmful social norms and stereotypes that blames vulnerable groups and eliminate stereotypes that perpetuate intersecting stigma and discrimination against adolescent mothers. Additionally, it is necessary to eradicate both the immediate and underlying causes of double violence around adolescent pregnancies, such as sexual rape and all forms of violence against these vulnerable groups. Addressing the complex issue of vulnerability experienced by these adolescent mothers also requires providing comprehensive and adolescent-friendly support services that recognize their vulnerabilities and help them overcome shame and blame, and promote their financial autonomy and privacy, as well as help them restore their self-esteem, and promote reproductive health and gender equity based on human rights.

The poverty-related vulnerability of adolescents and their pregnancies are interconnected phenomena that frequently coexist and reinforce each other [68, 69, 85]. In Rwanda, adolescents living in poverty are unable to gain access to basic necessities or social advancement; as a result, some adolescents are forced to engage in unprotected sexual activities just to survive or to acquire basic needs or gifts their parents do not have, increasing their chances of becoming pregnant unintentionally. Thus, achieving SDoHs such as SRHRs for all is difficult, as poverty or living in a low-income family further exacerbates their vulnerability, affecting their ability to deal with unintended pregnancy [86]. It is impossible to eliminate the problem of unintended adolescent pregnancies without considering how closely this issue is intertwined with existing poverty-related vulnerabilities. This justifies and supports the need for comprehensive approaches and strategies to address unintended adolescent pregnancies, which must first incorporate SDoHs for young girls and align with SDGs 3, 4, and 5 emphasizing healthy lives, quality education, and gender equality. To improve SDoHs for young women and for rapid progress towards SDGs 3 and 4,5, it is recommended that the Government of Rwanda and its stakeholders continue to invest all efforts in all initiatives to improve SDoHs for young women. Providing education and opportunity to young girls and women can substantially decrease their vulnerability to unintended pregnancies and break the cycle of poverty-related vulnerability within their communities.

This study has its both strengths and limitations. To our knowledge, this study is pioneering the application of critical and intersectionality theoretical frameworks to explore the social problems and concerns surrounding adolescent pregnancies within the Rwandan context, offering a comprehensive, multi-dimensional, and cultural perspective that advances knowledge on this complex issue. Despite travel limitations experienced by the primary researcher due to COVID-19, the use of well-trained RAs ensured a degree of cultural immersion through detailed fieldwork, providing valuable insights into the sociocultural intricacies of adolescent pregnancy. The use of hypothetical vignettes and indirect questioning also helped ensure the validity of the findings, by decreasing the likelihood of social desirability bias, due to the topic’s sensitive nature.

Due to the qualitative nature of this critical ethnographic inquiry, this study was conducted in one district located in the Eastern Province of Rwanda. Although this may be considered a limitation to the generalizability of our findings, this is an opportunity to acknowledge that this research involved a broad range of Rwandan society members with a variety of backgrounds. Future research could use different methodologies, such as mixed methods, and should be conducted and involve different community settings from different provinces of Rwanda to capture a wide picture of various factors shaping the meaning of unintended adolescent pregnancies that may not have been not captured in this particular setting.

We recognized that detailed demographic information of this study’s participants such as age, sex, religion, education level, and sector of employment—was not collected and was therefore not part of our detailed analysis or interpretation of this study’s findings. While such information could provide additional context, the primary focus of this study was to explore multidimensional perspectives surrounding the complexity nature of unintended adolescent pregnancy, and the insights generated were based on participants’ experiences, perspectives, or views and contributions as discussed in our various FGDs rather than their specific demographic characteristics.

Conclusion

This critical ethnographic study sheds light on the complex cultural and personal meanings of unintended adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda, which goes beyond individual behaviors and involves social, cultural, and structural factors. The findings have important implications for policy and practice to address this issue comprehensively. Efforts to prevent unintended adolescent pregnancy in Rwanda should take into account and confront the broader societal norms and cultural values that shape the perception of adolescent pregnancy as shameful and deviant. In addition, addressing poverty and vulnerability in families, providing access to SRH services, education, and empowering adolescent girls can help prevent unintended pregnancies and improve maternal and perinatal health outcomes. Interventions should also involve engaging with communities, religious leaders, and other stakeholders to promote positive social norms and reduce stigma associated with unintended adolescent pregnancy. These interventions may focus on inclusive dialogue and capacity-building initiatives, such as community meetings that allow community members, local leaders, policymakers, and stakeholders to discuss adolescent pregnancy and collaboratively develop local strategies. Promoting positive social norms may entail reshaping sociocultural narratives to highlight the resilience and the importance of comprehensive SRHR education, recognizing the dignity of young mothers, supporting their educational and employment opportunities, and fostering community networks that celebrate rather than banish them. Ultimately, addressing unintended adolescent pregnancy requires an upstream approach that reaches at the root causes of why young women in Rwanda are vulnerable. An upstream approach requires addressing systemic and sociocultural factors contributing to the meaning of adolescent pregnancy in the Rwandan context and young girls’ vulnerability, including poverty and inadequate access to quality education and healthcare. Collaboration among health, education, and social sectors is crucial for developing integrated strategies that confront the root causes of unintended adolescent pregnancy, creating a supportive framework that empowers young women, reduces intersecting stigma, and fosters healthier communities in Rwanda.
