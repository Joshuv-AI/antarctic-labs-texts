Cardiogenic shock and crescentic pauci-immune glomerulonephritis associated with systemic lupus erythematosus and ANCA-vasculitis overlap: a case report
Authors: Ana Rita Bello, Andreia Cristina Gaspar Carnevale, António Tralhão, Christopher Strong, Catarina Brízido, João Presume, Ivo Laranjinha, Rita Calça, Patrícia Matias, Célia Gil, Margarida Gonçalves, Rita Theias, Patrícia Branco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus is an autoimmune disorder that may be characterized by renal disease, which can manifest as lupus nephritis with haematoproteinuria, and cardiac involvement, ranging from subclinical manifestations to cardiogenic shock. While cardiovascular disease is common, myocarditis is a rarer manifestation with a challenging diagnosis due to atypical presentation and lack of sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsy. The renal involvement may allow for a kidney biopsy, which usually demonstrates glomerulonephritis with immune complex deposition. However, a rare pauci-immune histopathological pattern may be present, especially if associated with positive anti-neutrophil cytoplasm antibodies (ANCA). In this report, we describe a case of cardiogenic shock related to lupus myocarditis, successfully supported with veno-arterial extracorporeal circulatory membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) and immunosuppression, associated with renal involvement as a crescentic pauci-immune glomerulonephritis due to lupus nephritis and ANCA-positive vasculitis overlap. This case illustrates the complexity in management and diagnosis in the presence of such rare cardiac and renal manifestations, while further contributing to our knowledge and awareness of uncommon organ affliction caused by this auto-immune disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04567-5.Keywords: Cardiogenic shock, Mechanical circulatory support, Pauci-immune glomerulonephritis, Lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune (AI) disorder primarily characterized by renal disease, dermatologic manifestations, arthritis, and serositis [1]. Cardiac involvement is heterogeneous and extends from subclinical manifestations to cardiogenic shock (CS) [1–3]. Nevertheless, cardiovascular disease remains the main cause of death in SLE patients [4]. Myocarditis occurs in 5–10% of patients and diagnosis may be challenging due to atypical presentation and lack of sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) [5, 6]. Advanced imaging and accurate integration of other affected systems play a crucial role [5, 6]. The concomitant renal involvement may allow for a kidney biopsy and potentially facilitate a diagnosis, usually demonstrating glomerulonephritis with immune complex deposition in a “full-house” pattern [7]. The location of the deposits usually influences the clinical manifestations and degree of renal disfunction [8, 9]. The broad spectrum of immune-mediated diseases can add further difficulty in reaching a definite diagnosis and implementing an appropriate immunosuppression (IS) regime.

Case description

A 45-year-old male visited the emergency department due to fatigue, shortness of breath and inferior limb edema, progressively aggravating during the previous month. His medical history was remarkable for previous smoking habits, asthma, psoriasis, and an episode of myocarditis 5 years ago, with no history of previous substance abuse. Four years ago, the patient was admitted due to fever of unknown origin and the study suggested the presence of an AI disorder [positive anti-Smith (anti-Sm) and anti-centromere; positive anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) > 1:1280], with a favorable response to hydroxychloroquine, without evidence of renal involvement [normal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary sediment]. Regular follow-up in the internal medicine outpatient clinic was maintained, although without a definite diagnosis, being treated solely with acitretin (25 mg id) for psoriasis. During this period he maintained a normal renal function (with an average serum creatine of 1 mg/dL and an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 100 ml/min) and no proteinuria.

Upon observation, the patient was hemodynamically stable (blood pressure 121/67 mmHg, heart rate 79 bpm), afebrile (36,2 °C), with a peripheral oxygen saturation of 94%. Initial blood work highlighted the presence of anemia (hemoglobin 10,6 g/L), acute kidney injury (AKI; serum creatinine of 2.58 mg/dL; urea 233 mg/dL), an elevated NT-proBNP (> 35.000 pg/ml) with a stable high-sensitivity troponin T (40–45 ng/L, reference value < 14 ng/L) and a marked elevation of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (> 120 mm/h), despite slight elevation of C reactive protein (3 mg/dL). Nephrotic-range albuminuric proteinuria (with a urinary albumin to creatinine ratio of 3340 mg/g; no additional 24-hour urine protein test was conducted) and microhematuria were present. Chest radiogram demonstrated the presence of bilateral pleural effusion. A 12-lead-electrocardiogram revealed a sinus rhythm, with abnormal R wave progression in pre-cordial leads and nonspecific ST-T wave changes (Fig. 1). A transthoracic echocardiogram documented severe biventricular dilation and dysfunction [left ventricle (LV) ejection fraction < 25%; TAPSE 8 mm], with moderate to severe functional mitral regurgitation and mild pericardial effusion (Sup.material Video 1A and 1B).

The patient was admitted for stabilization and further investigation. However, he presented an unfavorable evolution with cardiac arrest in pulseless electric activity, which reverted after 3 cycles of advanced life support. Immediate orotracheal intubation was performed, vasoactive therapy with noradrenaline and dobutamine was initiated for hemodynamic stability, and the patient was admitted to an intensive care unit. Progression to cardiogenic shock (CS) was assumed, probably in relation to a previously undiagnosed AI disease, with further study demonstrating C3 consumption (but not C4) and positive ANA, anti-double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (dsDNA), anti-Sm, anti-Sjögren’s syndrome-related antigen A (SSA/Ro), anti-ribonucleoprotein (RNP) and anti-centromere titles. The study was also positive for ANCA anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO; 155 U/mL) (Table 1). This motivated the initiation of corticosteroid with methylprednisone pulses (1 g id for 3 days), followed by prednisolone (1 mg/kg id), later resulting in negative anti-dsDNA on day 4 but ANA (> 1/1280, in a speckled pattern) and anti-MPO (248.3 UA/mL) remained positive.

Signs of hypoperfusion and increasing lactate levels persisted, despite progressive escalation of hemodynamic support (vasoactive-inotropic score of 120 in < 24 h), so a tertiary center was contacted for mechanical circulatory support (MCS). Due to patient instability, local rescue with veno-arterial extracorporeal circulatory membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was performed. He was transferred to a cardiac intensive care unit (CICU), where an intra-aortic balloon pump was inserted for LV unloading. Continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration was performed due to anuric AKI. Complete suspension of inotropic support was possible on day 4 after MCS.

Endomyocardial biopsy of the right ventricle was performed on day 3 of admission, under corticosteroid therapy, revealing a normal myocardium, and was negative for cardiotropic virus. Prednisone tapering was performed throughout the admission (initially 60 mg id, reduced to 40 mg id after 8 days and 30 mg id after 14 days), and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) was introduced (1500 mg bid) on day 6, as at this time a diagnosis of SLE, with cardiac and renal affection, was considered the most likely cause, and this IS is deemed effective for the treatment of both manifestations.

The CICU admission had major occurrences, including MCS and IS complications: a ventilator associated pneumonia due to Strenotrophomonas maltophilia on day 8; cardiac tamponade due to increasing pericardial effusion, successfully solved with drainage on day 8 (with study showing reactive mesothelial and inflammatory cells but negative cytology and cultures); intestinal ischemia and concomitant cytomegalovirus colitis (isolated in the tissue, with positive viremia of 4164 UI/ml; the biopsy showed focal areas of submucosal small vessel vasculitis and fibrinoid necrosis, possibly solely due to ischemia), requiring a partial colectomy on day 9 and Ganciclovir therapy; and blood dyscrasia after one week of MCS, with anemia and thrombocytopenia possibly aggravated due to IS and Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole antibiotics, with frequent need for packed red cells and platelet transfusions. Due to bicitopenia IS was readjusted by decreasing the MMF dose (to 1000 mg bid, on day 10); this was implemented two days after the colectomy and no further dose reduction was done. After the resolution of the events above, the patient was successfully decannulated on day 14 after admission to the CICU, extubated on day 17, and suspended renal replacement therapy on day 22. Low-dose guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure was started (Bisoprolol 1,25 mg; Ramipril 1,25 mg). To complete the heart failure work-up the patient underwent coronary angio-CT, that revealed the absence of atherosclerotic plaques, and cardiac magnetic resonance. The latter, performed over a month after admission, demonstrated a significant improvement in LV ejection fraction (41%) due to global hypokinesia and biventricular dilation (Sup.material Video 2 A and 2B) and diffuse increased T1 and T2 mapping values suggested the presence of an inflammatory process (Fig. 3). Mild mid-mural late gadolinium enhancement was visualized in the septum.

Cardiac magnetic resonance with diffuse increase in T1 (A) and T2 (B) mapping values, suggesting the presence of an inflammatory process. Mid-mural late gadolinium enhancement is seen (C) in anterior septal wall

Normalization of serum creatinine and urea occurred throughout admission. To further characterize renal involvement and establish a final diagnosis for accurate IS regime, a kidney biopsy was undertaken on day 47, with a prime suspicion of systemic lupus erythematosus, according to EULAR/ACR classification criteria [10]. Histopathology revealed fibrinoid necrosis and cellular crescents (>50% glomeruli with cellular and fibrocellular crescents), as well as focal areas of mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity and membranoproliferative pattern, indicative of both vasculitic and proliferative glomerular lesions. The immunofluorescence by light microscopy was negative (no detected immunoglobulins, C1q, C3 or kappa and lambda light chains), including on paraffin-embedded tissue after pronase digestion, characteristic of a pauci-immune glomerulonephritis (Fig. 4). However, electronic microscopy revealed dispersed electron-dense deposits in subepithelial, subendothelial, intramembranous, and mesangial locations; no tubuloreticular inclusions were observed (Fig. 5). These deposits of variable location suggested a component of immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis contributing to the glomerular lesion. A diagnosis of crescentic pauci-immune glomerulonephritis associated with class III lupus nephritis was established.

The patient presented a favorable evolution, with complete recovery of functional status after a long period of rehabilitation. Hospital discharge occurred on day 54 post-admission, on 5 mg of prednisone and 1000 mg bid of MMF. He demonstrated progressive improvement in cardiac and renal function, with an eGFR of 50 ml/min, despite persisting proteinuria (4440 mg/g at discharge) and microhematuria. ANA and anti-MPO remained positive, with negative anti-dsDNA. Currently he is under follow-up in Cardiology, Nephrology and Rheumatology outpatient clinic, without major cardiovascular or renal events during the past two years (with stable cardiac function and decreased proteinuria down to around 1.2 g/g and creatinine at around 1.40 mg/dL). The patient had one additional admission, for gastrointestinal tract reconstruction, which was uneventful.

Discussion

LM is a potentially life-threatening condition [11]. The exact incidence of CS is unknown, as the reality of LM is only represented in isolated case reports and small cohorts [6, 11]. In this case, a previous admission increased suspicion for an AI disorder as the cause for new-onset heart failure, and serologies were compatible with SLE diagnosis [10], more specifically, the positive ANA and anti-dsDNA (being over 97% specific for SLE) [12]. While the surprisingly rapid decrease in dsDNA after only 3 days of IS suggested it could have been a false positive, the presence of other positive markers still indicated an AI cause (Table 1). Other findings were also compatible with SLE EULAR classification criteria, including polyserositis, renal dysfunction with proteinuria, and possibly anemia (although a negative direct Coombs test did not suggest an AI cause). Despite severe LV disfunction, troponin elevation was not significant; however, literature indicates that negative myocardial injury biomarkers do not exclude the presence of LM [5, 6]. EMB lacks sensitivity in myocarditis (10–30%), which is often dismissed due to the associated risks [6, 13]. In this patient, the results were inconclusive, although the previous initiation of IS might have compromised the outcome. After the exclusion of coronary disease (considered unlikely due to patient presentation and medical history, poor high-sensitivity troponin elevation, and spontaneous recovery under IS without revascularization), a cardiac magnetic resonance was performed. Myocardial edema and fibrosis (translated by elevated T2 and T1 mapping, respectively) were found, which was consistent with the diagnosis of myocarditis [1, 3, 5].

LM usually concurs with recovery of LV ejection fraction with appropriate IS treatment [10, 11, 13, 14]. Management commonly described includes induction therapy with cyclophosphamide and glucocorticoid [1, 10, 11]. However, due to the high infectious risk, the patient was started in MMF, with a favorable response, as its benefit in this context has been previously demonstrated [11]. The role of mechanical circulatory support in CS due to LM is scarcely described, most often represented in successful case reports [11, 14], which may not be representative of the true prognostic reality. Nevertheless, this case contributes to the limited evidence regarding VA-ECMO in LM, suggesting its value for short-term support. However, it comes with several risks and complications, of which this case is a very representative example. This highlights the need for an experienced team in CS management to achieve improved outcomes and the importance of early referral, as this patient presented with a rapid clinical deterioration.

Despite the cardiac manifestations being compatible with SLE, the kidney biopsy posed a new challenge in the definite diagnosis. LN is predominantly associated with immune complex deposition due to circulation of autoantibodies [15, 16], so the pauci-immune pattern observed in the kidney biopsy required consideration of the alternative diagnosis of ANCA-associated vasculitis. Although myocarditis due to ANCA-positive vasculitis is rare, its presentation is very heterogenous. The presence of vasculitis in the colectomy biopsy and subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement suggests that microvascular injury could also support this diagnosis. Furthermore, according to the EULAR/ACR criteria, a pauci-immune pattern and positive anti-MPO are sufficient for the diagnosis of microscopic polyangiitis [17]. While the reported presence of electron-dense deposits could further solidify a concomitant diagnosis of LN, previous reports have detected immunocomplexes by electronic microscopy in up to 50% of ANCA vasculitis cases, which may influence the disease course with higher proteinuria and a greater number of crescents [18].

In the context of the clinical manifestations suggesting a diagnosis of SLE, the discrepant findings can have several explanations. Assuming the sole diagnosis of LN, the laboratory findings could be explained as about 20% of patients with SLE have ANCA positivity (mainly MPO), usually associated with crescentic and more severe disease [12, 19]. Similarly to the EMB, the kidney biopsy was performed after implementation of IS, in this case several weeks, with possible clearing of some immune complex deposits, partially explaining the pauci-immune pattern. This theory is supported by previous reports that demonstrated the persistence of immunoglobulins even after 3 years of treatment and resolution of active lesions [20]. However, some cases of pauci-immune LN, albeit rare, have been reported, with fibrinoid necrosis but absent endocapillary hypercellularity and decreased immune complex deposition, with ANCA possibly also having a role in this histopathological pattern [9, 15, 21, 22]. SLE and ANCA-vasculitis overlap has also been previously described in literature [23], once again presenting with a pauci-immune crescentic pattern, with variable mesangial and endocapillary lesions [16, 24] as manifestations of both SLE and ANCA disease, as one facilitates the development of the other. Considering the clinical manifestations and biopsy findings, the most likely scenario is indeed of overlap, where both immunocomplexes and ANCA influence the kidney disease, promoting a more severe type with crescents and proteinuria. The presence of other antibodies, such as anti-ribonucleoprotein, might even suggest a more expansive disease such as mixed connective tissue disease; while kidney disease is a lot less common in this diagnosis, there have been case reports of associated pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis [25]. Multiple antibody positivity could also suggest a drug-induced vasculitis; however, while retinoids (more specifically isotretinoin) can be a common cause, acitretin specifically has not been described in kidney disease [26].

There are no established recommendations for the treatment of this clinical condition. For maintenance therapy, while MMF is already a recommended option for LN [27], for ANCA-vasculitis Rituximab or Azathioprine are preferred, with better reported outcomes when compared with MMF [28]. A meta-analysis suggested that it might be a valid option for remission maintenance in ANCA-vasculitis patients with kidney involvement; however, the remission rates were lower in cases with more than renal manifestations of vasculitis [29]. Despite this, the authors decided to continue the current therapy, considering the remarkable cardiac and renal recovery. At present, the patient is being considered for continued immunosuppression — albeit at a reduced dose — likely indefinitely, given the very high and potentially fatal risk of recurrence.

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexity of decision making in acutely ill patients with uncertain diagnosis and the challenges posed by LM in cardiogenic shock. An early referral for mechanical circulatory support is crucial in this setting. Furthermore, a specialized team is required to manage VA-ECMO complications and organ dysfunction in the presence of a multisystemic disease. The kidney involvement with a rare pauci-immune crescentic proliferative glomerulonephritis, compatible with overlapping of ANCA vasculitis and LN class III, further demonstrates the complexity of this case.

Lastly, this case underscores the importance of multidisciplinary work to reach a diagnosis and initiate appropriate therapy.
