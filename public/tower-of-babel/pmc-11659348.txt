SATB2 is an Emergent Biomarker of Anaplastic Thyroid Carcinoma: A Series with Comprehensive Biomarker and Molecular Studies
Authors: Dingani Nkosi, William E Crowe, Brian J Altman, Zoltán N Oltvai, Ellen J Giampoli, Moises J Velez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) is a rare and aggressive thyroid malignancy typically comprised of undifferentiated tumor cells with various histologic morphologies, which makes the diagnosis challenging. These tumors commonly show loss of thyroglobulin and TTF1 with preservation of cytokeratin (67%) and Paired Box Gene 8 (PAX8) (55%) expression. Identification of a sensitive immunohistochemical stain to aid in the diagnosis of ATC would be beneficial. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) against special AT-rich sequence-binding protein 2 (SATB2) protein is a sensitive and specific marker expressed in colorectal adenocarcinoma and bone or soft tissue tumors with osteoblastic differentiation. However, SATB2 is also expressed in other sarcomatous/undifferentiated neoplasms lacking osteoblastic differentiation. Using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) we showed that there is variable expression of SATB2 mRNA expression in ATCs. To evaluate the role of SATB2 protein expression in ATC, we performed PAX8, SATB2, pancytokeratin (AE1/AE3 & CAM5.2), claudin-4 and TTF1 immunostaining on 23 cases. ATCs showed retained expression of PAX8 in 65% (15/23); SATB2 was detected in 74% (17/23); pancytokeratin was expressed in 65% (15/23); claudin-4 was expressed in 35% (8/23) and TTF1 showed expression in 13% (3/23) of cases. Furthermore, 83% (5/6) of ATCs which lacked SATB2 expression, retained PAX8 expression, while 88% (7/8) of the tumors without PAX8 expression were positive for SATB2. Differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid cancers (n = 30), differentiated high grade thyroid carcinoma (n = 3), and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (n = 8) were negative for SATB2 immunoreactivity. Next-generation selected cases detected the commonly identified oncogenic variants including those in BRAF, RAS, TP53, and TERT promoter. Overall, we hereby demonstrate that SATB2 IHC may be used to support the diagnosis of ATC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12022-024-09833-0.Keywords: SATB2, Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, ATC, Thyroid cancer

Introduction

Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) is an aggressive malignancy, which accounts for 1–4% of all thyroid cancers[1–4]. ATC is more common in female elderly patients, primarily over 60 years of age [5, 6]. The majority of the patients with anaplastic thyroid carcinoma have distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis with a median survival of 1 to 6 months [2, 5, 6]. Currently, a multidisciplinary therapeutic approach comprising of surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies is recommended in the treatment of ATC [3, 5, 6].

ATC is often composed of undifferentiated cells with variant or heterogeneous histologic morphologies. Some of the common histological patterns in ATC include spindle cell morphology, pleomorphic morphology, osteoclast-like giant cell-rich and epithelioid-squamous morphology [2, 3]. Consequently, ancillary immunohistochemical stains show variable expression of different markers in ATC. The neoplastic cells are usually negative for thyroglobulin (96% of cases), calcitonin, and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) (70% of cases); but stain positively for cytokeratin (67% of cases) and Paired Box Gene 8 (PAX8) (up to 70% of cases) [7–9]. Given that some of these cases may lack PAX8 and/or keratin expression, identifying an alternative immunohistochemical marker or genetic alteration could have diagnostic utility and potentially serve as a therapeutic target. We evaluated the role of an immunohistochemical panel consisting of PAX8, SATB2, pancytokeratin, claudin-4, and TTF1 in the diagnosis of ATC.

Special AT-rich sequence-binding protein 2 (SATB2) is a DNA-binding transcriptional regulator that has been shown to play a major role in skeletal and craniofacial development [10–12]. Mice with SATB2 knockout or individuals with germline deletions have been shown to have craniofacial defects and compromised osteoblastic differentiation[12]. SATB2 immunohistochemistry (IHC) is a sensitive marker expressed in tumors of colorectal origin and in bone and soft tissue tumors with osteoblastic differentiation[13–16]. SATB2 has been shown to be detected in 100% of skeletal osteosarcomas and other tumors with osteoid matrix including osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, fibrous dysplasia, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma [16]. In the gastrointestinal tract, SATB2 has been utilized as a diagnostic marker; and a combination with CDX2 provides prognostic information in DNA mismatch repair protein deficient colon cancer [13, 17, 18]. SATB2 has also been demonstrated to be expressed by other non-osteoblastic differentiating tumors including dedifferentiated liposarcoma, gynecologic carcinosarcomas, BCOR::CCNB3 (Ewing-like) sarcoma, sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma [19–21].

Our seminal case consisted of a lung biopsy of a suspected metastatic lesion in a 64-year-old female patient with a clinical presentation of a rapidly growing thyroid/neck mass. Histologic sections showed malignant spindled tumor cells which were negative for pancytokeratin (AE1/AE3 and CAM5.2), claudin-4, TTF1, PAX8, and thyroglobulin. Subsequent immunohistochemical studies showed expression for SATB2, MDM2 (rare and focal), and CDK4 by IHC. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for MDM2 amplification to evaluate for liposarcoma was negative. Molecular analysis by DNA-based next-generation sequencing identified an HRAS mutation. Our findings coupled with the subsequent surgical confirmation of a thyroid mass eventually supported the diagnosis of ATC. Our index case led us to investigate whether the addition of SATB2 and claudin-4 can be used to support the diagnosis of ATC.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Specimens

We retrospectively selected thyroid biopsies and resections with a final diagnosis of ATC evaluated at the University of Rochester Medical Center (URMC) from 2005 to 2022. We identified 23 cases (9 resections and 14 excisional biopsies) of ATC and 30 cases with other thyroid cancers, namely, medullary thyroid carcinoma (n = 7), papillary thyroid carcinoma (n = 7), follicular thyroid carcinoma (n = 4), oncocytic (formerly Hürthle cell) carcinoma (n = 2), follicular adenoma (n = 7), non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary like nuclear features (n = 3), differentiated high-grade thyroid carcinoma (n = 3), and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinomas (n = 8). Representative hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides were reviewed to confirm the diagnosis and determine the morphology of the tumors.

Immunohistochemistry and Scoring System

Five micron-section tissue slides were cut from the processed paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using H&E automated strainers (Sakura Finetek, Inc.; Leica Biosystems, Division of Leica Microsystems Inc.). Immunohistochemical stains were performed as follows: TTF1 8G7G3/1 (Agilent; Santa Clara, CA), PAX8 (M) monoclonal BC12 (Biocare; Pacheco, CA) and pancytokeratin; a combination of AE 1/3 (Agilent; Santa Clara, CA) and CAM5.2 (Cell Marque; Rocklin, CA), stained on Dako Omnis using EnVision FLEX detection kit. SATB2 EP281 (Cell Marque; Rocklin, CA), and claudin-4 EP417 (Cell Marque; Rocklin, CA), stained on Leica BOND III using Refine Polymer detection kit.

IHC expression was scored based on diffuseness or percent of nuclear tumor cell staining and intensity of staining by two independent board-certified pathologists. Intensity was scored on a scale from 0 to 3 as follows: 0 for absence of staining, 1 + for weak, 2 + for moderate, and 3 + for strong staining. Diffuseness was scored on a scale from 0 to 3 as follows; 0 for < 1%, 1 for 1 to 25%, 2 for 25 to 50%, and 3 for > 50% tumor nuclei staining. The scores were then added to give a sum score between 0 and 6. Scores 2 and higher were counted as positive staining. Positive and negative controls were evaluated for all immunohistochemical stains. The scoring system has been previously used by Berg et.al in assessing GATA3 IHC [22].

Next Generation Sequencing

For internally tested specimens, genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks using the Qiagen DNeasy tissue kit per the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Sequencing libraries were prepared for sequencing on the Thermo Fisher’s Oncomine Focus Assay (OFA) panel per the manufacturers’ protocols. The enriched DNA libraries were sequenced on an Ion Proton (ThermoFisher, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) (OFA panel). FASTQ BAM files were processed through vendor-provided bioinformatics pipelines. Variant call files (vcf) were filtered to remove sub-threshold calls with less than 500X coverage and/or variant allele frequency (VAF) less than defined, validated thresholds ranging from 1 to 5%, depending on the type of mutation, as follows: 5% for single nucleotide variants (SNV); 1% for insertion-deletion mutations < 3bp; and 5% for insertion-deletion mutations 3bp or larger. Clinically relevant mutations from this VCF were manually annotated and reported by a board-certified molecular genetic pathologist. Sequenced regions (i.e., mutational hotspot regions, consisting of indicated exons) of the clinically ordered gene set for the OFA panel are in supplementary Table 2.

Copy number (cn) variations for were assessed for the following genes: ALK, BRAF, CCND1, CDK4, CDK6, EGFR, ERBB2, FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, FGFR4, KIT, KRAS, MET, MYC, MYCN, PDGFRA, PIK3CA using the vendor provided software (Ion Reporter, ThermoFisher) enhanced with an in-house Python script. The Ion Reporter copy number procedure build into the workflow was used to provide the basic copy number data. Individual samples cn values were normalized by dividing by the corresponding negative control cn values (HapMap) and then multiplying by 2, in order to reduce intra-run scatter. For each gene, the mean copy number, standard deviation, 5% and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the set of gene amplicons were then calculated and plotted. The output graphs included scatter plots of individual amplicon cn values together with the mean and the 5% and 95% CI plotted as error bars. A gene is reported to have a gain when the 5% CI is greater than the 4-cutoff value.

Prior FoundationOne CDx (Foundation Medicine, Inc) results were available on select cases (n = 5) which accessed 324 genes including copy number variations, substitutions, indels, and gene rearrangements.

SATB2 Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA concentration and purity were determined with the NanopDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE), and RNA quality was assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). cDNA was prepared using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed on all samples in triplicate using 12 ng of cDNA per 10-ul reaction in 384-well plates with TaqMan™ assays and TaqMan™ Universal Master Mix II, no UNG on the QuantStudio 12K Flex™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) with the following cycling parameters—50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 1 min. Following amplification, CT values were determined using the default settings of the QuantStudio 12K Flex Software v1.2.3. cDNA synthesis and quantitative RT-PCR were performed by the Genomics Research Center at the University of Rochester. TaqMan assays used for this analysis were: 18S (Hs99999901_s1), HPRT (Hs02800695_m1), GAPDH (Hs02758991_g1), ACTB (Hs01060665_g1), B2M (Hs00187842_m1), and SATB2 (3 assays—Hs01546836_m1, Hs00392652_m1, Hs00392655_m1).

Results

Variable mRNA Expression of SATB2 in Anaplastic Thyroid Carcinoma

We identified 23 cases with a diagnosis of ATC from our database from 2005 to 2022 with available FFPE blocks. The average patient age at diagnosis was 66 years (range 43–83), with more males (n = 15) than females (n = 8). The specimens were mainly excisional biopsies (n = 14) followed by resections (n = 9). Morphological examination of the cases showed epithelioid (n = 8), spindle cell (n = 4), osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells (n = 1), and combined spindle cell and epithelioid (n = 10) morphologies (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). Concurrent differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid cancer was identified in 8 of the cases; classic PTC (n = 2), PTC, tall cell variant (n = 4), and oncocytic carcinoma (n = 2).We did not identify any visible osteoid matrix in any of the cases.

Hematoxylin–eosin stains of different ATC tumor morphologies observed in our samples: epithelioid (A), spindle cell (B), spindle cell and epithelioid (C), and osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cell rich (D)

The Human Protein Atlas shows that SATB2 mRNA and protein expression is highly enriched in the cerebral cortex, colon, rectum, appendix, small intestine, and kidney tissue [23]. Normal thyroid gland tissue is reported to not have SATB2 protein expression but shows minimal SATB2 mRNA expression. To assess the mRNA expression of SATB2 in ATCs, we performed quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) from the FFPE tissue. The results demonstrated variable expression of SATB2 mRNA expression, with either high, moderate, or low expression when compared to the positive control (colon tissue) (Fig. 2).

Relative mRNA expression and IHC intensity of SATB2 in ATC tumors and other thyroid malignancies (Poscontrol = colon, Neg control = thyroid, follicular adenoma = FA, follicular thyroid carcinoma = FTC, papillary thyroid carcinoma = PTC, medullary thyroid carcinoma = MTC)

To evaluate whether the mRNA expression correlated with the protein expression of SATB2, we performed a comparative analysis between the SATB2 mRNA expression and SATB2 IHC staining intensity. Interestingly, our results showed that ATCs without expression of SATB2 (zero intensity) IHC, (sample numbers 5, 6, 8, 10) had minimal to moderate amounts of SATB2 mRNA expression. In contrast, all ATC tumors with any expression of SATB2 by IHC had some moderate to high expression of SATB2 mRNA. These results suggest that expression of SATB2 mRNA does not always lead to significant levels of protein overexpression as detected by IHC. To further support our observed results we re-analyzed RNA sequencing data of different thyroid malignancies using an R2 genomics analysis and visualization platform (a public online data mining and discovery platform) [24, 25]. The RNA sequencing data demonstrated significantly high SATB2 expression in ATC (n = 10,) compared to follicular thyroid carcinoma (n = 6), papillary thyroid carcinoma (n = 6), and normal thyroid (n = 6) as shown in supplemental Fig. 1. These results are supportive of the hypothesis that SATB2 protein expression detected by IHC can be used to support the diagnosis.

SATB2 IHC staining has been demonstrated in tumors of colorectal origin and with osteoblastic differentiation. However, the role of SATB2 IHC in ATC remains unknown. To evaluate whether SATB2 can be utilized in the diagnosis of ATC, we immunophenotyped ATC tumors using SATB2, PAX8, claudin 4, TTF-1, and pan-cytokeratin. IHC scoring of SATB2 was based on the intensity and diffuseness of the stain as previously described (Fig. 3).

Hematoxylin–eosin stain and corresponding SATB2 IHC intensity scores for representative ATC tumors samples (A–H) are shown

IHC assessment of the ATCs showed retained PAX8 in 65% (15/23); SATB2 was expressed in 74% (17/23); pancytokeratin was retained in 65% (15/23); claudin-4 was retained in 35% (8/23), and TTF1 was retained in 13% (3/23) of cases (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). There was variable IHC expression from focal (1%) to diffuse (100% of tumor cells) expression with weak (1 +) to strong (3 +) intensity (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). Additional analysis showed that 83% (5/6) of ATCs which lacked SATB2 expression, retained PAX8 expression, while 88% (7/8) of the cases without PAX8 expression were positive for SATB2 (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). There was one case identified that was negative for SATB2 and PAX8 staining but positive for Claudin-4 and pancytokeratin expression (Supplementary Table 1). Three cases were only positive for SATB2 (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, all cases had positive expression of at least one or more immunohistochemical stains utilized in the study; indicating the current IHC panel increases the sensitivity of detecting protein expression when evaluating ATCs.

We further assessed SATB2 immunostaining in different thyroid malignancies including medullary thyroid carcinoma, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), follicular thyroid carcinoma, follicular adenoma, oncocytic carcinoma, non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary like nuclear features (NIFTP), differentiated high-grade thyroid carcinoma, and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma. Our results demonstrated that all differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid cancers and poorly differentiated thyroid malignancies were negative for SATB2 (Table 2). Six cases of ATC with a combined PTC component showed IHC expression of SATB2 restricted to the anaplastic component (See supplemental Fig. 2). Overall, the sensitivity of SATB2 was 74% (95% CI, 52–90%) and specificity was 100% (95% CI, 88–100%).

Lastly, we performed next-generation sequencing (NGS) on select ATC samples (n = 18) to assess for actionable gene mutations or copy number variations. Five cases had prior FoundationOne (Foundation Medicine, Inc.) comprehensive NGS results while the other samples (n = 13) were analyzed by our in-house Oncomine Focus Assay (OFA) NGS system comprised of a 34 gene panel. Prior studies have demonstrated that ATC is not characterized by any distinct genomic alterations. Commonly identified oncogenic variants in ATC include those in BRAF, RAS, TP53, and TERT promoter [3, 7, 8]. TP53 and TERT gene mutations were identified in the five cases tested by the FoundationOne NGS assay, while the in-house OFA NGS panel detected oncogenic variants mostly in BRAF and NRAS followed by FGFR3 and PIK3CA mutations (Fig. 4). Note, the TERT promoter and TP53 mutations were not among the genes tested by our in-house NGS assay.

Discussion

Diagnosing ATC is challenging given the tumor cells often lack differentiation, showing loss of both PAX8 and pancytokeratin IHC expression. Identification of other sensitive markers may aid with diagnostic accuracy and have treatment implications. Our data demonstrates incorporating SATB2 and claudin-4 into an IHC panel, including TTF1, pancytokeratin, and PAX8 may increase the sensitivity of diagnosing ATC.

SATB2 had moderate to high relative mRNA expression in ATC tumors which was complemented by positive SATB2 immunoreactivity. To further reinforce that SATB2 is differentially expressed in ATC we evaluated RNA sequencing databases which demonstrated that SATB2 was highly expressed in ATC (n = 10) compared to follicular thyroid carcinoma (n = 6), papillary thyroid carcinoma (n = 6), and normal thyroid (n = 6) [24, 25]. Similarly, using re-analyzed single-cell sequencing data from PTC/ATC tumor and normal thyroid tissues, we observed more clustering of SATB2 in anaplastic tumor cells compared to the PTC component and normal thyroid tissues [26]. Mao, R et al. reported an ATC [27] case which exhibited multiple morphologies mainly consisting of monocyte-like cells with scattered osteoclast giant cell-like multinucleated cells and papillary thyroid carcinoma sections [27]. The anaplastic tumor cells were positive for SATB2 while the papillary thyroid carcinoma sections were negative [27]. Likewise, two more case reports of ATC reported SATB2 expression in the tumor cells [28, 29]. Comprehensive analysis of SATB2 expression in tissue microarrays (TMA) with more than 15,000 samples from 120 different tumor types demonstrated a positivity rate of 36% in ATC (n = 42) [30]. Similarly, a recent study on ATCs (where biopsies and TMAs constructed from resections were examined) showed expression of different lineage markers including SATB2 (22%, 8/36) and GATA3 (31%, 11/36) [31]. This study also showed that TTF1 was consistently negative while PAX8 showed variable intensity in 13 cases (36%) [31]. The low apparent sensitivity of SATB2 in the two studies compared to our study may be related to the inherent limited tissue sampling in biopsies and TMAs; which may limit the ability to access for heterogeneity of SATB2 expression and precludes detection of lower percentages of tumor cell positivity.

Conner et al. detected SATB2 nuclear immunoreactivity in all (100%) skeletal osteosarcomas (n = 43) and in 87% of primary extraskeletal osteosarcoma tumors (n = 9) [16, 21]. Other malignant soft tissue tumors, including dedifferentiated liposarcoma, fibrosarcoma, and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas have been shown to express SATB2; particularly when acquiring osteoblastic differentiation [15, 16, 32]. SATB2 immunoreactivity in ATC and other soft tumors may be attributed to the embryonic stage of osteoblastic differentiation which is devoid of visible osteoid matrix [15]. In our cohort of cases, we identified two cases of ATC with a papillary thyroid carcinoma component. SATB2 immunoreactivity was negative in the differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid cancer components while it was positive in the anaplastic tumor sections. The observed distinction in SATB2 staining in our two cases suggest SATB2 might be used as a marker for a dedifferentiated state and/or supports disease progression of a thyroid malignancy to its poorly differentiated ATC subtype. Different molecular mechanisms have been proposed for the role of SATB2 in cancer initiation and progression. Overexpression of SATB2 in epithelial cells has been shown to induce epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) which promotes cell morphology changes, cell motility, migration, and invasion [33–36]. Additionally, the SATB2-transformed cells acquire phenotypes of cancer stem cells and express stem cell markers and pluripotency-maintaining factors [33, 36]. Knock down expression of SATB2 in PTC cell lines has been shown to significantly inhibit migration, proliferation, and invasion [37]. These results further support the possibility of using SATB2 as a marker of cancer progression or dedifferentiation if a differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid cancer component exists in a resection or biopsy.

Claudin-4 was retained in 35% (8/23) of ATCs, which additionally showed co-expression of SATB2 (4/8), pancytokeratin (6/8), and PAX8 (7/8). Claudin-4 has been shown to be overexpressed in different epithelial malignancies including thyroid cancer [38, 39]. No significant differences in claudin-4 expression have been reported between the well-differentiated or differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid neoplasms and poorly differentiated/aggressive thyroid neoplasms [38–40]. Likewise, we observed that almost all cases with claudin-4 expression co-expressed PAX8. PAX8 is a transcription factor expressed in the nuclei of normal cells and neoplastic tissues from the kidney or thyroid [41, 42]. Approximately up to 40% of the undifferentiated/anaplastic thyroid carcinoma lose expression of PAX8 [7, 42–44]. Similarly, we identified 30% (n = 7/23) of ATCs that were negative for PAX8 and claudin-4 but positive for SATB2 (n = 7/7) and pancytokeratin (n = 4/7) expression. Additionally, in our study, 22% (n = 5/23) of ATCs were positive for PAX8 staining while negative for SATB2. PAX8 expression has been demonstrated to be preserved in ATC with an epithelioid morphology (≥ 65–100%) and a coexisting differentiated thyroid component [42–44]. In contrast, cases of ATC with either giant cell, pleomorphic, sarcomatoid, or spindle cell components are more likely to lose PAX8 staining (≤ 50%) [42–44]. Similarly, our results showed that cases with loss of PAX8 and SATB2 retention were associated with spindle cell morphology, while PAX8 positivity and lack of SATB2 expression were noted in cells with epithelioid morphology. Furthermore, we identified three ATCs with strong and diffuse expression of SATB2. Taken together, these results support PAX8 and SATB2 are divergent in lineage. We speculate that PAX8 is expressed in anaplastic tumor cells until cellular transformation or a divergent phenotype is acquired as noted by SATB2 positivity, with loss of site-specific markers.

Several studies have investigated whether ATC has distinct genetic alterations in comparison to other thyroid malignancies. The commonly identified mutations in ATC were found in TP53, BRAF, TERT promoter, PIK3CA, and the RAS genes [7, 45–48]. RAS-activating mutations and BRAF p.V600E mutations are commonly identified in well-differentiated or differentiated follicular cell-derived thyroid cancers [8, 46]. Detection of RAS mutations is associated with follicular adenoma or follicular carcinoma, even when co-occurring in the same thyroid gland, while identification of BRAF mutation is linked with classic papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) [45, 46, 49]. Similarly, in our study, we identified BRAF and NRAS as the common mutations which were associated with a coexisting PTC or other follicular malignancies. TERT and TP53 mutations were only identified in five cases which were previously sent for FoundationOne NGS testing, while our in-house NGS panel was limited by its inability to test for TERT and TP53 mutations in our samples.

A major shortcoming in our study was our inability to collect and evaluate, intrathyroidal spindle epithelial tumors with thymus-like elements, primary thyroid sarcomas, or other sarcomatoid neoplasms arising from or extending to the thyroid for SATB2 expression. We did, however, identify a case of metastatic high-grade sarcoma to the thyroid which stained positive for SATB2. The patient was initially diagnosed with a high-grade sarcoma arising from the groin mass which was morphologically compared with the thyroid mass to confirm the diagnosis. The metastatic sarcoma additionally showed cartilage and osseous components, the latter of which is supportive of osteoblastic differentiation, as further highlighted by SATB2 expression. Given these limitations, SATB2 should not be utilized alone to differentiate ATC from other potential soft tissue tumors arising within the thyroid region. Notably, David Dum et al. showed squamous cell carcinomas of the head and neck (larynx, pharynx, and floor of mouth) lack SATB2 expression [30].

In summary, a multifaceted approach, using clinical information, imaging studies, tumor morphology, and immunohistochemistry should be considered to support the diagnosis of ATC. Furthermore, this study raises the possibility of utilizing SATB2 as a marker of dedifferentiation or a potential targetable therapeutic biomarker.
