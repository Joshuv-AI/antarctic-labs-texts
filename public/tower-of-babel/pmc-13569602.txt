Potential Role for the Ubiquitin Ligase SIAH2 in Modulating Bone Morphology and Adiposity
Authors: Matthew C Scott, Bhaswati Ghosh, Gail Kilroy, Beata Lecka-Czernik, Z Elizabeth Floyd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarrow adipose expansion, with obesity or ageing, increases fracture risk. Skeletal stem cells can commit to osteoblast or adipocyte endpoints, an inverse relationship influenced by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ). Seven in absentia homologue 2 (SIAH2) is a ubiquitin ligase that acts in peripheral adipose tissue stromal progenitors, promoting PPARγ activity and adipogenesis. The pivotal role of PPARγ in bone marrow adipose tissue accumulation prompted us to evaluate SIAH2’s role in the balance between osteogenesis and adipogenesis. Using adherent skeletal stem and/or progenitor cells (SSPCs) isolated from the marrow of wild-type (WT) and Siah2−/− mice, we assessed in vitro osteogenic and adipogenic capacity. Bone morphology and adiposity were assessed, in vivo, using WT and Siah2−/− mice in the context of dietary, high-fat diet, and pharmacological, rosiglitazone, PPARγ stimulation. We hypothesized increased osteogenesis in Siah2−/− SSPCs and expected Siah2−/− mice would be resistant to the effects of PPARγ activation on trabecular morphology and marrow fat expansion. Our results show that Siah2−/− SSPCs display increased capacity for both osteogenesis and adipogenesis in vitro. Further, exploratory experiments in vivo indicate Siah2−/− mice have fewer, yet thicker, trabeculae on a low fat diet, while Siah2−/− mice on a high-fat diet paired with rosiglitazone trend toward more marrow adipose, decreased bone mass and increased trabecular spacing compared to WT. Our results suggest SIAH2 moderates both osteogenic and adipogenic commitment as the absence of SIAH2 results in progenitor cells that will more readily commit to either outcome. These observations underscore SIAH2’s context-specific modulatory effects on PPARγ activities determined by tissue, nutrients and pharmacological cues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00223-026-01596-6.Keywords: Seven in absentia homolog 2, PPARγ, Trabecular bone, Adipogenesis, Osteogenesis, Skeletal stem cells, Skeletal progenitor cells, Skeletal stem and/or progenitor cells

Introduction

Bones undergo remodeling throughout the lifespan to maintain their strength and physiological function. Remodeling is largely determined via the interaction of bone forming osteoblasts and bone resorbing osteoclasts, but the aggregate of contributing factors is complex. Bone marrow adipose tissue (BMAT) is a substantial modulatory factor in bone remodeling, and at the tissue level, increased BMAT is generally associated with reduced bone mass and increased fracture risk [1], a negative relationship that is best characterized in anorexia nervosa but also described in diseases such as type II diabetes mellitus and obesity [2]. At the cellular level, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and marrow adipocytes are derived from non-differentiated cells within the marrow space. While osteoclasts are derived from myeloid progenitors [3], common stromal progenitors of mesenchymal lineage (skeletal stem cells, SSCs) exist for bone marrow adipocytes and osteoblasts [4–6]. Differentiation of SSCs toward adipogenic, in lieu of osteogenic, endpoints is thought to play some role in the negative relationship between BMAT and bone mass.

The determinants of SSC differentiation toward adipogenesis or osteogenesis are numerous and have been reviewed previously [7, 8]. Of the contributing factors, master transcriptional regulators Peroxisome Proliferator-activated Receptor Gamma 2 (PPARg2) and Runt-related Transcription Factor 2 (RUNX2) are generally accepted as the “tipping” point for adipogenic or osteogenic commitment, respectively. Reciprocal control of PPARγ2 and RUNX2 is modulated by the canonical Wnt signaling pathway [9]. Wnt signaling stabilizes β-catenin to up-regulate RUNX2 transcription and subsequently down-regulate PPARγ2, resulting in osteoblastogenesis. Alternatively, up-regulation of PPARγ2 results in PPARγ2-mediated de-stabilization of β-catenin, inhibiting osteogenesis and promoting adipogenesis. Other examples of factors that inversely modulate PPARγ2 and RUNX2 include TAZ (transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif), p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and Protein Phosphatase 5 [10–12]. Continued identification of PPARγ2 and RUNX2 modulators may provide new vectors for improved understanding and/or treatment of increased marrow adiposity and fracture risk.

Seven-in absentia homolog 2 (SIAH2) is a ubiquitin ligase commonly studied for its role in cancer and hypoxia [13, 14], but our lab previously showed SIAH2 also influences adipogenesis in peripheral fat depots. SIAH2 promotes adipogenesis by forming a complex with the pro-adipogenic early B-cell factor 1 (EBF1) and pro-bone forming zinc-finger protein 521 (ZFP521) [15] to destabilize ZFP521 and stabilize EBF-1, leading to activation of PPARγ transcriptional activity and adipogenesis [16]. Considering the role of ZFP521 in bone formation and the pivotal role of PPARγ in the balance between adipogenesis and osteogenesis, SIAH2 may contribute to the balance between bone and bone marrow adiposity. Frew et al. reported SIAH2’s potential role in bone remodeling in their initial characterization of globally deleted Siah2 in mice on a C57BL/6J background (Siah2−/−) [17]. Characterization of the Siah2−/− mice, using mononuclear cells isolated from whole bone marrow, described increased osteoclastogenesis, suggesting SIAH2 modulates bone resorption. Based on in vitro data alone, increased osteoclastogenesis would result in more bone resorption; however, bone histology on 10-week-old wild-type and Siah2−/− mice found no significant difference in the surface of bone occupied by bone resorbing osteoclasts with no difference in overall bone structure [17]. In contrast, we found that impaired adipogenesis in Siah2−/− adipose tissue stromal vascular fraction cells was associated with increased expression of β-catenin, Wnt10a, Wnt10b and Wnt6 [18], factors that promote osteogenesis. Upregulation of the genes encoding β-catenin and Wnt signaling proteins in the stromal cell population suggests SIAH2 may inhibit bone formation while promoting adipocyte development.

In the current study, we assessed the Siah2−/− mouse model with a specific interest in the effect of SIAH2 on osteogenesis and adipogenesis in response to an obesogenic diet. We combined in vitro studies of osteogenesis in skeletal stem and/or progenitor cells (SSPCs) from male wild-type and Siah2−/− mice withexploratory experiments, in vivo, analyzing SIAH2’s effect on bone morphology in the context of an obesogenic diet combined with modulation of PPARγ transcriptional activity. Herein, we report loss of SIAH2 increased osteogenic protein expression and mineral nodule formation in vitro. Micro-computed tomography predicts more robust trabecular morphology in Siah2−/− mice fed a low-fat diet, but contrary to our expectations, the metabolic challenge of a high fat diet coupled with PPARγ agonism may be deleterious to trabecular structure in the Siah2−/− mice, corresponding to a possible increase in marrow adiposity compared to wild-type mice. Our results support a role for SIAH2 in bone remodeling and suggest SIAH2 acts to limit bone loss and marrow adiposity with obesity.

Methods

Animal Experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice, wild-type (WT) and Siah2−/− (SKG), were multi-housed with a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 24 °C. Mice were randomized by bodyweight to a defined 10% low-fat (LF) or sucrose matched 45% high-fat diet (HF) beginning at 4–5 weeks of age and fed ad libitum. After four weeks on the defined LF or HF diets, HF mice were randomized to the HF alone (HF), HF supplemented with PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone (ROSI), or HF supplemented with PPARγ antagonist GW9662 (GW). The HF, ROSI, and GW mice were single-housed as part of this randomization, and LF diet mice remained multi-housed. The LF and HF diets (n = 8/group) continued for an additional 12wks. The ROSI or GW-supplemented HFs were formulated at 50 mg/kg diet (Research Diets custom diet) for 5–7 mg/kg bodyweight/day intake of ROSI or GW based on 35 g bodyweight and 4 g food intake/day/mouse. Food intake was measured daily to quantify ROSI and GW intake.

Animal weight was measured weekly, and body composition was assessed bi-weekly via nuclear magnetic resonance (Bruker). At end of the study, tissues were harvested between 7-11am. Animals were randomly selected for harvesting skeletal muscle tissue for molecular analysis and/or isolating the tibia for bone analysis without predetermined exclusion criteria in either category. Tibias were excised and placed in fixative prior to analysis of bone architecture. All animal experiments were carried out according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Pennington Biomedical Research Center Animal Care and Use Committee (PB-22-013).

Blood Glucose, Insulin, and Glucose Tolerance Tests

For the glucose tolerance test, mice were fasted 4 h prior to administering 2 g glucose/kg fat-free mass/mouse by intraperitoneal injection [19], carried out in all diet groups at the end of study. Tail venous blood was collected and assayed for glucose levels using a glucometer (Breeze2, Bayer Health Care). Fasting serum glucose levels were measured using a glucometer (Breeze2) and fasting insulin levels were assayed via ELISA (Crystal Chem) at the end of study.

Trabecular Architecture

Proximal tibias were scanned using the µCT35 (Scanco Medical AG). Scans were performed at the following settings: 70kVp, 114µA, 500 projections/180°, 300ms integration, and 6 μm voxels. The trabecular area of interest, a 1.4 mm z-height starting 70 μm below intercondylar notch, included 200 slices manually contoured to exclude cortex. Analysis and quantification of trabecular parameters was conducted using Evaluation Program V6.5-3 (Scanco Medical AG) and followed standard guidelines [20].

Marrow Adiposity

Decalcified tibias were osmium-stained as described previously [21]. In short, tibias were placed in a decalcification solution of ethylenediamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) and disodium salt dehydrate (NaOH). After decalcification for 14d, tibias were submerged in 1% osmium tetroxide for 48 h, rinsed, and scanned. The entire osmium-stained tibia was scanned in the µCT 35 at 70kVp, 114µA, 500ms integration time, and a voxel resolution of 12 μm. After scanning, DICOMS were reopened in ORS Dragonfly (Version 2022.2). Marrow space was manually contoured at every 5th slice for the first 250 slices and every 20th slice thereafter, followed by interpolation. A threshold pixel/voxel value of 13,762 was applied to the marrow space ROI (max grey value = 32,767) which is comparable to thresholds reported for osmium stained adipose using a threshold of 420/1000 [21]. Marrow was separated into proximal and distal at the tibiofibular junction; percent adiposity reported as the percentage of labeled voxels.

Cell Culture

All cell culture experiments were performed using primary bone marrow cells isolated from murine hind-limb long bones (tibiae and femora). To collect bone marrow cells, hind-limb long bones were cleaned of non-bone tissue and placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). All subsequent steps were performed in sterile conditions. Epiphyses were gently removed via forceps and bones were placed into 200 µl pipettes tips, which were cut and placed into 1.5 ml micro centrifuge tubes containing 100 µl of basal media (MEMα supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S)). The bone marrow was spun down from tibiae and femora after epiphysis removal by centrifugation at ≥ 10,000 x g for 15s as previously described [22]. The pelleted cells, total bone marrow, were resuspended in basal media and filtered through a 70 μm filter prior to plating for experimental use or liquid nitrogen cryopreservation in FBS and 10% DMSO. For osteogenic and adipogenic outcomes, adherent skeletal stem and/or progenitor cells (SSPCs) were isolated by culturing total bone marrow for 48 h on tissue culture plates. After 48 h, non-adherent cells were removed by aspiration, adherent cells were rinsed once with sterile PBS, and basal media was refreshed. Adherent cells, SSPCs, were allowed to proliferate, refreshing media every 48–72 h, and were passaged once before experimental plating. For osteoclast outcomes, non-adherent cells (bone marrow mononuclear cells) were isolated from total bone marrow cultures by collecting media (non-adherent cells in suspension) from the total marrow culture followed by centrifugation at 400 x g for 5 min to pellet the non-adherent population. Pellets were resuspended, counted, and plated for experiments.

To evaluate osteoblastogenesis, SSPCs were isolated and plated as described above. Cells were plated at 1 × 106 cells per cm2 into 12-well (staining and gene expression) or 6-well (protein isolation) tissue culture plates (Corning) in basal media. SSPCs were allowed to continue proliferating with basal media exchange every 48–72 h. When cells reached confluency (95–100%), they were induced by supplementing basal media to 8mM beta-glycerol phosphate, and 1mM ascorbic acid. Supplemented media was exchanged every 48 h until protein isolation (6d post-induction), RNA isolation (day 14) or Alizarin Red staining (14 or 28 d post-induction).

Bone marrow mononuclear cells were used to conduct osteoclastogenesis assays as described previously for TRAP staining and resorption pit assays [22, 23]. Cells were pelleted, resuspended in osteoclast differentiation media (MEMα, 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 30 ng/ml m-CSF, 50 ng/ml RANKL), and counted. Cells were plated in 48-well tissue culture plates or calcium coated 48-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well. Media was changed every 48 h until endpoints: 7 days for TRAP staining of multinucleated cells and18 days for visible resorption pits.

To evaluate adipogenesis, SSPCs were maintained in MEMα with 10% FBS, 1% pen/strep until ~ 90% confluent before detaching the cells with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C and redistributing in a 24 well format at ~ 1 × 105 cells/well in DMEM, high glucose, 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep, 20 µM rosiglitazone and 2 µM insulin as described [22]. The basal media was exchanged every 48 h until the cells were 80–90% confluent. At that point, the media was changed to DMEM, high glucose, 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep, 20 µM rosiglitazone, 2 µM insulin, 50 mM isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX) and 1 µM dexamethasone (induction media). After maintaining the cells in this induction media for 4 days, the media was exchanged for basal DMEM, high glucose, 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep, 20 µM rosiglitazone and 2 µM insulin. Four days later (day 8 post induction), the cells were harvested for RNA and imaging analysis.

Staining, Image Acquisition, and Image Quantification

Alizarin Red and TRAP staining were performed by removing media and gently rinsing wells with PBS. Cells were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (15 min) for Alizarin Red or 2.5% glutaraldehyde (30 min) for TRAP (Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase) staining. Alizarin-Red solution (Sigma Aldrich, MI) was used to visualize plaque development in bone marrow cell cultures differentiated to osteoblasts by adding 0.5 ml of solution to each well of a 12-well plate and incubating at room temperature for 30 min prior to aspirating the staining solution. TRAP staining solution was mixed according to the manufacturer guidelines as described [22], added to each well of a 48-well plate, and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min followed by removal of the staining solution. To visualize osteoclast resorption pits, the media was aspirated, and 10% sodium hypochlorite was added to each well of the unstained calcium-coated plates, followed by rinsing with distilled water. After rinsing, plates were allowed to dry before image acquisition.

Image acquisition for Alizarin Red, TRAP, and resorption assays were performed using an iPhone 15 1x back-facing camera (Apple, CA) and the Camera + app version 24.24 (LateNightSoft, Spain). Wells were imaged at 1x or 100x using the back-facing camera directly or a cell phone eyepiece adapter (BoliOptics, CA) interfaced with an inverted microscope. Pit formation images were taken with the plate on a black background so pits could be visualized as dark areas within the well. In preparation for quantification, images were opened in GNU Image Manipulation Program (GIMP, Charlotte, CA, USA), clipped, and exported (TIFF). Images were re-opened in ImageJ and converted to 8-bit for analysis. For quantifying Alizarin Red staining, 1x images were converted to grayscale and inverted; stain was quantified as mean gray value. To quantify osteoclast size, 100x images of each well were opened and a region of interest (ROI) was manually drawn around each TRAP+ cell with ≥ 3 nuclei for area calculations; nuclei count per osteoclast was done manually. To quantify resorption pit area, 1x images were opened (ImageJ) and converted to 8-bit grayscale. A Threshold of ≤ 80 was applied to label pits and analyze particles was used to identify individual pits and their area within each image.

To image neutral lipid accumulation post adipogenic induction, SSPCs were grown and differentiated on poly-D-lysine (Gibco, Cat# A3890401)-coated coverslips (diameter 12 mm) placed in a 24-well culture plate. On day 8 post-induction, cells were rinsed with PBS three times followed by fixation for 1 h in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at RT. Coverslips were washed with 30mM glycine in PBS three times. Cells were then incubated with 5µM of BODIPY493/503 (Invitrogen, Cat#D-3922) for 15 min at RT to visualize neutral lipids. Cells were washed with PBS three times and mounted with Prolong Gold Antifade Reagent with DAPI for confocal microscopy. Nuclei and lipid positive cells, cells with well-defined/circular lipid droplets, were counted manually. Percent of BODIPY493/503-positive cells were graphed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.

Protein Expression

Cultures were rinsed with cold Phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4 (PBC) and harvested in nondenaturing buffer containing 10 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% Igepal with protease inhibitors (1 µM PMSF, 1 µM pepstatin, 1 µg/ml leupeptin, 5 µg/ml aproptinin, 1 µM E64), phosphatase inhibitors (2 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM NaF, 2 mM Na4O7P2, 2 mM βGP) and 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide. Lysates were sonicated (Branson 450 Sonifier) and centrifuged at 13.4 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined via BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated in polyacrylamide (National Diagnostics) gels containing SDS and transferred to nitrocellulose (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked in 4% nonfat milk in 5mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0 with 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-RUNX2, Santa Cruz Biotech sc-390351; anti-beta-catenin, Cell Signaling 9562; anti-beta-actin, GeneTex, GTX109639) and the results visualized with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) and enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher/Pierce). Equal loading was confirmed by MemCode staining.

Gene Expression

Total RNA was collected from cultures using TRI Reagent (Molecular Research Center Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA) and RNeasy Mini kits (Qiagen, Germantown, TN, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Isolated RNA (500 ng) was reverse transcribed using MultiScribe Reverse Transcriptase (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with random primers at 37 °C for 2 h. Real-time PCR was performed with SYBRGreen Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on the QuantStudio 6 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) set to a comparative Ct with meltcurve. The assays were performed in triplicate, and the results were normalized to 18s or Rlp13α (adipogenesis or osteogenesis, respectively) and analyzed using the 2−∆∆CT method with WT control group used as the calibrator. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.1.2) or JMP statistics (Version 7.0.0). Specific analysis details are described in figure legends. An alpha of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Based on our previous findings, we hypothesized that skeletal stem cells (SSPCs) from Siah2−/− mice would demonstrate increased osteogenic protein expression and increased mineral nodule formation during osteogenic induction compared to SCCs obtained from wild type C57BL/6J mice. With this in mind, we collected SSPCs from Siah2−/− (SKG) and wild-type (WT) male mice and differentiated them in osteogenic media (Fig. 1). Cells were differentiated for 14 or 28 days and stained using Alizarin Red to visualize calcium deposition consistent with mineralization of extracellular matrix. Cells from Siah2−/− mice showed increased mineralization compared to those from the wild-type controls (Fig. 1a, b). To determine early osteogenic protein expression, we performed a follow-up experiment in which the Siah2−/− (SKG) and wild-type (WT) SSPCs were differentiated in osteogenic media for 6 days prior to cell lysate collection to assay protein expression of RUNX2 and β-catenin. Western blot analysis did not show a significant effect on RUNX2 or β-catenin expression, although RUNX2 trended higher in BMCs from Siah2−/− mice (Fig. 1c, d). To evaluate gene expression, SSPCs from WT and SKG mice were differentiated in osteogenic media for 14 days prior to lysate collection and analysis. Cells from SKG mice showed increased expression for key osteogenic transcription factors (Runx2 and Sp7) and genes associated with matrix mineralization (Bglap and Dmp1; Fig. 1e).

Although early osteogenic protein expression did not show a significant difference between SKG and WT SSPCs at day 6 of differentiation (Fig. 1c, d), osteogenic induction resulted in significantly higher in vitro mineralization (day 14 and 28) and osteogenic gene expression (day 14), suggesting increased osteogenic potential for SIAH2 null cells (Fig. 1a, b, e). Considering the interplay between osteoblast and osteoclasts, we re-examined osteoclastogenesis using bone marrow mononuclear cells from WT and SKG mice (Fig. 2). Consistent with Frew et al. [17], we found that cells from SKG mice produced a larger number of multinucleated, TRAP positive cells, or osteoclasts (Fig. 2a, b). We noted, however, that the osteoclasts derived from the cells of SKG mice appeared smaller, so we quantified average osteoclast size from WT and SKG cells where SKG-derived osteoclasts were significantly smaller (Fig. 2c). This raised the question of resorption potential for SKG-derived osteoclasts compared to WT. To test resorption activity, we used calcium-coated plates to assay osteoclast differentiation and calcium resorption in vitro. There was no difference in resorption pit characteristics between strains after maintaining the osteoclast cultures for 18 days. Despite noting smaller osteoclasts, SKG osteoclasts did not show diminished resorptive capacity in vitro; both strains were still able to form comparable resorption pits as shown in Fig. 2, d-g.

Our in vitro results (Figs. 1 and 2) show that SSPCs from SKG mice have increased bone forming potential and have no difference in bone resorbing potential. With our in vitro results suggesting a net bone anabolic effect in the SKG bone, we wanted to re-examine the ex vivo bone morphology in the globally SIAH2 deleted mice in the context of dietary metabolic stressors that are harmful to bone. To test this, male SKG and WT mice at 4 weeks of age were randomized into 10% low-fat (LF) and 45% high-fat (HF) diet groups. To test a potential role for PPARγ in our observations, at 8 weeks of age, mice in the high-fat diet group were further randomized to high-fat diet (HF), high-fat diet with rosiglitazone (HF/ROSI) as a high affinity PPARγ agonist, or a high-fat diet with GW9662 (HF/GW), as a highly selective PPARγ antagonist to limit PPARγ endogenous ligand binding. Mice continued this diet for an additional 12 weeks (Fig. 3a). The mice consumed an average of 3.5–3.8 mg rosiglitazone/kg body weight/day and 3.4–3.5 mg/kg body weight/day of GW9662 with the WT mice consuming a small, but statistically significant higher amount of mg/kg body weight/day rosiglitazone compared to the SKG mice (Fig. 3b). Analysis of week 4–14 bodyweights show that both SKG and WT mice have significantly higher body weights on a high-fat diet compared to the low-fat diet. In addition, rosiglitazone significantly increased body weight independent of genotype in mice fed a high fat diet compared to the high fat diet alone (Fig. 3c). A similar relationship was found for percent fat mass (Fig. 3d), where both strains showed an expected increase in percent fat mass and corresponding decrease in percent lean mass (Fig. 3e) with the high fat diets. The trend toward higher percent fat in the SKG mice on a high-fat diet with rosiglitazone corresponded to a small, but statistically significant, reduction in percent lean mass compared to WT mice on a high-fat diet with rosiglitazone (Fig. 3e). Interestingly, SKG mice in the HF/ROSI group consumed less food, and therefore less rosiglitazone (Fig. 3b), despite the comparable increase in percent fat mass and bodyweight. In short, WT and SKG mice maintained comparable adiposity when challenged with a high fat diet coupled activation or inhibition of PPARγ activity, indicating fat mass expansion is preserved with global SIAH2 deficiency.

In an earlier study using the high fat diet model of obesity, we found that global SIAH2 depletion improved glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity [24]. While insulin resistance negatively impacts bone health, improved insulin sensitivity with PPARγ full agonism is detrimental to bone health [25]. Given SIAH2-mediated regulation of PPARγ activity, we evaluated metabolic parameters in the WT and SKG mice in the high fat diet with rosiglitazone as a PPARγ agonist or GW9662 as a PPARγ antagonist. Glucose levels were significantly higher for all mice on the HF diet compared to LF diet mice, and SKG mice in the HF/ROSI group had higher glucose compared to the within diet controls (WT HF/ROSI, Fig. 4a). However, rosiglitazone in the HF diet lowered insulin levels in both genotypes comparable to insulin levels in the LF diet groups, consistent with improved insulin sensitivity due to PPARγ activation (Fig. 4b). This is consistent with increased fat mass with rosiglitazone (Fig. 3d) and the reduced insulin levels observed in WT and SKG mice with rosiglitazone compared to the HFD alone or supplemented with GW9662 (Fig. 4b). Glucose handling in our cohort was also examined using a glucose tolerance test (GTT) to compare WT and SKG mice across the diet groups. SKG mice on a LF diet had a significantly lower area under the curve (AUC) for blood glucose across the 2 h GTT and a lower blood glucose corresponding to peak insulin levels with IPGTT [26] (GTT at 30 min) compared to WT mice. Although elevated compared to the LF diet, AUC for glucose in the HF-fed SKG mice trended lower than the HF-fed WT mice (Fig. 4c, d). Rosiglitazone in the HF diet reduced the glucose AUC in both genotypes to levels comparable to the SKG LF diet in contrast to the elevated AUC with the GW9662 supplemented HF diet (Fig. 4c, d). Importantly, loss of SIAH2 lowered peak glucose levels independent of diet or rosiglitazone supplementation but did not override elevated glucose levels with inhibition of PPARγ activity (Fig. 4e), suggesting the effect of Siah2 on glucose homeostasis is mediated by PPARγ.

We previously found that early osteogenic gene expression is increased in Siah2−/− adipose tissue stromal progenitor cells [18]. Tibias were decalcified, osmium stained and scanned according to standard guidelines [21]. Our original expectation for the SKG trabecular and marrow adiposity outcomes were more robust morphology and decreased adiposity, even for those on a high-fat diet with rosiglitazone. This expectation was based on previous data from our lab, showing impaired adipogenesis and increased osteogenic signaling in Siah2 null SSPCs [18]. This expectation was further supported by the results shown in Fig. 1, where Siah2 null cells show increased mineralization in vitro. Still, our ex vivo outcomes for trabecular bone morphology and marrow adiposity were opposite, indicating SIAH2’s role in the balance between adipogenesis and osteogenesis within the bone marrow is more nuanced compared to our previous work with adipose derived stromal cells. While our in vivo adiposity data lacked sufficient replicates for statistical comparisons to the SKG HF/ROSI group (n = 2), the mean percent marrow adiposity of those two mice was notably higher (59%, SKG vs. 33%, WT) and provide a preliminary indication that SIAH2 plays an inhibitory role in marrow adipogenesis. To test this, we used a more feasible in vitro approach where WT and SKG SSPCs were induced towards adipogenesis as previously described [22]. In line with our results for marrow adiposity in the SKG mice, Siah2 null cells were more responsive to adipogenic induction (Fig. 7), displaying increased prevalence for lipid droplet positive cells and increased expression of key adipogenic genes (Pparg2, Adipoq, Lpl, and Fabp4).

Discussion

Changes in commitment of skeletal stem and/or progenitor cells (SSPCs) to adipogenic or osteogenic endpoints is a key contributor to the association between marrow fat expansion and bone degradation with age and obesity [2, 28]. To mitigate fracture risk with obesity or aging, understanding the mechanisms underlying the cellular commitment to building bone or increasing bone marrow fat is essential. We previously reported the E3 ubiquitin ligase SIAH2 functions as a key modulator of commitment to adipogenesis by promoting ZFP423-mediated activation of PPARγ [16, 18, 29]. Those studies also pointed to increased osteogenic potential in Siah2 null adipose tissue stromal cells [18], consistent with our current observation that SIAH2 deficiency is associated with increased osteogenesis in vitro. Frew et al. previously found differences in hematopoietic cell populations in the Siah2 null mouse, reporting increased osteoclastogenesis in vitro, but no change in bone histology in vivo [17]. In addition to observing increased osteogenesis, our results in the Siah2 null mice concur with Frew and colleagues, finding that loss of SIAH2 increases osteoclastogenesis in vitro. However, we found that the increased number of smaller osteoclasts did not translate into a functional change in bone resorptive capacity compared to the wild-type cells. When combined with increased osteogenesis in vitro, we anticipated the neutral effect on bone resorptive capacity would correspond to an in vivo phenotype of more robust trabecular bone morphology in the Siah2 null mice when challenged with an obesogenic high fat diet.

To begin testing a possible effect of Siah2 on trabecular structure, we carried out a preliminary experiment using a limited number of bone samples obtained from the WT and Siah2 null mice. While bone volume and trabecular thickness was greater in the Siah2 null mice on a LF diet, a combination of high fat diet and rosiglitazone resulted in decreased bone volume and poorer trabecular structure when SIAH2 was absent. These changes are especially notable as mice from a C57BL/6 background are resistant to the negative effects of rosiglitazone on bone volume despite displaying increased marrow adipose [30], an outcome recapitulated in the C57BL/6 wild-type cohort shown here in Figs. 5 and 6. The unexpected negative effect of Siah2 deletion on bone morphology in response to the high fat diet coupled with PPARγ stimulation (HF/ROSI), was paired with a possible increase in proximal marrow adiposity compared to the wild type mice. The effect of Siah2 deletion on bone marrow lipid accumulation suggests that SIAH2 suppresses bone marrow adipose tissue expansion, contrary to SIAH2 promoting adipogenesis in peripheral adipose tissue. Although consistent with ex vivo increased adipogenesis in the SKG SSPCs, the results related to possible SIAH2-mediated suppression of marrow adiposity in vivo are exploratory rather than definitive due to the limited number of SKG HF/ROSI samples available for measuring marrow adiposity. Although Siah2 deletion impairs adipogenesis in peripheral adipose tissue, expansion via adipocyte hypertrophy still occurs to meet the metabolic and lipid storage demands of an obesogenic diet, shown previously [24] and shown here (Fig. 4d). Our results suggest that SIAH2 promotes peripheral adipose tissue expansion but may inhibit excess bone marrow fat accumulation in response to PPARγ agonism, a possibility supported in vitro (Fig. 7) and by the exploratory in vivo data(Fig. 6).

Recent evidence shows that bone marrow adipocytes are functionally different from peripheral white or brown adipocytes [31, 32] with an interplay between bone marrow adipogenesis and osteogenesis regulated by PPARγ and PPARα [33]. Although SIAH2 modulates PPARγ activation in a 3T3-L1 model of adipogenesis [29] and regulates selected PPARγ target genes in peripheral adipose tissue that are involved in lipid metabolism [24], it is possible that SIAH2 is acting at the commitment stage of SSPCs, balancing the osteogenic/adipogenic decision in the bone marrow. A possible target of SIAH2’s action is the zinc finger protein 521 (ZFP521). ZFP521 antagonizes RUNX2 by repressing RUNX2 transcriptional activity [34, 35]. Although RUNX2 is required for osteoblast lineage commitment [36], it blocks later steps of osteoblast maturation [37], leading to bone loss in transgenic mice overexpression Runx2 [38]. Overexpression of ZFP521 reverses the severe osteopenia in those transgenic mice [34], indicating the balance between ZFP521 and RUNX2 acts at different stages of bone development to promote bone formation. SIAH2-mediated degradation of ZFP521 at the early commitment stage [16] is consistent with the increased osteogenesis we observe in vitro. A potential role for SIAH2 in modulating ZFP521 protein levels at the later stage of bone development raises the possibility that SIAH2 acts at two different stages of bone development to influence the interplay between bone and adipocyte formation in bone marrow.

While our paper presents novel findings for the effect of SIAH2 on the interplay between adipogenesis and osteogenesis, our study has important limitations. Our exploratory in vivo trabecular and marrow adiposity data, Figs. 5 and 6, are derived from a small sample of mice for each strain/diet group (n = 2–4), lowering our statistical power and limiting the interpretation of our findings. Specifically, the in vivo animal cohort evaluated in Figs. 3 and 4 (n = 7–8) had tibias excised from an unbiased subset of each group and some samples were lost during dissection (tibia broken, typically at epiphysis). All intact tibiae not originally sent for trabecular analysis were used for osmium staining and marrow adiposity analysis (Fig. 6, n = 2–6). Additionally, mice on the HF diets were single housed while LF mice remained multi-housed, limiting our interpretation of differences between low and high fat diet groups due to the influence of housing on metabolic and potentially, bone parameters. Further, our work does not address sex dependent effects of Siah2 deletion on osteogenesis and adipogenesis as we used male mice for the experimental work shown here. Considering the known sex dependent differences on marrow adipose expansion and bone health [39], our results may not translate to females. Another limitation to our findings is the lack of mechanistic explanation for results presented here. Our lab has previously reported adipose tissue expansion via adipocyte hypertrophy for Siah2−/− mice on a high-fat diet [24], but those data did not examine marrow adipocytes. We did not perform histological evaluation for in vivo adipocyte morphology, nor did we address histological outcomes for the quantifying in vivo bone formation or resorption. Thus, our proposed mechanistic interpretations of our in vivo outcomes are only supported by our in vitro data. Lastly, rosiglitazone-activated PPARγ may also increase bone resorption in vivo by directly stimulating osteoclastogenesis [40], a process we did not assess in vitro.

Models of obesity show that bone marrow adiposity increases with expanding peripheral adiposity [41], and that increased bone marrow adiposity is associated with reduced bone quality and increased fracture risk [41]. Our study provides a second report on the potential for SIAH2 to impact bone, with novel evidence for its potential role in osteoclast morphology, osteogenic differentiation, and the balance between marrow adipose tissue and bone morphology. Our data indicates the E3 ubiquitin ligase SIAH2 is a potential factor in the complex relationship between marrow fat expansion and bone integrity as determined by tissue, nutrients and pharmacological cues.
