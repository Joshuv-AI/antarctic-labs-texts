Riding the Storm: Microeukaryote Community Responses to Hurricane Disturbance
Authors: Andrea Aurora Rivera‐Zizumbo, Patricia Velez, Yunuen Tapia‐Torres, Luis E Servín‐Garcidueñas, Omar Lagunas‐Calvo, Jaime Gasca‐Pineda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSandy beaches are dynamic ecosystems where terrestrial and marine organisms coexist, resulting in diverse communities where microeukaryotes (protists, microalgae, and meiofauna) are key drivers. Understanding their diversity patterns and responses to stressors is essential for elucidating ecological processes, yet information on this subject remains limited. Herein, microeukaryotic diversity in a sandy beach in Acapulco, Guerrero, was analyzed via ITS1 metabarcoding assessing community structure changes following hurricane Otis. Overall, the number of ASVs decreased substantially from 260 before the hurricane to 93 after. Groups like Porifera, Platyhelminthes, Rotifera, Amoeboza, and Choanoflagellata disappeared after the hurricane. Ochrophyta, Cnidaria, Ctenophora, and metazoans in general decreased in richness and abundance, whereas Ciliophora proliferated, particularly toward the supralittoral zone. ASVs turnover varied among zones; middle and lower coastal zones lost over 50% ASVs, and the supralittoral zone almost doubled its richness. These changes in community structure were related to total phosphorus and total nitrogen concentrations, suggesting possible bottom‐up effects where nutrients available influence microbial resources for heterotrophic microeukaryotes. The observed patterns reflect high sensitivity of microeukaryotes to high‐impact natural disturbances and suggest low recovery potential within the first 6 months following the event. Whether recovery occurs over longer interannual scales remains to be evaluated.Keywords: community ecology, ITS1 region, metabarcoding, multi‐taxa inventorying

Introduction

Marine sandy beaches are dynamic ecosystems that dominate the coastal landscape in temperate and tropical zones. They are estimated to cover 75% of the world's coastlines (McLachlan 2001; Brown and McLachlan 2002), and in Mexico their total extension is 7984.66 km (Silva‐Cazarín et al. 2014). The dynamism of these ecosystems is defined by the interaction of three factors that constantly modify the physical environment: waves, tidal regime, and the nature of the sand (Defeo and McLachlan 2005; Defeo et al. 2009). Waves promote the transport of organic matter, organisms, and sand. Tides determine the moisture content and oxygenation of the sand surface. The main material that is transported and rearranged by wind and water movement is sand, which varies in size, shape, and accretion/erosion dynamics (Swart 1983; Defeo and McLachlan 2005; Defeo et al. 2009).

In these ecosystems, there is a convergence of materials and organisms that move from the adjacent coastal dunes and the neighboring ocean, resulting in diverse interstitial communities. Microscopic bacteria, archaea, protists, microalgae, fungi, and meiofauna inhabit the spaces between the sand grains, distributed along a gradient of physical and environmental conditions (Defeo et al. 2009; Okamoto et al. 2022). Within this gradient, three zones have been characterized where species turnover occurs: the supralittoral or subaerial zone (dunes and upper beach), the middle coastal zone (swash zone), and the lower zone (surf zone) (Dahl 1952; Brazeiro and Defeo 1996; McLachlan 2001; Defeo and McLachlan 2005; Gheskiere, Vincx, Urban‐Malinga, et al. 2005). The supralittoral zone experiences the most intense exposure to wind and solar radiation, making it prone to desiccation and high temperature fluctuations, while in the lower zone, there is a predominance of water saturation in constant motion by currents (Vohra 1971; Raffaelli et al. 1991; Rodil et al. 2006; Okamoto et al. 2022).

The interstitial communities of microeukaryotes (eukaryotes < 1 mm in size), comprise protists, fungi, chlorophytes and rhodophytes, meiofauna and eggs and juvenile stages of benthic or marine macrofauna (Fenchel 1978; Bik et al. 2012; Azovsky et al. 2013). Among microeukaryotes, fungi are the most studied group in coastal environments acting as important human pathogens and playing key ecological roles as saprobes (Simister et al. 2015; Brandao et al. 2021; Vélez et al. 2021; Walker and Robicheau 2021). However, microalgae, protists, and meiofauna are crucial to the ecological functioning of sandy beaches as they drive important ecological processes such as primary production, trophic webs dynamics, nutrient recycling, relocation of sediments and microorganisms, and the release dissolved matter through excretion (Pereira et al. 2018; Schratzberger and Ingels 2018; Castro et al. 2021; Giraldo et al. 2024).

Nowadays, sandy beaches face threats of anthropogenic origin (i.e., pollution, coastal engineering, urban development, mining and tourism industry) and natural disturbances such as sea‐level rise, storms, and hurricanes, that are becoming more frequent and intense due to climate change (Brown and McLachlan 2002; Defeo et al. 2009). The effect of anthropogenic activities and natural phenomena has been described for macrofauna (Harris et al. 2011; Machado et al. 2017; Afghan et al. 2020; Goad et al. 2025), but little is known about the response of microeukaryotes to this type of stressors.

Particularly, the intense winds, heavy rainfall, and storm surges typical of hurricanes result in beach erosion, changes in flooding regimes, formation of washover deposits, and relocation of sediments (Conner et al. 1989; Giraldo et al. 2024; Valderrama‐Landeros et al. 2025). These physical disturbances affect the habitat and the structure of interstitial communities (Giere 2009; Taghon et al. 2017; Giraldo et al. 2024). On October 25, 2023, hurricane Otis made landfall on the Pacific coast of Mexico, near the coastal city of Acapulco, becoming the strongest hurricane ever recorded in the country, reaching category 5 on the Saffir‐Simpson scale (García‐Franco et al. 2024). However, to date there is no information on the ecological impacts of this catastrophic event on beach biodiversity, particularly on microeukaryote communities.

Information on the diversity and community assemblage of sandy beach microeukaryotes is scarce, as most studies focus on the direct extraction and observation of benthic marine fauna (Armonies and Reise 2000; Gheskiere, Vincx, Urban‐Malinga, et al. 2005; Gingold et al. 2010; Harris et al. 2011; Oliveira‐Santos et al. 2017). This methodological approach incorporates a number of inherent biases, including: (1) overlooking a number of microeukaryotic groups due to their tiny size; (2) limitations on morphology‐based identification of rare, cryptic, and new‐to‐science species that are often reported in these types of ecosystems (Armonies and Reise 2000; Gheskiere, Vincx, Urban‐Malinga, et al. 2005; Oliveira‐Santos et al. 2017); and (3) the difficulty in extracting and preserving soft‐bodied organisms. The development of genetic techniques like metabarcoding provides a means to address this methodological bias, expanding our understanding of overall ecosystem biodiversity from an environmental DNA sample. Metabarcoding has been widely applied in biodiversity studies to estimate species richness and monitoring of temporal variations of community structures, showing great effectiveness (Leasi et al. 2018; Martínez et al. 2020; Castro et al. 2021).

In this study, we evaluated the diversity of overlooked microeukaryotes (protists, microalgae, and meiofauna) on a marine sandy beach in Acapulco, Guerrero, determining: (1) changes in community structure across intertidal zones of the beach, and (2) the effect of hurricane Otis on the microeukaryote community. For this we implemented a metabarcoding technique analyzing the ITS1 region of ribosomal DNA. Our results on ASVs turnover shed new light on the high sensitivity of microeukaryotes to high‐impact natural disturbances within this complex ecosystem.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Sampling

Samples were collected in May 2023 and April 2024 at the Revolcadero sandy beach, in the Punta Diamante region of Acapulco de Juárez, Guerrero, Mexico, a dissipative beach characterized by fine‐textured and gray silty sand (SCT 2014; Figure 1). Fourteen sand samples were collected each year at a distance of 5 m, along a transect from the supralittoral zone to the low tide zone. Sand cores were collected on each site at a depth of 20 cm using a 10 cm diameter corer. Samples were placed in hermetically sealed plastic bags and kept in the dark at a temperature of 4°C for analysis in the laboratory.

Revolcadero sandy beach in the state of Guerrero, Mexico.

Environmental Variables

Sediment samples were dried at 70°C and homogenized for nutrient quantification. Total nitrogen (TN) was determined following the Kjeldahl method (Bremmer 1996) for acid digestion with sulfuric acid, potassium sulfate, and selenium. Total phosphorus (TP) was obtained by digestion with a mixture of sulfuric acid and nitric acid. Finally, organic carbon (OC) was quantified by oxidation with potassium dichromate and sulfuric acid.

Environmental DNA Isolation and Sequencing

Direct DNA extraction of 0.5 g from each sample was obtained using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) standardizing the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA concentration and purity were quantified using a NanoDrop microvolume spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) and sequenced with the Illumina MiSeq (2 × 300) to generate 50,000 reads per sample in paired‐end format at the Genomic Services Laboratory (LANGEBIO, Irapuato, Guanajuato, Mexico). The ITS1 region (rDNA) was amplified using the primer set ITS1F (5′‐CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA‐3′) (Gardes and Bruns 1993) and ITS2 (5′‐GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC‐3′) (White et al. 1990). Raw sequence data is available at NCBI GenBank: PRJNA1424402.

Bioinformatic Analysis

The ITS1 raw paired‐end sequences were processed with the DADA2 package (Callahan et al. 2016) in R software v4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025) for primers removal, sequence quality filtering (MaxEE = 2, MaxNs = 0 and MinLen = 50), ASVs (amplicon sequence variants) inference, assembly of forward and reverse reads (MinOverlap = 12, MaxMismatch = 0), exclusion of chimeras (Remove Bimera Denovo) and the taxonomic assignment of sequence variants by the RDP Naive Bayesian Classifier algorithm using UNITE v10.0 as reference database for the identification of eukaryotes (Abarenkov et al. 2024).

Diversity Analysis

In order to estimate microeukaryotes local diversity (α), richness and abundance of ASVs were estimated per sampling site and per year. With this data, Shannon and Inverse Simpson Index were calculated. A Wilcoxon signed‐rank exact test was performed to assess statistical significance in alpha diversity changes after the hurricane, and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Holm's method to control the family‐wise error rate (FWER). For regional diversity (β), Jaccard index and Bray–Curtis's dissimilarity were computed between sites and years of sampling. Lastly, a Canonical Correspondence Analysis was performed to elucidate the relationship between microeukaryotes community and nutrients in sand, like Organic Carbon (OC), Total Phosphorus (TP), and Total Nitrogen (TN). All analyses were carried out using the R software v4.5.1 environment through vegan, BiodiversityR, and CCA packages (Kindt and Coe 2005; González and Déjean 2023; Oksanen et al. 2025; R Core Team 2025).

Morphological Analysis

In addition to the metabarcoding approach, the direct observation and identification to higher taxa (phylum, subphylum, or class) of interstitial fauna from sand samples was conducted to further explore the taxonomic identification of organisms. Three sand samples were selected representing the supralittoral zone (site 1), the middle coastal zone (site 7), and the lower zone (site 12) for each year. From these samples, two spoonfuls of sand (approximately 30 g) were moistened with 70% alcohol and observed under a stereoscopic microscope. The identification of organisms or their fragments was based on a morphological characterization.

A general increase in nutrient levels was observed after the hurricane. For 2024, TN was higher in all sites, with a peak in site 3 where its concentration quadrupled. The TP raised more than 250% in the supralittoral zone (sites 1–4) but decreased through the middle and lower coastal zones (sites 8–14), changing the tendency from 2023, where TP increased from supralittoral to lower zone. Raw Sequencing Results

For the samples collected in 2023, we registered between 29,900 and 110,800 raw reads per sampling site, whereas for 2024 we registered between 29,500 and 146,900 raw reads. After the bioinformatic processing of sequences, 1601 ASVs were assigned taxonomically for 2023 samples and 1287 ASVs for 2024. From this dataset, ASVs corresponding to non‐assigned Viridiplantae, Anthophyta, Bryophyta, Chordata, and Fungal species were excluded as these groups fell outside the scope of this work.

Alpha Diversity

Before hurricane Otis, the richness in Revolcadero sandy beach was 260 ASVs (159 corresponding to animals, 99 protists, and 2 chlorophytes) which declined to 93 ASVs (49 animals, 42 protists, and 2 chlorophytes) in 2024, after the hurricane (Wilcoxon signed‐rank exact test, adjusted p = 0.262).

In 2023, a pattern of low richness was observed in the supralittoral zone (sites 1–6), which increased toward the middle and lower zone of the beach (sites 7–13) with the presence of organisms from marine zooplankton (e.g., larvae of animals such as cnidarians, ctenophores, and porifers) (Figure 2a). Sites 3 and 4 did not present any microeukaryotic ASV; in the case of site 3, the only ASV registered corresponded to Alamania punicea, an epiphytic orchid, and for site 4, it was reported that the presence of ASVs from non‐assigned Viridiplantae, division Anthophyta, order Malpighiales, and Alamania punicea was noted.

Richness of ASVs per sampling site in 2023 (a) and 2024 (b).

Animal groups such as flatworms, porifers, and rotifers, as well as amoeboid protists and choanoflagellates, were no longer detected after the hurricane (Figure 2b). By 2024 the highest richness was concentrated in sites 7–11; meanwhile, sites 2 and 4 presented the lowest richness. It is noteworthy that site 13 experienced a 90% reduction in its richness in contrast with site 3, which went from 0 to 16 ASVs.

A similar gradient of increasing abundance from the supralittoral to the lower zone was recorded in 2023 (Figure 3a). The impact of the hurricane resulted in the decrease of abundance within the middle and lower zones, whereas the supralittoral experienced an increase, particularly in site 3 (Wilcoxon signed‐rank exact test, adjusted p = 0.171). In 2024, ctenophore and ochrophyte abundances declined, whereas ciliate protists became more abundant (Figure 3b). At site 13, with the reduction in richness, there was also a proliferation of ASVs of unidentified animals (Metazoa NA).

Abundance of ASVs per sampling site in 2023 (a) and 2024 (b).

According to the Shannon index, there was an increase in microeukaryote diversity toward the supralittoral (sites 1–6, except site 2) and a decrease in the middle and lower zones after hurricane Otis (Figure 4a,b) (Wilcoxon signed‐rank exact test, adjusted p = 0.216). On the other hand, the inverse Simpson index indicated that communities along the beach are diverse, except for sites 2 and 13, which showed high levels of dominance after the hurricane (Wilcoxon signed‐rank exact test, adjusted p = 0.012*).

Alpha diversity: Shannon and Simpson's inverse indexes for each site in 2023 (a) and 2024 (b).

Beta Diversity

The middle coastal zone accounted for 47% of the overall ASVs of the region, being the highest ASVs reservoir, followed by the lower zone before the hurricane. After the hurricane, these reservoirs decreased to 22% and 9%, respectively. The supralittoral zone, which before Otis accounted only for 6% of regional ASVs, in 2024 increased the representation to 11% (Figure 5).

Proportion of regional ASVs composition per zone and sampling year.

The Jaccard index reflected that there was great heterogeneity in the composition of microeukaryotes on the sandy beach. In 2023, the most similar sites were 12 and 13, sharing 35% of the ASV composition, and in 2024, sites 9 and 10 shared 40% (Figure 6a). Despite sharing < 40% of ASVs, there was a clustering pattern among the sites in the middle and lower zones in both 2023 (sites 8–14) (similarity = 0.196) and 2024 (sites 7–12) (similarity = 0.183), which in turn clustered together. After the hurricane, the sites at the supralittoral (sites 1, 2, 3, and 6) were grouped with lower sites 13 and 14, sharing approximately 20% of ASVs (similitude = 0.132), whereas before the hurricane, the composition of the supralittoral zone was completely different from the other zones. On the other hand, the Bray–Curtis index showed the grouping of sites with the highest abundances, in 2023 sites 10–14, and in 2024 sites 7–11. Due to the high variability in abundances at the other sites, no similarity patterns were detected (Figure 6b).

Beta diversity: Jaccard distances based on ASV composition (a) and Bray–Curtis distances based on ASV abundances (b) per site and sampling year.

The heterogeneity among the different zones of the sandy beach was the result of the presence of a large number of rare ASVs. The relative abundances of overall ASVs showed a clear pattern of community structure. Less than 10% of ASVs were highly abundant and shared across multiple sites. These included protists of the genera Euplotes and Haslea, unidentified animals (Metazoa NA), and cnidarians such as Muggiaea atlantica . In contrast, the vast majority of ASVs had low relative abundances (below 20%) and were restricted to one site (Figure S1).

The comparative analysis before and after the hurricane per zone showed that in the supralittoral zone 3 ASVs remained in the area even after the hurricane (2 Metazoa NA and protist Euplotes rariseta). ASVs turnover in the zone was characterized by the presence of Muggiaea atlantica , Ctenophora, ciliates (Urostylida, Uronema, Holosticha, and Euplotes balteatus), Cercozoan protists, diatoms (Haslea), and some Metazoa NA. For the middle coastal zone, hydrozoans ( Eudoxoides spiralis , E. mitra , Muggiaea atlantica ), Ctenophora, Cercozoa, diatoms (Skeletonema), and Metazoans NA were shared before and after the hurricane. New ASVs reported for this zone included ciliates (Euplotes balteatus, Anteholosticha, Oxytrichidae, Holosticha, Urostylida), Cercozoa, diatoms (Haslea, Stephanodiscus, Chaetoceros tenuissimus ), protist algae Ochrophyta, chlorophytes (Micromonas commoda), hydrozoans (Eudoxoides), and cnidarians (Actiniidae), and Metazoans NA.

Finally, in the lower zone the ASVs shared between years included ctenophores (Bolinopsis) and cnidarians ( Muggiaea atlantica ) while the gains were mainly represented by diatoms (Stephanodiscus, Haslea), ciliates (Urostylida), and Metazoans NA.

The supralittoral was the only zone where the gains outweighed the losses of ASVs after the hurricane in contrast to the middle and lower zones where there was a dramatic loss of ASVs (Figure 7).

Influence of Environmental Variables on Diversity Patterns

According to the Canonical Correspondence Analysis, prior to hurricane Otis diversity was positively associated with concentration of organic carbon and total phosphorus and negatively with concentrations of total nitrogen (27.16% variation explained by the model). In this model, phosphorus and nitrogen were significant variables (TP p = 0.044, TN p = 0.043) particularly for animals (TP p = 0.041, TN p = 0.005) (Figure 8a). After hurricane Otis, the most diverse sites had a negative relation with all nutrients (25.97% variation explained by the model), with phosphorus being the only significant variable (TP p = 0.033), particularly for protists (TP p = 0.011) (Figure 8b).

Canonical correspondence analysis between microeukaryote diversity and nutrients in sand (OC, organic carbon; TP, total phosphorus; TN, total nitrogen) before (a) and after (b) hurricane Otis.

Morphological Identification of Sand Microeukaryotes

A higher representation of animal groups was observed before hurricane Otis. Fragments of organisms from Cnidaria, Echinodermata, Mollusca, and Foraminifera were identified in the supralittoral zone before the hurricane, whereas in the following year only Cnidaria was recovered. The middle coastal zone hosted the largest number of morphotypes with the presence of Cnidaria, Echinodermata, Mollusca, Arthropoda, Crustacea, Ostracoda, and Foraminifera. Finally, in the lower zone, Echinodermata, Mollusca, Nematoda, Arthropoda, Ostracoda, and Foraminifera registered presence in 2023, but Nematoda and Arthropoda were not found by 2024 (Figure 9).

Discussion

Beach Zonation

Interstitial organisms are in constant movement by the action of wind, tides and swash; this dynamism may cause some scattering, resorting their vertical and horizontal position (McLachlan 1980; Schlacher and Thompson 2013). However, our results showed that despite the great heterogeneity among sampling sites, there was a clear pattern of spatial structure in which around 65%–75% of microeukaryotic ASVs are exclusive to each beach zone and, even after the hurricane, the community zoning was maintained although with less accuracy (36%–60% exclusive ASVs per zone). The supralittoral, middle coastal and lower zones showed differences in the occurrence and density of microeukaryotes. Diversity decreased from the lower zone to the middle zone, which were compositionally more similar than the supralittoral zone. According to McLachlan and Jaramillo (1995) the boundaries between zones are sharpest at the upper shore and diminish toward the surf zone as microeukaryotes are swept from one zone to another by tides, causing an overlap in the composition resulting in blurred boundaries (McLachlan 1980; Schlacher and Thompson 2013).

In lower and middle zones, it was reported the presence of anemones, siphonophores, ctenophores, hydrozoans, porifers, protists, algae, and diatoms, organisms of marine origin (i.e., animal larvae, zoo and phytoplankton) that are transported shoreward by onshore winds, internal tides and upwelling fronts (Shanks et al. 2014; Morgan et al. 2017). This constant influx of marine organisms is reflected in the high diversity values recorded for these areas before the hurricane.

The low diversity recorded in the supralittoral zone can be attributed to two main factors. First, harsh environmental conditions typical of this area, such as high temperatures, desiccation, direct UV and air exposure, which act as an abiotic filter, maintaining only those organisms capable of tolerating these stress factors (Vohra 1971; Giere 2009; Tikhonenkova et al. 2015; Pereira et al. 2018; Okamoto et al. 2022). Second, in 2023 this zone showed lower OC and TP content. These nutrients had been related to microbial activity and density of bacteria which represent an important resource for heterotrophic protists and meiofauna (Koop and Griffiths 1982; Malm et al. 2004; Mouawad et al. 2012). Since Revolcadero is a touristic beach, routine wrack cleaning results in the removal of organic matter affecting ecological processes like microbial decomposition and nutrients availability, further impacting interactions and interstitial food webs (Malm et al. 2004; Gheskiere, Vincx, Weslawski, et al. 2005; Harguinteguy et al. 2012; Morton et al. 2015). Therefore, beach cleaning may limit resources for bacteria‐feeding protists and meiofauna, thus reducing microeukaryote diversity.

Effects of Hurricane Otis

Hurricane disturbance led to changes in community structure of microeukaryotes along the beach: a reduction in richness and abundance in the lower and middle beach zones, while in the supralittoral zone these two parameters increased. Also, it was reported a significant change in Simpson's inverse, suggesting that the hurricane primarily affected the evenness of the communities. Negative effects have been reported for meiofauna after hurricane Matthew (October 2016) in Santa Marta, Colombia, where beside the reduction in richness and density (from 16,762 to 3629 organisms), Giraldo et al. (2024) reported changes in composition. For example, decreases in relative abundance of copepods while nematodes increased, showing their tolerance and adaptability to disturbance. Also, for macrofauna, Goad et al. (2025) reported a density decline in ghost crab populations after hurricane Lee and their movement to the upper beach zones to seek refuge immediately post‐hurricane.

Even though there is evidence for diversity decreasing immediately after hurricanes, the response of organisms depends on many factors like their tolerance and adaptations to disturbances, changes in beach morphodynamics, nutrients availability and human activities (Dreyer et al. 2005; Harris et al. 2011; Witmer and Roelke 2014). For example, we reported an increase in richness and density of ciliates protists after the hurricane as ciliates inhabiting high‐energy beaches develop penicular or fibrillar skeletal structures extending their resistance to exposure and disturbance (Wright 1984; Azovsky et al. 2013).

Conner et al. (1989) noted that following hurricanes, there is an input of sediment, nutrients, and organic matter, as well as the dispersal of organisms. Herein, the nutrients input was confirmed as there was an increase in OC, TP, and TN concentrations after Otis, especially of TN and TP in the supralittoral zone. Regarding organism dispersal, siphonophore, ctenophore, and chlorophyte ASVs were passively transported from the lower zone to the upper beach.

Meiofauna and heterotrophic protists like ciliates feed on microalgae, detritus, and bacteria, affecting directly (i.e., breakdown of organic matter, excreting nutrients, sediments reworking) and indirectly (i.e., controlling bacterial and microalgal populations through grazing) primary production and nutrient recycling on the beach. In turn, they represent important feeding resources to predators like marine invertebrates (Giere 2009; Harguinteguy et al. 2012; Tikhonenkova et al. 2015; Schratzberger and Ingels 2018). Changes in microeukaryote communities affect these essential ecological processes impacting the ecosystem services provided by sandy marine beaches.

Even though previous studies (i.e., Hennig et al. 1983; Conner et al. 1989; Santiago‐Vera 2021; Giraldo et al. 2024) have reported the recovery of meiofaunal communities after hurricanes due to nutrient enrichment, our findings indicate otherwise. After 6 months, the community had not returned to pre‐hurricane structure, suggesting that ecological processes have not been restored either. Further studies on ecological traits of these organisms should be conducted to evaluate the resilience of the community in a climate change scenario where these types of natural disturbances are becoming increasingly frequent and intense. Additionally, it is recommended to implement continuous monitoring over broader temporal scales to track the recovery of the intertidal community.

Morphological results also showed a reduction in the richness and representation of taxonomic groups, complementing the eDNA‐derived results. Samples were maintained at −4°C, with no addition of any solution to preserve biological tissues (i.e., formalin) as the main objective of this research was the extraction of eDNA. As a result, soft‐bodied organisms were lost, so the characterization was based only on hard‐bodied forms like shells, exoskeletons, or calcareous structures. The observed differences in community composition between approaches reflect that no method has a complete coverage of diversity, suggesting the necessity of integrative studies to maximize the scope of community studies.

Metabarcoding: Advantages, Limitations, and Prospects

The ITS1 metabarcoding proved to be a useful tool to monitor temporal variations in intertidal community structure after disturbance. Through metabarcoding, communities of protists, meiofauna, and microscopic algae were characterized simultaneously, in contrast to morphological approaches that focus on a specific group due to the different extraction techniques, preservation methods, and taxonomic expertise required for each group (Tikhonenkova et al. 2015; Leasi et al. 2018; Martínez et al. 2020). It also enabled the representation of rare taxa and juvenile stages of marine fauna, which are often excluded from morphological studies as they are difficult to preserve or lack diagnostic characteristics (Leasi et al. 2018; Castro et al. 2021).

However, the main limitation of metabarcoding in this study was the presence of unassigned sequences (i.e., Metazoa NA). This bias may be explained as this technique largely depends on genetic repositories used for taxonomic assignment and the choice of genetic locus as barcode (Bik et al. 2012; Leasi et al. 2018; Castro et al. 2021). For example, through morphological identification it was reported the presence of important meiofauna and protist groups like Nematoda, Ostracoda and Foraminifera, which were not found in the eDNA. This might be related to the poor representation of these groups in the reference database used in this study. In the case of nematodes, common interstitial organisms belong to the class Enoplea and subclass Chromadoria (Gheskiere, Vincx, Urban‐Malinga, et al. 2005; Hua et al. 2016), which were represented by only 25 sequences in UNITE (UNITE website unite.ut.ee/ consulted in January 2026); for ostracodes there were 27 sequences and no foraminifera sequences were found. This scarcity of information is related to the molecular markers commonly used to identify this species, in foraminiferans the universal barcode is 18S rDNA (Pawlowski et al. 2016), for ostracods 18S and COI (Nigro et al. 2016; Echeverría‐Galindo et al. 2021), and for nematodes 18S, COI, and ITS (Nega 2014; Avó et al. 2017). UNITE reports more than 6000 nematode ITS sequences, but only 25 for intertidal species, showing that even though ITS region is widely applied for the diagnosis of these organisms, sandy beaches have been poorly explored.

The underrepresentation of intertidal microeukaryotes in public repositories highlights the need for studies that integrate morphological analysis with molecular data in order to enrich public databases with curated and taxonomically assigned sequences to improve the scope of metabarcoding. Additionally, the incorporation of more than one genetic marker for metabarcoding analysis may enhance taxonomic coverage of environmental sequences for more precise evaluations of community structure and biodiversity.
