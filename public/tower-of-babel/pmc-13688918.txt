Multiomics, multi-species profiling, and animal validation identify mitoxyperilysis as a conserved therapeutic target in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis
Authors: Hanjie Liu, Cong Qi, Ruixia Lu, Chuan An, Yangzhiyuan Wang, Shumiao Li, Rui Zhu, Xingzhong Feng, Huijuan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & aimsMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is driven by complex intercellular communication and regulated cell death pathways. Mitoxyperilysis, a recently characterized form of regulated lytic cell death, remains poorly defined in MASH. Here, we integrated multiomics, cross-species transcriptomics, machine learning, and in vivo models to dissect mitoxyperilysis activation and its therapeutic relevance in MASH.MethodsSingle‑cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets from mouse and human MASH livers were analyzed. Cell–cell communication was inferred using CellChat, and metabolite‑mediated cell–cell communication (mCCC) was assessed with MEBOCOST. Mitoxyperilysis activity was scored via AUCell and AddModuleScore. Eleven machine learning classifiers identified transcriptomic signatures distinguishing MASH from controls, and key regulators were subjected to in silico knockout (scTenifoldKnk). A choline‑deficient, L‑amino acid‑defined high‑fat diet (CDAHFD) mouse model was used for in vivo validation.ResultsSingle‑cell profiling revealed that mitoxyperilysis activity was highly enriched in myeloid cells, particularly monocyte‑derived macrophages (Mo‑Macrophages) and neutrophils, and was strongly induced by high‑fat high‑sucrose diet. CellChat and mCCC analyses demonstrated that Mo‑Macrophages served as central hubs of rewired inflammatory and metabolic crosstalk in MASH. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed the expansion of pro‑inflammatory hepatocyte subsets and conserved upregulation of mitoxyperilysis components (e.g. BAX, TLR4, NINJ1) in both mouse and human MASH. Cross‑species profiling across mouse, rat, hamster, non‑human primate, and human datasets demonstrated evolutionarily conserved activation of the entire mitoxyperilysis pathway. Machine learning identified Bax as the sole overlapping driver gene linking diet‑induced transcriptomic signatures to mitoxyperilysis. In silico Bax ablation perturbed lipid metabolism and immune pathways. In CDAHFD-induced MASH mice with typical pathological phenotypes, core mitoxyperilysis genes were transcriptionally upregulated, and Bax/Bid/Bak1 upregulation was validated at the protein level. While RhoA mRNA increased, total RhoA protein remained stable, with its ubiquitination elevated and activity suppressed.ConclusionsMitoxyperilysis is an evolutionarily conserved, myeloid‑enriched pathway activated in MASH. Bax acts as a critical regulatory node, while RhoA is modulated via ubiquitination and functional suppression. Targeting this axis may provide a novel therapeutic strategy for MASH.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00902-4.Key words: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), Mitoxyperilysis, Single-cell transcriptomics, Spatial transcriptomics, Cross-species analysis

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH, formerly non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, NASH) is the progressive inflammatory subtype of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a global epidemic of chronic liver disease defined by hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocellular ballooning injury that can advance to significant fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 2]. Pooled estimates from the last two years place MASLD prevalence at about 38% in the general adult population of well-studied countries, and at around 65% in those who also have type 2 diabetes [3]. While a small number of pharmacotherapies have recently received FDA approval for MASH with fibrosis, their limited efficacy in advanced disease and narrow patient eligibility underscore the critical need for novel therapeutic targets rooted in a more complete understanding of MASH pathogenesis [4]. Bridging this mechanistic gap is therefore an urgent clinical priority.

Central to the pathogenesis and progressive fibrosis of MASH is the self-amplifying interplay between metabolic stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and maladaptive innate immune activation [5]. Sustained nutrient overload in hepatocytes impairs mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and drives excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, rendering these organelles highly vulnerable to structural and functional damage [6, 7]. This mitochondrial distress is not merely a passive consequence of steatosis; it actively engages innate immune pathways, most notably the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the release of the potent pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 [8, 9]. Concurrently, mounting evidence indicates that a distinct form of regulated necrosis, one that directly links mitochondrial damage to plasma membrane rupture, plays a non-redundant, decisive role in driving the self-perpetuating necroinflammatory loop that is the hallmark of progressive MASH [10]. However, while individual components of this cascade have been extensively studied in isolation, their integration into a unified, spatially organized signaling framework has remained elusive [11]. A recently conceptualized pathway, termed “mitoxyperilysis” elegantly unifies these phenomena by proposing a two-stage spatially regulated mechanism. First, innate immune activation and metabolic disruption induce mitochondrial dysfunction and mTORC2-dependent relocation of damaged mitochondria to the plasma membrane. This spatial licensing enables localized delivery of ROS, triggering membrane-proximal lipid peroxidation (mitoxyperiosis) that ultimately executes plasma membrane rupture (lysis). The subsequent release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) further amplifies innate immune activation and perpetuates tissue inflammation [12, 13]. Despite its conceptual appeal, the precise cellular reservoirs, regulatory logic, and evolutionary conservation of this pathway within the complex hepatic microenvironment of MASH remain largely unexplored, representing a critical barrier to therapeutic translation.

Given the extraordinary cellular heterogeneity of the liver and the spatially restricted nature of MASH lesions, comprehensive dissection of this pathway requires a systems-level approach that simultaneously preserves single-cell resolution and spatial tissue architecture. To address these critical knowledge gaps, we performed an integrated multi-modal, cross-species analysis of mitoxyperilysis in MASH. We first constructed a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of murine MASH livers to map cell-type-specific pathway activation. We then validated these findings using spatial transcriptomics in both mouse and human liver samples, linking pathway activity directly to histopathological lesions. Cross-species comparisons assessed the evolutionary conservation of mitoxyperilysis signatures, while a machine learning pipeline identified its central regulatory hub, which was functionally validated in an independent MASH mouse model. Our findings establish mitoxyperilysis as a conserved pathogenic mechanism in MASH and provide a high-confidence therapeutic target for this prevalent liver disease.

Materials and methods

ScRNA-seq data acquisition and preprocessing

Herein, we investigated single-cell transcriptomic changes in mice maintained on a control diet (CD) versus an HFHSD. The datasets used were retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Specifically, the datasets included 6 samples from mice fed a CD and 6 samples from HFHSD-induced MASH (GEO accession numbers: GSE199638, GSE269059, and GSE208750) (Table S1; Fig. 1A) [14–16]. The datasets underwent quality control (QC) procedures to guarantee the dependability and consistency of the transcriptomic datasets. Following LogNormalize normalization and Harmony integration, we performed dimensionality reduction and clustering, and identified cell clusters based on marker genes.

Cell type annotation

Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes for mouse liver cells, with identities further validated via dot plot visualization. We referenced established marker gene sets for major hepatic and immune populations: NK/T cells (Ccl5, Klrc2, Klre1), Kupffer cells (C1qb, C1qc, Csf1r), Mo-Macrophages (C1qb, Flt3, Cd163, Ifi211), B cells (Cd79a, Ebf1, Pax5), hepatocyte subsets (Mup7/13/15 and Sult2a8/Sds/Rida), endothelial cells (Tie1, Robo4, Gpr182), neutrophils (S100a9, S100a8, Cxcr2), and cholangiocytes (Krt19, Fxyd3).

Trajectory inference

The trajectory inference of cells was performed using CytoTRACE2 and Slingshot [17, 18]. CytoTRACE2 was employed to calculate differentiation potential scores across subpopulations and to identify potential progenitor populations. Subsequently, Slingshot was utilized to infer developmental trajectories, employing default settings.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Using CellChat, we systematically evaluated cell-cell communication networks, enabling the detection and visualization of both statistically and biologically meaningful interactions [19].

Mitoxyperilysis activity profiling in single-cell transcriptomes

The mitoxyperilysis-related gene signatures were curated exclusively based on the landmark study that formally defined this novel programmed cell death pathway [13]. Genes were partitioned into five functionally distinct modules according to their mechanistic roles in the mitoxyperilysis cascade, with all inclusions validated in the original study (Table S2). Cell-level pathway activity was quantified using AUCell with default parameters and visualized on UMAP embeddings. Normalized module scores for signature components were computed via Seurat’s AddModuleScore function across cell populations and experimental groups. Mo-Macrophages were isolated, re-clustered, visualized by t-SNE, and functionally annotated using Gene Ontology enrichment analysis.

MEBOCOST analysis

MEBOCOST is a newly developed computational tool implemented in Python, designed to infer metabolite-mediated cell-cell communication (mCCC) based on scRNA-seq/snRNA-seq. This approach utilizes a manually curated database of metabolite sensors and their interacting partners to identify sender and receiver cells, taking into account metabolite efflux and influx rates as well as the expression levels of relevant enzymes and sensors. The significance of each communication event is assessed, and events are filtered according to the predefined criteria established in the database [20].

Transcriptomic profiling and functional enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis between the HFHSD and CD groups was conducted in R. Volcano plots were generated using R packages. Functional enrichment analyses, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) annotation (biological process, BP; cellular component, CC; molecular function, MF), were performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was implemented in R to identify coordinately regulated pathways and biological processes from the ranked whole-transcriptome data.

Spatial transcriptomic data collection and preprocessing

Mouse liver spatial transcriptomic data were obtained from the GEO under accession number GSE248077, including CD and MASH model groups. Human liver ST data were retrieved from the Human MASLD Spatial Multi-omics Atlas (HMSMA, https://db.genomics.cn/stomics/hmsma/) and the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) under accession number HRA007511, covering healthy control and MASH patient samples [2]. All ST data were quality‑controlled, normalized, and preprocessed in R using standard pipelines for spatial transcriptomic analysis.

Cell annotation and functional enrichment analysis in spatial transcriptomes

Major liver cell populations were annotated based on canonical cell‑type marker genes. Differential gene expression analysis between MASH and control groups was performed in R, with genes defined as significantly dysregulated at |log₂(fold change)| >1 and adjusted p < 0.05. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R to identify functionally perturbed biological processes and signaling pathways.

Bioinformatic mining of cross-species MASH transcriptomic profiles

Public transcriptomic datasets associated with MASH across multiple species were retrieved from the GEO database, encompassing five phylogenetically diverse species spanning murine (two independent Mus musculus models: GSE120977 and GSE200409) [21, 22], rat (Rattus norvegicus, GSE134715) [23], golden hamster (Mesocricetus auratus, GSE227411) [24], non-human primate (Papio hamadryas, GSE139981) [25], and human (Homo sapiens, MASH patient cohort, GSE89063) [26]. All raw expression profiles were standardized and preprocessed uniformly in R. Differential gene expression analysis was performed to identify transcriptomic alterations between disease and control groups. Volcano plots were constructed to visualize the overall dysregulated patterns of gene transcription, while box plots were applied to quantitatively compare the expression levels of core mitoxyperilysis-related genes across different species. The conserved transcriptional alteration patterns of mitoxyperilysis signaling components were further systematically compared among preclinical animal models and human samples to elucidate the evolutionary conservation of pathway dysregulation in MASH pathogenesis.

ML analysis

Standardized expression matrices were extracted from the scRNA-seq dataset (GEO accession numbers: GSE199638, GSE269059, and GSE208750) and transposed to format cells (samples) as rows and genes (features) as columns. Sample group labels were consolidated into two conditions: CD and HFHSD. To reduce dimensionality and focus on the most informative features, the top 500 highly variable genes were retained based on gene expression variance. To mitigate class imbalance, stratified sampling was applied, capping the number of cells at 300 per group. The final dataset was split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. Eleven ML classifiers were then trained on the training set, including Logistic Regression, SGD Classifier, Decision Tree, Random Forest, AdaBoost, Extra Trees, Support Vector Machine, Gaussian Naive Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbors, Multi-layer Perceptron, and Ridge Classifier. Model stability was assessed using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set, with mean cross-validation (CV) scores and score distributions reported. Final model performance was comprehensively evaluated on the independent test set using multiple metrics, including macro-averaged ROC curves with corresponding AUC values, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Confusion matrices were generated to visualize class-specific classification patterns for each algorithm. ML-derived signature genes were explicitly defined as the union of the top 50 most important features ranked by feature importance scores from the classifiers, after removing duplicate genes.

ML-based candidate gene identification and in silico perturbation analysis

First, 11 ML algorithms, including Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Multi-layer Perceptron, AdaBoost, Random Forest, SGD Classifier, Extra Trees, Ridge Classifier, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors, were benchmarked for their ability to classify liver transcriptomes from CD and HFHSD groups. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, cross-validation mean accuracy, and AUC-ROC.

To identify key regulators bridging ML-derived transcriptomic signatures and mitoxyperilysis, an intersection analysis was performed between ML-derived signature genes and a curated set of mitoxyperilysis-related genes. The overlapping gene Bax was selected as the primary candidate for functional validation. In silico Bax knockout (KO) was performed using the scTenifoldKnk framework to predict transcriptomic perturbations following gene ablation [27]. Perturbation magnitude and statistical significance of gene expression changes post-KO were quantified. Functional enrichment analyses of perturbed genes were conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R, including GO and KEGG pathway enrichment.

Animal experiments

The animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tsinghua University. Male C57BL/6 mice (B6J, ~21 g, 6 weeks of age) were raised in an SPF facility under stable conditions. Following 1 week of environmental acclimatization, mice were randomly divided into a normal control group (B6J+CD; n = 5) and a CDAHFD-induced MASH group (B6J+CDAHFD; n = 5). Body weight was monitored and recorded weekly throughout the feeding period. After 6 weeks of feeding, all mice were euthanized, and liver and other target tissues were harvested for further analysis.

Biochemical and histopathological analysis

Liver segments were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation, followed by routine paraffin embedding. Serial 4 μm sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) for histopathological assessment. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) were assessed across all the mouse groups.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) analysis

RNA was extracted from liver tissue samples using RNA extraction solution (Wuhan Saiwei’er Biotech, G3013) after homogenization with a 3D frozen grinder, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for qPCR (with gDNA Remover; Wuhan Saiwei’er Biotech, Cat. No. G3337). RT-qPCR was conducted and the relative expression of target genes was normalized using the 2−ΔΔCt approach, with Gapdh as the internal reference gene. Detailed primer sequences are provided in Table S3.

Western blotting (WB)

The total protein concentration in the collected samples was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo, USA). Proteins were separated and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Prior to electrophoresis, equal amounts of total protein (50 μg per lane) were mixed with 5×SDS loading buffer and denatured by heating at 95 °C for 10 minutes. The denatured proteins were then separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), which was performed with a 5% stacking gel and a 10% separating gel at a constant voltage of 80 V for the stacking gel and 120 V for the separating gel. After electrophoresis, the separated proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes via the wet transfer method at 100 V. Afterwards, the PVDF membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-BAX (Cat: 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), anti-BAK1 (Cat: 29552-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-BID (Cat: 10988-1-AP, Proteintech), as well as anti-RhoA antibody (Cat: 10749-1-AP, Proteintech).

GST-RBD pull-down assay for active RhoA-GTP detection

Transform pGEX-6p-1-GST-RBD into BL21, induce with 0.5 mM IPTG at 30 °C for 2 h, lyse by sonication in TBS, and immobilize the fusion protein on glutathione agarose. Lyse liver tissues in Buffer B (must contain 10 mM MgCl₂, be EDTA-free, and include protease inhibitors). For positive and negative controls, pre-treat aliquots of lysate with 100 µM GTPγS or 1 mM GDP at 30 °C for 15 min. Incubate equal amounts of protein with ~50 μg GST-RBD beads for 1 h at 4 °C, wash five times with Buffer B, and elute by boiling in SDS loading buffer. Analyze pull-down and input samples by anti-RhoA western blot, and confirm comparable GST-RBD loading by Coomassie staining or anti-GST blot.

In vivo ubiquitination assay

Lyse liver tissues in E1A buffer containing deubiquitinase inhibitors, save an aliquot as input, and incubate equal protein amounts with anti-ubiquitin beads overnight at 4 °C. After washing, analyze the precipitate by anti-RhoA western blot; ubiquitinated endogenous RhoA appears as a high-molecular-weight smear above ~21 kDa. Normalize the smear signal to input RhoA (and a loading control) to compare relative ubiquitination between groups. If the signal is weak, immunoprecipitate RhoA first and then blot with anti-ubiquitin antibody.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and all statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Differences between multiple groups were evaluated by t-test. A p value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptional profiling of mouse liver under varied diets

We constructed a single-cell transcriptional atlas of mouse livers from CD-fed control mice and HFHSD-fed mice with induced MASH. Twelve scRNA-seq datasets (6 per group) were retrieved from GEO database (Fig. 1A). QC filtering removed low-quality cells, with key metrics (nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, mitochondrial and ribosomal gene proportions) confirming reliable cellular data post-filtering (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1A). Unsupervised clustering identified 8 major liver cell populations (Hepatocytes; Endothelial cells; Kupffer cells; Monocyte-derived Macrophages, Mo-Macrophages; Neutrophils; B cells; NK/T cells; and Cholangiocytes), visualized via t-SNE/UMAP projections (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1B), with annotations validated by canonical marker gene expression (Fig. 1D). Diet-associated shifts in cell-type proportions were observed between groups: Compared with the CD group, HFHSD feeding induced significant shifts in liver cell-type proportions (Fig. 1E), most notably expanding Kupffer cell frequency while reducing the relative abundance of B cells and NK/T cells. Sample origin-colored UMAP/t-SNE showed minimal batch effects across datasets (Fig. 1F, Fig. S1C). CytoTRACE2 analysis revealed heterogeneous developmental potential across cell types (Fig. 1G–H), and Slingshot trajectory inference delineated three major lineage trajectories in the atlas (Fig. 1I).

Cell-cell communication landscape in CD and HFHSD mouse liver

To investigate diet-induced rewiring of intercellular communication in the liver, we performed cell-cell interaction analysis comparing CD and HFHSD groups. At the global level, while the total number of inferred cell-cell interactions was slightly reduced in the HFHSD group (528 vs. 604 in CD), the overall interaction strength was markedly increased (13.236 vs. 10.263 in CD; Fig. 2A). Circle plots and differential heatmaps revealed substantial rewiring of communication networks: HFHSD feeding induced prominent increases in interaction counts and strength among myeloid populations (Mo-Macrophages, Neutrophils, Kupffer cells), while interactions involving lymphoid cells (B cells, NK/T cells) and some parenchymal cell connections were weakened (Fig. 2B–C).

Signaling pathway activity analysis further highlighted a shift in communication programs. Homeostatic pathways such as VEGF and OSM dominated in the CD group, whereas HFHSD feeding drove widespread upregulation of inflammatory and immune-related pathways, including GALECTIN, COMPLEMENT, and CXCL signaling (Fig. 2D–E). Focusing on the GALECTIN pathway, network visualization confirmed enhanced signaling strength between myeloid cells (Mo-Macrophages/Neutrophils) and other immune cells in the HFHSD group, indicating amplified pro-inflammatory crosstalk (Fig. 2F). To identify key cellular hubs driving these changes, we examined ligand-receptor interactions originating from major parenchymal and myeloid populations. Hepatocyte 2-derived signaling to immune cells, as well as Mo-Macrophage-derived interactions with B cells, neutrophils, and NK/T cells, were significantly enhanced under HFHSD, with multiple ligand-receptor pairs (e.g., complement components, integrins, and chemokines) showing increased communication probability (Fig. 2G–H).

Mitoxyperilysis activity is enriched in myeloid cells and induced by HFHSD in the liver

To map mitoxyperilysis pathway activity at single-cell resolution, we combined AUCell signature scoring and Seurat’s AddModuleScore analysis across the liver scRNA-seq atlas. AUCell analysis of the curated mitoxyperilysis gene signature showed a skewed distribution of activity, with an AUC threshold (>0.057) applied to retain cells with detectable pathway activity (39,541 cells; Fig. 3A). UMAP visualization of continuous AUC scores and binary high/low activity groups revealed that mitoxyperilysis activity was predominantly localized to myeloid populations, including Mo-Macrophages and Neutrophils, while parenchymal cells (Hepatocytes, Cholangiocytes) exhibited minimal activity (Fig. 3B–C). Consistent with this, AddModuleScore analysis across major liver cell types confirmed that Neutrophils and Mo-Macrophages harbored the highest overall mitoxyperilysis module scores, identifying them as the primary cellular compartments for this pathway (Fig. 3D). To assess the impact of metabolic stress on mitoxyperilysis, we compared sub-signature scores between CD and HFHSD groups. The five functional sub-modules (Driver, Activator, Regulator, Output mediator, Rupture effector; Table S2) were all upregulated in the HFHSD group, confirming widespread activation of the pathway under metabolic challenge (Fig. 3E).

Given the prominent activity in Mo-Macrophages, we performed subclustering to resolve heterogeneity within this population, identifying five transcriptionally distinct subclusters (0–4; Fig. 3F). GO enrichment analysis revealed divergent functional programs across Mo-Macrophage subclusters, with key significantly enriched specializations including: Cluster 0 (adaptive immune signaling), Cluster 1 (complement activation/humoral immunity), Cluster 2 (tryptophan catabolism), Cluster 3 (acute inflammatory response/neutrophil-mediated immunity), and Cluster 4 (megakaryocyte differentiation) (Fig. 3G). Critically, mitoxyperilysis module scores varied significantly across subclusters, with cluster 3 exhibiting the highest activity, indicating that this pro-inflammatory Mo-Macrophage subset is the primary mediator of HFHSD-induced mitoxyperilysis (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that mitoxyperilysis activity is cell-type specific, concentrated in myeloid cells, and strongly induced by HFHSD, with pro-inflammatory Mo-Macrophage subpopulations serving as key drivers in the context of metabolic liver disease.

Dietary remodeling of mCCC in the liver

To investigate how metabolic stress reshapes intercellular signaling in the liver, we analyzed mCCC across CD and HFHSD groups, focusing on immune-metabolic crosstalk. We quantified mCCC events under CD and HFHSD conditions (Fig. S2A). The mCCC network plot (Fig. 4A) revealed profound rewiring of metabolic communication under HFHSD. Compared to the sparse, low-score interactions in CD controls, the HFHSD-associated network exhibited denser, higher-strength signaling (green edges), indicating enhanced metabolic crosstalk between cell types. Quantification of mCCC events (Fig. 4B) confirmed a marked increase in communication activity in the HFHSD group. Mo-Macrophages emerged as a central hub: as receivers, they showed the highest number of incoming events (22 events), while as senders, they drove 10 outgoing events. Other myeloid populations (neutrophils) and parenchymal/endothelial cells (hepatocytes, endothelial cells) also displayed increased mCCC events as receivers under HFHSD, whereas communication events in the CD group were limited and evenly distributed across cell types. The mCCC pathway plot (Fig. 4C) depicts Mo-Macrophage-derived metabolic signaling cascades under CD and HFHSD conditions.

To support these signaling events, we evaluated the expression of metabolic machinery in Mo-Macrophages. Violin plots of mean aggregated enzyme expression (Fig. 4D) showed significant upregulation of enzymes associated with adenine metabolism in HFHSD-exposed Mo-Macrophages, consistent with increased production of these signaling metabolites. Correspondingly, key metabolite sensors, particularly Anxa2, were markedly upregulated in HFHSD Mo-Macrophages (Fig. 4E), suggesting enhanced responsiveness to metabolic signals within the inflammatory microenvironment. Collectively, these data demonstrate that HFHSD feeding rewires the liver’s metabolic communication landscape, with Mo-Macrophages acting as central mediators of mCCC. Enhanced secretion of immunomodulatory metabolites, coupled with upregulated metabolic enzyme and sensor expression, drives widespread metabolic crosstalk in the HFHSD liver, potentially amplifying inflammatory and disease-associated signaling.

Metabolic and mitoxyperilysis transcriptomic signatures in HFHSD-Exposed mouse livers

To systematically characterize transcriptomic perturbations induced by HFHSD in the liver, we performed differential gene expression analysis and multi-dimensional functional enrichment by comparing CD control and HFHSD groups. Volcano plot profiling identified robust transcriptional alterations in genes governing glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as those linked to mitoxyperilysis. HFHSD intervention markedly upregulated core genes involved in lipid uptake, trafficking and storage, including Cd36, Fabp4, Plin2, Apoe and Abca1. In contrast, key molecules mediating insulin signaling and glucose homeostasis, such as Irs2, Insr and Apoa2, were significantly downregulated, indicating a metabolic shift toward excessive lipid deposition accompanied by compromised hepatic insulin sensitivity under HFHSD challenge (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, mitoxyperilysis-related genes were profoundly activated in the HFHSD group, covering pro-apoptotic regulators (Bax), pattern recognition receptors (Tlr2, Tlr7), inflammatory signaling adaptors (Myd88, Tnfrsf1b), and the cell rupture effector Ninj1. The mTOR signaling modulator Rictor was significantly suppressed, further reflecting the disruption of metabolic homeostasis upon chronic nutritional stress (Fig. 5B).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further deciphered the biological pathways perturbed by HFHSD (Fig. 5C). The enriched pathways were classified into three functional modules: human disease-related pathways including lipid and atherosclerosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and AGE-RAGE signaling in diabetic complications; cellular processes encompassing apoptosis, ferroptosis and cytoskeletal remodeling; as well as signaling cascades such as mTOR and Toll-like receptor pathways. GO enrichment across BP, CC, and MF dimensions further delineated the functional signatures of HFHSD-induced hepatic transcriptional changes. BP terms were mainly enriched in innate immune activation, inflammatory regulation, oxidative stress response and mitochondrial organization, along with dysregulated glucose metabolism and cellular antioxidant processes (Fig. 5D). CC enrichment indicated aberrant alterations in mitochondrial compartments (mitochondrial matrix, mitochondrial protein complex) and lysosomal membrane components (Fig. 5E). MF terms were primarily associated with GTPase regulatory activity, lipid transport capacity, antioxidant function and actin binding ability (Fig. 5F).

GSEA further validated coordinated pathway dysregulation at the transcriptome-wide level. KEGG-based GSEA revealed significant enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation, TNF signaling, IL-17 signaling and insulin resistance pathways. Moreover, GO-based GSEA confirmed the enrichment of stress-related and immune-associated biological processes, including cellular response to starvation, inflammatory activation and mitochondrial complex assembly. Collectively, HFHSD feeding triggers extensive transcriptomic reprogramming in the liver, characterized by aberrant lipid metabolism, impaired insulin signaling, and concurrent activation of inflammatory, apoptotic and mitoxyperilysis programs. Mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress and innate immune activation act as pivotal molecular events bridging metabolic stress and the progression of hepatic inflammation and cell death under HFHSD exposure.

Spatial transcriptomic profiling of MASH livers

To investigate the spatial and cellular basis of MASH pathogenesis, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic datasets from both mouse models (GEO repository, CD: n = 6; MASH: n = 7) and human patients (HRA007511 dataset) (Fig. 6A). QC metrics, including gene counts, feature counts, and mitochondrial/ribosomal gene percentages, confirmed high-quality data suitable for downstream analysis (Fig. 6B). Using canonical cell type markers, we annotated major liver cell populations, including hepatocyte subsets, Kupffer cells, and immune cells (Fig. 6C). t-SNE visualization and proportional analysis revealed profound remodeling of the liver cellular landscape in MASH livers (Fig. 6D). Specifically, MASH mice exhibited a significant expansion of pro-inflammatory Aldh1b1+ hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, alongside a marked reduction of homeostatic Mup+ hepatocytes (Fig. 6D–E), indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory cellular microenvironment.

Next, we examined transcriptomic changes associated with MASH progression. Volcano plots revealed widespread dysregulation of genes involved in glucose/lipid metabolism and mitoxyperilysis signaling (Fig. 6F–G). In line with metabolic dysfunction, genes mediating lipid uptake and processing (e.g., Cd36, Fabp4, Scd2) were upregulated in MASH livers, while key regulators of insulin signaling (Irs2) and hepatocyte homeostasis (Cyp51) were downregulated (Fig. 6F). Concurrently, mitoxyperilysis-associated genes, including pro-inflammatory mediators (Tnf, Tlr4, Tlr7), apoptotic effectors (Bax, Bak1), and the cell rupture gene Ninj1, were significantly upregulated in the MASH group, with concurrent suppression of the mTOR pathway modulator Rictor (Fig. 6G). A heatmap of these mitoxyperilysis-related genes confirmed consistent upregulation across MASH samples (Fig. 6H). Functional enrichment analysis further contextualized these transcriptomic shifts. GO enrichment revealed upregulation of pathways related to innate immune activation, oxidative stress response, and actin cytoskeleton regulation, alongside downregulation of fatty acid metabolic processes (Fig. 6I). KEGG pathway analysis identified enhanced signaling in inflammation-related cascades (NOD-like receptor, Toll-like receptor) and mitoxyperilysis-associated key pathways (mTOR signaling pathway, glutathione metabolism, and actin cytoskeleton regulation), with concurrent suppression of metabolic pathways including PPAR signaling and peroxisome function (Fig. 6J).

To validate the clinical relevance of these findings, we analyzed mitoxyperilysis gene expression in human MASH liver samples. Consistent with mouse data, key genes involved in mitoxyperilysis signaling, including inflammatory receptors (TLR2, TLR4, TLR7, TNF), apoptotic mediators (BAX, BAK1), and downstream effectors (ACTR2, ACTR3, RHOA), were significantly upregulated in human MASH livers compared to controls (Fig. 6K). Together, these spatial transcriptomic analyses demonstrate that MASH is characterized by profound cellular and molecular remodeling, including expansion of pro-inflammatory cell populations, dysregulated metabolic signaling, and conserved activation of mitoxyperilysis pathways in both mouse and human disease contexts.

Cross-species transcriptomic profiling reveals evolutionarily conserved activation of the mitoxyperilysis pathway in MASH

Volcano plot visualization of DEG profiles unveiled pervasive transcriptomic rewiring in MASH contexts across all interrogated datasets, characterized by a conserved transcriptional signature of mitoxyperilysis pathway activation. Complementary box plot analyses further quantified the expression dynamics of core mitoxyperilysis effector genes, spanning pattern recognition receptors (Tlr2, Tlr4, Tlr7, Tlr8), pro-inflammatory signaling mediators (Tnf, Il1b, Il18, Myd88), pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family effectors (Bax, Bak1, Bid), cytoskeletal remodeling regulators (Actr2, Actr3, Rhoa), and key metabolic signaling modulators (Akt1, Rictor, Mtor).

Across all phylogenetic lineages interrogated, we observed robust, evolutionarily conserved upregulation of mitoxyperilysis pathway components in MASH models relative to control conditions. Notably, Ninj1, the terminal effector of plasma membrane rupture during regulated necrosis, exhibited statistically significant induction in every dataset analyzed, underscoring its non-redundant, conserved role in the execution of mitoxyperilysis. Concurrently, innate immune pattern recognition receptors (Tlr2/4/7) and their downstream pro-inflammatory cytokine targets (Tnf, Il1b) were uniformly upregulated, demonstrating that inflammatory priming of mitoxyperilysis is a shared, conserved feature of MASH pathogenesis across species. Pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family genes (Bax, Bak1) were similarly and consistently induced, while the mTOR complex 2 modulator Rictor was significantly downregulated in the majority of preclinical models, consistent with metabolic stress-mediated suppression of homeostatic survival signaling cascades. While the core mitoxyperilysis transcriptional signature was universally conserved across all species, subtle but biologically plausible species-specific variations in the magnitude of gene expression perturbations were observed. For instance, the fold changes of Il18 and Tnf were more pronounced in rodent models relative to primate datasets, though the directional dysregulation of all core pathway components remained consistent across lineages. Critically, the human MASH patient dataset recapitulated the key transcriptional patterns observed in preclinical models, with significant upregulation of inflammatory, pro-apoptotic, and membrane rupture-associated genes, confirming the clinical translatability and human relevance of mitoxyperilysis pathway activation in progressive liver disease.

Collectively, these cross-species transcriptomic analyses provide definitive evidence that mitoxyperilysis pathway activation constitutes an evolutionarily conserved molecular hallmark of MASH, manifesting across phylogenetically diverse preclinical models and human patients. The consistent upregulation of core pathway components across lineages underscores the central, non-redundant role of mitoxyperilysis in driving hepatic inflammation and regulated necrosis in MASH pathogenesis, thereby validating the translational potential of targeting this conserved pathway as a therapeutic strategy for human MASH and related metabolic liver disorders.

ML analysis

To identify robust transcriptomic classifiers for distinguishing CD and HFHSD-induced MASH liver samples, we evaluated 11 ML algorithms (Fig. 8). Macro-averaged ROC curves revealed near-perfect discriminative ability for most models (AUC ≥ 0.994), with Logistic Regression, SGD Classifier, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and SVM achieving perfect AUC = 1.000; only K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) showed suboptimal performance (AUC = 0.830) (Fig. 8A). Consistent with ROC analysis, Logistic Regression and SVM exhibited top-tier performance across accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and CV mean accuracy (all ≥ 0.994). Random Forest, AdaBoost, and Extra Trees also maintained high performance (all metrics ≥ 0.963), while KNN scored lowest across all metrics (Fig. 8B). Confusion matrices confirmed minimal misclassifications for Logistic Regression (1 error) and SVM (1 error), with slightly higher errors for other top models; KNN showed more frequent misclassifications (Fig. 8C).

Identification of Bax as a mitoxyperilysis regulator via ML and in silico perturbation

To identify key mitoxyperilysis regulators linked to diet-induced transcriptomic changes, we prioritized candidates using top-performing ML models (Fig. 9A). A Venn diagram integrating ML-derived signature genes with mitoxyperilysis-related genes identified Bax as the sole overlapping candidate (Fig. 9B). To functionally validate Bax, we performed in silico gene knockout using the scTenifoldKnk framework to assess transcriptomic perturbations following Bax ablation. scTenifoldKnk analysis revealed widespread transcriptomic dysregulation post-virtual knockout, with the top 20 perturbed genes ranked by log₂ fold change (Fig. 9C). Perturbation magnitude plots further visualized significant gene expression shifts induced by Bax knockout (Fig. 9D). Functional enrichment analyses of perturbed genes uncovered enrichment in lipid metabolic processes, immune effector regulation, and oxidative stress responses (Fig. 9E). KEGG pathway analysis additionally identified dysregulation of core metabolic pathways, including PPAR signaling, fatty acid degradation, and peroxisome function (Fig. 9F).

In vivo validation of mitoxyperilysis in the CDAHFD MASH model

To validate mitoxyperilysis activation in a physiologically relevant mouse model of MASH, we subjected C57BL/6J (B6J) mice to either a CD or a CDAHFD for 6 weeks (n = 5 per group; Fig. 10A). Longitudinal monitoring revealed divergent body weight trajectories: CD-fed mice exhibited steady weight gain, while CDAHFD-fed mice showed progressive weight loss, with significant intergroup differences emerging from week 1 onward (Fig. 10B). HE staining confirmed robust MASH pathology in the CDAHFD group, characterized by extensive macrovesicular steatosis (blue arrows) and inflammatory infiltration (red arrow), whereas CD-fed livers retained normal architecture (Fig. 10D). Serum biochemical analyses validated hepatocellular injury and metabolic dysregulation: CDAHFD mice showed markedly elevated ALT and AST levels, with no significant change in ALP (Fig. 10E). Concurrently, TC, TG, and LDL were significantly increased in the CDAHFD group, recapitulating human MASH-associated dyslipidemia (Fig. 10F).

RT-qPCR analysis of core mitoxyperilysis genes revealed widespread transcriptional upregulation in CDAHFD livers compared to controls. Key components including pro-apoptotic effectors (Bax, Bid), inflammatory mediators (Il18, Il1b), pattern recognition receptors (Tlr2, Tlr4, Tlr7, Tlr8), and signaling molecules (Tnf) were significantly induced, with Bax, Il18, and Tlr4 showing the most pronounced upregulation (Fig. 10G). These data establish the CDAHFD-induced model as a robust platform for MASH research and confirm mitoxyperilysis pathway activation in vivo, supporting its role in disease pathogenesis. Consistent with these transcriptional changes, immunoblotting confirmed elevated protein expression of pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members Bak1, Bid, and Bax in CDAHFD-exposed livers (Fig. 10H). Critically, while Rhoa mRNA was transcriptionally upregulated, total RhoA protein levels remained unaltered between groups. Instead, we observed a marked increase in RhoA polyubiquitination and a corresponding reduction in active GTP-bound RhoA in CDAHFD-treated livers (Fig. 10I), revealing that the immune activation and metabolic disruption in MASH drive RhoA inhibition primarily through post-translational ubiquitination, rather than transcriptional modulation. Collectively, these multi-level validations establish the CDAHFD-induced model as a robust platform for MASH research and identify RhoA ubiquitination as a novel, disease-relevant regulatory mechanism underlying mitoxyperilysis pathway activation, supporting its role in MASH pathogenesis.

Discussion

MASH represents a critical frontier in hepatology, as it constitutes the aggressive form of MASLD that propels patients toward cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma [28]. Current clinical management relies heavily on lifestyle modifications, which suffer from poor long-term adherence and limited efficacy [29]. Consequently, an urgent need exists to unravel the complex pathogenic mechanisms driving MASH progression, particularly the interplay between hepatocyte death and inflammatory cascades, to identify actionable molecular targets for therapeutic intervention [30, 31]. To address this gap, our study employed an integrative multi-omics strategy combining single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, cross-species comparative analyses, and ML to dissect the role of a newly defined cell death pathway, designated as mitoxyperilysis, in MASH pathogenesis. By constructing a comprehensive single-cell atlas of mouse livers under HFHSD feeding, we identified a cell-type-specific activation of this pathway within myeloid cells, particularly pro-inflammatory Mo-macrophages, and confirmed its evolutionary conservation across five species from rodents to humans. Through the application of 11 ML algorithms, Bax emerged as a critical regulatory hub bridging transcriptional signatures with mitoxyperilysis activation, a finding further substantiated by in vivo validation using the CDAHFD-induced MASH mouse model. Below, we systematically discuss how these results collectively establish Mitoxyperilysis as a conserved molecular hallmark of MASH and illuminate the central role of Bax in orchestrating this pathogenic cascade.

Our comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the murine liver in response to HFHSD feeding revealed profound remodeling of the hepatic immune microenvironment, characterized by specific expansion of Kupffer cells and relative reduction in B cells and NK/T cells. This cellular shift aligns with the established paradigm that MASH progression is driven by an innate immune-dominant inflammatory milieu at the expense of adaptive immune surveillance [32, 33]. The pathological rerouting of signaling resources away from homeostatic maintenance toward pro-inflammatory cascades was particularly evident in the selective activation of GALECTIN, COMPLEMENT, and CXCL pathways predominantly within myeloid cells, which collectively drive sterile inflammation and tissue damage. Collectively, these single-cell data demonstrate that MASH is fundamentally a disease of disrupted cellular sociology, where myeloid cell-centric communication networks are central drivers of the self-amplifying inflammatory cycle rather than mere consequences of steatosis. Cell-type-specific analysis of the novel mitoxyperilysis pathway provided a functional correlate to the observed immune remodeling. Mitoxyperilysis activity was predominantly enriched in Mo-macrophages and neutrophils. Within the Mo-macrophage compartment, a specific pro-inflammatory subpopulation (Cluster 3) displayed the highest pathway activity, indicating that mitoxyperilysis execution is not a universal property of all myeloid cells but a hallmark of a distinct activation state. Furthermore, remodeling of metabolite-mediated cell-cell communication positioned Mo-macrophages as a central hub, suggesting that specific metabolites (e.g., fatty acids, cholesterol) may prime the mitoxyperilysis machinery via receptors such as TLRs, establishing a direct molecular link between nutritional overload and immune-mediated cellular destruction [34, 35]. Spatial transcriptomics and cross-species validation significantly enhanced the translational relevance of our findings. Spatial analysis confirmed that activation of mitoxyperilysis effector genes (Bax, Tlr2/4/7, Ninj1) was not restricted to isolated cells but was a prominent feature of histopathological lesions in both mouse and human MASH tissues. This robust conservation across species with divergent metabolic physiologies strongly argues that mitoxyperilysis is not a byproduct of a specific dietary model or species-specific immunity, but rather a core ancestral pathomechanism of MASH amenable to universal therapeutic targeting.

Our methodological innovation lies in the use of an ensemble of 11 machine learning algorithms to bridge transcriptome-wide changes with specific biological pathways. Near-perfect classification accuracy (AUC ≥ 0.994) achieved by models including logistic regression and support vector machines confirmed that the MASH transcriptomic signature is highly discriminative and robust [36]. Critically, Bax emerged as the sole overlapping gene between machine learning-selected features and the mitoxyperilysis gene set. As the archetypal effector of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), BAX likely functions as the central hub linking metabolic/inflammatory stress signals to mitochondrial dysfunction, subsequent inflammation, and eventual cell rupture in the mitoxyperilysis pathway [37, 38]. In silico knockout experiments further supported this role, predicting that Bax deletion causes broad transcriptional perturbations in lipid metabolism, immune regulation, and oxidative stress—consistent with its upstream regulatory position in the pathway. Finally, we adopted the CDAHFD-induced MASH mouse model for in vivo functional verification of our bioinformatics results. Core genes governing mitoxyperilysis were transcriptionally upregulated in diseased livers, and this expression pattern was recapitulated at the protein level for pro-death effectors including Bax, Bak1 and Bid. Intriguingly, Rhoa transcription was enhanced while its total protein content stayed stable, accompanied by increased polyubiquitination and decreased active RhoA. This indicates that RhoA function is modulated primarily through post-translational modification in the MASH context. This work bridges computational prediction and biological validation, and the consistently upregulated Bax offers a valuable candidate for future targeted intervention research.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite rigorous Harmony batch correction and cross-validation, our bioinformatic analyses relied primarily on public datasets with inherent technical heterogeneity, which may modestly affect mitoxyperilysis activation score reproducibility across independent cohorts. Second, in silico Bax knockout predictions were only validated at the mRNA level in the CDAHFD model; definitive causal evidence requires gain/loss-of-function studies in conditional knockout mice. Finally, the identified spatial and metabolic communication networks lack functional validation via direct co-culture or fluxomics, and all experiments were performed exclusively in male mice, precluding assessment of sex-dependent effects.

In summary, this study systematically delineates the mitoxyperilysis pathway as a conserved, myeloid-centric molecular driver in MASH, bridging immune dysfunction, metabolic stress, and hepatic death. By integrating single-cell, spatial, and cross-species transcriptomics with ML, we identify Bax as a pivotal regulatory hub that connects upstream metabolic signals to downstream execution of membrane rupture. Our findings provide a robust framework for developing targeted therapies to disrupt this death-inflammatory cascade. Future work should focus on functional validation of the mitoxyperilysis pathway as a therapeutic target, combining genetic perturbation to establish causality and preclinical pharmacological intervention to advance clinical translation.
