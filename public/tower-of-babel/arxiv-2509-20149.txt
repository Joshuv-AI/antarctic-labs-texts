Enhancing Requirement Traceability through Data Augmentation Using Large Language Models
Jianzhang Zhang, Jialong Zhou, Nan Niu, Chuang Liu

Abstract
Requirements traceability is crucial in software engineering to ensure consistency between requirements and code. However, existing automated traceability methods are constrained by the scarcity of training data and challenges in bridging the semantic gap between artifacts. This study aims to address the data scarcity problem in requirements traceability by employing large language models (LLMs) for data augmentation. We propose a novel approach that utilizes prompt-based techniques with LLMs to generate augmented requirement-to-code trace links, thereby enhancing the training dataset. Four LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4) were used, employing both zero-shot and few-shot templates. Moreover, we optimized the encoder component of the tracing model to improve its efficiency and adaptability to augmented data. The key contributions of this paper are: (1) proposing and evaluating four prompt templates for data augmentation; (2) providing a comparative analysis of four LLMs for generating trace links; (3) enhancing the model’s encoder for improved adaptability to augmented datasets. Experimental results show that our approach significantly enhances model performance, achieving an F 1 F_{1} score improvement of up to 28.59%, thus demonstrating its effectiveness and potential for practical application.

Introduction

Requirement traceability (RT) is defined as "the ability to describe and follow the life of a requirement, in both a forwards and backwards direction"
[
1
]
. It plays a critical role in software engineering by establishing links between requirements and software artifacts, thereby ensuring that all work products in the software development process consistently meet user needs. Such links are essential for developers to effectively understand and manage changes in requirements, ultimately enhancing the efficiency of software development and maintenance
[
2
,
3
]
.

Creating and maintaining requirement traceability links manually is often labor-intensive and cost-prohibitive. Over the past decades, various automated methods have been proposed to address these challenges. These methods can be categorized broadly into two types: information retrieval-based methods, such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)
[
4
]
, Latent Semantic Indexing (LSI)
[
5
]
, and Vector Space Model (VSM)
[
6
]
; and classification-based methods, which include both traditional machine learning approaches
[
7
]
and deep learning models
[
8
]
. However, these approaches often fail to bridge the semantic gap between different artifacts, leading to suboptimal results
[
8
]
.

Recent advances in LLMs
[
9
]
have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding and text generation across a wide range of tasks
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Researchers have leveraged LLMs to address requirement traceability tasks, with promising results. For instance, Lin et al.
[
13
]
proposed a CodeBERT-based architecture to evaluate multiple tracking models, demonstrating the effectiveness of the Single model in bridging the semantic gap between natural language artifacts (NLAs) and programming language artifacts (PLAs). However, training high-performance deep learning models requires substantial amounts of labeled data, which is often unavailable due to the high cost of manually creating requirement traceability links.

To address the issue of data scarcity, this study proposes a novel approach that utilizes LLMs for data augmentation in requirement traceability tasks. By leveraging the extensive prior knowledge encoded in LLMs, we generate synthetic trace links between NLAs and PLAs using both zero-shot and few-shot prompting techniques
[
14
,
15
]
. Specifically, we experiment with generating trace links through four distinct prompt templates and assess the impact of different encoders and maximum sequence lengths on model performance. Our approach aims to alleviate the data sparsity problem, enhance model training, and ultimately improve the accuracy of trace link generation.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We propose four prompt generation methods and systematically compare their effectiveness in data augmentation for requirement traceability. Our analysis provides new insights into prompt engineering techniques and guides researchers in choosing suitable prompts for different application scenarios.

•

We enhance existing datasets using four mainstream LLMs and evaluate their performance under different prompting methods. Through detailed comparisons, we provide a scientific basis for model selection and highlight the advantages and limitations of different LLMs in the context of requirement traceability.

•

We improve the encoder of the Single model to enhance its adaptability to diverse augmented datasets. The improved encoder demonstrates higher efficiency and accuracy in handling complex data, thereby significantly boosting the overall performance of the model.

•

We demonstrate that combining data augmentation methods with encoder improvements effectively alleviates the problem of insufficient training data, leading to substantial performance gains across multiple datasets.

2
Approach

We employ LLMs to generate additional data to meet training requirements, while enhancing the encoder of the Single model for improved accuracy in trace link generation. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the approach involves two data generation processes. First, requirement artifacts (source) from the original dataset are combined with prompt templates and input into the LLM to generate corresponding code, thereby establishing trace links. Second, code artifacts (target) from the original dataset are combined with prompt templates and input into the LLM to generate corresponding requirements, also forming trace links. The newly generated LLM-based data, together with the original data, are used to create an enriched dataset for fine-tuning the Single model.

Data Augmentation

2.2.1
Prompt Construction

To construct prompts, specific placeholders in the prompt templates defined in Section 2.2 are replaced as follows:

•

lang: Replaced with the target programming language, such as
Java

•

code: Replaced with the code text from the original dataset.

•

requirements: Replaced with the requirement text from the original dataset.

•

example code: Replaced with a fixed code example from the original dataset.

•

example requirements: Replaced with a fixed requirements example from the original dataset.

For few-shot prompts, we ensure that the task examples used for each dataset are consistent, and the values of {requirements} and {code} differ from those used in the task examples.

2.3
Model Training

This section describes the negative sampling and training strategies used in this study.

2.3.1
Negative Sampling

The original and augmented datasets only contain positive samples. To fulfill the requirements for model training, negative sampling is employed. The approach involves replacing the programming language (PL) component of a natural language-programming language (NL-PL) pair with an unrelated PL. To address computational overhead and reduce model bias
[
17
]
, one unrelated NL-PL pair is generated for each positive pair, ensuring a balanced ratio of positive and negative samples in the dataset.

2.3.2
Training Strategies

We utilize the Single model for training, as depicted on the right side of Figure
1
. The natural language text (NL), separator token
[SEP]

[
12

This sequence is fed into a BERT-based encoder to generate a hidden state matrix, which is subsequently reduced to a feature vector via an average pooling layer. The classification head then uses this feature vector to predict whether the input NL and PL are related.

3.2
Encoder

The encoder is the core component of the Single model, responsible for extracting features from both natural language and programming language texts. In this study, we utilized two encoders: CodeBERT and Jina.

CodeBERT is a pre-trained model based on the BERT architecture, designed specifically for understanding and generating tasks involving both programming languages and natural language. It is trained on a large-scale bimodal and unimodal dataset from GitHub, covering six programming languages: Python, Java, JavaScript, PHP, Ruby, and Go. CodeBERT can handle sequences of up to 512 tokens, capturing deep contextual information. Although CodeBERT was not originally trained on
C#
datasets, its generalization capabilities
[
21
]
suggest that it can effectively handle
C#
data, as confirmed by our experimental results.

Jina is a multilingual embedding model capable of handling English and 30 widely used programming languages, including Java, JavaScript, and
C#
. Based on a BERT architecture, Jina supports the symmetric bidirectional variant of ALiBi
[
22
]
, allowing for a sequence length of up to 8192 tokens. The model backbone is pre-trained on the GitHub code dataset.

3.3
Training Settings

All experiments, including training and evaluation, were conducted on two P100 GPUs with 16GB of memory each. The initial learning rate was set to
1\times 10^{-5}
, with a batch size of 8, and the training ran for 20 epochs. To account for the impact of randomness, experiments were repeated using five different random seeds: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, and 2018. Unless otherwise specified, the maximum sequence length was set to 512. During fine-tuning, the dataset was divided into ten parts: eight parts for training, one part for validation, and one part for testing.

3.4
Evaluation Metrics

We used Accuracy, Precision, Recall,
F_{1}
Score, and
F_{2}
Score to evaluate the performance of the Single model on the test set. Accuracy is defined as the ratio of correctly classified samples to the total number of samples. Precision measures the proportion of true positive samples among those predicted as positive by the model. Recall represents the proportion of true positive samples among all actual positive samples. The
F_{1}
Score is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a balanced assessment of model performance. The
F_{2}
Score is a weighted harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, placing greater emphasis on Recall.

4
Experimental Results Analysis

The overall goal of this study is to Improve the effectiveness of requirement traceability using advanced data augmentation techniques, LLMs, and encoder optimization to create a robust solution that outperforms existing approaches. To achieve this goal, we formulate the following research questions, each addressing a critical aspect of the methodology:

•

RQ1: Which prompt template is most effective for generating augmented training data?

The selection of prompt templates can significantly influence the quality of data generated by LLMs, thereby impacting model performance. This question aims to identify the most effective prompt template for augmenting training data, comparing the performance of different zero-shot and few-shot prompt structures.

•

RQ2: Which large language model (LLM) provides the best performance for data augmentation in requirement traceability?

Given the diversity in architecture and training datasets among LLMs, their capabilities in generating accurate requirement traceability data can vary. This question seeks to determine which LLM—among Gemini 1.5, Claude 3, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4—delivers superior performance in this specific application.

•

RQ3: How can the encoder be optimized to enhance the performance of the Single model?

The encoder plays a critical role in extracting meaningful features from both natural language and programming language texts. This question explores strategies for optimizing the encoder, focusing on utilizing pre-trained models like CodeBERT and Jina, and investigates how these modifications contribute to improving the overall model performance.

•

RQ4: How does the proposed model compare with existing approaches in terms of requirement traceability performance?

To evaluate the effectiveness of our approach, it is essential to compare it with other existing methods. This question aims to determine the extent of improvement offered by our model in comparison to established baselines, using metrics such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall,
F_{1}
Score, and
F_{2}
Score.

4.1
Analysis of Prompt Template Effectiveness for Data Augmentation

To reduce the effort required for manually creating and maintaining requirement traceability links while meeting model training requirements, we designed four distinct prompt templates for data augmentation. To evaluate the effectiveness of these templates, we generated data using four different closed-source LLMs and trained the Single model with CodeBERT as the encoder. The performance results, using data generated by different templates, are illustrated in Figure
2
. The label ’None’ indicates the absence of data augmentation.

Figure 2
:
Impact of Different Data Augmentation Methods on Single Model Performance across EBT, eTOUR, iTrust, and RETRO Datasets.

The results indicate that data augmentation using LLMs effectively supports model training and enhances performance. Across the four datasets, our data augmentation methods led to substantial improvements in multiple metrics for the eTour and iTrust datasets. For the EBT dataset, while improvements were observed, they were less pronounced, likely due to the extreme data sparsity in EBT. Regarding the RETRO dataset, despite CodeBERT not being specifically trained on C# data, it demonstrated acceptable performance. Notably, all data augmentation methods—except the few-shot requirement generation—led to improved model performance on the RETRO dataset.

Overall, the zero-shot approach for generating requirements, the zero-shot approach for generating code, and the few-shot approach for generating code consistently resulted in performance improvements. However, the executability of the generated code remains a concern.

Furthermore, this study constructed an enhanced dataset for a single project. For developing a large-scale requirement traceability dataset, we recommend using the zero-shot method to generate requirements, as it is generally easier to obtain a substantial amount of high-quality original code from platforms such as GitHub compared to obtaining qualified original requirements.

Answer to RQ1
:
The results show that prompt selection significantly affects data augmentation quality and model performance. The zero-shot prompts for generating requirements and code, along with the few-shot prompt for code generation, consistently improved performance, particularly for the eTour and iTrust datasets. For large-scale requirement traceability, the zero-shot approach for generating requirements is recommended due to its efficiency and ease of obtaining high-quality code.

4.3
Optimization Strategies for Enhancing Encoder Performance in the Single Model

RQ3 focuses on enhancing the encoder to improve the performance of the Single model in the requirement traceability task. To address this, we explored two sub-questions: (1) Does an encoder trained on a more diverse programming language dataset perform better? (2) Does extending the input sequence length improve the encoder’s effectiveness?

Previous experiments demonstrated that CodeBERT can achieve acceptable performance on programming languages not included in its training data; however, relying solely on generalization capabilities is insufficient. Thus, we evaluated Jina, an encoder pre-trained on a broader dataset of programming languages. The programming languages supported by CodeBERT and Jina are summarized in Table
3
.

To assess the effectiveness of Jina, we conducted experiments using augmented datasets generated by the GPT-4 and zero-shot requirements methods. The results, presented in Figure

In prior experiments, the maximum sequence length of the encoder was set to 512, with content exceeding this length being truncated. This truncation could lead to information loss and negatively affect model performance. To evaluate whether extending the sequence length could improve the performance of the Single model, we conducted experiments using the Jina encoder with maximum sequence lengths of 512 and 1024. The results are presented in Figure

Figure 8

Impact of Input Sequence Length on the Performance of the Single Model

The findings indicate that for the EBT and eTOUR datasets, increasing the sequence length led to decreased performance. In contrast, for the iTrust and RETRO datasets, a longer sequence length resulted in improved performance. Overall, extending the sequence length does not guarantee enhanced model performance, and its impact appears to be dataset-dependent.

Answer to RQ3
:
The experimental results suggest that optimizing the encoder by using a pre-trained model with broader programming language coverage, such as Jina, consistently improves the Single model’s performance compared to CodeBERT. However, increasing the input sequence length does not universally enhance performance and its impact varies across datasets. Therefore, leveraging an encoder trained on a diverse dataset is beneficial, while extending sequence length should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

Discussion

The primary threat to internal validity is the noise present in data generated by the LLMs. Despite utilizing effective prompt templates and controlling the output as much as possible, some noise may still persist. To mitigate this issue, we performed post-processing to clean the LLM-generated outputs. For generated code, we used regular expressions to extract the relevant parts, as the LLM outputs were enclosed in triple quotation marks. For generated requirements, we reviewed the beginning and end of the outputs to remove extraneous content, such as introductory or summary statements, if specific keywords were present. Additionally, Markdown syntax elements were removed to ensure a consistent format.

External validity is subject to two main threats. First, we evaluated our model using only four datasets involving Java, JavaScript, and
C#
. While our model demonstrated strong performance on these datasets, further validation is needed across additional programming languages and datasets, including larger-scale datasets that we plan to construct. Second, we relied solely on closed-source LLMs for data augmentation, primarily due to their convenience and superior performance. In future work, we plan to incorporate open-source LLMs, such as Llama 3, for broader applicability in data augmentation.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

This study introduced the use of LLMs for generating requirement traceability links as a form of data augmentation, comparing four different prompting methods and four LLMs for data generation. Additionally, we evaluated the performance of encoders pre-trained on diverse programming language datasets and analyzed the impact of varying sequence lengths on model effectiveness. The experimental results demonstrated that generating requirements and code using the zero-shot approach, as well as using the few-shot approach for code generation, consistently improved model performance. We recommend using the zero-shot method for generating requirements, as the source code used for requirement generation is generally more accessible. No significant differences in data generation performance were found among the four LLMs: Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3, GPT-3.5 Turbo, and GPT-4. The results further indicated that encoders pre-trained on more diverse programming language datasets achieved superior performance, whereas extending sequence length did not consistently lead to performance improvements.

In future work, we plan to expand the applicability of our approach by evaluating it on a broader set of programming languages and datasets. We also intend to explore the use of open-source LLMs for data augmentation and apply our proposed model in practical requirement traceability scenarios.
