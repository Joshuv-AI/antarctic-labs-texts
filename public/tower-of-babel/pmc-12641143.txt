Knowledge of Obstetric Fistula and Its Associated Factors Among Women of Childbearing Age in Buno Bedele Zone, Southwest Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Bashatu Berkessa Tola, Gizachew Abdissa Bulto, Eden Girmaye Tefera, Fikadu Wondimu Abdissa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsObstetric fistulas are one of the most serious and catastrophic complications that occur during childbirth. Increasing awareness among women may contribute positively to its reduction and prompt treatment initiation. Although limited studies have been conducted on the knowledge of obstetric fistulas, they were mainly conducted among women who sought care at health institutions. This study aimed to assess the knowledge of obstetric fistulas and associated factors in Southwestern Ethiopia.MethodsThis was a community‐based cross‐sectional study of women of reproductive age in the Buno Bedele Zone between August 26 and September 26, 2021. Multistage sampling techniques were employed to select 612 participants. Data were collected using structured questionnaires administered through interviews. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify associated factors.ResultsAbout 48.3% (95% CI = 43.9, 52.2) of women of childbearing age were found to have good overall knowledge of obstetric fistula. Factors influencing the knowledge of obstetric fistula included urban residency (AOR = 4.23, 95% CI: 2.09, 8.57), attending primary school (AOR = 4.43, 95% CI: 2.25, 8.69), presence of media in the household (AOR = 3.65, 95% CI: 1.84, 7.25), attending postnatal care (AOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.22, 5.74), participation in pregnant women's conferences (AOR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.19, 4.21), and receiving education on obstetric fistula (AOR = 3.64, 95% CI: 1.95, 6.79).ConclusionsLess than half of the women had good knowledge of obstetric fistulas. Residence, educational status, media access, history of postnatal care, and pregnant women's conferences were identified as factors affecting the knowledge of obstetric fistula. Thus, health managers and professionals must work together to provide health education on obstetric fistula at each maternal service delivery point, at pregnant women's conferences, and through the media.

Introduction

Obstetric fistula (OBF) “is a condition characterized by abnormal communication between the vagina and bladder, rectum, or both.” It predominantly arises from unattended prolonged and obstructed labor, particularly in contexts where access to quality healthcare services is lacking [1, 2, 3, 4]. Globally, over three million women encounter either short‐ or long‐term complications related to pregnancy or childbirth [5]. According to estimates by the World Health Organization, OBF annually affects approximately 50,000–100,000 women globally. Furthermore, in 2018, over two million young women with untreated OBF resided in sub‐Saharan Africa and Asia [1, 5, 6]. Although it has a crippling and devastating effect on the lives of affected girls and women, it has been largely overlooked, particularly in rural and remote regions of developing nations [7].

In Ethiopia, approximately 142,100 women of childbearing age have experienced lifelong vaginal fistula symptoms, and 110,800 women have symptoms of vaginal fistula, which is the highest number in sub‐Saharan Africa [8]. Different stakeholders, including Ethiopia's Ministry of Health, have been working to eliminate obstetric fistulas in Ethiopia [9, 10]. The Catherine Hamlin Fistula Foundation has focused on the management and prevention of OBF by increasing the number of qualified midwives in rural areas and creating an active training program for providers in Ethiopia [10, 11]. However, the prevalence of OBF is highest and varies among regions. In Oromia, it is estimated that 9822 women of childbearing age currently suffer from untreated vaginal fistulas, while 23,674 women have encountered this condition at some point in their lives [12].

Despite the significance of this issue, awareness and understanding of obstetric fistulas among women remain limited in low‐income countries. In Africa, awareness ranges from a mere 13.8% of women in Gambia to 57.8% in Nigeria [13, 14, 15]. In Ethiopia, the pooled prevalence of OBF awareness is 41.24% [16], with regional variations ranging from 36.4% in Benja district to 69.7% in the Bench Sheko zone [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

Women's knowledge of preventive measures, clinical presentations, risk factors, and potential complications of obstetric fistula empowers them to make informed choices and seek medical attention at healthcare facilities. This indicates that to reduce OBF and its complications, interventions must be targeted to enhance knowledge. It is also believed that learning about one's condition helps in working towards the prevention of OBF [14, 23]. Examining the knowledge of women about OBF would shed light on interventions that would help reduce maternal morbidity and mortality and improve the wellbeing of women [24]. In addition, knowledge of OBF among women of childbearing age should be used to strengthen prevention strategies for healthcare facilities, community levels, and policymakers [3]. However, the existing literature on OBF in Ethiopia does not provide much information on the status of knowledge regarding the disease [22, 25]. Most previous studies in Ethiopia have been conducted among women who visited health facilities for different services, which might not reflect knowledge in the communities. However, this study was community‐based and included the types, complications, and psychosocial consequences of OBF, which were not addressed in previous studies conducted in Ethiopia. The findings of this study will inform the design of community‐based interventions aimed at increasing awareness of OBF prevention and promoting early treatment‐seeking behaviors within communities in similar settings. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the status of knowledge of OBF and its associated factors among women of childbearing age in the Buno Bedele Zone, Southwestern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Design and Area

The study employed a community‐based cross‐sectional study design in the Buno Bedele zone, Oromia region, Southwestern Ethiopia from August 26 to September 26, 2021. Bedele Town serves as the capital of the Buno Bedele zone and is situated 492 km from Addis Ababa. The total population of this zone was 841,158, comprising 420,854 males and 420,304 females. The number of women of childbearing age was estimated to be approximately 156,708. There are four hospitals and 32 health centers in the zone [26].

Source and Study Population

The source population included all women aged between 15 and 49 years living in the Buno Bedele zone of the Oromia region in Ethiopia. We randomly selected women from this age group residing in the chosen districts during the study period.

Eligibility Criteria

Women aged 15–49 years who were present during the data collection period and had resided in the study area for a minimum of 6 months before the study were included. Nevertheless, those who were severely ill and unable to communicate or respond were excluded from the study.

Sample Size and Sampling Techniques

The sample size was calculated using a single proportion formula, with the assumptions of a 95% confidence interval (CI), margin of error of 5%, and 40.8% awareness of OBF among women of childbearing age, from the study conducted in Bench Sheko, Southwest Ethiopia [21]. Considering a 10% rate of nonresponse and a design effect of 1.5, the final sample size was 612.

To determine the sample size for the second specific objective, the Epi Info Stat calc was utilized, based on the assumptions of 80% power, 95% CI, and 10% nonresponse rate. Factors such as educational status, attendance at pregnant women's conferences, and the location of delivery, which are linked to awareness of obstetric fistulas, were considered [21]. Finally, the sample size for the first specific objective, 612, was used in this study.

A multi‐stage sampling approach was utilized, beginning with three out of 10 districts selected through simple random sampling. The study population was then distributed among the three districts in proportion to their population size. Using the lottery method, 30% of kebeles (small administrative units) within the three districts were chosen. Health extension workers provided lists of women of reproductive age living in selected kebeles, which served as the sampling frame. The sample size was allocated to each kebele in proportion to the number of women of reproductive age, and a systematic sampling method was used to select study units. If eligible women were not available for data collection, two additional visits were made. If they were still unavailable after these visits, they were classified as non‐responders (Figure 1).

Variables of the Study

The dependent variable in this study is the knowledge of OBF. The independent or exposure variables are categorized as follows: Socio‐demographic Factors, which include age, religion, ethnicity, residency, education level of women, occupational status of women, and media exposure. Reproductive and Obstetric Factors encompass the age of first marriage, number of pregnancies, age of first pregnancy, current pregnancy status, history of induced abortion, awareness of obstetric complications, antenatal care follow‐up in previous pregnancies, place of delivery for recent childbirth, history of postnatal care utilization, and history of family planning utilization. Other factors considered are the distance from health institutions, education on maternal and child health, participation in pregnant women conference, and knowing someone who has experienced OBF.

Operational Definitions

Knowledge of obstetric fistula: defined as having Information on the types, risk factors, presentations, preventive methods, treatment, complications, and psychosocial consequences of OBF [25]. Good knowledge: Participants who answered knowledge‐related questions about OBF were assigned 1 if they correctly responded otherwise 0 and then those who scored equal to or above the overall mean value of 13 [22]. Poor knowledge: Participants who scored below the overall mean of the questions related to OBF [17, 20, 21, 22].

Data Collection Tool and Techniques

The structured English questionnaire was prepared by thoroughly reviewing a variety of relevant studies [22, 24, 25, 27, 28, 29, 30], adapted to fit the local context, and then translated into Afan Oromo and back into English by a language expert to ensure accuracy. Data collection was conducted through interviews using a structured Afan Oromo version of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into four sections: sociodemographic information, reproductive health and obstetrics data, additional information, and questions regarding knowledge of obstetric fistulas. To assess OBF knowledge, responses to 35 questions were evaluated, covering topics such as types, risk factors, symptoms, prevention strategies, treatments, complications, and psychosocial effects. Data were collected by nine health extension workers under the supervision of three BSc midwives.

Data Quality Control and Management

Before commencing actual data collection, a training session lasting 1 day was conducted for both the data collectors and supervisors. A pre‐test of the questionnaires was conducted 1 week before the actual survey and in the Didessa district on 5% [31] of the subjects. Necessary modifications were made to the use of words and some ambiguities were corrected. From the pre‐test, the reliability of the data test was performed for knowledge measuring tools of 35 items which is Cronbach's alpha of 0.84. Close daily supervision was provided throughout the data collection period.

Data Processing and Analysis

The data were initially coded and entered using Epi‐data version 3.1, then exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, and means were used to summarize the data. To identify factors associated with obstetric fistula knowledge, binary logistic regression analysis was initially employed, following the standard procedures outlined by Hosmer et al. [32]. On binary logistic regression, variables with p < 0.25 were selected and included in a multivariable logistic regression to identify associated factors. The level of statistical significance was assessed using an adjusted odds ratio, with a 95% CI calculated, and a p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance [32]. All reported p values were two‐sided. No subgroup or exploratory analyses were performed in this study. The adequacy of the final model's fit was determined using the Hosmer‐Lemeshow test, with p = 0.68. The assessment of collinearity diagnostics was conducted using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics. The analysis revealed that all variables had VIF values ranges between 1.06 and 1.43, and the tolerance values were more than 0.7, which indicates that there was no multicollinearity among the independent variables in the final model [31, 32]. Finally, the findings are displayed in tables, graphs, and text.

Ethics Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at Ambo University (Ref. No: PGC/165/2021). Subsequently, a letter of cooperation was written by the zonal health department for each kebele administration. Written informed consent was obtained from respondents aged 18 years and above. In the case of those aged below 18 years, written informed consent was obtained from their parents, and agreement to participate or assent was also obtained from the participants before the interview was conducted. All the collected information was kept confidential.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

This study involved 601 women of reproductive age, with a 98% response rate. The mean age of participants was 27.42 years, with 6.54 years of standard deviation. Nearly one‐third of the respondents, specifically 182 (30.3%), were aged between 25 and 29 years. Of the total participants, 258 (42.9%) were Muslims, while 506 (84.2%) were Oromo by ethnicity. The majority 434 (72.2%) were rural residents, and 425 (70.7%) were married. Nearly one‐third of the participants, 195 (32.4%) had no formal education, and 230 (38.3%) were farmers (Table 1).

Reproductive Health and Obstetrics Related Characteristics

Of the respondents, 276 (58.7%) were under 18 years of age at the time of their first marriage. Three hundred forty‐three (57.1%) had heard about obstetric complications and 441 (73.4%) had a history of pregnancy. The majority of those with a history of pregnancy 370 (83.9%) had received Antenatal Care (ANC) follow‐up during their previous pregnancy. Nearly three‐quarters of the participants 305 (71.6%) had given birth in a health facility, with the majority 359 (84.3%) delivered through spontaneous vaginal delivery. However, only one‐fifth of the 91 patients (21.1%) had used postnatal care.

Knowledge of Obstetric Fistula

Half of the respondents 305 (50.7%) had heard of OBF. Health professionals were the primary source of information for over half of the respondents 167 (54.8%). A total of 239 (39.8%) women knew at least one or two types of OBF. Two hundred eighty‐six (47.6%) women were able to mention two or more OBF symptoms. Concerning risk factors for OBF, 273 (45.4%) participants were able to list 4 or more risk factors for OBF, and 255 (42.4%) of the women knew two or three about OBF treatment. Nearly half of participants (46.4%) of women knew at least three or more prevention methods for OBF and 257 (42.8%) of the study participants were mentioned two or more OBF complications. Regarding the psychosocial consequences of OBF, 269 (44.8%) participants were able to list at least three or more psychosocial consequences of OBF (Table 3).

Factors Associated With Knowledge of Obstetric Fistula

In the bivariate analysis, variables such as the age of respondents, educational status, occupational status, media in the household, residency, age at first pregnancy, ANC follow‐up during the previous pregnancy, place for their recent childbirth, PNC utilization, history of induced abortion, history of modern family planning use, participation in PWC, and having health education on OBF were factors associated with having overall good knowledge of OBF at a p < 0.25.

Residency, educational status, media in the household, history of PNC utilization, participation in PWC, and health education on OBF were factors associated with having overall good knowledge of OBF at a p < 0.05 on the multivariable analysis.

From the multivariable model, individuals residing in urban areas were 4.23 times more likely to have good knowledge of OBF than those from rural areas (AOR = 4.23, 95% CI: 2.09, 8.57). Respondents who attended primary education were 4.43 times more likely to have good knowledge of OBF than those who could not read and write (AOR = 4.43, 95% CI: 2.25, 8.69). In addition, women who had attained at least a secondary education were 4.08 times more likely to have good knowledge about OBF than those who did not attend formal education (AOR = 4.08, 95% CI: 1.31, 12.80).

The study also showed a significant association between having media in the household and knowledge of obstetric fistulas. Women who had access to media in their homes had 3.65 times more odds of having good knowledge of OBF than those without media access (AOR = 3.65, 95% CI: 1.84, 7.25).

The odds of having good knowledge were 2.65 times higher among women who had previously used PNC were as compared to those who didn't (AOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.22, 5.74). Additionally, women who participated in PWC had 2.24 times higher odds of having good knowledge of OBF than those who did not (AOR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.19, 4.21). Women who received education on OBF were 3.64 times more likely to possess good knowledge of OBF than those who did not receive (AOR = 3.64, 95% CI: 1.95, 6.79) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the magnitude of OBF knowledge and its associated factors among women aged 15–49 years in the Buno Bedele Zone, Ethiopia. This study revealed that only approximately 48.3% (95% CI = [43.9, 52.2%]) of reproductive‐age women possessed good knowledge of OBF. This was lower than that reported in previous studies conducted in Ghana (62.8%, Nigeria 57.8% [14, 33] and Nigeria (70.7%) [30]. The observed differences may be attributed to the methodological context of the studies used. These studies were conducted at the facility level, where the participants had access to health‐related information. In contrast, the present study was conducted within a community, where access to information was more variable. In Northwestern Nigeria, among those affected by vesicovaginal fistulas in a fistula repair facility, this could be due to having received more information about OBF from health workers. Furthermore, since both studies were conducted on pregnant women attending routine antenatal care (ANC), the participants were likely to have greater access to maternal health information from healthcare professionals, particularly regarding OBF.

Another reason for the possible disparity could be socio‐demographic differences. In the case of Ghana, 65% of the population lives in urban settings [33], where people generally have a better understanding of health‐related matters than those in rural regions, although this study focused mainly on rural areas.

This figure was also lower than that reported in the Bench Sheko zone of Ethiopia's SNNPR (69.7%) [22]. The difference could be due to a community‐level intervention on OBF in the case of the Bench study area, which may have improved and increased the community's knowledge status of OBF, whereas there was no strong community‐level intervention on obstetric fistula in this study. Thus, the community's knowledge status of OBF may be lower than in the previous study.

This result was higher than the findings from Nigeria's Kebbi state (2.8%) and Ethiopia's Amhara Regional State Delanta district (40.5%) [25, 34]. This disparity might be due to the study period, as these studies were conducted before 5 years. Another explanation for the difference could be the study population. In the Delanta study, the focus was on young women aged 15–19 years, which might have contributed to a lower level of knowledge. The level of awareness of obstetric fistula increases with age [29], but the current study was among women aged 15–49 years of age, and most of the participants were over the age of 20, which might have contributed to a higher knowledge of OBF.

The current study identified different factors that were found to be significantly associated with knowledge of OBF. Women residing in urban areas tended to have better knowledge of OBF than those living in rural areas. This is similar to the results of studies conducted in Burkina Faso [35] and Northern Nigeria [29]. This could be due to the different levels of access to information about OBF among urban and rural women. Women from urban areas have greater access to various media, are more readily able to access healthcare services, and find it easier to pursue education. These factors contribute to heightened awareness and knowledge as well as shifts in attitudes, social norms, and behaviors, ultimately leading to positive public health outcomes.

The study indicated that respondents with a primary education and above were more likely to possess knowledge of OBF than those who had no formal education. It was also found that women who have attended secondary school and above have a significant association with knowledge of OBF. This is in agreement with previous studies in the Bench Sheko zone, SNNPR, Ethiopia and Delanta district, Amhara Regional State of Ethiopia [22, 25]. One possible reason for this is that individuals with formal education are more likely to access information, inquire about health‐related issues, and obtain health services than those who have not pursued formal education. One possible explanation is that, as people attain higher levels of education, their knowledge becomes more refined; education fosters insight, encourages explanation, and enhances reasoning about various events and circumstances. In addition, educated reproductive‐aged women might have access to and the ability to read and understand written resources.

Media ownership in their households was also found to increase women's overall knowledge of OBF. The likelihood of having good knowledge regarding OBF was 3.65 times greater among study participants with access to media at home than among those without such access. This is similar to the results of a study conducted in Nigeria [29]. This might be due to the fact that those study participants who had media in their house would have a higher opportunity of getting health‐related information, particularly about OBF. This finding implied the provision of tailored information for the public on obstetric fistula prevention and treatment through different media channels.

According to this study, women with a history of postnatal care were 2.65 times more likely to possess knowledge of OBF than those with a history of postnatal care. This could be because the study participants who had previously received postnatal care were more likely to learn about childbirth complications.

This study revealed that pregnant women's conference attendance was associated with higher knowledge of OBF. These findings are corroborated by a study conducted in the Bench Sheko zone, Ethiopia [23], which determined that individuals who had previously participated in PWC were more likely to receive information on safe motherhood topics. These topics include safe and healthy pregnancy and childbirth, complications such as obstetric fistula (OBF), and the advantages of institutional delivery, all of which are provided by health personnel through health education and counseling. This underscores the necessity for comprehensive educational programs during pregnancy conferences to prevent adverse maternal outcomes associated with pregnancy and childbirth.

This study revealed that receiving health education on OBF was a statistically significant predictor of OBF knowledge. Participants who attended OBF education were more likely to exhibit better knowledge than those who did not. This can be explained by the role of education in enhancing comprehension, suggesting that individuals who attend education on obstetric fistulas possess more knowledge about it.

Limitations

This study shares the limitation of a cross‐sectional design, which makes it difficult to establish causal relationships between the variables. Given that the present study relied on self‐reported data and recall of past events, there is a potential for recall and response bias in responses to questions requiring participants to remember previous events. Moreover, using the mean value as a cut‐off point for determining knowledge level is sample‐dependent and might vary across different populations, or studies are acknowledged as a limitation.

Conclusions

The study found that less than half of the women possessed overall good knowledge of obstetric fistulas in the study area. Residence, higher educational status, access to media in the household, history of postnatal care, participation in pregnancy conferences, and health education on OBF were factors associated with knowledge of obstetric fistula.

Therefore, Health managers at the Regional, Zonal, and district levels, and health professionals should consider these factors to strengthen the interventions geared towards addressing the knowledge gaps through the provision of health education on OBF at each maternal service delivery point, at pregnant women conferences, and through the use of mass media. Furthermore, researchers were recommended to test the effects of different interventions, such as integrating PNC and PWC, to improve the knowledge of OBF among women of reproductive age.
