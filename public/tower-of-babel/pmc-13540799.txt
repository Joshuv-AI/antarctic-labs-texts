Intracranial meningiomas mimicking gliomas: A systematic review of clinical, radiological, and histopathological characteristics
Authors: Ali K. Al-Shalchy, Nooruldeen H. Ali Al-Khafaji, Rania H. Al-Taie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Intracranial meningiomas usually appear as extra-axial tumors with distinctive radiological features that aid in diagnosis. However, a rare subset shows atypical imaging traits and can closely resemble gliomas, leading to diagnostic challenges and impacting surgical decisions and treatment. This review aimed to systematically analyze the literature on intracranial meningiomas that present like gliomas and to summarize their clinical, radiological, pathological, and outcome features.Methods: A systematic review following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines was performed. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar from inception to June 2026. The review included studies on intracranial meningiomas that were suspected to be gliomas based on radiological, clinical, or intraoperative assessments. Data on patient demographics, clinical presentation, imaging features, pathology results, treatments, and outcomes were collected and summarized descriptively.Results: A total of 17 studies involving 28 patients met the inclusion criteria. The median age was 47 years, with a range from 4 to 74 years, and 64.3% of the patients were male. Preoperative suspicion of glioma was noted in 85.7% of cases, with 42.9% specifically suspected of being high-grade gliomas. Most lesions appeared intra-axial (75.0%), were located intraparenchymally (96.4%), lacked dural attachment (89.3%), and showed peritumoral edema (67.9%). Cystic changes occurred in 46.4% of patients. Histopathology mainly identified World Health Organization Grade I meningiomas (78.6%), despite imaging suggesting a more aggressive nature. Gross total resection was achieved in 78.6% of patients and was generally associated with favorable outcomes. Recurrence and death were each reported in one patient (3.6%).Conclusion: Intracranial meningiomas that resemble gliomas are uncommon lesions often showing atypical radiological features such as an intra-axial appearance, lack of dural attachment, significant peritumoral edema, and cystic changes. Although they are frequently misdiagnosed before surgery, most of these lesions are low-grade meningiomas, which tend to have good postoperative results. Being aware of these unusual presentations can help improve diagnosis and support better surgical planning.

INTRODUCTION

Meningiomas are the most common primary tumors inside the skull in adults, accounting for about 40% of all primary central nervous system neoplasms and over half of nonmalignant brain tumors.[19,22] They usually arise from arachnoid cap cells and present as extra-axial lesions with distinctive radiologic features, such as a broad dural attachment and a dural tail.[30] However, a rare subset shows atypical growth patterns, growing within the brain tissue or lacking a clear dural attachment. These lesions often exhibit imaging features typical of glial tumors, including extensive surrounding edema, cystic change, uneven enhancement, necrosis, bleeding, and apparent invasion of the brain tissue. As a result, they are often misdiagnosed before surgery as gliomas, especially high-grade gliomas and glioblastomas.[10,29]

Accurately distinguishing meningiomas from gliomas is crucial because the surgical approach, prognosis, and need for additional therapies vary significantly between these tumors. However, intracranial meningiomas that resemble gliomas are not well understood, as existing data mostly consist of isolated case reports and small series. Consequently, their demographic data, imaging characteristics, pathology, treatment options, and outcomes have not been systematically reviewed. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic literature review to identify intracranial meningiomas that present like gliomas and to provide a detailed overview of their clinical features, radiological patterns, histology, management, and results.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic review was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.[21] Searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar from their inception through June 2026. The search combined keywords related to meningioma and glioma mimicry, such as “meningioma,” “intraparenchymal meningioma,” “intra-axial meningioma,” “subcortical meningioma,” “glioma,” “high-grade glioma,” “glioblastoma,” “astrocytoma,” “mimic,” “misdiagnosis,” and “differential diagnosis.” Reference lists of selected studies were manually reviewed to find additional relevant reports. Duplicate records were eliminated before the screening process.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text reviews of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Studies were included if they reported intracranial meningiomas suspected to be gliomas based on radiological, clinical, or intraoperative findings. Case reports and case series were eligible. Exclusions included studies describing conventional dural-based meningiomas without glioma-like features, spinal meningiomas, review articles, conference abstracts, editorials, and nonEnglish articles lacking sufficient clinical data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized spreadsheet. The variables collected encompassed study details, patient demographics, clinical presentation, tumor location, radiological findings, preoperative diagnosis, operative observations, extent of resection, histopathology, adjuvant therapy, recurrence, and clinical outcomes. Special focus was given to radiological features indicative of glioma mimicry, such as intra-axial appearance, lack of dural attachment, absence of a dural tail sign, peritumoral edema, cystic alterations, necrosis, hemorrhage, and specific enhancement patterns.

Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series, as appropriate.[11,20] Two reviewers independently conducted the evaluations, resolving disagreements by consensus. All included case reports exhibited high methodological quality, with most addressing all checklist domains. The case series by Im et al., Kim et al., and Li et al. were also deemed high quality, meeting most JBI criteria. Overall, the risk of bias across the studies was regarded as low [Tables 1 and 2].[1-4,7,9,12-17,23,24,26,28,29]

Statistical analysis

Given that intracranial meningiomas rarely mimic gliomas and most available data come from case reports and small series, a descriptive analysis was conducted. Continuous variables were summarized with medians and ranges, whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and crude proportions. Crude estimates were derived by dividing the number of patients with a specific feature by the total patient count. No comparative or meta-analytic pooling was undertaken due to the limited sample size and the significant clinical heterogeneity across the included studies.

RESULTS

Study selection and demographics

The literature search identified 1293 records, of which 1119 underwent title and abstract screening after duplicate removal. Following full-text assessment of 25 studies, 17 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis, comprising a total of 28 patients [Figure 1]. The median age at presentation was 47 years (range, 4–74 years), with a male predominance observed in 18 patients (64.3%), whereas 10 patients (35.7%) were female [Table 3 and Supplementary 1].

Clinical presentation and radiological characteristics

The most common presenting symptoms were focal neurological deficits (42.9%), headache (39.3%), and seizures (25.0%). Visual symptoms were reported in 10.7% of patients, whereas 7.1% of lesions were discovered incidentally. Radiologically, the majority of lesions demonstrated an intra-axial appearance (75.0%) and were located within the brain parenchyma (96.4%). Subcortical involvement was observed in 46.4% of cases. Classical meningioma-associated imaging features were frequently absent, with lack of dural attachment and absence of a dural tail sign reported in 89.3% and 75.0% of patients, respectively [Table Supplementary 2].

Peritumoral edema was present in 67.9% of cases and represented one of the most frequent imaging findings contributing to diagnostic uncertainty. Additional radiological features included mass effect (75.0%), cystic components (46.4%), calcification (39.3%), midline shift (35.7%), intratumoral cysts (28.6%), necrosis (28.6%), hemorrhage (10.7%), and diffusion restriction (3.6%). Heterogeneous enhancement was observed in 53.6% of patients, while ring enhancement was identified in 14.3%.

Glioma mimicry and surgical management

Preoperative diagnostic confusion was common, with 24 patients (85.7%) initially suspected of harboring a glioma. High-grade glioma was specifically considered in 12 patients (42.9%), whereas glioblastoma and astrocytoma were suspected in 10.7% and 3.6% of cases, respectively. These findings highlight the substantial overlap between the radiological appearance of atypical meningiomas and primary glial neoplasms.

Surgical resection was the primary treatment modality. Gross total resection was achieved in 22 patients (78.6%), whereas subtotal resection was reported in 3 patients (10.7%). Intraoperative evidence of brain invasion was documented in 39.3% of cases, whereas hypervascularity and intraoperative hemorrhage were observed in 14.3% and 7.1%, respectively [Table 4 and Supplementary 3].

Histopathological findings and outcomes

Histopathological examination revealed that most tumors were World Health Organization (WHO) Grade I meningiomas (78.6%), whereas Grade II and Grade III lesions each accounted for 10.7% of cases. Histological subtypes were heterogeneous and included sclerosing (28.6%), microcystic (21.4%), angiomatous (17.9%), chordoid (7.1%), transitional (7.1%), and fibrous (3.6%) variants. Rhabdoid features were present in 10.7% of tumors, and meningioangiomatosis-associated meningioma was identified in one patient (3.6%). Histological brain invasion was confirmed in 21.4% of cases.

Clinical outcomes were generally favorable. Neurological improvement following treatment was reported in 53.6% of patients. Tumor recurrence occurred in one patient (3.6%), and one death (3.6%) was reported during follow-up. Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered in 10.7% of cases, whereas no patients received adjuvant chemotherapy [Table Supplementary 4].

DISCUSSION

This systematic review consolidates current findings on intracranial meningiomas that resemble glioma-like lesions. Although meningiomas are generally recognized as extra-axial tumors with characteristic radiological features, the lesions discussed here often exhibited atypical imaging traits that closely resembled primary glial tumors. Consequently, preoperative diagnosis was often challenging, with 85.7% of patients initially suspected of having a glioma and 42.9% specifically believed to have a high-grade glioma. Similar diagnostic difficulties have been observed in cases involving intraparenchymal, cystic, and nondural-based meningiomas, where both radiological and intraoperative observations initially indicated glioma, later confirmed by histopathology.[5,6,10,13]

Our cohort’s radiological features clarify why this misdiagnosis occurs frequently. Most tumors were intra-axial, did not attach to the dura, and showed significant peritumoral edema. Nearly half had cystic changes, and it was common to see heterogeneous enhancement and a mass effect. These imaging traits closely resemble those of diffuse gliomas and glioblastomas, especially when typical meningioma signs such as a dural tail or broad dural base, are missing.[6,10,29] Earlier studies propose that intraparenchymal meningiomas may originate from ectopic arachnoid cap cells in the pia mater or Virchow–Robin spaces, explaining their atypical location and nondural growth pattern.[6,25]

Most lesions in our review were WHO Grade I meningiomas based on histopathology, despite their aggressive appearance on imaging. We identified several uncommon histologic variants, such as microcystic, angiomatous, chordoid, rhabdoid, and sclerosing meningiomas. The microcystic and angiomatous variants are especially prone to extensive vasogenic edema and show heterogeneous imaging features, often mimicking high-grade gliomas.[5,8,15] Similarly, atypical and rhabdoid meningiomas can display radiologic signs that suggest aggressive glial tumors, even though they are distinct pathological types.[18,27] The predominance of WHO Grade I tumors in our cohort emphasizes that imaging findings do not always accurately reflect the biological behavior of these lesions.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for intracranial meningiomas that resemble gliomas. In this review, gross total resection was accomplished in 78.6% of patients, generally leading to positive outcomes. Complete removal not only confirms the diagnosis but also treats most low-grade lesions.[6,13,30] However, surgery can be challenging because these tumors often lack a dural attachment and may seem to invade nearby brain tissue. In such cases, careful microsurgical dissection is essential to remove as much of the tumor as possible while protecting neurological functions.[8,15,25]

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting this review’s findings. First, the evidence base is limited to case reports and small case series, leading to a small sample size and potential publication bias. Second, there was considerable heterogeneity among studies in areas such as radiological reporting, histopathological details, surgical procedures, follow-up periods, and outcome evaluations. Third, incomplete data reporting for individual patients hindered thorough analysis of multiple variables and the ability to identify factors linked to glioma misdiagnosis or outcomes. Finally, because intracranial meningiomas that imitate gliomas are rare, a formal meta-analysis was not possible, so this review remains a descriptive overview of existing literature.

CONCLUSION

Intracranial meningiomas that resemble gliomas present a rare but significant diagnostic challenge. These tumors often display atypical radiological features, such as an intra-axial appearance, lack of dural attachment, prominent peritumoral edema, and cystic changes, which can lead to misdiagnosis as high-grade gliomas before surgery. Although their imaging features may suggest aggressiveness, most are WHO grade I meningiomas and have favorable outcomes after surgical removal. Recognizing this unusual presentation can improve diagnostic accuracy, aid in surgical planning, and prevent inappropriate treatments. More case reports and multicenter research are necessary to better understand the radiologic spectrum and biological behavior of these uncommon lesions.
