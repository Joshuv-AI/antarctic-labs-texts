Letter to the editor: Impact of abscess type on outcomes following posterior fixation for thoracolumbar pyogenic spondylitis: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiajun Deng, Yongchun Xiao, Hongsheng Lin
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent multicenter study by Gamada et al. [1], who evaluated the influence of epidural abscess, iliopsoas abscess, and empyema on outcomes after minimally invasive posterior fixation for thoracolumbar pyogenic spondylitis. Their study is valuable because it moves beyond treating all abscesses as a single entity and demonstrates that clinically distinct abscess locations may follow different postoperative courses.

In our view, the principal strength of this work lies in identifying an abscess-specific clinical phenotype. More importantly, it creates an opportunity to move beyond the anatomical presence of an abscess and toward clarifying how abscess characteristics should influence the timing of stabilization, decompression, drainage, and antimicrobial treatment.

While Gamada et al. [1] reported shorter total intravenous antibiotic treatment in patients with epidural abscess and longer treatment in those with iliopsoas abscess, Table 4 of their study suggests that these differences were numerically driven mainly by the preoperative period. Patients with epidural abscess received 1.2 fewer preoperative weeks but only 0.3 fewer postoperative weeks, whereas those with iliopsoas abscess received 1.2 more preoperative weeks but only 0.2 more postoperative weeks. Clinically, this pattern may reflect differences in the timing of surgical escalation rather than a direct effect of abscess location on the response to fixation. Epidural disease accompanied by neurological concern may prompt earlier surgery, whereas iliopsoas abscesses may undergo longer medical treatment before fixation. Because surgical timing itself may influence outcomes in severe pyogenic spinal infection, it should be considered an important treatment variable [2].

From this perspective, future analyses may be more informative if surgery is used as time zero and postoperative response is evaluated separately. Relevant outcomes would include postoperative intravenous duration, total antimicrobial duration including oral therapy, time to C-reactive protein response, persistent or recurrent bacteremia, infection-related reoperation, and readmission. Recent guidelines support directly administered oral therapy or an early intravenous-to-oral switch in appropriately selected patients, making intravenous duration alone an incomplete measure of antimicrobial burden [3].

We also believe that the greatest translational value of this study will come from integrating abscess location with features that determine source-control decisions. For epidural abscesses, neurological progression and canal compromise are central. For iliopsoas abscesses, gas formation, skeletal origin, systemic severity, and suitability for percutaneous drainage may be more clinically relevant than maximum diameter alone [4]. Empyema should prompt early multidisciplinary assessment and coordinated pleural source control, because contemporary guidance emphasizes timely drainage and escalation according to clinical response [5]. Stratification according to the source-control procedure performed at the index operation would further distinguish the effect of posterior fixation from that of simultaneous decompression or drainage.

Overall, Gamada et al. [1] should be congratulated for providing a clinically meaningful foundation. Their findings may represent not the endpoint of abscess classification, but the basis for a treatment-oriented framework linking abscess phenotype, surgical timing, and source-control strategy.
