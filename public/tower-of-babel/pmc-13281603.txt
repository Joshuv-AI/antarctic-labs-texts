Successful multidisciplinary surgical management of a giant mucinous cystadenoma: a case report
Authors: Abdirizak Ali Abdi, Saido Mohamud Hassan, Nasro Hassan Gargaar, Mohamed Abdirahman Omar, Abdirizak Sharif Ali, Mohamed Mohamud Ali, Ali Haji Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiant ovarian cysts are becoming less prevalent as a result of breakthroughs in imaging, routine gynecological surveillance, and early intervention in wealthy countries. However, in resource-constrained nations, delayed access to treatment, a lack of regular prenatal and gynecological follow-up, and socioeconomic constraints all contribute to late presentation and management difficulties.Case presentationWe discuss the case of a 35-year-old Somali multiparous woman (G9P7) with a five-year history of increasing abdominal distension caused by a large mucinous cystadenoma. Abdominal ultrasound, contrast-enhanced CT scans, and tumor marker assessments all contributed to the diagnosis. She was assessed at our tertiary care center after a delay in seeking to a surgical care. A massive uniloculated cystic mass was found and entirely removed with unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. A multidisciplinary team performed definitive surgical care, which was finished successfully without major complications, and the patient made a good postoperative recovery. Histopathological investigation confirmed the diagnosis of mucinous cystadenoma, with no signs of malignancy.ConclusionThis case demonstrates the significance of earlier detection and interdisciplinary surgical care in achieving favorable outcomes for large ovarian tumors in resource-limited settings. The effective treatment of a long-standing huge mucinous cystadenoma in a young Somali woman demonstrates the importance of imaging, tumor markers, and coordinated surgical expertise in lowering morbidity and improving prognosis, Raising physician awareness and enhancing access to diagnostic and surgical procedures are critical to avoiding delayed presentations and related consequences.

Background

Giant ovarian cysts (GOCs) are uncommon tumors of the ovary presenting with diameters greater than 10 cm [1]. These are less frequently seen in high-income countries and consequently are rarely described in the literature, owing to availability of resources and advanced imaging technologies leading to early diagnosis of small or medium-sized tumors [2]. These tumors are generally asymptomatic at early stages, causing symptoms only after reaching enormous dimensions, and consequently are diagnosed late in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. Cystadenomas are neoplasms lined by mucin-producing epithelial cells, and they are mostly benign; however, close follow-up is necessary as they are recognized as precursors of ovarian cancer and may slowly transform to borderline tumors and invasive ovarian cancer [3]. The majority of ovarian masses encountered in the premenarchal or child- hood stages are non-neoplastic lesions such as benign functional cysts. The clinical signs and symptoms of ovarian masses are usually nonspecific; therefore, making the correct diagnosis preoperatively is occasionally a challenge, and early management may be necessary to save the patient's life and preserve fertility [4]. Epithelial tumors account for 8%-10% of all ovarian tumors and are histologically classified as mucinous or serous. The most common benign epithelial ovarian tumor is cystadenoma, of which 75% are serous and 25% are mucinous cystadenoma (MCA) [4]. Here are four major categories of ovarian tumors:1. Epithelial tumors (65–75%)—serous or mucinous cystadenoma/carcinoma, clear cell carcinoma, and Brenner tumor.2. We report the case of a 35-year-old Somali multiparous lady (G9P7) who had been experiencing growing abdominal distension for five years due to a huge mucinous cystadenoma. She had definitive surgical treatment via exploratory laparotomy with unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, which was completed successfully and without severe problems. The patient had a good postoperative recovery. We intend to highlight the diagnostic and management issues associated with big ovarian cysts, as well as the need of immediate surgical intervention in resource-limited settings.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old Somali multiparous woman (G9P7) with uncontrolled chronic epilepsy and residual poliomyelitis paralysis in the left hand and leg reported to our tertiary care center with a five-year history of growing abdominal distension. The swelling rapidly grew in size, causing stomach discomfort, early satiety, limited mobility, and cosmetic concerns. She had no history of gynecologic cancer or abdominal surgery. Limited access to health services was cited as the reason for the delay in seeking help.

Perioperative planning included the continuation of antiepileptic medications, neurological evaluation, and anesthetic precautions to reduce seizure risk. In addition, the patient's remaining poliomyelitis-associated paralysis necessitated precise intraoperative placement and postoperative physiotherapy. A multidisciplinary team of gynecologists, surgeons, anesthesiologists, medical laboratory scientist, neurologists, and rehabilitation specialists participated.

A midline laparotomy revealed a massive uniloculated ovarian cystic tumor originating from the left ovary. The cyst was excised entire, and a left salpingo-oophorectomy was carried out. The estimated blood loss was low, and there were no intraoperative problems see (Fig. 4 A, B, C). The cyst was totally removed, whole and without rupture. Following surgery, the patient was monitored in a high-dependency facility. She had a smooth recovery, accepted oral feeding by the second postoperative day, and gradually mobilized with physiotherapy assistance due to pre-existing paralysis. She was discharged on the fifth day after continuing her antiepileptic medication.

The patient had no symptoms of infection or dehiscence at the two-week follow-up, and the wounds had healed well. She was able to tolerate a regular diet and noted that her stomach ache had subsided. With continued physical treatment, her mobility had improved and there had been no recurrent seizures. The patient's motor impairments caused by poliomyelitis did not worsen. Mucinous cystadenoma is one of the common tumors that account for about 15–20% of all ovarian neoplasms. About 80% of mucinous tumors are benign and occur mostly in women of reproductive age [6]. Risk factors for ovarian cysts include; pregnancy, hypothyroidism, smoking, infertility treatment, and so on [6]. The size and laterality play a major role in the determination of tumor origin whether primary or metastatic. In contrast with metastatic tumors, the primary tumors tend to be larger and unilateral [7]. Adnexal masses can be difficult to evaluate and manage because of the large differential diagnosis and variable urgency associated with differing etiologies. In the emergency department, a relevant history should also include risk factors for cancer. A family history of ovarian or breast cancer is a strong risk factor for a patient developing ovarian cancer with an increase from 1.6% to about 5% with one relative [8]. A full physical examination is also warranted in any patient with abdominal pain or concern for malignancy. While the pelvic examination has not been shown to identify adnexal masses well, it is still recommended to assess for other etiologies [8]. It is important to consider emergent diagnoses, such as ectopic pregnancy, tubo-ovarian abscess, and adnexal mass with associated ovarian torsion [8].

Giant ovarian cysts are rare in modern clinical practice. However, patients may emerge with unusually big masses in low-resource areas where access to prompt medical care is limited. One such instance is a 35-year-old Somali lady who had a 30-cm uniloculated mucinous cystadenoma. The benign epithelial ovarian tumors known as mucinous cystadenomas usually appear in women in their third to fifth decades of life. Although uniloculated variations, like the one our patient had, have also been reported, they are frequently multilocular. Due to persistent abdominal distension, these tumors can resemble ascites or pregnancy and can grow to huge sizes, perhaps surpassing 20–30 cm. Lack of prenatal or routine gynecological care, socioeconomic constraints, and limited health-seeking behavior are frequently linked to delayed presentation.

Complete surgical enucleation of the cyst is the gold standard approach for the management of a giant chylous cyst. Conservative surgical intervention, cystectomy with salpingo-oophorectomy, is adequate for young patients with benign ovarian lesions [9].

Our situation demonstrates the persistent difficulties in settings with inadequate resources, where individuals frequently put off seeking medical care until serious problems or symptoms arise. In order to enable earlier diagnosis and intervention, this highlights the necessity of community-based awareness, enhanced imaging accessibility and reinforced referral systems.

Our case study adds to the expanding body of research on big ovarian tumors by showing that careful preoperative planning, a multidisciplinary approach, and precise surgical technique can provide positive results even in low-resource settings.

Conclusion

Even though they are uncommon, giant benign mucinous cystadenomas continue to present a considerable clinical and surgical challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where treatment and diagnosis delays are frequent. To improve patient outcomes, routine transvaginal ultrasound use is crucial for early detection. Reducing the burden of advanced disease and minimizing delays can be achieved by increasing community knowledge and enlisting primary health professionals in the identification and follow-up of women who arrive with abnormal abdominal swellings. To successfully manage such large tumors, a combined surgical approach is still essential.
