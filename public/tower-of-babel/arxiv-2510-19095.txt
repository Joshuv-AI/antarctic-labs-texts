Recursive decoding of binary rank Reed–Muller codes and Plotkin construction for matrix codes Thanks: Inria & Laboratoire LIX, École Polytechnique, Institut Polytechnique de Paris, 91120 Palaiseau CEDEX, France
Alain Couvreur and Rakhi Pratihar

Abstract
. In 2021, Augot, Couvreur, Lavauzelle and Neri introduced a new class of rank metric codes which can be regarded as rank metric counterparts of Reed–Muller codes. Given a finite Galois extension 𝕃 / 𝕂 \mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K} , these codes are defined as some specific 𝕃 \mathbb{L} –subspaces of the twisted group algebra 𝕃 ⁡ [ G ] \mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}] . We investigate the decoding of such codes in the “binary” case, i.e., when G = ( ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ ) m \textrm{G}=(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m} . Our approach takes its inspiration from the decoding of Hamming metric binary Reed–Muller codes using their recursive Plotkin “ ( u | u + v ) (u~|~u+v) ” structure. If our recursive algorithm restricts to a specific subclass of rank metric Reed–Muller codes, its asymptotic complexity beats that of the recently proposed decoding algorithm for arbitrary rank metric Reed–Muller codes based on Dickson matrices. Also, this decoder is of completely different nature and leads a natural rank metric counterpart of the Plotkin construction. To illustrate this, we also propose a generic Plotkin-like construction for matrix rank metric codes with an associate decoder, which can be applied to any pair of codes equipped with an efficient decoder.



Keywords
: Binary rank metric Reed-Muller codes, decoding, matrix codes, Plotkin construction

Introduction

Rank metric codes
were introduced by Delsarte
[
16
]
with a
combinatorial interest as sets of bilinear forms on a pair of
finite-dimensional vector spaces over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, whereas
Gabidulin independently reintroduced a variant of rank metric codes

[
18
]
as
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
and proposed a first
decoding algorithm. Due to a broad diversity of applications of
applications of these codes, such as crisscross error correction

[
46
]
, network coding
[
28
]
, space-time coding

[
33
,
44
,
45
]
and public-key cryptography
[
19
]
, the
theoretical interest to study rank metric codes is constantly rising
leading to new mathematical and algorithmic challenges. In the finite
field setting, the very first class of efficiently decodable rank
metric codes is given by Gabidulin
[
18
]
using
q
-polynomials of
bounded degree. This construction is actually included in a more general
framework introduced in
[
8
]
as subspaces of the skew group
algebra
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
for an arbitrary finite Galois extension
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}

with Galois group
\textrm{G}=\text{Gal}(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K})
. This point of view has been
particularly useful in defining a rank analogue of Reed–Muller codes,
also called
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-Reed–-Muller codes
in
[
8
]
, where

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m})
specifies a generating set of
the abelian group
G
(See §
2.5
for a discussion on
Reed–Muller-type codes in different metrics).

Towards various applications, for instance post–quantum cryptography,
it is particularly important for a family of codes to have efficient
decoding algorithms. Indeed, on one hand, McEliece-like schemes

[
39
]
require codes with an efficient decoder and whose structure
can be hidden to the attackers. Such a scheme has been instantiated
with codes both in Hamming (see for instance

[
39
,
40
,
4
]
) and in rank metric (see for
instance
[
19
,
21
,
5
,
32
]
). On the other hand, many
Alekhnovich-like schemes in code or lattice based cryptography require
a decoder to conclude the decryption phase and get rid from a residual
noise term. See for instance
[
2
,
3
]
. For these
reasons, there is a strong motivation in broadening the diversity of
decodable codes in rank metric: first to improve our understanding of
decoding problems and second in view toward applications to future new
cryptographic designs. However, the known classes of rank metric codes
with efficient decoding algorithms are only the following few; simple
codes
[
48
,
20
]
, some families of MRD codes including
Gabidulin codes
[
31
]
and its variants, cf.
[
11
, Chapter
2]
, low-rank parity-check (LRPC) codes
[
6
]
and the
interleaved version of the aforementioned codes
[
47
,
43
]
. In

[
8
]
, a construction of rank metric analogues of Reed–Muller
codes is proposed and, for decoding, the authors suggested the use of the

rank error–correcting pairs
paradigm
[
38
]
. This led to a
polynomial–time decoder that could correct an amount of errors that remained
far below half the minimum distance.
In
[
13
]
, the authors of the present article give a
deterministic decoding algorithm for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-Reed–Muller codes that
involved so–called
G
-Dickson matrices
and which corrects
any error patter of rank up to half the minimum rank distance. In the present
article, we investigate a recursive structure of these codes in the
“binary–like” case,
i.e.
, when
\textrm{G}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/{2\mathbb{Z}})^{m}
and
propose a new decoding technique resting on this recursive structure.

It is worth noting that the structure we identified can be considered
as a rank metric analogue to the recursive structure that the Hamming
metric binary Reed–Muller codes possess. More precisely, in the
Hamming setting, any codeword of
\text{RM}_{2}(r,m)
can be written as

(u~|~u+v)
where
u\in\text{RM}_{2}(r,m-1)
and
v\in\text{RM}_{2}(r-1,m-1)
. The general
(u~|~u+v)
construction was introduced
by Plotkin
[
35
]
and known as
Plotkin construction
for
linear codes with Hamming metric. This recursive construction and its
use for decoding have been studied extensively. Besides binary
Reed–Muller codes and their decoding, the Plotkin construction has
striking applications since it naturally appears in the construction
of polar codes or in the post–quantum signature
Wave

[
10
,
15
]
. One can also mention that this
construction iteratively applied on Reed–Solomon codes permits to
achieve the capacity of the discrete symmetric channel as proved in

[
37
]
.

Our contributions

In the present article, we demonstrate a recursive structure of
binary–like rank metric Reed–Muller codes and give a decoding
algorithm that can correct up to half minimum distance. This algorithm
may fail on some unlikely instances but has a better asymptotic
complexity than the Dickson-based decoding of
[
13
]
. In short,
for
N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|\textrm{G}|
, the aforementioned Dickson-based approach
corrects
t
errors
\mathcal{O}(tN^{2})
operations in
\mathbb{L}
while the decoder
proposed in this article, requires
\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-1}N)
operations in

\mathbb{L}
, where
\omega
is the complexity exponent of linear algebra.

Next, inspired by the structure of binary–like rank metric
Reed–Muller codes, we adapt the recursive structure to propose
Plotkin-like construction for matrix rank metric codes over any field.
As a consequence, we provide new efficiently decodable matrix rank
metric codes over finite fields which are not equivalent to Gabidulin
codes. This new framework provides new families of rank metric codes
equipped with a decoder. To our knowledge, this is the first
successful attempt of providing a rank metric counterpart of the
Plotkin construction.

Organization of the article

Section
2

introduces the notation used in this paper, as well as basic
notions regarding rank metric codes as subspaces of skew group
algebras including binary rank metric Reed–Muller codes. In Section

3
, we discuss a recursive structure of binary rank
metric Reed–Muller codes. A decoding algorithm for these codes
resting on the recursive structured is presented in Section

4
. We show that the recursive decoding approach permits
to correct error patterns of rank up to half the minimum distance,
whenever some folding of the error matrix preserves the rank. The
complexity of this novel decoder is studied in depth in
Section
5
. Finally, Section
6
presents
a rank metric analogue of Plotkin construction by adapting the
recursive structure of binary rank metric Reed–Muller codes for
matrix rank metric codes over finite fields. We demonstrate how the
recursive decoding algorithm can be adapted to efficiently decode a
Plotkin construction and provides new efficiently decodable
matrix rank metric codes inequivalent to Gabidulin codes.

.
Finally, when handling complexities we will use Landau notation for comparison.
Namely, for

going to infinity we denote

\displaystyle f(m)=\mathcal{O}(g(m))\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\exists M>0,\quad\text{such that}\quad\forall m\geq M,\ f(m)\leq\kappa g(m)\quad\text{for some }\kappa>0;

\displaystyle f(m)=\Omega(g(m))\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\exists M>0,\quad\text{such that}\quad\forall m\geq M,\ f(m)\geq\kappa g(m)\quad\text{for some }\kappa>0;

\displaystyle f(m)=\Theta(g(m))\quad

\displaystyle f(m)=\circ(g(m))\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad f(m)=g(m)\varepsilon_{m}\quad\text{where}\quad\varepsilon_{m}\rightarrow 0.

Also we denote

2.2.
Matrix codes

Delsarte introduced rank metric codes in
[
16
]
as
\mathbb{K}
-linear
subspaces of the matrix space
\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}
where the rank
distance of two codewords (
i.e.
,
matrices)
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}
is given by

d_{\text{Rk}\,}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\text{Rk}\,(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}).

Such matrix spaces are called
matrix rank metric codes
and
denoted by
[m\times n,k,d]_{\mathbb{K}}
–codes
where
k
denotes the

\mathbb{K}
-dimension of the code and
d
denotes the minimum distance,
i.e.
the minimum of the rank distances of any two distinct codewords. The
dual
of a matrix rank metric code
\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}
is the matrix rank metric code

\mathscr{C}^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}\colon\textrm{Tr}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\top})=0\text{ for any }{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathscr{C}\}.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that a more abstract point of view can be adopted by
considering subspaces of the space of
\mathbb{K}
-linear maps from a finite
dimensional
\mathbb{K}
-linear space
V
to another
\mathbb{K}
-linear space

W
. This point of view is somehow considered in the sequel when we
deal with subspaces of skew group algebras (see
§
2.4
).

2.3.
Vector codes

Since the works of Gabidulin
[
18
]
, the classical literature
on rank metric codes also involves
\mathbb{L}
-linear subspaces
of
\mathbb{L}^{n}
, where the
\mathbb{K}
–rank
or
rank
(we will omit the field
\mathbb{K}
when it is
clear from the context) of a vector is defined as

\forall\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{L}^{n},\quad\text{Rk}\,_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbf{a})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\dim_{\mathbb{K}}{\left\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{K}}.

Next, the distance between two vectors
\mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{L}^{n}
is
defined as

d_{\text{Rk}\,}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{Rk}\,_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}).

\mathbb{L}
–subspaces of
\mathbb{L}^{n}
are called
vector rank metric codes

and denoted by
[n,k,d]_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}
codes where
k
denotes the

\mathbb{L}
–dimension of the code and
d
denotes the minimum distance.

It is well–known that such vector codes actually can be turned into
matrix codes by choosing a
\mathbb{K}
–basis
\mathcal{B}=({\beta}_{1},\dots,{\beta}_{m})
of

\mathbb{L}
and proceeding as follows. Given an element
x
of
\mathbb{L}
denote by
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)}
its coefficients in the basis
\mathcal{B}
,
i.e.
,

x=x^{(1)}{\beta}_{1}+\cdots+x^{(m)}{\beta}_{m}
. Consider the map

\text{Exp}_{\mathcal{B}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{L}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}\\
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})&\longmapsto&\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&x_{n}^{(1)}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
x_{1}^{(m)}&\cdots&x_{n}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}.\end{array}\right.

Then, any vector code
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{L}^{n}
can be turned into a matrix
code by considering
\text{Exp}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathscr{C})
. The induced matrix code
depends on the choice of the basis
\mathcal{B}
but choosing another basis provides
an isometric code with respect to the rank metric.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that if an
\mathbb{L}
–linear rank metric code can be turned into a
matrix code, the converse is not true. A subspace of

\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}
can be turned into a
\mathbb{K}
-linear subspace of
\mathbb{L}^{n}

by applying the inverse map of
\text{Exp}_{\mathcal{B}}
but the resulting
code will not be
\mathbb{L}
–linear in general. A matrix rank metric code
\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}
is
\mathbb{L}
-linear if its
left idealiser
contains a subring isomorphic to
\mathbb{L}

[
34
]
, where the left idealiser

L(\mathscr{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times m}\colon{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathscr{C}\text{ for all }{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathscr{C}\}

is a ring with respect to sum and product of matrices.
Thus, codes of the form

\text{Exp}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathscr{C})
when
\mathscr{C}
ranges over all
\mathbb{L}
–subspaces of

\mathbb{L}^{n}
form a proper subclass of matrix codes in
\mathbb{K}^{m\times n}
.

2.4.
Rank metric codes as

\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
–codes

The study of rank metric codes as
\mathbb{L}
-subspaces of the skew group algebra

\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
has been initiated in
[
8
]
.
It generalizes the study of rank metric codes over arbitrary cyclic Galois extensions in

[
9
]
.
We recall below the definitions and
basic notions of rank metric codes in this setting.

Consider an arbitrary but fixed finite Galois extension
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
with

\textrm{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{Gal}(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K})
. The
skew group
algebra
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
of
G
over
\mathbb{L}
is defined as

\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Bigg\{\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}}a_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}~\colon~a_{\texttt{g}}\in\mathbb{L}\Bigg\}

and endowed with its additive group structure and the following composition law derived from the group law of
G
:

(a_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g})\circ(a_{\texttt{h}}\texttt{h})=(a_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}(a_{\texttt{h}}))(\texttt{g}\texttt{h}),

which is extended by associativity and distributivity.
This equips
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
with a non-commutative algebra structure.

Theorem 2.3
.

Any element
A=\sum_{\texttt{g}}a_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
defines a

\mathbb{K}
-endomorphism of
\mathbb{L}
that sends
x\in\mathbb{L}
to

\sum_{\texttt{g}}{a_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}(x)}
. This correspondence induces
a
\mathbb{K}
-linear isomorphism between
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
and

\textrm{End}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{L})
.

Proof.

It follows from Artin’s lemma on linear independence of characters. A proof can be found, for instance, in
[
24
, Thm. 1]
. ∎

Thus, the
rank
of an element
A\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
is well-defined as its rank when viewed as a
\mathbb{K}
-linear endomorphism of
\mathbb{L}
.
From the above theorem, it is clear that with respect to a fixed basis

\mathcal{B}=({\beta}_{1},\dots,{\beta}_{m})
of
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
, we get
\mathbb{K}
–linear rank–preserving isomorphisms

(1)

\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]\cong\textrm{End}_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{L})\cong\mathbb{K}^{m\times m}.

Also, w.r.t. the basis
\mathcal{B}=\{{\beta}_{1},\ldots,{\beta}_{m}\}
, every element
A\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
can be seen as a vector

\textbf{a}=(A({\beta}_{1}),\ldots,A({\beta}_{m}))\in\mathbb{L}^{m}.

The aforementioned definition of rank for a vector of
\mathbb{L}^{m}

coincides with the rank of
A
when regarded as a
\mathbb{K}
–endomorphism
of
\mathbb{L}
(according to Theorem
2.3
).

Remark 2.4
.

In the particular case of a Galois extension of finite fields

\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
, the group
G
is cyclic and there
are many characterizations of
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
studied in
[
52
]
. One of
the very well–known characterization is in terms of
linear
polynomials
studied by Ore
[
41
]
followed by his work on the
theory of non-commutative polynomials
[
42
]
. Let

\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
for some prime power
q
and a positive
integer
m
, then the
linear polynomials
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
are given by

L(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}x^{q^{i}},\ \ \text{for\ some\ }d\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{and}\quad a_{0},\dots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}

and endowed with the composition law to give a structure of (non
commutative) ring. With this point of view, the skew group algebra

\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[\textrm{G}]
is isomorphic to the ring of linear polynomials modulo
the two–sided ideal generated by
x^{q^{m}}-x
.

Definition 2.5
.

An
\mathbb{L}
-linear rank metric code

\mathscr{C}
in the skew group algebra
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
is an
\mathbb{L}
-linear subspace of
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
, equipped with the rank distance. The dimension of
\mathscr{C}
is defined as its dimension as
\mathbb{L}
-vector space. The minimum rank distance is defined as

d(\mathscr{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\min\{\text{Rk}\,(A)~\colon~A\in\mathscr{C}\setminus\{0\}\},

where the rank of
A\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
is the rank of the

\mathbb{K}
–endomorphism it induces on
\mathbb{L}
(according to
Theorem
2.3
).

We denote the parameters of an
\mathbb{L}
-linear rank metric code

\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
of dimension
k
and minimum distance
d
by

[\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}],k,d]
. If
d
is either unknown or clear from the context, we
simply write
[\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}],k]_{\mathbb{K}}
.

As observed earlier, an element of
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
can be seen as a

\mathbb{K}
-linear endomorphism of
\mathbb{L}
. Therefore, if we fix a
\mathbb{K}
-basis

\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{L}
, then, one can
transform an
[\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}],k,d]_{\mathbb{K}}
–code
\mathscr{C}
into an

[m\times m,k,d]_{\mathbb{K}}
matrix code or into an
[m,k,d]_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}
vector code.

2.5.
Rank metric Reed–Muller codes

The class of classical
q
-ary Reed–Muller codes is among the most widely studied class of linear codes in the Hamming metric. Introduced independently by Muller and Reed in 1954 in the binary case (i.e.,
q=2
), the class of
q
-ary Reed–Muller codes
\textrm{RM}_{q}(r,m)
of order
r
and type
m
was generalized by Kasami, Lin and Peterson
[
26
,
27
]
in the 1960’s as the code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of length
q^{m}
obtained by evaluation of polynomials in
m
variables of degree bounded by
r
at the points of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
. An extension to the projective setting is proposed and studied in
[
29
,
30
,
49
]
.

Counterparts of Hamming metric Reed–Muller codes in the rank and the sum-rank metric have been defined by evaluations of certain multivariate versions of non-commutative Ore polynomials
[
42
]
. For an introduction to the multivariate extension of the algebra of Ore polynomials that has been used to define the sum-rank metric counterpart of Reed–Muller codes, refer to
[
12
, § 1]
. An iterated construction of multivariate skew polynomials leading to Reed–Muller codes in skew metric is studied in
[
23
]
.

For the present work, we recall the construction of
[
8
]
using skew group algebras.
The so–called
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-Reed–Muller codes
of
[
8
]
can be
considered as a “multivariate” version of the Gabidulin codes
defined from cyclic extensions in
[
9
]
. Let

\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
be an abelian extension with

\textrm{G}=\mathbb{Z}/{n_{1}\mathbb{Z}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{Z}/{n_{m}\mathbb{Z}}
with a system
of generators
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
, a
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-monomial

\theta_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{m}^{i_{m}}
describes the
m
-tuple

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}/{n_{1}\mathbb{Z}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{Z}/{n_{m}\mathbb{Z}}
. Every element in
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
has a unique
representation as a
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-polynomial

P=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})}b_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})}\theta_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{m}^{i_{m}}.

We define

\deg_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(P)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\max\{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{m}~\colon~b_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})}\neq 0\}.

For
0<r\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}(n_{i}-1)
,
the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-Reed–Muller code of order
r
and type

\boldsymbol{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{m})
is defined as

\text{RM}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(r,\mathbf{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{P\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]~\colon~\deg_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(P)\leq r\}.

By fixing a
\mathbb{K}
-basis
\beta=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{N}\}
of

\mathbb{L}
, the code
\text{RM}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(r,\mathbf{n})
can be seen in the vector form as

\{(P(\beta_{1}),\ldots,P(\beta_{N}))~\colon~P\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}],\,\deg_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(P)\leq r\}\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{N},

where
N=|\textrm{G}|
and
equivalently, every codeword can be seen as
N\times N
matrices with
entries in
\mathbb{K}
. When
m=1
,
i.e.

G
cyclic, we recover
Gabidulin codes
[
16
,
18
]
when
\mathbb{L}
is finite and their
generalisation to a cyclic extension
[
9
]
when
\mathbb{L}
is
arbitrary.

Remark 2.6
.

It is worth noting that contrary to Gabidulin codes,

\boldsymbol{\theta}
–Reed–Muller codes cannot be defined over finite fields as soon as the underlying Galois group is not cyclic. Rather,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
–Reed–Muller codes can be defined, e.g., over abelian extensions of number fields, local fields, function fields of algebraic curves over finite fields.

2.6.
Binary-like rank metric Reed–Muller codes.

In the present article, we fix a positive integer
m
and focus on the
particular case of rank metric Reed–Muller codes with

\textrm{G}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m}
and we call these
binary rank metric
Reed–Muller codes
since the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-polynomials resemble those in the
case of binary Reed–Muller codes with Hamming metric. In particular,
binary rank metric Reed–Muller codes have exactly the same parameters
as their Hamming metric counterpart. See
[
8
, Prop. 48 & Thm. 50]
.
We denote by
N
the
“code length”:

N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}2^{m}.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
r,m
be positive integers such that
m>1
. A

rank metric binary Reed–Muller code
of length
N=2^{m}
over
the extension
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
of order
r
and type
m
is the following

\mathbb{L}
-subspace of the skew group algebra
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
:

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bigg\{f\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]~\colon~f=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G},\ \text{w}_{\text{H}}(\texttt{g})\leq r}f_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}\bigg\},

where
\text{w}_{\text{H}}(\texttt{g})
is the Hamming weight of the element
g
seen
as a vector in
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m}
. By convention, for any
r<0
, we
define
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
to be the zero subspace of
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
.

Remark 2.8
.

Note that whenever
r\geq m
, then
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)=\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
.

Remark 2.9
.

To keep in mind the analogy with binary Hamming metric Reed–Muller
codes, we denote
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
with
m
instead of

\textbf{n}=(2,\ldots,2)
as generally defined (see Section
3.1

where Hamming metric binary Reed–Muller codes are shortly discussed). Moreover, we note the
extension
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
as a subscript and omit the mention of a set of
generators
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. This choice is motivated by the fact that we will
handle various field extensions and hence it will be useful to keep
track of which extension we are considering.

3.
Recursive structure of binary rank metric Reed–Muller
codes

In this section, we show a recursive structure of
binary rank metric Reed–Muller codes analogous to the classical case
of Hamming metric Reed–Muller codes. First, to motivate this work, we recall the
recursive structure of classical Reed–Muller codes in the Hamming metric setting and
how this structure is used for decoding.

3.1.
The Hamming case

Recall that the binary Reed–Muller codes of order

r
and type
m
are defined as the evaluation of polynomials in

\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
at all the vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. For a fixed
ordering of the
2^{m}
elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
in the reverse lexicographic
order, the code
\text{RM}_{2}{(r,m)}
is of the form

\{(f(p_{1}),\ldots,f(p_{2^{m}})):f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}],\deg\,f\leq r\}.

A crucial structure of these codes comes from the
observation that any polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}]

of degree
r
can always be decomposed into two parts:

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1})+x_{m}h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}),

where
\deg g\leq r
and

\deg h\leq r-1
.
A consequence of these observations is
the following recursive structure of binary Reed–Muller codes, first
studied by Plotkin.

Lemma 3.1

(Plotkin, 1960)
.

When sorting the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
in the lexicographic order,
the binary Reed–Muller code
\text{RM}_{2}(r,m)
can be constructed
recursively using smaller order Reed–Muller codes:

{\text{RM}_{2}(r,m)=\{(\mathbf{u}~|~\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})~\colon~\mathbf{u}\in\text{RM}_{2}(r,m-1),\,\mathbf{v}\in\text{RM}_{2}(r-1,m-1)\}.}

The recursive structure permits to prove that the minimum
distance of
\text{RM}_{2}(r,m)
is
2^{m-r}
and leads to an
efficient decoding algorithm correcting up to half their minimum
distance in quasi-linear time in the block length
[
1
]
.
This decoder is shortly discussed in the beginning of Section
4
.

3.2.
The recursive structure of rank metric Reed–Muller codes

To explore the recursive structure of the
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
, first we
note that if
\textrm{G}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m}
, the Galois extension
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}

can be of the following two types.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}
be a Galois extension with

\textrm{G}=\text{Gal}(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/{2\mathbb{Z}})^{m}
where
m\geq 1
. Then
there exist
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{L}
such that

\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
where
\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1}),\dots,\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})
are
quadratic extensions over
\mathbb{K}
. Moreover, for any
i
,
the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{i}
has the following shape:

\begin{cases}X^{2}-a_{i}\text{ for some }a_{i}\in\mathbb{K}\text{ when }{\rm Char}(\mathbb{K})\neq 2,\\
X^{2}+X+a_{i}\text{ for some }a_{i}\in\mathbb{K}\text{ when }{\rm Char}(\mathbb{K})=2.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\textrm{G}=\left<\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}\right>
and
\mathbb{L}^{H_{i}}
is the fixed
field of

H_{i}=\langle\theta_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\theta_{i}},\ldots,\theta_{m}\rangle
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. Since
\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}H_{i}=Id
,
the compositum of the
\mathbb{L}^{H_{i}}
’s in
\mathbb{L}
is
\mathbb{L}

itself. Considering the cardinalities of the
H_{i}
’s, we have

\mathbb{L}^{H_{i}}=\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{i})
where the minimal polynomial
of
\alpha_{i}
is a quadratic polynomial. If
Char
\mathbb{K}\neq 2
, then by completing the square, the minimal polynomial of

\alpha_{i}
over
\mathbb{K}

becomes
X^{2}-a_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
.
Similarly, when
\text{Char}\ \mathbb{K}=2
, the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{i}

over
\mathbb{K}
is a quadratic polynomial
X^{2}+sX+t
for some
s,t\in\mathbb{K}
.
Then, replacing
\alpha_{i}
by
s^{-1}\alpha_{i}
we get a minimal polynomial

X^{2}+X+s^{-2}t
.
∎

The case of characteristic
\neq 2
will be referred to as the
Kummer
case
since all the minimal intermediary extensions are Kummer ones and the
case of characteristic
2
will be referred to as the
Artin–Schreier

case.

3.2.1.
The Kummer case

First, we consider the case
\text{Char}\,\mathbb{K}\neq 2
,
i.e.
, a Kummer
type extension
\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
where
each one of the

\alpha_{i}^{2}=a_{i}
for some

We also introduce the intermediary extension

\mathbb{L}_{m-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m-1}).

The Galois group

\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m}
which satisfy

where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker Delta. Finally, we set

\textrm{G}_{m-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\langle\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m-1}\rangle

and note that
\text{Gal}(\mathbb{L}_{m-1}/\mathbb{K})\cong\textrm{G}_{m-1}
.
This is summarized in the diagram below.

\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
\mathbb{L}_{m-1}=\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m-1})
\mathbb{K}
\scriptstyle{\left<\theta_{m}\right>}
\scriptstyle{\textrm{G}/\left<\theta_{m}\right>\cong\textrm{G}_{m-1}}
G

In addition, we will equip
\mathbb{L}
and its intermediary subfields with
a specific
\mathbb{K}
–basis that is iteratively constructed as follows. We take

\mathcal{B}_{1}=(1,\alpha_{1})
to be a
\mathbb{K}
–basis of
\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1})
.
Then, for any

\mathcal{B}_{i}\cup\mathcal{B}_{i}\alpha_{i+1}

The statement to follow describes the recursive structure of binary rank metric
Reed–Muller codes. In the Hamming setting, the vectors in the codes had
a recursive structure. In the rank setting the recursive structure
can be read via their matrix representation.

Proposition 3.3

(Recursive structure in the Kummer case)
.

Let
{\rm Char~}\mathbb{K}\neq 2
,
\text{Gal}(\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/{2\mathbb{Z}})^{m}
and
r
be an
integer and
m
be a nonnegative integer. The code
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
of Definition
2.7
has the
following recursive structure.

•

If
r\leq-1
then it is the zero code, composed only with the

2^{m}\times 2^{m}
zero matrix.

•

Otherwise, it is defined as:

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}&\quad&{a_{m}({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1})}\\
&&\\
{{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}}&\quad&{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\colon\quad\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in\text{RM}_{{\mathbb{L}_{m-1}}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m-1),\\
&{\mathbf{B}}_{i}\in\text{RM}_{{\mathbb{L}_{m-1}}/\mathbb{K}}(r-1,m-1)\end{aligned}\right\},

where
a_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\alpha_{m}^{2}\in\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

Let us give a decomposition of elements
F=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}}f_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}
of
\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}]
.
By considering separately
\theta_{m}
and noting that
G
splits
into the disjoint union of the two cosets
\textrm{G}_{m-1}
and

\textrm{G}_{m-1}\theta_{m}
, we can split
F
as

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}+\left(\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}\theta_{m}}\texttt{g}\right)\theta_{m}.

Next,
\mathbb{L}
splits into
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}_{m-1}\oplus\mathbb{L}_{m-1}\alpha_{m}.

Thus, for any
a\in\mathbb{L}
, let
a=a^{0}+a^{1}\alpha_{m}
with
a^{0},a^{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{m-1}
.
Then, we get a splitting of
F
as

(2)

F=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}}^{0}\texttt{g}+\alpha_{m}\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}}^{1}\texttt{g}+\left(\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f^{0}_{\texttt{g}\theta_{m}}\texttt{g}\right)\theta_{m}+\alpha_{m}\left(\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f^{1}_{\texttt{g}\theta_{m}}\texttt{g}\right)\theta_{m}.

Set

A_{0}=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}}^{0}\texttt{g},\qquad A_{1}=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}}^{1}\texttt{g},\qquad B_{0}=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f^{0}_{\texttt{g}\theta_{m}}\texttt{g},\quad\text{and}\quad B_{1}=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f^{1}_{\texttt{g}\theta_{m}}\texttt{g},

then the degree constraint on
F\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)

entails

A_{0},A_{1}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}_{m-1}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m-1)\quad\text{and}\quad B_{0},B_{1}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}_{m-1}/\mathbb{K}}(r-1,m-1).

Equation (
2
) can then be rewritten as

(3)

F=A_{0}+\alpha_{m}A_{1}+(B_{0}+\alpha_{m}B_{1})\theta_{m}.

Recall that we chose a
\mathbb{K}
–basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{L}
such that

\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{m-1}\cup\mathcal{B}_{m-1}\alpha_{m}
where

\mathcal{B}_{m-1}
is a
\mathbb{K}
–basis of
\mathbb{L}_{m-1}
.
By
denoting
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1},{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
the respective representations
of
A_{0},A_{1},B_{0},B_{1}
in
\mathcal{B}_{m-1}
, we get the desired

F
in
\mathcal{B}
.

Indeed, as
{\theta_{m}}_{|\mathbb{L}_{m-1}}=\text{id}_{\mathbb{L}_{m-1}}
and thus

F_{|\mathbb{L}_{m-1}}=(A_{0}+B_{0})+\alpha_{m}(A_{1}+B_{1}),

the left-hand half of the matrix in the statement corresponds to

F_{|\mathbb{L}_{m-1}}
.
Similarly, the right-hand half corresponds to the restriction of
F
to
\mathbb{L}_{m-1}\alpha_{m}

u\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{L}_{m-1}\alpha_{m}

with
u\in\mathbb{L}_{m-1}

and thus the shape.
∎

For the forthcoming complexity analysis, the structure of the
generating matrix of the vector code in
\mathbb{L}^{N}
obtained by

\mathcal{B}

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider the code
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
as the
\mathbb{L}

\mathbb{L}^{N}

\boldsymbol{\theta}

\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Any element of the code is obtained by the evaluation at the elements of

\mathcal{B}
of a
\boldsymbol{\theta}

F=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}}f_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}=\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}}\texttt{g}+\left(\sum_{\texttt{g}\in\textrm{G}_{m-1}}f_{\texttt{g}\theta_{m}}\texttt{g}\right)\theta_{m}.

Hence

F=F_{0}+F_{1}\theta_{m},

where
F_{0},F_{1}\in\mathbb{L}[\textrm{G}_{m-1}]
of respective
\boldsymbol{\theta}

\leq r
and
\leq r-1

\displaystyle\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}}(F)

\displaystyle=(\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{0})+\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{1})~|~\alpha_{m}\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{0})-\alpha_{m}\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{1}))

\displaystyle=(\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{0})~|~\alpha_{m}\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{0}))\ +\ (\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{1})~|~-\alpha_{m}\text{ev}_{\mathcal{B}_{m-1}}(F_{1})),

4.
Decoding

4.1.
The recursive decoding in the Hamming case

Let us quickly recall the behaviour of the decoder for binary
Reed–Muller codes
\text{RM}_{2}(r,m)
of order
r
and type
m
in Hamming
metric, resting on the recursive structure mentioned in Lemma

3.1
. See for instance
[
17
]
. The algorithm
is recursive: we assume to be able to decode any code

\text{RM}_{2}(r^{\prime},m^{\prime})
for either
r^{\prime}<r
or
m^{\prime}<m
. Let

\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}_{l}~|~\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{e}_{r})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2^{m}}

be the received vector
such that
(\mathbf{u}~|~\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\in\text{RM}_{2}(r,m)
and

(\mathbf{e}_{l}~|~\mathbf{e}_{r})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2^{m}}
has Hamming weight less or equal to

2^{m-r-1}-1
. By
folding
the received word
i.e.

by summing up its two halves, we get

(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}_{l})+(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{e}_{r})=(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{e}_{l}+\mathbf{e}_{r})\quad\text{with}\quad\text{w}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e}_{l}+\mathbf{e}_{r})\leq\text{w}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e}_{l}~|~\mathbf{e}_{r})\leq 2^{m-r-1}-1.

Since
\mathbf{v}\in\text{RM}_{2}(r-1,m-1)
, which has minimum distance
2^{m-r}
,
a recursive call to the algorithm permits to recover
\mathbf{v}
by decoding

\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{e}_{l}+\mathbf{e}_{r}
. Therefore, we now have to recover
\mathbf{u}
from

(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}_{l}~|~\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}_{r})
, where

\text{either}\quad\text{w}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e}_{l})\leq\frac{2^{m-r-1}-1}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad\text{w}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e}_{r})\leq\frac{2^{m-r-1}-1}{2}\cdot

Therefore, at least one of the two recursive
calls respectively applied to
\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}_{l}
and
\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{e}_{r}
will succeed to recover
\mathbf{u}
.

4.2.
A difficulty when switching to the rank case

The aforementioned recursive decoding cannot be adapted to the rank
setting in a straightforward manner. Indeed, in the step of recovering
the part
\mathbf{u}
, we crucially use the fact that in Hamming metric, when
cutting a weight
t
vector in two halves
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e}_{l}~|~\mathbf{e}_{r})
,
then
w_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e})=w_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e}_{l})+w_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{e}_{r})
.
This property almost never holds in rank metric.
Indeed take
t<N/2
and a rank
t
matrix
{\mathbf{E}}\in\mathbb{K}^{N\times N}
.
When splitting
{\mathbf{E}}
into two halves

{\mathbf{E}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}_{l}&|&{\mathbf{E}}_{r}\end{pmatrix}

with
{\mathbf{E}}_{l},{\mathbf{E}}_{r}\in\mathbb{K}^{N\times\frac{N}{2}}
, then the most likely situation is that both

{\mathbf{E}}_{l},{\mathbf{E}}_{r}
have rank
t
.

Therefore, in order to adapt the Hamming metric recursive decoder to the rank setting, we actually had to circumvent two difficulties:

(1)

find a matrix analogue of folding that permits to get rid of the contribution of the Reed–Muller code with the least minimum distance, namely the matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m-1)
in (
3.7
);

(2)

address the aforementioned issue,
i.e.
once the folded codeword is decoded,
i.e.

once
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r-1,m-1)
are recovered we have to use a completely different approach to recover
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
.

4.3.
Overview of our algorithm

We give a recursive decoding algorithm for binary Reed–Muller
codes with rank metric for the Kummer case using the recursive structure identified in Proposition
3.3
.
Suppose we are given

\mathbf{Y}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}&\quad&{a_{m}}({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1})\\
&&\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}&\quad&{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathbf{C}}\,\in\,\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}&\quad&{\mathbf{E}}_{01}\\
&&\\
{\mathbf{E}}_{10}&\quad&{\mathbf{E}}_{11}\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathbf{E}}\,\in\,{\mathbb{K}}^{2^{m}\times 2^{m}}},

where
\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}})\leq 2^{m-r-1}-1
. Since the code
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)

has minimum distance
2^{m-r}
, the error’s rank is less than half the
minimum distance.

Recursivity

To describe the recursive algorithm, we assume that we can decode up to half the minimum distance of any rank metric Reed–Muller code
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}^{\prime}}(r^{\prime},m^{\prime})

over a field
\mathbb{K}^{\prime}
such that
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{L}
with any
parameters
r^{\prime}\leq r
and
m^{\prime}<m
.

Overview of the algorithm

First, we briefly
describe the main steps of our decoding algorithm.

•

Step 1. Folding
\mathbf{Y}
.
We apply an operation that does
not increase the error’s rank and permits to get rid of the

{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
’s and reduce to a decoding instance consisting in correcting

2^{m-r-1}-1
errors for
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r-1,m-1)
.

•

Step 2. Recursive calls.
Recursively calling the algorithm
permits to recover
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
and get partial information on

{\mathbf{E}}
.

•

Step 3. Recovery of the
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
’s.
Under some
assumption on
{\mathbf{E}}
, the partial information we got on the error
permits to recover the
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
’s by solving linear systems.

4.4.
Full description and proof of correctness of the algorithm

4.4.1.
Step 1. Folding

In the Hamming case, the decoding algorithm starts by folding the
received vector to get rid of the contribution of
\mathbf{u}
,
i.e.

of the contribution of
\text{RM}_{2}(r,m-1)
which is the one with the
smallest minimum distance. Similarly, here we look for an operation
that will not increase the rank of the error term while getting rid of

{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)
.

Denote by

\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{K}^{2^{m-1}\times 2^{m-1}}
the identity matrix. The
following operation will be the analogue of the folding in the
Hamming case

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{Y}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
-\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\frac{1}{{a_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}+{\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\ {\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}};

Remark 4.1
.

It is worth noting that if the matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{i},{\mathbf{B}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{E}}_{ij}
have their entries in
\mathbb{K}
, then the matrices obtained in reductions
(
6
) and (
7
) have their entries in

\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})
. Also, the operations on
{\mathbf{E}}
by left-and-right multiplication do not increase the rank,
i.e.
,
\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}}^{\prime})\leq\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}})
.

Remark 4.2
.

The folding operation could also have been

(7)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{Y}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}-\frac{2}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}-\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\frac{1}{{a_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}+{\mathbf{E}}_{10}+\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11},

which is nothing but the conjugate of (
6
). The use of
(
7
) is equivalent to that of (
6
).

4.4.2.
Step 2. A recursive call to recover
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}

Reduction (
6
) yields the sum of an element of

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r-1,m-1)
and an error term of rank

\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}}^{\prime})\leq\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}})\leq 2^{m-r-1}-1
, which is less than half
the minimum distance of
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r-1,m-1)
. Based on
our assumption that Reed–Muller codes of parameters
r^{\prime}\leq r
and

m^{\prime}<m
can be decoded up to half their minimum distance, a recursive
call to the algorithm applied on the expression (
6
)
permits to recover

\frac{2}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}.

Next, since
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
have their entries in
\mathbb{K}
, they are
immediately deduced from above by extracting the
\mathbb{K}
part and the

\alpha_{m}
part exactly as a complex matrix is decomposed into its
real and imaginary part.

Initialization of the recursive process.
In case

r=-1
, then we are decoding the zero code and hence our decoder
will immediately return
0
. In case
r=0
, then
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
are
elements of
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(-1,m-1)
and hence are both
equal to
0
. Therefore, for
r=0
, we immediately have access to
the
{\mathbf{B}}_{i}
’s. In this situation the folding should however be
computed: (
6
) will yield the linear combination
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}

of the
{\mathbf{E}}_{ij}
’s, which is useful for Step 3 to follow.

4.4.3.
Step 3. Recovery of
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}

Removing
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
from
\mathbf{Y}
, we
consider the new decoding problem:

(8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}&\quad&{a_{m}}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\\
&&\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}&\quad&{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}}\end{pmatrix}}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{C}}}\,\in\,\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}&\quad&{\mathbf{E}}_{01}\\
&&\\
{\mathbf{E}}_{10}&\quad&{\mathbf{E}}_{11}\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathbf{E}}\,\in\,{\mathbb{K}}^{2^{m}\times 2^{m}}}.

As explained in Section
4.2
, to recover

{\mathbf{A}}_{0},~{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
, the technique used in the Hamming case does not
apply. However, the previous step permitted us also to recover from
(
6
) the following matrix:

(9)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\frac{1}{{a_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}+{\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\frac{1}{{\alpha_{m}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}},

Here we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The matrix

{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})^{\frac{N}{2}\times\frac{N}{2}}
has
rank
t
and so do their foldings and so on until depth
r+1
,

i.e.
we assume any iterated folding of
{\mathbf{E}}
of size

2^{m-i}\times 2^{m-i}
with
i\leq r+1
to have rank

t
.

Remark 4.3
.

This assumption is actually what happens “most of the time”.
When applied
to finite fields we analyse in Section
6.3
the
probability that the folding of such a matrix
{\mathbf{E}}
keeps the same
rank and we prove that this probability is exponentially close to

1
.
The case of infinite fields is discussed later on in
Section
6.6
where we show that, under some choice of
error model, we can bound from below the failure probability and make
it exponentially close to
1
.

Now, consider

(10)

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-\alpha_{m}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}\\
{\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathbf{F}}}.

The bottom part

(11)

{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{F}}_{1},\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathbf{F}}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}({\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{E}}_{11})

gives an instance of the decoding problem for the code

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)
. Moreover, since

(12)

\small{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{E}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}{\mathbf{F}},

then
t=\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}})\geq\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{F}})\geq\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}}^{\prime})
and, thanks to
Assumption
1
, all these matrices have rank
t
. Next, the
right–hand equation of (
12
) entails that the row space of

{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
is contained in that of
{\mathbf{F}}
and, these row spaces are
actually equal because they both have dimension
t
. Thus, the row
space of the matrix
{\mathbf{F}}_{1}
(defined in (
11
)) is contained in
that of
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
.

Since
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
is known, we know its row space

V\subseteq\mathbb{K}(\alpha)^{\frac{N}{2}}
and the previous discussion
asserts that
V
contains the row space of
{\mathbf{F}}_{1}
. Similarly to the
Hamming case, with this partial knowledge of the row space (that can
be regarded as a rank metric counterpart of the support in the Hamming
metric) of
{\mathbf{F}}_{1}
, the decoding boils down to the resolution of a
linear system and permits to recover
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}
.
This decoding approach while knowing the row space is well–known in
the literature (see, for instance
[
22
, § IV.1]
or

[
7
, § III.1]
) and can be regarded as the rank counterpart
of
erasure decoding
. To ease the complexity analysis given in
Section
5
we give an
ad hoc
description of
the procedure in the subsequent paragraph. Note that
V
has dimension
at most
t=2^{m-r-1}-1
which is less than the minimum distance of

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)
which guarantees the uniqueness of the
solution of the decoding.

Finally, once
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}
is found, since both

{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
have their entries in
\mathbb{K}
, they can be immediately
deduced.

4.4.4.
Erasure decoding: further details on the recovery of
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}

Let us
quickly explain how the decoding of (
10

are given

(13)

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{0}=({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{0})+{\mathbf{F}}_{1}\ \in\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})^{\frac{N}{2}\times\frac{N}{2}},

({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{0})\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)

\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})

{\mathbf{F}}_{1}

Let us switch to a vector representation by representing elements of

\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})^{\frac{N}{2}\times\frac{N}{2}}

\mathbb{L}^{\frac{N}{2}}

Since the space
V
is known, the matrix

{\mathbf{R}}
is known and we aim to compute
\mathbf{x}
.
For this sake, let
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{L}^{(\frac{N}{2}-k)\times\frac{N}{2}}

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)

when regarded as a
k

\mathbb{L}

\mathbb{L}^{\frac{N}{2}}

\mathbf{x}
can be computed as follows.

Lemma 4.4
.

The vector
\mathbf{x}
of (
15
)
is the unique solution of the linear system:

(16)

{\mathbf{H}}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{0}}^{\top}={\mathbf{H}}{(\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{R}})}^{\top}=\left({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\top}.

Proof.

Only the uniqueness should be proven. Suppose that another

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{x}
is solution as well. Then

{\mathbf{H}}((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\mathbf{R}})^{\top}=0
or equivalently

(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\mathbf{R}}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)
. Since
{\mathbf{R}}
has
full rank and
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
we deduce that

(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\mathbf{R}}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Thus, as a nonzero element of

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r,m-1)
, we have

\text{Rk}\,_{\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\mathbf{R}}\geq 2^{m-1-r},

while
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{t}
has length
t

and hence has
\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})
–rank at most
t\leq 2^{m-1-r}-1
. Since

{\mathbf{R}}
has entries in
\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})
then

\text{Rk}\,_{\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\mathbf{R}}\leq\text{Rk}\,_{\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq t\leq 2^{m-1-r}-1.

A contradiction.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
-1\leq r\leq m
be integers. Given an input

(17)

\mathbf{Y}={\mathbf{C}}+{\mathbf{E}}

where

{\mathbf{C}}\in\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)

\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}})=t\leq 2^{m-r-1}-1

under Assumption
1
, Algorithm
2

{\mathbf{C}}

Proof.

Note first, that the since
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
has minimum distance

constraint on
\text{Rk}\,({\mathbf{E}})
entails that
{\mathbf{C}}
is the unique solution to the decoding problem
(

We prove the result by induction on

For

then
{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{0}
and hence the algorithm succeeds.
For

\mathbf{Y}

\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{K}_{1}

\mathbb{K}_{2}

that

(18)

[\mathbb{K}_{1}:\mathbb{K}]+[\mathbb{K}_{2}:\mathbb{K}]\leq\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}_{1},\mathbb{K}_{2})\leq[\mathbb{K}_{1}:\mathbb{K}][\mathbb{K}_{2}:\mathbb{K}]

since such multiplication involves more than

[\mathbb{K}_{1}:\mathbb{K}]+[\mathbb{K}_{2}:\mathbb{K}]
elements of
\mathbb{K}
and that the naive
multiplication costs
[\mathbb{K}_{1}:\mathbb{K}][\mathbb{K}_{2}:\mathbb{K}]
operations in
\mathbb{K}
. When

\mathbb{K}_{1}=\mathbb{K}_{2}
, we simply use
\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}_{1})
to denote the number of operations in
\mathbb{K}
that a multiplication of two elements in
\mathbb{K}_{1}
costs. Following
[
51
, Corollary 11.11]
, we
will assume that if
\mathbb{K}_{1}
is an extension with
[\mathbb{K}_{1}:\mathbb{K}]=\ell
, then

(19)

\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}_{1})=\mathcal{O}(\ell\log\ell).

Finally, at some places, we will be interested specifically in the multiplication by
\alpha_{m}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Recall that
N=[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{K}]
.
The multiplication of an element of
\mathbb{L}
by
\alpha_{m}
costs

\mathcal{O}(N)
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

Let us start with the Kummer case. An element
v
of
\mathbb{L}
is
written
v=v_{0}+v_{1}\alpha_{m}
for

v_{0},v_{1}\in\mathbb{L}_{m-1}=\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m-1})
.
Thus,
v_{0},v_{1}
are represented as elements of
\mathbb{K}^{\frac{N}{2}}
and

\alpha_{m}v=a_{m}v_{1}+\alpha_{m}v_{0}.

In practice, it consists in a permutation of the entries of the
element of
\mathbb{K}^{N}
representing
v
and then the multiplication of
the
\frac{N}{2}
entries of
v_{1}
by
a_{m}
which is in
\mathbb{K}
.
This yields
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.

In the Artin–Schreier case, the multiplication by
\alpha_{m}
:

\alpha_{m}(v_{0}+v_{1}\alpha_{m})=v_{1}a_{m}+(v_{0}+v_{1})\alpha_{m}

which leads to the same asymptotic complexity estimate.
∎

Before
starting the analysis, we focus on a specific routine which is used in
Step 3: the calculation of
syndromes
. That is to say products

{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
where

{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{L}^{(N-\dim_{\mathbb{L}}\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m))\times N}
is the
structured parity–check matrix for
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
as introduced
in Proposition
3.5
. Taking advantage of this
structure permits a faster computation compared to arbitrary matrix
products.

5.1.
Fast syndrome calculation

As mentioned in Sections
4.4.4
, Step 3, presented in
Section
4.4.3
, requires the computation of products

{\mathbf{H}}{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{0}}^{\top}
and
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
, where
{\mathbf{H}}
is a
parity–check matrix of an RM code,
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{0}\in\mathbb{L}^{N/2}

and
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})^{\frac{N}{2}\times t}
.

A naive version of the calculation of (for instance)

{\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{\top}
gives a cost in
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
.
However, we can take advantage of the recursive structure of
{\mathbf{H}}
to
perform a fast syndrome calculation.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{H}}
be a matrix of the shape
{\mathbf{H}}(r,m)
as defined in
Equation (
4
) of Proposition
3.5
. Let
\mathbb{K}^{\prime}
be some
intermediary field
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{K}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{L}
. Let

\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{K}^{\prime})^{N}
, then the product
{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
can be computed
in
\mathcal{O}(N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}^{\prime},\mathbb{L}))
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

We prove the result in the Kummer case, the Artin–Schreier case
being proved in the very same manner. Let us first consider the
complexity of computing
{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
with
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{L}^{N}
. Denote
by
\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m)
this complexity. From
Proposition
3.5
,

{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{y}^{\top}={\mathbf{H}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)&\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)\\
{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)&-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}.

The calculations of
{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\top}
and

{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top}
cost
2\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m-1)
. Then we need to
multiply
{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top}
by

-\alpha_{m}^{-1}=-\frac{\alpha_{m}}{a_{m}}
which, from
Lemma
5.1
represents a cost of
\mathcal{O}(N)

operations in
\mathbb{K}
per entry, hence a cost in
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.
Since the rows
{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)
are rows of
{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)
, the
vectors
{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\top}
and

\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top}
can be deduced for free
from the previous calculations. There only remains to compute the sums

{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\top}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}(r,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\top}+\alpha_{m}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}(r-1,m-1)\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top},

which also costs
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.
Thus, we have the recursive formula:

\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m)\leq 2\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m-1)+\kappa N^{2}

for some constant
\kappa
.
Therefore,

\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m)\leq\kappa\left(N^{2}+2{\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}^{2}+\cdots+2^{m-r-1}{\left(\frac{N}{2^{m-r-1}}\right)}^{2}\right)+2^{m-r}\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,r).

Next, from Remark
3.6
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m)\leq\kappa\Bigg(N^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle+2^{m-r-1}{\left(\frac{N}{2^{m-r-1}}\right)}^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+2^{m-r}\kappa\left({\left(\frac{N}{2^{m-r}}\right)}^{2}+\cdots+2^{r-1}{\left(\frac{N}{2^{m-1}}\right)}^{2}+2^{r}\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(0,0)\right).

Then,
\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(0,0)=\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}^{\prime},\mathbb{L})
. This yields an overall
cost

\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m)\leq\kappa^{\prime}N^{2}+N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}^{\prime},\mathbb{L})

for some other constant
\kappa^{\prime}
. Then, from
(
18
), we deduce that

\mathcal{C}_{\text{syn}}(r,m)=\mathcal{O}(N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}^{\prime},\mathbb{L})).

∎

5.2.
Complexity analysis of the decoding algorithm

Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r,m,\mathbb{K})
the complexity of decoding

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
up to half the minimum distance,
i.e.

errors of rank up to
2^{m-r-1}-1
. We aim to evaluate this
quantity.

5.2.1.
Complexity of Step 1

The first step of the decoding consists in folding
\mathbf{Y}
. Thus,
consists in summing up
\frac{N}{2}\times\frac{N}{2}
sub-blocks of

\mathbf{Y}
. Since
\mathbf{Y}
has entries in
\mathbb{K}
multiplications by
\alpha_{m}

are for free: we represent any element of
\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})
by

u+\alpha_{m}v
for
u,v\in\mathbb{K}
.

Thus, this step represents a cost in
\kappa(N/2)^{2}
operations in
\mathbb{K}
for
some positive constant
\kappa
.

5.2.2.
Complexity of Step 2

The second step consists in a recursive call of the decoder on
decoding instances for
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})}(r-1,m-1)
, leading
to a cost of

\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r-1,m-1,\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})).

5.2.3.
Complexity of Step 3

The third step consists in the recovery of
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
. If it
boils down to linear algebra, the recursive structure of the codes
permits to perform structured linear algebra much faster than with
generic algorithms.
Indeed, according to Section
4.4.4
, the decoding procedure
consists in

(1)

Computing a matrix
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})^{t\times\frac{N}{2}}

whose rows span the space
V
containing the error’s row space;

(2)

Computing the syndrome
{\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{y}_{0}
and the matrix
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
;

(3)

Solving the linear system (
15
).

Thus, in terms of computations, Step 3 consists in the following steps.

(3.a)

From the matrix
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
that was obtained from
Step 2 (see (
9
)), compute a basis of its row space
V
to
get the matrix
{\mathbf{R}}
. This can be performed by putting
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
in row
echelon form. This can be done in less than

\kappa(N/2)^{2}t^{\omega-2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})),\ \ \text{for some }\kappa>0

(see for
instance
[
25
, § 3.3]
).

(3.b)

From Proposition
5.2
, the calculation of
{\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{0}
can be performed in less than

\kappa(N/2)\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})\quad\text{operations,
for some }\kappa>0.

(3.c)

Similarly to the previous case, using fast syndromes
calculations, the computation of the matrix
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}
corresponds to
computing
t
products of
{\mathbf{H}}
by a vector in

\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})^{N}
. From
Proposition
5.2
, this
yields a cost bounded by

\kappa t(N/2)\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}),\mathbb{L}))\quad\text{operations in
}\mathbb{K},\text{ for some }\kappa>0.

(3.d)

Solve the linear system (
16
) to get
\mathbf{x}
. This
is an
(N/2)\times t
linear system with entries in
\mathbb{L}
, yielding
a cost in
\mathcal{O}(Nt^{\omega-1}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
operations. This gives a complexity bounded by

\kappa(Nt^{\omega-1}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))\quad\text{operations in
}\mathbb{K},\text{ for some }\kappa>0.

\frac{N}{2^{s}}
.

(3.e)

Compute
\mathbf{f}_{0}=\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{R}}
where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{L}^{t}
and deduce

\mathbf{a}_{1}-\alpha_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{0}
. The calculation of
\mathbf{f}_{0}

represents

\mathcal{O}(t^{2}N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}),\mathbb{L}))\qquad\text{operations in}\ \mathbb{K}.

This can be improved by expanding

\mathbf{x}
into an
\frac{N}{2}\times t
matrix
\mathbf{X}
with entries in

\mathbb{K}
. The cost of the product
\mathbf{X}{\mathbf{R}}
is bounded by

\kappa(N/2)^{2}t^{\omega-2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m})),\ \text{for some }\kappa>0.

We now have all the elements to give formulas for the complexity of
our algorithm.

Theorem 5.3
.

Under Assumption
1
,
the decoding of
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}}(r,m)
can be performed with a deterministic
algorithm in

\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-1}N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))\ \ \text{operations in }\mathbb{K},

which gives
\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-1}N^{2}\log N)
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

Summarizing the costs of the various steps we get a
recursive formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r,m,\mathbb{K})\leq\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r-1

\displaystyle,m-1,\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}))+

\displaystyle\kappa\Big((N/2)^{2}+(N/2)^{2}t^{\omega-2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}))+(N/2)\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})

\displaystyle+t(N/2)\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}),\mathbb{L})+t^{\omega-1}(N/2)\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})+t^{\omega-2}(N/2)^{2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}))\Big),

for some positive constant
\kappa
. Since
\omega
is known to be

\geq 2
, some terms are negligible, which
gives

\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r,m,\mathbb{K})\leq\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r-1,m-1,\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m}))+\kappa\Big(\frac{N}{2}t^{\omega-1}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})\Big).

After recursive calls up to depth
r+1

we get:

\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r,m,\mathbb{K})\leq\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(-1,m-r-1,\mathbb{K}(\alpha_{m-r},\dots,\alpha_{m}))+\kappa\left(\frac{N}{2}+\frac{N}{4}+\cdots+\frac{N}{2^{r+1}}\right)t^{\omega-1}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}).

The cost of a recursive call for a Reed–Muller code of degree
-1
,
i.e.
of dimension
0

is nothing but the cost of Step 1, and hence is bounded by
\kappa(N^{2}/2^{r})
.
This yields

\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r,m,\mathbb{K})\leq\kappa\left(\frac{N^{2}}{2^{r}}+Nt^{\omega-1}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})\right).

Since
\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})=\Omega(N\log N)
, the
N^{2}/2^{r}
term in the right–hand side is negligible
compared to the other term, which leads to:

\mathcal{C}_{\text{dec}}(r,m,\mathbb{K})=\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-1}N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L})).

This provides the complexity. Using (
19
), we deduce the
\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-1}N^{2}\log N)
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Note that our complexity formula differs from that of

[
14
]
. Indeed, compared to our former short paper, we changed
the initialization step of the recursive decoder which originally
involved the Dickson decoder
[
13
]
. The new version is fully independent from
the Dickson decoder and benefits from a much cheaper initialization step.

Also, note that
[
14
, Rem. III.8]
refers to the Dickson decoder
while claiming a complexity in
\mathcal{O}(kt^{\omega}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
. This claim
was based on a first version of
[
13
]
which was not fully correct. This has been
corrected in the sequel. The true complexity of the Dickson decoder
[
13
]

is discussed in the subsequent Section
5.3
.

5.3.
Comparison with the Dickson–based approach

The recursive algorithm we presented in this paper fares well compared
to the Dickson matrix–based decoding algorithm in

[
13
, Thm 5.15]
. Indeed, according to this reference, the
Dickson–based decoder costs requires the Gaussian elimination of a
rank
t
matrix in
\mathbb{L}^{N\times N}
and
\mathcal{O}(kN)
evaluations of
elements of
G
at elements of
\mathbb{L}
. Even when neglecting the
Galois action part, this leads to a complexity of

\mathcal{O}(tN^{2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
operations in
\mathbb{K}
.

In comparison, our algorithm has a complexity of

\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-1}N\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
operations in
\mathbb{K}
, which,
since
t\leq N
is always asymptotically better because
\omega
can be
taken to be
<3
. Our algorithm is even particularly better in the
linear rate regime:
k=\Omega(N)
where
t
is sublinear in
N
.

Remark 5.5
.

Getting into
[
13
, § 5.5]
, one could argue that the Gaussian
elimination part can be performed in

\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-2}N^{2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
operations in
\mathbb{K}
. This claim
is disputable since the aforementioned Gaussian elimination involves
formal variables that are specialised “on the fly” during the
decoding step. Even if one could achieve this

\mathcal{O}(t^{\omega-2}N^{2}\mathcal{M_{\mathbb{K}}}(\mathbb{L}))
operations for the decoding,
our new algorithm would still remain the best one in the regime
t=\circ(N)
and would
have the same complexity as the Dickson approach in the regime
t=\Theta(N)
.

6.
Plotkin construction for matrix codes

Inspired by the previous sections where an analogue of Plotkin

(u~|~u+v)
structure was identified for rank metric binary
Reed–Muller codes, we abstract this structure to provide a rank
analogue of Plotkin construction. A particular interest of this
approach is that contrary to rank metric Reed–Muller codes that can
be defined only over infinite fields, we can here propose a structure
that can be applied to any rank metric codes. In particular our
construction works over finite fields, which is the context we
consider from now on even if one could define the construction in a
much broader generality. Thus, in the sequel the ground field will be
a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q
odd.

Caution.
In the previous section, the Reed–Muller
codes corresponded to codes of length
N

i.e.
,
N\times N

matrix codes. Moreover, following the notation of
[
8
,
13
]
, we
used
m
for the number of generators of the Galois group which yields

N=2^{m}
. In the sequel, we go back to more usual notation on rank
metric codes by considering spaces of
m\times n
matrices. Hence,
contrary to the previous section,
m
(and also
2m
) will stand for
the number of rows. Later, in Section
6.6
we
establish a connection with rank Reed–Muller codes and for this
specific subsection, and only in this one, we will go back to the
notation used in the previous sections.

6.1.
Construction and first properties

Definition 6.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let
\mathscr{C}
be an
[m\times n,k_{1},d_{1}]_{q}
code
and
\mathscr{D}
be an
[m\times n,k_{2},d_{2}]_{q}
code. We define the
Plotkin construction of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
to be the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear
matrix rank metric code:

\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}&\quad&{a({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1})}\\
&&\\
{{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}}&\quad&{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\colon\quad\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathscr{C},\\
&{\mathbf{B}}_{i}\in\mathscr{D}\end{aligned}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m\times 2n}.

Remark 6.2
.

The rationale behind choosing odd
q
is to adapt the decoding
algorithm for Kummer extensions to finite fields. For
q
even, we
propose to consider the Plotkin construction of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
to
be the following
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear matrix rank metric code imitating the
recursive structure appeared for the Artin–Schreier extensions:

\mathscr{C}\diamond_{a}\mathscr{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}&\quad&{a({\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1})+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}\\
&&\\
{{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}}&\quad&{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\colon\quad\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathscr{C},\\
&{\mathbf{B}}_{i}\in\mathscr{D}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Proposition 6.3
.

In the context of Definition
6.1
,

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}=2(\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathscr{C}+\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathscr{D}).

Proof.

It suffices to observe that the maps

({\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{0})\mapsto({\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0})\qquad\text{and}\qquad({\mathbf{A}}_{1},{\mathbf{B}}_{1})\mapsto({\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1},a({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1}))

are injective.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The dimension formula also holds true when
q
is even and
\mathscr{C}\diamond_{a}\mathscr{D}
defined as in Remark
6.2
. Indeed, as the map

({\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1},{\mathbf{B}}_{1})\mapsto({\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1},a({\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1})+{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0})

is injective.

Lemma 6.5
.

For
q
odd, the dual of the Plotkin construction
\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}
of matrix rank metric codes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
is the Plotkin construction of their duals. More precisely,

(\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D})^{\perp}=\mathscr{C}^{\perp}\diamondsuit_{a^{-1}}\mathscr{D}^{\perp}.

Proof.

Using Proposition
6.3
we observe that the
dimensions coincide. Therefore, it suffices to establish that

\textrm{Tr}({\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}}^{\top})=0
for any
{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}
and

{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp}\diamondsuit_{a^{-1}}\mathscr{D}^{\perp}
.
For
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in\mathscr{C},{\mathbf{B}}_{i}\in\mathscr{D}
and
\mathbf{S}_{i}\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp},\mathbf{U}_{i}\in\mathscr{D}^{\perp}
,

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}({\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\textrm{Tr}\left(\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}&\quad&{a({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1})}\\
&&\\
{{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}}&\quad&{{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}}&\quad&{\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{1}^{\top}}\\
&&\\
{a^{-1}(\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}_{1}^{\top})}&\quad&{\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\textrm{Tr}\Bigg(({\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0})(\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top})+({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1})(\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}_{1}^{\top})+({\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1})(\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{1}^{\top})+({\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0})(\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=2\textrm{Tr}\Bigg({\mathbf{A}}_{0}\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}+{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{1}^{\top}\Bigg)=0.

It completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 6.6
.

Note that the result does not hold when
q
is even for the codes
defined in Remark
6.2
. Still, one can prove that:

{(\mathscr{C}\diamond_{a}\mathscr{D})}^{\perp}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{1}&\quad&\mathbf{S}_{1}+\mathbf{U}_{1}\\
&\\
a(\mathbf{S}_{1}+\mathbf{U}_{1})+\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{1}&\quad&\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{1}+\mathbf{U}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{1}\end{pmatrix}~:~\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{S}_{i}\in\mathscr{C}^{\perp},\\
&\mathbf{U}_{i}\in\mathscr{D}^{\perp}\end{aligned}\right\}.

To obtain this structure, we started from rank Reed–Muller in the
Artin Schreier case and expressed the dual (which is also a rank
Reed–Muller code) in the dual basis
\mathcal{B}_{m}^{*}
as described in
Remark
3.9
.

6.2.
Decoding

In the sequel, for convenience’s sake we
restrict to the case of square matrices,
i.e.
,
m=n
.

6.2.1.
When
a
is not a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

If
a
is not a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then the decoding of such a code can
be performed by running in a same fashion as in Section
4

for binary rank metric Reed–Muller codes.

Precisely, suppose that we are given when given two codes

\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
and
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}

equipped with decoders such that
\mathscr{D}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
can correct
errors of rank
t
and
\mathscr{C}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
can correct “erasures”
of rank
t
,
i.e.
can decode when the error term has rank

\leq t
and its row space is contained in a known space
V
of
dimension
t
. Suppose that we are given

\mathbf{Y}={\mathbf{C}}+{\mathbf{E}},\quad\text{where}\quad{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D},\quad\text{and}\quad\text{Rk}\,{\mathbf{E}}\leq t.

Denote

{\mathbf{C}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+{\mathbf{B}}_{0}&a({\mathbf{A}}_{1}-{\mathbf{B}}_{1})\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+{\mathbf{B}}_{1}&{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-{\mathbf{B}}_{0}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{E}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}&{\mathbf{E}}_{01}\\
{\mathbf{E}}_{10}&{\mathbf{E}}_{11}\end{pmatrix}.

Choose a square root
\sqrt{a}
of
a
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.
Then one can perform the decoding as follows:

(1)

Fold
\mathbf{Y}
as

(20)

\mathbf{Fold}(\mathbf{Y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{Y}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
-\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{2}{{\sqrt{a}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\frac{1}{a}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}+{\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\frac{1}{a}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\ {\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}}.

(2)

The error term
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
has rank
\leq t
. Thus, apply the decoder of

\mathscr{D}
to the above folding to recover

\frac{2}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
and hence the pair
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}

(immediate since the
{\mathbf{B}}_{i}
’s have entries in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
while

\sqrt{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
).

(3)

With the knowledge of
{\mathbf{B}}_{0},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
we are reduced to the following
decoding problem:

(21)

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}&a{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}&{\mathbf{A}}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+{\mathbf{E}}.

Next, using a partial folding of
\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}
, we get the following decoding problem

(22)

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-\sqrt{a}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}\\
{\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}\end{pmatrix}

and under Assumption
2
below (which is very similar to Assumption
1

but without also involving recursive calls)
the row space of the error term is known. Applying our erasure
decoder to the lower part:

{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+({\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}),

we can recover
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}
and immediately deduce
the pair
{\mathbf{A}}_{0},{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
which yields the whole
{\mathbf{C}}
.

Assumption 2
.

In the process above, the folded error
{\mathbf{E}}^{\prime}
of (
20
)
has the same rank as
{\mathbf{E}}
.

6.2.2.
When
a
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

If

\mathbb{F}_{q}

of

\mathbf{Y}={\mathbf{C}}+{\mathbf{E}}
, the decoding will run as follows:

(1)

Fold
\mathbf{Y}
in two different manners:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Fold}_{1}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{Y}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\

\displaystyle\mathbf{Fold}_{2}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{Y}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{2}{{\sqrt{a}}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}-\frac{1}{{\sqrt{a}}}{\mathbf{E}}_{00}-\frac{1}{a}{\mathbf{E}}_{01}+{\mathbf{E}}_{10}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}.

(2)

Apply the decoder of
\mathscr{D}
to the two above foldings to recover

\frac{2}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
and
-\frac{2}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{B}}_{0}+2{\mathbf{B}}_{1}

{\mathbf{A}}_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+({\mathbf{E}}_{10}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11})\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{A}}_{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+({\mathbf{E}}_{10}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbf{E}}_{11}).

6.2.3.
The Artin–Schreier case

In characteristic
2
, the Artin–Schreier case can be decoded in a similar fashion by separating the cases when
a
can be written as
\alpha^{2}+\alpha
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
the case where
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

6.3.
Probability analysis

According to Assumption
1
, for the algorithm to succeed, we
need the following
folding
of
{\mathbf{E}}
to have same rank as

{\mathbf{E}}
. The folding has, up to scalar multiplication the following
form:

\text{Fold}({\mathbf{E}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&b\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}\begin{pmatrix}b^{\prime}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

for some nonzero
b,b^{\prime}
which are either elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
or elements
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}

whose squares are in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, depending on whether
a
is a square or a non square
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

To estimate the probability
that
\text{Fold}({\mathbf{E}})
has rank
t
we proceed in two steps.
First we evaluate the probability:

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Rk}\,\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&b\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}{\mathbf{E}}\right)=t\right).

Then, we evaluate the overall probability. It turns out that the probability behaves
differently if
a
is a square or a non square. Thus, we treat the two situations separately.

6.3.1.
If
a
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

Note first that the right kernel of
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&b\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}
has dimension
m
. Denote by
K
this kernel. Second,
note that since
{\mathbf{E}}
is a uniformly random
2m\times 2n
matrix of
rank
t
, its column space
U
is a uniformly random subspace of

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
of dimension
t
. For the rank not to collapse during the
first part of the folding we should have
U\cap K=\{0\}
. Here
we use the following statement.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
K
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
of dimension
m
and
U
be a
uniformly random subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
of dimension
t<m
.
Then

\mathbb{P}\left(U\cap K\neq 0\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{t-m-1}).

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}_{U}
be an arbitrary
t\times 2m
generator matrix of
U
,

i.e.
a matrix whose rows span
U
. Then
{\mathbf{M}}_{U}
is a
uniformly random full–rank
t\times 2m
matrix and for any

\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t}\setminus\{0\}
then
\mathbf{m}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}
is a uniformly
random vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{m}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}\in K\right)=\frac{q^{m}-1}{q^{2m}-1}=q^{-m}(1+\circ(1)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|U\cap K\setminus\{0\}|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{m}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}\in K\right)

\displaystyle=(q^{t}-1)q^{-m}(1+\circ(1))

\displaystyle=q^{t-m}(1+\circ(1))

Next, if
U\cap K\neq\{0\}
then it has cardinality
\geq q

and, by Markov inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(|U\cap K\setminus\{0\}|\geq q-1\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(|U\cap K\setminus\{0\}|\right)}{q-1}.

Hence the result.
∎

As a consequence of this lemma, the probability that the first folding
keeps rank
t
is in
1-\mathcal{O}(q^{t-m-1})
.
For the second folding, assuming that it has rank
t
, then
the obtained matrix has a row space
V
of dimension
t
which is
nothing but the row space of
{\mathbf{E}}
. Hence it is a uniformly random
space of dimension
t
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
.

Denote by
K^{\prime}
the left kernel of
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b^{\prime}\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}
, then,
according to Lemma
6.7
,

\mathbb{P}\left(V\cap K^{\prime}\neq 0\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{t-n-1}).

In summary, the probability that both folding have rank
t
is

1-\mathcal{O}(q^{t-\min(m,n)-1})
.

6.3.2.
If
a
is not a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

In this situation, then
b,b^{\prime}
are in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Moreover,
similarly to the case of rank metric Reed–Muller codes studied in
Section
3
we assume
b^{2}={b^{\prime}}^{2}=a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For
the first folding, one can do the same reasoning with the only
difficulty that the column space
U
of
{\mathbf{E}}
is in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
and
the right kernel of
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&b\mathbf{I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}
is in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{2m}
. However, this situation is even better
as explains the following lemma.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
K
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{2m}
of dimension
m
.
Let
U
be
a uniformly random subspace of dimension
t
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
and

U_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}U\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(U_{2}\cap K\neq 0\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{2t-2m-2}).

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}_{U}
be an arbitrary
t\times 2m
generator matrix of
U
. Let

b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Elements of
U_{2}
are

\mathbf{m}_{1}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}+b\mathbf{m}_{2}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}
for
\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t}
.
Similarly to the proof of Lemma
6.7
,
\mathbf{m}_{1}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}+b\mathbf{m}_{2}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}

turns out to be a uniformly random element of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{2m}
and we deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{m}_{1}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}+b\mathbf{m}_{2}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}\in K\right)=q^{-2m}(1+\circ(1)).

Next,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|U_{2}\cap K\setminus\{0\}|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2t}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{m}_{1}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}+b\mathbf{m}_{2}{\mathbf{M}}_{U}\in K\right)

\displaystyle=q^{2t-2m}.

Finally, as for Lemma
6.7
, Markov inequality permits to
conclude.
∎

For the second folding, the situation gets more complicated since
the obtained matrix after the first folding is not a uniformly
random matrix of rank
t
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{m\times 2n}
. Indeed its row
space
V_{2}
is actually
V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
where
V
is the row space
of
{\mathbf{E}}
. In short, its row space has a basis in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
which
does not hold for any such rank
t
matrix in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{m\times 2n}
. Still, one can prove that its row space is uniform among the

t
–dimensional spaces of the form
V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Then, the
following statement will permit to conclude.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
V
be a uniformly random subspace of dimension
t

of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
. Let
K
be the left kernel of
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b^{\prime}\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left((V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})\cap K\neq\{0\}\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{2t-2n-2}).

Proof.

First let us prove that any nonzero element
\mathbf{v}
of the left kernel of
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}
satisfies
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}+b\mathbf{v}_{1}
, were
\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
are linearly independent.
Indeed suppose
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\lambda\mathbf{v}_{1}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then,

\mathbf{v}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(\lambda+b)\mathbf{v}_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{v}_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=0.

Recall that
\mathbf{v}_{1}
has entries in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and denote

\mathbf{v}_{10},\mathbf{v}_{11}
its left and right halves. That is to say

\mathbf{v}_{1}=(\mathbf{v}_{10}~|~\mathbf{v}_{11})
.
Then, the above equality yields
\mathbf{v}_{10}+b\mathbf{v}_{11}=0
and since
\mathbf{v}_{10},\mathbf{v}_{11}

have entries in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
while
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we deduce that
\mathbf{v}_{1}=0

and hence
\mathbf{v}=0
.

Now, let
{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t\times 2n}
be a full rank matrix whose rows span
V
. An element of

V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
has the shape

(\mathbf{m}_{0}+b\mathbf{m}_{1}){\mathbf{M}}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{m}_{0},\mathbf{m}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t}.

Given a pair of linearly independent vectors

\mathbf{m}_{0},\mathbf{m}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t}
, then
(\mathbf{m}_{0}+b\mathbf{m}_{1}){\mathbf{M}}
is of the
form
\mathbf{v}_{0}+b\mathbf{v}_{1}
with
\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
linearly
independent. Moreover,
(\mathbf{m}_{0}+b\mathbf{m}_{1}){\mathbf{M}}
is uniformly random among such vectors.
Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((\mathbf{m}_{0}+b\mathbf{m}_{1}){\mathbf{M}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\ker_{\text{Left}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right|}{\left|\{\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}+b\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{2n}~|~\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}\ \text{linearly independent}\}\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2n}-1}{(q^{2n}-1)(q^{2n}-q)}=\frac{1}{q^{2n}-q}\cdot

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left(|V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\cap K|\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{0},\mathbf{m}_{1}}\mathbb{P}\left((\mathbf{m}_{0}+b\mathbf{m}_{1}){\mathbf{M}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
b\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=0\right)

where the sum is taken over all the pairs
\mathbf{m}_{0},\mathbf{m}_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{t}
. Note that the above probability is
0
if
\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2}
are collinear and
1/(q^{2n}-q)
otherwise, which yields

\mathbb{E}\left(|V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\cap K|\right)=\frac{(q^{t}-1)(q^{t}-q)}{q^{2n}-q}=\mathcal{O}(q^{2t-2n}).

Finally, by Markov inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\cap K\neq 0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(|V\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\cap K\neq 0|\geq q^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{2t-2n-2}.)

∎

With these lemmas, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Rk}\,(\text{Fold}({\mathbf{E}})<t)\right)=\mathcal{O}(q^{2t-2\min(m,n)-2})
.

\mathscr{D}

\mathcal{O}(mn)+C(t)+D(t)

\mathscr{C}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
and
t

Remark 6.11

Of course, exactly as in the case of rank metric binary Reed–Muller codes

6.5.
An instantiation with Gabidulin codes

The Plotkin construction gives a scheme to obtain efficiently decodable rank metric codes by combining a pair of rank metric codes with efficient decoders. As mentioned before, there are essentially three families of structured codes previously known to have efficient decoding algorithms and they are mostly
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
-linear: Gabidulin codes, LRPC codes and Simple codes. However, these codes as components of the Plotkin construction can provide new families of rank metric codes with efficient decoding algorithms. We see below such an example of Plotkin construction of a code by considering a pair
(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})
of Gabidulin codes.

Let
\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times m}
be two matrix representation
of Gabidulin codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
of respective
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
–dimensions

k_{1},k_{2}
. Recall that these codes have respective minimum distances

m-k_{1}+1
and
m-k_{2}+1
. Moreover, Loidreau’s algorithm
[
31
]

permits to decode
\mathscr{D}
up to
\frac{m-k_{2}}{2}
in
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})

operations in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and it is possible to correct up to
m-k_{1}

rank erasures for
\mathscr{C}
. Suppose that
m=2k_{1}-k_{2}
so that

(23)

t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(m-k_{1})=\frac{m-k_{2}}{2}\cdot

Then, according to Proposition
6.3
and
Theorem
6.10
, the code
\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}
has
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–dimension

2m(k_{1}+k_{2})
and benefits from a decoder correcting up to
t
errors.

Remark 6.12
.

To conclude, note that
\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}
contains some

\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}_{0}&(0)\\
(0)&{\mathbf{A}}_{0}\end{pmatrix},

with
{\mathbf{A}}_{0}\in\mathscr{C}
of weight
m-k_{1}+1
. Hence the minimum distance
of
\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}
is less than
2m-2k_{1}+2
. If

m-k_{2}>2
, then (
23
) entails that
the latter minimum distance is strictly below the rank Singleton
bound
2m-k_{1}-k_{2}+1
. Therefore,
\mathscr{C}\diamondsuit_{a}\mathscr{D}
is never
MRD and hence cannot be equivalent to a Gabidulin code. The obtained
family of rank–metric codes is hence a new one equipped with an

6.6.
About the error model

Back to the
case of Rank Reed–Muller case and suppose that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
and

\mathbb{L}
is some
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m}
–extension of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Take note that, for this subsection, we go back to the notation of the
previous sections where
m
denotes the number of generators of the
Galois group. Our codes are spaces of
N\times N
matrices where

N=2^{m}
. Let us consider the code
\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{Q}}(r,m)
for some
nonnegative integer
m
and let
t=2^{m-r-1}-1
be the number of errors we
expect to correct.

Let
p
be a prime integer that splits totally in
\mathbb{L}
;
Chebotarev density theorem asserts the existence of infinitely many
such primes. Here let us consider an error model where errors are
matrices
{\mathbf{E}}
of rank
\leq t
whose entries lie in some interval

[0,T]
for some
T\geq p
and whose reduction
\overline{{\mathbf{E}}}

modulo
p
also has rank
t
.

Under this error model, one can check that Assumption
1
holds
for a single folding if it holds modulo
p
. Thus, from
Section
6.3.1
, the modulo
p
case gives a lower
bound for the probability that the folding of the error keeps the same
rank: it is at least
1-\mathcal{O}(p^{t-N-1})
. Since to decode

\text{RM}_{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{Q}}(r,m)
we will perform
\mathcal{O}(r)
recursive calls,
Assumption
1
holds with a probability at least

1-\mathcal{O}(rp^{t-N-1})=1-\mathcal{O}(rp^{2^{m-r-1}-2^{m}-2}).

In short, the failure probability is doubly exponentially close to
0
.

7.
Conclusion

We proposed a novel algorithm for decoding
“binary–like” rank metric Reed–Muller codes. This decoder rests on
the use of a recursive structure that we identified on these codes. In
its design, this decoder is extremely simple and basically boils down
to linear algebra. If compared to the algorithm proposed in

[
13
]
, this new algorithm might fail on some rare instances, but has a
much better complexity.

Finally, our research on rank Reed–Muller codes led us to identify
what a natural rank analogue of the Plotkin
(u~|~u+v)
construction
could be. This novel construction gives a generic frame to design new
families of efficiently decodable rank metric codes from old ones. We
expect this novel operation on rank metric codes to open interesting
perspectives in the near future.
