Incidence and risk factors of poor prognosis in patients with OVCF following day surgery of percutaneous kyphoplasty: a retrospective study
Authors: Minghang Li, Yiming Zhang, Lianyong Wang, Jinglong Liu, Qingqian Zhao, Chao Chen, Hanming Bian, Gang Liu, Ye Tian, Yang Liu, Wei Zhang, Xigao Cheng, Qiang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrevious studies have shown that percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) performed as a day surgery achieves similar efficacy to inpatient treatment. However, with the increasing adoption of day-case PKP, some patients experience poor postoperative prognosis, such as delayed discharge, new-onset vertebral fractures, and persistent low back pain. Despite this, research on the risk factors associated with poor prognosis following day-case PKP remains limited. This study aims to investigate the risk factors for poor prognosis in patients undergoing day-case PKP.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 424 patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) who underwent day-case PKP in the Spine Surgery Department of Tianjin Hospital between January 2020 and December 2022. Detailed clinical data were collected for each patient, including age, sex, height, weight, history of hypertension, diabetes, prior fractures, smoking history, length of hospital stay, bone cement volume, paraspinal muscle area and fat infiltration, as well as Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores. Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis were performed to identify risk factors for poor prognosis following day-case PKP.ResultsUnivariate analysis revealed significant differences between the new vertebral fracture group and the control group in terms of QCT values (X²=8.933, P = 0.003), history of prior fractures (X²=19.882, P < 0.001), and FCSA (t = 2.626, P = 0.009). Significant differences were also found between the persistent low back pain group and the control group regarding smoking history (X²=9.425, P = 0.002) and Fat Infiltration Percentage (FI%) (t = 2.962, P = 0.003). Patients in the delayed discharge group had higher preoperative ODI scores (78.462 ± 6.555 vs. 73.198 ± 12.375, P = 0.033). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified QCT values (OR = 0.112, P = 0.035), history of prior fractures (OR = 5.266, P < 0.001), and FCSA (OR = 0.935, P = 0.031) as independent risk factors for new-onset vertebral fractures following day-case PKP (P < 0.05). Smoking history (OR = 4.793, P = 0.002) and FI% (OR = 1.059, P = 0.001) were found to be independent risk factors for persistent low back pain (P < 0.05).ConclusionLow bone mineral density, history of old fractures and smaller FCSA were independent risk factors for new vertebral fractures in patients. Smoking history and FI% increased the risk of residual low back pain in patients at 12 months postoperatively. Higher preoperative ODI scores were the main reason for patients’ delayed discharge.Keywords: Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, Day-case PKP, New-onset vertebral fracture, Delayed discharge, Residual low back pain, Paravertebral muscle area, Paravertebral muscle fat infiltration

Introduction

With the accelerating global aging process, osteoporosis has become a major challenge in elderly healthcare management [1]. According to the first national epidemiological survey on osteoporosis, the prevalence of osteoporosis among individuals over the age of 50 was 19.2%, rising significantly to 32.0% in those aged 65 and above [2]. Among osteoporosis-related complications, osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) have drawn considerable attention due to their high incidence and substantial clinical impact [3, 4]. OVCF is defined as a compression fracture of the vertebral body occurring in osteoporotic patients following low-energy trauma. Notably, in postmenopausal women in China, the incidence of OVCF exhibits a clear age-dependent pattern, increasing from 13.4% to 58.1% with advancing age [5]. These patients often experience severe pain, leading to significant functional limitations that greatly impact their quality of life. Additionally, they impose a substantial burden on both the healthcare system and family caregiving resources [6, 7].

Currently, the treatment options for OVCF include conservative management and surgical intervention [8, 9]. Conservative treatment requires prolonged bed rest for symptom relief and fracture healing; however, extended immobilization may lead to severe complications such as deep vein thrombosis and hypostatic pneumonia, which can be life-threatening. Among surgical approaches, percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) is the most commonly used technique. This minimally invasive procedure involves the injection of bone cement into the fractured vertebral body under fluoroscopic guidance, providing immediate biomechanical stability and pain relief. With advancements in surgical instruments and navigation technology, PKP has become an effective inpatient treatment for OVCF [10]. However, the traditional inpatient model typically requires a postoperative observation period of 5 to 15 days, leading to increased consumption of medical resources and financial burden on patients. A healthcare economics study in the United States estimated that the annual hospitalization cost for OVCF treatment reaches $746 million, with projections exceeding $25 billion by 2025 [11].

Against this backdrop, the day-surgery model has gained increasing attention due to its efficiency and cost-effectiveness. This model requires patients to complete the entire hospitalization process, from admission to discharge, within 48 h. It has been shown to effectively reduce healthcare expenditures and accelerate functional recovery [12]. The United Kingdom’s National Health Service (NHS) has implemented day-surgery programs, saving approximately £2 billion in medical expenses annually. Existing evidence-based medical research indicates that for OVCF patients with intact neurological function, day-case PKP offers significant advantages in shortening hospital stays and reducing direct medical costs. Furthermore, postoperative follow-up data show no statistically significant difference in pain relief rates and vertebral height restoration compared to traditional inpatient treatment [13]. However, in clinical practice, some day-surgery patients still experience adverse outcomes such as new-onset vertebral fractures, residual back pain, or delayed discharge, highlighting the need for further optimization of current treatment protocols.

Most existing studies on PKP-related complications have focused on traditional inpatient models. Some studies have identified risk factors for new-onset vertebral fractures, including advanced age, number of treated vertebrae, bone mineral density (BMD), number of injured vertebrae, and history of previous fractures [14]. Other studies have suggested that rib fractures, body mass index (BMI), spinal cord or nerve compression, infection, and adjacent vertebral fractures may contribute to residual pain following PKP [15]. These findings help clinicians assess patient prognosis and provide a theoretical basis for preventing postoperative complications. However, most of these studies have been conducted in inpatient settings, and there is currently a lack of research investigating the risk factors for poor prognosis and delayed discharge in the day-case PKP model. With the rapid global promotion of the day-case surgical model, relevant research is urgently needed to provide a theoretical basis for prognostic assessment in day-case surgery patients [16].

Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the safety and efficacy of day-case PKP for treating OVCF and to analyze the incidence and risk factors associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, including residual back pain, new-onset OVCF, and delayed discharge in day-surgery patients.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Hospital, Tianjin, China. A total of 424 patients who underwent day-case PKP for OVCF in the Department of Spine Surgery at Tianjin Hospital between January 2020 and December 2022 were enrolled (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1)Patients with single-segment osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures without severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases. (2)Follow-up duration exceeding 12 months with complete follow-up data. (3)Patients who were able to tolerate the surgical procedure and maintain a prone position for at least 60 min. (4)Patients with unstable fractured vertebrae. (5)Patients experiencing significant localized pain that markedly affected daily life.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1)Pathological fractures, including those caused by tumors, hemangiomas, or other malignancies. (2)Patients with infections at the surgical site. (3)Patients allergic to iodine-based contrast agents. (4)Patients with spinal canal stenosis, coagulation disorders, or neurological deficits.

Definition of poor prognosis: (1)Residual low back pain: Defined as a visual analog scale (VAS) score > 4 at the 12-month follow-up. (2)New-onset OVCF: The occurrence of an additional vertebral fracture within 12 months of follow-up (Fig. 2). (3)Delayed discharge: A total hospitalization duration exceeding 48 h.

Data collection

Patient information was obtained from electronic medical records and included the following parameters: age, sex, height, weight, hypertension, diabetes, history of previous fractures, smoking history, presence of chronic OVCF, length of hospital stay, volume of injected bone cement, and whether delayed discharge occurred.

Radiographic data were collected to assess volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD), fracture location, vertebral height restoration ratio, cross-sectional area (CSA) of the paraspinal muscles at the L3 level, psoas major muscle area, and percentage of fat infiltration in the paraspinal muscles.

Patients were assessed for postoperative outcomes through in-person follow-up visits or telephone interviews at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Outcomes included changes in Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and VAS scores as well as the occurrence of new-onset vertebral fractures. All radiological measurements were independently conducted by two senior spine surgeons, and the final values were determined by calculating the mean of both measurements. To minimize bias, all authors involved in data collection and analysis were blinded to patient outcomes throughout the study. We incorporate rigorous quantitative reliability analyses using ICC statistics. The standard of consistency evaluation is that ICC ≥ 0.75, excellent consistency; 0.75 > ICC ≥ 0.4, good consistency.

Surgical procedure and perioperative management

All patients in this study underwent PKP was adopted in this study. Preoperative evaluations, including anesthesia assessments, were completed before hospital admission, and surgery was scheduled in advance. Patients underwent PKP within 24 h of admission and were mobilized 2 h postoperatively with the aid of a spinal brace. Prior to discharge, nurses provided education on lumbar and back muscle strengthening exercises. Patients were discharged within 24 h of admission. During surgery, patients were placed in a prone position. A C-arm fluoroscope was used to localize the target vertebra. After fluoroscopic confirmation, the surgical field was marked, disinfected with povidone-iodine, and draped with sterile covers. Local infiltration anesthesia was administered using 1% lidocaine around the marked site. Once adequate anesthesia was achieved, a 0.5 cm longitudinal incision was made at the marked site, sequentially dissecting the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and deep fascia to expose the underlying bone. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the puncture needle was inserted into the pedicle and advanced under lateral fluoroscopy until it reached the anterior-middle third of the vertebral body. The needle was then withdrawn, and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) bone cement was prepared and injected into the vertebral body under continuous fluoroscopic monitoring.The volume of injected cement was 2–3 mL for thoracic vertebrae and 3–5 mL for lumbar vertebrae. After cement solidification, the trocar was withdrawn, and the incision was closed with sterile dressings. Postoperative X-ray imaging was performed immediately to evaluate vertebral morphology restoration. All procedures were conducted under local anesthesia with monitored anesthesia care.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and analyzed using the χ² test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the t-test. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify influencing factors. For correlation analyses, Pearson’s correlation was used for normally distributed data, while Spearman’s correlation was applied to skewed and ordinal data. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General patient characteristics and clinical prognosis

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 331 adult patients who underwent single-segment day-case PKP were included in this study, comprising 45 males and 286 females, with a mean age of 71.07 ± 8.85 years. The average BMI was 24.34 ± 3.41 kg/m², and the mean vBMD was 68.67 ± 28.44 g/cm³. The mean preoperative vertebral height was 23.81 ± 3.8 mm, which increased to 25.24 ± 3.85 mm postoperatively (Table 1). Regarding pain relief and functional improvement, the mean preoperative VAS score was 6.78 ± 0.89, which significantly improved to 1.98 ± 0.9 at the final follow-up (P < 0.05). The mean preoperative ODI score for all patients was 73.61 ± 12.08, which significantly improved to 21.94 ± 6.14 at the final follow-up (P < 0.05). Postoperatively, 30 patients experienced residual low back pain, 23 patients developed recurrent OVCF, and 26 patients had a hospital stay exceeding 48 h. The results demonstrate interrater reliability (ICC = 0.91) and intrarater reliability (ICC = 0.87). The data consistency over excellent consistency standards.

Discussion

OVCF is a common fracture type in the elderly population, with an increasing incidence as population aging accelerates. This has become a major challenge in terms of public health resource allocation and economic burden on patients [17]. The day-case surgical model, due to its efficiency and cost-effectiveness, has gradually emerged as an important strategy for optimizing healthcare resource utilization [18]. In China, day-case PKP is defined as the completion of admission, surgery, and discharge within 24 h, with a maximum hospital stay of 48 h in special cases. Although day-case PKP offers notable advantages in shortening hospital stays and reducing direct medical costs, some patients still experience new-onset vertebral fractures, delayed discharge, and residual low back pain postoperatively [19]. While numerous studies have investigated factors associated with inpatient PKP outcomes, the risk factors affecting poor prognosis in patients undergoing day-case PKP remain unclear [20].

Risk factors for new-onset vertebral fractures after PKP

New-onset vertebral fractures are a major concern affecting post-PKP quality of life. A study reported that 27.8% of OVCF patients experienced new-onset vertebral fractures after PKP, with most fractures occurring in adjacent vertebrae [20]. A recent study by Mills et al. found that bone cement augmentation does not increase the risk of new vertebral fractures, and that low BMD may be the primary contributing factor [21]. Moreover, osteoporosis medication has been shown to reduce this risk by 19%. Similarly, Cheng et al. identified low vertebral HU values as an independent risk factor for new-onset vertebral fractures following vertebral augmentation [22]. Schreiber et al. [23] further demonstrated that lower HU values are indicative of reduced BMD. In our study, we utilized vBMD measurements, which provide a more precise assessment of bone density, to analyze potential risk factors. Our findings revealed that low vBMD is an independent risk factor for new-onset vertebral fractures after day-case PKP, aligning with previous research conclusions [23]. Moreover, we found that a history of previous fractures also significantly increased the risk of new-onset vertebral fractures. This aligns with research by Ning et al., who reported that patients with prior fractures are more prone to subsequent fractures [24]. Relevant studies have shown that prolonged bed rest or localized immobilization accelerates systemic and localized bone loss, thereby exacerbating the severity of osteoporosis [25]. This bone loss occurs immediately after surgery or immobilization, and the reduction in mechanical loading on the limbs is a key risk factor for subsequent fractures [26–28]. Therefore, osteoporosis management is crucial for preventing new-onset vertebral fractures after PKP in OVCF patients. While osteoporosis medications are commonly recommended, the detrimental effects of immobilization on BMD are often overlooked. The day-case PKP model enables patients to shorten hospital stays and resume daily activities earlier, thereby reducing the risk of disuse osteoporosis.

Our study also identified a reduced FCSA of the paraspinal muscles as an independent risk factor for new-onset vertebral fractures. The vertebrae, muscles, and nervous system collectively maintain spinal stability, and dysfunction in any of these components can lead to spinal impairment. Ogon et al. reported that a smaller CSA of the paraspinal muscles weakens their shock absorption and stabilizing function when the spine is subjected to internal or external loads [29]. Previous studies have suggested that paraspinal muscle degeneration, characterized by muscle atrophy and increased fat infiltration, may be a contributing factor to the increased risk of new-onset vertebral fractures [30]. Our results showed that FCSA was significantly lower in the new-onset vertebral fracture group compared to the non-fracture group (P < 0.05). This may be due to paraspinal muscle atrophy, which leads to reduced muscular support and increased compressive forces on the anterior column, thereby elevating the risk of vertebral fractures [31]. Additionally, a reduction in paraspinal muscle mass may contribute to lower BMD, further increasing fracture susceptibility [32]. Interestingly, our study also found that patients with multiple vertebral fractures exhibited higher levels of paraspinal muscle fat infiltration, suggesting a potential underlying mechanism that warrants further investigation.

Risk factors for delayed discharge after PKP

One of the primary objectives of implementing day-case PKP is to optimize healthcare resource allocation and reduce the financial burden on patients. Additionally, minimizing patient-to-patient and doctor-to-patient contact time can help reduce the risk of potential disease transmission [33]. However, in clinical practice, some patients fail to be discharged as planned, leading to delayed discharge. In analyzing the potential causes of delayed discharge, we found no significant differences in BMD, history of fractures, BMI, FCSA, or FI% between the delayed and non-delayed groups. However, higher ODI scores on the day before surgery and on the first postoperative day were identified as the primary factors associated with delayed discharge. Interestingly, VAS scores did not differ significantly between the two groups. Although radiological characteristics did not show significant variations, patients with higher preoperative and postoperative day 1 ODI scores were more likely to experience delayed discharge. This suggests that subjective perception of functional status may influence discharge decisions through the pain-avoidance behavior cycle, prompting some patients to request prolonged hospitalization despite the absence of severe pain. Furthermore, even if postoperative pain is fully relieved, perioperative adverse events such as orthostatic intolerance, postoperative nausea or vomiting, and unsatisfactory rehabilitation approaches can still prolong hospital stays [34–36]. In our cohort, however, the predefined analysis focused on functional status as the main correlate of delayed discharge. We observed that patients remaining hospitalized for > 48 h had significantly higher ODI scores both preoperatively and on postoperative day 1, whereas pain scores did not differ substantially across time points. Importantly, we did not collect standardized data on perioperative complications or rehabilitation adherence, which limited our ability to analyze these domains. Future prospective studies will therefore incorporate systematic capture of perioperative events and rehabilitation adherence indicators, allowing quantification of their independent contributions to delayed discharge in day-case PKP.

Risk factors for residual low back pain after PKP

This study investigated risk factors for residual low back pain following day-case PKP and identified smoking and fat infiltration of the paraspinal muscles as independent risk factors. Previous studies have reported that patients experience significant pain relief after vertebral augmentation, yet some patients continue to suffer from persistent low back pain [37]. In our study, residual low back pain was defined as a VAS score ≥ 4 at the 12-month follow-up, as this level of pain significantly affects sleep quality and daily life. Among patients with single-level vertebral fractures, the incidence of residual low back pain was 9.06% (30 cases). Several factors have been proposed as contributors to post-PKP residual pain, including bone cement leakage, spinal cord or nerve compression, BMI, BMD, nonunion of fractures, and paraspinal fascial injury [38, 39]. Our findings suggest that FI% and smoking are independent risk factors for residual low back pain after day-case PKP. Multiple studies have shown that paraspinal muscle degeneration and fat infiltration are strongly associated with low back pain [40, 41]. Paraspinal muscle fatty degeneration is increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to chronic low back pain [42]. However, the underlying mechanisms linking fat infiltration and back pain remain unclear. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including inflammatory processes, denervation, and chronic disuse atrophy [43]. Postoperative back pain following day-case PKP may result from multiple interacting pathophysiological mechanisms. Additionally, smoking was identified as an independent risk factor for residual low back pain, a finding consistent with previous studies [44]. Notably, current smokers exhibited higher pain scores compared to former smokers and lifelong non-smokers. Notably, current smokers exhibited higher pain scores compared to former smokers and lifelong non-smokers. Smoking may impair vertebral healing after PKP by reducing local blood supply, suppressing osteoblast activity, and promoting chronic inflammation, which together increase the risk of non-union or pseudoarthrosis [45, 46]. Such impaired healing can lead to persistent micro-motion at the fracture site or suboptimal bone–cement integration, both of which are structural sources of pain. Additionally, smoking-related systemic inflammation and nociceptor sensitization may further lower the pain threshold, making residual back pain more likely [47]. Therefore, we recommend that patients undergoing day-case PKP should quit smoking and engage in appropriate back muscle exercises to reduce the risk of postoperative residual pain [48].

Limitations

Despite its findings, this study has several limitations. First, all cases were obtained from a single clinical center, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Future large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are needed to validate our findings. Additionally, due to the long follow-up duration, some patients were unable to complete all scheduled follow-up assessments. This loss to follow-up bias may have impacted the validity of the study results. In addition, the limitation of this study is the lack of vertebral healing assessment based on imaging. Without standardized assessment of fracture healing, we cannot determine the specific impact of nonunion and other factors on chronic residual pain. Future prospective work will incorporate imaging endpoints to quantify the role of nonunion after PKP in long-term prognosis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that day-case PKP is a safe and effective treatment for OVCF. However, some patients experience adverse postoperative outcomes, including residual low back pain, new-onset OVCF, and delayed discharge. For the first time, this study explores the risk factors for new-onset vertebral fractures, delayed discharge, and residual low back pain in the context of day-case PKP. Data analysis revealed that low BMD, a history of previous fractures, and reduced FCSA of the paraspinal muscles were independent risk factors for new-onset vertebral fractures. Additionally, smoking history and increased FI% were associated with a higher risk of residual low back pain at 12 months postoperatively. Furthermore, a higher preoperative ODI score was identified as the primary factor contributing to delayed discharge. These findings suggest that clinicians should pay closer attention to high-risk patients when implementing and expanding the clinical application of day-case PKP, with the goal of improving patient outcomes.
