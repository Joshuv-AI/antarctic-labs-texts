Assessing the Completeness of Adverse Event Reporting in Clinical Trials of Psoriasis Treatments: A Registry–Publication Comparison Study
Authors: Madeline Rowe, Madison Dinh, Ryan Sherry, Kellen Keefer, Rachel Hazlitt, Ahmed Elghzali, Daniel Archer, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesThe interventional landscape for psoriasis has expanded considerably with the introduction of systemic and biologic agents, intensifying the importance of comprehensive adverse event (AE) documentation to inform safe clinical practice. While regulatory initiatives such as the Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act Final Rule (FDAAA Final Rule) were designed to enhance clinical trial transparency, disparities between AE data presented in ClinicalTrials.gov and peer-reviewed literature continue to occur, potentially complicating interpretation of publicly available harms information used in evidence synthesis and clinical decision-making. Variability in how serious adverse events (SAEs), other adverse events (OAEs), deaths, and treatment discontinuations due to AE are documented can distort safety perceptions and influence clinical practice. This study compares AE reporting between ClinicalTrials.gov and corresponding peer-reviewed publications for psoriasis clinical trials and evaluates whether completeness and concordance differ before versus after implementation of the FDAAA Final Rule.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional analysis using systematic retrieval of psoriasis trials from ClinicalTrials.gov with results posted between 2009 and 2024. Data on AEs–encompassing SAEs, OAEs, treatment discontinuation due to AE, and deaths–were independently abstracted by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through adjudication. Trials were categorized according to FDA regulatory status, and variations in AE documentation were examined using descriptive statistics, chi-squared tests, Bland–Altman and funnel plots, and regression modeling.ResultsThe study’s primary endpoint was completeness and agreement of AE documentation between ClinicalTrials.gov and corresponding peer-reviewed publications across four predefined domains: SAEs, OAEs, discontinuation due to AE, and death. Pre-Final Rule AE reporting patterns were mixed across domains. After implementation of the FDAAA Final Rule, registry entries more consistently reported SAEs, OAEs, and mortality than corresponding publications. Although death documentation in registry entries showed improvement after the rule’s enactment, publications demonstrated persistent inconsistency. Following implementation of the FDAAA Final Rule, SAEs were present in 53% of registry records versus 40% of publications. OAEs were captured in 51% of registry entries but only 22% of publications. Disparities in SAE totals occurred in over 90% of trials, frequently with registry entries recording higher counts. Numerous discrepancies stemmed from incomplete calculations, ambiguous characterizations, or AE information confined to narrative text. Both visual and statistical assessments revealed a pattern of underreporting in publications, with minimal temporal improvement irrespective of trial scale or sponsorship type.ConclusionsNotwithstanding current regulatory frameworks, AE documentation for psoriasis trials remains fragmented with substantial inconsistency between registry and published data. These shortcomings undermine rigorous safety assessment and evidence-based clinical recommendations. Improved patient care requires increased integration of registry data into evidence synthesis, better clarification of AE definitions, and standardized AE reporting practices.RegistrationPreregistered on The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251081207) and Open Science Framework (4jaz3).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01561-z.

Introduction

The therapeutic landscape for psoriasis has rapidly expanded, offering dermatologists a wide range of options to tailor treatment to individual patient needs [1]. While topical agents remain first-line for mild-to-moderate disease, patients with more severe and treatment-resistant psoriasis often require systemic agents, including traditional immunosuppressants such as methotrexate and cyclosporine, or newer biologics that target key immune pathways such as IL-17, IL-23, or TNF-α [2–15]. As treatment strategies become increasingly personalized, clinicians must weigh efficacy, safety, comorbidities, and patient preferences when selecting an appropriate regimen.

Clinicians consider both the likelihood of symptomatic improvement—such as reductions in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores—and the risk of adverse events (AEs) when determining the most appropriate therapy. Many psoriasis treatments are used long term and in patients with comorbid conditions such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, or diabetes [16]. As a result, concerns about serious infections, hepatotoxicity, malignancy, and other safety outcomes are often central to clinical decision-making. Reliable AE data from clinical trials, product labeling, and postmarket surveillance help dermatologists set expectations, guide monitoring strategies, and counsel patients effectively. When this information is incomplete or selectively reported, treatment decisions may be based on an inaccurate assessment of risk [17, 18].

Although peer-reviewed publications are widely used to interpret clinical trial findings, product labeling provides the definitive clinician-facing safety summary, and ClinicalTrials.gov results reporting offers a standardized public format that can complement published reports and support evidence synthesis; harms data are also required to be reported on ClinicalTrials.gov under the Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act of 2007 (FDAAA 801) and its 2017 Final Rule (Final Rule) [19]. However, it remains unclear how consistently or completely AE data are reported across these two sources. This unknown raises concerns about the accuracy and completeness of safety data that inform prescribing, patient counseling, and guideline development. To date, no study has systematically evaluated the completeness and concordance of AE reporting in psoriasis trials. This study compares AE data between ClinicalTrials.gov and peer-reviewed publications for clinical trials (CTs) of psoriasis treatments to identify reporting discrepancies and promote greater transparency in dermatologic research.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a registry-to-publication comparison study to evaluate AE reporting in psoriasis clinical trials, analyzing multiple sources of trial-level data. We report our methods adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines where applicable (systematic search and study selection) and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational cross-sectional reporting, due to a nontraditional intervention meta-analysis [20].

Our methodology was adapted from established protocols [17, 18]. The Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences Institutional Review Board determined this study did not meet the definition of human subjects research and was exempt from oversight under 45 CFR 46.102(d) and (f) [21]. The protocol was preregistered on The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [22]. No financial support was received for the conduct of this study, or for the preparation or publication of this manuscript.

Search Strategy

On 28 June 2025, two independent reviewers (M.R. and M.D.) conducted a systematic search of ClinicalTrials.gov to identify phase 2–4 or “not applicable” trials with posted results between 27 September 2009 and 31 December 2024. This temporal scope was selected to align with the Final Rule, which mandates results submission within 1 year of trial completion (42 CFR §11.44) [23]. Our analysis included only trials with publicly available results, excluding any with reporting delays or withheld outcomes.

To enhance search precision, we manually constructed a search query using psoriasis-specific terms, quoted phrases, and Boolean operators, rather than relying on ClinicalTrials.gov’s automatic synonym expansion (using the Unified Medical Language System) [24]. The complete search strategy is publicly available on the Open Science Framework (OSF) [25].

Two reviewers (M.D. and M.R.) independently identified corresponding publications by screening links on ClinicalTrials.gov and conducting supplemental manual searches in PubMed (via the National Center for Biotechnology Information [NCBI]) and Google Scholar. The search excluded other online databases, gray literature, and requests for unpublished data from trial authors.

We used ClinicalTrials.gov as the sole source for AE data due to its standardized, FDAAA 801-mandated reporting format, which is well-suited for comparison with publications. Consistent with prior safety assessments, registries such as the European Union Clinical Trials Register and World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were excluded for inconsistent harms reporting [26, 27]. Data were cleaned and de-duplicated in Google Sheets using its native filter functions, and a full list of retrieved records is available on the OSF.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies were registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with posted results and evaluated a primary intervention for psoriasis. These interventions included pharmacologic (e.g., methotrexate, cyclosporine) and biologic (e.g., TNF-α or IL-23 inhibitors) therapies. We excluded studies that were phase 1, observational, pilot, or feasibility designs, lacking a peer-reviewed publication, enrolling an unrelated population, or not reporting psoriasis-specific AEs.

Dataset Criteria and Selection

Our analysis included two trial categories: those with mandatory FDA reporting requirements and those without such requirements that were still relevant for assessing AE reporting in psoriasis research before versus after implementation of the FDAAA Final Rule. Trials that began on or after 18 January 2017 were assessed for applicable clinical trial (ACT) status using the criteria outlined in the ACT checklist, as defined by 42 CFR §11.22(b) [28]. For studies that began before the Final Rule’s implementation, additional clarification was drawn from the NIH’s 2009 guidance document on ACT criteria [29]. Eligible ACTs were interventional studies of FDA-regulated drugs or devices with a US nexus (i.e., a US site, product exported from the USA, or conducted under an investigational new drug/investigational device exemption [IND/IDE]) [23]. Specifically, drug trials had to be phase 2–4, while device trials were required to be randomized, non-feasibility studies. Inclusion also required a corresponding English-language publication and, for mixed-population studies, clearly delineated AE data for psoriasis.

The non-FDA-regulated dataset included trials exempt from mandatory reporting under 42 CFR Part 11 because they did not qualify as ACTs. This group comprised trials that failed ACT criteria and expanded access trials (21 U.S.C. §360bbb), which are exempt from both federal registration and reporting mandates [30].

General Data Extraction

Two reviewers (M.R. and M.D.) independently extracted data in a blinded, duplicate manner from ClinicalTrials.gov and matched publications using a standardized Google Form. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (R.S.). To ensure consistency, the reviewers first completed a training session and a calibration exercise on a pilot sample of ten studies.

We extracted general trial data: National Clinical Trial identifier, key dates (start, completion, results posting), trial phase, sponsor, and funding source. Harms data included counts of SAEs, OAEs, discontinuation due to AEs, and all-cause mortality. For each trial and harms domain, we recorded (1) whether the domain was reported and (2) the unit used (events versus participants), when stated. If the publication reported harms using thresholds (e.g., OAEs reported only above an incidence cutoff), placebo-adjusted/subtracted values, or narrative summaries without extractable counts, these were treated as not directly comparable for numeric concordance analyses. We documented any discrepancies between the registry entries and publication, such as differing OAE categorizations, use of reporting frequency thresholds, and selective omission of harms.

We captured whether publications referenced the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Harms extension and if skin-specific safety outcomes (e.g., injection-site reactions) were predefined in either the registry or publication. AEs were defined per 42 CFR §11.10(a) as any unfavorable medical occurrence during the trial, regardless of causality [23]. We operationalized SAEs as events meeting serious criteria as reported in each source, and OAEs as nonserious AEs reported in harms tables. For completeness, we treated terms such as “side effects” and “complications” as equivalent to AEs. Any reporting ambiguities or other contextual findings were also noted. The complete data extraction form is available on our OSF repository.

Data Analysis

The primary outcomes were (1) completeness of AE reporting in each source and (2) concordance of AE reporting between ClinicalTrials.gov and corresponding publications across four predefined domains: SAEs, OAEs, discontinuation due to AE, and death, all stratified pre- versus post-Final Rule. Secondary outcomes included (1) a 7-point composite AE completeness score assessing regulatory compliance on ClinicalTrials.gov and (2) regression analyses identifying trial characteristics (sponsor type, phase, sample size, blinding, and duration). We used descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, or medians with interquartile ranges [IQRs], as appropriate for data distribution) to characterize trial attributes (e.g., phase, sponsor, and enrollment) and summarize AE reporting patterns across ClinicalTrials.gov and publications. To evaluate the concordance of SAE counts between these sources, we generated Bland–Altman plots.

We visually inspected a funnel plot (proportion of SAEs versus enrollment size) for asymmetry to explore trial size-related reporting bias. Chi-squared tests were used to compare categorical variables (e.g., presence/absence of AE reporting) across sponsor types and study phases. For non-normal outcomes such as SAE counts, we applied the Mann–Whitney U test. Time series analyses were used to explore temporal patterns in AE reporting.

To quantify reporting completeness, we developed a 7-point composite AE score on the basis of the Final Rule (42 CFR §11.48[a][4]) [19, 31]. The score ranged from 0 to 7, with 1 point assigned for reporting any data (including zero values) in seven domains: all-cause mortality, participants with SAEs, total SAE events (by system/arm), participants with OAEs (≥ 5%), total OAE events (≥ 5%), number at risk for OAEs (≥ 5%), and the specified OAE frequency threshold. Each domain received equal weight (1 point) as a distinct regulatory compliance requirement, rather than a clinical safety severity metric. Following prior work, entries such as “0” or “rnc” (reported, not calculated) were considered reported, while nonmandated fields (e.g., Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities [MedDRA]) were excluded [32, 33]. This composite score served as the dependent variable in linear regression models designed to identify trial characteristics (sponsor type, study phase, sample size, blinding, and trial duration) associated with more complete AE reporting, rather than clinical severity or the seriousness of adverse events. To evaluate potential changes over time, we also conducted segmented regression analysis with 18 January 2017, the effective date of the Final Rule, as the breakpoint. The Durbin–Watson statistic indicated autocorrelation in the post-2017 (1.758) period. Nonetheless, we interpreted post-2020 trends with caution owing to smaller sample sizes. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 2024.09.1) and Python (version 3.13.5), with significance defined as p < 0.05 [34–36].

Risk of Bias and Confidence in Findings

Traditional methodological bias tools were not applied, as our study focused on the consistency of AE reporting rather than clinical outcomes. Confidence in our findings is supported by our rigorous methodology, including systematic searches, dual independent data extraction, and careful matching of registry entries to publications.

Protocol Registration, Amendments, and Deviations

The protocol was preregistered with PROSPERO and posted to the OSF before data extraction, with all subsequent modifications and their rationales documented in this manuscript and on the OSF [37, 38].

Results

Identification and Inclusion of Eligible Studies

The initial ClinicalTrials.gov search retrieved 499 records. On the basis of eligibility criteria, 266 were excluded. Following a full-text review of remaining studies, 214 were included in the final analysis. Detailed reasons for exclusion at each stage are outlined in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Trial General Characteristics

Our analysis included 214 clinical trials, with characteristics summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Most trials were phase 3 (64%; 136/214), used a parallel intervention model (98%; 210/214), and conducted with a primary purpose of treatment (96%; 206/214). Drug interventions (81%; 174/214) and industry funding (95%; 204/214) were most common (Supplementary Table S1).

Adverse Event Reporting Before and After Final Rule

In our analysis, 63.6% (136/214) trials were initiated pre-Final Rule and 36.4% (78/214) post-Final Rule (Table 1). ClinicalTrials.gov demonstrated greater completeness of SAE and OAE reporting compared with corresponding publications, a trend observed both pre- and post-Final Rule implementation. After the Final Rule, a higher proportion of studies reported OAEs: 51% (40/78) versus 39% (53/136) among pre-Final Rule registry entries; p < 0.001. Similarly, mortality reporting showed a distinct improvement in registry completeness post-Final Rule. Before its enactment, only 41% of trials in ClinicalTrials.gov included mortality, compared with 100% after. In addition, publications continued to underreport deaths in both periods, 16% (22/136) and 17% (13/78), respectively (p < 0.001). Median post-Final Rule AEs were considerably higher in registry entries, with 20.0 (IQR: 4.0–37.0), compared with publications at 3.0 (IQR: 0.0–9.5; p = 0.001). Reporting of OAEs was significantly more frequent in ClinicalTrials.gov registry entries (43% [93/214]) than in publications (17% [36/214]; p < 0.001). Among pre-Final Rule trials, OAEs were reported in 39% (53/136) of registry entries compared with 14% (19/136, p < 0.001) of publications. Post-Final Rule, this discrepancy persisted: OAEs were reported in 51% (40/78) of registry entries and only 22% (17/78, p < 0.001) of publications. Notably, 49% (38/78) of registry entries failed to report OAEs post-Final Rule, compared with77% (60/78, p < 0.001) of publications. Among post-Final Rule trials, the median number of patients affected by OAEs was 125 (IQR: 46.8–231) in ClinicalTrials.gov compared with 193 (IQR: 104–281) in publications (p = 0.021).

Concordance and Documentation of Adverse Event Reporting

Widespread reporting discrepancies were identified between ClinicalTrials.gov and the corresponding publications (Table 2). Patient counts were mismatched in 69.6% of studies; among these, registry entries more frequently reported higher counts than publications (79.9% versus 17.4%). Discrepancies in SAE reporting were even more common. Critically, 40% of studies failed to report SAE counts in either source. Among the remaining studies that did provide SAE data, counts were mismatched in 91.1% of cases, with registry entries more often reporting higher SAE counts (34.9%) or being the sole source of SAE data (2.1%) compared with publications (17.4%). Comparative Analysis of SAE Reporting Across ClinicalTrials.gov and Publications

A Bland–Altman analysis of SAE counts revealed a systematic bias toward higher reporting on ClinicalTrials.gov compared with corresponding publications, with a mean difference of 9.92 SAEs (Fig. 1). The limits of agreement were wide, reflecting substantial reporting variability, particularly for trials with a higher average number of SAEs. Importantly, several trials demonstrated substantial discrepancies relative to the overall event counts with differences reaching 12–36 events. For instance, one trial reported 26 SAEs in the registry, but only 14 in the publication (a difference of 12), while another reported 38 SAEs in the registry versus 2 in the publication (a difference of 36). These disparities reflect underreporting in publications and raise critical concerns about the completeness of accessible safety information. While most trials clustered near zero difference, ten trials were outliers, with nine reporting more SAEs on the registry and one reporting more in its publication. The asymmetric distribution of these outliers—predominantly favoring higher registry counts—suggests systematic underreporting in publications, rather that random reporting variation after SAE reporting had improved in registry entries. Discussion

Principal Findings

Our study reveals substantial discrepancies in AE reporting between ClinicalTrials.gov and peer-reviewed publications for psoriasis trials, raising concerns about the reliability of published safety data used in clinical decision-making. ClinicalTrials.gov entries showed higher completeness after the Final Rule era, whereas publications demonstrated persistently incomplete harms reporting, and discordance between sources remained substantial. Post-Final Rule, the median number of SAEs reported on ClinicalTrials.gov was nearly seven times higher than in corresponding publications (20.0 versus 3.0, p = 0.001), with 91.9% of trials showing mismatched SAE counts. Publications also more frequently omitted data on OAEs and mortality. These gaps persisted despite regulatory efforts to improve transparency, highlighting ongoing shortcomings in harms reporting.

Our findings mirror a well-established trend across specialties. Publications frequently underreport or inconsistently report harms compared with trial registry entries. Hartung et al. observed that such discrepancies often favored a more positive safety profile, indicating potential bias beyond mere oversight [33]. Similarly, Tang et al. found that 13% of publications omitted known SAEs reported on ClinicalTrials.gov, and 16% failed to include SAE counts by treatment group [39]. This pattern has been repeatedly confirmed across specialties, with underreporting of SAEs and mortality [40, 41]. Our results align with these studies, confirming the trend in psoriasis trials and supporting Song et al.’s findings of poor adherence to CONSORT Harms guidelines in dermatology journals [42]. Although the Final Rule improved safety data reporting within ClinicalTrials.gov, it did not reduce discordance in publications. In fact, the gap in SAE reporting widened, suggesting registry regulations alone have not improved transparency in the literature clinicians most often consult.

Implications for Practice

The omission and underreporting of harms in publications shifts the safety burden to postmarketing surveillance systems, which detect signals only after widespread use [43]. Importantly, these postmarketing systems complement, not replace, the extensive premarketing safety review that informs product labeling; incomplete harms reporting in publications can still shape perceptions and evidence synthesis, particularly when clinicians and guideline developers interpret published trial results. Although dermatologists rely on the FDA’s voluntary MedWatch program, this passive surveillance is limited by underreporting and bias [44, 45]. Product labeling remains the definitive clinician-facing safety summary because it is grounded in regulators’ comprehensive review of all trial safety data, including detailed tabulations contained within Clinical Study Reports. Our analysis does not evaluate concordance with product labeling or regulatory submissions; rather, it examines public transparency and consistency between two widely accessible sources: ClinicalTrials.gov results entries and peer-reviewed publications. Nonetheless, discordance between these public sources can still influence safety perceptions because clinicians, guideline panels, and evidence syntheses frequently rely on publications and may consult trial registries to contextualize harms reporting. Improving internal consistency across public sources while clearly describing analytic populations, definitions, and reporting thresholds can reduce misinterpretation and improve downstream evidence synthesis without supplanting product labeling as the clinical reference standard. When publications lack complete harms data, prescribers may be unaware of important risks. In one example, the FDA identified 127 consumer reports, only to learn the manufacturer had received 21,000 dermatologic AE complaints [46]. This slow, voluntary data accumulation delays a full safety profile, placing patients at risk [47, 48]. Although we did not formally compare registry or publication harms data against product labeling, discrepancies in publicly reported harms can contribute to confusion when clinicians encounter differences between trial publications, registry entries, and labeling summaries, underscoring the importance of transparent reporting practices and clear definitions across all public trial outputs. For patients with chronic conditions such as psoriasis, an unlisted side effect can erode trust in treatment and providers, threatening the therapeutic alliance essential for long-term care [49].

Since regulatory mandates alone cannot rectify reporting shortcomings, a multistakeholder approach is necessary to foster transparency in dermatology. Such a complex issue requires a multidisciplinary approach across the research pipeline, particularly with journals that can adopt strategies that enhance transparency without burdening editorial teams [50]. One approach is publishing harms-specific companion reports as brief, structured articles focused on trial AE data, which can supplement main publications, providing comprehensive safety information unrestricted by space limits. For resource-constrained journals, automated tools could flag AE data discrepancies between ClinicalTrials.gov and manuscripts during peer review, ensuring consistency without extensive manual work. The adoption of standardized reporting frameworks such as CONSORT extension for harms and structured data using MedDRA would greatly enhance the reliability of these tools [51, 52]. Ensuring transparency in clinical trial safety reporting requires alignment between editorial standards and investigator-level reporting practices.

To ensure data integrity and transparency, investigators and authors should ensure concordance between ClinicalTrials.gov registry entries and published manuscripts. Any deviations between these sources—such as analyzing different safety populations or excluding events—must be explicitly disclosed in the methods section. While product labeling and Clinical Trial Reports represent the most comprehensive safety data summaries, journal publications should align with their corresponding registry entries to maintain consistency across publicly accessible sources. To ensure clinical relevance, authors should also proactively report common dermatologic AEs using standardized severity grading, such as the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [53, 54].

To improve transparency in harms reporting, real-world safety data must be integrated into clinical practice and guidelines. Initiatives such as the Cutaneous Procedures Adverse Events Reporting Registry systematically capture AEs from dermatologic procedures, providing detailed safety data often missing from clinical trials to inform evidence-based guidelines. By supporting and leveraging such registry entries, professional societies can strengthen clinical recommendations, ultimately advancing safer and more informed dermatologic care [55, 56].

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of our study is using ClinicalTrials.gov, a federally mandated and publicly accessible source enabling systematic AE comparisons across trials. By including both pre- and post-Final Rule trials, we assessed not only the extent of reporting issues but also the regulation’s real-world impact. Combining quantitative analysis with a review of reporting formats highlighted both numerical discrepancies and varied harms presentations. Limitations included restricting analysis to ClinicalTrials.gov as the primary registry source, which, while federally mandated, may not represent the most comprehensive safety data available. Trial duration and intensity of monitoring may differ systematically by trial size; although we included duration as a covariate, residual confounding may persist, and variation in follow-up time may partly explain dispersion of SAE rates among smaller trials. Other limitations included excluding trials registered elsewhere, and potential misclassification when matching registry entries to publications despite thorough methods. By excluding trials lacking peer-reviewed publications and those without psoriasis-specific AE data, we may have systematically excluded studies with the weakest safety reporting. This selection bias likely means our findings underestimate the true magnitude of underreporting in psoriasis trials. Our sample consisted predominantly of phase 3 trials with limited phase 2 and phase 4 representation, and uneven trial distribution across drug classes. These imbalances precluded reliable phase- or class-stratified analyses, which would have produced unstable estimates and potentially misleading subgroup conclusions. We therefore focused on overall registry–publication concordance patterns. Additionally, differences in trial start dates and publication delays may have limited our ability to fully capture the Final Rule’s impact, as published results dates and trial initiation dates can vary widely.

Conclusions

Our cross-sectional analysis of AE reporting in psoriasis trials revealed persistent discrepancies between ClinicalTrials.gov data and published reports. While some trials showed accurate safety data, many had discordant information, raising concerns about the reliability of published AE evidence guiding clinical practice. These findings highlight the need to align trial registry entries and publications through standardized AE reporting frameworks, mandatory CONSORT Harms adherence, and greater stakeholder accountability. Enhancing transparency in AE reporting is vital for research integrity, evidence-based medicine, and patient safety in psoriasis care.
