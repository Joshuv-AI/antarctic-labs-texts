Light‐Harvesting Protein LHCB4 Is Important for Stable Dimeric Organization of Photosystem II
Authors: Tereza Vánská, Monika Opatíková, Roman Kouřil, Zuzana Kučerová, Václav Karlický, Iva Ilíková, Marek Rác, Pavel Roudnický, Vojtěch Chýlek, Petr Ilík
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLight‐harvesting protein LHCB4 is a monomeric antenna of photosystem II (PSII) that connects the PSII core (C) with strongly (S) and moderately (M) bound LHCII trimers, forming the stable dimeric supercomplex C2S2M2. LHCB4 is known to stabilize LHCII attachment to the PSII core dimer (C2). Here, we show that LHCB4 is essential for the intrinsic stability of the PSII core dimer itself, a fundamental structural unit required for efficient photochemistry. In the lhcb4 mutant, clear‐native PAGE revealed the absence of C2S2M(2) and only small amounts of C2S2 and C2S supercomplexes. Single‐particle electron microscopy identified two types of C2S(2) supercomplexes: a canonical configuration with an empty LHCB4 site and a more abundant “packed” structure, arising from a relative shift of the C(S) monomers. However, most PSII particles were present as monomeric CS complexes with attached LHCB5 antenna. In contrast, PSII analyzed directly in thylakoid membranes retained the canonical C2 organization and even formed nonparallel associations of C2S2 supercomplexes, indicating that the “packed” C2S2 and CS forms arise during isolation due to reduced PSII dimer stability. The PSII core dimer also readily disintegrates in the mutant lacking all monomeric antenna proteins (NoM), implying that LHCB4 is critical for the stability of the C2 dimer itself. Consistently, lhcb4 plants exhibited reduced thermal stability of PSII macro‐organization and photochemistry in vivo. Our findings demonstrate that LHCB4 is not merely a peripheral antenna connector, but also an important structural determinant of PSII core dimer stability, underscoring its essential role in efficient photochemistry and photoprotection.

Introduction

The primary reactions of oxygenic photosynthesis involve balanced cooperation of two supramolecular pigment–protein complexes embedded in the thylakoid membrane—photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII). Both photosystems consist of a core complex and associated light‐harvesting complexes (LHCs), which increase the capacity for light capture. The absorbed light energy is transferred to the reaction centers in the photosystem cores, where it drives photochemical reactions.

From a structural point of view, a fully assembled PSII consists of a dimeric core complex (C2), an outer layer of minor LHCs, and the major light‐harvesting complex II (LHCII). The minor LHCs, represented by LHCB4, LHCB5, and LHCB6 antenna proteins (also known as CP29, CP26, and CP24, respectively), are monomeric, whereas LHCIIs are present as trimers composed of various combinations of the LHCB1, LHCB2, and LHCB3 proteins (see e.g., Caffarri et al. 2009; Croce and van Amerongen 2011; Xu et al. 2017). In land plants, the LHCII antenna typically comprises two strongly bound trimers (S2), formed by a combination of LHCB1 and LHCB2 (Hankamer et al. 1997) or by LHCB1 only (in spruce, Opatíková et al. 2023), and two moderately bound trimers (M2, formed by LHCB1–3) (see Croce and van Amerongen 2011). The trimeric LHCIIs are connected to the PSII core dimer (C2) by all three minor antennae LHCB4–6, forming the stable supercomplex C2S2M2 (Albanese et al. 2016; Caffarri et al. 2009; Su et al. 2017). The only known exception to date is the Pinaceae family, where the antenna proteins LHCB3 and LHCB6 are absent (Kouřil et al. 2016, 2020; Opatíková et al. 2023), and LHCB4 is constitutively replaced by its homolog LHCB8 (formerly called LHCB4.3, Grebe et al. 2019).

From a functional perspective, PSII supercomplexes with large antennae, such as C2S2M2, are essential for efficient photosynthesis under low light. When plants acclimate from low‐ to high‐light conditions, this configuration typically shifts from C2S2M2 to C2S2, the smallest physiologically relevant PSII supercomplex (Albanese et al. 2016; Ballottari et al. 2007; Kouřil et al. 2013; Morosinotto et al. 2006). This transition is accompanied by downregulation of LHCB3 and LHCB6 (Kouřil et al. 2013; Štroch et al. 2022) and upregulation of LHCB8 (Floris et al. 2013). Since LHCB8 has reduced affinity for LHCB6 (Albanese et al. 2016, 2018, 2019), the replacement of LHCB4 with LHCB8 likely plays a key role in reducing PSII antenna size during high‐light acclimation. A recent study by Caferri et al. (2025) demonstrated that constitutive replacement of LHCB4 with LHCB8 in an Arabidopsis mutant prevents C2S2M2 assembly, leaving PSII supercomplexes in the C2S2 state.

The facts outlined above imply that the minor antenna proteins LHCB4/LHCB8 play an important role in the organization of PSII. LHCB4/LHCB8 are encoded by three genes LHCB4.1, LHCB4.2 (for LHCB4.1 and LHCB4.2 proteins), and LHCB4.3 (for LHCB8). Under normal conditions, the first two are expressed at comparable levels, whereas LHCB4.3 expression is roughly 20 times lower, placing LHCB8 among the rare LHC proteins (Ilíková et al. 2021; Jansson 1999; Klimmek et al. 2006; Zybailov et al. 2008). For simplicity, we will use the designation LHCB4 for all proteins LHCB4.1, LHCB4.2, and LHCB8.

The importance of LHCB4 for PSII supercomplex assembly and function has been studied in Arabidopsis using antisense aslhcb4 plants (van Oort et al. 2010; Yakushevska et al. 2003) as well as insertion triple mutant lhcb4 (lhcb4.1 lhcb4.2 lhcb4.3) (Betterle et al. 2009; de Bianchi et al. 2011; Chen et al. 2018; Miloslavina et al. 2011). Using nondenaturing Deriphat‐PAGE, it has been shown that the absence of all LHCB4 isoforms destabilizes the attachment of LHCII trimers to the PSII core dimer, which leads to a significant decrease in PSII supercomplex level (de Bianchi et al. 2011; Caferri et al. 2025; Guardini et al. 2022). Nevertheless, analysis of a small number of PSII particles from the lhcb4 mutant using single particle electron microscopy (EM) suggested the presence of C2S2 supercomplexes with an abnormal shape, although a detailed structural description of this form of the PSII supercomplex was limited by low resolution (de Bianchi et al. 2011). In addition to these structural effects, the absence of LHCB4 was reported to increase susceptibility of PSII to photoinhibition (de Bianchi et al. 2011) and to lower the extent of nonphotochemical quenching (NPQ amplitude, e.g., de Bianchi et al. 2011), indicating an important role of LHCB4 in the NPQ process (Dall'Osto et al. 2017; Lapillo et al. 2020; Nicol et al. 2019).

In this study, we investigated PSII assemblies in Arabidopsis lhcb4 mutant lacking all LHCB4 isoforms using a combination of very mild clear‐native PAGE (CN‐PAGE) and single‐particle EM of separated PSII (super)complexes. This approach, validated in our previous studies on the structural roles of the other two monomeric LHCB proteins, LHCB6 (Ilíková et al. 2021) and LHCB5 (Vánská et al. 2025), documents an additional role of LHCB4. The absence of this minor antenna not only destabilizes M‐trimer tethering to the PSII core but also weakens the interaction between PSII core monomers within the C2 dimer. We also showed that the structural modification of PSII assemblies due to the absence of LHCB4 destabilizes PSII in vivo, which likely underlies the higher stress sensitivity of the lhcb4 mutant.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material

Arabidopsis thaliana wild type (WT, accession Columbia) and the triple lhcb4 mutant (lhcb4.1 lhcb4.2 lhcb4.3, de Bianchi et al. 2011) were grown in soil in a FytoScope Walk‐In chamber (Photon Systems Instruments, Drásov, Czech Republic) for 6 weeks. The growth conditions in the chamber were as follows: 8/16 h light/dark photoperiod, 22/20°C day/night temperature, 110 μmol photons m−2 s−1 (400–700 nm), and 60% relative humidity.

Isolation of Thylakoid Membranes

Arabidopsis plants were dark‐adapted for 30 min, after which the rosettes were cut at the base and immediately processed for thylakoid isolation, following the protocol of Dau et al. (1995) with modifications. Plant material was homogenized in ice‐cold grinding buffer containing 35 mM HEPES/NaOH (pH 7.2), 0.4 M NaCl, 0.4 M sucrose, 4 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 5 mM sodium ascorbate, and 2 mg mL−1 BSA. The homogenate was filtered through a double layer of Miracloth and centrifuged at 5000 g for 6 min at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in resuspension buffer (25 mM HEPES/NaOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 0.9 mM EDTA) and centrifuged again under the same conditions for 10 min. For the final wash, the pellet was resuspended in storage buffer (50 mM HEPES/NaOH, pH 7.2, 0.4 M sucrose, 15 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2·6H2O) and briefly centrifuged (5000 g, 5 min, 4°C). The resulting pellet, containing thylakoid membranes, was gently resuspended in a minimal volume of storage buffer. All steps were carried out at 4°C or on ice under dim green light.

Clear Native Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (CN‐PAGE)

CN‐PAGE was performed according to Nosek et al. (2017), with minor modifications as described by Vánská et al. (2025). Thylakoid membrane suspensions were solubilized using n‐dodecyl‐α‐d‐maltopyranoside (α‐DDM) at a detergent‐to‐chlorophyll mass ratio of 10:1 for subsequent single‐particle analysis (Figure 3) and 5:1 for subsequent Western blot analysis (Figure 5). The thylakoid–detergent mixture was supplemented with sample buffer (50 mM HEPES/NaOH, pH 7.2, 0.4 M sucrose, 5 mM MgCl2, 15 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol), bringing the total volume to 30 μL. After gentle mixing, the samples were incubated for 1 min at 4°C and centrifuged at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were loaded onto 4%–8% polyacrylamide gradient gels (10 μg of chlorophyll per lane) prepared as described by Wittig et al. (2007) except that no stacking gel was used and the samples were loaded directly onto the resolving gel.

Electrophoresis was performed using the Bio‐Rad Mini‐PROTEAN Tetra Cell system with a Bis–Tris/Tricine buffer system: cathode buffer (50 mM Tricine, 15 mM Bis–Tris/HCl, pH 7.0) and anode buffer (50 mM Bis–Tris/HCl, pH 7.0). Gels were run under cooled conditions in the dark. The initial separation was conducted at a constant current of 3.5 mA per gel for 15 min, followed by a step increase to 7 mA maintained until the migration front of chlorophyll pigments reached the bottom edge of the gel. Visualization of native protein complexes was performed using an Amersham Imager 600RGB system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) in transmission mode under white light illumination and in fluorescence mode with an excitation wavelength of 460 nm and a bandpass filter of 690–720 nm.

2D‐PAGE and Immunoblotting

2D‐SDS‐PAGE was performed according to Laemmli (1970) modified according to Opatíková and Kouřil (2024). The selected sections of the CN‐PAGE gel strips from the first dimension were incubated in SDS buffer (25 mM Tris (pH 7.5/HCl), 35 mM SDS, 15 mM DTT) for 35 min and then applied to the SDS gel. The electrophoresis conditions were as follows: 5 mA/30 min = > 10 mA/2 h = > 20 mA/1.5 h at room temperature.

The separated proteins were transferred from the SDS gel to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane using the Trans‐Blot Turbo RTA Mini 0.2 lm PVDF Transfer Kit (BioRad) with conditions 2.5 A, 25 V for 25 min. The presence of the PSBB (CP47) protein was detected using a primary Anti‐PSBB antibody (AS04 038, Agrisera) in tandem combination with the secondary antibody with conjugated horseradish peroxidase (HRP) enzyme. The chemiluminescent signal was recorded after developing with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Merck) and visualized using a gel scanner Amersham Imager 600RGB (GE HealthCare Life Sciences).

Electron Microscopy and Single Particle Analysis

The selected CN‐PAGE bands were subjected to single‐particle electron microscopy analysis. The gel bands were excised, finely chopped, and incubated in elution buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 15 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and α‐DDM at its critical micellar concentration 0.008%). Gel pieces were submerged in the elution buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C to allow passive elution of the pigment‐protein complexes. The resulting supernatants were used directly for subsequent specimen preparation for transmission electron microscopy without further processing according to Kouřil et al. (2014) by applying them to glow‐discharged carbon‐coated copper grids followed by negative staining with 2% uranyl acetate.

Electron micrographs were collected using a Tecnai G2 F20 microscope (FEI Technologies) with an Eagle 4 K CCD camera (FEI Technologies, Hillsboro, USA) at ×134028 magnification. The pixel size at the specimen level after binning the images to 2048 × 2048 pixels was 0.224 nm. The 160,100, 45,618, 27,495, 64,421, and 64,374 projections were picked in semiautomated mode from 5152, 3664, 8549, 4168, and 4773 micrographs of specimens prepared from the gel bands assigned as the PSI/C2S/CS, C2S, C2S2 (lhcb4), PSI/C2S/CS (WT), and PSI/C2S/CS (NoM), respectively. The data sets were subjected to reference‐free 2D classification using the SCIPION image processing framework (de la Rosa‐Trevín et al. 2016). In the lhcb4 mutant, the C2S2 band contained overall ~7883 and ~2169 particles assigned to “packed” and canonical C2S2 form, respectively; the C2S band contained overall ~5033 and ~3589 particles assigned to “packed” and canonical C2S form, respectively; the PSI/CS/C2 band contained overall ~66,226 and ~3888 particles assigned to CS and C2 PSII form, respectively. For WT, the PSI/C2/CS band contained overall ~3075 and ~3822 particles assigned to CS and C2 PSII form, respectively.

Electron micrographs of grana membranes from A. thaliana triple lhcb4 mutant (209 micrographs), isolated according to Kouřil et al. (2013), were recorded at ×83562 magnification. The pixel size at the specimen level after binning the images to 2048 × 2048 pixels was 0.36 nm. Analysis focused on the identification of specific PSII megacomplexes in the grana membranes was performed using the SCIPION framework, where approximately 97,000 projections of PSII particles were initially selected in manual and auto‐picking modes (de la Rosa‐Trevín et al. 2013; Scheres 2015), which were analyzed and further classified into specific classes. To determine the minimal distances between PSII complexes in the grana membranes from A. thaliana triple lhcb4 mutant, 29,155 PSII projections were selected using SCIPION from micrographs. The coordinates of the PSII particles were then exported and analyzed using Microsoft Excel.

Mass Spectrometry

Proteins extracted from thylakoid membranes were subjected to filter‐aided sample preparation as described elsewhere (Wiśniewski et al. 2009). Peptide solutions were analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), performed using an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) online‐coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). LC–MS/MS data, acquired in data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, were processed using MaxQuant, and results tables were evaluated in the KNIME Analytics Platform within a containerized environment (https://github.com/OmicsWorkflows/KNIME_docker_vnc). See the Supporting Information for full details regarding the analyses and data evaluation.

Chlorophyll Fluorescence and P700 Absorption Measurements

Measurements were performed on attached, fully expanded leaves. Plants were sampled at mid‐photoperiod and dark‐adapted for 30 min prior to measurements. All measurements were performed under dim green light. Very fast chlorophyll fluorescence induction curves (OJIP) were recorded using a Plant Efficiency Analyser (PEA; Hansatech) on the adaxial side of dark‐adapted leaves. A 10‐s saturating red‐light pulse (6600 μmol photons m−2 s−1) was applied. The maximum quantum yield of PSII photochemistry was calculated as F V/F M (F V = F M − F O). Fluorescence parameters were evaluated using Biolyzer software (R. M. Rodríguez, University of Geneva). Simultaneous PSI and PSII performance was assessed using a Dual‐PAM‐100 system (Heinz Walz). Measurements were carried out under red actinic light (800 μmol photons m−2 s−1) combined with saturating pulses (10,000 μmol photons m−2 s−1, 300 ms). Calculated parameters were obtained using Dual‐PAM software (Klughammer and Schreiber 2008a, 2008b, see also Kramer et al. 2004). To obtain the signal corresponding to the fully oxidized primary donor of PSI (P700), an automated Dual‐PAM routine was performed on dark‐adapted leaves before illumination with actinic light. The routine involved a preprogrammed saturation pulse applied after 10 s of far‐red preillumination. The FO ' values required for the calculation of photosystem II parameters during the induction measurement were calculated using the approximation of Oxborough and Baker (1997), as described in the manual for the Dual‐PAM‐100 device.

Circular Dichroism (CD) Spectroscopy

Room temperature CD spectra were recorded in the range of 400–750 nm with a J‐815 spectropolarimeter (Jasco). Intact leaves were supported by a flat cell, and CD spectra were measured with a measuring beam perpendicular to the leaf similarly (Tóth et al. 2016). The spectra of intact leaves were recorded in steps of 0.5 nm with an integration time of 1 s, a band‐pass of 3 nm, and a scanning speed of 100 nm min−1. For temperature dependences of CD spectra, the samples were thermostated in a Peltier folder (Jasco) in the temperature range of 20°C–70°C in 2°C increments with 1.5 min incubation at each measuring temperature before recording the spectra. The CD spectra were normalized to the chlorophyll QY absorption band. In order to minimize the influences of the overlapping excitonic CD bands, the amplitudes of the (+)685 nm and (+)505 nm ψ‐type CD bands were calculated as the difference of the CD signal at (+)685 nm to 730 nm and (+)505 to 550 nm. Measured temperature dependences of the CD bands were fitted with a sigmoidal curve that resulted in the estimation of transition temperatures (Tm). Tm marks the disassembly of the chiral macrodomains of the complexes and is defined as the temperature at which the intensity of the CD band is decreased to 50% of its value at 20°C.

Fluorescence Temperature Curves (FTC)

Thermostability of PSII function in the WT and lhcb4 mutant was assessed using a PlanTherm PT100 instrument (Photon Systems Instruments, Drásov, Czech Republic) as described by Ilík et al. (2018). Prior to the measurements, plants were dark‐adapted for 30 min, after which the leaves were detached. Samples were heated from room temperature to 70°C in a water bath at a rate of 2°C min−1. Simultaneously, chlorophyll fluorescence intensity (FO level, minimal fluorescence) was recorded and plotted as a function of temperature (FTC). The temperature corresponding to the maximum curvature point of the FTC during the fluorescence rise was calculated using ProfileCon software (Photon Systems Instruments).

Gas Exchange Measurements

Net CO2 assimilation rate (A) was determined using a GFS‐3000 portable gas exchange system (Heinz Walz GmbH). The adaxial side of the measured leaf was illuminated with a mixture of red (90%) and blue (10%) light at an irradiance of 95 μmol photons m−2 s−1 (PAR). Airflow through the leaf chamber was maintained at 400 μmol s−1 with the chamber temperature at 23°C. Concentrations of CO2 and H2O were set as 400 μmol mol−1 and 17,100 μmol mol−1, respectively. This setup ensured 60% relative humidity in the chamber. These conditions were selected to mimic the conditions in the growth chamber. For the measurements, we used fully expanded leaves of 6‐week‐old plants from the growth chamber without dark acclimation. The data points were recorded after stabilization (2–3 min). The net CO2 assimilation rate (A) (μmol CO2 m−2 s−1) was calculated according to von Caemmerer and Farquhar (1981).

Thermoluminescence Measurements

Thermoluminescence curves were measured using a Thermoluminescence TL 6000/ST device (Photon Systems Instruments). Thylakoid membranes (see above) were resuspended in buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 5 mM MgCl2, 15 mM NaCl) with or without DCMU (5 μM). Four hundred microliters of the thylakoid membrane suspension corresponding to 18 μg of chlorophylls was placed on a metallic disc and cooled from room temperature to 5°C, followed by one single turnover flash prior to the measurement. The thermoluminescence curves were recorded while the sample was warmed up to 85°C at a heating rate of 0.5°C s−1.

Results and Discussion

Phenotypic Characterization and PSII Function of the lhcb4 Mutant

The Arabidopsis lhcb4 mutant has previously been studied and many valuable insights into the structural and physiological roles of LHCB4 have been obtained (Betterle et al. 2009; de Bianchi et al. 2011; Chen et al. 2018; Miloslavina et al. 2011). However, plant phenotypes can vary significantly even with minor changes in growth conditions (e.g., compare lhcb6 mutant phenotypes in Ilíková et al. 2021, and de Bianchi et al. 2008). Therefore, as the first step, we performed comprehensive characterization of the lhcb4 mutant under our growth conditions to verify that its key phenotypic characteristics align with previously published data, ensuring valid comparisons with earlier studies.

The lhcb4 mutant showed no clear phenotypic differences compared to WT (Figure 1A) and the fresh weight of rosettes as well as pigment concentrations of the mutant and WT were comparable (Table S1, see also de Bianchi et al. 2011). The absence of LHCB4 proteins in the lhcb4 plants was verified by immunoblotting (Figure 1B) and by mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of isolated thylakoid membranes (Figure 1C). The latter also confirmed that the lhcb4 mutant has lower content of LHCB3 (see also Chen et al. 2018) and a pronounced reduction in the relative amount of LHCB6 (Figure 1C; see also Andersson et al. 2001; de Bianchi et al. 2011). Our proteomic analysis additionally showed that no other LHCB protein is significantly affected and that the loss of LHCB4 does not markedly affect relative abundances of the major photosynthetic complexes (PSI, PSII, LHCII, cytochrome b6/f complex, and ATP synthase; Figure 1D).

To evaluate the basic functional photosynthetic parameters of the lhcb4 mutant, we performed the measurements of chlorophyll fluorescence induction on leaves. The maximum quantum yield of PSII photochemistry (FV/FM ratio) was lower in the lhcb4 mutants compared to WT, which is a result of significantly higher minimum chlorophyll fluorescence level FO (Table S1, Andersson et al. 2001; de Bianchi et al. 2011). The increase in FO is often interpreted as the presence of light‐harvesting complexes loosely bound to the PSII core subunits (e.g., Miloslavina et al. 2011; Ware et al. 2015), although the functional antenna size of PSII (TR0/RC parameter, Table S1) was similar in both lhcb4 mutant and in WT (see also, e.g., Miloslavina et al. 2011). The CO2 assimilation rate of lhcb4 plants measured under growth conditions was also not significantly different from that of WT, which corresponds with similar biomass of both plant genotypes (Table S1).

Analysis of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters during slow fluorescence induction under high light conditions (800 μmol photons m−2 s−1) revealed a slightly lower yield of PSII photochemistry (Y(II)) in the lhcb4 plants (Figure 1F), which coincides with a higher donor‐side limitation of PSI (Y(ND)) and with a lower quantum yield of PSI photochemistry (Y(I)) (Figure 1G). Our data confirm the findings of previous studies that lhcb4 plants have lower nonphotochemical quenching parameter NPQ than WT (Figure 1E, Andersson et al. 2001; Betterle et al. 2009; de Bianchi et al. 2011; Guardini et al. 2020). The NPQ parameter can be expressed as a ratio between regulated (Y(NPQ)) and nonregulated (Y(NO)) nonphotochemical quenching (Klughammer and Schreiber 2008a). Thus, the lower NPQ reflects higher Y(NO) relative to Y(NPQ) (see Figure 1F), suggesting a shift from regulated toward nonregulated energy dissipation in the lhcb4 mutant. This altered energy partitioning could be associated with the previously reported higher susceptibility of the lhcb4 mutant to photodamage (de Bianchi et al. 2011).

From a structural perspective, the elevated Y(NO) may arise from compromised architectural or functional integrity of the PSII complex, which could affect electron transfer and recombination pathways within the PSII core. To evaluate this possibility, we used PSII thermoluminescence to sensitively probe the energetic states of PSII redox cofactors in isolated thylakoids (for a review see, e.g., Ducruet and Vass 2009). The thermoluminescence B band, reflecting the recombination of the QB −S2(3) + charge pair, was slightly decreased and shifted to higher temperatures when compared to WT (Figure 2), suggesting increased stability of this charge pair. In contrast, the peak temperature of the thermoluminescence Q band (detected with an inhibitor of the electron transfer between QA and QB), reflecting the recombination of the QA −S2 + charge pair, was similar in lhcb4 and WT, whereas its amplitude was markedly reduced in the mutant (Figure 2). Given that overall PSII content and functional antenna size are comparable between genotypes (see Figure 1D and TR0/RC in Table S1), the lower Q‐band amplitude could reflect a higher P680+Pheo− and/or P680+QA − recombinations in the PSII core of the lhcb4 mutant. Higher probability of these recombinations can explain the higher level of Y(NO) (see e.g., Barbato et al. 2020) in the lhcb4 mutant (Figure 1F). The higher P680+Pheo− recombination promotes the formation of P680 in its triplet state, leading to the singlet oxygen generation and enhanced photooxidative damage (for a review, see Vass 2012). This mechanism may also explain the previously observed higher susceptibility of PSII to photoinhibition in the lhcb4 mutant (de Bianchi et al. 2011).

Although the lhcb4 mutant shows normal growth, pigment content, and photosynthetic capacity, chlorophyll fluorescence and thermoluminescence analyses reveal subtle but significant PSII alterations: elevated Y(NO), stabilized QB −S2(3) + pair, and possible higher charge recombinations within PSII, likely increasing photodamage susceptibility. These functional changes suggest that the loss of LHCB4 likely leads to some perturbations in PSII organization, providing a strong rationale for a structural analysis of PSII supercomplexes to link these functional effects to specific architectural rearrangements.

PSII Supercomplexes in the lhcb4 Mutant

Mild separation of photosynthetic complexes by CN‐PAGE (Figure 3A) confirmed that lhcb4 mutants have significantly lower amount of PSII supercomplexes than WT, a result which agrees with previous studies (de Bianchi et al. 2011; Caferri et al. 2025; Guardini et al. 2022). Figure 3A shows both visible‐light image and a black‐and‐white fluorescent scan of separated pigment–protein complexes from WT and lhcb4 thylakoid membranes. In the WT sample, a CN‐PAGE band pattern typical for Arabidopsis thaliana is observed (see, e.g., Nosek et al. 2017; Ilíková et al. 2021; Vánská et al. 2025). It shows large PSII assemblies (megacomplexes; PSII mc), a set of bands with PSII supercomplexes with different numbers of LHCII trimers (C2S2M2, C2S2M, C2S2/C2SM, C2S), distinct bands corresponding to PSI with co‐migrating PSII core dimer C2 (PSI/C2) and free light‐harvesting complexes (LHCs). We denoted the PSI/C2 band as PSI/C2/CS due to the appearance of CS complexes (see below).

The lhcb4 mutant showed a markedly different pattern, lacking bands corresponding to large PSII assemblies (i.e., megacomplexes and C2S2M2 and C2S2M supercomplexes, Figure 3A). The largest PSII supercomplexes detected in the gel were assigned, according to their migration distance, to C2S2 and C2S supercomplexes. Due to their relatively low abundance, these supercomplex bands were clearly visible in the gel only in the chlorophyll fluorescence imaging mode, which is more sensitive than plain visible‐light image. Notably, fluorescence imaging further revealed that in lhcb4, unlike in WT, the band usually attributed to PSI/C2 is strongly fluorescent (Figure 3A). Closer inspection showed that, in agreement with previous studies on lhcb4 mutants (Caferri et al. 2025; Guardini et al. 2022), this region forms a double band consisting of the PSI complex (weakly fluorescing at room temperature) and an adjacent co‐migrating band containing PSII complexes (highly fluorescing at room temperature) under the PSI complex. The PSII core complexes below PSI are either C2 dimers and/or CS monomers, both having migration distance similar to PSI.

To clarify how the absence of LHCB4 affects the structure of PSII (super)complexes, all three protein bands separated by CN‐PAGE from lhcb4 mutant thylakoids were systematically analyzed by single‐particle EM. To our surprise, in the first, uppermost C2S2 band we identified two distinct supercomplex forms (Figure 3B). The less abundant form (see Figure S1) represents the canonical WT‐like C2S2 supercomplex with an empty LHCB4 binding site (Figures 3B,C). In this configuration, the S trimer lacks its usual interaction partner, which probably makes the complex highly fragile and difficult to isolate. This would explain its low abundance in CN‐PAGE (Figure 3A) and why it was not detected in previous studies of the lhcb4 mutant. The more abundant form (see Figure S1) represents a LHCB4‐lacking C2S2 supercomplex with a more “packed” architecture (Figure 3B,C). A comparison with the newly observed WT‐like C2S2 lacking LHCB4 reveals that the “packed” C2S2 arises from a relative shift of the two CS monomers, with no major displacement of LHCB5 or the S trimers (Figure 3B,C; see also Video S1). This form probably corresponds to the PSII supercomplex structure previously described by de Bianchi et al. (2011). However, the lower resolution of their projection map likely prevented recognition of the shift in the C2 cores, leading the authors to attribute the structural difference to the altered positioning of LHCB5 and the S trimers in the absence of LHCB4. Since this “packed” C2S2 supercomplex was found to be the dominant form of our C2S2 band, it likely represents the more stable configuration of isolated C2S2 lacking LHCB4.

Single‐particle EM analysis of the second band from the lhcb4 mutant, the C2S band, similarly revealed two C2 configurations (Figure 3B,C), with the “packed” form again more abundant (Figure S2).

Finally, single‐particle EM was performed also on the third band, corresponding to the highly fluorescent PSI/C2/CS double band described above (Figure 3A). Our analysis confirmed the presence of PSI particles in this region (Figure S3). However, the majority of the identified particles were CS monomers comprising a PSII core monomer, LHCB5, and one S LHCII trimer (Figure 3B,C). This CS form was much more prevalent than canonical C2 particles, with a CS to C2 ratio of ~17 in lhcb4, compared to ~0.8 in WT (Figure S4, see also Methods). Taken together, these data indicate that LHCB4 protein normally contributes to the stabilization of the interaction between PSII core monomers within the canonical C2S2 supercomplex. In its absence, PSII assemblies tend to reorganize either into the “packed” C2S2 configuration or, more frequently, to dissociate into CS supercomplexes.

Organization of PSII Supercomplexes in Thylakoid Membranes of the lhcb4 Mutant

To determine whether the canonical and “packed” PSII core configurations identified in the lhcb4 mutant using CN‐PAGE and single‐particle EM are also present in thylakoid membranes, we performed EM analysis of PSII cores directly in the membrane. PSII particles can be recognized in the membrane based on the high contrast provided by the oxygen‐evolving complex together with the lumenal domains of intrinsic core subunits. Single‐particle analysis of picked densities of PSII particles and successful fitting of the averaged PSII projections in the thylakoid membrane using the canonical WT‐like C2 model (Figures 4E and S5) indicates that only the canonical C2 configuration is present in lhcb4 in situ. The “packed” C2S(2) supercomplex was not detected in the membranes, which implies that it does not represent a stable physiological state, but reflects the structural instability of PSII supercomplexes lacking LHCB4, which manifests itself during isolation.

Using the coordinates of individually picked PSII particles, we analyzed the minimal distances between neighboring PSII particles within the membrane. This analysis revealed a narrower distribution of mutual distances between individual PSII particles in the membrane of lhcb4 compared to WT. At the same time, most PSII particles in lhcb4 thylakoids were closer to each other than in WT (peak distance at 202 Å in lhcb4 and 217 Å in WT, Figure 4A,B). This result corresponds with the absence of M trimer in PSII supercomplexes in lhcb4 mutant (Figure 3).

The observed closer spacing of PSII particles with WT‐like organization of PSII core dimers in lhcb4 membranes prompted us to examine whether these particles form also higher‐order PSII assemblies (megacomplexes). In thylakoid membranes of plants, PSII supercomplexes are known to associate into megacomplexes through defined spatial arrangements of neighboring PSII supercomplexes in the membrane plane (Kouřil et al. 2020; Nosek et al. 2017; Shan et al. 2024). We therefore analyzed the local membrane environment around individually picked PSII particles and searched for specifically associated neighboring PSII particles within the lhcb4 membrane. This analysis revealed two distinct types of PSII megacomplexes (Figures 4C,D), characterized by inter‐PSII distances of 166 and 209 Å. However, a majority of PSII particles in the lhcb4 membrane do not participate in the megacomplex formation, as no specific spatial association with neighboring complexes was detected for most particles (Figure 4E).

Both revealed PSII megacomplexes in the lhcb4 are formed by nonparallel associations of PSII supercomplexes (Figures 4C,D). Fitting of the projection maps of the megacomplexes demonstrates that the observed assemblies are compatible with two C2S2 supercomplexes lacking LHCB4 (Figure 4C,D). The fitting with larger supercomplexes, such as C2S2M2, would lead to steric clashes of the M trimer, indicating that the PSII megacomplexes are formed exclusively by C2S2 and mediated primarily by LHCB5. The identified nonparallel associations are consistent with our previous study on WT Arabidopsis (Nosek et al. 2017), which described interactions between PSII supercomplexes mediated by LHCB5, and with the absence of such nonparallel associations of PSII supercomplexes in the lhcb5 mutant (Vánská et al. 2025). As no PSII megacomplexes were detected in CN‐PAGE of the lhcb4 mutant (Figure 3A), we conclude that the megacomplexes lacking LHCB4 observed in thylakoid membranes are too labile to be preserved as distinct bands in CN‐PAGE.

Low Stability of PSII Core Dimer Lacking All Minor Antenna Proteins

The higher abundance of CS supercomplexes compared to C2S2 obtained using CN‐PAGE in the lhcb4 mutant (Figure 3) suggests that the interaction between monomeric cores in the C2 dimer lacking LHCB4 might be weaker than the attachment of the S trimer to the monomeric core via LHCB5. The formation of the “packed” C2S2 with shifted CS monomers (Figure 3 and Video S1) supports this view. In order to estimate the structural stability of the “packed” C2S2, we fitted the electron density map of this complex with a model composed of two shifted CS monomers and searched for potential steric clashes between the CS monomers. In this rigid‐body fitting, the S‐LHCII trimer of one CS unit approaches the PSII core subunits of the neighboring CS monomer very closely, leading to clashes (Figure S6). Because the “packed” complex is clearly present in the EM data, there must be small local displacements of interacting subunits in this structure that are not captured in the rigid structural model. Such adjustments are nevertheless likely to introduce structural strain or suboptimal packing within the dimeric assembly. This reduced structural compatibility may facilitate dissociation of the “packed” C2S2 into monomeric CS complexes during isolation, as observed in the CN‐PAGE analysis of the lhcb4 mutant (Figure 3). This scenario could mean that the presence of LHCB5, forming highly stable CS monomers, could be responsible for the disruption of the PSII core dimer in the absence of LHCB4.

A question arises as to whether the PSII core dimer will also be unstable in the absence of both LHCB5 and LHCB4 antenna proteins. To address this possibility, we have analyzed the stability of PSII core dimer in the NoM Arabidopsis mutant, which lacks LHCB4, LHCB5, and implicitly also LHCB6 (Dall'Osto et al. 2014). As expected, CN‐PAGE of the NoM samples showed no PSII supercomplexes above the PSI/C2/CS band (Figure 5A), and single‐particle EM analysis of the PSI/C2/CS band confirmed that PSII is present exclusively in the C2 form without CS particles (Figure S7). This observation agrees with the inability of C2 to bind the S trimer in the absence of monomeric antenna proteins.

To determine whether C2 is stable or whether it is prone to disintegration into core monomers in NoM, we performed 2D‐PAGE analysis of the lower CN‐PAGE regions and compared them with WT and the lhcb4 mutant (Figure 5). The disintegration of C2, monitored by immunoblotting against the CP47 subunit, was pronounced in NoM compared to lhcb4 and WT (Figure 5B). In WT, a very weak CP47 signal in and below the PSI/C2/CS band clearly shows that the majority of PSII is present in larger supercomplexes (Figure 3A). In lhcb4, the PSI/C2/CS band contains a large amount of CP47 because of the abundant CS complexes (Figures 3A and S3), but the absence of a strong signal below this band shows that C2/CS is not largely disintegrated. In contrast, NoM shows a high amount of CP47 in the PSI/C2/CS band as well as below this band (Figure 5B), indicating pronounced disintegration of C2 into smaller fragments. Taken together, these results lead to the conclusion that LHCB4 itself plays a crucial structural role in maintaining the stability of the PSII core dimer, likely through its stromal‐loop‐mediated interactions with PSII core subunits.

Stability and Function of PSII Assemblies in the lhcb4 Mutant In Vivo

Our analysis revealed that PSII supercomplexes lacking LHCB4 exhibit reduced stability and tend to rearrange/dissociate even under relatively mild disturbance, e.g., during solubilization of thylakoid membranes for CN‐PAGE (Figure 3). Our next question was whether this instability occurs in vivo, and to test this, we applied gradual heating to intact leaves and assessed PSII assembly stability using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Garab and van Amerongen 2009).

In general, the dominant CD bands (ψ‐type) observed in leaves, chloroplasts, and isolated grana membranes arise from long‐range dipole–dipole interactions between chlorophylls and carotenoids within PSII and LHCII complexes (Garab 2014, 2016; Garab and van Amerongen 2009; Lambrev and Akhtar 2019). It is well established that the amplitudes of the positive bands peaking at approximately 505 and 685 nm reflect both LHCII content and long‐range lateral macro‐organization of PSII supercomplexes (Tóth et al. 2016). We observed a pronounced reduction in both ψ‐type CD band amplitudes in lhcb4 relative to WT (Figure 6A). Since the abundance of LHCII in lhcb4 is comparable to that in WT (Figure 1D), this decrease can be interpreted by the reduced extent of lateral macro‐organization of PSII supercomplexes in thylakoid membranes due to LHCB4 absence. Consistently, lhcb4 exhibited lower thermal stability of these CD bands (Figures 6B–D and Table S2), revealing markedly reduced heat tolerance of PSII macro‐organization.

The decreased thermal stability of PSII assemblies in lhcb4 as deduced from CD spectroscopy nicely corresponds with impaired heat tolerance of PSII photochemistry, as evidenced by changes in chlorophyll FTCs (Figure S8). During gradual heating of leaf segments, lhcb4 exhibited an earlier and steeper increase in the fluorescence FO level than the WT. This shows that the heat‐induced accumulation of QA − resulting from PSII conformational changes/thermal damage occurs in lhcb4 at lower temperatures than in WT (e.g., Schreiber and Armond 1978; Kouřil et al. 2004; Lípová et al. 2010, and references therein).

In summary, our results demonstrate that LHCB4 plays an essential role not only in mediating LHCII attachment to PSII, but also in maintaining the intrinsic stability of the PSII core dimer. In the absence of LHCB4, PSII dimer assemblies show increased vulnerability and readily destabilize during isolation, resulting in the formation of aberrant “packed” supercomplexes and in monomerization, although wild‐type‐like organization of the PSII dimer is largely preserved in thylakoid membranes. Despite this preserved architecture in situ, the absence of LHCB4 affects PSII function, as evidenced by affected charge recombination pathways within the PSII core. This structural vulnerability of PSII assemblies is manifested in vivo as lower heat stability of lateral PSII macro‐organization and PSII photochemistry.
