Early Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitor Use After Acute Kidney Injury in Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Yu-Ting Chou, Jui-Yi Chen, Zheng-Hong Jiang, Li-Chun Lin, Vin-Cent Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat outcomes are associated with a sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor prescription within 30 days vs 31 to 90 days after discharge among adults with type 2 diabetes recovering from dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury?FindingsIn this cohort study using a target trial emulation design in 4406 patients, early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription was associated with a lower risk of adverse kidney events after propensity score matching, with no significant differences in mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events.MeaningThese findings support timely initiation of SGLT-2 inhibitors during the post–acute kidney injury recovery period and provide a rationale for prospective randomized trials.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is associated with an increased risk of subsequent kidney disease progression, cardiovascular events, and death. These risks may be particularly high among patients with type 2 diabetes and those with AKI requiring dialysis (AKI-D).1,2 The period after AKI, often conceptualized as acute kidney disease, represents a clinically important transition phase during which patients may remain at risk of recurrent kidney injury and long-term decline in kidney function.2,3

Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors reduce kidney and cardiovascular events in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD).4,5,6 However, patients recovering from severe AKI have been underrepresented in major randomized clinical trials.7 As a result, the optimal timing of SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation after AKI-D remains uncertain.

Clinicians may hesitate to prescribe SGLT-2 inhibitors soon after AKI because of concerns about incomplete kidney recovery, volume depletion, hemodynamic instability, ketoacidosis, and recurrent kidney injury.8,9,10 Conversely, delaying initiation may defer potential kidney and cardiovascular benefits in patients who are clinically stable after discharge. Evidence addressing this clinical decision window remains limited.8,9,11

We therefore emulated a target trial using a large electronic health record network to evaluate whether early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription within 30 days after discharge, compared with late prescription between 31 and 90 days, is associated with kidney, cardiovascular, mortality, and selected safety outcomes among adults with type 2 diabetes recovering from AKI-D.

Methods

Study Design

This cohort study used deidentified electronic health record data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network from January 1, 2015, to September 1, 2024.12 The study emulated a hypothetical trial comparing SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription within 30 days after hospital discharge with prescription between 31 and 90 days after discharge among adults with type 2 diabetes recovering from AKI-D (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

TriNetX includes deidentified demographic, diagnosis, procedure, medication, laboratory, and encounter data from participating health care organizations. Patient sex (female, male) and race (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, White, unknown) and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, unknown) were obtained from structured electronic health record fields. The TriNetX platform adheres to the regulatory standards of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and General Data Protection Regulation. The use of deidentified data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network was determined to be exempt from institutional review board review by the Western Institutional Review Board, with a waiver of informed consent as no identifiable patient-level data were analyzed. The study adhered to the ethical principals of the Declaration of Helsinki13 and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Study Population

We identified adults aged 18 years or older with type 2 diabetes who had AKI-D during an index hospitalization between January 1, 2015, and September 1, 2024 (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). To meet the operational definition of acute kidney disease, eligible patients were those ever to receive dialysis during the index hospitalization but to have successfully discontinued dialysis after discharge. A new-user design was adopted, excluding individuals who had received SGLT-2 inhibitors within 1 year before discharge. Dialysis modality was not restricted and included intermittent hemodialysis, continuous kidney replacement therapy, hemofiltration, and acute peritoneal dialysis as captured by available procedure codes (eMethods in Supplement 1).

To ensure no legacy effect from previous SGLT-2 inhibitor exposure, patients were required to have no documented SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription from 90 days before discharge through day 0 as a washout period.14 The early initiation group included patients with a first documented SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription within 30 days after discharge. The late initiation group included patients with no prescription during the first 30 days and a first prescription between 31 and 90 days after discharge. The index date for outcome follow-up was 90 days after discharge. At this landmark, patients were required to be alive, not undergoing dialysis, not having had a transplant, and not having a recorded estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were adverse kidney events, all-cause mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Adverse kidney events were defined as a composite of reinitiation of dialysis, incident end-stage kidney disease or CKD stage 5, or recorded eGFR of less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 after the 90-day landmark. A MACE was defined as a composite of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular death. Patients were followed up from the index date until the first occurrence of a study outcome, loss to follow-up, death, administrative censoring (March 14, 2025), or a maximum follow-up of 5 years, whichever occurred first.

Selected 6-month safety outcomes included urinary tract infection, diabetic ketoacidosis, heart failure, hypoglycemia, diabetic retinopathy, volume depletion, and genitourinary infection. Outcomes were identified using recorded diagnosis codes, procedure codes, medication records, and laboratory values when applicable (eMethods in Supplement 1).

Covariates

Covariates were predefined based on clinical relevance and existing literature and were assessed within 1 year prior to the index event to facilitate adjustment for baseline differences between treatment groups. These variables included demographics (age, sex, and race and ethnicity); laboratory values (hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], eGFR, blood pressure, hemoglobin, serum albumin level, low-density lipoprotein, and alanine transaminase); body mass index [BMI]); kidney disease severity measures (presented as eGFR and documentation of proteinuria); and medication use, including insulin, metformin, sulfonylureas, renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogs, and antihypertensive medications such as β-blockers. Comorbidities were comprehensively evaluated, including heart failure, hypertension, myocardial infarction, and neoplasms.

Sensitivity and Safety Analysis

To reduce guarantee-time bias and immortal time bias, multiple landmark analyses15 were conducted with a different selection period for follow-up (3 months, 5 months, and 1 year after the discharge date) to assess the robustness of the findings. Additionally, fixed-time horizon survival analyses were performed at 1, 2, and 3 years of follow-up, and results were compared with those from the overall study period to evaluate temporal consistency. To further address interpretative bias from composite end points, specificity analyses were performed to disentangle and assess individual outcome components separately.

To ensure early detection and management of short-term safety outcomes, including hypoglycemia, diabetic retinopathy, volume depletion, diabetic ketoacidosis, and urinary tract infection, a 6-month follow-up period after the index date was implemented, facilitating the assessment of the SGLT-2 inhibitor’s acute effects. A 6-month window thus provided adequate time to capture these events while minimizing confounding from longer-term complications.16

Positive and Negative Control Analyses

To assess the validity of the study design, a negative control exposure analysis was conducted using acetaminophen, an agent not expected to influence kidney or cardiovascular outcomes. Similarly, negative control outcomes including traffic accidents, melanoma, hemorrhoids, and atopic dermatitis were evaluated to detect potential residual confounding or spurious associations.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the combination and monotherapy groups were summarized as means with standard deviations for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent 2-sample t tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. To minimize confounding and emulate the randomization process, one-to-one propensity score matching was performed using logistic regression with greedy nearest neighbor matching and a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standardized differences. Covariate balance between matched groups was considered adequate when the standardized difference was less than 0.1, indicating minimal baseline differences.17 In addition, the propensity score density plots showed substantial overlap between the 2 groups, supporting adequate common support after matching (eFigures 2 and 3 in Supplement 1).

For the primary analysis, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models under an intention-to-treat framework. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to visualize cumulative incidence, with between-group comparisons evaluated using the log-rank test. The assumption of proportional hazards was evaluated using the generalized Schoenfeld residuals method. To assess robustness to unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated, with higher values indicating greater resistance to potential bias from unmeasured confounders.18

We used a bayesian linear interaction model to evaluate longitudinal changes in each biomarker between early and late SGLT-2 inhibitor users. The model included time, treatment group, and their interaction term to test whether biomarker trends differed between groups (eMethods in Supplement 1). Posterior estimates and 95% credible intervals were derived analytically, and Bayes factors (approximated using the bayesian information criterion) were used to quantify the strength of evidence for group-by-time interactions.19

All analyses were conducted using the TriNetX platform and R, version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 129 179 patients met the eligibility criteria, of whom 4406 (3.4%) had a documented SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription within 90 days after hospital discharge and 124 773 had no documented SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription during this period. Before propensity score matching among patients with a documented SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription, 2268 initiated treatment within 30 days after discharge (mean [SD] age, 63.5 [12.9] years; 932 female [41.1%] and 1336 male [58.9%]; 102 of Asian [4.5%], 404 of Black or African American [17.8%], 1407 of White [62.0%], and 355 of unknown [15.7%] race; 191 of Hispanic or Latino [8.4%] and 2077 not of Hispanic or Latino [91.6%] ethnicity), and 2138 initiated treatment between 31 and 90 days after discharge (mean [SD] age, 62.7 [13.5] years; 928 female [43.4%] and 1210 male [56.6%]; 111 of Asian [4.9%], 411 of Black or African American [18.1%], 1288 of White [60.2%], and 328 of unknown [15.3%] race; 229 of Hispanic or Latino [10.7%] and 1909 not of Hispanic or Latino [89.3%] ethnicity) (Figure 1; Table 1). After 1:1 propensity score matching, 1912 patients were included in each group for the outcome analysis. Baseline characteristics were generally well balanced after matching, with standardized differences less than 0.10 for most measured covariates (Table 1). In the matched cohort, the mean (SD) age was 63.0 (13.0) years in the early initiation group and 62.8 (13.5) years in the late initiation group. Female sex (810 [42.4%] vs 800 [41.8%]) and male sex (1102 [57.6%] vs 1112 [58.2%]) were similarly distributed between groups, as were race (Asian, 102 [4.5%] vs 109 4.8%]; Black or African American, 408 [18.0%] vs 420 [18.5%]; White, 1156 [60.5%] vs 1172 [61.3%]) and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, 204 [10.7%] vs 211 [11.0%]; not Hispanic or Latino, 1708 [89.3%] vs 1701 [89.0%]).

Recorded comorbidities were also generally balanced after matching, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, liver disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, CKD, proteinuria, and neoplasms. Measured laboratory and clinical variables, including eGFR, BMI, systolic blood pressure, alanine aminotransferase, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, albumin, and HbA1c, were similar between groups after matching. The leading recorded cause of AKI was sepsis-related injury among 591 patients (26.1%) in the early initiation group and 605 patients (28.7%) in the late initiation group. Hypovolemia-related AKI was the second most common recorded cause, occurring in 534 patients (25.0%) and 635 patients (29.7%) in the early and late initiation groups, respectively. Baseline medication use before hospitalization, including RAS inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, antihypertensive agents, β-blockers, statins, metformin, sulfonylureas, and insulin, was also balanced after matching. The use of RAS inhibitors, hydoxymethylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor agonists after the index date was comparable between the 2 groups (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

All-Cause Mortality

During a median follow-up of 3.4 years (IQR, 1.1-3.3 years), all-cause mortality occurred in 139 patients (7.3%) in the early initiation group and 132 patients (6.9%) in the late initiation group. Early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription was not associated with all-cause mortality compared with late prescription (adjusted HR [AHR], 1.06; 95% CI, 0.84-1.35; P = .63) (Table 2; Figure 2A).

MACEs

Major adverse cardiovascular events occurred in 119 patients (9.7%) in the early initiation group and 110 (9.5%) in the late initiation group. Early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription was not associated with a risk of MACEs compared with late prescription (AHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.80-1.34; P = .81) (Table 2; Figure 2B). Similar findings were observed for individual cardiovascular components, including acute myocardial infarction, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Adverse Kidney Events

Adverse kidney events occurred in 74 patients (3.9%) in the early initiation group and 119 (6.2%) in the late initiation group. Early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription was associated with a lower risk of adverse kidney events compared with late prescription (AHR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.46-0.83; P = .001) (Table 2; Figure 2C). The E-value for the point estimate was 2.62, and the E-value for the confidence interval bound closest to the null was 1.72, suggesting that an unmeasured confounder of this magnitude would be required to fully explain the observed association.

In the component analysis of adverse kidney events, early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription was associated with a lower risk of progression to eGFR less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 (AHR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.48-0.89; P = .006). No statistically significant difference was observed in the risk of reinitiation of dialysis (AHR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.73-1.30; P = .87) (eTable 3 and eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). These findings suggest that the association with the composite adverse kidney outcome was primarily driven by the eGFR-based component rather than by reinitiation of dialysis. In fixed-time horizon analyses, early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription was associated with a lower risk of adverse kidney events at 1 year (AHR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.36-0.92; P = .02), 2 years (AHR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.41-0.83; P = .002), and 3 years (AHR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.44-0.83; P = .001).

Exploratory Subgroup Analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses showed no statistically significant interactions for all-cause mortality or MACEs across the predefined clinical strata, including baseline eGFR, ejection fraction, BMI, HbA1c level, recorded proteinuria status, GLP-1 receptor agonist use, and RAS inhibitor use (Figure 3). For adverse kidney events, an interaction was observed among GLP-1 receptor agonist users. The association between early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription and adverse kidney events was more pronounced among patients receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists (AHR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.04-0.44) than among those not receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists (AHR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.60-1.17) (P for interaction = .004) (Figure 3). Because these subgroup analyses were exploratory, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating. Nevertheless, the association observed among GLP-1 receptor agonist users suggests a potential synergistic kidney-protective effect when early SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation is combined with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy.

Safety Outcomes and Sensitivity Analysis

During the 6-month safety assessment period after the index date, no statistically significant differences were observed between early and late SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation groups in recorded urinary tract infection, diabetic ketoacidosis, heart failure, hypoglycemia, diabetic retinopathy, volume depletion, or genitourinary fungal infection (eTable 4 and eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). These analyses were based on events recorded in the electronic health records. Sensitivity analyses using alternative initiation thresholds at day 15 and day 45 showed consistency in kidney outcome benefit in the early intervention group (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Negative Control Analysis

No statistically significant associations were observed for negative control outcomes, including traffic accidents, melanoma, atopic dermatitis, and hemorrhoids, or for the negative control exposure analysis using acetaminophen with adverse kidney events (eFigure 5 in Supplement 1). These findings did not suggest substantial residual systematic bias, although they cannot exclude residual confounding by indication or incomplete outcome capture.

Discussion

This cohort study using a target trial emulation design of adults with type 2 diabetes recovering from AKI-D found that SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription within 30 days after hospital discharge was associated with a lower risk of adverse kidney events compared with prescription between 31 and 90 days. No significant differences were observed in all-cause mortality, MACEs, or recorded short-term safety outcomes. This association was primarily driven by preservation of kidney function, as reflected by a lower incidence of eGFR less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 rather than reduced recurrent dialysis. These findings extend previous evidence supporting SGLT-2 inhibitors after acute kidney disease by suggesting that the timing of initiation during the early postdischarge recovery phase may influence subsequent kidney outcomes, highlighting a potentially modifiable therapeutic window after severe AKI.

The question of when to initiate or resume kidney-protective therapy after AKI remains clinically challenging. Clinicians may defer SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription during the post-AKI recovery period because of concerns regarding volume status, hemodynamic changes, ketoacidosis, recurrent kidney injury, or incomplete kidney recovery. Evidence from populations with acute heart failure has suggested that earlier postdischarge SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation is associated with improved clinical outcomes, although early benefits may not be explained solely by durable natriuresis or objective decongestion.20,21 In patients with diabetes and acute myocardial infarction, prior evidence also has suggested potential cardiovascular and kidney associations with SGLT-2 inhibitor use, including findings from meta-analytic data and the SGLT2-I AMI PROTECT Registry.22,23 However, these populations differ substantially from patients recovering from AKI-D. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as establishing a universal 30-day biological threshold but rather as supporting further investigation of postdischarge timing during the high-risk transition from AKI to acute kidney disease and CKD.

The observed association with adverse kidney events is biologically plausible but remains hypothesis-generating. In patients with stable CKD and diabetic kidney disease, SGLT-2 inhibitors have been shown to reduce kidney disease progression and kidney failure outcomes.4,5 Proposed mechanisms include modulation of tubuloglomerular feedback; reduction in intraglomerular pressure; improved renal oxygen use; and attenuation of inflammatory, oxidative, endothelial, and metabolic stress pathways.24,25,26,27 These mechanisms may be relevant during recovery from severe AKI in which maladaptive repair and progressive nephron loss may contribute to transition from AKI to CKD. Nevertheless, patients recovering from AKI-D have heterogeneous recovery trajectories, and the absence of a significant difference in reinitiation of dialysis in our component analysis suggests that the composite kidney association was primarily influenced by the eGFR-based component rather than prevention of recurrent dialysis.28

In exploratory subgroup analyses, the association between early SGLT-2 inhibitor prescription and adverse kidney events was more pronounced among patients receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists. Both GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT-2 inhibitors have been associated with favorable cardiovascular and kidney outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes, and emerging evidence has suggested that combination therapy may provide additive cardiorenal benefits.29,30,31 A recent study in patients with type 2 diabetes and acute kidney disease also reported favorable outcomes with combined SGLT-2 inhibitor and GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy compared with monotherapy.32 However, because our subgroup analyses were exploratory and the study was not designed to test treatment synergy, confirmation is required in dedicated studies.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, although residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, we used target trial emulation, a new-user design, propensity score matching, landmark analysis, negative control analyses, and E-value calculations to mitigate this concern. Differences in baseline clinical status, including detailed CKD stage distribution and proteinuria severity, may have influenced the timing of SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation after discharge. However, we incorporated clinically relevant variables, including eGFR and qualitative proteinuria status, into the matching process and achieved good balance in measured baseline characteristics. Nevertheless, unmeasured factors, such as the severity and duration of the index hospitalization, may have influenced both prescription timing and subsequent outcomes and could not be fully captured in this analysis. Similarly, changes in pharmacologic therapy after discharge may have acted as important cointerventions. Although medication use during the first 3 months after discharge, including major cardioprotective and kidney-protective therapies, was evaluated (eTable 2 in Supplement 1), electronic health records could not reliably capture every medication restart, discontinuation, dose adjustment, or adherence. Consequently, residual confounding related to postdischarge treatment changes cannot be completely excluded. Second, due to the lack of structured AKI onset timestamps in the TriNetX platform and the retrospective nature of the analysis, the specific clinical indications for initiating acute dialysis could not be independently verified, and some cases may have reflected indications other than those presumed. Third, electronic health record data are limited by variable coding accuracy, incomplete capture of clinical variables (eg, medication adherence, laboratory result timing), and possible exposure misclassification due to reliance on prescription records. To minimize this risk, we adopted a new-user design, ensuring that all participants initiated SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy after discharge to reduce bias related to prior exposure. Similarly, events that occurred outside of participating health care organizations, did not lead to health care encounters, or were not coded may have been missed. Fourth, the discontinuous nature of electronic health records prevented a granular assessment of the immediate postinitiation eGFR trajectory or distinguished transient hemodynamic eGFR dip from progressive kidney injury in individual patients.33 While we could not explicitly show the reversibility of this hemodynamic perturbation in every patient, the robust association between early SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation and reduced risk of adverse kidney events supports the premise that any initial functional decline is benign and mechanistic (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). Fifth, the study design may have led to possible immortal time and guarantee-time biases, although landmark methods can reduce but not eliminate residual time-related confounding or confounding by clinical readiness for treatment initiation.34,35,36 Besides, due to the nature of an observational study, causal inference remains limited despite the emulation of key trial design features. Finally, the 30-day threshold was selected as a clinically meaningful postdischarge transition window, but it should not be interpreted as a definitive biological cutoff. To assess the robustness of this definition, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative initiation thresholds at day 15 and day 45, which showed consistency in kidney outcome benefit in the early intervention group (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). These findings suggest that while early initiation may be beneficial, the optimal timing of SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation remains to be determined.

Conclusions

This cohort study using a target trial emulation design in adults with type 2 diabetes recovering from AKI-D showed that initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors within 30 days after hospital discharge was associated with a lower risk of adverse kidney events compared with initiation between 31 and 90 days, without an increase in recorded short-term adverse events. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in all-cause mortality or MACEs. These findings raise the possibility that the early post-AKI recovery period may be a clinically relevant time frame for SGLT-2 inhibitor initiation and support prospective randomized clinical trials to clarify the optimal timing, efficacy, and safety of treatment.
