LIG1 overexpression enhances DNA repair and immune escape leading to poor prognosis in osteosarcoma
Authors: Wenju Fu, Guangman Tang, Yu Tang, Peng Yan, Qiangqiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS), the most common primary malignant bone tumor in adolescents, exhibits high metastasis and poor response to current therapies. DNA Ligase I (LIG1), a key enzyme in DNA replication and repair, has been implicated in multiple cancers, but its role in OS remains unclear.MethodsTranscriptomic and clinical data from the TCGA–OS cohort were analyzed to assess LIG1 expression, immune infiltration, and prognosis. Functional studies using gain- and loss-of-function assays evaluated its impact on OS cell viability, clonogenicity, and apoptosis. The effects of LIG1 inhibition by epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) were also examined.ResultsLIG1 was significantly upregulated in OS tissues and correlated with reduced overall and disease-free survival. High LIG1 expression was linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment with fewer cytotoxic T cells and increased Tregs and M2 macrophages. Functionally, LIG1 promoted OS cell proliferation and survival, while its inhibition by EGCG suppressed tumor growth.ConclusionsLIG1 drives OS progression and immune evasion by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. Background

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in adolescents and young adults, characterized by aggressive local invasion and a high propensity for pulmonary metastasis [1]. Despite advances in surgery and chemotherapy, the 5 year survival rate for OS remains poor, especially in metastatic or relapsed cases. Its high heterogeneity, drug resistance, and limited response to immunotherapy continue to hinder effective clinical management [2–4]. Despite advances in multimodal therapy, the 5 year survival rate of metastatic OS has stagnated at < 30%, underscoring the urgent need to identify pathogenic drivers and therapeutic targets to guide precision medicine [5, 6].

Defective DNA damage response (DDR) promotes genomic instability that drives osteosarcoma initiation and resistance while simultaneously reshaping the immune microenvironment by altering neoantigen load and immune surveillance [7–11]. Among various DNA repair enzymes, DNA ligase I (LIG1) plays an essential role in Okazaki fragment ligation and base excision repair (BER), serving as a core factor in maintaining genomic stability [12]. Overexpression of LIG1 has been reported in several solid tumors, including colorectal, ovarian, and liver cancers, where it is closely associated with enhanced cellular proliferation, abnormal S-phase progression, and poor clinical outcomes [13, 14]. For instance, recent studies indicate that LIG1 overexpression promotes the survival of breast cancer cells under DNA damage stress and collaborates with cell cycle regulators such as CDK2 and E2F1 to drive tumor progression [15, 16]. Moreover, high-throughput analyses suggest that LIG1 may influence multiple oncogenic pathways, including the p53 axis, ATM/ATR signaling, and epigenetic regulation [17, 18]. Nevertheless, the expression pattern, oncogenic functions, and regulatory mechanisms of LIG1 in OS remain poorly defined, representing a major gap in current knowledge.

Immune evasion constitutes a hallmark of OS progression and therapeutic failure, facilitated by a heterogeneous and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [19–23]. Given the interplay between DDR dysregulation and immune modulation, DNA repair enzymes such as LIG1 may represent key regulators of immune escape. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated success in certain solid tumors, their efficacy in OS remains limited, underscoring the necessity to explore intrinsic regulatory factors of immune escape [24–27]. Existing studies indicate that dysregulated expression of DNA repair genes may impair immune surveillance by interfering with antigen presentation, dampening interferon signaling, and upregulating immune checkpoint molecules [28, 29]. As a core component of the DNA repair machinery, LIG1 may modulate the cGAS–STING signaling pathway and influence type I interferon production, thereby reshaping the tumor immune landscape [30–32]. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests a positive correlation between LIG1 expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB), as well as expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules, such as PD-L1, implicating its potential role in multi-level immune regulation [33]. Nevertheless, comprehensive investigations into LIG1’s immunological function in OS are still lacking.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the OS tumor microenvironment is characterized by an abundance of immune cells, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [34, 35]. These cells play crucial roles in modulating immune escape and therapeutic responses [36–40]. Consequently, understanding how tumor-intrinsic factors, such as LIG1, regulate immune cell recruitment, polarization, and checkpoint molecule expression may reveal novel mechanisms of immune evasion and provide a theoretical foundation for developing combination immunotherapy strategies [41].

We systematically investigated LIG1 expression and function through multi-omics and functional analyses to clarify its roles in proliferation, immune evasion, and metabolic regulation, thereby defining its clinical utility as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target [42–46]. This study may provide novel insights into the role of LIG1 in mediating immune evasion and OS progression, thereby contributing to the development of individualized treatment and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Target prediction of itaconic acid using PharmMapper

To predict potential molecular targets of itaconic acid, compound structure files were obtained from established chemical databases. The Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) platform was initially accessed to search for the compound by name. Upon locating the relevant entry, the associated molecular structure file in Mol2 format was downloaded directly by selecting the compound image. Alternatively, the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was employed. The compound name was entered into the search bar, and from the search results, the 2D structural representation was selected. Using the “Download” option, the structure file in SDF format was retrieved by selecting the “SDF Save” function. The acquired molecular structure file (either in Tripos Mol2 or MDL SDF format) was subsequently uploaded to the PharmMapper web server for target prediction. PharmMapper is a pharmacophore-based target identification tool containing over 7000 receptor-based models, which collectively map to 1627 drug targets, including 459 human proteins [47]. PharmMapper utilized its comprehensive pharmacophore database to match the uploaded structure of eicosapentaenoic acid against its stored models [48]. The platform then provided a ranked list of the most likely binding targets based on the best-fit alignment between the query compound and the pharmacophore models. The top N predicted targets, along with their respective binding conformations, were downloaded for subsequent analysis.

Differential gene expression analysis and volcano plot construction

Tumor expression data were retrieved from the OS database to conduct a differential expression analysis. Differential gene expression between these two groups was assessed using the limma (Linear Models for Microarray Data) package in R. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|logFC|) greater than 0.585 and an adjusted p value below 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. A volcano plot was generated to visualize genes with significant differential expression, highlighting those with both high fold change and statistical significance [49].

Machine learning model construction for prognostic gene evaluation

To further investigate the prognostic relevance of four core upregulated genes (LIG1, PGK1, DCXR, and AK5), we developed a panel of machine learning models within a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework [50, 51]. A total of 15 prognostic models were developed, including Elastic Net with varying alpha parameters, Lasso, Ridge, stepwise Cox, and CoxBoost regression, to ensure robustness across different algorithms. Among these, the model denoted as Elastic_net_0.9 demonstrated promising discriminative ability for survival outcomes. To assess its performance, patients were stratified into two groups—High Elastic_net_0.9 and Low Elastic_net_0.9—based on their risk scores. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were then performed across multiple survival endpoints to evaluate the model's predictive power.

Genomic copy number variation analysis using GISTIC2

To identify genome-wide copy number alterations (CNAs), this study made use of GISTIC2.0 (Genomic Identification of Significant Targets in Cancer) to pinpoint key genomic targets with significant alterations, which evaluates CNVs based on GISTIC scores [52]. CNV data were processed from 451 tumor samples, enabling high-resolution detection of chromosomal gains and losses. Results were visualized as bar plots, where red indicated regions of copy number gain and green denoted regions of copy number loss, corresponding to positive and negative GISTIC scores.

Quantification of gene expression subtypes and genomic fraction analysis

Researchers measured genomic changes in tumor samples by assigning a genomic distance metric to each clone. The distance measurement consisted of half the inter-center distances between neighboring clones added together, while terminal clones with one adjacent probe measured their distance to the chromosomal end. The fraction of genome altered (FGA) was calculated as the proportion of autosomal regions with copy number changes. Similarly, the fraction of genome gained (FGG) and fraction of genome lost (FGL) quantified genomic gains and losses, respectively [53].To evaluate whether significant differences existed in FGA, FGG, or FGL across different gene expression tumor subgroups, we employed a one-way analysis of Variance (ANOVA). We conducted Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (TukeyHSD) post hoc comparisons after detecting significant variance among different groups. The analysis produced group mean pairwise difference estimates along with their confidence intervals and adjusted p values which allowed for comprehensive examination of intergroup variability.

Association between copy number variation metrics and gene expression profiles

This study explored the association between gene expression levels and copy number variation (CNV) scores by performing Spearman's rank correlation analysis, complemented by scatter plot visualization. Scatter plots were used to visualize both linear and non-linear associations, while Spearman’s correlation analysis was selected as it does not assume data normality [54, 55]. Values close to -1 or 1 on the correlation coefficient scale demonstrate stronger relationships. We calculated corresponding p values to determine the statistical validity of the data set.

Gene expression differences across copy number variation types

The TCGA Genome Characterization Center carried out a whole-genome microarray analysis for the experimental characterization of CNVs. CNV estimates at the gene level were generated using the GISTIC2 algorithm within the TCGA FIREHOSE pipeline, which classifies copy number states into five levels: – 2 (homozygous deletion), – 1 (single-copy loss), 0 (diploid), + 1 (low-level gain), and + 2 (high-level amplification). To examine the effect of CNV status on gene expression, we compared the expression levels of the 5 CNV classes. Statistical differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, also known as the H test. This is a non-parametric statistical method designed to evaluate whether at least one group among three or more independent samples differs significantly from the others [56, 57]. Unlike parametric tests, it does not assume a specific data distribution; instead, it ranks all data points and compares the distribution of ranks among groups.

Chi-square test for survival and mortality across gene expression quartiles

Patients were stratified into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on LIG1 expression, with Q1 representing the highest 25% and Q4 the lowest 25%. To assess whether the distribution of survival and mortality events differed significantly among these expression-defined subgroups, a chi-square test was conducted.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

The Kaplan–Meier survival curve was used to estimate the effect of LIG1 expression on the prognosis of patients. The survminer package was applied to determine the optimal cutoff for dichotomizing expression, ensuring that each group contained at least 30% of the samples. The statistical significance of the survival curves for high-expression and low-expression cohorts was assessed by means of the survfit function [58].

Univariate cox survival analysis

To assess the prognostic significance of individual genes in comparison with traditional clinical parameters, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted using the survival package in R. The hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were computed to quantify the relative risk associated with each variable. The results of the regression analysis were visualized using the forestploter package [59]. A meta-analysis was carried out employing the inverse-variance approach, utilizing the outcomes derived from univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses [60, 61]. The logarithm of the hazard ratio (logHR) served as the central metric for data synthesis. Hazard ratios (HRs) were stratified into two categories: values below 1 were interpreted as suggestive of a gene’s potential tumor-inhibiting function, whereas values exceeding 1 were considered indicative of a possible tumor-promoting effect. HRs were interpreted solely by magnitude (< 1, tumor-suppressive; > 1, tumor-promoting), independent of gene-specific biological context. All statistical computations and data visualizations were executed using the meta-package in R software (version 4.3.2).

Evaluation of MeTIL signature

To assess the MeTIL (methylation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes) characteristics, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the methylation levels of individual MeTIL markers to derive a composite score, referred to as the MeTIL score. Scores were standardized using Z-transformation [(x−μ)/σ] to eliminate unit-based variance. The samples were then stratified according to the mean level of LIG1 gene expression. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to assess the difference in MeTIL scores between the two groups, and to investigate the relationship between the level of gene expression and the TIP score, as well as the correlation between TIP scores. Visualization of these correlations was achieved using the linkET package.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Samples exhibiting the highest 30% expression levels of the target gene were categorized into the high-expression cohort, whereas those falling within the lowest 30% were grouped as low-expression. To identify differentially expressed genes between these two cohorts, the limma package was employed, generating log2 fold change (log2FC) values for each gene. Differentially expressed genes were ranked by log2 fold change, and enrichment analysis was performed using hallmark and KEGG gene sets.

To explore potential biological pathways associated with differential expression, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted via the GSEA function within the clusterProfiler package [62]. The analysis utilized hallmark gene sets and KEGG metabolic pathways to detect significantly enriched biological processes. For each gene set, an enrichment score (ES) was calculated, followed by statistical significance assessment and adjustment for multiple comparisons. The most significantly enriched pathways were then visualized using bubble plots for intuitive interpretation.

GSVA scoring of metabolic gene sets

Using the GSVA package in R, we conducted pathway-level analysis of metabolic gene sets derived from the KEGG database by applying the z-score-based enrichment scoring approach. To stratify the samples, those falling within the top 30% in terms of gene expression were classified as the high-expression group, while the bottom 30% were assigned to the low-expression group. Differential pathway activity between these two groups was subsequently evaluated using the limma package. Functional annotations for 14 distinct cellular states in cancer were obtained from the CancerSEA database [63]. The z-score-based scoring approach used in GSVA was originally proposed by Lee et al. [64], which integrates the expression of multiple key genes within a pathway to estimate its activity level in individual samples. Following this method, we used GSVA to calculate z-scores for gene sets corresponding to the 14 functional states defined by CancerSEA, generating a composite z-score for each sample. Scores were normalized using the scale function in R to generate functional state scores. To assess the relationship between gene expression profiles and these scores, we performed Pearson correlation analysis.

Cell culture

The human osteosarcoma cell line U2OS (ATCC, USA) was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and cultures were routinely tested to exclude mycoplasma contamination. Total RNA was isolated using MagZol Reagent (Magen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA with the HiScript QRT SuperMix (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) following the standard reaction system. The quality and concentration of RNA were assessed spectrophotometrically prior to reverse transcription.

Gain and loss of function studies

For the gain- and loss-of-function experiments, the LIG1 gene was either overexpressed or knocked down in U2OS cells using lentiviral transduction. The targeting sequences were sourced from Open Biosystems. For overexpression, a lentiviral vector encoding full-length human LIG1 cDNA (pLenti-CMV-LIG1-Puro) was packaged in HEK293T cells using standard second-generation packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G). Viral supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered, and used to infect U2OS cells with 8 µg/mL polybrene. Puromycin selection (1 µg/mL) was initiated 48 h post-infection and maintained for 5 days. For knockdown studies, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting LIG1 (obtained from Open Biosystems) were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector. Lentivirus was produced and transduced as described above. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT–PCR and Western blot analysis 72 h after transduction.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning, USA) were used to evaluate migration and invasion abilities. For migration assays, 5 × 104 transfected U2OS cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as chemoattractant. For invasion assays, the upper chamber membranes were precoated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA). After 24 h incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope in five randomly selected fields.

Cell viability assay

For the cell proliferation assay, cells that were transfected with knockdown, overexpression, and control plasmids were seeded into 24-well plates. Following a 16-h incubation, the cells were treated with or without LPS for 0, 6, and 24 h. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Beijing, China, Cat#C0038), in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in triplicate into 60-mm culture dishes and maintained under standard conditions for a period of 14–20 days. Subsequently, colony formation was assessed by staining with 0.4% crystal violet (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Plating efficiency (PE) was determined as the proportion of seeded cells that successfully formed colonies under standard culture conditions.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were cultured on slides for 24 h. After the exposure, the cells were irradiated and incubated for 1 h or 24 h. All treatments were performed, while the cells were attached to the slides. Following incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.5% (v/v) Triton™X-100 in PBS. A blocking step was carried out for 1 h with 10% (v/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Finally, the slides were incubated with a 1:10,000 dilution of a fluorescent-labeled monoclonal antibody (Millipore).

Annexin V staining for apoptotic cells

The cells were first washed with cold PBS and then trypsinized before being resuspended in 1X binding buffer. A 100 μl portion of the cell suspension was gently mixed with 5 μl of Annexin V/FITC (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and 10 μl of propidium iodide (PI) solution (50 μg/ml in PBS). The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Following the incubation, 400 μl of 1X binding buffer was added to each sample. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on an FACScan™ (BD Biosciences) (≥ 10,000 events per sample). Data analysis was subsequently carried out using CellQuest™ software (BD Biosciences).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t test, while multiple group comparisons were conducted by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Survival curves were generated using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Multi-omics analysis of eicosapentaenoic acid-targeted genes reveals the potential role of LIG1 in osteosarcoma

The volcano plot (Fig. 1A) showed significant differential expression in OS following eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) treatment. Key upregulated genes included LIG1, PGK1, DCXR, AK5, and SF3A3, while MGAT1, PEPD, ARSA, and LYZ were downregulated. These changes indicate that EPA affects critical metabolic and immune pathways in tumor cells. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in nucleotide metabolism, GMP biosynthesis, and purine metabolism (Fig. 1B). The DEGs were predominantly linked to vesicular transport and secretion, with molecular functions related to oxidoreductase activity and manganese ion binding. KEGG analysis reinforced the significance of nucleotide and purine metabolism in tumor progression. Cox regression revealed that upregulated genes (LIG1, AK5, DCXR, PGK1) were associated with poor prognosis (HR > 1, P < 0.05), with significant findings across overall survival (OS, Fig. 1E), disease-specific survival (DSS, Fig. 1C), and progression-free interval (PFI, Fig. 1D). In contrast, downregulated genes (LYZ, MGAT1, and PEPD) correlated with better survival outcomes (HR < 1). LIG1 expression was significantly elevated in OS tissues compared to normal bone tissues (P < 0.01, Fig. 1F). Single-cell analysis showed that LIG1 is mainly expressed in CD8 + exhausted T cells, with lower levels in CD4 + regulatory T cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, malignant cells, monocytes/macrophages, osteoblasts, and plasmocytes (Fig. 1G). LIG1 thus influences the tumor immune microenvironment.

Immune cell profiling revealed that LIG1-positive OS samples had higher proportions of monocytes/macrophages, malignant cells, fibroblasts, and CD8Tex, while LIG1-negative samples showed more CD4Tconv cells (Fig. 1H). LIG1 overexpression appears to promote tumor progression and immune suppression through its impact on immune landscape modification. Immune subtyping of TCGA data revealed that LIG1-high tumors were enriched in C1 (wound healing), C2 (IFN-γ dominant), and C4 (exhausted T cell) subtypes, whereas LIG1-low tumors predominantly displayed C3 (inflammatory) and C6 (TGF-β dominant) subtypes (Fig. 1I). A decrease in LIG1 expression aligns with enhanced immune activity. The combined results reveal that LIG1 functions as a principal controller in the OS immune microenvironment and affects both tumor advancement and patient prognosis by modulating immune responses. Research findings indicate that simultaneous targeting of LIG1 and immune therapies could lead to better treatment results for patients with OS.

Multi-model prediction validates LIG1 as a key prognostic molecule for osteosarcoma

To further evaluate the prognostic value of upregulated genes in OS, 15 machine learning models incorporating LIG1, PGK1, DCXR, and AK5 were developed using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), with LIG1 showing the highest regression coefficient, underscoring its key role in prognosis prediction (Fig. 2A). For further validation of the model’s generalization ability, we applied these 15 LIG1-centered models to both TCGA and GSE–OS data sets (Fig. 2B). The Concordance Index (C-index) was calculated for each model across different cohorts, with the Elastic_net_0.9 model consistently exhibiting high C-index values in multiple data sets. This demonstrated the stability and robust prognostic prediction ability of the model across diverse patient cohorts. To assess the risk stratification capability of the Elastic_net_0.9 model at various survival endpoints, patients were categorized into high-risk (High Elastic_net_0.9) and low-risk (Low Elastic_net_0.9) groups. Survival analysis across multiple endpoints (OS, Fig. 2C, D; DSS, Fig. 2E, F; DFS, Fig. 2G, H; DFI, Fig. 2I, J; PFS, Fig. 2K, L; PFI, Fig. 2M, N) consistently showed worse outcomes in the High Elastic_net_0.9 group compared to the Low Elastic_net_0.9 group, with log-rank P < 0.05 for all comparisons. These findings highlight LIG1 as a core predictive marker whose overexpression consistently indicates poor prognosis across multiple survival endpoints in osteosarcoma.

High LIG1 expression is an independent predictor of poor prognosis in osteosarcoma (OS)

To assess the impact of LIG1 expression on survival outcomes in OS, we categorized patients into four groups based on LIG1 expression levels and performed survival analyses using the chi-square test (Fig. 3A). Patients were stratified into quartiles by LIG1 expression, and those in the highest quartile (Q1) exhibited a significantly greater proportion of deaths, indicating an association between elevated LIG1 expression and poorer prognosis in OS.

Subsequently, we performed Kaplan–Meier survival analyses to assess the association between LIG1 expression levels and four major survival outcomes: overall survival, disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI), and progression-free interval (PFI) (Fig. 3B–E). In each of these analyses, patients exhibiting lower LIG1 expression consistently showed significantly improved survival outcomes compared to those with higher expression levels (log-rank p values: OS = 0.008; DSS = 0.015; DFI = 0.027; PFI = 0.023). These results reinforce the potential of LIG1 as an adverse prognostic biomarker in OS. To determine whether LIG1 serves as an independent prognostic factor, we conducted Cox proportional hazards regression analyses at both the univariate and multivariate levels while controlling for possible confounding variables, such as age, sex, and metastatic status (Fig. 3F). Univariate and multivariate analyses both confirmed that high LIG1 expression is an independent predictor of poor overall survival, even after adjusting for age and TNM stage (HR = 1.479, P < 0.001). These findings underscore the role of LIG1 as an independent prognostic biomarker for overall survival. To further validate these findings, we performed a meta-analysis of univariate Cox regression results from multiple independent data sets (Fig. 3G). The pooled hazard ratio (HR = 1.18, 95% CI [1.07; 1.29]) confirmed the consistent association between high LIG1 expression and poor survival across various cohorts, including TCGA–OS and other public data sets. The observed moderate heterogeneity still supported the robustness of LIG1 as a negative prognostic factor. In addition to overall LIG1 expression, we examined the prognostic value of a specific splice variant, LIG1_AA_Exon13.1. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with high expression of this isoform had significantly worse progression-free interval (PFI) outcomes compared to those with low expression (log-rank P = 0.005), indicating the importance of this particular splice variant in influencing patient prognosis in OS (Fig. 3H). Finally, we investigated how LIG1 expression correlates with immunotherapy outcomes in patients with OS. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that individuals exhibiting elevated LIG1 levels experienced significantly reduced PFS following immunotherapy (log-rank P = 0.004). This observation implies that heightened LIG1 expression may facilitate immune evasion or suppression, thereby diminishing the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy in OS cases (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that high LIG1 expression not only serves as a poor prognostic marker in OS but also correlates with inferior survival in patients undergoing immunotherapy. This positions LIG1 as a potentially crucial biomarker for both prognosis and therapeutic response in OS.

Genomic copy number variations regulate LIG1 expression and are associated with poor biological features in osteosarcoma

The TCGA–OS project's whole-genome CNV analysis of 257 OS samples revealed significant copy number gains on chromosomes 1 and 12 and frequent losses on chromosome 13 (Fig. 4A). These genomic alterations were associated with LIG1 upregulation, suggesting a role of CNV in the regulation of LIG1 expression. The research shows that these genomic changes likely function as primary driver hotspots within OS. We next analyzed the relationship between LIG1 expression and CNV by stratifying patients according to LIG1 levels. As shown in Fig. 4B, the high-expression group (Q1) exhibited significantly higher frequencies of genomic alterations, including the Fraction of Genome Altered (FGA), Gained (FGG), and Lost (FGL), compared with other groups. These findings indicate that elevated LIG1 expression is linked to extensive genomic instability, which may underlie more aggressive phenotypes and resistance to conventional therapies. Next, we assessed whether the elevated expression of LIG1 is directly influenced by genomic copy number alterations. Spearman's correlation analysis between LIG1 GISTIC scores and its mRNA expression levels revealed a significant positive correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.23, P = 0.000211) (Fig. 4C). Although the correlation was moderate, it was statistically significant, suggesting that part of the upregulation of LIG1 expression is driven by increased copy number, which reflects its involvement in complex regulatory networks. Furthermore, we compared LIG1 expression levels across different CNV types, including deep deletion (C1), shallow deletion (C2), diploid (C3), and gain (C4) (Fig. 4D). The results demonstrated a clear trend of increasing LIG1 expression from deep deletion to shallow deletion, diploid, and finally, copy number gain. Specifically, samples with copy number gains (C4) showed significantly higher levels of LIG1 expression compared to other CNV types, with a p value < 0.001. This finding further supports the critical role of genomic copy number alterations in driving the upregulation of LIG1 expression. In summary, our results indicate that LIG1 upregulation is not only associated with poor prognosis in OS but also closely linked to genomic instability. The expression of LIG1 appears to be partially regulated by CNV alterations, positioning LIG1 as a key functional gene driven by CNV in OS. This highlights LIG1's potential as an important biomarker for identifying genomic instability subtypes and guiding precision therapeutic strategies in OS.

Elevated levels of LIG1 expression are linked to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, indicating its potential involvement in facilitating immune evasion mechanisms in osteosarcoma

Elevated LIG1 expression is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in OS. Analysis of methylation-based TIL (MeTIL) scores revealed significantly lower values in the high LIG1 expression group, indicating reduced lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. 5A). Consistently, cytotoxicity (CYT) and tumor-associated lymphoid structure (TLS) scores were both decreased in patients with high LIG1 expression (Fig. 5B, C), suggesting impaired antitumor immune activity. Tumor Immune Phenotype (TIP) scoring further demonstrated that elevated LIG1 expression was negatively correlated with multiple stages of the cancer-immunity cycle, including antigen presentation, T-cell recruitment, and immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5D). Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed that high LIG1 expression was accompanied by an increased abundance of Tregs and M2 macrophages, while overall TIL infiltration was suppressed (Fig. 5E, G, H). This shift toward immunosuppressive cell populations reinforces the role of LIG1 in promoting immune evasion. Moreover, TMEscore analysis, which integrates immune and stromal components, showed a significant inverse correlation with LIG1 expression (R = – 0.34, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5F), further supporting its contribution to an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Collectively, these findings indicate that LIG1 reshapes the OS immune landscape by reducing cytotoxic immune activity and fostering an immunosuppressive state enriched in Tregs and M2 macrophages. These results highlight LIG1 as a potential therapeutic target for restoring effective antitumor immunity in osteosarcoma.

High expression of LIG1 is associated with enrichment in cell cycle and DNA repair pathways, along with specific metabolic reprogramming features in osteosarcoma

We performed GSEA on TCGA–OS data to evaluate pathway enrichment between high and low LIG1 expression groups, integrating Hallmark and KEGG metabolic gene sets (Fig. 6A). High LIG1 expression was significantly associated with enrichment of pathways regulating cell cycle progression, DNA repair, and metabolic processes. Specifically, enrichment of Hallmark G2M checkpoint, E2F targets, and Mitotic spindle pathways suggests that elevated LIG1 expression facilitates accelerated cell cycle progression. Metabolic pathways including KEGG pyrimidine metabolism, lysine degradation, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism were also enriched, indicating that LIG1 may drive specialized metabolic adaptations to support rapid tumor cell proliferation. The prominent enrichment of pathways related to stress response and DNA repair including the Hallmark Unfolded protein response and DNA repair confirms LIG1's role in preserving genomic stability throughout tumor development.

GSVA further validated these findings, demonstrating significant alterations in metabolic pathways linked to high LIG1 expression (Fig. 6B). GSVA analysis revealed marked upregulation of pathways involved in N-glycan biosynthesis and pyrimidine and purine metabolism, providing essential precursors for nucleic acid synthesis and supporting rapid tumor cell proliferation. Conversely, mitochondrial energy production pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and biotin metabolism, were significantly downregulated in high LIG1 samples, suggesting a metabolic shift toward glycolysis. This suggests that elevated LIG1 expression may drive a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation toward glycolysis, resembling the Warburg effect observed in many malignancies. To gain deeper insights into the potential functional implications of LIG1 expression in oncogenic processes, we conducted a correlation analysis between LIG1 expression Z-scores and GSVA-derived enrichment scores across 14 tumor-related biological states, encompassing aspects, such as cell cycle regulation, DNA damage response, DNA repair mechanisms, and proliferative activity (Fig. 6C). The results revealed that higher LIG1 expression was significantly and positively associated with multiple cancer-related functional programs. In particular, notable positive correlations were observed between LIG1 and cell proliferation (R = 0.39, p = 7e-05), as well as DNA repair processes (R = 0.57, p = 7.7e-10). Additional strong associations were identified with DDR pathways and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), further supporting the role of LIG1 in tumor progression. Collectively, these findings suggest that LIG1 overexpression promotes oncogenesis by facilitating DNA repair capacity, accelerating cell cycle progression, and inducing metabolic alterations favorable for tumor development. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the contribution of LIG1 to cancer progression and offer a foundation for future studies focused on targeting LIG1 in therapeutic strategies.

LIG1 expression correlates significantly with key factors in cell proliferation, immune checkpoints, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and inflammatory pathways in osteosarcoma

To investigate the regulatory role of LIG1 in tumor cells and the immune microenvironment, we examined its association with multiple molecular markers. Quartile analysis of LIG1 expression revealed significant associations with immune checkpoint molecules, proliferation markers, epithelial stability, and EMT. Supplementary Fig. 1A–C demonstrates a strong correlation between increased LIG1 expression and elevated levels of the cell proliferation marker MKI67 (p < 0.001), the immune checkpoint molecule CTLA4 (p = 0.029), and the epithelial marker CDH1 (p = 0.002). Elevated MKI67 reflects enhanced tumor cell proliferation, while increased CTLA4 suggests immune evasion through suppression of T cell activity. Higher CDH1 expression indicates that LIG1 overexpression may help maintain epithelial phenotype stability in tumor cells.

Further examination of LIG1 expression revealed negative correlations with multiple molecules involved in inflammation, programmed cell death, and EMT regulation (Supplementary Fig. 1D–M). High LIG1 expression was associated with significant downregulation of inflammation- and apoptosis-related genes, including CASP1, CASP4, IL1B, and IL18 (p < 0.001–0.026). Furthermore, EMT-related markers KRT19, SNAI1, and VIM were also significantly reduced in high LIG1 tumors (p < 0.001–0.021), suggesting that LIG1 overexpression may suppress inflammation and modulate mesenchymal traits.

Expression of LIG1 resulted in changes to both cell cycle control mechanisms and T cell immune checkpoint molecules. In the high-LIG1 group, CCND1 and HAVCR2 (TIM-3) showed increased expression (p = 0.001 and 0.029, respectively). By contrast, TIGIT expression was reduced (p = 0.049). LIG1 acts to promote cell cycle progression while simultaneously altering immune regulatory pathways in tumor settings. High LIG1 expression is linked to accelerated tumor growth, reduced immune activity, and modulation of inflammatory and EMT pathways. These findings highlight the dual role of LIG1 in promoting tumor progression and immune evasion, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in combination with immune pathways.

Functional validation of LIG1's oncogenic role in osteosarcoma pathogenesis

To confirm the prognostic significance of LIG1 identified through multi-omics analyses, we performed gain- and loss-of-function studies in OS cells. LIG1 overexpression (OE–LIG1) significantly elevated mRNA levels (2.8-fold vs. EV controls, P < 0.001; Fig. 7A), while two independent shRNAs (sh1-LIG1/sh2-LIG1) achieved > 60% knockdown (P ≤ 0.001 vs. sh-NC; Fig. 7A). Critically, this genetic manipulation directly modulated cellular resilience: under LPS-induced stress, OE-LIG1 cells exhibited time-dependent enhancement of viability (1.9-fold increase at 48 h vs. EV, P = 0.001), whereas LIG1-depleted cells showed progressive growth suppression (45–52% reduction, P ≤ 0.005; Fig. 7B). Extending to molecular phenotyping, qPCR analysis revealed that LIG1 knockdown coordinately downregulated proliferative (MKI67), immune checkpoint (CTLA4), and epithelial stability (CDH1) markers while upregulating apoptosis initiators (*CASP1/4*) and EMT-related KRT19 (all P < 0.05 vs. NC; Fig. 7C). Importantly, these transcriptomic shifts were functionally consequential: clonogenic capacity increased 2.1-fold in OE–LIG1 cells (P < 0.001) but collapsed by 70% post-knockdown (P < 0.001; Fig. 7D, E)—a phenotype rescued through LIG1 re-expression. Consistent with these observations, LIG1 ablation triggered profound apoptosis (3.2-fold increase vs. NC, P < 0.001; Fig. 7F) and suppressed proliferation markers PCNA/Ki67, evidenced by diminished immunofluorescence intensity and co-localization (Fig. 7G). Transwell assays further demonstrated that LIG1 overexpression significantly enhanced both migration and invasion capacities of U2OS cells, whereas LIG1 knockdown markedly suppressed these phenotypes (P < 0.01; Fig. 7H). Collectively, these results establish LIG1 as an essential regulator of osteosarcoma cell survival, proliferation, and metastatic potential.

Pharmacological targeting of LIG1 suppresses osteosarcoma cell proliferation, colony formation, and EMT

Based on genetic evidence that LIG1 has an oncogenic role, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of its pharmacological inhibition using EGCG. EGCG is a small molecule known to modulate DNA repair. EGCG induced a dose-dependent suppression of LIG1 expression, with high-dose treatment resulting in a 1.8-fold reduction compared to control (P = 0.001; Fig. 8A). This suppression translated into marked cytotoxic effects: high-dose EGCG treatment decreased cell viability by 60% at 48 h (P = 0.004; Fig. 8B). The effect of EGCG on cell viability was both dose- and time-dependent. EGCG treatment recapitulated LIG1 knockdown effects, upregulating pro-apoptotic genes (CASP1, CASP4) and downregulating markers of proliferation (MKI67), immune checkpoints (CTLA4), and EMT (KRT19) (P < 0.05; Fig. 8C). These molecular shifts correlated with a profound reduction in clonogenic capacity, as EGCG treatment suppressed colony formation by over 75% (P < 0.001; Fig. 8D). To delineate the functional contribution of LIG1 to EGCG response, we performed rescue experiments involving genetic LIG1 knockdown or overexpression. qPCR analysis revealed that EGCG + sh1-LIG1 treatment led to synergistic transcriptional remodeling: CASP1 and CASP4 were further upregulated (1.8- and 1.9-fold vs. NC; P < 0.001), while MKI67, CTLA4, and KRT19 expression levels were further reduced (0.3-, 0.4-, and 0.4-fold vs. EGCG alone; P < 0.01). In addition, CDH1 expression increased (1.3-fold vs. NC), suggesting a partial reversal of EMT (Fig. 8E). Functionally, EGCG + sh1-LIG1 led to a further 40% reduction in colony formation compared to EGCG alone (P = 0.003), while LIG1 overexpression restored clonogenicity by nearly 50% (P = 0.01), underscoring LIG1’s critical role in sustaining long-term proliferative potential (Fig. 8F). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed these findings: EGCG treatment markedly suppressed nuclear co-localization of PCNA and Ki-67, indicating proliferation arrest, whereas LIG1 overexpression rescued these proliferative markers, reinstating cell cycle progression (Fig. 8G).

These data demonstrate that EGCG exerts potent anti-tumor effects in OS by targeting LIG1. The therapeutic efficacy of EGCG is mechanistically dependent on LIG1 suppression, highlighting LIG1 as a promising molecular target for OS intervention.

Discussion

OS is a highly malignant primary bone tumor that predominantly affects children and adolescents [65, 66]. It is marked by pronounced genomic instability, aggressive invasiveness, and a high propensity for metastasis, leading to extremely poor outcomes in patients with metastatic or recurrent disease [67–69]. Unfortunately, the currently available treatments remain of limited effectiveness. Emerging evidence suggests that DNA repair mechanisms are closely linked to tumor immune evasion, and that the remodeling of the immune microenvironment is a key factor underlying resistance to immunotherapy in OS [70]. As a core enzyme in DNA repair pathways, LIG1 plays a critical role in maintaining genomic stability and may also be involved in immune escape and therapeutic response, thereby influencing OS progression and clinical outcomes. Although LIG1 has been associated with poor prognosis in several solid tumors, its expression pattern, regulatory mechanisms, and clinical value in OS have not yet been comprehensively characterized. In this study, we systematically interrogated public data sets from TCGA and GEO, integrating single-cell transcriptomics, immune infiltration profiling, survival analysis, and in vitro functional experiments [71, 72]. Specifically, we explored the association between LIG1 overexpression and immune microenvironment remodeling, aberrant DNA repair mechanisms, increased tumor heterogeneity, and deteriorating clinical prognosis. Our goal was to evaluate LIG1 as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target, thereby providing both a theoretical rationale and experimental evidence to support individualized immunotherapy and combination intervention strategies in OS [73].

Our analyses of TCGA–OS and multiple external validation cohorts revealed that LIG1 expression in OS tissues is significantly elevated compared to adjacent normal tissue, consistently across data sets. Survival analyses indicated that high LIG1 expression correlates with poorer outcomes, including shorter overall survival, disease-free survival (DFS), and progression‑free survival (PFS). Crucially, multivariate Cox regression and several machine learning‑based prognostic models (e.g., LASSO‑Cox and random forest) confirmed the robust prognostic value of LIG1 independent of conventional clinical variables, such as age, sex, or tumor stage.

Mechanistic analysis indicated that LIG1, a key ligase in DNA replication and repair, enhances cell cycle and DNA repair signaling, thereby allowing tumor cells to tolerate genomic damage and sustain malignant proliferation. LIG1 may enhance DNA replication and repair, allowing tumor cells to withstand genomic damage and maintain proliferation. Although the direct impact of LIG1 inhibition on DNA damage accumulation and chemosensitivity (e.g., to cisplatin) remains to be evaluated, this represents a promising future direction, where LIG1 targeting could potentiate DNA‑damaging therapies [74].

Genomic profiling revealed that focal copy number amplifications on chromosomes 1 and 12, which harbor known oncogenes, may partially drive LIG1 overexpression, implicating chromosomal instability in its upregulation. High‑LIG1 tumors also exhibited altered microsatellite instability profiles and elevated tumor mutation burden (TMB), implying that LIG1 may cooperate with other genetic instability mechanisms to promote tumor heterogeneity and evolution. Additional analyses indicated that transcription factors E2F1 and MYC have binding motifs in the LIG1 promoter region, potentially mediating transcriptional activation, and that LIG1 is located near active enhancer regions (marked by H3K27ac and H3K4me1), suggesting epigenetic regulation. Although DNA methylation status at the LIG1 promoter was not directly examined, this remains an important aspect for future investigation.

This study further elucidated that LIG1 overexpression is closely associated with remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. Immune infiltration analysis via CIBERSORT, TIMER, and xCell revealed that high‑LIG1 tumors are enriched for immunosuppressive cell populations, including M2‑polarized macrophages and Tregs while showing decreased infiltration of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells and activated NK cells—consistent with an immune‑evasive “cold tumour” phenotype. Furthermore, LIG1 expression is positively correlated with immune checkpoint molecules, such as PD‑L1 (CD274), CTLA‑4, and TIGIT, suggesting that LIG1 may contribute to establishing an immunosuppressive milieu that limits the effectiveness of ICIs.

Moreover, pathway enrichment indicated that high‑LIG1 tumors upregulate immunosuppressive cytokine pathways, including IL‑10 and TGF‑β signaling, which foster M2 macrophage polarization and Treg expansion while concurrently downregulating antigen processing and MHC class I/II presentation pathways—likely reducing tumor immunogenicity. Although we did not compare LIG1 to known OS biomarkers (such as TP53 mutations or ALK rearrangements), future prospective studies integrating such comparisons would better clarify its combined predictive value and clinical utility [75].

Metabolic pathway analyses (GSEA and KEGG) demonstrated that LIG1–high tumors are enriched in fatty acid metabolism, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, implying LIG1’s involvement in metabolic reprogramming that supports tumor proliferation and generates a metabolically hostile immune microenvironment. Protein–protein interaction network analysis revealed that LIG1 is co-expressed with MCM2, PCNA, RPA1, and other genes involved in chromatin remodeling, DNA repair, and cell cycle regulation—further supporting its pivotal role in coordinating DNA replication and repair in highly proliferative tumors [76, 77].

In vitro functional assays demonstrated that LIG1 overexpression enhances OS cell proliferation and clonogenicity, whereas LIG1 knockdown induces apoptosis, reduces proliferation- and immune‑escape-related gene expression, and significantly impairs cell migration and invasion—suggesting a role in metastasis. We acknowledge that in vivo metastasis validation, such as tail vein injection assays in mice, was not performed in this study. This remains a limitation, as it prevents us from directly confirming whether LIG1 regulates distal colonization in vivo. Future work will incorporate such models to strengthen the causal evidence for LIG1’s role in osteosarcoma metastasis.

In terms of therapeutic targeting, we demonstrated that the natural compound EGCG effectively suppresses LIG1 expression, activates pro-apoptotic gene programs, and significantly inhibits tumor cell viability. Importantly, we observed synergy between EGCG and LIG1 knockdown, while forced LIG1 overexpression partially diminished the anti-tumor effect of EGCG—indicating that LIG1 serves as a key mediator of EGCG’s mechanism of action. Further studies have shown that EGCG enhances the sensitivity of tumor cells to DNA damage by interfering with the DNA repair regulatory network, particularly by weakening the base excision repair pathway. This provides a robust experimental rationale for the combination of LIG1 inhibitors with natural compounds or other treatment modalities in OS [79].

This study elucidates the multifaceted role of LIG1 in the development and progression of osteosarcoma through multi-omics analyses and in vitro functional validation. This study is limited by its reliance on TCGA and GEO data sets and the absence of multicenter or in vivo validation, leaving the causal role of LIG1 in tumor progression and immune remodeling unconfirmed [80, 81]. The upstream regulators, precise mechanisms, and interactions with other oncogenic pathways remain ambiguous, and the pharmacodynamics and safety of inhibitors such as EGCG necessitate further evaluation [82, 83]. Future research should integrate multicenter clinical cohorts with animal and organoid models to elucidate the mechanistic and clinical significance of LIG1 [84–86]. In addition, the development of potent, specific inhibitors, alongside exploration of combination strategies with DNA damage repair inhibitors and immune checkpoint blockade, is essential to provide a robust theoretical and experimental foundation for precision therapy in osteosarcoma [87, 88].

Overall, this study uniquely integrates multi-omics analyses and functional validation to demonstrate that LIG1 contributes to the progression of OS through mechanisms involving genomic instability, immune evasion, metabolic reprogramming, and tumor heterogeneity [89, 90]. LIG1 emerges as a promising prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target. Future translational research should explore strategies that combine DNA damage repair inhibitors, immune checkpoint blockade, and LIG1-targeted therapies to overcome therapeutic resistance and improve clinical outcomes in patients with OS [1, 91–94].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LIG1 functions as a key prognostic biomarker and oncogenic driver in OS. Through its functions in immune evasion, metabolic adaptation, cell cycle regulation, and DNA repair, LIG1 accelerates tumor progression. Poor clinical outcomes and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment are linked to its overexpression. These findings not only broaden our understanding of OS pathobiology but also support the potential of LIG1 as a therapeutic target. Combining LIG1 inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade may offer a novel strategy to enhance treatment efficacy in patients with this aggressive bone malignancy.
