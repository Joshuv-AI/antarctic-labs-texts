Nutritional deficits in paediatric extracorporeal membrane oxygenation patients: the impact of lipid-free parenteral nutrition on caloric intake and outcomes
Authors: Mohammed Almutham, Manar Alawwad, Ibraheem Shammakhi, Samia Alhashim, Mohammad Alsharhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimWhen extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) was first introduced for paediatric patients, lipid emulsions were mostly administered directly into the ECMO circuit. However, clinicians have observed that lipid emulsions tend to separate and agglutinate within the circuit, raising concerns about potential complications. Many institutions therefore opted to exclude lipids from parenteral regimens, inadvertently leading to inadequate nutritional support for these critically ill patients. This study measured the caloric deficit resulting from the lack of lipid provision to paediatric ECMO patients in relation to the patients’ basal energy expenditure (BEE) and explored the resulting nutritional outcomes.Methods and resultsThis was a retrospective, single-centre study conducted at a tertiary care hospital. All paediatric ECMO patients receiving parenteral nutrition (PN) from the period of April 2020 to April 2023 were included in the study.Twenty-three patients were identified. Of these, 18 (78.26%) did not achieve the target BEE. The average calorie deficit of all patients was 40.23% of the calculated BEE (SD=17.468%). Of the patients who had a calorie deficit, eight (50%) died. No significant differences were found in patients’ weights before and after stopping PN (p=0.3594).ConclusionThis study explored the challenge of meeting the nutritional needs of paediatric ECMO patients when lipids are excluded from PN. Significant caloric deficits require alternative strategies to prevent patient malnourishment without affecting the ECMO circuit’s functionality.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Nutritional management of paediatric patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is complex, and concerns about lipid agglutination within ECMO circuits have led many centres to omit lipids from parenteral nutrition (PN). This practice can result in significant energy deficits and may affect recovery and outcomes, but data quantifying this deficit in paediatric ECMO patients remain limited.

Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a technique that is used to bypass the cardiopulmonary systems in cases of failures of the heart or lungs.1 It provides an artificial method for oxygenating and decarboxylating a patient’s blood and allows the mechanical ventilation during its use as a bridging therapy.2 It works by draining the blood from a patient’s vascular system, circulating it outside the body through a mechanical pump and reintroducing the oxygenated blood to the patient’s vascular system. While in the circuit and outside the native circulation system, the blood passes through an oxygenator and heat exchanger, in which carbon dioxide (CO2) is removed from the blood and oxygen is added.3

ECMO patients undergo specific stressors that could affect their clinical and nutritional status due to the method of oxygenation and the details of their clinical backgrounds. These patients are critically ill and thus in special need of optimal nutrition. However, various challenges arise when providing nutrition to ECMO patients, especially paediatric patients. When resting, 20%–25% of cardiac output is distributed to the splanchnic circulation, and this can be double after feeding. In cases of shock, the splanchnic and peripheral tissue circulation vasoconstrict, which results in an increased oxygen demand and creates an intestinal imbalance. This could lead to issues like intercellular oedema, necrosis and apoptosis, which could in turn rupture the intestinal epithelial layer and bacterial translocation.4 5

While there is not enough data regarding the delivery of nutrition to ECMO patients receiving parenteral nutrition (PN), the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) has stated that it is reasonable to focus on the early and careful increase of calories provided by enteral nutrition (EN) and begin PN within 3–5 days for malnourished children and within 5–7 days for well-nourished children if EN alone does not meet the nutrient delivery target.6 Despite this recommendation, PN is still used more than EN for paediatric patients in the early phase of ECMO.7

Despite the scarcity of data regarding nutrition for paediatric ECMO patients, its importance cannot be ignored because it can affect patient outcomes, including mortality rates. Nevertheless, nutritional support for ECMO patients often remains suboptimal, with frequent delays in initiating EN, omission of lipids and cumulative deficits compared with measured or estimated requirements.8 When ECMO was first introduced for paediatric patients, lipid emulsions were infused via a separate intravenous catheter or directly into the ECMO circuit. However, lipid emulsions have been implicated in ECMO circuit complications in different reports. Lipid agglutination can lead to fat deposition on oxygenator fibres, causing rising transmembrane pressures, reduced oxygen transfer and carbon dioxide clearance and premature oxygenator exchange. Reports describe visible lipemic layering in tubing and bubble traps, circuit foaming and increased clot formation within the circuit, all of which compromise membrane longevity and circuit performance, which led various institutions to omit lipids from PN, resulting in the underfeeding of patients who need robust nutritional support.9 10

The German Society for Nutritional Medicine (DGEM) released special guidelines for clinical nutrition in critical care medicine in 2021 and stated that ECMO patients can receive lipids through a central venous line made with newer polymethyl-pentene membranes.8 Despite this recommendation, which was also adopted by ASPEN,11 many facilities still do not provide lipids for ECMO patients, either through a separate central line or through the ECMO circuit. This study measured the caloric deficits that result from not providing lipids to paediatric ECMO patients in relation to those patients’ measured basal energy expenditure (BEE) requirements and examines the outcomes that resulted from this deficit.

Methods

Design

This was a retrospective, single-centre study conducted at KFSHRC, a tertiary care hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. All paediatric ECMO patients receiving PN from April 2020 to April 2023 were included in the study, while ECMO patients not receiving PN (or vice versa) were excluded.

Data collection

Patient data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics, clinical history and nutritional parameters. Information on age, gender, weight and z-scores was extracted both at baseline and at the time of PN discontinuation. The primary indications for PN initiation, duration of PN therapy and any concurrent EN use were recorded. The nutritional intake data included total caloric and protein delivery, BEE calculations and glucose infusion rates (GIRs). The target achievement for BEE was calculated based on the Scofield equation. Biochemical markers (eg, blood urea levels, glucose levels) were collected to evaluate metabolic responses to PN. Additionally, patient outcomes, including survival, were documented to analyse those outcomes’ potential associations with nutritional adequacy. Weight measurements between baseline and after PN discontinuation were recorded to assess the nutritional impact. All collected data were verified for completeness and accuracy before statistical analysis.

Primary and secondary endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study was the measurement of the caloric deficit resulting from not providing lipids to paediatric ECMO patients in relation to their BEE for all patients on PN and EN. Secondary endpoints included assessing the impact of larger caloric deficits on patients’ in-hospital mortality and measuring the difference in weight between baseline and the time of PN discontinuation.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Continuous variables, including age, z-scores and calorie deficits, were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were reported as means with SD, whereas abnormally distributed variables were presented as medians with IQRs. Categorical variables—including gender distribution, nutritional targets and clinical outcomes—were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Correlation analysis was conducted to assess relationships between calorie deficits and mortality using Spearman’s rank correlation for non-parametric distributions. Additionally, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to evaluate changes in weight at baseline and after the discontinuation of PN. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Data were analysed using SPSS (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) to ensure an accurate, reproducible analysis.

Results

The study included 23 patients who were on ECMO and PN. Of these patients, 12 (52.17%) were female and 11 (47.82%) were males. The median age of the patients was 5.1 months, with an age range from 8 days to 7 years and 1 month. The indication for starting PN in 18 (78.26%) patients was due to intolerance of EN during the postoperative phase. The patients’ z-scores ranged from −3.78 to 1.47, indicating a wide variation in the nutritional or growth status among the participants (table 1). The mean z-score was −1.33, with a median of −1.18.

Target protein dosing was achieved in 13 (72.22%) of the 18 patients who did not achieve the target BEE. High urea was the reason protein dosing could not be achieved in the other five patients. Moreover, none of the 18 patients reached the maximum GIR. This was due to significant hyperglycaemia in 3 (16.66%) patients and to overfill in PN preparation after increasing dextrose in 15 (83.34%) patients. Of these 18 patients, only 9 (50%) survived. On the other hand, four out of the five patients who did not have a calorie deficit passed away. The Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) between the size of the calorie deficit and mortality was −0.22. The mean calorie deficit for patients who died was 123.66 kilocalories (kcal), while the mean calorie deficit for patients who survived was 188.72 kcal. Contrary to expectations, this reflects a weak negative correlation between the level of calorie deficit and mortality. However, in all cases of mortality, this outcome may have been due to deterioration in patients’ overall clinical status rather than a failure to achieve the target BEE.

Additionally, no significant difference was found in patients’ weights between baseline and after discontinuing PN (p=0.3594). This could be attributed to ECMO patients often receiving significant volumes of fluids (eg, for resuscitation, medications or nutrition). This can lead to fluid retention, which would mask fat or muscle catabolism.

Discussion

Omitting lipids from PN may be particularly concerning in critically ill paediatric patients, who not only require lipids for normal growth and development but also face additional metabolic demands due to critical illness. This is especially true for paediatric patients due to the critical role lipids play in children’s growth, development and overall health.12 Lipids are a vital source of essential fatty acids (EFAs) and a dense source of calories, both of which are necessary to support the high metabolic demands of growing children. The absence of lipids can lead to EFA deficiency, which may result in impaired neurological development, compromised immune function and disrupted cellular membrane integrity.13 Moreover, without the caloric contribution from lipids, the energy needs of paediatric patients may not be met, leading to increased reliance on carbohydrates and amino acids for energy. This imbalance increases the risk of metabolic complications, such as hyperglycaemia and protein catabolism, which can impede growth and tissue repair.14 Additionally, lipid-free PN can exacerbate oxidative stress, as lipids in PN formulations often include antioxidants like vitamin E, which help mitigate free radical damage.15 Paediatric patients, particularly neonates and those with critical illnesses, are particularly vulnerable to the consequences of nutritional inadequacies, given their limited physiological reserves and ongoing developmental processes.16 Therefore, the omission of lipids from PN not only compromises the immediate nutritional status but also poses long-term risks to their growth, neurocognitive development and overall outcomes, making it a practice that should be avoided unless absolutely necessary and under strict medical supervision.

This study therefore examined the impact of omitting lipids from PN regimens on the nutritional outcomes of paediatric patients undergoing ECMO. ECMO is a lifesaving intervention, but its associated metabolic and nutritional challenges often complicate the management and care of critically ill patients. The results of this study have provided valuable insights into the nature of the deficits experienced by these patients and highlight the critical need for improved nutritional strategies to address their unique requirements.

The findings revealed that most (78.26%) patients did not achieve their target BEE, with a mean caloric deficit of 40.23%. These results aligned with those of prior studies that have noted significant nutritional challenges in ECMO patients, including the difficulty of meeting energy demands without the use of lipids. For instance, a study by Brackmann et al., found that lipid-free PN regimens can create substantial energy deficits in critically ill paediatric patients, potentially contributing to suboptimal outcomes.17 However, despite the significant energy shortfall in this cohort, target protein requirements were met in most (72%) patients, underscoring the role of protein-sparing approaches in minimising the adverse effects of energy deficits. Similar findings have been reported in studies that emphasised the importance of prioritising protein delivery when full caloric targets cannot be achieved.18 However, hyperglycaemia and formula preparation challenges further limited optimal energy provision.

In addition, the results reflected a weak negative correlation between caloric deficits and mortality, with no clear relationship suggesting that larger deficits directly increased mortality risk. These results diverged from those of earlier research, such as that of Bechard et al., which linked greater caloric deficits to worse clinical outcomes in paediatric critical care patients.18 However, the lack of a strong association in this study may be explained by the overriding impact of disease severity and clinical deterioration, which were the primary contributors to mortality in this cohort. Notably, 80% of the patients who did meet their target BEE perished, emphasising that, despite its importance, adequate nutrition cannot fully offset the effects of critical illness and other underlying factors.

No significant differences were observed in patients’ weights at baseline and after PN discontinuation. Interpretation of weight changes in this cohort must consider the confounding effects of renal replacement therapies and diuretic use, which were common in our population. More than half of the patients (13 patients) required ultrafiltration, and seven received peritoneal dialysis. Fluid overload from medications, resuscitation efforts and nutritional support can obscure true weight loss, masking muscle or fat catabolism. This highlights the limitations of using weight as a sole marker of nutritional status in this population. Employing more precise methods, such as body composition analysis, could provide a clearer understanding of nutritional outcomes and the true impact of caloric deficits.

Future research should explore innovative approaches to lipid delivery in ECMO patients, such as using alternative lipid formulations or infusion techniques that are compatible with ECMO circuits. Larger, prospective studies are also needed to assess the long-term effects of caloric deficits on patients’ growth, recovery and clinical outcomes. Employing advanced nutritional assessment tools to evaluate changes in body composition could provide a clearer understanding of the true impact of energy deficits. Finally, investigations into tailored nutrition interventions, such as combining EN and PN to optimise caloric and protein delivery, would be valuable in addressing the complex needs of this critically ill population.

Limitations

An important limitation of our study is the inability to determine a cause-and-effect relationship between failure to achieve BEE, calorie deficits and clinical outcomes. Patients supported with ECMO are critically ill and carry a high baseline mortality risk, which complicates attempts to disentangle the independent contribution of nutritional practices, particularly lipid omission, from the overall impact of disease severity and multisystem organ failure. Additionally, the reliance on weight as the primary outcome further limited the study’s ability to draw definitive conclusions about the nutritional status of these patients.

Conclusion

This study has underscored the challenges of providing optimal nutrition to paediatric ECMO patients, particularly when lipids are excluded from PN. The significant caloric deficits observed call for alternative strategies to achieve energy goals without compromising ECMO circuit integrity. While the correlation between caloric deficits and mortality was weak, the findings stress the importance of a holistic approach to managing ECMO patients, where nutrition is one critical component. Future research should focus on innovative solutions for lipid delivery and the long-term implications of nutritional deficits on recovery and growth in this vulnerable population.
