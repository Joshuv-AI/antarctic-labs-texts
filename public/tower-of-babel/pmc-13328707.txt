Agreement among brief patient health questionnaire (PHQ) versions for measuring symptoms of depression in the Brazilian national health survey (PNS)
Authors: Leidiane Rafaela Fernandes de Oliveira, Bruna Carolina Rafael Barbosa, Mariana Cassemira Aparecida Vidigal, Adriana Lúcia Meireles, Luiz Menezes-Júnior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the agreement of the 2-item (PHQ-2) and 8-item (PHQ-8) Patient Health Questionnaires compared to the PHQ-9 and clinical diagnosis for depression screening in Brazilian adults, and to examine associated sociodemographic and behavioral factors. We analyzed cross-sectional data from 148,733 participants in the 2013 and 2019 Brazilian National Health Surveys. ROC curve analyses identified optimal cutoffs for PHQ-8 and PHQ-2 against the PHQ-9 (≥ 10) and clinical indicators. Logistic regression and Ratio of Odds Ratios (ROR) compared associations across screening tools and self-reported diagnosis. The PHQ-8 (cutoff ≥ 10) showed near-perfect agreement with the PHQ-9 (AUC = 0.982). For the PHQ-2, a ≥ 3 cutoff optimized specificity (96.4%) and overall agreement (94.7%), with an AUC of 0.877. Optimal cutoffs against clinical indicators shifted lower (≥ 4 for PHQ-8; ≥ 1 for PHQ-2). Both brief versions identified associations profiles consistent with the PHQ-9 (e.g., higher odds in women, low-income individuals, and those with chronic diseases). However, ROR analyses revealed a significant healthcare gap: higher income and advancing age strongly predicted formal diagnosis, while Black/Brown populations and those with poor health were significantly underrepresented clinically compared to symptom screening. All scales showed strong reliability (α, ω > 0.85), including the PHQ-2 (r = 0.55; ρsb = 0.71). The PHQ-8 is a psychometrically equivalent alternative to the PHQ-9 in Brazil, and the PHQ-2 is a reliable, ultra-brief tool ideal for rapid, large-scale population screening. Introduction

Depression, or depressive disorder, is considered by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a multifactorial mental health condition, constituting a public health issue and one of the leading causes of global disability1. It is estimated that depression accounts for the highest proportion of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among all mental disorders. In 2021, depressive disorders were responsible for approximately 56.3 million DALYs (95% UI: 39.3–76.5 million) worldwide2.

In Brazil, the scenario is no different. The country also faces the challenge of a growing burden of mental disorders, particularly depressive disorders, whose prevalence has increased significantly in recent years3. Data from the Brazilian National Health Survey (Pesquisa Nacional de Saúde—PNS) indicate an increase in the self-reported prevalence of depression among the adult population from 7.9% in 2013 to 10.8% in 20193,4. In response to this situation, the Brazilian Ministry of Health has begun to recognize mental disorders as a priority within the scope of Noncommunicable Diseases and Conditions (NCDs), incorporating specific goals for prevention, screening, and continued care within primary health care (PHC), as outlined in the Strategic Action Plan (2021–2030) for addressing NCDs in Brazil5.

The investigation of the prevalence of depression and its symptoms across different population strata has been conducted through various epidemiological methods, highlighting the complexity and the multiple dimensions involved in mental health research, a topic still marked by social stigma and methodological challenges4. In this context, the use of standardized and validated instruments that allow for the consistent identification of depressive disorders in large populations is particularly noteworthy. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is one of the most widely used tools for depression screening, due to its validity and applicability across different settings and population groups6,7. However, the inclusion of the PHQ-9 within longer data collection questionnaires, such as those used in population-based surveys, may pose logistical challenges. Consequently, the use of abbreviated versions of the instrument, such as the PHQ-8 and PHQ-2, has emerged as a viable alternative, demonstrating good concordance with the PHQ-9 in studies conducted in different countries. Nevertheless, these shorter versions have been scarcely explored in Brazilian population-based research, which limits their broader application in national contexts8,9.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the agreement of the shortened versions PHQ-2 and PHQ-8 in a representative sample of the Brazilian adult population, in comparison with the full instrument, PHQ-9 and self-reported diagnosis of depression, for depression screening. Additionally, it sought to investigate the sociodemographic and health-related factors associated with the presence of depressive symptoms identified by the three scales.

Methods

Design and data collection

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the PNS, a nationally representative household-based survey conducted in 2013 and 2019 by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) in partnership with the Ministry of Health. The PNS employed a complex, multistage probabilistic sampling design, stratified by census tracts and clustered in three stages: 1) selection of municipalities, 2) census sectors and 3) households. In total, 69.954 households were surveyed in 2013 and 94.114 in 2019. In each selected household, one resident (aged ≥ 18 years in 2013 and ≥ 15 years in 2019) was randomly chosen to respond to a detailed questionnaire encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, health conditions and lifestyle. Data collection involved face-to-face interviews and physical measurements. Were applied to correct for non-response and to allow post-stratification adjustments, ensuring population representativeness according to age, sex, and educational attainment.

For this study, considering the differences in age groups between the 2013 and 2019 PNS samples, only individuals aged 18 years or older were included to ensure the comparability of estimates. Participants younger than 18 years were excluded from the analyses.

Ethical considerations

All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The Brazilian National Health Survey (PNS) was approved by the National Commission for Ethics in Research (CONEP) (Report No. 10853812.7.0000.0008) in June 2013, in compliance with Resolution No. 466/2012 of the National Health Council (CNS). Furthermore, research involving human participants was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent. Participation was voluntary, with guarantees of anonymity and the right to withdraw at any time.

Variables

Information was collected on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle, and health-related conditions. Sociodemographic variables included sex (male or female), age (18–29, 30–44, 45–59, 60–74, and ≥ 75 years to capture differences across life stages), monthly family income (< ½, ½–1, 1–2, 2–3, or > 3 Minimum Wages (MW) to assess socioeconomic gradients), education level (incomplete primary, incomplete high school, incomplete higher education, or complete higher education), marital status (married or not married) and self-reported skin color/race (black, brown, indigenous, white, or yellow, following standard classifications used in Brazilian demographic studies to reflect the country’s racial and ethnic diversity10. In addition, type of census situation (urban or rural) and region (north, northeast, southeast or midwest).

Lifestyle and health-related variables included current tobacco use (yes or no), heavy episodic alcohol consumption (defined as consuming five or more alcoholic drinks on a single occasion within the past 30 days: yes or no), self-reported medical diagnosis of depression (yes or no), presence of at least one chronic non-communicable disease (hypertension, diabetes, cancer: yes or no), and self-rated health status (good or very good vs. fair/poor/very poor to capture perceived overall health).

Mental health

Mental health was assessed using three validated versions of the PHQ. The nine-item version (PHQ-9) evaluated the frequency of depressive symptoms over the previous two weeks, with response options ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (almost every day), and a maximum total of 27 points. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 was adopted to indicate clinically significant depressive symptoms, in accordance with international guidelines7. To address ethical considerations in population-based research, the PHQ-8 as used as an alternative version, omitting the item on suicidal ideation (Thoughts that you would be better off dead or hurting yourself), while retaining the remaining eight items, the same scoring scale (0–24) and the same cut-off ≥ 10. The PHQ-2, a brief screening tool focused on the core symptoms of depression (anhedonia and depressed mood), was also employed. Scores range from 0 to 6, with cut-off point of ≥ 2 and ≥ 3 commonly recommended11,12. All versions were translated into Brazilian Portuguese and culturally adapted, with evidence of internal consistency and validity 13,14. The selection of these scales aimed to balance diagnostic accuracy (PHQ-9), operational feasibility (PHQ-8) and agility (PHQ-2), ensuring comparability with international studies and applicability across different public health scenarios.

In addition to symptom-based screening, four clinical and therapeutic indicators of depression were assessed to evaluate healthcare access and establish external clinical references for the screening scales. These variables included a self-reported medical diagnosis, assessed by asking whether the participant had ever been diagnosed with depression by a physician or mental health professional; antidepressant medication use, based on a question regarding current pharmacological treatment in the past two weeks; and psychotherapy attendance, assessed by asking if the participant was currently undergoing psychotherapy specifically for depression. Finally, regular medical follow-up was derived from a question evaluating routine healthcare utilization. This variable was dichotomized so that only participants who explicitly reported regular attendance were classified as having regular follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were carried out using Stata/MPⓇ version 14.0, with a significant level of 5%. The concordance analyses between the different versions of the PHQ scales were conducted without incorporating sample weights, as they assessed the consistency between measurement methods and non-population estimates. For all other analyses, including prevalence calculations, descriptive statistics, graphical analyses and regression models, the complex sample design of the 2013 and 2019 PNS was considered, using the “svy” command to incorporate sample weights weighted by post-stratified expansion factors, considering sex, age, race/color and education level, to correct for losses and ensure national representativeness of the exam subsample. The coefficient of variation (CV), which measures the relative variability of the estimates, remained consistently below 10–15%, indicating the robustness and precision of the results obtained. All data are presented as point measurements and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

The internal consistency reliability of the PHQ scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients, providing an estimate of the homogeneity of the items.

To assess the agreement of the reduced PHQ scales (PHQ-8 and PHQ-2), we performed Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analyses, using the PHQ-9, at the standard threshold of ≥ 10 as the primary reference, sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative PHQ-9 cut-offs (≥ 8, ≥ 11, and ≥ 12) as reference standards. Furthermore, additional ROC analyses were performed using the four clinical and therapeutic indicators as external references: self-reported medical diagnosis of depression, current use of antidepressant medication, active psychotherapy attendance, and regular medical follow-up. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the ability of each subscale to discriminate between the presence and absence of symptoms, with AUCs ranging from 0.5 (no discrimination) to 1 (perfect discrimination). To determine the ideal cut-off points, we selected the values that simultaneously: (1) maximized the Youden index, (2) had the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC) and (3) had the highest kappa value of agreement with the reference. This approach ensured the choice of thresholds with the optimum balance between sensitivity and specificity, while maintaining epidemiological consistency with the reference parameters. Resulting in a threshold of ≥ 10 for PHQ-8 and ≥ 3 for PHQ-2, with reference to symptoms of depression (PHQ-9 > 10), and ≥ 4 for PHQ-8 and PHQ-9, and ≥ 1 for PHQ-2, with reference to clinical depression parameters. This analysis was carried out for the total sample and also for specific subgroups, defined by sex, age, education level, and previous medical diagnosis of depression (Supplementary Material).

Agreement between the different versions of the PHQ was assessed using different statistical approaches. We used the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) to evaluate the agreement between the continuous scores. Furthermore, we calculated categorical agreement measures, including Cohen’s kappa (weighted), and the percentage of simple agreement, providing a comprehensive assessment of the agreement between the risk classifications. For continuous variables, the ICC is interpreted as follows: < 0.50 = poor; 0.50–0.74 = moderate; 0.75–0.90 = good; > 0.90 = excellent15. For categorical agreement, Cohen’s weighted kappa uses: < 0 = worse than chance; 0–0.20 = slight; 0.21–0.40 = fair; 0.41–0.60 = moderate; 0.61–0.80 = substantial; > 0.80 = almost perfect16. Simple percent agreement > 80% is typically considered good, though it should be interpreted alongside chance-corrected measures17.

To formally assess whether the magnitude of the associations between sociodemographic and clinical predictors and depression differs depending on the screening or diagnostic instrument used (PHQ-9, PHQ-8, PHQ-2, and clinical diagnosis), a comparative analysis using pooled modeling strategies with interaction terms was conducted. First, the dataset was reshaped from a wide to a long format, allowing the multiple outcome measures assessed for the same individual to be stacked into a single response variable, accompanied by a newly generated categorical variable indicating the source instrument. Subsequently, multiple logistic regression models were fitted, strictly incorporating the survey weights and the complex sample design (using the svy prefix in Stata). The models included the independent variables of interest, the instrument indicator term, and multiplicative interaction terms between the instrument and each independent variable. This statistical approach enables the estimation of the Ratio of Odds Ratios (ROR), which quantifies the relative difference in the effect size of an independent variable on the outcome when comparing two distinct scales. An interaction p-value greater than 0.05 indicates no statistically significant difference in the magnitude of the association between the compared instruments. To provide a comprehensive matrix of pairwise comparisons across all assessment tools (e.g., PHQ-9 vs. PHQ-8; PHQ-2 vs. Diagnosis), the models were iteratively estimated by alternating the instrument designated as the reference category.

Results

This study analyzed data from 148,773 individuals aged 18 years or older, based on the 2013 and 2019 editions of PNS. The PHQ-9 and PHQ-8 demonstrated similarity in item composition, with the PHQ-8 differing only by the exclusion of the suicidal ideation item. In contrast, the PHQ-2 focused exclusively on the core symptoms of anhedonia and depressed mood, representing the most concise version of the instrument (Table 1).

Correlational analyses revealed strong associations between the different versions of the questionnaire. The PHQ-8 exhibited an extremely high correlation with the full PHQ-9, indicating nearly identical measurement properties (r = 0.9974), while the PHQ-2 showed a somewhat lower but still substantial correlation (r = 0.8757), suggesting it captures the essential features of depression even its brevity (Supplementary Fig. 1). Heatmap analysis revealed that PHQ-9 items are consistently interrelated, with the strongest association found between the two core symptoms: anhedonia (Item 1) and depressed mood (Item 2) (r = 0.55). Correlations between individual symptomatic items and clinical treatment indicators were generally lower, ranging from 0.12 to 0.31. The items most strongly correlated with clinical indicators were Item 2 (r = 0.31), Item 6 (r = 0.26), and Items 1 and 3 (r = 0.25). Conversely, a nearly perfect correlation was observed between self-reported medical diagnosis and antidepressant medication use (r = 0.90), while psychotherapy and regular medical care showed moderate associations with clinical diagnosis (r = 0.50 and r = 0.59, respectively) (Fig. 1).

The frequency distributions across PHQ scales showed that most participants reported minimal depressive symptoms, with 42.9% scoring zero on both the PHQ-9 and PHQ-8, and 65.3% scoring zero on the PHQ-2. The prevalence of clinically significant symptoms (using standard cutoffs) varied slightly between instruments, with 9.4% scoring ≥ 10 on the PHQ-9, 9.1% on the PHQ-8, and 10.4% scoring ≥ 3 on the PHQ-2 (Fig. 1). The prevalence of self-reported medical diagnoses (9.0%) was similar to that obtained through symptom-based screening using the standard threshold of ≥ 10 (9.4%). Regarding other clinical indicators, 7.2% of the population reported taking antidepressants, 3.2% were undergoing psychotherapy, and 2.8% were receiving regular medical follow-up for depression (Figs. 2, 3).

In the ROC curve analysis, both the PHQ-8 and PHQ-2 demonstrated high concordance when compared to the full PHQ-9, although performance varied based on the chosen reference threshold. The PHQ-8 exhibited near-perfect equivalence to the full PHQ-9 across all severity levels, with AUC values consistently reaching 0.999. The optimal cutoff for the PHQ-8 shifted in tandem with the reference: ≥ 8 for a PHQ-9 threshold of ≥ 8; ≥ 10 for PHQ-9 thresholds of ≥ 10 and ≥ 11; and ≥ 11 when the reference was set at ≥ 12. The PHQ-2 also demonstrated high discriminative capacity, with AUCs ranging from 0.945 to 0.972. Despite the variations in the PHQ-9 reference thresholds, the optimal cutoff for the PHQ-2 remained stable at ≥ 3 in all scenarios (Fig. 4). When evaluated against external clinical anchors (Fig. 5), all scales maintained moderate-to-high concordance. The AUC values were highest for psychotherapy (range: 0.761–0.796) and medication use (range: 0.751–0.790), and slightly lower for regular medical care (range: 0.681–0.712). Across all four clinical references, diagnosis, medication, psychotherapy, and medical follow-up, the optimal cutoff points shifted significantly lower than those used for symptomatic screening, settling at ≥ 4 for both PHQ-9 and PHQ-8, and ≥ 1 for the PHQ-2 (Fig. 5).

Comparative analysis of prevalence estimates between instruments revealed small but systematic differences. The PHQ-2 produced slightly higher prevalence estimates than the PHQ-9 (average + 1.03%), with the difference being more pronounced among women (+ 1.38%) and rural residents (+ 1.12%). The PHQ-8 showed minimal divergence from the PHQ-9 (average -0.29%) (Supplementary Tables 2, 3). Reliability analyses confirmed strong internal consistency for both the PHQ-9 (α = 0.862; ω = 0.853) and PHQ-8 (α = 0.862; ω = 0.862), while the PHQ-2 showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.708; ω = 0.708) (Supplementary Table 1). Given that the PHQ-2 is an ultra-brief scale, its reliability was assessed using the inter-item correlation (r = 0.548) and the Spearman-Brown coefficient (ρsb = 0.707), both of which indicate satisfactory internal consistency for a two-item measure. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent performance across demographic strata, though with some variation in optimal cutoff points for certain populations (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6).

Analysis of Ratio of Odds Ratios (ROR) confirmed that PHQ-9 and PHQ-8 are statistically equivalent across all categories (p > 0.05), indicating that excluding the suicidal ideation item does not alter association strengths in the Brazilian population. Conversely, the PHQ-2 showed discrepancies with the PHQ-9, overestimating associations for individuals aged 45–59 years (ROR = 1.15*) and rural residents (ROR = 1.07*), while underestimating fair/poor health status (ROR = 0.82**) and the 2019 prevalence increase (ROR = 0.84*). The most pronounced differences were found between clinical diagnosis and screening scales. Diagnosis showed significantly stronger associations with advancing age (60–74 years: ROR = 2.51**), higher income (> 3 MW: ROR = 1.69**), and higher education (ROR = 1.25*). In contrast, associations were weaker for Black (ROR = 0.70**) and Brown (ROR = 0.89*) populations, and for those with fair/poor health (ROR = 0.56**), compared to the PHQ-9. These diagnostic discrepancies remained consistent regardless of the screening tool used, with ROR values for diagnosis vs. PHQ-2, showing similar magnitude and direction to diagnosis vs. PHQ-9, suggesting a systematic gap between symptom burden and clinical access across these subgroups (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study evaluates the performance of two abbreviated versions of the PHQ-9 for mental health screening among Brazilian adults against both standard symptomatic thresholds and external clinical indicators. The findings indicated that both the PHQ-8 and PHQ-2 demonstrated excellent agreement across different PHQ-9 cut-off points. Moreover, the sociodemographic and health-related characteristics associated with depressive symptoms were similar across all symptom-based versions. Higher prevalence of depressive symptoms was observed among women, non-married individuals, older adults, and individuals in the lowest income bracket. These findings are consistent with existing literature, which highlights the influence of gender, social support, age, and socioeconomic status as key determinants of depression18.

An important applied contribution of our study is the comparison of association magnitudes between symptom-based scales and self-reported clinical diagnosis within a nationally representative sample. While the PHQ-9, PHQ-8, and PHQ-2 showed strong statistical equivalence in capturing symptom burden, significant discrepancies emerged when compared to formal medical diagnosis. Specifically, higher socioeconomic status and advancing age were much stronger predictors of a formal diagnosis than of self-reported symptoms. Conversely, the association between depression and Black or Brown race, as well as fair/poor self-assessed health, was significantly weaker when measured by clinical diagnosis than by the PHQ-9. This indicates a substantial treatment and diagnostic gap: historically marginalized and lower-income populations carry a high burden of depressive symptoms but face significant systemic barriers to accessing specialized mental health care and receiving a formal diagnosis19. Recognizing this discrepancy is vital, as relying solely on clinical diagnosis data may drastically underestimate the true epidemiological burden in vulnerable subgroups.

These shortened instruments may serve as valuable tools for the screening of mental health disorders in the general population, particularly in contexts where time and resources are limited. The use of shortened scales in health assessments has gained prominence due to the need for methodological instruments that are both scientifically validated and feasible in terms of applicability20. Reduced versions of these instruments represent an effective and rapid alternative for screening depressive symptoms, enabling more precise and targeted interventions, enhancing participant adherence, and contributing to the acquisition of higher-quality data21. Moreover, these instruments are particularly useful in contexts where available time for data collection is limited22.

Previous studies support our findings and demonstrate the efficacy of using shortened scales for the screening of depressive symptoms. A study by Ali and colleagues (2021) evaluated the psychometric properties of the DASS-8 (Depression Anxiety Stress Scale), a reduced version of the DASS-21, in individuals over 18 years old during the COVID-19 lockdown period in Saudi Arabia. The eight-item version showed excellent internal consistency (\alpha = 0.94), as well as good agreement with the PHQ-9, and measurement invariance across age and gender groups, suggesting that DASS-8 can be used as an alternative to DASS-21 for screening mental disorder symptomatology within this population23. Another study analyzed samples from five surveys (n = 2,096) conducted in Brazil, the United Kingdom, and the United States to develop and evaluate the agreement of a new abbreviated version of the DASS-21, named the 12-item Mini-DASS. The authors concluded that the 12-item Mini-DASS preserves the psychometric properties of the DASS-21, demonstrating invariance among the three countries evaluated and between genders, establishing it as a reliable and valid instrument for measuring symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress24.

The global increase in depressive disorders warrants discussion. The National Survey on Drug Use and Health revealed that approximately 21.9 million Americans reported at least one major depressive episode (MDE) in 202225. The survey questions were based on the diagnostic criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), which defines an MDE as a period of at least two weeks of depressed mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure, accompanied by at least five symptoms such as changes in appetite or weight, insomnia or hypersomnia, fatigue, psychomotor agitation or retardation, difficulties concentrating, suicidal ideation, or feelings of guilt. Also, these symptoms must cause significant functional impairment and not be attributable to other causes26. Furthermore, the survey showed that the prevalence of MDE was higher among young adults aged 18 to 25 years (17.5%) compared to other age groups—26 to 49 years (10.2%) and those over 50 years (4.5%)24. Regarding the Brazilian context, in its latest report on mental health, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO/WHO) identified Brazil as the country with the highest prevalence of depression in Latin America27.

In this context, the use of short instruments becomes relevant given the necessity for swift and effective screening strategies. However, the public health benefits of these shortened scales must be cautiously weighed against the trade-off between brevity and sensitivity. Notably, the PHQ-2 demonstrates a reduced sensitivity at the ≥ 3 cutoff (79.0%). This highlights that while ultra-brief tools optimize time resources, they may under-identify individuals with elevated depressive symptoms, underscoring that they should be applied with clinical caution and not replace comprehensive assessments when resources permit. Furthermore, as demonstrated by our results, optimal cutoffs shift downward (to ≥ 1 for PHQ-2 and ≥ 4 for PHQ-8) when attempting to identify individuals already integrated into the healthcare system (e.g., active psychotherapy or medication use), suggesting that clinical care often captures individuals at varying stages of symptom remission or management.

Crucially, it is necessary to clearly distinguish between public health screening contexts and clinical assessment settings. In large-scale epidemiological surveys, the PHQ-8 is operationally advantageous because omitting the suicidality item avoids the ethical dilemma of uncovering immediate psychiatric emergencies without the infrastructure to intervene28. However, because of this omission, the PHQ-8 is not fully conceptually equivalent to the PHQ-9. In clinical environments, where identifying high-risk individuals and ensuring patient safety is the primary goal, the PHQ-8 should be interpreted with caution. Utilizing scales that omit suicidality in clinical practice can lead to critical missed opportunities for crisis intervention; therefore, brief tools like the PHQ-2 or PHQ-8 should only serve as initial steps, strictly followed by comprehensive psychiatric evaluation if symptoms are detected29.

While standard psychometric scales like the PHQ-9, PHQ-8, and PHQ-2 remain crucial for large-scale epidemiological screening, the landscape of depression recognition is rapidly evolving. Recent studies have demonstrated the high accuracy of advanced, multimodal AI-driven approaches, including computer audition-based disease detection30, interpretable representations for physiological signals31, and dynamic adversarial adaptation networks using EEG data32–34. Furthermore, the integration of multimodal physiological signals with Large Language Models (LLMs) offers promising new avenues for both recognition and intervention35. Future public health strategies may benefit from combining the accessibility of traditional self-reported tools, which our findings show maintain consistent effect sizes across their variations, with these emerging computational technologies.

In scientific research, particularly studies with large sample sizes, the rapid administration of these methodological instruments optimizes time resources and facilitates the collection of essential data that assists in understanding the distribution and prevalence of depressive symptoms in the assessed population36. Moreover, the use of shortened scales is highly suitable for application in populations at risk for depression, since extensive questionnaires may negatively affect participant adherence to the study21. Finally, the evaluation of agreement of these scales across different countries and contexts reinforces the generalizability and applicability of these questionnaires23,24.

The high agreement of the PHQ-2 and PHQ-8 scales observed in this study reinforces the premise that shorter tools can serve as highly consistent alternatives to longer versions for population screening. However, it is crucial to recognize a fundamental methodological limitation: because the PHQ-8 and PHQ-2 are nested subsets derived directly from the PHQ-9, evaluating them against the full scale within the same dataset introduces mathematical and structural dependence. This inherent overlap mathematically inflates correlation coefficients, AUC, and agreement metrics. Consequently, this study evaluates the internal concordance between screening tools rather than true independent diagnostic validity. The absence of a clinical diagnostic gold standard, such as a structured psychiatric interview (e.g., SCID or MINI), limits the interpretation of diagnostic accuracy.

Although these abbreviated scales offer practicality and efficiency, they may not capture the full complexity of mental health constructs. Additionally, it is important to note the interpretational limits of internal consistency metrics in this study. While Cronbach’s alpha is the most widely reported metric, its dependence on the number of items often results in lower values for ultra-brief scales like the PHQ-2. However, the high inter-item correlation and Spearman-Brown coefficients observed here suggest that the PHQ-2 maintains satisfactory internal reliability, independent of its limited length. Future studies should prioritize multi-method reliability assessments when comparing scales of significantly different lengths. To overcome the limitations of a nested design and cross-sectional data, future studies should prioritize comprehensive psychometric testing, including structural validity and measurement invariance analyses, and apply these scales across independent external samples to fully establish their stability in diverse survey settings.

Furthermore, it is essential to recognize the intrinsic limitations of the study design. Despite its national representativeness and methodological rigor, the cross-sectional nature of the PNS prevents causal inferences, and the use of self-reported data may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Finally, the PHQ-9 itself serves as a screening tool rather than a definitive clinical diagnosis, which may lead to misclassification. These limitations must be carefully considered when interpreting the results. However, the substantial sample size of 148,733 Brazilian adults broadens the generalizability of the findings, providing valuable insights into the epidemiological comparability of the reduced PHQ scales in public health settings.

Conclusion

In the present study, both the PHQ-8 and PHQ-2 exhibited performance comparable to the full PHQ-9, and identifying same factors associated with depressive symptoms among Brazilian adults. While the PHQ-8 demonstrated higher reliability, these findings underscore the utility of both abbreviated versions, especially the PHQ-2, as rapid, accessible, and low-cost tools for large-scale population screening. Crucially, comparing these symptom-based scales with external clinical indicators revealed a significant diagnostic gap. Although vulnerable populations (such as Black and Brown individuals) reported high symptom burdens, advancing age and higher socioeconomic status were substantially stronger predictors of receiving a formal medical diagnosis. Ultimately, our findings emphasize that relying solely on clinical diagnoses drastically underestimates the mental health burden in marginalized subgroups, reinforcing the necessity of brief screening tools to accurately capture the true epidemiological distribution of depression.
