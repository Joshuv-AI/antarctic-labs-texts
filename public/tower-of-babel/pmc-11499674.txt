Preliminary proteomic analysis of mouse lung tissue treated with cyclophosphamide and Venetin-1
Authors: Paulina Czaplewska, Marc Müller, Natalia Musiał, Marcin Okrój, Anna Felberg-Miętka, Joanna Sadowska, Wioleta Dudzińska, Anna Lubkowska, Beata Tokarz-Deptuła, Marta Fiołka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cyclophosphamide (CPAm) is a widely used chemotherapeutic agent that exhibits potent anti-cancer properties but is often associated with debilitating side effects. Despite its efficacy, the management of CPAm-induced toxicities remains a significant clinical challenge. There has been growing interest in exploring complementary and alternative therapies to mitigate these adverse effects in recent years, and this may be a chance for the earthworm-derived preparation, Venetin-1. Its rich composition of bioactive compounds has demonstrated promising pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects. These properties suggest its potential to counteract various systemic toxicities induced by CPAm. We conducted a comprehensive study to investigate the effect of Venetin-1 on cyclophosphamide-induced toxicity. Mice were administered CPAm for four days, followed by application of the earthworm preparation in two doses (50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg b.w). Importantly, the preparation did not cause any side effects in all mice, ensuring the safety of the intervention. We then determined global changes in the proteome using proteomics and quantitative SWATH-MS analysis, which is a robust and reliable method. This allowed us to identify up- and downregulated proteins in each studied group, providing valuable insights into the mechanism of action of Venetin-1. As shown by the results, Venetin-1 had a significant effect on the proteome of mouse lung tissue. It was possible to determine quantitative changes in 400 proteins, and the analysis after administration of Venetin-1 showed a change in the global proteomic profile from upregulated to down-regulated. The stimulating properties of the preparation concerning the complement system were also confirmed in a separate validation experiment. Venetin-1 shows promise in reducing the harmful effects of cyclophosphamide on lung tissue. It encourages tissue regeneration, reduces inflammation, supports autophagy, and boosts the immune system. However, more research is needed to thoroughly elucidate and describe the benefits of Venetin-1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76143-0.

Introduction

The lungs are one of the critical organs for the functioning of the human and animal organisms. It is therefore essential to keep them in the best shape possible, especially while the patient is undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy to treat various types of cancer. Progress in this field is significant, but there are still drugs that have been used for years and, despite their undeniable impact on the diseased, also bring side effects. This is the case of cyclophosphamide (CPAm), which has been used in oncological treatment since the 1950s1,2. It is a derivative of mustard gas, which is metabolised in the liver as an inactive form of the prodrug, and its active metabolites, i.e. phosphor amide and acrolein, have an alkylating effect on nucleic acid (DNA)3,4. Cyclophosphamide and its metabolites are mainly excreted in the urine5. Rapidly dividing cancer cells exposed to CPAm are particularly affected by its alkylating effect, which disrupts DNA and RNA synthesis, disturbs protein synthesis, and prevents cell division due to the insufficiently efficient DNA repair mechanism6. Thanks to this effect, it is still a frequently used drug (monotherapy or combination therapy) for many different types of cancers (leukaemia, Hodgkin’s disease)7,8, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma9, multiple myeloma10, solid tumours (ovarian cancer, breast cancer, small cell lung cancer, neuroma immature cells, sarcomas)11, and in immunosuppressive treatment for organ transplants12,13.

However, cyclophosphamide also has a dark side due to its lack of cell selectivity. When administered to patients, it exerts many side effects, affecting healthy cells and organs. This is evident in the rapidly dividing bone marrow (inhibition of hematopoiesis leading to leukopenia and thrombocytopenia) or epithelial tissue. A suppressed immune system is responsible for increased susceptibility of the organism to infections. Very often, the toxic effect of cyclophosphamide affects the bladder (cystitis and hematuria, less often hemorrhagic cystitis, interstitial bladder inflammation, and hardening of its wall), which is mainly caused by CPAm metabolites14. In the case of lung tissue, cyclophosphamide causes lung damage due to the lack of detoxifying enzymes (aldehyde oxidase and dehydrogenase) that enable the transition from the toxic aldehyde form to the less toxic carboxyl form15. This results in characteristic clinical symptoms, such as cough, fever, gas exchange abnormalities, parenchymal infiltrates, and pleural thickening16. Side effects of cyclophosphamide treatment affecting the lungs may cause respiratory system disorders17. CPAm metabolites contribute to an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and, consequently, overproduction of neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages, which results in the development of pulmonary fibrosis. More specifically, CPAm metabolites contribute to an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) accompanied by a transient increase in monocyte-derived macrophages and the formation of heterogeneous macrophage populations, which results in the development of pulmonary fibrosis18.

Since the benefits of using cyclophosphamide in therapies still outweigh the side effects, efforts should be made to find and use an effective and selective preparation to prevent, limit or eliminate adverse effects on healthy cells. There is an opportunity here for many natural substances isolated from plants and used as part of natural therapies, for example, in Chinese medicine. In the literature, the use of such substances as melatonin19,20, curcumin21,22, icariin23, quercetin24,25, raspberry ketones (RKs)26, Red Ginseng27, extracts from Zataria multiflora28, Euterpe oleracea Mart. Berry29, or compositions of Chinese herbs30 has already been proposed. In Chinese medicine, all lung ailments were treated with earthworm preparations. Studies show a positive effect of a preparation based on the coelomic fluid from the earthworm Dendrobaena veneta, Venetin-1. This liquid subjected to thermal processing (heating at 70oC) and dialysis (10 kDa) loses its toxic properties towards healthy cells and has anti-yeast and anti-cancer properties.

Work carried out on Candida albicans with Venetin-1 showed inhibition of growth of yeast cells by activation of apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy and destruction of the cell wall as well as changes in the structure of mitochondria and cell nuclei. In the case of cancer, coelomic fluid affects the HeLa, A549, Pa17, PC-12, or Hep-231–35 cancer cell lines. Studies on healthy BEAS-2 A cell lines showed that the Venetin − 1 from coelomic fluid did not damage bronchial epithelial cells, and no statistically significant changes were observed in the cell proteome. It has also been recently shown that, in the case of A549 cells, Venetin-1 causes the activation of proteins responsible for sensitising cells to therapy. Chemically, Venetin-1 is a protein-polysaccharide complex with microparticles with a 58.23 + 3.93 nm diameter. It has confirmed lysozyme activity, and the main components are proteins from the lysenin group (lysenin and lysenin-related protein 2)36,37. Research conducted on blood samples by Poniedziałek and colleagues showed that Venetin-1 at a concentration of 50 µg/ml exhibited antiplatelet properties by targeting the P2Y12 receptor, cyclooxygenase-1, and protease-activated receptor-138.

In this study, we decided to check whether Venetin-1 and its anti-cancer properties can support standard therapies while protecting healthy cells. For this purpose, mice were administered the standard drug cyclophosphamide and Venetin-1 in two concentrations. The preparation had no adverse effect on mice, and the proteomic analysis of lung tissue showed changes in protein expression after the earthworm preparation was administered.

Materials and methods

Venetin-1

Venetin-1 was prepared according to the procedure described by Fiołka36. Briefly, coelomic fluid was obtained from the earthworm Dendrobaena veneta. The earthworms were grouped with 10 individuals per group and washed with 0.9% NaCl. Then, they were subjected to electrostimulation (4.5 V for 1 min). The obtained liquid was centrifuged (6000xg, 10 min), and the separated supernatant was filtered (0.22 Millipore filter). After heat treatment (70oC) and dialysis (10 kDa membrane), the obtained filtrate was freeze-dried and delivered to the laboratory prepared for administration to mice. Each portion of Venetin-1 was prepared in the same way by Prof. Marta Fiołka. The protein concentration was standardised (Bradford test) and the FTIR spectrum was checked (profile identical to the adopted standard).

Animals and treatment

Mice were obtained from the Department of Toxicology - Poznań University of Medical Sciences. The Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments, University of Life Sciences in Poznań, approved the study (Approval No. 5/2022, 25.02.2022). The studies involved male mice of the C57BL/6 strain (7–8 week old, 26–31 g weight), in which we induced CPAm immunosuppression.

The mice were housed singly under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle at room temperature (21 ± 2 °C) with a relative humidity of 55–60%. The mice were fed with water and a standard laboratory diet. After a week of adaptation, the animals were divided into four experimental groups (three mice in each group):

GROUP 1 (Intact Control, hereinafter referred to as Ctrl in the manuscript) – the mice received a 0.9% NaCl solution by intraperitoneal injection daily for 14 days. GROUP 2 (CPAm – Control, hereinafter referred to as CPAm in the manuscript) – the mice received CPAm at 80 mg/kg b.w. (Sigma-Aldrich USA) by intraperitoneal injection once a day for the first 3 days of the experiment and a 0.9% NaCl solution by intraperitoneal injection on the remaining days (4–14 days). GROUP 3 (CPAm – Venetin-1 50, hereinafter referred to as Ven50 in the manuscript) – the mice received CPAm at 80 mg/kg b.w. (Sigma-Aldrich USA) intraperitoneal injection once a day for the first 3 days of the experiment and Venetin-1 at a dose of 50 mg/kg b.w. by intraperitoneal injection on the remaining days (4–14 days). GROUP 4 (CPAm – Venetin-1 100, hereinafter referred to as Ven100 in the manuscript) – the mice received CPAm at 80 mg/kg b.w. (Sigma-Aldrich USA) by intraperitoneal injection once a day for the first 3 days of the experiment and Venetin-1 at a dose of 100 mg/kg b.w. by intraperitoneal injection on the remaining days (4–14 days). On day 15 of the experiment, the mice were euthanised, and blood (cardiac puncture) and organs (lungs) were collected. Blood and organs were collected for biochemical analyses and proteomic studies. The Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments, University of Life Sciences in Poznań, approved the study (Approval No. 5/2022, 25.02.2022), and the methods were applied in accordance with the approved guidelines. Euthanasia was performed by dislocation of the cervical vertebrae (in accordance with i) the proposal approved by the Local Research Ethics Committee, ii) with the Journal of Laws of 2015, item 266, ACT of 15 January 2015 on the protection of animals used for scientific or educational purposes, which implements Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and the Council of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, and with iii) AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals: 2020 Edition). This method, without prior sedation, is approved for use in rodents weighing up to 150 g. The animal management and experimental procedures of this study were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All the protocols were approved by the Local Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 5/2022) in accordance with the Journal of Laws of 2015, item 266, ACT of 15 January 2015 on the protection of animals used for scientific or educational purposes, which implements Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and the Council of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

Biochemical analysis

Preparation of lung tissue for proteomic analysis

The lungs were washed several times with phosphate buffer (10 mM) to remove blood particles. Then, homogenisation was carried out using a manual tissue homogeniser (Omni TH with Omni Tip, Omni International). The process was carried out in a lysis buffer containing 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), dithiothreitol, and 100 mM TRIS-HCl. Then, the samples were sonicated (QSonica). Samples digested with trypsin were prepared according to the standard FASP procedure on 10 kDa membrane microcones (Merck Millipore)39. After peptide digestion, they were desalted in the C18 phase according to the procedure described for Stage Tips40,41.

Mass spectrometry

The registration of spectra in data-dependent (IDA) and data-independent (DIA) formats was performed according to the procedure described in a previous publication by Czaplewska34. Quantitative analysis was performed using the SWATH methodology after creating the library and recording SWATH spectra with variable windows.

Data analysis

The protein data files acquired after SWATH MS were consecutively loaded into MarkerView. The data set was grouped into 4 groups after the experimental setup. The created table was then copied into Excel for further data analysis.

The in-depth data analysis was performed using RStudio utilising R-4.3.1. The data were cleaned using the tidyr and dplyr packages, and integrity was checked for missing values and overlaps. Afterwards, the data were normalised with limma to set up the comparison analysis, and cyclic loess normalisation was chosen. Finally, the package qpcR and the BioConductor database were used to compare the different groups and samples with each other. All four groups were compared together to find specific patterns and significant differences distinguishing the groups. Afterwards, the groups were compared individually to identify significant differences and different expressed proteins between the groups. The results of these comparisons were visualised using the ggplot2, ggVennDiagram, and patchwork packages.

Pathway enrichment analysis

An open-source String 11.042, KEGG43 and Cytoscape 3.9.044 software was employed to perform Pathway Enrichment Analysis. The input data for the Pathway Enrichment Analysis consisted of the gene names of the proteins identified and quantified in lung tissue treated with cyclophosphamide and Venetin-1 during the SWATH-MS proteomic experiment as described above. Then, the Log10 (Fold Change) values corresponding to these proteins were overlaid with the significantly enriched pathways. Finally, the resulting pathways were evaluated in the context of the presence of proteins characterised by a statistically significant fold change (p < 0.05) in each group.

qPCR experiment

Human non-small lung cancer cell (NSCLC) line H2087 (purchased in American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) was used as a model. Cells were grown at 37 °C in 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere to 80% confluency in RPMI 1640 medium with L-glutamine (Mediatech) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (PANBiotech). RNA was isolated with the RNAeasy kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The supplementary materials provide a detailed description and the values of individual technical parameters (Supplementary materials, Analysis details).

Results

Animal experiment

In the planned experiment on mice, the immunosuppression effect was achieved by introducing cyclophosphamide. The procedure was performed as described in the Materials and Methods section and is schematically presented in Fig. 1. Briefly, four groups of mice were selected. The control group was given a physiological saline solution throughout the 14 days of the experiment. The CPAm group was given a cyclophosphamide solution for the first 3 days of the experiment and a physiological saline solution during the remaining days. The last two groups were also administered cyclophosphamide for the first three days. On the fourth day, the administration of a Venetin-1 solution in two concentrations was started - the Ven50 group at a concentration of 50 mg/kg b.w. and the Ven100 group − 100 mg/kg b.w. This dosage was maintained until the end of the experiment. After euthanasia, the material was collected for testing. During the experiment, the mice showed no signs of any negative impact of the preparation on their vital functions, and none of them died before the end of the experiment.

SWATH-MS quantitative proteomic analysis of lung tissue

Lung tissue collected from all study participants was intended for quantitative analysis. Sample preparation, FASP trypsin digestion, and C18 purification were performed as described in the Materials and Methods section. The quantitative analysis used the Sequential Window Acquisition of all THeoretical Mass Spectra (SWATH-MS) methodology. The spectral library prepared for the tested material consisted of 1,700 proteins, of which 400 were successfully quantified. The collected quantitative data are available in the supplementary materials in Table ST1. To obtain a global view of the proteomic changes, the data were subjected to a standard bioinformatic procedure, and three experimental groups with administered CPAm, CPAm with 50 mg/kg b.w. Venetin-1, and CPAm with 100 mg/kg b.w. Venetin-1 were compared to the control group. Proteins were filtered and considered significant based on the following criteria: p < 0.05 and Fold Change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.7. Thus, a list of up-and-down-regulated proteins was obtained for each of the analysed groups of mice. The correctness of the results was checked, and the generated heat map and PCA analysis are available in the supplementary materials (Figures S1-S3).

The Venn diagrams and Volcano plots (Fig. 2) show changes in the profile of up- and down-regulated proteins, considering unique proteins and proteins shared by the analysed groups. The preliminary quantitative proteomic analysis showed that the administration of CPAm mainly contributed to increased protein expression in the mouse lung tissue. In turn, the expression of two proteins (40 S ribosomal protein S8, Rps8 and 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase [(3E)-enoyl-CoA-producing], mitochondrial, Decr1) exhibited significant downregulation in the Ven50 and Ven100 groups. After the administration of Venetin-1, the number of upregulated proteins increased to 17 at the concentration of 50 mg/kg b.w. and further grew to 46 proteins at the concentration of 100 mg/kg.

Table 2 shows changes in downregulated proteins meeting the conditions for statistical significance. The Rps8 and Decr1 proteins remained at reduced levels even after the administration of Venetin-1. However, their FC gradually decreased as the preparation concentration increased (Rps8 from FC 0.4 to 0.1 and Decr1 from FC 0.6 to 0.3). In the case of the group of proteins downregulated in the Ven50 group, the newly appearing proteins had not previously occurred in the group of proteins with statistically significant changes for CPAm. However, in the Ven100 group, nine proteins were characterised by increased expression in lung tissue after the treatment with cyclophosphamide, and their expression decreased after introducing the higher dose of Venetin-1 (Table 3).

Functional enrichment analysis

In order to link the results obtained from the quantitative analysis with protein functions, the data were analysed using a String server, and the visualisations of the results in the form of an interaction network presented in Fig. 3 were obtained in the Cytoscape program. The presented graphs account for up- and down-regulated proteins and their interactions, as shown in the tables above. The enrichment analysis performed on the obtained results shows five main GO categories: biological processes BP, cellular components CC, molecular function MF, KEGG, and Reactome. Five main terms are presented for each group, and the complete analysis is included in the supplementary materials. The enriched terms for biological processes for the cyclophosphamide-only group refer to the organisation of cellular components and their biogenesis, the organisation and processes of actin filaments, and the general organisation of organelles. In the groups with Venetin-1, both doses for this category show a response to stress and supramolecular organisation of fibres. For Ven50, the top five biological processes include terms related to chemical response and the term of development processes. In the Ven100 group, there are terms relating to the regulation of lipid metabolism, cellular response to chemicals, and NADPH oxidation. In the case of molecular functions in the CPAm group, all terms from the top five refer to functions related to protein binding. In the groups administered Venetin-1, there are also functions related to protein binding as well as responses in the form of antioxidant activity for Ven50 and G protein activity for Ven100.

The results from the KEGG analysis were obtained only for the CPAm and Ven50 groups, and here, differences in the response to the administered therapies are already visible. In both groups, the top 5 significantly enriched signalling pathways are focal adhesion and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton. Further, the cyclophosphamide group includes terms related to ECM-receptor interaction, amoebiosis, and small cell lung cancer, and the Ven50 group includes terms related to the ribosome, processing and antigen presentation, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy.

Enrichment in the Reactome category reveals responses in the haemostasis, immune system, platelet degranulation, and signalling by Rho GTPases categories for CPAm and Ven50 in all the three groups. For the two groups administered Venetin-1, platelet activation, signalling, and aggregation were used in the Reactome category. In the Ven100 group, the top five include the GPVI-mediated activation cascade and adaptive immune system.

Verification of the effect of Venetin-1 on the expression of complement C3

A separate experiment was designed to verify the ability of the earthworm preparation to influence the expression of complement C3. Venetin-1 was used to stimulate non-small lung cancer cell line H2087, previously confirmed to express complement C3 naturally. The qPCR analysis revealed that the concentration above 50 ug/ml significantly elevated the expression of C3 (Fig. 6). The credibility of the results was ensured by applying two different pairs of primers.

Discussion

Cyclophosphamide is a prodrug that undergoes metabolic activation in cells to produce its active form, phosphoramide mustard - an active alkylating agent. The metabolic pathway of CPAm involves several steps, such as conversion to 4-hydroxycyclophosphamide by the cytochrome P450 enzyme system (specifically by CYP4B6 and CYP3A4)45. The processing also produces acrolein, a toxic metabolite responsible for most of the side effects of CPAm. In the presented studies, cyclophosphamide was administered intraperitoneally to mice, and cytochrome P450 4B1 (Cyp4b1) was the protein that showed an increased Fold Change value in the examined lung tissue46. This protein is characteristic of lung tissue in humans44 and mice of both sexes 47. Its expression was altered in several specific cancers, including lung cancer48,49. The FC 1.6 value obtained from the quantitative tests indicated its increased activity and potential role in metabolism to active forms. After introducing Venetin-1 into the peritoneum of mice at the highest dose, the FC value for this protein dropped to 0.6, which indicates significantly reduced activity. The primary metabolism of cyclophosphamide occurs in the liver. Still, it is possible that, during intraperitoneal administration, part of the compound went directly to the lungs, which was converted into active forms by cytochrome P450 4b1. We did not find direct literature evidence that Cyp4b1 is involved in cyclophosphamide metabolism, but there are data proving that it metabolises cyclic amines50.

Cyclophosphamide therapies are problematic due to their toxicity to healthy tissues, leading to increased oxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation stimulating inflammatory cells, such as macrophages, neutrophils, and monocytes51,57,58. This results in DNA and protein damage. In lung tissue, cyclophosphamide toxicity manifests early as lymphocytic infiltration, interstitial pneumonitis, alveolar damage, and bronchiolitis, and later as lung fibrosis52. Cyclophosphamide also exacerbates inflammation53–55 and, in low doses, has significant immunomodulatory effects by selectively eliminating regulatory T cells (Tregs)56. However, its impact on macrophages is less understood.

The presence of inflammation is closely associated with proteins from the S100 group, which play a dual role in this process. They act as inflammation-responsive proteins and can amplify the inflammatory signal59. The research revealed that the Ca2+-binding protein S100a9 was the top-upregulated protein in lung tissue in all the examined groups. The FC value for the phosphamide-treated group was 8.6. After introducing the dose of 50 mg/kg b.w. Venetin-1, the value increased to 11.4; at the highest dose, it dropped by almost half to 6.5. With the extensive use of highly effective immunosuppressive agents like cyclophosphamide, the immune response system is altered during treatment, and the immune response is replaced by the S100a9-mediated pathological response. In the group treated with cyclophosphamide, an increased FC 2.9 value was determined for Vimentin (Vim), a protein crucial in stabilising intracellular structure60, an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (ETM) activator61, and the NLRP3 inflammasome trigger62. It is activated in the early response to inflammation, which in this case was triggered by the presence of CPAm.

Interestingly, in the results obtained for lung tissue, there was no increase in the expression of the S100a8 protein, which, together with S100a9, forms a complex known as calprotectin. The analyses also showed an increase in two other proteins from the S100 group, namely S100A11 (FC in the CPAm group 5.1, in the Ven50 group 3.9) and S100A10 (FC 4.5 in the CPAm group). The latter, i.e. S100A10, with increased expression, is related to the migration of leukocytes and cancer cells and metastasis. It is also constitutively expressed in macrophages and some other immune cells63. This state was maintained only in the group administered cyclophosphamide. The increased expression of S100A11 persisted in the cyclophosphamide-treated Ven50 group. This protein co-localises with microtubules and type III intermittent filaments59,64,65. Overproduction is associated with many types of cancer, such as pancreatic, intestinal, and breast cancer, and can induce the growth of cancer cells64,66,67.

Class II non-muscle myosin has three isoforms: NM IIA, NM IIB, and NM IIC encoded by three genes: MYH9, MYH10, and MYH14, known as essential regulators of cell adhesion and polarity during their migration. It is related to the organisation of actin and the formation of its filaments during the process68. Our studies showed increased expression of myosins, actins, and tubulins (Myh10 FC 1.6 and Myh9 3.5; Alpha-actin-1 (FC 3.9), Alhpa-actin-2 (FC 5.2), and Alpha-actin-4 (FC 3.4); Tubb2a, FC 5.1, Tubb2a, FC 5.1) in the lung tissue from the cyclophosphamide-treated mice. This indicates significant activation of processes related to cell migration in lung tissue, as evidenced by the results of the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 4) and the Reactome presented in Fig. 5. They indicate the activation of Rho GTPases. The administration of Venetin-1 at the dose of 50 mg/kg caused a slight decrease in the expression of the proteins mentioned above. However, they remained at an elevated level, which means that the migration processes were still activated. The situation changed when the higher dose of Venetin-1 was administered, i.e. 100 mg/kg. Here, migratory processes were inhibited, and cell division control protein 42 (Cdc42, FC 0.5) and Ras-related C3 botulinum toxic substrate (Rac1, FC 0.4) were down-regulated.

The influence of Venetin-1 on migration processes may also be evidenced by the appearance of a reduced FC value for Zyxin (Zyx). This protein allows cells to adhere to the extracellular matrix, interacts with Actn1, and is involved in signal transduction. Downregulation of this protein occurred already at the lower dose of the preparation.

Potential protective role of Venetin-1

Among the proteins related to the immune system, H-2 class I histocompatibility antigen D-D alpha chain (H2-D1, FC 5.5) was a highly upregulated protein, whose expression did not change after the introduction of Venetin-1 at the dose of 50 mg/kg b.w. (FC 5.4), and dropped slightly to FC 3.2 at 100 mg/kg b.w. The results indicate that the dose of cyclophosphamide used in the experiment can still partially stimulate the immune system, and the effect is maintained at the same level when Venetin-1 is used. This proves the possibility of stimulating the immune system to act after using a chemotherapeutic. Another protein was the H-2 class I histocompatibility antigen, K-D alpha chain (H2-K1), which in turn appeared after the introduction of the earthworm preparation (Ven50 FC 3.0, Ven100 FC 2.4). The appearance of the H2-K1 protein after the introduction of Venetin-1 may indicate the beginning of lung tissue regeneration. It is an example of airway stem/progenitors acting club-like to lineage-negative epithelial progenitors (LNEPs), resulting in H2-K1 expression. This mechanism triggers the transformation of H2-K1 cells into alveolar cells, which restores the proper functioning of lungs69–71.

The analysis of the results also indicated the presence of increased FC values for complement C3. Values at the FC3 level were determined in both groups with the chemotherapeutic agent cyclophosphamide and in both groups receiving Venetin-1. Factor C3 is an inflammatory mediator in various types of cancer and a precursor for C3a and C5a, which can attract immune cells72. To determine whether the C3 activation in the groups treated with Venetin-1 was a remnant of the previously administered CPAm, the effect of the preparation on the activation of complement C3 gene expression was tested. It was shown that the preparation itself also had a natural ability to stimulate the endogenous expression of complement C3 in lung cancer cells (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Graph presenting the results of stimulation of the complement C3 system in H2087 cells by the Venetin-1 preparation.

Administration of Venetin-1 caused an increase in the expression of the uteroglobin (Ugb) protein (FC 3.5 in the Ven50 group and FC 1.9 in the Ven100 group). This protein is secreted by Clara cells and is an anti-inflammatory factor in epithelial cells73–75, an anti-chemotactic, immunomodulatory factor76,77, and an inhibitor of the proliferation of various cancer cell lines78,79. The inhibition of adherence and PLA2-mediated migration of neutrophils and monocytes is responsible for the anti-chemotactic effect of Ugb80. Previous literature data show that mice lacking Clara cells experienced significant lung damage after treatment with bleomycin and can inhibit pulmonary fibrosis81. Reduced Ugb expression is mentioned in lung cancer studies78 and in cigarette smoking patients82. Knock-out Ugo in mice predisposes them to develop multi-organ invasive tumours83. Therefore, the role of protection against carcinogen-induced tumourigenesis of uteroglobin is visible. The stimulating effect on Ugo production was visible even at the lower dose, i.e. 50 mg/kg.

Another protein whose expression was increased under the influence of Venetin-1 is eosinophil peroxidase (Exp) (Ven50 FC 2.5, Ven100 FC 2.1). This protein is produced by eosinophils present in the lungs, which, through the production of e.g. Epx and major basic protein (Mbp), eosinophilic cationic protein (Ecp), and neurotoxin (EDN), reach and kill parasites, viruses, and bacteria83. In the case of cancer, the role of eosinophils depends on the type of cancer cells and the location of the cancer so that they can be both a positive and negative prognostic factor. A growing body of research suggests that eosinophils influence the tumour environment, improve immune checkpoint blockade efficacy, modulate the phenotype and effector functions of intra-tumoural immune cells, and kill tumour cells ex-vivo84–91. Cederberg and colleagues confirmed their hypothesis that lung eosinophils reduce tumour cell colonisation and metastatic growth92.

Previous work on Venetin-1 indicated its effect on the mitochondria of yeast cells and cancer cells. In this case, administration of the preparation to mice increases the expression of the mouse mitochondrial chaperone 10 kDa heat shock protein (Hspe1). Naturally, it is responsible for the import of proteins and macromolecular assembly. In complex with Hsp60, it participates in the correct folding of imported proteins, prevents misfolding, and promotes refolding in stressful situations in the mitochondrial matrix93–95. The expression level in lung tissue was FC3.6 in the Ven50 group and 1.9 in the Ven100 group. This protein also has protective functions in the cell by modulating intracellular cytoprotective signalling and activating the BCL-2 pathway96. Due to their involvement in the folding and refolding process, these mechanisms protect cells against producing non-functional proteins that may be subject to ubiquitination97.

The work carried out so far on the effect of Venetin-1 on yeast cells has demonstrated the activation of the chaperone-mediated autophagy mechanism98. The proteomic analysis confirmed this by identifying Lysosome-associated membrane glycoprotein 2 LAMP2 overexpression34. Also, in the examined lung tissue, overexpression of this protein was observed after the treatment with the preparation at the level of FC 2.6 and FC 1.6 in the Ven50 and Ven100 groups, respectively. This may indicate that the self-degradation process is triggered and the protein with cytosolic KFERQ-like motif can be recognised by Hsc70 and transferred by LAMP2 to the lysosome membrane. This mechanism also plays a vital role in the immune response. LAMP2 is also activated on the cell surface during metastasis or activation of macrophages in the cancer process99,100. However, there is not much research in this field.

Conclusion

The presented studies have shown that the inclusion of Venetin-1 during cyclophosphamide administration can have a positive effect of regeneration of lung tissue damaged by cyclophosphamide. The proteomic analysis showed a change in the protein expression after the administration of the earthworm preparation, which activated processes related to autophagy and anti-inflammatory processes in the mouse lungs. This effect was enhanced when the dose was increased from 50 to 100 mg/kg b.w. There was also an apparent effect on the pathways related to Rho GTPases and the activation of the complement system, which was further verified in an experiment on cells. The findings provide grounds for further research into the use of Venetin-1 as a preparation for reducing the side effects of cyclophosphamide and other toxic chemotherapeutics. Further research endeavours are warranted to validate its clinical efficacy and safety in larger cohorts and to elucidate optimal treatment regimens.
