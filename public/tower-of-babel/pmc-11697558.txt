Protocol for in situ and in vivo dissection of mouse lung-draining lymph nodes
Authors: Enshuo Zhang, Baicheng Zhao, Yupeng Liu, Gang Liu, Shuting Lu, Liqin Xiong, Xufeng Pan, Haitang Yang, Feng Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryLung-draining lymph nodes (LNs) play crucial roles in infection, inflammation, and cancer, but in situ and in vivo studying models are technically challenging. Here, we present a protocol for the in situ and in vivo dissection of mouse lung-draining LNs, ensuring postoperative survival. We describe steps for lymphadenectomy including localizing lung-draining LNs, open thoracotomy, and LN dissection. We then detail procedures for surgical sample preservation and postoperative care. This protocol offers broad applications in immunological and oncological research.Subject areas: cancer, immunology, model organisms

Before you begin

Lymph nodes (LNs) are essential secondary lymphoid organs that are critical to the immune response. The dysfunction of LNs has been linked to various conditions, including infections, tumors, and autoimmune disorders. Lymphatic vessels drain lymph fluid, carrying macromolecules from the surrounding interstitial spaces, shaped by its originating organs or tissues. This fluid converges at regional LNs, influencing their functions. Therefore, the distinct anatomical location of LNs shapes their local tissue environment. The lung, being highly immunological, contains multiple draining LN stations. However, in situ mouse models for systematically studying lung-draining LNs under physiological and pathological conditions are currently lacking. This is primarily due to the technical challenges associated with open thoracotomy, intrathoracic mediastinal LN exposure, and dissection, which often result in the death of the operated mice. Consequently, this hinders subsequent researches focusing on lung-draining LNs. The protocol below describes the specific lung-draining LN dissection steps in C57BL/6J models. It offers an approach to elucidate the roles of lung-draining LNs under various conditions, such as respiratory infections, inflammatory, local/systematic immune disorders or lung cancer progression.1 Since different tissue-derived lymph fluids can profoundly influence the molecular and cellular composition of draining LNs,2 this protocol provides a more pathophysiologically and clinically relevant situation to study respiratory system diseases than the current non-in situ subcutaneous model, thereby opening up new avenues of research and discovery in infection, immunology and oncology.

This protocol includes steps of locating lung-draining LNs with methylene blue indicator,3,4 endotracheal anesthesia, muscle incision and LN dissection, as well as post-operative care. The whole procedure takes 7 days in total.

Lymphadenectomy procedure

Timing: 2 days (day −1 to day 0)

This section outlines the steps of localizing lung-draining LNs, open thoracotomy and LN dissection as well as surgical sample preservation (optional).

1.In situ methylene blue injection (Day −1; 1 day before the surgery).Note: This procedure aids in identifying the lung-draining LNs. Methylene blue, a clinically used dye, is injected with the syringe for insulin (Video S1).a.Turn on the anesthesia system and connect it to the anesthesia chamber.i.Set the oxygen flow on the flowmeter to a range of 0.8–1.2 L/min.ii.Turn on the fluosorber to absorb isoflurane from the system.iii.Adjust the isoflurane concentrator between 2%–3%.Note: Ventilator is not needed in this step.b.Position each mouse in the anesthesia chamber.c.Confirm deep anesthesia by gently pressing the footpad. The mouse shows no response if the deep anesthesia is successfully achieved.d.Make a 1 cm incision on the left side of the xiphoid process, cutting through the skin and subcutaneous fat.e.Use a hemostat or forceps to bluntly dissect the chest wall muscles until the lung lobes can be observed clearly through the chest wall, expanding with respiratory movements that are clearly visible within the thoracic cavity.f.Draw 10 μL of the original methylene blue solution without dilutions using an insulin syringe (30G).g.Slowly insert the needle 3 mm into the lung lobe vertically through the intercostal space.i.Inject the solution gradually.ii.Hold the needle in place for more than 10 s.iii.Slowly withdraw the needle.h.Suture the skin wound using absorbable surgical sutures, ensuring no sutures are left to minimize infection.i.Place the mouse in a warm and dimly lit environment until it fully regains consciousness.Note: In our practice, administering the injection into the left lower lung lobe one day before the procedure yields the highest success rate and optimal drainage outcomes. However, researchers may adjust the injection site and timing based on specific experimental conditions.CRITICAL: Inappropriate in situ lung injection hinders the accurate localization of the lung-draining LNs. Ideally, the blue dye should diffuse within the injected lung lobes, without staining the entire thoracic cavity or chest wall.

Expected outcomes

Upon disconnection from the ventilator, the mice immediately begin spontaneous breathing, gradually increasing in both respiratory rate and tidal volume troubleshooting 3. Approximately 10 min post-procedure, the mice regain consciousness from anesthesia and exhibit unimpaired mobility (Video S3). By 30 min post-procedure, the mice exhibit full restoration of normal activity levels (Videos S4 and S5). Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining of lung-draining LN samples reveals typical structures of a LN (Figure 4), including cortex, follicles, and medulla.

Limitations

While our protocol is optimized to ensure repeatable and reliable results in our practice, it is important to recognize several limitations due to anatomical heterogeneity and surgical side effects.

Anatomical difference

During our practice, we observed that the lower mediastinal LNs are not always located in an easily accessible ventral position but are occasionally situated dorsally. This dorsal positioning renders the dissection process comparatively more challenging and potentially less thorough.

Potential surgical stress response

The invasive thoracotomy itself may induce a stress response and potentially influence on study outcomes by introducing confounding variables. Therefore, it is important to properly set a control group by following a sham procedure and diligently monitor confounding variables in the process of experimental design and data analysis.

Ethical concerns

The invasive surgery itself raises ethical concerns regarding animal welfare. Therefore, it is crucial to adhere to ethical guidelines and requirements to ensure the well-being of animals involved in this study.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Upon thoracotomy, the mice exhibit labored breathing and subsequently experience mortality after several respiratory cycles (related to step 3f).

This issue typically arises when endotracheal intubation is not performed successfully.

Potential solution

Endotracheal intubation verification: Intubating the mouse’s glottis can be challenging. To ensure effective intubation before proceeding with thoracotomy, we recommend using one of the following verification methods:

Mirror method: Place a pre-cooled mirror near the external opening of the intubation tube. If air is being exchanged, condensation will form on the mirror’s surface.

Cotton wisp method: Alternatively, position a small cotton wisp at the tube opening. The wisp will move in response to the mouse’s respiratory airflow, providing visual confirmation of successful intubation.

Problem 2

After opening the thoracic cavity, mediastinal LNs are not stained, or the whole thoracic cavity is diffusely stained (related to step 3g).

This issue primarily occurs when the needle insertion during in situ injection is too shallow or too deep.

Potential solution

After proper exposure, check the lower left lung lobe for signs of the injection site. The needle insertion depth needs to be increased, if no trace is found. The insertion depth should be reduced if needle marks are present on both the ventral and dorsal sides of the lung lobe.

Problem 3

Upon disconnection from the ventilator, the mice may experience respiratory failure and eventually mortality after a period (related to expected outcomes).

This problem often results from intraoperative lung injury or forgetting to evacuate residual air from the lungs post-procedure.

Potential solution

During the surgery, handle the lung lobes with extreme gentleness. If any signs of bleeding occur, immediately halt the procedure and either adjust the technique or switch to blunt forceps. Before closing the thoracic cavity, check the lung lobes for any signs of bleeding. If fluid remains in the thoracic cavity, use sterile gauze to absorb it thoroughly.
