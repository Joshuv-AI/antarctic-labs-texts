Functional validation of spliceogenic COL4A3 and COL4A4 variants by minigene assays refines molecular diagnosis of Alport syndrome
Authors: Lina Wang, Zhi Wang, Yan Zhao, Xuyan Liu, Qiaomei Cui, Shuimi Zhang, Ran Zhang, Leping Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlport syndrome (AS) is a hereditary progressive kidney disease caused by pathogenic variants in the COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 genes. Aberrant pre-mRNA splicing represents a major disease mechanism in AS, and both synonymous and intronic variants can disrupt splice-site recognition or regulatory elements, leading to exon skipping or intron retention. However, the clinical interpretation of putative spliceogenic variants remains challenging, particularly when subtle splicing changes coexist with background alternative splicing.ResultsFourteen synonymous and intronic variants in COL4A3 and COL4A4 were screened using in silico splicing prediction tools and subsequently functionally evaluated by minigene assays in HEK293T and HeLa cells. Thirteen variants exhibited aberrant splicing, most commonly as exon skipping, with variable effects on transcript structure and abundance. Several variants predominantly generated frameshift transcripts with premature termination codons, whereas others resulted in in-frame exon skipping or produced low-level aberrant transcripts comparable to wild-type splicing. By integrating quantitative splicing outcomes with population frequency data and predicted protein consequences, all variants were reclassified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, resulting in four pathogenic, nine likely pathogenic, and one likely benign variant.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that synonymous and intronic variants in COL4A3 and COL4A4 can exert pathogenic effects primarily through disruption of pre-mRNA splicing rather than through direct alteration of the coding sequence. Incorporation of quantitative functional splicing data into ACMG/AMP variant interpretation provides a robust framework for resolving variants of uncertain significance and improves the molecular diagnosis of AS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00989-w.Keywords: Alport syndrome, COL4A3, COL4A4, Minigene, Pre-mRNA splicing, Variant classification

Introduction

Type IV collagen serves as the principal structural scaffold of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) and other basement membranes, playing a vital role in maintaining the selective filtration barrier and tissue integrity [1]. The α3, α4, and α5 chains of type IV collagen are encoded by COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5, respectively. Pathogenic variants in these genes are the established molecular cause of Alport syndrome (AS), a hereditary nephropathy characterized by hematuria, progressive renal failure, and frequent extrarenal manifestations such as sensorineural hearing loss and ocular abnormalities [2, 3].

COL4A3 and COL4A4 are responsible for the autosomal forms of AS, which include both recessive and dominant inheritance patterns. Compared with X-linked AS, autosomal AS often has a milder and more heterogeneous clinical course. This variability makes molecular diagnosis and variant interpretation particularly challenging [4].

With the widespread use of next-generation sequencing, increasing numbers of COL4A3 and COL4A4 variants have been identified, many of which are suspected to be spliceogenic but remain difficult to classify. Aberrant splicing is an important disease mechanism in AS and may arise not only from canonical splice-site variants, but also from exonic or intronic changes that disrupt splicing regulatory elements or activate cryptic splice sites [5, 6]. Under current ACMG/AMP guidelines, in silico prediction provides only supporting evidence (PP3) [7]. As a result, many suspected spliceogenic variants remain classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) unless functional evidence is available.

Transcript analysis can provide direct evidence of splicing defects. However, its application to COL4A3 and COL4A4 is limited because these genes are predominantly expressed in kidney tissue, which is rarely available in routine clinical practice. In addition, RNA analysis from peripheral tissues may be affected by low expression levels, tissue-specific splicing, and transcript instability [8]. Minigene splicing assays therefore offer a practical and well-established alternative for evaluating the functional consequences of candidate variants in a controlled cellular context [9, 10].

In this study, we selected candidate spliceogenic variants in COL4A3 and COL4A4 identified through clinical genetic testing of individuals with suspected autosomal AS. Variants were prioritized using in silico splicing prediction tools and functionally assessed via minigene assays in HEK293T and HeLa cells. By systematically characterizing their effects on pre-mRNA splicing, we aim to provide empirical evidence to facilitate VUS reclassification and refine the molecular diagnosis of AS.

Materials and methods

Variant nomenclature and computational analysis

COL4A3 and COL4A4 variants were numbered according to cDNA sequences (RefSeq NM_000091.5 and NM_000092.5) following Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) guidelines, with c.1 designated as the first nucleotide of the translation start codon. Variants were retrieved from the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and ClinVar (accessed June 2024). SpliceAI, MaxEntScan, and SPiP were used to predict splicing effects. SnapGene and UniProt database assessed protein consequences of splicing errors. VulExMap predicted exon susceptibility to aberrant splicing, and DeepCLIP analyzed RNA-binding proteins involved in exon regulation.

Variant selection and minigene construction

All included variants were selected under disease annotations related to Alport syndrome. Among the selected variants, two (c.1409-5T > A and c.3882 + 5G > C in COL4A3) were derived from HGMD, while the remaining variants were obtained from ClinVar. Furthermore, additional clinical evidence for the synonymous variant c.441G > A (COL4A3) was obtained from a recently published study. The detailed clinical phenotypes and inheritance patterns of representative variants are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Variants meeting the following criteria were selected for splicing analysis: (1) located at intronic positions − 3 to − 20 or + 3 to + 6 relative to exon–intron boundaries, or synonymous variants that may affect splicing regulatory elements; (2) SpliceAI Δ score ≥ 0.4; and (3) additional supporting evidence from at least one complementary predictor (MaxEntScan or SPiP), indicating either a significant reduction in canonical splice-site strength or the activation of cryptic splice sites. Genomic DNA from healthy human blood (Promega purification kit) served as template to amplify target exon fragments (primers in Table S2) for cloning into pSPL3 vector to generate wild-type (WT) plasmids. A schematic overview of the exon capture and minigene construction strategy is shown in Figure S1. Mutant plasmids were created using QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene) with mutagenic primers (Table S3). All constructs were verified by sequencing (Figure S2).

Minigene splicing assay

HeLa (RRID: CVCL_0030) and HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063) cells (RPMI-1640, 10% FBS, 1% pen/strep, 37 °C, 5% CO2 ) were transfected at 75% confluence in 12- or 24-well plates (three biological replicates). After 48 h, RNA was extracted (TRIzol, isopropanol), reverse-transcribed (Evo M-MLV Plus). The resulting complementary DNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction using the SD6 (5′-TCTGAG TCACCTGGACAACC-3′) and SA2 (5′-ATCTCAGTGGTATTTGTGAGC-3′) primers: 95 °C 15 min → 32 × (95 °C 30 s, 55 °C 30 s, 72 °C 30 s) → 72 °C 5 min. Products were resolved on 1.5% agarose, stained with GelRed and quantified by fragment analyzer. Aberrant splicing products were further validated by DNA Sanger sequencing, and the corresponding sequencing chromatograms are shown in the Results section. All variants identified in this study were reclassified in accordance with the standards and guidelines recommended by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG).

Statistical analysis

Aberrant transcript percentage = (aberrant intensity / total intensity) × 100%. Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 6.02; P < 0.05 was significant.

In silico analysis of variants

Bioinformatic predictions indicated that the splicing effects of these 14 variants were heterogeneous rather than restricted to a single mechanism. In our study, most variants were predicted to disrupt canonical splice sites, with impairment of the 5′ donor site representing the predominant alteration. Several variants were predicted to induce more complex alterations in splice-site selection. For example, COL4A3 c.3132 A > C and COL4A4 c.1203 A > G were predicted to alter the balance between canonical and cryptic donor sites, while COL4A3 c.1759 − 11T > A, as well as COL4A4 c.2969–10 A > G, were predicted to activate cryptic or novel acceptor sites. COL4A4 c.3289 + 3 A > G was predicted not only to weaken the canonical donor site but also to induce a mild increase in nearby cryptic donor signals, suggesting a potential redistribution of splice-site usage.

VulExMap predicted 13 vulnerable exons in the COL4A3 gene and 11 in the COL4A4 gene (Figure S3). Among the synonymous variants initially prioritized in this study, c.987G > A in exon 17 and c.3132 A > C in exon 37 of COL4A3 were located in exons predicted to be susceptible to splicing perturbation. DeepCLIP analysis further suggested variant-specific effects on splicing factor binding affinity: COL4A3 c.987G > A was predicted to reduce HNRNPA1 binding, c.1575 + 4 A > G to enhance SRSF1 binding, and c.1409 − 5T > A to increase binding of both HNRNPA1 and HNRNPL, whereas COL4A4 c.930 + 5G > A was predicted to strengthen HNRNPL binding (Table S4).

To evaluate the predictive performance of these in silico tools, the predicted splicing effects were subsequently compared with the experimental outcomes obtained from the minigene assays.

Minigene analysis

Minigene splicing assays showed that 13 of the 14 selected variants induced aberrant splicing, with exon skipping representing the most common outcome. Among the aberrant transcripts identified, some were predicted to introduce premature termination codons (PTCs) due to frameshifts, whereas others preserved the reading frame, resulting in in-frame deletions or insertions. Overall, the minigene findings were broadly consistent with the bioinformatic predictions, particularly for variants predicted to disrupt canonical donor sites; however, several variants exhibited more complex transcript patterns than expected from in silico analysis alone. Predominant exon-skipping

Variants c.987G > A, c.1575 + 4 A > G, and c.3882 + 5G > C led to predominant skipping of exon 17 (Fig. 2A), exon 24 (Fig. 2B), and exon 43 (Fig. 2C), respectively. Exon-skipping with residual canonical transcript(CT)

Variants c.441G > A and c.1759 − 11T > A showed mixed splicing patterns, with both canonical and aberrant transcripts detected. For c.441G > A, the minigene assay demonstrated predominant skipping of exon 7 (Fig. 2D), resulting in an in-frame deletion of 18 amino acids within the triple-helical domain. For c.1759 − 11T > A, bioinformatic analysis suggested altered acceptor-site recognition; however, the predominant experimental outcome was exon 26 skipping with retention of a minor canonical transcript (Fig. 2E). This aberrant transcript caused a frameshift and introduced a PTC (p.Ser589GlyfsTer102).

Minor alternative splicing on a heterogeneous WT background

Variant c.3132 A > C minigene construct yielded predominantly canonical transcripts together with a minor exon 37-skipped product (Fig. 2F). However, multiple transcript products were already present in the corresponding WT construct, which exhibited three major products: full exon inclusion (approximately 50%), partial exon retention corresponding to the first half of exon 37 (approximately 38%), and background transcripts. These findings indicate substantial baseline splice heterogeneity at this locus in the minigene setting. Under these conditions, the low-abundance exon 37-skipped product observed in the mutant construct was not regarded as sufficient evidence of a distinct pathogenic splicing effect.

Generation of multiple transcripts

Variant c.1409 − 5T > A generated three distinct transcripts. In silico analysis predicted weakening of the canonical acceptor site together with activation of nearby competing acceptor signals. Consistent with this prediction, the mutant minigene produced two visible bands in Fig. 2G, with overlapping peaks in the larger amplicon. TA cloning and direct sequencing revealed that the larger amplicons consisted of a normal transcript and an additional transcript containing a 3 bp insertion upstream of exon 23, whereas the smaller amplicon contained only 263 bp, corresponding to exon skipping. These events are predicted to result in proteins harboring either a single–amino acid insertion or an in-frame deletion within the collagen IV triple-helical domain (p.Lys469_Gly470insAla and p.Gly470_Pro501del, respectively).

Exon-skipping with residual canonical transcript

Variants c.975 + 3 A > G and c.2056 + 3 A > G showed partial splice disruption, yielding both canonical and aberrant transcripts (Fig. 3C and D), broadly consistent with the bioinformatic prediction. These variants caused skipping of exon 16 and exon 26, respectively, resulting in in-frame deletions of 15 and 23 amino acids within the triple-helical domain. The relative abundance of aberrant transcripts differed modestly between HEK293T and HeLa cells.

Intron retention

Sequencing analysis confirmed that variant c.2969–10 A > G markedly weakened the original acceptor site (score from 0.99 to 0.49) and activated a new splice acceptor site (score 0.87), leading to insertion of 9 nucleotides at the 3′ end of intron 32 (Fig. 3E). Consequently, three amino acids were inserted after residue 989 (p.Arg989_Gly990insAlaSerSer).

Generation of multiple transcripts

Variants c.930 + 5G > A and c.1203 A > G generated multiple splicing products, consistent with the more complex splice-site alterations predicted by in silico analysis. For c.930 + 5G > A, the WT construct already showed baseline splice heterogeneity, but the mutant construct shifted toward aberrant products corresponding to exon 15 skipping and combined exon 14/15 skipping (Fig. 3F). Both transcripts preserved the reading frame and were predicted to produce in-frame deletions in the triple-helical domain.

For c.1203 A > G, disruption of the canonical donor site resulted in three distinct transcripts. The first transcript corresponded to the canonical splicing product (368 bp). The second aberrant transcript arose from activation of an alternative donor site, generating a 402-bp product that retained exon 18 together with an additional 34 bp from the downstream intron (Fig. 3G). This transcript caused a frameshift and was predicted to encode a truncated protein (p.Met403ThrfsTer38). The third transcript resulted from exon 19 skipping, leading to an in-frame deletion (p.Gly367_Ala401del) and loss of 35 amino acids within the collagenous triple-helical domain.

Classification of DNA variants

Given that the pathogenic mechanism of the target genes is predominantly loss-of-function, application of the PVS1 criterion was restricted to variants that produced PTCs and were predicted to result in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD). For variants leading to in-frame amino acid deletions or insertions within the collagen triple helix, a well-established functional domain essential for collagen IV assembly and stability, PM1_supporting was applied to reflect their location in a critical functional region. All variants fulfilled the PM2 criterion, as they were either absent from population databases or observed at allele frequencies below the maximum credible threshold for AS. For variants producing multiple splicing products in minigene assays, only aberrant transcripts that were clearly distinct from the WT splicing pattern were considered in the pathogenicity assessment, while background or WT–like transcripts were excluded from evidence weighting. Variants resulting in partial exon loss with uncertain functional consequences were assigned downgraded PVS1 strength. Overall, by integrating in silico splicing predictions and population frequency data (Table S5), 4 variants were classified as pathogenic (P), 9 as likely pathogenic (LP), and 1 as likely benign (LB) [3, 11].

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized the splicing consequences of a series of COL4A3 and COL4A4 variants using minigene-based functional assays, and integrated these results with predicted protein-level effects. This comprehensive evaluation enhances the accuracy of variant pathogenicity interpretation in AS and informs downstream genetic counseling and clinical management.

Our retrospective comparison showed that SpliceAI is useful for prioritizing candidate splice variants, particularly at canonical or near-canonical splice sites, but it did not always predict the predominant transcript outcome. This was evident for c.1409 − 5T > A, c.1759 − 11T > A, c.1203 A > G, and c.930 + 5G > A. Notably, c.1759 − 11T > A was predicted to create a novel acceptor site whereas the minigene assay predominantly showed exon 26 skipping. A similar pattern has been reported by Wimmer et al., who found that variants interrupting the AG-exclusion zone in NF1 frequently resulted in exon skipping rather than simple cryptic acceptor activation [12]. This suggests that the predicted novel acceptor created by c.1759 − 11T > A may primarily perturb recognition of the native 3′ splice site, leading predominantly to exon 26 skipping.

More broadly, our results demonstrate that the biological impact of spliceogenic variants can only be fully understood by jointly considering transcript diversity, relative transcript abundance, and protein-level structural consequences, particularly within the collagen IV triple-helical domain [3, 7, 13].

Truncating splice products represent high-confidence deleterious outcomes

A substantial subset of variants primarily generated aberrant transcripts that introduce frameshifts and PTCs. This pattern was observed in multiple variants affecting canonical or near-canonical splice donor/acceptor regions in both COL4A3 and COL4A4. In multiple constructs, aberrant products accounted for nearly all detectable transcripts across both HEK293T and HeLa cells. Mechanistically, these events are predicted to result in a marked reduction of functional collagen IV α3 or α4 chains, either through NMD or through the production of severely truncated polypeptides [14]. If NMD is incomplete, severely truncated polypeptides may still be produced.

COL4A3/COL4A4 encode essential components of the α3α4α5(IV) heterotrimer. Reduced chain availability should impair trimer assembly and GBM integrity [15]. In addition, the C-terminal NC1 domain is critical for chain selectivity and trimerization [16]. Truncations that remove NC1 are therefore predicted to abrogate productive assembly [13, 17]. Consistent with these mechanisms, splice variants dominated by truncating transcripts represent a category with clear and substantial pathogenic potential and are most plausibly associated with more severe Alport phenotypes, such as earlier-onset proteinuria and faster progression toward end-stage kidney disease [15].

In-frame exon skipping within the triple-helical domain: modest transcript changes with disproportionate structural impact

A second major outcome class consisted of in-frame exon skipping that preserves the reading frame but deletes discrete segments of the collagen IV triple-helical domain. This was observed for several variants, including COL4A3 c.441G > A and c.987G > A, that generated in-frame deletions within the collagen IV triple-helical domain. Although these products do not introduce PTCs, they should not be assumed benign. The collagen IV triple helix depends on constrained Gly–X–Y repeat architecture, precise chain length coordination, and correct inter-chain registration. Even relatively short in-frame deletions can destabilize local helix structure, impair alignment, and weaken network formation. Moreover, collagen IV assembly is cooperative and nonlinear. A subpopulation of structurally abnormal α chains may disproportionately weaken overall network integrity, even when some normal transcript is retained. Therefore, the fractional abundance of in-frame skipping products becomes a critical determinant of biological impact.

Taken together, spliceogenic variants that mainly generate in-frame exon-skipped transcripts may help explain the intermediate or variable clinical expressivity observed in AS, characterized by early hematuria, later proteinuria, and heterogeneous progression rates [15]. These results argue that interpretation of spliceogenic variants should not rely solely on a binary classification of splice disruption, but also account for the relative abundance of altered isoforms and the structural tolerance of the affected protein domain.

Small in-frame insertions illustrate the limits of splice-only interpretation

The COL4A4 c.2969–10 A > G variant illustrates a distinct scenario: splicing was markedly altered, yet the resulting transcript introduced only a small in-frame insertion (three amino acids) within the triple-helical domain. This underscores that the presence of a splicing alteration alone does not define severity. Instead, predicted functional impact likely depends on the insertion site and local tolerance within the encoded domain. This category therefore represents a functional “gray zone”. Additional evidence, such as patient-derived RNA, segregation, and deeper structural interpretation may be required for confident classification [3, 7].

WT splicing complexity reveals exon vulnerability and informs variant interpretation

For the COL4A4 c.930 + 5G > A variant, the WT minigene construct produced three distinct transcripts: Ex14–15 (approximately 60%), Ex14 alone (approximately 30%), and vector-derived background (approximately 10%). This baseline complexity indicates that exon 15 is intrinsically vulnerable to alternative splicing, likely reflecting a delicate balance between competing splice-site recognition events. In the mutant context, this balance was shifted toward enhanced exon skipping (Ex15 or Ex14–15), generating in-frame deletions of 20 or 38 amino acids within the triple-helical domain. At the protein level, the mutation therefore does not merely introduce a new splice form but amplifies structurally unfavorable isoforms in an already permissive splicing environment, increasing the overall burden of defective collagen chains.

Clinical phenotype correlation further supports the relevance of these functional findings

The HGMD-derived COL4A3 variant c.1409-5T > A, reported in homozygous ARAS patients with renal failure, hearing loss, and ocular abnormalities, predominantly caused exon 23 skipping together with a minor 3-bp insertion in our assay, indicating that structurally disruptive nontruncating splice defects may still underlie severe disease. In contrast, c.3882 + 5G > C, reported in a patient with hearing loss and compound-heterozygous ARAS, produced complete exon 43 skipping with a frameshift and premature termination, consistent with a loss-of-function effect. Likewise, the relatively mild or variable phenotypes reported for c.441G > A are in line with its in-frame exon-skipping pattern.

Several limitations should be acknowledged

Minigene constructs contain only limited exon-intron sequences and therefore do not fully reproduce the endogenous genomic context, meaning that some splice outcomes may be context-dependent or assay-specific. This is exemplified by COL4A3 c.3132 A > C, in which transcript heterogeneity was already observed in the WT construct, supporting the interpretation that the minor abnormal product reflects context-dependent splice-site usage rather than a clearly pathogenic effect in vivo. Similarly, for COL4A4 c.930 + 5G > A, inclusion of a multi-exon genomic fragment revealed context-dependent exon 15 skipping and combined exon 14/15 skipping, highlighting the importance of genomic context in interpreting minigene splicing results. In addition, the assays were performed in HEK293T and HeLa cells, which may not fully reflect kidney-specific splicing regulation relevant to COL4A3/COL4A4. RNA-based analyses are also subject to technical constraints, including RNA instability and possible underrepresentation of aberrant transcripts because of nonsense-mediated decay. Accordingly, minigene findings should be interpreted together with computational prediction, clinical data, and, where available, patient-derived RNA evidence.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that aberrant RNA splicing is a major pathogenic mechanism underlying COL4A3 and COL4A4 variants and that functional splicing assays are essential for accurate variant interpretation. By integrating minigene-based splicing analyses with bioinformatic prediction, population frequency data, and protein-level considerations, we refined ACMG/AMP classification and improved the molecular diagnostic yield for AS. These findings highlight the importance of quantitative and contextual evaluation of spliceogenic variants, including comparison with WT splicing patterns, to distinguish true pathogenic effects from background splicing variability. Incorporation of functional RNA-level analyses into diagnostic workflows will be critical for resolving variants of uncertain significance and enabling precise molecular diagnosis of collagen IV–associated nephropathies.
