Symmetric Algebraic Circuits and Homomorphism Polynomials
Anuj Dawar, Benedikt Pago, Tim Seppelt

Abstract
The central open question of algebraic complexity is whether 𝖵𝖯 ≠ 𝖵𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{VP}\neq\mathsf{VNP} , which is saying that the permanent cannot be represented by families of polynomial-size algebraic circuits. For symmetric algebraic circuits, this has been confirmed by Dawar and Wilsenach (2020, 2025), who showed exponential lower bounds on the size of symmetric circuits for the permanent. In this work, we set out to develop a more general symmetric algebraic complexity theory. Our main result is that a family of symmetric polynomials admits small symmetric circuits if and only if they can be written as a linear combination of homomorphism counting polynomials of graphs of bounded treewidth. We also establish a relationship between the symmetric complexity of subgraph counting polynomials and the vertex cover number of the pattern graph. As a concrete example, we examine the symmetric complexity of immanant families (a generalisation of the determinant and permanent) and show that a known conditional dichotomy due to Curticapean (2021) holds unconditionally in the symmetric setting.

Introduction

The study of
algebraic circuit complexity
(also called
arithmetic circuit complexity
) aims to understand the power of circuits to succinctly express (or compute) polynomials. In short, we are interested in establishing how many operations of addition and multiplication are needed in a circuit that computes a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}[{\cal X}]
, for some field
\mathbb{F}
and set of variables
{\cal X}
. We are usually interested in how this complexity grows with
n
for a family of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The central conjecture in the field (known as
\mathsf{VP}\neq\mathsf{VNP}
) is that there are no polynomial-size circuits for the
permanent
in the way that there are for the
determinant
.

Both the determinant and permanent are examples of polynomials over matrices. That is to say that we can treat the set of variables
{\cal X}
as the entries
x_{ij}
of a square matrix. Moreover, the permanent is
symmetric
in the sense that permuting the rows and columns of the matrix does not change the polynomial. The determinant has a smaller group of symmetries, being invariant under a permutation applied
simultaneously
to the rows and columns. This motivates the study of
symmetric algebraic circuits
introduced in
[
20
,
21
]
, which are circuits where symmetries of the polynomial computed are reflected in the automorphisms of the circuits.
In
[
20
,
21
]
, an exponential gap between the symmetric circuit complexity of the determinant and the permanent was established:
There are polynomial-size symmetric circuits (in the sense of
invariance under simultaneous row and column permutations) that
compute the determinant but any family of symmetric circuits computing
the permanent is of exponential size. It is the latter, exponential
lower bound, that is the main technical achievement of that
paper. Notably, this result also opens up a new approach to the
question of separating
\mathsf{VNP}
from
\mathsf{VP}
and challenges us to find the minimum group of symmetries for a polynomial for which we can prove unconditional lower bounds.
Thus, the study of symmetric circuits is motivated by the possibility of proving strong lower bounds which are presently out of reach without the assumption of symmetry.

Among the restrictions of circuit models that have been extensively studied in the literature, symmetry is arguably one of the newest and most interesting ones: Symmetric circuits not only admit provable lower bounds but are still surprisingly powerful, in that they can efficiently compute the determinant.
By contrast, for example the restricted model of
multilinear
formulas
requires super-polynomial size for both determinant and
permanent
[
47
]
, and the same is true for
bounded-depth
multilinear circuits

[
48
]
. Also for the well-studied class
of
monotone
circuits, we have exponential lower bounds for the
permanent
[
35
]
. This does not apply to the determiinant
as it is not a monotone function, but with a suitably adapted
definition of monotonicity the same methods also yield exponential
lower bounds for the determinant
[
37
]
.

In the present paper, we approach the study of symmetric algebraic circuits more systematically than has been done before. We develop a framework that links the expressive power of such circuits to the theory of graph
homomorphism counting
. Using this, we are able to completely characterise – within a large and interesting class of polynomials – which polynomials admit efficient symmetric circuits and which ones do not. This characterisation is the main result of the paper.

To be more precise, the polynomials we consider are over (not necessarily square) matrices of variables, have rational coefficients and are invariant under arbitrary permutations of the rows and columns. These are families
(p_{n,m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomials where
p_{n,m}\in\mathbb{Q}[{\cal X}_{n,m}]
, and
{\cal X}_{n,m}
is the set of variables
\{x_{ij}\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}

\pi\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
,
\sigma\in\mathbf{Sym}_{m}

denotes the polynomial obtained from

by replacing every occurrence of

Examples of such families are the permanent, for

The reason for focussing on this class of

Related work.

Symmetric circuits
are an emerging area of research in recent years.
We have already mentioned the lower bound for symmetric circuits for the permanent from
[
21
]
.
In a similar vein, it has been shown that the determinant admits no small
\mathbf{Alt}_{n}\times\mathbf{Alt}_{n}
-symmetric circuits, even though it does have polynomial size
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric ones
[
19
]
.
Other known lower bounds are for example for the symmetric formula complexity of computing the product of permutation matrices
[
33
]
, and a lower bound for certain types of symmetric AC
0
circuits for the parity function
[
52
]
. More recent work establishes lower bounds for symmetric bounded-depth circuits with modular counting gates
[
36
]
.
In
[
1
]
, a precise characterisation of the power of uniform polynomial-size symmetric Boolean circuits with threshold gates was given: Such circuit families are equally powerful as fixed-point logic with counting, a formalism that is also related to the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm. In particular, the graph properties computable with these circuits have bounded counting width, too. Similarly, in
[
22
]
, rank logic is characterised via a class of symmetric circuits with more involved types of gates.

Another direction that has been explored are symmetric algebraic circuits in the context of algebraic proof complexity, more specifically in the circuit-based
Ideal Proof System

[
15
]
. We hope that this direction will benefit from the framework we develop here, possibly leading to new lower bounds in proof complexity.

Another aspect of our work are the
homomorphism polynomials
. Interestingly, their complexity has been studied before with respect to a different restricted model, namely
monotone
circuits. In
[
39
,
2
]
it is shown that the monotone circuit complexity of homomorphism polynomials is controlled by the treewidth of the pattern graphs in a similar way as we show this for symmetric circuits (and moreover, the circuit depth relates to the depth of the tree decompositions). It should be mentioned, though, that our setting is more general in two ways: We consider
linear combinations
of homomorphism polynomials, rather than single ones, and our set-up is non-uniform, while in
[
39
,
2
]
, the pattern graph is taken to be the same fixed graph for all sizes of host graphs.
Certain types of homomorphism polynomials (defined differently from the ones we study) have also been shown to be
\mathsf{VP}
-complete
[
25
]
.

Our results also compare nicely to what is known on the
computational
complexity of
homomorphism
and
subgraph counting
.
As already mentioned, it was shown in
[
9
]
that the computational complexity of graph parameters expressible as linear combinations of homomorphism counts (so-called
graph motif parameters
) depends only on the treewidth of the pattern graphs. The problem is in
\mathsf{FPT}
if the treewidth is bounded, and
\mathsf{\#W}[1]
-hard, otherwise.
Our Theorem
yields the same tractability criterion for the symmetric circuit complexity of the polynomials expressing these parameters but our proof techniques are very different.
Moreover, as already mentioned,
[
9
]
concerns only uniform graph motif parameters, whereas we also cover the non-uniform case where we are counting different patterns for each host graph size.

The relationship between the complexity of subgraph counting and the
vertex cover number
of the pattern graphs that we obtain in
Theorem

7.8
also appears in
[
9
]
. In
[
45
, Theorem 1.3]
, a tight connection between the counting width of subgraph counts (for a fixed constant-size pattern) and the vertex cover number of the pattern is established.
Our
Theorem

7.8
generalises this result to the case of sublinearly growing patterns.

2.2
Symmetric algebraic circuits

An
algebraic circuit
over a variable set
{\cal X}
and a field
\mathbb{F}
is a connected
DAG
with multiedges
such that each vertex is labelled with an element of
{\cal X}\cup\mathbb{F}\cup\{+,\times\}
. The vertices of a circuit are called
gates
, the edges
wires
.
Gates labelled with elements from
{\cal X}\cup\mathbb{F}
are called

input gates
and they are not allowed to have incoming
edges. Every element of
{\cal X}\cup\mathbb{F}
is allowed to appear at most once as the label of a gate – this is no restriction because one can simply identify the respective gates. We assume that a circuit contains exactly one gate without outgoing wires, and this is called the
output gate
.

An algebraic circuit represents (or computes) a polynomial in

\mathbb{F}[{\cal X}]
in the obvious way: the gates
+
and
\times

are simply interpreted as addition and multiplication on
polynomials, and we care about the polynomial at the output
gate. Arrows are directed from a computation result towards
the next gate where that result is used as an input. The set
of
children
of a gate
g
, denoted
\child(g)
, is the
set of gates
h
such that
(h,g)
is a wire of the circuit.
Multiedges are allowed so that for example the polynomial

x^{2}
can be represented with just one input and one
multiplication gate. The
size
of a circuit
C
, is
defined as the number of gates plus the number of wires, counted with multiplicities. It is denoted
\lVert C\rVert
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group that acts on
{\cal X}
. Then a circuit
C
is
\Gamma
-symmetric
if the action of every
\pi\in\Gamma
on the input gates
\mathcal{X}
extends to an automorphism of the circuit:
For every input gate
g
, let
\ell(g)
denote its label. This is either a variable or a field element.
Field elements are fixed by every
\pi\in\Gamma
.
We say that a permutation
\pi\in\Gamma

extends to a circuit automorphism
of the circuit
C

if there exists a
\sigma\in\mathbf{Sym}(V(C))
such that
\ell(\sigma(g))=\pi(\ell(g))
for every input gate,
and such that
\sigma
is an automorphism of the multigraph structure of
C
(i.e. it maps edges to edges, non-edges to non-edges, and preserves the operation types of the gates).
For more details, see
[
21
]
.

A
\Gamma
-symmetric circuit is called

if it has no non-trivial circuit automorphism that fixes every input gate. This is equivalent to saying that for every
\pi\in\Gamma

C^{\prime}
that represents the same polynomial with
\lVert C^{\prime}\rVert\leq\lVert C\rVert
.

Proof.

For Boolean circuits, the transformation of a non-rigid into a rigid symmetric circuit is described in the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 7]
.
It is not hard to see that a similar transformation can be carried out for algebraic circuits, and that it satisfies
\lVert C^{\prime}\rVert\leq\lVert C\rVert
.
∎

Note that in a rigid
\Gamma
-symmetric circuit, we can identify
\Gamma
with the automorphism group of the circuit.
Another advantage of rigid circuits is
that we obtain a well-defined notion of
support
of gates, and
that there is a strong link between the size of these supports and the
orbit sizes of the gates.
In the following, we always assume that
\Gamma
is
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
or
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
.
In the former case, our variable set is
{\cal X}_{n}=\{x_{ij}\mid i,j\in[n]\}
, and
\pi\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
acts simultaneously on both indices, so
\pi(x_{ij})=x_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}
.
In the latter case, the variable set is
{\cal X}_{n,m}=\{x_{ij}\mid i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
. Then a pair of permutations
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
applied to
x_{ij}
yields
x_{\pi(i)\pi^{\prime}(j)}
.
However, the support of a subgroup
\Delta
of
\Gamma
is always defined
with respect to the action of
\Gamma
on
[n]
in the case of

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
and on
[n]\uplus[m]
in the case of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
.

Complexity measures for symmetric circuits

We study the complexity of symmetric polynomials in terms of properties of their symmetric circuits.
An important measure for symmetric circuits is
\maxOrb(C)
, the
maximum orbit size
of any gate in
C
, formally:

\maxOrb(C)\coloneqq\max_{g\in V(C)}|\{\sigma(g)\mid\sigma\in\Gamma\}|.

If
C
is rigid, and
\Gamma
and
C
are such that every stabiliser
group of a gate admits a unique minimal support, then we can also
speak about the
support size
of
C
. In particular, this is
possible if
\Gamma\in\{\mathbf{Sym}_{n},\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}\}
and all gates have a support of size
\leq n/2
, because then, Lemma
2.1
applies.
We write
\sup(g)\subseteq[n]\uplus[m]
to denote the
minimal support
of the group
\mathbf{Stab}_{\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}}(g)
in
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
, and as before,
\sup_{L}(g)
and
\sup_{R}(g)
are its restrictions to
[n]
and
[m]
, respectively. Similarly, in a
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric circuit,
\sup(g)\subseteq[n]
denotes the minimal support of the group
\mathbf{Stab}_{\mathbf{Sym}_{n}}(g)
.
The
support size
of a circuit
C
is then:

\maxSup(C)\coloneqq\max_{g\in V(C)}|\sup(g)|.

We have the following relations between
\maxOrb
and
\maxSup
.

Lemma 2.4

(restate=constantSupportOfGates,label=lem:constantSupportOfGates,name=)
.

Let
(\Gamma_{n,m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
be either the sequence
(\mathbf{Sym}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
or
(\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
(C_{n,m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\Gamma_{n,m}
-symmetric rigid circuits.

1.

If
\maxOrb(C_{n,m})
is polynomially bounded in
n+m
, then there exists a constant
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all large enough
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds
\maxSup(C_{n,m})\leq k
.

2.

If
\maxOrb(C_{n,m})
is at most
2^{o(n+m)}
, then
\maxSup(C_{n,m})\leq o(n+m)
.

Proof.

In the case
\Gamma_{n}=\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
, this is stated in
Theorems 6.2 and 6.3 in
[
21
]
.
One can check that for
\Gamma_{n}=\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
, the proofs of these theorems also go through. We refrain from spelling out all details but the core argument is this:
If the orbit size of a gate is polynomially bounded, then by the orbit-stabiliser theorem, the index of its stabiliser group in
\Gamma_{n}
is also polynomial, i.e. can be bounded by
\binom{n}{k}
, for some constant
k
.
Theorem 6.2 in
[
21
]
relies on Theorems 4.6 and 4.10 in
[
22
]
. The former of them states the following: If
\Delta\leq\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
is a subgroup of index at most
\binom{n}{k}
, then
\Delta
contains an alternating group
A
on
(n-k)
points. The proof of
[
22
, Theorem 4.10]
then shows that if
\Delta
is the stabiliser group of a gate in a circuit, then the gate is stabilised not only by
A
, but by the full symmetric group on the
n-k
many points.
This means that the remaining
k
points constitute a support for the gate. Now if
\Delta\leq\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
is the stabiliser group of a gate, then we can consider
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\leq\Delta
defined as the subgroups of
\Delta
consisting of those members of the product that have the neutral element in the first or second component, respectively.
Because the orbit size of the gate is polynomially bounded,
the index of
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
in
\mathbf{Sym}_{n},\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
,
respectively, is at most
\binom{n}{k}
(or
\binom{m}{k}
),
for some constant
k
. Then the same reasoning as before
applies (this is analogous to the argument made, in the context of
the alternating group, in
[
19
]
).
This finishes the first part of the theorem. The second part follows from the first part as shown in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 6.3]
, and this is independent of whether
\Gamma_{n}=\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
or
\Gamma_{n}=\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
.
∎

The converse of
Lemma

2.4
, part 1, is also true:

Lemma 2.5

(restate=constantSupportImpliesPolyOrbit,label=lem:constantSupportImpliesPolyOrbit,name=)
.

Let
(C_{n,m})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of rigid
\Gamma_{n,m}
-symmetric circuits, for
\Gamma\in\{\mathbf{Sym}_{n},\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}\}
. Assume that there is a constant
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\maxSup(C_{n,m})\leq k
, for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
\maxOrb(C_{n,m})\leq(n+m)^{k}
.

Proof.

We consider the case
\Gamma=\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
, the other case is similar.
Since
C\coloneqq C_{n,m}
is rigid, every
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
has a unique extension to a circuit automorphism
\sigma
.
Let
g\in V(C)
be a gate. Its stabiliser subgroup in
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
contains
\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}_{\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}}(\sup(g))
.
This means that for any two pairs
(\pi_{1},\pi_{1}^{\prime}),(\pi_{2},\pi_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
, it holds: If
(\pi_{1},\pi_{1}^{\prime})(s)=(\pi_{2},\pi_{2}^{\prime})(s)
for every
s\in\sup(g)
, and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathbf{Aut}(C_{n})
are the unique circuit automorphisms that
(\pi_{1},\pi_{1}^{\prime}),(\pi_{2},\pi_{2}^{\prime})
extend to, then
\sigma_{1}(g)=\sigma_{2}(g)
. Thus, if
\sigma_{1}(g)\neq\sigma_{2}(g)
, then also
(\pi_{1},\pi_{1}^{\prime})
and
(\pi_{2},\pi_{2}^{\prime})
must disagree on some
s\in\sup(g)
.
Therefore, the number of possible distinct images of
g
under the circuit automorphisms that the action of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
extends to is at most the number of different images of
\vec{\sup}(g)
, where this denotes
\sup(g)
written as a tuple in an arbitrary fixed order:

\displaystyle|\{\sigma(g)\mid\sigma\text{ is the unique circuit automorphism extending a }(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}\}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\{(\pi,\pi^{\prime})(\vec{\sup}(g))\mid(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}\}|\leq(n+m)^{k}.

The last inequality is due to the fact that
|\sup(g)|\leq k
, so the number of different
k
-tuples in
[n]\uplus[m]
is at most
(n+m)^{k}
.
Since
C
does not have other circuit automorphisms due to its rigidity, this is indeed an upper bound on the orbit size of the gate.
∎

If we are considering a sequence
(C_{n,m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\Gamma_{n,m}
-symmetric circuits, then we say they have
polynomially bounded orbit size
if there is a polynomial
p
such that asymptotically,
\maxOrb(C_{n,m})\leq p(n+m)
. Note that the overall size
\lVert C_{n,m}\rVert
may grow super-polynomially even if the orbit size is polynomially bounded. In that case, symmetry itself is not the reason why the circuits are large: They simply contain a super-polynomial number of distinct orbits.

Relationship between supports of children and parent gates

The children of a gate
g
can be partitioned into orbits with respect to the action of
\mathbf{Stab}(g)
because any permutation that induces a circuit automorphism fixing
g
must also fix the children of
g
setwise. We can also consider the orbit partition of children of
g
with respect to
\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))\leq\mathbf{Stab}(g)
, which may be finer.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
C
be a
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric circuit such that
n,m\geq 3\cdot\maxSup(C)
.
Let
g\in V(C)
an internal gate,
h
a child of
g
.
Let
O_{h}\coloneqq\text{$\mathchar 525$}_{\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))}(h)
and
S(h)\coloneqq\bigcap_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}\sup(h^{\prime})
.
Then
S(h)\subseteq\sup(g)
, and for every
h^{\prime}\in O_{h}
,
(\sup(h^{\prime})\setminus S(h))\cap\sup(g)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

For the first part, let
s\in S(h)
, so in particular,
s\in\sup(h)
. Assume for a contradiction that
s\notin\sup(g)
. Then let
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
be such that
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})(\sup(h)\setminus\sup(g))
does not contain
s
, and
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
fixes
\sup(g)
pointwise. Such a permutation exists because we are assuming
n
and
m
to be large enough compared to the support sizes. Then
h^{\prime}\coloneqq(\pi,\pi^{\prime})(h)\in O_{h}
but
s\notin\sup(h^{\prime})
, so
s\notin S(h)
. Contradiction.
For the second part, assume for a contradiction that there exists
s\in(\sup(h^{\prime})\setminus S(h))\cap\sup(g)
, for some
h^{\prime}\in O_{h}
. Since
s\in\sup(g)
, every permutation in
\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))
fixes
s
. So
s
must then be in
\sup(h^{\prime})
for every
h^{\prime}\in O_{h}
. So it is in
S(h)
. Contradiction. ∎

2.3
Graphs and Homomorphisms

A
simple graph
is a tuple
G=(V(G),E(G))
of a finite set
V(G)
and a set
E(G)\subseteq\binom{V(G)}{2}
.
A
multigraph
is a tuple
G=(V(G),E(G))
of a finite set
V(G)
and a multiset
E(G)
of elements in
\binom{V(G)}{2}
.
When a distinction is not necessary, we call both simple graphs and multigraphs
graphs
.
For a graph
G
, write
\lVert G\rVert\coloneqq\lvert V(G)\rvert+\lvert E(G)\rvert
.

A
tree decomposition
of a graph
F
is a pair
(T,\beta)
of a tree
T
and a map
\beta\colon V(T)\to 2^{V(F)}
such that the following conditions hold:

1.

\bigcup_{t\in V(T)}\beta(t)=V(F)
,

2.

for every edge
e\in E(F)
, there exists a node
t\in V(T)
such that
e\subseteq\beta(t)
,

3.

for evert vertex
v\in V(F)
, the subgraph of
T
induced by the vertices
t\in V(T)
such that
v\in\beta(t)
is connected.

The
width
of a tree decomposition
(T,\beta)
is
\max_{v\in V(T)}|\beta(v)|-1
.
The
treewidth

\tw(F)
of a graph
F
is the minimum width of a tree decomposition of
F
.

Let
F
and
G
be graphs.
A
homomorphism

h\colon F\to G
is a map
h\colon V(F)\to V(G)
such that
h(uv)\in E(G)
for all
uv\in E(F)
.
We write
\hom(F,G)
for the number of homomorphisms from
F
to
G
.

Homomorphism counts behave nicely with respect to graph operations.
For two graphs
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
, write
F_{1}+F_{2}
for their
disjoint union
, i.e.
V(F_{1}+F_{2})\coloneqq V(F_{1})\uplus V(F_{2})
and
E(F_{1}+F_{2})\coloneqq E(F_{1})\uplus E(F_{2})
.
For multiple graphs
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}
, we write
\coprod_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}
for
F_{1}+\dots+F_{n}
.
For two graphs
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, write
G_{1}\times G_{2}
for their
categorical product
, i.e.
V(G_{1}\times G_{2})\coloneqq V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
and
(v_{1},v_{2})
and
(w_{1},w_{2})
are adjacent in
G_{1}\times G_{2}
if, and only if,
v_{1}w_{1}\in E(G_{1})
and
v_{2}w_{2}\in E(G_{2})
.
The following identities hold for all graphs
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
,
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, and all connected graphs
K
, cf. e.g.
[
41
, (5.28)–(5.30)]
:

\displaystyle\hom(F_{1}+F_{2},G)

\displaystyle=\hom(F_{1},G)\hom(F_{2},G),

(1)

\displaystyle\hom(F,G_{1}\times G_{2})

\displaystyle=\hom(F,G_{1})\hom(F,G_{2}),\text{ and }

(2)

\displaystyle\hom(K,G_{1}+G_{2})

\displaystyle=\hom(K,G_{1})+\hom(K,G_{2}).

(3)

For graphs
G
and
H
, write
G\boxplus H
for the graph with vertex set
V(G\boxplus H)\coloneqq V(G)\uplus V(H)
and
E(G\boxplus H)\coloneqq E(G)\uplus E(H)\uplus\{vw\mid v\in V(G),w\in V(H)\}
.
For a graph
F
and
U\subseteq V(F)
,
write
F[U]
for the
subgraph induced by
U
and
F-U
for the subgraph obtained by deleting
U
, i.e.
V(F[U])\coloneqq U
,
E(F[U])\coloneqq E(F)\cap\binom{U}{2}
, and
F-U\coloneqq F[V(F)\setminus U]
.
For every graph
F
, it holds that

\hom(F,G\boxplus H)=\sum_{U\subseteq V(F)}\hom(F[U],G)\hom(F-U,H).

(4)

For graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, write
G_{1}\cdot G_{2}
for their
lexicographic product
, i.e.
V(G_{1}\cdot G_{2})\coloneqq V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
and
(v_{1},v_{2})
and
(w_{1},w_{2})
are adjacent in
G_{1}\cdot G_{2}
if, and only if,
v_{1}=w_{1}
and
v_{2}w_{2}\in E(G_{2})
, or
v_{1}w_{1}\in E(G_{1})
.
For a graph
F
and a partition
\mathcal{R}
of
V(F)
,
write
F/\mathcal{R}
for the graph with vertex set
\mathcal{R}
and
RS\in E(F/\mathcal{R})
if, and only if,
R\neq S
and there exist
r\in R
and
s\in S
such that
rs\in E(F)
.
By
[
56
, Theorem 16]
,
for every graph
F
,

\hom(F,G_{1}\cdot G_{2})=\sum_{\mathcal{R}\in\Gamma(F)}\hom(F/\mathcal{R},G_{1})\hom(\coprod_{R\in\mathcal{R}}F[R],G_{2}).

(5)

where
\Gamma(F)
denotes the set of all partitions
\mathcal{R}
of
V(F)
such that for all
R\in\mathcal{R}
the graph
F[R]
is connected.

2.4
Counting Logic and Bijective Games

Counting logic is first-order logic
augmented by counting quantifiers of the form
\exists^{\geq i}x
,
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
. A graph (or another finite structure)
G

satisfies a formula
\exists^{\geq i}x\varphi(x)
, written as
G\models\exists^{\geq i}x\varphi(x)
, if there exist at least
i
many
distinct
v\in V(G)
such that
G\models\varphi(v)
. The
k
-variable
fragment of counting logic is denoted
{\cal C}^{k}
.
More details on counting logic and references may be found
in
[
14
]
. For our purposes, the main interest is the
equivalence relation that this induces on graphs. Two structures
G,H

are called
{\cal C}^{k}
-equivalent
, denoted
G\equiv_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}H
, if
G
and
H
satisfy exactly the same
sentences of
{\cal C}^{k}
. This family of equivalence relations has many
characterisations, in combinatorics, logic, algebra and linear
optimisation. In particular, it is known that
G\equiv_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}H
if, and only if,
G
and
H
are not
distinguished by the
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman test for graph
isomorphism (see
[
6
]
).

For our purposes, a particularly useful characterisation is in terms
of
k
-pebble bijective games
, introduced by
Hella
[
34
]
. This is a game played by two players called

Spoiler
and
Duplicator
on the graphs
G
and
H
using
k

pairs of pebbles
(a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{k},b_{k})
. In a game position,
some (or all) of the pebbles
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
are placed on vertices
of
G
while the matching pebbles among
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
are
placed on vertices of
H
. Where it causes no confusion, we
do not distinguish notationally between the pebble
a_{i}
(or
b_{i}
)
and the vertex on which it is placed. In each move of the game, Spoiler
chooses a pair of pebbles
(a_{i},b_{i})
and Duplicator has to respond by
giving a bijection
f:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
which agrees with
the map
a_{j}\mapsto b_{j}
for all
j\neq i
. Spoiler chooses
a\in V(G)
and the pebbles
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are placed on
a
and
f(a)

respectively. If the resulting partial map from
G
to
H
given
by
a_{i}\mapsto b_{i}
is not a partial isomorphism, then Spoiler has
won the game. We say that Duplicator has a
winning
strategy
if, no matter how Spoiler plays, she can play forever
without losing. It is known that
G\equiv_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}H
if, and only if, Duplicator has a winning
strategy in the
k
-pebble bijective game played on
G
and
H
.

[
6
]
showed that for each
k
there
are graphs
G
and
H
with
O(k)
vertices which are not
isomorphic but such that
G\equiv_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}H
. The graphs

G
and
H
are constructed from a base graph
F
which is
sufficiently richly connected (in particular it has treewidth larger
than
k
) by replacing each vertex
v
of
F
by a small gadget whose
size depends on the degree of
v
. The construction is known as the

CFI
construction, and the gadgets as the

CFI
gadgets. There are many variations of the
construction in the literature, and we rely on the construction
from
[
49
]
, known as the

CFI
construction without internal
vertices (see also
[
51
]
). Details can be found in Appendix
C
.

3.1
Characterisation of symmetric circuit complexity by homomorphism polynomials

We start by observing that any symmetric polynomial is expressible as a linear combination of
homomorphism
or
subgraph polynomials
.
Let
F
be a bipartite multigraph with bipartition
A\uplus B
.
For integers
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
homomorphism polynomial
\hom_{F,n,m}\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}_{n,m}]
and the
subgraph polynomial
\sub_{F,n,m}\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}_{n,m}]
of
F
are defined as

\displaystyle\hom_{F,n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{h\colon A\uplus B\to[n]\uplus[m]}\prod_{ab\in E(F)}x_{h(a)h(b)},\quad\quad\sub_{F,n,m}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathbf{Aut}(F)|}\sum_{h\colon A\uplus B\hookrightarrow[n]\uplus[m]}\prod_{ab\in E(F)}x_{h(a)h(b)}.

Here, we think of
h
as mapping
A
to
[n]
and
B
to
[m]
.
For example,
\hom_{K_{2},n,m}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\sum_{w\in[m]}x_{vw}
and
\sub_{K_{n,m},n,m}=\prod_{v\in[n]}\prod_{w\in[m]}x_{vw}
.
If
A_{G}\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\times[m]}
is the bi-adjacency matrix of an undirected bipartite graph
G
with bipartition
V(G)=[n]\uplus[m]
, then
\hom_{F,n,m}(A_{G})
evaluates to the number of bipartition-respecting homomorphisms from
F
to
G
, and
\sub_{F,n,m}(A_{G})
is the number of occurrences of
F
as a subgraph in
G
. We also write
\hom_{F,n,m}(G)
and
\sub_{F,n,m}(G)
for the polynomials evaluated in the bi-adjacency matrix of
G
.
Let

\mathfrak{G}_{n,m}\coloneqq\left\{\sum\alpha_{i}\hom_{F_{i},n,m}\mid\text{bipartite multigraphs }F_{i},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}_{n,m}].

Then we have:

Lemma 3.1
.

For
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in\mathbb{Q}[\mathcal{X}_{n,m}]
,
the following are equivalent:

1.

the polynomial
p
is
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric,

2.

p\in\mathfrak{G}_{n,m}
,

3.

p=\sum\alpha_{i}\sub_{F_{i},n,m}
for some bipartite multigraphs
F_{i}
and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

For the proof,
Item

3
can be concluded from
Item

1
by partitioning the monomials in
p
into their orbits, and showing that each of these orbits is a subgraph polynomial for some pattern
F
. Via Möbius inversion (
Corollary

A.3
), subgraph polynomials can be expressed as linear combinations of homomorphism polynomials.

Thus, both subgraph and homomorphism polynomials can be seen as spanning sets for the vector space of matrix-symmetric polynomials.
We are now interested in the ones that admit polynomial size symmetric circuits.
For
k,n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, write

3.4
Symmetric complexity of the immanants

So far, we have studied
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric families. There are also polynomials in the variables

which are symmetric under the simultaneous action of

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{n}

We have chosen to develop our theory for
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric families but we believe that most of it can be
adapted to the
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric case. Then, the relevant
homomorphism or subgraph polynomials involve
directed

the immanants as a concrete
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric example and show that
their symmetric circuit complexity is subject to a dichotomy similar
to what is known for their computational complexity

7
]

Immanants
are families of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric polynomials

(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that are defined via so-called

of the symmetric group. An irreducible character of

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
is a homomorphism
f
from
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
to the multiplicative group

which is constant on the conjugacy classes

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}

\imm_{f}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}}f(\pi)\prod_{i\in[n]}x_{i,\pi(i)}.

If
f
is constantly

, then
\imm_{f}
is the permanent. The
determinant is obtained by letting
f=\sgn
. While all immanents are

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric, the symmetry groups of particular members of this
family may be larger. In particular, the permanent is, in fact,

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
while the determinant is symmetric under a
subgroup of this of index
2
consisting of those pairs of
permutations
(\pi,\sigma)
for which
\sgn(\pi)=\sgn(\sigma)
. The

\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
symmetric circuit complexity of the permanent and the determinant was
studied in
[
21
]
and for the larger symmetry
groups in
[
19
]
.

In
[
7
]
, Curticapean shows a complexity dichotomy for the immanants: In the tractable case,
\imm_{f}
is in
\mathsf{VP}
(i.e. admits polynomial size algebraic circuits) and is computable in polynomial time. In the intractable case, it is not in
\mathsf{VP}
, unless
\mathsf{VFPT}=\mathsf{VW}[1]
, and also, it is not computable in polynomial time, unless
\mathsf{FPT}=\#\mathsf{W}[1]
. The complexity is controlled by a certain parameter of
f

We show that the same parameter of
f
produces the analogous
dichotomy with regards to the complexity of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric
algebraic circuits for the immanants, but without the need for
complexity-theoretic assumptions for the hardness part. This
generalizes the resuls of
[
21
]
.
The irreducible characters

correspond naturally to partitions

[n]
(see, for example,
[
53

\lambda

\lambda

Let

A family

(restate=immanantDichotomy, label=thm:immanantDichotomy,name=)

4.1
Homomorphism Polynomials of Labelled Graphs

The main tool for proving
are homomorphism polynomials of labelled graphs.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\ell,r\in\mathbb{N}
.
An
(\ell,r)
-labelled bipartite graph

is a tuple of a bipartite multigraph
F

\ell

Write

for the class of all
(\ell,r)
-labelled bipartite graphs.

For

Let

Operations on Labelled Graphs and Homomorphism Polynomial Maps

Fix

4.2.1
Swapping Left and Right Labels

We first note that the left and right labels can be interchanged.
In order to simplify notation, we subsequently focus on operations involving only the left labels.
The following purely syntactic lemma justifies this approach.
To that end,
define for
\boldsymbol{F}=(F,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)

the

-labelled bipartite graph

Analogously, for
\varphi=\sum\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{F}^{i}_{n,m}\in\mathfrak{G}_{n,m}(\ell,r)

define
\varphi^{*}\coloneqq\sum\alpha_{i}(\boldsymbol{F}^{i})^{*}_{m,n}\in\mathfrak{G}_{m,n}(r,\ell)

The following
is then immediate.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)

and
\varphi\in\mathfrak{G}_{n,m}(\ell,r)

1.

If
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathcal{T}^{k}(\ell,r)

then
\boldsymbol{F}^{*}\in\mathcal{T}^{k}(r,\ell)
.

2.

For
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and

\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}

\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{F}^{*}_{m,n}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})
.

3.

If
\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell,r)

\varphi^{*}\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{m,n}(r,\ell)
.

4.2.3
Disjoint Union and Tensor Products

The
disjoint union
of labelled graphs is defined as follows:
For elements
\boldsymbol{F}=(F,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)

\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r+r^{\prime}}

We use
\otimes
to denote disjoint unions in order to be aligned with the notation of
[
43
,
55

for homomorphism tensors where the disjoint union of labelled graphs corresponds to the Kronecker product of their homomorphism tensors.

Lemma 4.10
.

For
\ell,\ell^{\prime},r,r^{\prime},k,k^{\prime},n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)

\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell^{\prime},r^{\prime})

1.

If

and
\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{k^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime},r^{\prime})

\boldsymbol{F}\otimes\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{k^{\prime\prime}}(\ell+\ell^{\prime},r+r^{\prime})

2.

For
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r+r^{\prime}}

If
\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}(\ell,r)
and
\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{T}^{k^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime},r^{\prime})

Proof.

For the first assertion, note that a tree decomposition for

and

4.2.4
Gluing and Point-wise Products

The final elementary operation is gluing.
For elements
\boldsymbol{F}=(F,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}=(F^{\prime},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)
,
define
\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)

If

(\varphi\odot\varphi^{\prime})(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq\varphi(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\varphi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\varphi\varphi^{\prime}

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)

Let
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell,r)

If
\boldsymbol{F},\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{k}(\ell,r)
,

\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{k}(\ell,r)
.

2.

For
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
,

(\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime})_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}).

3.

The point-wise product
\varphi\odot\varphi^{\prime}
of
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
is in
\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell,r)
.

Proof.

For a detailed proof, see
[

[

Intuitively, for the first claim, if

and

and

The bags of the new decomposition are induced by

Hence, it remains to show that

\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\vdash n}\mu_{\pi}(\Pi^{\pi}_{i}\boldsymbol{F})_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})

for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
.
Note that, crucially, the coefficients in the above expression do not depend on
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
.

The left hand-side expression can be rewritten as follows noting that, for every bijection
h\colon[n]\hookrightarrow[n]
, it is

\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})=\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/h(v)],\boldsymbol{w})

by commutativity of the product.
The second equality follows from
Lemma

A.2
,
the third from
Equation

7
.

\displaystyle\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{h\colon[n]\hookrightarrow[n]}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/h(v)],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\vdash n}\mu_{\pi}\sum_{h\colon[n]/\pi\to[n]}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/(h\circ\pi)(v)],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\vdash n}\mu_{\pi}(\Pi^{\pi}_{i}\boldsymbol{F})_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w}).\qed

Now we consider the general case: applying the product operator to a linear combination of labelled homomorphism polynomials.
In preparation, we note the following
Fact

4.16
.

Fact 4.16
.

Let
\Gamma
denote a finite group acting on a finite set
X
.
For an element
x\in X
, write
\beta_{x}
for the number of pairs
(z,\gamma)\in X\times\Gamma
such that
\gamma(z)=x
.
If
x,y\in X
are in the same orbit under
\Gamma
,
then
\beta_{x}=\beta_{y}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\iota(x)=y
for some
\iota\in\Gamma
.
Then the map
(z,\gamma)\mapsto(z,\iota\gamma)
sends a pair
(z,\gamma)
such that
\gamma(z)=x
to the pair
(z,\iota\gamma)
satisfying
\iota\gamma(z)=\iota(x)=y
.
This map is bijective.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.13
.

Write
\varphi=\sum_{t\in T}\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{F}^{t}_{n,m}
for some finite set
T
,
coefficients
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{t}\in\mathcal{T}^{k}(\ell,r)
.
The symmetric group
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
acts on the set of functions
f\colon[n]\to T
by composition.
The orbits of this action are in bijection with maps
\lambda\colon T\to\{0,\dots,n\}
such that
\sum_{t\in T}\lambda(t)=n
.
Write
\Lambda
for the set of all such functions.
A function
f\colon[n]\to T
belongs to the orbit
O_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\Lambda
if
|f^{-1}(t)|=\lambda(t)
for all
t\in T
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
.
First, product and sum are interchanged. The resulting sum over all functions
f\colon[n]\to T
is then grouped by orbits:

\displaystyle(\Pi_{i}\varphi)(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in[n]}\sum_{t\in T}\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{t}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\colon[n]\to T}\prod_{v\in[n]}\alpha_{f(v)}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{f(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sum_{f\in O_{\lambda}}\prod_{v\in[n]}\alpha_{f(v)}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{f(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}\sum_{f\in O_{\lambda}}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{f(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w}).

where
\alpha_{\lambda}\coloneqq\prod_{t\in T}\alpha_{t}^{\lambda(t)}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

From now on, we consider each orbit
\lambda\in\Lambda
separately.
By
Fact

4.16
, there exists a positive number
\beta_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
f\in O_{\lambda}
, the number of pairs
g\colon[n]\to T
and
h\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
such that
g\circ h=f
is
\beta_{\lambda}
. In any such pair, we necessarily have
g\in O_{\lambda}
.
Towards applying Möbius inversion, we denote permutations
h\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
as injective maps
[n]\hookrightarrow[n]
.

\displaystyle\beta_{\lambda}\sum_{f\in O_{\lambda}}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{f(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in O_{\lambda}}\sum_{h\colon[n]\hookrightarrow[n]}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{(g\circ h)(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in O_{\lambda}}\sum_{h\colon[n]\hookrightarrow[n]}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{g(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/h(v)],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in O_{\lambda}}\sum_{\pi\vdash n}\mu_{\pi}\sum_{h\colon[n]/\pi\to[n]}\prod_{v\in[n]}\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}^{g(v)}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/(h\circ\pi)(v)],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in O_{\lambda}}\sum_{\pi\vdash n}\mu_{\pi}(\Pi^{\pi}_{i}(\boldsymbol{F}^{g(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{F}^{g(n)}))_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})

Here, the first equality follows from
Fact

4.16
.
The second equality is obtained by first replacing the product over
v\in[n]
by a product over
h(v)
for
v\in[n]
and then replacing the sum over
h\colon[n]\hookrightarrow[n]
by a sum over
h^{-1}
for
h\colon[n]\hookrightarrow[n]
.
The third equality follows from
Lemma

A.2
.
The final equality is implied by
Lemma

4.14
.

Combining the identities above, we obtain

(\Pi_{i}\varphi)(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\frac{\alpha_{\lambda}}{\beta_{\lambda}}\sum_{g\in O_{\lambda}}\sum_{\pi\vdash n}\mu_{\pi}\Pi_{i}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{F}^{g(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{F}^{g(n)})_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w}).

(8)

Note that neither the
\alpha_{\lambda}
nor
\beta_{\lambda}
depend on
\boldsymbol{v}
.
Hence,
\Pi_{i}\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)
.
∎

Remark 4.17
.

Theorem

4.13
indeed generalises
Lemma

4.15
.
If
T=\{\boldsymbol{F}\}
is a singleton and there are no coefficients,
then
\Lambda
consists of a single map
\lambda
.
Then,
\alpha_{\lambda}=1
and
\beta_{\lambda}=n!
.
The orbit

contains only one map
g
and
\Pi_{i}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{F}^{g(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{F}^{g(n)})=\Pi_{i}^{\pi}\boldsymbol{F}
.

4.2.7
Restricted Products

Finally, we derive a corollary of
Theorem

4.13
concerned with the restricted product operator
\Pi_{i,J}
which is defined as follows:
Let
i\in[\ell]
and
J\subseteq[\ell]\setminus\{i\}
.
For
\varphi\in\mathfrak{G}_{n,m}(\ell,r)
,
define
\Pi_{i,J}\varphi\in\mathfrak{G}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)

via

(\Pi_{i,J}\varphi)(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})\coloneqq\prod_{v\in[n]\setminus\{v_{j}\mid j\in J\}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w}).

for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
.
Note that
\Pi_{i}\varphi=\Pi_{i,\emptyset}\varphi
.

Corollary 4.18
.

Suppose that
\ell+r\leq k
.
For
\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell,r)
,
i\in[\ell]
and
J\subseteq[\ell]\setminus\{i\}
,

\Pi_{i,J}(\varphi)\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)
.

Proof.

For
J\subseteq[\ell]
, write
V_{J}\coloneqq[n]\setminus\{v_{j}\mid j\in J\}
.
The proof is by induction on
|J|
.
The base case
|J|=0
is established in
Theorem

4.13
.

For the inductive step, let
j\in J
and write
J^{\prime}\coloneqq J\setminus\{j\}
.
We first distinguish cases based on the form of the tuple
\boldsymbol{v}
.
This is a priori problematic since the desired expression is required not to depend on
\boldsymbol{v}
.
We overcome this problem by implementing the case distinction itself using homomorphism polynomial maps.

Write
\varphi=\sum_{t\in T}\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{F}^{t}
and
suppose first that
v_{j}\not\in\{v_{j^{\prime}}\mid j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}\}
, i.e.
V_{J^{\prime}}=V_{J}\uplus\{v_{j}\}
.
Recall the definition of
\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}\in\mathcal{T}^{k}(\ell,r)
from the proof of
Lemma

4.12
.
We rewrite
\Pi_{i,J}(\varphi)
in terms of expressions to which the inductive hypothesis applies by considering the following polynomial:

\displaystyle\prod_{v\in V_{J^{\prime}}}\left(\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})+\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v_{j}],\boldsymbol{w})+\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v_{j}],\boldsymbol{w})\right)\prod_{v\in V_{J}}\left(\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})+\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:diell}}{=}\left(\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v_{j}],\boldsymbol{w})+1\right)\prod_{v\in V_{J}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in V_{J^{\prime}}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})+\prod_{v\in V_{J}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w}).

If otherwise
v_{j}\in\{v_{j^{\prime}}\mid j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}\}
,
then the expression for
J
equals the expression for
J^{\prime}
.
Formally,

\prod_{v\in V_{J^{\prime}}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})=\prod_{v\in V_{J}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w}).

In order to combine the two cases considered above,
observe that, for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
, by
Lemma

4.9
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}(\Sigma_{i}\boldsymbol{D}^{j^{\prime},j})_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{D}^{j^{\prime},j}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{D}^{j^{\prime},j}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

is equal to the number of indices
j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}
such that
v_{j}=v_{j^{\prime}}
.
This is a value from
\{0,\dots,\ell\}
.
Here, it is crucial that
i\not\in J
, as assumed.
Let
p\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
denote a polynomial such that
p(0)=0
and
p(1)=p(2)=\dots=p(\ell)=1
.
By
Lemmas

4.9
and
4.11
,

\psi\coloneqq p(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}(\Sigma_{i}\boldsymbol{D}^{j^{\prime},j})_{n,m})\in\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{k}(\ell-1,r)
.
Then

\psi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})=\begin{cases}1,&v_{j}\in\{v_{j^{\prime}}\mid j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}\},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Combining the above,
it follows that the expression
\prod_{v\in V_{J}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})
of interest is equal to

\displaystyle\psi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w})\prod_{v\in V_{J^{\prime}}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle+(1-\psi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/],\boldsymbol{w}))\left(\prod_{v\in V_{J^{\prime}}}\left(\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})+\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})\right)-\prod_{v\in V_{J^{\prime}}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{v}[i/v],\boldsymbol{w})\right).

This expression depends uniformly on
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
.
In other words,

\displaystyle\Pi_{i,J}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\psi\ \Pi_{i,J^{\prime}}(\varphi)+(1-\psi)\left(\Pi_{i,J^{\prime}}(\varphi+\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}_{n,m})-\Pi_{i,J^{\prime}}(\varphi)\right).

Here, we regard
1
as the element of
\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)
which is uniformly
1
, cf.
Example

4.7
.
The induction hypothesis applies to
\Pi_{i,J^{\prime}}
.
Hence,
\Pi_{i,J^{\prime}}(\varphi),\Pi_{i,J^{\prime}}(\varphi+\boldsymbol{D}^{i,j}_{n,m})\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)
.
By
Lemma

4.11
,
\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)
is closed under pointwise products.
Hence,
\Pi_{i,J}(\varphi)\in\mathfrak{T}^{k}_{n,m}(\ell-1,r)
.
∎

4.3
Homomorphism Polynomials from Symmetric Circuits

In this section we prove the direction
2

\Rightarrow

1
of
using the results we have established so far. For the proof, we start with a rigid symmetric circuit
C
with
\maxSup(C)\leq k
, and show via induction from the inputs to the output that at each gate
g
, the computed polynomial is in
\mathfrak{T}^{2k}_{n,m}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))
.
Recall that
\vec{\sup}_{L}(g)
and
\vec{\sup}_{R}(g)
denote ordered tuples whose entries are the elements of
\sup_{L}(g)
and
\sup_{R}(g)
(see
Section

2.1
). The ordering of the tuples represents the assignment of the labels to the support elements:
The label
i\in[\ell]
is mapped to the
i
-th entry of
\vec{\sup}_{L}(g)
, and analogously for
\vec{\sup}_{R}(g)
.

For the inductive step of the proof, we assume that
g
is a multiplication or summation gate such that every child
h
of
g
computes a polynomial in
\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(h),\vec{\sup}_{R}(h))
. Then we show that the polynomial computed at
g
is in
\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))
. In the following, if
g
is a gate, then
p_{g}
denotes the polynomial that this gate outputs.

Observation 4.19
.

Let
n,m,\ell,r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathcal{G}(\ell,r)
and let
(\pi,\sigma)\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
.
Then
(\pi,\sigma)(\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}))=\boldsymbol{F}_{n,m}(\pi(\boldsymbol{v}),\sigma(\boldsymbol{w}))
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in[n]^{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in[m]^{r}
.

Proof.

For
\boldsymbol{F}=(F,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})
,
we have
(\pi,\sigma)(\{h\colon A\uplus B\to[n]\uplus[m]\mid h(\boldsymbol{a})=\boldsymbol{v},h(\boldsymbol{b})=\boldsymbol{w}\})=\{h\colon A\uplus B\to[n]\uplus[m]\mid h(\boldsymbol{a})=\pi(\boldsymbol{v}),h(\boldsymbol{b})=\sigma(\boldsymbol{v})\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
C
be a rigid
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric circuit and
g\in V(C)
a gate such that the polynomial computed at
g
is of the form
p_{g}=\varphi(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))
for some
\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}^{2k}_{n,m}(\ell,r)
.
Let
g^{\prime}\in\text{$\mathchar 525$}_{\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}}(g)
. Then the polynomial computed by
g^{\prime}
is
p_{g^{\prime}}=\varphi(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g^{\prime}),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g^{\prime}))
.

Proof.

Let
(\pi,\sigma)\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
such that
(\pi,\sigma)(g)=g^{\prime}
. So the polynomial computed by
g^{\prime}
is
(\pi,\sigma)(p)
.
Then the lemma follows from
Observation

4.19
.
∎

The next lemma shows the inductive step in case that the children of the gate
g
form a single orbit.

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
C
be a rigid
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric circuit, and
g\in V(C)
a multiplication or a summation gate with
|\sup(g)|\leq k
.
Assume that for every
h\in\child(g)
, it also holds
that
|\sup(h)|\leq k
and the polynomial computed at
h
is
p_{h}=\varphi(\vec{\sup}_{L}(h),\vec{\sup}_{R}(h))
for some
\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}^{2k}_{n,m}(|\vec{\sup}_{L}(h)|,|\vec{\sup}_{R}(h)|)
.
Let
h\in\child(g)
and let
O_{h}\coloneqq\text{$\mathchar 525$}_{\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))}(h)
.

Then
\prod_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}
and
\sum_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}

\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))

Proof.

As in
Lemma

2.6
, let
S(h)=\bigcap_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}\sup(h^{\prime})
, and
S_{L}(h)=S(h)\cap[n]
,
S_{R}(h)=S(h)\cap[m]
.
Note that for every
h^{\prime}\in O_{h}

\vec{\sup}_{L}(h^{\prime})
and
\vec{\sup}_{R}(h^{\prime})

in the same positions because

by Lemma
2.6
. Since all these gates are in the same
\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))
-orbit, the positions of
S(h)
in
\vec{\sup}_{L}(h^{\prime})
and
\vec{\sup}_{R}(h^{\prime})
are the same. In the following, when we write
\vec{S}_{L}(h)
and
\vec{S}_{R}(h)
, we refer to the ordering of
S_{L}(h)
and
S_{R}(h^{\prime})
as in
\vec{\sup}_{L}(h^{\prime})
and
\vec{\sup}_{R}(h^{\prime})

For simplicity, assume in the following that the first
|S_{L}(h)|
positions in each tuple
\vec{\sup}_{L}(h^{\prime})
are occupied by the elements of
S_{L}(h)
and the first
|S_{R}(h)|

\vec{\sup}_{R}(h^{\prime})
are occupied by
S_{R}(h)
.
Since
\text{$\mathchar 525$}_{\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))}(h)
is a subset of
\text{$\mathchar 525$}_{\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}}(h)

Lemma

4.20
, all these gates compute the same instantiated homomorphism polynomial map, just with different instantiation of the labels.
Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(\ell,r)
denote this homomorphism polynomial map, where
\ell=|\sup_{L}(h)|,r=|\sup_{R}(h)|
.
Recall the definition of
\boldsymbol{J}
from
Example

4.7
.
First consider
\prod_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}
.

\displaystyle\prod_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}\varphi(\vec{\sup}_{L}(h^{\prime}),\vec{\sup}_{R}(h^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{\boldsymbol{v}\in([n]\setminus\sup_{L}(g))^{\ell-|S_{L}(h)|}}\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in([m]\setminus\sup_{R}(g))^{r-|S_{R}(h)|}}\varphi(\vec{S}_{L}(h)\boldsymbol{v},\vec{S}_{R}(h)\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\boldsymbol{v}\in([n]\setminus\sup_{L}(g))^{\ell-|S_{L}(h)|}}\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in([m]\setminus\sup_{R}(g))^{r-|S_{R}(h)|}}\varphi(\vec{S}_{L}(h)\boldsymbol{v},\vec{S}_{R}(h)\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{J}_{n,m}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g)\setminus\vec{S}_{L}(h),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g)\setminus\vec{S}_{R}(h))

\boldsymbol{J}_{n,m}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g)\setminus\vec{S}_{L}(h),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g)\setminus\vec{S}_{R}(h))=1

4.10

Analogously,

(\varphi\otimes\boldsymbol{J})\in\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(|\sup_{L}(g)|+|\sup_{L}(h)\setminus\sup_{L}(g)|,|\sup_{R}(g)|+|\sup_{R}(h)\setminus\sup_{R}(g)|)

\prod_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{2k}_{n,m}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))

If

\sum_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{2k}_{n,m}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))

We now generalise the above lemma so that it covers all orbits of children of

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
C
be a rigid
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric circuit.
Let
g\in V(C)
be a multiplication or summation gate with
|\sup(g)|\leq k
.
Assume that for every
h\in\child(g)
, the polynomial

is in
\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(h),\vec{\sup}_{R}(h))
.
Then the polynomial computed at
g
is in
\mathfrak{T}_{n,m}^{2k}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))
.

Proof.

We treat the case when
g
is a multiplication gate in detail.
The other case is analogous.
Partition the set
\child(g)
into orbits under
\mathbf{Stab}^{\bullet}(\sup(g))
.
To each such orbit
O_{h}
, we apply
Lemma

4.21
,
which shows that
p_{O_{h}}\coloneqq\prod_{h^{\prime}\in O_{h}}p_{h^{\prime}}\in\mathfrak{T}^{2k}_{n,m}(\vec{\sup}_{L}(g),\vec{\sup}_{R}(g))

8
Symmetric complexity of the immanants

The permanent and determinant are the extreme intractable and tractable case of
immanant
polynomials. Immanants are families of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric polynomials
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that are typically defined via so-called
irreducible characters
of the symmetric group. An irreducible character
f\colon\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is a class function, i.e. it only depends on the multiset of cycle lengths of the input permutation. Given such an
f
, the corresponding immanant is defined as

\imm_{f}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}}f(\pi)\prod_{i\in[n]}x_{i,\pi(i)}

We view these as polynomials over the field
\mathbb{C}
.
If
f
is constantly
1
, then
\imm_{f}
is the permanent. The determinant is obtained by letting
f=\sgn
.

Note that while the permanent is
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric, this is not true for every immanant
(indeed, not even of the determinant).
But for every
f
,
\imm_{f}
is
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric because for any
\sigma\in\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
, the monomial
\prod_{i\in[n]}x_{\sigma(i),\sigma(\pi(i))}
encodes a permutation from the same conjugacy class as
\pi
, so it has the same value under
f
. Therefore, we study the complexity of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric algebraic circuits for
\imm_{f}
.
The
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
-symmetric polynomials that we have mostly been working with naturally expressed properties of weighted undirected bipartite graphs; now,
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric polynomials express properties of weighted
directed
(not necessarily bipartite) graphs (see also
Remark

4.1
).

In
[
7
]
, Curticapean shows a complexity dichotomy for the immanants: In the tractable case,
\imm_{f}
is in
\mathsf{VP}
(i.e. admits polynomial size algebraic circuits) and is computable in polynomial time. In the intractable case, the family of polynomials is not in
\mathsf{VP}
, unless
\mathsf{VFPT}=\mathsf{VW}[1]
, and also, it is not computable in polynomial time, unless
\mathsf{FPT}=\#\mathsf{W}[1]
. The complexity is controlled by a certain parameter of
f
.

We show that the same parameter of
f
produces the analogous dichotomy with regards to the complexity of
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric algebraic circuits for the immanants. The benefit of considering symmetric circuits is that the lower bound is not conditional on any complexity-theoretic assumptions.
We now introduce the relevant parameter of
f
. As explained in
[
7
]
, the irreducible characters
f
correspond naturally to partitions of
[n]
. If
\lambda
is a partition of
[n]
, we denote by
\chi^{\lambda}\colon\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
its corresponding irreducible character, and we also write
\imm_{\lambda}
for
\imm_{\chi^{\lambda}}
. Partitions of
[n]
are denoted as tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
, where each entry
k_{i}
denotes a part of size
k_{i}
.
For example, the permanent is
\imm_{(n)}
, and the determinant is
\imm_{(1,\dots,1)}
. For other partitions, the rule for computing their corresponding irreducible characters is more involved and not needed here (for details, see
[
7
]
).
If
\lambda
is a partition of
[n]
, let
b(\lambda)=n-s
, where
s
denotes the number of parts.

Formally,
[
7
]
considers a family
\Lambda
of partitions. It is said that
\Lambda

supports growth

g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a partition
\lambda^{(n)}
in
\Lambda
with
b(\lambda^{(n)})\geq g(n)
and size
\Theta(n)
. According to the dichotomy from
[
7
]
,
(\imm_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is tractable if there is a constant that bounds
b(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Otherwise, if
\Lambda
supports growth
\omega(1)
, then the immanant family is (conditionally) intractable. If
\Lambda
even supports growth
\omega(n^{k})
, then
(\imm_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is
\mathsf{VNP}
-complete.

Our analogous result for symmetric circuits is the following.

Lemma 8.3

For every

It can be checked that after every construction step of

The second step is to add a set

9
Conclusion

In recent years, a rich theory of symmetric computation has been emerging which has established a tight and surprising relationship between logical definability, circuit complexity and also computation in other models such as linear programming (see
[
13
,
11
]
for pointers). A central plank of this is the use of variations of the Cai-Fürer-Immerman construction to establish unconditional lower bounds in the context of models of computation with natural symmetries. The work in
[
20
,
21
]
is a significant example of this, showing an unconditional exponential separation between the complexity of the determinant and the permanent polynomials in the context of symmetric algebraic circuits.

The present work gives a sweeping generalisation of these results in two different directions. First, considering matrix polynomials symmetric under arbitrary permutations of the rows and columns (i.e. the
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
action), we give a complete characterisation of the tractable cases in terms of homomorphism polynomials of bounded treewidth, linking this study to other complexity classifications such as
[
9
]
. For polynomials on
square
matrices symmetric under the simultaneous application of a permutation to the rows and columns (i.e. the
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
action), we generalise the separation of the determinant and permanent by giving a complete classification of the symmetric complexity of all immanants.

The work raises a number of directions for future research. It suggests that there is a close connection between the counting width of polynomials, which we define, and their symmetric algebraic complexity. We have not established this in full generality and thus the first important direction is to prove or disprove
. A step in this direction would be to understand the complexity of linear combinations of homomorphism polynomials where neither
Theorem

6.9
nor
Theorem

6.1
applies.
That is to say, there is more than one homomorphism polynomial involved and the size of the pattern graphs is not sublinearly bounded.

Our most general results are for matrix polynomials symmetric under
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{m}
, where we have a complete characterisation of tractable families. The polynomials on square matrices symmetric under the
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
action include many that are not
\mathbf{Sym}_{n}\times\mathbf{Sym}_{n}
-symmetric (for instance, the determinant and many other immanants). While we give a complete classification of the immanant polynomials, there are many other such polynomial families. It seems plausible that a complete classification in terms of homomorphism polynomials from directed graphs might be achievable in this case, and we leave it for future work.

It would be interesting to relate this to other methods for
establishing lower bounds in algebraic complexity and to natural
algorithmic methods in that field. Indeed, one of the striking
features of the study of symmetric computation models is that a
surprising range of algorithms that are used (for instance in
combinatorial optimisation) are indeed symmetric and thus the
unconditional lower bounds show the limitations of standard
techniques.

Finally, this work may provide the means to prove lower bounds for
symmetric circuits for interesting polynomial families beyond the
determinant and the permanent. One potential application would be in
proof complexity. There has been considerable recent interest in the

Ideal Proof System

[
29
]
, in which refutations of unsatisfiable Boolean formulas or systems of polynomial equations take the
form of algebraic circuits. One could conceivably use the methods
developed here to show that certain families of formulas with natural
symmetries do not admit succinct symmetric refutations.

\hom(F,G_{0})\geq\hom(F,G_{1})

with strict inequality if, and only if, there exists a bipartition-respecting weak oddomorphism
F\to G
.

Note that the identity map
G\to G
is a bipartition-respecting weak oddomorphism. Hence,
\hom(G,G_{0})>\hom(G,G_{1})
.

Proof.

For a homomorphism
\psi\colon F\to G
, write
\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{i})
for the number of homomorphisms
h\colon F\to G_{i}
such that
\rho h=\psi
.
Note that

\hom(F,G_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\colon F\to G\\
\psi\text{ respects bipartitions}\end{subarray}}\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{i}).

By
[
49
, Theorem 3.6]
,

\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{0})\geq\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{1})
for every homomorphism
\psi\colon F\to G
.
This implies that
\hom(F,G_{0})\geq\hom(F,G_{1})
where
F
,
G_{0}
, and
G_{i}
are regarded as graphs with fixed bipartition.

It holds that
\hom(F,G_{0})>\hom(F,G_{1})
if, and only if, there exists a bipartition-respecting homomorphism
\psi\colon F\to G
such that
\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{0})>\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{1})
.
By the proof of
[

\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{0})>\hom_{\psi}(F,G_{1})
if, and only if,
\psi

∎

Corollary C.8
.

For every connected simple bipartite graph
G

\emb(G,G_{0})>\emb(G,G_{1})
.

Proof.

Write
A\uplus B
for the fixed bipartition of

By
Corollary

, for

\emb(G,G_{i})=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(A)}\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi(B)}\mu_{\pi}\mu_{\sigma}\hom(G/(\pi,\sigma),G_{i}).

Here,

can be assumed to be simple by discarding possible multiedges.
The number of vertices in

is
|A/\pi|
on the left side and
|B/\sigma|
on the right side.
By
Corollaries

C.7
and

\displaystyle\emb(G,G_{0})-\emb(G,G_{1})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\bot}\sigma_{\bot}\left(\hom(G/(\bot,\bot),G_{0})-\hom(G/(\bot,\bot),G_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\hom(G,G_{0})-\hom(G,G_{1})

where
\bot
denotes the discrete partitions of

.
∎

Appendix D
Hereditary Treewidth, Vertex Cover Number, and Matching Number

In this section, we show that the three graph parameters hereditary treewidth, vertex cover number, and matching number are functionally equivalent.
That is, we prove
Lemma

7.4
.

A
matching
of
F
is a set
M\subseteq E(F)
such that for all distinct
e_{1},e_{2}\in M
it holds that
e_{1}\cap e_{2}=\emptyset
.
Define the
matching number

\mn(F)
of
F
as the size of the largest matching in
F
.

Theorem D.1
.

For every graph
F
,

\frac{1}{2}\hdtw(F)\leq\mn(F)\leq\binom{\hdtw(F)+2}{2}
.

We first consider the upper bound:

Lemma D.2
.

For every graph
F
,

\mn(F)\leq\binom{\hdtw(F)+2}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
F
be a graph.
By
[
9
, Fact 3.4]
,
if
F^{\prime}
is a graph with at most
\mn(F)
edges and no isolated vertices then
\tw(F^{\prime})\leq\hdtw(F)
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\binom{k+2}{2}\geq\mn(F)\geq\binom{k+1}{2}
.
Then
F^{\prime}
can be taken to be the
(k+1)
-clique, which has treewidth
k
.
Hence,
\hdtw(F)\geq k
.
Thus,
\binom{\hdtw(F)+2}{2}\geq\binom{k+2}{2}\geq\mn(F)
.
∎

By
[
9
, 220]
, one gets an asymptotic linear upper bound on
\mn(F)
in terms of
\hdtw(F)
.

Lemma D.3
.

For every graph
F
,

\hdtw(F)\leq 2\mn(F)
.

Proof.

We first show that
\tw(F)\leq 2\mn(F)
by constructing a tree decomposition of width
\mn(F)
from a maximum matching.

Let
M
be a maximum matching in
F
, i.e. a matching of cardinality
\mn(F)
.
Write
V\coloneqq\bigcup_{e\in M}e
for the set of vertices incident to matched edges.
Every edge
e\in V(F)
is incident to at least one vertex in
V
.
Let
W\coloneqq V(F)\setminus V
denote the set of vertices not incident to any matched edge.

Define a tree decomposition
\beta
over the star graph with centre
x
and tips indexed by
w\in W
as follows.
Let
\beta(x)\coloneqq V
and
\beta(w)\coloneqq\{w\}\cup V
.
Since no two vertices in
W
are adjacent, this is a valid tree decomposition of width
2\mn(F)
.

Now consider a surjective homomorphism
h\colon F\to F^{\prime}
.
Let
V^{\prime}\coloneqq h(V)\subseteq V(F^{\prime})
and write
W^{\prime}\coloneqq h(W)\setminus V\subseteq V(F^{\prime})
.
Define a tree decomposition
\beta^{\prime}
for
F^{\prime}
over the star graph with centre
x^{\prime}
and tips indexed by
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
.
Let
\beta^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\coloneqq V^{\prime}
and
\beta(w^{\prime})\coloneqq\{w^{\prime}\}\cup V^{\prime}
.
Every bag is of size at most
2\mn(F)+1
and the bags cover the entire graph
F^{\prime}
as
h
is an edge- and vertex-surjective homomorphism.
It is a valid decomposition because all
w^{\prime}_{1}\neq w^{\prime}_{2}
in
W^{\prime}
are non-adjacent since their preimages under
h
are non-adjacent.
∎

A
vertex cover
is a set
C\subseteq V(F)
such that every edge
e\in E(F)
is incident to
C
, i.e.
e\cap C\neq\emptyset
.
Write
\vc(F)
for the size of the smallest vertex cover in
F
.

Fact D.4
.

For every graph
F
,
\mn(F)\leq\vc(F)\leq 2\mn(F)
.

Theorems

D.1
and
D.4
yield
Lemma

7.4
.

Appendix E
The Non-Uniform Homomorphism Distinguishing Closure

In this appendix, we prove some further properties of the non-uniform homomorphism distinguishing closure and conduct a comparison to the homomorphism distinguishing closure introduced by

49
]
.

Firstly note that it does not matter if the definition of
\cl_{n}(\mathcal{F})
is replaced with the following:

\cl_{\leq n}(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\{F\mid\forall G,H\text{ on $\leq n$ vertices}.\ G\equiv_{\mathcal{F}}H\implies\hom(F,G)=\hom(F,H)\}.

Lemma E.1
.

For
\mathcal{F}
as in
Proviso

6.2
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\cl_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\cl_{\leq n}(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

Clearly,

\cl_{\leq n}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\cl_{n}(\mathcal{F})
.
For the converse direction,
let
G
and
H
be graphs on at most
n
vertices.
Suppose that
G\equiv_{\mathcal{F}}H
.
By
Proviso

6.2
,
K_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
and hence
G
and
H
both have

vertices.

[
56
, Theorem 7]
,

G+(m-n)K_{1}\equiv_{\mathcal{F}}H+(m-n)K_{1}

Let
F\in\cl_{n}(\mathcal{F})

and write
F=F^{\prime}+\ell K_{1}
where
F^{\prime}
does not contain isolated vertices and

.
Then

\displaystyle\hom(F,G)

\displaystyle=\hom(F^{\prime}+\ell K_{1},G)

\displaystyle=\hom(F^{\prime},G)\cdot m^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\hom(F^{\prime},G+(m-n)K_{1})\cdot m^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\hom(F,G+(m-n)K_{1})\cdot\frac{m^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}

Hence,
\hom(F,G)=\hom(F,H)

Corollary E.2
.

For
\mathcal{F}
as in

6.2
