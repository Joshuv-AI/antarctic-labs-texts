Detecting drug-induced nephrotoxicity using simultaneous label-free autofluorescence multiharmonic microscopy
Authors: Aneesh Alex, Jindou Shi, Eric J Chaney, Minh Doan, Ciarán P Fisher, Leslie Obert, Crysthiane Ishiy, Alexander Ho, Darold R Spillman, Marina Marjanovic, Jan Roger, M Reid Groseclose, Steve R Hood, Stephen A Boppart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-induced nephrotoxicity (DIN) is a major cause of drug development failure, yet many cases are detected only late in clinical trials or after approval. Conventional methods to detect DIN often lack sensitivity and specificity, particularly for early or region-specific kidney injury. Here, we evaluate Simultaneous Label-free Autofluorescence Multiharmonic (SLAM) microscopy as a rapid, label-free imaging approach to quantify kidney microstructural and functional metabolic changes associated with DIN. SLAM simultaneously captures endogenous NAD(P)H and FAD autofluorescence, collagen-derived second harmonic generation, and third-harmonic generation from structural interfaces. SLAM images were acquired from the cortex and outer medulla (OM) of rat kidneys after cisplatin dosing at days 1, 6, and 29 post-administration. Imaging revealed region-specific injury, with the OM showing greater sensitivity to DIN than the cortex. The most prominent changes—tubular degeneration and hyaline casts—peaked at day 6, whereas tubular dilation and fibrosis-related features persisted to day 29. Feature-based classification captured these spatial and temporal patterns, achieving higher balanced accuracy in the OM (0.944) than in the cortex (0.860). Key model drivers included granularity and inter-channel correlations, underscoring the value of multi-channel SLAM data. Overall, these results demonstrate the potential of SLAM microscopy for sensitive, region-specific detection and characterization of DIN in preclinical safety studies.SLAM microscopy provides a rapid, label-free way to detect, localize, and classify drug-induced nephrotoxicity with improved sensitivity and regional specificity, potentially strengthening preclinical kidney safety screening and reducing late-stage development failures.Keywords: SLAM, drug-induced nephrotoxicity, label-free imaging, cisplatin, new approach methodologies

Drug-induced nephrotoxicity (DIN) is a significant concern during preclinical drug development and in clinical studies. The kidneys’ crucial role in filtering blood and excreting waste products makes them particularly vulnerable to damage (Radi 2019). The pathogenesis of DIN often involves complex interactions of drugs and their metabolites with various kidney regions, resulting in acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease (Kwiatkowska et al. 2021). Typically, DIN is detected in preclinical studies using a combination of biomarkers measured from blood or urine samples and histopathology. Standard measurements used to monitor kidney function and toxicity include blood tests for serum creatinine (sCr) or blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and urine analysis for biomarkers such as kidney injury molecule-1 or N-acetyl-beta-d-glucosaminidase (Connor et al. 2024). However, these biomarkers are often influenced by various physiological factors independent of kidney function, and have limited sensitivity and specificity needed to detect DIN at early stages (Griffin et al. 2019). Furthermore, various parts of a nephron, including glomeruli, proximal tubules, and distal tubules, differ in their sensitivity to nephrotoxicants due to the distinct roles played by each region in drug excretion and reabsorption of compounds from the urinary filtrate (Chevalier 2016). As a result, blood or urinary biomarkers measuring whole kidney responses to drug treatments provide limited insight into region-specific variations in DIN.

Given the structural and functional heterogeneity of various regions within the kidney and the differential sensitivity of those regions to DIN, it is crucial to investigate localized responses to drug treatments. During preclinical drug safety evaluation studies, histopathology is utilized for identification of the affected regions and the extent and severity of DIN (Antognini et al. 2024). Histological findings can help to determine the nature of kidney injury, identify biomarkers, and provide mechanistic insights related to nephrotoxicity at a cellular and tissue level. However, morphological alterations in histopathology are primarily detected after initial stages of renal injury and have limitations in determining the causes of kidney dysfunction (Bonventre et al. 2010). Hence, there is a critical need for the development of imaging techniques and biomarkers that can be used for more accurate region-specific structural and functional assessment of kidneys.

Among imaging technologies, optical imaging approaches allow visualization of kidney structure and function at high spatial resolution and with superior sensitivity. Most of the optical imaging studies conducted on preclinical models of DIN rely on the use of exogenous molecular probes for detecting specific biomarkers of kidney damage (Huang et al. 2019; Sitara et al. 2024). Label-free optical imaging techniques make it possible to extract structural and functional information from a tissue sample without the use of exogenous contrast agents. Significant advancements have been made over the last 2 decades in the field of label-free optical imaging to improve the speed, sensitivity, and specificity of the intrinsic signals acquired from the specimen (Borile et al. 2021; Shaked et al. 2023). Simultaneous label-free autofluorescence multiharmonic (SLAM) microscopy is one such label-free nonlinear optical imaging technique that enables fast and efficient acquisition of third-harmonic generation (THG), three-photon excited fluorescence (3PF), second-harmonic generation (SHG), and two-photon excited fluorescence (2PF) signals from fresh unstained tissue specimens (You et al. 2018; Boppart et al. 2019). SLAM microscopy performs simultaneous imaging of endogenous fluorophores such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (phosphate) (NAD(P)H) from 3PF, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) from 2PF, non-centrosymmetric structures such as collagen fibers and myosin from SHG, and structural interfaces such as cellular membranes, lipid droplets, and extracellular vesicles from THG.

SLAM microscopy eliminates the need for following labor-intensive and time-consuming sample preparation and staining workflow. It allows the samples to be kept unaltered, removes any possibility for exogenous artifacts, and can shorten the evaluation time for detecting DIN. Due to its non-perturbative nature, complementary readouts can be obtained from the same tissue sample and permit further genetic or molecular testing on that sample (Shaked et al. 2023). Additionally, SLAM microscopy utilizes near-infrared laser light centered around 1,040 nm for simultaneously generating multiphoton fluorescence and harmonic signals from the tissue sample. Due to the reduced scattering and absorption of excitation light in the near-infrared wavelength region, this technique is well-suited for 3D volumetric imaging of thicker tissue samples or complex in vitro models (CIVMs) (Majer et al. 2024; Roh et al. 2024; Tan et al. 2025). Furthermore, label-free images obtained using SLAM microscopy from tissue specimens can be trained using deep learning approaches to create familiar, virtual hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-style images (Yoon et al. 2025). This capability of SLAM microscopy was highlighted in a previous study where real-time, label-free 3D histology-like images were generated directly from fresh rat and human tissue samples (Sun et al. 2020). In addition to providing these scientific advantages, label-free optical imaging methods such as SLAM also help to lower the overall operational costs required for image generation by removing the need for dyes, antibodies, or chemical reagents, and eliminating the labor-intensive processes involved in traditional histopathology (Liu et al. 2022).

In this study, we investigated the potential of SLAM microscopy for detecting DIN in rat kidney specimens at different time points following cisplatin treatment. Cisplatin is a widely used and highly effective platinum-based chemotherapy drug used in the treatment of various malignancies, including testicular, ovarian, bladder, and lung cancers (Dasari and Bernard Tchounwou 2014). Clinically, the main dose-limiting toxicity associated with cisplatin treatment is nephrotoxicity, and acute kidney injury occurs in 20% to 30% of patients undergoing treatment with cisplatin (Miller et al. 2010). The pathogenesis of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity often involves complex interactions that result in tubular damage, interstitial nephritis, or glomerular injury, potentially culminating in acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease. To investigate time-dependent region-specific changes associated with cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity in a label-free manner using SLAM, images were collected from the cortex and outer medulla (OM) regions of the kidney tissue sections obtained from rats sacrificed at 3 different time points following cisplatin dosing (day 1, day 6, and day 29). Furthermore, we investigated the potential of the SLAM imaging technique to classify images collected from different time points based on the label-free signatures, and interpreted these signatures to obtain a deeper understanding of the localized effects of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity and the nature of recovery after treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals and tissue preparation

This animal study was ethically reviewed and carried out at AbbVie Deutschland GmbH & Co. KG (Knollstrasse, 67061 Ludwigshafen, Germany) as part of the Innovative Medicines Initiative (IMI2) Joint TransQST project (Grant agreement no. 116030) in accordance with European Directive 2010/63/EEC and the German Federal Animal Welfare Act. Animal dosing regimen and sample collection details have been described previously (Wijaya et al. 2025). Although the IMI2 Joint TransQST study consisted of 2 experimental series (a dose-response study and a single-dose temporal dynamic study), only a limited set of samples available from the single-dose temporal dynamic study was used for SLAM imaging in this study. Briefly, 12 male Sprague-Dawley rats (Crl: CD [SD]; 8 to 9 wk old; body weight 320 to 360 g) were randomly placed into 4 groups. Rats in the treatment group received cisplatin (Teva, 1 mg/ml diluted with 0.9% saline) at a dosage of 5 mg/kg via a single intravenous bolus administration in the tail vein on day 0. For kidney functional biomarker measurements, 200 µl of blood was collected from the sublingual vein under sevoflurane (Sevorane, AbbVie Deutschland GmbH) anesthesia at defined post-treatment time points. Serum was immediately isolated and stored at −20°C until analysis. Kidney injury biomarkers were quantified using commercial assay kits, and serum biochemical profiles (BUN and sCr) were measured on a cobas c501 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturers’ standard protocols. Rats were then sacrificed at designated time points (day 1, day 6, and day 29; 3 animals/time point) post-dose. Rats in the control group received vehicle alone (0.9% saline) and were sacrificed on the day 1 time point. After euthanizing the rats, both kidneys were collected and transversally divided into halves. The lower halves of the left and rightkidneysy were fixed with 10% formaldehyde for immunostaining and histopathological assessment. The upper half of the right kidney was embedded with Tissue-TEK O.C.T. compound (Sakura) at −80°C in a cryomold, then snap frozen in liquid nitrogen pre-cooled isopentane, and was used for subsequent SLAM imaging studies.

SLAM imaging

The optical imaging platform used in this study for SLAM microscopy has been previously described in detail (Lee et al. 2019). Briefly, a high peak power femtosecond laser centered at 1,040 nm (Spectra-Physics, Femtotrain) was operated at a 10 MHz pulse repetition rate. The laser beam was used to pump a photonic crystal fiber (NKT Photonics, LMA-PM-15) to generate a supercontinuum, which was then sent to a pulse shaper (Biophotonics Solutions Inc., FemtoJock-D) to compress and tailor the pulses to a bandwidth from 980 to 1,100 nm. The beam was delivered to a 25×, 1.05 NA water-immersion objective lens (Olympus, XLPLN25XWMP2) with an average power of 20 mW at the sample plane. Four sequential sets of dichroic beam splitters and bandpass filters (Semrock) were used to separate the collected signal (334 to 404 nm for THG, 405 to 504 nm for 3PF, 542 to 566 nm for SHG, 593 to 700 nm for 2PF).

As shown in Fig. 1A, 100 μm thick transverse sections of the frozen kidney samples were obtained using a cryostat (Leica CM3050 S) and were mounted on a 0.17-mm thick cover glass for imaging. Tissue sections were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, and a few drops of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were added on top of the tissue section to keep it moistened. The custom-built SLAM microscope was set up as an inverted microscope, and the backscattered signals were captured simultaneously using 4 PMTs corresponding to THG, 3PF, SHG, and 2PF channels (Fig. 1B). The sample was placed on a 2D piezoelectric stage (SmarAct, SLC-24150-LC) to collect images from different regions of each tissue section. The field of view of each image was set to 180 μm × 180 μm. As indicated in Fig. 1A, SLAM images were collected from 30 different fields of view within the cortex and OM regions, respectively, of each tissue section.

Image analysis

SLAM images captured from the kidney cortex and OM regions were analyzed using a custom-developed image analysis pipeline comprising 4 main stages: (i) image preprocessing, (ii) tubular region segmentation, (iii) feature extraction, and (iv) downstream analyses (Fig. 3A). In the image preprocessing stage, optical redox ratio (ORR) images were computed from 2PF (FAD) and 3PF (NAD(P)H) signals using the formula: FAD/[FAD + NAD(P)H]. 2PF intensity-based thresholding approach using Otsu’s method was employed to generate masks of tubular regions. Using the multi-channel SLAM images and corresponding tubule segmentation masks, a broad spectrum of hand-engineered features was extracted via CellProfiler (Stirling et al. 2021), capturing morphological, intensity-based, textural, and inter-channel correlation characteristics of the tubules. A flowchart showing the key steps included in the CellProfiler image analysis pipeline is shown in Fig. S8. To reduce redundancy, highly correlated features (Spearman’s ρ > 0.8 in absolute value) were grouped, and one representative feature from each group was retained for further analysis. Subsequently, downstream analyses were conducted to distinguish between control and treatment groups across different time points. For unsupervised analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to visualize group-wise distributions in the reduced feature space. For supervised classification, support vector machine (SVM) models with linear kernels were trained using a 70/30 split of randomly selected training and test data. The SVMs were trained to distinguish SLAM images acquired from the cortex and OM regions of the kidney tissue samples belonging to 4 different study groups (i.e., control, day 1, day 6, and day 29), separately. Balanced accuracy (bACC) was used as the evaluation metric to account for potential class imbalance. Feature importance was assessed using permutation importance scores derived from the trained SVM models, enabling ranking of features based on their contributions to classification performance.

Statistical analysis

Given the nonnormal distribution of extracted image features (Fig. S10) and the limited sample size per study group, statistical differences in feature distributions between groups were assessed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Statistical significance is indicated as ns (P ≥ 0.05), * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), and **** (P < 0.0001).

Results

Label-free optical imaging of rat kidney tissue samples

SLAM microscopy was used to generate label-free, multimodal images of rat kidney tissue sections, enabling visualization of microstructural features in the cortex and OM regions. A representative SLAM image comprising 4 output channels obtained simultaneously from the cortex of a rat kidney tissue section is shown in Fig. 1C–F. For visualization, each channel was pseudo-colored and contrast-adjusted independently. The THG channel (magenta) highlights structural interfaces (Table 1), clearly outlining tubular boundaries and revealing tubular epithelial nuclei as dark circular features (white arrows; Fig. 1C). The 3PF (cyan) and 2PF (yellow) channels capture autofluorescence from NAD(P)H and FAD, respectively (Fig. 1D and F), and therefore visualize cellular microstructure across the tubules. Both NAD(P)H and FAD coenzymes play a major role in the energy metabolism of cells (Chance et al. 1979). Because the SLAM imaging technique can image both these coenzymes simultaneously, it enables computation of the optical redox ratio (ORR), calculated as [FAD/(FAD + NAD(P)H)] from these images (Fig. 1J–L). ORR has been used as a label-free biomarker of metabolic state in prior studies (Skala et al. 2007; You et al. 2019). The SHG signal (green) is generated by non-centrosymmetric structures arranged in a specific order and primarily arises from fine collagen fibers in tissue samples. As shown in Fig. 1E, collagen fibers distributed between the renal tubules were observed in the SHG channel along with minimal bleed-through of autofluorescence signal from NAD(P)H and FAD. However, the SHG signal from collagen fibers was much stronger compared with the leaked autofluorescence signals. A corresponding 4-channel merged SLAM image is shown in Fig. 1G. In the merged image, 2PF (FAD, yellow) and 3PF (NAD(P)H, cyan) signals arising from the tubular structures of the kidney cortex dominated the signals from the other 2 channels. Characteristic morphological features of the tubular epithelial cells in the kidney cortex region, and collagen fibers and blood vessels (orange arrow) in the tubulointerstitial space are clearly visible in the merged SLAM image.

Additionally, SLAM images were obtained from other regions of the kidney tissue sections, including glomeruli and OM (Fig. 1H and I). The glomeruli, which are composed of networks of capillaries and specialized cells (podocytes, mesangial cells, and other cell types) enclosed within the Bowman’s capsule, appear as darker regions surrounded by the tubules in the SLAM images (yellow arrows in Fig. 1H). Elongated tubular structures corresponding to collecting tubules, proximal straight tubules (segment 3) and thick ascending limbs, and collagen fibers in the interstitial connective tissue providing structural support to renal tubules were observed in the SLAM images obtained from the OM region (Fig. 1I). Corresponding ORR images determined based on the SLAM images obtained from these regions are shown in Fig. 1J–L. Spatial variations in ORR across tubules, glomeruli and tubulointerstitial space were observed in these regions, with higher ORR associated with a higher level of oxidative phosphorylation than glycolysis. Together, these images demonstrated the capability of the SLAM imaging to visualize both microstructural and functional metabolic features across distinct anatomical regions of a kidney tissue section in a nonperturbative and label-free manner.

Identification of morphological changes associated with cisplatin treatment

Acute kidney injury after cisplatin treatment is followed by renal tissue degeneration and regeneration, and the balance between the acute injury and tissue regeneration responses governs the long-term impact of cisplatin treatment on overall renal function (McSweeney et al. 2021). Hence, the time taken for tissue regeneration is a key factor to be considered to minimize the toxic effects of cisplatin treatment and to determine dosing intervals in patients. One of the main objectives of this study was to visualize and evaluate the structural and functional changes associated with cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity at different time points post-treatment. Figure 2 shows representative merged SLAM images obtained from transverse sections of rat kidney at different time points post-treatment. Individual channels of the SLAM images and corresponding ORR images are shown in Figs S1–S5.

Qualitatively, SLAM images obtained from the kidney cortex region of the control and treatment groups at day 1 time point showed no major differences in terms of tubular morphology (Fig. 2A and B) and glomerular features (Fig. 2E and F). By day 6, notable signs of tubular degeneration including loss of structural integrity of tubular epithelial cells, presence of hyaline casts inside the tubule lumen and loss of brush border were observed in the SLAM images (Fig. 2C). These structural alterations coincided with increased BUN and sCr levels (Fig. 2), established indicators of kidney function (Wijaya et al. 2025), suggesting acute functional impairment at this time point. By day 29, the tubular structures in the kidney cortex region appeared more intact compared with the day 6 time point. However, tubular dilation, loss of brush border, and tubular epithelial attenuation (marked by *) persisted in a subset of tubules in the cortex (Fig. 2D). Consistently, BUN and sCr levels at day 29 remained elevated relative to the control and day 1 groups, indicating incomplete functional recovery at this time point.

Unlike the considerable changes observed in tubular features of the kidney cortex, glomerular structures looked comparatively similar at different time points post-treatment (Fig. 2E–H). In the OM region, tubular features observed at the day 1 time point were comparable between the control and treatment groups (Fig. 2I and J). However, similar to the changes observed in the cortex region, significant structural modifications such as tubular degeneration and luminal hyaline casts were detected in the OM region at the day 6 time point (Fig. 2K). Although these signs of tubular damage were not detected in the SLAM images obtained at the day 29 time point, a considerably higher amount of collagen fibers suggestive of fibrogenesis was observed in the images obtained from the OM region (Fig. 2L). Overall, the highest degree of tubular degeneration in the kidney cortex and OM regions following cisplatin treatment was detected in SLAM images captured at the day 6 time point. However, indicators of tubular damage and fibrosis were still detectable in both these regions at the day 29 time point post-treatment. The morphological changes observed in the SLAM images at different time points were validated by comparison with histology images obtained from the serial tissue sections (Fig. 2M–P and Fig. S6). Histopathology findings based on the morphological changes observed in the H&E images are provided in Table 2. The pathologist utilized the International Harmonization of Nomenclature and Diagnostic (INHAND) terminology to describe the pathologic changes in the tissue. There was good correspondence between SLAM and H&E images across all time points post-treatment in terms of the microstructural changes observed at the cortex and OM regions of kidney tissue sections.

Quantitative analysis of spatiotemporal changes in kidney cortex and outer medulla associated with cisplatin treatment

As shown in Fig. 2, pronounced changes in micromorphological features were observed in the SLAM images obtained from both the kidney cortex and OM regions at different time points following cisplatin treatment. In order to quantitatively compare these changes, tubular regions in the SLAM images were delineated by using Otsu’s thresholding method. Masks were then generated for the corresponding regions, and structural, functional, and correlative features were measured for those regions (Fig. 3A). The number and percent distribution of image objects selected for downstream analysis from each time point are shown in Fig. S7.

PCA based on the top 141 uncorrelated features extracted from the SLAM images was used to determine the distribution pattern of image data points obtained from the kidney cortex and OM regions. Interestingly, the image data points from the cortex and OM regions formed separate clusters as shown in the PCA plot (Fig. 3B). This plot depicts the distinct differences in microstructural features captured in the SLAM images from the cortex and OM regions, respectively, across different time points. To visualize treatment-associated variation without confounding regional differences, data were plotted separately for the cortex (Fig. 3C–F; Fig. S9A) and OM (Fig. 3G–J; Fig. S9B). In the cortex, control and day 1 distributions largely overlapped, whereas day 6 and day 29 shifted in different directions; day 29 also showed greater dispersion (Fig. 3F). A similar trend was observed in the OM: control and day 1 distributions overlapped, whereas day 6 and day 29 formed distinct clusters.

Classification and interpretation of cisplatin treatment-associated longitudinal changes observed in cortex and OM

Following the visualization of the distribution of SLAM image data points obtained at different time points from kidney cortex and OM, an SVM classifier was utilized to classify these images based on the extracted features. Confusion matrices corresponding to the performance of the SVM classifier for classifying the SLAM images obtained at different time points from cortex and OM regions are shown in Fig. 4A and B, respectively.

In the cortex (Fig. 4A), classification performance varied across time points, reflecting progressive cisplatin-induced changes. Control samples were correctly classified at 90.00%, with minor confusion (5.00%) with day 1 and day 29. Day 1 showed moderate accuracy (76.47%), with misclassifications into day 6 (11.76%) and later time points. Day 6 achieved the highest accuracy (92.59%), consistent with peak morphological disruption, though 7.41% were misclassified as day 29. A lower accuracy was obtained on day 29 (85.00%), with 10.00% predicted as day 6 and 5.00% as control. The overall bACC was 0.860, reflecting moderate temporal separability in the cortex based on the features extracted from the SLAM images.

In contrast, the OM (Fig. 4B) demonstrated higher classification performance, with a bACC of 0.944. Control and day 1 samples were classified with high accuracy (93.33% and 100.00%, respectively). Day 6 and day 29 samples also showed strong separability (92.86% and 91.30% accuracy), with minimal confusion across groups. These results indicate more distinct, temporally resolved cisplatin-induced alterations in the OM compared with the cortex. Overall, these findings highlight that SLAM-derived features capture region- and time-specific signatures of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity, with the images obtained from the OM offering greater temporal discriminability than the ones from the cortex.

To interpret these classification results from the cortex and OM regions at different time points, the contributions of individual features extracted from the SLAM images were examined. Fig. 4C and D rank feature importance in the SVM classification model corresponding to cortex and OM regions, respectively. Interestingly, correlation and granularity features were the top-ranking features in both regions, which highlights the significance of multi-channel information captured in the SLAM images. The 2 most influential features driving classification across time points are shown in Fig. S10. The violin plots shown in this figure, with embedded boxplots, reveal substantial variation in the distribution of data points across time points, underscoring the importance of accounting for the spatial heterogeneity captured in the SLAM images when comparing features derived from different time points. As shown in Fig. 4C and D, the most important feature for classification in the cortex region was the correlation between the 3PF and THG channels, whereas the OM classification model was driven most strongly by the correlation between the SHG and THG channels.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the utility of SLAM microscopy for visualizing, quantifying, and interpreting microstructural and functional metabolic changes in rat kidney tissue associated with cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity. Currently, histopathological techniques are used in preclinical safety studies for visualizing microstructural changes associated with DIN. The cellular changes identified in H&E-stained kidney tissue sections are used in conjunction with kidney-damage-specific biomarkers such as clusterin, kidney injury molecule-1, or N-acetyl-beta-d-glucosaminidase for confirming and assessing the severity of DIN. However, these approaches have limitations in differentiating types of kidney injury and are not sensitive enough to detect early or mild forms of DIN (Bonventre et al. 2010). To address some of the limitations of the conventional approaches used for detecting DIN, emerging optical imaging methods such as SLAM that can provide both structural and functional information from kidney tissue sections in a label-free manner can be utilized in preclinical safety studies.

A common histopathological feature of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity is renal damage through tubular cell injury and death (McSweeney et al. 2021). The active uptake of cisplatin is mediated by the transporter proteins expressed on the basolateral membrane of the renal tubular cells, and excessive accumulation of cisplatin in those cells induces tubular cell death via apoptosis and/or necrosis. Consistent with this pathophysiology, SLAM images revealed morphological alterations of tubules in the rat kidney cortex and OM regions associated with cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity at day 6, and persistent, albeit reduced, abnormalities at day 29 post-treatment (Fig. 2). An increase in 3PF and 2PF signals was observed in the tubular epithelial cells at the day 6 time point in both the cortex and OM regions (Figs S1 and S4). Similarly, ORR values corresponding to the tubular regions in the cortex and OM regions were elevated at the day 6 time point (Fig. S5). These results are consistent with previous studies that have shown that ORR values significantly increase in apoptotic cells as a result of additional energy demand and activation of specific metabolic pathways during these processes (Bower et al. 2017; Sergeeva et al. 2017; Shirmanova et al. 2022). In addition, the damage to tubular epithelial cells following cisplatin treatment and the resulting defective repair process can promote tubulointerstitial fibrosis in the kidney (Shi et al. 2018; Budu et al. 2021). Upon evaluation of the collagen images captured in the SHG channel from different time points post-treatment, an increase in collagen-associated signal, which can be indicative of fibrogenesis, was noticed on fay 29 (Fig. S3). Although regional variations in collagen fiber distribution were observed between the kidney cortex and OM regions, a relatively higher proportion of collagen fibers was observed in the peritubular regions of the OM region, especially at the day 29 time point post-treatment compared with the control. These differences observed in the collagen fiber distribution likely contributed to the better classification performance of the SVM model in the OM region (bACC = 0.944) compared with the cortex region (bACC = 0.860). Notably, the correlation between the SHG and THG channels capturing the features related to collagen fiber distribution and tubulointerstitial fibrosis was the most important feature for classification in the OM region (Fig. 4D). Altogether, these results show that multi-channel SLAM imaging can capture established signs of cisplatin-associated nephrotoxicity, including tubular degeneration, presence of luminal hyaline casts, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis without using any exogenous contrast agents.

Based on the observed microstructural changes in different channels of the SLAM images and subsequent quantitative analysis of the spatiotemporal changes observed in the rat kidney cortex and OM regions, the OM region appears to be more sensitive to the nephrotoxic effects of cisplatin treatment compared with the cortex region. These results are in agreement with the IMI2 Joint TransQST study in which samples collected from the same animals were examined using a transcriptomic analysis approach, and spatiotemporal responses in rat kidney glomeruli, cortex and OM regions after a single dose of cisplatin treatment were investigated (Wijaya et al. 2025). Although signs of DNA damage were observed in both cortex and OM regions, activation of kidney injury biomarkers was strongest in the OM region, and demonstrated that the measurements from nephron segment-specific renal injury biomarkers were more sensitive to cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity than whole kidney responses. Likewise, previous studies demonstrated significant alterations in lipid distribution in the cortex and OM regions of rat kidneys following cisplatin treatment measured by mass spectrometry imaging (Moreno-Gordaliza et al. 2018). Consistent with those results, notable changes were observed in the THG channel images that correspond to signals from lipids and lipid-rich membranous structures within the tissue section at different time points following cisplatin treatment in this study. As shown in Fig. 4C and D, correlation of 3PF and SHG channels with the THG channel was the top-ranking feature for the classification model in both cortex and OM regions, respectively.

For this exploratory SLAM imaging study, designed to assess the capability of SLAM to detect spatiotemporal alterations associated with cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity, images were acquired from only a limited number of kidney tissue samples available at predefined post-treatment time points and did not include control samples at each of those time points (day 1 for the control group and days 1, 6, and 29 for the treatment group). Future studies incorporating SLAM imaging of control samples at each corresponding time point, as well as evaluation across multiple cisplatin dose levels, will be required to rigorously determine the sensitivity of this novel imaging approach for detecting DIN. In addition, this was a single-dose study in which spatiotemporal changes associated with a single 5 mg/kg intravenous dose of cisplatin administered on day 0 were clearly evident in H&E-stained sections, and these H&E images were used to validate the cellular and subcellular alterations observed in SLAM images at different time points. Future studies at lower dose levels are needed to establish the sensitivity of SLAM microscopy for detecting less severe, dose-dependent cellular alterations. Moreover, additional studies employing other nephrotoxic compounds will be important to define the specificity of SLAM microscopy for identifying treatment-specific biomarkers and for characterizing the nature of kidney injury associated with different nephrotoxic insults at both the cellular and tissue levels.

For conducting the preclinical safety evaluation of new drug candidates before human trials, the regulatory agencies worldwide are emphasizing the need to reduce, refine, or potentially replace animal testing procedures with New Approach Methodologies, including toxicity evaluation on CIVMs and using AI-based computational models (Stoykova 2025; Yao et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2026). New Approach Methodologies are designed to improve and accelerate the drug safety evaluation process with more effective, human-relevant models and methods. CIVMs such as organoids or organ-on-a-chip platforms emulate the complexity of human tissues, permitting the investigation of organ-level responses to new drug candidates and associated toxicity (Ewart et al. 2022). Compared with 2-D cell cultures extensively used in early stages of preclinical safety evaluation for high-throughput screening of drug candidates and cytotoxicity testing, CIVMs require advanced imaging techniques capable of visualizing and quantifying sub-cellular changes associated with drug treatment occurring in a more complex 3-D cellular microenvironment. Given its capability to image thicker samples by providing deeper imaging depth and complementary multi-channel information simultaneously, the SLAM microscopy technique is well-suited for visualizing and quantifying microstructural and functional metabolic changes occurring in CIVMs under different treatment conditions (Majer et al. 2024; Roh et al. 2024). Additionally, the label-free nature of SLAM microscopy enables longitudinal studies on live CIVMs to investigate time-dependent toxicity-associated changes, eliminating the need to generate sacrificial sample replicates at each time point.

In conclusion, the capability of SLAM microscopy as a powerful preclinical safety evaluation research tool for studying DIN has been demonstrated. It enabled visualization and quantification of region-specific variations in the rat kidney cortex and OM following cisplatin treatment, and to do so in a label-free manner. Both microstructural and functional metabolic changes occurring in the kidney at different time points post-treatment were characterized, and quantitative analysis of the features extracted from the SLAM images provided a deeper understanding of the spatial patterns and temporal extent of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity and the nature of recovery. Although both the kidney cortex and OM regions showed signs of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity, it was determined that the OM region was more sensitive to the toxic effects of cisplatin in comparison to the kidney cortex. Longitudinally, peak effects of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity were observed at the day 6 time point, and features associated with incomplete recovery such as tubular dilation/attenuation and tubulointerstitial fibrosis persisted at day 29 post-treatment in the kidney cortex and OM regions, respectively. Among the various features extracted from the SLAM images, correlation between different output channels and granularity were the most important ones influencing the classification of the spatiotemporal changes associated with cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity. This highlights the importance of simultaneous multi-channel image acquisition achieved using SLAM to enable accurate detection and characterization of DIN. Overall, these results support SLAM as a promising complementary imaging method to conventional DIN assessments, with the potential to improve sensitivity to early and region-localized injury in preclinical safety studies.
