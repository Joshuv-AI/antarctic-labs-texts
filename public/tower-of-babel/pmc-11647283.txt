Customised 96-ocular TaqMan (iCAM) microarray PCR card for rapid diagnosis of microbial keratitis
Authors: Yunfei Yang, Ahmed Roble, Rashmi Deshmukh, James Myerscough, Martin D Curran, Madhavan S Rajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo validate the diagnostic performance of a custom 96-micro-organism TaqMan PCR card (iCAM) for microbial keratitis (MK) from a single corneal epithelial sample.MethodsPatients over the age of 18 referred to Cambridge University Hospital with MK were recruited in this single-site prospective cohort study between September 2021 and January 2023. An ocular-specific, customised microarray card (iCAM) was constructed according to primer and probe nucleotide sequences developed in our department to detect bacteria, viruses, Acanthamoeba and fungi commonly implicated in MK using a single corneal epithelial sample. Part of the corneal epithelial sample was taken for conventional cultures per local protocol, followed by iCAM array. Microbial detection rate and positive predictive value (PPV) were evaluated.Results38 corneal epithelial samples from 32 patients with MK and 4 control samples from healthy participants were obtained from 36 consecutive patients. A causative microbe was isolated in 15/34 samples (44%) using the iCAM test, compared with 15 by conventional methods (44%). iCAM test processing time varied between 6 and 24 hours compared with up to 7 days for conventional tests. Combined, the microbial detection rate was 65%, with the correlation between methods at 62%. The iCAM test could detect all major micro-organism groups with 56% sensitivity and 60% PPV.ConclusionsThe iCAM test can detect bacterial, fungal, viral and protozoan organisms using one corneal epithelial sample. The limitations include small patient cohort size and reduced volume of available corneal epithelial sample when shared between the iCAM PCR test and conventional culture methods utilised in the study. A multicentre trial is being planned to validate the clinical impact of using iCAM test on accuracy of diagnosis, early institution of appropriate antimicrobials and clinical outcomes.Trial registration numberISRCTN17422545.Keywords: Cornea, Infection, Public health, Microbiology, Diagnostic tests/Investigation

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study investigates the diagnostic value of a custom microarray PCR with the ability to detect bacteria, virus, fungi and Acanthamoeba using a single corneal scrape sample with a turnaround time of 24 hours. The study showed that iCAM PCR has the potential to avoid multiple tests required to identify the causative organisms in ocular samples as is the case in current conventional culture techniques.

Introduction

Microbial keratitis, defined as infection of the cornea by bacteria, viruses, protozoa or fungi, constitutes the most common non-surgical ophthalmic emergency in the UK with an estimated incidence between 40 and 52 cases per 100 000 attendances per year.1 Globally, microbial keratitis is thought to cause unilateral blindness in 1.5–2 million cases per year annually.2 3 Timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment are critical in preventing corneal perforation, scarring and endophthalmitis with potential need for corneal graft, or more rarely, enucleation.4

Diagnosis is based on assessment of risk factors, clinical signs and results of investigations. The reference standard for diagnosis and treatment planning in microbial keratitis remains a combination of stains (Gram, Grocott, Giemsa, potassium hydroxide and calcofluor white) and culture (media varying from blood, chocolate, Sabouraud and non-nutrient agar).2 Median culture positivity rates are reported to be around 50.4% (range 32.6%–79.4%)5,7 compared with staining alone (positivity in 27.3%–61.6%2 8 9). Challenges to obtaining a good positivity rate include previous antibiotic use, sampling technique and sample yield as well as delay between sample acquisition and incubation. Furthermore, microbial culture is slow and can take up to 3–5 days for identification of bacteria and around 5–7 days for fungi.10

PCR is considered an adjunctive or potential alternative to current methods with the primary advantages being rapidity, high sensitivity and ability to amplify very small amounts of genetic material. PCR consists of denaturation, primer annealing and elongation over 30 cycles to obtain over 2 billion copies of DNA to allow detection.11 Primers are selected from universally conserved sequences in the DNA. In bacteria, 16S ribosomal DNA is preferred with many highly conserved regions of nucleotide sequences interspersed with nine variable regions that facilitate taxonomic classification based on the nucleotide sequence variability, and 18S ribosomal DNA is preferred for fungal rDNA.2 Multiplex real-time PCR and ‘simultaneous singleplex’12 monoplex assays allow multiple pathogen detection, with cycle threshold ‘Ct’ values assisting interpretation of results. Custom plates can then be designed with specific pathogens/organ systems/resistance panels in mind.13

Here, we perform a cohort study in a single UK tertiary centre to evaluate the 96-ocular pathogen/micro-organism custom microarray to assess the sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional microbial assessment protocols. The primary aims were to compare diagnostic performance compared with conventional methods of isolating the causative organism in infective keratitis.

Materials and methods

Development of the iCAM microarray

We performed a retrospective 8-year (2014–2022) audit of the range of micro-organisms detected from corneal scrapes at Addenbrooke’s Hospital, in conjunction with a comprehensive review of the literature, which allowed us to identify the most common ocular pathogens, facilitating the creation of a selection of micro-organisms to populate an iCAM TaqMan array card (see online supplemental figure 1). The majority of the real-time PCR assays used to populate the card were taken directly from our previously established syndromic TaqMan array cards.14,17 In the case of Acanthamoeba, Toxoplasma gondii, Microsporidia species, Fusarium species and Fusarium solani, the literature was reviewed for well cited and fully validated real-time PCR assays. In the absence of a published validated assay, one was designed in-house following the guidelines set out previously.13 Where possible, each organism was covered by two independent target sequences (sometimes 3 and 4 PCR target sequences) to minimise false positive results (online supplemental figure 1). Assay primer/probe sequence details are available on request.

We prospectively identified patients attending between September 2021 and January 2023 referred to the Addenbrooke’s Hospital corneal service with a clinical diagnosis of microbial keratitis, following the departmental guidelines of an ulcer of >1 mm size and clinical features of infective keratitis with corneal infiltrate. The inclusion criteria for recruitment were patients older than 18 years of age. Four patients with no corneal pathology were recruited as control cases with the corneal epithelial sample collected for processing. Data were collected from Epic Electronic Healthcare Records, including age of patient, date of scrape, microbial isolates, visual acuity, risk factors for keratitis and previous use of topical steroids.

After obtaining informed consent, corneal epithelial samples were collected by experienced clinicians under local anaesthetic. Corneal epithelial scrape was obtained with a sterile No. 15 Surgical Scalpel blade using slit lamp microscopy. This evaluation study was not intended to report on clinical or visual outcomes of patients with microbial keratitis, and therefore, the outcome measures were limited to microbial detection rate with iCAM test compared and correlated to conventional microbiology used in clinical practice.

Conventional microbiological assessment

A standard corneal scrape kit included a glass slide for Gram stain, blood agars, one chocolate agar plate and Sabouraud dextrose agar. For suspected Acanthamoeba infections, samples were also sent for PCR in nuclease-free water. Similarly, for suspected viral infections, a swab was taken and sent in viral transport medium (∑-Virocult) as per department protocol. Gram stains were reported urgently, and culture plates were reviewed at 48 hours for growth before further assessment up to 7 days later.

iCAM Microarray PCR

Corneal epithelial samples for iCAM PCR were collected sequentially after taking the sample for conventional microbial assessment during the same patient episode/visit and immediately inoculated in transport medium (∑-Virocult) before handing it to the research lab. Nucleic acid was extracted from 0.4 mL of samples using an EZ1 virus mini kit (V.2.0) on an EZ1 advanced XL (Qiagen) instrument and eluted in 120 μL volume. Extraction controls (sterile RNase-free water) were also included with every extraction run and processed alongside clinical specimens. The custom prespotted TaqMan array card (TAC, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA) that we had termed iCAM card was designed to include all common ocular pathogens/micro-organisms (n=96, see online supplemental figure 1). Each card can run up to 8 samples with 48 connected wells per sample, giving a total of 384 wells. Data were then amalgamated on the Thermo Fisher QuantStudio 7 Flex platform using QuantStudio Real_time PCR software V.1.72. Each sample (nucleic acid) was loaded twice to occupy two sample slots as outlined previously.14 Human RNAse P and the bacteriophage MS2 were used as internal controls. Reverse transcriptase real-time PCR was undertaken according to the following amplification protocol: 50°C for 5 min (reverse transcription step), 95°C for 20 s and then 45 cycles of 95°C for 1 s, followed by 60°C for 20 s, with a fluorescence reading taken on the FAM channel at each cycle.

TaqMan array cards are custom made to your design request in batches of 50 plates or more, with the primers and probes lyophilised in the wells of the cards ensuring a shelf-life of up to 2 years. To ensure all assays were made and spotted correctly, iCAM TaqMan array cards were batch tested on arrival against our large bank of DNA extracts from a diverse range of micro-organisms, commercially available pathogen DNA controls (Vircell.com), available EQA panels from Quality Control for Molecular Diagnostics (www.qcmd.org/en/qcmd_eqa_schemes/) and our panel of nine synthetic control plasmids (www.genscript.com) containing our target PCR sequences as outlined previously.14 Positive control material was available for all assays on the iCAM card and was diluted to a concentration creating Ct values ranging between 15 and 20. By strategically pooling the positive control material, it was possible to check a batch of plates in a checkerboard fashion to ensure that all the assays have been spotted correctly into their assigned wells/reaction pods.13 All assays passed this quality control check and no cross-reaction between the various PCR assays was observed with any of the positive control material.

The nine synthetic plasmids (with the target sequences stitched together in the pUC57 plasmid) were instrumental in helping to establish the limit of detection of 3–30 genome equivalents per PCR reaction well for each assay, equating to 3000–30 000 genome equivalents/mL of specimen. This process has been previously detailed13 14 and entailed diluting the plasmids 10-fold down to −10 and testing them in triplicate. The endpoints where all three replicates were positive defined the LOD. The Ct values for the endpoints ranged from Ct 35 to 38.

Detection of a clear exponential amplification curve with a cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤38 for any single gene target was reported as a positive result for the relevant micro-organism.

Statistical analysis was performed by using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365) and R Studio V.4.3.0 (RStudio, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). Contingency table analysis with McNemar’s test was performed to analyse result agreement between test methods.

Results

A total of 38 corneal epithelial samples, of which 34 were from 32 patients and 4 from control participants (38 eyes), were taken. A comparative analysis of the two methods was performed on the patient cases only (control participants not included). The mean age of patients was 55±21 years (range 18–97 years) and there was a female predominance in the population (45% male). A positive result was identified in 22 cases (65%) of cases with infectious keratitis using either the iCAM microarray PCR or conventional microbiology techniques.

Positive iCAM PCR results

Microbes were isolated in 15 cases using iCAM array (44%) compared with 15 cases in conventional methods (44%) in the 34 corneal epithelium samples. The presence of human RNAse P, the internal control for the presence of human DNA was also assessed. RNAse P was detected in 25 samples (74%), while it was not detected in the remaining 9 samples (26%). However, four of these samples negative for RNAse P still gave a positive result on microarray with Moraxella catarrhalis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus aureus, Acanthamoeba species and herpes simplex virus (HSV) type 1 detected. The extraction process control (MS2 bacteriophage), which is spiked into every specimen prior to extraction, was PCR positive in all specimens ruling out the presence of PCR inhibitors, suggesting inadequate cellular material may have been taken during the sampling procedure for some patients. All the extraction controls (n=9) included in the study were found to be PCR negative ruling out any contamination events during the study’s nucleic acid extraction process.

Range of species identified

The four control (C1–C4) samples from normal subjects had a negative result with iCAM (both the RNase P gene and MS2 bacteriophage amplified as expected). Among the patient cohort a total of eight genera of microbes were isolated using both iCAM microarray PCR and conventional microbiology tests while five genera were only isolated using conventional microbiology (table 1). One patient had polymicrobial keratitis with both HSV type 1 and S. aureus detected.

Summary of results

Overall iCAM microarray successfully detected species across all major groups of micro-organisms whereas conventional methods primarily detected bacterial species. Of note, Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates were more frequently detected via conventional culture methods. This may reflect the sequential order of sampling (namely taking iCAM samples after conventional cultures) in the context of the small volumes used in corneal epithelial scrapes. In the five cases where there existed non-concordance between iCAM and conventional culture of P. aeruginosa (online supplemental table 1), only one case failed RNAse P in the iCAM result. Interestingly, the second sample from Patient X taken 21 days later (online supplemental table 1) was positive on the iCAM card for P. aeruginosa but failed to grow any organism, more than likely reflecting the use of topical antibiotic therapy. The average processing time to process ocular samples for iCAM PCR was 20–24 hours.

Moreover, with regard to rarer organisms detected; Candida, Microsporidia and Acanthamoeba, each case was in keeping with both the clinical picture and response to treatment, requiring several months of treatment with the relevant antimicrobials for clinical resolution.

Results agreement between iCAM microarray and conventional methods

Results correlated in 21 (62%) of cases between conventional culture methods and iCAM microarray (see online supplemental table 1). Specific to positive results, in 14 of 22 cases (64%) a different organism was found within the comparative analysis between the two methods. All the results between the two methods correlated down to the species level with the exception of Moraxella, just to the genus. In eight cases, iCAM microarray detected microbes where conventional methods did not.

Diagnostic value

Overall, the iCAM microarray panel showed a sensitivity of 56% and positive predictive value of 60% when compared with conventional methods (table 2). McNemar’s test demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Χ2=0.07692, p=0.7815). It is important to note that the small study number diminishes the statistical power.

Discussion

Our iCAM study obtained a positive microbial isolate in 44% of cases, which is comparable to local regional results in the East of England between 2014 and 2022 (42.95% positive)18 and in the general literature (32.6%–54%). However, the sensitivity reported in this study is lower than some of the reports in the literature with rates as high as 83% for bacterial and 85% in fungal keratitis in a study using a direct PCR with electrophoresis assay without extracting DNA.19 Despite this, our study had demonstrated the feasibility of a single sample that could be tested for 96 subspecies of commonly implicated ocular pathogens with a processing time of 24 hours. A beneficial attribute also clearly demonstrated here is its ability to mitigate against a narrow range of tests being selected for conventional microbiology as the iCAM card detected HSV type 1 and Acanthamoeba species on these two occasions; identifying Acanthamoeba a week earlier in Patient Y. Conventional corneal culture methods suffer from limited specificity, with some authors more recently discussing the use of a ‘decision to treat’-based utility comparison.20

The largest PCR-based study was performed by Kim et al examining 108 consecutive corneal ulcer specimens by Gram and KOH staining and PCR.10 They reported greater yield and concordance with culture in fungal keratitis than bacterial ulcers. Moreover, direct PCR sequencing often revealed unusual species such as Abiotrophia defective, Stenotrophomonas and Porphyromonas but also included false positives for non-pathogenic organisms. In our study, we had a few rare organisms (Acanthamoeba, Candida and Microsporidia). It is possible that some positive results may be attributed to non-pathogenic commensals from the ocular surface, potential environmental contaminants as well as the course of clinical resolution. However, the organisms were in keeping with the clinical picture, course of resolution (requiring several months of treatment with appropriate antimicrobial) and are known species that can cause microbial keratitis. Furthermore, this is also supported by the cycle threshold (Ct values), which are inversely proportional to the quantity of target nucleic acid, with results >38 typically being considered potential environmental contaminants.21

Our study was designed as a cohort study to test the feasibility to screen for 96 microbes commonly implicated in ocular infections using a single sample. Nonetheless, we have a few limitations, first, the small sample size. A significant proportion of samples were culture and PCR negative and in particular the conventional culture methods appeared to have better detection potential for P. aeruginosa than iCAM tests. The poor performance of the iCAM card assays to detect P. aeruginosa in this study was in stark contrast to the excellent performance seen with the same two assays used on our respiratory Taqman array card.14 Interestingly, when the nucleic acid extracts from all the iCAM concordant/discordant specimens were subjected to our laboratory’s routine real-time PCR assay for detection of P. aeruginosa, the results mirrored the iCAM PCR results. The real-time PCR limit of detection may be a factor for some of the discordance observed as a sample that is reported as scant growth (and possibly light growth) by conventional culture may fall below the PCR detection limit. Also to consider is the difficulty in using minute samples such as the corneal scrapes, particularly, if the sample has been shared for both conventional and iCAM tests, especially where the sample for microarray PCR was taken after initial samples for conventional microbiology. This may also have been a confounding factor impacting Ct values, which may have been more significant depending on the order of sampling. Future methods for improving yield include utilising the entire epithelial sample for iCAM test in future trials (sharing the same sample) to reassess the microbial detection rate or using a centrifuge to better concentrate the small volume of sample. Nevertheless, we aim to retain conventional culture plates such as blood agar in future trials using iCAM PCR to substantiate its ability to detect microbial species, such as P. aeruginosa before drawing firm conclusions on superiority or indeed equal performance.

Early and accurate isolation of a causative organism is key to the success of infectious keratitis management including the timing of initiation of steroids and discharge planning. Microarray PCR carries a major advantage of a processing time of under 24 hours as compared with the two or longer days for a definitive result by culture. In theory, microarray PCR would provide a rapid and accessible means of quantifying up to 96 targets simultaneously on a single sample, with the primary disadvantage being a reduced sensitivity when compared with monoplex assays.13 Array card-based mechanisms can in some cases be less sensitive than culture as the technique detects down to the range of 3000 copies/mL. Processing time for the PCR itself is rapid and has been shown clinically to provide a result rapid enough to influence the clinical choice of antibiotics.22 Microarray PCR has been previously reported in assessing for organisms causing endophthalmitis,23 herpetic eye disease and uveitis.24

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the iCAM microarray analysis is able to identify a broad range of microbes including bacterial, viral, protozoan and fungal species including rarer species such as Microsporidia. It has the potential to replace routine cultures. Future studies should focus on improving sampling, processing to improve detection rates and its clinical impact in the overall management of microbial keratitis.
