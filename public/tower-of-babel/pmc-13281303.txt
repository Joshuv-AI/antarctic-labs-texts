SLC26A2 as a key regulator and therapeutic target in hepatocellular carcinoma: evidence from pan-cancer and mechanistic studies
Authors: Rui Wang, Xijie Zhang, Bo Ren, Wence Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) faces a critical shortage of prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. While solute carrier family 26 member 2 (SLC26A2) is known to be involved in skeletal disorders and even tumors, its specific role in HCC pathogenesis remains undefined.MethodsWe utilized public databases to conduct a comprehensive analysis of SLC26A2 across 33 different cancer types. Additionally, we performed in vitro and in vivo experiments to investigate the functional role of SLC26A2 in the biological behavior of HCC and to explore its mechanistic pathways.ResultsA pan‑cancer analysis revealed significant variability in SLC26A2 mRNA and protein levels, with prognostic implications across cancers. In HCC, SLC26A2 was identified as an independent risk factor for poor overall survival (HR = 1.539, 95% CI 1.084–2.186, p = 0.016) and correlated with higher pathological grade. Functional assays showed that silencing SLC26A2 inhibited HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis; conversely, overexpression led to opposite outcomes. Notably, silencing SLC26A2 significantly increased intracellular ROS, which was linked to subsequent modulation of the JNK/ERK/p38 MAPK signaling pathway—an effect suggested to be reversible by the antioxidant N‑acetylcysteine. In vivo studies demonstrated that silencing SLC26A2 suppressed subcutaneous tumor growth in an HCC xenograft model. Additionally, bioinformatics analysis predicted a competing endogenous RNA regulatory axis comprising the SNHG3/LINC00662–hsa-miR‑122‑5p–SLC26A2.ConclusionOur study identifies SLC26A2 as a clinically relevant biomarker and candidate therapeutic target in HCC. Mechanistically, SLC26A2 modulates JNK/ERK/p38 MAPK activity in a manner involving ROS signaling. While its biological roles extend beyond HCC, the clinical implications are most pronounced in this malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1186/s40246-026-00970-7.

Introduction

According to GLOBOCAN 2022, there were over 865,000 new cases of liver cancer and approximately 758,000 deaths from the disease worldwide [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for around 90% of all primary liver cancer cases [2]. The incidence and mortality rates of liver cancer vary significantly by geographic location, with the highest burdens observed in East and Southeast Asia, as well as parts of Africa. This variation is primarily attributed to the prevalence of chronic hepatitis virus infections. Other significant risk factors include alcohol-related liver disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, aflatoxin exposure, and metabolic syndrome [1, 3].

Despite advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, the prognosis for HCC remains poor, primarily due to late-stage diagnosis, tumor heterogeneity, and limited treatment options [4]. Curative treatments, such as surgical resection, liver transplantation, and local ablation, are only applicable for early-stage disease. For intermediate- and advanced-stage HCC, transarterial chemoembolization and systemic therapies are the main treatment options [5]. Multi-kinase inhibitors like sorafenib and lenvatinib are commonly used as first-line treatments. Recently, there have been advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors, specifically the combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab. However, response rates can vary, and drug resistance continues to be a significant challenge [4, 6].

Novel biomarkers are urgently needed for early diagnosis and prognosis assessment of HCC, as well as for discovering therapeutic targets to optimize treatment outcomes. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulation of specific solute carrier (SLC) family members drives HCC pathogenesis through distinct metabolic and signaling axes. In HCC, SLC7A11 (xCT) promotes tumor growth by facilitating cystine uptake for glutathione synthesis, thereby conferring resistance to ferroptosis and activating the NRF2 antioxidant pathway [7]. Similarly, SLC2A1 (GLUT1) mediates glucose influx to maintain glycolytic flux under hypoxia, directly linking nutrient sensing to the activation of the mTOR cascade [8]. In terms of metabolic rewiring, SLC family members play pivotal roles in HCC. Specifically, SLC25A47 promotes lipid synthesis by modulating NAD+ levels to regulate SIRT3 activity, thereby mediating AMPKα activation and enhancing SREBP activity [9]. Under low-glucose conditions, SLC2A4 drives HCC cell invasion and metastasis through the AMPKα2/HNF4A/BORIS signaling axis [10], while SLC25A51 overexpression facilitates hepatocarcinogenesis by activating SIRT5 to promote aerobic glycolysis [11]. Regarding metal ion homeostasis, loss of SLC46A1 leads to iron deficiency in hepatic tumors, correlating with the significant downregulation of iron content observed in liver tumors [12]. In the realm of non-coding RNA interactions, circSLC3A2 acts as a sponge for miR-490-3p, upregulating PPM1F expression to exert oncogenic effects [13]. Notably, SLC3A2 is closely linked to chemotherapy resistance: lncRNA SNHG1 promotes sorafenib resistance by activating the Akt pathway via SLC3A2 regulation [14], and TEAD1 induces sorafenib resistance by transcriptionally activating SLC3A2 and the subsequent mTOR signaling cascade [15]. Concerning the tumor microenvironment, G9a-mediated H3K9me2 silences SLC7A2 expression, thereby suppressing HCC metastasis and immune evasion; conversely, SLC7A2 promotes CXCL1 upregulation via the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway to recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells, establishing an immunosuppressive niche [16]. From an epigenetic perspective, SLC12A1 is significantly upregulated in HCC and is regulated by histone methylation, promoting proliferation via the WNK1/ERK5 pathway; notably, the SLC12A1 antagonist bumetanide delays xenograft tumor formation and reduces HCC tumor size in vivo [17]. Additionally, SLC16A1-AS1 is upregulated in HCC and predicts poor survival, potentially promoting proliferation by methylating and downregulating miR-141 [18]. Finally, regarding pharmacokinetics, SLCO1B1 plays a critical role in the hepatic uptake of anionic antitumor drugs, such as irinotecan, paclitaxel, and bile acid-conjugated derivatives of cisplatin; overexpression of SLCO1B1, regulated by the multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 promoter, overcomes reduced drug uptake and increased efflux, thereby improving the efficacy of chemotherapy in HCC [19]. The ​solute carrier family 26 member 2 (SLC26A2)​, also known as the ​diastrophic dysplasia sulfate transporter​, is a transmembrane anion exchanger that facilitates the transport of sulfate, chloride, bicarbonate, and other oxoanions across plasma membranes [20]. Initially identified as a connection with ​chondrodysplasias and skeletal dysplasia disorders [21], SLC26A2 has more recently been observed to be dysregulated in various cancers. Although aberrant SLC26A2 expression has been implicated in tumor proliferation and chemoresistance in several malignancies [22–24], its precise expression profile, prognostic value, and—most importantly—its functional role in driving HCC progression and shaping the TME are currently unknown.

Our goal is to investigate the expression, prognostic value, and functional relevance of SLC26A2 across various cancer types, with a particular emphasis on HCC. By integrating data from public databases and conducting experimental validations, we aim to clarify the potential role of SLC26A2 in HCC and assess its viability as a ​novel therapeutic target.

Methods and materials

Data download and processing

RNA-seq data (HTSeq-FPKM) were obtained from the UCSC Xena portal (https://xenabrowser.net/), covering 33 types of tumors, including adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), lymphoid neoplasm diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), brain lower grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), mesothelioma (MESO), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), sarcoma (SARC), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), Thymoma (THYM), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), and uveal melanoma (UVM). Supplementary Table 1 lists the number of normal and tumor samples for each cancer type. Clinical characteristics and prognostic indicators, including overall survival (OS), disease-free interval (DFI), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI), were downloaded. Additionally, expression profile data of human normal tissues were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project.​ The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LIHC miRNA expression profile was also downloaded. Single-nucleotide variation (SNV) and copy number variation (CNV) data were retrieved from the TCGA-GDC (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). To evaluate differences in ​SLC26A2​ protein levels between normal and tumor tissues, data from the Clinical Proteomics Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) were used (https://proteomics.cancer.gov/programs/cptac). Furthermore, the immunotherapy cohorts GSE35640, GSE67501, and GSE91061 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) to investigate the impact of SLC26A2 on immunotherapy.

Analysis of SLC26A2 expression

The mRNA expression of SLC26A2 in various normal human tissues was analyzed using the GTEx cohort. Following this, the differences in SLC26A2 mRNA expression and protein levels between normal and tumor tissues were compared across various cancer types in the TCGA and CPTAC cohorts, respectively.

Genomic variation profile of SLC26A2

Analyzing CNV and SNV of SLC26A2 using the cBioPortal portal (http://www.cbioportal.org/). The GISTIC2.0 module in GenePattern (https://cloud.genepattern.org/gp/pages/index.jsf) is used to identify regions of the genome that have been significantly amplified or deleted.

Association with clinical outcomes

Survival analysis was conducted using the R “survival” package. Patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression value. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to assess prognostic factors, while a univariate Cox model was employed to evaluate their prognostic value.

Analysis of associations between SLC26A2 expression and immune features, genomic instability, and DNA mismatch repair/methyltransferase-related genes

We used TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) to systematically investigate how SLC26A2 is associated with immune cell infiltration patterns across different cancer types [25]. The correlation between SLC26A2 expression and immune-related genes, tumor mutation burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes, and methyltransferase-related genes was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Additionally, the likelihood of tumor immune evasion was assessed using gene expression profiles via the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using gene sets obtained from the MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Samples were categorized into high-expression and low-expression groups based on SLC26A2 expression levels. GSEA was then conducted to identify differences in signaling pathway enrichment patterns between the two groups.

Drug sensitivity analysis

SLC26A2 expression and drug sensitivity data from CellMiner (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/) were analyzed for correlations using the “limma” and “impute” packages. The results were plotted with “ggplot2” and “ggpubr” packages.

Development of a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network involving SLC26A2 in HCC

Considering the essential role of miRNAs in the regulation of gene expression, we used the ENCORI database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn) to predict miRNAs that interact with SLC26A2 [26]. We selected candidate miRNAs based on two criteria: Spearman’s correlation < −0.3 and survival significance (log-rank p < 0.05). These selected miRNAs guided the prediction of lncRNA from the same database, with final lncRNA candidates validated using the same statistical thresholds. The interactions among miRNAs, lncRNAs, and SLC26A2 provided the foundation for constructing our competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network.

Cell culture and reagent

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines Huh7 and Hep3B were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. They were cultured in either Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, USA) or Minimum Essential Medium (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (which contains 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin). The cultures were incubated at ​37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) was acquired from MedChemExpress and dissolved in deionized water.

Cell transfection

To knock down SLC26A2, we used lentivirus-delivered short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs). A non-targeting scrambled shRNA served as the negative control. The target sequences were as follows: sh-NC: 5ʹ-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3ʹ; sh-SLC26A2-1: 5ʹ-ACTTCAAGGAGTTTGTTATTA-3ʹ; sh-SLC26A2-2: 5ʹ-TGGTGTGATCACAATTGTAAA-3ʹ. For overexpression, the full-length SLC26A2 cDNA was cloned into a vector, with an empty vector serving as the negative control. The efficiencies of both knockdown and overexpression were validated using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and western blot (WB).

Cell viability assay

In a 96-well plate, 5000 cells were seeded in each well. Then, 10 µL of the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (APExBIO, K1018) was added to each well. The plate was incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a plate reader.

RT-qPCR

To extract total RNA, use TRIzol reagent (Servicebio, Cat: G3013). Next, reverse transcribe the RNA into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, Cat: G3329). For quantification, perform qPCR with SYBR Green master mix (Servicebio, Cat: G3328) and analyze the data using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. The primer sequences used are as follows: SLC26A2 Forward: 5ʹ-GTTGGCAGCACTGTAACCTTTAT-3ʹ; SLC26A2 Reverse: 5ʹ-CCATTAGTCCGAGGAAGGTTGAG-3ʹ; GAPDH Forward: 5ʹ-GGAGTCCACTGGCGTCTTCA-3ʹ; GAPDH Reverse: 5ʹ-GTCATGAGTCCTTCCACGATACC-3ʹ.

WB

To prepare protein samples, begin by lysing cells in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Cat: P0013C), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Cat: P1045). Next, quantify the protein concentration with a Bradford protein assay kit (Biosharp, Cat: BL521A500T). Load equal amounts of protein onto an SDS-PAGE gel and run the gel at an appropriate voltage. After the gel run, transfer the separated proteins to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). Block the membrane with either 5% non-fat milk (Servicebio, Cat: GC310001) or bovine serum albumin (BioFroxx, Cat: 4240GR025) in TBST buffer (Servicebio, Cat: G0001). Incubate the membrane with the specified primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following the overnight incubation, apply HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, Cat: SA00001-1, Cat: SA00001-2, 1:10,000) to the membrane. Develop the signal using an ECL reagent and acquire images with a chemiluminescence imager. The primary antibodies used in this study include: SLC26A2 (Immunoway, Cat: YN6775, 1:5000), BCL-2 (Immunoway, Cat: YM8319, 1:5000), Bak (Immunoway, Cat: YM8371, 1:5000), caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:5000), JNK (Proteintech, Cat: 66210-1-Ig, 1:5000), phospho-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185) (Proteintech, Cat: 60666-1-Ig, 1:5000), ERK1/2 (Proteintech, Cat: 11257-1-AP, 1:5000), phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) (Proteintech, Cat: 80031-1-RR, 1:5000), p38 MAPK (Proteintech, Cat: 14064-1-AP, 1:5000), phospho-p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) (Proteintech, Cat: 28796-1-AP, 1:2000), β-tubulin (Proteintech, Cat: 10094-1-AP, 1:10,000), and GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat: 10494-1-AP, 1:10,000).

Colony formation assay

To culture the cells, place 1000 cells per well in a 6-well plate and incubate for 10 to 14 days. Replace the culture medium every 2 to 3 days. After the incubation period, fix the cells with paraformaldehyde. Stain them with crystal violet, rinse thoroughly with water, and then allow them to air dry. Finally, count the colonies.

Invasion/migration assay

Coat Transwell inserts with Matrigel (Corning, Cat: 356234) for invasion assays or leave them uncoated for migration assays. Seed treated cells (20,000 cells per plate) in serum-free medium in the upper chamber, and place complete medium in the lower chamber. Incubate for 24 h. After incubation, fix cells with paraformaldehyde, stain with crystal violet, and wash to remove non-invaded or non-migrated cells. Finally, cells count stained cells under a microscope.

Apoptosis assay

Collected cells were washed with cold PBS, resuspended in a binding buffer, and then stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI (Bestbio, BB-4101) before immediate flow cytometry analysis.

5-Ethynyl-2ʹ-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

The cells (20,000 cells per plate) were cultured in the EdU working solution (APExBIO, Cat: K2240) for 2 h. They were then fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with Triton X-100, subjected to a click reaction, and counterstained with DAPI. Images were subsequently captured.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using a ROS detection kit from Beyotime (Cat: S0033S). Cells stably expressing sh-NC, sh-SLC26A2-1, or sh-SLC26A2-2 were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were treated with 1 mL of DCFH-DA working solution diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium. After a 20-minute incubation, the cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess DCF fluorescence intensity, a marker of ROS levels.

Animal studies

The animal study was conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, as published by the NIH, and adhered to the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement). The experimental protocol was approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Second Clinical Medical College of Lanzhou University (Approval No. D2024-858). A total of 12 male BALB/c nude mice, aged 5 weeks, were used in the study, with 6 mice assigned to each group: the sh-SLC26A2-2 knockdown group and the sh-NC control group. This sample size was determined based on established conventions in the field for similar subcutaneous xenograft models [27–30], ensuring adequate statistical power while adhering to the 3Rs principle. Each mouse was individually identified with ear tags, and groups were assigned at random using a computer-generated sequence to minimize selection bias. Investigators were blinded to group assignments during tumor size measurements, which were taken weekly using calipers to measure length and width, and during endpoint analyses, including Ki-67 immunohistochemistry (IHC), TUNEL assays, and tumor volume calculations. The group identities were revealed only after all measurements and analyses were completed. In the tumor implantation experiment, 5 × 10^6 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the right back of each mouse. After a 3-week observation period, the mice were sacrificed, and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis. Tumor size was regularly monitored, and volume was calculated as: Volume = (length × width²) × 0.5.

Ki-67 staining

Paraffin sections were dewaxed using eco-friendly solvents I-III and then hydrated using anhydrous ethanols I-III. Following antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution. The sections were subsequently blocked with 3% BSA and incubated with a primary antibody. After that, an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied. For color development, DAB was added, and the reaction was stopped with tap water. The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated, and rinsed. Finally, the sections were dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions (75% ethanol, 85% ethanol, anhydrous ethanol I/II), followed by butanol and xylene I, before being mounted and imaged using bright-field microscopy.

TdT-mediated dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) assay

Follow the manufacturer’s instructions (Servicebio, Cat: G1507). First, dewax the sections to water (sequential washing with environmentally friendly dewaxing solutions I-III, anhydrous ethanol, and distilled water); then perform proteinase K repair; block endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide; prepare and add the TUNEL reaction mixture; after washing, add Streptavidin-HRP; perform DAB coloration; counterstain the nuclei with hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiate; finally, dehydrate and clear the sections through anhydrous ethanol, n-butanol, and xylene, mount the slides, and observe under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics and visualization analyses were conducted using R (v4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (v9.5). The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined as follows: *p < 0.05, ​**p < 0.01, ​​***p < 0.001, and ns indicates no significant difference. Survival analysis for Kaplan–Meier curves was performed using the log-rank test. Pairwise comparisons were evaluated with Student’s t-test, and tumor growth was assessed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Results

Analysis of SLC26A2 expression across multiple databases​

Analysis of the GTEx dataset revealed that SLC26A2 mRNA expression is significantly high in tissues such as the salivary gland, adrenal gland, lung, small intestine, and pituitary gland. In contrast, its expression levels are relatively low in skeletal muscle, heart, brain, blood, and liver (Fig. 1A).

According to the TCGA database, compared with normal tissues, SLC26A2 is upregulated in CHOL, ESCA, GBM, LIHC, THCA, and UCEC, but downregulated in BRCA, COAD, HNSC, KIRC, LUAD, LUSC, PCPG, and READ (Fig. 1B). Moreover, it exhibits higher expression levels in tumor tissues such as ACC, PRAD, UCEC, SKCM, and PCPG, while showing lower levels in TGCT, DLBC, LGG, KICH, and LIHC (Fig. 1C, tumor samples only, no normal). In the CPTAC database, compared with normal tissues, the protein level of SLC26A2 is significantly elevated in BRCA_TCGA, GBM_CPTAC, HCC_HBV, LSCC_CPTAC, PDAC_CPTAC, and UCEC_CPTAC1, but significantly downregulated in COAD_PNNL, HNSCC_CPTAC, LUAD_Academia, and OV_JHU (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, HCC_HBV, UCEC_CPTAC2, and OV_PNNL display relatively high SLC26A2 protein levels, whereas OV_JHU, HNSCC_CPTAC, and COAD_PNNL exhibit relatively low levels (Fig. 1E, tumor samples only, no normal).

Using cBioPortal, we investigated genomic alterations in SLC26A2 across various cancers (Fig. 1F). Our findings reveal that SLC26A2 exhibits notable single-nucleotide mutation rates in UCEC, SKCM, and ACC, at 4.16%, 3.62%, and 2.20%, respectively. In terms of copy number variations, KIRC and CHOL exhibit high somatic amplifications amplification frequencies of 5.87% and 2.78%, respectively. Additionally, UVM shows the highest somatic deletion frequency at 1.25%. These findings highlight SLC26A2 as a potential genetic marker and underscore the need for further research into its role in tumorigenesis and cancer progression across different malignancies.

The influence of SLC26A2 on prognosis across various cancers

High levels of SLC26A2 mRNA were associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in patients with KICH, LGG, LIHC, and UCS (Fig. 2). In contrast, better OS was observed in patients with LAML and SKCM (Fig. 2). Elevated SLC26A2 expression was associated with improved DFI in BLCA and PCPG (Fig. 2). For DSS, high SLC26A2 expression correlated with worse outcomes in patients with GBM, KICH, LGG, and USC, while it was associated with better DSS in patients with SKCM and THYM (Fig. 2). Regarding PFI, elevated SLC26A2 mRNA levels were associated with worse outcomes in BLCA, CESC, KICH, LGG, and UCS (Fig. 2). Conversely, better PFI was seen in patients with COAD, SKCM, and UCEC (Fig. 2).

Univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that SLC26A2 serves as a protective factor for OS in LAML and SKCM. However, it functions as a risk factor in KICH, LGG, LIHC, and UCS (Supplementary Fig. S1). For DFI, SLC26A2 was identified as a risk factor in some cancer types where it was associated with poorer outcomes. In terms of DSS, SLC26A2 was a protective factor in SKCM but a risk factor in LGG. Regarding PFI, SLC26A2 acted as a protective factor in UCEC, COAD, and SKCM, while it was identified as a risk factor in BLCA and LGG.

Given the significant upregulation of SLC26A2 in HCC tissues and its prognostic implications (Figs. 1, 2), we subsequently focused on delineating its functional and mechanistic role specifically in HCC.

Association of SLC26A2 with immune features, TMB, and MSI

We analyzed the correlation between SLC26A2 expression levels and immune cell infiltration in various cancers. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, in most cancers, the expression of SLC26A2 was positively correlated with B cell, cancer associated fibroblast, T cell CD4+, common lymphoid progenitor, endothelial cell, macrophage, mast cell, neutrophil, and T cell regulatory infiltration, but negatively correlated with T cell gamma delta, T cell NK, and T cell follicular helper infiltration.

In the context of genes related to immune stimulation, SLC26A2 expression is predominantly positively correlated with immune-stimulator genes across most cancers (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Notably, strong positive correlations are observed with genes such as CXCL12, CXCR4, ENTPD1, IL6R, NT5E, TNFSF13, TNFSF13B, TNFSF15, TNFSF18, and TNFSF4. In contrast, when examining immune-inhibitor genes, SLC26A2 expression also shows positive correlations with genes including CD244, CD274, CD96, CSF1R, CTLA4, KDR, LAG3, LGALS9, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, and TGFB1 in many cancers (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Furthermore, higher levels of SLC26A2 expression are significantly associated with MHC genes in cancers such as LGG, LIHC, LAML, HNSC, OV, and THCA (Supplementary Fig. S3C).

Regarding receptor-related genes, SLC26A2 expression shows significant positive correlations with a range of these genes across multiple tumor types (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Additionally, in the study of chemokines, SLC26A2 expression is positively correlated with chemokine-related genes in cancers, including TGCT, THCA, LGG, LIHC, PAAD, GBM, and HNSC. However, in UCEC, PRAD, READ, ACC, and BRCA, SLC26A2 exhibits significant negative correlations with chemokine-related genes (Supplementary Fig. S3E).

Moreover, SLC26A2 is also significantly positively correlated with MSI in MESO, but it shows a significant negative correlation with MSI in DLBC, COAD, HNSC, STAD, THCA, PRAD, and LGG (Supplementary Fig. S3F). SLC26A2 shows a remarkable positive correlation with TMB in LGG, whereas it shows a notable negative correlation with TMB in PAAD, COAD, STAD, KIRP, LUSC, LIHC, CESC, and HNSC (Supplementary Fig. S3G).

These results collectively illuminate the multifaceted role of SLC26A2 in modulating immune responses across different cancers, highlighting its potential as a biomarker or therapeutic target in cancer immunotherapy.

The relationship between SLC26A2 and DNA MMR and methyltransferase-related gene

The TCGA data indicate a positive correlation between five DNA MMR genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM) and SLC26A2 across most cancers (Supplementary Fig. S3H). This suggests that SLC26A2 may be an indicator of active DNA MMR. Furthermore, SLC26A2 expression is strongly correlated with three DNA methyltransferase genes (DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B) in certain cancers (Supplementary Fig. S3I), suggesting a link to increased DNA methylation. Collectively, these findings indicate that elevated SLC26A2 levels are associated with increased DNA MMR activity and DNA methylation, suggesting its potential role in tumor DNA maintenance and epigenetic regulation.

The relationship between SLC26A2 and immunotherapy

The research findings from the immunotherapy cohorts GSE35640, GSE67501, and GSE91061 indicated that patients resistant to immunotherapy had significantly higher SLC26A2 expression (Supplementary Fig. S4A). This suggests a negative correlation between SLC26A2 expression and immunotherapy response. Furthermore, SLC26A2 expression effectively predicted resistance to immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. S4B), suggesting that elevated SLC26A2 levels may contribute to treatment resistance and serve as a biomarker of non‑response. Future validation through real-world clinical cohorts is necessary.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Pharmacogenomic analysis using the CellMiner database revealed a significant positive correlation between high SLC26A2 expression and increased sensitivity to various anticancer agents. These agents include pyrazoloacridine, oxaliplatin, asparaginase, etoposide, epothilone B, teniposide, and elliptinium acetate. In contrast, elevated levels of SLC26A2 were associated with decreased responsiveness to several other treatments, including lapatinib, PD-98,059, dabrafenib, vemurafenib, erlotinib, vandetanib, and ibrutinib (Supplementary Fig. S5). These findings suggest that SLC26A2 may modulate drug response and warrant further verification in future experiments.

Biological function analysis of SLC26A2

The GSEA results indicated that samples with high SLC26A2 expression were significantly enriched in several tumor-related signaling pathways across various cancer types. These pathways include epithelial-mesenchymal transition, KRAS signaling-up, UV response down, androgen response, inflammatory response, hedgehog signaling, and TNFA signaling via NF-κB. In contrast, samples with low SLC26A2 expression were significantly enriched in DNA repair, Myc targets, and oxidative phosphorylation. These findings suggest that SLC26A2 may play a role in tumorigenesis or tumor progression by modulating these critical biological processes (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Examine the effect of SLC26A2 on HCC within the TCGA-LIHC cohort

Cox regression analysis revealed that SLC26A2 is an independent risk factor for the OS of HCC (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, SLC26A2 demonstrated a highly significant ability to differentiate between HCC and non-HCC cases (Fig. 3C). Additionally, it was found that HCC patients with higher pathological grades exhibited elevated levels of SLC26A2 compared to those with lower pathological grades (Fig. 3D). Following this, a nomogram was developed incorporating SLC26A2 to predict the prognosis of HCC (Fig. 3E). The calibration curve indicated robust predictive ability (Fig. 3F). ROC curve analysis indicated that the nomogram possessed a strong capacity to predict OS in HCC patients (Fig. 3G).

To explore the role of SLC26A2 in predicting immunotherapy response in HCC, we employed the TIDE framework to assess individual patient responses. Our analysis revealed that patients with high SLC26A2 expression had a significantly lower response rate to immunotherapy (Fig. 3H). In contrast, these patients displayed reduced levels of the dysfunction signature, Merck-18, and MSI expression signature.

Collectively, these results suggest that SLC26A2 may contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and predict poor response to immunotherapy in HCC. However, further verification through basic experiments or clinical studies is needed in the future.

Construction of a ceRNA regulatory network involving SLC26A2 in HCC

To investigate whether specific non-coding RNAs regulate SLC26A2, we first used the bioinformatics tool ENCORI to predict potential upstream miRNAs targeting SLC26A2 (Supplementary Table 2). Since miRNAs are generally thought to have a negative regulatory relationship with their target genes, we filtered the results based on a correlation coefficient of less than − 0.3, which left us with only hsa-miR-122-5p (Supplementary Fig. S7A). We then assessed the prognostic significance of hsa-miR-122-5p and found that high expression levels of this miRNA were associated with a favorable prognosis in HCC (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Taken together, these findings suggest that hsa-miR-122-5p is the most likely miRNA regulator of SLC26A2 in HCC. We utilized the ENCORI database to predict potential upstream lncRNAs of hsa-miR-122-5p (Supplementary Table 3). For subsequent analysis, we performed expression, survival, and correlation using the Wilcoxon test, log-rank test, and Spearman correlation, respectively. According to the ceRNA hypothesis, candidate lncRNAs should show a negative correlation with hsa-miR-122-5p and a positive correlation with SLC26A2, and exhibit significant associations with patient prognosis. Based on these criteria, we identified two lncRNAs (SNHG3 and LINC00662) as key candidates. These lncRNAs demonstrated a negative correlation with hsa-miR-122-5p (correlation coefficient < −0.3) (Supplementary Fig. S7C-D), a positive correlation with SLC26A2 expression (Supplementary Fig. S7E-F), and were significantly upregulated in HCC compared to non-HCC (Supplementary Fig. S7G-H). Additionally, patients with elevated expression levels of either SNHG3 or LINC00662 exhibited poorer prognoses (Supplementary Fig. S7I-J). Ultimately, through bioinformatics analysis, we constructed a potential integrated lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory network (SNHG3/LINC00662-hsa-miR-122-5p-SLC26A2) to demonstrate the interactions among these molecules (Supplementary Figure S7K). Further experimental verification is required.

Discussion

The solute carrier transporter family comprises membrane transport proteins that regulate the uptake and efflux of ions, metabolites, and signaling molecules. These processes are essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Among these transporters, SLC26A2 was initially identified for its essential roles in cartilage development and sulfate transport [31, 32]. While its role in skeletal dysplasia, such as diastrophic dysplasia, is well-established [33], its involvement in cancer has only recently started to gain attention.

Our pan-cancer and HCC-focused study reveals SLC26A2 as a multifaceted player in tumor biology, characterized by tumor-specific expression patterns, prognostic relevance, immune modulation, and associations with drug sensitivity. Notably, our data imply a possible role for ROS as intermediate regulators​ linking SLC26A2 to MAPK signaling in HCC. Changes in SLC26A2 expression were accompanied by corresponding shifts in intracellular ROS levels, which, in turn, were associated with alterations in MAPK activity and tumor phenotypes. These findings indicate a functional interaction between SLC26A2, ROS, and MAPK signaling in the progression of HCC, necessitating further investigation.

SLC26A2 is traditionally associated with sulfate transport in cartilage, but it actually belongs to a larger family of anion exchangers that can transport chloride, bicarbonate, and oxoanions [34]. Recent studies indicate that ion transporters, particularly those in the SLC family, are frequently dysregulated in cancers and play significant roles in tumorigenesis, metastasis, therapy resistance, and immune evasion [35, 36]. Our research shows that SLC26A2 displays tumor-type-specific expression: it is significantly upregulated in cancers, such as cholangiocarcinoma, glioblastoma, and HCC, while it is downregulated in breast, lung, and prostate adenocarcinomas. This suggests that SLC26A2 is not universally oncogenic or tumor-suppressive; rather, its impact is context-dependent, likely influenced by differences in tumor microenvironments, metabolic requirements, and lineage-specific dependencies.

This concept reflects a growing acknowledgment that solute carriers are becoming key players in tumor physiology. They influence not only nutrient availability but also pH homeostasis, redox balance, and transport of signaling molecules [37–39]. For example, specific members of the SLC family, such as SLC7A11 (also known as xCT), have been linked to glutamine metabolism, ferroptosis regulation, and T cell function in cancer [40, 41]. Our data also suggest that SLC26A2 may act as a metabolic and signaling regulator within the tumor ecosystem; however, the specific contributions of its substrates across different cancers remain to be further investigated.

Our pan-cancer survival analyses indicated that high SLC26A2 expression is associated with poor outcomes in several cancers, including KICH, LGG, HCC, and UCS. Conversely, it serves as a favorable prognostic marker in LAML and SKCM. These contrasting associations highlight the complex role of SLC26A2, which can act as a promoter or suppressor of cancer, depending on tumor type and the surrounding microenvironment. In HCC, SLC26A2 has been identified as an independent risk factor for poor OS. Higher levels of SLC26A2 expression are associated with more advanced tumor grades and decreased differentiation. This aligns with previous research that increased expression of anion transporters and genes involved in sulfate metabolism can contribute to tumor aggressiveness. The mechanisms underlying this link are not fully understood but may involve extracellular matrix remodeling, growth signaling, and inflammation [42].

From a clinical perspective, SLC26A2 holds potential as a prognostic biomarker for HCC. To contextualize its predictive value within the current clinical landscape, we compared SLC26A2 with established HCC biomarkers. While traditional serum markers such as Alpha-fetoprotein and Glypican-3 are widely used in clinical practice, they are constrained by limited sensitivity and specificity, particularly for early-stage HCC diagnosis and prognosis assessment [43, 44]. Compared to these conventional markers, our data indicate that SLC26A2 expression, especially when integrated into a nomogram, provides robust predictive accuracy for OS and correlates specifically with tumor grade and MAPK-driven phenotypes. Furthermore, unlike generic proliferation-based gene signatures, SLC26A2 uniquely links metabolic transport, redox homeostasis, and immune modulation, suggesting a more comprehensive reflection of the tumor ecosystem.

Regarding therapeutic response prediction, while existing biomarkers for drug sensitivity in HCC (e.g., VEGF expression for sorafenib response [45]) are often pathway-specific, our analysis suggests that SLC26A2 expression correlates with sensitivity to a broader spectrum of compounds. This implies that SLC26A2 may serve not only as a prognostic indicator but also as a potential companion diagnostic biomarker to stratify patients who might benefit from specific chemotherapeutic or targeted agents, potentially offering an advantage over narrower biomarkers [46].

One of the most intriguing findings in our study is the significant connection between SLC26A2 and the TME. We observed that SLC26A2 expression is positively correlated with the infiltration of various immunosuppressive cell types, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), macrophages, and MDSCs. Additionally, there is a positive association between SLC26A2 expression and the levels of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1 (CD274), CTLA4, and LGALS9. In contrast, SLC26A2 expression was negatively associated with cytotoxic lymphocytes, including NK cells and gamma delta T cells.

These findings indicate that SLC26A2 may contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, facilitating immune evasion and resistance to immune checkpoint therapies. This is especially important as the use of immunotherapies, such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 agents, is increasing in the treatment of HCC and other cancers [47–49]. Based on bioinformatic analysis of public cohorts, high SLC26A2 expression was associated with a predicted poor response to immunotherapy; however, this finding requires further validation in real‑world clinical cohorts. This finding is consistent with research showing that ion transporters and metabolic regulators can influence T cell function, dendritic cell activation, and cytokine signaling within the tumor microenvironment [50–52]. In the future, it will be important to investigate, in real-world clinical cohort studies, the relationship between SLC26A2 expression levels in tumor patients who have received immunotherapy and their treatment responses. Furthermore, additional basic research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which SLC26A2 modulates the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Furthermore, SLC26A2 showed a positive correlation with TMB in certain cancers, such as LGG, whereas it showed a negative correlation in others, such as HCC and PAAD. Additionally, it demonstrated inverse associations with MSI in various contexts. These findings suggest that SLC26A2 may play a role in DNA damage response, epigenetic regulation, or mismatch repair processes. However, the exact mechanisms involved remain unclear and require further investigation.

GSEA analysis showed that high SLC26A2 expression is associated with the activation of several oncogenic signaling pathways. These pathways include EMT, KRAS signaling, angiogenesis, inflammatory response, Hedgehog signaling, and TNF-α signaling via NF-κB. Together, these pathways are well-established drivers of cancer cell survival, invasion, metastasis, and chronic inflammation [53–55].

Functional assays demonstrated that knocking down SLC26A2 was associated with reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation and altered JNK and p38 activity. This change coincided with decreased cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and increased apoptosis. In contrast, overexpression of SLC26A2 was associated with enhanced ERK phosphorylation and altered activity of JNK and p38, characteristics consistent with malignant progression. To assess the functional contribution of this signaling axis, we conducted rescue experiments. Treatment with the JNK inhibitor SP600125 reduced the anti-proliferative effects observed with SLC26A2 knockdown, while the MEK1/2 inhibitor Trametinib diminished the pro-proliferative effects resulting from SLC26A2 overexpression. Mechanistically, our findings suggest that SLC26A2 affects the phosphorylation status of JNK, ERK, and p38 MAPK. Importantly, we observed that SLC26A2 regulates MAPK signaling in a way that involves ROS: knocking down SLC26A2 increased intracellular ROS levels, which correlated with subsequent changes in JNK, ERK, and p38 activation. Conversely, treatment with the antioxidant NAC reversed both the ROS accumulation and the alterations in MAPK pathways caused by SLC26A2 deficiency. This supports a conceptual model of an SLC26A2–ROS–MAPK cascade. Overall, these data suggest that SLC26A2 influences HCC phenotypes by modulating the JNK/ERK/p38 MAPK signaling pathway, with ROS as a potential mediator. These results position SLC26A2 as a regulator within the MAPK cascade, a pathway well-known for its critical role in HCC progression and drug resistance [56–58]. The data suggest that SLC26A2 influences HCC phenotypes by modulating the JNK/ERK/p38 MAPK signaling pathway, and this process appears to be closely associated with alterations in intracellular ROS levels. Our findings are consistent with a model in which SLC26A2 functions either upstream of or in parallel with MAPK signaling, possibly by modulating ROS production. However, the precise molecular interactions involved remain to be clarified. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which SLC26A2 regulates ROS homeostasis remain to be fully elucidated. Specifically, it remains unknown whether SLC26A2 directly interacts with mitochondrial components or regulates metabolic processes that control ROS generation. Additionally, we need formal genetic evidence to confirm whether ROS is an essential intermediate in this pathway rather than just a correlated bystander. Ultimately, unraveling the complex interactions between anion transport, redox balance, and kinase signaling will be vital for translating these findings into effective therapeutic strategies for HCC.

Based on bioinformatics predictions, we propose a putative ceRNA axis in which SNHG3 and LINC00662 form a predicted sponging complex for hsa-miR-122-5p. This bioinformatic prediction​ suggests that SNHG3/LINC00662 may derepress SLC26A2 by sequestering hsa-miR-122-5p, thereby contributing to its oncogenic overexpression. Our model is supported by inverse correlations between lncRNAs and hsa-miR-122-5p, positive correlations between lncRNAs and SLC26A2, and their association with HCC prognosis. Notably, hsa-miR-122-5p, one of the most abundant liver-specific microRNAs, is frequently downregulated in HCC and acts as a tumor suppressor by targeting genes involved in proliferation and anti-apoptosis [59–61]. This mechanism aligns with the broader role of lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks in cancer biology, in which dysregulated ceRNA interactions activate oncogenes and silence tumor suppressors. The involvement of hsa-miR-122-5p underscores the liver-specific relevance of this predicted axis and provides a plausible explanation for the elevated SLC26A2 levels observed in HCC. However, it is important to note that this ceRNA network is currently a computational prediction; its existence and functional significance remain to be experimentally validated. While the proposed ceRNA network is biologically plausible and aligns with known regulatory mechanisms, it is primarily a computational prediction at this stage. Future work should include experimental validation through approaches such as luciferase reporter assays to confirm hsa-miR-122-5p binding to the 3′UTR of SLC26A2, RT-qPCR to verify expression correlations in clinical samples, and functional rescue experiments to establish causality.

The multifaceted role of SLC26A2 in cancer biology—regulating ion homeostasis, signaling pathways, immune responses, and genomic stability—positions it as a valuable therapeutic target. By targeting SLC26A2 directly or modulating its regulatory network (for example, through hsa-miR-122-5p replacement therapy or lncRNA inhibition), it may be possible to disrupt multiple cancer hallmarks simultaneously. Additionally, the observed correlations between SLC26A2 expression and drug sensitivity suggest that SLC26A2 levels may predict responses to conventional chemotherapies and targeted treatments, paving the way for personalized therapeutic strategies.

Several limitations should be noted. Firstly, while our data support a model where SLC26A2 influences MAPK signaling in a manner involving ROS, the precise molecular hierarchy of this cascade remains to be formally established. Future investigations should employ real‑time live‑cell redox imaging and genetic approaches to definitively distinguish whether ROS acts as a necessary intermediate or a correlative byproduct. Secondly, there is currently a lack of specific pharmacological modulators for SLC26A2; however, recent advances in targeting ion transporters present promising possibilities [62]. Then, while our results are robust in HCC models and across pan-cancer datasets, further validation in diverse cancer types and prospective clinical cohorts is needed to confirm the broader therapeutic potential of targeting SLC26A2. Finally, while our study provides valuable in silico predictions regarding the ceRNA network and immunotherapy response, these findings are hypothesis‑generating rather than conclusive. Future research should focus on wet‑lab validation (e.g., luciferase assays, RNA pull‑down, and clinical sample verification) to establish causality.

Conclusion

In summary, our comprehensive pan-cancer and mechanistic study highlights SLC26A2 as a clinically significant molecule in oncology. Through multi-omics analysis, we found important correlations between SLC26A2 expression and patient prognosis, as well as bioinformatic associations with immune infiltration patterns and predicted therapy resistance, particularly in HCC. Experimental validation showed that SLC26A2 plays a role in tumorigenesis by regulating JNK/ERK/p38 MAPK signaling pathways. While the exact molecular link between SLC26A2 and ROS homeostasis remains to be investigated, our findings position SLC26A2 as a promising biomarker and potential therapeutic target in HCC.
