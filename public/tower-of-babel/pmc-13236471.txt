Trivalent fowl adenovirus vaccine (serotype-4/8b/11) provides efficient protection with long duration of immunity
Authors: Dam-Hee Park, Kyeong-Cheol Min, Na-Ri Kim, Sung-Sik Yoo, Injoong Yoon, Jongseo Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ImportanceFowl adenovirus (FAdV) is a significant viral pathogen with major economic impact on the global poultry industry. Recent epidemiological studies indicate that predominant strains in Asia, including Korea, are FAdV-4 (species C), 8b (species E), and 11 (species D).ObjectiveThis study evaluated a novel trivalent inactivated FAdV vaccine containing antigens from FAdV-4, 8b, and 11.MethodsAn SD1356-like FAdV-8b strain, representing newly emerging variants in Asia and the Middle East, was included as an immunogen. Prior to vaccination-challenge trials, pathogenicity of FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 was assessed in chickens via oral, intramuscular, and intravenous routes. These models were used to determine the minimum protective dose. Cross-neutralization assays were performed using sera from monovalent vaccinations for each serotype. Duration of immunity was evaluated in specific pathogen-free chickens following a single vaccine dose.ResultsIntravenous challenge produced the highest mortality across all serotypes, including the relatively low-pathogenic FAdV-11. A vaccine dose of 107.0 TCID50/bird induced strong neutralizing antibody responses and provided complete protection against virulent strains. Sera showed no cross-neutralization between heterologous serotypes, emphasizing the necessity of multivalent formulations. Protective antibody titers were maintained up to 78 weeks of age.Conclusions and RelevanceThe trivalent vaccine provides effective protection against predominant FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 strains. These findings support the use of multivalent vaccines to achieve broad and sustained immunity against emerging FAdV infections in poultry.

INTRODUCTION

The fowl adenovirus (FAdV) is a highly contagious viral pathogen that causes substantial economic losses in the global poultry sector, resulting in poor performance and high mortality rates. The FAdV is taxonomically grouped into five species from A to E and further divided into 12 serotypes (1–8a, 8b–11) based on their restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) profiles and serum neutralization assays [12]. The FAdV is a double-stranded DNA virus with a genomic size of 43–45 kb encoding several structural and nonstructural proteins [3]. The adenovirus capsid consists of hexon, penton base, and fiber protein. Among them, the hexon gene is the subtype-specific antigenic determinant because of the hypervariable region that produces loops of L1, L2, and L4. L1 or the hexon loop-1 is the most variable region, which is commonly used as a target to genetically determine the FAdV serotypes based on phylogenetic analysis [4]. The hexon loop-1 gene can also be analyzed for routine diagnostics in the field [5]. Recent investigations on the FAdV capsid showed that the fiber protein is also essential because of the possibility of its role in virulence and tissue tropism [6].

FAdV is associated with a wide range of diseases in avian hosts, with each serotype showing different clinical signs and lesions, ranging from high to low mortality rates. Most FAdV serotypes cause inclusion body hepatitis (IBH), showing gross lesions of pale liver, hepatomegaly, and hemorrhages on the liver surface [7]. The histopathologic lesions of IBH are characterized by hepatic necrosis with eosinophilic or basophilic intranuclear inclusion bodies in hepatocytes [8]. IBH is also well known for its sudden onset of mortality [9]. A FAdV-4 infection exhibits the highest mortality rates among the serotypes by showing typical lesions of hydropericardium syndrome (HPS) or hydropericardium-hepatitis syndrome (HHS) characterized by accumulation of straw-colored, clear transudates in the pericardial sac associated with hepatic and necrotic lesions [10]. Another typical lesion of FAdV, called gizzard erosion, can be observed in FAdV-1-infected chickens [1112]. An FAdV infection can occur at any age, but the severity of the disease varies according to age. The pathogenicity of FAdV infections increases dramatically when young chicks are infected [13].

FAdVs can be transmitted horizontally via the oral-fecal route because of the high shedding titers in the feces [14]. The horizontal transmission of FAdVs can be commonly observed in birds associated with immunosuppression due to other viral infections such as infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) and chicken infectious anemia virus [1516]. Despite this, some studies suggest that FAdV can also function as a stand-alone pathogen without any predisposing factors or co-infections [1718]. Vertical transmission is one of the important biological properties of FAdVs, and having the virus transmitted to progenies via embryonated eggs is well known [1920]. Therefore, the maternal antibodies passed down from the parents to their progeny play a substantial role in protecting against the disease.

Certain FAdV serotypes can be detected depending on the geographical sphere. In the past, FAdV-4 from species C was considered the predominant serotype circulating in Asia, particularly in Korea and China [2122]. Nevertheless, outbreaks of FAdV-4 declined rapidly following the introduction of commercialized inactivated FAdV-4 vaccines. FAdV-8b from species E and FAdV-11 from species D outbreaks began to increase [23], suggesting that current commercialized vaccines (FAdV-4) do not provide cross-protection against these serotypes. Recent studies have identified a notable global increase in the incidence of serotypes 8b and 11, suggesting an emerging concern for the poultry sector worldwide [2425]. Vaccination is recognized as one of the primary control measures against this disease.

An inactivated trivalent FAdV (4, 8b, and 11) vaccine was developed to achieve efficient disease control because the efficacy of trivalent vaccines has been well studied and proven [2326]. This study evaluated the pathogenicity of several FAdV strains (4, 8b, and 11) and determined the minimum protective dose of the developed trivalent vaccine against these strains in vaccination-challenge trials. In addition, monovalent FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 vaccines were also prepared separately from the parental strains of the trivalent vaccine to evaluate the cross-neutralization properties of the induced antibodies among serotypes.

METHODS

Vaccine preparation

The samples containing the FAdV strains used in this study, ADL190734 (FAdV-4), ADL190167 (FAdV-8b), and ADL182047 (FAdV-11), were kindly provided by Avinext, Korea. The seeds for the trivalent vaccine were selected based on a previous epidemiological study of Korean FAdVs conducted between 2013 and 2019 [27].

The viruses were isolated from livers showing multiple petechial hemorrhages and hepatomegaly from the carcasses of infected chickens. The 10% liver homogenates were prepared by mincing the liver tissues into small pieces with sterilized scissors in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% gentamicin. After three freezing and thawing cycles, the homogenates were centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatants of the liver homogenates were collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter (Sartorius, Germany). The filtered liver homogenates were inoculated into specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated eggs via the chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) with a volume of 0.1 mL. The CAMs were collected after 3–5 days of incubation at 37°C. The viruses were then purified and propagated through serial passage in SPF embryonated eggs, using the modified methods described elsewhere [2829]. The 10% CAM homogenates were prepared according to the procedure reported previously and subsequently inoculated into SPF embryonated eggs and went through three serial passages. The viruses were then inoculated into chicken leghorn male hepatocellular (LMH) cells for propagation. The trivalent FAdV vaccine was prepared by harvesting the propagated viruses from LMH cells and inactivating them by adding 37% formaldehyde with a final concentration of 0.2%. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests were performed to confirm the absence of potential pathogens that could grow in SPF embryonated eggs and LMH cells, including infectious bronchitis virus, Newcastle disease virus, avian influenza virus, poxvirus (POXV), infectious laryngotracheitis, mycoplasma, and IBDV; all results were negative. The inactivated viruses were then emulsified with a water-in-oil adjuvant called CAvant WO-60 (CAVAC, Korea) [30]. This specific adjuvant was a water-in-oil emulsion type, containing high-grade injectable mineral oil along with a refined system of non-ionic hydrophilic and non-ionic lipophilic surfactants. This formulation can induce high levels of antigen-specific immunoglobulin G antibodies, enhancing the immune response effectively. The combination of these surfactants ensures stability and efficacy in promoting a strong, prolonged immune response, making it suitable for use in vaccines requiring robust immunogenicity. The physicochemical properties of the vaccine were evaluated based on its acidity, peroxide value, iodine value, saponification value, and viscosity.

Molecular serotype identification of FAdV isolates

The serotypes of the FAdV isolates were verified by amplifying the region coding for the L1 loop of the hexon gene using Ex Taq DNA polymerase (Takara, Japan) with primer pairs, hexon A (5’-CAARTTCAGRCAGACGGT-3’) and hexon B (5’-TAGTGATGMCGSGACATCAT-3’) [31]. The PCR products were confirmed by electrophoresis in a 2% agarose gel. The nucleotide sequences of the FAdV were examined using the Sanger sequencing method, conducted by SFC probes (Korea). The sequences of FAdV-4, 8b, and 11, along with 48 additional FAdV sequences, were extracted from GenBank. These sequences were assembled and aligned using the ClustalW multiple alignment method within the BioEdit software (version 7.2.5) [32]. The alignment data were then exported to the Mega version 10 software (http://www.megasoftware.net) for phylogenetic analysis. A phylogenetic tree of the FAdV L1 loop of the hexon gene was constructed using the maximum-likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Pathogenicity of the FAdV isolates

The pathogenicity of the FAdV isolates (FAdV-4, ADL190734; FAdV-8b, ADL190167; FAdV-11, ADL182047) was determined; 120 SPF chickens at 9 weeks of age were allocated randomly into 12 groups (n = 10 per group). Nine groups of 90 SPF chickens were challenged with FAdV isolates via the intravenous, intramuscular, and oral routes. The number of experimental birds used in this study was determined based on the power sampling size [33]. The remaining 30 chickens were kept as negative controls and challenged with PBS. The clinical signs were monitored until 14 days of challenge. Cloacal swabs were taken at 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 days post challenge (dpc) to determine the positivity rates by conventional PCR [31].

Serological analysis

The neutralizing antibody titers for each FAdV serotype used in this study were evaluated using a serum neutralization test on LMH cells infected individually with each serotype. Prior to testing, all sera underwent heat-inactivation for 30 min at 56°C. The interaction between the constant virus (200 tissue culture infectious dose 50 [TCID50]) and two-fold diluted antisera was observed daily up until seven days post-inoculation (dpi). The neutralizing antibody titers were established by finding the reciprocals of the maximum serum dilution that resulted in the inhibition of the FAdV-specific cytopathic effect (CPE).

Minimum protective dose of the inactivated trivalent FAdV vaccine

After confirming that the physicochemical property value of the trivalent vaccine was within the normal range, 120 SPF chickens at 6 weeks of age were used to determine the minimum protective dose of the inactivated trivalent FAdV vaccine. The SPF chickens were equally divided into 4 groups (n = 30) depending on the dose (each antigen dose of 106.5, 107.0, and 107.5 TCID50/bird), including the control group. Each group was further divided into three subgroups (n = 10) depending on the challenged FAdV serotypes (FAdV-4, ADL190734; FAdV-8b, ADL190167; and FAdV-11, ADL182047). The chickens were vaccinated via the intramuscular route at 6 weeks of age with 0.5 mL per dose, which was the standard dose used widely for inactivated poultry vaccines [34353637]. The serum samples were collected at 21 dpi to measure the neutralizing antibody titers. After serum collection, all birds were challenged with virulent homologous FAdV strains via the intravenous route. After the challenge, cloacal swabbing was performed at 5 dpc, and the challenged chickens were monitored daily for 14 days to observe the clinical signs.

The duration of immunity (DOI) test of the inactivated trivalent FAdV vaccine

The DOI of the trivalent FAdV vaccine was determined by vaccinating ten 14-week-old SPF chickens at a dose of more than 107.0 TCID50/bird. The chickens were monitored until they reached 78 weeks of age, and they were bled at 8-week intervals to measure the neutralizing antibody titers against FAdV-4, 8b, and 11.

Cross-neutralization assay

The cross-neutralizing antibody response of each vaccine strain against heterologous FAdV serotypes was evaluated by preparing the inactivated monovalent vaccines representing each serotype (FAdV-4, 8b, and 11) from the parental strains of the trivalent vaccine at a dose of 107.0 TCID50/bird. Each monovalent vaccine was inoculated into 6-week-old SPF chickens (n = 10 per vaccine) via the intramuscular route; 10 SPF chickens were kept as the negative controls. The serum was collected at 21 dpi for cross-neutralization tests, following the standard protocol [38].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed with Graphpad prism 11 software (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Neutralizing antibody titers (log2) were compared among vaccination-dose groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post hoc multiple-comparison Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference test. Mortality proportions and viral detection rates were compared using Fisher’s exact test. The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Phylogenetic analysis of FAdV strains

Based on the hexon loop 1 (L1) sequence, FAdV strains ADL19073, ADL190167, and ADL182047 were identified as species C, serotype 4; species E, serotype 8b; and species D, FAdV-11, respectively (Fig. 1). The FAdV-8b ADL190167 strain was grouped into the SD1356-like FAdV-8b sub-cluster 1, which consists of recently isolated Korean FAdV-8b strains. This strain also showed high nucleotide similarity with the strains from the FAdV-8b sub-cluster 2, which represents old Korean FAdV-8b strains.

Pathogenicity of FAdV isolates

The experiment of the pathogenicity of the FAdV isolates used in this study showed that a challenge via the intravenous route resulted in the highest mortality rates compared to other routes. The FAdV-4 isolate exhibited a 100% mortality rate following intravenous challenge. In contrast, the mortality rates were significantly lower in the other intramuscular/oral routes, with observed mortality rates of 60% and 0%, respectively. A comparable trend was observed with FAdV-8b and 11, where the mortality rates were 80% and 60%, respectively, via the intravenous route. Nevertheless, no mortalities were observed in the birds challenged via the intramuscular and oral routes.

All mortalities were observed within 5 dpc, exhibiting typical postmortem lesions of a FAdV infection, such as swelling, paleness, hemorrhage of the liver, and the development of HPS. The dead chickens were necropsied and examined for FAdV gross lesions such as liver swelling, diffused or focal paleness, HPS, and petechial hemorrhage (Fig. 2). Postmortem histopathological analysis confirmed the lesions of classic IBH in all non-vaccinated control groups (Fig. 3).

Postmortem examination of vaccinated-challenged groups inoculated with the minimum protective dose (107.0 TCID50/bird) and positive challenge controls showed distinct differences in gross lesions. All positive challenge controls exhibited enlarged and pale livers, with some showing multiple petechial hemorrhages, whereas the vaccinated-challenged groups did not display any lesions. DISCUSSION

Since FAdVs were first reported in 1949, they have been recognized as critical viral pathogens for decades in the worldwide poultry sector because of their widespread prevalence and substantial economic implications [39]. The global distribution of FAdV serotypes varies according to country and region, based on their distinct geographical locations [23]. Developing FAdV vaccines that can confer broad cross-protection against multiple serotypes can be complicated because the FAdV does not naturally cross-neutralize among different serotypes. The dominant serotypes currently prevalent in Asia, leading to severe economic implications, are FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 [2127]. A trivalent vaccine targeting FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 was developed and evaluated to develop a broadly protective vaccine against these serotypes. In addition, recent epidemiological studies showed that a new group of FAdV-8b derivatives that shared genetic similarities with the Chinese SD1356 strain has emerged during recent years, forming a distinct cluster with the former 764 strain-like FAdV-8b strains in Korea [27]. Hence, the vaccine seed for serotype 8b was selected from the new cluster of SD1356-like strains. This particular strain from China has been clustered collectively with recent emergent strains from Korea, and FAdV-8b strains identified in neighboring countries like Japan, as well as distant countries such as Iran and Indonesia [21]. This emphasizes the vital importance of including an SD1356-like strain representative of a newly forming cluster worldwide in this newly updated trivalent vaccine.

In this study, the pathogenicity of the FAdV isolates was the highest in FAdV-4, followed in order by 8b and 11. Chickens challenged with FAdV-8b or 11, whether orally or through intramuscular routes, did not exhibit any mortality. In contrast, birds given FAdV-4 showed a 60% mortality rate via the intramuscular route, while no deaths were observed when the virus was administered orally. Challenging the isolates in the form of liver homogenates via the intravenous route could induce higher pathogenicity, with FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 showing 100%, 80%, and 60% mortality rates, respectively. FAdV-4 is the serotype that induces severe pathogenicity in various experimental settings [23]. On the other hand, the challenge methods implemented in this study, particularly intravenous inoculation, were sufficiently potent to induce high mortality rates across all groups, even those involving relatively low-pathogenic serotypes. Previous studies, although not involving birds, reported similar findings where viruses from the Poxviridae family showed increased virulence when challenged intravenously [40]. The route of challenge appears to play an essential role in disease progression. A more rapid disease severity was observed in other mammals infected with POXVs via the intravenous route compared to other challenge methods [41]. Moreover, when the challenge virus is introduced intravenously into the host, it may bypass the initial defense mechanisms such as mucosal barriers and local immune responses, which allows the virus to quickly reach various organs and tissues, leading to a more widespread and severe infection [42]. In the present study, evident hepatic gross lesions of FAdV were observed during necropsies (Fig. 3). This indicates the potential for formulating a more accurate FAdV challenge model, preventing any underestimation of the potency of the challenge strain. Furthermore, for a comprehensive evaluation of the pathogenicity and virus shedding, viral DNA was extracted from cloacal swabs and amplified via PCR. All cloacal swabs collected from each of the challenge groups (G1–G9) at 3 and 5 dpc showed 100% positive rates (Table 1). Considering that all mortalities occurred within 5 dpc, cloacal swabbing was planned for 5 dpc in the vaccination-challenge trials to determine the minimum protective dose of the inactivated trivalent vaccine. This preliminary challenge experiment allowed finalization of the challenge protocol. In the vaccination-challenge trials, chickens vaccinated with doses of 107.0 TCID50/bird or higher with the inactivated trivalent vaccine induced robust neutralizing antibody levels, resulting in complete protection against the FAdV challenge. By contrast, vaccine doses below 107.0 TCID50/bird, such as 106.5 TCID50/bird, failed to induce adequate neutralizing antibody levels, failing to confer complete protection and leading to high mortality rates. Incorporating an adequate amount of antigen in the vaccine is important because the efficacy of inactivated vaccines is linked directly to the induction of a humoral immune response and the production of neutralizing antibodies. Consequently, the minimum protective dose of the FAdV inactivated trivalent vaccine was established as 107.0 TCID50/bird. The history of vaccine protection against intravenous challenge exposure is relatively difficult to achieve in other viruses [43]. Considering that high mortalities were observed even with the relatively low pathogenic FAdV strains 8b and 11 during the preliminary challenge trials, which was not the case with other routes, this specific route may have served to rigorously test the efficacy of the vaccine in the subsequent vaccination-challenge trials.

Overcoming antigenic interference in cocktail vaccines containing more than one antigen is a crucial factor for determining the protective efficacy of the vaccine. With the emergence of new multiple serotypes and subgroups, the development of inactivated multivalent vaccines presents a viable strategy for comprehensive FAdV prevention. Nevertheless, the potential for immune interference often makes the development of cocktail vaccines a complex task in practice. Fortunately, the results from this study indicate minimal immunological interference between the three antigens in their ability to prompt virus-specific immune responses. This offers a proof-of-concept that could suggest a way for the further development of multivalent vaccines offering broad protection against FAdV. This competition could explain why higher titers are required for trivalent vaccines to induce sufficient antibodies. In addition, the use of adjuvants plays a critical role in enhancing the immunogenicity of trivalent vaccines. Adjuvants are particulary important in inactivated vaccines in order to induce high immune responses. Several studies have revealed the effectiveness of various adjuvants, such as poly [di (sodium carboxylatoethylphenoxy) phosphazene] [EO1] and avian beta defensin saponin, [c2] in boosting the immune responses in inactivated vaccines. Previous studies have revealed the effectiveness of various adjuvants, such as poly [di (sodium carboxylatoethylphenoxy) phosphazene] and avian beta defensin saponin, in boosting the immune responses in inactivated vaccines [4445]. Thus, the choice of adjuvant in the proposed trivalent vaccine formulation likely contributed to its enhanced immunogenicity and protective efficacy.

The usage and protective efficacy of bivalent vaccines against IBH have been examined because of the well-known poor cross-protectivity between FAdV-8b and 11 [9]. In an inactivated form, such vaccines could confer adequate protection against challenge strains throughout FAdV-D to FAdV-E [46]. Similarly, bivalent live and inactivated vaccines containing FAdV-8b and 11 antigens have been proven to be equally effective and have the potential to control IBH [47]. In this specific study, the two types of vaccines could induce neutralizing antibodies against FAdV strains from FAdV-D and FAdV-E equally. TCID50/bird. Lastly, regarding cross-neutralization assays with sera collected from chickens inoculated with monovalent vaccines containing each type of antigen from the trivalent vaccine, the lack of cross-neutralization among these single antigens suggests that the broadened protection observed with the trivalent formulation was achieved by combining the independent protective effects of each antigen, rather than through any novel synergistic interaction. Immunological interference was not observed when the three antigens were combined. The sera from chickens inoculated with an experimental monovalent vaccine for each serotype did not show cross-neutralization against other heterologous serotypes. There are numerous controversies regarding whether inactivated FAdV vaccines provide cross-protection among the heterologous serotypes. The serotypes of FAdVs are generally classified based on their RFLP profiles and serum neutralization assays [1]. On the other hand, some articles claim that inactivated monovalent FAdV vaccines show cross-neutralization among heterologous serotypes [47]. According to the present data, the antibodies induced by the experimental monovalent vaccines based on Korean FAdV strains did not confer cross-protection among heterologous serotypes, supporting the necessity of a multivalent inactivated vaccine, which can provide 100% protection against the challenge of homologous FAdV strains.

Some regions around the world primarily use autogenous vaccines because of their cost-effectiveness and the advantage of rapid development. According to some studies, however, autogenous vaccines can exhibit lower efficacy than oil-adjuvanted cell-cultured IBH vaccines [48]. In addition, because FAdV-4, 8b, and 11 occur in many countries worldwide [23], the trivalent adenovirus vaccine developed in the present study, including the updated SD-1356-like 8b strain, could help reduce the impact of newly emerging adenovirus outbreaks in Asia, the Middle East, and other regions around the world. Beyond the scope of this experimental setting, the trivalent vaccine will also be tested under field conditions. The approach of developing a multivalent vaccine is anticipated to enhance control and prevention efforts against the FAdV in the near future.
