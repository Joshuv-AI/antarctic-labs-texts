Existence, uniqueness, and time-asymptotics of regular solutions in multidimensional thermoelasticity on domains with boundary
Piotr Michał Bies

Abstract
In the paper, we investigate the nonlinear thermoelasticity model in two- and three-dimensional convex and bounded domains. We propose new boundary conditions for the displacement. These conditions are not usual in thermoelasticity. Whereas, we posit the Neumann boundary condition for the temperature. We prove the existence of global, unique solutions for small initial data. The temperature positivity is also shown. Next, we investigate the long-time behavior of solutions. We show that the divergence-free part of the displacement oscillates. On the other hand, we prove that the potential part and the temperature are strongly coupled. The non-rotation part is strongly affected by heat propagation. It turns out that it tends to 0 0 as x x approaches infinity. Additionally, the temperature converges to a constant function. Our techniques are firmly based on the functional ℱ \mathcal{F} adopted from Bies, P. M., Cieślak, T., Fuest, M., Lankeit, J., Muha, B., and Trifunović, S. , Existence, uniqueness, and long-time asymptotic behavior of regular solutions in multidimensional thermoelasticity , arXiv: 2507.20794, 2025. The functional is based on the Fisher information and higher-order derivatives of the displacement. It responds well to the new boundary conditions. It allows us to close a priori estimates. We also need L ∞ L^{\infty} -estimates for the temperature here. The Moser iterative procedure ensures it. The Helmholtz decomposition is applied to the displacement. The boundary conditions are crucial here. The boundary integrals that appear in the calculations at this point disappear thanks to these conditions. This allows us to split the problem into two separate ones. Each of them is associated with one part of the Helmholtz decomposition.



Keywords:
thermoelasticity, strong solutions, time-asymptotics, the second law of thermodynamics, the Fisher information

Introduction

Let us take
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that
\Omega
is open, bounded, and convex with a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. We want to investigate the following problem

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{tt}-(2\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}+\mu\curl\curl\mathbf{u}=-\nu\nabla\theta&\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta=-\nu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}&\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\ \mathcal{N}\mathbf{u}=0,\ \nabla\theta\cdot\mathbf{n}=0&\quad\text{ on }(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\
\mathbf{u}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{u}_{0},\ \mathbf{u}_{t}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{v}_{0},\ \theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0}>0,&\end{cases}

(1.1)

where the operator
\mathcal{N}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\mathbf{u}=\begin{cases}\curl\mathbf{u}&\textrm{for }d=2,\\
\curl\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{n}&\textrm{for }d=3.\end{cases}

Functions
\mathbf{u}\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\theta\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are unknown. We interpret
\mathbf{u}
as a displacement of the body and
\theta
as its temperature. The existence of a global-in-time, strong, and unique solution for small initial data, as well as the long-time asymptotic behavior of solutions, are the main results of the paper.

Let us assume that
d=2
for a while. For given vector field
\mathbf{w}=(w^{1},w^{2})\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define
\curl\mathbf{w}=w_{x_{1}}^{2}-w^{1}_{x_{2}}
. On the other hand, for a scalar function
g\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
\curl g=\nabla^{\perp}g=(g_{x_{2}},-g_{x_{1}})
. Then, a vector field
\mathbf{w}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfies an analogous formula as vector fields for
d=3
, i.e.

\displaystyle\curl\curl\mathbf{w}=\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{w}-\Delta\mathbf{w}.

The constant
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
is given. Constants
\lambda
and
\mu
are also fixed and are so-called the Lamé moduli (see
[
21
,
28
]
). They satisfy

\displaystyle\mu>0\quad\textrm{ and }\quad 2\mu+d\lambda>0.

Other constants are assumed to be equal
1
for simplicity. The operator

\displaystyle(2\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}-\mu\curl\curl\mathbf{u}

(1.2)

is called the Lamé operator. It can also be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\left(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+\lambda\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}I\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla^{T}\mathbf{u}\right)
is the linearized deformation tensor (see
[
30
,
28
]
). In this form, we see a symmetry of the operator. However, for our purposes, the form in (
1.2
) is more suitable. We can treat the Lamé operator as a generalization of the Laplacian. It is more appropriate to consider the former than the latter from an engineering point of view.

We posit the Neumann boundary condition for the temperature. It is a natural setting in this context (see
[
11
,
21
]
). It means that the heat cannot escape from the body. The standard boundary condition for the displacement is of the Dirichlet type (see
[
21
,
32
,
31
,
20
,
10
,
11
,
25
,
26
]
and others). On the other hand, the Neumann boundary condition for
\mathbf{u}
is also studied (see
[
21
,
25
,
29
]
) or even the Neumann condition for the stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})+\lambda\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}I-\nu\theta I
. We and T. Cieślak considered the Dirichlet condition for
\mathbf{u}
in one-dimensional domains in
[
4
,
5
]
. We prove the existence and uniqueness of global strong solutions and investigate the time-asymptotic behavior. Our methods are hardly based on estimates, including the Fisher information. The application of them to the system with the Dirichlet condition for the displacement in higher dimensions presents certain technical challenges related to boundary integrals. Hence, first, we, in cooperation with T. Cieślak, M. Fuest, J. Lankiet, B. Muha, and S. Trifunović, studied the problem on the multidimensional torus in
[
6
]
. We omitted issues interlocked with the boundary conditions in this manner.

J. Březina and E. Feireisl study the compressible magnetohydrodynamics system in 3d in
[
8
]
. They propose a few physically grounded boundary conditions for the magnetic field vector. One of these is the conditions we have set for displacement, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathcal{N}\mathbf{u}=0\quad\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega.

(1.3)

Furthermore, the authors state that the Dirichlet boundary condition for the magnetic field renders the compressible magnetohydrodynamic system overdetermined. Perhaps, the same issue we have in thermoelasticity.

It is novel to posit (
1.3
) for the displacement in the context of thermoelasticity. Nevertheless, they fit well with our methods. They enable us to prove the same results as in
[
6
]
. Indeed, we are able to close calculations with a functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t})^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}dx+(2\mu+\lambda)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx\right)

involving the Fisher information and the higher-order derivatives of
\mathbf{u}
thanks to (
1.3
). In addition, the displacement equipped with (
1.3
) responds well to the Helmholtz decomposition. It means that
\mathbf{u}
can be decomposed into the curl-free part and the divergence-free part, which satisfy the appropriate equations. Let us point out that the functional
\mathcal{F}
is a key tool in our analysis.

System (
1.1
) emerges as the simplest nonlinear system in the context of thermoelasticity. It is coherent with the following Helmholtz free energy function

\displaystyle H(\nabla\mathbf{u},\theta)=\frac{\lambda}{2}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}+\mu|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{u})|^{2}+\theta-\theta\log\theta-\nu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}.

We refer the reader to
[
21
,
11
,
29
]
for a rigorous derivation of the model. Let us perceive that the system exhibits the conservation of energy. Indeed, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 1.1
.

Regular solutions
\theta,\mathbf{u}
of (
1.1
) satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{|}u_{t}|^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\theta dx\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{|}v_{0}|^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0})^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\theta_{0}dx.\end{split}

(1.4)

Proof.

Let us multiply the first equation in (
1.1
) by
\mathbf{u}_{t}
and integrate over
\Omega
. Integrating by parts, thanks to the boundary conditions for
\mathbf{u}
, gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

(1.5)

Next, we integrate the lower equation in (
1.1
) over
\Omega
. Due to the boundary conditions for
\theta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\theta dx=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

We add the above equality to (
1
). It asserts the claim.
∎

In the definition below, we explain what solutions to equation (
1.1
) we will be looking for. First, we introduce the following function space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:=\left\{\mathbf{w}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\colon\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}

with the norm

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathcal{H}}:=\left(\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\curl\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(1.6)

We refer to Subsection
2.2
for further information about
\mathcal{H}
and its norm.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that
(\mathbf{u},\theta)
is a solution to (
1.1
) if:

•

The initial data is of regularity

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{H},\quad\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathcal{H},\quad\theta_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega).

We also require that there exists
\tilde{\theta}>0
such that
\theta_{0}(x)\geq\tilde{\theta}
for all
x\in\Omega
.

•

Solutions
\theta
and
\mathbf{u}
satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{u}&\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{H})\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty;\mathcal{H})\cap W^{2,\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega)),\\
\theta&\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}_{\operatorname{loc}}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega)).\end{split}

(1.7)

•

The momentum equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{tt}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}dx+(2\mu+\lambda)\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\varphi}dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}\curl\mathbf{u}\cdot\curl\boldsymbol{\varphi}dx=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}dx

(1.8)

is satisfied for almost all
t\in(0,\infty)
and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}
.

•

The entropy equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta_{t}\psi dx+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\psi=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}\psi dx

(1.9)

holds for almost all
t\in(0,\infty)
and for all
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

•

Initial conditions are attained in the following sense:

\theta\in C([0,\infty);H^{1}(\Omega)),

u\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}),\ u_{t}\in C([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega)).

Remark 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
be as in Definition
1.2
. Then, it can be easily shown that they satisfy the momentum equation and the heat equation in (
1.1
) pointwise almost everywhere on
(0,\infty)\times\Omega
. Moreover, the boundary conditions are satisfied in the sense of the trace.

We establish the existence and uniqueness of solutions for small initial values of (
1.1
) in the first main result of the article. We formulate it in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a constant
D>0
, dependent on
d
,
|\nu|
, and
\Omega
, such that for any initial values satisfying

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+(2\mu+\lambda)\|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mu\|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\nabla\theta_{0}}{{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{0}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq D

there exists an unique solution to problem (
1.1
) as described in Definition
1.2
. Moreover, we have that there exist constants
C,c>0
such that

\displaystyle c\leq\theta\leq C\quad\textrm{for almost all }(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\Omega.

(1.10)

In the second main result, we characterize the long-time behavior of solutions obtained in Theorem
1.4
. The Helmholtz decomposition plays a crucial role here. It turns out that the potential part of the displacement behaves completely different than the divergence-free part as time approaches infinity. The latter demonstrates the independence of the former, as well as of the temperature. It will be shown that it satisfies the homogeneous wave equation and exhibits oscillatory behavior. We have a different issue with the curl-free part of the displacement. It is strongly coupled to the temperature. The heat propagation process causes the damping effect, which eventually leads to the vanishing of the rotation-free part of
\mathbf{u}
. On the other hand, the temperature tends to a constant function. An analogous asymptotic behavior we have in the case of (
1.1
) on the torus (see
[
6
]
). Let us also notice that the potential part of
\mathbf{u}
and the temperature exhibit the same asymptotics as
u
and
\theta
in the 1d case (see
[
5
]
). Thus, we see that they behave as the second principle of thermodynamics predicts. We denote the rotation-free part of
\mathbf{u}
,
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\mathbf{v}_{0}
as
\boldsymbol{\chi}
,
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}
, respectively. Let us formulate our result regarding the long-time behavior of solutions of (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let us take the initial data such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+(2\mu+\lambda)\|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mu\|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\nabla\theta_{0}}{{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{0}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq D,

(1.11)

where the constant
D
is taken from Theorem
1.4
. Moreover, let us assume that
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
are solutions of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.2
, which start respectively from initial data
\mathbf{u}_{0}
,
\mathbf{v}_{0}
and
\theta_{0}
. Then, the following convergences hold

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to 0\textrm{ in }\mathcal{H}\textrm{ when }t\to\infty,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to 0\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\Omega)\textrm{ when }t\to\infty,

\displaystyle\theta(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\to\theta_{\infty}\textrm{ in }L^{2}(\Omega)\textrm{ when }t\to\infty,

where
\theta_{\infty}:=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\theta_{0}dx\right)/|\Omega|
.

Regarding the long-time behavior, we should mention works

. The constant

Preliminaries

This section is devoted to introducing general results that will be utilized later. We start by citing the lemma from
[
15
]
. It will be crucial in proving Lemma
3.1
. It states that a certain integral, which will appear in the proof, is nonnegative.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth, bounded, and convex set. Suppose that
v\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega.

Then, the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\nabla v|^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\leq 0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega

holds.

The next lemma presents the unusual inequality between the norms of the gradient of a function and its Laplacian. The proof can be found in
[
17
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
\frac{\partial v}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, then the following inequality

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

is satisfied and
C
depends only on
d
and
\Omega
.

As in previous papers about thermoelasticity (
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
), the following inequality will also be important. It estimates the integral that will appear in the calculations. The proof can be found in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
w\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be a positive function such that
\frac{\partial w}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}w^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}w|\nabla^{2}\log w|^{2}dx,

where
C=1+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}+\frac{d}{8}
.

In the following theorem, the Helmholtz decomposition of a vector field is stated. It will be applied to decompose the displacement in Subsection
5.1
. For the proof, see e.g.
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let us assume that
\mathbf{v}\in H^{k}(\Omega)
is a vector field, where
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Then,
\mathbf{v}
can be decomposed uniquely as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=H\mathbf{v}+H^{\perp}\mathbf{v},

where
H\mathbf{v},H^{\perp}\mathbf{v}\in H^{k}(\Omega)
satisfy

\operatorname{div}H\mathbf{v}=0\quad\textrm{ and }\quad H\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}\textrm{ on }\Omega,

and there exists function
\varphi\in H^{k+1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx=0\quad\textrm{ and }\quad H^{\perp}\mathbf{v}=\nabla\varphi.

Moreover,
H\mathbf{v}
and
H^{\perp}\mathbf{v}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

2.1
Entropy

We introduce simple facts about the entropy of (
1.1
) in this subsection. Let us assume that
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
are solutions of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.2
. Moreover, let us assume for a while that

(it will be proven below). Then, we can study the evolution of the entropy. Indeed, let us denote

. Then, the following equation is satisfied

\displaystyle\tau_{t}-\Delta\tau-|\nabla\tau|^{2}=-\nu\operatorname{div}u_{t}

.
An analogous line of reasoning to that in
[
11
]
leads us to the following proposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\theta>0
and
\mathbf{u}
be solutions of the problem (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.2
. Then, the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\tau dx=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tau|^{2}dx

(2.2)

is satisfied for almost all
t\in(0,\infty)
. Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\nabla\tau\in L^{2}((0,\infty)\times\Omega)

and

\displaystyle\tau\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{1}(\Omega)).

Proof.

Equation (
2.2
) follows immediately from Equation (
2.1
). Indeed, we integrate (
2.1
) over
\Omega
and utilize the boundary conditions for
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
. Next, let us fix

and let us integrate (
2.2
) over
[0,t]
. It gives that

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\tau dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tau|^{2}dxds=-\int_{\Omega}\tau_{0}dx,

where
\tau_{0}:=\ln\theta_{0}
. We add the above inequality to (
1.4
). It gives that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\theta-\tau)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tau|^{2}dxds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0})^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\theta_{0}-\tau_{0})dx.

The last integral on the right-hand side is finite and positive, so integrals on the left-hand side are bounded for almost all
t
. It asserts the thesis.
∎

Let us perceive that (
2.2
) is a quantitative version of the second principle of thermodynamics. In Section
5
, it will play a crucial role in demonstrating that the
\omega
-set of system (
1.1
) consists of a single element.

2.2
Eigenfunctions of vector Laplacian

In this subsection, we will study the eigensystem of the following problem: we want to find
\mathbf{w}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta\mathbf{w}=\xi\mathbf{w},&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\mathcal{N}\mathbf{w}=0,&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(2.3)

Thus, we want to find eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
with the boundary conditions for the displacement in (
1.1
). They will allow us to construct the sequence of the approximate solutions of (
1.1
) in Subsection
4.1
.

Let us remind the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\colon\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega\right\}.

We define a bilinear form
B\colon\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}):=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{v}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{w}dx+\int_{\Omega}\curl\mathbf{v}\cdot\curl\mathbf{w}dx.

(2.4)

It is obvious that
B
is bounded. Moreover, Theorem IV.4.8 in
[
7
]
and Remark 3.5 of Chapter I in
[
18
]
guarantee that
B
is coercive on
\mathcal{H}
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
. Hence, by the Lax-Milgram Theorem, we obtain that for any
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exists a unique
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\textrm{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}.

It is easy to see that the above formula is a weak formulation of the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{f},&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\mathcal{N}\mathbf{w}=0,&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Moreover, Theorem IV.9.6 of
[
7
]
gives us that
\mathbf{w}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
. Let us notice that there is a proof only for
d=3
, but it is analogous in the case when
d=2
. Therefore, we can state the following result.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let us assume that
\Omega
is a simply connected domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a boundary of class
C^{2,1}(\Omega)
. Then, for every
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exists a unique
\mathbf{w}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathbf{w}
is a solution of
(
2.5
)
. The Poisson equation in (
2.5
) is satisfied pointwise for almost all
x\in\Omega
, and the boundary conditions are satisfied in the sense of traces. Moreover, the following inequality is satisfied

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\mathbf{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
C>0
depends on
\Omega
and
d
.

Let us note that the coercivity of the bilinear form in (
2.4
) entails that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
from (
1.6
) is equivalent to the standard norm on
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Now, we can apply the standard theory of symmetric and compact operators in Hilbert spaces (see
[
14
, Theorem 7 of Appendix D]
) to the operator solving problem (
2.5
). As an inference, we get the existence of an orthogonal basis (with
B
defined in (
2.4
) as scalar product)
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of eigenfunctions as in (
2.3
), which in addition is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let us denote the eigenvalues of (
2.3
) by
\xi_{k}
. Because
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}
satisfy
-\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}=\xi_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}
in the interior of
\Omega
, due to the standard results for the regularity of solutions of elliptic equations (see
[
7
,
14
]
), we get that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. However, Theorem
2.6
says that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Albeit applying similar methods to those in
[
7
]
, it can be shown that the eigenfunctions are arbitrarily well-regular as long as the boundary’s regularity allows it.

3
A priori estimates

In this section, we derive estimates that will be used to prove the existence of global solutions in the next section. Let us remind the functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t})^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}dx+(2\mu+\lambda)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx\right).

It plays a crucial role in estimates. It consists of the Fisher information and higher-order derivatives of the displacement. Actually, this is a version of the functional that has been adopted to address our issue. Similar ones can be found in
[
4
,
6
,
5
]
. We start proving the inequality in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
be smooth solutions of (
1.1
) such that
\theta>0
. Then, the following inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta)\leq-\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla^{2}\log\theta|^{2}dx-\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

holds.

Proof.

Direct calculations based on (
1.1
) yield

\displaystyle(\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})_{t}=\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}-\frac{\nu}{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}.

We multiply the above equation by
-4\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
. After integration, we obtain that the left-hand side is equal to

\displaystyle-4\int_{\Omega}(\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})_{t}\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}dx=4\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\nabla(\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})_{t}dx=2\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx.

Let us deal with the right-hand side. We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx

\displaystyle=-4\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})^{2}dx-\int_{\Omega}\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta^{\frac{3}{2}}}dx+2\mu\int_{\Omega}\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

(3.1)

First, we calculate
I_{1}
. The integration by parts yield

\displaystyle I_{1}=4\int_{\Omega}\Delta\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}dx.

We apply the Bochner formula
\Delta\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}-|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}
and arrive at

\displaystyle I_{1}=-4\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx+2\int_{\Omega}\Delta|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx.

Lemma
2.1
entails

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq-4\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx

(3.2)

We integrate by parts in
I_{2}
and obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}=4\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\nabla\frac{|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}}{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}dx=8\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot(\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})^{T}}{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}dx-4\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{4}}{\theta}dx

In
I_{3}
we get

\displaystyle I_{3}=\nu\int_{\Omega}\Delta\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx-\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

(3.3)

Let us note that

\displaystyle-4\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx+I_{2}

\displaystyle=-4\int_{\Omega}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\left((\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})_{x_{k}x_{l}}-\frac{(\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})_{x_{k}}(\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})_{x_{l}}}{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-4\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla^{2}\log\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx

(3.4)

Plugging (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3
) into (
3
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}dx\leq-\int_{\Omega}\theta|D^{2}\log\theta|^{2}dx-\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx+\nu\int_{\Omega}\Delta\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

(3.5)

Let us remind that for a vector field
\mathbf{w}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{w}=-\curl\curl\mathbf{w}+\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{w}.

(3.6)

We multiply the wave equation in (
1.1
) by
-\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t}
and integrate over
\Omega
. Utilizing the above formula and integrating by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t})^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}_{t}|^{2}dx+(2\mu+\lambda)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx+\lambda\int_{\Omega}|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle=\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\Delta\mathbf{u}_{t}dx=\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx-\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{t}=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\Delta\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx.

The integral
-\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{t}dx
disappears after integration by parts and the utility of boundary conditions of
\mathbf{u}_{t}
. We add the above equality to (
3.5
). The claim follows.
∎

In the lemma below, we continue to estimate
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
be smooth solutions of (
1.1
) such that
\theta>0
. Then, the following inequality is satisfied

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta)

\displaystyle\leq-C_{1}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{2}\mathcal{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta)\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2},

(3.7)

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
only depend on
d
,
|\nu|
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta)\leq-\int_{\Omega}\theta|\nabla^{2}\log\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}dx-\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx=I_{1}+I_{2}.

The term
I_{1}
can be estimated by Lemma
2.3
. It gives

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq-C_{1}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

On the other hand, the Hölder inequality yields

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}\mathcal{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta)\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}.

∎

In the next lemma, we present one of the main components in the proof of the existence of solutions. The constant
D
from the result below will be fundamental in the rest of the paper.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let us assume that
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
are smooth solutions of (
1.1
) such that
\theta>0
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\leq C\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}(\mathcal{F}-D),

where
C
and
D
depend on
d
,
|\nu|
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

We shall estimate the norm
\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}
in (
3.7
). We use the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality and the Young inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}(\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}).

Now, we utilize Lemma
2.2
. It gives us

\displaystyle\|\nabla\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}(\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Delta\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2})\leq C_{2}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

We plug the above inequality into (
3.7
), and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}\leq-C_{1}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{2}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathcal{F}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

We utilize the Young inequality and arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon-C_{1})\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{\varepsilon}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon-C_{1})\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{C_{2}}{\varepsilon}\|\nabla^{2}\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathcal{F}.

Let us take
\varepsilon:=\frac{C_{1}}{2}
. We obtain the assertion with
C=\frac{2C_{2}}{C_{1}}
and
D=\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{4C_{2}}
.
∎

As an immediate consequence of the previous lemma, we obtain the following theorem. It provides the global estimates of
\mathcal{F}
for solutions with initial values smaller than
D
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let us assume that
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
are smooth solutions of (
1.1
) which satisfy
\theta>0
. There exists a constant
D=D(d,|\nu|,\Omega)
such that if

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+(2\mu+\lambda)\|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mu\|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\nabla\theta_{0}}{{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{0}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq D,

then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}_{t},\theta)\leq\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0},\theta_{0})

for all
t\geq 0
.

Let us notice that the right-hand side of the inequality in the above theorem is finite because the bound from below of
\theta_{0}
is positive. We will need a global estimate for the temperature in
L^{\infty}
to complete our estimates. This will be inferred as a consequence of the following general lemma. We will prove it utilizing the Moser-Alikakos iteration (see
[
24
,
1
]
). We already applied this method in the context of thermoelasticity in
[
5
,
6
]
. However, it had been earlier used for 1d thermoelasticity in
[
13
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let us assume that
\eta
is a smooth function defined on
(0,\infty)\times\Omega
and
f\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Moreover, let us assume that
\eta
satisfies

\eta_{t}-\Delta\eta\leq(\eta^{+}+1)f\qquad\textrm{for almost all $(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\Omega$}

(3.8)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega.

Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, dependent on
d
,
|\nu|
,
\Omega
,
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}
,
\|\eta^{+}(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, and
\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{1}(\Omega))}
such that

\displaystyle\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\Omega)}\leq C.

Proof.

Let us take
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let us multiply (
3.8
) by
\left(\eta^{+}\right)^{2^{n+1}-1}
and integrate over
\Omega
. After integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}(\eta^{+})^{2^{n+1}}dx+\left(\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\right)\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left((\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq+\int_{\Omega}\left((\eta^{+})^{2^{n+1}}+(\eta^{+})^{2^{n+1}-1}\right)fdx

\displaystyle\leq C(\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}+1),

(3.9)

where in the last inequality we used the Young inequality. Now, we apply the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality

\displaystyle\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C(\|\nabla\left((\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\right)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2p}\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2(1-p)}+\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}),

where
p=\frac{3}{4}
for
d=2
and
p=\frac{9}{10}
for
d=3
. We apply the Young inequality to the above one and arrive at

\displaystyle\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C(\varepsilon\|\nabla\left((\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\right)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}),

where
\alpha=3
for
d=2
and
\alpha=9
for
d=3
. Let us denote
a_{n}:=\frac{1}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2\cdot 4^{n}}
and choose
\varepsilon:=\frac{a_{n}}{2C}
. Plugging the above inequality into (
3
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}(\eta^{+})^{2^{n+1}}dx+a_{n}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left((\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{2}dx\leq C(a_{n}^{-\alpha}\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+1).

It leads us to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}(\eta^{+})^{2^{n+1}}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left((\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\right)\right|^{2}dx\leq C2^{n+\alpha n}(\|(\eta^{+})^{2^{n}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+1).

Applying the Gagliardo-Nirenberg and the Young inequalities once more yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{2^{n+1}}(\Omega)}^{2^{n+1}}+C_{1}\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{2^{n+1}}(\Omega)}^{2^{n+1}}\leq C_{2}2^{n+\alpha n}(\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{2^{n}}(\Omega)}^{2^{n+1}}+1).

Solving the above differential inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2^{n+1}}(\Omega))}^{2^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n+\alpha n}C(\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2^{n}}(\Omega))}^{2^{n+1}}+1)+\|\eta^{+}(0)\|_{L^{2^{n+1}}(\Omega)}^{2^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(2^{n+\alpha n}(\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2^{n}}(\Omega))}^{2^{n+1}}+1)+\|\eta^{+}(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2^{n+1}}).

Let us denote
m_{n}:=\max\{\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2^{n}}(\Omega))},\|\eta^{+}(0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},1\}
. Then, the above inequality can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle m_{n+1}^{2^{n+1}}\leq C(2^{n+\alpha n}+1)m_{n}^{2^{n+1}}.

It gives us

\displaystyle m_{n+1}\leq 2^{\frac{n+\alpha n}{2^{n+1}}}C^{\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}}m_{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Solving this recursion inequality yields

\displaystyle m_{n}\leq\prod_{k=1}^{n}C^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}2^{\frac{k\alpha}{2^{k}}}m_{0}\leq m_{0}C^{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}}2^{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{\frac{k\alpha}{2^{k}}}}.

Because both series on the right-hand side of the above inequality are convergent, we get

\displaystyle\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2^{n+1}}(\Omega))}\leq C.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\eta^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq C.

∎

Let us remind that
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\left(\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\curl\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
be smooth solutions of (
1.1
) such that
\theta>0
. Moreover, let us assume that the initial values satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+(2\mu+\lambda)\|\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mu\|\curl\curl\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\nabla\theta_{0}}{{\theta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{0}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq D,

where
D
is taken from Theorem
3.4
. Then,

\displaystyle\|\theta\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\Omega)}\leq C,

where
C=C(\Omega,d,|\nu|,\lambda,\mu,\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}},\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)},\|\theta_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\tilde{\theta},\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)})
.

Proof.

We utilize Lemma
3.5
for
\theta
as
\eta
and
|\nu||\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}|
as
f
. Due the lower equation in (
1.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta\leq(\theta+1)|\nu||\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}|.

By Theorem
3.4
, we have an estimate for
\|\nu\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. Hence, we obtain the thesis, because
\theta^{+}=\theta
.
∎

In addition, the lower boundedness of the temperature can be derived from Lemma
3.5
. We do not need it in the proof of the existence of solutions. However, it will be important in our considerations about the time-asymptotics of (
1.1
). Moreover, this is a valuable result in its own right.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let us assume
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
are smooth solutions of (
1.1
) with the positive temperature. Then, there exists the positive constant
C
depedent on
\Omega
,
d
,
|\nu|
,
\lambda
,
\mu
,
\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
,
\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}
,
\|\theta_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
,
\tilde{\theta}
, and
\|\theta_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
such that

\displaystyle\theta\geq C\qquad\textrm{ for almost all }(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\Omega.

Proof.

Let us remind the notation
\tau:=\log\theta
. We want to estimate
\|\tau\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\Omega)}
. The bound for
\|\tau^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\Omega)}
we have from Theorem
3.6
. Hence it is left to constrain the norm

. We know that

satisfies equation (

\displaystyle(-\tau)_{t}-\Delta(-\tau)+|\nabla\tau|^{2}=\nu\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}.

Hence, we have (

∎

For every

5
Time-asymptotics of solutions

In this section, we will investigate the asymptotics of solutions derived from Theorem
1.4
. We will see that the displacement decomposes into two parts, which behave differently when
t\to\infty
. We will apply the Helmholtz decomposition on
\mathbf{u}
. Then, it will separate into the potential part (also called the rotation-free part) and the divergence-free part. It turns out that the divergence-free part can be considered completely independently of the temperature and the potential part. Indeed, it will be shown that it satisfies the homogeneous wave equation. Consequently, it oscillates indefinitely.

On the other hand, the rotation-free part of the displacement and the temperature are coupled in the same system as
1.1
. The potential part and
\theta
behave in the same way as the heated string in
[
5
]
. Namely, the curl-free part is influenced by the propagation of heat. It means that the whole energy, interlocked with the displacement, is dissipated into heat as
t\to\infty
.

5.1
Decomposition of solutions

In this subsection, we apply the Helmholtz decomposition (Theorem
2.4
) to (
1.1
). Namely, we will demonstrate that the problem divides into two distinct issues.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
be solutions of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.2
. Then,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=H\mathbf{u}
solves the following problem

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{\gamma}=0&\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\ \mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=0&\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(0,\cdot)=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}(0,\cdot)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}.&\end{cases}

(5.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}:=H\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}=H\mathbf{v}_{0}
. On the other hand,
\boldsymbol{\chi}:=H^{\perp}\mathbf{u}
and
\theta
solve

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}-(2\mu+\lambda)\Delta\boldsymbol{\chi}=-\nu\nabla\theta&\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta=-\nu\theta\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}&\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\ \nabla\theta\cdot\mathbf{n}=0&\text{ on }(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}(0,\cdot)=\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0},\ \boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(0,\cdot)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0},\ \theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0}>0,&\end{cases}

(5.2)

where
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}:=H^{\perp}\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}:=H^{\perp}\mathbf{v}_{0}
. In addition, the regularity of both
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}
is equivalent to that of
\mathbf{u}
in (
1.7
). Moreover, we have the following decomposition of the energy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma}|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}|^{2}dx+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\curl\gamma_{0}|^{2}dx

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}|^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\theta dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{0}|^{2}dx+\frac{2\mu+\lambda}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{0}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\theta_{0}dx.

Proof.

Let us deal with the wave equation from (
1.1
). We multiply it by
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}+\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and integrate over
\Omega
. We obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}+\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}-(2\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}+\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma})dx

\displaystyle=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}+\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma})dx=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}-\nu\mu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma}dx.

We integrate by parts the integrals on the right-hand side of the above equality. The first integral disappears easily. Let us note that
\curl\mathbf{u}=\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. It implies that
\mathcal{N}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Thus, in the second integral, we switch
\curl
from
\curl\curl\gamma
onto
\nabla\theta
. This results in the integral also vanishing.

Let us consider the second integral on the left-hand side. We know that
\boldsymbol{\chi}=H^{\perp}\mathbf{u}
. Thus, there exists a function
\varphi
such that
\nabla\varphi=\boldsymbol{\chi}
. Therefore, it yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{tt}-(2\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\chi})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}-\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma})dx=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\varphi_{tt}-(2\mu+\lambda)\nabla\Delta\varphi)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}-\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma})dx.

We integrate by parts the integrals on the right-hand side of the above equality. It results in its disappearance. Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{tt}-\mu\curl\curl\boldsymbol{\gamma}|^{2}dx=0.

This, with the formula

satisfies (

We know that

1.1

∎

Remark 5.2

The boundary condition for

Proposition 5.3
.

Let

Proof.

It is obvious that (

Now, let us assume that (

∎

Let us now study the asymptotics of the potential part of

Proposition 5.4
.

Let us take two triples of the initial values

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\left(\|\mathbf{u}_{0,1}-\mathbf{u}_{0,2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\mathbf{v}_{0,1}-\mathbf{v}_{0,2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\theta_{0,1}-\theta_{0,2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

and

Let us denote
\mathbf{u}:=\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}
and
\theta:=\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
. We subtract the equations for
(\mathbf{u}_{2},\theta_{2})
from the equations for

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{tt}-(2\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}+\mu\curl\curl\mathbf{u}=-\nu\nabla\theta,\\
\theta_{t}-\Delta\theta=-\nu\theta\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}-\mu\theta_{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}.\end{cases}

(5.4)

We multiply by
\mathbf{u}_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{t}^{2}dx+(2\mu+\lambda)\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}|\curl\mathbf{u}|^{2}dx\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\nu\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{t}\cdot\nabla\theta dx

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(5.5)

Whereas, multiplying the second equation in (
5.4
) by
\theta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\theta|^{2}dx=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}dx-\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta\theta_{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{t}dx

\displaystyle=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}dx+\nu\int_{\Omega}\theta\nabla\theta_{2}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}\theta_{2}\nabla\theta\cdot\mathbf{u}_{t}dx=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

We bound the integral

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq|\nu|\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{1,t}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\|\theta\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(5.7)

The integral

\displaystyle I_{3}\leq|\nu|\|\theta_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)}\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(5.8)

Let us proceed with

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\nu|\|\theta\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\theta_{2}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

Next, after the application of the Young inequality, we get

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C(\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}).

We plug (

5.8

\displaystyle\leq 4\varepsilon\|\nabla\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C(\|\nabla^{2}\theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}).

Let us denote

We integrate over

(\boldsymbol{\chi},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t},\theta)

\{t_{n}\}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}(t_{n},\cdot)

\displaystyle\to 0\textrm{ in }\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}(t_{n},\cdot)
