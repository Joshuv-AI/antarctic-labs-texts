Convergence of a class of gradient-free optimisation schemes when the objective function is noisy, irregular, or both
Christophe Andrieu, Nicolas Chopin, Ettore Fincato, Mathieu Gerber

Abstract
We investigate the convergence properties of a class of iterative algorithms designed to minimize a potentially non-smooth and noisy objective function, which may be algebraically intractable and whose values may be obtained as the output of a black box. The algorithms considered can be cast under the umbrella of a generalised gradient descent recursion, where the gradient is that of a smooth approximation of the objective function. The framework we develop includes as special cases model-based and mollification methods, two classical approaches to zero-th order optimisation. The convergence results are obtained under very weak assumptions on the regularity of the objective function and involve a trade-off between the degree of smoothing and size of the steps taken in the parameter updates. As expected, additional assumptions are required in the stochastic case. We illustrate the relevance of these algorithms and our convergence results through a challenging classification example from machine learning.

Introduction

We are interested in the convergence properties of iterative algorithms designed to minimise an objective function
l:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
in the
following, non-exclusive, scenarios: (a)
l
is not smooth (i.e. non-differentiable or even
discontinuous); or (b)
l
is the output of a black box; or (c) only noisy evaluations of
l
can be obtained.
Such scenarios preclude the use of standard gradient descent methods: in (a) the
gradient of
l
does not exit, in (b) little is known about
l
and even when the gradient exists it is inaccessible, and in (c) the function
l
itself cannot be evaluated exactly. Numerous algorithms have been developed to address such situations, including, among many others, simulated annealing
(
15
;
12
)
, genetic algorithms
(
23
)
, function smoothing techniques
(
8
)
and model-based, or variational, search methods,
(
24
;
10
)
. In this contribution we develop novel convergence theory covering the latter two approaches simultaneously.

More specifically, we assume the function
l
to be of the form

l(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(\theta,U)]
, where
U
is a random variable whose distribution
\mathbb{P}
is independent of
\theta
, and we let
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathsf{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the noisy evaluation of
l
one may obtain for any given input
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
the noiseless scenario is recovered by taking
\ell(\theta,U)=l(\theta)
. For
any
\gamma>0
a smooth approximation
\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathsf{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
\ell
can be defined as follows,

\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)=\psi^{-1}\left(\int\psi\left(\ell(x,u)\right)\phi_{d,\gamma}(x-\theta)dx\right)\,,

(1)

where
\phi_{d,\gamma}(x)
is the probability density function of a
\mathscr{N}_{d}(0,\gamma I)
distribution, and
\psi
a diffeomorphism. The scalar parameter
\gamma>0
determines both how far
\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
is from

\ell(\theta,u)
(the smaller, the closer), and how smooth
\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
is (the larger, the smoother). This smooth approximation of
\ell
is differentiable under fairly mild conditions and,
in this work, for a given

\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we consider the generalised gradient descent recursion:

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\beta_{n}\nabla\mathscr{L}_{\gamma_{n}}(\theta_{n},U_{n+1}),\quad n\geq 1,

(2)

where the
U_{n}
’s are independent and identically distributed from
\mathbb{P}
,
and where
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are sequences of positive numbers.

In Section
2.1
we present general results regarding the convergence of the generic time-inhomogeneous gradient descent algorithms (
2
). The main results in Section
2.1
are Theorems
1
and
2
, where the deterministic and stochastic scenarios are treated separately. Letting
L_{\gamma}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,U)]
for all
\gamma>0
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the convergence of (
2
) is established in the sense that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla L_{\gamma_{n}}(\theta_{n})\|=0
under general conditions on the two functions
(\theta,\gamma,u)\mapsto\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
and
(\theta,\gamma,u)\mapsto\nabla_{\theta}\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
, and on the two sequences
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. In Section
2.2
(Propositions
1
and
2
) we then specialise these results to the following two popular scenarios:
\psi(x)=x
, in which case

\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell(x,u)\phi_{d,\gamma}(x-\theta)\mathrm{d}x\,,

(3)

and
\psi(x)=\exp(-x)
, in which case

\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)=-\log\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\ell(x,u)}\phi_{d,\gamma}(x-\theta)\mathrm{d}x\Big).

(4)

The connection between these two particular instances of recursion (
2
) with smoothing (i.e. mollification) techniques and model-based search methods is discussed in Section
2.3
, while in Section
3
we apply our theory to an intricate example from the machine learning literature. We stress that convergence results of recursions of the type (
2
) in scenarios (
3
) and (
4
) are, to the best of our knowledge, scarce; see Section
2.3
for a discussion.

The expectation involved in the expression for the gradient of
\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
may not be tractable, in which case it is replaced with an estimator based on iid samples from
\mathscr{N}_{d}(0,\gamma I_{d})
. When
\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
is as defined in (
3
) we can easily obtain an unbiased estimator of this gradient, in which case our results remain valid; this only requires notational change. In contrast, for
\mathscr{L}_{\gamma}(\theta,u)
as defined in (
4
) constructing an unbiased estimator of the gradient is not trivial due to the presence of a ratio. Classical approaches to study such recursions rely on using the ideal dynamics (
2
) as a reference, of which the algorithm effectively implemented is a perturbation; our result is therefore the first step in such an analysis. Alternatively, it is possible to modify the algorithm to employ debiasing techniques for self-normalised importance sampling
(
2
;
4
)
. In this manuscript we do not attempt to compare performance of the two approaches, which is left for future work.

3
Example: A discontinuous and noisy optimization problem

3.1
Set-up

We illustrate the relevance of our theory on a classification example based on
5
. We let
\{(z_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\{-1,1\}\colon i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{data}}\}
be a training dataset (with
p\geq 2
), assumed to
arise from a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
, and we wish to construct a
score function
s\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the empirical version of the probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\left[s(Z)-s(Z^{\prime})\right](Y-Y^{\prime})<0\big)\,,\quad(Z,Y),(Z^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny{iid}}}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}

(8)

is as small as possible. The quantity (
8
) is often called the area under curve (AUC) risk function, and this criterion has the advantage to be less sensitive to class imbalance
than other more standard classification criteria.

For any
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
we let
s_{\vartheta}(z)=\vartheta^{\top}z
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and below we focus on the scenario where the function
s
must be chosen from the set
\{s_{\vartheta},\,\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that the observations in the training sets are labeled so that

\displaystyle y_{i}=\begin{cases}1&i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{+}\}\\
-1&i\in\{n_{+}+1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{data}}\}\end{cases},\quad n_{+}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{\rm data}}}\mathbf{1}(y_{i}=1).

Letting
I=\{(i,j)\colon i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{+}\},j\in\{n_{+}+1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{data}}\}\}
, the parameter
\vartheta
is then chosen by minimizing the empirical risk
E:\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto[0,\infty)
, defined by

\begin{split}E(\vartheta)&=\frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in I}\mathbf{1}\{s_{\vartheta}(z_{i})<s_{\vartheta}(z_{j})\}}{n_{\mathrm{data}}(n_{\mathrm{data}}-1)}=\frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in I}\mathbf{1}\{\vartheta^{\top}(z_{i}-z_{j})<0\}}{n_{\mathrm{data}}(n_{\mathrm{data}}-1)},\quad\quad\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.\end{split}

(9)

3.2
Definition of the functions
l(\theta)
and
\ell(\theta,u)

While minimising the empirical risk
E(\vartheta)
is a classical problem in machine learning, two issues arise when this procedure is used for choosing a score function
s\in\{s_{\vartheta},\,\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
. The first problem is that
\vartheta=0
is a global minimum of
E(\cdot)
, which
is pathological, and the second problem is that the mapping
\vartheta\mapsto E(\vartheta)
is rescaling invariant.

To address these two problems, we can exploit the fact that for
\vartheta\neq 0
we have
E(\vartheta)=E(\vartheta/\|\vartheta\|)
to restrict our attention to minimizers of the function
E
that lie on
\mathscr{S}_{p}
, the unit hypersphere in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. To do so, we let
\sigma\colon\mathscr{S}_{p}\setminus\{\mathbf{e}_{p}\}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
be the stereographic projection of centre
\mathbf{e}_{p}:=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, defined by

\sigma(\vartheta)=\left(\frac{\vartheta_{1}}{1-\vartheta_{p}},\frac{\vartheta_{2}}{1-\vartheta_{p}},\ldots,\frac{\vartheta_{p-1}}{1-\vartheta_{p}}\right),\quad(\vartheta_{1},\dots,\vartheta_{p})\in\mathscr{S}_{p}.

(10)

It can be easily shown that the mapping
\sigma
is bijective, with inverse mapping
\sigma^{-1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\rightarrow\mathscr{S}_{p}
, which is Lipschitz continuous and defined by

\sigma^{-1}(\theta)=\left(\frac{2\theta_{1}}{\|\theta\|^{2}+1},\ldots,\frac{2\theta_{d-1}}{\|\theta\|^{2}+1},\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}-1}{\|\theta\|^{2}+1}\right),\quad(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}.

With this notation in place, our classification problem can be reformulated as choosing an element in the set
\{s_{\sigma(\theta)},\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\}
by minimizing the function
l:\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
defined by

\displaystyle l(\theta)=E\circ\sigma^{-1}(\theta),\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}.

Remark 2
.

This definition of the function
l
implicitly assumes that the point
\mathbf{e}_{p}=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is not a minimizer of the empirical risk
E(\cdot)
, since there exists no
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
such that
\sigma^{-1}(\theta)=\mathbf{e}_{p}
.

To reduce computations one may replace the sum appearing in (
9
) by an
unbiased estimate, following the standard mini-batch approach popular in machine
learning. To this aim let
n_{\mathrm{batch}}\in\{1,\dots,\sharp I\}
denote the batch size and, for any element
u=\big((i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{n_{\mathrm{batch}}},j_{n_{\mathrm{batch}}})\big)
of
\mathsf{U}:=I^{n_{\mathrm{batch}}}
, let

\mathscr{E}(\vartheta,u)=\frac{2n_{+}(n_{\mathrm{data}}-n_{+})}{n_{\mathrm{data}}(n_{\mathrm{data}}-1)n_{\mathrm{batch}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mathrm{batch}}}\mathbf{1}\{s_{\vartheta}(z_{i_{k}})<s_{\vartheta}(z_{j_{k}})\},\quad\forall\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(11)

With this notation in place, we let
\ell(\theta,u)=\mathscr{E}(\sigma^{-1}(\theta),u)
for all pairs
(\theta,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\times\mathsf{U}
. Then, letting
\mathbb{P}
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathsf{U}
, we can easily check that for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
we have
\mathbb{E}[\ell(\theta,U)]=l(\theta)
if
U\sim\mathbb{P}
. Observe that the two functions
\theta\mapsto l(\theta)
and
\theta\mapsto\ell(\theta,u)
are discontinuous.

Since each term in the sum appearing in (
11
) is equal to zero or one, it readily follows that
A1
holds while (
5
) is satisfied for some bounded function
J:\mathsf{U}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and with
\alpha=0
. In this context, condition
A2
holds for any
\eta\in[2,\infty)
and condition
A3
holds for any
\beta\in[0,2]
and
\upsilon\in(0,1]
. The following proposition, proved in Section
5.4
, establishes that the functions
l
and
\ell
defined above also satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
3
.

Proposition 4
.

For any
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let
D_{\vartheta}=\left\{(i,j)\in I\colon\vartheta^{\top}(z_{i}-z_{j})=0\right\}
and assume that the following condition holds

\displaystyle\vartheta\neq 0\implies(z_{i_{1}}-z_{j_{1}})^{\top}(z_{i_{2}}-z_{j_{2}})\geq 0,\quad\forall(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})\in D_{\vartheta}.

Then, the functions
l
and
\ell
defined above satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
3
.

Remark 3
.

The condition imposed on the observations may appear strong. However we remark that it holds with probability
one if, for example, the observations
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n_{{\rm data}}}
are independent realizations of a distribution with positive density
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure. It is indeed highly improbable that more than one of the differences
\{z_{i}-z_{j},(i,j)\in I\}
belong to a hyperplane
\vartheta^{\top}z=0
for some
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words for any
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
we have
\sharp D_{\vartheta}\in\{0,1\}
.

3.3
Numerical experiments

We consider the Fashion-MNIST dataset
(
22
)
,
which consists of
n_{\text{data}}=7\times 10^{4}
grayscale images,
of size
p=28\times 28=784
, of fashion items organised in ten categories
(t-shirt/top, trousers, etc.). We set
y_{i}=1
if item
i
falls in the first
category (t-shirt/top),
y_{i}=-1
otherwise, leading to class unbalance
(
n_{+}=7\,000
,
n_{-}=63\ 000
). Recall that one of the appeals of the AUC criterion
is that it is less sensitive to class unbalance.

For this dataset, even a single evaluation of the AUC criterion takes several
seconds on a recent computer, making the optimisation of this function
particularly challenging.
To address this issue, we apply two standard machine learning tricks:
(a) we split the data into a training set (90% randomly selected datapoints) and a test
set (the remaining 10%). Our optimiser is run on the former, and evaluated on the latter.
(b) we use the mini-batch strategy described in the previous sub-section,
with
n_{\text{batch}}=10^{3}
.

We set
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{1}/n^{\kappa}
, with
\gamma_{1}=0.2
,
\kappa=0.2
,
and
\beta_{n}=\beta_{1}/n^{\iota}
, with
\beta_{0}=0.2
,
\iota=0.5
.
(Thus, the conditions of Theorem
1
hold, since we can take
\alpha=0
.)
We focus on the map
\psi(x)=\exp(x)
, and thus the gradient descent recursion takes the form given
by (
7
).
As in
1
,
we replace the expectation with an importance sampling estimate computed over a set of

N=1024
i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) Gaussian variates.
In addition, we use the same rescaling strategy as in the aforementioned paper; that is, remarking that
the minimiser of
l(\theta)
does not change if we multiply
l
by an arbitrary, positive constant,
we rescale
l
at each iteration to keep the ESS (effective sample size), or equivalently,
the variance of the normalised importance sampling weights, constant.
Formally, our convergence results do not result when we apply this rescaling strategy, but,
in practice, rescaling seems to accelerate convergence significantly. Analysing the convergence
of the algorithm based on rescaling (and Monte Carlo estimates) is left to future work;
see next section.

We run our gradient descent algorithm 10 times.
Figure
1
shows how the AUC risk (evaluated on the
test data, at
\vartheta_{n}=\sigma^{-1}(\theta_{n})
, the current
iterate at iteration
n
) evolves over time.
Remarkably, convergence occurs essentially in about
10^{3}
iterations, which amounts
to less than 3 epochs; an epoch is the number of iterations required to
access once (on average) each of the data pairs in
\mathsf{U}
.

For reference, we also include as a baseline the AUC risk of
L^{2}-
penalised
logistic regression (as implemented in python package scikit-learn), which is
another method to construct a linear score for predicting the class. Of
course, this is just a sanity check, since logistic regression provides a linear score
which is obtained by minimising a likelihood function (rather than the AUC criterion).
Interestingly, the default implementation in scikit-learn issues a warning
message suggesting lack of convergence, which is another indication that the
considered dataset is particularly challenging.

4
Conclusion and future work

As already discussed, this paper generalises significantly our previous results

(
1
)
, in various ways (i.e.,
noisy and noiseless scenarios,
\beta_{n}\neq\gamma_{n}
, and the map
\psi

in (
1
) may be either
\psi(x)=x
, or
\psi(x)=\exp(-x)
).

Nevertheless, practical implementations of the algorithms are not yet covered,
such as the Monte Carlo variant of (
7
)
(when the expectation is replaced by a biased self-normalised importance sampling estimate), or
the adaptive scaling strategy we use in our numerical experiment, despite our empirical observation of its beneficial impact in terms of convergence speed. In addition, there may be scenarios where one would like to use a non-Gaussian
kernel; for instance, when the domain of the objective function is a manifold
or a set of a nature different from that of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this vein, we have already obtained
preliminary results for smoothing kernels derived from the Wishart distribution, addressing the situation where the domain of interest is the set of positive symmetric matrices of size
d
. Another useful extension would involve relaxing our assumptions on the noise, and in particular allow for its distribution to be state dependent.

We however leave all these extensions to future work.

5.1.1
A useful technical lemma

Lemma 1
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
such that
\inf_{n\geq 1}a_{n}>-\infty
,
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
[0,\infty)
, and let
(\epsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\varrho_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be two bounded sequences in
[0,\infty)
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\varrho_{n}/\epsilon_{n})=0
and such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\epsilon_{n}=\infty
. Assume that
a_{n+1}\leq a_{n}-\epsilon_{n}b_{n}+\varrho_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. Then,
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}b_{n}=0
.

Proof.

We prove the result by contradiction and assume that there exists a constant
b>0
and an
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

for all
n\geq n_{1}
. We also assume that
n_{1}
is sufficiently large so that
\varrho_{n}\leq(b/2)\epsilon_{n}
for all

. Then, for all
n\geq n_{1}
we have

implying that, for all

,

\displaystyle a_{n}\leq a_{n_{1}}-b\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}+\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{n-1}\varrho_{m}=a_{n_{1}}-\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}\Big(b-\frac{\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{n-1}\varrho_{m}}{\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}}\Big)\leq a_{n_{1}}-\frac{b}{2}\sum_{m=n_{1}}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}

and thus that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}=-\infty
. This contradicts the fact that, by assumption,
\inf_{n\geq 1}a_{n}>-\infty
and the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

&\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\nabla L_{\gamma_{n+1}}(\theta_{n+1})-\nabla L_{\gamma_{n}}(\theta_{n})\|^{\eta}\Big]}{\epsilon^{\eta}}\\
&\leq\bar{C}^{2}\delta_{n}\epsilon^{-\eta}\end{split}

\displaystyle\delta+\epsilon,\,\,\|\nabla L_{\gamma_{n}}(\theta_{n})\|\leq\delta\Big)<\infty.

From the Borel-Cantelli lemma it follows that the set

∎

The Bayes-Laplace Sandwich Theorem

Theorem 6

(Bayes-Laplace Sandwich)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally integrable function. Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
g_{\psi}(\theta)=-\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-g(\theta+z)}\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z
, where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z=1
. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\theta+z)\frac{e^{-g(\theta+z)}\psi(z)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-g(\theta+z^{\prime})}\psi(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}z\leq g_{\psi}(\theta)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\theta+z)\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z.

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, by using Laplace Principle (
42
), we have

\displaystyle g_{\psi}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\theta+z)\frac{e^{-g(\theta+z)}\psi(z)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-g(\theta+z^{\prime})}\psi(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}z+\mathrm{KL}\left(\frac{e^{-g(\theta+\cdot)}\psi}{\psi(e^{-g(\theta+\cdot)})},\psi\right)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\theta+z)\frac{e^{-g(\theta+z)}\psi(z)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-g(\theta+z^{\prime})}\psi(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}z

where the inequality uses the fact that
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
.
On the other hand, by Jensen’s Inequality, we have

\displaystyle g_{\psi}(\theta)=-\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-g(\theta+z)}\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\theta+z)\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z.

and the proof of the theorem is complete.

c=(\gamma/\tilde{\gamma})^{d/2}\geq 1
.

To prove the first part of the lemma assume first that
\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)\geq 0
, so that

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=\left(1-c\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{2\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2}\right)\right)\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{2\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2}\right)\right)\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{2\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

where the last inequality uses the fact that
\exp(-x)\geq 1-x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Assume now that
\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)<0
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)\right|=\left(\frac{\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)}{\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)}-1\right)\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)\leq(c-1)\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)\leq\frac{cd}{2}\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

where the last inequality uses the fact that since

(1+x)^{k/2}-1=k/2\int_{0}^{x}(1+u)^{k/2-1}{\rm d}u\leq k/2(1+x)^{k/2}x,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall x\in(0,\infty),

we have

\displaystyle\Big(1+\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}}\Big)^{k/2}-1\leq\frac{k}{2}\,(\gamma/\tilde{\gamma})^{k/2}\,\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(43)

Consequently

\left|\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)\right|\leq\frac{cd}{2}\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}}\left[1+\gamma^{-1}\|z\|^{2}\right]\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

showing the first result of the lemma.

Similarly, to show the second part of the lemma assume first that
\gamma^{-1}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)\geq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle|\gamma^{-1}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)|

\displaystyle=\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\left[\frac{\tilde{\gamma}}{\gamma}-c\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{2\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2}\right)\right]\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{2\gamma\tilde{\gamma}}\|z\|^{2}\right)\right]\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{2\gamma\tilde{\gamma}^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

while, if
\gamma^{-1}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)<0
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)-\gamma^{-1}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)|

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1}\left[\frac{\gamma}{\tilde{\gamma}}\frac{\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)}{\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)}-1\right]\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{-1}\left[\frac{\gamma}{\tilde{\gamma}}c-1\right]\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{-1}\frac{c(d+1)}{2}\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(d+1)}{2}\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}^{2}}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)\,,

where the third inequality uses (
43
). Consequently

\left|\gamma^{-1}\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)-\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\phi_{d,\tilde{\gamma}}(z)\right|\leq\frac{c(d+1)}{2}\frac{\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}}{\tilde{\gamma}^{2}}\left[\gamma^{-1}\|z\|^{2}+1\right]\phi_{d,\gamma}(z)

and the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
X
be a
[0,\infty)
-valued random variable,
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-decreasing function and
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-increasing function such that
g(x)>0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X)]}{\mathbb{E}[g(X)]}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{f(X)}{g(X)}\bigg].

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathrm{Cov}\Big(\frac{1}{g(X)},f(X)\Big)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{f(X)}{g(X)}\bigg]-\mathbb{E}\big[1/g(X)\big]\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{f(X)}{g(X)}\bigg]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X)]}{\mathbb{E}[g(X)]}

where the first inequality follows from the well-known covariance inequality
18
while the last inequality holds by Jensen’s inequality.

Appendix D
Epi-convergence and lower-semicontinuity

Definition 1

(Lower semi-continuity)
.

(
16
, Def. 1.5)

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be

1.

lower semi-continuous (
\mathrm{lsc}
) at
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

\liminf_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}f(\theta)\geq f(\theta_{0}).

(47)

2.

lower semi-continuous if the above holds for any
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

By definition, any lower semi-continuous, lower-bounded function has a minimum on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 2

(Strong lower semi-continuity)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be

1.

strongly lower semi-continuous (
slsc
) at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

if it is lower semi-continuous at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and there
exists a sequence
\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
,

\theta_{n}\to\theta
, with
f
continuous at every
\theta_{n}
, and
f(\theta_{n})\to f(\theta)
.

2.

strongly lower semi-continuous if the above holds for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 3

(Epi-convergence)
.

A sequence of functions
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

epi-converges to a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
if, for each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

1.

\liminf_{n}f_{n}(\theta_{n})\geq f(\theta)
for any sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta

2.

\lim_{n}f_{n}(\theta_{n})=f(\theta)
for some sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta
.

Thus, we say that
f
is the epi-limit of
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.

.

Let

1.
