Disseminated coccidioidomycosis with cutaneous, pulmonary and suspected CNS involvement in an elderly patient with Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Ricardo Villanueva Gaona, Danny Linggonegoro, Mariana Ramirez Posada, Jean Yuh Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAn elderly patient with recently diagnosed Parkinson’s disease presented with disseminated coccidioidomycosis (DCM) manifesting as ulcerative nasal lesions, pulmonary involvement and acute-on-chronic cognitive decline. Culture-confirmed cutaneous disease, positive serology, and imaging demonstrated pulmonary and central nervous system (CNS) involvement, establishing the diagnosis. The patient achieved marked clinical improvement with high-dose oral fluconazole (800 mg, later tapered to 400 mg daily), with serial imaging documenting resolution of CNS and pulmonary lesions. Lymphocyte subset analysis revealed preserved immune function with subtle findings suggestive of age-related immunosenescence. This case demonstrates that DCM can occur in elderly patients without traditional immunocompromise and emphasises the importance of maintaining high clinical suspicion in endemic areas.

Background

Coccidioidomycosis is endemic to arid regions of the southwestern USA, Mexico and parts of Central and South America. The disease is acquired through inhalation of arthroconidia from Coccidioides species. Approximately one-third of exposed individuals develop pulmonary disease, while less than 2% experience extrapulmonary dissemination.15 Cutaneous manifestations frequently mimic other infectious or neoplastic conditions, contributing to diagnostic delays.68 This case highlights the unique diagnostic challenge posed by disseminated disease in a patient with Parkinson’s disease, whose symptoms may overlap with central nervous system (CNS) coccidioidomycosis.

Case presentation

A male patient in his 70s with recently diagnosed Parkinson’s disease, transient global amnesia, remote traumatic brain injury and benign prostatic hyperplasia presented to the emergency department with painful nasal ulceration and acute on chronic mental status deterioration. He had resided in California for over 50 years and worked as a construction worker. He reported no recent travel, corticosteroid use or known immunodeficiency.

Several months prior, he experienced low-grade fevers and chest discomfort. Evaluation at an outside facility revealed leukocytosis and chest CT showing multifocal right lung consolidations, bilateral ground-glass opacities and pleural effusion. Haemophilus influenzae was detected on the respiratory panel. Coccidioides antibody screening was negative at that time. Following a 10-day course of doxycycline and amoxicillin–clavulanate, his respiratory symptoms resolved.

Subsequently, he developed a painful erythematous plaque with erosive papules on the nasal bridge. Despite treatment with topical mupirocin, oral cephalexin and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole for presumed cellulitis, the lesion progressed to a necrotic ulcer with purulent drainage. New pustular papules appeared on his chest and upper back.

One month after symptom onset, the patient presented with worsening confusion, inability to recognise family members and declining functional independence. Physical examination revealed a 2 cm ulcerated nasal plaque with overlying crust and multiple erythematous papules and plaques on the trunk (figure 1). He was afebrile with no focal neurological deficits on initial examination.

Investigations

Initial laboratory studies showed leukocytosis with elevated neutrophil, eosinophil and basophil counts. Chest radiography was unremarkable, and head CT revealed no acute intracranial findings. The patient was discharged with instructions to follow-up with dermatology and neurology the next day.

At his dermatology appointment, his cognitive status had further deteriorated, and he reported new periocular pain and tingling without vision changes, raising concern for herpes zoster ophthalmicus. This prompted urgent hospital admission. Biopsies from nasal, upper back and upper chest lesions were obtained for histopathology, direct immunofluorescence (DIF), varicella zoster virus (VZV) DNA PCR and bacterial, acid-fast bacillus (AFB) and fungal cultures.

Preliminary biopsies revealed fungal spherules consistent with Coccidioides species (figure 2). All samples showed pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia, suppurative and granulomatous dermal inflammation with giant cells, and large spherical yeast containing endospores highlighted by periodic acid-Schiff with diastase (PASd) staining. Gram stain and AFB smear were negative. Fungal culture from the nasal tip lesion was positive for Coccidioides immitis/posadasii within 48 hours.

DIF testing with antibodies against IgG, IgA, IgM, C3 and fibrinogen showed no immunoreactant deposition, excluding autoimmune bullous dermatoses. VZV PCR was negative, excluding herpes zoster.

Serum Coccidioides antibodies were tested at two laboratories. Initial immunoassay was positive for IgG and IgM, confirmed by reference laboratory immunodiffusion and complement fixation (CF) titre of 1:8. Serial testing showed declining titres: CF 1:2 at 3 months (fluconazole level 53.9 µg/mL), unchanged at 4 months (fluconazole 47.4 µg/mL) and negative by CF at 7 months (immunodiffusion positive), demonstrating consistent treatment response.

Given pulmonary disease and cognitive decline, a lumbar puncture was conducted. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, dimorphic fungal PCR and Coccidioides antigen by enzyme immunoassay (EIA) yielded negative results. The initial laboratory immunoassay for antibodies was positive only, while the reference laboratory and CF testing were negative.

Brain MRI with contrast revealed a 5 mm heterogeneously enhancing lesion in the left occipital lobe with surrounding oedema and microhaemorrhages, consistent with a fungal abscess (figure 3). Additional punctate enhancing foci were present without leptomeningeal enhancement. Chest CT revealed scattered ground-glass nodules, residual lingula consolidation and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Abdominal CT showed mild omental stranding, suggesting possible early peritoneal dissemination. Spine MRI was negative. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed no vegetations or fungal endocarditis.

Initial presentation

Periocular pain and tingling with nasal ulceration raised immediate concern for herpes zoster ophthalmicus, a sight-threatening emergency. Prophylactic intravenous acyclovir was administered but discontinued when ophthalmology found no ocular involvement and VZV PCR was negative.

Bacterial cellulitis was considered, given purulent drainage, but failure to respond to antibiotics and negative cultures made this unlikely. Basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma were considered, given the nasal location and ulcerated morphology, but rapid progression and systemic symptoms argued against malignancy. Autoimmune bullous dermatoses were excluded by negative DIF.

With pulmonary findings

Identification of lung consolidations shifted the differential toward systemic disease. Coccidioidomycosis emerged as the primary consideration given endemic exposure and lesion features. Other endemic mycoses, tuberculosis and metastatic malignancy were considered but less likely given the epidemiology and negative AFB testing.

With CNS symptoms

Acute cognitive decline introduced diagnostic complexity. While coccidioidal meningitis was the primary concern, the CSF profile was atypical. Lumbar puncture showed normal cell count (6 red blood cells in x 106/L), glucose (60 mg/dL) and protein (42 mg/dL). Classic Coccidioides meningitis typically presents with lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein and low glucose. The normal CSF required alternative explanations: (1) systemic inflammatory encephalopathy; (2) delirium superimposed on Parkinson’s disease and (3) subclinical inflammation.

Dimorphic fungal PCR was negative for C. immitis/posadasii, Histoplasma, Blastomyces and Sporothrix. CSF Coccidioides antigen EIA was negative; antibody immunoassay was positive at the initial lab but negative at the reference lab.

Despite atypical features, the positive CSF serology, enhancing brain lesion, rapid improvement with fluconazole and sustained remission over 10 months supported genuine CNS involvement.

Final diagnosis

Disseminated coccidioidomycosis (DCM) was established through multiple lines of evidence: culture-confirmed cutaneous disease, characteristic histopathology with PASd-positive spherules, elevated serum CF titre (1:8), positive CSF serology, compatible imaging findings and dramatic treatment response. While PCR was negative and CSF serology showed interlaboratory variation, these findings reflect known limitations rather than uncertainty.

Treatment

On presentation with mental status deterioration and periocular symptoms, a single prophylactic dose of intravenous acyclovir was administered and discontinued after herpes zoster was ruled out. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were withheld per infectious disease recommendation, as the patient was haemodynamically stable without sepsis and preliminary biopsy results were expected within 24 hours. Following confirmation of fungal spherules on histopathology, treatment with high-dose oral fluconazole (800 mg daily) was initiated. This dose was chosen for suspected CNS involvement to ensure adequate penetration. Mental status improved within days, and the patient was discharged with close follow-up.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient showed gradual improvement after antifungal therapy. Mental status stabilised, skin lesions resolved and serial CF titres declined from 1:8 to 1:2 at 3 months, then to negative by 7 months. Brain MRI 2 months later showed marked resolution; the 5 mm occipital lesion had reduced enhancement and resolved oedema. At 10 months, MRI showed complete resolution, appearing as a treated coccidioidoma with only focal susceptibility artefact and no surrounding oedema. Chest CT 2 months post-discharge demonstrated a marked reduction in pulmonary nodular consolidation, with the previously noted 34×22 mm nodular consolidation within the lingula measuring only 18×12 mm. Trace bilateral pleural effusions persisted, and scattered ground-glass opacities remained but were reduced in extent.

Fluconazole was tapered from 800 mg to 600 mg daily at 3 months. At 7 months, the patient resumed daily activities with physical therapy. Due to sustained improvement, the dose was tapered to 400 mg daily at the 10-month visit. He continues lifelong fluconazole for suspected CNS involvement, with no relapse. Dermatological scarring is minimal. Neurology continues to monitor Parkinson’s disease progression.

Discussion

While most coccidioidomycosis infections present with mild symptoms, less than 2% disseminate to extrapulmonary sites. Dissemination typically occurs in immunocompromised individuals, though cases in apparently immunocompetent hosts have been reported.2 3 5 9 The incidence of coccidioidomycosis has increased 10-fold over two decades, reaching nearly 22 000 cases by 2023.25 1012 This increase is partially explained by climate change, increased migration and environmental factors.6 13

This case presents several important clinical and diagnostic challenges in managing DCM in an elderly patient with neurodegenerative disease. Our case is distinctive in several ways: (1) elderly age (late 70s) with Parkinson’s disease, a combination not previously reported with DCM; (2) presentation with acute cognitive decline as a prominent feature rather than classic meningitis symptoms; (3) absence of traditional immunocompromise with subtle findings suggesting immunosenescence and (4) significant diagnostic challenges including discordant test results. The initial negative serology shows that false negatives occur early, before antibody development. Combined testing using EIA, immunodiffusion and CF was essential. The discordance between the reference laboratory and initial CSF testing underscores the importance of clinical context. The negative PCR, despite positive serology and imaging, shows molecular test limitations.14 15 Serial CF decline mirrored recovery, demonstrating serological monitoring value.

Lymphocyte subset analysis showed preserved CD4+ and normal CD4/CD8 ratio, but low natural killer (NK) cells (85.1 /µL), low B cells (34 /µL), elevated CD8+ effector memory T cells (50.6%) and elevated CD4+ terminally differentiated effector memory T cells, reflecting age-related immunosenescence rather than overt immunocompromise.16 17 These subtle changes, combined with Parkinson’s disease, may predispose elderly patients to disseminated fungal infections through mechanisms distinct from traditional immunosuppression.16 18 19

The link between Parkinson’s disease and immune dysregulation is well established; chronic CNS infections correlate with Parkinson’s risk, though not yet with Coccidioides.20 21 This case raises questions about bidirectional effects between neuroinflammation and fungal infection.22 23

Several mechanisms may explain the cognitive decline observed in this case: (1) cytokine-mediated encephalopathy; (2) delirium superimposed on Parkinson’s disease and (3) subclinical meningeal inflammation. Nevertheless, rapid cognitive improvement, imaging resolution and sustained remission support infection involvement through overlapping mechanisms.

High-dose fluconazole (800 mg daily) was prescribed for suspected CNS involvement. Dose reductions to 600 mg daily at 3 months and subsequently to 400 mg at 10 months after discharge were guided by therapeutic drug monitoring and clinical response. Long-term therapy is recommended due to the 80% recurrence rate following discontinuation in CNS coccidioidomycosis. For clinically stable patients with declining serological titres, long-term CSF or neuroimaging surveillance is generally unnecessary unless new neurological symptoms emerge.1 7 14 15 24 25

Culture or PCR confirmation from lung and CNS tissues was not obtained, limiting definitive proof of dissemination. CNS diagnosis relies on clinical correlation, imaging findings and serology from one laboratory. Alternative explanations for cognitive decline cannot be entirely excluded.

Patient’s perspective

I would like to share my experience regarding my illness and the care I received at the medical centre. All staff were professional, knowledgeable, kind and patient, and I am truly grateful for the care I received.

Before arriving at the medical centre, I had been experiencing symptoms for several months. Initially, I was treated for pneumonia and influenza, but despite this treatment, my condition did not improve; in fact, I got worse. While I cannot recall all the details of that time, I vividly remember the feelings of fear and worry, particularly the fear that I might die.

Prior to my visit, no one could determine what was wrong with me. I believe it is crucial for physicians, residents and emergency room staff to be vigilant about persistent coughs. I feel that testing for valley fever should be included as part of the protocol for patients presenting with symptoms similar to pneumonia, COVID-19 and influenza.

Additionally, I am interested in learning about any new research related to Parkinson’s disease and valley fever, as I believe this could be important for understanding my condition and improving future care.

Learning points

Disseminated coccidioidomycosis should be considered in elderly patients in endemic areas, even without traditional immunocompromise, as age-related immunosenescence may increase dissemination risk.
