Incidence and Early‐Life Risk Factors for Food Allergy in a Spanish Birth Cohort
Authors: Jorge García‐Ezquiaga, Patricia Torija‐Berzal, Sergio Quevedo‐Teruel, Teresa Bracamonte‐Bermejo, Patricia Alonso‐López, Luis Echeverría‐Zudaire, María Luz García‐García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe prevalence of food allergy has increased in recent decades. We aimed to determine the cumulative incidence of food allergy in early childhood and to identify associated risk factors.MethodsWe conducted a prospective birth cohort study including 1006 newborns recruited over one year at a single hospital in Spain and followed until 4 years of age. Data were collected at birth and through structured telephone interviews during follow‐up. Children with suspected food allergy underwent skin prick testing, specific serum IgE measurement and open oral food challenges when indicated.ResultsThe cumulative incidence of food allergy was 7.2% (72/1006). Independent risk factors for IgE‐mediated food allergy included hospitalization for bronchospasm (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.37; 95% CI 1.07–5.26), atopic dermatitis (aOR 7.52; 95% CI 3.77–15.01), maternal asthma (aOR 3.12; 95% CI 1.30–7.53) and paternal allergic rhinitis (aOR 2.79; 95% CI 1.31–5.94). Proton‐pump inhibitor use, absence of daycare attendance and early‐onset atopic dermatitis were associated with non‐IgE‐mediated food allergy.ConclusionIn this Spanish birth cohort, food allergy affected 7.2% of children by age 4. Atopic dermatitis was the main risk factor for IgE‐mediated food allergy, while potentially modifiable early‐life exposures were associated with non‐IgE‐mediated forms.Keywords: atopic dermatitis, food allergy, IgE‐mediated allergy, non‐IgE‐mediated allergy, oral food challenge, risk factors

Introduction

Food allergy (FA) represents a growing health burden globally, particularly in paediatric populations. Over the past few decades, the prevalence of FA has increased significantly, especially in high‐income countries, where it now affects up to 8% of children [1, 2]. This rapid rise cannot be solely attributed to genetic predisposition, suggesting a complex interplay between environmental, immunologic and behavioural factors [3].

A major limitation in current epidemiological data is the wide variability in prevalence estimates between studies. This discrepancy is largely explained by methodological differences, particularly regarding the diagnostic approach. Many studies rely on parent‐reported symptoms, which tend to overestimate true FA prevalence, while fewer rely on objective testing such as skin prick tests (SPT), specific IgE levels or oral food challenges (OFC) [4, 5]. OFC, especially when performed under controlled settings, remains the reference standard for diagnosing IgE‐mediated FA and is also used in non‐IgE‐mediated cases. However, it is rarely employed in large‐scale population‐based studies due to its logistical complexity and cost [6].

The Australian HealthNuts study and the EuroPrevall birth cohort have provided important insights into FA epidemiology, highlighting early‐onset atopic dermatitis (AD), family history of atopy and ethnicity as significant risk factors [7, 8]. Yet, the generalizability of these findings to other regions, including southern Europe, remains uncertain. Cultural, dietary, genetic and microbiome differences may influence the natural history of FA. High‐quality prospective data from countries such as Spain are notably scarce.

Moreover, while much attention has focussed on IgE‐mediated FA, non‐IgE‐mediated FA remains under researched despite its clinical relevance. This phenotype often manifests with gastrointestinal symptoms, can be more difficult to diagnose due to the absence of positive SPT or IgE, and is frequently confused with other conditions such as gastroesophageal reflux [9]. Recent studies are shedding light on non‐IgE‐mediated FA. For instance, food protein‐induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES)—a severe non‐IgE FA—is being increasingly recognized as clinical awareness grows. Cow's milk is reported as the most common FPIES trigger globally, although predominant triggers vary by region (e.g., fish is a common trigger in some Mediterranean countries) [10]. In recent years, early‐life exposures have gained attention as modifiable contributors to allergic disease development. These include mode of delivery (e.g., caesarean section), breastfeeding patterns, antibiotic and antacid use, daycare attendance and environmental microbial diversity. While some studies have proposed that caesarean delivery and acid‐suppressive therapy may increase FA risk, results have been inconsistent. Furthermore, the potential protective effect of daycare attendance and early pet exposure remains debated [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

In the light of these gaps, population‐specific longitudinal studies are needed to provide precise estimates of FA incidence and to better understand the distinct risk profiles associated with IgE‐ and non‐IgE‐mediated FA. Spain, with its distinct dietary patterns, microbial exposures and healthcare practices, presents a valuable setting for such investigation. Therefore, in this prospective cohort study, we aimed to estimate the cumulative incidence of both IgE and non‐IgE‐mediated FA in a representative paediatric population during the first four years of life. The secondary goal was to identify early‐life risk factors associated with each phenotype, using standardized diagnostic protocols including open OFCs.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a prospective birth cohort study at Severo Ochoa University Hospital (Madrid, Spain) between 2014 and 2015 (PREVALE study). A total of 1198 newborns were screened for eligibility; 1006 were enrolled and followed through 4 years of age. Inclusion criteria encompassed all infants born at the hospital during the 12‐month recruitment period whose parents or guardians provided informed consent. Newborns were excluded only if their families were unable to participate in telephone follow‐up (due to language or communication barriers) or if consent was declined. Inclusion criteria and all study procedures were approved by the hospital's ethics committee, and parents provided written informed consent.

Exposure Assessment and Follow‐Up

At baseline, paediatricians completed a structured questionnaire covering perinatal history, family history of atopy, and early health indicators. Subsequent data were collected through eight structured telephone interviews, up to four years of age, five of which were conducted during the first year of life. During these follow‐up calls, we enquired about any illnesses and interventions, recording factors such as infections, feeding milestones and medication exposures. In particular, any use of systemic antibiotics in the first year of life (oral or intravenous) was noted, whereas topical (e.g., nasal or ocular) antibiotic use was not counted. Parents were instructed to report any suspected adverse food reactions during follow‐up. They were given guidance on how to recognize potential FA symptoms—for example, immediate reactions such as hives, swelling, vomiting or wheezing after a meal, as well as delayed reactions such as repetitive vomiting or diarrhoea a few hours post‐ingestion. At each telephone contact, parents were specifically asked about any such symptoms. No formal symptom scoring tool was used; instead, any report of a suspected reaction prompted a clinical evaluation by the study team. In cases of reported suspicion, children were evaluated with SPT, specific serum IgE testing and open OFCs as indicated. The decision to perform these diagnostic tests was driven by the child's history. For a suspected IgE‐mediated reaction (acute onset symptoms), we first performed SPT and/or serum specific‐IgE testing for the implicated food allergen. If either test was positive or clinical suspicion remained high, an open OFC was carried out to confirm the allergy, provided it was safe to do so (OFC was omitted only if the initial reaction was life‐threatening or anaphylactic, making a challenge unethical). For a suspected non‐IgE‐mediated reaction (e.g., delayed gastrointestinal symptoms without immediate signs), allergy skin/blood tests were usually negative; in such cases diagnosis relied on eliminating the offending food from the diet and later performing an open OFC under medical supervision to verify if tolerance had been achieved. All OFCs were performed under medical supervision following World Allergy Organization (WAO) and European Academy of Allergy & Clinical Immunology (EAACI) guidelines using age‐appropriate protocols.

Definitions

IgE‐mediated FA was defined as a reaction with evidence of IgE sensitization (positive SPT and/or specific IgE) and clinical symptoms consistent with an IgE mechanism, confirmed by OFC when indicated and not contraindicated, while non‐IgE‐mediated FA was defined as a reaction with negative IgE tests but symptoms and OFC outcomes consistent with non‐IgE immune mechanisms. AD was diagnosed by a paediatrician or dermatologist based on typical clinical features and chronic relapsing course. ‘Early‐onset AD’ was defined as AD onset at or before 6 months of age.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the cumulative incidence of IgE‐mediated FA during the first four years of life. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of non‐IgE‐mediated FA and identification of early‐life risk factors associated with each phenotype.

Statistical Analysis

Incidence proportions were calculated as the number of children with FA (IgE‐ or non‐IgE‐mediated) by age 4, divided by the total cohort (N = 1006). Bivariate analyses (chi‐square or t‐tests) were used to screen potential risk factors. Multivariable logistic regression models were then constructed separately for IgE‐mediated and non‐IgE‐mediated FA, including covariates with p < 0.10 in bivariate analysis or with established clinical relevance. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The required sample size was estimated as 1117 infants (to detect a 3% FA incidence with ±1% precision at 95% confidence). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1198 newborns were eligible for participation. After excluding those lost to follow‐up or who did not meet inclusion criteria, 1006 children finally completed the four‐year follow‐up and were included in the final analysis. Most of them (91.6%) were born full‐term, 68.0% were delivered vaginally, and 53.9% were male (Table 1). A family history of atopy, defined as at least one first‐degree relative with an allergic disease, was reported in 56.9% of participants. Specifically, family histories included asthma (20.2%), AD (22.8%), AR (38.4%) and FA (11.8%). Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Incidence of Food Allergy

By age 4, 72 children had experienced one or more food allergies, yielding a cumulative incidence of 7.2% for any FA (91 distinct allergic episodes in total, as some children reacted to multiple foods). The cumulative incidence of FA rose steeply during the first year of life and continued to increase more gradually thereafter: 5.9% by age 1, 7.7% by age 2, 8.0% by age 3% and 9.0% by age 4 (Figure 1). The cumulative incidence by age refers to the proportion of children with at least one confirmed FA. The majority of initial allergic reactions in infancy were to cow's milk or hen's egg, whereas most new‐onset allergies developing between ages 2 and 4 were to foods such as nuts and fish.

Allergenic Food Introduction

The timing of introduction of common allergenic foods did not differ significantly between children who developed allergies to those foods and those who did not (Table 2). In the cohort, the mean age of introduction was approximately 5.4 months for cow's milk, 9.9 months for hen's egg, 8.7 months for fish, 11.2 months for legumes and 27.9 months for peanut/tree nuts. For example, the mean age of introduction of egg was ~9.9 months in both groups, with no significant difference for those who became egg‐allergic versus non‐allergic (p = 0.263). Notably, delayed introduction (e.g., beyond 12 months for nuts) was common in this cohort but did not correlate with higher allergy incidence. Due to the limited number of cases of IgE‐ and non‐IgE‐mediated allergy to each specific food, age at introduction was analysed in relation to overall FA, without stratifying by allergy type. Therefore, Table 2 presents data for total FA cases (IgE and non‐IgE combined). This has been clarified in the table footnote to ensure transparency.

No Associations With Some Factors

We found no significant association between gestational age, sex, birthweight, or mode of delivery and the risk of developing either phenotype of FA. Maternal smoking (during pregnancy or infancy) was also not associated with FA outcomes in the child. These null findings are further detailed in the Discussion.

IgE‐Mediated Food Allergy

IgE‐mediated FA was documented in 48 children, representing 4.8% of the cohort. The most common triggers of IgE‐mediated allergic reactions were hen's egg, tree nuts (with walnut and peanut being prominent) and cow's milk. In total, two‐thirds of IgE‐mediated allergy cases were due to egg or tree nuts. Multivariate risk factor analysis revealed several independent predictors for IgE‐mediated FA (Table 3). The adjusted associations are illustrated in Figure 2. Infants who had been hospitalized for bronchospasm (severe wheezing) in the first years of life had increased odds of later developing IgE‐mediated FA (aOR 2.37; 95% CI 1.07–5.26; p = 0.034).

A history of AD (eczema) at any time in the first 4 years was a strong determinant, associated with a more than sevenfold higher risk of IgE‐mediated allergy (aOR 7.52; 95% CI 3.77–15.01; p < 0.001). Having an atopic mother or father influenced the risk in different ways: maternal history of asthma conferred about a threefold increased odds (aOR ~3.13; 95% CI 1.30–7.53; p = 0.011) of IgE‐mediated FA in the child, while paternal history of allergic rhinitis was associated with nearly a threefold increase in odds (aOR ~2.80; 95% CI 1.31–5.95; p = 0.008). In contrast, none of the early environmental exposures examined (such as daycare attendance or early antibiotic use) showed a significant effect on IgE‐mediated FA in adjusted analyses. Notably, use of acid‐suppressing medications (proton pump inhibitors or H2‐blockers) in infancy was not associated with IgE‐mediated allergies in our cohort.

Non‐IgE‐Mediated Food Allergy

Non‐IgE‐mediated FA occurred in 29 children (2.9% of the cohort). This phenotype typically manifested as gastrointestinal reactions (e.g., repetitive vomiting or diarrhoea after ingestion) without evidence of IgE. The vast majority of non‐IgE‐mediated cases were triggered by cow's milk proteins, and a smaller number were triggered by fish; only rare cases were attributed to other foods (such as egg, which was an uncommon cause of non‐IgE reactions in our study). In multivariate analysis, we identified three main independent risk factors for non‐IgE‐mediated FA (Figure 2). Lack of daycare attendance in the first three years of life was associated with higher risk (aOR 2.58; 95% CI 1.16–5.72; p = 0.020), suggesting that children who stayed at home had greater odds of developing non‐IgE FA. Early‐life exposure to acid‐suppressive medications emerged as a particularly strong factor: infants who received proton pump inhibitors (most commonly prescribed for reflux symptoms) had markedly higher odds of non‐IgE‐mediated FA (aOR 8.96; 95% CI 2.89–27.81; p < 0.001). Additionally, early‐onset AD (beginning by ≤ 6 months of age) was associated with increased risk of non‐IgE‐mediated FA (aOR 2.52; 95% CI 1.14–5.53; p = 0.022). Unlike the IgE type, a family history of atopy (including maternal or paternal allergic conditions) did not significantly predict non‐IgE FA in the child.

Outcomes of Food Allergies (Tolerance Acquisition)

A substantial proportion of affected children outgrew their FA during early childhood. Over the 4‐year follow‐up, 41 out of the 91 documented allergic reactions (45%) resolved, meaning the child achieved tolerance to the food. Cow's milk allergy showed the highest rate of resolution: by age 4, over 90% of non‐IgE‐mediated milk allergy cases and ~ 70% of IgE‐mediated milk allergy cases had spontaneously resolved. Hen's egg allergy had also remitted in approximately 60% of affected children by the end of the study period. In contrast, none of the children who had allergies to fish, shellfish or tree nuts (such as walnut/peanut) developed tolerance to those foods during the first 4 years of life. Determination of tolerance was based on clinical follow‐up evaluations and confirmed by successful reintroduction of the food (either gradually at home for mild cases or via supervised OFCs in the clinic) without any adverse reaction.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective birth cohort study in Spain to report longitudinal data on the incidence of FA in early childhood, distinguishing between IgE‐ and non‐IgE‐mediated phenotypes through comprehensive follow‐up and diagnoses confirmed by OFC. The cumulative incidence of FA in our cohort (7.2% by age 4) is comparable to reports from other developed regions. For instance, the HealthNuts study in Australia found that approximately 10% of one‐year‐old children had an IgE‐mediated FA confirmed by OFC, while the EuroPrevall study in Europe found the incidence of FA by age 2 to range between 1.5% and 3.5% across different countries. Our data (4.8% IgE‐mediated by age 4, plus 2.9% non‐IgE‐mediated) suggest that extending follow‐up to four years of age and actively confirming diagnoses allows the identification of a broader spectrum of FA cases, including those that might be missed in shorter or less intensive studies. These results also highlight that FA constitutes a significant health issue in Spanish children, in accordance with prevalence rates reported in other high‐income countries [19, 20].

Atopic Dermatitis and the Atopic March

Early‐onset AD (≤ 6 months) was associated with an increased risk of non‐IgE‐mediated FA, while AD at any time during follow‐up emerged as the strongest independent predictor of IgE‐mediated FA in our study. This supports the atopic march paradigm, in which early‐onset eczema serves as an early indicator (and possibly facilitator) of allergic sensitization through transcutaneous exposures. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have documented the link between eczema and subsequent FA [21, 22, 23].

The underlying mechanism is thought to involve skin barrier dysfunction allowing allergens to penetrate and engage the immune system, skewing towards an allergic T2 response. Recent interventional trials are assessing whether improving skin barrier care in infants with eczema can reduce the development of food allergies [24]. In contrast, the absence of a general association between AD and non‐IgE‐mediated FA in our cohort suggests that this phenotype may not follow the classic atopic march and might involve distinct pathways.

Respiratory Issues—Bronchospasm

An intriguing observation was that infants hospitalized for bronchospasm had higher odds of IgE‐mediated FA. Severe bronchospasm in infancy could be a marker of genetic or environmental predisposition to allergy (many of those infants might be on an atopic trajectory that includes wheezing and later FA) [25]. It could also reflect early‐life viral infections that might somehow promote allergic sensitization or be more common in atopy‐prone infants [26]. Although we controlled for multiple factors, this association suggests that a history of significant wheezing illness should prompt paediatricians to monitor for potential allergic manifestations—including FA—even though no studies to date have directly linked bronchospasm or wheezing to the later development of FA.

Familial Factors

As expected, a family history of atopy was a relevant risk factor, but interestingly the specific type of atopy and the affected parent influenced the outcome. Maternal asthma was a strong risk factor for IgE‐FA in the child, while paternal allergic rhinitis (AR) also increased the risk. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that maternal atopy—particularly asthma or multiple allergies—confers a higher risk of allergic disease to offspring compared to paternal atopy [27, 28]. Paternal AR being a factor might indicate genetic heritability of the propensity for allergic sensitization. Interestingly, a family history of FA (in either parent) did not predict FA in offspring, possibly due to underdiagnosis or symptoms resolution in previous generations. Analysing maternal and paternal atopic histories separately, rather than combining them into one variable, proved valuable—it allowed us to uncover the distinct contributions of each parent's allergies to the child's risk.

Feeding Practices

We did not find evidence that the timing of allergenic food introduction significantly influenced food allergy development in this cohort. Although nuts were introduced relatively late compared to current recommendations, allergy prevalence was not higher than in settings promoting earlier introduction. Unlike LEAP and EAT, which focussed on high‐risk infants with severe eczema or existing egg allergy [22, 29], our cohort represented the general population, which may explain the absence of a detectable protective effect. However, randomized trials and meta‐analyses suggest that early introduction of allergenic foods in high‐risk populations reduces the risk of food allergy [30]. Thus, our null finding may reflect the characteristics of our cohort or the influence of other overriding risk factors.

Acid‐Suppressive Medications

We observed a notable association between acid‐suppressive medication use (especially PPIs) in infancy and FA, particularly non‐IgE‐mediated FA. A study conducted by Mitre et al. in 2018 found that infants prescribed PPIs had higher odds of various allergic diseases later in life. The plausible mechanism is that gastric acid suppression leads to incomplete digestion of proteins, and the resultant larger protein fragments might predispose to allergic sensitization in the gut [14]. It is also possible that early prescription of PPIs reflects, in some cases, misdiagnosis of symptoms related to non‐IgE‐mediated cow's milk allergy, such as vomiting or gastroesophageal reflux, thus acting as a confounding factor in the observed association. However, given that PPIs are often over‐prescribed in infancy for relatively mild symptoms, our data contribute to the caution against liberal use of acid suppressants in babies unless clearly indicated. Notably, we observed a disparity between the effects of PPIs and H2 blockers on allergy development. PPIs were associated with a nearly ninefold increase in non‐IgE‐mediated allergy, whereas H2 blockers showed only a modest effect. This disparity may reflect the more profound acid suppression by PPIs, which could impair protein digestion and alter the microbiota more significantly.

Microbial Exposure—Daycare and Pets

Our findings showing that lack of daycare exposure was associated with increased non‐IgE FA supports the hygiene hypothesis in the context of FA. Daycare attendance likely increases exposure to microbial diversity and viral infections, which may educate and balance the developing immune system towards tolerance [31]. Notably, we did not find a statistically significant protective effect of pet exposure on FA. Some studies have suggested that indoor pets (especially dogs) might decrease FA risk by carrying environmental microbes or by introducing allergens early [32, 33]. This is consistent with the ‘biodiversity hypothesis’, which links early microbial exposure to reduced risk of allergic diseases, particularly IgE‐mediated forms. Experimental data also support a role of the gut microbiota in protecting against FA through induction of regulatory T cells and oral tolerance [18, 34].

Null Findings

It's worth emphasizing the factors we examined that did not show significant impact: male sex (often a risk factor for atopy in infancy), prematurity (some prior studies indicated extreme prematurity might reduce allergy risk, but our cohort had few very preterm infants and we saw no difference overall), mode of delivery (no difference between vaginal vs. C‐section) and maternal smoking (no effect). These null findings contribute to the ongoing understanding that some hypothesized risk factors (like caesarean or antibiotics) might have smaller or context‐specific effects than once thought [16, 35, 36, 37, 38].

Strengths and Limitations

This study's strengths include its prospective design, large sample size with minimal attrition, and rigorous FA diagnosis using OFC to confirm it. Inclusion of both IgE and non‐IgE‐mediated FA offers a broader epidemiologic perspective. Limitations include reliance on open OFCs, which may introduce classification bias, though objective criteria were applied. The absence of microbiome or immunological data limits mechanistic insight. Finally, being a single‐center urban study with follow‐up only until age 4 may restrict generalizability and underestimate late‐onset FA.

In conclusion, this birth cohort study provides new data on the incidence of paediatric FA in Spain and highlights distinct early‐life risk factors for IgE‐ versus non‐IgE‐mediated allergies. Our findings emphasize the importance of eczema as an early warning sign for IgE‐mediated FA, and they raise consideration of how certain modifiable exposures (like acid‐suppressive therapy or daycare attendance) might influence the development of non‐IgE‐mediated allergic conditions.
