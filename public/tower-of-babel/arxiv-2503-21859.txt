Simple-stable and Bowditch representations of the four-punctured sphere group in Gromov-hyperbolic spaces.
Suzanne Schlich

Abstract
. In this paper, we study representations from the four-punctured sphere group into isometry groups of Gromov-hyperbolic spaces. We prove that the set of simple-stable representations (in analogy with Minsky’s notion of primitive-stability) and the set of Bowditch representations (inspired by Bowditch’s work on the free group of rank two, generalized by Tan, Wong and Zhang) are equal. Along the way, we study the combinatorics of simple closed curves on the four-punctured sphere and prove a result which quantifies the redundancy of subwords of certain given lengths within simple words.



1.
Introduction

When
S
is an orientable surface of finite type (possibly with boundary) with negative Euler characteristic, the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}(S)
of
S
, which is the deformation space of hyperbolic structures on
S
has been an object of huge interest in the last decades. One of its important features is its interaction with the mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
of the surface
S
, that is the group of orientation preserving homeomorphisms of the surface up to homotopy : Fricke’s Theorem says that the mapping class group acts properly discontinuously on the Teichmüller space (see for example Theorem 12.2 in
[
FM12
]
). One can adopt a more algebraic point of view on this framework by noticing that the Teichmüller space can be thought of as a space of representations from the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(S)
into
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, the isometry group of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, by considering the holonomy of a given hyperbolic structure. More precisely,
\mathcal{T}(S)
is a subset of the character variety
\chi(\pi_{1}(S),\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
which is the quotient of the space of representation
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{1}(S),\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
by the action of the group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
by conjugacy (in fact, when considering character varieties, we usually work with the hausdorffization of this space, which is the biggest Hausdorff quotient). When
S
is a closed surface, Goldman proved (
[
Gol88
]
) that
\mathcal{T}(S)
correspond exactly to two components of the character variety (corresponding to the two possible orientations on the surface), consisting entirely of discrete and faithful representations.
The mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
is a subset of the outer automorphism group
\mathrm{Out}(\pi_{1}(S))=\mathrm{Aut}(\pi_{1}(S))/\mathrm{Inn}(\pi_{1}(S))
, which in the case of closed surfaces, is exactly an index two subgroup of the outer automorphism group. This result is known as the Dehn-Nielsen-Baer Theorem (see for example Chapter Eight in
[
FM12
]
). Thus, Fricke’s Theorem tells us about the action of the mapping class group (or of the outer automorphism group) on the
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
character variety. In general, the action of the mapping class group on the complement of the Teichmüller space is unknown. For closed surfaces, Goldman conjectured (
[
Gol88
]
) that this action should be ergodic.

When replacing
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
by other isometry groups
\mathrm{Isom}(X)
of Gromov-hyperbolic spaces
X
, there is a natural analogue of the Teichmüller space by considering convex-cocompact representations. Here we will say that a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(S)\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
is convex-cocompact if its associated orbit map is a quasi-isometric embedding (see Definition
1.1
). When
X
a rank-one symmetric space, this is equivalent to saying that there exists a non-empty convex subset of
X
, invariant by the action of
\rho(\pi_{1}(S))
, on which
\rho(\pi_{1}(S))
acts properly discontinuous and cocompactly. It is a classical result that, as for the Teichmüller space, the set of convex-cocompact representations is open and that the mapping class group acts properly discontinuously on it. Determining if this domain is maximal or if there can exist other domains of discontinuity in the character variety is a central issue in the study of the action of the mapping class group. Bowditch on the one hand (
[
Bow98
]
), and Minsky on the other hand (
[
Min13
]
), have proposed two different but somehow related approaches to construct open domain of discontinuity in the
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
character variety. While their work was focusing on representations of free groups (of rank two in Bowditch’s work) in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, they have natural analogues when considering representations of fundamental group of surfaces in isometry groups of metric spaces. We will discuss in detail Bowditch’s and Minsky’s approaches in section
1.1
and
1.2
.

In this article, we will be interested in the specific case where
S
is the four-punctured sphere, which we denote by
S_{0,4}
. We will consider representations in
\mathrm{Isom}(X)
, where
X
is a
\delta
-hyperbolic, geodesic and visibility space. By
\delta
-hyperbolic
we refer to hyperbolicity in the sense of Gromov, with hyperbolicity constant
\delta
. Hence
X
is in particular a metric space for which we denote
d
the associated metric. The
geodesicity
hypothesis means that there always exist a geodesic between two distinct points in
X
and the
visibility
hypothesis means that there always exist a bi-infinite geodesic between two distinct points in the Gromov-boundary of the hyperbolic space
X
.

The aim of this article is to compare the notions of simple-stability (Definition
1.2
in section
1.1
), denoted
\mathcal{SS}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
, and of Bowditch representations (Definition
1.3
in section
1.2
), denote
\mathcal{BQ}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
, in the case of the four-punctured sphere group
S_{0,4}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic, geodesic and visibility space.

The sets of Bowditch representations and of simple-stable representations of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
in
\mathrm{Isom}(X)
are equal.

\mathcal{BQ}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))=\mathcal{SS}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X)).

A similar result was proved by the author in
[
Sch24
]
in the case of the free group of rank two. For the free group of rank two and
X=\mathbb{H}^{3}
, this was first done by Lee and Xu (
[
LX19
]
) and independently Series (
[
Ser19
]
,
[
Ser20
]
). The proofs and the techniques developed here are independent of the work of Lee-Xu, and Series in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

Examples of spaces
X
which satisfies the hypothesis of our Theorem include all rank one symmetric spaces (real hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, complex hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
, quaternionic hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}_{H}^{n}
and the hyperbolic plane over the Cayley number
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{O}}
, see
[
Kna96
]
for the classification) or even non proper spaces such as
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}
for example.

We say that a closed curve on
S_{0,4}
is
simple
if there exists a representative in its free homotopy class which has no self-intersection, and which does not bound a disk or a once-punctured disk. We denote by
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
the set of free homotopy classes of (unoriented) simple closed curves on
S_{0,4}
. Although this set is technically in bijective correspondence with a subset of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})/\sim
, where
\sim
identifies conjugate and inverse elements in
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, we will sometimes, with a slight abuse of notation, simply write
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
to refer to a (cyclically reduced) word
\gamma
in the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
representing a simple closed curve in
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, thus identifying curves on the surface
\mathrm{S}_{0,4}
and representatives in the fundamental group.

1.1.
Simple-stability

Let us denote by
\mathcal{C}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}))
the Cayley graph of the group
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
(recall that
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\simeq\mathbb{F}_{3}
). This graph is endowed with a distance
d
which comes from the word length. More precisely, when
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, we will denote
|\gamma|
the reduced word length of
\gamma
(in the set of generators used to define the Cayley graph) and for all
u,v\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
,
d(u,v)=|u^{-1}v|
. Recall that we also denote
d
the distance in
X
, but in context there should be no ambiguity to which distance we refer. Note that the Cayley graph depends on the choice of a generating set for
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, but any two such graphs are quasi-isometric. Since in the following, we will only be interested in properties that are invariant under quasi-isometries, the notions we will define will not depend on this choice of generators and so, for simplicity we do not refer about the generating set. When
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
,
\gamma
has an axis in the Cayley graph
\mathcal{C}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}))
, that is a
\gamma
-invariant geodesic in
\mathcal{C}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}))
. We will use the notation
L_{\gamma}
to refer to this geodesic.

Fix a basepoint
o\in X
.
To any representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
, we can associate an orbit map, which we denote by
\tau_{\rho}
, and which is a map from
\mathcal{C}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}))
to
X
such that
\tau_{\rho}(1)=o
,
\tau_{\rho}
is
\rho
-equivariant, and
\tau_{\rho}
sends the edges of the graph to hyperbolic segment in
X
. Note that under these assumptions we have
\tau_{\rho}(\gamma)=\rho(\gamma)o
, for all element
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
. Moreover, since
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
is finitely generated, we automatically have, using the triangle inequality, that
\tau_{\rho}
is Lipschitz-continuous.

Let us recall the definition of a quasi-isometric embedding, which will be used in following :

Definition 1.1
.

Let
C>0
and
D\geq 0
be two constants. A map
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
between two metric spaces is a
(C,D)
-quasi-isometric embedding
if, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
, we have :

\frac{1}{C}d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-D\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq Cd(x,x^{\prime})+D.

In
[
Min13
]
, Minsky defined the notion of
primitive-stability
for representations of free groups
\mathbb{F}_{n}
into
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
by considering the set of primitive elements in
\mathbb{F}_{n}
and requiring that the orbit map restricted to geodesic corresponding to primitive elements are mapped, via the orbit map, to uniform quasi-geodesics. His definition can easily be adapted to other contexts, in particular to the case where the group is the fundamental group of a surface (closed or not), by replacing in the definition the set of primitive elements by the set of simple elements in the group (the elements in the group corresponding to simple closed curves on the surface). Hereafter we write the definition of simple-stable representations of the four-punctured sphere group, because this paper is dedicated to this particular surface, but the definition of course also makes sense for any other surfaces.

Definition 1.2

(Simple-stability)
.

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
be a representation. We say that
\rho
is
simple-stable
if there exist two constants
C>0
and
D\geq 0
such that for any simple closed curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, the orbit map
\tau_{\rho}
restricted to the geodesic
L_{\gamma}
is a
(C,D)
-quasi-isometric embedding.

We denote
\mathcal{SS}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
the set of simple-stable representations from
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
to
\mathrm{Isom}(X)
.

In particular, we immediately see from the definition that the set of simple-stable representations contains the set of convex-cocompact representations, as defined above in the introduction.

Minsky proved (in
[
Min13
]
), that in the character variety, the set of primitive-stable representations in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is open,
\mathrm{Out}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
-invariant and that
\mathrm{Out}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
acts on it properly discontinuously. Moreover, he also proved that the set of primitive-stable representations strictly contains the set of convex-cocompact representations. In particular, this shows the existence of a non discrete primitive-stable representation in this setting. The openness still holds when considering simple-stable representations in the more general setting of
\delta
-hyperbolic spaces. For details, a proof is written by the author in section 4.4 of
[
Sch24
]
. Note that although the article
[
Sch24
]
focuses on the group
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the proof of openness works exactly the same for other groups. Since the set of simple closed curves on the surface is invariant under the action of the mapping class group, we easily deduce that
\mathcal{SS}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
is
\mathrm{MCG}(S_{0,4})
-invariant. The fact that the action is properly discontinuous follows the same lines than in the case of primitive-stable representations.
Thus we have that
\mathcal{SS}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
is an open domain of discontinuity for the action of the mapping class group.

Let us make a brief remark on the case of the once-punctured torus. Its fundamental group is the free group of rank two and it happens that there is a bijective correspondence between the set of simple closed curves on the once-punctured torus and the primitive elements in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Moreover, the mapping class group of the once-punctured torus is the outer automorphism group of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Thus, here the notions of primitive-stability in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and simple-stability of the once-punctured torus coincide (but this is the only punctured surface for which this happens).

1.2.
Bowditch representations

Recall that we introduced above the word length
|\gamma|
of an element
\gamma\lx@nobreakspace\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
. We will also consider its cyclically reduced word length, which we denote
\|\gamma\|
.
For
A
an isometry of the space
X
, we define its displacement length as the quantity
l(A):=\underset{x\in X}{\inf}d(Ax,x)
. Note that both the cyclically reduced word length and the displacement length are conjugacy invariants.

We now give the definition of a Bowditch representation, and we will explain the denomination after.

Definition 1.3

(Bowditch representations)
.

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
be a representation. We say that
\rho
is a
Bowditch representation
if there exist two constants
C>0
and
D\geq 0
such that for all simple closed curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, we have
\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\|\gamma\|-D\leq l(\rho(\gamma))
.

We denote
\mathcal{BQ}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
the set of Bowditch representations from
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
to
\mathrm{Isom}(X)
.

We will see in section
4.2
that the additive constant actually plays no role, and that we get an equivalent definition by removing it. Also note that the other inequality (
\displaystyle l(\rho(\gamma))\leq C^{\prime}\|\gamma\|
) is always true for some constant
C^{\prime}
, because the group
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
is finitely generated.

It is quite straightforward from the definition that simple-stable representations are in particular Bowditch representations. A proof of this inclusion can be found in section 4.2 in
[
Sch24
]
(here the group which is studied is
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, but nothing relies on the specific structure of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, so the proof also works for
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, or other fundamental groups of surfaces). Therefore the whole difficulty of Theorem
1.1
is to prove that Bowditch representations are simple-stable.

In
[
Bow98
]
, Bowditch studied representations of the free group of rank two
\mathbb{F}_{2}
in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{2}=<a,b>
be a free generating set for
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
denote the set of conjugacy class of primitive elements in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. He introduced the three following conditions on a representation
\rho:\mathbb{F}_{2}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
:

(1)

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{2}),\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(\gamma))\in\mathbb{C}\backslash[-2,2]

(2)

The set
\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{2}):|\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(\gamma))|\leq 2\}
is finite.

(3)

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho([a,b]))=-2

Bowditch proved that the representations satisfying those conditions form an open domain of discontinuity in the character variety. His approach makes extensive use of the Farey triangulation of the hyperbolic plane and more specifically its dual tree, and uses the language of Markoff maps. His work was later generalized by Tan, Wong and Zhang in
[
TWZ08
]
, who made an analogue study in the case where the value of the commutator is no more necessarily
-2
, hence removing the third condition above. Both Bowditch and Tan-Wong-Zhang proved a "Fibonacci-growth" property on these representations, which we can, combining their works, reformulate as follows :

(1)

\rho\text{ satisfies the conditions \eqref{cond:BQ2} and \eqref{cond:BQ3}}\iff\exists C>0,\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F}_{2}),\quad\frac{1}{C}\|\gamma\|\leq l(\rho(\gamma)).

This equivalence inspires the more general definition of a Bowditch representation (Definition
1.3
) in the more general setting of
\delta
-hyperbolic spaces and for fundamental groups of surface (possibly with boundary). Note that it is not clear from Definition
1.3
that this defines an open subset in the character variety, nor that the mapping class group acts properly discontinuously on it. However as mentioned in section
1.1
, it is not hard to prove that the set of simple-stable representations is an open domain of discontinuity, we therefore obtain the following corollary :

Corollary 1.2
.

The set
\mathcal{BQ}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),X)
is an open domain of discontinuity for the action of
\mathrm{MCG}(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}))
on
\chi(\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Isom}(X))
.

Maloni, Palesi and Tan studied in
[
MPT15
]
the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-character variety of the four-punctured sphere together with the action of the mapping class group on it. They defined, in the same spirit as Bowditch and Tan-Wong-Zhang, a set of representation defined by conditions on the traces and proved that it forms an open domain of discontinuity. More precisely, their conditions on
S_{0,4}
are as follows :

(BQ1)

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(\gamma))\in\mathbb{C}\backslash[-2,2]

(BQ2)

The set
\{\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}):|\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(\gamma))|\leq K(\mu)\}
is finite.

Here,
K(\mu)
is a constant which depends on
\mu=(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(a)),\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(b)),\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(c)),\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(d)))\in\mathbb{C}^{4}
, where
a,b,c,d
are the four boundary components of
S_{0,4}
. In addition, Maloni, Palesi and Tan proved that the representations satisfying the above two conditions also have "Fibonacci growth", in other words that the equivalence (
1
) is again true in this context. We can thus combine their work with our Theorem
1.1
to obtain :

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. The following conditions are equivalent :

(1)

\rho
satisfies conditions (BQ1) and (BQ2) of Maloni-Palesi-Tan

(2)

\rho
is simple-stable.

In an other direction, Maloni and Palesi also analysed the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
character variety of the three-holed projective plane in
[
MP20
]
and together with Yang they studied its
\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})
character variety in
[
MPY21
]
.

1.3.
Redundancy of subwords of simple words in
S_{0,4}
and strategy of the proof

We now explain what is the general strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.3.1.
Redundancy of subwords of simple words

First, we will develop a study of the combinatorics of simple closed curves on the four-punctured sphere. This is done in sections
2
and
3
. In particular, we prove Theorem
3.8
, which quantifies, after defining some specific lengths, how much an arbitrary subword of one of these lengths repeats itself inside simple words of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
(we will explain in more details what we mean by this).

We will identify the four-punctured sphere with a quotient of the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in order to see the simple closed curves as quotient of straighlines with rational slope in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular, this will highlight the (bijective) correspondence between rational numbers and equivalence classes of simple closed curves on the four-punctured sphere (Proposition
2.1
). We will call these rationals the "slopes" associated to simple closed curves and their continued fractions expansions
[n_{1},\dots,n_{r}]
will be of particular interest. In particular, considering the truncated fractions expansion
[n_{1},\dots,n_{i}]
for all
1\leq i\leq r
, we will define some "special lengths", denoted
l_{i}(\gamma)
(see Definition
2.1
) and which will play a crucial role in our study and in Theorem
3.8
. In section
2.3
, we will study the combinatorics of a simple closed curve
\gamma
at different scales, each of them corresponding to a level
i
in the continued fraction expansion of the slope of
\gamma
(that is each integer
0\leq i\leq r)
. In particular, Lemma
2.8
will decompose a simple closed curve
\gamma
seen as a word in
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, for each level
i
, as a concatenation of words which will only depend on the integers
n_{1},\dots,n_{i}
.

Then, section
3
will be dedicated to the statement and the proof of the "Special-lengths" Theorem
3.8
, which will be a major tool for our proof of Theorem
1.1
, and might be of independent interest. Since Theorem
3.8
has a few technical assumptions, for the sake of clarity we give here a slightly weaker version (Theorem
1.4
) but which gets rid of some of the technicalities. In this theorem, we first fix a simple closed curve
\gamma
. Now recall that we have defined some "special lengths" associated to
\gamma
:
l_{1}(\gamma),\dots,l_{r}(\gamma)
. We choose one of these lengths and an arbitrary subword of
\gamma
of this specific length (in fact of this specific length minus five). Then we prove that this subword and its inverse occur a certain number of times in
\gamma
which we can quantify uniformly in
\gamma
: the occurrences of this subword and its inverse in
\gamma
occupy at least one thirtieth of the word
\gamma
.

Theorem 1.4

(Special-lengths)
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
. Fix
10\leq i\leq r(\gamma)
such that
|\gamma|\geq 10l_{i}(\gamma)
.

Let
w\in\mathbb{F}_{3}
be any subword of
\gamma
of length
l_{i}(\gamma)-5
.

Then we can decompose the word
\gamma
as a concatenation
\gamma=u_{1}\ldots u_{q}
such that there exists a subset
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,q\}
satisfying the following :

(1)

For all
k\in\mathcal{I}
,
u_{k}\in\{w,w^{-1}\}
.

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}|u_{k}|\geq\frac{1}{30}|\gamma|
.

Our approach to proving this theorem will be geometric, and so in section
3.1
we will first need to introduce some generalities about tilings of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
adapted to a lattice of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, set up our notations and state a few facts needed in the following. Then section
3.2
will provide a short reminder about Farey neighbors.

The proof will start by associating to every simple closed curve
\gamma
and every level
1\leq i\leq r
of the continued fraction expansion of the slope of
\gamma
a lattice
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(section
3.3.1
). This lattice will be particularly useful to understand the word
\gamma
at the level
i
: within this geometric framework one can "read" the word
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by following an horizontal line and the subwords of lengths approximately
l_{i}(\gamma)
will be easier to visualize and to understand (section
3.3.2
and
3.3.3
). Finally, section
3.3.4
will combine the results on lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of section
3.1
with our study of subwords of
\gamma
of length approximately
l_{i}(\gamma)
in section
3.3.2
and
3.3.3
to prove Theorem
3.8
.

1.3.2.
Strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1

Having established the combinatorial results in the group and in particular Theorem
1.4
, the proof will basically follow the same strategy than the proof of the corresponding theorem for the free group of rank two in
[
Sch24
]
. However, since the combinatorics of simple closed curves on the four-punctured sphere is different and more complicated than the combinatorics of primitive elements in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the statements and their proofs have to be adapted. For completeness, we present now the main steps and ideas of the rest of the proof.

In section
4
, we will first study a uniform quasi-geodesicity setting in section
4.1
which will later, when combined with Lemma
4.4
, appears as a local uniform quasi-geodesicity property of Bowditch representations. In Lemma
4.3
of section
4.2
, we will show that the images of simple elements under a Bowditch representation are always hyperbolic. In particular, denoting
L_{\gamma}
the axis of
\gamma
in the Cayley graph of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
and
\tau_{\rho}
the orbit map of a Bowditch representation
\rho
, we deduce that
\tau_{\rho}(L_{\gamma})
is always a quasi-geodesic.

In section
5
, we will prove Proposition
5.1
, which will be the main step for proving Theorem
1.1
. It states that the simple leaves
L_{\gamma}
are sent by the orbit map to uniform neighborhoods of the axes of the hyperbolic isometries
\rho(\gamma)
. To do so, we proceed by contradiction and to this purpose we introduce a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of simple elements in
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
such that the images of the orbit map on the simple leaf
L_{\gamma_{n}}
becomes further and further away from the axis of
\rho(\gamma_{n})
as
n
increases. We will then study the continued fraction expansion of the slope of
\gamma_{n}
which we denote by
[N^{n}_{1},\dots,N^{n}_{r(n)}]
. The uniform quasi-geodesicity setting studied in section
4.1
, together with Lemma
4.4
will enable us to show that the sequences
(N^{n}_{i})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
must be bounded for all
i
, and, as a consequence, the depth
r(n)
of the continued fraction expansion will tend to infinity (Lemma
5.2
). We will next introduce the notion of a
K
-excursion of the orbit map (section
5.3
) and notice that we can extract from the sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of
K_{n}
-excursions as large as we want (
K_{n}\to\infty
) in Lemma
5.3
. We will also introduce the notion of an
\varepsilon
-quasi-loop, and since large excursions will correspond to quasi-loop (Lemma
5.4
), we will find a quasi-loop in each
\gamma_{n}
(for large
n
). But we will be even more precise and, using the Special-lengths Theorem (
3.8
) together with the fact that once found a quasi-loop, we can find many others of smaller lengths "inside", we will find a uniformly bounded from below proportion of the word
\gamma_{n}
consisting of disjoint quasi-loops (Lemma
5.5
). We will repeat our argument for the remainders in
\gamma_{n}
that do not yet consist of disjoint quasi-loops in order to find an arbitrarily large proportion of the word
\gamma_{n}
consisting of disjoint quasi-loops (Lemma
5.7
). To formalize this idea we will use a recursive argument in the proof. This mean that we will find an arbitrarily large proportion of the word
\gamma_{n}
which does not displace the basepoint much, and this will be in contradiction with the Bowditch’s hypothesis.

At last, section
6
will conclude the proof by showing that a Bowditch representation of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
satisfying the conclusion of Proposition
5.1
is simple-stable by using similar techniques.

\gamma
a simple closed curve on
\mathrm{S}_{0,4}

\gamma

\gamma

\bullet

Fact 1
: Every letter in
\gamma

It suffices to show that if a word contains the pattern
\ldots s^{2}\dots

. But since this new arc is not innermost, it has to "escape" the region bordered by

\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)\in[-\infty,-1]

\varphi

is half the word length of

.

Remark 2.3
.

Notice that the map
\mathrm{Slope}
depends on the choice of a basis
\{a,b,c\}
for
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})=\nolinebreak\mathbb{F}_{3}
. The choice of another basis leads to another map

. However, we can link the slope map of two different bases. Denote
\mathrm{Slope}_{a,b,c}
the slope map in the basis

.

Let

be such that
\mathrm{Slope}_{a,b,c}(\gamma)=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}]
. Fix

and let

be another basis of
\mathbb{F}_{3}

and
\mathrm{Slope}_{a,b,c}(c^{\prime}a^{\prime})=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}+1]

\mathrm{Slope}_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}}(\gamma)=[n_{i+1},\ldots,n_{r}]

The
\mathrm{Slope}
map only depends on the class of an element up to conjugacy and inversion. Therefore, for an element

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{3}=\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})=\langle a,b,c\rangle

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
be a cyclically reduced element representing a simple closed curve on
\mathrm{S}_{0,4}
. There exists two words
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the element
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
.

Proof.

First notice that if the Lemma is true for some
\gamma
, it is still true for the inverse of
\gamma
and for a conjugate of
\gamma
. Hence, since any two elements of
\mathbb{F}_{3}
(induced by simple closed curves) with the same slope are equal up to conjugacy and inversion, it is sufficient to show the lemma for any element with the same slope as
\gamma
.

Let
\frac{p}{q}=\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
. There exists another rational
\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
such that
|pq^{\prime}-p^{\prime}q|=\pm 1
. Now use Lemma
2.4
to show the existence of a mapping class
\varphi\in\mathrm{MCG}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
such that
\mathrm{Slope}(\varphi(ca))=\frac{p}{q}
and
\mathrm{Slope}(\varphi(cb))=\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
. Choose a representative of
\varphi
in
\mathrm{Homeo}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
and with a slight abuse of notation still denote it by
\varphi
. Let
\eta=\varphi(ca)
. Then
\eta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
and
\mathrm{Slope}(\eta)=\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
. Moreover, by Lemma
2.6
, we deduce that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(ca)^{n}c(ca)^{-n}b\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
. Then, since
\varphi\in\mathrm{Homeo}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, we also obtain that
\varphi((ca)^{n}c(ca)^{-n}b)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. But we have
\varphi((ca)^{n}c(ca)^{-n}b)=\eta^{n}\varphi(c)\eta^{-n}\varphi(b)
hence the corollary with
\delta_{1}=\varphi(c)
and
\delta_{2}=\varphi(b)
.
∎

We now end this section by writing any element
\gamma
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
as a concatenation of "building blocks", each of them using some "approximation"

of
\gamma
. We can approximate
\gamma
at different levels, indexed by the integer

, corresponding to the successive steps in the continued fraction expansion of the slope of
\gamma
.

3.2.
A few reminders about
Farey neighbours

In the next section, we will in particular consider a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined using the continued fraction expansion of a rational corresponding to a slope of a curve. Then we now need to say a few things about fractions,
Farey neightbours
and the link with continued fraction expansion.

In this section, we will consider fractions
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
, and we will always assume that they are written as irreducible fractions (which means that
p
and
q
are coprime) with non-negative denominator. By convention, we also consider the fraction
\displaystyle\frac{1}{0}
which we will sometimes refer to as
+\infty
.

We say that two distinct fractions
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
, are
Farey neighbours
whenever
|pq^{\prime}-pq|=1
. This is equivalent to saying that the two vectors
(q,p)
and
(q^{\prime},p^{\prime})
form a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. In this case, we denote by
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\oplus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
the
Farey sum
of the two fractions
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
, which is defined by
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\oplus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}=\frac{p+p^{\prime}}{q+q^{\prime}}
(note that this last fraction is automatically irreducible with positive denominator). The fractions
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\oplus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
are also Farey neighbours, and so are
\displaystyle\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\oplus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
. Suppose that
qq^{\prime}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
. Then we have :

\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p}{q}\oplus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\leq\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}.

Now define
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\ominus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}=\frac{p-p^{\prime}}{q-q^{\prime}}
. The fractions
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\ominus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
are Farey neighbours, and so are
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}\ominus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
. Note that with this definition
\ominus
is commutative.

We have either
\displaystyle(\frac{p}{q}\ominus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}})\oplus\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}=\frac{p}{q}
or
\displaystyle(\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\ominus\frac{p}{q})\oplus\frac{p}{q}=\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
.

Link with continued fraction expansion.

Let
n_{1}\in\mathbb{Z},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
n_{r}\geq 2
. Denote
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}]
,
\displaystyle\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}}=\frac{1}{0}
and for all
1\leq i\leq r
,
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}]
. Then, it is not hard to check that for all
1\leq i\leq r,\quad\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
are Farey-neighbours. We can compute their Farey sum and Farey difference and we obtain :

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}

\displaystyle=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}+1]

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}-1]&\text{ if }n_{i}\geq 2\text{ or }i=1\\
{[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i-2}]}&\text{ if }n_{i}=1\text{ and }i\geq 3\\
\frac{1}{0}=\infty&\text{ if }n_{i}=1\text{ and }i=2\end{array}[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}-1]\right.

Notice that in this case we always have
\displaystyle(\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}})\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
.

Let us make two observations :

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}

\displaystyle\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\neq\frac{p}{q}

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}

\displaystyle\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}=-1\text{ if and only if }i=1\text{ and }n_{i}=0

Moreover, since
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}]
, we either have
\displaystyle[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}+1]\leq[n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}]\leq[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}]
or
\displaystyle[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}]\leq[n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}]\leq[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}+1]
, that is :

\displaystyle\text{ either }\qquad\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}.

\bullet

If
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
, this forces
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
and so
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\geq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\geq\frac{p}{q}
.

Hence
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
.

\bullet

If
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
, this forces
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
and so
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\leq\frac{p}{q}
.

Hence
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
.

Thus, in every case, the rationals
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
are on either side of
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
.

3.3.
Special-lengths Theorem

For
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
a simple closed curve on
S_{0,4}
of slope
[n_{1}(\gamma),\dots,n_{r(\gamma)}(\gamma)]
, recall that we have defined the lengths
l_{i}(\gamma)
and
l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)
in Definition
2.1
. These lengths will play an important role in our study of the redundancy of arbitrary subwords of simple words. Indeed, the following theorem, to which this section is dedicated, says that these lengths are "special" in some sense : we prove that the subwords of
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
of these specific lengths (in fact precisely
l_{i}(\gamma)-5
) are redundant in the sense that they and their inverses can be found many other times in the word
\gamma
, and their appearances can be quantified by a uniform constant.

Theorem 3.8

(Special-lengths)
.

Set
\alpha=\frac{1}{30}
. Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
. Consider
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)=[n_{1}(\gamma),\ldots,n_{r(\gamma)}(\gamma)]
the continued fraction expansion of
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
. Fix
0\leq i\leq r(\gamma)
such that
l_{i}(\gamma)\geq 10
(in particular, note that by inequality (
8
), this condition is fulfilled whenever
i\geq 10
). Let
W\in\mathbb{F}_{3}
be a subword of (a cyclic-permutation of)
\gamma
(or its inverse) such that
|W|\geq 3(l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)+l_{i}(\gamma)+1)
and
w\in\mathbb{F}_{3}
be a subword of (a cyclic-permutation of)
\gamma
(or its inverse) of length
l_{i}(\gamma)-5
.

Then we can decompose the word
W
as a concatenation
W=u_{1}\ldots u_{q}
such that there exists a subset
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\ldots,q\}
satisfying the following :

(1)

For all
k\in\mathcal{I}
,
u_{k}\in\{w,w^{-1}\}
.

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}|u_{k}|\geq\alpha|W|
.

We will prove Theorem
3.8
in section
3.3.4
. For this purpose, given a simple closed curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
and a fixed integer
0\leq i\leq r(\gamma)
, we will define in section
3.3.1
a lattice
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
together with a basis
(u,v)
of
\Lambda
which will be particularly convenient for studying subwords of
\gamma
of length
l_{i}(\gamma)
. This will allow us to "read" the word
\gamma
by following a horizontal line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(section
3.3.2
) and then we will consider for this specific choice of
\Lambda
and
(u,v)
the tiles
S(x,u,v)
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined in section
3.1
to study some specific subwords of
\gamma
: the one read in the rectangle
S(x,u,v)
, which will be of lengths approximately
l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)
(section
3.3.3
).

3.3.1.
A tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

In this section, we associate to a simple closed curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
and an integer
1\leq i\leq r(\gamma)
a lattice
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Fix
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
a simple closed curve. Let us assume that
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)\geq 0
and write
[n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}]=\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
the continued fraction expansion of
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
. For
1\leq i<r
, we write
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}]
and for
i=0
,
\displaystyle\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}}=\frac{1}{0}
. Now we fix
1\leq i<r
for all this section and if
i=1
, we assume that
n_{i}\neq 0
.

Denote
u=(q_{i},p_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
v=(q_{i-1},p_{i-1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Notice that, because of the assumption
i<r
, we have
\mathrm{Slope}(u)\neq\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
and
\mathrm{Slope}(v)\neq\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
. After possibly exchanging
u
and
v
, suppose that
\mathrm{Slope}(u)<\mathrm{Slope}(v)
. Then, we have :

(1)

\mathrm{Slope}(u)<\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)<\mathrm{Slope}(v)
.

(2)

(u,v)
form a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

Moreover, using the equalities (
18
) and (
19
) of section
3.2
and the hypothesis
(i,n_{i})\neq(1,0)
, we have :

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Slope}(u+v)=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\neq\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Slope}(v-u)=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}\neq-1

Let us write as irreducible fractions :

\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}=\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma),\quad\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}=\mathrm{Slope}(u),\quad\frac{p^{\prime}_{i}}{q^{\prime}_{i}}=\mathrm{Slope}(v)

The map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
.

Consider
f_{\gamma,u,v}
an element of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
which sends the lines of slope
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to the lines of slope
0
and the lines of slope
-1
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to the lines of slope
+\infty
. Choose
f_{\gamma,u,v}
such that
f_{\gamma,u,v}
preserves the orientation, and scale it so that the abscissa of the vector
f_{\gamma,u,v}(u+v)
is
1
and the ordinate of the vector
f_{\gamma,u,v}(v-u)
is also
1
(such a map always exists). Let us give
f_{\gamma,u,v}
explicitly. The map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
is the product of a diagonal matrix
D
with a matrix
M
which sends lines of slope
-1
to lines of slope
+\infty
and lines of slope
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
to lines of slope
0
. Therefore for
M
we can choose the inverse of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}q&-1\\
p&1\end{pmatrix}
which gives
\displaystyle M=\frac{1}{p+q}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-p&q\end{pmatrix}
. Then
\displaystyle Mu=\frac{1}{p+q}\begin{pmatrix}p_{i}+q_{i}\\
-pq_{i}+qp_{i}\end{pmatrix}
and
\displaystyle Mv=\frac{1}{p+q}\begin{pmatrix}p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}\\
-pq^{\prime}_{i}+qp^{\prime}_{i}\end{pmatrix}

and in order to get the right rescaling we need to impose :
\displaystyle D=\displaystyle(p+q)\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{p(q_{i}-q^{\prime}_{i})-q(p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i})}\end{pmatrix}
. Hence we obtain :

(20)

f_{\gamma,u,v}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}}&\frac{1}{p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}}\\
\frac{-p}{p(q_{i}-q^{\prime}_{i})-q(p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i})}&\frac{q}{p(q_{i}-q^{\prime}_{i})-q(p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i})}\end{pmatrix}

Also note that
(p_{i}+q_{i},p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i})\in\{(l_{i}(\gamma),l_{i-1}(\gamma)),(l_{i-1}(\gamma),l_{i}(\gamma))\}
and then
p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}=l_{i}(\gamma)+l_{i-1}(\gamma)=l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)
. Now we can compute
f_{\gamma,u,v}(u)
and
f_{\gamma,u,v}(v)
:

\displaystyle f_{\gamma,u,v}(u)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p_{i}+q_{i}}{p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}},\frac{-pq_{i}+qp_{i}}{p(q_{i}-q^{\prime}_{i})-q(p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle f_{\gamma,u,v}(v)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}}{p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}},\frac{-pq^{\prime}_{i}+qp^{\prime}_{i}}{p(q_{i}-q^{\prime}_{i})-q(p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i})}\right)

We deduce :

(21)

\max((f_{\gamma,u,v}(u))_{1},(f_{\gamma,u,v}(v))_{1})=\frac{\max(p_{i}+q_{i},p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i})}{p_{i}+q_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}+q^{\prime}_{i}}=\frac{\max(l_{i}(\gamma),l_{i-1}(\gamma))}{l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)}=\frac{l_{i}(\gamma)}{l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)}

Since we have
\mathrm{Slope}(u)<\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)<\mathrm{Slope}(v)
, we deduce that
\mathrm{Slope}(f_{\gamma,u,v}(u))<0<\mathrm{Slope}(f_{\gamma,u,v}(v))
. We had also noticed that
\mathrm{Slope}(u+v)\neq\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
and
\mathrm{Slope}(v-u)\neq-1
, hence we can also deduce that

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Slope}(f_{\gamma,u,v}(u+v))

\displaystyle\neq 0

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Slope}(f_{\gamma,u,v}(v-u))

\displaystyle\neq\infty

Note also that the slope of
f_{\gamma,u,v}(u+v)
and
f_{\gamma,u,v}(v-u)
are of opposite signs. Indeed, before applying the map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
, the slope of
u+v
is
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
and the slope of
v-u
is
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
. By the result of the end of section
3.2
, the rationals
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\oplus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\ominus\frac{p_{i-1}}{q_{i-1}}
are on either side of
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
, (with
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
the slope of
\gamma
), hence after applying the map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
, we deduce that these two slopes are of opposite signs, since the slope
\displaystyle\frac{p}{q}
is sent to
0
by
f_{\gamma,u,v}
.

The map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
sends the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to another lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, denote it by
\Lambda=f_{\gamma,u,v}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Hence
2\Lambda=f_{\gamma,u,v}(2\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Recall that we had defined the type of a point in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we can then also define the
type
of a point in
\Lambda
as the type of the corresponding point in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We obtain four types of points in
\Lambda
given by the four classes of the elements of
\Lambda
mod
2\Lambda
. We had also defined three sets of lines :
L_{A},L_{B}
and
L_{C}
so we can look at their images by
f_{\gamma,u,v}
. Since the lines in
L_{C}
have slope
-1
, the lines in
f_{\gamma,u,v}(L_{C})
have slope
+\infty
. Also note that a line of slope
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)
has for image a line of slope
0
. Thus the lines in
f_{\gamma,u,v}(L_{A})
have a negative slope and the lines in
f_{\gamma,u,v}(L_{B})
have a positive slope (recall that the lines in
L_{A}
have slope
0
and the lines in
L_{B}
have slope
+\infty
). Two non parallel lines in
f_{\gamma,u,v}(L_{A})\cup f_{\gamma,u,v}(L_{B})\cup f_{\gamma,u,v}(L_{C})
always intersect in a point of
\Lambda
.

In order to simplify the notation, from now on we simply denote by
L_{A},L_{B},L_{C},u,v
the images of
L_{A},L_{B},L_{C},u,v
by
f_{\gamma,u,v}
.

Now that the lattice
\Lambda
is fixed as well as the basis
(u,v)
of
\Lambda
satisfying
\mathrm{Slope}(u)<0<\mathrm{Slope}(v)
, we can use the notations of section
3.1
to talk about the rectangle
S(x,u,v)
and
R(x,u,v)
, for a point
x\in\Lambda
.
Note that, by the choice of the scaling of
f_{\gamma,u,v}
, the lengths of the sides of
S(x,u,v)
are
1
.

3.3.2.
Reading the word
\gamma
and its subwords

We can still read the word corresponding to
\gamma
in the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
using the lattice
\Lambda
and the three new sets of lines
L_{A},L_{B}
and
L_{C}
: Follow a line
l_{0}
of slope
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which avoids the lattice
\Lambda
and record
a,b
or
c
each time the line
l_{0}
crosses a line
l
of
L_{A},L_{B}
or
L_{C}
with power
\pm 1
depending on the transverse orientation of
l
at the intersection point with
l_{0}
. To obtain the full word
\gamma
(more precisely a cyclic permutation of
\gamma
or its inverse), we have to follow
l_{0}
between two points which are identified by an element of
2\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
, that is between two points
x
and
y
on
l_{0}
such that
y=x+2\lambda
, with
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
(here
\mathcal{P}(\Lambda)
denotes the set of primitive elements in
\Lambda
, that is the elements in
\Lambda
which can be completed to a basis of
\Lambda
). More generally, by following
l_{0}
along a segment
I\subset[x,y]\subset l_{0}
, we read a subword of
\gamma
(or a subword of a cyclic-permutation of
\gamma
or its inverse). We denote by
W(I)
this subword. We are now interested in the link between the length of the segment
I
and the word length of the word
W(I)
we read by following
I
. More precisely, we need the following lemma :

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
W
be a subword of
\gamma
(or more generally a subword of a cyclic permutation of
\gamma
or its inverse). There exists a horizontal segment
I
in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that we read
W
by following
I
and, denoting
l(I)
the length of the segment
I
and
|W|
the word length of
W
, we have :

|W|-3\leq l(I)l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)\leq|W|+1

Remember that the integer
i
which appears in this Lemma
3.9
has been fixed at the beginning of section
3.3.1
. Everything that has been defined using
i
, therefore depends on it : the map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
, the lattice
\Lambda
, the three sets of lines
L_{A},L_{B}
and
L_{C}
, etc… We have chosen not to index these objects by
i
in order to reduce the amount of notation.

Proof.

Let us start by computing the spacing between two consecutive lines in
L_{C}
, and denote it by
E_{C}
. Recall that
L_{C}
consists of parallel lines of slope
+\infty
which are the images of the lines of equations
y=-x+2k,k\in\mathbb{Z}
, under the map
f_{\gamma,u,v}
. Then, since the point
(x,y)=(\frac{2q}{p+q},\frac{2p}{p+q})
belongs both to the line of equation
y=-x+2
and to the line of equation
y=\frac{p}{q}x
, its image by
f_{\gamma,u,v}
belongs both to the line of equation
y=0
and to the line of equation
x=E_{C}
. We compute
f_{\gamma,u,v}(x,y)
and we obtain
(\frac{2}{l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)},0)
. Hence
E_{C}=\frac{2}{l^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)}
.

Now, since
W
is a subword of
\gamma
, we can read it somewhere following a subsegment
I
of
l_{0}
, that is a horizontal segment.
Now remember that since
\mathrm{Slope}(\gamma)\geq 0
, the letters of
\gamma
alternate between letters in
\{c,c^{-1}\}
and letters in
\{a,a^{-1},b,b^{-1}\}
. This means that
I
crosses exactly one line in
L_{A}\cup L_{B}
between two lines in
L_{C}
. Denote
n_{C}
the number of intersection points between
I
and
L_{C}
, or equivalently the number of
c
and
c^{-1}
in
W
.

\bullet

If
|W|
is even, then
|W|=2n_{C}
, and we can choose
I
such that :

(n_{C}-1)E_{C}\leq l(I)\leq n_{C}E_{C},\quad\text{ which is equivalent to }\quad(|W|-2)E_{C}\leq 2l(I)\leq|W|E_{C}.

\bullet

If
|W|
is odd and
|W|=2n_{C}+1
, then we can choose
I

such that :

(n_{C}-1)E_{C}\leq l(I)\leq(n_{C}+1)E_{C},\quad\text{ which is equivalent to }\quad(|W|-3)E_{C}\leq 2l(I)\leq(|W|+1)E_{C}.

\bullet

If
|W|
is odd and
|W|=2n_{C}-1
, then we can choose
I
such that :

l(I)=n_{C}E_{C},\quad\text{ which is equivalent to }\quad 2l(I)=(|W|+1)E_{C}.

Therefore, in every cases we have

(|W|-3)E_{C}\leq 2l(I)\leq(|W|+1)E_{C}

and using the previously calculated value of
E_{C}
, we obtain the required inequality.
∎

4.
Properties on Bowditch representations and simple-stable representations

Let
(X,d)
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic, geodesic and visibility space, and
o
a basepoint in
X
. Recall that we have already introduce the
displacement length
of an isometry
A
of
X
in section
1.2
.
Another quantity that we will use extensively is the
stable length
, defined by
l_{S}(A)=\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\frac{1}{n}d(A^{n}o,o)
. This definition is independent of the choice of the basepoint
o
in
X
. The stable length satisfies the following usefull properties :
l_{S}(A^{n})=nl_{S}(A)
(which is not true in general for the displacement length) and
l_{S}(A)\leq\frac{1}{n}d(A^{n}o,o)
. Also recall that when
A
is a hyperbolic isometry of
X
, then the map from
\mathbb{Z}
to
X
that sends
n
to
A^{n}o
is a quasi-isometry, by definition. It implies that
A
has two fixed points in the boundary of
X
, one attracting and the other repelling, denoted by
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
respectively.

4.1.
A uniform quasi-geodesicity setting

Fix
G,D_{1}
and
D_{2}
three isometries of
X
. We can consider the set
\mathcal{W}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
of (finite) words on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{G,G^{-1},D_{1},D_{1}^{-1},D_{2},D_{2}^{-1}\}
. For
W\in\mathcal{W}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
, we denote by
|W|
its word length (
W
is seen as a word on the alphabet
\mathcal{A})
. We also consider
\mathcal{H}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
the set of bi-infinite (reduced) words on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, that is,
H=(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{H}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
if and only if for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
H_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
and
H_{n}\neq H_{n+1}^{-1}
. When we have a bi-infinite word
H=(H_{n})\in\mathcal{H}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
, we associate to it a bi-infinite sequence of finite words
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
in the following way :

W_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{0}H_{1}\ldots H_{n-1}&\text{for }n>0\\
I_{d}&\text{for }n=0\\
H_{-1}^{-1}\ldots H_{n}^{-1}&\text{for }n<0\end{array}\right.

Hence
W_{n}\in\mathcal{W}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, the word length of
W_{n}
is
|W_{n}|=|n|
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and the following recursive formula holds for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
:
W_{n+1}=W_{n}H_{n}
. We denote by
\mathcal{G}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
the set of bi-infinite sequences of finite words associate to bi-infinite words in
\mathcal{H}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
.

In this section, we will study a particular class of bi-infinite words
H=(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and their associate bi-infinite sequences of words
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Let us fix an integer
N\geq 1
and define
\mathcal{H}_{N}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
to be the subset of
\mathcal{H}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
consisting of the bi-infinite words
H=(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
which satisfy the following condition :

If
n_{1}<n_{2}
are two integers in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
H_{n_{1}},H_{n_{2}}\in\{D_{1},D_{1}^{-1},D_{2},D_{2}^{-1}\}
and for all
n_{1}<n<n_{2}
,
H_{n}\in\{G^{\pm 1}\}
, then :

\bullet

If
H_{n_{1}}\in\{D_{1}^{\pm 1}\}
, then
H_{n_{2}}\in\{D_{2}^{\pm 1}\}
, for all
n_{1}<n<n_{2}
,
H_{n}=G^{-1}
and
n_{2}-n_{1}-1\geq N
.

\bullet

If
H_{n_{1}}\in\{D_{2}^{\pm 1}\}
, then
H_{n_{2}}\in\{D_{1}^{\pm 1}\}
, for all
n_{1}<n<n_{2}
,
H_{n}=G
and
n_{2}-n_{1}-1\geq N
.

Thus, the bi-infinite words in
\mathcal{H}_{N}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
are those of the form :

\ldots D_{1}^{\pm 1}\underbrace{G^{-1}\ldots G^{-1}}_{\geq N}D_{2}^{\pm 1}\underbrace{G\ldots G}_{\geq N}D_{1}^{\pm 1}\underbrace{G^{-1}\ldots G^{-1}}_{\geq N}D_{2}^{\pm 1}\underbrace{G\ldots G}_{\geq N}D_{1}^{\pm 1}\ldots

We denote by
\mathcal{G}_{N}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
the set of bi-infinite sequences
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{G}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
associate to bi-infinite words in
\mathcal{H}_{N}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
.

Fix
o
a basepoint in
X
. Starting from a bi-infinite sequence
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{A}(G,D_{1},D_{2}))
, we define the sequence of points in
X
:
x_{n}=W_{n}o,\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We will prove in this section that the sequences of points defined by the elements of
\mathcal{G}_{N}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
are uniform quasi-geodesics, that is the existence of two reals
\lambda>0
and
k\geq 0
such that for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have :
\frac{1}{\lambda}|n-m|-k\leq d(x_{n},x_{m})\leq\lambda|n-m|+k
. We also say that
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a
(\lambda,k,L)
-local-quasi-geodesic
if we have
\frac{1}{\lambda}|n-m|-k\leq d(x_{n},x_{m})\leq\lambda|n-m|+k
whenever
|n-m|\leq L
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic, geodesic and visibility space, and
o\in X
any basepoint. Pick
G
a hyperbolic isometry and
D_{1},D_{2}
two isometries of
X
. Suppose that
D_{1}(G^{-})\neq G^{-}
and
D_{2}(G^{+})\neq G^{+}
. Then, there exists
\lambda>0,k\geq 0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, such that for all bi-infinite sequence
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{G}_{N}(G,D_{1},D_{2})
, the sequence of points
x_{n}=W_{n}o
is a
(\lambda,k)
-quasi-geodesic.

Proof.

\bullet

Step 1 : Quasi-isometry on a half-period

We first show that there exist two constants
\lambda>0
and
k\geq 0
, only depending on
\delta,G,\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
and
o
, such that the two following inequalities are satisfied :

(39)

\frac{1}{\lambda}|G^{n}D_{1}G^{-m}|-k\leq d(G^{n}D_{1}G^{-m}o,o)\qquad\text{ for all }n,m\geq 0

(40)

\frac{1}{\lambda}|G^{-n}D_{2}G^{m}|-k\leq d(G^{-n}D_{2}G^{m}o,o)\qquad\text{ for all }n,m\geq 0

By assumption, the two points at infinity
D_{1}(G^{-})
and
G^{-}
are distinct, so by visibility of the space
X
we can consider a geodesic, called
\Lambda
, with endpoints
D_{1}(G^{-})
and
G^{-}
. Now consider
p
a projection map on
\Lambda
, that is
p:X\to\Lambda
satisfying
\forall x\in X,d(x,p(x))=d(x,\Lambda)=\underset{y\in\Lambda}{\inf}\,d(x,y)
(such a map exists but is not necessarily unique). Since the (half) quasi-geodesic
(G^{-n}o)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the geodesic
\Lambda
have
G^{-}
as a common endpoint, we have, by stability of quasi-geodesics in
\delta
hyperbolic spaces, the existence of a constant
K_{1}>0
(only depending on
\delta,G,D_{1}
and
o
) such that
\{G^{-n}o\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the half geodesic
[p(o),G^{-})
remain in the
K_{1}
-neighborhood of each other. We deduce the following :

(41)

d(G^{-n}o,p(G^{-n}o))\leq K_{1},\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}

With the same argument, namely that the (half) geodesic
(D_{1}G^{-m}o)_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\Lambda
share the same endpoint
D_{1}(G^{-})
, we deduce the existence of a constant
K_{2}>0
(only depending on
\delta,G,D_{1}
and
o
) such that

(42)

d(D_{1}G^{-m}o,p(D_{1}G^{-m}o))\leq K_{2},\text{ for all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

Then we can write the following inequalities :

\displaystyle d(G^{n}D_{1}G^{-m}o,o)

\displaystyle=d(D_{1}G^{-m}o,G^{-n}o)\text{ because $G^{-n}$ is an isometry }

\displaystyle\geq d(p(D_{1}G^{-m}o),p(G^{-n}o))-d(p(D_{1}G^{-m}o),D_{1}G^{-m}o)-d(p(G^{-n}o),G^{-n}o)

\displaystyle\geq d(p(D_{1}G^{-m}o),p(G^{-n}o))-K_{1}-K_{2}\text{ by inequalities \ref{K1-S} and \ref{K2-S}}.

But since
G^{-n}o\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}G^{-}
, we also deduce
p(G^{-n}o)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}G^{-}
and, again, since
D_{1}G^{-m}o\underset{m\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}D_{1}(G^{-})
, we have
p(D_{1}G^{-m}o)\underset{m\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}D_{1}(G^{-})
. Then, for
n
and
m
sufficiently large,
p(G^{-n}o)
belongs to
[p(o),G^{-})\cap[p(D_{1}o),G^{-})
and
p(D_{1}G^{-m}o)
belongs to
[p(o),D_{1}(G^{-}))\cap[p(D_{1}o),D_{1}(G^{-}))
. This implies that for
n
and
m
sufficiently large, the four points
p(G^{-n}o),p(D_{1}G^{-m}o),p(D_{1}o)
and
p(o)
are aligned in one of the two following orders on the geodesic
\Lambda
:
p(G^{-n}o),p(o),p(D_{1}o),p(D_{1}G^{-m}o)
or
p(G^{-n}o),p(D_{1}o),p(o),p(D_{1}G^{-m}o)
. In both cases, for
n
and
m
sufficiently large :

(43)

d(p(G^{-n}o),p(D_{1}G^{-m}o))\geq d(p(G^{-n}o),p(o))-d(p(o),p(D_{1}o))+d(p(D_{1}o),p(D_{1}G^{-m}o)).

We also have,

\displaystyle d(p(G^{-n}o),p(o))

\displaystyle\geq d(G^{-n}o,o)-d(G^{-n}o,p(G^{-n}o))-d(p(o),o)

(44)

\displaystyle\geq d(G^{-n}o,o)-2K_{1}\text{ by inequality \eqref{K1-S}}

and similarly :

(45)

\displaystyle d(p(D_{1}G^{-m}o),p(D_{1}o))

\displaystyle\geq d(D_{1}G^{-m}o,D_{1}o)-2K_{2}\text{ by inequality \eqref{K2-S}}.

We can now prove inequality (
39
) :

\displaystyle d(G^{n}D_{1}G^{-m}o,o)

\displaystyle\geq d(p(D_{1}G^{-m}o),p(G^{-n}o))-K_{1}-K_{2}

\displaystyle\geq d(p(G^{-n}o),p(o))-d(p(o),p(D_{1}o))+d(p(D_{1}o),p(D_{1}G^{-m}o))-K_{1}-K_{2}\text{ by \eqref{min-G-n-DG-m}}

\displaystyle\geq d(G^{-n}o,o)+d(D_{1}G^{-m}o,D_{1}o)-d(p(o),p(D_{1}o))-3K_{1}-3K_{2}\text{ by \eqref{half1} and \eqref{half2} }

\displaystyle=d(G^{n}o,o)+d(G^{m}o,o)-d(p(o),p(D_{1}o))-3K_{1}-3K_{2}

\displaystyle\geq(n+m)l_{S}(G)-d(p(o),p(D_{1}o))-3K_{1}-3K_{2}

In the last inequality, we used that
d(G^{n}o,o)\geq nl_{S}(G)
, as recalled in the beginning of this section.
Since
(n+m)l_{S}(G)=(n+m+1)l_{S}(G)-l_{S}(G)=|G^{n}D_{1}G^{-m}|l_{S}(G)-l_{S}(G)
, we have proved the inequality (
39
) for
n
and
m
sufficiently large, prescribing
\lambda=\frac{1}{l_{S}(G)}
(recall
l_{S}(G)>0
when
G
is hyperbolic), and
k=l_{S}(G)+d(p(o),p(D_{1}o))+3K_{1}+3K_{2}
. Since there is only a finite number of values of
G^{n}D_{1}G^{-m}
, for
n
and
m
smaller than a fixed constant, the inequality (
39
) is still true for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, after possibly changing the values of
\lambda
and
k
.

In order to prove the inequality (
40
), we change
D_{1}
to
D_{2}
and
G
to
G^{-1}
and use the hypothesis
D_{2}(G^{+})\neq G^{+}
.

\bullet

4.1

4.2.
Properties on Bowditch representations of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})

We establish the useful fact that all the images of simple closed curves by a Bowditch representation are hyperbolic isometries. We prove a slightly stronger result, namely that given a Bowditch representation, we can forget the additive constant in the definition and replace the displacement length by the stable length.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
be a Bowditch representation with constant
C,D
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
. Then
\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\|\gamma\|\leq l_{S}(\rho(\gamma))
and
\rho(\gamma)
is hyperbolic.

Proof.

We assume (without loss of generality) that
\gamma
is cyclically reduced. We introduce
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
given by Corollary
2.7
. Therefore, by this corollary, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the word
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
is simple. In order to apply the Bowditch hypothesis to
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
, we want to study the cyclically reduced word length of
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
. The word
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
is not necessarily reduced, but the key point is that
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
only depend on
\gamma
and not on
n
, so the possible simplifications in the word
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
are independent of
n
, for
n
large enough. For completeness, let us write the details :

Consider two integers
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
satisfying
n_{1}|\gamma|>|\delta_{1}|
and
n_{2}|\gamma|>|\delta_{2}|
. Denote
\delta^{\prime}_{1}=\gamma^{n_{1}}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n_{1}}
and
\delta^{\prime}_{2}=\gamma^{-n_{2}}\delta_{2}\gamma^{n_{2}}
. The elements
\delta^{\prime}_{1}=\gamma^{n_{1}}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n_{1}}
and
\delta^{\prime}_{2}=\gamma^{-n_{2}}\delta_{2}\gamma^{n_{2}}
may have some simplifications, but because of the assumptions on
n_{1}
and

(and the fact that

\bullet

Moreover, for all

we have :

\displaystyle\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{n_{2}}\gamma^{n-n_{1}-n_{2}}\gamma^{n_{1}}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n_{1}}\gamma^{-n+n_{1}+n_{2}}\gamma^{-n_{2}}\delta_{2}

so the element
\gamma^{n-n_{1}-n_{2}}\delta^{\prime}_{1}\gamma^{-n+n_{1}+n_{2}}\delta^{\prime}_{2}
is a cyclic permutation of
\gamma^{n}\delta_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta_{2}
. Now notice that the observations made above on the first and last letters of

is simple and cyclically reduced.

Then, we use the Bowditch hypothesis on

to write the following inequalities :

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\|\gamma^{n}\delta^{\prime}_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta^{\prime}_{2}\|-D

\displaystyle\leq d(\rho(\gamma^{n}\delta^{\prime}_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta^{\prime}_{2})o,o)

\displaystyle\leq d(\rho(\gamma^{n})o,o)+d(\rho(\delta^{\prime}_{1})o,o)+d(\rho(\gamma^{-n})o,o)+d(\rho(\delta^{\prime}_{2})o,o)

But since

is cyclically reduced, we have
\|\gamma^{n}\delta^{\prime}_{1}\gamma^{-n}\delta^{\prime}_{2}\|=2n|\gamma|+|\delta^{\prime}_{1}|+|\delta^{\prime}_{2}|=2n\|\gamma\|+|\delta^{\prime}_{1}|+|\delta^{\prime}_{2}|
(the last equality holds since

\displaystyle\frac{2}{C}\|\gamma\|+\frac{|\delta^{\prime}_{1}|+|\delta^{\prime}_{2}|}{nC}-\frac{D}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}d(\rho(\gamma^{n})o,o)+\frac{1}{n}(d(\rho(\delta^{\prime}_{1})o,o)+d(\rho(\delta^{\prime}_{2})o,o))

And then by taking the limit when

(and dividing by 2):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\|\gamma\|\leq l_{S}(\rho(\gamma))\qquad\text{ by definition of the stable length},

which is the desired inequality. Hence, for every simple word

,

, thus

is hyperbolic.

5.
Uniform tubular neighborhoods for Bowditch representations of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})

This section is dedicated to proving Proposition
5.1
, which says that the orbit map restricted to simple leaves stays in a uniform tubular neighborhood of the axes of simple elements in
X
. This is the core of the proof of the simple-stability of Bowditch representations of the four-punctured sphere group, and the remaining part will be done in section
6

\mathbb{F}_{2}
, already done by the author in
[
Sch24
]
(Proposition 5.2). Some of the lemmas have a proof that needs to be adapted, because the combinatorics of the words are different in this case. On the other hand, for some lemmas the proof will be identical, and in this case we will refer in the proofs to the corresponding lemmas in
[
Sch24
]
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic, geodesic and visibility space and

a basepoint. When

is a hyperbolic isometry of

and

in the boundary of

\mathrm{Axis}(A)
to be the union of all the geodesics in

joining the two distinct points

and
A^{-}
(such a geodesic always exists because

\mathrm{Axis}(A)

-invariant and so follows the

-invariance of the map

. For a subset

and

, let us denote

the

-neighborhood of

.

Now recall that
L_{\gamma}
denotes the geodesic in the Cayley graph of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})=\mathbb{F}_{3}
which is the axis of
\gamma
, and that for any element
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
, we proved in Lemma
4.3
that

is hyperbolic so
\mathrm{Axis}(\rho(\gamma))
is well-defined.

Proposition 5.1

(Uniform tubular neighborhoods)
.

Let

be a
\delta
-hyperbolic, geodesic and visibility space, and
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
a Bowditch representation. Then:

\exists K>0,\qquad\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\qquad\tau_{\rho}(L_{\gamma})\subset N_{K}(\mathrm{Axis}(\rho(\gamma)))

Proof.

Pick
\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})\to\mathrm{Isom}(X)
a Bowditch representation of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
,

a basepoint and let

be two constants such that :

(49)

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\qquad\frac{1}{C}\|\gamma\|\leq l(\rho(\gamma))\quad\text{ and }\quad\forall u\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4}),\quad d(\rho(u)o,o)\leq C^{\prime}|u|.

Recall that the existence of the constant

comes from the hypothesis that the representation is Bowditch and Lemma

By contradiction, let us suppose that there exists a sequence

satisfying the following hypothesis :

5.3.

Let
\gamma
be a cyclically reduced simple element in
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})

is the axis of

We define the map :

Definition 5.1
.

\gamma

in

, is the

. Let

. We say that

is a

-excursion
if

is an excursion such that

.

At last, we say that
\gamma
has an excursion (respectively a
K
-excursion) if there exists

such that
[u,v]
is an excursion (respectively a
K
-excursion).

The following lemma gives the existence of excursions as large and as long as we want.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exist two sequences of positive reals

and

, such that

,

The proof is exactly the same as when studying Bowditch representations of

, see Lemma 5.11 in
[

.

6.1.
A few lemmas on continuous maps

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
x<y
be two reals and
f:[x,y]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous map. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Denote
l=|y-x|
. Let us define :

L_{f,\varepsilon}=\{l^{\prime}\in[0,l]\mid\exists(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in[x,y]^{2},\,\,l^{\prime}=|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|\text{ and }|f(y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|\}

Then the set
L_{f,\varepsilon}
is a closed subset of
[0,l]
.

Proof.

Let
(l_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
L_{f,\varepsilon}
such that
l_{n}\to l_{\infty}\in[0,l]
. Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two sequences of
[x,y]
such that
|f(y_{n})-f(x_{n})|\leq\varepsilon(y_{n}-x_{n})
and
y_{n}-x_{n}=l_{n}
. Up to subsequence, since
[x,y]
is compact, we can assume that
x_{n}\to x_{\infty}\in[x,y]
and
y_{n}\to y_{\infty}\in[x,y]
. Then by continuity, we obtain
|f(y_{\infty})-f(x_{\infty})|\leq\varepsilon(y_{\infty}-x_{\infty})
and thus
l_{\infty}\in L_{f,\varepsilon}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
g:[x,y]\to\mathbb{R}
a continuous map such that
g(x)=g(y)
and
g(z)\geq g(x)
for all
z\in[x,y]
. Denote
l=|x-y|
. Then there exists two reals
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in[x,y]
such that
g(x^{\prime})=g(y^{\prime})
,
g(z)\geq g(x^{\prime})
for all

and
\displaystyle\frac{l}{2}\leq|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|<l
.

Proof.

This lemma and its proof can be found in Lemma 5.8 in
[
Sch24
]
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
x<y
be two reals and
f:[x,y]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous map. Let
\varepsilon>0
such that
|f(y)-f(x)|\lx@nobreakspace\leq\varepsilon|y-x|
. Denote
l=|y-x|
.
There exists two reals
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in[x,y]
such that
\displaystyle\frac{l}{2}\leq|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|<l
and
|f(y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

Let us define
\displaystyle s=\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}
. Then by hypothesis on
f

.

\bullet

Suppose that there exists

such that

. Then both

. Moreover either
\displaystyle z-x\geq\frac{y-x}{2}=\frac{l}{2}
or
\displaystyle y-z\geq\frac{y-x}{2}=\frac{l}{2}
, and thus the claim is proved by setting

or

.

\bullet

Now suppose that for all

Lemma 6.4

Let
x<y

\displaystyle d(\tau_{\rho}(x),\tau_{\rho}(y))

\displaystyle\leq d(\tau_{\rho}(x),p_{\gamma}(\tau_{\rho}(x)))+d(p_{\gamma}(\tau_{\rho}(x)),p_{\gamma}(\tau_{\rho}(y)))+d(\tau_{\rho}(y),p_{\gamma}(\tau_{\rho}(y)))

to
\mathbb{R}
such that, on every integer point
x\in L_{\gamma}
,
\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(x)=E_{\gamma}(x)
and between two integer points, we do a linear interpolation, that is :
\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(x)=tE_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)+(1-t)E_{\gamma}(\lceil x\rceil)
, with

such that
d(\lfloor x\rfloor,x)=1-t
and
d(\lceil x\rceil,x)=t
. The map
\widetilde{E}
is continuous. The next Lemma aims to compare
E_{\gamma}
and
\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}

Lemma 6.6
.

For all
x,y\in L_{\gamma}
, we have :

|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(y)|-(4C^{\prime}+8K_{\delta})\leq|\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(x)-\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(y)|\leq|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(y)|+4C^{\prime}+8K_{\delta}

Proof.

\bullet

We first prove that for all
x\in L_{\gamma}
:

(57)

|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|\leq C^{\prime}+2K_{\delta}

Indeed, by Lemma
6.5
, we have
|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|\leq d(\tau_{\rho}(x),\tau_{\rho}(\lfloor x\rfloor))+2K_{\delta}
. But
d(x,\lfloor x\rfloor)\leq 1
and
\tau_{\rho}
is
C^{\prime}
-Lipschitz, hence the inequality is true.

\bullet

We secondly prove that for all
x\in L_{\gamma}
:

(58)

|\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(x)-\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|\leq C^{\prime}+2K_{\delta}

Indeed :

\displaystyle|\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(x)-\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|

\displaystyle=|tE_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)+(1-t)E_{\gamma}(\lceil x\rceil)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|\text{ by definition of $\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}$}

\displaystyle=|1-t||E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lceil x\rceil)|

\displaystyle\leq d(\tau_{\rho}(\lfloor x\rfloor),\tau_{\rho}(\lceil x\rceil))+2K_{\delta}\qquad\text{by Lemma \ref{lien-E-d}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}+2K_{\delta}\qquad\text{because }d(\lfloor x\rfloor,\lceil x\rceil)\leq 1

\bullet

We deduce that for all
x,y\in L_{\gamma}
, we have :

(59)

|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|-(2C^{\prime}+4K_{\delta})\leq|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(y)|\leq 2C^{\prime}+4K_{\delta}+|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|

Indeed, by the triangle inequality we have :

\displaystyle|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|+|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|+|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{\prime}+4K_{\delta}+|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|\text{ by \eqref{E-x-floorx}}

and on the other hand

\displaystyle|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|

\displaystyle\leq|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)|+|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(y)|+|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{\prime}+4K_{\delta}+|E_{\gamma}(x)-E_{\gamma}(y)|\qquad\text{ again by \eqref{E-x-floorx}}

\bullet

Similarly, we deduce using (
58
) and the fact that
\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)=E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor),\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)=E_{\gamma}((\lfloor y\rfloor)
, that for all
x,y\in L_{\gamma}
, we have :

(60)

|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|-(2C^{\prime}+4K_{\delta})\leq|\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(x)-\widetilde{E}_{\gamma}(y)|\leq 2C^{\prime}+4K_{\delta}+|E_{\gamma}(\lfloor x\rfloor)-E_{\gamma}(\lfloor y\rfloor)|

\bullet

Finally, the desired inequality follows from (
59
) and (
60
).

∎

Let us now prove that
\rho
is simple-stable. By contradiction, suppose that it is not. Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find a cyclically reduced simple element
\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{S}_{0,4})
together with two integer points
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
on
L_{\gamma_{n}}
such that

(61)

d(\tau_{\rho}(x_{n}),\tau_{\rho}(y_{n}))\leq\frac{1}{n}d(x_{n},y_{n})-1

In particular, we have that
d(x_{n},y_{n})\geq n
. We can make the assumption that the elements
\gamma_{n}
are pairwise distinct. Indeed, if the sequence
{(\gamma_{n})}_{n}
only takes finitely many values, then, up to subsequence, we can suppose that
\gamma_{n}=\gamma
for some simple word
\gamma
. But
\rho(\gamma)
is a hyperbolic isometry so there exist two constants
C_{\gamma}
and
D_{\gamma}
(depending on
\gamma
) such that
\tau_{\rho}(L_{\gamma})
is a
(C_{\gamma},D_{\gamma})
-quasi-geodesic. Then, since
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
belong to
L_{\gamma}
, we have :

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{\gamma}}d(x_{n},y_{n})-D_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq d(\rho(x_{n})o,\rho(y_{n})o)\leq\frac{1}{n}d(x_{n},y_{n})-1

\displaystyle\text{so}\qquad\frac{1}{C_{\gamma}}-\frac{D_{\gamma}}{d(x_{n},y_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{d(x_{n},y_{n})},

\displaystyle\text{then, taking the limit when $n\to\infty$,}\quad\frac{1}{C_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq 0,\quad\text{which is absurd}.

Thus we can suppose that the elements
\gamma_{n}
are pairwise distinct and therefore
\|\gamma_{n}\|\to\infty
.

Denote by
[N_{1}(\gamma_{n}),\ldots,N_{r(\gamma_{n})}(\gamma_{n})]
the continued fraction expansion of the slope of

. As in the proof of the previous section (section

Lemma 6.7

such that for all

The proof is the same as the proof of Lemma

Lemma 6.8
