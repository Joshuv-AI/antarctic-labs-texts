Digital Fabrication of Complete Dentures Using Multifunctional Custom Trays: A Case Report
Authors: Zhenyan Liao, Ruyi Li, Jing Luo, Jian Liao, Qianbing Wan, Jian Wang, Zihan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConventional complete denture fabrication is a complex, multi‐step clinical procedure. This clinical report presents a novel digital modality utilizing multifunctional custom trays based on a virtual patient. These trays consolidate key clinical steps—including definitive impressions, esthetic preview of the maxillary teeth, and jaw relation records—into a single appointment, thereby reducing procedural complexity and minimizing the repeated insertions and removals of different appliances throughout the treatment course. This functional integration enhances patient comfort, facilitates improved patient‐physician communication during the treatment planning phase, and enables more predictable esthetic outcomes through early anterior tooth preview. The complete denture can be fabricated within three clinical visits.Keywords: complete denture, custom trays, digital workflow, gothic arch tracing devices, virtual patient

Introduction

With the intensifying trend of global population aging, the demand for prosthetic rehabilitation in edentulous patients is continuously increasing [1, 2]. Complete dentures remain the most common treatment modality for elderly edentulous individuals [3, 4]. However, traditional workflows are complex, requiring multiple clinical visits and heavily rely on the clinician's experience [5, 6]. Achieving a precise impression often involves taking a preliminary impression, fabricating a patient‐specific custom tray, and then using that tray for the final impression [7, 8]. This multi‐step process can cause significant discomfort for patients with a strong gag reflex [9, 10]. Recording jaw relationships necessitates the use of devices such as facebows and Gothic arch tracings, while the tooth arrangement process requires manual fabrication of wax rims by the clinician to convey information to the dental technician [11, 12].

The introduction of digital technologies such as virtual patients and 3D printing has enabled an integrated workflow from data acquisition to denture fabrication, significantly improving production accuracy and operational efficiency, thereby bringing transformative changes to complete denture therapy [13, 14, 15]. Facial scanning allows direct capture of the patient's facial esthetic information, while intraoral scanning eliminates the need for traditional preliminary impressions, substantially reducing chairside time and enhancing patient comfort [16, 17]. 3D printing technology enables rapid production of diagnostic trays, custom trays, and trial wax setups, markedly improving both clinical and laboratory efficiency and shortening treatment duration, among other benefits [18, 19, 20].

Therefore, we propose the construction of a virtual patient to assist in designing multifunctional custom trays. This approach aims to combine definitive impression making, maxillary teeth esthetics, and centric relation recording into a single step during one appointment, with the entire treatment completed within three clinical visits. In this patient, this strategy enhanced patient comfort, enabled early esthetic preview, and reduced procedural complexity.

Case History and Examination

An 89‐year‐old edentulous male with a history of hypertension and cerebral infarction (2 years prior) presented to the Department of Prosthodontics and Implantology, the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. He had multiple residual roots in the oral cavity previously, with no history of denture wear. All residual roots had been extracted 3 months prior to presentation. The chief complaint was a desire for dental rehabilitation to restore masticatory function. Clinical examination revealed midfacial concavity and reduced vertical dimension in the lower third of the face, with normal salivary secretion. Intraoral examination revealed a Class II classification according to the American College of Prosthodontists Prosthodontic Diagnostic Index (ACP PDI) for complete edentulism, characterized by moderate complexity (Figure 1a). Asymmetric resorption of the mandibular alveolar bone was observed, along with a discrepant maxillomandibular arch relationship. Considering his age and the inherently complex nature of traditional complete denture fabrication, encompassing cast pouring, articulator mounting, denture base wax‐up, and artificial tooth arrangement, a digital fabrication approach was selected to enhance patient comfort, enable early esthetic preview, and reduce procedural complexity.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

At the initial visit, the patient was seated upright and instructed to perform repetitive mouth opening and closing movements until muscular fatigue was achieved. With the mandible in the physiological rest position, the vertical dimension at rest (VDR) was recorded by measuring the subnasale–menton distance using a Willis gauge (Changsha Tiantian Dental Equipment Co. Ltd.). The vertical dimension of occlusion (VDO) was then established by subtracting 2 mm from the recorded VDR. Then, the heavy‐bodied silicone impression material (Silagum Putty Soft, DMG) was loaded onto the centric tray, which was fabricated using an AccuFab‐D1s 3D printer (SHINING 3D) with the manufacturer's proprietary resin material (DM15). The tray was then seated intraorally, and the patient was guided into a slow closing movement to cover the residual ridge crest at the previously measured vertical dimension, thereby obtaining a preliminary centric relation record (Figure 1b,c). Next, the obtained centric tray was scanned with an intraoral scanner (TRIOS S1P‐2, 3Shape) to generate digital data in the standard tessellation language (STL) file format (Figure 2a). The edentulous maxillary and mandibular alveolar ridges and surrounding tissues were scanned using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 3, 3Shape), which captured their surface details and converted the physical anatomy into digital data (Figure 2b). Following direct registration between the intraoral scan data and the maxillomandibular relationship record, preliminary digital casts with an approximate jaw relation were constructed (Figure 2c). Two sets of facial scan data were acquired with the Bellus3D Arc7: one without any equipment (Figure 2d) and another with a centric tray (Figure 2e). The use of the centric tray indeed affects changes in the perioral soft tissues, but the alignment of both datasets relies on relatively stable landmarks, specifically the endocanthion, exocanthion, and pronasale. The primary goal of this alignment is to determine the relative positional relationship between the upper lip and the maxillary edentulous ridge in a resting state, which can guide the arrangement of maxillary teeth. All gathered digital data were merged and imported into Exocad software (Exocad GmbH) to generate a definitive three‐dimensional virtual patient model (Figure 2f).

To address the procedural complexity of traditional complete denture fabrication—while enhancing patient comfort and enabling early esthetic evaluation—multifunctional custom trays combined with Gothic arch tracing devices and maxillary teeth arrangement were designed. The digital design of the custom tray commenced with the delineation of its borders on the intraoral scan data. A precise boundary was established approximately 2 mm short of the mucobuccal and mucolabial folds to define the tray extension, thereby preventing soft tissue impingement during functional movements. Subsequently, a uniform 2 mm space to accommodate the impression material was created by inwardly offsetting the entire intaglio surface of the tray using the software's offset function. Using the 3D virtual patient model with data imported into SolidWorks software (Dassault Systèmes), the maxillary occlusal plane was oriented with guidance from the Ala‐Tragus Line. The incisal edge position of the anterior teeth was established approximately 2 mm below the lower border of the upper lip at rest. The overall width of the six anterior teeth (canine to canine) was determined with reference to the commissural line, and tooth shape was selected according to the patient's arch form and facial contour. The facial midline served as a reference to ensure the symmetrical arrangement and overall design of the maxillary prosthetic dentition. The maxillary teeth were selected and initially arranged to facilitate esthetic evaluation. These arrangement data were then integrated into the maxillary tray design, enabling the custom tray to provide an esthetic preview of the maxillary teeth (Figure 2g–i). The Gothic arch tracing devices were specifically incorporated into the custom trays to facilitate jaw relation recording. For their integration, screws were positioned at a height determined from the virtual model, and the top and bottom plates (2.5 mm thick) were modeled based on digitally created sketches. The resulting components were exported as STL files. Finally, these attachments were assembled with the maxillary and mandibular denture bases using dental CAD software (exocad, Invisalign, US). On this basis, multifunctional custom trays were designed and subsequently fabricated via stereolithography (SLA) using a Prismlab RP‐400D 3D printer (Prismlab) and the manufacturer's proprietary dental model resin material (Figure 3).

At the second clinical visit, the maxillary custom tray was coated with light‐bodied silicone material (Honigum Light, DMG) on its intaglio surface and seated intraorally to evaluate retention, stability, and esthetics (Figure 4a). The preliminary tooth arrangement provided adequate upper lip support and improved the lateral facial profile. The mandibular tray was likewise loaded with the same silicone material and placed intraorally (Figure 4b). The material was evenly applied to the tissue‐bearing surfaces of both the maxillary and mandibular trays prior to intraoral placement, after which the patient was directed to close into the mouth‐closed position for functional border molding. Subsequently, the mandibular tray was removed, the tracing stylus (Figure 4c) was inserted, and its height was adjusted to the previously recorded vertical dimension. With the maxillary and mandibular custom trays in contact, slow lateral and anteroposterior mandibular movements were performed by the patient. The appliance was then removed, and the maxillary Gothic arch tracing plate was coated with ink. Following reinsertion of the appliance, the movements were repeated to obtain the mandibular tracings (Figure 4d). Finally, the interocclusal relationship was recorded using an A‐silicone bite registration material (O‐Bite, DMG) (Figure 4e,f). The resulting assembly was scanned to construct final digital casts, based on which virtual complete dentures were designed and subsequently fabricated (Figure 5) using a five‐axis milling machine (Ideal Mill M) with a millable monolithic resin disc (YAMAHACHI DENTAL). The milled dentures exhibited flat cervical contours, which were then manually ground away by the technician, followed by resin packing and conventional polishing (wet polishing with abrasive and dry polishing with polishing paste).

At the third clinical visit, the CAD‐CAM‐fabricated complete dentures were delivered to the clinic (Figure 6a). Following standard protocols, an experienced dentist clinically evaluated the fit of the dentures. The dentures demonstrated satisfactory functional fit and esthetics (Figure 6b). The patient was satisfied with the prostheses and reported improvements in facial profile and masticatory function with the full‐arch restoration (Figure 6c).

Conclusion

In summary, the multifunctional digital custom trays combined with a virtual‐patient–driven workflow not only achieve enhanced structural integration, esthetic visualization, and procedural synchronization, but also align more closely with the patient‐centered and facially driven design philosophy of modern digital complete dentures, thereby offering a promising direction for future digital edentulous rehabilitation.

Discussion

This case report describes a digital workflow in which multifunctional custom trays, based on a virtual patient model, enabled the consolidation of definitive impressions, maxillary esthetic preview, and jaw relation recording into a single clinical appointment. This integrated approach facilitated early esthetic assessment, reduced the need for repeated intraoral manipulations, and improved patient comfort. It aligns with the ongoing digital transformation in prosthodontics while reinforcing a patient‐centered treatment philosophy. Moreover, this workflow addresses several inherent limitations of both conventional techniques and existing digital protocols.

In conventional and early digital workflows, definitive impression making, jaw relation recording, and esthetic evaluation are often performed in separate clinical steps, leading to increased procedural complexity and potential for cumulative errors. Recently, a growing number of clinicians have been focusing on integrating multiple discrete clinical steps into a single, unified procedure with the aid of digital technologies. The technique described by Li et al. emphasized the use of positioning hemispheres on digitally fabricated custom trays to enhance accuracy and controllability during impression making [18]. Miao et al. introduced a modular system in which custom trays, occlusion rims, and Gothic arch tracing devices were assembled via tenon‐and‐mortise joints. The design allowed for individualized functional impressions and jaw relation records in a closed‐mouth position, adhering to the biofunctional prosthetic system philosophy [19]. However, these cases still only involved the partial integration of certain steps, rather than fusing all steps into a single, unified whole.

In the present case, the multifunctional trays enabled simultaneous functional impression, centric relation recording, and esthetic preview of the maxillary teeth within a single device. This integration may have minimized cumulative technical errors commonly associated with transferring records across multiple devices, thereby potentially improving the internal consistency of the digital workflow and reducing the need for post‐insertion occlusal or esthetic adjustments [21, 22, 23]. In this patient, functional consolidation of multiple steps into a single device enhanced patient comfort, enabled early esthetic preview, and reduced procedural complexity.

Another advantage of this digital approach is the integration of facial scanning data into the tray design through the construction of a virtual patient, which facilitates more precise esthetic evaluation by enabling anterior tooth preview directly from the impression stage. The embedded initial maxillary teeth arrangement allowed simultaneous verification of lip support, midline alignment, incisal edge position, and anterior tooth display during the definitive impression appointment. This ability to front‐load esthetic assessment within the impression phase aligns with contemporary facially driven denture design philosophies and may reduce the likelihood of downstream esthetic corrections.

Similarly, the digitally designed and 3D‐printed Gothic arch tracing device (GATD) used in this workflow offered additional benefits over conventional techniques. Conventional GATD placement on wax rims typically requires multiple assembly steps, increasing operational complexity. Moreover, prefabricated GATDs may not adequately match individual edentulous arch morphologies. In contrast, the custom‐fabricated GATD employed in this workflow can be tailored to each patient's arch morphology, thereby reducing procedural complexity and enhancing patient‐specific adaptation compared with conventional approaches.

Despite these advantages, several limitations should be acknowledged. As a single case without a control group, outcomes were assessed subjectively; future studies should include objective measures such as masticatory performance tests or electromyography. The absence of long‐term follow‐up also limits evaluation of treatment stability, including dimensional accuracy, occlusal integrity, and patient satisfaction over time. Additionally, the virtual patient construction depends on high‐quality scans, as errors in any dataset may propagate and affect reliability. The non‐removable Gothic arch guide plate may restrict tongue movement during border molding, while the lack of a trial denture step may increase intraoperative adjustments. Moreover, the precision of custom digital GATDs versus conventional devices requires further comparative investigation, and neuromuscular factors in elderly patients warrant clinician oversight during functional registration.

A specific technical limitation should also be noted: a slight non‐parallelism was observed between the tracing plates in the Gothic arch tracing (Figure 4f), which deviates from the ideal configuration. Despite this deviation, the centric relation record was deemed clinically acceptable, given the consistent biting points (Figure 4d) and the patient's uneventful adjustment to the definitive dentures without any adverse symptoms. We postulate that this deviation may originate from spatial registration errors during the superimposition of maxillary and mandibular intraoral scan data (Figure 2a–c), a known challenge in edentulous digital workflows. A recent study demonstrated that the manual multi‐point combined global registration method provides superior accuracy compared with the best‐fit alignment algorithm for edentulous jaw impressions, with its advantage being particularly pronounced for the maxilla [24]. Therefore, we recommend that centric relation be validated through multiple clinical checks rather than relying solely on apparatus geometry, and that future digital protocols incorporate fiducial markers or manual alignment strategies to minimize such discrepancies.

The single‐appointment consolidation of impression making, aesthetic evaluation, and jaw relation recording minimized repeated appliance manipulations and appeared to enhance patient comfort and procedural simplicity in this patient, which may contribute to more consistent and predictable outcomes. The virtual patient model further facilitated a facially driven design, ensuring prosthetic parameters aligned with the patient's craniofacial anatomy. Future studies with larger cohorts and long‐term follow‐up are needed to assess whether this workflow can be standardized across diverse ridge morphologies and facial anatomies. Additionally, incorporating mandibular movement tracking devices may further enhance occlusal registration and verification.
