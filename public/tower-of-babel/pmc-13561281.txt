Small-bowel incarceration and perforation through a hernia defect associated with a pubic fracture: a case report
Authors: Toru Kume, Takeshi Ohki, Ryosuke Imazato, Machiko Kawaguchi, Takuji Yamada, Shingo Yamashita, Yukiyasu Okamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:We encountered an extremely rare case of delayed small-bowel incarceration and perforation resulting from herniation through a defect at the site of a pubic fracture.Presentation of case:A 69-year-old man receiving conservative treatment for a pubic fracture presented with abdominal pain approximately 3 months after the initial injury. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed free intraperitoneal air, ascites, and small-bowel dilatation, prompting emergency surgery. Intraoperative findings demonstrated ileal incarceration within a depression at the pubic fracture site, consistent with a Richter-type hernia accompanied by small-bowel perforation. Partial ileal resection with functional end-to-end anastomosis was performed, and the hernial defect was packed with omental tissue. Osteomyelitis observed at the fracture site was treated with antibiotics. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 40.Discussion:Although rare, small-bowel herniation through a pelvic fracture site can result in incarceration and perforation. In this case, hernia repair was challenging because of contamination at the hernia site, which made mesh placement difficult, and the presence of a rigid hernia defect formed by an acquired bony defect, which was difficult to close with sutures. Based on a review of 11 cases reported between 2008 and 2026, including the present case, no standardized repair method has been established to date.Conclusion:Treatment strategies should be individualized and include omental filling, fracture stabilization, and closure or management of the hernia.

Introduction

Pelvic fractures are commonly caused by high-energy trauma, such as traffic accidents or falls, and account for approximately 5% of all traumatic fractures[1]. Common complications include visceral injury, hemorrhage, genitourinary injury, and nerve damage[2]. Bowel injury, ranging from simple mesenteric tears to complete luminal disruption, occurs in only 1–2% of cases involving intra-abdominal structures. Within this range, bowel entrapment within the fractured segments accounts for only a very small number of cases[2].

Herein, we report a rare case of delayed small-bowel incarceration and perforation at the site of a pubic fracture.

Presentation of the case

A 69-year-old man presented to our hospital with abdominal pain. His medical history included a right inguinal hernia and a pubic fracture.

In March 2025, he visited a local orthopedic clinic, presenting with difficulty walking and was diagnosed with a pubic fracture. There was no history of trauma, and further evaluation for a pathological fracture was planned; however, the patient did not return for follow-up.

He developed periumbilical abdominal pain on 2 June 2025 and presented to our emergency department on 5 June 2025.

On admission, his vital signs were as follows: Glasgow Coma Scale score, E4V5M6; body temperature, 37.2 °C; blood pressure, 107/86 mmHg; heart rate, 112 beats/min; respiratory rate, 24 breaths/min; and oxygen saturation, 95% on room air.

Physical examination revealed a flat, soft abdomen with tenderness in the periumbilical region and right lower quadrant without rebound tenderness.

Laboratory testing showed a white blood cell count of 4500/μL and an elevated C-reactive protein level of 14.3 mg/dL. Liver and renal function test results were within normal limits.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed free air within the peritoneum and pelvic ascites. Partial dilatation of the small intestine suggested the presence of ileus, although the transition point was unclear. The wall of the small bowel adjacent to the pubic fracture appeared edematous, and the density of the mesenteric fat was increased. Although diverticula of the ascending colon were present, no associated inflammatory changes were observed. There were no signs of appendicitis. A right inguinal hernia was also identified. Based on these findings, a small-bowel perforation associated with the pubic fracture site or with the reduction of an incarcerated inguinal hernia was suspected, and emergency surgery was performed.

Following the placement of a 12-mm umbilical port, laparoscopic examination revealed diffuse intraperitoneal contamination by intestinal fluid. Marked edema and dilation of the small intestine were observed in the lower abdomen. A lower midline incision was subsequently made.

Exploration of the pelvic floor revealed incarceration of an ileal segment approximately 30 cm proximal to the terminal ileum within a depressed defect created by separation of the pubic fracture fragments, forming a Richter-type hernia. The hernial orifice measured approximately 1.5 cm (Fig. 2). The incarcerated ileum was perforated (Fig. 3), resulting in leakage of intestinal contents. Approximately 15 cm of the affected ileum was resected, and reconstruction was performed using functional end-to-end anastomosis. The abdominal cavity was irrigated with 10,000 mL of saline. The hernial defect at the site of the pubic fracture was packed with omental tissue and secured with three non-absorbable sutures.

Oral intake was resumed on postoperative day 5. On postoperative day 11, inflammatory marker levels remained elevated; however, computed tomography revealed no anastomotic leakage or intra-abdominal abscesses. Osteomyelitis at the fracture site was diagnosed (Clavien–Dindo classification II), and oral amoxicillin was administered for 8 weeks.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 40.

Discussion

We searched PubMed using the keywords “pelvic fracture,” “bowel obstruction,” and “hernia” to identify cases of herniation of intra-abdominal organs through defects associated with pelvic fractures. The first case was reported by Arnold et al in 1907, as cited in Tanaka et al[3]. Since 2008, 11 cases[2–11], including the present case, have been reported with sufficiently detailed clinical descriptions (Table 1). In the present case, the patient had no history of trauma, and the fracture was considered a fragility fracture of the pelvis (FFP). FFP is an increasingly recognized clinical entity associated with reduced pelvic bone mineral density, resulting in decreased resistance to external forces[12]. The FFP classification system categorizes FFP into four main types and provides recommendations for appropriate treatment. However, evidence regarding the origin, natural progression, and most appropriate treatment for FFP remains limited[12]. The present case was classified as Rommens type I and initially treated conservatively.

In some reported cases, bowel incarceration or herniation is diagnosed immediately or within 1 week after injury[2,7,8,10,11]; however, delayed presentations occurring 2–4 weeks after injury have also been reported[3–5,9].

In the present case, preoperative diagnosis was challenging for several reasons. First, the ileal incarceration and perforation occurred at the pubic fracture site approximately 3 months after the diagnosis of an ischiopubic fracture, representing a substantially longer interval than previously reported. Second, the fracture had been diagnosed at another hospital, and the patient had discontinued follow-up; therefore, detailed information regarding the fracture history was unavailable at the time of presentation to our hospital. Third, because the small bowel was incarcerated in a Richter-type manner, identifying the transition point of the bowel obstruction was difficult. Finally, the presence of a right inguinal hernia further complicated the preoperative diagnosis.

In the present case, the small-bowel perforation resulted from Richter-type incarceration of the small intestine within a depression associated with osteophyte formation at the site of the pubic fracture. Because the hernial defect was contaminated with intestinal fluid, mesh-based hernia repair was considered to carry a high risk of infection. Furthermore, peritoneal closure alone was considered insufficient for definitive hernia repair and was thought to carry a risk of recurrent incarceration or re-injury of the bowel. Therefore, we elected to fill the defect with tissue from the greater omentum.

Unlike conventional inguinal or abdominal wall hernias, hernias formed within depressions created by pelvic fractures present considerable technical challenges for surgical repair.

Six of the eleven cases reported since 2008 were accompanied by perforation of the small bowel[2–4,9,10] indicating that contamination at the site of the hernia is common. In contaminated cases, mesh-based hernia repair and internal fixation using plates or screws carry a higher risk of postoperative infection. Furthermore, regarding external fixation for pelvic fractures, Bushnell and Dirschl reported a case in which the fixation screws used resulted in injury to the bowel wall[4].

Omental filling was used in two cases, including the present case[5], whereas mesenteric fat was used in one case[11]. Provided that fracture displacement does not progress, further enlargement of the hernial defect is unlikely, and adhesion of the filling tissue may help prevent recurrence.

Other reported repair methods were orthopedic fixation in four cases[3,4,6,8] and simple peritoneal closure or bone spur resection in two cases[2,10]. No recurrence of herniation has been reported to date, and no consensus has been established regarding the optimal repair method.

Conclusion

Small-bowel herniation, incarceration, and perforation, although rare, can occur as complications of pelvic fractures. Appropriate repair strategies, including defect filling, fracture fixation, and closure of the hernial orifice, should be individualized based on the clinical context.
