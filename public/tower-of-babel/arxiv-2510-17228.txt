DSEBench: A Test Collection for Explainable Dataset Search with Examples Journal: Knowledge-Based Systems
Qing Shi, Jing He, Qiaosheng Chen, Gong Cheng

Abstract
Dataset search has been an established information retrieval task. Current paradigms either retrieve datasets that are relevant to a keyword query or find datasets that are similar to an input target dataset. To allow for their combined specification of information needs, in this article, we investigate the more generalized task of Dataset Search with Examples (DSE) and further extend it to Explainable DSE that requires identifying the metadata and content fields of a dataset that indicate its relevance to the query and similarity to the target datasets. To facilitate this research, we construct DSEBench, a test collection that provides high-quality dataset- and field-level annotations to enable the evaluation of explainable DSE. We also employ a large language model to generate numerous annotations to be used for training. We establish extensive baselines on DSEBench by adapting and evaluating a variety of sparse, dense, and LLM-based retrieval, reranking, and explanation methods.

Introduction

In the era of open data, the ability to search for useful datasets has become increasingly important. With the proliferation of datasets available from various sources, researchers and practitioners face the challenge of efficiently finding datasets that meet their specific needs. The task of
dataset search
is critical for fields ranging from data science
[
1
]
and machine learning
[
2
]
to social sciences and public policy, where access to the right data can significantly influence research outcomes and decision-making processes.

Motivation.
Existing research mainly follows two paradigms
[
3
]
: keyword-based dataset search
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
focuses on retrieving datasets that are
relevant to a query
; similarity-based dataset discovery
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
aims to find datasets that are
similar to an input target dataset
(for example, have similar metadata or content).
However, neither approach fully captures the scenarios in which users possess
both
an information need expressed as a query and a few target datasets as examples.
Moreover, although explainable information retrieval (IR) is a trending research topic
[
11
]
,
the explainability in dataset search
remains underexplored. Current systems rarely provide justifications for query relevance or dataset similarity, leaving users without insight into the factors driving retrieval decisions.

Figure
1
illustrates a scenario mentioned above. A user searches for datasets relevant to the query “elementary junior high school students united states” and provides a target dataset “Education and Youth 2010 - Shape” as an example. This target dataset may serve to convey implicit preferences for datasets containing student-level indicators from a specific time period (2010) and an urban educational context. Candidate datasets should be ranked based on both query relevance and target similarity, which are reflected by matched metadata fields in this example.
Specifically, the candidate dataset “Student Gender Grade Ethnicity By Neighborhood 2007 2010” is positive because it satisfies both criteria: its relevance to the query is established by terms like “Student” and “Schools” in its metadata fields; its similarity to the target dataset is evident through other fields, such as the year “2010” in its
title
field and the term “education” in its
tags
field.
In contrast, there are two types of negative candidates. The first, “Series Information File for the 2018 TIGER/Line Shapefile, Current Elementary School Districts State-based”, is relevant to the query (matched via “Elementary School” and “U.S.”) but is not similar to the target dataset (it contains geographic data, not student indicators). The second, “School Improvement 2010 Grants”, is similar to the target dataset (sharing the year “2010” and the topic “Education”) but is not relevant to the query (it focuses on “grants”, not “students”).
Identifying which of the many possible metadata and content fields indicate query relevance and target similarity is key to providing explainable search results.

These judgments are supported by specific fields in the metadata.

Task Definition.

We formulate the above scenario as follows, which is a generalization of two established tasks: keyword-based dataset search and similarity-based dataset discovery. Given a textual query
q
and a set
D_{t}
of target datasets known to be relevant to the query,
Dataset Search with Examples (DSE)
is to find a list
D_{c}
of top-ranked result datasets that are most relevant to
q
and also similar to the datasets in
D_{t}
. As an extension,
Explainable DSE
requires identifying, for each result dataset
d\in D_{c}
, a subset
F_{d}
of its metadata and content fields that indicate
d
’s relevance to
q
and similarity to
D_{t}
. For example, for the positive candidate dataset in Figure
1
, an explanation
F_{d}
may consist of the fields
\{\texttt{title},\texttt{description},\texttt{tags},\texttt{summary}\}
, where
title
and
description
establish relevance to the query,
title
,
tags
, and
summary
characterize similarity to the target dataset.

Limitations of Existing Test Collections.

Research in this setting of a composite problem is hindered by the lack of test collections. First, current test collections for dataset search
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
are designed for conventional ad hoc dataset retrieval that accepts only a textual query as input. Their evaluation frameworks
cannot seamlessly incorporate target datasets into the input
. Second, these test collections provide only relevance judgments at the dataset level, i.e., to what extent a dataset is relevant to a query. They
lack fine-grained annotations
that indicate which specific fields of a dataset contribute to its relevance or similarity. Third, the number of annotated relevance judgments in existing test collections is limited due to the high cost of human annotation. This
scarcity of labeled data
poses a challenge for model training.

Our Work.

The above limitations highlight the critical need for new evaluation efforts to support the evaluation of explainable DSE. As a pioneering work along this research direction, our test collection
DSEBench
constructed in this article is distinguished by the following characteristics.

1.

We provide high-quality, human-annotated query relevance and target similarity to enable DSE evaluation.

2.

We provide fine-grained field-level annotations to facilitate the study of explanation methods for explainable DSE.

3.

We provide a large, filtered set of annotations generated by large language models (LLMs) to be used as training data.

4.

We present the results of the evaluation of a variety of adapted retrieval, reranking, and explanation models to establish baseline performance on DSEBench.

Code and data
are available on GitHub.

Related Work

2.1
Dataset Search Benchmarks

Dataset search research can be broadly categorized into three paradigms: relevance-focused, similarity-focused, and combined approaches. One line of research focuses on
relevance
, retrieving datasets that are textually relevant to a user’s query. This is the goal of general-purpose systems such as Google Dataset Search
[
4
]
and specialized RDF dataset search engines
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. Evaluation of such systems is supported by established benchmarks like NTCIR
[
12
]
, ACORDAR
[
13
,
14
]
, and a recent collection that matches natural language needs with datasets
[
15
]
. WTR
[
19
]
focuses on retrieving Web tables. These benchmarks support the evaluation of datasets’ query relevance, but do not consider similarity to the target datasets.

Another line of research, particularly in the database domain, is based on
similarity
, where the goal is to find datasets similar to a given input dataset
[
3
]
. For evaluating this task, various test collections have been constructed. For example, LakeBench
[
20
]
provides a corpus of tables from a data lake and establishes ground truth for relational tasks such as identifying joinable or unionable table pairs. These test collections primarily support tasks such as the discovery of datasets in data lakes
[
9
,
10
]
and the retrieval of tables
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. The evaluation frameworks are consequently table-centric, designed around the relational structure of tables. This focus makes the benchmarks, and the specialized retrieval methods evaluated on them, less applicable to datasets stored in non-tabular formats.

A third emerging paradigm considers
both relevance and similarity
. For example, the Aurum system
[
24
]
supports discovering datasets through a combination of keyword search and similarity-based navigation. Although such systems exist, to the best of our knowledge, there is no established benchmark for systematically evaluating systems on this combined task.
This lack of a standard test collection hinders the development and comparison of methods for DSE, a gap that our work aims to fill. Furthermore, existing benchmarks do not provide the annotations necessary to evaluate the explainability, which are offered by our test collection.

2.2
Explainable Dataset Search

An area related to explainability in dataset search is the generation of dataset snippets
[
25
,
6
]
. The goal is to create concise, human-readable summaries to help users quickly understand the potentially large content of a dataset. However, these methods focus on summarizing a single dataset in isolation. They are not designed to explain why a dataset is a good match for a specific DSE input by identifying the key fields that establish both relevance to the query and similarity to the target datasets.
Our work is the first to provide a test collection with field-level annotations to directly support the evaluation of this explanation task.

As defined in our task, explainable DSE requires identifying the specific fields that justify a dataset’s ranking. This can be framed as a feature attribution problem, where the dataset’s fields (e.g.,
title
,
description
,
tags
) are features. Consequently, post hoc explanation methods from the broader explainable AI field are applicable
[
26
,
11
]
. Techniques like LIME
[
27
]
and SHAP
[
28
]
, which attribute a model’s prediction to its input features, can be adapted to identify the indicator fields that contribute the most to a dataset’s relevance and similarity scores, thereby providing the required explanation. Due to the dual criteria (i.e., query relevance and target similarity) that influence the final ranking, explainability becomes paramount in DSE. Users need to understand why a particular dataset is retrieved and ranked highly.
We will adapt these explanation methods to explainable DSE and evaluate their effectiveness.

2.3
Relevance Feedback

Relevance Feedback (RF) is a classic IR technique that uses user judgments on initially retrieved documents to refine the search query or ranking model
[
29
,
30
]
. DSE is conceptually similar to RF in that both leverage user-provided examples to guide the search process. In explicit RF, for example, users manually label documents as relevant, which are then used to reformulate the query
[
31
]
.

Despite this high-level similarity, there are fundamental differences between DSE and RF. First, the user interaction model differs: in DSE,
the query and examples are a single, upfront input,
while RF is an iterative process that begins only after an initial search is performed. Second, the role of the examples is distinct: RF primarily refines the topical representation of the query, while
the target datasets in DSE define a desired profile (e.g., metadata fields such as provenance) that complements topicality.
Despite the differences, it is possible to adapt RF methods to DSE, e.g., by treating the target datasets in DSE as labeled relevant datasets in explicit RF or as clicked datasets in implicit RF. However, as shown in our evaluation,
such naive method adaptation is suboptimal, reflecting the unique challenges of DSE
.

3
Construction of DSEBench

As the first test collection for DSE and explainable DSE, we built DSEBench by adapting NTCIR
[
12
]
, a popular test collection for keyword-based dataset search offering 46,615 datasets, 92,930 data files, 192 queries, and 10,536 relevance judgments. We used the English subset of NTCIR, having to exclude its Japanese version due to the lack of language proficiency among our annotators, which would influence annotation quality on Japanese data.

3.1
Datasets

It would be insufficient to only take metadata into account in dataset search. The actual data content is also useful
[
32
]
. The datasets in NTCIR offer semi-structured metadata and text-rich data files. These data files contain a wealth of information that is not covered by the metadata, necessitating the use of their content.

3.1.1
Dataset Fields

The metadata fields, along with a content summary of the data files, form the
fields of a dataset
, including four metadata fields that are commonly associated with a dataset provided by its publisher, namely
title
,
description
,
tags
,
author
, and a content field referred to as
summary
which we extracted from the data files (Section
3.1.2
).
In the remainder of the article, when we refer to a dataset, we indicate these five fields.

3.1.2
Content Summary

The diverse formats and large sizes of the data files make it challenging to digest their information in dataset search. Some data files are formatted in a machine-readable way, such as RDF, but are not easily readable by human annotators. Some files contain so much data that humans find it difficult to quickly locate useful information. This complexity motivates the generation of
content summaries
to aid in the quick comprehension of the content of large data files, allowing the efficient extraction of meaningful insights. They ensure that
the content of a dataset is usable and useful in dataset search, while also maintaining readability for human annotators
. Their effectiveness in improving retrieval accuracy has been demonstrated
[
25
,
6
]
.

To handle different data formats of the files, our summary generation (Section
3.1.3
) involved techniques such as text summarization and table header extraction. Specifically, we computed abstracts for unstructured textual files, while for structured and semi-structured data files we extracted their schema information (e.g., table headers or attribute names) since the raw data instances are often too many to be processed in full. This ensures that the main content of the data files is represented in a concise and informative summary.

detected their real formats
using

Format-Specific Summaries.

For different data formats, we define their content summaries in format-specific ways.

1.

Tabular Files
, i.e. CSV, XLSX, and XLS: Their content summary consists of all
table headers
to provide a concise description of the structure and key attributes of the data.

2.

Textual Files
, i.e. PDF, TXT, HTML, DOCX, and DOC: Their content summary is an
abstract of the textual content
that distills key ideas or topics from the text.

3.

JSON and XML Files
: The content summary of a JSON file is a set of all
keys
in the data. The content summary of an XML file is a set of all
tags
in the data. These keys and tags give an overview of the structure and data types represented, helping to understand data organization.

4.

RDF Files
: The content summary of an RDF file is a set of all
predicates
in the triple-structured data. These predicates represent the core attributes and relations of the entities described, effectively summarizing the data schema without overwhelming the reader with full details of the entities.

5.

Other Files
, e.g. HDF: Their content summaries were left empty.

3.1.3
Summary Generation

For different data formats, we implemented different methods for generating content summaries.

For
tabular files
, we analyzed 50 samples and found that 92% of the CSV files use the first row for column headers, allowing accurate rule-based extraction. XLSX and XLS files have more complex table structures that cannot be effectively extracted using simple rules, as illustrated in Figure
2
. We followed
[
33
]
to extract their headers based on the features inherent in Excel tables. Our evaluation of 100 tabular files reported an overall extraction accuracy of 91%.

Figure 2
:
An example of complex table.

For
textual files
, we performed text summarization to distill their content. As a pre-processing step, from PDF files we extracted text using Optical Character Recognition (OCR), and from HTML files we removed HTML tags. Taking into account the large length of some files, we used
bart-large-cnn

[
34
]
for text summarization, which accommodates up to 1,024 input tokens, truncating the remaining text. We set the output length to 300 tokens for a trade-off between retaining sufficient information and ensuring compactness for readability.

For
JSON, XML and RDF files
, their content summaries were easily extracted using
json
,
xml
, and
rdflib
, respectively.

3.2
Test and Training Cases

Recall that the input of an (explainable) DSE task consists of a query
q
and a set
D_{t}
of target datasets that are known to be relevant to the query. Although we could reuse the queries in NTCIR, for each query, we must find at least one relevant dataset as a target dataset to form a complete input
(q,D_{t})
of a DSE task.

3.2.1
Adapted Test and Training Cases

NTCIR contains 192 queries associated with 10,536 relevance judgments where 141 and 1,434 have a graded relevance score of 2 (i.e. highly relevant) and 1 (i.e. partially relevant), respectively. We adapted them as follows.

Test Cases.

For each of the 141 query-dataset pairs annotated highly relevant, we kept the query as
q
and the dataset as a singleton set
D_{t}
of target datasets to form the input of a test case for DSE.

Training Cases.

For the 1,434 query-dataset pairs annotated partially relevant, we adapted them in an analogous way. However, for quality considerations, we used them to build not test cases but training cases to be used for training supervised retrieval models. In particular, we filtered these query-dataset pairs by removing those where the query or the dataset is also involved in any input of the 141 test cases to
avoid train-test overlap
. After filtering, there remained 672 inputs of training cases for DSE.

3.2.2
Synthetic Training Cases

Since 672 training cases would be insufficient for fine-tuning large models, we synthesized a larger number of training cases from the 45,472 datasets that are not annotated relevant to any query in NTCIR. Considering the unaffordable cost of manually building training cases from this large number of datasets, we relied on the following
model-based approach
.

Synthesization.

To construct an input
(q,D_{t})
of a DSE task, for each of the above 45,472 datasets, we concatenated its
title
and
description
, and fed them into a
T5-base
model
[
35
]
that was fine-tuned to
generate a query
as
q
such that the given dataset should be relevant to the generated query and therefore qualified to serve as a singleton set
D_{t}
of target datasets. Our fine-tuning used the 141 and 1,434 query-dataset pairs mentioned in Section
3.2.1
that are annotated highly and partially relevant in NTCIR, respectively. We divided them into 80% for training and 20% for validation. We chose the average score of ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L as our validation metric, based on which an optimal learning rate of
1e-5
was selected from
\{1e-6,5e-6,1e-5\}
, and a batch size of 8 was selected from
\{8,16\}
, with training lasting 20 epochs.

Filtering.

To improve the quality of the synthetic queries, we applied a rigorous filtering process with requirements on non-triviality, conciseness, and specificity. Specifically, we removed queries that duplicated the title of the dataset, queries with excessive repetition of words, or queries that were too broad and lacked concrete search intent. In addition, we ensured that the generated queries did not overlap with the inputs of the test cases described in Section
3.2.1
.

Results.

Finally, we kept 3,810 distinct queries generated from 5,065 datasets. Together with those 672 inputs adapted in Section
3.2.1
, we prepared 5,737 inputs of training cases for DSE.

3.3
Pooling

It would be impractical to manually judge the relevance and similarity of 46,615 datasets to 141 test queries and 5,737 training queries. Following common practices in IR evaluation, we used the pooling method to reduce the number of judgments required.

Retrievers.

For the pooling, we adapted four popular retrievers.

1.

BM25
and
TF-IDF
are classic sparse retrieval models.

2.

BGE
(
bge-large-en-v1.5
)
[
36
]
is a general text embedding model trained on a large corpus of unsupervised English textual data such as Wikipedia.

3.

GTE
(
gte-large
)
[
37
]
is a general text embedding model trained with multi-stage contrastive learning.

Implementation Details.

For each dataset, we concatenated its fields to construct a
pseudo-document
to be indexed in an inverted index for sparse models or in a vector index for dense models. Given the input
(q,D_{t})
of a test or training case for DSE, since the task requires finding datasets relevant to both the query and the target datasets, we concatenated the query
q
with the pseudo-document of each target dataset
d_{t}\in D_{t}
into an expanded query to retrieve datasets.
2
2

2

To prevent short but important fields from being overlooked by retrieval models, following common practices
[
38
,
39
]
, we repeated the query, the
tags
field, and the
author
field 100 times during concatenation.
Except for BM25, the other three retrievers calculate the cosine similarity between the vector representation of the expanded query and the vector representation of each candidate dataset
d_{c}
as
d_{c}
’s ranking score. We collected the 20 top-ranked candidate datasets from each retriever.

Results.

With four retrievers, for the inputs of 141 test cases, we pooled 7,415 candidate datasets, or 52.59
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
triples per case to be annotated. For the inputs of 5,737 training cases, we pooled 337,976 candidate datasets, or 58.91 triples per case.

3.4
Annotation

To achieve a trade-off between annotation quality and cost, we invited human experts to annotate the 7,415
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
triples from the test cases. The 337,976 triples from the training cases were annotated with an LLM and filtered by quality-oriented heuristics.

3.4.1
Human Annotation

We recruited 24 graduate and senior-year undergraduate students in computer science as annotators. All of them have sufficient experience in dataset search.

Dataset-Level Judgments.

Given a triple
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
, following the task definition of DSE, an annotator needed to judge how much the candidate dataset
d_{c}
is
relevant
to the query
q
, and how much
d_{c}
is
similar
to the target datasets in
D_{t}
. In accordance with NTCIR, relevance and similarity were scored using a graded scale:

1.

0: irrelevant or dissimilar,

2.

1: partially relevant or partially similar,

3.

2: highly relevant or highly similar.

Field-Level Judgments.

For each partially and highly relevant or similar dataset, the annotator was also required to identify in the candidate dataset
which fields indicate its relevance or similarity
, which can be used in the evaluation of explainable DSE.

Annotation Tool.

Annotation was performed using a Web tool as illustrated in Figure
3
. To avoid position bias in field judgments, the fields of each dataset were presented in random order.

Figure 3
:
A screenshot of our tool for human annotation. The left panel shows an input of DSE, including a query and a target dataset. The right panel presents a candidate dataset with annotation options. In this example, the candidate dateset is annotated partially similar to the target dataset. Accordingly, the second column of checkboxes are enabled, and three fields of the candidate dataset are annotated indicators of its similarity. However, the candidate dateset is annotated irrelevant to the query, so the first column of checkboxes representing indicators of relevance are disabled.

Task Assignment.

To obtain high-quality annotations, we assigned each of the 7,415
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
triples from the test cases to
two independent annotators
. If they provided the same score, it would be accepted as the final score. If they provided different scores, we would assign the triple to a
third annotator
, and the final score was determined on the basis of a majority vote, that is, by the third annotator. However, if three scores differed from each other, we would take their mean value (i.e. 1) as the final score.

Field-level disagreements were resolved analogously. If two annotators disagreed on whether a field indicates relevance or similarity, a third annotator would make the final judgment via majority vote.

3.4.2
LLM Annotation

We prompted a popular and cost-effective LLM, GLM-3-Turbo
[
40
]
, to annotate 337,976
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
triples from the training cases. In accordance with human annotation, we collected dataset-level and field-level judgments from LLM outputs.

Filtering.

We found that LLM provided many inaccurate dataset-level judgments, so we applied heuristic filtering. For query relevance, we removed the cases where the occurrence of query terms in candidate datasets was either implausibly low despite being labeled relevant or implausibly high despite being labeled irrelevant. For target similarity, we filtered out the cases where the overlap between a target dataset and a candidate dataset was inconsistent with the assigned label, such as when the string similarity of the fields was too high for a dissimilar pair or when the dissimilar pairs shared an excessive number of tags.

Although these heuristics might also mistakenly filter out some high-quality judgments, we checked the filtered judgments and, in general, observed
substantially improved quality
. Specifically, the accuracy for query relevance and target similarity judgments increased from 73% and 69%, respectively, to more than 85% based on a manual evaluation of 3,378 triples sampled. Considering this satisfactory quality and the large size of LLM annotations, they form useful training cases for weak supervision.

We did not apply any heuristic filters to the field-level judgments because our investigation revealed their relatively high accuracy but complex error patterns that are not easily detectable.

Results.

4
Evaluation of DSE

A typical IR pipeline consists of
first-stage retrieval
and
reranking
. Following this convention, we used DSEBench to separately evaluate retrieval and reranking models for DSE.

All our experiments were conducted on an Intel Xeon Gold 5222 CPU with four NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs for dense models.

4.1
Training, Validation, and Test Sets

Recall that DSEBench provides 141 test cases and 5,699 training cases for DSE. To ensure future evaluation results are comparable to each other, we provide two official training-validation-test splits.

1.

Five-Fold Split:
We randomly partitioned 141 test cases into five nearly equal-sized folds to support five-fold cross-validation. Three folds are used as training data, one fold is retained as validation data, and the remaining one fold is used to test the model. The performance of the model on the test set is averaged over five runs.

2.

Annotator Split.
We divided 5,699 training cases into 80% as training data and 20% as validation data. The 141 test cases are used to test the model.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

Standard IR evaluation metrics were used on the test set:
MAP
@5,
NDCG
@5,
Recall
@5 (R@5), MAP@10, NDCG@10, and Recall@10 (R@10), where NDCG@10 was also used for the selection of hyperparameters on the validation set.

These metrics rely on gold-standard relevance labels. Recall that each triple
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
annotated in DSEBench is associated with two dataset-level judgments: the relevance of the candidate dataset
d_{c}
to the query
q
, and the similarity of
d_{c}
to the target datasets in
D_{t}
, both in the range of
\{0,1,2\}
. Although both scores are provided and can be combined in any reasonable way, following the task definition of DSE, query relevance and target similarity are indispensable, so their product was taken as the gold-standard graded relevance label of
(q,D_{t},d_{c})
in the range of
\{0,1,2,4\}
to ensure that a candidate dataset receives a high overall relevance score only if it is highly relevant to the query
and
highly similar to the target datasets; a low score in either aspect would significantly penalize the overall relevance.

4.3.1
Experimental Setup

In this experiment, for a query with a set of target datasets, we evaluated the top-ranked datasets returned by each tested retrieval model among all the 46,615 datasets.

Retrievers.

We adapted and evaluated seven representative retrieval models.

1.

BM25

,
BGE
(

(

.

2.

DPR

[
41

[

[

We also evaluated a classic RF method by adapting it to the DSE task.

1.

Rocchio

[
31

is an explicit RF method that uses the vector representations of the positive and negative feedback to refine the vector representation of the query.

Implementation Details.

To adapt retrieval models, following the implementation in Section
3.3

In particular, for supervised dense retrieval models, we evaluated their variants in three configurations:
not fine-tuned

, and
fine-tuned with the annotator split
as defined in Section
4.1

\{0,1,2,4\}

\{1e-5,5e-6\}
and batch size from
\{8,16\}

1e-5
and a batch size of 8 for DPR, and a learning rate of
1e-5
and a batch size of 16 for ColBERTv2 and coCondenser. With the annotator split, we selected a learning rate of
1e-5
and a batch size of 16 for DPR, and a learning rate of
1e-5
and a batch size of 8 for ColBERTv2 and coCondenser.

To adapt the RF method, i.e. Rocchio in the TF-IDF space, we evaluated its variants in two configurations:
Rocchio-P
using the target datasets as positive feedback, and
Rocchio-PN
using the target datasets as positive feedback and, inspired by
[
44
]

The weights for combining the vector representations of the original query, positive feedback, and negative feedback in refining the query vector were 1.0, 0.75, and 0.15, respectively. The refined query was used to retrieve datasets.

5
Evaluation of Explainable DSE

Building on DSE, explainable DSE further requires identifying the indicator fields for the query relevance and target similarity of a retrieved dataset. We used DSEBench to evaluate the explanation methods for explainable DSE.

5.1
Experimental Setup

5.1.1
Evaluation Metrics

We used a standard IR evaluation metric, F1-score, to compare indicator fields identified by a tested explanation method with the gold standard annotated in DSEBench.

5.1.2
Explainers

We adapted and evaluated three representative post hoc explainers.

1.

Feature
Ablation
assesses the importance of a field by excluding it from retrieval and observing the changes in the ranking scores outputted by the retrieval model.

2.

Surrogate models approximate the behavior of a complex model by training a simple model to estimate the contribution of each field to the output ranking scores. We evaluated two classic methods of this kind,
LIME

[
27
]
and
SHAP

[
28
]
.

We also evaluated an
LLM
-based method using
GLM-4-Air

[
40
]
.

5.1.3
Implementation Details

For the Ablation explainer, we excluded one field at a time and calculated the ratio of the resulting ranking score to the original score. If the ratio for a field dropped below a threshold of 0.95, we outputted it as an indicator field. If no field met this threshold, we selected the field with the largest score drop as an indicator field.

LIME relied on a binary field classifier, SVM in our implementation, as the proxy model to approximate the behavior of the retrieval model. Considering that a dataset has five fields, we set the number of perturbed samples used to train the classifier to a sufficiently large value of 50. Fields with a predicted negative score were outputted as indicator fields.

For SHAP, we chose the partition explainer as its explainer algorithm. Fields with a computed positive Shapley value were outputted as indicator fields.

For LLM, we evaluated its variants in two configurations:
zero-shot
and
few-shot
. The few-shot prompt included three verified examples selected from the training cases provided by DSEBench.

Since most of the explanation methods tested are coupled with the concrete retrieval model, we combined them with each of the retrieval models evaluated in Section
4.3
, not including the RF method, as it is incompatible with the explanation methods tested.

Conclusions

In this article, we investigated explainable DSE, a composite IR task that generalizes and extends two established dataset search paradigms: keyword-based dataset search and similarity-based dataset discovery. To facilitate its research, we constructed and published DSEBench, the first test collection for explainable DSE, providing high-quality human-annotated test cases and a large number of LLM-annotated training cases, thus suitable for evaluating both unsupervised and supervised models. The annotations include not only the query relevance and target similarity of the datasets but also their indicator fields as an explanation. With DSEBench, we adapted a wide variety of existing retrieval, reranking, and explanation methods to explainable DSE and reported their evaluation results to establish extensive baselines to be compared in future research. DSEBench and the adapted methods we implemented provide an empirical foundation for future research on explainable DSE, which in turn could benefit conventional keyword-based dataset search and similarity-based dataset discovery provided by current systems and promote the research in explainable IR.

Our work has the following limitations that we plan to address in future work. First, DSEBench is constructed from a single test collection for dataset search, the English version of NTCIR, where the datasets mainly offer government data. There are datasets in other domains and languages, such as academic datasets, which will be considered in the next version of DSEBench. Second, the current inputs in DSEBench contain a singleton set of target datasets. A more general setting is to consider multiple target datasets with a query. However, this requires a larger number of datasets that are known to be relevant to the query as the target datasets, which will reduce the number of test cases that can be generated. Third, in our evaluation, the query and the fields of the target datasets are concatenated in a straightforward way into an expanded query. It might be an idea to consider models that can directly exploit such structured inputs.
