Mycobacterium tuberculosis transmission among child and adolescent contacts of people with pulmonary tuberculosis in Brazil: a multi-center prospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren who are contacts of individuals with active pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) are at risk of acquiring Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection (TBI). We evaluated the determinants of TBI in child and adolescent contacts.MethodsPediatric (≤ 18 years old) close contacts of PTB patients enrolled in a multi-center Brazilian observational cohort were followed up to 6 months. Clinical evaluation, radiographic examination, and interferon gamma release assay (IGRA) were performed. Positive IGRA result at baseline or month 6 (IGRA-conversion) was considered an indicator of TBI. Multivariable mixed-effects logistic regressions were performed to identify factors associated with TBI.ResultsAmong 562 close contacts enrolled, 171 (30.4%) had a positive IGRA at baseline, and 37 (9.5%) converted at month 6. Positive AFB, persistent cough and cavity on chest X-ray of TB index were independently associated with a positive IGRA result at baseline. IGRA-conversion was independently associated with older age among contacts and chest X-ray cavitations in the index cases. Contacts ≥ 15.9 years old had 3.4 (95%CI: 1.49–7.78, p = 0.004) adjusted odds ratio of IGRA-conversion than those < 15.9 years old.ConclusionAmong pediatric close TB contacts, the prevalence of IGRA positivity increased with age. Older children have substantially higher susceptibility to TBI upon exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12268-5.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a serious public health problem worldwide, especially in under-developed and developing countries [1]. Globally, nearly one-quarter of the population is estimated to be infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) [1], and approximately 56 million people are at high risk of developing TB infection (TBI) from a recent exposure [1]. Children and adolescents represent a vulnerable subgroup within this population. In 2024, individuals under 15 years accounted for 16% of all active TB cases globally [2], and more than 2,000 deaths were attributed to TB in this age group worldwide [2]. Young children under 5 years of age are at high risk of developing severe forms of TB [3].

In Brazil, one of the 30 countries with the highest incidence of TB [2], this disease continues to affect children and adolescents significantly. Between 2015 and 2023, 848,680 TB cases were reported, of which 67,497 (7.95%) were in children and adolescents [4]. Morever, in 2023, 3,419 (4.3%) new TB cases were diagnosed in Brazilian children under 15 years old. In this same year, TB cases in children under 5 years showed an increase compared to 2015, from 899 to 1,469 [5, 6].

A key driver of TBI in children is recent exposure to an infectious case, particularly within the household. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [7] it was observed that children exposed to a person with active TB at home were almost 4 times more likely to experience TBI than children of the same age in the same community without known exposure. In Brazil, previous studies have reported TBI prevalence around 20% in exposed children [8]. Households clearly represent areas of Mtb transmission, but the effect of this transmission on the overall burden of disease at the community level is not established [9]. The need to identify specific factors associated with Mtb transmission has been underscored by studies in which < 30% of children living in the home of TB patients become infected [9, 10], suggesting that additional host, environmental, or pathogen-related factors may influence the risk of transmission.

Testing for TBI is performed using the tuberculin skin test (TST) or an interferon gamma release assay (IGRA) [10]. Both screening tests have limitations in sensitivity and specificity for active disease and TBI in young children, likely due to an immature immune response. Immunological dysfunction associated with malnutrition can also affect test sensitivity [11, 12]. Of these two options, the advantage of IGRA lies in its enhanced specificity and independence from BCG vaccination. Moreover, it requires only a single visit and does not depend on a personal evaluation, resulting in more standardized and reliable results [13].

In this study, we evaluated risk factors associated with Mtb transmission among children and adolescents who were close contacts of pulmonary TB cases. We used IGRA results to identify determinants of infection in a multi-center Brazilian cohort.

Methods

Ethics statement

All clinical investigations were conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All regulatory documents were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of all participating sites (Main Ethics Committee: Instituto Nacional de Infectologia Evandro Chagas; approval number - CAAE: 25102414.3.2009.5543). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legally responsible guardians, at the time of study enrollment. The anonymity of study subjects was preserved; all research data were de-identified.

Overall study design

Regional Prospective Observational Research in Tuberculosis (RePORT) - Brazil (www.reportbrasil.org) is a longitudinal cohort study of culture-confirmed pulmonary TB cases and their close contacts. There were five study sites in three Brazilian states: Amazonas (1), Bahia (1) and Rio de Janeiro (3). The cohort and its representativeness of all TB cases reported in Brazil have been described previously [14, 15]. All procedures were performed according to Brazilian National TB Guidelines [10].

Clinical and demographic data (sex, age, symptoms, race, alcohol, drugs and tobacco consumption, and comorbidities), chest x-ray, blood and smear samples of the TB cases were obtained at baseline, in the second month and at the end of treatment (month 6) 1. After the diagnosis of the index TB case, all contacts identified were invited to participate in RePORT-Brazil. Those contacts who agreed to participate were screened with a medical evaluation, IGRA, and chest X-ray at baseline; the IGRA test was repeated in six months if negative at baseline. In addition to the above in-person visits, telephone calls were performed every six months until month 24 for clinical follow-up regarding TB symptoms. Contacts with a positive IGRA result at baseline did not repeat the IGRA at month six.

Study population and procedures

Close contacts of individuals with culture-positive pulmonary TB were enrolled between June 2015 and June 2019 [14]. Close contacts were defined as having ≥ 4 h of contact per week with the TB index case at any time during the 6 months prior to index TB case diagnosis. Close contacts were classified according to five age groups: <5 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, 15–18 years [3] to describe clinical, epidemiologic characteristics and IGRA results.

According to Brazilian guidelines, TB preventive treatment (TPT) is recommended for close contacts with i) a positive IGRA, or ii) ≤ 5 years of age or iii) HIV infection [10]. The recommended regimen is isoniazid at 5-10 mg/kg for 6 to 9 months [10]. Details of the number of contacts in this cohort who received TPT, and their treatment outcome have been published previously [16]. The IGRA test performed was the QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus, which has been successfully implemented in the RePORT sites [17].

All information from contacts < 5 years old was collected from parents or legal guardians. The information of those ≥ 5 years old was collected from them together with their caregivers. All contacts identified by TB cases and with whom the study team was able to communicate, were encouraged to be evaluated clinically and to initiate and complete TPT (if recommended), regardless of their follow-up in the study.

Finally, TBI was defined as a positive IGRA at baseline, and IGRA-conversion was defined as a positive IGRA at month 6 among persons negative at baseline. Although IGRA is not formally validated for children under 5 years of age, some guidelines support its use in children over 3 years of age [18]. Children whose IGRA result was indeterminate (at either baseline or month 6) were considered to have an indeterminate IGRA result but were not excluded.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as proportions and compared using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test (2 × 2 comparisons) or Pearson’s chi-square test (with Yates correction). Gaussian distribution was assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. A multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression model [19], with a random effect per “TB case” variable (to decrease possible selection bias, because only one “TB case” can have contacts with different IGRA results), was performed to assess for associations between clinical characteristics of TB cases and contacts with a positive IGRA at baseline or month 6 (i.e., IGRA conversion). Contacts with indeterminate IGRA results or those with IGRA negative at baseline who did not have a second IGRA were excluded. Contacts with negative IGRA at baseline and month 6 were used as reference. LASSO regression was used to select the variables included in the regression model.

In addition, a piecewise logistic regression model was performed to identify the breakpoint age of contacts associated with IGRA-conversion results. This approach fits separate regression segments before and after a candidate breakpoint to determine the age at which the probability of conversion changes significantly. We used 4000 bootstrapped samples for internal validation [19]. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in the final multivariable model.

Results

Characteristics of the study population by age groups

Between 2015 and 2019, RePORT-Brazil enrolled 562 child and adolescent contacts younger than 18 years old. Approximately 73 (13.0%) individuals with IGRA negative at baseline did not have the second IGRA performed at month 6, and 23 (16 [2.7%] at baseline and 7 [2.2%] at month 6) had an indeterminate result (Fig. 1). The frequency of IGRA-positive result at baseline increased with age, varying from 21% in children < 5 years to 35.2% in contacts with 15–18 years (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Similarly, IGRA-conversion results increased significantly with the age of contacts (p < 0.001). Importantly, children under 5 years had a higher proportion of indeterminate IGRA test results (4%, p < 0.001) and lost to follow-up (23%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

For TB index cases, the median age and sex distribution were similar across groups. TB index cases of contacts < 5 years presented a more frequent consumption of illicit drugs (53.2%) if compared to other age groups (p = 0.001). Whereas TB index cases of contact aged 15–18 years had a high proportion of cavities on chest X-ray (55.2%) (p < 0.001) and the highest frequency of persistent cough (36.8%) if compared to other groups (p = 0.014). No significant differences were observed in HIV status, positive AFB smear, baseline cough, or HbA1c levels of index cases across contact age groups (Table 1).

Characteristics of TB contacts by IGRA result

Among the 489 individuals included in the analysis, 35.6% (n = 174) were IGRA-positive at baseline, and 7.6% (n = 37) converted from negative to positive IGRA at month 6, reflecting both the baseline burden of TBI and the incidence of new infections during the follow-up period. Contacts who converted their IGRA result between baseline and month 6 reported significant median age among the three groups (p = 0.007) (Table 2).

Determinants of IGRA positivity and conversion in pediatric TB contacts

Clinical and laboratory information were assessed to explore the relationship between the baseline characteristics of index TB case and their contacts and the positivity or conversion of IGRA test result in close contacts < 18 years (Fig. 2). We performed a multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression and observed that positive AFB in sputum (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR]:2,50; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.45-4,33; p = 0.001), persistent cough (aOR:1.60; 95%CI:1.01–2.54; p = 0.046) and presence of cavities on chest X-ray (aOR:1.85; 95%CI:1.20–2.85; p = 0.005) of TB cases were associated with IGRA-positive result among pediatric population (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The evaluation of close contacts is an important strategy to identify children with TBI or active TB and provide preventive or curative treatment, thereby reducing the number of TB cases and Mtb transmission. This study highlights key determinants of TBI among children and adolescents who were close contacts of pulmonary TB cases in Brazil. In this multicenter cohort study, we found that increased age among children and adolescents, as well as exposure to TB index cases with persistent cough, sputum smear positivity, and chest x-ray cavities were associated with greater odds of TBI. Additionally, contacts aged ≥ 15.9 years were at increased risk of IGRA-conversion, suggesting recent infection potentially acquired both within and beyond the household.

We identified three clinical features of the index TB case as independently associated with IGRA positivity in contacts: positive smear at baseline, persistent cough and cavitation on chest x-ray. These factors are known to increase the probability of TB transmission [20], since cavitary lesions have a greater number of bacilli than nodular lung lesions. High oxygen levels within the cavity provide the enabling environment, with high rates of bacterial replication leading to a large bacillary load on the inner edge of the cavity [21].

Previous studies have reported TBI prevalence among children and adolescents ranging from 10% to 20% in Mexico and Brazil, depending on the population studied and diagnostic methods used [7, 22]. These findings are comparable to the prevalence observed in our cohort, supporting the representativeness of our sample. The proportion of IGRA-positive results at baseline increased with age, ranging from 21% in children under 5 years to 35% in adolescents aged 15–18. Although young children are particularly vulnerable to severe TB disease, their lower IGRA positivity in our cohort may be related to immunological factors that influence test performance [11, 12].

In our cohort, we also found a higher frequency of indeterminate IGRA in children under 5 years old (4%) than in other groups. This result is consistent with previous studies [23, 24]. Kay et al. found a higher occurrence of indeterminate IGRA results in children younger than 1 year of age (23%), 9% in children aged 1 to 3 years, 5% in children aged 2 to 4 years, all significantly higher when compared to people between 5 and 18 years old (2%). A recent study conducted in a primary care population in the United States found an overall rate of indeterminate IGRA of 0.5% and 1.3% in children aged 2 to 5 years [25]. The higher proportions in young children may be related to age, concurrent infections, corticosteroid use, immunological immaturity and malnutrition [26]. Recent evidence suggests that IGRAs are a reasonable alternative to TST even in children under 2 years old, particularly in high-burden settings where BCG vaccination is universal [27].

Conversely, adolescents in our cohort had a high frequency of positive IGRA. This upward trend is consistent with prior studies showing that older children and adolescents tend to have higher TBI prevalence, potentially reflecting cumulative exposure over time. A South African study reported that 45% of the adolescent population had a positive IGRA [28], a difference that may be explained by exposure time of contacts and the higher active TB burden in that region [28].

Similarly, IGRA conversion also was most frequent in adolescents between 15 and 18 years, almost double that observed in younger children. Using piecewise regression, we identified 15.9 years as the optimal age breakpoint for increased odds of IGRA-conversion. Our findings of a higher proportion of TBI in the adolescent age group may be due to an increased risk of exposure in these individuals by the sum of the risks of transmission at home and in the community. Factors such as changes in social behavior patterns, consumption of alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs, psychiatric issues such as anxiety and depression, and immunological changes linked to puberty may be involved in the greater vulnerability of this age group [29]. Similar patterns have been reported in other high-burden settings; a study conducted among adolescents in Tanzania found that social interactions outside the household, including informal gatherings, were independently associated with IGRA positivity even after adjusting for known risk factors [30]. Similar age-related patterns were also observed in a study conducted in rural Uganda, where adolescents had twice the prevalence of TB infection compared to younger children, and household contact was less predictive of TBI in older age groups [31]. These findings underscore the importance of considering both age-specific biological and social factors in TB transmission dynamics, and suggest that community-based strategies may be particularly relevant for adolescents.

These factors, when combined, increase the chance of poor outcomes, including challenges related to adherence and follow-up [29, 32, 33]. In our cohort, TPT completion was low across all age groups, ranging from 10% to 13%, as previously reported [16]. Emerging evidence suggests that current models of care do not meet the specific needs of TB-exposed adolescents.

In addition, undiagnosed and untreated adolescents with TBI can progress to active TB and transmit Mtb, increasing the number of infected people due to the high number of social contacts. In our study, five participants developed active TB during follow-up and none had initiated TPT despite being eligible, underscoring missed opportunities for prevention. Emerging evidence suggests that current models of care do not meet the specific needs of TB-exposed adolescents. Therefore, it is necessary to have specific public policies for TB control for this age group that aim to increase screening, diagnosis, adherence to preventive and curative treatment, and follow-up of this population with its specificities.

Of note, in our cohort, no significant sex differences were observed in IGRA positivity among pediatric contacts. Although sex-related immunological and social factors may influence TB infection risk, evidence remains limited and inconclusive in children and adolescents. Given the higher rate of IGRA conversion observed in adolescents aged 15–18 years, future studies should explore risk factors for TB infection beyond household exposure, including mobility and community-level transmission.

The study had limitations. The nutritional evaluation included only body mass index, and no other parameters specific to the child and adolescent population. No data was collected on the personal/family relationship between the index TB case and contacts or the time and type of exposure to Mtb. Additionally, skin tests such as TST or CY-TB were not included, which may have provided additional insights, particularly in specific subgroups evaluated. The use of IGRA in children under 5 years of age represents a limitation, given the lack of formal validation in this age group. Repeat IGRA testing was only performed in participants who were initially IGRA-negative or indeterminate, which limited our ability to evaluate reversion patterns. Despite these limitations, we believe that this is the first prospective study conducted in Brazil evaluating TBI in close contacts < 18 years-old of patients with pulmonary TB using the IGRA testing.

In conclusion, aging increases the proportion of TB contacts with IGRA conversion, even among children. IGRA-positivity, especially in older children, may be due to exposure other than from this household contact in Brazil. Such findings emphasize the critical need to distinguish between prior and recent TBI. This is one of the first large-scale, prospective studies in Brazil to evaluate IGRA conversion among pediatric and adolescent contacts of pulmonary TB, contributing new evidence on the use of IGRA in a highly BCG-vaccinated population. Identifying an age-related increase in conversion, including a breakpoint at 15.9 years, adds valuable insight into TB infection risk stratification in this age group. The screening of close contacts is an important strategy for the active search of new cases of TBI and active TB, with the objective of reducing undiagnosed TB cases, preventing the progression of TBI to disease and the transmission of Mtb in the community.
