Risk factors for pregnancy loss across gestational periods in Holstein cattle under a Mexican intensive, temperate semi-arid production system
Authors: Miguel Ángel Lammoglia-Villagómez, Ivan I Avalos-Rosario, Reese Fletcher, Daniel Sokani Sánchez-Montes, Jorge Luis Chagoya-Fuentes, Luis Javier Montiel-Olguín, Eliab Estrada-Cortés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate whether pregnancy loss during early, mid, or late gestation periods is associated with parity, uterine health, or season of breeding in a Mexican intensive, temperate semi-arid production system. In a retrospective cohort observational study (n = 772), primiparous and multiparous cows with a retained placenta and/or metritis were categorized as having uterine disease (UTD), whereas cows without these conditions were categorized as healthy (HLT). Two seasons of breeding were considered: dry (November-April) and rainy (May-October). Pregnancy loss was classified into three gestation periods: early (33 to 42 days, n = 772), mid (43 to 110 days, n = 706), and late (111 to 202 days, n = 599). Data were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression and Cox proportional hazard regression models. Overall, cows that experienced uterine disease had a higher risk of losing their pregnancy during the early (P = 0.0280), mid (P = 0.0441), and late (P = 0.0340) evaluation periods compared to healthy cows. Concurrently, multiparous cows exhibited a higher risk of pregnancy loss during the mid (P = 0.0048) period compared to primiparous cows. The Cox proportional hazard model indicated that the probability of remaining pregnant over time was consistently lower for UTD cows (P = 0.0010) and multiparous cows (P = 0.0006) compared to their healthy and primiparous counterparts, respectively. In conclusion, postpartum uterine diseases and multiparity are critical risk factors that compromise long-term pregnancy maintenance in dairy cattle managed under intensive, temperate semi-arid production systems in México.Keywords: Gestational periods, Metritis, Parity, Pregnancy failure

Introduction

Pregnancy loss (PL) has been recognized as one of the main problems affecting reproductive efficiency in dairy cattle (Wiltbank et al., 2016). In Holstein cattle, PL has been associated with several factors, including uterine diseases, parity, season of the year (Pinedo et al., 2020), heat stress (Albaaj et al., 2022), low progesterone concentrations (Moore et al., 2005), and other physiological and environmental influences (Mee et al., 2023). In commercial dairy farms, the timing of pregnancy diagnosis varies according to management practices. However, the first evaluation is typically carried out around 30 days post-insemination, with confirmations made at approximately days 40, 60, 100, and 200 post-insemination (Albaaj et al., 2022; Pinedo et al., 2020; Wiltbank et al., 2016). Pregnancy loss can occur at any stage of gestation, with the highest risk reported in early gestation and substantial cumulative losses accumulating during mid-gestation due to broader diagnostic windows (Albaaj et al., 2022; Fernandez-Novo et al., 2020). Nevertheless, the economic impacts are greater when a pregnancy is lost during the advanced stages of gestation (Cabrera, 2012). Thus, the identification of factors influencing pregnancy loss during different gestation periods is required to generate strategies to ameliorate this important issue.

Uterine diseases are considered one of the main factors affecting the establishment and maintenance of pregnancy in dairy cattle (Bruinjé et al., 2024). Uterine diseases represent a significant clinical challenge since their prevalence ranges from 20 % to 30 % in dairy herds. These uterine conditions are associated with a shift in microbiome structure within the reproductive tract, a process potentiated by systemic immunosuppression during the early postpartum period (Galvão et al., 2019; Husnain et al., 2023). Mechanistically, the proliferation of specific taxa, such as Escherichia coli and Fusobacterium spp., compromises the localized uterine environment while simultaneously disrupting pituitary function, impairing oocyte and follicular development, and consequently reducing overall cow fertility (Bromfield et al., 2015). The negative long-term effects of uterine diseases have been previously reported. Cows that experience uterine disease during the first 21 days postpartum have a higher loss of pregnancy, a lower calving rate, and a lower pregnancy rate after embryo transfer than healthy cows, even though the artificial insemination (AI) or embryo transfer (ET) occurred four to seven months after the recovery of a uterine problem (Bruinjé et al., 2024; Estrada-Cortés et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2016a). Additionally, it has been documented that bacterial uterine infection results in a transcriptome alteration of the endometrium, oviduct, and granulosa cells three months post-infection (Horlock et al., 2020). Collectively, these findings indicate that early-lactation uterine infections exert prolonged adverse effects on subsequent reproductive performance, ultimately compromising the cow's capacity to maintain future pregnancies.

Primiparous and multiparous cows typically exhibit distinct differences in reproductive performance during the postpartum period (Santos et al., 2009). These variations stem from physiological divergence in high-producing cows, where energetic challenges alter both systemic metabolic indicators and reproductive hormone profiles (Gärtner et al., 2019; Ruprechter et al., 2020). For instance, multiparous cows show marked imbalances in insulin like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), insulin, and adiponectin (Gärtner et al., 2019; Morales-Piñeyrúa et al., 2018; Ruprechter et al., 2020), as well as lower serum concentrations of pregnancy-associated glycoproteins (PAGs) compared to primiparous herdmates (Bramante et al., 2026; Mercadante et al., 2016). Additionally, the establishment and maintenance of pregnancy depend on coordinated crosstalk between the conceptus and the endometrium (Ribeiro et al., 2016b; Tinning et al., 2023). In this regard, parity-associated differences have been identified in ovarian follicular fluid metabolites (Bender et al., 2010) and uterine luminal fluid amino acid profiles (Forde et al., 2017). Consequently, these compounding physiological disparities likely account for the distinct patterns of pregnancy loss observed between these parity groups.

Climatic shifts across annual seasons manifest primarily as variations in precipitation and temperature, directly influencing the seasonal yield, quality, and overall availability of forage (Butler, 2014; Santana et al., 2025). Consequently, reproductive performance is commonly affected by the season of the year (Pinedo et al., 2020). For instance, several studies have reported an increase in pregnancy loss during the rainy season due to heat stress driven by elevated temperature and humidity (García-Ispierto et al., 2006; Mellado et al., 2016). However, the directional effect of seasonality on pregnancy loss within high-altitude and semiarid production systems, such as those in Mexico, remains uncertain. This is particularly relevant in TMR-fed operations, where the nutritional quality of feed ingredients is also dependent on climatic conditions (López-Jara et al., 2025; Santana et al., 2025). Therefore, the season of the year represents a critical factor that must be integrated into reproductive management decisions and herd evaluations.

While the effects of uterine disease, parity, and season on the establishment and maintenance of pregnancy during the early gestation period are well-documented (Bruinjé et al., 2024; Pinedo et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2009), there is limited information regarding their effects on mid- and late- gestation (Albaaj et al., 2022; Wiltbank et al., 2016). Understanding the carryover effects that influence pregnancy maintenance during mid- and late gestation is essential for making better management decisions and reducing the economic losses associated with gestational failure. Furthermore, environmental conditions vary across studies, meaning that the impact of factors such as uterine disease, parity, and season may depend on the specific production system or regional climate. However, information regarding the interplay of these factors remains limited within Mexican production systems. Therefore, it was hypothesized that uterine disease, multiparity, and rainy-season service elevate the risk of pregnancy failure; uterine health status exerts a persistent negative effect across early, mid, and late gestation, while the detrimental effects of parity and season are restricted to the early and mid- gestation period. This study aimed to evaluate whether pregnancy loss during early, mid, or late gestational periods is associated with parity, uterine health, and season of breeding in a Mexican intensive, temperate semi-arid production system.

Materials and methods

Animals and general management

A retrospective cohort observational study was implemented over 19 months (November 2020 - June 2022). To account for missing data, a strict inclusion criterion was applied during data extraction: primiparous and multiparous Holstein cows were only included in the study if they had complete and verifiable records for all variables of interest. Animals with incomplete or missing information during the study period were excluded from the beginning of the analysis, yielding a final cohort of 772 cows. The farm was located in the state of Hidalgo, México, and milked approximately 1 000 cows, with an average production of 36.5 kg/cow/day. Cows included in the study had a mean (± standard deviation) lactation length of 305.0 ± 15.0 days and a mean (± standard deviation) voluntary waiting period of 65.0 ± 5.0 days. The study was conducted on a dairy farm located 2620 m above sea level within a temperate semi-arid climate. Average annual rainfall is 436.3 mm, concentrated mainly between May and October, with negligible precipitation from November to April. Climatic conditions from May to October are characterized by average temperatures of 16.1 - 19.4 °C and ∼40 % relative humidity, whereas the November to April period exhibits temperatures of 14.2 - 18.6 °C and a lower relative humidity of ∼10 %.

Cows were housed in modern barns and fed a total mixed ration (TMR) with a projected nutritional value to remain consistent across seasons. The TMR was composed of alfalfa hay, corn silage, rolled corn, soybean meal, cottonseed, wheat straw, dried grains, molasses, vitamins, and minerals. During the dry-off period, cows received a TMR containing 60 % forage and 16 % protein. From 14 days before the expected date of calving, cows received a TMR containing 53 % forage, 47 % grains, and 18 % protein. After calving, cows were fed a TMR of 47 % forage, 53 % grains, and 16 % protein.

Cows' health management followed the standard sanitary guidelines and vaccination protocols recommended for the production zone. Specifically, vaccination to prevent abortifacient pathogens included protection against diseases caused by Bovine Viral Diarrhea Virus (Types 1 and 2), Infectious Bovine Rhinotracheitis Virus, Bovine Parainfluenza 3, Bovine Respiratory Syncytial Virus, Campylobacter fetus, and Leptospira canicola, grippotyphosa, hardjo, icterohaemorrhagiae and pomona. Farm personnel initiated the vaccination protocol using TSV3 (Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ, USA) within the first 5 days of life, followed by the administration of a modified-live virus vaccine (CattleMaster Gold FP 5 L5; Zoetis) at 3, 5, and 12 months of age. Pregnant cows were vaccinated (CattleMaster Gold FP 5 L5; Zoetis) at 21 days prepartum and then at 60 and 210 days of gestation. Additionally, cows were administered Leptospira boosters (Leptoferm 5; Zoetis) every 4 months; one of the administrations was performed before the rainy season.

Postpartum uterine health of cows

Trained farm staff and veterinarians monitored the postpartum uterine health of cows in the first 21 days postpartum. Retained placenta was diagnosed within the first 48 h after parturition by the presence of fetal membranes. Cows were assigned to the metritis group regardless of clinical grade. Diagnosis was based on the presence of an enlarged uterus, purulent and/or bloody vaginal discharge (irrespective of odor), with or without the presence of fever, and confirmed via ultrasonography (Easy-Scan, BCF Technology; Universal goggles). Routinely, cows diagnosed with grade 1 metritis received only supportive therapy, including energy supplements and electrolytes. Conversely, cows with grade 2 and 3 metritis received a comprehensive protocol consisting of supportive therapy, systemic antibiotic therapy, and anti-inflammatory therapy. Animals exhibiting neither retained placenta nor metritis were classified as healthy (HLT), whereas those diagnosed with these pathologies were classified into the uterine disease (UTD) category. Cows with pathologies were combined into a single category because these conditions represent a continuous pathological timeline rather than isolated events.

Season of breeding

Two seasons of breeding were defined based on the artificial insemination date: dry and rainy. The dry season spanned from November to April, whereas the rainy season encompassed the months from May to October. As described above, both seasons exhibited distinctive environmental conditions.

Reproductive management of cows

On the farm, following a 65-day voluntary waiting period, all cows were enrolled in a Presynch-Ovsynch protocol. The Presynch phase consisted of two PGF2α injections given 14 days apart. Seven days after the second injection, the Ovsynch protocol was initiated: GnRH on day 0, PGF2α on day 7, and a second GnRH injection 56 h later. Timed artificial insemination (TAI) was performed 16 h after the final GnRH administration. However, cows showing estrus behavior at any point of the Presynch-Ovsynch protocol received the AI. At the first pregnancy diagnosis, open cows with a functional corpus luteum received a PGF2α injection and were AI upon estrus detection. Conversely, open cows without a functional corpus luteum were enrolled in an Ovsynch protocol. Additionally, cows with more than three services received an intravaginal progesterone device (1.9 g) and a GnRH injection on day 0. On day 7, management included device removal and a PGF2α injection; estradiol benzoate was administered 24 h later, and AI was performed upon detection of estrus. Of the total cows analyzed in this study, 153 completed the full Presynch-Ovsynch protocol for TAI, while 490 were AI prematurely due to early estrus expression. Regarding the resynchronization management groups, 30 cows received AI after displaying estrus behavior induced by a PGF2α injection, and 99 cows were AI upon enrollment in the progesterone-based protocol.

Periods of pregnancy diagnosis

All pregnancy diagnoses (PD) were performed via ultrasonography (Easy-Scan, BCF Technology; Universal goggles) by the same veterinarian to ensure diagnostic consistency. Routine PD were performed at approximately 33, 42, 110, and 202 days post-AI, according to the management established on the farm. Pregnancy status was confirmed during the early period by the detection of a viable embryo with an active heartbeat, whereas subsequent diagnoses were based on the visualization of placentomes and fetal movement. Based on consecutive evaluations, pregnancy loss was categorized into three mutually exclusive intervals: early (losses detected from 33 to 42 days), mid (losses detected from 43 to 110 days), and late (losses detected from 111 to 202 days). To account for the 7-day variation around each scheduled PD, a strict threshold was applied: cows examined within 3 days of the target mean were retained in that baseline period, whereas those examined 4 days after the mean were assigned to the adjacent upper range.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Studio, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Pregnancy loss was analyzed as a binary response by using logistic regressions adjusted for a binomial distribution, via the GLIMMIX procedure. In the first step, pregnancy loss was analyzed using independent univariate logistic regressions to evaluate the effects of uterine health category (UTD or HLT), season of breeding (dry or rainy), and parity (primiparous or multiparous). In the second step, effects with a P ≤ 0.35 were selected to build the multivariate models as described by Potter et al. (2010). Pairwise Chi-square (χ2) tests via the FREQ procedure with the CHISQ option were conducted as a conservative screening tool to identify potential dependencies among effects. When a pair of effects exhibited a significant association (P < 0.05), only one was retained for the multivariate model to avoid potential collinearity and predictor redundancy. Finally, multivariate logistic regression models were constructed using the selected non-collinear predictors. The first set of models evaluated the effects of uterine health, parity, and their interaction for each pregnancy loss period (early, mid, and late), whereas the second set evaluated the effects of uterine health, season of breeding, and their interaction across the same periods, resulting in a total of six separate multivariate models. Odds ratios and 95 % confidence intervals were calculated for both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Following the preliminary analyses, factors with significant effects were further subjected to a Cox proportional hazards regression model using the PHREG procedure. This approach allowed us to model the cumulative probability of cows remaining pregnant over the course of the evaluation period. Additionally, a post-hoc power analysis (α = 0.05, and 80 % target power) was performed for each evaluation period using the PROC POWER procedure. For all analyses, effects were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study hypothesized that under an intensive, temperate semi-arid system in México, postpartum uterine disease would exert a persistent negative carryover effect across all three gestational windows, while the detrimental impacts of multiparity and the rainy season would be restricted to the early and mid-gestation. These assumptions were partially supported by the findings. Although the impact of uterine health aligned with the initial hypothesis, multiparity affected pregnancy loss exclusively during mid-gestation, and season of breeding exerted no significant effect across any gestational period.

In the final multivariate logistic regression models, cows experiencing uterine disease carried a consistently higher risk of pregnancy loss across the early (OR = 2.1; 95 % CI = 1.1 – 4.0), mid (OR = 1.9; 95 % CI = 1.0 – 3.4), and late (OR = 3.2; 95 % CI = 1.1 – 9.5) gestational periods compared to unaffected cows. Aligning with these findings, the Cox proportional hazard model demonstrated that the hazard of pregnancy failure was significantly higher in cows with uterine disease (HR = 1.9; 95 % CI = 1.3 – 2.6) compared to the unaffected counterparts throughout the entire monitoring period (Fig. 1A). Negative effects of uterine diseases on the establishment and maintenance of pregnancy have been described previously (Bruinjé et al., 2024; Carvalho et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2016a).

Uterine infections induce multi-level reproductive failures by altering endocrine signaling (Haziak et al., 2014) and reducing oocyte competence (Dickson et al., 2020). Furthermore, localized endometrial transcriptomic alterations and immune cell infiltration compromise the overall uterine environment necessary for embryo survival (Horlock et al., 2020). Long-term negative effects of uterine diseases have been reported on reproductive traits like pregnancy rate after artificial insemination or embryo transfer (Estrada-Cortés et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2016a). However, limited information has been reported on the effects of uterine infection on pregnancy loss, particularly in the mid and late stages of gestation. The reported alterations caused by uterine infection in the reproductive system, in some way, can explain the long-term consequences in the maintenance of pregnancy. Indeed, uterine infections cause persistent transcriptomic alterations in the uterus, oviduct, and ovaries months after clinical resolution (Horlock et al., 2020).

In the multivariate logistic regression models, multiparous cows had a higher risk of pregnancy loss during the mid-evaluation period (OR = 2.4; 95 % CI = 1.3–4.4) compared with primiparous cows (Table 4). Consequently, the hazard of pregnancy failure was significantly higher in multiparous cows (HR = 1.7; 95 % CI = 1.2–2.3) relative to their primiparous counterparts at the conclusion of the study (Fig. 1B). These findings align with previous reports in Holstein cows (Santos et al., 2009; Zobel et al., 2011).

The elevated risk in multiparous cows is a multifactorial phenomenon driven by the physiological demands of higher milk production and compounding postpartum metabolic strain. Compared to primiparous cows, multiparous cows exhibit pronounced hormonal and metabolic imbalances, including altered profiles of IGF-1 and insulin, as well as non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) and β-hydroxybutyrate (Gärtner et al., 2019; Morales-Piñeyrúa et al., 2018; Ruprechter et al., 2020). Additionally, research shows that multiparous and high-yielding individuals exhibit lower serum concentrations of placental PAGs (Albaaj et al., 2026; Bramante et al., 2026; Mercadante et al., 2016) and reduced progesterone levels during gestation compared to primiparous cows (Bruinjé et al., 2017). Exogenous progesterone supplementation could be an alternative to compensate for this physiological deficiency in multiparous cows. While these specific physiological parameters were not directly measured in the present trial, such compounding endocrine deficiencies reported in the literature may partially explain the higher pregnancy losses observed in our multiparous cohort and suggest the potential benefit of implementing parity-specific reproductive management.

In the present study, pregnancy loss rates varied across gestation, reaching 8.5 % in the early (33–42 d), 15.2 % in the mid (43–110 d), and 4.0 % in the late (111–202 d) periods. The fact that the highest percentage of losses occurred during mid-gestation can be attributed to both methodological and biological factors. The elevated loss rate observed during mid-gestation is partly explained by a methodological consequence of the study design, as this window encompasses a much wider evaluation range (68 d) than the early period (10 d). Beyond this temporal exposure factor, this finding may reflect an additive biological risk. The mid-gestation period appears to act as a critical window where the lingering carryover effects of uterine disease and the physiological strain of multiparity converge, cumulatively driving up the rate of gestational failure.

Mid-gestation encompasses the transition into early fetal development. Although the observational, retrospective design of this cohort study precluded the direct measurement of circulating hormones or pathogens, literature-established mechanisms offer plausible explanatory pathways. For instance, mid-gestation losses are frequently linked to inadequate placental development (Farin et al., 2006; Hill et al., 2000) or luteal insufficiency leading to a suboptimal, progesterone-deficient uterine environment (Rhinehart et al., 2009; Lonergan & Sánchez, 2020). Additionally, endemic infectious agents like Bovine Viral Diarrhea virus and Neospora canis are well-documented causes of abortion during this window (Almería et al., 2010; Lanyon et al., 2014). While these unmeasured variables represent inherent data limitations of commercial registries, they underscore the multifactorial nature of the pregnancy failures observed during this gestation window.

In the present study, key strengths include a robust 19-month longitudinal evaluation that captured seasonal variations across two consecutive years and tracked three distinct gestational windows. Crucially, this design includes advanced gestational periods for which literature data remain highly limited. Additionally, the study benefited from highly consistent herd management, including standardized feeding, reproductive, and health protocols. By evaluating distinct risk factors and tracking separate physiological cohorts (primiparous vs. multiparous cows) under uniform baseline conditions, this design minimizes environmental confounding and enhances the validity of the epidemiological associations.

However, retrospective observational cohort studies inherently possess certain limitations. In this case, because the data were obtained from a single dairy farm situated in a high-altitude region with specific environmental dynamics, the generalizability of these reproductive outcomes to other production systems or climates may be limited. Likewise, several confounding factors that potentially influence pregnancy loss were not evaluated, such as hormonal and metabolic status, body condition score, clinical or subclinical endometritis, and specific abortifacient agents (like Bovine Viral Diarrhea virus and Neospora canis, among others). Additionally, small sample sizes within specific sub-groups precluded the inclusion of estrus synchronization protocols and metritis severity grades in the final multivariable models. A post hoc power analysis using a standard 80 % power threshold (α = 0.05) indicated that the late gestation stage was underpowered relative to the early and mid-gestation stages. The use of unequal gestational intervals alongside interval censoring may introduce event misclassification and inherently impact the total number of losses detected within each period. Consequently, while these findings offer valuable insights into long-term carryover effects, they should be interpreted within the context of these epidemiological boundaries.

Conclusions

In a Mexican intensive, temperate semi-arid production system, postpartum uterine diseases and multiparity act as pivotal risk factors that significantly compromise pregnancy maintenance over time. While the detrimental impact of multiparity on gestational stability is predominantly concentrated during the mid-gestation period, postpartum uterine disorders consistently cross-cut all gestational windows, elevating the risk of both early embryonic and late fetal losses. The absence of statistically significant risk factors during late gestation should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power in this specific window. Collectively, these findings highlight that optimizing reproductive efficiency in intensive herds requires the implementation of targeted management and prophylactic strategies during the transition period to minimize uterine health disorders and safeguard long-term pregnancy retention. Finally, further research incorporating larger cohort sizes and a broader range of potential physiological and environmental risk factors is warranted to comprehensively map the multifactorial causes of pregnancy loss across the entire gestation period.
