Leveraging genomic diagnostics for prognostics and therapeutics in pediatric acute leukemia

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvancements in the rapidity, cost efficacy, and sensitivity of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) have facilitated molecular risk stratification and precision medicine‐based treatment for pediatric leukemia. The benefit of uniform cytomolecular analyses for clinical trial risk assignment is clear. However, the clinical impact of comprehensive NGS for pediatric leukemias at an institutional level is not well described. We report the genomic spectrum of one of the largest cohorts of pediatric and adolescent/young adult (AYA) acute leukemias examined to date (n = 1442) via institutional NGS testing from our large tertiary care center. We evaluated the clinical utility of genomic results for informing prognosis and treatment. We identified high utility of the comprehensive DNA‐based mutational panel and RNA‐fusion panel, which detected leukemia‐associated variants in 99% of specimens. We observed 65% of B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia cases and 69% of patients with acute myeloid leukemia harbored a prognostic molecular alteration. In total, 325 of 1134 patients (29%) harbored potentially targetable molecular biomarkers, and 23% of cases with follow‐up data received precision medicine‐based therapy. Paired diagnostic and relapsed leukemia samples aided in differentiation between treatment‐related leukemia versus lineage drift/switch. These findings demonstrate the broad utility of comprehensive molecular sequencing for pediatric/AYA leukemia at an institutional level to improve outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Comprehensive genomic characterization of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and other less common leukemias occurring in children and adolescents/young adults (AYAs) has enhanced understanding of the initiation and progression of leukemogenesis. Advancements in the rapidity and cost efficacy of next‐generation genomic diagnostics and correlation with clinical outcomes of patients have further facilitated molecular risk stratification algorithms and precision medicine‐based treatment approaches. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Clinical trials for children and adolescents with B‐cell ALL (B‐ALL) or AML rely upon integration of somatic genomic data with early treatment response to identify patients with higher risk leukemias for therapeutic intensification and those with lower risk leukemias for whom treatment de‐intensification may be appropriate. While the benefit of uniform cytomolecular genetic analyses for the purpose of clinical trial risk assignment or selection of appropriate standard‐of‐care therapies is clear, the clinical impact of implementation of comprehensive next‐generation sequencing (NGS) platform analyses of pediatric leukemias at an institutional level is less well described. Recent studies evaluating the utility of NGS panels have reported clinically impactful results ranging from 66% to 89%. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 However, such studies were conducted in relatively small cohorts, included adult patients, or examined multiple cancer types, and most have not included genome‐wide copy number analysis. A persistent knowledge gap remains regarding the utility of comprehensive NGS analyses of pediatric leukemia cases as a clinical standard‐of‐care outside of the clinical trial setting.

Our clinical genomic diagnostics laboratory has developed, validated, implemented, and evolved over time comprehensive pediatric cancer‐customized targeted DNA and RNA‐based NGS panels to detect sequence variants including single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions (indels), internal tandem duplications (ITDs), copy number variants (CNVs), and oncogenic fusions. 1 , 7 This testing is routinely performed in conjunction with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and chromosome analyses for all patients with newly diagnosed or relapsed leukemia treated at our institution. 8 Since 2020, our standard of care is also to perform paired tumor/germline NGS at the time of any new leukemia diagnosis (and for referred relapsed patients without prior germline testing), 9 as well as to repeat somatic testing at relapse. DNA isolation was performed directly from skin biopsy samples for sequencing and interpretation in tandem with the leukemia sample for prompt reporting. Herein, we report the genomic spectrum of one of the largest cohorts of pediatric and AYA acute leukemia cases via comprehensive institutional NGS testing from our large tertiary care center, as well as the clinical utility of these results for informing prognosis and therapeutic decision making.

METHODS

Data source

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP; IRB#17‐014759). NGS data were abstracted from the CHOP Diagnostic Genomics Database for patients with B‐ALL, T‐cell ALL (T‐ALL)/T‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (T‐LBL), AML, or acute leukemia of ambiguous lineage (ALAL), including acute undifferentiated leukemia (AUL) and mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL), 10 who underwent custom‐designed NGS panel testing between June 2016 and June 2024 (Supporting Information S2: Table S1). FISH, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array, and chromosome analysis were also abstracted if available. Constitutional trisomy 21 (Down syndrome)‐associated leukemia was included. The cohort includes patients who received clinical care at CHOP and patients treated at other institutions for whom leukemia specimens were sent to CHOP for genomic testing. Specimens containing <10% blasts were excluded due to potential false negative testing results (n = 22).

Next‐generation sequencing, variant classification, and clinical impact determination

NGS analysis included a DNA‐based pediatric hematologic cancer panel (HEMEP; 118 genes) and an RNA‐based fusion panel (FUSIP; 117 genes) (Supporting Information S3: Table S2) developed by CHOP investigators. 1 All CHOP patients received molecular (HEMEP + FUSIP) and cytogenetic profiling upfront as standard of care with transcriptome sequencing performed if clinically indicated. Referral patients received molecular and cytogenetic profiling as clinically indicated. Testing and interpretation were performed prospectively at the time of new diagnosis or relapse. The DNA‐based panel includes TPMT and NUDT15 genes with specific polymorphisms associated with increased risk of thiopurine toxicities. 11 , 12 In a subset of samples, transcriptome sequencing was performed following a negative FUSIP. Variants were classified according to the Tier system outlined by the Association for Molecular Pathology/American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Physicians (AMP/ASCO/CAP) guidelines. 13 Variant allele fraction (VAF) cutoff for clinical report was 5% for SNV/indels. Constitutional (germline) variants were classified based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. 14 Pharmacogenomic haplotypes were classified based on the star allele guidelines for TPMT and NUDT15, with *I denoting wild type and other designations (e.g., allele *2 or *3) defining haplotypes with one or more variants. 11 , 15 Details on variant calling were previously published. 1 For B‐ALL and AML, molecular alterations classified as prognostic were based on International Consensus Classification (ICC) and European Leukemia Network (ELN) guidelines (Supporting Information S4: Table S3). 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Several prognostic molecular alterations are used in current risk stratification on Children's Oncology Group (COG) clinical trials 22 , 23 and as our local standard of care. Other molecular alterations are not yet used in risk stratification but have been reported in association with unfavorable or favorable outcomes and thus may inform the medical team's assessment and prognostic discussions with patients and families. Such alterations were considered prognostic for the purposes of our study. Assignment of clinical relevance and therapeutic potential was performed independently by two pediatric hematologist–oncologists with expertise in childhood leukemia. Potentially targetable genetic alterations with drugs available commercially (sometimes off‐label in pediatrics) or via clinical trials were established a priori as having therapeutic potential (Supporting Information S4: Table S4).

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics were compared by leukemia subtypes using χ 2 tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables. Student's t‐test was used for continuous variables, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for comparison of continuous probability distribution. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.0.3). A two‐sided P‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Pediatric patient characteristics

The study cohort included 1442 leukemia specimens (diagnosis n = 1014, relapse n = 428) from 1322 individual patients (diagnosis n = 1008, relapse n = 373). Fifty‐nine patients had paired primary/relapse specimens; six patients had multiple primary leukemia samples (Figure 1A and Supporting Information S1: Figure S1A). Sample types included bone marrow (n = 1166), peripheral blood (n = 161), fresh/frozen (n = 33), or formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue (n = 33) from various tissue sources, cell pellets from bone marrow or peripheral blood (n = 30), extracted nucleic acids (n = 11), pleural fluid (n = 5), and cerebrospinal fluid (n = 3) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1B). Germline samples were almost always obtained from skin biopsies performed at the time of bone marrow aspirate/biopsy. Analyzed leukemias included 959 (66.3%) specimens from 906 patients with B‐ALL, 323 (22.3%) specimens from 268 patients with AML, 141 (10.0%) specimens from 135 patients with T‐ALL/T‐LBL (n = 110/n = 25), and 19 (1.3%) specimens from 19 patients with ALAL (Figure 1B). The median age at acute leukemia diagnosis was 9.0 years (range 0–32.5 years; Table 1 and Figure 1C). Patients with T‐ALL/T‐LBL were diagnosed at an older age (median = 11.8) compared to those with B‐ALL (median = 8.0; Student's t‐test P‐value < 0.001), AML (median = 10.1; Student's t‐test P‐value = 0.026), and ALAL (median = 9.2). The cohort was 53.6% male (n = 708/1322) with a male predominance in T‐ALL/T‐LBL (n = 99/135, 73.4%, chi‐squared P‐value < 0.001) 25 (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1C). Electronic medical record reported race and ethnicity information was available for 54.9% of cases. The majority were White (n = 429, 59.1%) followed by Other/Multiple Races (n = 155, 21.3%), Black or African American (n = 94, 12.9%), and Asian (n = 48, 6.6%). Overall, 13.5% reported Hispanic ethnicity (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1D).

Comprehensive NGS DNA and RNA‐fusion panel testing was performed on 89.7% (1293/1442) of leukemia specimens. Remaining specimens underwent DNA‐only (5.5%, 80/1442; HEMEP) or RNA‐only (4.8%, 69/1442; FUSIP) profiling (Figure 1D) due to prior RNA or DNA‐based testing, low DNA/RNA yield or quality, or when specifically looking for development of a resistance mutation. A subset of patients with DNA testing (214/1256, 17.0%) also had concurrent testing of a normal germline specimen (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1E) given our laboratory's initiation of routine paired leukemia/germline testing in 2020.

Molecular results

NGS analysis identified clinically significant Tier 1 or Tier 2 SNVs/indels, CNVs, and/or fusions in 97.4% (1404/1442) of leukemia specimens (97.4%; 1287/1322 patients) with the highest yield in ALAL (100%, 19/19) followed by AML (99.4%, 321/323), T‐ALL/LBL (99.3%, 140/141), and B‐ALL (96.4%, 924/959; Supporting Information S1: Figure S1F/G). Specimens with comprehensive DNA/RNA testing had the highest yield of 99.0% (1280/1293), with a similar yield for DNA‐only (98.8%, 79/80) and lower for RNA‐only (65.2%, 45/69; Supporting Information S1: Figure S1H).

In total, 1403 unique SNVs/indels were identified across 98 genes associated with hematologic malignancies (Supporting Information S5: Table S5). Most frequently mutated genes were NRAS, KRAS, FLT3, NOTCH1, TP53, CREBBP, PTPN11, WT1, and JAK2 (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2A). Common activating SNVs included missense variants in KRAS, NRAS, JAK2, and CREBBP, whereas inactivating variants in TP53, PTEN, and WT1 were detected throughout the genes. Various in‐frame indels were also observed in FLT3 and NOTCH1. Genes with differential frequency of SNVs/indels by disease included increased frequency of NRAS, KRAS, CREBBP, and PAX5 alterations in B‐ALL; NOTCH1, PTEN, FBXW7, and PHF6 in T‐ALL; and FLT3, CEBPA, RUNX1, NPM1, and ASXL1 in AML (chi‐squared P‐values < 0.01; Figure 1E).

A total of 4909 CNVs were detected across all 22 autosomes and the X chromosome (Supporting Information S6: Table S6). T‐ALL/LBL samples were found to have the highest frequency of CNVs (124/141, 87.9%), followed by B‐ALL (801/959, 83.5%), ALAL (12/19, 63.2%), and AML (178/323, 55.1%; Supporting Information S1: Figure S2B). Whole chromosome gains and losses were frequent in B‐ALL (high hyperdiploid/hypodiploid) and AML (monosomy 7/trisomy 8), whereas segmental chromosomal alterations were more common in T‐ALL/LBL (Figure 1F). Constitutional trisomy 21 was identified in 36/907 (4.0%) and 29/268 (10.9%) of B‐ALL and AML patients, respectively (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2C).

In total 114 unique fusions were identified in 632 samples with the highest prevalence in ALAL (73.7%, 14/19), followed by AML (50.5%, 163/323), B‐ALL (42.6%, 403/959), and T‐ALL (36.9%, 52/141) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2D/E and S3; Supporting Information S7: Table S7). Rearrangements of KMT2A with various fusion partners were identified in all leukemia subtypes with a higher predominance of AFF1 fusion in B‐ALL and MLLT3 and MLLT10 fusions in AML (Figure 1G). Alterations exclusive to specific leukemia subtypes included ETV6::RUNX1, P2RY8::CRLF2, and TCF3::PBX1 fusions and PAX5 rearrangements in B‐ALL; STIL::TAL1 fusion and NOTCH1 rearrangements in T‐ALL/T‐LBL; and RUNX1::RUNX1T1, PML::RARA, CBFB::MYB11, CBFA2T3::GLIS2 fusions, and MECOM rearrangements in AML.

Whole transcriptome sequencing (RNA‐seq) was performed for 11 cases with negative initial FUSIP results. Leukemia‐associated fusions were detected in four cases (36.3%), high expression of diagnostically relevant genes in two cases (18.2%), and no additional findings in five cases (45.5%; Supporting Information S8: Table S8).

Leukemia cases without initially identified diagnostic oncogenic alterations

Thirty‐nine cases were negative for Tier 1 and Tier 2 alterations. Twenty‐four cases (61.5%) were from outside institutions with FUSIP‐only profiling. Amongst the other 15 cases, 93.3% (n = 14) underwent HEMEP/FUSIP testing and 6.7% (n = 1) received HEMEP only. Additional in‐house or outside testing by FISH, karyotype, SNP microarray, and/or transcriptome sequencing was performed for 16 cases. Leukemia‐associated alterations were identified in 50% (8/16) of cases (Supporting Information S9: Table S9), including three cases of IGH rearrangements. Taken together, we identified leukemia‐associated genetic alterations in 97.9% (1412/1442) of cases.

Germline pathogenic variants

Confirmed germline or suspected germline alterations associated with cancer predisposition were identified in 13.8% (182/1322) of assessed patients across 42 genes or chromosomal alterations (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4 and Supporting Information S10: Table S10). Among genomically identified germline alterations, the diagnosis was previously known in 36.2% (66/182) of patients, including constitutional trisomy 21 and one germline ELANE variant. For patients without a prior germline diagnosis, confirmed germline variants were identified in 6.6% (12/182) of patients through a combination of paired tumor/normal analysis (n = 8) or targeted gene testing (n = 4). Overall, germline predisposition was identified in 5.9% (78/1322) with a further 6.8% (90/1322) recommended for follow‐up germline testing and genetic counseling (Figure 1H). One patient harbored a mosaic constitutional variant in TP53 that was first identified at 39% VAF in the leukemic bone marrow (~30% blasts) and at 22% VAF in a skin biopsy sample, as well as 39% VAF in a bone marrow sample post‐induction therapy (<5% blasts).

B‐acute lymphoblastic leukemia

High hyperdiploidy (51–65 chromosomes) was common (n = 212/959, 22.1%), whereas low hypodiploidy or near haploid (≤39 chromosomes) was rare (n = 28/959, 2.9%). SNVs/indels were frequently observed in genes associated with the RAS/MAPK pathway (KRAS, 18.4%; NRAS, 18.5%; FLT3, 8%), epigenetic regulation (CREBBP, 8.3%), transcription factors (PAX5, 6.5%), and DNA damage (TP53, 6.8%; Figure 2A). Genetic rearrangements were detected in 44.8% of patients; the most common was ETV6::RUNX1 (14.6%), followed by P2RY8::CRLF2 (8.4%), KMT2A rearrangement with various partners (5.4%), and TCF3::PBX1 (4.4%). DUX4 rearrangements may be underreported as DUX4 is not included on the fusion panel and is notoriously challenging to detect by routine clinical sequencing methods. In leukemias that harbored P2RY8::CRLF2 fusions, 24.5% (20/81) were subclonal and co‐occurred with iAMP21 (n = 7), ETV6::RUNX1 (n = 6), PAX5 rearrangements (n = 5), and high hyperdiploidy (n = 2). The strongest co‐occurrences included TP53 variants with a low hypodiploid genome; KRAS, NRAS, PTPN11, or CREBBP variants with a high hyperdiploid genome; and JAK1/2/3 SNVs/indels with CRLF2 rearrangements (Figure 2E).

T‐acute lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoblastic lymphoma

The most frequent SNVs/indels were observed in genes associated with the NOTCH pathway (NOTCH1, 55.3%; FBXW7, 16.3%), PI3K pathway (PTEN, 17.0%), and epigenetic regulation (PHF6, 19.9%). Whole chromosomal gains/losses were infrequent in T‐ALL. Partial losses of chromosome 9 involving CDKN2A/B occurred in 68.8% (97/141) of cases, followed by loss of whole or partial chromosome 13, including RB1 in 10.6% (15/141; Figure 2B). Gene rearrangements were detected in 39.7% (56/141) of cases, most commonly STIL::TAL1 fusion (14.2%), NUP214::ABL1 fusion (8.5%), NOTCH1 rearrangements (7.1%), and KMT2A rearrangements (4.3%). Frequent mutational co‐occurrences included JAK1 and JAK3 SNVs/indels, NRAS SNVs/indels, NUP214 rearrangements with WT1 SNVs/indels, trisomy 8 with KMT2A rearrangements, and CCND3 SNVs/indels with PICALM::MLLT10 fusion (Figure 2F). Several specific alterations were identified exclusively in T‐ALL and not in T‐LBL, including WT1, NRAS, and JAK1 SNVs/indels, loss of chromosome 1p, and PICALM::MLLT10 fusions (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5).

Acute myeloid leukemia

The most frequent SNVs/indels were associated with the RAS/MAPK pathway (NRAS, 13.3%; KRAS, 9.3%), transcription factors (RUNX1, 7.1%), PI3K pathway (FLT3, 13.0%), Wnt pathway (WT1, 10.2%), and DNA damage (TP53, 7.1%; Figure 2C). Whole chromosome gain/loss was observed in 34.1% (110/323) of cases, most frequently trisomy 21 (11.8%; 9.0% constitutional, 2.8% somatic), trisomy 8 (9.3%), and/or monosomy 7 (7.1%). Gene rearrangements were detected in 50.7% of AML cases; the most common included KMT2A rearrangements (22.6%), RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion (6.5%), and NUP98 rearrangements (5.3%). As expected, GATA1 SNVs/indels were detected in all ML‐DS cases. 26 Other co‐occurrences included combination of TP53, ASXL1, and IKZF1 SNVs/indels and trisomy 8, RUNX1 SNVs/indels with monosomy 7, and NPM1 with SETD2 SNVs/indels (Figure 2G).

Acute leukemia of ambiguous lineage

Within the small ALAL cohort (n = 19), SNVs included those observed in both AML and T‐ALL, such as WT1 (n = 7, 36.8%) and FLT3 (n = 4, 21.1%) (Figure 2D). Gene rearrangements included rearrangement of KMT2A (n = 6), ETS transcription factors (n = 3), TCF3 (n = 2), and SEC. 16A::NOTCH1, PICALM::MLLT10, NUP214::ABL1, PAX5::KIAA1217, and HSH2D::IL2RB fusions (n = 1 each).

Cohort‐level comparison of genetic alterations at leukemia diagnosis versus relapse

We compared the mutational landscape of diagnosis and relapse cases within the B‐ALL (690 primary/269 relapse), T‐ALL (112 primary/29 relapse), and AML (197 primary/126 relapse) cohorts (Figure 3A–C). Relapsed B‐ALL cases had a higher prevalence of TP53, NF1, KDM6A, and CREBBP SNVs/indels, KMT2A rearrangements, hypodiploid genomes, loss of CDKN2A/B, and IKZF1 deletion (including IKZF1+ subtype). 27 In contrast, KRAS and PTPN11 SNVs/indels, high hyperdiploid genomes, and ETV6::RUNX1 fusions were enriched in the diagnostic cohort (Figure 3A). In T‐ALL, higher prevalence of NRAS SNVs/indels and lower prevalence of NOTCH1, JAK1, ETV6, CTCF, CREBBP SNVs/indels, and PICALM::MLLT10 fusions were observed in relapsed as compared with diagnostic cases (Figure 3B). Lastly, SNVs/indels in RUNX1, TP53, SETD2, WT1, and ASXL1, trisomy 8, and monosomy 7 were more prevalent in relapsed AML cases, whereas trisomy 21 and GATA1 SNVs/indels were enriched in diagnostic specimens (Figure 3C).

We also compared the frequency of variants across biological sex and observed increased frequency of JAK1/2, NSD2, and KDM6A SNVs/indels in males with B‐ALL, increased frequency of FBXW7 SNVs/indels in females with T‐ALL, and increased frequency of GATA1 and ETV6 SNVs/indels in males with AML (Supporting Information S1: Figure S6A–C).

Patients with paired primary and relapse NGS testing

Our cohort contained 63 patients with matched primary and relapse samples, including 26 B‐ALL, 25 AML, 6 T‐ALL/LBL, and 6 with discordant immunophenotypes (Figure 3D and Supporting Information S1: Figure S7). Somatic driver alterations were retained between diagnosis and relapse in most patients (n = 54). Additional somatic events at relapse were detected in some sample pairs due to clonal evolution. In three patients with AML, molecular alterations identified at initial diagnosis were discordant with relapse suggesting independent primaries (Figure 3E). 28 , 29 Amongst primary‐relapse pairs, six patients had B‐ALL at diagnosis and AML at relapse. Based on molecular findings, two cases were determined to be myeloid neoplasm post cytotoxic chemotherapy, three cases ALL‐to‐AML lineage switch, and one second primary AML (Figure 3F).

Prognostic implications of leukemia‐associated alterations

In addition to aiding diagnosis, Tier 1 and/or Tier 2 alterations may also guide prognostic assessment (Supporting Information S11: Table S11). Using best‐available clinical outcomes data and/or current clinical trial risk stratification algorithms, favorable B‐ALL alterations observed in this cohort included NUTM1 rearrangements, high hyperdiploid genomes, and ETV6::RUNX1 fusions. Intermediate alterations included TCF3::PBX1, ZNF384 rearrangements, and PAX5 P80R. Unfavorable alterations included KMT2A rearrangements in infancy, Philadelphia chromosome‐like (Ph‐like) fusions, hypodiploid genomes, and iAMP21 (Figure 4D). 23 Favorable alterations in AML included RUNX1::RUNXT1 and CBFB::MYH11 fusions, and NPM1 and biallelic CEBPA mutations. Unfavorable alterations in AML included high‐risk KMT2A rearrangements (partnered with AFDN, AFF1, MLLT1, MLLT10, MLLT4, MLLT6, and SEPT9), NUP98 rearrangements, FLT3 ITD (with an allelic ratio > 0.1) in the absence of NPM1 mutation, TP53 mutations, and ETV6 rearrangements and 12p deletion (Figure 4E). 22

Prognostic alterations were identified in 64.2% (616/959) of B‐ALL and 67.7% (195/288) of AML, after excluding cases of APL (n = 11) and ML‐DS (n = 24; Figure 4A and Supporting Information S1: Figure S8A). In B‐ALL, prognostic findings were stratified as favorable (344/616, 55.8%), intermediate (109/616, 17.7%), and unfavorable (163/616, 26.5%), with favorable alterations occurring in younger (median = 4.9) patients compared to intermediate (median = 8.3) and unfavorable (median = 13.5) patients (Student's t‐test, P < 0.001). Prognostic findings in AML were stratified into favorable (58/195, 29.7%) and unfavorable (137/195, 70.3%) findings with patients with favorable alterations having a median age of 12.3 years and unfavorable alterations having a bimodal age distribution (Kolomogirov–Smirnov test P‐value < 0.001; Figure 4B/C).

Leukemia cases without initially identified prognostic molecular alterations

Next, we investigated the cases that did not yield prognostic biomarkers from NGS testing (AML = 91; B‐ALL = 342; Supporting Information S12: Table S12). In B‐ALL, 21.3% (73/342) of samples had alterations identified by NGS not typically used in current clinical risk stratification due to patient age, such as KMT2A rearrangements in non‐infant pediatric patients and CRLF2 rearrangements in children with standard risk B‐ALL. Additional FISH (n = 205), karyotype (n = 181), and/or whole genome microarray (n = 81) data were available for 63.9% (53/83) of AML and 50.6% of B‐ALL (173/342) cases (Supporting Information S1: Figure S8B).

In B‐ALL, cytogenetic testing yielded additional findings in 23.7% (41/173) of cases with 29 considered prognostic (n = 10 favorable, n = 3 intermediate, and n = 16 unfavorable; Figure 4F/G). Favorable alterations included ETV6::RUNX1 fusion (n = 2), IGH::DUX4 fusion (n = 1), and high hyperdiploid genome (n = 7). Intermediate alterations included IGH::PAX5 fusion (n = 1), ETV6 rearrangement (n = 1), and TCF3::PBX1 fusion (n = 1). Unfavorable alterations included Ph‐like kinase gene fusions (n = 10), hypodiploid/near haploid genome (n = 2), iAMP21 (n = 1), MYC rearrangement (n = 1), KMT2A rearrangement in an infant (n = 1), and BCR::ABL1 fusion (n = 1).

In AML, 32.1% (17/53) of cases yielded additional findings that were useful for risk stratification, resulting in 2 favorable and 15 unfavorable classifications (Figure 4F/G). Favorable findings included RARA rearrangements (n = 2), while unfavorable findings included complex karyotype (n = 6), chromosomal translocations suggestive of KAT6A::CREBBP fusion (n = 5), ETV6 (n = 1) or MECOM rearrangements (n = 1), monosomy 7 (n = 1), and loss of chromosome 17p encompassing TP53 (n = 1).

Findings were not detected by NGS testing due to a lack of comprehensive testing in 13.8% (8/58: no RNA sample, n = 6; no DNA sample, n = 2) or low blast percentage in 20.7% (12/58) of cases. In 34.5% (20/58) of cases, chromosome analysis identified structural rearrangements suggestive of a fusion, where RNA‐fusion profiling did not identify a fusion transcript (24.2%, 14/58) or was part of complex karyotypes (10.3%, 6/58). For example, BCR::ABL1 rearrangement was not detected on fusion panel testing for one patient with a t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) translocation identified by karyotype. Lastly, 31.0% (18/58) cases had rearrangements detected by FISH involving genes known to be challenging to profile by NGS, such as IGH (n = 16) and MECOM (n = 2; Supporting Information S1: Figure S8C). Non‐NGS based testing most frequently yielding molecular findings was karyotype and FISH (Figure 4H). Additional cytogenetic analyses yielded results useful for risk stratification in 3.7% (46/1247) of samples (29/959 B‐ALL and 17/288 AML).

Findings relevant to precision medicine therapies

NGS testing was able to identify targetable alterations in 325 patients, 23.1% (209/906) of B‐ALL and 50.9% (116/228) of AML patients (Figure 5A). Relevant alterations included KMT2A and NUP98 rearrangements and NPM1 mutations (targetable by menin inhibitors); BCR::ABL1, ABL class Ph‐like fusions (tyrosine kinase inhibitors); JAK2 and EPOR rearrangements, P2RY8::CRLF2 fusions, IGH::CRLF2 fusions, and JAK2 and JAK1 SNV/indels (JAK inhibitors), and FLT3‐ITD and ZNF384 rearrangements (FLT3 inhibitors) (Figure 5B/C). Clinical treatment data was available for 52.3% (170/325: 99/209 B‐ALL, 71/116 AML) of patients of whom 16.5% (28/170) were treated with a targeted therapy (SRC/ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor n = 15, JAK/STAT inhibitor n = 11, menin inhibitor n = 7, FLT3 inhibitor n = 5, and IDH2 inhibitor n = 1; Figure 5D). Patients with BCR::ABL1 fusion (Ph+ ALL; n = 11) were the only subset of patients who routinely received targeted therapy given clinical indications and drug approvals.

Lastly, our NGS panel includes genes involved in thiopurine metabolism, which are routinely assessed in children with ALL/LBL to inform potential dose modifications of mercaptopurine and thioguanine. 11 We identified genotypes associated with reduced thiopurine metabolism in 5.3% (66/1256) for NUDT15 and 7.1% (89/1256) for TPMT (Figure 5E/F and Supporting Information S13: Table S13). For patients with these genotypes, dose adjustments are made to chemotherapy according to COG protocols.

DISCUSSION

Herein, we present detailed molecular findings of 1442 diagnostic or relapsed acute leukemia specimens from 1322 pediatric patients analyzed by NGS and adjunctive cytogenetic testing at our institution between 2016 and 2024. We demonstrate the utility of our comprehensive DNA‐based mutation/copy number alteration and RNA‐based fusion panels, which together detected clinically significant variants in 99% of specimens. The few cases in which a clinically significant alteration was not identified were largely sent from outside institutions with incomplete testing requested (primarily fusion‐only). Conventional cytogenetic, FISH, and/or subsequent transcriptome sequencing testing was able to identify an oncogenic driver in nearly all these cases.

Diagnostic molecular alterations in B‐ALL and AML in conjunction with early disease response after initial blocks of chemotherapy are used to identify low‐risk patients, who can be cured with less intensive treatment, and high‐risk patients, who currently require chemotherapy intensification or hematopoietic stem cell transplant for cure. Overall, 65% of patients with B‐ALL and 69% of patients with AML harbored a prognostic molecular alteration, underscoring the clinical utility of NGS testing. Presently, molecular markers are not used for risk stratification in T‐ALL or T‐LBL, but data from recent studies may change this approach in the near future. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33

A subset of patients in this study received molecular testing for paired diagnostic and relapse samples, which was critical in understanding leukemia mutational evolution within the same disease, therapy‐associated leukemia, and/or occurrence of a second primary leukemia.

Importantly, 28.7% of patients (325/1134) with acute leukemias harbored molecular alterations with potential drug targets. However, only 15% (28/183) of patients with available clinical data received a targeted therapy, which is primarily due to a lack of access or lack of regulatory approval of such drugs in pediatrics. An exception includes tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib or dasatinib) approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for frontline therapy of children and adolescents with BCR::ABL1‐rearranged ALL. Given potential for off‐label use in children of some FDA‐approved drugs for adults, targeted therapies have been variably used outside of clinical trials by pediatric hematologist–oncologists in the relapsed leukemia setting and/or as post‐transplant maintenance therapy (e.g., FLT3 inhibitors, BCL‐2 inhibitors, and menin inhibitors).

While our comprehensive DNA/RNA‐based NGS panel was able to detect driver alterations in nearly all studied cases, a known limitation of our current RNA‐based fusion panel is that enhancer and promoter co‐opting fusions that do not lead to chimeric transcripts can be challenging to detect. Thus, we continue to perform NGS testing in conjunction with multi‐modal FISH and SNP array as standard of care with karyotype performed for a small number of cases when clinically indicated. 8 Our current fusion panel captures over 700 known fusions and is updated periodically based upon evolving knowledge in the field and ongoing dialogue amongst cancer genomics and oncology experts at our institution.

Our collective experience demonstrates that an integrated comprehensive genomic profiling for pediatric acute leukemia enhances nuanced diagnosis, prognostication, and targeted therapy. As sequencing technologies and analysis software further develop, comprehensive molecular profiling of pediatric acute leukemias may incorporate long‐read sequencing, transcriptome sequencing, and methylation analysis. Long‐read sequencing has been shown to lower turnaround times and robustly detect enhancer and promoter co‐opting fusions. 34 , 35 , 36 Whole transcriptome and methylation analysis may aid in risk classification and biomarker identification and is now more rapid and cost‐effective. 37 , 38 , 39 In summary, comprehensive NGS analysis provides high yield for the identification of diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic biomarkers in pediatric leukemias, which have revolutionized current standard practice. Data from the present study demonstrate that integrated comprehensive genomic profiling should be offered to all children with acute leukemias at diagnosis and relapse to guide precision‐based cancer care.
