Insights from young doctors: why climate change matters to paediatricians and what actions we can take
Authors: James Patrick Finlay, Helena Clements, Erva Nur Cinar
Published: 2025

Background

Children bear 90% of the primary disease burden caused by climate change. Over 500 million live in areas vulnerable to extreme weather events. Without mitigation strategies, 131 000 children could die each year from climate-related illnesses by 2030.1

Children bear the greatest burden from a crisis they did not create. By 2050, 8 times more children could be subjected to extreme heat than those that are currently being impacted. Climate change risks manifest differently across the life course of children. Rising temperatures are linked to premature births. From infancy to adolescence, climate change risks vector-borne diseases expanding their reach, children going hungry as food systems destabilise and worsening of diarrhoeal and respiratory morbidity. The mental health toll is no less acute. Children struggle to sleep during heatwaves and adolescents face growing eco-anxiety—grief, guilt and helplessness as they confront a threat they feel powerless to change.2

Role of paediatricians and practical recommendations for all clinicians

Healthcare contributes to 4%–5% of global greenhouse gas production.3 Healthcare professionals are uniquely positioned to drive meaningful change against climate change and paediatricians have a moral duty to advocate for children and their health.1 Our duty extends beyond diagnosis—to bear witness, to advocate and to act. We must become stewards of the future, not only healers of the present.

Daily, we see the growing impact of climate change on infants, children and young peoples’ bodies and minds. From asthma exacerbated by air pollution to eco-anxiety, climate change has become a critical paediatric problem.

We have a role in improving awareness, educating on eco-anxiety and advocating for young people to promote environmental sustainability. Our training doesn't always prepare us to respond, and we face real barriers to engagement.

Time constraints are the most frequently cited challenge, followed by a lack of knowledge. Nearly one in three believe their involvement in climate advocacy would make no meaningful difference. Some worry speaking out could pose personal or professional risks. These insights reflect more than individual hesitancy but a culture in healthcare that too often sidelines advocacy, particularly with political or systemic issues. Our delay to action risks the lives of today’s children and future generations, creating substantial intergenerational health inequity.1

Clinical actions: integrating climate change into paediatric practice

Child health professionals can integrate climate change into practice through environmental history taking, screening for climate-related conditions and recognising the importance of social determinants of health and climate-related illness.

In terms of treatment, paediatricians can offer climate-focused health counselling particularly for asthma, allergies and neurodevelopment. This includes encouraging families to check air quality reports and minimising exposure during periods of high pollution. Hospital visits offer the opportunity to counsel families on extreme heat safety, clean air, healthy homes and sustainable healthy diets. However, food and fuel poverty must be considered particularly in those facing health inequalities.

Climate change worsens childhood malnutrition globally. We should advise on food security and encourage breastfeeding and sustainable nutrition.

Climate anxiety can be addressed through age-appropriate conversations and participation in climate-positive actions such as offering children and adolescents platforms to advocate for sustainability.

Sustainability can be integrated into prescribing by considering dry powder inhalers as opposed to metered-dose inhalers with hydrofluorocarbon propellants when clinically appropriate and teaching children to take tablets rather than liquid medicine.4

The global challenges of climate change and antibiotic resistance are interlinked. Rising global temperatures hasten antibiotic resistance. An increase in infectious disease vectors and reservoirs can increase antibiotic use.5 Meanwhile, excess antimicrobial prescribing leads to undeniable waste and increased carbon footprint. A study showed the impact of estimated annual unnecessary outpatient antibiotic prescriptions in the USA contributed the same emissions as driving a petrol-fuelled car around Earth 194.3 times.6 Even when antibiotics are clinically indicated, how we prescribe them matters. Choosing oral over intravenous formulations when appropriate reduces environmental impact while supporting antibiotic stewardship.5

Institutional actions: making healthcare more sustainable

Paediatricians may propagate sustainable practice in local hospitals and networks in various ways. Examples include advocating for the adoption of a net-zero strategy. Telemedicine reduces unnecessary travel emissions and was widely implemented following the COVID-19 pandemic. Improving waste segregation and reducing energy consumption and single-use items in neonatal, imaging departments and operating theatres can assist decarbonisation of healthcare.3

Sustainability poses particular challenges in neonatology and paediatric critical care but presents opportunities to make significant improvements. Examples include promoting breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact and natural lighting and reducing single-use equipment across all neonatal and paediatric units.

Organisations such as Health Care Without Harm and My Green Doctor provide practical ways to integrate climate awareness and education into medical facilities.7 8

Policy-level actions: advocacy for systemic change

At a systemic level, we should advocate for climate policy through organisations such as Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH), American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and WHO.

In 2020, the RCPCH declared a climate emergency and formed a climate change working group. ‘Preserving the World for Future Generations Report’ was commissioned, which reiterated 94400 children and young peoples’ voices. RCPCH strives to become net zero by 2040. They produced climate change resources for educating families and climate advocacy.9

We can advocate for clean air laws, investment in low-carbon healthcare solutions and research on climate change impacts on child health.

The climate vulnerability index is used to classify the risk of climate change to health; however, this lacks specificity regarding children and adolescents. Research to quantify climate-related risks across different stages of childhood and adolescence is urgently needed to inform evidence-based policy and interventions.

Outside the health sector, child health professionals can work alongside educators, urban planners and environmental scientists to create infant, child and youth-friendly climate policies. We should support climate-resilient school policies (tree planting for shade, reducing school exposure to extreme heat).

We recognise rotational training contributes to the NHS’s carbon footprint, training deaneries should be encouraged to address the commuting challenges to reduce healthcare-related emissions.

Individual actions

Paediatricians can take individual actions to reduce their carbon footprint in their personal life by using sustainable transport, reducing waste, banking sustainably and conserving household energy. Plant-based diets can reduce carbon emissions while improving health.

Professionally, they can incorporate environmental sustainability by reducing unnecessary prescription for inhalers with high carbon footprint, unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions and utilising online meetings.3 5 6

Introducing the conversation with colleagues can prompt change in clinical environments.

Engaging with climate change education and advocacy groups, such as RCPCH Climate Change Working Group and AAP Section on Climate Change and Health, allows a larger impact.

‘We might fail, but to not try is unforgivable’.10
