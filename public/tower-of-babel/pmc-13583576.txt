Impact on Ice: A Case of Left Main Dissection from a Hockey Check
Authors: Ryan T. Sless, Rushil Dave, Alan Barolet, Adriana C. Luk
Published: 2026

A 30-year-old male patient presented with retrosternal chest pain after receiving a check during a recreational hockey game. An electrocardiogram (ECG) was suggestive of an ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and he suffered from a ventricular fibrillation arrest. Catheterization demonstrated occlusion of his left anterior descending (LAD) artery, secondary to left main (LM) coronary dissection. He developed progressive shock and went for emergent coronary artery bypass grafting. Ten cases of traumatic LM dissection are presented in the literature, although none occurred during a sporting event. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a wide differential in young patients with potential traumatic injury to the chest wall when they present to the emergency department (ED) with chest pain.

History of Presentation

A 30-year-old male patient presented to the ED with a history of persistent chest pain after receiving a check to the chest from the butt end of a hockey stick during a recreational game of hockey. The initial chest pain was 9 of 10 and was associated with diaphoresis and nausea, but with rest on the bench, it settled slightly. Over the next 2-3 hours, the chest pain persisted, radiating to his jaw and shoulder, and the patient decided to present to the ED. He denied alcohol use, recreational drug use, and cocaine use on the day. His blood pressure was 125/78 mm Hg, he was saturating well on room air, and his heart rate was 80 beats per minute and regular. Upon auscultation, S1 and S2 were normal, with no S3, S4, or murmurs. His jugular venous pressure was 2 cm above the sternal angle, and he had no peripheral edema. Auscultation of the chest revealed normal breath sounds without obvious wheeze or crackles. His peripheral pulses were present and intact. An electrocardiogram performed in the ED revealed significant ST elevation in the anterolateral leads.

The patient subsequently suffered from a cardiac arrest due to ventricular fibrillation requiring 2 shocks, 2 rounds of cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and 1 mg epinephrine. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved, and a code STEMI was activated. The patient was transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit while the catheter laboratory team was arriving, and an echocardiogram demonstrated severe left ventricular dysfunction with an ejection fraction of ∼25%, severe hypokinesis of the apical, anterior, and anterolateral walls, and no obvious valvular abnormalities. No aortic dissection flap was seen, and no colour Doppler was suggestive of a ventricular septal defect. The patient was given 160 mg of aspirin. A second antiplatelet and heparin were withheld, given concern for potential aortic dissection and need for urgent cardiac surgery.

Past Medical History and Medications

The patient’s past medical history was unremarkable, and he was on no medications.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included STEMI secondary to an acute coronary occlusion, coronary artery dissection, or coronary vasospasm vs noncoronary causes, such as aortic dissection, myopericarditis, cardiac contusion and/or trauma, or noncardiac causes with benign early repolarization.

Investigations

Laboratory investigations included an initial troponin T level of 20 ng/L (normal < 26 ng/L), a negative toxicology screen, and normal complete blood count, electrolytes, and creatinine. Post-infarct/revascularization, troponin I peaked at 144,132 ng/L. His cholesterol profile was normal, with a low-density lipoprotein level of 1.45. Management (Medical and Interventions)

The patient was then taken to the cardiac catheterization laboratory for coronary angiography. The angiogram revealed an anatomically normal right coronary artery and a large filling defect in the distal left main with complete occlusion of the LAD artery, likely secondary to a dissection (Video 1 , view video online). The circumflex and intermediate arteries were wired, but wiring the LAD was difficult, despite multiple attempts by the interventionalist. ST-segment elevation remained during the catheterization, and the patient continued to experience retrosternal chest pain. While the patient was on the catheterization laboratory table, he had deterioration in his hemodynamics, with rising lactate level suggestive of cardiogenic shock, and an intra-aortic balloon pump was inserted. He also became hypoxemic secondary to pulmonary edema and was intubated. Given the ongoing ischemia and progressive cardiogenic shock with inability to restore flow into the LAD artery, the patient went for emergency coronary artery bypass graft surgery. He was found to have an LM dissection without clot in the LAD artery. He underwent a left internal mammary artery to LAD artery graft and a saphenous vein graft to obtuse marginal artery. He required vasopressor and inotrope support intraoperatively with epinephrine and milrinone. A perioperative transesophageal echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%, with normal right ventricular systolic function and no valvopathy. Postoperatively, he was successfully weaned of inotrope support and had a relatively uncomplicated course.

Discussion: Association with Current Guidelines, Position Papers, and Current Practice

This presentation illustrates a case of LM coronary artery dissection as a result of focal blunt force trauma in a young male patient while he was playing hockey. Cardiac complications, including cardiac contusion, ventricular arrhythmias, ventricular rupture, and coronary artery dissection, although uncommon (5%-15%) can be life-threatening complications of blunt cardiac injury secondary to trauma.1 Of those, coronary dissection remains the rarest complication. When it occurs, it is more often associated with motor vehicle collision–related steering wheel injuries or motorcycle accidents.2 The LAD artery is the most commonly affected artery (76%), with right coronary artery (12%) and circumflex (6%) involvement being considerably rarer.2 Coronary dissection is thought to be secondary to the proximity of the LAD artery to the anterior chest wall, the most common site of blunt chest trauma. We believe this case is the first to be documented of LM artery dissection as a result of an accident during a sporting activity, and more specifically, from isolated focal chest trauma while playing hockey. The pathophysiology is thought to be secondary to shearing of the coronary vessel wall, causing intimal tearing,2 (2) and thus, although incredibly unlikely, an appropriately timed and placed check to the chest, when the left coronary artery is resting against the anterior chest wall, may result in this traumatic consequence.

Follow-up Evaluation

A postoperative echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 38%, a normal right ventricle size with mildly reduced function, mild mitral regurgitation, and a trivial pericardial effusion (Video 2 , view video online). He was discharged with clopidogrel, acetylsalicylic acid, atorvastatin, carvedilol, and spironolactone. He was managed with quadruple guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction at maximum-tolerated doses. Genetic testing was negative.

Conclusions

This case highlights a traumatic LM dissection in a young male patient secondary to a check delivered with a hockey stick. The chest pain history and clinical course of this patient highlights the importance of prompt recognition and assessment of all patients presenting to the emergency department with a chest pain history. Although pain in a young individual after a game of hockey, a very physical sport, might be easy to ignore, obtaining an ECG in all patients, despite their age and clinical history, is important, to ensure prompt recognition of flow-limiting ischemia and cardiac catheterization as necessary.
