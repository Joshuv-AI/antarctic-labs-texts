A combined experimental and DEM study on the mechanical and failure characteristics of fissured sandstone under complex stress conditions
Authors: Shanshan Liu, Can Xie, Rui Wang, Shaohui Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In geotechnical engineering, fissured sandstone is frequently subjected to cyclic loading-unloading under complex construction conditions. Its mechanical properties and catastrophic failure characteristics directly influence the stability and safety of engineering structures. The mechanical behavior and failure mechanism of fissured sandstone under uniaxial and biaxial cyclic loading-unloading were studied using laboratory experiments, CT scanning, and numerical simulations. First, uniaxial compression experiments on intact and fissured sandstone specimens were conducted to obtain the stress-strain responses and peak strengths, providing the basis for subsequent numerical model calibration. This was followed by uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading experiments on the fissured specimens. By combining CT scanning and numerical simulation, the fissure evolution process was revealed from a macro-meso perspective. The reliability of the numerical model was verified. Based on the validated model, biaxial cyclic loading-unloading simulations were further carried out to investigate the mechanical responses and failure characteristics of fissured sandstone under complex stress conditions. Under uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading, the peak strength and elastic modulus ascended as the fissure angle grew, while crack initiation stress a trend of first decreasing and then increasing. Moreover, the extent of rock damage increased with the fissure angle. Under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading, an increase in fissure angle does not significantly change the peak strength and elastic modulus of the specimens but aggravates the failure dominated by shear. Compared with uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading, specimens subjected to biaxial cyclic loading-unloading exhibited higher crack initiation stresses, more extensive crack propagation, and more severe damage at the same fissure angle. The findings of this study provide theoretical support for the stability assessment and failure analysis of fissured rock masses under cyclic loading-unloading conditions.

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of deep underground space exploitation and infrastructure construction, underground engineering projects such as tunnels, mining roadways, and hydropower caverns are extending progressively into greater depths, where the in-situ stress environment becomes increasingly complex. During construction processes including drill-and-blast excavation, mechanical tunneling, and stress redistribution induced by support systems, the surrounding rock mass is frequently subjected to repeated cyclic loading and unloading. The strength degradation and progressive damage accumulation induced by such loading paths are widely recognized as key factors governing the long-term stability of underground rock engineering. Therefore, investigating the mechanical behavior and failure mechanisms of fissured sandstone under cyclic loading–unloading conditions is of considerable scientific significance and also provides valuable guidance for stability assessment and disaster prevention in underground engineering.

Extensive studies have been undertaken from multiple perspectives and scales regarding the mechanical behavior and failure mechanisms of fissured rock masses. Early research mainly focused on the basic failure law of rock masses. Through uniaxial and biaxial compression experiments, the entire process mechanism of rock material failure was preliminarily revealed, which lays the experimental foundation for specimen failure mechanics1–6. In terms of the theoretical model, based on continuum damage mechanics and statistical damage theory, combined with fracture parameters and Drucker-Prager yield criterion, it provides an effective analytical framework for quantitatively describing the stiffness degradation and strength attenuation of fissured rock masses under external force. With the deepening of research, the influence of the geometric characteristics of the fissure itself has received considerable attention. Many studies have been conducted using laboratory experiments or numerical simulations. The significant control of geometric parameters, such as fissure angle, length, and density, on the strength, deformation characteristics, and final failure mode of specimens under static loading is analyzed. The transformation conditions of tensile, shear, and mixed failure modes were studied, and the progressive failure process of the fissured specimen under static loading was revealed11–24. With the application of non-destructive monitoring technologies, such as acoustic emission and digital image correlation, micro-fissure events and surface deformation fields inside rocks can be captured in real time. Thus, the localization and evolution of rock damage and the precursory characteristics of macroscopic instability are revealed more finely25–28. To explore more complex stress paths, the research method has been developed into a deep integration of experiments and numerical simulations. By constructing a numerical model that can reflect the geometric characteristics of cracks, the propagation trajectory, interaction of cracks, and overall mechanical response of specimen under different loading conditions are reproduced by researchers, and the internal relationship between stress redistribution and damage accumulation is clarified29–34. In recent years, the focus of research has extended to cyclic loading and meso-scale observations. Under cyclic loading-unloading conditions, researchers have investigated the macroscopic fatigue life and mechanical parameter evolution of sandstone and other rocks, as well as the particle-scale deformation and failure mechanism35. More importantly, three-dimensional, non-destructive, and dynamic visualization of the internal structure of rocks has been realized with the maturity and application of microscopic observation techniques such as computed tomography (CT) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Researchers can directly observe, in detail, how cracks initiate, propagate, and lead to failure; and a macro-meso correlation between rock damage evolution and macroscopic mechanical behavior degradation can be established. This has greatly promoted the study of the multi-scale failure mechanism of rock36–39.

In summary, existing research has achieved fruitful results in the static strength, damage theory, parameter influence, and observation technology of fissured specimens, which lays a solid foundation for the research in this study. However, the systematic revelation of the macro-meso failure mechanism of fissured sandstone during cyclic loading-unloading is still insufficient, especially the damage accumulation and fissure evolution law under the complex stress path of cyclic loading-unloading under confining pressure need to be further discussed. Therefore, uniaxial compression laboratory experiments of intact and fissured specimens were conducted, and the stress-strain curves and peak strengths were obtained. Offering a basis for the design of subsequent loading-unloading experiments, the results of this part also lay a foundation for the determination of meso-mechanical parameters in numerical simulations. Guided by the above experiments, uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading laboratory experiments of fissured sandstones were conducted, and CT scanning experiments and corresponding numerical simulations were conducted simultaneously. The experiment results of the three different methods jointly clarify the progressive failure mechanism of the fissured specimens under uniaxial loading-unloading conditions and verify the reliability of the numerical experiment results. Finally, to be closer to the actual stress environment of specimen engineering, cyclic loading-unloading numerical experiments of fissured sandstones under confining pressure were further conducted to explore the mechanical properties and failure behavior of rock mass under complex stress paths. The conclusions provide insights into the stability of and disaster prevention strategies for underground engineering projects.

CT scanning experiments

The inspection was conducted using a Bruker 3D-XRM Sky-Scan 2211 X-ray computed tomography (CT) system comprising an X-ray source, rock mass stage, CCD detector, and flat-panel detector, with a maximum spatial resolution of 100 nm. This instrument enables non-destructive imaging of internal mesoscopic structures in large rock masses up to 200 mm in diameter and high-resolution scanning of sub-micron features in smaller rock masses. The system can reconstruct three-dimensional (3D) models of the scanned objects, as illustrated in Fig. 5. CT imaging is based on the principle of differential X-ray attenuation coefficients, where variations in the attenuation coefficients of the mineral constituents within rocks are used to acquire projection data for tomographic reconstruction. In this study, sandstone rock masses were scanned at a resolution of 26 μm. The projection data were processed using dedicated computer software to reconstruct 3D models. The CT images were postprocessed and visualized using Avizo image analysis software. The image quality is influenced by the scanning parameters, specifically the X-ray tube voltage and current. For this investigation, a voltage of 110 kV and a current of 80 µA were used to scan cylindrical rock masses measuring 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in length, respectively.

Uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading numerical experiment of fissured specimens

There are many methods to construct rock fissures, such as the particle removal method, discrete fissure network framework embedded smooth joint model method, and prefabricated fissure cementation material method. The cracks formed using the third method were smooth and unaffected by particle size. Therefore, the prefabricated fissure cementation material method was used to construct cracks in this study. The specific construction process is as follows: In the pre-loading stage, according to the size of the crack, the wall is generated at the crack position; then, the particles in the wall are removed and loading is continued; after that, cementation is generated, the wall is deleted, and the crack is finally determined. The numerical model of the fissure specimen is illustrated in Fig. 7. The velocity loading method was used in the simulation, and the specific loading-unloading processes corresponded to the uniaxial loading-unloading conditions of the fissured specimens in Sect. 2.1.

Progressive failure processes analysis

Figure 10 shows the failure mode of fissured sandstone under uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading. The failure modes of fissured sandstones with different angles exhibited differences. In the figure, TW is the wing crack, TV is the vertical tensile crack, TAW is the anti-wing crack, TS is the tensile-shear crack, ST is the shear-tensile crack, S is the shear crack, TOP is the out-of-plane tensile crack, SOP is the out-of-plane shear crack. When the fissure angle is 15 °, the failure is mainly composed of vertical tensile cracks, shear cracks, and tensile-shear mixed cracks. Simultaneously, vertical tensile cracks were formed in the middle of the prefabricated cracks and extended to the ends of fissured sandstone specimens. When the fissured angle is 30 °, the failure mode was dominated by wing, shear, and secondary cracks. Wing and shear cracks initiate and propagate from the fissure tip, inducing secondary cracks. When the angle increased to 45 °, the failure of fissured sandstone specimen was composed of shear cracks and tensile-shear mixed cracks, and the shear failure characteristics were obvious. Tensile cracks were generated at the middle and left tips of prefabricated fissure. The crack direction changed to form a tensile-shear mixed crack during the development process. When the fissure angle is 60 °, the failure of the fissured sandstone specimens is composed of tensile-shear mixed cracks, shear cracks, and wing cracks. Following the generation of shear and wing cracks at the fissure tip, the left wing crack changed its expansion direction, resulting in a combined tensile-shear crack. When the fissure angle was 75 °, the failure of the fissured sandstone specimen was composed of shear cracks, tensile-shear mixed cracks, and secondary cracks. Microcrack developing occurred at the fissure tip, taking on tensile and shear characteristics. The tensile cracks change direction during the development process to form tensile-shear mixed cracks, whereas the shear cracks expand the secondary shear cracks during the development process. The comparison shows that with an increase in the fissure angle, the phenomenon of rock surface spalling is more obvious, the number of rock blocks formed after fissure increases, and the overall damage degree increase.

Progressive failure processes analysis

The progressive failure diagrams of fissured sandstone under uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading are shown in Fig. 13. Increasing fissure angle led to marked alterations in the crack initiation stress, propagation path, and ultimate failure mode of the fissured sandstone. In fissured sandstone with a low angle (15°), the formation of microcracks commenced at the fissure tips as early as the third cycle of loading-unloading. The crack initiation of specimens with a medium angle (45°) was the earliest. In the second cycle, microcracks appeared inside the rock. Compared with other angles of the fissured sandstone specimens, the crack development of the fissured sandstones with a high angle (75°) required greater stress, and the elastic stage was the longest. This phenomenon shows that the crack initiation is affected by the angle between the crack plane and the direction of principal stress, and there is a critical angle (approximately 45°) at which the crack is most likely to start. In terms of the type and propagation path of crack propagation, the initial crack type of specimens with lower fissure angles (15°-30°) was mainly tensile and propagated perpendicular to the fissure surface. With an increase in stress, some tensile cracks developed inside the specimen. Subsequently, these cracks deflected from their initial propagation paths and, driven by the local stress field, transitioned into tensile-shear mixed cracks. Shear cracks were initiated at the other end of prefabricated fissures. This process reflects the transformation of rock failure from a single tensile failure to a tensile-shear composite failure. The shear mechanism of specimens with medium and high fissure angles (45°-75°) was significantly enhanced. The cracks generated inside the fissured specimens were mostly initiated at the fissure tip, and the types were mainly shear or tensile-shear mixed forms. These cracks frequently derive secondary cracks during the expansion process and form a complex network. When the stress reached a level near the peak in the fifth cycle, the fissured specimens at all angles entered the accelerated failure stage. At this stage, the crack propagation rate inside the rock mass increased exponentially, and finally formed a penetrating macroscopic fissure network. When the prefabricated fissure angle of the fissured sandstone was larger, the crack network formed inside the rock was more complex, and the number of rock blocks that were eventually broken and split was larger. It can be observed that shear-dominated destruction is more likely to lead to overall fragmentation. The numerical simulation results were in good agreement with the laboratory experiment results based on the final failure mode of the fissured rock mass, as shown in Fig. 14.

Progressive failure processes analysis

The progressive failure diagram of the fissured sandstone specimens with different angles under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions is shown in Fig. 17. The application of confining pressure significantly changed the initiation and propagation behavior of cracks; however, its evolution stage could still be summarized as three stages: elasticity, stable propagation, and accelerated penetration. The phenomenon of fissured specimens in the crack initiation and elastic stages is similar to that under uniaxial conditions. The crack initiation stress varied non-monotonically with the change in the crack inclination angle, and the crack initiation stress was the lowest when the angle was 45°. However, compared with the uniaxial condition, the initiation of microcracks in fissured sandstone at the same angle was significantly delayed (e.g., the 75 ° crack initiated in the fourth cycle). This phenomenon indicates that the early initiation of cracks in the specimen can be effectively restrained by confining pressure. When the fissured specimen was in the stable propagation stage, the crack propagated stably at a lower speed. At this stage, the role of the shear mechanism of the specimen is enhanced. The crack types are mainly wing cracks, shear cracks, and their mixed forms, and the proportion of tensile cracks is reduced. When the fissured specimen is in the accelerated penetration stage, the specimen stress is close to its peak stress. All the angle-fissured specimens entered the fatigue stage, and the number of cracks in the specimen increased rapidly, forming a penetrating network. At this stage, the development of secondary cracks in the high-dip (≥ 45°) fissure specimen is more serious, producing a final failure pattern of complexity.

The comparison between the failure processes under uniaxial and biaxial conditions yielded the following conclusions. The existence of confining pressure can prolong the elastic stage and increase the crack initiation stress of all inclined-fissure specimens during loading. This phenomenon shows that the confining pressure leads to an increase in the overall bearing capacity and crack resistance of the specimen. The increase in confining pressure conditions in fissured sandstone at all angles promoted the transformation of the failure mode to shear dominance, and the number of shear cracks increased significantly. Although the crack initiation of a fissured specimen is delayed under confining pressure, the crack network formed inside the rock has higher complexity and connectivity, and a large number of secondary cracks are developed. This will lead to a more serious final fragmentation of the specimen. This shows that confining pressure not only inhibits the early damage of fissured rock but also accumulates higher strain energy, which is finally released in a more severe form of fragmentation. The angle determines the microscopic mode of failure and the difficulty of crack initiation in the rock.

In summary, the failure evolution is governed by the combined influence of fissure angle and the stress state. The angle of a fissured specimen determines the microscopic mode of rock failure and the difficulty of crack initiation. The confining pressure macroscopically increases the crack initiation threshold of the fissured specimen and strengthens the shear failure mechanism. Eventually, a more complex penetrating network and more severe fragmentation failure are generated inside the specimen. The established law offers a theoretical underpinning for evaluating the failure progression of fissured rock under diverse loading conditions.

Results comparison

In this study, the mechanical response and failure evolution mechanisms of fissured sandstone under uniaxial and biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions were systematically analyzed. The results are summarized as follows: In terms of mechanical properties, a positive correlation was observed between the fissure angle and the peak strength of sandstone under tow loading conditions. The effect of the fissure angle on the peak strength was more pronounced under uniaxial cyclic loading conditions than that under biaxial cyclic loading conditions. This variation indicates that the peak strength reduction caused by fissures is diminished by multiaxial stress paths. Under two loading conditions, the elastic modulus increased with the fissure angle, although the rate of increase was smaller than that of the peak strength increase. Under identical fissure angles, the elastic modulus consistently remained higher under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions than that under uniaxial conditions. This variation is consistent with the principle that multiaxial stress can reduce the peak strength reduction caused by fissures. In terms of the progressive damage process, fissure initiation primarily occurs at the ends of pre-existing fissures under uniaxial cyclic loadings. As the fissure angle increased, the failure mode gradually transitioned from tensile failure to composite tensile-shear failure, ultimately culminating in shear failure. However, under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions, the fissure concentration effect at the fissure tip diminishes, the elastic stage is significantly prolonged, and microcrack propagation is delayed until higher stress levels are reached. Furthermore, the failure pattern showed little variation with the fissure angle, differing from the phenomenon observed under uniaxial conditions, where the fissure patterns depend on the angle of the fissure.

A comparison of the findings of this study with existing research on fissured sandstone under static loading conditions revealed the following observations: In terms of mechanical properties, the fissure caused cumulative damage within the specimen, resulting in a peak strength lower than that under static loading conditions48,49. In terms of the progressive damage process, the cyclic loading-unloading behaviors of the fissured specimens were compared with those under static loading conditions. Microcracks consistently developed at the fissure tips of the specimens during the experiments. Specimens with the same fissure angle exhibited a greater number of cracks, denser crack distribution, and ultimately more severe failure during cyclic testing48,49. This phenomenon indicates that cyclic stress enhances the propagation and coalescence of microcracks within the specimens, leading to a substantial accumulation of structural damage prior to failure. This reduces the load-bearing capacity and stability of the fissured specimens, resulting in failure patterns characterized by greater complexity and evolution.

Conclusions

Laboratory experiments, CT scanning experiments, and numerical simulations of uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions were performed on sandstones with prefabricated fissured angles of 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 75°. Additionally, numerical simulations of biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions were performed.

The conclusions are summarized as follows:(1) The mechanical properties of sandstone are significantly altered by the presence of fissures. Under uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading, the peak strength and elastic modulus exhibited a positive correlation with the fissure angle. However, the increase in elastic modulus was less pronounced compared to the more rapid enhancement of peak strength. With increasing fissure angle, a nonlinear relationship was observed in crack initiation stress, while damage accumulation was intensified and failure modes exhibited greater complexity. Across all angles, crack propagation accelerated most rapidly at peak stress.(2) Under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions, the peak strength of the fissured sandstone increased with an increase in the fissure angle, although the rate of increase was more gradual than that observed under uniaxial conditions. A positive correlation exists between the fissure angle and the elastic modulus of the sandstone, although at a relatively modest rate. In terms of failure mode, the failure mode of the specimen is primarily shear failure. Cracks primarily initiated at the ends of pre-existing fissures, exhibiting only a few developing cracks near the walls on both sides. Crack density and damage degree were positively correlated with the fissure angle.(3) Compared with uniaxial conditions, the range of peak strength and elastic modulus of fractured sandstone under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions decreases with the change of fracture angle. The peak strength and elastic modulus of sandstone exhibited a monotonically increasing yet progressively decaying rate of growth with respect to the fissure angle. The peak intensity was significantly influenced by fissure angle. In terms of failure mode, it was less influenced by the fissure angle, with shear failure being predominant. The crack initiation stress increased, and the elastic stage was significantly longer, accompanied by a greater number of cracks propagating through the specimen and a more severe ultimate failure of the specimen.These findings provide a theoretical basis for the support design and risk assessment of surrounding rock. In engineering practice, differentiated anchoring and support schemes can be adopted according to zones delineated by fracture inclination angles, and different levels of monitoring can be applied to different inclination zones. (1) The mechanical properties of sandstone are significantly altered by the presence of fissures. Under uniaxial cyclic loading-unloading, the peak strength and elastic modulus exhibited a positive correlation with the fissure angle. However, the increase in elastic modulus was less pronounced compared to the more rapid enhancement of peak strength. With increasing fissure angle, a nonlinear relationship was observed in crack initiation stress, while damage accumulation was intensified and failure modes exhibited greater complexity. Across all angles, crack propagation accelerated most rapidly at peak stress.

(2) Under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions, the peak strength of the fissured sandstone increased with an increase in the fissure angle, although the rate of increase was more gradual than that observed under uniaxial conditions. A positive correlation exists between the fissure angle and the elastic modulus of the sandstone, although at a relatively modest rate. In terms of failure mode, the failure mode of the specimen is primarily shear failure. Cracks primarily initiated at the ends of pre-existing fissures, exhibiting only a few developing cracks near the walls on both sides. Crack density and damage degree were positively correlated with the fissure angle.

(3) Compared with uniaxial conditions, the range of peak strength and elastic modulus of fractured sandstone under biaxial cyclic loading-unloading conditions decreases with the change of fracture angle. The peak strength and elastic modulus of sandstone exhibited a monotonically increasing yet progressively decaying rate of growth with respect to the fissure angle. The peak intensity was significantly influenced by fissure angle. In terms of failure mode, it was less influenced by the fissure angle, with shear failure being predominant. The crack initiation stress increased, and the elastic stage was significantly longer, accompanied by a greater number of cracks propagating through the specimen and a more severe ultimate failure of the specimen.

These findings provide a theoretical basis for the support design and risk assessment of surrounding rock.
