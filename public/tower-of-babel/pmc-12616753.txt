Lessons from community during COVID-19: understanding needs and preferences for the development of a disease prevention and health promotion initiative
Authors: Tara Kenworthy LaMarca, Gabriela Guevara, Margia Ambroise, Frantzia Jeanty, C Mindy Nelson, Viviana E Horigian, Lisa Gwynn, Elizabeth R Pulgaron
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany patients face barriers when it comes to confident healthcare decision making. During the COVID-19 pandemic, individuals had to weigh the risks and benefits of potential health risks, and scientists had the challenge of summarizing and communicating risks from various scientific domains. Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) methodology combines the concerns and expertise of researchers and community members to improve health. This study aimed to (1) identify topics and methods for delivering health information about COVID-19 through mixed methods of (a) quantitatively examining reasons for and against vaccination and testing and trusted sources of information and (b) qualitatively examining intervention preferences, and (2) to design an intervention based on the needs and preferences of the community and in partnership with community health champions. Quantitative data illustrated concerns related to testing, vaccination, and trust that impacted school community members’ willingness to engage in healthy behaviors. Qualitative data demonstrated that key intervention preferences of school community members included transparency of health messaging, messaging from trusted sources of information, desire for general health information, caution with incentives and mandates, and formatting the intervention for the audience. These data informed a customized town-hall style intervention to increase knowledge and confidence in COVID-19 health decision-making for parents. This study demonstrates the importance of understanding community needs, identifying strategies that promote trust, and collaborating with communities to prevent disease, promote health, and enhance health equity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12982-025-01059-7.Keywords: Healthcare decision making, Community-based participatory research (CBPR), Mixed methods, COVID-19, Health promotion, Disease prevention

Introduction

There are many sources of health information, including misinformation, that patients must navigate in order to make health decisions [1]. As a result, many patients, especially parents, face challenges with making confident healthcare decisions for themselves and their families and need support making decisions on needs including managing long-term conditions, intensive care, and immunization [2]. Several factors may contribute to confusion about healthcare decision-making for parents. A review of parental decision-making specific to immunizations highlights the complexity of factors influencing parental decision to vaccinate including cognitive, attitudinal, and social-cognitive influences [3, 4]. Furthermore, trust in information about vaccines has been questioned by parents for years, including those who delay or forgo vaccines related to the belief that pediatricians do not provide “balanced” information about risks and benefits [5]. After the COVID-19 outbreak, individuals were faced with complex decisions that required them to weigh risks and benefits of various decisions (e.g., how to avoid illness, how not to spread illness), and scientists were challenged to translate science from multiple disciplines into practical guidance and communicate it effectively [6]. The proliferation of misinformation during the COVID-19 pandemic intensified challenges with health literacy and decision-making [7]. Marginalized communities may be especially hesitant to trust the medical system due to a history of mistreatment [8]. Barriers like misinformation and mistrust may lead to failure to engage in health promotion and disease prevention strategies, thereby contributing to outbreaks of preventable disease.

We have learned that research methodologies must incorporate communities in design and dissemination of research studies and interventions to address disparities and drive change. For example, Community Based Participatory Research (CBPR) combines the expertise of researchers and community members throughout the research process to improve community health [9]. Community engaged research allows communities to have a voice in the health information they receive and has been effective for improving health outcomes in marginalized communities [10]. Therefore, we applied CBPR in this study to combine the expertise of community members and researchers to create a tailored intervention that would aim to increase parental confidence in healthcare decisions during the COVID-19 pandemic.

In an effort to promote health through enhancing confident decision-making and community participation in research, the current study engaged a school community in the development of a tailored health education initiative (also referred to as the “intervention”) centered on COVID-19. The aims of this study were to (1) identify topics and methods for delivering health information about COVID-19 through mixed methods of (a) quantitatively examining reasons for and against vaccination and testing and trusted sources of information and (b) qualitatively examining intervention preferences, and (2) to design an intervention based on the needs and preferences of the community. This study took place in racially/ethnically diverse, urban, school-based settings.

Materials and methods

Setting

Funded by the National Institutes for Health (NIH) Rapid Acceleration of Diagnostics Underserved Populations (RADx-UP) Return to School Initiative [11], the University of Miami engaged in a three-year, multi-phase study aimed at improving health outcomes as children returned to school following the outbreak of COVID-19. All RADx-UP grantees collected common data elements as required by the larger consortium of participating sites. While the analysis in this manuscript centers on understanding individuals’ experiences with COVID-19 and developing a COVID-19 health education initiative, additional study components (not presented here) included implementation of school-based COVID-19 testing, vaccination, and wastewater testing. Future analysis will examine the acceptability, feasibility, and health behaviors associated with the intervention as well as other study components. There was an overarching aim of community involvement, which included engaging school health champions, who were student or staff members of the school community who acted as liaisons between the community and research team, throughout the study [12]. Specific tasks with which school health champions assisted included community outreach, advertisement campaigns, study recruitment, coordinating research events, providing feedback on the health education initiative, and translating health promotion materials. One school champion also contributed to a study manuscript and conference presentations.

Participants

Participants were parents, staff, and students recruited from nine public schools in one school district in Miami-Dade County, Florida. This included three high schools, two middle schools, and four elementary schools, all served by the University of Miami’s Dr. John T. Macdonald Foundation School Health Initiative (SHI). The SHI provides medical and mental healthcare free of cost to students in under-resourced communities. Potential participants were included if they were affiliated with one of the nine schools served by the SHI, able to speak/read English, Spanish, or Creole, and (1) a student between the ages of 14–21 years-old, (2) the parent/guardian (i.e., “caregiver”) of a student and over the age of 18, or (3) a teacher over the age of 18. Student and caregiver participants enrolled independently of each other, that is to say they were not necessarily linked as student-caregiver pairs. The goal of including individuals in various roles was to incorporate the knowledge and concerns of the school community as a whole, which includes school staff, students, and their families.

Some participants who completed quantitative measures were invited to participate in focus groups. Only participants from a subset of the schools served by the SHI (two high schools, one middle school, and three elementary schools) were invited to participate in focus groups. These schools were selected because they were randomly assigned to be “intervention” schools, where the health intervention would be implemented. SHI schools where focus groups were not hosted were “control” schools that would not receive the intervention. Typically, it takes 4–8 focus groups to reach saturation in qualitative research [13], but we planned to include additional groups as needed to accommodate groups stratified by school and participant type. We aimed to enroll up to ten individuals in each focus group and included participants on a first-come first-served basis.

Measures/materials

Quantitative. Quantitative measures examined for this study included measures from the RADx-UP common data elements [14]. Data examined for this study included basic demographics (e.g., gender, age, race, ethnicity, language(s) spoken), reasons for getting COVID-19 testing and vaccination or not (i.e., attitudes), as well as sources they trust to provide correct information of COVID-19. Questions asking, “Why would you get a COVID-19 vaccine?” and “Why would you NOT get a COVID-19 vaccine” provided a variety of response options and participants were asked to check all that apply. The following questions asked participants to rate various concerns on a 5-point scale from “Not at all,” “Slightly, “Somewhat,” “Moderately,” “Very much,” or “Prefer not to answer” with regard to what encourages them to get tested and what discourages the from getting tested. Finally, participants were asked how much they trust various sources of information about COVID-19, and they were to select a response option from “Not at all,” “A little,” “Somewhat,” “A great deal,” “Don’t know,” and “Prefer not to answer/” See Supplement S1 for questions.

Qualitative. Focus groups followed a semi-structured discussion guide that was developed by the research team. One doctoral-level research team member drafted the questions and all other research staff were given the opportunity to review and revise; everyone agreed upon the final questions. The focus group questions of interest in this study were about how to communicate health information and how to implement health education programs. For example, participants were asked how they thought testing and vaccination could help students, families, and their community and how they would explain this to others, and how we should implement the health education program (e.g., format). See Supplement S2 for all related questions. Additional focus group questions centering on participants’ COVID-related concerns were previously analyzed and published; they can be found in Kenworthy and colleague’s paper [15]. The entire focus group transcripts were reviewed in the event that participants discussed their intervention preferences in relation to any prompt during the course of the focus group.

Procedures

This study was approved by the University’s Institutional Review Board and the school district’s Office of Research. Participants were recruited by flyers, school announcements, advertising at school events, school messaging systems, and word of mouth, with the support of school health champions. Prior to completing the quantitative measures, adult participants completed consent and children under 18 completed assent and parental consent. Measures were administered via paper forms or electronic surveys, and participants received a $15 gift card for completing the survey. Participants who completed quantitative measures were eligible to participate in focus groups. Preliminary focus group methodology has been previously described [15], as the first seven focus groups were analyzed to allow for rapid understanding of initial participants’ COVID-related concerns. The present study includes an additional three focus groups at elementary schools that were not included in the original paper because they were the final focus groups conducted. The qualitative data in the present study focuses on intervention preferences, in contrast to the initial analysis that detailed qualitative concerns. In total, ten 45-min to one hour focus groups were conducted on school grounds by a doctoral-level member of the research team between September 2021 and July 2022. Focus groups were led by one of three doctoral-level research staff, all of whom were women and of diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds (non-Hispanic White, Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic Black). One of the leaders was bilingual in Spanish and English; two were native to the local region. An additional study staff member facilitated consent, note-taking, and distributing payment. Participants received a $25 gift card for completing the focus group. Focus groups were audio recorded to allow for verbatim transcription. One focus group was conducted in Spanish and was transcribed and translated by a bilingual study staff member.

Analysis

Quantitative. The data from all quantitative measures were uploaded to the software SPSS, where quantitative analysis was conducted using descriptive statistics. We did not conduct analytic statistics due to missingness of demographic data that would have limited the power to detect differences between groups.

Qualitative. Qualitative analysis followed procedures described by Kenworthy et al. [15]. A general inductive approach was applied to derive themes from the data [16]. The study team of coders was diverse and included women of Hispanic White, non-Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic Black, race/ethnicities. Notably, four of the coders were native to the local region. For the initial seven focus groups, two doctoral-level study staff reviewed the transcripts, identified preliminary themes, and created a codebook. Three additional coders (study staff who spanned bachelors, masters, and doctoral level training) coded transcripts individually, then reviewed codes with a partner. Any discrepancies between pairs were discussed and 100% consensus was ultimately reached. Three additional focus groups that took place at elementary schools after an initial analysis and publication were coded and analyzed for this study. These focus groups were coded following the original codebook and procedures, with two coders (one doctoral level-staff who was involved in initial coding and one masters-level staff who was newly trained to complete these final focus groups) coding independently then meeting to reach consensus on any discrepant codes. The coders reached 100% consensus. Themes were then analyzed using NVIVO [17].

Triangulation. The research team then aggregated and integrated quantitative and qualitative data and reviewed with school health champions to identify key components to be addressed in the health intervention. School health champions were also invited to an initial pilot of the intervention, and they provided feedback afterward to improve the intervention prior to its larger roll-out.

Results

Participants

Quantitative. A total of 513 participants completed the quantitative survey. The mean age was 38.1 years (SD = 15.1). Most of the participants were from minority groups, with 68% and 27% identifying as Black (n = 348) and Hispanic (n = 136), respectively, and 57% speaking a language other than English at home (n = 293). Seventy-three percent of participants identified as female (n = 375) (see Table 1).

Qualitative. A total of 62 individuals participated in 10 focus groups. Four focus groups consisted of caregivers only, four included school staff only, and two included both caregivers and students. Students and caregivers were combined in groups, rather than having groups for students alone, because inviting students and their caregivers to attend the same focus group made it more feasible and reduced burden for both individuals to participate. One of the ten focus groups was conducted in Spanish, based on need, whereas the remaining groups were in English. We did not link demographic data between measures and focus groups, and therefore we do not have demographics for the subset of focus group participants.

Quantitative findings

Vaccination. The main reasons participants reported being willing to get a COVID-19 vaccine included wanting to keep their family safe (n = 362, 71%), wanting to keep themselves safe (n = 310, 60%), wanting to keep their community safe (n = 258, 50%), and wanting to feel safe around other people (n = 221, 43%) (Fig. 1). Some of the main concerns participants reported for not getting the COVID-19 vaccine included side effects from the vaccine (n = 108, 21%), not trusting that the vaccine will be safe (n = 66, 13%), not knowing enough about how well as COVID-19 vaccine works (n = 59, 12%) and not liking needles (n = 48, 9%) (Fig. 2).

Testing. Participants reported that the main reasons that encouraged them to get tested included to get treated early if they are positive (64%, n = 326) and to know they are safe to not give COVID-19 to their friends and family (62%, n = 316) and anyone they are around (62%, n = 316), to which they responded with moderately or very much (Fig. 3). On the other hand, participants reported that the main reasons that discouraged them to get tested included that if they are positive, officials need to contact the people they have been in contact with (32%, n = 162) and that even if they don’t have it when tested, they can still get COVID-19 later (29%, n = 151), to which they also responded with moderately or very much (Fig. 4).

Trust. Participants reported that the biggest sources they trusted at least somewhat on COVID-19 included their doctor or health care provider (70%, n = 357), the U.S. Coronavirus Task Force (52%, n = 269), and their close friends and members of their family (52%, n = 267). The sources they trusted the least included their contacts on social media (n = 163), their faith leader (n = 103) and the people they go to work/school with and other people they know (n = 100), where 32%, 20%, and 20% of people responded with not trusting them at all, respectively (Fig. 5).

Theme 1: Transparency. The primary message consistently delivered by participants was the importance of transparency. Participants highlighted their desire to be informed about what is known and unknown about COVID-19, and how or why health recommendations change over time. The concept of transparency was often connected to concerns about reliability of information (e.g., difficulty making health decisions because of lack of trusted information). Transparency was sometimes combined with the notion that if individuals are given all the facts, they will be able to make the best choice for their family without being told directly what to do (1a). In fact, sometimes being told directly what to do was a barrier to individuals engaging in health behaviors (see Theme 4: Incentives and mandates).

Theme 2: Sources of information. Participants provided information about where they receive information about COVID-19. In our sample, sources of information included friends and family, experts, teachers, community leaders, public figures, news, social media, and school leaders. Most individuals reported trusting COVID-19 information from experts, friends, family, those with firsthand experience, community leaders, and teachers. It was acknowledged that youth may trust messages from peers over experts, parents would like to hear from other parents (2a), and adults also reported getting information from friends, family, and co-workers. However, even trusted experts, such as physicians, sometimes spread information that discouraged recommended health behaviors like vaccination (2b). Similarly, parents reported concern about information that discouraged health behaviors being spread at school by teachers (2c), though they also acknowledged that teachers are influential and trusted by students. Some participants noted preference for experts from their community and described the influence of other community leaders, such as pastors, as sources of information (2d). Participants were less likely to trust the news, public figures, social media, and district leaders for information. Public figures such as celebrities were seen as a potential draw for engaging the school community in health campaigns (2e), but others such as politicians were seen as more controversial and less trusted. Generally, adults were reported to watch the news for information, but often local news did not provide much helpful information about COVID-19 (2f). Additionally, despite some skepticism about information on social media, students acknowledged they are constantly consuming social media content (2 g). Finally, elementary school staff were concerned that school leadership was not doing enough to protect their health, for example by keeping the school clean and sanitized (2 h), but this was not noted by participants in other roles or schools.

Theme 3: Desire for health knowledge. Several participants communicated that many individuals in the community lack basic health information, including how to manage common health conditions like diabetes, when to visit the doctor, and how to engage in other practices like hand hygiene to promote health and prevent disease (3a). These sentiments were only expressed by school staff, though they were speaking for their community, and no parents, students, or participants from elementary schools raised the idea of incorporating basic health knowledge into interventions.

Theme 4: Incentives and mandates. Incentives and mandates were met with ambivalence by our participants. Some conceded that they were motivated to get vaccinated or engage in health behaviors based on incentives or mandates (4a). Yet, individuals also reported that these strategies, especially incentives, could induce skepticism and deter them from engaging in the health behavior (4b). Some participants reported that incentives and mandates would have to be significant (e.g., thousands of dollar incentives; threat of losing their jobs) to outweigh their concerns about vaccines; others were willing to accept more modest incentives and comply with mandates with less severe consequences.

Theme 5: Format. Ideas for platform of the intervention generally fell into two categories: town hall (5a) or social media (5b). In terms of details of the programming, participants recommended that the intervention be interactive, for example being offered at a pep rally (5c), including video components or an activity, or having interactive stations like a museum, and be targeted separately toward caregivers versus students (5d). Finally, the timing of the intervention would need to meet the scheduling restrictions for caregivers (5e) or other target audiences, and the location should take into consideration preferences of the audience and available spaces at the school as well (5f). School staff provided more opinions on format than participants from other roles. Reports on desired format of the intervention varied by school and participant type. For example, teachers noted their desire to have an intervention during school hours, whereas parents requested options outside of work hours. This may vary based on the school community (e.g., types of working hours parents typically have, etc.). Students were inclined to desire social media intervention, whereas adults more often requested in-person town halls.

Intervention design and components

Triangulation. We integrated the data presented here and previously published [15] about COVID-19-related concerns, focus group data regarding intervention preferences, and input from school health champions, to create the health intervention. The triangulation process for each component of the intervention is noted in the subsections that follow. The research team led the triangulation of data and the development of the intervention design and components, and consistent with CBPR methodology, we sought feedback from health champions and initial participants at the pilot intervention to adapt the intervention as needed.

Methods for health intervention (i.e., format): The quantitative data indicated that participants trusted doctors or other officials and people they knew, which was consistent with qualitative data indicating that experts, friends, family, teachers, and those with firsthand experience were trusted sources. Therefore, the presentation included a panel of local experts from the SHI clinics (e.g., a pediatrician, psychologist) and community members (e.g., a teacher/administrator from the school, a high school student).

The intervention format was a town hall, which was suggested in focus groups. This was more feasible for the study team and less controversial than developing an intervention on social media, which was often distrusted. Pre-prepared content was housed in a slideshow, which could be used flexibly to showcase COVID-19 facts and figures, based on audience interests.

Given the diverse demographics of the school communities, at least one facilitator per intervention was bilingual and bicultural. The content (slideshow and videos) was multilingual (English, Spanish, and/or Creole). Subtitles and in vivo translation were used when participants preferred different languages during a session (which was often the case).

The intervention targeted caregivers due to the qualitative data indicating that caregivers typically made decisions regarding COVID-19 testing and vaccination for their families, the request for separate interventions for adults versus children, and the research team’s knowledge about the importance of healthcare confidence for caregivers. To accommodate caregiver schedules, the intervention was held in the evenings, after school, and in-person. Champions encouraged the study team to offer dinner and childcare, so both of these modest incentives were incorporated alongside the interventions.

Topics for health intervention: The quantitative data presented here and qualitative data previously published [15] were integrated to address the main concerns of the community regarding health and COVID-19. The survey data indicated that concerns about vaccines were often related to safety (e.g., wanting to keep family safe or fear that vaccines were not safe). Participants were encouraged to get tested to keep others safe as well but were hesitant about contact tracing and potential of getting COVID, even if testing negative. Vaccine and testing concerns were also common topics in previously published qualitative data, with the addition of themes related to cultural traditions and beliefs, understanding changing policies, challenges with trusting information about COVID, and concerns about risk of illness. Finally, qualitative data from the present study indicated the desire for basic health knowledge and transparency (i.e., knowing the facts about what is known and unknown).

An SHI pediatrician and medical student developed the content of the slideshow focused on salient topics from surveys and focus groups. They presented what was known about COVID-19 at the time, including an emphasis on the safety of vaccines and tests and how to keep oneself and others safe, from a culturally informed lens. Additional topics related to mental health were added, per suggestion of champions and expertise of members of the research team. The slideshow included the following topics: (1) where we are (i.e., current local COVID-19 trends), (2) mental health (i.e., common fears about COVID-19, effects of isolation, and how to cope), (3) prevention (i.e., mitigating strategies such as testing, masking, and distancing), (4) vaccination explained (i.e., how vaccines work, dispelling common myths), (5) effects of COVID-19 vaccination compared to infection (i.e., side effects and symptoms), and (6) remedies (i.e., complementary medicine, supplements, or herbal remedies).

We developed the materials and messaging to present the current science and decision-making strategies of community members; emphasis was placed on the importance of the audience feeling confident about COVID-19-related decisions and making the best decision for their family. We intentionally designed the presentation to clearly present what was known and what was unknown at the time, in the spirit of transparency. Audience members were encouraged to ask clarifying questions and guided the direction of conversation. Each intervention session was tailored according to participants’ interests, questions, and concerns.

Discussion

This study employed mixed methods to assess community needs related to COVID-19 knowledge and preferences about how to receive health information. Surveys of students, caregivers, and staff from school communities indicated that concerns related to vaccination included side effects, lack of trust in and understanding of the vaccine, and disliking needles and testing included consequences of testing positive and potential for contracting COVID-19 even if they test negative. Previously published qualitative research from this study is generally consistent with these quantitative findings [15]. Regarding preferences for intervention, we found that participants were more trusting of experts (such as doctors and the U.S. Coronavirus Task Force), close friends and family, those with firsthand experience, community leaders (though survey results indicated faith leaders were not as trusted), and teachers than public figures, news, social media, school leaders, or other people they knew but were not as close to. While some recommendations varied by respondent group, in general, participants indicated desire for transparency in the delivery of health information, information about general health (not just COVID specific), were ambivalent about incentives and mandates, and identified preferred formats (e.g., town halls or social media; timing that worked for the intended audience). Building trust and effective public health messaging are common challenges during pandemics, even pre-dating COVID-19, particularly among vulnerable populations [18]. These things should be considered and addressed even before a pandemic occurs, for example through understanding the culture and values of vulnerable populations, involving trusted individuals in the design of interventions, and being honest about uncertainties about the pandemic [18].

In partnership with school health champions, the study team was able to integrate community needs and preferences into the design of a tailored intervention to increase confidence in healthcare decision-making related to COVID-19. For example, we included individuals who the community trusted in the intervention delivery, emphasized their choice in when and how to implement the intervention, and included the topics identified as most relevant to the community at that time. Further tailoring occurred at each specific intervention session as the audience guided the content covered via their questions and requests. It may be especially important to engage the community in tailoring interventions for historically underrepresented or minoritized groups, to address specific priorities and concerns, maximize trust, and minimize misinformation. The importance of tailoring interventions for ethnically minoritized groups has been shown in prior systematic reviews indicating positive health and satisfaction outcomes, reducing health disparities [19]. Additionally, by using CBPR methodology, which both emphasizes research partnerships and stresses feeding back data to communities and translating data into interventions, we were able to ensure rapid application of research knowledge to improve community health [20].

There are several limitations to this study. CBPR comes with several challenges, such as lack of generalizability and applicability to other populations since it is specific to the community. Maintaining strong community partnerships beyond research is also very time consuming and demanding, as it runs the risk of burnout and strain for the researchers [21]. Additionally, CBPR places equity and inclusion more central than more robust research designs that include randomization and controls, which can limit the value of the research [22]. In a systematic review on papers which used CBPR in the last decade, research quality of the studies did not rate high in terms of research design rigor [23]. However, the advantage of this approach is that it encourages adaptations based on community-specific needs. Additionally, qualitative and mixed methods data collection are not meant to be generalizable but to capture the depth of information along with community-specific feedback and perceptions. Another limitation is the scope of this paper, which is specific to data and decision-making that impacted intervention design; data on implementation, satisfaction and effectiveness of the intervention will be presented in future research. Additionally, due to missing data, we grouped categories of participants together in both our quantitative and qualitative analyses, which may have masked unique patterns by role. Though we included individuals with different roles in the school community (i.e., caregivers, students, and school staff), we did not link student-caregiver or student–teacher pairs and were therefore unable to examine how health decision making could be influenced within family or school relationships. We also combined caregivers and students in the same focus groups, which may have impacted students’ willingness to speak openly about their experiences due to the presence of adults.

Overall, this study included the intended audience in the creation and implementation of the health promotion and disease prevention intervention during the exceptionally stressful and tense time period of the pandemic. The collaborative and bidirectional approach was intentional and supportive of a shared decision-making model. The goal of this approach was to increase confidence of parent medical decision making and address concerns specific to the minoritized population being targeted. Significant thought, intention, and care was used to create a flexible, yet participant led COVID-19 intervention. Other investigators successfully used similar CBPR methodology to inform health interventions during the pandemic (e.g., [22, 24]). Future studies should consider this approach to meet unique needs of communities during future public health challenges. It is critical for health equity to partner with communities to gain understanding of their concerns and priorities as it relates to disease prevention and health promotion and to build interventions together that will most appropriately address their needs.
