Efficacy and Safety of Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy Followed by CDK4/6 Inhibitors Plus Endocrine Therapy in Patients With HR‐Positive, HER2‐Negative Breast Cancer: A Retrospective Single‐Centre Cohort Analysis
Authors: Xiaopei Dong, Fan Li, Yang Yuan, Hua Song, Yingzhe Wang, Yi Hao, Yinping Dong, Tao Wang, Li Bian, Shaohua Zhang, Jianbin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEndocrine therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitor has been the main treatment for HR+/HER2− breast cancer in the adjuvant setting; however, the data on the application in the neoadjuvant setting is limited. We compared neoadjuvant efficacy and safety of sequential chemotherapy‐CDK4/6 inhibitors with chemotherapy alone in HR+/HER2− breast cancer patients.MethodsFemale patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer who received neoadjuvant therapy at the Fifth Medical Center of the PLA General Hospital from November 2019 to March 2025 were enrolled. The objective response rate (ORR), pathological complete response (pCR), and event‐free survival (EFS) between the two treatment groups were analyzed.ResultsAmong the 181 enrolled patients, 142 received chemotherapy alone (NCT cohort) and 39 received CDK4/6 inhibitors plus endocrine therapy (NCT‐ET cohort) after chemotherapy as preoperative therapy. Overall pCR was similar across cohorts (3.5% in NCT cohort vs. 2.6% in NCT‐ET cohort, p = 1.000), as was ORR (72.5% vs. 69.2%, p = 0.685). The 3‐year EFS rate was numerically higher in the NCT‐ET cohort (96.7% vs. 83.6%, p = 0.111). However, among patients who failed to achieve partial response (PR) within 4 cycles of chemotherapy, switching to endocrine therapy was associated with improved ORR (61.3% vs. 30.4%, p = 0.005). Overall pCR (3.2% vs. 0.0%, p = 0.356) and 3‐year EFS rate (95.7% vs. 78.0%, p = 0.105) were numerically higher in the NCT‐ET cohort. The total incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse effects was higher in the NCT‐ET cohort (69.2% vs. 47.8%). However, for patient received endocrine therapy after chemotherapy, grade ≥ 3 events decreased from 66.7% during chemotherapy phase to 20.5% after switching to endocrine therapy.ConclusionsDespite no statistically significant differences in observed overall pCR and 3y‐EFS between patients treated with NCT or NCT‐ET, the sequential endocrine strategy was associated with improved ORR among patients who did not achieve PR after four cycles of chemotherapy and a lower incidence of adverse events during endocrine therapy.Keywords: breast cancer, HER2‐negative, hormone receptor‐positive, neoadjuvant therapy

Introduction

Neoadjuvant therapy is currently the standard treatment for patients with locally advanced or inoperable breast cancer [1, 2, 3]. In patients with triple‐negative breast cancer and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2‐positive (HER2+) disease, neoadjuvant therapy yields a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 50% to 70%, and pCR is associated with improved prognosis [4, 5]. However, owing to the relative insensitivity to chemotherapy in hormone receptor‐positive (HR+)/HER2‐ tumors, the pCR rate is lower than that in triple‐negative or HER2+ disease [6]. Meanwhile, chemotherapy‐related toxicities can substantially impair patients' quality of life [7].

Adjuvant endocrine therapy serves as the cornerstone of systemic treatment for HR+/HER2− breast cancer [8, 9]. Previous clinical trials have demonstrated that neoadjuvant endocrine therapy yields comparable rates of objective response rates (ORR), pCR, and breast‐conserving surgery to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT) among postmenopausal patients [10, 11]. Nevertheless, endocrine therapy alone is currently restricted to elderly patients or those with severe comorbidities who are unable to tolerate surgery or chemotherapy [12, 13].

Cyclin‐dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors have revolutionized the therapeutic landscape for HR+/HER2− breast cancer. Landmark adjuvant trials including NATALEE, MONARCH‐E, and DAWNA‐A have firmly established that the addition of CDK4/6 inhibitors to endocrine therapy significantly improves invasive disease‐free survival and distant recurrence‐free survival, highlighting the critical role of CDK4/6 inhibition in the adjuvant setting [14, 15, 16]. However, the clinical value of CDK4/6 inhibitors in the neoadjuvant setting remains controversial. In particular, it remains unclear whether chemotherapy can be safely omitted without compromising long‐term prognostic outcomes.

In real‐world clinical practice, a subset of patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer are switched to sequential endocrine therapy after receiving initial neoadjuvant chemotherapy, primarily due to severe chemotherapy‐related toxicities or suboptimal tumor response. This clinical practice paradigm provides a potential strategy to address the ongoing debate over chemotherapy de‐escalation. Nevertheless, prospective clinical evidence directly comparing the efficacy and prognostic outcomes of upfront NCT versus neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by sequential endocrine therapy (NCT‐ET) remains lacking in HR+/HER2− breast cancer. Herein, we aimed to comprehensively compare treatment responses and long‐term prognostic outcomes between these two neoadjuvant therapeutic strategies in patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

We collected clinical and pathological data from 181 women patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer who received neoadjuvant therapy at the Fifth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital between November 2019 and March 2025. Patients were categorized into two groups according to treatment strategy: 142 received NCT alone (NCT cohort), and 39 received NCT‐ET (NCT‐ET cohort). The main inclusion criteria were primary tumors with estrogen receptor (ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PR) expression ≥ 10% [17, 18] and HER2‐negative expression, and patients that had received neoadjuvant therapy. Hormone‐receptor and HER2 status at study enrollment were determined based on preoperative core‐needle biopsy specimens. HER2 status was assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC). Cases with an IHC score of 2+ underwent confirmatory testing using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). HER2‐negative status was defined as IHC 0 or 1+, or IHC 2+ accompanied by a negative FISH result. Patients with equivocal HER2 findings without interpretable FISH results were excluded from this study. Exclusion criteria were incomplete neoadjuvant therapy or response assessment data, concurrent primary malignancy, or male sex.

Treatment

In the NCT cohort, 136 patients received pirarubicin in combination with either nab‐paclitaxel or docetaxel (AT regimen); 6 received nab‐paclitaxel, doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide (TAC regimen). At chemotherapy initiation, all planned doses were calculated according to body surface area. The target doses were: nabpaclitaxel 260 mg/m2, either pirarubicin or doxorubicin 50 mg/m2, docetaxel 75 mg/m2, and cyclophosphamide 500 mg/m2. All regimens were administered in 21‐day cycles. Dose modifications were permitted in subsequent cycles based on hematological toxicity and individual patient tolerance. In accordance with the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology Breast Cancer (CSCO‐BC) guidelines, long‐acting granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) was routinely administered 24–72 h post‐chemotherapy for myelosuppression prophylaxis among patients with clinical indications.

The treatment for patients in the NCT‐ET cohort was divided into two phases: Chemotherapy phase: 35 patients received pirarubicin in combination with either nab‐paclitaxel or docetaxel (AT regimen), 3 nab‐paclitaxel, doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide (TAC regimen), and 1 pirarubicin plus cyclophosphamide (AC regimen). The treatment intervals, administered doses, and other management protocols were consistent with those of the NCT cohort. Endocrine therapy phase: All 39 patients received CDK4/6 inhibitors, and 18 were premenopausal and 21 were postmenopausal. All premenopausal patients concurrently received ovarian function suppression (OFS). 29 patients received abemaciclib (initial dose: 150 mg twice daily in 24 patients; 100 mg twice daily in 5 patients) combined with letrozole (n = 11), exemestane (n = 9), anastrozole (n = 8), or fulvestrant (n = 1). 9 patients received palbociclib (initial 125 mg once daily, 21‐day‐on/7‐day‐off, 28‐day cycles) plus letrozole (n = 6), exemestane (n = 2), or anastrozole (n = 1). 1 patient received dalpiciclib 125 mg once daily on the same 28‐day schedule, combined with exemestane. Two patients required dose reduction of abemaciclib from 150 mg to 100 mg twice daily after one month of treatment owing to grade 3 treatment‐related toxicities: one developed hepatic injury, while the other experienced concurrent grade 3 leukopenia and thrombocytopenia.

Of the 181 enrolled patients, 142 completed neoadjuvant chemotherapy up to definitive surgery: 103 achieved a partial response (PR), and 39 had stable disease (SD). The remaining 39 patients were switched to neoadjuvant endocrine therapy owing to inadequate tumor response (n = 23) or intolerable treatment‐related toxicities (n = 16). Median time from the final chemotherapy cycle to surgery was 30 days (range, 20–40 days) in the NCT cohort. In the NCT‐ET cohort, the median interval from completion of sequential endocrine therapy to surgery was 20 days (range, 10–30 days). Surgical timing was determined by treating physicians according to tumor response, patient clinical status, and breast‐cancer clinical guidelines, reflecting routine real‐world clinical practice instead of pre‐specified study‐defined criteria. No cases of disease progression were documented across the entire cohort during neoadjuvant therapy.

Efficacy Assessment and Follow‐Up

Efficacy was assessed per the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1). For primary breast tumors, measurable lesions with a longest diameter ≥ 10 mm were defined as target lesions; regional lymph nodes with a short‐axis diameter ≥ 15 mm were also classified as target lesions. Retrospective response reassessment was undertaken using clinically archived imaging data. Evaluations were performed at routine clinical follow‐up time‐points: every two cycles during neoadjuvant chemotherapy and every two months during the sequential endocrine‐based therapy phase. Image review and response categorization were completed independently by two senior breast‐cancer oncologists. Any inter‐rater disagreements were resolved by consensus after discussion with a third senior oncologist. Response evaluation relied primarily on contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging of the breast and axilla and breast ultrasonography.

We retrospectively extracted patients' demographic, clinical and pathological data, neoadjuvant‐therapy‐associated efficacy and safety information, and subsequent‐treatment data from inpatient and outpatient medical records. Toxicity severity was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. Survival outcomes were mainly obtained from medical records and supplemented by telephone follow‐up with patients or their relatives when needed. The last follow‐up date was December 31, 2025. Median follow‐up was 30.5 months (range, 6–73 months).

End Points

The end points of this study were pCR at the time of definitive surgery; objectives were objective response rate (ORR) and 3‐year event‐free survival (EFS) rate. ORR was defined as the percentage of patients obtaining complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) following neoadjuvant therapy relative to all subjects receiving preoperative treatment. The definition of pCR was divided into two parts: Overall pCR was defined as no invasive residual in breast or nodes, noninvasive breast residuals allowed (ypT0/Tis ypN0). Breast‐only pCR was defined as the absence of residual invasive cancer in the breast, irrespective of ductal carcinoma in situ and nodal involvement (ypT0/is, any ypN) [1, 19]. All pathological specimens, including primary breast tumor and regional lymph‐node tissues, were processed and diagnosed following our institutional standardized pathological protocol. For this retrospective study, diagnostic data were extracted from archived clinical pathology reports. All original reports were generated following consensus assessment by two experienced breast pathologists at our center.

EFS defined as the time from initiation of diagnosis to the first occurrence of disease progression that precluded definitive surgery, local or distant recurrence, a second primary cancer, or death from any cause [19]. Mixed‐landmark EFS defined as origin from either post‐four‐cycle chemotherapy or the time of treatment switch to endocrine therapy to the first occurrence of disease progression that precluded definitive surgery, local or distant recurrence, a second primary cancer, or death from any cause. Censoring for EFS was defined as follows. Patients with an EFS endpoint event were recorded as events at the date of occurrence. Those without an EFS event were censored at their last valid follow‐up date. Patients lost to follow‐up were censored at the date of their last confirmed event‐free assessment.

Ethical Statement

This retrospective study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval no. KY‐2026‐2‐28‐1). Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee in accordance with institutional ethical requirements. All data extraction and research‐related statistical analyses were performed after ethical approval had been obtained.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in categorical variables between groups were compared using the Pearson χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test. Propensity‐Score Matching (PSM), including 1:1 and 1:2 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement, was conducted in R software (version 4.4.1). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate event‐free survival. Hazard ratios and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined with the use of univariate Cox regression analysis for survival endpoints. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% CIs were obtained via multivariable regression analysis. For each regression model, complete data were available for its included covariates and endpoints; no patients were excluded from the model because of missing values for these variables. Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS 27.0, and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.0 and R software (version 4.4.1). p < 0.05 indicated statistical significant difference.

The overall pCR was 3.5% in the NCT cohort and 2.7% in the NCT‐ET cohort (p = 1.000). Given the low overall pCR rate, we further compared breast‐only pCR between the two treatment groups. The results showed that breast‐only pCR was numerically higher in the NCT cohort relative to the NCT‐ET cohort (7.0% vs. 2.7%, p = 0.461; Table S2), but the difference was not statistically significant.

Median follow‐up was 32 (range, 6–73) months for the NCT group and 26 (range, 9–72) months for the NCT‐ET group. By the data cutoff of December 31, 2025, 21 patients developed recurrence or distant metastasis: 20 in the NCT cohort (14 visceral/bone metastasis, 6 chest‐wall/lymph‐node metastasis), and one in the NCT‐ET cohort with concurrent liver and bone metastasis. Three all‐cause deaths occurred, all in the NCT cohort. After excluding 5 patients with postoperative conversion to HER2‐positive status, the 3‐year EFS rate was 83.6% (95% CI: 76.4%–91.5%) in the NCT cohort and 96.7% (95% CI: 90.5%–100.0%) in the NCT‐ET cohort (HR = 0.225, 95% CI 0.030–1.688, p = 0.111; Figure 2). We also performed analyses for mixed‐landmark EFS, the 3‐year EFS rate was 83.5% (95% CI: 76.2%–91.4%) in the NCT cohort and 96.7% (95% CI: 90.5%–100.0%) in the NCT‐ET cohort (HR = 0.219, 95% CI 0.029–1.649, p = 0.105; Figure S2). No statistically significant between‐group difference was observed, and the interpretation is limited owing to the small number of events.

Subgroup Analysis

Among 87 patients who failed to achieve PR within 4 cycles of chemotherapy, 56 continued chemotherapy and 31 switched to endocrine therapy. The analytic results revealed that patients switched to endocrine therapy achieved a higher ORR (61.3% vs. 30.4%, p = 0.005) compared with those receiving continuous chemotherapy. While there were no statistically significant differences in overall pCR (3.2% vs. 0.0%, p = 0.356) and 3‐year EFS rate (95.7% vs. 78.0%, HR = 0.214, 95% CI 0.027–1.688, p = 0.105) between the two groups.

Further analysis showed that grade ≥ 3 adverse events during NCT (OR = 2.712, 95% CI, 1.016–7.240; p = 0.046), baseline breast Ki‐67 index < 30% (OR = 0.251, 95% CI, 0.093–0.676; p = 0.006) were associated with a higher likelihood of switching to endocrine therapy (Table S3).

Safety

The predominant grade ≥ 3 adverse effects observed in this study were neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity, nausea/vomiting, and diarrhea. Overall, the incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events was 47.8% in the NCT cohort and 69.2% in the NCT‐ET cohort, with neutropenia identified as the main toxicity in both cohorts (Table 3). Further stratified analysis of the NCT‐ET cohort showed that the incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse reactions was 66.7% during the NCT phase and declined significantly to 20.5% after switching to the ET phase.

Discussion

In this retrospective real‐world study, we compared the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone versus neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by sequential endocrine therapy plus CDK4/6 inhibitors in patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer. There are several troubling implications. Despite no statistically significant differences in observed pCR, ORR, and 3‐EFS between the NCT and NCT‐ET groups, among patients who failed to achieve partial response within 4 cycles of chemotherapy, switching to endocrine therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitors was associated with improved ORR compared with continuing chemotherapy. Furthermore, although the overall incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events was higher in the NCT‐ET group, toxicity decreased markedly after switching from chemotherapy to endocrine therapy, supporting a more favorable safety profile in the endocrine phase. These results provide real‐world evidence for sequential chemotherapy de‐escalation using CDK4/6 inhibitor–based endocrine therapy in the neoadjuvant setting for HR+/HER2− breast cancer.

HR+/HER2− breast cancer is the most prevalent subtype, characterized by relatively favorable long‐term prognosis but low sensitivity to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [6]. Consistent with previous studies, pCR rates in both groups were low (3.5% in NCT and 2.6% in NCT‐ET), confirming that pCR is not an ideal surrogate endpoint for this subtype. Unlike triple‐negative and HER2‐positive breast cancer, in which pCR strongly predicts survival benefit, the low pCR rate and weak correlation with EFS observed in our cohort further support that pathological response is less meaningful for neoadjuvant risk stratification in HR+/HER2− disease [1, 5]. Our findings align with large pooled analyses showing that residual cancer burden (RCB) and survival surrogates outperform pCR in this population [20, 21].

CDK4/6 inhibitors have become a standard component of adjuvant therapy for HR+/HER2− breast cancer based on the NATALEE, MONARCH‐E, and DAWNA‐A trials, which demonstrated significant improvements in invasive disease‐free survival [14, 15, 16] However, their role in the neoadjuvant setting remains undefined, particularly regarding whether chemotherapy can be safely reduced or omitted. Most prior studies focused on concurrent chemo‐endocrine regimens, which showed modestly higher pCR but increased toxicity. A meta‐analysis showed that the pCR rate of the chemo‐endocrine group was slightly higher than that of the chemotherapy alone group (6.5% vs. 3.8%), but the difference was not significant [22]. Another meta‐analysis including 11 RCT reached similar conclusions and indicated that the incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events in the chemo‐endocrine group was significantly higher than that in the chemotherapy alone group [23]. In contrast, our study evaluated a sequential strategy—initial chemotherapy followed by CDK4/6 inhibitor–based endocrine therapy—an approach that reflects real‐world decision‐making for patients with toxicity or suboptimal response. To our knowledge, this is one of the few real‐world studies to directly investigate this sequential NCT‐ET paradigm.

A clinically meaningful finding was the benefit of switching therapy in patients with poor early response to chemotherapy. Among those not achieving PR within 4 cycles of NCT, switching to CDK4/6‐inhibitor‐based therapy was associated with improved ORR compared with patients who continued chemotherapy (61.3% vs. 30.4%, p = 0.005). This suggests that early response‐guided switching can rescue tumor response without compromising oncological outcomes. In these patients, continued chemotherapy offered limited additional benefit, whereas targeted endocrine therapy restored meaningful disease control. This strategy addresses the ongoing debate about chemotherapy de‐escalation and supports response‐adapted neoadjuvant approaches.

Safety analysis further highlighted the practical value of the sequential strategy. The overall rate of grade ≥ 3 adverse events was higher in the NCT‐ET group, largely driven by toxicities accumulated during the initial chemotherapy phase. After switching to endocrine therapy, the rate of grade ≥ 3 adverse events dropped sharply from 66.7% to 20.5%, with neutropenia and chemotherapy‐related gastrointestinal toxicity substantially reduced. These results demonstrate that NCT‐ET allows effective de‐escalation: early chemotherapy provides initial cytoreduction, while subsequent endocrine therapy maintains disease control with a more tolerable safety profile. This is particularly relevant for patients vulnerable to chemotherapy toxicity, including those with comorbidities or poor functional status.

Multivariable analysis identified baseline tumor stage and tumor size after four cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (or upon switching to endocrine therapy) as independent factors for objective response during neoadjuvant treatment. The neoadjuvant treatment group exhibited no significant association with EFS. These findings suggest that sequential endocrine therapy may not compromise neoadjuvant efficacy or long‐term prognosis. Notably, baseline Ki‐67 < 30% and grade ≥ 3 chemotherapy toxicity were independent predictors of switching to endocrine therapy, indicating that clinicians intuitively favor de‐escalation in patients with biologically less aggressive disease or treatment intolerance. Our data validate this clinical practice as safe and effective.

This study has several limitations. First, this single‐center retrospective study was limited by sample size, potentially introducing selection bias and wide confidence intervals with insufficient statistical power for survival endpoints; thus, survival outcomes should be interpreted as trends rather than definitive conclusions. Second, neoadjuvant chemotherapy cycles and endocrine therapy duration were not standardized—reflecting real‐world practice but potentially introducing internal heterogeneity. Third, patient‐reported outcomes and quality‐of‐life data were not collected. Fourth, between‐group differences in postoperative systemic therapy represented an important confounder for EFS comparisons. Given the limited EFS events, a robust multivariable model could not be constructed, precluding sensitivity analyses adjusted for postoperative therapy. Future large‐scale prospective studies with sufficient events are warranted to validate these findings after adjusting for postoperative therapy. Fifth, core needle biopsy–based enrollment carries an inherent limitation of neoadjuvant studies—potential pre‐ and postoperative HER2 discordance. Another important limitation is retrospective adverse‐event ascertainment. Incomplete source records prevented reliable distinction between treatment‐related toxicity, disease progression and other non‐treatment causes for hospitalizations, dose reductions, treatment delays or discontinuations, precluding quantitative analyses of toxicity‐related clinical events. Despite these limitations, our study provides important real‐world data on a clinically actionable sequential strategy and fills the evidence gap regarding neoadjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor use after chemotherapy.

Conclusion

For patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer, no statistically significant inter‐group differences were observed in pCR, ORR, and 3‐year EFS between neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by sequential endocrine therapy plus CDK4/6 inhibitors and chemotherapy alone. This strategy was associated with improved ORR in patients with early poor response to chemotherapy and reduces severe toxicity after switching to endocrine therapy. The sequential NCT‐ET approach represents a practical, response‐adapted de‐escalation strategy that balances efficacy and safety. Prospective trials are warranted to confirm these findings and define optimal patient selection for this neoadjuvant paradigm.
