Perspectives of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers on how the Heart@Home mobile application improves self-management in heart failure care: A qualitative study
Authors: Alicha Chumintrachark, Kanokporn Pinyopornpanish, Chanchanok Aramrat, Chutamas Pairoj, Nida Buawangpong, Suphawita Pliannuom, Sarapee Seesopon, Mesaya Khangtanyakit, Nopakoon Nantsupawat, Wichuda Jiraporncharoen, Chaisiri Angkurawaranon, Arintaya Phrommintikul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to investigate the perspectives of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers on how the Heart@Home application improves self-management following a two-week period of usage and how to improve the application for further utilization.MethodsThis qualitative study used a case study approach. We conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews with patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers and evaluated patients' quality of life before and after using the application. Purposeful sampling was employed, and thematic analysis was performed for qualitative data analysis.ResultNine patients, seven caregivers, and two healthcare providers were included in the analysis. The potential benefits for health behavior modification were noted through several mechanisms, including increasing self-awareness of patient's current health status, guide for managing symptoms related to health status changes, and providing emotional assessment, reassurance and a sense of being cared for. In addition, more function in application is needed to increase engagement with application usage. Although the pre-post pilot analysis shows no statistically significant changes in participants' quality of life, as measured by the EQ-5D questionnaire, promising trends were observed in the domains of self-management and daily activity.ConclusionThe Heart@Home mobile application shows promise in promoting self-management and behavior modification among heart failure patients. Future development should focus on improving technical stability, incorporating personalized feedback, and enabling direct messaging with healthcare providers to enhance the application's effectiveness in managing heart failure.Keywords: mHealth, Heart failure, Self-management, Behavior modification, Mobile application

Introduction

Heart failure is one of the major global-burden diseases with a high mortality rate. It is a chronic condition that requires long-term treatment and continuous care (Thailand HFCo, 2020; McDonagh et al., 2021; Heidenreich et al., 2022). Heart failure is also a complex disease with constantly changing pathophysiology, requiring close monitoring of various factors such as exacerbation of heart failure symptoms, fluid overload or peripheral edema, dehydration, side effects of medication, hypotension, and deteriorating kidney function (Thailand HFCo, 2020; McDonagh et al., 2021; Heidenreich et al., 2022). The symptoms of heart failure often alternate between stable and worsening phases, making it difficult to manage these symptoms (McDonagh et al., 2021; Heidenreich et al., 2022).

After diagnosis, patients often require readmission to the hospital due to exacerbation of heart failure (Thailand HFCo, 2020). Frequent hospital readmissions impact the physical, mental, emotional, and social well-being of the patient, preventing them from living a normal life and reducing their quality of life compared to other chronic diseases. It also affects caregivers and family members (Thailand HFCo, 2020). The factors associated with readmission include respiratory infections, cardiac arrhythmias, non-adherence to treatment plans regarding diet, improper medication intake, and lack of awareness of heart failure symptoms leading to delayed medical attention (Thailand HFCo, 2020). About half of these factors can be prevented by patient self-management (Thailand HFCo, 2020). Empowering patients with knowledge about their condition and self-management strategies is crucial for effective heart failure care (Tinoco et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2023). Patients are generally encouraged to self-manage their illness by monitoring symptoms, adhering to treatment regimens, and managing symptoms related to any changes (Toukhsati et al., 2015). Monitoring and follow-up of patient health, especially during the transition period after discharge from the hospital to home, is important in the comprehensive care of heart failure patients, including prevention, promotion, treatment, and rehabilitation (Tulu et al., 2021). If patients are unable to self-manage, their symptoms may worsen and emergency treatment may be required.

Mobile health (mHealth) interventions, particularly mobile applications, are promising tools for patient education and self-monitoring in heart failure (Bezerra Giordan et al., 2022a; Coorey et al., 2020). Recent systematic reviews have identified several mHealth apps designed for heart failure self-management, such as HeartMapp and SUPPORT-HF, which have resulted in improvements in self-care behaviors, quality of life, and potential reductions in hospital readmissions in various countries (Bezerra Giordan et al., 2022a; Coorey et al., 2020; Essa et al., 2022; Athilingam et al., 2016; Rahimi et al., 2015). The main functions of these apps typically include assessments (e.g., clinical parameters, vital signs, and body weight), recommendations on diet and exercise, and supportive features such as reminders and interactive text messaging (Cruz-Ramos et al., 2022; Bezerra Giordan et al., 2022b). In Asian countries, mHealth interventions for heart failure may potentially be in the early stages of development. A recent scoping review of mobile applications for self-management of cardiovascular diseases found that while heart failure is among the most frequently targeted conditions globally, only a few such applications have been reported in Asia and none from Thailand for heart failure (Cruz-Ramos et al., 2022). For instance, a study in South Korea by Son et al. demonstrated the positive effects of a 24-week interactive text message-based mobile health intervention on enhancing self-care behaviors of heart failure patients (Son et al., 2023).

In Thailand, there has been an increasing attempt to integrate mHealth into healthcare to promote self-management in recent years, particularly for chronic diseases, for example, hypertension (Chaiphattharatada et al., 2024), diabetes (Meenasantirak et al., 2025), and chronic kidney disease (Sangrawee et al., 2022). Published studies have reported potential improvement of health behaviors, and perceived usefulness of mHealth interventions (Sanon and Temdee, 2025). In the context of heart failure, where patients require ongoing care at home, these findings suggest that mHealth has strong potential to be both acceptable and beneficial within the Thai context. However, while mHealth has been evaluated for various health conditions, published research on mHealth interventions specifically for heart failure management remains limited. This is a significant gap given the context of heart failure in Asia, where patients often develop the condition nearly a decade earlier than their Western counterparts, underscoring the urgent need for action in the region (AstraZeneca, 2025). Understanding the perspectives of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers within this specific cultural and healthcare context is crucial for developing and implementing effective interventions that can address the unique challenges of heart failure management in Thailand. There is a growing need for locally developed and culturally appropriate solutions to address this public health challenge, which is compounded by the high mortality rates (Jiampo et al., 2024) and the significant economic and social burden it places on the healthcare system (Yingchoncharoen et al., 2021).

In the Thai healthcare context, mHealth has often been implemented through ad-hoc communication tools, particularly text messages or calls via the LINE application, to deliver health interventions and provide follow-up after clinical care (Pliannuom et al., 2025; Hongchuvech et al., 2021). The LINE application is a free chat platform that is widely used in Thailand for social communication and has also been adopted by healthcare providers for telehealth services, particularly for patient follow-up and reducing hospital workload and patient travel. However, these interactions rely heavily on personnel availability and manual responses, which can be time-consuming and difficult to sustain given the large patient volume. Moreover, LINE functions primarily as a basic messaging platform and does not provide a structured system for patient assessment. As a result, its use can lead to inefficiencies, inconsistent data reporting, and limited capacity for systematic monitoring and triage. Integrated features such as automated data capture, visualization dashboards, and alert systems are essential to enable scalable and reliable remote patient management.

Recognizing these limitations and the critical need for a culturally appropriate, the Department of Family Medicine, in collaboration with Cardiology Specialty, Department of Internal Medicine, Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University, has developed the “Heart@Home” application for home-based care of heart failure patients. While there are commercially available mHealth applications for heart failure, the majority are designed for Western populations and are not available in the Thai language. This often creates a significant barrier to effective use, especially for an older patient population with varying levels of e-health literacy. This application focuses on four aspects to facilitate self-management which include self-reporting of diet and fluid intake, emotional status, blood pressure and body weight measurement, and abnormal symptoms such as dyspnea or edema. This application is designed to be a more systematic and integrated solution for remote patient monitoring and uniquely tailored for the Thai population with a user interface in the local language and a color-coded alert system specifically designed to facilitate early detection of symptom changes. This study aims to investigate the usability of the application and the changes in health behavior observed in patients, caregivers and healthcare providers, providing insights for further development and implementation in future clinical trials.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study, using a case study approach, involved interviewing adult and older adult patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers after utilizing the Heart@Home application for a two-week period. The study was conducted at a tertiary care center in Northern Thailand. As a referral center, the clinic manages a high volume of complex heart failure cases, especially from across the northern Thai region. Patients are typically scheduled for follow-up visits every 2 weeks to 3 months depending on their clinical status. Given these relatively long intervals between in-person visits and the high complexity of cases, ongoing monitoring and timely communication between visits are essential to ensure patient safety and timely intervention. The case study approach is particularly useful for gaining an in-depth understanding of an issue, event, or phenomenon within its natural, real-life context, which aligns with our objective to thoroughly explore the experiences and perspectives of the participants (Crowe et al., 2011). Ethical approval was secured from the Medical Ethical Committee of Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand (No. 159/2023). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to an interview session. The privacy and confidentiality of human subjects were rigorously protected throughout this study. The study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines for ensuring complete and transparent reporting of qualitative research (O'Brien et al., 2014). Before the start of the two-week study period, all participants received a standardized, in-person training session from a research assistant to ensure they understood how to use and navigate the Heart@Home application and its features.

Study population and sampling method

The recruitment population were those who attended the heart failure clinic and were invited via in-person invitation. Participants included patients or their caregivers depending on who was the main user of the Heart@Home application. Purposeful sampling was employed. Patients with heart failure or caregivers whose family members have been diagnosed with heart failure, aged 20 years or older, and are capable of using a smartphone along with the Heart@Home application, were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria for participants were: (i) A current history of depression, identified by a PHQ-9 score of 7 or higher (Lotrakul et al., 2008); (ii) Existing treatment plans for underlying conditions necessitating hospital or medical facility care, such as scheduled surgery or regular dialysis; and (iii) Application users (patients or relatives) with cognitive impairment, determined by a Thai Mental State Examination score lower than 24 out of 30 points (Muangpaisan et al., 2015). Healthcare providers, comprising the heart failure clinic nurse and a cardiologist, were both recruited for the study, given their roles in providing care via the application. Regarding the study's sample size, prior recommendations in qualitative research indicate a minimum of 12 participants to attain data saturation (Vasileiou et al., 2018). Hence, we initially aimed to enroll a minimum of 12 cases, continuing recruitment until data saturation was achieved.

Heart@Home application: how does it work?

The Heart@Home mobile application (Fig. 1) was developed based on recommendations for self-care of heart failure patients from the Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology (McDonagh et al., 2021). Before implementation in the study, the developer tested the application with the research teams and the healthcare providers who used it for two weeks to ensure they could correctly utilize all functions of the application. The application consists of four aspects of self-care monitoring to facilitate self-management which include self-report diet and fluid intake, emotional status, blood pressure and body weight measurement, and abnormal symptoms such as dyspnea or edema. The application allows patients to record their symptoms, health behaviors, vital signs, and weight. Throughout the 2-week period, patients or caregivers were assigned to use the Heart@Home mobile application to record this information. They were not allowed to record the data retrospectively. A team of healthcare professionals monitor the data and send messages at least once a day. The application uses color-codes with healthcare team actions to notify severity of the data as follows: (1) Green: No action required. The patient's condition is stable; (2) Yellow: The healthcare providers will contact the patient via chat or telephone to provide further guidance and advice. This indicates that the patient's condition requires attention, but immediate hospitalization is not necessary; and (3) Red: The healthcare providers will urgently contact the patient via chat or telephone and instruct them to come to the hospital immediately. This color signifies a potentially serious or life-threatening situation that requires prompt medical attention.

Data collection

Data were collected from June 1, 2023, to August 31, 2023, at two distinct time points. At the initial in-person clinic visit, trained research assistants (who were not part of the healthcare team) approached eligible participants. Sociodemographic data, including age, gender, and education, were collected using a self-reported questionnaire. During this same visit, participants also completed the eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) and EQ-5D-5L. Subsequently, at the conclusion of the 2-week follow-up period, the same research assistants conducted a follow-up using a semi-structured interview and EQ-5D-5L was administered once more.

An individual session of semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted by a trained female research assistant who was not part of the healthcare team. The interviews took place via video call from a private room at the research office. The key interview guides are described in Table 1. The interview questions focused on the perception of using the Heart@Home application and how it improves patients' self-management. All interviews were recorded using a video recorder and telephone recorder. The interviews lasted for approximately 30 min on average. The interviews were transcribed verbatim, and data collection and analysis were carried out iteratively by KP and AC. The interviews ended when data saturation of the core analytic content had been achieved. No repeated interviews were carried out.

eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS)

The eHEALS comprises 10 questions, with the initial two items concerning the patient's perception of the internet's usefulness and importance of the internet for health-related information (Norman and Skinner, 2006). The subsequent eight items gauge eHealth literacy (eHL), encompassing the consumer's combined knowledge, comfort, and perceived skills of the consumer in locating, evaluating, and applying eHealth information to health issues. Responses are provided on a five-point scale ranging from 1, indicating “strongly disagree,” to 5, indicating “strongly agree.” Those who has ≥5 out of 8 eHEALS items were categorized as having high eHL (Khatun et al., 2015).

The EQ-5D-5L

The EQ-5D-5L is a standardized instrument utilized to measure health-related quality of life (Herdman et al., 2011). In this study, the questionnaire was employed specifically to assess the quality of life of the patients. The descriptive system comprises five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has five levels, ranging from no problems to extreme problems (scored 1 to 5).

Data analysis

The researcher team utilized thematic analysis, adopting both deductive and inductive approach. Deductive coding organized the data based on the existing framework of self-management in heart failure (Toukhsati et al., 2015), followed by inductive coding to identify new emergent themes. By doing so, the rigor and trustworthiness of the findings were enhanced (Bingham, 2023). To ensure thorough understanding and accuracy, each transcript was carefully reviewed multiple times by two independent researchers (AC and KP). Codes were generated directly from the qualitative data. The preliminary findings were then discussed by CA, WJ, and NB to refine the analysis further. Codes were developed by identifying patterns within the text data, and through a process of comparison and discussion. The codes were classified into themes. The themes were searched, reviewed, and named. The researchers reached a consensus on the final themes and subthemes. For qualitative data management and analysis, NVivo version 12 software was used.

We employed several strategies to ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of our qualitative findings. Credibility was strengthened by triangulating perspectives from patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon, and by using verbatim transcripts to preserve participants' perspectives. Dependability was supported through independent coding and analysis by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Confirmability was ensured by maintaining an audit trail documenting coding decision. Transferability was promoted by reporting descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, and examples of quotations.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage for categorical variables and mean with standard deviation for continuous variables, were used to summarize participant characteristics and application usage. Statistical analysis was carried out using a Paired t-test, was performed to assess changes in scores of patients' quality of life before and after participation. STATA version 16 was used for this analysis.

Results

Characteristics of heart failure patients and caregivers

Seventeen participants (10 heart failure patients and 7 caregivers) were initially engaged to use the Heart@Home application for a two-week period. One patient lost to follow-up due to internet connectivity issues and did not complete the post-evaluation and interview. As a result, a total of 16 participants were recruited for the analysis. Among this number, there were 9 females (56.25 %) and the average age was 59.75 (SD 9.88) years. Fifteen participants (93.75 %) were categorized as having high eHL. The pre-post intervention analysis showed no statistically significant changes in participants' confidence in using the application or in any domain of quality of life after the two-week period of application use. However, trends toward improvement were observed in self-management (1.68 ± 1.19 vs. 1.25 ± 0.57, p = 0.068) and the self-behavior/activity dimension (2 ± 0.32 vs. 1.31 ± 0.11, p = 0.060).

Application usage and engagement

An analysis of the application's backend data revealed that participants actively entered health information throughout the 14-day study period, indicating consistent engagement with the daily monitoring features. The most frequently entered data points were dietary and fluid intake, with an average of 35.85 entries per participant (SD 22.14) over 14 days. Body weight and blood pressure were entered an average of 11.78 times per participant (SD 4.93), emotional status 10.64 times (SD 4.79), and symptoms 9.42 times (SD 3.77).

Perspectives of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers on how the Heart@Home mobile application improves self-management in heart failure care

Based on the framework of self-management in three key areas required from patients—monitoring symptoms, adhering to treatment regimens, and managing symptoms or adapting behavior related to changes—most patients and caregivers reported that using the application daily encouraged them to adopt healthier behaviors, particularly in relation to diet and fluid intake. Patients and caregivers also provided insights into the potential mechanisms through which the application exerts its impact, as outlined below.

Increasing self-awareness of patient's current health status through a monitoring function

The application educated the patients/caregivers to monitor their blood pressure or body weight by themselves and allow them to see the trends from the graph in the interface. Moreover, the color-coded function that shows severity of symptoms and mental health encouraged the self-awareness of both patients and caregivers.

“We can know what his blood pressure is every day. Normally, we don't measure and write it down every day, right? This app lets us monitor how much oxygen and how much water intake he has every day.”

(Patient's wife, 63 years old.)

“For symptoms, we can interpret the severity of the symptom by looking at the color in the application. If it's green, it means the symptoms are good. If it's yellow or red, it's a cause for caution. We can check my mom's symptoms every day.”

(Patient's daughter, 56 years old)

“It's good that patients can record their weight themselves, send their food, and their vital signs. This way, they can monitor their daily progress and take care of themselves. It increases patients' awareness of self-care”

(Cardiologist).

In addition, knowing that healthcare personnel are monitoring their health data allow them to behaving better. Some patients thought it was like homework from school that they had to do their best to impress the healthcare personnel that checked on them every day. For example, patients and caregivers changed their food preparation and consumption due to the need to report to the healthcare team via this application. The application motivated patients to eat healthier and make dietary changes.

“I think I adjust my dietary intake each day. I changed my diet to the menu recommended by the doctor. I'm a bit worried if I don't eat healthy and have to report it in the application.”

(Male patient, 63 years old).

Available guide for managing symptoms related to health status changes.

Once the patient recorded their health information, it led to further action regarding the real-time suggestion from healthcare providers, or from the alert system in the application.

“This application makes me feel safer and more confident in taking care of myself. It also enables me to communicate with healthcare providers more easily. If I have a health problem, I can manage it by myself using the advice provided in the feedback.”

(Male patient, 62 years old, living alone.)

“The application helped us check the severity of the patient's symptoms. One day, the patient had leg swelling. When I checked the application, it interpreted the symptom as yellow, meaning the patient's condition may require consulting a doctor. We were quite worried, but luckily, we had a follow-up appointment that day. The doctor advised us and adjusted the patient's medication.”

(Patient's daughter, 52 years old)

Providing emotional assessment, reassurance and a sense of being cared for

The application enabled patients and caregivers to monitor patient mood changes and then helped caregivers to be more aware of the patient's emotional status and communicate with the patient for more detail.

“Emotional monitoring helps me know what my mother's mood each day is. If the mood is green in the application, we can evaluate that today's mood is okay. But if someday the mood is yellow, I will ask to explore my mother's problem, which will help me understand my mother better. Sometimes, I take her to travel to different places to lighten up her mood.”

(Patient's daughter, 56 years old)

The application provided patients with a sense of comfort and security, knowing that their health was being regularly monitored by the healthcare team, which helped them to adhere to the treatment regimen.

“The application empowers me to maintain healthy behavior every day. It feels safe knowing that we're being cared for and watched over by healthcare professionals.”

(Male patient, 60 years old)

“It gives us peace of mind that there's this application from the hospital. We will still be able to be taken care of and have our behavior observed after being discharged from the hospital. It's beneficial for the patient's health and for us who are taking care of the patient as well. When we record information in this application, it helps us understand how we should adjust the patient's behavior each day because of the advice provided by the healthcare provider in the application. And we learn how to take care of our own health from that information too.”

(Patient's daughter, 45 years old)

“It also helps with the patient's mental well-being because I think some patients who use the application may feel good, as if someone is constantly monitoring them, and they will send updates.”

(Cardiologist)

More function in application is needed to increase engagement with application usage

While the Heart@Home application provided benefits by encouraging better health behaviors and emotional reassurance, the users also encountered some challenges. Here are comments and suggestions for further improvement including allowing changes to previously recorded health monitoring data, providing personalized feedback to patients based on the recorded data, adding in-app two-way communication features, and enabling notifications for important health-related reminders on the provider's side.

The inability to enter data retrospectively resulted in incomplete graphs and tracking. This limitation made it difficult for patients to maintain accurate records of their health data.

“Some days I'm too busy to record data in the application; my work is so intense that I don't have time to use the application. Sometimes when I want to complete the data, it takes the whole day, for example, recording meals for each day. When I finally have time to record, it's already midnight, and the application has reset to the new day. I want to go back and record the data, but I can't.”

(Male patient, 54 years old)

Participants expressed a desire for the app to provide summarized feedback, interpretation of entered data, and personalized recommendations for improvement. This feature would help patients to better understand their health data and make necessary changes to their self-care routines.

“When a patient records every topic in the app, I want the app to provide feedback and interpretation for all topics every day, indicating what is good, what is bad, and how to improve. Additionally, the app could ask questions that caution us, such as ‘What did you eat today that caused your body weight to go up?’”

(Male patient, 73 years old)

Participants suggested an in-app chat feature for direct communication with healthcare providers. This feature would allow patients to conveniently ask questions, seek advice, and receive timely support from their healthcare team.

“I want the application to add two-way communication features, such as a chat function with a doctor, so that I can ask healthcare professionals about my health concerns.”

(Patient's daughter, 45 years old)

“I want it to have a chat feature because otherwise, it will be one-way communication. We will have to go back to using the phone and then open the app, which is inconvenient. I want there to be a chat channel in there, and it will be done”

(Nurse).

Healthcare providers recommended adding a patient profile page that includes relevant information such as diagnosis, date of diagnosis, and vaccination status. They also suggested alerts for important health-related reminders, such as annual flu vaccinations.

“There should be a patient profile page with information about their diagnosis, when they were diagnosed, and whether they have received their annual vaccines. If possible, when it's time for the annual flu vaccine, it should alert them that it's time for the annual flu vaccination”

(Cardiologist)

Discussion

The findings from our study provide valuable insights into the experiences and perceptions of heart failure patients using the Heart@Home mobile application for self-care management. The results highlight the potential of mobile health interventions in the promotion of self-care and behavior modification in heart failure patients, while also identifying areas for improvement to enhance user experience and engagement.

One of the key findings of this study was the impact of the Heart@Home application on patients' self-care and behavior modification. Patients reported increased adherence to daily blood pressure monitoring, improved weight tracking and management, and dietary adjustments. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown the effectiveness of mobile health interventions in promoting self-care behaviors among heart failure patients (Son et al., 2023; Hägglund et al., 2015; Seto et al., 2012; Athilingam et al., 2017; Alnosayan et al., 2017; Cajita et al., 2016; Werhahn et al., 2019). A unique contribution of this study is the identification of the psychological and social mechanisms that underpin these behavioral changes. Our qualitative findings reveal that the application's effectiveness extends beyond simple data monitoring by fostering a sense of accountability. Patients described being motivated to adopt healthier habits because of the sense of being monitored by healthcare providers. This finding highlights the psychological factors that contribute to behavioral change occurs in this context (Tougas et al., 2015). Furthermore, the app's ability to provide emotional reassurance and a sense of being cared for emerged as a significant motivator for patients, highlighting the importance of the relational aspect in mHealth interventions. The mood-recording feature enabled patients and caregivers to monitor emotional well-being and take basic supportive actions, emphasizing the importance of assessing the psychological aspect, which is increasingly recognized as central in heart failure management (Celano et al., 2018; Radhakrishnan et al., 2016; Gelbrich et al., 2014). The application empowered patients to manage their health independently, which is crucial for sustaining long-term disease management and improving quality of life (Riegel et al., 2017; Woods et al., 2019). The findings are in alignment with the empowerment theory (Gibson, 1995). According to this theory, the empowerment process involves four stages: discovering reality, critical reflection, selecting appropriate methods and practices, and maintaining effective practices. The Heart@Home application facilitates these stages by enabling patients to monitor their health status (discovering reality), reflect on their condition and behaviors (critical reflection), receive guidance for further action (selecting appropriate methods), and sustain healthy behaviors through regular use of the application (maintaining effective practices). This empowerment process leads to increased self-esteem and self-efficacy in managing the health of the individuals, resulting in positive changes as observed in our study.

The application also provided emotional reassurance and a sense of being cared for, as patients felt that their health was closely monitored by the medical team. This finding highlights the potential of mobile health interventions in providing a sense of comfort and security for patients, particularly those who may feel isolated or disconnected from their healthcare providers (Piette et al., 2015; Kitsiou et al., 2015; Fairbrother et al., 2014; Mortara et al., 2009). Our study also contributes to the existing literature by highlighting the need for culturally appropriate and locally developed mHealth solutions for the Asian context, a topic on which research remains limited. By exploring the perspectives of a Thai population, we provide crucial insights into the specific design features and functionalities such as a local language interface, color-coded alerts, and the desire for personalized feedback that are essential for effective implementation in this region.

The study has identified several challenges and barriers to the effective use of the Heart@Home application technical issues, such as application crashes and internet connectivity problems, which were a significant barrier to regular usage. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported technical difficulties as a common barrier to mHealth adoption (Hägglund et al., 2015; Athilingam et al., 2017; Gagnon et al., 2016). To address the issue of the application crashing when internet connection is lost, developers should focus on improving the stability of the application, particularly in offline situations. The application should be designed to continue functioning even when the user doesn't have internet access, though this poses a challenge as the user would need to connect to the internet later to send the recorded data to the main server (Chaudhry et al., 2010; Ware et al., 2018). One solution to this limitation is using Bluetooth for data connection, as it is a widely available feature on smartphones and tablets, making it an accessible option for data transfer. The use of Bluetooth by this application could send data to the main server without relying only on an internet connection. This approach has been explored in several studies that have demonstrated the feasibility and benefits of this option. In other heart failure population studies, Bluetooth is often used in senser device such as ECG or vital signs devices (Athilingam et al., 2016; Seto et al., 2012; Sohn et al., 2020).

Manual data entry and application design also affected the overall user experience. These challenges highlight the importance of developing user-friendly and reliable mobile applications to ensure optimal engagement and adherence (Zapata et al., 2015). Mobile application developers should consider implementing intuitive and efficient data input methods, such as voice recognition, barcode scanning, or integration with wearable devices (Cai et al., 2017), and simplify the user interface with clear instructions (Paz Castro et al., 2017). The application login interface can use an easier, shorter id and password that the user can change by themselves, or a barcode scanning interface that can scan the barcode from a patient's hospital name tag. Priorities would be to increase testing as to how easy it is to use, making the application work better, and designing an interface that is simple to navigate even with limited internet access (Woods et al., 2019; Zapata et al., 2015; Peng et al., 2019).

There were needs that mentioned by participants including personalized feedback, in-app two-way communication features, and notification for provider site. The self-determination theory supports the importance of personalized feedback and two-way communication in promoting patient adherence by emphasizing the role of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in fostering intrinsic motivation (Ryan and Deci, 2000). Other research studies have demonstrated that personalized feedback and interactive features in mobile health interventions can improve patient adherence and outcomes in chronic disease management (Piette et al., 2012; Nundy et al., 2013). Without personalized feedback or interaction, patients may lose interest in using the application beyond the two-week study duration. Two-way communication is crucial for improving understanding and relationships between patients and healthcare providers (Bashi et al., 2017; Schmidt et al., 2010). To facilitate this, we plan to implement a chat feature that enables direct communication between patients and healthcare team. This feature will allow patients to ask questions, report concerns, and receive timely responses from healthcare professionals. While manual feedback from providers is a solution, it can be resource intensive (Boyne and Vrijhoef, 2013). Automated feedback using artificial intelligence is an interesting alternative, but it may have limitations in managing complex patient experiences and require significant technical resources and costs to develop (Aamodt et al., 2019; Singareddy et al., 2023). Therefore, we propose a hybrid approach that combines manual feedback with an AI chatbot to manage common problems while alerting healthcare teams to more complex issues (Singareddy et al., 2023). This two-way communication is crucial for improving the relationship between patients and providers, allowing patients to report concerns and receive timely responses, thereby improving their engagement and care (Schmidt et al., 2010; Guo et al., 2017).

Strength and limitations

The strengths of our study are that we highlight the mechanisms on how use of a mobile application enhance self-management and have identified the steps for further improvement in the application to be used in Thai heart failure patients. The findings from our study suggest that the application was well received by the majority of participants, with high levels of satisfaction reported. Although the pre-post pilot analysis shows no statistically significant changes in their quality of life, promising trends were noted in the domains of self-management and daily activity. These trends suggest that the Heart@Home application may have the potential to support patients in managing their condition and maintaining their daily activities. In addition, this study collected data from three groups of users: patients, caregivers, and health care providers. This is a triangulation technique increases the validation of the results (Creswell and Miller, 2000). However, this study is not without its limitations. First, the participants were recruited from a single geographic location, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the current study found improvements in self-care behaviors and trends toward improved quality of life, the sample size is small. The findings do suggest that the Heart@Home application has the potential to be effective in a larger population. This finding is common in pilot studies, which are designed to test the feasibility and acceptability of an intervention before conducting larger-scale trials (Leon et al., 2011). In addition, the short intervention duration of two weeks may not capture long-term changes in self-care behaviors and health outcomes. However, the purpose of this study was to assess the feasibility of the Heart@Home application, rather than to evaluate its long-term effectiveness. The positive trends observed in self-care behaviors and quality of life within this short timeframe suggest that the application has the potential to promote sustained improvements in patient outcomes, which can be further investigated in future long-term studies. Finally, the lack of a control group limits the ability to compare the observed changes from normal follow-up with patients and the Heart@Home application. However, the primary focus of this pilot study was to gather preliminary evidence on the potential benefits of the application and to inform the design of future randomized controlled trials. The consistent improvements in self-care behaviors and the positive feedback from participants indicate that the Heart@Home application is a promising intervention and needs further investigation in a controlled setting.

In conclusion, the Heart@Home mobile application shows promise in promoting self-care and behavior modification in heart failure patients. While technical issues and usability challenges need improvement, the application has the potential to provide benefits in health behavior modification through several mechanisms. These include increasing self-awareness, enhancing communication between patients and caregivers, promoting better behavior when being monitored by someone else, guiding further action, and providing a sense of being cared for. Future development should focus on improving technical stability, incorporating personalized feedback, and expanding accessibility to enhance the impact of the application on heart failure management.
