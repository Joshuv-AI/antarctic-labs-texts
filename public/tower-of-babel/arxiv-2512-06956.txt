Statistical analysis of Inverse Entropy-regularized Reinforcement Learning
Denis Belomestny, Alexey Naumov, Sergey Samsonov

Abstract
Inverse reinforcement learning aims to infer the reward function that explains expert behavior observed through trajectories of state–action pairs. A long-standing difficulty in classical IRL is the non-uniqueness of the recovered reward: many reward functions can induce the same optimal policy, rendering the inverse problem ill-posed. In this paper, we develop a statistical framework for Inverse Entropy-regularized Reinforcement Learning that resolves this ambiguity by combining entropy regularization with a least-squares reconstruction of the reward from the soft Bellman residual. This combination yields a unique and well-defined so-called least-squares reward consistent with the expert policy. We model the expert demonstrations as a Markov chain with the invariant distribution defined by an unknown expert policy π ⋆ \pi^{\star} and estimate the policy by a penalized maximum-likelihood procedure over a class of conditional distributions on the action space. We establish high-probability bounds for the excess Kullback–Leibler divergence between the estimated policy and the expert policy, accounting for statistical complexity through covering numbers of the policy class. These results lead to non-asymptotic minimax optimal convergence rates for the least-squares reward function, revealing the interplay between smoothing (entropy regularization), model complexity, and sample size. Our analysis bridges the gap between behavior cloning, inverse reinforcement learning, and modern statistical learning theory.

Introduction

Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is concerned with the recovery of a reward
function that explains observed expert behavior in a Markov decision process (MDP).
Rather than prescribing optimal behavior directly, IRL assumes that the expert policy
is (near-)optimal for some unknown reward, and aims to reconstruct this reward
function from observed trajectories. Such formulations are particularly relevant
in imitation learning, where the primary goal is to leverage expert demonstrations
to reproduce or generalize expert behavior.

A well-known challenge in classical IRL is that the solution is
not unique
:
many reward functions can explain the same observed expert behavior. This inherent
ambiguity is often referred to as the “ill-posedness” of IRL. Entropy regularization provides an important step towards resolving this difficulty.
In entropy-regularized reinforcement learning
16
,
10
, the expert is assumed to optimize
not only the expected discounted reward but also an entropy bonus, thereby producing
smoother and more stochastic policies. This has several advantages: entropy
regularization improves numerical stability, encourages exploration, and naturally
leads to Gibbs (softmax) optimal policies,
2
. The corresponding “soft” Bellman
equations replace the hard max operator of classical RL by the smooth log-sum-exp,
yielding both theoretical tractability and practical robustness. In this setting,
the optimal policy associated with a given reward is unique. However, uniqueness of the reward itself does not follow from entropy regularization
alone. To resolve the reward ambiguity, one can combine entropy regularization with
a least-squares reconstruction step based on the soft Bellman residual. In this inverse entropy-regularized RL (
ER-IRL
)
with least squares , the recovered reward is

unique
: the combination of soft optimality and a quadratic identification
criterion yields a well-posed inverse problem.

Given expert demonstrations in form of the Markov chain
(S_{t},A_{t})_{t=1}^{N}
with the invariant
distribution
d_{\pi^{\star}}(s)\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)
,
ER-IRL
aims to identify a reward
function
R
such that the optimal entropy-regularized policy induced by
R

matches the expert policy
\pi^{\star}
. One natural approach is to estimate the expert
policy itself by maximum likelihood-a procedure equivalent to
behavior cloning
-and
then to back out a reward function consistent with the estimated policy. We refer to
21
for statistical analysis of the behavior cloning in entropy-regularized RL. This
yields a two-step procedure: (
i
) policy estimation via penalized
maximum-likelihood, and (
ii
) reward reconstruction via least-squares or
Bayesian formulations of the soft Bellman residual equations. Importantly, the
regularization of the policy estimator plays a crucial role in controlling
estimation error and ensuring identifiability of the recovered reward.

Literature overview

In entropy-regularized IRL, expert demonstrations are modeled as samples from a Markov chain whose transition probabilities encode both a latent reward and an entropy or KL-based regularizer. This probabilistic structure frequently reduces reward inference to a least-squares-type statistical estimation problem, since soft Bellman relations and linearly solvable MDPs induce linear constraints linking rewards, value differences, and empirical log transition statistics. Uchibe’s model-free deep IRL method
22
makes this structure explicit by parameterizing the log density ratio between expert and passive dynamics and estimating it via logistic regression, effectively projecting empirical Markov transition frequencies onto a parametric model of the soft-optimal dynamics. Uchibe’s later work on model-based imitation learning with entropy regularization
23
further develops this principle by jointly regularizing both the model and the policy, thereby ensuring that the induced controlled Markov chain satisfies smoothness and stability conditions that are amenable to regression-based reward estimation. These formulations highlight how entropy regularization not only facilitates robust policy inference but also supports reward estimation through linear or linearized relationships between observed transitions and latent cost parameters. A similar least-squares interpretation underlies maximum entropy deep IRL
24
, where aligning empirical feature expectations with those induced by soft value iteration can be linearized as a regression step projecting empirical successor features onto the reward-induced soft occupancy measures. In continuous-control settings, the inverse optimal control approaches of Levine and collaborators
8
exploit Laplace approximations around locally optimal trajectories; these approximations yield linear equations in reward gradients, turning the IRL estimation step into a regularized least-squares fit to deviations of observed trajectories from locally optimal flows. The broader methodological context is summarized in the surveys
3
,
1
, which situates entropy-regularized IRL, density-ratio estimation, and probabilistic inverse optimal control within a unified conceptual framework.

Although
ER-IRL
is closely related to conditional density estimation-since an entropy-regularized policy is a conditional density of actions given states-this connection is only intermediate: our goal is not merely to estimate the policy, but to use it as a stepping stone toward recovering the underlying reward function. This contrasts with classical conditional density estimation, where the conditional density itself is the target of inference.
The connection is nonetheless informative. Recent minimax analyses for conditional density estimation under total variation smoothness
14
and empirical-entropy methods
4
clarify the statistical difficulty of estimating action-state conditional distributions. However,
ER-IRL
introduces two fundamental deviations from the standard setting. First, reward recovery requires solving an inverse problem: the reward appears only implicitly through the soft Bellman or linearly solvable MDP relations, so error propagation from policy estimation to reward estimation must be controlled. Second, the expert demonstrations arise from a Markov chain, rather than i.i.d. samples, which affects concentration properties.

Despite this unifying structure, a comprehensive statistical theory for entropy-regularized IRL is still lacking. Robust guarantees on estimator consistency, convergence rates, identifiability, or minimax optimality remain largely undeveloped, especially when reward functions, transition models, or density ratios are parameterized by deep networks. Much of the existing literature relies on heuristic approximations-soft value iteration, linearization of continuous dynamics, Monte Carlo estimation of normalization constants, or fitted density ratios-whose statistical behavior under finite samples is not yet well understood. As a result, although entropy-regularized IRL provides elegant algorithmic structure and empirically strong performance, its rigorous statistical foundation remains an open problem, and developing such guarantees for these least-squares-based frameworks represents an important direction for future research.

Our contributions.

In this work, we develop a statistical framework for
ER-IRL
grounded in penalized likelihood estimation of the expert’s policy, followed by a least-squares reconstruction of the underlying reward. Within this formulation, we derive nonasymptotic, high-probability error bounds for the recovered reward function, characterize precisely how the choice and magnitude of regularization influence estimation accuracy, and obtain fast convergence rates enabled by deep neural network approximation of the policy and quadratic approximation of the KL divergence. Moreover, we show that these rates are minimax optimal over suitable functional classes by establishing a matching information-theoretic lower bound. Our analysis links classical principles from behavior cloning and maximum-likelihood policy estimation to contemporary statistical guarantees for
ER-IRL
. In doing so, it clarifies the bias-variance tradeoffs induced by entropy smoothing and demonstrates how entropy regularization interacts with the statistical complexity of reward learning in high-dimensional settings.

Entropy-regularized RL

We will start with an entropy regularized version of classical reinforcement learning problem. We briefly recall the well-known definition of Markov’s decision process (MDP), the definition of policy, and formulate the problem of finding optimal policy under additional entropy regularization. A more detailed description can be, for example, found in

9
.
We consider an infinite-horizon, discounted MDPs of the form

\mathcal{M}=(\mathsf{S},\mathsf{A},P,R,\gamma),

where
\mathsf{S}
is the (measurable) state space, typically a Borel subset of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathsf{A}=\{1,2,\ldots,|\mathsf{A}|\}
is a finite action set,

P(ds^{\prime}\mid s,a)=p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\,ds^{\prime}
is a Markov kernel on
\mathsf{S}
(the transition law),

R:\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded measurable reward function,
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor.
By a Markov kernel we mean: for each
(s,a)
,
P(\cdot\mid s,a)
is a probability measure on
(\mathsf{S},\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{S}))
with Lebesgue density
p(\cdot\mid s,a)
, and for each measurable
B\subseteq\mathsf{S}
the map
(s,a)\mapsto P(B\mid s,a)
is measurable.
A stationary Markov
policy
is a Markov kernel
\pi:\mathsf{S}\to\Delta(\mathsf{A})
,

s\longmapsto\pi(\cdot\mid s)=\big(\pi(a\mid s)\big)_{a\in\mathsf{A}},\qquad\pi(a\mid s)\geq 0,\ \ \sum_{a\in\mathsf{A}}\pi(a\mid s)=1.

Unless stated otherwise, we work with stationary policies; in the discounted setting this is without loss of generality for optimal control.
Given an initial state
S_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
(some distribution on
\mathsf{S}
), a policy
\pi
, and the transition kernel
P
,
the process evolves as
A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t})
and
S_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid S_{t},A_{t})
.
The (unregularized) discounted return is

G^{\pi}\;=\;\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,R(S_{t},A_{t}),\qquad V^{\pi}(s)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,G^{\pi}\mid S_{0}=s\,\right].

The performance measure of the agent in classic RL is
V^{\pi}(s)
. In contrast, in entropy-regularized reinforcement learning (see
16
,
10
,
15
) to encourage exploration and produce smoother control laws, we add a Shannon-entropy bonus with temperature
\lambda>0
:

J_{\lambda}(\pi)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\,\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\Big(R(S_{t},A_{t})+\lambda\,\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t}))\Big)\,\Bigg]

with
\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s))\;:=\;-\sum_{a\in\mathsf{A}}\pi(a\mid s)\log\pi(a\mid s).

Define the
entropy-regularized value function
and
Q
-function:

V_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\Big(R(S_{t},A_{t})+\lambda\,\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t}))\Big)\ \Big|\ S_{0}=s\Bigg],

and

Q_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,a)\;=\;R(s,a)+\gamma PV_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,a),

where for
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
we denote
Pf(s,a):=\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[f(S^{\prime})]
.
The goal is to maximize
J_{\lambda}(\pi)
(equivalently
V_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s)
pointwise in
s
) over policies. Following Theorem 1 and 2 in
12
, we can show that
the optimal value function
V^{\star}\equiv V^{\star}_{\lambda}
solves the entropy-regularized Bellman optimality equation

V^{\star}(s)\;=\;\max_{\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta(\mathsf{A})}\left\{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)Q^{\star}(s,a^{\prime})+\lambda\,\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|s))\right\}.

(2.1)

where

Q^{\star}(s,a)\;=\;R(s,a)+\gamma PV^{\star}(s,a).

(2.2)

Note that (
2.1
) is a convex optimization problem over the simplex
\Delta(\mathsf{A})
with closed-form solution.
By the variational identity for the log-sum-exp (Gibbs variational principle),
we obtain the
soft Bellman optimality equation

V^{\star}(s)\;=\;\lambda\log\sum_{a\in\mathsf{A}}\exp\!\Big(Q^{\star}(s,a)/\lambda\Big)

(2.3)

Furthermore, the maximizer is the Gibbs/softmax distribution

\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\big(Q^{\star}(s,a)/\lambda\big)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathsf{A}}\exp\!\big(Q^{\star}(s,a^{\prime})/\lambda\big)}.

(2.4)

Notice that as
\lambda\to 0
we recover the well-known Bellman equations (see e.g.
20
) since smooth maximum approximates maximum and (
2.4
) approximates a uniform distribution over
\arg\max
.
It is often convenient to write a “soft advantage”

A^{\star}(s,a)\;=\;Q^{\star}(s,a)-V^{\star}(s),

(2.5)

so that (
2.4
) becomes

\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)\;=\;\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}A^{\star}(s,a)\Big),

since

\sum_{a^{\prime}}\exp\!\big(Q^{\star}(s,a^{\prime})/\lambda\big)=\exp\!\big(V^{\star}(s)/\lambda\big)
.

Inverse Entropy-regularized Reinforcement Learning

In the entropy-regularized setting, we assume that the observed behavior is (near-) optimal for some unknown reward function

R^{\star}:\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{A}\;\rightarrow\;\mathbb{R}.

Let

\mathcal{M}_{0}=(\mathsf{S},\mathsf{A},P,\gamma,\lambda)

denote the known components of the MDP
\mathcal{M}
: the state space
\mathsf{S}
, action set
\mathsf{A}
, transition kernel
P(\cdot|s,a)
, discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and entropy weight (temperature)
\lambda>0
; see Section
2

(S_{1},A_{1})\ldots,(S_{N},A_{N})

\rho^{\star}(s,a):=d_{\pi^{\star}}(s)\,\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)

d_{\pi^{\star}}(s^{\prime})\;=\;\int_{\mathsf{S}}d_{\pi^{\star}}(s)\,p_{\pi^{\star}}(s^{\prime}\mid s)\,ds\qquad\text{for all }s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S},

with

p_{\pi^{\star}}(s^{\prime}\mid s):=\sum_{a}\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)\,P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a).

Remark 3.1
.

In much of the reinforcement learning literature, the
discounted
occupancy measure
of a policy
\pi^{\star}
is defined by

d_{\pi^{\star}}^{\mathrm{disc}}(s)\;:=\;(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,\mu_{t}(s),

where the marginal densities
\mu_{t}
are computed starting from a fixed initial

\mu_{0}
for
S_{0}
and evolving under

.
It is important to note that, in our setting, the density

is assumed to be an

of the

P_{\pi^{\star}}.

In general, these two objects do
not
coincide.
Equality would require a specific compatibility between the initial
distribution
\mu_{0}
, the kernel
P_{\pi^{\star}}
, and the discount
factor
\gamma.

For example, if
\mu_{0}=d_{\pi^{\star}}^{\mathrm{disc}}
then we have

\displaystyle\int d_{\pi^{\star}}^{\mathrm{disc}}(s)p_{\pi^{\star}}(s^{\prime}\mid s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int(1-\gamma)\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}\mu_{t}(s)\,p_{\pi^{\star}}(s^{\prime}\mid s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big[d_{\pi^{\star}}^{\mathrm{disc}}(s^{\prime})-(1-\gamma)\mu_{0}(s^{\prime})\Big]=d_{\pi^{\star}}^{\mathrm{disc}}(s^{\prime}).

Our goal is to recover a reward function
R^{\star}:\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{A}\to\mathbb{R}
,
or a family of reward functions such that the optimal soft policy induced by some reward
R

\pi_{R}^{\star}(a\mid s)\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\left(Q_{R}^{\star}(s,a)/\lambda\right)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathsf{A}}\exp\!\left(Q_{R}^{\star}(s,a^{\prime})/\lambda\right)},

with
Q_{R}^{\star}(s,a)\;=\;R(s,a)+\gamma PV^{\star}(s,a)
and

defined in (
2.3
)
matches the expert action distribution
\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)
(at least on the support of

\pi^{\star}_{R}(\cdot\mid s)\;\approx\;\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid s),\quad\forall s\text{ with }d_{\pi^{\star}}(s)>0.

A natural approach is to maximize the log-likelihood of the expert samples under the candidate optimal policy:

\hat{R}\;\in\;\arg\max_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log\pi^{\star}_{R}(A_{t}\mid S_{t})\;-\;\Omega(R),

where
\Omega(R)
is a regularizer to ensure identifiability or to encourage smoothness or sparsity of the learned reward and
\mathcal{R}
is some reward class. In this paper, we use a different approach based on more explicit relation between
\pi^{\star}_{R}
and
R
in the Entropy-regularized RL.

3.1
Shaping invariance

We first note that the reward is identifiable only up to potential-based shaping:

R^{\prime}(s,a)=R(s,a)+\gamma Pf(s,a)-f(s),

for some shaping function
f:\mathsf{S}\to\mathbb{R}

This transformation leaves the induced optimal policy

Define

Then the triple

Hence

is the optimal soft policy for the MDP

It follows from Proposition

Consider the least–squares objective

Q^{\star}(s,a)=f^{\star}(s)+r_{a}(s),\quad R_{LS}^{\star}(s,a)=r_{a}(s)+\big(B_{a}f^{\star}\big)(s).

3.2
Least squares IRL via behaviour cloning

Consider a class of candidate policies
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}\;\subseteq\;\Big\{\pi:\mathsf{S}\to\Delta(\mathsf{A})\;\big|\;\pi(\cdot\mid s)\text{ measurable in }s\Big\}.

Based on observations from the uniformly ergodic Markov chain
(S_{t},A_{t})_{t=1}^{N}
with the unique invariant distribution
\rho^{\star}
,
the estimator of
\pi^{\star}
in the class
\mathcal{F}
is the
penalized maximum-likelihood estimator

\widehat{\pi}\;\in\;\arg\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{F}}\Bigg\{\widehat{L}_{N}(\pi)+\mathcal{R}(\pi)\Bigg\},

(3.4)

where

\widehat{L}_{N}(\pi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}-\log\pi(A_{t}\mid S_{t}),

and
\mathcal{R}(\pi)\geq 0
is a regularization term (e.g., a norm penalty, entropy regularization, or other complexity-control term). This estimator minimizes the
empirical negative log-likelihood
associated with the loss

\ell(\pi;S,A)=-\log\pi(A\mid S),

which is exactly the conditional log-loss used in supervised multiclass classification.
The population counterpart of the empirical risk is

L(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{(S,A)\sim\rho^{\star}}[-\log\pi(A\mid S)]=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim d_{\pi^{\star}}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid S)\mid\pi(\cdot\mid S))\right]+\mathbb{E}_{S\sim d_{\pi^{\star}}}\!\left[\mathcal{H}(\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid S))\right],

(3.5)

where
\mathcal{H}(p)
is the Shannon entropy of
p
.
Since the entropy term does not depend on
\pi

is equivalent to minimizing

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim d_{\pi^{\star}}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}(\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid S)\mid\pi(\cdot\mid S))\right].

Thus, in the infinite-data limit, the minimizer of

is
\pi^{\star}
itself.
This approach is known as

•

The expert policy
\pi^{\star}
is estimated by supervised learning.

•

The model is trained to maximize the likelihood (equivalently, minimize the negative log-likelihood)
of observed expert actions given the observed states.

•

The regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\pi)
controls complexity, prevents overfitting, and can enforce desired structure,
such as entropy regularization or sparsity.

5
Extensions

Note that the least-squares objective (
3.2
)
can be interpreted as a classical regression problem in the Hilbert space

L^{2}(\rho)
with features given by the Bellman operators. To make this connection explicit, note that

(B_{a}f)(s)\;=\;f(s)-\gamma\,(P_{a}f)(s)\;=\;f(s)-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim P_{a}(\cdot\mid s)}[f(S^{\prime})],

so that for each
(s,a)
, the model is linear in the unknown function
f
.
We can view
-r_{a}(s)
as the regression target and
(B_{a}f)(s)
as the regression prediction

-r_{a}(s)\;=\;(B_{a}f)(s)\;+\;\varepsilon(s,a),

where
\varepsilon(s,a)
is the regression residual.
The least-squares criterion
J(f)
, see (
3.2
), then coincides with the mean squared error

J(f)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{(S,A)\sim\rho}\big[\varepsilon(S,A)^{2}\big],

which is minimized by the
L^{2}(\rho)
-projection of
-r_{a}(s)
onto the linear span of
\{B_{a}f\}
.

Projection of rewards onto a given basis.

Let
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{K}\}
be a fixed collection of basis functions

\psi_{k}:\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and let

\rho
be a reference distribution on state–action pairs.
Consider now the least–squares projection problem

\min_{f}\;\min_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\rho}\!\Big[\big(R_{f}(s,a)-\alpha_{1}\psi_{1}(s,a)-\cdots-\alpha_{K}\psi_{K}(s,a)\big)^{2}\Big].

To make the structure explicit, fix
f
and define the inner product

\langle g,h\rangle_{\rho}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\rho}[\,g(s,a)\,h(s,a)\,],\qquad\|g\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\;:=\;\langle g,g\rangle_{\rho}.

Let
R_{f}\in L^{2}(\rho)
be given, and consider the finite-dimensional subspace

\mathcal{V}\;:=\;\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{K}\}\;\subset\;L^{2}(\rho).

The inner minimization over
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K})
is the standard
least–squares approximation of
R_{f}
by elements of
\mathcal{V}
:

\min_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K}}\big\|R_{f}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\psi_{k}\big\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K})^{\top}
and write

\Psi(s,a)\;:=\;\big(\psi_{1}(s,a),\ldots,\psi_{K}(s,a)\big)^{\top}.

Define the Gram matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
and the vector

b(f)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
by

G_{ij}\;:=\;\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{\rho},\qquad b_{i}(f)\;:=\;\langle R_{f},\psi_{i}\rangle_{\rho},\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,K.

Then the normal equations for the inner problem are

G\,\alpha^{\star}(f)\;=\;b(f),

and, whenever
G
is invertible, the unique minimizer is

\alpha^{\star}(f)\;=\;G^{-1}b(f).

The corresponding projected reward is

\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}(s,a)\;:=\;\Psi(s,a)^{\top}G^{-1}b(f),

which is exactly the
L^{2}(\rho)
–orthogonal projection of
R_{f}
onto

\mathcal{V}
. Thus, the full problem
can be equivalently written as

\min_{f}\big\|R_{f}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\big\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2},

since for each fixed
f
the inner minimization over
\alpha
returns the
projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}
.
In other words, we search over all representable rewards
\{R_{f}\}
and pick the
one whose
L^{2}(\rho)
–projection onto the basis span

\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{K}\}
has the smallest
residual.
Geometrically,
ER-IRL
in this setting finds the reward
R_{f}
whose orthogonal
projection onto the feature space
\mathcal{V}
best explains the data, while
the residual
\|R_{f}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}
quantifies the part
of the reward that cannot be captured by the chosen basis functions.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathsf{S}\to\mathbb{R}

(e.g.
L^{2}(\mu)
), and define the linear operator

\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\rho),\qquad(\mathcal{B}f)(s,a)\;:=\;(B_{a}f)(s).

Then we may write

R_{f}\;=\;r+\mathcal{B}f,

where
r(s,a):=r_{a}(s)
.
Since
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}:L^{2}(\rho)\to L^{2}(\rho)
is the orthogonal
projection onto the finite–dimensional subspace

\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{K}\}
, it is a linear,
self–adjoint, idempotent operator.
Hence

R_{f}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\;=\;(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}})(r+\mathcal{B}f)\;=\;c+\mathcal{T}f,

where

c\;:=\;(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}})r
and
\mathcal{T}\;:=\;(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}})\mathcal{B}.

The objective can then be written as

\big\|R_{f}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\big\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\;=\;\|c+\mathcal{T}f\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2},

which is a quadratic functional in
f
:

\|c+\mathcal{T}f\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\;=\;\|c\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}+2\langle c,\mathcal{T}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\langle\mathcal{T}f,\mathcal{T}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

The first–order optimality condition (in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
)
is

\mathcal{T}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}f^{\star}\;=\;-\,\mathcal{T}^{\ast}c,

where
\mathcal{T}^{\ast}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{T}
.
If the operator
\mathcal{T}^{\ast}\mathcal{T}
is injective with bounded
inverse on its range, there is a unique solution

f^{\star}\;=\;-(\mathcal{T}^{\ast}\mathcal{T})^{-1}\mathcal{T}^{\ast}c,

which minimizes
\|R_{f}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}
.

In the finite–dimensional case where
f
is parameterized, say

f_{\theta}(s)=\phi(s)^{\top}\theta
, the operator equation above reduces to a classical
quadratic optimization problem in
\theta
. Writing the objective as

\|c+\mathcal{T}f_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\;=\;\theta^{\top}H\theta+2\,\theta^{\top}g+\text{const},

for some matrix
H
and vector
g
determined by
\phi
,
r
,
\mathcal{B}
,
and
\{\psi_{k}\}
, the minimizer is given by the familiar normal equations

H\,\theta^{\star}\;=\;-\,g

with the solution

\theta^{\star}\;=\;-\,H^{-1}g,

provided that
H
is invertible.
Thus, even after introducing the projection onto the basis

\{\psi_{k}\}
, the
ER-IRL
projection problem remains a quadratic least–squares
problem and can be solved in closed form. The projection framework above generalizes the earlier setting in which we
considered only rewards
R_{f}(s,a)

without any additional basis expansion.
This corresponds exactly to the special case

\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\equiv 0,

so that the optimization problem reduces to

\min_{f}\|R_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}.

In this setting, the least–squares solution is determined solely by the
Bellman operators
\{B_{a}\}
and the soft policy–derived terms
r_{a}(s)
,
and the normal equations involve the operator

\sum_{a}B_{a}^{\top}W_{a}B_{a},

as discussed earlier.
Thus, the unrestricted problem without the
\psi_{k}
corresponds to projecting
onto the trivial subspace
\mathcal{V}=\{0\}
, while the augmented
problem projects
R_{f}
onto a nontrivial finite–dimensional subspace

\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{K}\}
.
The special case
\alpha=0
therefore provides the foundational structure:
reward recovery is entirely governed by the Bellman operators and the soft
policy.
Allowing
\alpha\neq 0
introduces additional degrees of freedom that can
capture reward components orthogonal to the Bellman–induced structure, leading
to a more expressive and flexible approximation scheme that includes the
original
ER-IRL
formulation as a strict subset. The theoretical analysis of the resulting statistical problem proceeds in close
analogy to the special case
\alpha=0
.
In that earlier setting, the reward class

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{Bell}}:=\big\{r_{a}(s)+(B_{a}f)(s)\big\}

is determined purely by the Bellman operators, and the least–squares estimator
is obtained by solving a quadratic problem of the form

\min_{f}\|R_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}.

The statistical properties of the estimator—consistency under appropriate
mixing conditions, high–probability error bounds, and minimax rates—follow
from classical Hilbert–space regression arguments applied to the linear
operator
f\mapsto B_{a}f
. When the reward is augmented by the finite set of basis functions

\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the estimator solves the problem

\min_{f}\;\big\|R_{f}-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}R_{f}\big\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{K}\}.

Because both
f\mapsto R_{f}
and the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}
are linear
operators, the objective is again a quadratic functional

\|c+\mathcal{T}f\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}

with
\mathcal{T}=(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{V}})\mathcal{B}
and hence the same structure as in the case
\alpha=0
.
Consequently, the statistical analysis carries over almost verbatim.
Thus, while the inclusion of basis functions
\{\psi_{k}\}
enlarges the reward
class, the key analytical ingredients and resulting statistical guarantees are
essentially the same as in the baseline case
\alpha=0
.

Bayesian interpretation

Consider the residuals

\varepsilon_{a}(s)\;:=\;(B_{a}f)(s)+r_{a}(s),\quad a\in\mathsf{A}

and assume a Gaussian likelihood with weights
W_{a}\succeq 0,
that is,

\varepsilon_{a}\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\ W_{a}^{-1}\big)

independently over
a\in\mathsf{A}
.
Place a Gaussian prior

f\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}(m,\ C),\,C^{-1}=\Lambda.

Stacking actions (
\mathcal{B}f:=(B_{a}f)_{a\in\mathsf{A}}
,
r:=(r_{a})_{a\in\mathsf{A}}
,
W:=\mathrm{diag}(W_{a\in\mathsf{A}})
), the likelihood is

r\mid f\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\big(-\mathcal{B}f,\ W^{-1}\big).

The negative log-posterior (up to constants) is given by

\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{B}f+r\|_{W}^{2}\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\|f-m\|_{\Lambda}^{2}.

Hence the MAP (Maximum a posteriori estimate)
f^{\star}
solves

\Big(\sum_{a}B_{a}^{\top}W_{a}B_{a}+\Lambda\Big)f^{\star}\;=\;-\sum_{a}B_{a}^{\top}W_{a}r_{a}\;+\;\Lambda m.

With a
flat prior
(
\Lambda=0
), this is exactly
ER-IRL
least-squares normal equation; with a
ridge
regularizer (
\Lambda=\eta I
), it is the regularized LS. The full posterior is Gaussian:

f\mid r\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\Big(\ \underbrace{(\mathcal{B}^{\top}W\mathcal{B}+\Lambda)^{-1}\big(\mathcal{B}^{\top}W(-r)+\Lambda m\big)}_{\text{posterior mean }=f^{\star}},\ \underbrace{(\mathcal{B}^{\top}W\mathcal{B}+\Lambda)^{-1}}_{\text{posterior cov.}}\ \Big).

Since
\widehat{R}_{a}=r_{a}+B_{a}f
is affine in
f
, its posterior is Gaussian:

\widehat{R}_{a}\mid r\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\Big(r_{a}+B_{a}f^{\star},\ \ B_{a}\,(\mathcal{B}^{\top}W\mathcal{B}+\Lambda)^{-1}\!B_{a}^{\top}\Big).

These covariances give
uncertainty bars
on the recovered reward.

Connection to the Bradley–Terry model.

Entropy–regularized IRL naturally leads to a probabilistic choice rule of the
form (
2.4
)
where
Q^{\star}
is the soft (entropy–regularized) action–value function.
This has exactly the same mathematical form as the

Bradley–Terry–Luce
(BTL) model (
5
), for discrete choice and pairwise
comparisons.
In the BTL model, each item
i
has a latent “utility”
u_{i}
, and the probability
that
i
is chosen over a set of alternatives
\mathcal{I}
is

P(i)\;=\;\frac{\exp(u_{i})}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\exp(u_{j})}.

Thus, conditional on a state
s
,
ER-IRL
induces a BTL choice model over actions
with utilities

u_{a}(s)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{\lambda}Q^{\star}(s,a).

Note that the
ER-IRL
soft
Q
–values
Q^{\star}(s,a)
encode these same preferences
up to a temperature parameter
\lambda

and the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid s)
is the BTL choice
distribution over actions induced by the utilities
\{u_{a}(s)\}
.
From the IRL perspective, we observe actions chosen by the expert and must infer
the underlying utilities
u_{a}(s)
(or equivalently
Q^{\star}(s,a)
and thus the
reward).
This is precisely analogous to estimating latent utility parameters in the
Bradley–Terry model from observed choices.
More deeply, the
ER-IRL
likelihood
is the log-likelihood of a sequence of contextual BTL choices, where the
context is the state
S_{t}
.

ER-IRL
therefore amounts to a contextual Bradley–Terry inversion
problem: from repeated state–dependent choices, infer the underlying
(preference–encoded) reward function. Finally, recall that in
ER-IRL
the soft advantage
r_{a}(s)
is proportional to the BTL utility
u_{a}(s)
.
The least–squares reward fitting step can therefore be interpreted as
recovering a reward function whose induced utilities best explain the observed
Bradley–Terry–style preference probabilities.
In this sense,
ER-IRL
can be viewed as a structured generalization of
the Bradley–Terry model in which utilities are constrained by Bellman
consistency and derived from an underlying reward function over state–action
pairs.

Proposition 6.3
.

In these notations

Change of measure gives

\mathsf{E}_{\pi_{2}}[\Delta e^{\Delta/\lambda}]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathsf{E}_{\pi_{2}}[\Delta^{2}]+\frac{\mathsf{E}_{\pi_{2},\tau}[\Delta^{3}(1-\tau)e^{\tau\Delta/\lambda}]}{\lambda^{2}},

r.v. independent of all other r.v.
