Learning with Multiple Correct Answers - A Trichotomy of Regret Bounds under Different Feedback Models
Alireza F. Pour, Farnam Mansouri, Shai Ben-David

Abstract
We study an online learning problem with multiple correct answers, where each instance admits a set of valid labels, and in each round the learner must output a valid label for the queried example. This setting is motivated by language generation tasks, in which a prompt may admit many acceptable completions, but not every completion is acceptable. We study this problem under three feedback models. For each model, we characterize the optimal mistake bound in the realizable setting using an appropriate combinatorial dimension. We then establish a trichotomy of regret bounds across the three models in the agnostic setting. Our results also imply sample complexity bounds for the batch setup that depend on the respective combinatorial dimensions.

Introduction

Many modern prediction tasks admit multiple correct outputs for a single input. A prominent example is language generation, where a prompt may have many acceptable completions that differ lexically or syntactically while expressing the same underlying content. This phenomenon arises in settings such as open-ended question answering, dialogue generation, and instruction following, where correctness is better viewed as membership in a set of valid responses rather than by a single target label
[
9
,
11
,
15
]
. Similar considerations arise in recommendation systems and information retrieval, where multiple outputs may equally satisfy the task requirements
[
7
,
13
]
. These settings call for learning formulations in which correctness is inherently set-valued rather than single-valued.

Learning models can be taxonomized along different axes. In this work we analyze and compare learning with multiple correct answers with respect to three such axes; Online vs Batch learning, Realizable vs Agnostic setups, and the type of feedback available to the learner.

This work considers an online learning setup in which each instance may admit multiple correct answers. In each round, the learner is presented with an example and its objective is to produce one correct answer. This setup naturally accommodates the following feedback models for the learner:

•

Mistake-unknown feedback
, where the learner observes only a single correct label in each round (as studied by
[
8
]
);

•

Mistake-known feedback
, where, in addition to a single correct label, the learner also observes whether its prediction was correct; and

•

Set-valued feedback
, where the full set of correct labels is revealed to the learner (as studied by
[
16
]
).

Distinguishing between feedback models is crucial, as different applications provide different forms of supervision. For example, large language model (LLM) training data often consist of prompts paired with a single demonstrated completion. During interaction with users, the model may additionally receive feedback indicating whether a generated completion is correct or acceptable, corresponding to the mistake-known setting.
Set-valued feedback also helps us understand the limits of a learner under maximal information feedback.

This work provides complete combinatorial (tree-based) characterizations of the optimal mistake bounds for any class in realizable case under all three feedback models, and establishes a novel trichotomy of regret bounds in the agnostic setting across these models. Our results also imply sample complexity bounds for the batch setup that depend on the combinatorial parameters of the online mistake bound characterizations, and apply also to infinite classes.

Related Works

Furthermore, we also show that in the agnostic set-valued setting the regret of a variety of hypothesis classes can be constant. There is no similar result in previous work.

Our Contributions

We summarize our contributions in the following.

•

We consider an online learning setup which unifies three feedback model for learning with multiple correct answers.

•

We characterize realizable online learnability under the three feedback models using combinatorial parameters
\mathsf{LDunknown}
,
\mathsf{LDknown}
, and
\mathsf{LDset}
. In particular, our realizable mistake-unknown result directly extends the work of
[
8
]
from finite to infinite hypothesis classes.

•

We show that in the agnostic setting, the regret of mistake-unknown multi-label learning can grow linearly in
T
, even for a hypothesis class of size
3
with
\mathsf{LDunknown}=1
. This renders the task of attaining meaningful regret bounds for general hypothesis classes impossible.

•

We show that the regret of agnostic mistake-known multi-label learning is always upper bounded by
O(\sqrt{T\log T\,\mathsf{LDknown}\,|\mathcal{Y}|})
. Existing results for multi-class learning also implies an
\Omega(\sqrt{T\,\mathsf{LDknown}})
lower bound for hypothesis classes with single correct label
[
5
]
.

•

Our proof of the regret bound for the agnostic mistake-unknown setup relies on the reduction to agnostic case
[
5
]
that uses the learners for the realizable case as
experts
to follow their prediction. Unlike full information feedback settings, we only learn whether the experts who recommended the learner’s predicted label were correct (rather than knowing the correctness of each expert). To overcome this lack of information, we borrow tools from the literature on bandits learning. The cost of this obstacle is a need to introduce an extra dependency on the size of the label set,
|\mathcal{Y}|
.

•

We show that the regret of agnostic set-valued multi-label learning for a variety of classes with
\mathsf{LDset}>1
(including the hypothesis class of size
3
mentioned above) admits a constant upper bound.
We also attain a

O\!\left(\sqrt{T\log T\,\mathsf{LDset}}\right)

upper bound for general hypothesis classes. Together with existing lower bounds for multi-class learning with a single correct label
[
5
]
, this shows that different hypothesis classes can exhibit different orders of regret growth with respect to
T
.

•

Across the three feedback models, we observe a trichotomy of regret bounds: linear in
T
,
O(\sqrt{T\log T})
, and constant.

•

We apply standard online-to-batch conversion techniques to all results obtained for the mistake-unknown and set-valued settings, yielding sample-complexity upper bounds for the corresponding batch learning problems.

Setup

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a domain set,
\mathcal{Y}
a finite set of labels and
T
a natural number. An adversary and a learner play a sequential game for
T
rounds. In each round
t\in[T]
, the adversary selects an instance
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
and a set of acceptable labels
S_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}

and reveals
x_{t}
to the learner. The learner makes a (potentially randomized) prediction
\hat{y}_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
and suffers loss

\bbold{1}[\hat{y}_{t}\notin S_{t}].

The learner’s goal is to minimize its cumulative loss over the
T
rounds.

We consider the following three feedback models:
For each round
t\in[T]
,

•

(Mistake-unknown).

In addition to choosing
(x_{t},S_{t})
, the adversary also selects a predetermined label
y_{t}\in S_{t}
and reveals it to the learner.

•

(Mistake-known).

In addition to revealing the predetermined label
y_{t}
as in the mistake-unknown case, the adversary also reveals a binary indicator specifying whether the learner made a mistake, namely
\bbold{1}[\hat{y}_{t}\notin S_{t}]
.

•

(Set-valued).

The adversary reveals the entire set
S_{t}
.

In all cases, the feedback is revealed only after the learner has chosen
\hat{y}_{t}
.

A multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is a set of functions (hypotheses) mapping
\mathcal{X}
to
2^{\mathcal{Y}}
. Denote by
\Pi(\mathcal{Y})
the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{Y}
.
An online learner
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z})^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
is a mapping that each time
t
, gets as input the past sequence of instances
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1}
and feedbacks
z_{1},\ldots,z_{t-1}
along with a new test point
x_{t}
and outputs a random variable
p_{\mathcal{A}}(t):=\mathcal{A}(x_{1},z_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1},z_{t-1})(x_{t})
over
\mathcal{Y}
. Depending on the feedback model,
\mathcal{Z}
is always chosen as
\mathcal{Y}
,
\mathcal{Y}\times\{0,1\}
, or
2^{\mathcal{Y}}
.

We say that the online multi-label learning problem satisfies the realizablity assumption if for some
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
and all
t\in[T]
we have
h^{*}(x_{t})=S_{t}
. We first consider studying of realizable multi-label learning where we study number of mistakes bound (cumulative loss) of the learner. In our study of realizable online multi-label learning, we only consider deterministic learning algorithms.

In the mistake-unknown setting, we have
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Y}
and for any
t
, the feedback
z_{t}
is a (single) label
y_{t}\in S_{t}
(where here
S_{t}=h^{*}(x_{t})
).

Definition 2.1

(Unknown Mistake Bound)
.

Unknown mistake bound of a deterministic learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and length
T\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{M}^{\unknown}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(x_{1},y_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{T},y_{T}\right):\\
\exists h^{*}\in\mathcal{H},\;y_{t}\in h^{*}(x_{t})\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin h^{*}(x_{t})\right]\right].

(2.1)

In the mistake-known setting, where
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Y}\times\{0,1\}
, the learner also observes as feedback whether it made a mistake. Formally, we have
z_{t}=(y_{t},b_{t})
where
b_{t}:=\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}]
, denotes the mistake bit.

Definition 2.2

(Known Mistake Bound)
.

Known mistake bound of a deterministic learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\{0,1\})^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and length
T\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{M}^{\known}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(x_{1},y_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{T},y_{T}\right):\\
\exists h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H},\;y_{t}\in h^{\star}\left(x_{t}\right)\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin h^{*}(x_{t})\right]\right],

(2.2)

Finally, in the set-valued domain, the learner is given full information. Particularly, we have
\mathcal{Z}=2^{\mathcal{Y}}
and at each time
t
,
z_{t}=S_{t}
and the entire label set is revealed.

Definition 2.3

(Set-Valued Mistake Bound)
.

Set-valued mistake bound of a deterministic learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times 2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and length
T\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{M}^{\set}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{h^{*}\in\mathcal{H},\,\left(x_{1},h^{*}(x_{1})\right),\ldots,\left(x_{T},h^{*}(x_{T})\right)}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin h^{*}(x_{t})\right]\right]

(2.3)

We will also consider more general agnostic online multi-label learning.
In this setting, we will define the
regret
of an online algorithm under the three different feedback settings we consider.

Definition 2.4

(Mistake-unknown Regret)
.

Mistake-unknown regret of a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to a multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and length
T\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{R}^{\unknown}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(x_{1},y_{1},S_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{T},y_{T},S_{T}\right):\\
y_{t}\in S_{t}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}]-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}\right]\right]

(2.4)

Definition 2.5

(Mistake-known Regret)
.

Mistake-known regret of a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\{0,1\})^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to a multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and length
T\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{R}^{\known}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(x_{1},y_{1},S_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{T},y_{T},S_{T}\right):\\
y_{t}\in S_{t}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}\right]-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}\right]\right]

(2.5)

Definition 2.6

(Set-valued Regret)
.

Set-valued regret of a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{X}\times 2^{\mathcal{Y}})^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to a multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and length
T\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{R}^{\set}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{\left(x_{1},S_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{T},S_{T}\right)}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}\right]-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}\right]\right]

(2.6)

Notations:

For any sequence
U=(u_{1},...,u_{m})
and
t\in[m]
we denote
U_{<t}:=(u_{1},...,u_{t-1})
, and
U_{\leq t}:=(u_{1},...,u_{t})
.

2.1
A Comparison Between Notions of Regret

[
8
]
introduce the following notion of regret for online mistake-unknown multi-label learning with respect to a multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
:

\sup_{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{T},y_{T})}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\Big(\bbold{1}\!\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin h(x_{t})\right]-\bbold{1}\!\left[y_{t}\notin h(x_{t})\right]\Big)\right].

Observe, however, the term
\bbold{1}\!\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin h(x_{t})\right]
measures the error of the algorithm
relative to the best hypothesis

h\in\mathcal{H}
, rather than with respect to the underlying true set of acceptable labels. In contrast, in the online setting we are interested in measuring the learner’s error with respect to the true loss sequence, which motivates Definition
2.4
.

As seen before, Definition
2.4
has the advantage of extending naturally to the mistake-known and set-valued settings. In contrast, regret notions defined relative to the best hypothesis do not admit such a direct generalization.

Furthermore, for single-label hypothesis classes
\mathcal{C}
(where each hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
maps
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
),
[
16
]
introduced the following notion of regret for online set-valued multi-label learning with respect to a

\sup_{(x_{1},S_{1}),\ldots,(x_{T},S_{T})}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\!\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}\right]-\inf_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\!\left[c(x_{t})\notin S_{t}\right]\right].

In this formulation, while the feedback is set-valued, the hypothesis class itself remains single-label. When multiple labels are acceptable for a given instance, it is therefore natural to consider hypothesis classes that may themselves assign multiple correct labels, as in our setup.

At first glance, an alternative formulation of regret to
Definition
2.4
would be

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{1},y_{1},S_{1}),\ldots,(x_{T},y_{T},S_{T}):\\
y_{t}\in S_{t}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\!\left[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}\right]\;-\;\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\!\left[h(x_{t})\nsubseteq S_{t}\right]\right].

(2.7)

The same principle of replacing “not equal (
\neq
) ” to “not a subset of (
\nsubseteq
)” can also be applied
to the mistake-known and set-valued feedback models. The following example shows
that this notion of regret makes the adversary overly powerful and can render the
feedback
y_{t}
completely non-informative.

Example 1
.

Consider the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{1},h_{2}\}
over the domain
\mathcal{X}=\{x\}
and
label set
\mathcal{Y}=[100]
, where
h_{1}(x)=\{1\}
and
h_{2}(x)=\{2\}
. Let
h^{*}
be chosen
uniformly at random from
\{h_{1},h_{2}\}
; without loss of generality, suppose

h^{*}=h_{1}
. In each round
t
, let
r
be drawn uniformly from
\{3,\ldots,100\}
,
and set
S_{t}=\{1,r\}
and
y_{t}=r
. For these choices of
S_{t}
, we have

\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\!\left[h(x_{t})\nsubseteq S_{t}\right]=0.

However, this choice of
S_{t}
and
y_{t}
makes the feedback
y_{t}
completely
non-informative under both the mistake-known and mistake-unknown models. In particular, in the mistake-unknown model, the probability of predicting
r
is

1/98
, while the probability of predicting
1
is
1/2
.
Consequently, the regret defined in (
2.7
) equals
T/2
.

Combinatorial Parameters

In online learning theory, combinatorial dimensions are often defined via trees. For each feedback model we consider, we introduce an associated tree-induced notion. We note that the combinatorial parameter we introduce for set-valued feedback model (Definition
3.1
) closely matches the set Littlestone dimension studied by
[
16
]
.

A set-valued tree
\mathcal{T}
is a complete
\mathcal{X}\times 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
valued,
\mathcal{Y}-
arry tree. Then, any path originating from root in this tree is defined by a sequence
\sigma=(\hat{y}_{1},...,\hat{y}_{t})\in\mathcal{Y}^{t}
. Denote
\nu(\sigma)=(\nu_{x}(\sigma),\nu_{S}(\sigma))
to be the final node in path
\sigma
.

Definition 3.1

(Set-Valued Littlestone Dimension)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a complete set-valued tree with depth
d
. We say
\mathcal{T}
is shattered by
\mathcal{H}
if, for any path
\sigma=(\hat{y}_{1},...,\hat{y}_{d})
from root to some leaf, there exists a
h_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
for each
t\in[d]
, we have

(i).

Consistency:

\nu_{S}(\sigma_{\leq t})=h_{\sigma}\bigl(\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t})\bigr)
,

(ii).

Mistake requirement:

\hat{y}_{t}\notin h_{\sigma}(\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t}))
.

The Littlestone dimension
\mathsf{LDset}
of
\mathcal{H}
in the set-valued online setting is then defined as the depth of the largest shattered tree.

It is worth observing that in the above definition,
\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t})
is the unlabeled instance sitting at the parent of node
\nu(\sigma_{\leq t})
.

A multi-label tree
\mathcal{T}
is a
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
valued,
\mathcal{Y}-
arry tree such that each node either has no children (a leaf) or
|\mathcal{Y}|
children labeled with each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Then, any path originating from root in this tree is defined by a sequence
\sigma=(\hat{y}_{1},...,\hat{y}_{t})\in\mathcal{Y}^{t}
. Denote
\nu(\sigma)=(\nu_{x}(\sigma),\nu_{y}(\sigma))
to be the final node in path
\sigma
.

Definition 3.2

(Mistake Known Multi-Label Littlestone Dimension)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a complete multi-label tree with depth
d
. We say
\mathcal{T}
is shattered by
\mathcal{H}
if, for any path
\sigma=(\hat{y}_{1},...,\hat{y}_{d})
from root to some leaf, there exists a
h_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
for each
t\in[d]
, we have

(i)

Consistency:

\nu_{y}(\sigma_{\leq t})\in h_{\sigma}\bigl(\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t})\bigr)
,

(ii)

Mistake requirement:

\hat{y}_{t}\notin h_{\sigma}(\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t}))
,

The Littlestone dimension
\mathsf{LDknown}
of
\mathcal{H}
in the mistake-known online setting is then defined as the depth of the largest shattered tree.

Definition 3.3

(Mistake Unknown Multi-Label Littlestone Dimension)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a multi-label tree and let

\mathsf{off}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
be a
mistake offset function
.
We say that
\mathcal{T}
is
d
-shattered
by
(\mathcal{H},\mathsf{off})
if for every root-to-leaf path

\sigma=(\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{T})
in
\mathcal{T}
, there exists a hypothesis

h_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{H}
such that:

(i)

Consistency:
For all
t\in[T],\,\nu_{y}(\sigma_{\leq t})\in h_{\sigma}\bigl(\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t})\bigr)
,

(ii)

Cumulative Mistake requirement:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}\left[\hat{y}_{t}\notin h_{\sigma}(\nu_{x}(\sigma_{<t}))\right]+\mathsf{off}(h_{\sigma})\;\geq\;d.

Specifically for any tree
\mathcal{T}
with depth
0
, we say
\mathcal{T}
is
d-
shattered by
(\mathcal{H},\mathsf{off})
if there exists
h_{\emptyset}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathsf{off}(h_{\emptyset})\geq d
.
The
mistake-unknown multi-label Littlestone dimension

\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H},\mathsf{off})

is the largest integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that there exists a
d
-shattered tree
by
(\mathcal{H},\mathsf{off})
. Moreover, we denote
\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H}):=\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H},{\mathbf{0}})
, where
{\mathbf{0}}(h)=0
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{off}(h)
accounts for the number of mistakes already incurred by
hypothesis
h
, reducing the remaining mistake budget available along any path.

Claim 3.4
.

For any multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and any offset function
\mathsf{off}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H},\mathsf{off})\geq\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathsf{off}(h)
.

Proof.

Take the tree
\mathcal{T}
of depth
0
that only has a root, which is also a leaf. It is obvious that
h^{\star}
with
\mathsf{off}(h^{\star})=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathsf{off}(h)
satisfies property (ii) of Definition
3.3
for the path
\sigma=\emptyset
. This concludes that
(\mathcal{H},\mathsf{off})
will
\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathsf{off}(h)
shatter
T
.
∎

d-shattered by

Consider any tree
\mathcal{T}

shattered by
\mathcal{H}

\sigma=(\hat{y}_{1},...,\hat{y}_{T})

Theorem 3.6
.

For any multi-label hypothesis class

5
Agnostic Online Multi-label Learning

Our first result in this section shows a separation between realizable and agnostic online learnability in the mistake-unknown setting by exhibiting a simple hypothesis class with a regret that always grows linearly in
T
. Moreover for the same class, we observe that the regret under the full feedback model of set-valued online learning is only constant. This raises the question of whether agnostic online learning is possible in the mistake-known setting which lies between the two in terms of the feedback information. Perhaps interestingly, we find that although the feedback is partial in the mistake-known setting, the regret is still always sub-linear and scales with
O(\sqrt{\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})|\mathcal{Y}|T\log(T)})
. Unlike the realizable learnability, this shows a strong gap between the two models of feedback in agnostic learnability. The upper bound on the regret in the mistake-known setting already implies a sub-linear regret for the set-valued agnostic online learning for any general class too. We however prove, using similar ideas,
that regret in set-valued setting can be upper bounded by
O(\sqrt{\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})T\log(T))}
with no dependence on the size of
\mathcal{Y}
.

We start by demonstrating a simple hypothesis class over a singleton domain and the label set

The same strategy, however, does not work in the mistake-known and set-valued setups, as the prediction of the learner at time

Our algorithm assigns a weight

that is initialized

For cases where the both the learner and the hypothesis agree on whether they made a mistake, the algorithm keeps the weight of such hypotheses constant.

We can then show that (i) the expectation of the sum of weights over all hypotheses is non-increasing, and (ii) that the expected sum of weights at round

must have scaled exponentially with the regret.

The key differences between our algorithm and that of
[

are
threefold: (i) our algorithm incorporates full knowledge of the true set of labels
S_{t}
;
(ii) in contrast to
[

(iii) while
[

]
show that the total sum of weights is non-increasing,
we show that its expectation is non-increasing.

5.2

In this section we show that for the mistake-known feedback model the regret in the agnostic setting is always sub-linear by constructing a learner that attains the following regret upper bound.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, and any
T\geq\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H})
, there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
such that

\mathrm{R}^{\known}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{H},T)\leq O\left(\sqrt{|\mathcal{Y}|T\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})\log(T)}\right).

To prove this theorem we will use the idea of reduction to realizable learning
[
5
]
. For any subset of size at most
\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})
we create a mistake-known SOA that only keeps in its history that subset. We aggregate SOAs over all such subsets to create a set of experts that give advice on which label to predict. We then use the advice given by experts to make a prediction. Observe that in contrast to the classic setting of prediction with expert advice, the learner will know the expert, i.e., the SOA, have made a mistake only if we follow its advice. We know that the mistake-known SOA needs to know whether it has made a mistake in the past while they lack this knowledge in rounds that they did not recommend the predicted label. Although this may seem to render the above-mentioned approach impossible, we will see that the only thing we require is that one of the SOAs will make a mistake only on the subset it has in its history and we can execute the SOAs by always feeding them a mistake bit of
1
.

The more important challenge of the lack of full information brings is that classic tools for prediction with expert advice rely on knowing for every expert their mistake history. Therefore, we will rely on the tools from the partial feedback setting studied in the bandit literature.

Our problem therefore is closer to the setting of non-stochastic bandits.
We therefore describe the setting we consider. The game plays between a learner
\mathcal{A}
and the adversary in
T

\{1,\ldots,K\}
of
K\in\mathbb{N}

of

g(t)=(g^{(1)}(t),\ldots,g^{(K)}(t))\in[0,1]^{K}
for the actions but does not reveal it to the learner. Each expert
E_{i}
(potentially receiving information from history) outputs probability distribution
p_{i}(t)=(p_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,p_{i}^{(K)})\in[0,1]^{K}
with
\sum_{j\in[K]}p_{i}^{(j)}=1
over the set of actions. The learner
\mathcal{A}
at each round uses the pieces of advice
p_{1}(t),\ldots,p_{N}(t)
from the experts, computes a probability distribution
p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)=(p_{\mathcal{A}}^{(1)},\ldots,p_{\mathcal{A}}^{(K)})\in[0,1]^{K}
with
\sum_{j\in[K]}p_{\mathcal{A}}^{(j)}=1
and draws an action
\hat{y}\sim p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)
. The adversary then only receives the reward
g^{(\hat{y})}(t)
. Define by

the uniform (randomized) expert such that its probability distribution

[

Let

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})
,

, and

the sequence of all ones of size

\mathsf{MKSOA}
be the Mistake Known SOA learner defined in Algorithm
1
. We will define a set
\mathcal{E}
of experts as follows.

Let
\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq[T]:|I|\leq\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H})\}
. For each set of indices
I\in\mathcal{I}
, we define an expert
E_{I}
that in round

predicts as
E_{I}(X_{<t},Y_{<t})(x_{t})=\mathsf{MKSOA}(X_{I_{<t}},Y_{I_{<t}},\mathbf{1}_{t-1})(x_{t})
, where
I_{<t}=\{i\in I:i<t\}
is the subset of indices in
I
that are smaller than
t
. In other words, at round
t
, the expert
E_{I}
only has in its memory the indices in
I
that appeared so far. Let
\mathcal{E}=\{E_{I}:I\in\mathcal{I}\}\cup\{E_{U}\}
, where
E_{U}
is the uniform expert that always outputs a uniform distribution over the label set
\mathcal{Y}
.

Denote
E_{I}^{(t)}:=E_{I}(X_{<t},Y_{<t})(x_{t})
. We will show that there exists an expert
E_{I^{*}}
such that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{I^{*}}^{(t)}\notin S_{t}]\leq\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H})+\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}].

We first show how this concludes the claimed regret upper bound. We show that this problem can be reduced to non-stochastic bandits with expert advice. The adversary at each time
t
, will (potentially randomly and based on the history) select a tuple
(x_{t},y_{t},S_{t})
. Each expert in
\mathcal{E}
defines a prediction and the learner is required to make a prediction
\hat{y}_{t}
based on the advice. The adversary then reveals the reward of choosing
\hat{y}_{t}
, i.e., it feeds back

. Observe crucially that the prediction of the experts at time

and the current instance

: the experts are SOAs that are only fed back the past history of instance and the mistake bit equal to

as the reward vector

with

. We know
\mathcal{E}
is a set of

The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
then uses the
\EXPfour
algorithm
[
1
]

T\leq\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|\ln(|\mathcal{E}|)}{e-1}
, we obviously have a mistake and thus regret bound of
T\leq O(\sqrt{|\mathcal{Y}|T\log(T)\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H})})
. Otherwise, by setting
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|\ln(|\mathcal{E}|)}{(e-1)T}}
in Lemma
5.3
we immediately get

\max_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\langle E_{I}^{(t)},g(t)\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{T}\langle p_{\mathcal{A}}(t),g(t)\rangle\leq 2\sqrt{(e-1)|\mathcal{Y}|T\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})\ln\left(\frac{eT}{\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})}\right)}.

On the other hand note that

\displaystyle\max_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\langle E_{I}^{(t)},g(t)\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{T}\langle p_{\mathcal{A}}(t),g(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{I}^{(t)}\in S_{t}]-\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\in S_{t}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}]\right]-\min_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{I}^{(t)}\notin S_{t}].

Therefore, we get that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}]\right]-\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}]\leq\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})+4\sqrt{|\mathcal{Y}|T\log(T)\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})},

as desired.

We turn to proving the existence of the claimed expert. Let
h^{*}\in\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}]
and
R\subset[T]
be the subset of indices on which

is correct. Clearly,
|R|\geq T-\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}]
. Consider the following thought experiment. For any
t\in[|R|]
let
R(t)\in[T]
denote the index at position
t
in
R
. If
\mathsf{MKSOA}(\emptyset,\emptyset,0)(x_{R(1)})\in S_{R(1)}
, then throw away
x_{R(1)},y_{R(1)}
and move to
R_{2}
. Then if
\mathsf{MKSOA}(\emptyset,\emptyset,0)(x_{R(2)})\in S_{R(2)}
again throw away
x_{R(2)},y_{R(2)}
and proceed. Let
i^{*}_{1}
be the first time in
R
such that
\mathsf{MKSOA}(\emptyset,\emptyset,0)(x_{R(i^{*}_{1})})\notin S_{R(i^{*}_{1})}
. We keep the instance
x_{R(i^{*}_{1})},y_{R(i^{*}_{1})}
in history and then increase the index to
R({i^{*}_{1}+1})
. We then observe
\mathsf{MKSOA}((x_{R(i^{*}_{1}+1)},y_{R(i^{*}_{1}+1)}),1)(x_{i^{*}_{1}})
and based on whether the prediction is correct we keep or throw it.

Formally, let
I^{*}(0)=\emptyset
and
n_{0}=0
. For
t\in[|R|]
, we observe
\mathsf{MKSOA}((X_{I^{*}(t-1)},Y_{I^{*}(t-1)}),\mathbf{1}_{n_{t}})(x_{t})
at time
R(t)
. If this prediction is in
S_{R(t)}
, we throw away
x_{R(t)},y_{R(t)}
, let
n_{t}=n_{t-1}
and
I^{*}(t)=I^{*}(t-1)
. Otherwise, we let
n_{t}=n_{t-1}+1
and let
I^{*}(t)=I^{*}(t-1)\cup R(t)
. We then move to
R(t+1)
and repeat this process until

. Let
I^{*}=I(|R|)
. Observe that this SOA will actually make a mistake on any
i^{*}\in I^{*}
and since

is realizable by

we must have
|I^{*}|\leq\mathsf{LDknown}(\mathcal{H})
. Therefore,
I^{*}\in\mathcal{I}
and hence
E_{I^{*}}\in\mathcal{E}
. In other words, the expert, i.e., the SOA mentioned above, that makes a mistake on every index in
|I^{*}|
and is correct on any instance in

is actually included in
\mathcal{E}
. Moreover, for this expert we have
\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{I^{*}}^{(t)}\notin S_{t}]\leq|I^{*}|+|[T]\setminus R|\leq\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H})+\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}]

5.3
Agnostic Online Learning Under Set-valued Feedback Model

In this section we focus on analysis of the regret in the set-valued agnostic online setting. Our first result in this section gives a characteristics of a family of hypothesis classes for which the regret is always at most logarithmic in the size of the hypothesis class. Our second result gives a general regret upper bound that scales
O(\sqrt{T\log(T)\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})})
, which compared to the result in Section
5.2
, is independent of label space size
|\mathcal{Y}|
and works even for infinite label spaces.

A family of hypothesis classes with constant regret.
In Section
5.1
, we established a constant set-valued regret bound for a
simple multi-label hypothesis class. Theorem
5.4
extends this result
by showing that the same algorithm achieves constant set-valued regret for a large family of
multi-label hypothesis classes. The analysis follows the same ideas as in part (ii) of
Theorem
5.4
. In particular, it suffices to show that the total sum of
weights remains non-increasing for this broader family of hypothesis classes. Proof of Theorem
5.5
is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For any finite-sized multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
such that

\forall h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\quad 2|h^{\prime}(x)\setminus h(x)|\leq|h(x)|,

the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
given in Algorithm
4
satisfies

\mathrm{R}^{\set}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\mathcal{H})\leq\log_{2}(|\mathcal{H}|).

Sub-linear regret bound independent of label set size.
In section
5.2
we showed a regret bound that is sub-linear but scales with
O(\sqrt{|\mathcal{Y}|})
. The following theorem highlights that in the set-valued setting the regret can be made independent of label set size. The proof of the following theorem follows techniques similar to those used in
[
16
]
, which in turn build on earlier proof techniques from
[
5
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

For any multi-label hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, and any
T\geq\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})
, there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
such that

\mathrm{R}^{\known}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{H},T)\leq O\left(\sqrt{T\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})\log(T)}\right)

For any subset
I\subseteq[T]
of size at most
\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})
we will create an expert
E_{I}
that predicts according to the prediction of the Set-valued SOA that has in its history only the past information revealed in rounds in
I
. Let
\mathcal{I}=\{I\subseteq[T]:|I|\leq\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})\}
. We then define
\mathcal{E}=\{E_{I}:I\in\mathcal{I}\}
be the set of all such experts which satisfies
|\mathcal{E}|\leq(eT/\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H}))^{\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})}
.

In contrast to the mistake-unknown setting, in the set-valued setting we have full information in the feedback. In other words, the learner observes the entire set of labels
S_{t}
after prediction and can determine for every expert whether they made a mistake (as opposed to the mistake-known where the mistake bit is only known for the experts that recommended the predicted label). Hence, we can leverage the tools from the prediction with expert advice literature to get tighter bounds.

Let
T,N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{N}\}
be a set of
N
experts such each expert
E_{i}
predicts a single label at each time
t
, which we denote by
E_{i}^{(t)}
. For
t\in[T],i\in[N]
denote by
b_{i}(t):=\bbold{1}[E_{i}^{(t)}\notin S_{t}]
the bit that indicates whether
E_{i}
made a mistake at time
t
. Let WMU be the algorithm that starts by setting a weight of
w_{i}(1)=1
to each expert. At each round
t\geq 2
the learner updates
w_{i}(t)=w_{i}(t-1)e^{-\eta b_{i-1}(t)}
. Let
Z_{t}=\sum_{i\in[N]}w_{i}(t)
. WMU then randomly draws an expert
E_{i}
with probability
w_{i}(t)/Z_{t}
and predicts according the the selected expert.

Lemma 5.6

(Theorem 21.11 of
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\eta=\sqrt{2\log(N)/T}
and assume
T>2\log(N)
. For the WMU algorithm defined above we have

\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{WMU}(t)\notin S_{t}]-\min_{i\in[N]}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{I}{(t)}\notin S_{t}]\leq\sqrt{2\log(N)T}.

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Note that similar to the arguments in the proof of Theorem
5.2
, we can prove that each expert defined above is a valid expert since the SOAs just make their prediction based on the past history of
X_{<t},Y_{<t},\mathcal{S}_{<t}
. Moreover, similarly, and from the guarantee that Set-valued SOA will make at most
\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})
mistakes on the longest realizable subsequence of
[T]
, we can see that there exists an expert
E_{I^{*}}
in the set
\mathcal{E}
defined above such that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{i^{*}}^{(t)}\notin S_{t}]\leq\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})+\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}].

Combining this with the guarantee of Lemma
5.6
implies that for the learner
\mathcal{A}
that applies WMU on these sets of experts we have

\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}]-\min_{E_{I}\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[E_{I}{(t)}\notin S_{t}]\leq\sqrt{2\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})T\ln\left(\frac{eT}{\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})}\right)}.

This implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[p_{\mathcal{A}}(t)\notin S_{t}]-\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bbold{1}[h(x_{t})\neq S_{t}]\leq\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})+\sqrt{2\mathsf{LDset}(\mathcal{H})T\ln\left(T\right)}

and concludes the proof.

6
A Batch Study of Learning from Multiple Correct Answers

We have so far studied the problem of learning from multiple correct answers in the online setting and under various feedback models. In this section, we show our online results can imply learnability in the different PAC settings too. We use standard Online-to-Batch conversion techniques and show the relevant combinatorial parameters defined in Section
3
can control the sample complexity of learning from multiple answers in the batch setting. We start with the main lemma that we exploit to turn our online learners into batch learners, the proof of which can be found in Appendix
C
. We then give formal definition of different Batch settings we consider.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\right)^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}
.
For any
U:=\{(x_{1},z_{1}),...,(x_{m},z_{m})\}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z})^{*}
define

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{o2b}}:\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\right)^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
as

\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\mathcal{A}_{o2b}(U)(x)\sim\Unif\big(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{m}(x)\big),\;\text{where }\;\forall t\in[m]:f_{t}(x):=\mathcal{A}(U_{<t})(x).

Then for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1]\times(0,1]
and
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over an i.i.d generated sample
U:=\{(x_{1},z_{1}),...,(x_{m},z_{m})\}
from
\mathcal{D}
we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}\!\left[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{o2b}}(U)\notin h^{*}(x)\right]\leq\frac{1+\sum_{t=1}^{m}\ell_{t}+12\log\left(\frac{2\log m}{\delta}\right)}{m},

where
\ell_{t}:=\bbold{1}\left[f_{t}\left(x_{t}\right)\notin h^{*}\left(x_{t}\right)\right]
.

We now give the definition of PAC learning from multiple correct answers.

Definition 6.2

(Realizable Multi-Label Learning, Definition 1 of
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a multi-label hypothesis class. We say that
\mathcal{H}
is
realizable multi-label learnable
if there exist function

m^{\unknown}:(0,1)\times(0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
, and a learner
\mathcal{A}:\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\right)^{*}\rightarrow\Pi(\mathcal{Y})^{\mathcal{X}}
such that for any
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
, and distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
realizable by
h^{*}
, any
(\varepsilon,\delta)\in(0,1)\times(0,1)

m\geq m^{\unknown}(\varepsilon,\delta)

1-\delta
over i.i.d. generated sample

from
\mathcal{D}
we have

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}\!\left[\mathcal{A}(U)(x)\notin h^{*}(x)\right]<\varepsilon.

The following corollary exhibits that the sample complexity of PAC learning with multiple labels in the realizable setting is controlled by
\mathsf{LDunknown}
. The proof simply follows by combining Lemma
C.1
and the mistake upper bound of
\mathsf{LDunknown}(\mathcal{H})
in Theorem
