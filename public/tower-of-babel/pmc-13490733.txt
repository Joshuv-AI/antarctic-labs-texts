Efficacy of targeted hepatocellular carcinoma therapy using modified response evaluation criteria in solid tumors and alpha-fetoprotein: A single center retrospective exploratory study
Authors: Kangqiang Lin, Lin Ye, Zhao Jiang, Renjian Li, Hao Shi, Yaqun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Backgrounds/AimsTo evaluate the combination of modified response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (mRECIST) with alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) in patients with intermediate-advanced hepatocellular carcinoma receiving combination therapy with a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) and a programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) inhibitor, focusing on stable disease (SD) patients with low AFP levels.MethodsWe analyzed 79 patients who received TKI plus PD-1 inhibitor combination therapy from 2018 to 2021. Based on AFP at 8 weeks, patients were divided into Group A (AFP ≥200 ng/mL, n = 26) and Group B (AFP <200 ng/mL, n = 53). Kaplan-Meier and Cox models assessed overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS).ResultsOnly Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage C independently predicted OS (hazard ratio [HR] 7.193, p < 0.001). For PFS, independent predictors included tumor diameter (HR 1.009, p = 0.025), mRECIST response (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.070), and AFP group (HR 0.474, p = 0.049). Virus type was excluded due to collinearity with antiviral therapy. Among SD patients, low AFP was associated with longer PFS than high AFP (24.0 vs. 3.0 months, p < 0.001). In Group B, low-AFP SD showed numerically longer PFS than partial response (24.0 vs. 15.0 months; HR 0.389; p = 0.116).ConclusionsThis exploratory analysis suggests that the prognostic value of AFP is largely mediated by tumor burden. While mRECIST response predicted PFS but not OS, low-AFP SD patients exhibited favorable outcomes in both PFS and OS. These findings indicate that integrating mRECIST with AFP may enhance response stratification, though prospective validation is necessary.

INTRODUCTION

Liver cancer is a malignancy with high incidence and mortality rates, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constituting 75%–85% of cases [1]. Surgical resection remains the most effective curative treatment for early-stage HCC [2-4]. Common treatment options for intermediate to advanced HCC include transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), immunotherapy (programmed cell death-protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 [PD-1/PD-L1] inhibitors), and molecular targeted therapy. These treatments are often combined in a multidisciplinary approach [4-6]. Regardless of the specific modality, accurate efficacy evaluation both before and during therapy is essential for: 1) reliably assessing clinical benefit and 2) guiding decisions on whether to continue or modify the current treatment regimen.

Currently, the efficacy of targeted therapy for HCC is evaluated worldwide primarily using modified response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (mRECIST) [7,8], which was adapted from the original RECIST. mRECIST determines treatment response based on changes in the diameter of target lesions. One rationale for adopting this design was to avoid over-diagnosis of progressive disease (PD), which could otherwise bias the overall response assessment. However, mRECIST may have inherent limitations in the following scenarios: 1) when tumors have irregular shapes or undergo non-uniform shrinkage after treatment, and 2) when evaluating molecular targeted agents whose primary mechanism is reducing tumor malignancy grade rather than inducing tumor shrinkage [7,8].

The mechanisms of action and corresponding efficacy manifestations of targeted agents, typified by sorafenib, mainly include [9-11]: 1) inhibition of tumor cell proliferation, which manifests as decreased or stable tumor diameter; and 2) blockade of angiogenesis, which manifests as changes in lesion density and, potentially, in diameter. Thus, tumor response to sorafenib is reflected not solely by changes in lesion size but also by changes in intratumoral cellularity or vascular density [12]. Clinically, it has been observed that among patients classified as having stable disease (SD) by mRECIST, those with stable or declining serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels often experience longer survival than those also classified as SD but with persistently rising AFP levels. Based on the known mechanisms of tumor response to sorafenib and other targeted agents, along with these clinical observations, we hypothesize that mRECIST may underestimate the therapeutic benefit in a subset of patients. Given this potential limitation of mRECIST, there is a need to identify complementary indicators to enable more objective and clinically applicable response assessments.

Serum AFP is a well-established tumor biomarker in HCC. Elevated serum AFP levels are common in HCC patients, and the AFP level correlates closely with tumor malignancy grade, with higher-grade tumors generally associated with higher AFP levels [13-15]. Studies evaluating mRECIST as a predictor of overall survival (OS) and a potential surrogate endpoint for objective response in intermediate-advanced HCC [16] have demonstrated that AFP is an independent prognostic factor within the mRECIST evaluation framework. AFP is currently the most widely used and readily available laboratory indicator of HCC malignancy grade, and it is routinely measurable in most hospitals, facilitating its clinical adoption and broad applicability.

Treatment efficacy in oncology can be broadly categorized into short-term and long-term outcomes based on the duration of survival benefit. Short-term efficacy and clinical benefit are typically reflected by surrogate indicators such as objective response rate (ORR) and time to progression [17-19], whereas long-term efficacy and definitive clinical benefit are determined by endpoint indicators such as OS and progression-free survival (PFS) [20-22]. The ultimate criterion for judging effective cancer treatment in clinical practice is whether the patient’s OS is prolonged—most commonly assessed through OS and PFS, the latter of which has shown moderate correlation with OS in advanced HCC (R = 0.84, hazard ratio [HR] ≤ 0.6) [20]. Accordingly, the present analysis employs these indicators to compare treatment benefits, with the goal of refining mRECIST-based assessments and potentially extending clinical benefits to a broader population of patients. Of note, the present study design does not presume that AFP serves exclusively as either a treatment response biomarker or a baseline prognostic variable; rather, we aim to explore the extent to which these two roles can be disentangled—and potentially integrated—within the mRECIST framework.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Patients with HCC who received targeted therapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University between July 2018 and July 2021 were consecutively enrolled. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University (Approval No.: YJSLL202173). All participating patients provided written informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

After excluding pregnancy, gonadal embryonal tumors, and active hepatitis, an AFP level that is progressively rising or consistently ≥ 200 ng/mL over 8 weeks serves as one of the diagnostic criteria for HCC. Additionally, the onset of the therapeutic effect of targeted agents and observable imaging changes typically occur within 8 weeks after treatment initiation [23]. Of note, Kawaoka et al. [23] employed the AFP ratio (8-week AFP/baseline AFP) for prognostic stratification. In the present study, due to the retrospective nature of the cohort, which systematically documented only qualitative AFP positivity, the 8-week absolute AFP value was used for group assignment.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Pathologically diagnosed “HCC” at Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B or C (intermediate-advanced). (2) AFP-positive HCC patients, defined as having a serum AFP level ≥ 200 ng/mL persisting for ≥ 8 weeks prior to treatment initiation (or ≥ 400 ng/mL for ≥ 4 weeks), with exclusions for pregnancy, gonadal embryonal tumors, and active hepatitis. All enrolled patients met the diagnostic criterion of AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL for at least 8 weeks before receiving targeted therapy. Therefore, the 8-week absolute AFP value used for group assignment was assessed against a known pre-treatment AFP level of at least 200 ng/mL. (3) Received targeted therapy for ≥ 2 months. (4) Underwent enhanced computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans and AFP testing pre- and post-operatively or during targeted therapy, allowing for evaluable assessment. (5) Liver function classified as Child-Pugh class A or B; BCLC stage B or C; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≤ 2; Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade ≤ 2.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis with other concurrent malignancies or non-intermediate-advanced HCC. (2) AFP-negative HCC patients. (3) Post-operative or treatment follow-up < 2 months. (4) Inability to take oral medication; CTCAE grade ≥ 3. (5) Concurrent active hepatitis or other conditions affecting AFP levels. (6) Missing AFP data during mRECIST evaluation in the same period.

Grouping

Treatment response was assessed by mRECIST at 8 weeks. AFP was measured at baseline and at 8 weeks. Patients were stratified into Group A (AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL, n = 26) and Group B (AFP < 200 ng/mL, n = 53) based on the 8-week AFP level. Complete response (CR), partial response (PR), SD, and PD outcomes (OS and PFS) were compared pairwise between the two groups. Specifically, SD in Group B (SB [(B]) was compared with PR in the overall cohort.

Treatment regimen and efficacy evaluation

All patients received a combination therapy of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) and PD-1 inhibitor. The TKIs included sorafenib, lenvatinib, apatinib, anlotinib, or their combinations, with the PD-1 inhibitor administered concurrently in all cases. For example, the initial oral dose of sorafenib was 400 mg twice daily. One treatment cycle was defined as 4 weeks, during which CT/MRI re-examinations and telephone follow-ups were conducted every 4 weeks until intolerable toxicity or disease progression necessitated drug discontinuation. Supportive care, including liver protection, choleretics, anti-allergy treatments, antiemetics, analgesics, and antihypertensives, was permitted during sorafenib/targeted therapy. Due to the frequent rise in bilirubin and transaminase levels following TACE, intensified hepatoprotective therapy was administered to maintain treatment adherence and continuity while minimizing liver function deterioration. For newly emerged clinical manifestations or signs attributable to adverse reactions from sorafenib/targeted therapy that severely impacted quality of life, the dose could be reduced (e.g., to 200 mg twice daily or 200 mg once daily) or temporarily suspended.

Efficacy evaluation: Intrahepatic tumor response was assessed using mRECIST [4,7]. ORR was defined as (CR + PR)/total cases × 100%. The disease control rate was defined as (CR + PR + SD)/total cases × 100%. PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of targeted therapy until disease progression or death from any cause; patients who were lost to follow-up or event-free at the data cutoff date were censored. Adverse events were evaluated according to CTCAE version 5.0.

Follow-up

Data on survival status, medication adherence, and adverse events were collected during follow-up visits at the Hepatobiliary Surgery Office of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. The follow-up period ranged from 0.5 to 1.5 years, with a data cutoff date of January 31, 2022. Throughout the targeted therapy, patients returned to the outpatient clinic or were hospitalized every 4 to 8 weeks for re-evaluation. This re-evaluation included serum AFP, complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, a coagulation profile, and contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the liver.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as number (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.

Cox proportional hazards models were employed for both univariate and multivariate analyses. To avoid overfitting due to the limited number of events (OS: 28 events; PFS: 44 events), simplified multivariate models were constructed. For OS, four core variables (BCLC stage, tumor diameter, mRECIST response, AFP group) were included, yielding an events-per-variable ratio of 7.0. For PFS, five core variables (tumor diameter, mRECIST response, AFP group, antiviral therapy, surgery history) were included, resulting in an events-per-variable ratio of 8.8. The virus type was excluded from the primary PFS model due to its collinearity with antiviral therapy (variance inflation factor [VIF] > 6; Supplementary Table 1). Multicollinearity was assessed using the VIF, with all VIF values being < 10 (Supplementary Table 1), indicating no severe multicollinearity. Full models (14 variables) are presented in Supplementary Table 2 and 3. Additional subgroup analyses, including OS based on objective response (ORR), PFS stratified by AFP level and mRECIST response, and a comparison between CR and SD with low AFP, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.) was utilized.

To evaluate the performance of the 200 ng/mL AFP threshold for stratifying PFS, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with the Youden index was performed, and sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative AFP cutoffs (100, 8.69, and 400 ng/mL) with the log-rank test.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared to Group A (AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL), Group B (AFP < 200 ng/mL) had a significantly higher proportion of patients with a history of surgical resection (43.4% vs. 19.2%, p = 0.035) and a higher proportion receiving antiviral therapy (66.0% vs. 88.5%, p = 0.034). Additionally, there was a significantly different distribution of mRECIST response categories (p = 0.005). No significant differences were observed in age, sex, Child-Pugh grade, BCLC stage, ECOG performance status, targeted drug type, virus type, tumor diameter, or TACE history (all p > 0.05). According to the AFP-based diagnostic inclusion criteria, all enrolled patients had a documented pre-treatment AFP level ≥ 200 ng/mL. Therefore, an 8-week AFP value below this threshold in Group B almost certainly indicates a treatment-related decline from the pre-treatment abnormal level.

Overall survival

Median OS was not reached in the CR group, and was 25.0 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 16.7–33.3) for the PR group, 12.0 months (95% CI 3.9–20.1) for the SD group, and 14.0 months (95% CI 10.6–17.4) for the PD group. The log-rank test revealed no statistically significant difference in OS among the four mRECIST response categories (p = 0.075) (Fig. 1A). Among the 17 patients with SD, those with low AFP (SD [B], n = 12) showed a trend toward longer OS compared to those with high AFP (SD [A], n = 5) (median OS 17.0 vs. 10.0 months; log-rank p = 0.181; HR 0.428, 95% CI 0.096–1.916; Fig. 1C).

In univariate Cox regression analysis, BCLC stage C (HR 6.884, p < 0.001), AFP < 200 ng/mL (HR 0.346, p = 0.005), and continuous AFP level (per 1-ng/mL increase: HR 1.000, p = 0.009) were significantly associated with OS. In the simplified multivariate model (Table 2), only BCLC stage C remained an independent predictor of OS (HR 7.193, 95% CI 2.613–19.797, p < 0.001). Tumor diameter, mRECIST response (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.209), and AFP group (HR 0.671, p = 0.444) did not reach statistical significance.

Progression-free survival

Median PFS was not reached in the CR group, and was 9.0 months (95% CI 4.8–13.2) for the PR group, 24.0 months (95% CI 5.2–42.8) for the SD group, and 6.0 months (95% CI 2.5–9.5) for the PD group. The log-rank test demonstrated a statistically significant difference in PFS among the four mRECIST response categories (p = 0.021) (Fig. 1B).

In univariate analysis, both mRECIST response and AFP group were significantly associated with PFS. In the simplified multivariate model (Table 2), independent predictors of PFS included: tumor diameter (per 1-mm increase: HR 1.009, 95% CI 1.001–1.016, p = 0.025) and AFP group (HR 0.474, 95% CI 0.225–0.995, p = 0.049). mRECIST response showed a statistical trend toward being an independent predictor of PFS (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.070), with PR and SD demonstrating significantly lower risks of progression compared to PD. Virus type was excluded from the primary model due to its collinearity with antiviral therapy (VIF > 6); in the revised model, antiviral therapy was not independently associated with PFS (HR 1.278, 95% CI 0.608–2.684, p = 0.518). A history of surgery showed a non-significant trend toward increased risk (HR 1.985, 95% CI 0.979–4.026, p = 0.057).

In a sensitivity analysis retaining virus type instead of antiviral therapy, the direction of the association was consistent with the primary model, and mRECIST response remained a significant predictor of PFS (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.042; Supplementary Table 4).

Subgroup analysis in patients with stable disease

Among the 17 patients with SD as defined by mRECIST, those with AFP < 200 ng/mL (SD [B], n = 12) had significantly longer PFS than those with AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL (SD [A], n = 5) (median PFS 24.0 vs. 3.0 months; log-rank p < 0.001; HR 0.103, 95% CI 0.015–0.732; Fig. 1D).

The primary analysis compared PFS between SD (B) (n = 12) and PR in Group B (PR [B]) (n = 23) within Group B. The censoring rate in the SD (B) group was 75.0%. Median PFS was 24.0 months (95% CI 1.1–46.9) for SD (B) versus 15.0 months (95% CI 2.7–27.3) for PR (B) (log-rank p = 0.116; HR 0.389, 95% CI 0.138–1.096; Fig. 1E).

An exploratory comparison of SD (B) with the overall PR group (n = 36, including patients from both Group A and Group B) showed a median PFS of 24.0 months for SD (B) versus 9.0 months for the overall PR group (log-rank p = 0.040; Fig. 1F). ROC analysis identified 8.69 ng/mL as the optimal AFP cutoff for predicting 1-year PFS, based on the Youden index, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.746 (95% CI 0.637–0.856, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 2). At this cutoff, sensitivity and specificity were 77.5% and 69.2%, respectively. At the 200 ng/mL threshold, sensitivity was 50.0% and specificity was 84.6%. Sensitivity analyses using alternative AFP cutoffs (100, 8.69, and 400 ng/mL) provided consistent prognostic stratification in the SD subgroup (all log-rank p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 5). These findings indicate that the prognostic value of AFP stratification in SD patients is robust across a range of clinically relevant thresholds.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study evaluated the utility of combining mRECIST with serum AFP dynamics to assess the efficacy of targeted therapy in HCC. By integrating radiological response assessment with serial biomarker measurements, we aimed to develop a more accurate method for evaluating treatment response. Our findings demonstrate a significantly improved characterization of patients with SD when assessed by both parameters, underscoring the potential of this integrated approach in HCC management. Prior research has established that AFP plays a crucial role in HCC pathogenesis, potentially promoting tumor progression by influencing hepatocyte proliferation and survival [24]. Specifically, elevated AFP is closely associated with liver inflammation, fibrosis, and changes in the tumor microenvironment, which may collectively decrease tumor cell sensitivity to therapy [25].

In the present study, mRECIST captured tumor biological behavior through imaging, while concurrent AFP dynamics provided complementary information, likely yielding a more comprehensive assessment of treatment efficacy. Our study introduces a novel integrative approach that combines mRECIST radiological criteria with dynamic AFP monitoring in a temporally synchronized manner. This represents an advancement over prior investigations that have examined these two parameters in parallel. The key innovation lies not only in the selection of the AFP cutoff (200 ng/mL) but also in the integration methodology, which captures therapeutic response within a clinically decisive 8-week window. Distinctiveness of the AFP Cutoff and Integration Strategy: Although an AFP level of 200 ng/mL is recognized in HCC diagnostic criteria, its application as a dynamic, temporally defined stratification tool within mRECIST-defined SD patients is a novel contribution of this study. Previous investigations have typically used AFP as a standalone prognostic marker or applied various cutoffs without linking them to a specific therapeutic response timeline. In contrast, our approach leverages this specific threshold to stratify SD patients into those with high biological aggressiveness (AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL) and those with more indolent disease biology (AFP < 200 ng/mL). This dual-parameter assessment reveals biological heterogeneity among SD patients: those with elevated AFP may exhibit biomarker progression despite radiographic stability, indicating a higher risk of subsequent clinical deterioration.

Mechanistic and clinical advancements over prior approaches: existing literature indicates that AFP promotes hepatocyte proliferation and influences the tumor microenvironment [24,25]. Building on this evidence, our study demonstrates that combining mRECIST with AFP dynamics may provide a more comprehensive reflection of tumor behavior. While mRECIST captures macroscopic changes in tumor vascularity and size, AFP elevation may indicate ongoing molecular resistance pathways or microenvironmental adaptations that are not immediately detectable by imaging. This integrated model reveals previously hidden heterogeneity within the SD category, which earlier approaches relying solely on imaging or infrequent AFP measurements could not easily discern.

Moreover, our method may improve upon earlier models by aligning biomarker assessment with the typical timeframe of targeted therapy response. Since both radiographic changes and AFP dynamics are most informative within the first 8 weeks after treatment initiation, their concurrent evaluation may provide clinicians with a real-time, complementary tool to identify non-responders more accurately and earlier. This is particularly relevant for molecularly targeted agents, where morphological stability on imaging may not fully reflect the underlying biological activity of the tumor. In summary, the novelty of this study lies in its unique integration protocol, which employs a standardized AFP cutoff applied within a defined therapeutic window to enhance mRECIST-based response assessment. This strategy may not only refine response classification but also provide an exploratory mechanistic rationale for discrepancies between imaging and biomarker profiles, thereby offering a more precise and clinically applicable framework for HCC management.

Our findings align with previously published reports. For example, dynamic changes in AFP have been shown to closely correlate with prognosis in HCC patients [26], which further supports our observations. Although some studies suggest a complex, non-linear relationship between AFP and tumor burden [27], our study aims to clarify the role of AFP within a combined evaluation framework by integrating radiological and biomarker assessments. This approach provides additional theoretical support for its clinical application. By adopting a multi-dimensional assessment strategy, our study emphasizes the potential value of combining imaging and biomarker evaluation in personalized HCC treatment. It is well established that over 80% of HCC cases arise in the setting of liver cirrhosis [28]. The inherent architectural distortion and hemodynamic alterations associated with cirrhosis can obscure or mimic intrahepatic tumors [28], adding considerable complexity to imaging-based evaluation. Atypical intrahepatic lesions, as defined by mRECIST, are non-enhancing areas that lack the characteristic intratumoral arterial enhancement pattern. These atypical features may be observed in well-differentiated tumors with immature neovascularization, steatotic or sclerotic variants, and poorly differentiated infiltrative tumors [29]. These atypical lesions include HCC with marginal arterial enhancement, where neovascularization is concentrated at the tumor periphery and is often accompanied by central ischemia or necrosis. Accordingly, dense lipiodol deposition within tumors on post-TACE CT scans should be regarded as necrosis rather than viable tumor tissue [30] and should be excluded from measurements of the longest viable tumor diameter [31]. Conversely, when residual or recurrent viable tumor and necrosis coexist and are irregularly mixed due to prior locoregional interventions, accurately identifying and delineating the viable tumor component can be particularly challenging.

Data suggest that distinct patterns of disease progression in HCC carry different prognostic implications [32,33]. Specifically, the development of new extrahepatic lesions and the occurrence of vascular invasion are biologically distinct progression events that contrast with the growth of pre-existing intrahepatic or extrahepatic lesions, as well as the emergence of new intrahepatic lesions. Post-progression survival is reported to be longer for patients with intrahepatic progression compared to those who develop new extrahepatic lesions or experience vascular invasion [32]. However, mRECIST evaluates all progression events equally, which may introduce inaccuracies in assessing the true survival benefit derived from targeted therapy.

In this retrospective analysis of 79 patients with intermediate to advanced HCC receiving targeted therapy, several key observations emerged regarding the prognostic value of combining mRECIST with AFP (Table 1, 2; Fig. 1). First, concerning the role of AFP as a surrogate marker for tumor burden, our univariate analysis demonstrated that both the AFP group (≥ 200 vs. < 200 ng/mL) and the continuous AFP level (per 1 ng/mL increase) were significantly associated with OS, which is consistent with previous reports (Table 2) [7,8]. However, after adjusting for BCLC stage and tumor diameter in the simplified multivariate model, these associations were no longer statistically significant (AFP group: HR 0.671, p = 0.444; continuous AFP level: HR 1.000, p = 0.312) (Table 2). This suggests that the prognostic information conveyed by AFP is largely mediated by tumor burden rather than representing an independent biological effect. This finding supports the concept that AFP primarily functions as a surrogate marker of tumor load, indicating that a post-treatment decline in AFP likely reflects treatment-induced tumor shrinkage [9,10].

Second, regarding the predictive value of mRECIST, our simplified multivariate model confirmed that mRECIST response exhibited a statistical trend toward independent prediction of PFS (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.070). Both PR and SD showed significantly lower risks of progression compared to PD (Table 2). This finding supports the utility of mRECIST for early efficacy assessment [4,11]. However, the mRECIST response did not independently predict OS (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.209) (Table 2). The discrepancy between the significant PFS findings and the non-significant OS results warrants specific consideration. This discordance may most plausibly be explained by the confounding effect of post-progression treatments [12]. In real-world clinical practice, patients who experience disease progression on first-line targeted therapy often receive subsequent lines of treatment. These may include alternative tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., switching from sorafenib to lenvatinib or regorafenib), salvage TACE, or immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combination regimens. Such post-progression therapies can significantly prolong OS, independent of the initial treatment response. This can dilute or mask any OS benefit attributable to the first-line regimen and the baseline stratification method. This phenomenon is well recognized in advanced HCC trials, where the availability of effective sequential therapies has made OS an increasingly challenging endpoint to interpret. In these cases, PFS has shown moderate to strong correlation with OS. Therefore, in the context of this exploratory study, PFS may serve as a more sensitive and appropriate endpoint for capturing the early-to-intermediate benefits of the combined mRECIST/AFP stratification strategy. A related interpretive question concerns the prognostic information conveyed by the 8-week AFP level—specifically, whether it serves primarily as a static marker of baseline tumor burden or as a dynamic indicator of treatment response.

If the 8-week AFP level served exclusively as a surrogate for baseline tumor burden, its prognostic value should largely disappear among patients with comparable tumor status. Our data provide a direct test of this premise: within the mRECIST-defined SD subgroup, where tumor diameter is radiographically stable by definition, AFP retained a strong and independent capacity to stratify PFS (HR 0.103, p < 0.001). This finding is challenging to attribute solely to baseline tumor characteristics and suggests that the 8-week AFP value captures treatment-induced biological changes that are not immediately visible on imaging. Conversely, if AFP reflected only treatment response, it should retain independent prognostic significance in the OS model after full adjustment—which it did not (p = 0.444). The attenuation of AFP’s prognostic value in the OS analysis is consistent with the confounding effects of post-progression therapies and the natural history of underlying cirrhosis, which can obscure the signal of an early treatment-response biomarker. Taken together, these observations support a model in which the 8-week AFP level conveys information along a continuum: it partly reflects pre-treatment tumor burden and partly reflects the biological response achieved during the initial weeks of therapy. The SD subgroup, by fixing the radiographic component, effectively isolates the treatment-response component, revealing its prognostic significance. While the relative contribution of each component cannot be definitively disentangled in this small retrospective dataset, the existence of both is supported by the data. This framework—that AFP in the era of targeted therapy plus immunotherapy functions as an integrated biomarker rather than being purely static or purely dynamic—may inform the design of future prospective studies involving serial AFP measurements.

Third, one of the most important findings of this study is the demonstration of marked heterogeneity within the mRECIST-defined SD category. Patients with low AFP (SD [B]) had significantly longer PFS than their high-AFP counterparts (SD [A]) (24.0 vs. 3.0 months; p < 0.001; HR 0.103; Fig. 1D), and also showed a trend toward longer OS (median OS 17.0 vs. 10.0 months; p = 0.181; HR 0.428, 95% CI 0.096–1.916; Fig. 1C), further supporting the biological distinction within this group. In the primary analysis restricted to Group B, a biologically comparable population, SD (B) demonstrated a clinically meaningful prolongation of PFS relative to PR (B) (24.0 vs. 15.0 months; HR 0.389; p = 0.116; Fig. 1E). The high censoring rate in the SD (B) group limited statistical power, likely explaining why this 61% risk reduction did not achieve formal significance. An exploratory comparison of SD (B) with the overall PR group (including patients from both Group A and Group B) showed a statistically significant difference (24.0 vs. 9.0 months; p = 0.040; Fig. 1F), although this comparison is confounded by the inclusion of high-AFP patients from Group A. Collectively, these data suggest that low-AFP SD patients may represent a biologically favorable subgroup with survival outcomes that are at least comparable to, and potentially better than, those of patients achieving a PR. This finding has important clinical implications: low-AFP SD patients may not require premature modification or discontinuation of their current treatment regimen, and incorporating AFP into mRECIST-based response assessments could help prevent unnecessary and potentially harmful treatment changes. An important factor to consider when interpreting the superior PFS observed in the SD (B) group is the baseline imbalance in surgical history between Group A and Group B. As shown in Table 1, Group B patients had a significantly higher rate of prior curative-intent surgical resection (43.4% vs. 19.2%, p = 0.035). A history of surgical resection is generally linked to more favorable tumor biology, including a smaller initial tumor burden, earlier-stage disease at diagnosis, and a less aggressive disease phenotype, all of which may contribute to a better prognosis independent of subsequent targeted therapy. Although our simplified multivariate Cox model for PFS included surgical history as a covariate, it did not identify it as a statistically significant independent predictor (HR 1.985, 95% CI 0.979–4.026, p = 0.057). The wide confidence interval and marginal p-value suggest that the model may have been underpowered to fully account for this potential confounder. Therefore, the possibility that the favorable PFS observed in the SD (B) subgroup is partly due to inherently more favorable tumor biology, rather than solely the combined mRECIST/AFP stratification, cannot be entirely dismissed. This residual confounding underscores the exploratory nature of our subgroup findings and highlights the need for prospective studies with balanced baseline characteristics and larger sample sizes. Moreover, in a sensitivity analysis that retained virus type instead of antiviral therapy, surgery history emerged as a statistically significant predictor of PFS (HR 2.066, 95% CI 1.024–4.172, p = 0.043; Supplementary Table 4). This finding supports the idea that patients with a surgical history may represent a biologically distinct subgroup with more favorable tumor biology. However, given the borderline significance in the primary model (p = 0.057) and the limited sample size, this variable should be viewed as a significant confounder requiring adjustment rather than an independent prognostic factor.

Fourth, regarding the impact of viral hepatitis and antiviral therapy, our multivariate analysis identified antiviral therapy as a clinically relevant variable. Virus type (hepatitis B virus [HBV] vs. non-HBV) was excluded from the primary multivariate model due to its near-complete collinearity with antiviral therapy (VIF > 6; Supplementary Table 1), which resulted in unstable effect estimates when both variables were included simultaneously. In the revised model, antiviral therapy was not independently associated with PFS (HR 1.278, 95% CI 0.608–2.684, p = 0.518). A sensitivity analysis that substituted virus type for antiviral therapy confirmed the consistency of our findings, with mRECIST response remaining a significant predictor of PFS (global likelihood-ratio p = 0.042; Supplementary Table 4). However, the lack of statistical significance for both variables across the two models likely reflects limited statistical power due to the small number of non-HBV patients (n = 19). These findings collectively underscore the critical importance of adequate antiviral management in patients with HBV-related HCC. Interestingly, these variables were not significantly associated with OS, possibly because progression events are more directly linked to viral activity, while the effect on OS may be diluted by subsequent therapies administered after progression.

Synthesis of the findings: baseline clinical characteristics were compared between the two groups to assess comparability (Table 1). A multivariate Cox regression model was then used to evaluate the survival benefit of targeted therapy in HCC patients. Most baseline characteristics were reasonably well balanced between Group A (AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL) and Group B (AFP < 200 ng/mL), with no statistically significant differences observed in age (p = 0.092), sex (p = 0.153), Child-Pugh grade (p = 0.353), BCLC stage (p = 0.381), virus type (p = 0.113), or antiviral therapy rate (p = 0.034) (Table 1). As expected based on the grouping criteria, AFP levels differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1). In the subgroup of patients with SD, PFS was significantly longer in SD (B) than in SD (A) (log-rank p < 0.001; HR 0.103, 95% CI 0.015–0.732; Fig. 1D). Collectively, these findings suggest that mRECIST alone may be insufficient for accurately assessing the efficacy of targeted therapy in intermediate-advanced HCC, while combining mRECIST with AFP may enable more precise and clinically meaningful response stratification.

This study has several limitations that should be considered carefully. First, the single-center, retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as patient enrollment and treatment allocation may have been influenced by institutional protocols and physician preferences rather than randomization. This limitation reduces the generalizability of our findings to broader HCC populations treated in diverse clinical settings [34]. Unlike large multicenter trials such as the REFLECT study or prospective registries that capture more heterogeneous populations [35], our cohort may not fully represent the full spectrum of HCC patients, particularly those with rare etiologies or atypical clinical presentations.

Second, our study included patients who received various targeted agents (e.g., sorafenib, lenvatinib, apatinib), introducing heterogeneity in both mechanisms of action and therapeutic efficacy. While this reflects real-world clinical practice, where multiple TKIs are often used sequentially or interchangeably, it complicates the evaluation of the combined mRECIST/AFP assessment method across different drugs. In vitro studies have demonstrated that HCC cell lines exhibit significantly different sensitivities to various TKIs, with distinct transcriptomic profiles linked to responsiveness to sorafenib, lenvatinib, and regorafenib [36].

This biological heterogeneity, combined with the varying clinical potency of these agents observed in clinical trials, may complicate our assessment of the universal applicability of this evaluation method across all targeted therapies.

Additionally, the potential confounding effects of baseline liver function and hepatitis activity on AFP dynamics present a significant limitation. Although we adjusted for basic liver function parameters, residual confounding may remain due to fluctuations in hepatic reserve and inflammatory activity, particularly in patients with underlying viral hepatitis. Recent evidence suggests that hepatitis activity can influence AFP expression independently of tumor response [37]. Moreover, HCC patients with viral etiologies may exhibit different response patterns to targeted therapies compared to those with non-viral etiologies. This could potentially interact with AFP dynamics in ways that our study was not statistically powered to detect. Fourth, the lack of standardized imaging re-evaluation protocols raises concerns about interobserver variability in mRECIST assessments. Unlike clinical trials that employ blinded independent central review to minimize assessment bias [38], our radiological evaluations were conducted by institutional radiologists, but no formal inter-reader consistency measures were implemented.

This is particularly relevant for mRECIST, which relies on subjective interpretation of viable tumor enhancement and requires expertise to differentiate treatment-related inflammatory changes from true tumor progression. The documented limitations of conventional MRI-based criteria in accurately capturing therapeutic response, especially in the setting of tumor heterogeneity [39], further underscore this concern. Future studies would benefit from implementing deep learning-based approaches, which have shown promise in automating tumor segmentation and improving the objectivity of response assessment [39].

Fifth, the universal co-administration of PD-1 inhibitors in this cohort introduces potential confounding effects that could impact both AFP dynamics and mRECIST-based response classification. ICI therapy can independently influence serum AFP levels due to immune-mediated hepatic inflammation, which means that transient AFP elevations may indicate immune activation rather than true tumor progression. This phenomenon is increasingly recognized in the literature, including the systematic review by Tian et al. [37]. Furthermore, ICI-specific response patterns, such as pseudoprogression and delayed tumor response, may complicate mRECIST assessment at the 8-week time point. This can potentially lead to misclassifying patients as having SD or even PD when they are actually benefiting clinically. While the uniform administration of immunotherapy across all patients ensures that between-group comparisons are not influenced by varying ICI exposure, the observed AFP dynamics and mRECIST response patterns should be interpreted as the combined effect of TKI and ICI therapy rather than TKI monotherapy alone. This limitation must be explicitly considered when applying our findings to TKI-only treatment contexts.

A major limitation of this study is the extremely small sample size in the pivotal subgroups. Specifically, the SD subgroup stratified by AFP level included only 5 patients in SD (A) (AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL) and 12 patients in SD (B) (AFP < 200 ng/mL). With such small numbers, a few individual cases can significantly skew the results, and the statistical power to detect genuine between-group differences is severely limited. Consequently, the observed PFS difference between SD (A) and SD (B), while statistically significant, may lack robustness and carries an elevated risk of both type I and type II errors. Furthermore, the overall cohort (n = 79) is modest in size, and the OS event count was low (n = 28). Thus, these subgroup findings must be regarded as strictly exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive evidence. They primarily serve to inform the design of future, adequately powered prospective studies. The high censoring rate in the SD (B) group (75.0%) further limited the statistical power of the primary Group B comparison, as reflected in the wide confidence interval for median PFS (1.1–46.9 months) (Fig. 1D). Similarly, the OS comparison between SD (A) and SD (B) was underpowered due to the limited number of patients and events, and the observed trend should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Additionally, the OS event count (28) was low; although we used a simplified model (events-per-variable ratio 7.0) to avoid overfitting, the analysis may still be underpowered to detect small effects. AFP was measured only at baseline and 8 weeks, and dynamic changes over time might provide additional prognostic information. The heterogeneity of targeted drugs (multiple agents and combinations) may have influenced outcomes, although we did not observe significant differences among drug types in the multivariate analysis. In the virus analysis, the number of patients without HBV was limited (n = 19), leading to unstable estimates. Therefore, virus type was excluded from the primary PFS multivariate model, and a sensitivity analysis was performed (Supplementary Table 4). Future large-scale, prospective, multi-center studies with standardized imaging protocols and longer follow-up are needed to validate our findings.

Another methodological consideration pertains to the use of the 8-week absolute AFP value rather than the AFP ratio (8-week/baseline) as the grouping variable. The study by Kawaoka et al. [23], which we cited as precedent, employed the AFP ratio, which would more directly capture the magnitude of treatment-related AFP decline relative to each patient’s pre-treatment baseline. Due to the lack of systematically recorded quantitative baseline AFP values for all patients in this retrospective cohort, we could not replicate the ratio-based approach. However, the study's inclusion criteria required all enrolled patients to meet the diagnostic criterion for AFP-positive HCC (i.e., AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL persisting for ≥ 8 weeks prior to treatment). Thus, a patient classified in the low-AFP group (AFP < 200 ng/mL) at 8 weeks likely experienced a meaningful decline from a previously documented abnormal baseline of at least 200 ng/mL, rather than representing a persistently low-AFP state. Conversely, a patient remaining in the high-AFP group (AFP ≥ 200 ng/mL) at 8 weeks likely indicates a failure to achieve substantial AFP reduction. This interpretation aligns with the observed PFS outcomes. Nevertheless, we fully acknowledge that the absolute AFP value cannot replace a formal AFP ratio or percentage change analysis. Future prospective studies should incorporate serial quantitative AFP measurements at baseline and post-treatment time points to enable a more precise characterization of AFP dynamics. The 200 ng/mL AFP threshold used in this study was adopted from established HCC diagnostic criteria rather than being derived from a formal data-driven optimization specific to treatment response stratification. To evaluate the appropriateness of this threshold, an ROC analysis was performed, which identified 8.69 ng/mL as the optimal cutoff according to the Youden index for discriminating 1-year PFS (AUC 0.746, 95% CI 0.637–0.856; Supplementary Fig. 2). At the 200 ng/mL threshold, specificity was high (84.6%) but sensitivity was moderate (50.0%), consistent with a conservative cutoff that prioritizes the identification of patients with a definitive AFP response while minimizing false positives. Sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs of 100, 8.69, and 400 ng/mL yielded consistent prognostic stratification in the SD subgroup (all log-rank p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 5), indicating that the primary findings are robust to the choice of threshold within this range. The 200 ng/mL cutoff is retained as the primary threshold due to its established clinical familiarity and conservative performance characteristics, which align with the exploratory nature of this study. The optimal AFP threshold for treatment response stratification in the TKI plus ICI era requires independent validation in larger, prospective cohorts.

Given these limitations, future studies should aim to enroll larger, ideally multi-center cohorts to enhance statistical power and generalizability. Further mechanistic investigations are warranted to elucidate the biological basis of AFP dynamics in response to targeted therapy.

In summary, this single-center, retrospective, exploratory study suggests that among patients with intermediate to advanced HCC receiving combination therapy with a TKI and a PD-1 inhibitor, the prognostic value of AFP is largely mediated by tumor burden. Meanwhile, mRECIST response has been confirmed as a meaningful predictor of PFS but not OS. Within the mRECIST-defined SD group, low AFP levels (< 200 ng/mL) identified a subgroup with notably favorable outcomes for both PFS and OS. However, due to the very small size of these subgroups and the high censoring rate in the low-AFP SD group, these observations are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. These hypothesis-generating findings highlight the potential for integrating AFP dynamics into mRECIST-based response assessments and provide a clear rationale for validation in future large-scale, prospective studies.

In conclusion, based on these exploratory findings, we hypothesize that incorporating serial AFP measurements may enable more refined prognostic stratification for intermediate-advanced HCC patients classified as SD by mRECIST. Specifically, low-AFP SD patients (SD [B]) exhibited a consistent trend across survival endpoints, showing significantly longer PFS than SD (A) and numerically longer PFS and OS compared to patients achieving PR within the same biological stratum. However, these observations are strictly hypothesis-generating given the very small sample size of the stratified SD subgroups and the single-center retrospective design. Prospective validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is essential before this integrated mRECIST/AFP approach can be recommended for routine clinical decision-making.
