Development of a novel 18F-labeled radiotracer targeting granzyme B for imaging early tumor responses to immunotherapy
Authors: Hongchuang Xu, Jingming Zhang, Zhuxin Wei, Xiao Wang, Min-Fu Yang, Xing Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunotherapy has emerged as a promising avenue for malignant tumors treatment, but methods to validate its in vivo antitumor activity remain limited. Granzyme B, a key mediator of immune-mediated cell death, is a potential biomarker for assessing immunotherapy efficacy. This study aimed to develop a radiotracer with high sensitivity and specificity for in vivo imaging of granzyme B.Methods10 granzyme B targeting inhibitors were synthesized and evaluated to improve binding affinity. The most promising compound was conjugated with 1,4,7-triazacyclononane-N,N',N"-triacetic acid chelator and radiolabeled with Al18F to generate radiotracer 18F-G1. Its imaging and pharmacokinetic properties were compared with those of the recently developed radiotracer 68Ga-grazytracer. 18F-G1 was applied to assess T cell-mediated and natural killer (NK) cell-mediated antitumor immunity. In addition, the cyclic guanosine monophosphate AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway-targeted therapies were also quantitatively evaluated using 18F-G1.Results18F-G1 was produced with high radiochemical purity of >98% through a simple radiolabeling procedure. It displayed a 4.2-fold improvement in granzyme B binding affinity compared with grazytracer. In the anti-programmed cell death protein-1 antibody-treated MC38 tumor model, 18F-G1 outperformed 68Ga-grazytracer with significantly higher tumor uptake (2.1-fold increase in maximum tumor uptake) and superior tumor-to-muscle contrast (7.46 vs 2.33). For NK cell-based therapy, 18F-G1 positron emission tomography (PET)/CT successfully monitored granzyme B release following NK-92MI cell injection, with peak signals observed 24 hours postinjection. 18F-G1 PET/CT detected robust immune activation in SR-717-treated tumors compared with other cGAS-STING pathway-targeted drug treatments, which correlated with the highest CD8+ T cell infiltration, granzyme B expression level and tumor growth inhibition.Conclusions18F-G1 is an effective granzyme B imaging radiotracer. Its superior imaging properties make it a promising tool for non-invasively evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of various types of immunotherapies with high sensitivity.Keywords: Immunotherapy, fMRI / PET, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Natural killer - NK

Granzyme B is a promising biomarker for cytotoxic immune activity, but current positron emission tomography (PET) tracers have limited sensitivity and clinical utility. A more reliable imaging tool is needed to support treatment decisions.

Background

In the era of cancer treatment, immunotherapy has marked a transformative milestone. By harnessing the body’s immune system, such as through immune checkpoint inhibitors that block the coinhibitory pathways of T-cell activation, immunotherapy has shown efficacy across various cancer types.1 However, the response rates varied across different tumor types,2,4 underscoring the need for tools to predict efficacy early and guide personalized treatment strategies.

Pathological staining of therapeutic targets such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) in tumor tissues provides valuable insights for subsequent targeted therapies.5 6 However, challenges like limited accessibility of biopsy samples and the heterogeneity of receptor expression within tumors often compromise the accuracy of these assessments. Traditional imaging modalities like CT and MRI, assess treatment efficacy based on macroscopic parameters like tumor volume and tissue density, but suffer from delayed feedback on therapeutic outcomes and the inability to recognize tumor pseudoprogression,7 8 potentially delaying optimal patient management.

Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging has emerged as a powerful non-invasive modality, capable of detecting molecular-level changes in tumor tissues using radiotracer targeting tumor-specific biomarkers.9 This makes PET imaging an indispensable tool for accurately evaluating treatment responses. Radiotracers such as those for programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1,10 11 CD8,12 OX-40,13 and granzyme B14 have shown significant potential for monitoring immunotherapy efficacy in preclinical and clinical studies. Among them, granzyme B, a serine protease released by CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, plays a pivotal role in inducing apoptosis in targeted cells15 and serves as both an indicator of immune activation and a predictor of therapeutic outcomes. Granzyme B-targeted PET imaging can therefore provide real-time insights into immunotherapy efficacy, aiding treatment evaluation and disease prognosis.

Previously, a linear peptide radiotracer (GZP) was radiolabeled with 68Ga for PET imaging of granzyme B and demonstrated the potential of immunotherapy response prediction.14 Recently, we developed a rigid tricyclic peptidomimetic granzyme B-targeting radiotracer, 68Ga-grazytracer, with enhanced in vivo stability and imaging contrast.16 This probe successfully monitored tumor responses to immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment and adoptive T cell transfer therapy in preclinical studies, and was validated for detecting immunotherapy efficacy in translational studies.16 17 Despite these advancements, current granzyme B-targeted radiotracers still have limitations. First, their relatively low granzyme B binding affinity results in modest imaging contrast, with tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-muscle ratios of less than 1 and 4, respectively, in preclinical studies. Second, the use of 68Ga-labeled radiotracers was constrained by the limited production capacity of 68Ga generators, which can supply material for only a few patients per day. Contrarily, 18F offers significant advantages, including cyclotron-based production for broader clinical accessibility, a longer half-life (110 min vs 68 min), and higher positron emission (97% vs 89%), all of which can enhance imaging sensitivity, spatial resolution, and clinical practicality.18 These limitations underscore the need for novel granzyme B targeting ligands with improved affinity, pharmacokinetics, and labeling strategies to further advance immunotherapy monitoring. In addition, current granzyme B-targeted PET imaging studies primarily focus on therapies involving T-cell activation,19 20 overlooking the potential of NK cells, which also release granzyme B and play a crucial role in cancer immunotherapies such as chimeric antigen receptor-NK therapy. The feasibility of granzyme B-targeted PET imaging for monitoring NK cell-based therapies remains to be explored.

In this study, we report the development of 18F-G1, a granzyme B-targeted radiotracer with improved affinity and pharmacokinetics. Designed to address the limitations of existing probes, 18F-G1 was optimized for in vivo imaging of granzyme B activity in both T cell- and NK cell-mediated therapies. PET imaging using 18F-G1 demonstrated superior tumor uptake, improved imaging contrast, and robust sensitivity for detecting early therapeutic responses, offering a promising framework for advancing precision oncology.

Methods

Chemicals and synthesis

1,4,7-triazacyclononane-N,N',N"-triacetic acid (NOTA-bis-tert-butyl ester) was purchased from Tanzhenbio (Nanchuang, China). All other chemicals were purchased from Bidepharm (Shanghai, China). Compound 2a−j and G1 were synthesized using solid phase synthesis and purified by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to reach >95% purity. The detailed procedures and characterization are described in the online supplemental supporting information.

Affinity determination

The affinity of ligands to granzyme B was determined using the fluorescence-based assay method.21 0.25 µg/mL recombinant human granzyme B protein (Abcam, ab285779), 100 µM substrate Acetyl-Ile-Glu-Pro-Asp-7-amido-4-methylcoumarin (Ac-IEPD-AMC) and different concentrations (100 pM to 10 µM) of granzyme B targeting ligands were coincubated in 20 µL assay buffer (50 mM HEPES, 10 mM CaCl2, pH 7.5) in 384 well PCR plate at 37°C for 1 hour shielded from the light. The fluorescence intensity of each well was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA) with excitation at 380 nm and emission at 460 nm. The enzyme inhibitory constant (Ki) was generated using the Cheng-Prusoff conversion. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, California, USA). Each assay was performed in triplicate.

Radiolabeling

For 68Ga radiolabeling, [68Ga]GaCl3 was obtained from a 68Ge/68Ga generator (maximum production 1.85 GBq, ITG, Germany) with 0.05 M HCl. 20 nmol grazytracer was added to the mixture of 300 µL 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5) and 370–629 MBq 68GaCl3. The mixtures were reacted for 10 min at 90°C and then purified using Sep-Pak C18 cartridges (Waters Corporation, USA). The radiochemical purity (RCP) of the 68Ga-grazytracer was determined using analytical radio-HPLC to be over 98%.

For 18F radiolabeling, 18F− in water was loaded onto a QMA cartridge (Waters Corporation, USA), then 0.4 mL of saline was used to elute the QMA cartridge to obtain concentrated 18F−. 30 nmol G1 was added to the mixture containing 25 µL of 0.5M potassium acid phthalate, 7 µL of 50 mM AlCl3 and 200 µL of 18F− in saline and maintained at room temperature for 2 min. 200 µL of ethanol was then added. The mixture was further reacted at 100°C for 10 min then purified using Sep-Pak C18 cartridges. The RCP of the radiotracers was determined using analytical radio-HPLC to be over 98%.

Determination of the partition coefficient

The partition coefficient was determined following a protocol from the literature, with slight modifications.22 2.0 mL of 1-octanol, 0.9 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (0.1 M, pH=7.4), and 0.1 mL of 18F-G1 (37–74 kBq) were combined in a 5 mL centrifuge tube. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min to ensure thorough mixing and then centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.1 mL samples were collected from each layer of the mixture. The collected samples were subjected to radioactivity measurement using a gamma counter (Hidex, Finland). The entire experiment was performed in triplicate. The partition coefficient (Log D) was calculated by dividing the average counts in 1-octanol by the average counts in PBS. The final value was reported as Log D±SD.

In vitro stability

18F-G1 was incubated with PBS or mouse serum at 37°C for 4 hours. Subsequently, the RCP of the radiotracer was determined using analytical radio-HPLC.

Cell culture and mouse models

MC38 murine colon carcinoma cell line, HT-29 human colon carcinoma cell line and HEK-293T cell line were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Typical Culture Collection (Shanghai, China). NK-92MI cell line was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (CL-0533). All cell lines were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. MC38, HT-29 and HEK-293T cell lines were cultured with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. NK-92 MI cell line was cultured with Minimum Essential Medium α (MEMα) medium containing 0.2 mM inositol, 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.02 mM folic acid, 100 U/mL recombinant IL-2, 12.5% horse serum, 12.5% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

All mice were obtained from the Animal Center at the Peking University First Hospital and acclimatized for at least 7 days before further experiments. 4-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were subcutaneously implanted with 1×107 MC38 cells in the left flank, and were used for treatment at 5 days postinoculation. 6-week-old male immunodeficient NXG mice were subcutaneously implanted with 1×107 HT-29 cells in the left flank, and were used for the following experiments when the xenografts reached 300–500 mm3.

Cytotoxicity test of NK-92MI

HT-29 cells used for killing assays were transduced with luciferase-GFP lentivirus (Hanbio Biotechnology, China), and the purity of 95–100% was used for subsequent experiments. For the cytotoxicity test, 5×103 target tumor cells (50 µL) were placed in a 96-well white flat bottom plate with triplicates and then co-cultured with corresponding ratios of NK-92MI cells (50 µL). The plates were centrifuged at 500 rpm for 3 min and then incubated for 24 hours or 48 hours in the CO2 incubator. Samples were treated with 1 µl D-luciferin (30 µg/mL) per 100 µl sample volume prior to measurement. The killing capacity of NK-92MI cells was calculated by measuring the luminescence for the residual live tumor cells using Varioskan Flash (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Toxicity test of 18F-G1

The toxicity of 18F-G1 was assessed using Kunming mice. 10 mice were used in the experiment with 4 mice divided into the control group. Mice in the experimental group were intravenously injected with 0.2 mL of 18F-G1 (18.5 MBq, 925 MBq/kg) via the tail vein, while mice in the control group received 0.2 mL of saline. The administered dose per mouse was scaled to 250 times that for a 70 kg person. Mice were observed over 21 days, physical information including diet, respiration, activity, defecation, skin, pain sensation and body weight was recorded every 2 days. At the end of the observation period, mice were sacrificed, blood samples were collected, centrifuged and used for aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) detection. In addition, major organs including heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney were harvested for HE staining.

Micro-PET imaging and biodistribution study of anti-PD-1 treated mice

Micro-PET scanning and image analysis were performed using small animal PET/CT scanner (Inliview 3000B, Novel Medical, China). 7 days after tumor inoculation, MC38 tumor-bearing C57BL/6J mice were intraperitoneally injected with PBS (vehicle control) or 200 µg anti-PD-1 antibody (clone RMP1-14, BE0146, Bio X Cell, USA) on days 0, 3 and 6. Subsequently, mice were performed on PET imaging at 30 min after intravenous administration of 7.4 MBq 68Ga-grazytracer or 18F-G1. PET images were analyzed, and the region of interest (ROI)-derived %ID/g values were calculated.

For the biodistribution study, mice were intravenously injected with 370 KBq 68Ga-grazytracer or 18F-G1 and sacrificed at 30 min p.i. Tumor and organs of interest were dissected and weighed. The radioactivity was measured using a gamma counter and calculated as the percentage uptake of injected activity per gram of tissue (%ID/g). For the blocking experiment, unlabeled G1 (50 mg/kg) was coinjected with 18F-G1.

Micro-PET imaging of cGAS-STING pathway targeted therapy

MC38 tumor-bearing C57BL/6J mice were intratumoral injected with 50 µL PBS containing 2% sodium alginate (vehicle control) or supplemented with an equivalent molar amount of SR-717 (250 µg) or TREX inhibitors (compound 211, 300 µg; compound 148, 321 µg) at day 0, 2, 4. 68Ga-grazytracer or 18F-G1 PET imaging was performed on day 5, images at 30 min postinjection (p.i.) were collected and the ROI-derived %ID/g values were calculated. Then the growth of the tumor was measured using a caliper every other day, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula volume=length × width2/2.

Micro-PET imaging of NK-92 MI cells therapy

HT-29 tumor-bearing NXG mice were intratumorally injected with 1×106 NK-92 MI cells, 1×106 HEK-293T cells (negative control) or same volume of PBS (vehicle control). On days 0 (baseline), 1 and 2, mice were subjected to micro-PET imaging of 68Ga-grazytracer or 18F-G1 at 30 min after injection and the ROI-derived %ID/g values were calculated.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

Fixed MC38 tumor tissue was embedded by paraffin and cut into 4 µm thick slices for further staining. Antigen retrieval was performed at 100°C in a pH 9 for 20 min, and then the slices were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin for 30 min at room temperature. For the immunohistochemistry staining study, a primary antibody against granzyme B (ab255598; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:200 dilution) was applied to the slices, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. A goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine was employed as the chromogen, and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Stained sections were scanned using a NanoZoomer-SQ pathological section scanner (Hamamatsu, Japan). For the immunofluorescence staining study, slices were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-CD8 antibody (ab217344; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:500 dilution). Subsequently, Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 10 min at room temperature. The slices were then restained with an anti-granzyme B antibody (ab255598, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:100 dilution) and its fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Finally, the slices were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and scanned using a Leica TCS-NT confocal microscope (Wetzlar, Heidelberg, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean values±SD. Significant differences of quantitative data were analyzed by conducting Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test depending on the number of treatment groups and distribution. Statistical significance was considered at a p<0.05.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of 18F-G1

A series of granzyme B-targeting inhibitors (2a-2j) was synthesized (online supplemental scheme S1–10) and characterized by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (online supplemental figures S1–S10). Their binding affinity to granzyme B was evaluated using a fluorescence-based assay (online supplemental table S1). The most potent inhibitor 2i with Ki of 4.5 nM was selected for further radiotracer development. It was conjugated with NOTA via solid-phase synthesis (online supplemental scheme S11). After cleavage from the resin, the final product G1 was purified using HPLC to achieve a purity of >95%. G1 was obtained with a reasonable yield (14%) and characterized by HRMS (online supplemental figure S11). G1 could be radiolabeled with 18F through a convenient procedure yielding >98% RCP, by heating the ligand with [18F]AlF in buffer at 100°C for 10 min. G1 demonstrated a 4.2-fold improvement in granzyme B binding affinity compared with the established radiotracer, grazytracer (Ki values: 7.3 nM vs 30.7 nM, figure 1A,B).

Molecular docking, energy minimization, and binding free energy calculations were conducted to explore the binding mechanisms of G1 and grazytracer with human granzyme B. The molecular docking result revealed that both compounds exhibited similar binding modes (online supplemental figure S12). The calculated binding free energy of G1 (−39.14 kcal/mol) was lower than that of grazytracer (−30.31 kcal/mol) (online supplemental table S2), indicating that G1 has a higher binding affinity for granzyme B. These findings were consistent with the experimental results.

The partition coefficient of 18F-G1 was determined to be −1.58±0.16 (Log D value±SD), indicating its hydrophilicity. In vitro and in vivo stability of 18F-G1 was evaluated using radio-HPLC. As shown in figure 1C and online supplemental figure S13, 18F-G1 remained intact after incubation in PBS or mouse serum at 37°C for 4 hours, and also remained stable in vivo at 2 hours p.i. The safety of 18F-G1 was further assessed in healthy Kunming mice. Mice were intravenously injected with an excess dose of 18F-G1 (18.5 MBq, 925 MBq/kg) and monitored for 18 days. Throughout the observation period, no significant differences in body weight were observed between the 18F-G1-treated group and the saline-treated control group (online supplemental figure S14). Similarly, blood biochemical parameters, including AST, ALT, and BUN, showed no significant differences between the two groups (figure 1D). HE staining analysis revealed no notable pathological changes in major organs including heart, liver, lung, spleen, and kidneys (online supplemental figure S15). Collectively, these results demonstrate the feasibility of using 18F-G1 for specific in vivo imaging of granzyme B with a favorable safety profile.

In vivo granzyme B imaging capacity of 18F-G1

We subsequently compared the in vivo granzyme B imaging capabilities of 18F-G1 and 68Ga-grazytracer in MC38 tumor-bearing immune-competent mice. Mice were treated with anti-PD-1 antibody to induce the release of granzyme B in the tumors on T cell activation. Micro-PET/CT imaging results revealed that tumors in anti-PD-1-treated mice exhibited significantly higher PET signals than those in PBS-treated controls (figure 2A). Quantification analysis showed that the tumor uptake of 18F-G1 in anti-PD-1 antibody treated group was 2.7 times higher than that of PBS-treated group (maximum tumor uptake: 2.21±0.70%ID/g vs 0.82±0.09%ID/g, p<0.01) (figure 2B). Immunohistochemistry staining further confirmed the upregulation of granzyme B following anti-PD-1 antibody treatment (figure 2A). A direct comparison of 18F-G1 and 68Ga-grazytracer in anti-PD-1-treated mice showed higher tumor uptake for 18F-G1. The tumor uptake of 18F-G1 was 2.1 times higher than that of 68Ga-grazytracer (maximum tumor uptake: 2.21±0.70%ID/g vs 1.05±0.25%ID/g, p<0.05) (figure 2B). The tumor-to-muscle ratio for 18F-G1 was 7.46±1.62, significantly higher than that of 68Ga-grazytracer (2.33±0.60) (figure 2C), demonstrating superior imaging contrast.

A biodistribution study was performed to further validate the above findings. The mice were divided into four groups: three groups were injected with 18F-G1, including the anti-PD-1 antibody treatment group, the PBS treatment group, and the unlabeled ligand blocking group; while the fourth group received anti-PD-1 antibody treatment and 68Ga-grazytracer injection. In the 18F-G1-injected groups, the tumor uptake in the anti-PD-1 antibody treatment group was 3.5 and 2.2 times higher than in the PBS treatment group and the blocking group, respectively (3.04±0.46% ID/g vs 0.86±0.10% ID/g and 1.35±0.30% ID/g) (figure 2D, online supplemental table S3). These results confirmed the granzyme B-targeting specificity of 18F-G1 in vivo. The biodistribution analysis in the anti-PD-1 antibody treatment group revealed that both 18F-G1 and 68Ga-grazytracer followed similar metabolic pathways, being primarily excreted via the urinary system (figure 2D, online supplemental table S3). Notably, the tumor uptake of 18F-G1 was 2.1 folds higher than that of 68Ga-grazytracer. Furthermore, the tumor-to-muscle ratio and tumor-to-blood ratio of 18F-G1 were 4.4 and 3.7 times higher than those of 68Ga-grazytracer, respectively (figure 2E,F). Overall, the biodistribution data were consistent with the imaging results, indicating that 18F-G1 exhibits better imaging contrast than 68Ga-grazytracer.

18F-G1 PET/CT imaging of NK cells-based therapy

Recent studies on granzyme B-targeted PET imaging studies have primarily focused on predicting the efficacy of treatments that activate cytotoxic T cells. However, beyond cytotoxic T cells, other kinds of immune cells, such as NK cells, can also release granzyme B to exert antitumor effect. These findings suggest the potential of granzyme B-targeted PET imaging for monitoring treatment response of NK cell-based therapies. A human NK cell line, NK-92MI, was used to validate this proposal. First, the tumor cell-killing ability of NK-92MI cells was evaluated in vitro. Human colorectal carcinoma cell line, HT-29, was incubated with NK-92MI cells at different ratios. A dose-dependent and time-dependent increase in cell death was observed, confirming the ability of NK-92MI cells to kill HT-29 cells (figure 3A). Next, the detection capability of 18F-G1 PET/CT was tested in vivo. NXG mice, which lack T cell, B cell, and NK cell immunity, were used to exclude interference from host-derived immune cells. NK-92MI cells were injected intratumorally to exert their antitumor effects, and 18F-G1 PET/CT imaging was performed before and after the injection (figure 3B). The results showed that there was no significant signal before the treatment (figure 3C). In the NK-92MI cells-treated group, an elevated signal was observed 1 day p.i., which returned to baseline by 2 days p.i. (figure 3D). In contrast, the PBS- and HEK-293T cells-treated group showed only minor fluctuations in tumor SUVmax values. Comparison of tumor uptake on day 1 revealed that the NK-92MI cell-treated group exhibited 2.04-fold and 1.60-fold higher uptake than the PBS-treated and HEK-293T cell-treated group, respectively (p<0.05) (figure 3E). Finally, 68Ga-grazytracer PET/CT was also employed to image the response to NK-92MI cell treatment. However, in contrast to 18F-G1 PET/CT, no obvious tumor signal was observed at 1 day p.i. Both the tumor uptake on day 1 and the tumor uptake ratio of day 1 relative to day 0 for 68Ga-grazytracer PET/CT were lower than those for 18F-G1 PET/CT (maximum tumor uptake: 0.50±0.14%ID/g vs 1.56±0.23%ID/g, p<0.01; uptake ratio: 1.41±0.07 vs 2.95±0.40, p<0.01;) (online supplemental figure S16). These findings confirmed the feasibility of using 18F-G1 PET/CT to monitor granzyme B released by NK cells, demonstrating its potential as a tool for evaluating NK cell-based therapies.

Micro-PET/CT imaging of immunotherapy targeting cGAS-STING pathway

Based on the above results, we further investigated the feasibility of using 18F-G1 PET/CT imaging to evaluate tumor response to immunotherapies involving different types of immune cells. The cyclic guanosine monophosphate AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) signaling pathway plays a critical role in the innate immune response to tumors.23 TREX1 inhibitors and STING agonists could activate the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to the release of type I interferon and proinflammatory cytokines, recruiting various types of immune cells into tumor microenvironment to exert antitumor effects24 25 (figure 4A). In this study, a STING agonist (SR-71726) and two TREX1 inhibitors (compound 148 and 21127) were selected for evaluation (figure 4B). MC38 tumor-bearing mice were injected intratumorally with equal doses of the compounds, followed by 68Ga-grazytracer or 18F-G1 PET/CT imaging to detect granzyme B release (figure 4C). As shown in figure 4D–G, the PET signal in the treatment groups was significantly higher than the control group, indicating immune activation. Moreover, 18F-G1 showed higher tumor uptake than 68Ga-grazytracer across all treatment groups, enabling clearer differentiation of granzyme B release levels. Among these groups, SR-717 exhibited the strongest immune activation, evidenced by the highest PET signal at the tumor site. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed this finding, showing the highest levels of CD8+ T cell infiltration and granzyme B expression in the tumors of the SR-717-treated group (figure 4H). These results were consistent with the micro-PET/CT imaging findings.

Discussion

Non-invasive imaging methods capable of detecting immunotherapy response may provide valuable insights into treatment efficacy, enabling more informed and timely adjustments to therapeutic strategies. Granzyme B has emerged as a promising biomarker for assessing the efficacy of immunotherapy, and radiotracers targeting granzyme B hold significant potential for monitoring treatment responses. In this study, we focused on the development and evaluation of 18F-G1, an optimized granzyme B-targeted radiotracer, as a tool for assessing immunotherapy outcomes. 10 granzyme B-targeting inhibitors were synthesized, and the most potent one was selected for PET imaging by conjugating with NOTA and radiolabeling with [18F]AlF. Compared with the early reported 68Ga-grazytracer,16 18F-G1 demonstrated several advantages. G1 exhibited a higher binding affinity to granzyme B than grazytracer. PET imaging studies showed that 18F-G1 provided superior imaging contrast over 68Ga-grazytracer. Biodistribution studies revealed similar pharmacokinetics for both radiotracers, but 18F-G1 achieved higher tumor uptake, resulting in significantly improved tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-muscle ratios. These enhancements enabled monitoring immunotherapy responses more accurately and sensitively.

The development of G1 provided new insights on the design of granzyme B inhibitors. While prior research has primarily targeted the P1–P4 positions within the Ac-IEPD-CHO scaffold,28,30 the P5 site received less attention. In this study, we designed 10 inhibitors with substitutions at the P5 site and investigated their potency. The results suggest that smaller substitutions at the P5 site enhance binding affinity more effectively, as reduced steric bulk allows better accommodation within the enzyme’s active site, promoting a more stable complex (online supplemental figure S12).

The application of 18F-G1 highlights its general utility in granzyme B-targeted PET imaging. In this study, 18F-G1 PET was effectively used to monitor immunotherapy responses involving T cell or NK cell activation. These findings expand the potential applications of granzyme B-targeted PET imaging across a wide range of immunotherapy modalities. This study also demonstrates that granzyme B-targeted imaging can serve as a valuable tool for monitoring the broad antitumor immune response triggered by targeting key immune-related pathways, such as the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in the activation of innate immune and the modulation of tumor microenvironment dynamics.

Limitations of this study should also be noted. Despite its improvements, the binding affinity of 18F-G1 (Ki=7.3 nM) remains suboptimal for highly sensitive imaging. Further structural optimization may help to further improve the PET imaging quality. This study was primarily conducted in preclinical models, and well-designed clinical trials are necessary to validate the diagnostic and prognostic utility of 18F-G1 in diverse patient populations.

Conclusion

18F-G1 represents a promising granzyme B-targeted PET imaging radiotracer with significant potential for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy. Future efforts should focus on optimizing its performance and extending its applications to clinical settings.
