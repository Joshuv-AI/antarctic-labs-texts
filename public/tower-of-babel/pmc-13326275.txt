Novel Technique for Salvaging an Embolized Balloon-Expandable Valve During Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement
Authors: Lida Koskina, Antony Kaliyadan, John Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSalvaging an embolized balloon-expandable valve (Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra) from the left atrium during transcatheter mitral valve replacement.Key Steps1) Secure the embolized valve in the left atrium. 2) Use multiple snares to crimp and snare the outflow of the valve into a more conical shape to facilitate valve retrieval. 3) Align the valve in the inferior vena cava to avoid obstruction. 4) Proceed with planned transcatheter mitral valve replacement. 5) Consider closure of the iatrogenic atrial septal defect (ASD).Potential PitfallsFailure to retrieve the valve from the left atrium may require emergent cardiotomy. Improper alignment or insecure deployment of the valve in the inferior vena cava can lead to obstruction or distal embolization. Transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) percutaneously repairs a damaged native or bioprosthetic mitral valve. Complications include valve misalignment, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, valve embolization, and delivery system failure.1

Few cases of delivery system failure have been reported; existing reports mainly involved balloon seal separation or rupture.2,3 Arai et al4 reported a case of delivery system shaft fracture that resulted in massive leakage and nondeployment of the valve during transcatheter aortic valve replacement.4

We present a unique case of delivery system failure due to shaft kinking, which led to partial deployment and atrial embolization of an Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra (S3U) valve during a TMVR procedure. We highlight a novel approach to salvage the embolized valve from the left atrium.

Case Summary

An 81- year old woman with prior history of coronary artery bypass grafting, ischemic cardiomyopathy, dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator and mitral valve replacement with a 25-mm Mosaic bioprosthetic valve presented with severe mitral regurgitation.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed severe bioprosthetic mitral regurgitation with no perivalvular leak (Video 1). Left heart catheterization showed stable severe multivessel coronary artery disease with patent saphenous vein graft–to–right posterior descending artery graft. The calculated Society of Thoracic Surgeons operative mortality risk was 8%, and percutaneous mitral valve-in-valve replacement was recommended.

Procedure Steps

Left radial artery access was obtained, and a 5-F pigtail catheter was positioned in the ascending aorta. Bilateral femoral venous access was obtained. Transseptal puncture was performed with a mid and posterior stick to allow for adequate height above the mitral valve. A Baylis pigtail wire was placed in the left atrium, the transseptal sheath was removed, and an Agilis catheter was placed in the left atrium. An exchange-length J-wire was advanced to the left ventricular (LV) apex through the bioprosthetic mitral valve and was followed with a pigtail catheter. We unsheathed a Safari extra small wire, removed the Agilis and pigtail catheters, and advanced the Edwards sheath over the wire and sutured it. We performed an atrial septostomy with a 12-mm Atlas balloon. A temporary pacemaker was advanced into the right ventricular (RV) apex. We confirmed the orientation of the 23-mm Edwards S3U valve and advanced the delivery system upside down through the sheath. We seated the valve over the balloon markers in the right atrial–inferior vena cava (IVC) junction and flexed the delivery system and crossed the intra-atrial septum (IAS). There was some difficulty manipulating and positioning the valve due to interaction with the implantable cardioverter-defibrillator lead and temporary pacemaker. The 23-mm S3U valve was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing (Video 2). Unfortunately, there was a kink in the inner catheter toward the end of the delivery system, and only a portion of the contrast volume from the indeflator was able to reach the delivery balloon (Figure 1). Thus, the S3U valve was unable to fully expand, and efforts to deflate it were unsuccessful given the creation of a functional 1-way valve. Once pacing was stopped, the patient had no blood pressure because of obstructing blood flow across the mitral annulus with a partially deployed valve. We attempted to remove the balloon catheter from the mitral position, but this maneuver caused the partially deployed valve to embolize back into the left atrium. We were cautious to maintain wire position with the Safari wire through the embolized valve that was sitting in the left atrium (Video 3). At this point, the patient recovered hemodynamically, thus we attempted to snare the embolized valve. We advanced a 35-mm Amplatz Goose Neck snare over our Safari wire. We were able to snare the outflow portion of the transcatheter valve and crimp it. We then advanced a second 35-mm Amplatz Goose Neck snare to the embolized valve and were successful in snaring the inflow portion of the valve that was in the left atrium. The outflow of the valve was crimped down to a conical shape, whereas the inflow had significantly more radial strength and was unable to be crimped. We were unable to remove the valve through the IAS while over the Safari wire because of the larger, uncrimped inflow portion of the valve. We recognized that we needed to retract the valve with the crimped outflow portion first; this necessitated pulling the Safari wire and rotating the valve with the 2 snares. With persistent gentle retraction, we brought the valve into the right atrium with both snares still attached (Video 4).Figure 1Delivery System MalfunctionImage showing kink in the delivery system.

To align the valve with the direction of IVC blood flow, we used a JR4 catheter and wire through the lumen of the valve with an 0.035-inch exchange-length wire and positioned a 20-mm True balloon. The undeployed True balloon acted as a rail, allowing us to use the 2 snares to orient the valve coaxially in the IVC. We dilated the valve using the 20-mm True balloon to partially deploy it and performed intravascular ultrasound confirming the IVC size. The outflow snare was removed, and we held onto the inflow snare while we positioned a 23-mm Z-Med balloon prepared to 22 mm with calipers. This balloon was positioned in the valve, the inflow snare was removed, and the valve was postdilated in the IVC, with an excellent result (Video 5).

Next, we elected to proceed with the initially planned TMVR procedure as previously described. The valve was deployed under rapid ventricular pacing. Postdilation with a 24-mm True balloon was performed (Video 6). Final TEE revealed a mean gradient of 2 mm Hg and trace mitral regurgitation (Video 7).

We closed the large IAS defect after delivery system removal. We measured the defect with a 35-mm Amplatzer sizing balloon. Stop color flow measurement was 18 mm by TEE and 18.5 mm by angiography (Video 8). A 20-mm atrial septal defect (ASD) occluder device was deployed in the standard fashion. The final result is shown in Video 9. Total procedure and fluoroscopy times were 200 and 83 minutes, respectively.

Potential Pitfalls

Valve salvage

Prior reports have described cases of valve embolization during attempted TMVR procedures. A singular case report described an embolized crimped valve that was detached from the balloon of the delivery system. The authors were able to capture the valve and position it properly before fully inflating.1 Another case report described embolization of 2 valves after placement, ultimately requiring sternotomy for retrieval.5

Our case describes a novel approach to salvaging a partially inflated valve that could not be deflated (due to equipment failure) and embolized into the left atrium. This required crimping the outflow of the valve into a more conical shape that could fit through the IAS. The base of the valve had more radial strength and was unable to be crimped down with our snare. Had we been unable to reshape the outflow of this valve and remove it from the left atrium, the patient would have required emergent surgery for retrieval.

Deployment in the IVC

Caval positioning of an Edwards valve was described during early feasibility trials as a potential treatment strategy for severe tricuspid regurgitation.6 It is important to position the valve with proper alignment with the direction of blood flow. An important consideration is to measure the size of the IVC using intravascular ultrasound, ensuring adequate stent apposition and avoiding valve embolization.

Abandon or continue?

Given our patient's severe mitral regurgitation and the large iatrogenic ASD, there was concern that she would develop RV failure from significant left-to-right shunting. Furthermore, closing the IAS without fixing the mitral valve would preclude further access to the left atrium. The patient remained hemodynamically stable, thus we decided to proceed with TMVR placement.

ASD closure

After the TMVR procedure, TEE interrogation showed a large, iatrogenic ASD with significant left-to-right shunting. There are no data regarding closure of a large, iatrogenic ASD as a result of retrieving an embolized valve. Available data for small, procedural ASDs due to delivery system passage and atrial septostomy are limited. One retrospective analysis showed that ASD closure after TMVR was associated with worse in-hospital mortality.7 This was not replicated in another retrospective observational study, which showed that patients who underwent ASD closure had lower short-term but higher long-term mortality.8 We proceeded with ASD closure to prevent further left-to-right shunting and reduce the risk of RV failure.

Conclusions

Valve embolization is a rare but potentially fatal complication during TMVR, and it usually requires surgery for salvage. We describe a novel approach to retrieve a partially inflated Edwards valve from the left atrium using a double-snare technique. Crimping the valve outflow, rotating the valve with retraction through the IAS, and reorienting with stable deployment in the right atrial–IVC junction was made possible with this technique. It is important to maintain a secure grasp with the 2 snares during this procedure.
