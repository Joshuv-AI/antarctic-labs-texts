Negative-Guided Subject Fidelity Optimization for Zero-Shot Subject-Driven Generation
Chaehun Shin, Jooyoung Choi, Johan Barthelemy, Jungbeom Lee, Sungroh Yoon

Abstract
We present Subject Fidelity Optimization (SFO), a novel comparative learning framework for zero-shot subject-driven generation that enhances subject fidelity. Beyond supervised fine-tuning methods that rely only on positive targets and use the diffusion loss as in the pre-training stage, SFO introduces synthetic negative targets and explicitly guides the model to favor positives over negatives through pairwise comparison. For negative targets, we propose Condition-Degradation Negative Sampling (CDNS), which automatically generates distinctive and informative negatives by intentionally degrading visual and textual cues without expensive human annotations. Moreover, we reweight the diffusion timesteps to focus fine-tuning on intermediate steps where subject details emerge. Extensive experiments demonstrate that SFO with CDNS significantly outperforms baselines in terms of both subject fidelity and text alignment on a subject-driven generation benchmark.

To address scalability, recent zero-shot approaches

,

fine-tune the pre-trained TTI model on a large triplet dataset composed of reference images, target texts, and target images in a supervised manner with the diffusion loss used in the pre-training stage.
This supervised fine-tuning implicitly guides the model to mimic the target data by leveraging the reference subject image.
Despite improved efficiency, these zero-shot approaches often fail to fully capture fine-grained subject details.
This limitation stems from a fundamental issue: supervised training solely on target images provides insufficient guidance about which specific details matter.

Supervised fine-tuning of a zero-shot subject-driven TTI model can be interpreted as adapting a pre-trained TTI model—originally trained on a broad distribution—to sample from a specific subject mode conditioned on a particular reference.
However, the mode-covering nature of the diffusion loss in the fine-tuning stage often fails to sufficiently narrow the sampling distribution toward the intended mode that captures the fine details of the target subject, unless it is explicitly guided regarding undesirable aspects.
This is clearly confirmed in Fig.
1
, in which the generated subjects from the supervised fine-tuned model are visually similar but fail to capture fine-grained details.
Therefore, we argue that it is necessary to explicitly incorporate
negative signals or regularization that suppress alternative modes
during fine-tuning to better focus on the desired subject attributes.

To address this, we incorporate synthetic negative targets with lower subject fidelity than positive counterparts into the fine-tuning of zero-shot subject-driven TTI models.
By leveraging the subject fidelity gap between these targets, we propose Subject Fidelity Optimization (SFO)—a comparison-based fine-tuning strategy that explicitly guides the model to favor positive targets over negative ones.
To ensure effective comparison learning, the differences between target pairs should be sufficiently noticeable, yet not excessively large, and diverse across the dataset
[
39
,
54
]
.
To generate such distinguishable and informative negative targets, we introduce Condition-Degradation Negative Sampling (CDNS), which intentionally degrades visual and textual conditions.
This facilitates the creation of negative targets with reliably lower subject fidelity and diverse pairwise gaps, enabling effective and human annotation-free training compared with conventional methods
[
39
,
57
]
.
Additionally, we focus fine-tuning on intermediate diffusion timesteps, where subject-specific details begin to clearly emerge, which are identified as critical yet challenging regions for fine-grained details by previous studies
[
18
,
6
,
7
]
.
Extensive experiments including ablation studies validate the effectiveness of SFO and the synergistic contributions of its core components to subject fidelity enhancement.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We highlight the necessity of negative targets in fine-tuning zero-shot subject-driven TTI frameworks, and propose Subject Fidelity Optimization (SFO), a timestep-reweighted comparison-based fine-tuning framework for zero-shot subject-driven TTI.

•

We introduce Condition-Degradation Negative Sampling (CDNS) for synthesizing negative targets automatically without costly human annotations.

•

We provide empirical validation through extensive experiments and ablations, demonstrating significant gains in subject fidelity and text alignment over existing baselines.

Related Works

2.1
Subject-Driven Text-to-Image Generation

Recent progress in text-to-image (TTI) diffusion models
[
40
,
43
,
7
,
21
]
has enabled high-quality image synthesis from textual prompts.
However, these models typically struggle with accurately depicting specific subjects unseen during pre-training.
To tackle this, initial studies
[
41
,
20
,
9
,
13
,
8
]
fine-tune diffusion models using a few (3–5) reference images.
Despite achieving high subject fidelity, these per-subject fine-tuning methods are computationally expensive.

To overcome these efficiency limitations, zero-shot approaches bypass per-subject tuning by fine-tuning the TTI model using the same diffusion loss as in pre-training stage while conditioning on vision-encoder embeddings
[
38
,
2
,
23
]
as additional information
[
56
,
22
,
36
,
4
,
26
,
52
]
.
Yet, these embeddings primarily capture only coarse semantics, causing a loss of fine subject details in the generated image.
Alternatively, JeDi
[
58
]
and subsequent works
[
45
,
14
]
reframe subject-driven TTI as an inpainting in image-set generation tasks, achieving improved results due to the targeted fine-tuning or inherent capability of high-performing recent TTI models.

With Diffusion Transformers (DiT)
[
37
]
and strong open-source TTI models
[
7
,
21
]
, recent methods
[
47
,
60
]

directly incorporate the latent tokens of a reference image into the input sequence of DiT and conduct joint attention over both the reference latent tokens and the text tokens.
These methods make minimal architectural adjustments and fine-tune the model in a supervised setting on a large triplet dataset using the same diffusion loss as pre-training.
However, their reliance on standard diffusion losses occasionally results in incomplete preservation of subtle subject traits.
This limitation motivates the investigation of alternative learning signals to further improve subject-driven TTI.

2.2
Comparative Learning Signals

A number of studies explore comparison-based learning approaches that leverage both positive and negative data, extending beyond traditional supervised approaches.
In contrastive learning
[
10
,
3
]
, models treat an augmented view of the same image as the positive target and other images as negatives, and train to attract positive pairs closer while pushing negatives apart.
Recently, preference optimization
[
34
,
39
,
51
]
has focused on training generative models to favor human-preferred data as positive targets over less-preferred data as negatives, either using a reward model or direct optimization.
Self-Play fine-tuning
[
5
,
57
]
further introduces iterative self-improvement, in which the model generates negative targets and compares them with the corresponding supervised targets, which serve as the positives.

Despite their different origins—contrastive learning focuses on semantic similarity and preference optimization centers on human judgment—both approaches leverage comparative signals to distinguish desirable from undesirable outcomes.
This common framework effectively maximizes a lower bound on mutual information between the data and the underlying labels (e.g., class or preference)
[
50
,
33
,
55
]
, leading to enhanced performance over purely supervised learning.

While recent subject-driven TTI methods
[
30
,
15
]
propose reward models based on self-supervised training and apply per-subject preference optimization or patch-wise weighted fine-tuning, they rely on reward models to estimate fidelity gaps and guide learning.
In contrast, we emphasize the importance of negative signals in fine-tuning zero-shot subject-driven TTI models and generate negative targets with fidelity gaps automatically—without relying on any reward model—to facilitate comparison-based learning and enhance subject fidelity.

x_{\mathrm{tgt}}
, target text

, and reference image
c_{\mathrm{img}}
by incorporating latent tokens of reference images into the input sequence of DiT
[
37
]
and using the same diffusion loss

This triplet-based supervised fine-tuning implicitly guides the model to mimic the target data by leveraging the reference image as additional information.
However, simply adding reference image conditioning and continuing training with the same loss may not be sufficient for subject-driven TTI, as it lacks explicit guidance on which aspects of the reference should be emphasized or corrected.
Moreover, due to the mode-covering nature of the diffusion loss, supervised fine-tuning often fails to sufficiently narrow the sampling toward a specific subject mode that reflects fine-grained details, and instead continues sampling from modes with irrelevant attributes.
To overcome this limitation, we propose incorporating negative targets alongside original targets and explicitly guiding the model to favor the desired subject with fine-grained details while suppressing irrelevant details.

3.2
Subject Fidelity Optimization

Inspired by the pairwise comparison objective in direct-preference optimization
[
39
,
51
,
5
,
57
]
, we propose a novel Subject Fidelity Optimization (SFO) strategy for further improving supervised fine-tuned zero-shot subject-driven TTI models with misaligned negative target data, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(c).
Given a quadruplet dataset that includes condition
c=(c_{\mathrm{text}},c_{\mathrm{img}})
along with a positive target image
x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}
that has better subject fidelity and a negative target image
x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}
that has worse subject fidelity, the following learning objective encourages the model to increase the probability of sampling the image with higher fidelity compared to the image with lower fidelity,

\mathcal{L}:=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}|c)}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}|c)}\Big)\Big)\Big)\Bigg],

(2)

where
p_{\theta}
is the optimized model distribution,
p_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the reference distribution which is a supervised fine-tuned model distribution in our case, and
\beta
is the regularization parameter.
This optimization is well-known for maximizing a lower bound on the mutual information between the generated image and the fidelity, conditioned on the reference image and text prompt
[
33
,
50
,
55
]
, and is thus theoretically grounded in enhancing subject fidelity.
By leveraging the equivalence between minimizing the KL divergence (i.e., maximizing data likelihood) and the diffusion loss as its surrogate
[
24
,
46
]
, the above objective can be approximated by the following SFO objective formulation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t),~(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\sigma\Big(-\beta\Big(\Delta_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)\Big)\Big)\Bigg],

(3)

\displaystyle\Delta_{*}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}t,c)=\big\|f_{*}(x_{t}^{+},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+})\big\|^{2}-\big\|f_{*}(x_{t}^{-},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-})\big\|^{2},

where
p(t)
is the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
,
x_{t}^{+}=(1-t)x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}+t\epsilon
,
x_{t}^{-}=(1-t)x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}+t\epsilon
are interpolated images between each target image and same random noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.
Intuitively, the loss encourages the model to match a vector field to the positive sample better than to its negative counterpart, so the generator learns to amplify subject-specific details that separate the two.

As SFO aims to enhance subject fidelity by capturing fine-grained details of the target subject, we focus on fine-tuning on timestep regions where such details begin to emerge during the generation process.
Rather than blindly following the uniform timestep distribution
t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
as used in theoretical formulations (Eq.
3
), we adopt insights from prior works
[
6
,
7
,
18
,
19
]
that emphasize the importance of middle timesteps.
Accordingly, we sample timesteps from a logit-normal distribution during fine-tuning process, where
\text{logit}(t)=\log(\frac{t}{1-t})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, to place more weight on this critical region.
This timestep reweighting enables our fine-tuning to effectively capture and reflect the fine subject-specific details from the reference image.

3.3
Condition-Degradation Negative Sampling (CDNS)

Figure 3:
Dataset construction comparisons
(a) We present examples of synthesized negative targets from each naïve Self-Play method and our CDNS with given conditions. (b) While the negative targets of naïve Self-Play has high similarity with positive samples, our negative targets from CDNS demonstrate diverse pairwise gap between targets and enable more effective optimization.

The core idea of SFO is to introduce the target pair
(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-})
and enhance subject fidelity through comparative signals between targets.
A promising approach for obtaining such pairs for comparison is Self-Play
[
5
,
57
]
, which performs preference optimization by treating the original targets in the supervised dataset as positive and pairing them with model-generated data as negative.
Self-Play demonstrates superior performance over conventional preference optimization methods
[
39
,
51
]
that rely on expensive human annotations for constructing target pairs.
Inspired by the effectiveness of Self-Play, we synthesize the negative target
x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}
for SFO from a supervised fine-tuned model upon the Subject200K dataset, in which we treat a natural scene photo as the positive target
x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}
and use a studio portrait on a white background
c_{\mathrm{img}}
and a contextual text description
c_{\mathrm{text}}
as conditions, as illustrated in rows
1
and
2
of Fig.
3
(a).

Effective comparative learning requires negative targets that are sufficiently distinct from positives—yet not overly dissimilar—and diverse pairwise gaps across the dataset
[
39
,
54
]
.
However, as naïve Self-Play generates negatives by conditioning on the same input used for the positive target in supervised fine-tuning, the synthesized negative targets rarely fail and replicate nearly identical context as shown in row
3
of Fig.
3
(a).
As a result, many synthesized negatives remain highly similar to positive targets in both subject details and contextual contents, limiting the differences between targets and leading to overly difficult comparisons that produce weak learning signals.

To address this, we propose Condition-Degradation Negative Sampling (CDNS) that amplifies the difference between target pairs by intentionally challenging the condition scenario during synthesis, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(b).
CDNS introduces two key modifications to Self-Play implementation in our settings: (1) we use the complex natural scene image
x_{tgt}^{+}
as the conditioning image instead of the clean studio portrait
c_{\mathrm{img}}
, which makes it more difficult for the model to reference the subject and leads to more frequent failures.j
(2) we replace the rich, context-specific prompt with a generic phrase such as
“a photo of
{
item name
}
”
, introducing more varied and ambiguous contexts.

By degrading both the visual and textual conditions, CDNS facilitates the generation of negative targets that deviate in subject fidelity and exhibit broader contextual diversity, as evidenced by the examples in row 4 of Fig.
3
(a) and shifted DINO similarity distribution in Fig.
3
(b) compared to the Self-Play baseline, leading to more effective and informative pairs for comparison-based learning.

Experiments

4.1
Effectiveness of Negative Targets in Fine-tuning Text-to-Image Model

Figure 4:
Effectiveness of negative targets in the toy experiment
(a) Qualitative results: With identical seeds, supervised fine-tuning still yields cars of various colors, whereas comparison-based fine-tuning consistently generates red cars, indicating that the sampling distribution has been sufficiently narrowed around the desired attribute. (b) Quantitative results: Comparison-based fine-tuning converges faster and attains a higher plateau than supervised fine-tuning in the red-car ratio, confirming the advantage of incorporating negative targets when fine-tuning TTI models.

As our first experiment, we validate our hypothesis that supervised fine-tuning provides an insufficient learning signal and that negative targets are essential, as discussed in Sec.
1
and Sec.
3.1
.
To this end, we conduct a toy experiment designed to simulate a simplified scenario for narrowing the sampling distribution of a Text-to-Image (TTI) model.

The original TTI model generates cars in various colors when given a generic text prompt
c_{\textrm{car}}
: “a photo of a car.” We attempt to fine-tune this model to generate only red cars for the same text prompt
c_{\textrm{car}}
, and compare two fine-tuning approaches to ablate the effect of negative targets: (1) Supervised fine-tuning (SFT), similar to Eq.
1
but without image condition, using only red car images
x_{\mathrm{red}}
paired with the prompt
c_{\textrm{car}}
, and (2) Comparison-based fine-tuning, similar to our SFO objective in Eq.
3
but excluding image condition, using red car images
x_{\mathrm{red}}
, the text prompt
c_{\textrm{car}}
, and negative samples
x_{\textrm{various}}
consisting of cars in various other colors.

In both cases, we attach a rank-16 LoRA module
[
13
]
to FLUX.1-Dev
[
21
]
and fine-tune only its weights until convergence (
800
iterations), using
500
red car images and
500
various-colored car images.
After fine-tuning, we generate
100
images using the prompt “a photo of a car” (identical to
c_{\mathrm{car}}
used during training), and evaluate the red car ratio, defined as the proportion of generated images classified as red cars using a car color classifier
[
48
]
.

Quantitative results and qualitative results are presented in Fig.
4
.
Although the supervised fine-tuned model attempts to learn the red car distribution, it fails to sufficiently narrow the distribution over various car colors, still generating non-red cars and achieving a red car ratio of only up to 55%.
In contrast, comparison-based fine-tuning explicitly leverages both red cars (as positives) and cars of other colors (as negatives), guiding the model to suppress non-red outputs. This leads to more successful red car generation, a higher red car ratio, and faster convergence.
These experimental results validate the necessity and advantage of incorporating a comparison-based learning signal to suppress undesired content, thereby enabling more effective fine-tuning of TTI models.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose Subject Fidelity Optimization (SFO) to address the limitations of existing zero-shot subject-driven TTI frameworks that rely on supervised fine-tuning with supervised dataset.
Our SFO extends the triplet dataset to a quadruplet dataset by incorporating synthesized negative targets, and leverages comparative signals to achieve desirable subject fidelity.
We also prioritize the middle timesteps—where fine-grained details first emerge—to enhance fine-tuning effectiveness.
We further propose Condition-Degradation Negative Sampling (CDNS), a novel technique that systematically degrades conditions to create informative and distinguishable negatives without requiring costly human annotations.
Our method consistently outperforms existing zero-shot approaches both qualitatively and quantitatively, including human perceptual evaluations.
Extensive ablation studies further demonstrate the effectiveness of each proposed component, thereby confirming each component’s significant contributions to overall performance.
We believe that our fine-tuning strategy can serve as a foundation for more advanced and efficient methods in subject-driven TTI.

Appendix A
Loss Derivation

Drawing inspiration from direct preference optimization
[
39
]
, which encourages the model to favor the positive target over the negative one, we adopt a pairwise comparison objective for zero-shot subject-driven TTI.
This objective is designed to distinguish positive targets with high subject fidelity from negative targets with lower fidelity.

We begin with the Bradley-Terry (BT) preference model
[
1
]
, which formulates the pairwise preference as a binary classification task between positive targets
x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}
and negative targets
x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle:=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\frac{\exp(f_{\phi}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},c))}{\exp(f_{\phi}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},c))+\exp(f_{\phi}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c))}\Bigg]

(4)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\sigma\Big(f_{\phi}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},c)-f_{\phi}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c)\Big)\Bigg],

(5)

where
c=(c_{\mathrm{text}},c_{\mathrm{img}})
represents the conditioning pair consisting of a text prompt and a reference subject image in subject-driven text-to-image generation,
\sigma(x)
denotes the sigmoid function
\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}
, and
f_{\phi}
is a learnable parametric function.

In our case, we implicitly define the parametric function as
f_{\phi}(x,c)=\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x|c)}
which reflects the relative likelihood of the target model with respect to the reference model.
Substituting this into the above, the equation becomes:

\mathcal{L}:=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},c)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}|c)}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}|c)}\Big)\Big)\Big)\Bigg],

(6)

where
\beta
is a hyperparameter that controls the extent to which the optimized model deviates from the reference model.

However, flow-matching models
[
24
,
25
]
cannot directly compute or model the log-probability
\log p_{\theta}(x)
.
To address this, prior works
[
24
,
46
]
approximate the maximization of data likelihood or the minimization of KL divergence with a flow matching loss, which serves as a surrogate objective for optimization:

\min~\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(x)||p_{\theta}(x))=\max~\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_{\theta}(x)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{q}\Bigg[C-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\|f_{\theta}(x_{t},t)-(\epsilon-x)\|_{2}^{2}~~dt}_{\text{flow matching loss}}\Bigg],

(7)

where
x_{t}=(1-t)x+t\epsilon
denotes the linear interpolation between original image
x
and Gaussian noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, and
C
is constant independent of
\theta
.

Based on this surrogate, we approximate the log-likelihood difference as:

\displaystyle\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x|c)}

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|c)-\log p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x|c)

(8)

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{0}^{1}\big(\|f_{\theta}(x_{t},t,c)-(\epsilon-x)\|_{2}^{2}-\|f_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{t},t,c)-(\epsilon-x)\|_{2}^{2}\big)~dt.

(9)

Consequently, our overall loss function is approximated as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+}|c)}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}|c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}|c)}\Big)\Big)\Big)\Bigg]

(10)

\displaystyle\simeq-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\big\|f_{\theta}(x_{t}^{+},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+})\big\|_{2}^{2}-\big\|f_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{t}^{+},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+})\big\|_{2}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\int_{0}^{1}\big\|f_{\theta}(x_{t}^{-},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-})\big\|_{2}^{2}-\big\|f_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{t}^{-},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-})\big\|_{2}^{2}\,dt\Big)\Big)\Big)\Bigg]

(11)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\int_{0}^{1}\Delta_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)~dt\Big)\Big)\Bigg],

(12)

where
\Delta_{*}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-}t,c)=\big\|f_{*}(x_{t}^{+},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+})\big\|^{2}-\big\|f_{*}(x_{t}^{-},t,c)-(\epsilon-x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-})\big\|^{2}
,

and we apply the Jensen’s inequality, leveraging the convexity of the function
-\log\sigma(x)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\int_{0}^{1}\Delta_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)~dt\Big)\Big)\Bigg],

(13)

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},t\sim p(t)=\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\Bigg[\log\Big(\sigma\Big(\beta\Delta_{\theta}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)-\Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{+},x_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{-},t,c)\Big)\Big)\Bigg],

(14)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFO}}

(15)

in which
\int_{0}^{1}\cdot dt
is equivalent to expectation with uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
.

While the aforementioned SFO objective based on flow matching originally employs a uniform timestep distribution
p(t)=\mathcal{U}[0,1]
across all steps, we empirically adjust this distribution
p(t)
as
\text{logit}(t)=\log(\frac{t}{1-t})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
to prioritize regions where fine subject details begin to emerge, as demonstrated in the ablation studies in Sec. 4.3 of the main paper and Sec.
C.6
.

Appendix B
Conditional Mutual Information between Images and Subject Fidelity

Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC), also known as InfoNCE
[
33
]
, is a widely used approach in self-supervised learning and is commonly employed to estimate mutual information.
In particular, it has been shown that the conditional mutual information between a target variable
X
and its implicit label
Y
, given a conditioning variable
C
, can be bounded by the InfoNCE loss
[
27
,
49
]
,

CMI(X;Y|C)\geq\mathrm{InfoNCE}:=\sup_{f}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\log\Bigg(\frac{\exp(f(x_{i},y_{i}))}{\exp(f(x_{i},y_{i}))+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\exp(f(x_{j},y_{j})}\Bigg)\Bigg],

(16)

where the positive pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
are sampled from the joint distribution
p(x,y|c)
, and negative pairs
(x_{j},y_{j\neq i})
are sampled from the product of marginals
p(x|c)p(y|c)
.

In our case, given the conditioning variable
C
, the conditional mutual information between the target image
X
and the implicit label
Y
—which indicates whether an image exhibits high subject fidelity—can be similarly bounded as:

\displaystyle CMI(X;Y|C)

\displaystyle\geq\log\Bigg(\frac{\exp(f(x_{i},y_{i}))}{\exp(f(x_{i},y_{i}))+\exp(f(x_{j},y_{j})}\Bigg)

(17)

\displaystyle=\log\Bigg(\frac{\exp(f(x^{+},y=1))}{\exp(f(x^{+},y=1))+\exp(f(x^{-},y=0)}\Bigg)

(18)

\displaystyle=\log\Bigg(\sigma\bigg(f(x^{+},y=1)-f(x^{-},y=0)\bigg)\Bigg),

(19)

where
x^{+}
denotes a positive target with high subject fidelity, labeled as
y=1
, and
x^{-}
denotes a negative target with low subject fidelity, labeled as
y=0
, both under the same conditioning
c
.

This bound corresponds exactly (up to a sign) to the formulation of the Bradley-Terry preference model assumed in Sec.
A
.
Therefore, our loss function can be interpreted as optimizing the model to maximize the conditional mutual information between the generated image and its subject fidelity with respect to the reference subject, given the condition.
This provides a theoretical justification for the subject fidelity improvement achieved by our method.

Human Evaluation Details

Following prior studies
[
41
,
45
]
, we conduct a human evaluation using an A/B test, in which participants compare the outputs of our SFO method against those of each baseline.
In each evaluation instance, participants are shown two generated images—one from our SFO method and one from a baseline—alongside a reference subject image and a target text prompt.
Participants are then asked to select the image that better satisfies each of the following objectives: subject fidelity, which measures the similarity between the subject in the generated image and the subject in the reference image; and text alignment, which measures how well the generated image reflects the target text prompt.
To ensure reliability, we additionally include a quality control pair that compares an SFO-generated image against a random noise image.
Participants who prefer the noise image over our SFO output are excluded from the analysis.

Our detailed instructions for questionnaire are as follows:

Subject Fidelity

•

Given a reference image and two machine-generated images, select which machine-generated output better matches the subject of the reference image for each pair.

•

Inspect the reference subject and then inspect the generated subjects. Select which of the two generated items reproduces the identity (item type and details) of the reference item. The subject might be wearing accessories (e.g. hats, outfits). These should not affect your answer. Do not take them into account. If you’re not sure, select Cannot Determine / Both Equally.

•

Given the provided subject in reference image, which machine-generated image best matches the subject of the reference image?

Text Alignment

•

Given a reference image and two machine-generated images, select which machine-generated output better matches the target text for each pair.

•

Inspect the target text and then inspect the generated items. Select which of the two generated items is best described by the target text. If you’re not sure, select Cannot Determine / Both Equally.

•

Given the provided target text, which machine-generated image is best described by the target text?

Timestep distribution

We explore various timestep distributions to empirically identify which regions should be prioritized during SFO fine-tuning for improved subject fidelity.
To this end, we replace the uniform distribution used in the theoretical formulation with a logit-normal distribution, varying its mean (
\mu
) and standard deviation (
\sigma
), as shown in Tab.
C
and Fig.
E
.
When the mean of the logit-normal distribution is increased (e.g.,
\mu=2
), fine-tuning is biased toward highly noised regions, where the noised positive image
x_{t}^{+}
and the noised negative image
x_{t}^{-}
become visually similar, making it difficult to distinguish subject fidelity.
This hinders pairwise comparison learning, leading to inferior results.
In contrast, using a logit-normal distribution with
\mu=0
or an reduced mean of
\mu=-2
shifts the focus to timesteps where differences in subject fidelity become more pronounced, thereby maximizing the effectiveness of comparison-based learning.

leads to performance improvements by intensively training around the middle timesteps, but still shows slightly inferior performance compared to our default logit-normal distribution.

Beta Ablation

We analyze the impact of the hyperparameter
\beta
in the comparison-based learning objective of our SFO.
In the loss formulation,
\beta
serves to regularize deviation from the reference model: a larger value strongly constrains the adaptation of the optimized model, while a smaller value permits greater deviation.
We evaluate
\beta\in\{500,1000,2000\}
, and the results are presented in Tab.
E
.
Although all settings already yield strong performance, we select
\beta=1000
as our default setting since it achieves the best overall results.

Additional LoRA

As shown in Tab.
E
, directly fine-tuning the SFT LoRA with the SFO objective does not lead to performance improvement under the same number of fine-tuning iterations (300 iterations), and only shows some effectiveness after significantly longer training (1500 iterations).
This implies that our SFO fine-tuning complements SFT—which implicitly mimics the target data by referencing the subject image—and benefits from an additional LoRA module for effective knowledge integration.
Using a separate LoRA module for SFO also allows an additional advantage, which provides independent control over model capacity only for subject fidelity optimization.

Appendix E
Limitations

While our method automatically synthesizes negative targets for pairwise comparison-based learning, it introduces additional time overhead for constructing negative targets before fine-tuning.
However, this time cost serves as a meaningful trade-off, as it prepares the model to reach performance levels that supervised fine-tuning alone cannot achieve.
Our SFO framework relies on the Subject200K dataset, which contains images at a resolution of
512\times 512
, and all validations in this paper are conducted at this resolution.
Nevertheless, our method is not inherently restricted to a specific resolution and can be easily extendable to higher-resolution supervised datasets expected to be released in the future.

Appendix F
Future Works

Multi-Subject Conditions

Our current work focuses on single-subject settings to introduce comparison-based learning for zero-shot subject-driven TTI.
As many recent works explore multi-subject scenarios
[
20
,
52
]
, we hope to extend our approach to multi-subject conditions by automatically generating negative targets that can handle multiple subjects simultaneously.

Data Filtering

Our current method directly utilizes a large number of synthesized negative targets without any filtering, relying instead on intentional degradation of conditioning information to increase the failure rate.
Although our method already achieves noticeably strong performance without any data filtering, we expect that incorporating a robust data filtering technique—capable of identifying and excluding negative targets that are similar to their corresponding positives in terms of subject fidelity—could further improve its effectiveness.

Appendix G
Broader Impacts

As our SFO method fine-tunes a generative model—specifically, a subject-driven TTI model that generates images containing a reference subject—it carries both positive and negative aspects.
On the positive side, the SFO fine-tuned model enables high-quality generation of images depicting specific subjects, facilitating user-friendly visual content creation by allowing users to simply provide a single reference image and a text prompt for what they want to generate.
However, like many other TTI models and applications, this technology can potentially be misused to generate harmful or inappropriate content.
The primary goal of our study is to advance academic understanding in this area, and we hope that our method will be applied responsibly and ethically to benefit people.
