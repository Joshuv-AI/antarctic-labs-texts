Approximate Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability Analysis for a Class of Two-Timescale Systems, with Application to Biological Models
Dylan Hirsch, Sylvia Herbert

Abstract
Hamilton-Jacobi reachability (HJR) is an exciting framework used for control of safety-critical systems with nonlinear and possibly uncertain dynamics. However, HJR suffers from the curse of dimensionality, with computation times growing exponentially in the dimension of the system state. Many autonomous and controlled systems involve dynamics that evolve on multiple timescales, and for these systems, singular perturbation methods can be used for model reduction. However, such methods are more challenging to apply in HJR due to the presence of an underlying differential game. In this work, we leverage prior work on singularly perturbed differential games to identify a class of systems which can be readily reduced, and we relate these results to the quantities of interest in HJR. We demonstrate the utility of our results on two examples involving biological systems, where dynamics fitting the identified class are frequently encountered.



I
INTRODUCTION

As computers have become faster, Hamilton-Jacobi reachability (HJR) is an increasingly useful tool for analyzing and controlling safety-critical systems using the theory of differential games
[
1
]
.
In particular, HJR determines the states, known as the backward reachable set (BRS), from which a system can be guaranteed able to achieve some goal, such as reaching a target or avoiding an obstacle, despite uncertain model dynamics or presence of an adversary

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
In the process, one also obtains a control law by which to achieve the goal.
Substantial work has been performed to extend and apply HJR to problems in domains such as aerospace, robotics, and reinforcement learning
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

The key factor limiting the more widespread use of HJR is the curse of dimensionality.
Even with modern processors, the algorithm typically becomes computationally intractable for systems of dimension 6 or higher.
This limitation produces a need for model reduction techniques that can be applied in the context of differential games.

In the control theory community, singular perturbation methods are mainstays of reduced modeling of autonomous and controlled systems, specifically those that evolve on multiple timescales
[
8
,
9
]
.
Although results on model reduction for singularly perturbed (SP) differential games also exist, they have yet to see widespread use, particularly in the context of reachability analyses
[
10
]
.
This gap is likely in part due to the complexity of analytically finding the proper reduced model for a given SP differential game, if the game admits such a reduced model in the first place.

I-A

Background and prior work

Some of the earliest work studying differential games with both fast and slow state dynamics was by Gardner and Cruz in the late 1970s, in which the authors demonstrated that naively performing model reduction via singular perturbation prior to evaluating the payoff at the Nash equilibrium gives a result different from evaluating the true payoff in the limit of increasingly separated timescales
[
11
]
.
In their study, the fast and slow systems were linear and the payoff was quadratic.
These authors refer to such a singularly perturbed differential game as ill-posed.
Indeed, the discovery of an ill-posed game in such a simple setting motivated the careful study of singular perturbation methods for differential games.
Research along these lines was continued by Khalil and Kokotovic soon after, demonstrating the link between well-posedness and the information structure of the game
[
12
]
.

Later on, the advent of viscosity solutions of Hamilton-Jacobi equations
[
13
,
14
]
and the study of their relationship to differential games
[
15
,
16
]
ignited renewed interest in the study of differential games with non-anticipative information structures, nonlinear dynamics, and non-quadratic payoff functions.
Leveraging these results, guarantees of well-posedness for these general differential games under the added complication of singularly perturbed dynamics was undertaken by Gaitsgory in
[
10
]
.
Here, Gaitsgory demonstrated that such a game could be decomposed into an “associated fast” differential game involving only the fast dynamics and a reduced differential game involving only the slow variables.
However, certifying well-posedness using the theory outlined in this work for a specific system is typically challenging, involving a number of assumptions that may be difficult to check.
Moreover, in practice, using the theory often requires one to explicitly obtain the value function for the associated fast game, which may be impractical.
These results have also been adapted for discrete-time settings
[
17
]
.

Extensive further work has been pursued on linear-quadratic differential games, with and without noise, on finite and infinite intervals, and for zero-sum and non-zero-sum settings
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
Additional works have proceeded for the nonlinear case by approaches rooted in weak solutions to Hamilton-Jacobi Bellman equations, but each typically involves conditions that may be challenging to verify for general systems
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

Thus, in this work, we instead choose to focus on a class of nonlinear systems for which well-posedness can be certified via Gaitsgory’s results, and perhaps more importantly, we demonstrate how one can use this convergence result to provide bounds on quantities of interest in HJR, such as the BRS, for such systems.

I-B

Contributions

In this work, we provide three main contributions.
First, we identify a class of differential games for which a reduced model can be analytically derived and rigorously justified via the results in
[
10
]
.
Second, we derive inner and outer approximations of the backward reachable sets for these systems.
Finally, we provide examples of how to apply these results in the context of models of biomolecular system models, where two-timescale dynamics arise frequently
[
25
]
.

II-A

Singularly perturbed system

We consider the following SP system, parameterized by a “small” parameter
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d})+M(\mathbf{z})A(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d})\mathbf{y}

(1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\dot{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d})+A(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d})\mathbf{y},

(2)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}
,
M:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}\times n_{\textup{y}}}
, and

In the above,

and
\mathbf{y}
represent the “slow” and “fast” state variables, respectively, and
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{d}
represent control and disturbance signals, respectively.

This formulation arises commonly in models of biological systems, where simple chemical reactions often have dynamics that are affine (or nearly affine) in the species’ concentrations (i.e.
\mathbf{y}
) and occur on much faster timescales than more complex processes.
We make the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
(Regularity)

There is some
K>0
such that
\|M(z)\|
+\|A(z,u,d)\|\leq K
and
\|f(z,u,d)\|+\|g(z,u,d)\|\leq K(1+\|z\|)
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
,
u\in\mathcal{U}
, and
d\in\mathcal{D}
.
Moreover,
f
,
g
,
M
, and
A
are all locally Lipschitz in
z
and continuous on their domains.

Assumption 2
(Stability of fast dynamics)

There is a symmetric, positive definite matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}\times n_{\textup{y}}}
such that

A(z,u,d)^{\top}P+PA(z,u,d)\prec 0

for each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
,
u\in\mathcal{U}
, and
d\in\mathcal{D}
.

Assumption 3
(Isaacs’ condition for reduced model)

For each
\lambda,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
,

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\lambda^{\top}\left[f(z,u,d)-M(z)g(z,u,d)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\lambda^{\top}\left[f(z,u,d)-M(z)g(z,u,d)\right].

The reduced model to which Assumption
3
is related will be introduced shortly.
In practice, these assumptions are often satisfied.
Indeed, Assumption
2
is an analog of the stability requirement of standard singular perturbation methods, and Isaacs’ condition is assumed commonly in differential games.

For each
\varepsilon>0
,
t<0
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
,

\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}

\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}},\mathbf{y}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}}\right):[t,0]\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}
be the Carathéodory solution of (
1
)-(

) for which
\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}}(t)=z
and
\mathbf{y}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}}(t)=y

; see Theorem 1.2.1 in

II-B

Description of the differential game

For each value of
\varepsilon>0
, we consider a differential game in which the control “player”
\mathbf{u}
wishes to drive the slow variable
\mathbf{z}
to be in some open set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
at time 0, and the disturbance “player”
\mathbf{d}
wishes to prevent this outcome.

When formulating the game mathematically, we wish to ensure the disturbance player only has information regarding the control player’s decisions up to the current time.
This restriction is codified via the following definition.

Definition 1

Given
t<0
, a map
\gamma:\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}\to\mathbb{D}_{[t,0)}
is a non-anticipative disturbance strategy on
[t,0)
if for each
s\in[t,0)
and
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}
, we have that
\gamma[u_{1}](\tau)=\gamma[u_{2}](\tau)
for a.e.
\tau\in[t,s)
whenever
u_{1}(\tau)=u_{2}(\tau)
for a.e.
\tau\in[t,s)
.

We denote the set of non-anticipative disturbance strategies on
[t,0)
by
\Gamma_{[t,0)}
.

The game proceeds as follows.
Denote by
t<0
the initial time and by
(z,y)
the initial system state.
The disturbance player selects a
\gamma\in\Gamma_{[t,0)}
, and the control player the selects a
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}
.
We let
\mathbf{d}=\gamma[\mathbf{u}]
be the resulting disturbance signal.
If
\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}}(0)\in\mathcal{S}
, the control player wins the game; otherwise the disturbance player wins.

II-C

Backward reachable set and the value function

The first quantity of interest in HJR is the backward reachable set (BRS). For each
\varepsilon>0
and
t<0
, we let

\displaystyle\textup{BRS}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\{

\displaystyle(z,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}\mid

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\Gamma_{[t,0)}~\exists\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}~\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\gamma[\mathbf{u}]}(0)\in\mathcal{S}\}.

Note that
\textup{BRS}_{\varepsilon}(t)
represents the set of initial states from which the control player will win the game corresponding to parameter
\varepsilon
and inital time
t
if both players act rationally.
For convenience, we also set
\textup{BRS}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\mathcal{S}
.

In the HJR framework, we implicitly encode the target set
\mathcal{S}
via a function
\ell
and then define a value function for the game, where
\ell
serves as the terminal payoff function.
More explicitly, we choose
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\to\mathbb{R}
to be a bounded, Lipschitz function for which

\mathcal{S}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}:\ell(z)<0\}.

For each
\varepsilon>0
, we define the value function
V_{\varepsilon}:(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

V_{\varepsilon}(t,z,y)=\begin{cases}\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{[t,0)}}\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}}\ell\left(\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\gamma[\mathbf{u}]}(0)\right)&t<0,\\
\ell(z)&t=0.\end{cases}

It can be seen from these definitions that for each
t\leq 0
,

\displaystyle\{(z,y)\in

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}\mid V_{\varepsilon}(t,z,y)<0\}\subseteq\textup{BRS}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle\subseteq\{(z,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}\mid V_{\varepsilon}(t,z,y)\leq 0\}.

(3)

II-D

Backward reachable tubes and backward avoid tubes

The BRS is the set of states from which the controller can ensure the system will be in the target set
at
the final time.
We may also be interested in the set of states from which the controller can ensure the system will be in the target set
by
or
until
the final time, known as the Backward Reachable Tube (BRT) and the Backward Stay Tube (BST), respectively.

For each
\varepsilon>0
and
t<0
, we let

\displaystyle\textup{BRT}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(t)=\{(z,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}\mid

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\Gamma_{[t,0)}~\exists\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}~\exists s\in[t,0]~\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\gamma[\mathbf{u}]}(s)\in\mathcal{S}\},

\displaystyle\textup{BST}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(t)=\{(z,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}\mid

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\Gamma_{[t,0)}~\exists\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}~\forall s\in[t,0]~\mathbf{z}_{t,z,y}^{\varepsilon,\mathbf{u},\gamma[\mathbf{u}]}(s)\in\mathcal{S}\}.

For convenience, we also set
\textup{BRT}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\textup{BST}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\mathcal{S}
.
Note that for each
t\leq 0
and
s\in[t,0]

\textup{BRS}_{\varepsilon}(s)\subseteq\textup{BRT}_{\varepsilon}(t),\text{ and }\textup{BST}_{\varepsilon}(t)\subseteq\textup{BRS}_{\varepsilon}(s).

(4)

II-E

Reduced model

We consider as a candidate for approximating the SP system the following “reduced” model:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{z}}=f(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d})-M(\mathbf{z})g(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}).

(5)

For each
t<0
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}
, and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{[t,0)}
we let
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t,z}^{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}}
be the unique Carathéodory solution of (
5
) for which
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t,z}^{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{d}}(t)=z
(existence and uniqueness of this solution follows from Assumption
1
; see Theorem 1.2.1 in
[
26
]
).
We then define the “reduced” value function
\bar{V}:(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\bar{V}(t,z)=\begin{cases}\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{[t,0)}}\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{U}_{[t,0)}}\ell\left(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{t,z}^{\mathbf{u},\gamma[\mathbf{u}]}(0)\right)&t<0,\\
\ell(z)&t=0.\end{cases}

We now explain how to use the reduced value function to obtain bounds on the BRS, BRT, and BST of the SP system.

IV-A

Genetic circuit with negative feedback

We first consider a model of a simple genetic circuit engineered in a bacterium (Figure
1
).
This circuit contains a gene G, which produces a transcription factor T.
A kinase K expressed naturally in the cell chemically modifies T into its active form T* via a reaction known as phosphorylation.
T* can then bind to the promoter region of G to down-regulate production of T, creating a negative feedback loop.
Additionally, an inhibitor molecule I can be added by the user to the cellular environment to up-regulate the production rate of T by reducing the ability of T* to bind to the promoter of G.
Because the foreign circuit pulls away machinery (e.g. RNAP, ribosomes, energy) used in other functions of the cell, quantities such as the growth rate of the cell, concentration of K, and production rate of T from G are known to fluctuate in a manner that is generally difficult to predict.
We assume the transcription factor is fluorescently tagged so that its concentration can be observed by the user.

\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}v(t,z)+H(z,\nabla_{z}v(t,z))=0,&(t,z)\in\Omega,\\
v(t,z)=\ell(z),&(t,z)\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega=(-\infty,0)\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
and
\partial\Omega=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}
.
Having verified the above assumptions, Theorem 4.2 in
[
10
]
implies
V_{\varepsilon}(t,z,y)\to\bar{V}(t,z)
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
uniformly on compact sets in
(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{z}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\textup{y}}}
, which is our desired result.
∎

Remark 2

We note two nuances in this proof which may catch the attention of the careful reader.
First, the theory in
[
10
]
uses the Friedman definition of the value function (see
[
26
]
), whereas we use the Elliot-Kalton definitions for
V_{\varepsilon}
and
\bar{V}
.
These definitions are indeed equivalent in our setting, because they are both the unique viscosity solutions to a corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs PDE by
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
29
]
.
Second, the assumptions made in
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
on boundedness of the system dynamics virtually never hold for systems (
1
)-(
2
) and (
5
). Moreover, the definition of a viscosity solution used in these two early works postulates boundedness and uniform continuity of the solution, whereas the definition in
[
10
]
merely postulates continuity.
It is known (and in fact even stated in
[
15
]
) that such strong assumptions are not required to characterize the value function as the unique viscosity solution of a Hamilton-Jacobi PDE.
Indeed the “existence” proofs from
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
(i.e. that the value functions of a differential game is a [possibly unbounded and continuous but not uniformly so] viscosity solution of the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs PDE) follow similarly under only Assumption
1
and continuity of the terminal payoff function
\ell
.
On the other hand, the uniqueness result follows under these weaker assumptions and less restrictive definition of a viscosity solution from Theorem 2.5 in
[
29
]
.
Both these observations are not new (c.f. Sections III.3.2, VIII.1, and the end of VIII in the classic text
[
30
]
).
