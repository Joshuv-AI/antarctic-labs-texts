Gene deletions differentially affect surface-associated biofilms and staphylococcal abscess communities
Authors: Darine D'Adam, Marco Chittò, Livia S Kiener, T Fintan Moriarty, Elian MA Kuhn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus is a major human pathogen that causes persistent infections characterised by the formation of aggregates such as surface-attached biofilm and staphylococcal abscess communities (SACs). Both consist of dense bacterial populations associated with self-produced matrices that impair immune cell and antibiotic access. Surface-attached biofilms have mostly protein- or polysaccharide-rich matrices, whereas SACs are spherical structures within tissue encased in a fibrin pseudocapsule and microcolony-associated meshwork. It remains unclear whether SAC are simply biofilms within tissue or if they display distinct features with unique genetic regulation. Here, we investigated genetic determinants of SAC and biofilm formation using four S. aureus deletion mutants targeting staphylokinase (Δsak), coagulase (Δcoa), the alternative sigma factor SigB (ΔrpoF), and sortase A (ΔsrtA). SACs were grown in collagen gels, whilst biofilms were grown on titanium discs, with bacterial viability, fibrin deposition, and biofilm biomass assessed by microscopy, image analysis, and crystal violet staining. Although deletion of coa or sak did not significantly impact either SAC or biofilm formation, biofilm surface coverage was markedly increased in ΔsrtA and biomass decreased in ΔrpoF; however, these mutations had no effect on SAC. These findings suggest that certain mutations can have different effects in the two experimental systems, and that the tested gene deletions were more important for surface-associated biofilm development than SAC formation.Keywords: Abscess, Biofilm, Aggregation, Matrix, Fibrin, Coagulation

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus persists in host tissues by forming multicellular aggregates, including surface-attached biofilms (hereafter referred to as biofilms for simplicity) and staphylococcal abscess communities (SACs) [1,2]. Both structures consist of dense bacterial populations associated with an extracellular matrix that limits immune cell infiltration and reduces antibiotic penetration, thereby promoting survival [3]. Despite these similarities, biofilms and SACs differ in several key aspects. Biofilms typically form on biotic or abiotic surfaces and are characterized by matrices rich in polysaccharides and proteins [4]. In contrast, SACs develop without substrate as tissue-embedded structures that exploit host coagulation pathways to generate a fibrin pseudocapsule and microcolony-associated meshwork that surrounds the SAC [[5], [6], [7]].

Although biofilm biology is well characterized, the factors contributing to SAC are less studied and the relationship between these two growth modes remains unclear. In particular, the genetic determinants shared between biofilms and SAC, and more importantly those that differ remain poorly understood, as direct comparative studies are lacking. Here, we compared SAC and biofilm formation of Methicillin Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) USA300 wild type (WT) and selected transposon single-gene deletion mutants selected for their role in key mechanisms of SAC or biofilm formation. Fibrin modulation was interrogated using mutants lacking either coagulase (Δcoa) or staphylokinase (Δsak): coagulase activates host prothrombin to convert fibrinogen into fibrin [6,7], whereas staphylokinase activates host plasminogen to promote fibrin degradation [8,9]. Surface adhesion was assessed using a sortase A–deficient strain (ΔsrtA), as sortase A anchors key adhesins to the bacterial cell wall [10]. In addition, global stress and virulence regulation were examined using a mutant lacking the alternative sigma factor SigB (ΔrpoF), which negatively regulates the Agr quorum-sensing system [11]. By directly contrasting aggregation phenotypes across these mutants, we aimed to identify genetic determinants underlying S. aureus persistence in both SAC and biofilm and identify whether these show distinct or, alternatively, equivalent effects on aggregate development.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

S. aureus USA300 (WT) and transposon mutants (Δsak, Δcoa, ΔrpoF, and ΔsrtA) were obtained from the Nebraska Transposon Mutant Library (NTML), generated by erythromycin-resistance cassette insertion [12]. Strains were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB, Oxoid) with shaking (100 rpm) or on Triptic soy agar (TSA, Oxoid) plates without shaking, both supplemented with erythromycin (5 μg/mL, Erythromycin A hydrate, Sigma) at 37 °C.

Growth kinetics were assessed by adjusting an overnight bacterial liquid culture to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.01 in fresh TSB and monitoring the OD600 every 10 min for 24 h (Cerillo Stratus microplate reader). Six replicates were performed.

Cell response to bacterial supernatants

Telomerase-immortalized human foreskin fibroblasts (hTERT-BJ1) were purchased from Clontech (USA) and cultured in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle – Alpha Modification (α-MEM, Gibco, Thermo Fischer) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, SeraPlus), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Pen/Strep, Gibco) and always incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 as described previously [13]. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with filter-sterilized bacterial supernatants from exponentially growing liquid cultures (grown as described above), diluted 1:10 in fresh culture medium.

CellTiter-Blue® (Promega) assay was performed on the hTERT-BJ1 over 7 days, with time points at day 1, 5 and 7, with n = 3 technical replicates. Positive control cultures were carried out in the appropriate cell culture medium without bacterial supernatant. Cells for negative control were incubated with Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), Thermo Fischer) for 5 min. The medium was refreshed on day 1, 3 and 5. On the day of measurement, all conditions were incubated with fresh media and CellTiter Blue® reagent according to manufacturer instructions for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm, using 600 nm as reference wavelength with a plate reader (TECAN, Switzerland). Viability was expressed as a percentage relative to positive controls. Data represent the mean of duplicate wells for each condition.

SAC formation

SACs were grown in collagen gel with 1:1 (v/v) pooled human plasma (Regional Blood Donation Service SRK Graubünden, Chur, Switzerland) for 15 h, as described previously [14]. Bacterial dilution was adapted by diluting in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma) instead of RPMI 1640 medium.

Biofilm formation

Biofilms were formed on sterile grade-2 titanium discs (9 mm diameter, 1 mm thickness) at 37 °C for 48 h in TSB with 1% pooled human plasma without shaking as previously described [15].

Crystal violet biomass quantification

Biofilm biomass was quantified using a standard crystal violet assay as previously described [16]. Briefly, biofilms were grown as before for 48 h in TSB + 1% human plasma but on PEG-coated 96-well plates instead of titanium discs. Wells were washed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria, and biofilms were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet, and air-dried. The bound dye was solubilized in ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Six replicated were performed.

Colony forming units (CFU) quantification

CFU quantification for SACs was performed as previously described [17], with minor modifications. Briefly, SAC-containing collagen gels were mechanically disrupted with 1 mm zirconium oxide beads (bullet blender, Next Advance) for 3 min (speed setting of 10) to release embedded bacteria, followed by serial dilution in PBS and plating on TSA.

For biofilms, bacteria were detached from titanium discs by a combination of sonication (15 min) and vortex to ensure complete disruption of the biofilm structure. The resulting bacterial suspensions were serially diluted in PBS, plated on TSA, and incubated overnight at 37 °C for CFU enumeration.

Fluorescence staining and confocal microscopy

Bacterial viability of SAC and biofilm was assessed using the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer's protocol with SYTO9 (live channel, excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm) and propidium iodide (dead channel, excitation 535 nm, emission 617 nm). Plasma/media were aspirated, gels/discs washed once with 500 μL PBS each, stained with 200 μL dye for 15 min at room temperature protected from light, then washed twice with 500 μL PBS (second wash left on). Fibrin was labelled by adding fluorescent fibrinogen (1 μL/mL; Fibrinogen from Human Plasma, Alexa Fluor™ 647 Conjugate, Invitrogen) to the human plasma at t = 0. Finally, samples were mounted on glass cover slips prior to imaging.

Phase contrast images of SAC were acquired using an Axio Slide scanner (Axio 7, Zeiss). Fluorescence imaging of biofilms and SACs was performed using an inverted Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope (Zeiss), with biofilms directly imaged on the titanium discs and SAC directly within the transwells. Z-stacks were acquired for SAC samples.

Image analysis

Fiji (ImageJ v1.53q) [18] was used to insert scale bars, adjust brightness/contrast consistently across groups, z-stack projection for SAC and quantify live/dead or fibrinogen areas, keeping threshold settings constant across replicates. Biofilm area was normalized to the disc for live and fibrin area, SAC area to combined live/dead signal for fibrin area. SAC live area was used as absolute values. Five replicates per treatment were analysed.

Data visualization and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 for Windows (GraphPad Software) was used for data visualization and statistical analysis. Data is shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For quantitative image analysis of fluorescence imaging (SAC and biofilm), repeated measures (RM) one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons was applied and data was assumed normally distributed. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Results

Effect of mutants on growth rate and cytotoxicity

The WT and all mutants exhibited similar planktonic growth when cultured in TSB at 37 °C with shaking (Fig. 1A), ultimately reaching equivalent optical density plateaus. All strains displayed a typical growth curve with an initial lag phase, followed by exponential growth prior to a stationary phase.

To assess cytotoxicity towards mammalian cells, fibroblast viability was measured following exposure to filter-sterilized overnight bacterial culture supernatants (1:10 dilution). The supernatants from mutants Δsak, Δcoa and ΔsrtA were found to have no cytotoxic effects, with no difference to those from the WT strain. The supernatant from the ΔrpoF strain, however, caused a marked reduction in cell viability by day 1 (43% viability) and further decrease by day 5 (3% viability) (Fig. 1B).

Effects of mutants on SAC formation

SAC were grown in collagen gels supplemented with human plasma for 15 h. The bright-field phase contrast image shown (Fig. 1C) depicts a typical WT SAC, characterized by dense bacterial aggregation. Confocal live/dead staining of 15 h-old SACs revealed comparable overall size and morphology across all strains, although substantial variability was observed between individual SAC samples in overall distribution of live and dead bacteria (Fig. 1D). Images of 5 replicates each showing fluorescent fibrinogen, live and dead channels and the merged images are in Fig. S1. Despite these qualitative differences between individual images, quantitative image-based analysis of live area did not reveal significant differences between mutants once 5 replicate datapoints were collated (Fig. 1E, left). Analysis of fibrin incorporation revealed a mean fibrin area of approximately 10% of total SAC area in WT (Fig. 1E, middle). The average fibrin area was increased by a lot in Δsak SACs (non-significant), whereas Δcoa, ΔsrtA, and ΔrpoF SACs displayed slightly reduced (non-significant) fibrin area compared to WT; (Fig. 1E, middle). CFU enumeration of SACs demonstrated comparable bacterial counts across all strains (Fig. 1E, right), confirming similar overall bacterial viability and growth.

Effects of mutants on biofilm formation

Fluorescence microscopy of live/dead-stained, 48-h biofilms grown on titanium discs revealed largely comparable overall biofilm appearance among WT, Δsak, Δcoa, and ΔrpoF strains. However, the ΔsrtA mutant showed a visibly increased surface coverage (Fig. 2A). Images of 5 replicates each showing fluorescent fibrinogen, live and dead channels and the merged images are in Fig. S2. Quantitative image analysis of the live signal confirmed this observation, showing a significant increase in biofilm-associated live fluorescence area for ΔsrtA compared to WT (p = 0.0464), while all other mutants exhibited values comparable to WT (Fig. 2B, left).

Analysis of fibrin area within biofilms showed similar levels across WT, Δsak, Δcoa, and ΔrpoF, while ΔsrtA biofilms again exhibited a higher fibrin-associated signal (Fig. 2B, middle), consistent with their increased surface coverage.

CFU quantification showed that ΔsrtA biofilms contained approximately 1 log10 more viable bacteria and coagulase-deficient biofilms (Δcoa) exhibited ∼1 log10 lower CFU counts compared to WT (Fig. 2C, right). These differences were not statistically significant. Deletion of sak or rpoF did not appreciably alter CFU levels relative to WT.

Crystal violet staining revealed comparable biofilm biomass (including matrix) among most strains, however, ΔsrtA once again displayed higher mean staining (non-significant) with high variability and ΔrpoF showed a significant reduction (p = 0.0226) in biomass compared to WT (Fig. 2C).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate how selected gene deletions differentially affect SAC and biofilm formation, the two primary aggregates formed by S. aureus in infected tissues. Biofilms are classically surrounded by a mainly bacterially produced matrix including host-derived factors, whereas SACs develop within host-derived fibrin networks formed in plasma-rich tissue environments. Therefore, deletions in genes for fibrin degradation (Δsak), fibrin formation (Δcoa), adhesin anchoring to the cell wall (ΔsrtA), and inhibition of Agr quorum sensing regulator (ΔrpoF) were included as these are the key factors involved in fibrin formation, virulence, and adhesion in biofilm and SAC. The biofilm phenotype changes observed here differed from those in SAC assays, indicating context-dependent effects of the tested gene deletions. Notably, absence of staphylokinase only led to an increase in fibrin area within SAC for some replicates (non-significant), while coagulase alone also did not produce statistically significant alterations in fibrin architecture in SACs or biofilm. This suggests that fibrin structuring is not governed by a single factor under these conditions, but instead reflects the combined activity of multiple, potentially redundant or competing mechanisms. On the other hand, deletion of srtA led to increased surface coverage in biofilm and ΔrpoF reduced biofilm biomass. Although both structures promote bacterial survival, single-gene deletions reveal environment-dependent effects, indicating that certain mutations can have different effects in the two experimental systems.

The average non-significant increase seen in fibrin area Δsak SACs compared to WT was driven by some replicates with high variability. As this effect was not accompanied by changes in CFU counts or SAC size, it indicates that the phenotype reflects altered matrix composition rather than bacterial growth. Biofilm produced by Δsak was comparable to the WT in all tested aspects. Staphylokinase is a secreted plasminogen activator that promotes fibrinolysis by converting host plasminogen into plasmin, thereby contributing to fibrin degradation and matrix remodelling [8,9]. Given the central role of fibrin in SAC architecture, we hypothesized that deletion of sak would enhance fibrin accumulation in SAC, even though this has not directly been tested to date. While fibrin incorporation into biofilms remains incompletely understood, literature indicates that staphylokinase plays a role in S. aureus spreading and biofilm degradation [8,9]. Here, the fibrin signal was less pronounced by imaging in biofilms than in SACs for all strains, which likely reflecting reduced overall fibrin incorporation in the biofilm model. However, even in the fibrin-rich SACs, the Δsak mutant did not lead to significant changes in fibrin incorporation, suggesting that staphylokinase functions as a matrix-modulating factor whose impact depends on fibrin availability within the surrounding environment and might be strain dependent.

The counter partner of staphylokinase, coagulase, is a secreted virulence factor that binds prothrombin to form a staphylothrombin complex, which converts fibrinogen into fibrin and promotes clot formation around bacterial clusters [7,19]. While this activity supports abscess formation and fibrin pseudocapsule development in vivo [5], loss of coa alone did not substantially alter fibrin architecture in the USA300 background here. This limited phenotype change may reflect compensatory activity from other fibrin-inducing factors, such as alternative prothrombin activator von Willebrand factor–binding protein (vWbp)[5,20]. Previous literature also saw that despite forming different fibrin structures, having either Coa or vWbp was sufficient to form SAC, suggesting redundance [6]. In relation to biofilm formation, previous literature has shown that coagulase lacking mutants or chemical inhibition of fibrin formation drastically reduced S. aureus adhesion and retention in catheters, while improving infection outcomes in vivo [14]. On the other hand, a study using JE2 transposon mutants lacking either Coa or vWbp production, showed impaired biofilm formation only in the Δcoa mutant [21]. The lack of effect from deleting coa in the current study might come from differences in growth medium and bacterial strain, highlighting the complexity of this regulation.

The biggest differential factor between SAC and biofilm in this study was from the srtA deletion mutant. Sortase A is responsible for anchoring several adhesins, such as FnbpA/B and ClfA/B, to the bacterial cell wall Tsompanidou et al., 2012. The ΔsrtA strain in our study did not show any phenotype in SAC formation, but exhibited significantly increased biofilm surface coverage, as reflected by a larger live bacterial area in confocal imaging. Notably, this increase in spatial coverage did not correspond to a proportional rise in viable bacterial numbers, as CFU counts were only modestly elevated. Crystal violet staining further revealed increased average biofilm biomass (non-significant), suggesting that the altered phenotype may reflect changes in biofilm architecture and matrix distribution rather than a substantial acceleration of bacterial growth. To note is also the high variability between replicates of the srtA mutant in the crystal violet experiment. As this high variability was increased compared to the other mutants and the parental strain, this suggests potential impaired stability of the biofilm matrix for ΔsrtA, making sample-loss during washing steps more likely. This also seems to be in line with previous reports describing enhanced colony spreading in sortase A chromosomal deletion mutants of S. aureus Tsompanidou et al., 2012. However, adding inhibitors of sortase A or both A and B has previously been shown to reduce S. aureus biofilm biomass [22], [21,23,24], which was not reproduced here. This is especially interesting since MRSA were previously shown to form polysaccharide independent, but highly sortase A dependent biofilm matrices [25], while srtA deletion did not affect Methicillin Susceptible S. aureus biofilms [26]. Under our in vitro conditions, sortase-dependent adhesins were not strictly required for SAC or biofilm accumulation, but significantly influenced biofilm architecture. The increased average fibrin signal observed in ΔsrtA biofilms (non-significant) likely reflects the broader spatial distribution of bacteria rather than increased overall fibrin incorporation. However, this interpretation is limited by the two-dimensional nature of the imaging approach for biofilm, which captures surface coverage but does not resolve biofilm depth or total fibrin volume. The high variability, as seen for fibrin signal in SAC, again suggests decreased matrix stability in this mutant, and an increased likelihood of losing parts of the sample during washing. Overall, the specific effect of ΔsrtA on biofilm only, and not on SAC suggests that the SAC model is less sensitive to perturbation by this mutation.

SigB is a global stress regulator that negatively modulates the Agr quorum-sensing system. Reduced SigB activity enhances Agr signaling, which promotes the expression of multiple secreted proteases and fibrinolytic factors, thereby contributing to matrix degradation and bacterial dispersal Jenul and Horswill, 2019, [27,28]. Previous literature also showed that deletion of rpoF, encoding SigB increased inflammation and cytotoxicity against neutrophils, cultured tissue cells, and lung epithelial cells [29], [30]. Consistent with this, the rpoF mutant in the current study, resulted in a pronounced increase in host cell cytotoxicity. This near-complete loss of fibroblast viability upon exposure to stationary-phase ΔrpoF supernatant, highlights a strong virulence-associated phenotype, or protease-mediated cell detachment. Consistent with a role in virulence factor secretion, ΔrpoF formed biofilms with significantly reduced total biomass, indicative of increased structural dispersion. This confirmes previous literature, showing that Agr plays a role in biofilm dispersion through protease secretion [31,32]. In contrast, SAC formation was largely preserved. Therefore, Agr inhibition seems to be less important for SAC compared to biofilm formation. However, the collagen-based in vitro model may physically retain SAC structure, potentially masking dispersion events. Together, these findings suggest that SigB influences the balance between aggregation, matrix stability, and virulence factor production.

Overall, identical genetic deletions differentially affected SACs and biofilms, indicating environment-dependent gene contributions rather than universal structural roles. Future studies should focus on if these differences originate from aggregate attachment (cell-cell interactions versus cell-surface interactions) or consider nutrient and plasma availability between models. Using matched culture conditions and time-resolved transcriptomic and biochemical analysis will be required to determine how coa, sak, srtA and rpoF expression, as well as coagulase and staphylokinase activity, differ between SACs and conventional surface-associated biofilms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that S. aureus community formation is highly context-dependent, with the relative importance of specific genes varying according to the surrounding environment. By directly comparing SACs formed in a three-dimensional matrix with biofilms grown on titanium surfaces, we show that aggregation behaviour is distinct between both models. While SAC formation and fibrin incorporation were largely preserved across all mutants, biofilm phenotypes were more sensitive to genetic perturbation and varied in a gene-specific manner. Genes involved in fibrin remodelling, such as sak and coa, exhibited only mild environment-dependent effects in fibrin-rich SACs and biofilms under the tested conditions. A stronger effect was seen for deletion of srtA, which led to increased biofilm area coverage, but did not affect the SAC structure. Additionally, disruption of global regulation via rpoF deletion had a strong impact on cytotoxicity, highlighting the importance of regulatory networks in modulating virulence across contexts. Furthermore, rpoF deletion reduced biofilm biomass, underscoring a decoupling between aggregation and virulence. These findings emphasize that certain mutations in relevant genes have different effects in tissue-embedded bacterial communities and surface-associated biofilms, but they do not necessarily establish that SACs and biofilms represent biologically distinct aggregation states. The model specific importance of the selected genes, underscore the need to account for environment-specific bacterial regulation when designing experiments, especially for designing specific therapeutic interventions.
