Study protocol for a randomized, parallel-group trial to compare the effectiveness of registered dietitian and occupational therapy services on fall risk among home-delivered meal recipients
Authors: Lisa A Juckett, Shivam Joshi, J Madison Hyer, Govind Hariharan, Kali S Thomas, Mequeil L Howard, Leah E Bunck, Melinda L Rowe, Andrea Devier, Kelly Parrett, LifeCare Alliance SixtyPLUS Team

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHome-delivered meal recipients are among the most susceptible to fall-related morbidity and mortality given their complex health characteristics. While meal services funded through federal appropriations aim to reduce malnutrition and promote health, the provision of meals alone may be insufficient for mitigating the extensive fall risk factors this population experiences. We will apply a novel, minimal sufficient balance randomization process to ensure baseline covariates are balanced across the following four treatment arms: (1) meals alone, (2) meals + RDN services, (3) meals + OT services, and (4) meals + RDN + OT services. Background

Home-delivered meal recipients are among the most vulnerable, community-dwelling older adults, facing elevated fall risk due to malnutrition, functional decline, and home safety hazards [1, 2]. Malnutrition can lead to reductions in muscle mass, impaired cognition, and increased frailty - all of which heighten fall susceptibility [3, 4]. Declines in daily function, such as difficulties completing basic meal preparation and food consumption tasks, can exacerbate malnutrition [5], further increasing the risk of fall-related morbidity and mortality. Moreover, 80% of home-delivered meal recipients are living with mobility impairments [6], which can increase difficulty with ambulation around the home, especially given the high prevalence of safety hazards in the homes of low-income, community-dwelling older adults [7].

Home-delivered meal programs are widely known for providing meals to older adults (age 60 and older) with the greatest social and economic need [8]. Meal services that are funded through federal Older Americans Act appropriations are intended to reduce malnutrition and promote health and well-being [9]. Though services (e.g., types of meals served; frozen or ready-to-eat meals) can vary across the nearly 4,000 home-delivered meal agencies in the U.S., federal legislation mandates that meals meet a minimum of one-third of the dietary reference intakes as well as state and local health codes for safe food service. While prior evidence indicates that meal services can reduce healthcare utilization [10], minimize feelings of loneliness [11], and improve food security [12], the provision of meals alone is likely insufficient for mitigating meal recipients’ complex fall risk factors. Further, the mere delivery of a meal – though important – is only one element in ensuring older adults have adequate nutrition to meet their health needs and reduce the risk of malnutrition, health decline, and subsequent falls. To maximize the benefit of home-delivered meals for reducing falls, we claim that skilled, clinical guidance is needed to augment meal services and promote (a) the appropriate selection of foods that align with older adults’ dietary requirements or restrictions and (b) the safe consumption of meals in the home environment.

To achieve these two activities – appropriate meal selection and safe meal consumption – we propose home-delivered meals should be supplemented with tailored services provided by registered dietitian nutritionists (RDNs) and occupational therapists (OTs). RDNs are experts in developing individualized nutrition plans, providing nutrition education, and counseling on diet-related health concerns [13] and are frequently consulted in home-delivered meal agencies [14]. Occupational therapists are trained to conduct thorough home safety evaluations, assess physical and functional needs, and provide interventions to minimize fall risk and promote independence in daily activities (e.g., eating, meal preparation, food storage) [5, 15]. While the combination of RDN and OT services is a plausible solution to mitigate falls, their effect on home-delivered meal recipients’ level of fall risk has yet to be empirically tested in a fully powered, randomized controlled trial. Accordingly, this protocol presents study procedures to determine the effectiveness of four different service models on home-delivered meal recipients’ perceived risk of falling: (1) meals alone, (2) meals + RDN services, (3) meals + OT services, and (4) meals + RDN services + OT services. In addition to reporting our planned study activities, we also present our innovative minimum sufficient balance randomization methods to ensure adequate balance of baseline covariates across our four study arms – a particularly important element of this study given the health complexities of the home-delivered meal population.

Methods

Design and setting

We will conduct a four-arm, parallel group, randomized controlled trial in partnership with one large, community-based agency within the Midwest United States. Our partner agency is a nationally recognized nonprofit human services organization and provides support to nearly 7,000 home-delivered meal clients each year. In addition to delivering meals, our partner agency also employs RDNs who conduct nutritional assessments and offer skilled nutritional guidance to meal recipients while licensed OTs at our partner agency complete in-home safety evaluations with clients and provide fall prevention interventions and education to clients and their caregivers, as needed.

In collaboration with our research team, our partner agency will assist in recruiting participants from their standard database of new clients who either self-refer, are referred by family, or are referred by a healthcare provider to receive home-delivered meal services. This study follows procedures from protocol version 3.0 (4/7/2025) approved by The Ohio State University (#2023H0248) and registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06059404; 9/22/2023). The study activities are reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) statement [16].

Study arms

Arm 1 (Meals only/control)

Participants assigned to the “meals only” group will receive up to 14 frozen meals weekly for 3 months, delivered 1x/week. Following consent, participants will receive a menu featuring 40 standard meal options and instructions on how to select or modify their meals through contacting our partner agency via phone, if desired. Additionally, participants will receive nutrition education and fall prevention handouts. The nutrition education handouts - developed by our agency’s RDN team and based on the U.S. Dietary Guidelines for Americans [17] will highlight which meals from our agency are classified as “heart-healthy” and “diabetic-friendly,” encouraging participants to choose meals that align with their dietary needs and health conditions. Meanwhile, the fall prevention handouts offer practical strategies for enhancing kitchen and dining area safety, as well as recommendations for modifying the home environment to minimize fall hazards.

Arm 2 (Meals + RDN services)

In addition to receiving frozen meals and the handouts outlined in Arm 1, participants in this group will receive nutrition services provided by an RDN from our partner agency. Within 30 days of obtaining informed consent, one of our RDNs will conduct a nutrition assessment over the phone and assign a nutrition diagnosis to each participant. During this initial consultation, the RDN will also direct participants in selecting frozen meals that align with their specific dietary requirements, provide tailored nutrition education, and encourage them to call back with any follow-up questions or nutritional concerns. Each week, participants will have the option to modify their meal selections based on the RDN’s curated recommendations for the participant that supports their individual health needs. Furthermore, the RDNs will complete at least one follow-up phone call within 30 days of completing the nutritional assessment.

Arm 3 (Meals + OT services)

Participants in this group will receive frozen meals, nutrition education and fall prevention handouts, and in addition will be contacted by an OT from our partner agency within 30 days of enrolling in the study. During the initial phone screening, the OT will assess fall-related risk factors, such as home safety risks, potential fall hazards, or the need for durable medical equipment (e.g., walker, cane, shower chair). Within the following 30 days, an in-home assessment will take place allowing the OT to provide initial fall prevention interventions, recommend equipment, introduce functional exercise programs, and offer relevant education. To monitor adherence to fall prevention strategies, a follow-up session - conducted either in-home or over the phone at the OT’s discretion - will occur within 45 days of the initial assessment. Throughout the three-month study period, participants in this group will have the opportunity to contact their OT with any questions or concerns.

Arm 4 (Meals + RDN + OT services)

Participants assigned to this arm will receive frozen meals, nutrition education and fall prevention handouts, and in addition receive a combination of both RDN and OT services. The services provided by the RDN and OT will mirror the interventions outlined in Arms 2 and 3, including allowing participants to have autonomy over their weekly meal selections, and contacting providers as needed (Fig. 1).

Primary outcome

Our primary outcome for the current study is perceived fall risk as measured by the Short Falls Efficacy Scale-International [18]. The FES-I is a 7-item self-report questionnaire with all items measured via a 1–4-point Likert scale to evaluate participants’ concern about the possibility of falling. The FES-I has excellent test-retest reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92), and higher scores indicate greater concern with falling. Scores can be interpreted as follows:

Secondary outcome

Given that prior work has established the benefit of home-delivered meals for improving nutritional status [19], we will evaluate the effect of our four service models on participants’ risk of malnutrition. At baseline and follow-up time points, we will administer the Mini Nutrition Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF) [20], which assesses food consumption, unintentional weight loss, mobility issues, acute illness, psychological function, and body mass index. The MNA-SF has been found to have both strong sensitivity (97.9%) and specificity (100%) and has a diagnostic accuracy of 98.7% for predicting malnutrition. Indicators of malnutrition are listed below:

8–11 points = At risk of malnutrition

Exploratory outcomes

We will also explore the potential effect of services on three self-reported outcomes - dietary intake, healthcare utilization, and quality of life. To do this, we will assess dietary intake using five self-report items drawn from the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) questionnaire [21] that evaluates types and frequencies of foods consumed on a weekly basis. Healthcare utilization will be evaluated using three items adapted from the National Health and Aging Trends Study [22] related to emergency department use, hospitalizations, and rehabilitation or skilled nursing facility admissions in the last three months? Lastly, the EQ-5D-5 L is a 6-item questionnaire that will be used to assess five quality of life dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) on a 5-point scale from no problem to extreme problems [23]. The EQ-5D-5 L concludes with a visual analog scale that allows participants to rate their health on a scale from 0 (worst health imaginable) to 100 (best health imaginable).

Sample size

Data from our pilot study [24] were used to perform power analyses for the full trial. Sample sizes ranging from 114 to 152 households per arm were simulated to estimate power via 1,000 bootstrapped samples per sample size. For each sample, a mixed model was utilized to estimate the statistical significance at α = 0.05. Our simulation study revealed that a sample size of 152 households per arm (n = 608) provided 85% statistical power to detect differences between intervention arms with the control arm while accounting for attrition. We emphasize the importance of enrolling households, rather than individuals, given that randomization will occur at the household level. We opted to randomize at the household level to ensure that participants who live together (e.g., spouses) are randomized to receive the same service model. Not randomizing at the household level will introduce the opportunity for participants in the same household to receive different services than those to which they were assigned. For example, one spouse who is randomized to receive meals only (Arm 1) will likely also benefit from the OT services provided to their spouse who is randomized to receive meals + OT services (Arm 3), introducing opportunities for contamination.

Recruitment

Potential participants will be recruited through our partner agency’s standard intake process. All older adults newly interested in receiving home-delivered meal services will be screened for study eligibility. If eligible, our assessment team will describe the study, its risks and benefits, and the duration of study participation. Those older adults who are interested in participating in the study will then schedule an in-home visit with a member of our assessment team who will conduct informed consent procedures, review study risks, benefits, group assignment, and expected duration, and answer any questions the participant may have.

Blinding

Our RDNs and OTs will be blinded to the intervention allocation, in that they will not know what additional services the participant may be receiving (i.e., RDNs will not know if their participants are also receiving OT services as part of their group assignment). However, due to the nature of the study, households are not fully blinded to their intervention allocation. Additionally, the principal investigator and statistician remain unblinded, as they are directly involved in the deployment of randomization.

Randomization

Achieving covariate balance is critical in randomized trials as it ensures equitable distribution of key baseline characteristics across treatment groups, thereby minimizing confounding and enabling a clearer estimation of the intervention effects. As such, an innovative covariate-adaptive randomization approach, known as the minimal sufficient balance (MSB) design, will be utilized for the randomization process. Briefly, MSB employs statistical tests to assess balance across both continuous and categorical covariates to proactively pursue balance during the enrollment phase while also preserving randomness in treatment allocation [25]. At the time of writing, this is the first trial to employ MSB in a 2 × 2 factorial design and the first to use MSB for a cluster randomized trial.

More specifically, an initial stratified block randomization for the first 156 households, referred to as the ‘burn-in’ period will be used with a stratum of household companionship status (yes/no). Following the burn-in period, MSB will be employed to assess balance across four key covariates: age, baseline FES-I scores, baseline MNA-SF scores, and household companionship status. For each household enrolled, statistical tests will be conducted on all previously enrolled households, and if any variable was calculated to have a p-value < 0.30, the MSB voting procedure will be triggered; otherwise, the same stratified block randomization used during the burn-in phase will be continued. If the voting procedure is triggered, a series of testing will be performed in parallel – one if the household is enrolled into an intervention arm and one if the household is not enrolled into an intervention arm. Given that MSB was originally developed for two-arm trials, we will employ the MSB protocol in parallel to assign OT intervention (yes/no) and to assign RDN intervention (yes/no) intervention, and the resulting combination of the two MSB procedures will provide the final arm assignment (Fig. 2).

Quantitative data collection

Members from our assessment team will be trained on all outcome measures through a combination of written materials, simulated encounters, in-person demonstrations, and observations with the co-PIs prior to data collection. After training is completed, assessment team members will be approved to obtain informed consent and collect baseline data by administering our five outcome measures (Falls Efficacy Scale-International, Mini Nutrition Assessment-Short Form, dietary intake and healthcare utilization questionnaire, and the EQ-5D-5 L) to participants within their home. Data will be electronically entered into REDCap [26] using a secure, HIPAA-compliant tablet approved by our partner agency’s technology department. Three months following the participants’ enrollment, an assessment team member will conduct a follow-up in-home visit and re-administer the same five outcome measures to each participant.

To explore sustained effects of our interventions, participants will be contacted via telephone by graduate research assistants at 6 months post-consent where the same four outcome measures will be re-administered. Prior to collecting data, the research graduate assistants will be trained on procedures for collecting outcome measure data over the telephone and appropriate REDCap data entry procedures. Qualitative data collection

To understand participants’ experience in receiving meal services during the 3-month intervention period, we will use purposive sampling and invite 60 participants (15 participants from each arm) to complete semi-structured interviews with our lead qualitative data collector. Participants will be contacted after their 3-month follow-up visit and asked about their interest in completing one 20–30 min interview in the participant’s home. The interview guide was developed in collaboration with our partner agency leaders to understand the mechanisms of change that contribute to participants’ health outcomes (e.g., fall risk, malnutrition risk). Sample questions include, “Please describe any changes you’ve experienced at home since you started receiving meals?” and “What are your thoughts on the advice you received from the dietitian and its impact on your health and independence?” as well as “Please share your experiences with the occupational therapy sessions and any changes you’ve noticed in your independence or safety?” (Topics covered and example questions can be found in Additional file 1). All interviews will be audio recorded, with the participant’s permission, and will be transcribed in preparation for analysis.

Data analysis

Quantitative data

Prior to hypothesis testing, descriptive statistics will be provided for the entire sample and stratified by the four study arms. For continuous outcome measures, we will provide the mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed variables and median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables. Means for continuous variables will be compared by one-way ANOVA or, in the case where medians are provided, a non-parametric test (Kruskal Wallis test) will be used. For categorical outcome measures, frequencies (relative frequency [%]) will be calculated. Categorical variables will be compared by chi-squared test, or Fisher’s exact test when expected counts are ≤ 5.

Multivariable analysis will be conducted using the arms as the independent variable to assess the continuous outcome measures. Effect sizes will be calculated using marginal estimates from a repeated- measures, mixed effects linear regression models controlling for patient demographic and clinical characteristics and including a random intercept for practice with a first-order auto regressive covariance structure (a.k.a. AR (1)), utilizing a between-within denominator degrees of freedom approximation. Such an approach is ideal for the following reasons: (1) the AR(1) covariance structure accounts for higher correlations among more closely observed time points (e.g., the correlation between months T1 and T2 would be more strongly correlated than between T1 and T3); and (2) the between-within denominator degrees of freedom approximation has been shown to be the optimal choice for trials with relatively small samples as it preserves the type-I error and maximizes the statistical power. Qualitative data

We will apply a hybrid thematic analysis approach [27], using both deductive codes based on the interview guide and inductive codes for emerging concepts. An initial codebook will be developed, with two graduate research assistants independently coding 5–10 transcripts to establish intercoder reliability. Discrepancies will be resolved through consensus discussions with the co-PIs, and the codebook will be refined iteratively. Once sufficient coding consistency is achieved (≥ 80% agreement), remaining transcripts will be single-coded. Codes will be organized into themes that reflect potential mechanisms of change – that is, the processes through which meals, RDN services, and OT services may contribute to improved health outcomes. Analysis will continue until thematic saturation is reached [28].

Event reporting and trial oversight

This study involves minimal risk to participants and does not require the establishment of a formal Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), consistent with regulatory guidelines at The Ohio State University (the IRB of record). Oversight of trial conduct will be managed by the study PI and the research team at Ohio State. Any adverse events (e.g., falls, near-falls, or fall-related injuries that occur during or immediately following OT intervention activities; reactions to new or modified dietary recommendations that are directly related to RDN guidance) or protocol deviations will be documented and reported to Ohio State’s IRB according to standard reporting requirements. We plan to hold weekly meetings with core members of our research team and partner agency to review study progress and assess the implementation of our approved study activities. We will also hold quarterly meetings with our full trial team and separate quarterly meetings with representatives from our funding agency to ensure activities align with our proposed workplan.

Dissemination

We plan to disseminate findings from this study to a diverse range of audiences. Because this is a single-site trial, our initial dissemination efforts will focus on developing agency-friendly materials that communicate study findings to our partner agency’s administrative leadership, donors, and Board of Trustees. Sharing results in this way will strengthen academic and community partnerships and foster new opportunities for collaboration to better serve the agency’s local clientele. Broader dissemination will include submission of study results to peer-reviewed journals, with a preference for open-access venues to ensure accessibility for organizations without subscription-based database access. Finally, we will present study findings at national and regional conferences that engage audiences in home- and community-based service delivery, allied health professions (e.g., dietetics, occupational therapy), and geriatrics and gerontology.

Discussion

This study will determine the effect of four different service models on reducing perceived fall risk among home-delivered meal recipients. Building on our prior work [24], this study serves as our definitive trial to estimate the value of integrating RDN and OT services into meal agencies and determine how these services may enhance standard home-delivered meal programming. We claim that this study will make unique contributions to the field of aging research in three distinct ways. First, the population of older adults in the United States is expected to grow exponentially over the next decade. By 2040, one-fifth of the U.S. population (that is, over 78 million adults) will be over the age of 65 [29], warranting the need for comprehensive, tailored health and social services that effectively promote safety and the ability for older adults to “age-in-place.” While home-delivered meals provide opportunities for older adults to receive balanced meals each day [8], the provision of meals alone may not meet the complex health and nutritional needs of meal recipients. Supplementing meals with RDN and OT services offers one promising solution to optimize safety, independence, and well-being of the aging population.

Second, we acknowledge that funding and resource restrictions may limit the extent to which other home-delivered meal agencies can provide RDN and OT services to their clients. However, findings from this study may demonstrate that meals combined with clinical services can reduce perceived fall risk and, as a result, plausibly reduce older adult healthcare costs. Our future findings can be disseminated to policymakers and insurance payors interested in minimizing healthcare expenditures related to fall-related morbidity and mortality. Recent estimates suggest that expenditures for non-fatal falls total $80 billion – a $30 billion increase from similar estimates in 2015 – with the majority paid by Medicare [30, 31]. Notably, prior work confirms that correcting for vitamin deficiencies, which can be addressed by RDNs, can avert nearly $250 million in direct medical costs and home modifications implemented by an OT, like the modifications that our study participants will receive, avert over $440 million [32]. Thus, our study will build upon this existing evidence by delivering tailored clinical services to potentially mitigate falls their associated healthcare expenditures.

Third, our novel use of a MSB randomization design will make a unique contribution to the aging literature. To our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to apply MSB randomization in a community-based RCT with an older adult sample. Our MSB randomization process will prevent serious imbalances in baseline covariates across our four treatment arms and will also preserve the randomness of treatment allocation [25]. Given the highly heterogeneous health characteristics of the home-delivered meal population [2], we claim that our use of MSB randomization will correct for potential covariate imbalances and allow for our team, as well as our peer scientists and policymakers, to have even greater confidence in our study findings.

Limitations

Though this study provides a novel addition to the literature, it is not without limitations. We recognize that this study is being conducted in partnership with a single community-based agency in the Midwest United States. As such, our results may not be as applicable to other regions with different demographic profiles or to other home-delivered meal agencies with different service infrastructures. Moreover, our outcome measures (e.g., Falls Efficacy Scale, dietary intake, healthcare utilization), rely on self-reported data, which can be subject to recall bias and inaccuracies compared to other data sources such as insurance records or claims data. Our follow-up period is also relatively short (3-months), which may not capture long-term effects of the interventions on fall risk; however, our procedures for collecting 6-month follow-up data via telephone aim to attenuate this limitation. Lastly, the effectiveness of RDN and OT services may vary depending on the individual clinician’s expertise and their consistency of service delivery, though our clinicians will complete a fidelity “checklist” after each encounter with participants to ensure the core components of RDN (i.e., initial assessment, meal selection, nutrition education, follow-up) and OT interventions (i.e., phone screen, initial assessment, home safety interventions, follow-up) are implemented as intended. A random selection of RDN and OT encounters will be audited to ensure fidelity is being appropriately documented.

Conclusions

This trial will provide valuable evidence on the effectiveness of integrating RDN and OT services into home-delivered meal programs. Our findings will contribute significantly to the field of aging research by demonstrating the potential benefits of supplementing meal services with skilled clinical guidance. As the population of older adults continues to grow, the need for effective, tailored health and social services becomes essential. This study underscores the importance of addressing the complex nutritional and functional needs of older adults to promote their ability to age-in-place safely and independently.
