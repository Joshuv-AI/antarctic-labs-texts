Garré’s osteomyelitis of the mandible in an adolescent: a case report
Authors: Wiem Feki, Imen Haddar, Marwa Bahloul, Zeineb Mnif, Thouraya Kammoun, Ines Maaloul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriostitis ossificans, also known as Garré’s osteomyelitis, is a rare form of chronic osteomyelitis characterized by new bone formation with periosteal reaction. It most frequently affects the mandible, with common sources of infection being dental caries and periodontal lesions. This condition mainly affects younger patients. The therapeutic approach usually involves elimination of the infectious source, administration of antibiotics, and surgery if there is no response. This case is notable because no definitive source of infection was identified, which represents the main novelty of this report. The differential diagnosis with mandibular tumors or other inflammatory bone diseases made this case particularly challenging.Case presentationWe report the case of a 13-year-old boy from Sfax, Tunisia, who presented with chronic osteomyelitis with proliferative periostitis of the mandible. He had no identifiable source of infection. Imaging and histological findings confirmed Garré’s osteomyelitis. The patient was successfully treated with intravenous antibiotics. At 10-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with a symmetrical facial morphology.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of considering Garré’s osteomyelitis in the differential diagnosis of mandibular swellings in adolescents, even in the absence of an evident infectious source. It also demonstrates the successful nonsurgical management of nonodontogenic Garré’s osteomyelitis.Keywords: Garré’s osteomyelitis, Mandible, Child, Management

Introduction

Garré’s osteomyelitis (GO) is a type of chronic osteomyelitis accompanied by hyperplastic periostitis [1]. The mandible is the most common site of this rare disease and is usually associated with odontogenic infections in children and young adults [2–4], such as dental caries, pulp necrosis, periapical abscesses, or periodontal lesions of molar teeth. Several case reports of GO of the mandible have been published in the literature [1–5].

The underlying pathology is the periosteal reaction to inflammation, hence it is more precisely known as chronic osteomyelitis with proliferative periostitis [1, 4]. Reactive periosteal bone formation develops due to chronic irritation or infection. This condition is usually nonsuppurative and can be asymptomatic without signs of inflammation [1, 4]. The recommended treatment is symptomatic, with analgesics, intravenous antibiotics, and eventually surgical intervention if complications such as abscess formation, fistula, bone sequestration, or persistent facial deformity occur.

However, the present case is unusual because no identifiable infectious source was found, which represents its main originality. Recent literature has reported new cases and insights on GO in children [1–10], confirming its relevance to current clinical practice.

In this case report, we describe the clinical, radiological, and histological findings of a rare pediatric case of GO of the mandible, without an obvious cause, which improved significantly under medical treatment.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 13-year-old boy presented with pain and swelling of the right jaw, without fever, evolving for 10 days.

Clinical findings

Clinical examination revealed facial asymmetry and a diffuse swelling of the right jaw, which was hard in consistency without erythema. The patient also had trismus with limitation of mouth opening. The oral examination did not show any dental caries.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory tests including complete blood count (CBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were within normal limits. Microbiological cultures were performed and returned negative.

A computed tomography (CT) scan of the facial mass showed an expansive osteolytic lesion in the right mandibular angle and ramus, breaking the bony cortex of both jugular and lingual sides, with a periosteal reaction all around, characteristic of Garré’s sclerosing osteomyelitis (Fig. 1, 2, 3 and 4). Tumor origin remains less likely on the scanographic aspect.

Coronal view showing associated compact periosteal reaction (green arrows). This lesion is in close contact with the inferior alveolar canal (red arrow)

Three-dimensional reconstruction image showing buccal bone changes, which indicate neo-hard tissue formation

Axial section in the soft tissue window showing swelling of homolateral parotid gland (blue star)

For clarity, the CT images demonstrated a multilayered periosteal reaction (“onion-skin” appearance) without soft tissue mass, suggestive of a benign inflammatory process.

Mandibular magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed for better characterization of the lesion, which showed spongy signal anomaly of the angle of the right mandibular ramus in T1 and T2 hyposignal with diffusion restriction and enhancement after injection of gadolinium. The cortex was broken in places with a periosteal reaction. The cortico-spongy differentiation was preserved (Fig. 5). Osteitis was then suggested, but tumor origin could not be eliminated.

Magnetic resonance imaging axial section showing cancellous bone signal abnormality hyposignal in T1, hypersignal in T2 (white arrow) with diffusion restriction and enhancement after injection with no sign of bone marrow replacement; swollen and infiltrated masseter muscle (white star) and submandibular gland (pink arrow)

Thus, a biopsy of the mandibular lesion was performed, showing inflammatory remodeling mainly composed of polymorphonuclear neutrophils compatible with osteomyelitis without signs of malignancy. A final diagnosis of Garré’s osteomyelitis was made on the basis of the histopathological findings.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient was put on intravenous antibiotics: amoxicillin–clavulanic acid for 2 weeks. Given the clinical improvement and the absence of dental caries, the maxillofacial team recommended therapeutic abstention with regular stomatological monitoring and CT scan of the mandible every 6 months.

Follow-up and outcomes

At 10-month follow-up, there is no swelling or pain, and the patient progressed well.

Discussion

Garré’s osteomyelitis (GO) is a distinctive subtype of chronic osteomyelitis characterized by a nonsuppurative, proliferative periosteal reaction, predominantly affecting the mandible in pediatric and adolescent populations. Following Garré’s original description in long bones [2], the condition was subsequently identified in the jaws, with early cases reported in the 1950s, highlighting its relevance in the maxillofacial domain. Since then, its recognition has increased due to characteristic clinical and radiological features. Odontogenic infections, especially those arising from periapical or periodontal pathologies of molars, remain the most commonly implicated cause [1, 5, 6]. In a recent pediatric case, infection associated with an impacted third molar was identified as the source of inflammation, underscoring the importance of detailed dental assessment in suspected cases of mandibular GO [1]. However, cases without a clear source have also been reported, necessitating histological analysis to exclude malignancies or other inflammatory bone diseases [10]. Radiological evaluation remains a pivotal component of the diagnostic process. The hallmark feature is a multilayered periosteal reaction—often referred to as the “onion-skin” appearance, typically observed on conventional radiographs and more precisely detailed on CT scans [4, 5]. This laminated pattern reflects repeated episodes of subperiosteal new bone deposition, usually triggered by chronic, low-grade infection. Singh et al. illustrated this phenomenon in a pediatric patient, where panoramic imaging revealed uniform lamellar bone formation along the mandibular cortex [5]. CT imaging further refines the diagnosis by revealing not only cortical thickening and periosteal layering, but also any underlying bone marrow involvement, sequestrum formation, or soft tissue spread [4, 7, 10]. Jayanthan et al. emphasized the importance of CT in detecting soft tissue changes associated with the disease, particularly when fistulas or abscesses complicate the clinical course [4]. These imaging findings, although characteristic, must be interpreted cautiously since similar periosteal reactions may occur in more aggressive conditions such as Ewing’s sarcoma or osteosarcoma [6, 8]. In terms of microbiology, cultures are frequently negative, a finding attributed to either low-virulence pathogens or a chronic inflammatory process persisting after bacterial eradication [8]. However, when positive, the causative agents often include common oral flora such as Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Streptococcus species [1, 4]. These organisms may contribute to intermittent phases of exacerbation and remission, complicating both diagnosis and treatment. Though classically asymptomatic, GO may present with facial swelling or asymmetry, especially in younger patients. Pain is generally absent unless the disease progresses or is complicated. Some reported cases have documented soft tissue extension, abscess formation, and even extraoral fistulas when the inflammation extends beyond the periosteum into adjacent structures [4, 7, 9]. Such complications can lead to functional impairments, including trismus due to masticator space involvement [7]. These more severe forms require prompt intervention to prevent long-term sequelae. Treatment is typically conservative, focusing on removing the infectious source—usually by extracting the affected tooth—and administering a course of antibiotics [5, 6]. In cases without active infection but with lingering inflammation, nonsurgical endodontic management has proven effective, as documented by Kim et al. [10]. However, when abscess or fistula formation occurs, surgical intervention, including incision and drainage, becomes necessary [4, 7]. Ultimately, the diagnosis of GO relies on a combination of clinical suspicion, radiographic imaging, and in uncertain cases, histopathological confirmation. Our case differs from most pediatric cases by the absence of odontogenic source and by the successful outcome with medical management only. As demonstrated in our case and supported by the literature, the disease often mimics more aggressive conditions, and early recognition is critical to avoid unnecessary overtreatment. Future studies and case reports are needed to further clarify the disease’s etiology, particularly in patients without obvious infectious foci.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that the etiology of chronic osteomyelitis with proliferative periostitis is unknown. On the basis of the clinical features and imaging findings, a preliminary diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis with proliferative periostitis was made. Bone biopsy confirmed the diagnosis. Medical treatment with antibiotics, combined with close monitoring, has led to significant clinical improvement. This case highlights successful nonsurgical management of a nonodontogenic form of Garré’s osteomyelitis.
