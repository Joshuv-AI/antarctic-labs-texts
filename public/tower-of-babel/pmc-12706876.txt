Impact of medication therapy management intervention on medication adherence among adult patients comorbid with diabetes mellitus and hypertension at outpatient clinics in Southwest Ethiopia: a cluster randomized controlled trial
Authors: Tadesse Sheleme, Firomsa Bekele, Wayessa Olika, Birbirsa Sefera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with diabetes and hypertension require long-term medication, which may increase the chance of non-adherence to medications. Medication therapy management has been shown to positively impact medication adherence. This study aimed to assess the impact of medication therapy management on medication adherence among patients comorbid with diabetes mellitus and hypertension.MethodsA cluster randomized controlled trial was conducted at outpatient clinics from January 03 to July 18, 2022. The hospital was randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. The patients attending hospitals assigned to the interventional group received medication therapy management services in addition to usual care. A systematic random sampling technique was used to select the study participants from each hospital. Medication adherence was assessed using the Adherence in Chronic Diseases Scale. SPSS version 24.0 was used for data analysis. The independent sample t-test and chi-square test were used to compare the variables.ResultOf 402 eligible patients, 201 were randomly assigned to the medication therapy management intervention group and 201 patients were randomized to the control group. No significant differences were observed in the baseline characteristics of the patients. The adherence rate in the intervention group increased by 50%, whereas it remained fairly constant in the control group, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe current study showed that medication therapy management intervention improved the patients’ medication adherence. Hospitals should strengthen and integrate clinical pharmacy services into their healthcare system.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.Gov NCT06112925.

Background

Chronic diseases have become a public health burden and one of the leading causes of mortality and disability [1]. Hypertension and diabetes are the most common chronic diseases, affecting 70 million and 29 million Americans, respectively [2]. The national prevalence of diabetes is approximately 6.5% [3]. Hospital-based studies have revealed that the prevalence of hypertension in Ethiopia ranges from 13.2% to 18.8% in Ethiopia [4]. The rate of cardiovascular disease is two or three times higher in adults with diabetes than in adults without diabetes. Hypertension is an extremely common comorbid condition in diabetes, affecting approximately 20–60% of patients with diabetes [5]. The high prevalence of hypertension comorbidity and the chronic nature of the disease necessitate the need for diabetes disease state management [3]. Appropriate management of these conditions, including the proper use of medications, can lead to better disease control, decreased disease-related complications, and improved overall health [2].

The number of patients with diabetes and/or hypertension is increasing at an alarming rate, with high rates of hospitalization and mortality in Ethiopia [3, 4]. Patients with diabetes are predisposed to clustering of cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension [6]. Hypertension is the leading cardiovascular disease attributable cause of morbidity and mortality in diabetic patients. Hypertension with diabetes increases the mortality risk by 7.2 times with a higher risk of death in developing countries such as Ethiopia [7].

Patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) and/or hypertension require long-term use of their medications, which may increase the chance of nonadherence to medications [8]. Non-adherence to medication has become a new public health burden because chronic diseases have supplanted acute diseases, leading to death and morbidity in modern medicine [9]. In addition to increasing morbidity and mortality, nonadherence has been estimated to cost 100–300 billion dollars of avoidable healthcare costs annually [10]. Poor medication adherence commonly contributes to adverse events and reduces control of chronic medical conditions [11]. Barriers to adherence may consist of complex treatment regimens, often along with long-term multi-therapies, side effects due to the medication, and insufficient, incomprehensible, or confusing information or instructions provided by the healthcare provider [12].

Chronic diseases are often exposed to polypharmacy, which involves large amounts of drugs and can therefore increase patient non-compliance with medication. This can lead to adverse effects, drug interactions, increased treatment costs, and patient confusion [1, 8, 13]. Additionally, these circumstances often result in poor control of blood glucose levels and blood pressure, which negatively affect the quality of life of these patients [13, 14]. The successful management of these disorders requires pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Desired therapeutic outcomes are achieved only when patients adhere to prescribed medications and practice non-pharmacological strategies [15].

Medication therapy management (MTM) involves a multifaceted approach of reviewing medications, providing disease-state management and self-management education, addressing medication adherence issues, and considering preventative health strategies to optimize medication-related health. The medication education and treatment advice from clinical pharmacists can increase the patient adherence of the treatment [16, 17].

MTM intervention has the potential to positively influence drug adherence and quality of prescribing [18]. Pharmacists’ MTM interventions play an integral role in assessing patients’ understanding of their conditions and therapeutic regimens [19]. MTM has been shown to be effective in lowering systolic and diastolic blood pressure and other health indicators, such as increasing patient knowledge, improving patient quality of life, and medication adherence [20, 21]. Despite the potential impact of MTM services on medication use, these services are still limited to developing countries [22]. However, medication regimens are becoming very complex and specialized, particularly for patients with comorbidities. To our knowledge, no well-controlled study has evaluated the effect of MTM on comorbid diabetes mellitus and hypertension in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting and period

The study was conducted at the outpatient clinics of four hospitals in the Ilu Ababor Zone and Buno Bedele Zone, namely Mettu Karl Hospital, Bedele Hospital, Darimu Hospital, and Didesa Hospital. Both Ilu Ababor and Buno Bedele zones are found in Oromia Regional State, Southwest Ethiopia, 600 km and 480 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, respectively. The Mettu Karl Hospital is a specialized hospital located in Mettu Town. It serves people from the Illu Ababor Zone, Gambella Regional State, and Southern Peoples Region. Hospital health services include outpatient departments, intensive care units, obstetrics and gynecology, inpatient services, and emergency intervention units. The hospital also has medical, surgical, and gynecological referral clinics. Bedele Hospital is a secondary hospital which is found in Bedele town. Its service units include outpatient and inpatient departments, emergency units, neonatal intensive care units, and obstetrics and gynecology services. Darimu Hospital and Didesa Hospital are primary hospitals located in Darimu Town and Denbi Town, respectively. They provide health services to people living in towns and the surrounding areas. Their service units included outpatient departments, obstetrics and gynecology, inpatient services, and emergency intervention units. This study was conducted between January 03 and July 18, 2022.

Study design

A health institution based cluster randomized controlled trial was conducted.

Population

The source population were of adult patients comorbid with diabetes and hypertension attending outpatient clinics in public hospitals in the Ilu Ababor and Buno Bedele zones. The study population were of adult patients comorbid with diabetes and hypertension who visited the outpatient clinics of public Hospitals in Ilu Ababor and Buno Bedele zones during the data collection period and fulfilled the inclusion criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older, had both diabetes and hypertension, and were receiving medication for diabetes and/or hypertension at selected hospitals in the Ilu Ababor and Buno Bedele zones. Patients who were excluded from the study were those who refused to participate in the study, mentally disabled or critically ill patients who could not respond, patients who did not complete the follow-up (lost to follow-up), changed follow-up sites, or pregnant women.

Sample size and sampling technique

Sample size was calculated by G*Power 3.1.9.7 version to detect a difference in drug adherence of greater than or equal to 15% between study groups based on previous study [3]. A two-tailed test was applied, considering a power of 80% (type II error of 20%) and level of significance (type I error [α]) of 5%, using a confidence level of 95%. The sample size was adjusted according to the standard criteria for cluster randomized trials using a design effect of 1.024. Considering the dropout rate of 20%, a sample size of 201 patients was required per group. The total sample size was 402 patients.

A systematic random sampling technique was used to select the study participants. Data obtained from the hospitals showed that the number of patients diagnosed with both DM and hypertension who attended follow-up visits was 545 at Mettu Karl Hospital, 380 at Bedele Hospital, 268 at Darimu Hospital, and 208 at Didesa Hospital. Based on these data, proportion allocation was performed for each hospital to select patients using every kth interval. Thus, every 4th patient was selected from the Mettu Karl and Darimu hospitals and every 3rd patient was selected from the Bedele and Didesa hospitals. A lottery method was used to identify the starting patients in each hospital. Accordingly 135 participants from Mettu Karl Hospital, 66 participants from Darimu Hospital, 130 participants from Bedele Hospital, and 71 participants from Didesa Hospital were enrolled.

Randomization and intervention

Each hospital was considered as a cluster. The hospital was randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group using the lottery method (Fig. 1).

Patients attending hospitals assigned to the interventional group received MTM services in addition to usual care, while those attending hospitals assigned to the control group received usual care only. The usual care is patients’ discussion with the attending physician and nurse regarding their disease, medications, and subsequent appointments, which may take approximately five minutes. The intervention was administered as soon as the patient was included in the intervention group and was repeated at 3rd month of the initial visit. Two consecutive follow-up visits were scheduled for 3rd month and 6th of the initial visit for each patient in all clusters. The study participants, attending healthcare providers, and data collectors were blinded to the treatment group.

The MTM services were provided by clinical pharmacists for approximately an hour. Clinical pharmacists reviewed the medication therapy and educated the patients about the importance of adherence to medication therapy and the consequences of non-adherence to the medications. Clinical pharmacists also provided education about health-promoting behaviors (healthy diet, regular exercise, salt reduction, and smoking cessation) and self-monitoring of diseases. The MTM pharmacists delivered the intervention via face-to-face meetings during the follow-up period. Lecturing, demonstrations, targeted questions, and brochures were used to deliver the education to the participants. Brochures containing written information in the Afan Oromo or Amharic language and culturally designed illustrative pictures that are easily understandable for participants who could not read were provided. The patients were asked to teach the information back to confirm their understanding.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was used to collect the data. The questionnaire was translated from English into Afan Oromo in order to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics and disease-related issues. It was back translated into English by an independent person to check for consistency. A checklist or data abstraction format was used to check and collect the medications that the patient was taking and the disease-related data. Data regarding patient adherence to medications were collected using the Adherence in Chronic Diseases Scale (ACDS).

After receiving usual care from the attending physician, all eligible patients were interviewed to complete questions on their sociodemographic characteristics. Patients’ baseline data were collected. Patient demographic details were collected during the initial visit, and medication adherence status re-assessed at 3rd and 6th of month of the study.

Two clinical pharmacists were assigned to MTM service providers for each hospital. They were provided with a 2-days intensive theoretical and practical training on the procedure to be followed during the intervention and how the intervention package was implemented. Data were independently collected by four [4] pharmacists and four [4] nurses. Four [4] pharmacists were assigned to supervise the data-collection process. All patients included in the study were followed up for six months. Supervisors and principal investigators closely followed the data collection process.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the change in medication adherence from baseline to six months in the control and intervention groups. Medication adherence was assessed using the Adherence in Chronic Diseases Scale (ACDS) [23]. The ACDS evaluates adherence in adult patients treated for chronic disease. This scale reflects real-life adherence to pharmacological treatment and explains the mechanisms that determine patient adherence.

Data quality assurance

To ensure the quality of data, data collectors were trained for two days on the objective of the study, the contents of the questionnaires, and how to maintain the confidentiality and privacy of the study subjects. To check the functionality of the questionnaire and data extraction forms, a pre-test was conducted on 5% of the sample. The collected data were checked daily for completeness and accuracy before leaving the facilities.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Prior to data entry, the principal investigator coded and edited the data. Data were entered into Epidata manager version 4.0.2 and export to the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 24.0. Data are summarized as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as percentages within the groups. The independent sample t-test and chi-square test were used to compare the variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Differences in the proportion of medication adherence between the control and interventional groups at baseline and the end of the study were determined using Pearson’s chi-square test.

Operational definition

Medication Adherence was assessed using the ACDS [23]. The ACDS is a 7-item questionnaire. Each question was associated with five predefined propositions. The questions were related to patients’ behavior, directly determining adherence or situations and opinions that may influence adherence. The total score ranged from 0 to 27 and was grouped into high/good adherence (≥ 27points scored), poor adherence (medium adherence (21–26 points scored), and low adherence (score ≤ 20 points scored)).

Results

Of 402 eligible patients, 201 were randomized to the MTM intervention group and 201 to the control group. A total 386 patients completed the study. The dropout rates in the control and intervention groups were 4.5% and 3.5%, respectively. The overall dropout rate was 3.98% (Fig. 2).

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants

No significant differences were observed in the baseline demographic or clinical characteristics of the participants. The proportion of male participants in the intervention and control groups was 57.2% % and 59.9%, respectively. Approximately 86.6% and 82.8% of the participants were married in the intervention and control groups, respectively (Table 1).

Medication usage

There was no significant difference in medication usage between the control and intervention groups, except for the use of ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers (Table 2).

Medication adherence

The medication adherence rate in the control group was 20.3% before the study and 22.4% at the end of the study period. The medication adherence rate in the intervention group was 23.7% before the study, which increased to 73.7% during the study period. The adherence rate in the intervention group increased by 50%, whereas it remained fairly constant in the control group; this difference was statistically significant (Table 3).

Discussion

MTM interventions are effective in improving health care outcomes through sustained medication adherence. MTM interventions significantly and consistently improved adherence to drug therapy for chronic medical conditions [18]. Clinical pharmacists are easily accessible medicine experts who can thoroughly evaluate numerous concerns relevant to the regimen face-to-face with the patient [24]. The current study evaluated the impact of pharmacist-led MTM interventions on medication adherence in patients comorbid with diabetes mellitus and hypertension. This study is the first cluster randomized control trial in Ethiopia to evaluate the impact of pharmacist-led MTM services on medication adherence in patients comorbid with diabetes and hypertension.

The present study showed that participants in both the intervention and control groups adhered poorly to their medications at the baseline. This may be because of the low socioeconomic and educational status of the patients in our study. A study conducted in Northwest Ethiopia showed that poor adherence to medications was predicted by low educational status and dissatisfaction with the services received at the outpatient clinic [25]. Patients with low socioeconomic and educational status are less likely to access education and healthcare services, including medication refills on time, which may increase patients’ difficulty in adhering to their treatment regimens.

The current study showed a significant difference in medication adherence between the control and intervention groups at the end of the study (p < 0.05). Adherence significantly improved in the intervention group at the end of the study period. Good medication adherence increased from 23.7% to 73.7% at the end of the 6 month in the intervention group. However, medication adherence did not significantly change from 20.3% to 22.4% in the control group. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Portugal, which reported significantly higher medication adherence in the intervention group at the end of the study (74.5% vs. 57.6%) [26]. This is also consistent with a randomized controlled trial conducted in the U.S., which showed that the mean adherence rate in the intervention group was 80.5% before the study and increased to 87.5% during the study period [27]. This finding is similar to that of another study that reported that pharmacotherapy adherence in elderly patients with diabetes and hypertension increased from 50.5% at baseline to 83.5% in the intervention group [28]. Our findings are also consistent with a previous randomized controlled study conducted in Ethiopia, which reported a marked and statistically significant increase in medication adherence from baseline to 6 months in the intervention group ( from 9.2% at baseline to 61% at 6 months) [3].

While this MTM intervention was successful in improving medication adherence, a significant portion of the intervention group (26.3%) remained non-adherent. This suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach, though effective for many, may not overcome all the complex barriers to adherence. These persistent challenges could stem from various factors, including socioeconomic barriers like financial hardship or lack of transportation, behavioral and psychological factors such as forgetfulness or fear of side effects, low health literacy, or simply adherence fatigue due to the chronic nature of the condition. To address this, future MTM interventions should move toward more personalized and targeted approaches that identify and tackle individual patient-specific barriers.

Despite the methodological strengths of this study, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that may influence the interpretation and generalizability of our findings. The relatively short duration of this study’s follow-up period is a significant limitation. Future research with a longer follow-up period is needed to determine the durability and long-term clinical significance of our results. Our study population was drawn exclusively from patients attending outpatient clinics, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of individuals with diabetic hypertension. The study’s power was constrained by a small number of clusters, which may have limited our ability to detect statistically significant differences. The potential for unmeasured confounders remains a limitation. Future research should consider a more comprehensive set of variables to provide a more nuanced understanding of the factors influencing patient outcomes.

Conclusion

The current study showed that MTM intervention which foced on adheherence counselling resulted in improved medication adherence in patients comorbid with diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Accordingly, medication adherence significantly improved at the end of the 6-month period in the intervention group. By interacting with other healthcare providers and patients via MTM, clinical pharmacists become essential members of the healthcare team and advance patient care. Thus, hospitals should strengthen and integrate clinical pharmacy services into their health care systems. It would be better if researchers assessed the impact of MTM intervention over a longer period.
