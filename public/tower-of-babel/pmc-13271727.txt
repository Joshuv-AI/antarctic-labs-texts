Rare Association of Mayer–Rokitansky–Kuster–Hauser Syndrome with Rectovestibular Fistula and Colonic Atresia
Authors: Audrey Pais, Parag Karkera, Pradnya S. Bendre, Siddhi Sanglikar, Hemant Pikale, Minnie Bodhanwala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRectovestibular fistula is the most common presentation of female anorectal malformation. Its association with Mayer–Rokitansky–Kuster–Hauser (MRKH) syndrome and Colonic atresia is uncommon. We present the staged management of a case of a female neonate with MRKH syndrome with rectovestibular fistula and colonic atresia. This is probably the first such case ever to be documented in literature. An absent vaginal opening with rectovestibular fistula should raise suspicion of MRKH syndrome. Detailed clinical and radiological evaluation with meticulous planning of the staged procedure is necessary for a successful outcome.

INTRODUCTION

Anorectal anomalies affect approximately 1 in 3500 live births, with the most common form being a vestibular fistula in girls. The association of rectovestibular fistula with vaginal atresia is seen in around 3% of anorectal malformations.[1] Mayer–Rokitansky–Kuster–Hauser Syndrome (MRKH) consists of complete vaginal agenesis in association with uterine agenesis or hypoplasia. The incidence of MRKH is 1 in 4000–5000 female births.[1] MRKH may consist of isolated uterovaginal agenesis (type 1) or may be associated with renal (40%–60%), skeletal (10%–12%), auditory (10%), and to a lesser extent, cardiac defects i.e. (type 2).[1] Association of colonic atresia with rectovestibular fistula, although uncommon, has been documented in the literature. But the combined association of colonic atresia with rectovestibular fistula with MRKH, to the best of our knowledge, has not been reported in the literature.

CASE REPORT

A 1-day-old female child was referred to us with an absent anal opening and had not passed stools since birth. On clinical examination, there was abdominal distension and bilious aspirates from the orogastric tube. On perineal examination, the anal opening was absent with two patent openings seen in the vestibule on both sides of a probably blind vaginal pit. The cranial one was the urethra, and the inferior one was a fistulous opening [Figure 1]. The abdominal radiograph had signs of small bowel obstruction. Ultrasound abdomen showed dilated small bowel loops with normal ovaries and an absent uterus. Patient was administered per-fistula saline wash but there was no passage of meconium. Contrast enema confirmed it as a rectovestibular fistula with the contrast going up to mid transverse microcolon [Figure 2].

With a probable preoperative diagnosis of an ileal atresia with a rectovestibular fistula, an exploratory laparotomy was done. It revealed a blind ending cecum with approximately 10 cm of micro-colon terminating as the rectovestibular fistula [Figure 3]. A caecostomy was done along with a colon-mucus fistula. The postoperative period was uneventful. Karyotyping was sent which was 46XX. The patient was temporarily lost to follow-up and came to the outpatient department at 2 years of age. On investigation, the patient had an afebrile urinary tract infection. Ultrasound abdomen and pelvis was suggestive of MRKH syndrome. It also revealed a moderate right hydroureteronephrosis. DTPA, DMSA scans, and a voiding cystourethrogram revealed a nonrefluxing, nonobstructive right megaureter with decreased-functioning right kidney. Magnetic resonance imaging was not needed as bilateral ovaries were identified on ultrasound along with a reported 46XX karyotype. Spine ultrasound was within normal limits. The distal loop cologram was suggestive of a residual micro-colon.

Intraoperative picture of creation of neovagina from fistula and neoanus of pulled through bowel

After complete evaluation, the patient underwent an exploratory laparotomy with a definitive corrective surgical procedure for colonic atresia and rectovestibular fistula with vaginal agenesis simultaneously. The laparotomy confirmed the absence of a uterus and vagina with bilateral normal ovaries. For the colonic atresia, a Santulli procedure was done: (end-to-side colo-ileal anastomosis with terminal ileal stoma). The Santulli procedure was preferred in view of improving the caliber of the micro-colon, as it was unused for 2 years. Also, the patient was not compliant with rectal washouts and was already lost to follow-up after initial surgery.

For the rectovestibular fistula with vaginal agenesis, abdomino-assisted anterior sagittal anorectoplasty was done, in which the terminal part of the rectovestibular fistula was disconnected from the sigmoid colon and retained with minimal anterior relocation to convert it into a neovagina. The proximal sigmoid colon was brought down and placed in the striated muscle complex as the neoanus [Figure 3]. In the postoperative period, the patient was passing stools from the abdominal stoma as well as the neo anus, with gradually reducing per-ostoma output.

After 3 months of corrective surgery and regular anal dilatations, the end ileostoma was closed uneventfully. At present, the child is on regular follow-up with adequate weight gain and on chemoprophylaxis.

DISCUSSION

In the presence of two openings seen in the introitus with an absent anus, the differential diagnoses include – (i) Imperforate anus with no fistula, more often associated with trisomy 21, (ii) anorectal agenesis with rectovaginal fistula, and (iii) rectovestibular fistula with vaginal atresia, cervicovaginal atresia, or uterocervicovaginal atresia, popularly known as MRKH Syndrome.[2]

Embryologic explanation for the association of anorectal malformation with colonic atresia would be that the obstruction derived from the cloacal malformation would have produced a heavy meconium impacted loop, which must have undergone intrauterine volvulus and segmental colonic infarct. This could have been favored by the nonfixation of the colonic mesentery.[3]

Levitt et al. reported an incidence of 0.6% for vaginal malformation with rectovestibular fistula in a large series of 1007 patients with anorectal malformations.[4]

In a case report by Levitt et al., a proximal portion of the rectum was moved posteriorly, leaving the distal-most rectum to become the neovagina, performed with a posterior sagittal approach without a laparotomy, thus avoiding the need for vaginal reconstruction. We have done a similar reconstruction with an abdomino-assisted anterior sagittal approach. For MRKH, it is important at the time of colostomy closure to look for the upper third of the vagina in the pelvis. If found, the neovagina should be anastomosed to this vaginal remnant to restore continuity between the internal genitalia (uterus and fallopian tubes) and neovagina.[4]

Nonsurgical techniques of neovagina creation by constant rod dilatation had good results. Numerous surgical techniques have been used to create a neovagina, most of them using autologous or heterologous tissues. Amnion membrane, cadaveric dura mater, various myocutaneous and fasciocutaneous and labia minora flaps, but the most frequently used tissues are split-thickness skin grafts (Abbé-McIndoe operation), peritoneum (Davydov procedure), and intestinal segments (ileum, cecum, or, more commonly, the sigmoid colon, rectum). Recently, the use of artificial dermis and recombinant growth factor, autologous buccal mucosa, and in vitro cultured vaginal tissue to create the new vagina has been reported.[5]

For management of colonic atresias, a contrast enema is a key to the diagnosis and must not be delayed. Primary anastomosis has high chances of anastomotic leaks, with delayed recovery resulting in higher morbidity and mortality rate as compared to staged procedures with diverting stomas. Options for definitive repair include delayed anastomosis which would involve similar risks of primary anastomosis with disparate luminal sizes, an endorectal pull-through, which would involve sacrifice of the distal colon, or Chimney stomas like Bishop-koop or a Santulli procedure.

The Santulli procedure is described as an anastomosis of the side of the proximal part to the end of the distal bowel segment, and to make an endostomy at the proximal end. This mechanism is to decompress the proximal part, allowing the distal part to enlarge. This is advantageous in the management of bowel discrepancy, allowing gradual commencement of function of the distal bowel, followed by formal closure of the proximal end ostomy. This minimizes anastomotic leak rates due to increased intraluminal pressures due to proximal and distal bowel discrepancies seen in primary anastomosis.

In our case, initial management was done with caecal colostomy followed by creation of neovagina from the terminal part of rectovestibular fistula and neoAnorectum from proximal colon via anterior sagittal anorectoplasty after Santulli procedure, thus utilizing the remnant atretic colon to its best capacity. An Endorectal pull-through or a similar procedure would have meant a stoma pull-down, losing the colon and pulling the caecum down as neoanus, leading to shorter bowel length and reduced absorptive capacity.

Absent vaginal opening in a case of rectovestibular fistula, along with nonpassage of stools, should raise suspicion of associated MRKH and colonic atresia. In view of its rarity, a detailed clinical and radiological evaluation is essential to diagnose and approach such a case. Meticulous planning of the staged surgical procedures will be needed for a successful outcome.
