Occult HBV among anti-HBc–reactive blood donors in Colombia: low HBsAg prevalence, genotype F3, and small-S variation relevant to HBsAg detectability
Authors: Henry Bautista-Amorocho, Jorge Alexander Silva-Sayago, Yeny Zulay Castellanos-Domínguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOccult hepatitis B infection (OBI) can sustain residual transfusion risk because HBV DNA may persist at very low levels while HBsAg remains undetectable by routine serology. We assessed OBI among anti-HBc–reactive, HBsAg-negative (anti-HBc+/HBsAg−) donors in Bucaramanga, Colombia and characterized small-S variation within genotype F3.MethodsIn 2021, we analyzed 61,188 donations in three licensed blood banks using routine serology. From anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donations, 219 randomly selected sera underwent nested PCR targeting the small-S/reverse-transcriptase overlap, followed by Sanger sequencing. Genotyping and phylogeny used a curated 960-nt small-S fragment. Associations with OBI were estimated by logistic regression.ResultsHBsAg reactivity was 0.21% and anti-HBc prevalence was 1.77%. OBI occurred in 16/219 anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donations (7.3%; 95% CI, 4.5–11.5), approximately 130 per 100,000 donations (95% CI, 80–204). Among the 16 OBI cases, 12 were anti-HBs reactive. Sex, age, and anti-HBs were not associated with OBI after adjustment. All sequences clustered within genotype F3. Small-S amino acid variation at positions previously linked to altered HBsAg detectability was observed in 11/16 (68.8%): A166G (5/16) outside “a”; “a” substitutions L127R, K141N, N146Y, C147S; variants at the pre-“a” boundary K122R/T123A; loop-proximal cysteine changes C149S/C149F and S117C; stop codons W156 and L216*; additional MHR changes included V106D, T118S, I150V, P151R, I152S, K160I.ConclusionsAll OBI cases belonged to genotype F3 and showed small-S variants at positions previously associated with HBsAg detectability, which may contribute to reduced detection rather than classical antigenic escape. Introduction

Occult hepatitis B infection (OBI) is defined by the persistence of replication-competent HBV DNA in the liver and/or low-level viremia in individuals who remain HBsAg-negative outside the window period, usually with anti-HBc reactivity and DNA < 200 IU/mL1. In blood donation, OBI is uncommon at the donation level but non-negligible within the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup, and its frequency varies by endemicity and by the sensitivity of the screening algorithm1,2. The clinical relevance is clear: OBI is transmissible, and the risk to recipients depends on immune status and inoculum, so small gains in detection translate into meaningful reductions of residual transfusion risk2.

During the past decade, donor surveillance has clarified how test architecture shapes yield. Studies comparing individual-donation nucleic acid testing (ID-NAT) with minipools show higher interception of window-period and low-level infections when ID-NAT is used, particularly in settings where serology detects most high-titer carriers but misses very low viremias3,4. Meta-analytic estimates indicate that OBI among anti-HBc–reactive donors tracks background exposure, and that anti-HBc alone has limited accuracy for identifying DNA-positive units without an ID-NAT layer1. These observations are pertinent to Latin America, where donor cohorts mix first-time and repeat donations, and where ID-NAT capacity is expanding but not universally mandated.

Colombia illustrates this landscape. Blood services routinely apply HBsAg and anti-HBc, while ID-NAT implementation is heterogeneous across institutions. Prior donor studies in the country documented OBI in the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup and described genotype F3 with substitutions in overlapping S/RT regions5. Other surveys that restricted ID-NAT to selected anti-HBc-reactive profiles reported lower detection, consistent with method sensitivity and selection criteria used at the time6. Regional programs in Mexico that incorporated ID-NAT reported measurable residual risk and emphasized the contribution of ID-NAT to safety under real-world constraints7. Together, these data suggest that context-adapted algorithms can raise safety without undermining supply. This is particularly relevant when ID-NAT is targeted to donor groups with the highest expected yield.

Mechanistically, donor-based sequencing links OBI to genotype background and to changes in pre-S/S or regulatory regions that may lower antigen expression or detectability, with variable patterns across genotypes8,9. In northern South America, genotype F—particularly F3—predominates, underscoring the need for genotype-aware interpretation to avoid misclassifying background polymorphisms as immune escape5. This virologic context also has practical implications. If OBI often reflects very low level replication under partial immune control, incremental DNA testing added to serology may provide the most direct way to reduce residual transfusion risk.

In this context, we characterized the serologic and molecular profile of HBV in a large, contemporary donor cohort from Santander, Colombia, and estimated OBI within the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup using a low-limit-of-detection (low-LOD) nested PCR workflow. We defined donor subgroups with the highest anti-HBc burden to estimate interception yield per targeted ID-NAT, situated genotype F3 sequences relative to regions linked to antigenic detection, and outlined a practical path for scalable hemovigilance and targeted ID-NAT that can reduce residual HBV transmission risk without compromising supply6,8,9.

This study is intended to inform transfusion services, hospital epidemiology units, and public health programs. We quantified OBI in anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donors using a low-LOD, genotype-aware approach and evaluated whether targeted individual donation NAT layered onto standard serology can mitigate residual risk while preserving throughput. This approach is relevant to transfusion medicine, hospital epidemiology, and public health in F3-dominant, low to intermediate endemic settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study from January through December 2021 in three licensed blood banks in Bucaramanga, Province of Santander, Colombia. All donors underwent a confidential pre-donation interview and a standardized questionnaire. Eligibility and routine screening followed Colombian national regulations and operational policies, aligned with World Health Organization recommendations for screening donated blood10.

Ethics

The protocol was approved by the UDES Ethics Committee (Acta 2168-19). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki11 and Colombian biomedical research regulations12. Written informed consent was obtained before donation and testing. Samples and records were pseudonymized with coded identifiers, and the analytic dataset contained no direct identifiers. The study added no procedures beyond routine donor screening and did not include minors.

Blood donor screening and selection for molecular testing

All donations were screened with commercial ELISAs from Biokit S.A. (Lliçà d’Amunt, Barcelona, Spain): BIOELISA HBsAg, BIOELISA anti-HBc, BIOELISA HIV-1/2 Ag/Ab, BIOELISA HCV, BIOELISA Syphilis (Treponema pallidum), and BIOELISA Chagas (Trypanosoma cruzi). Published evaluations of the Biokit BIOELISA HBsAg assay report an analytical detection limit of approximately 0.25 ng/mL, equivalent to about 0.05 IU/mL. Anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donors were additionally tested for anti-HBs (BIOELISA anti-HBs; Biokit). Initially reactive serologic results were retested in duplicate with the same assay at the UDES Biomedical Research Laboratory. Reactive donations were then managed according to the Colombian blood bank confirmation pathway, with referral of reactive donor samples for confirmatory evaluation at the Santander Public Health Laboratory using high sensitivity ELISA assays authorized for in vitro diagnostic use in Colombia. For the molecular substudy, we randomly selected 219 sera from anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donors for HBV DNA testing and included 20 HBsAg-positive samples as assay controls.

Case definition and denominators

Occult HBV infection (OBI) was defined as the presence of HBV DNA in HBsAg-negative donors, irrespective of anti-HBs status and outside the pre-seroconversion window period, in accordance with current EASL guidelines13. To ensure transparent reporting, we provide two measures: (i) the OBI proportion within the ID-NAT tested anti-HBc+/HBsAg– subgroup, and (ii) an estimated OBI rate per 100,000 donations, with stated assumptions and 95% confidence intervals.

DNA extraction and nested PCR amplification of HBV S/RT from blood donor sera

DNA was extracted from 200 µL of serum (QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). A nested PCR targeting the pre-S/S/RT overlap (small-S with RT extension) was designed on the EcoRI genome (GenBank X04615). Primers and cycling strictly followed the published protocol (14). Reactions (25 µL) used Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase High Fidelity (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a ProFlex PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Amplicons were resolved on 1.3% agarose and visualized with SYBR Safe (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

The outer amplicon (> 1.1 kb) was quality-trimmed to a validated 960-nt small-S segment for alignment, genotyping, and phylogeny. The analytical sensitivity of the ID-NAT system was 30–40 IU/mL, and samples were considered positive only when ≥ 2 of 3 replicates exceeded the detection threshold. This criterion was applied consistently to all reactive donations (Tables 1, 2, and 3).

Sequencing, multiple-sequence alignment, and phylogenetic analysis

Positive amplicons were gel-purified (QIAquick, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), cloned into pZero-1, transformed into E. coli TOP10, and verified by NsiI digestion. For each donor, three clones were Sanger-sequenced on an ABI 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). No sequence variation was detected among clones from the same donor. Traces were curated and consensus sequences edited in DNASTAR Lasergene v17.6. The 960-nt small-S fragment was aligned with MAFFT in MegAlign Pro (DNASTAR, Madison, WI, USA) under high-stringency settings (optimized gap costs) and manually inspected. Genotype/subgenotype were assigned by phylogenetic placement against curated references (A–H) in the ML tree. Comparator accessions are listed in Fig. 1; study accessions appear in Table 4.

Small-S (HBs) amino-acid variant analysis

Translated small-S sequences (210 aa; residues 17–226 of the small S (HBs) protein) were aligned and analyzed in MegAlign Pro, DNASTAR Lasergene v17.6 (DNASTAR Inc., Madison, WI, USA). Amino-acid multiple-sequence alignment used MAFFT (protein mode) with a BLOSUM80 substitution matrix and stringency-optimized gap costs, mirroring the nucleotide alignment workflow described above. We screened the major hydrophilic region (MHR, aa 99–169) and the “a” determinant (aa 124–147) for substitutions. To characterize small-S variation on a genotype-appropriate background, study sequences were aligned with curated genotype F reference sequences at the amino acid level. Variants were recorded as amino acid substitutions within the S ORF on a genotype F background rather than defined de novo as escape mutations. Functional interpretation was restricted to substitutions previously reported in the literature in association with occult HBV infection, altered HBsAg antigenicity, diagnostic escape, or reduced HBsAg expression or secretion. Because matched HBsAg positive F3 donor controls from the same setting were not available, these interpretations were considered biologically plausible rather than functionally demonstrated. Substitutions were recorded per donor and recurrence summarized. Residue numbering follows the EcoRI convention, and all calls were verified at the amino-acid level to exclude frame-related artifacts.

Sample size

Assuming an OBI prevalence of 5% among anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donors, a 95% confidence level, and ± 3% absolute precision, the required sample was 203 using standard single-proportion methods. We tested 219 specimens to maintain the target precision and accommodate potential exclusions.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were curated in Microsoft Excel 2023 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed in Stata v16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) or median (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as n (%) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) by the Wilson method; exact binomial CIs were used for very low proportions. We reported crude prevalences with 95% CIs and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) from multivariable logistic regression for OBI; given the limited number of OBI events (n = 16), firth-penalized logistic regression served as sensitivity; inference was unchanged.

Group differences in proportions were tested with Pearson’s χ², or Fisher’s exact when expected cell counts were < 5. Linear trend across ordered age groups was assessed with the Cochran–Armitage test. For effect size, prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% CIs were obtained from Poisson regression with robust variance; when multiple pairwise contrasts were explored, Holm’s procedure controlled the family-wise error rate.

For OBI as a binary outcome in anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donors, logistic models included sex, age (continuous), and anti-HBs; results are reported as aORs with 95% CIs. Model fit was checked with Hosmer–Lemeshow, and variance inflation factors screened for multicollinearity. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. Denominators are reported transparently: the OBI proportion within the ID-NAT–tested anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup and the OBI rate per 100,000 donations for the full cohort, each with CIs.

Results

Donor cohort and serologic screening

Between January and December 2021, the blood banks in Bucaramanga screened 61,188 donations. HBsAg reactivity was 0.21% (130/61,188; 95% CI 0.18–0.25), and anti-HBc reactivity was 1.77% (1,085/61,188; 95% CI 1.67–1.88). Among anti-HBc+/HBsAg– donations, 219 were tested by ID-NAT, identifying 16 OBI cases (7.3%, 95% CI 4.5–11.5), which corresponds to ~ 130 occult infections per 100,000 donations when referenced to the total donor pool. HBsAg did not vary by sex (men 0.23% vs. women 0.19%; χ² p = 0.264) or donation pathway, classified as first-time donors, repeat donors, and directed donors. The latter were defined as individuals who donated blood for a specific intended recipient, typically a relative or known patient (0.25%, 0.18%, and 0.19%, respectively; p = 0.255). By age, HBsAg increased from 0.13% (18–30) to 0.24% (31–40), 0.36% (41–50), and 0.33% (≥ 51) (global χ² p < 0.001; Cochran–Armitage p-trend < 0.001).

For anti-HBc, men exceeded women (2.02% vs. 1.43%; χ² p = 5.3 × 10⁻⁸); there was a clear age gradient (0.74%, 1.67%, 3.08%, 5.31% across 18–30, 31–40, 41–50, ≥ 51; χ² p < 0.001, p-trend < 0.001), and donation pathway differed, with first-time and repeat higher than directed (2.01%, 2.29% vs. 1.10%; χ² p < 0.001). Effect sizes were concordant: anti-HBc PRs were 7.22 (≥ 51 vs. 18–30; 95% CI 6.08–8.56), 1.41 (men vs. women; 1.25–1.60), and 2.09 (repeat vs. directed; 1.76–2.48). Detailed yields and CIs are shown in Table 1; full groupwise prevalences, χ², and crude PRs appear in Supplementary Table 1.

OBI detection among anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donors

We randomly selected 219 anti-HBc+/HBsAg− sera for HBV DNA testing by nested PCR. OBI was detected in 16 of 219 donations (7.3%; 95% CI, 4.5–11.5), corresponding to an estimated 130 OBI cases per 100,000 donations (95% CI, 80–204) when referenced to the full 2021 donor cohort. Twelve of the 16 OBI donors (75%) were anti-HBc and anti-HBs double-positive, compatible with low-level HBV DNA persistence in partially immune donors. Baseline characteristics by OBI status are shown in Table 2. In univariable tests, sex, age (grouped), and anti-HBs showed no association with OBI (all p > 0.20). Findings were unchanged in a multivariable logistic model including sex, age (continuous), and anti-HBs: adjusted odds ratios were ≈ 1 and overall fit was adequate (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.81). Sensitivity analyses using Firth-penalized logistic regression yielded similar adjusted estimates and p values (Table 3).

Analytical performance and internal validity checks

The nested-PCR limit of detection was 30–40 IU/mL. Each specimen was tested in triplicate and called positive with a ≥ 2/3 concordance rule; extraction blanks and no-template controls were negative in all runs. Among 20 HBsAg positive carriers included as assay controls, 11/20 (55%) yielded the S/RT amplicon, consistent with low or variable DNA load and occasional primer site mismatches. Taken together, the clean negative controls, the conservative positivity criterion, and the partial amplification observed in carriers support the high specificity of OBI calls while acknowledging limited analytical sensitivity near the LOD.

Genotyping and phylogeny

All HBV small-S sequences from Colombian donors belonged to genotype F and clustered within subgenotype F3 on the maximum-likelihood tree. (Fig. 1). The IQ-TREE maximum-likelihood phylogeny resolved a single, well-supported F3 clade with uniformly short internal branches, indicating limited divergence on an F3 background. Using the curated 960-nt small-S fragment provided sufficient signal to discriminate F subgenotypes. GenBank accession codes for reference sequences are shown on the tree; accessions for study isolates are listed in Table 4.

Small-S (HBs) variation within the major hydrophilic region (MHR) and the “a” determinant

Among the 16 donor sequences, 11 (68.8%) carried small-S amino acid substitutions in the S ORF (Table 4; Fig. 2). Ten of these sequences showed at least one change within the major hydrophilic region (MHR, aa 99 to 169), which includes the “a” determinant (aa 124 to 147), whereas one carried a C-terminal truncation outside this region (L216). The most frequent substitution was A166G, detected in 5 of 16 sequences (31.3%) in the distal MHR. Within the “a” determinant, the observed substitutions were L127R, K141N, N146Y, and C147S, whereas K122R and T123A were identified at the pre-“a” boundary. Additional cysteine-altering changes included C149S, C149F, and the cysteine-gaining substitution S117C. Nonsense mutations were observed at W156 and L216*. Other substitutions detected in single donors were V106D, T118S, I150V, P151R, I152S, and K160I. The number of substitutions per sequence ranged from 1 to 5, with the highest burden observed in COL-BUC 0221-04, which carried V106D, T118S, A166G, L127R, and N146Y (Table 4). Overall, the sequences showed a largely conserved “a” determinant on an F3 background, with limited variation concentrated in the MHR and rare truncating changes (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this low to intermediate endemic context, donor screening showed very low HBsAg reactivity (0.21%) but a higher anti-HBc prevalence (1.77%), with clear gradients by age, sex, and donation pathway. The sequence data confirm the predominance of genotype F3 among Colombian donors, consistent with reports from northern South America5,14, and indicate that most OBI cases carried naturally occurring small-S substitutions at positions previously discussed in relation to HBsAg detectability or expression in other settings, although their functional relevance in genotype F3 cannot be established from the present data. This epidemiologic and genetic profile aligns with large blood service studies in which anti-HBc reflects cumulative exposure rather than recent high-titer infection, and where age effects are greater than those of sex or donation pathway for HBsAg positivity4,15. The higher anti-HBc in men and its steady increase with age also agree with meta-analyses linking anti-HBc to background exposure and surveillance practices1. Together, these gradients delineate donor strata with the greatest expected molecular yield and provide a rationale for any incremental DNA testing combined with serology.

Among the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup, sex, age (grouped or continuous), and anti-HBs showed no association with OBI in univariable tests and no independent effects after adjustment. This mirrors donor cohorts in which demographic gradients attenuate once analysis is limited to anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donations, and anti-HBs positivity remains common among OBI cases4,13,15. The absence of predictors is plausible given the biologic heterogeneity of OBI (intermittent, very low-level replication) and the limited number of events (n = 16), which reduces power to detect modest effects. Operationally, these null findings indicate that demographics add little to risk stratification beyond serology and support targeted ID-NAT within this serologic group.

Anti-HBs positivity was frequent within the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup selected for molecular testing, occurring in 172 of 219 donors (78.5%) and in 12 of 16 OBI cases (75.0%). However, anti-HBs was not associated with OBI in either the univariable comparison or the adjusted model (75.0% vs. 78.8%, p = 0.75; adjusted OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.26–2.57, p = 0.73). This pattern is consistent with donor studies in which anti-HBs commonly coexists with anti-HBc in previously exposed individuals and may also be present in OBI4,13,15. In these settings, anti-HBs may reduce infectivity but does not exclude low-level viremia or eliminate residual transfusion risk when HBV DNA remains detectable15. In practical terms, without NAT, anti-HBc and anti-HBs double-positive donations may still include a small fraction of OBI cases and therefore remain a source of residual transmission risk4,15. Most available evidence derives from non-F or mixed-genotype settings, whereas genotype F-specific data, particularly for F3, remain limited5,8,9. Anti-HBs titers were not available in the present study, which further restricts assessment of the relationship between antibody levels and molecular detection.

All study sequences clustered within subgenotype F3, intermingling with Colombian references on a well-supported maximum-likelihood tree and showing uniformly short internal branches. This pattern indicates limited divergence on an endemic F3 background and supports genotype or subgenotype assignment by phylogenetic placement rather than single-site markers. Resolution using the curated small-S fragment of 960 nucleotides was sufficient to discriminate F subgenotypes, in line with prior Colombian donor series reporting F3 predominance and regional datasets that place Colombian isolates within the F3 radiation14,16.

Among the sixteen sequenced OBI donors, eleven (68.8%) carried small-S amino acid substitutions in regions relevant to HBsAg structure and antigenic recognition. The most recurrent finding was A166G, detected in five of sixteen sequences, consistent with reports describing this distal MHR change in OBI-associated sequence patterns17,18. Additional substitutions mapped within or near the “a” determinant, including L127R, N146Y, C147S, K122R, and T123A. In other HBV genetic backgrounds, these positions have been linked to altered antigenicity, reduced assay reactivity, or discordant serology19–26. We also observed cysteine-altering changes at C149 and truncating variants at W156 and L216, both of which affect structurally relevant regions of HBsAg and have been associated with altered folding, intracellular retention, or HBsAg negativity in prior studies24,27–29. Taken together, these findings are consistent with a potential contribution of small-S variation to HBsAg negativity in at least a subset of donors. However, this interpretation should remain cautious. Most of these variants were characterized in non-F backgrounds, several have not been functionally assessed in genotype F3, and some have also been reported in non-OBI strains. The surrounding amino acid context is therefore likely to modulate the effect of any individual substitution, and our data do not establish a direct or uniform functional mechanism for OBI in this F3 dominant cohort30,31.

Five OBI sequences lacked small-S substitutions with known functional impact. Several non-exclusive explanations may account for this pattern. One possibility is that very low-level viremia near the limit of detection leaves HBsAg undetectable while HBV DNA remains intermittently amplifiable, as shown in blood donor studies in which individual-donation NAT detected additional infections beyond serology alone15. Another explanation is that variation outside small-S, including pre-S or regulatory regions, or constraints imposed by the overlapping reverse-transcriptase frame, may influence HBsAg expression without requiring coding changes in residues 17 to 22621. A further possibility is that glycan remodeling or host-related pressures contribute through non-canonical or combinatorial effects that are not captured by single-site annotation, particularly when the “a” determinant remains conserved20. In addition, sequencing three clones per donor may miss minor quasispecies, and deep-sequencing studies have shown substantial S-region microdiversity in donor-like settings30–32. Finally, HBsAg detectability varies across assay platforms, and context-dependent under-reactivity has been described for substitutions near the MHR that do not meet classical definitions of escape17,33. Taken together, these scenarios are compatible with OBI on an F3 background in which the “a” determinant remains largely conserved and recurrent major antigenic escape is not apparent13.

Extrapolating to the annual denominator gives approximately 130 OBI cases per 100,000 donations (95% CI, 80–204), representing infections that routine serology alone cannot distinguish within the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donor population. In line with evaluations showing the added yield of HBV DNA testing beyond serologic screening4,15, a proportionate strategy is to maintain universal HBsAg and anti-HBc testing and to add targeted individual-donation NAT within this serologic subgroup. Impact should be monitored through hemovigilance, including DNA-positive discards and look-back outcomes. In a setting dominated by subgenotype F3, where branches are short and the “a” determinant remains largely conserved14,16, this approach addresses the main detection gap without changing the existing serologic platform, preserves throughput and supply, and is consistent with the observation that many OBI cases are anti-HBc and anti-HBs double positive13.

This study used a large single-year donor cohort screened under a uniform algorithm with predefined analytical safeguards, including triplicate testing with a two-of-three positivity rule and an analytical limit of detection of approximately 30 to 40 IU/mL. Testing was restricted to the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− subgroup, HBV DNA was not quantified, sequencing was limited to the small-S region with Sanger cloning and may have missed minority variants, and the single-city cross-sectional design lacked follow-up. An additional limitation is that OBI is often characterized by very low and intermittent viremia, which may fall below the detection threshold of the nested PCR used in this study. Accordingly, the molecular yield reported here may underestimate the true frequency of OBI within the anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donor subgroup. These constraints prevent direct correlation of NAT yield with viral load, whole-genome context, or clinical outcomes. Future studies should combine targeted NAT with quantitative HBV DNA testing on standardized platforms, extend sequencing to pre-S and regulatory regions or to whole genomes, and incorporate implementation metrics such as turnaround time, discard rates, and look-back results to refine thresholds and assess generalizability beyond F3 dominant settings.

Conclusions

In a large donor network from Santander, HBsAg reactivity was low (0.21%), anti-HBc prevalence was appreciable (1.77%), and OBI was detected in 7.3% of anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donations. All sequenced infections clustered within genotype F3, the “a” determinant was largely conserved, and A166G predominated outside a. Taken together, these findings are compatible with DNA signals near assay limits and patterns more compatible with altered HBsAg detectability than with recurrent classical antigenic escape.

For Colombia’s blood services, a proportionate approach is to keep universal HBsAg and anti-HBc, add targeted individual-donation NAT for anti-HBc+/HBsAg− donations, and monitor impact through routine hemovigilance (capture of DNA-positive donations, discard rates, and look-back investigations). Implementation can begin with regional pilots and scale according to anti-HBc prevalence and OBI yield. Given the F3 background and the conserved a determinant, assay replacement is unnecessary; priorities are individual-donation NAT capacity, standardized confirmatory algorithms, and sequence-informed surveillance to track S-region diversity. This strategy fits current infrastructure and should lower residual HBV transmission risk while preserving throughput and costs.
