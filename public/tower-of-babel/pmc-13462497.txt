Perceived Health Risk of Climate Change and Public Support for Sustainable Health Policies in South Korea
Authors: DW Hosmer, Seung-sik Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change poses significant health threats globally, while healthcare systems contribute substantially to greenhouse gas emissions. Understanding public support for sustainable healthcare policies is crucial for policy implementation. This study examined the association between climate change health risk perception and support for sustainable healthcare policies in South Korea after accounting for general climate change concern and relevant covariates.MethodsA national cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 1,500 Korean adults aged ≥19 years from August 29 to September 7, 2022. Participants were recruited from an online research panel using stratified proportionate quota sampling based on the national distributions of gender, age, and region. Binary logistic regression models were used to examine associations between perceived health risk from climate change and support for six sustainable healthcare policies, with adjustment for climate change concern and relevant demographic, socioeconomic, health-related, and household covariates.ResultsA majority of the public supported all six sustainable healthcare policies. After adjusting for covariates, higher perceived health risk from climate change was significantly associated with support across all policy domains. The strongest association was observed for Eco-friendly Dietary Options (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.67, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.42–1.96), whereas the weakest association was observed for Telemedicine Services (aOR = 1.26, 95% CI = 1.09–1.47).ConclusionsPerceived health risk from climate change was independently associated with public support for sustainable healthcare policies in South Korea. These findings suggest that framing healthcare decarbonization initiatives in terms of health protection and co-benefits may offer an additional communication approach alongside environmental framing.Keywords: climate change, health risk perception, sustainable development goal 3 (SDG3), health policy, South Korea

Introduction

Climate change has evolved from an environmental concern to a critical public health emergency threatening human health worldwide. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report confirms that human activities have unequivocally caused global warming, with widespread and rapid changes occurring across the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere. 1 Climate-related health impacts include heat-related illnesses, air pollution-related diseases, and expanding infectious disease vectors, placing unprecedented demands on healthcare infrastructure and services. 2 The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that healthcare systems must transition from passive victims to active responders, simultaneously protecting health while addressing their environmental impact. 3 This dual responsibility is intrinsic to the global mission of Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3), which recognizes that planetary health is a prerequisite for ensuring healthy lives and well-being for all at all ages.

Healthcare systems globally contribute approximately 4.4% of total greenhouse gas emissions, with some high-income countries reporting healthcare sector emissions as high as 9.8% of national totals. 4 This dual role—as both victim and contributor to climate change—has catalyzed unprecedented international policy initiatives for healthcare decarbonization. The UK’s NHS became the world’s first health system to embed net zero targets into legislation through the Health and Care Act 2022, establishing ambitious goals of net zero by 2040 for direct emissions and by 2045 for supply chain emissions. 5 In the United States, the Biden Administration’s Health Sector Climate Pledge has attracted over 100 healthcare organizations committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 50% by 2030 and achieving net zero by 2050. 6

South Korea faces particularly acute climate health challenges, experiencing temperature increases exceeding the global average. 7 Additionally, South Korea ranks first among OECD countries in outpatient visits per capita (17.5 annually vs. OECD average of 8.0) and second in hospital length of stay (19.6 days vs. OECD average of 8.1), with healthcare utilization continuing to rise. 8 This intensive healthcare utilization pattern is closely linked to healthcare sector carbon emissions.4,9 In South Korea, national-level efforts such as the “Korean Green New Deal” and mandatory ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) disclosures have recently focused attention on the sustainability of various sectors, including healthcare. 10 However, eco-friendly transition efforts remain concentrated in tertiary hospitals, with limited penetration into smaller health facilities and community settings. 11 This uneven implementation highlights the potential importance of public demand and acceptance in driving broader adoption of sustainable practices across all levels of the healthcare system. In this context, understanding the factors that influence public support for sustainable healthcare policies—including individual risk perceptions—becomes particularly relevant for policy development and implementation.

Understanding public acceptance of healthcare sustainability policies is crucial for successful implementation, especially in countries like South Korea where high healthcare utilization rates and diverse stakeholder interests require broad-based support for system-wide changes.8-13 However, the factors that drive public acceptance of sustainable healthcare policies remain understudied. 12 While general environmental policy research has identified various factors associated with policy support, 14 institutional and sector-specific challenges within healthcare—such as governance structures and the alignment of stakeholder values—highlight the need to identify area‐specific acceptance factors.15,16

Research suggests that individual health risk perceptions play a crucial role in shaping policy support, as people who perceive greater personal threats from environmental hazards are more likely to support protective interventions.17,18 Previous studies have established a link between general climate risk perceptions and support for broad environmental policies.19,20 However, a distinct empirical foundation is required to understand how health-focused climate risk perceptions relate to sustainability policies specifically within the healthcare sector. Furthermore, much of this research has been conducted in Western contexts, with comparatively less focus on Asian populations, 9 thereby overlooking the unique factors that may influence policy acceptance in different socio-cultural settings.

This study examined the association between climate change health risk perception and public support for sustainable healthcare policies in South Korea. We hypothesized that individuals with stronger perceptions of climate change health impacts would express greater support for sustainable healthcare policies after adjustment for general climate change concern and relevant covariates. This research addresses a gap in understanding the psychological factors of healthcare sustainability policy acceptance in Asian contexts, with implications for policy communication and implementation strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The study population consisted of 1,500 Korean adults aged 19 years and older. Participants were recruited from an online panel using stratified proportionate quota sampling based on the national distributions of gender, age (in 10-year increments), and region (17 provinces), utilizing the August 2022 resident registration population statistics from the Ministry of the Interior and Safety. The sample was recruited from a professional research agency’s master panel comprising 652,138 verified members. To ensure statistical validity, an a priori power analysis was performed for a binomial test (difference from constant proportion) using G*Power 3.1. 21 Assuming a constant proportion of 0.5, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a power (1 − β) of 0.8, the required sample size was calculated as 1,232 for an effect size (g) of 0.04 and 2,189 for a g of 0.03. As the study aimed to detect an effect size of g = 0.05, a target sample of 1,500 participants was considered sufficient and conservatively exceeded the estimated requirement for g = 0.04. To enhance the representativeness of the estimates, post-stratification weights were applied. Inclusion criteria were Korean nationality and age ≥19 years, while illiterate individuals and non-citizens were excluded. 22

Data Collection and Ethical Considerations

The survey was conducted online over a 10-day period from August 29 to September 7, 2022, following approval from the Institutional Review Board of Dankook University on July 14, 2022 (IRB No. DKU 2022-06-034). Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all respondents through an electronic form. The survey was initially distributed via email. To minimize potential selection bias, the sampling process involved real-time monitoring and targeted recruitment during the field period. For demographic groups (gender, age, or region) that remained under-represented after the initial round, additional invitations with survey URLs were sent to ensure the final sample aligned precisely with the national population quotas. All survey items were mandatory, ensuring no missing data in the final dataset (all 1,500 respondents included in the final analytic sample completed the survey). All collected personal information was encrypted by the data processor and destroyed after the analysis to ensure participant confidentiality.

Survey Instrument Development

The survey content and structure were adapted from the ‘Public Polling on Climate Change and Health Report’ conducted by NHS England and Ipsos MORI (September 2021) to facilitate international comparisons. While we acknowledge the inherent limitations of using single-item Likert scales for complex constructs, this approach was chosen to maintain consistency with international benchmarks (e.g., NHS England and Ipsos MORI) and enhance face validity in a policy-oriented context. 23 To ensure high content and construct validity, the survey instrument was developed through a comprehensive process, including a comparative analysis of existing national and international climate-health impact assessment surveys. While the core structure remained consistent with international benchmarks, the specific items and perspectives were modified and culturally adapted to fit the South Korean policy and environmental context. The draft was further refined and finalized through expert consultations involving public health and climate policy specialists to ensure agreement on content validity and to accurately and reliably reflect the intended constructs within the South Korean context. The final questionnaire comprised sections on general participant characteristics, gender, age, region, general perceptions of climate change, perceived health impacts of climate change, public demand for climate-health information, and awareness of climate-health impact assessments.

Measures

Dependent Variables: Six sustainable healthcare policy support items were measured on 3-point scales (1 = Support, 2 = Oppose, 3 = Don’t know), subsequently dichotomized as “Support” versus “Oppose/Don’t know” for analysis (see Supplemental Table S1 for full response distributions). Policies included: (1) Eco-friendly Dietary Options in healthcare facilities, (2) Green Vehicle Conversion (emergency vehicles to electric/hydrogen power), (3) Sustainable Treatments (environment-friendly treatment protocols and medications), (4) Public Transportation Access (expand bus/bike access to facilities), (5) Telemedicine Services (virtual patient care), and (6) Environmental Priority in Care (prioritize environmental impact in service delivery).

Primary Independent Variable: Perceived personal health risk from climate change was measured using a single 5-point Likert scale assessing “How seriously do you think climate change affects your personal health?” (1 = Not serious at all to 5 = Very serious). The item was adapted from established international benchmarks and was previously used in our preliminary descriptive study.22,23 An advisory panel of public health and policy specialists reviewed the item and reached consensus regarding its face validity, content validity, and applicability to the South Korean context.

Covariates: A range of potential confounding variables were measured. These included general climate change concern level; sociodemographic characteristics (gender, age, region); socioeconomic factors (educational attainment, occupation, monthly household income); health-related variables (self-reported health status); and household characteristics, including family size, the presence of a family member who regularly visits a doctor, and the presence of a pregnant family member. Religious affiliation was also included (see Supplemental Tables S1 and S2 for full details).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0, stats package). 24 Descriptive statistics characterized the sample and variable distributions. Binary logistic regression was used to examine the association between the primary independent variable (health risk perception) and the six dependent variables (policy support outcomes). 25

A separate binary logistic regression model was run for each of the six policy support outcomes. To isolate the association of health-specific climate change risk perception with policy support from that of general climate change concern, each model was adjusted for general climate change concern and the potential confounding variables detailed in the Measures section. These covariates were selected based on established literature on environmental policy support and health behavior because they have been associated with both risk perception and policy preferences.19,26-29 To explore whether the association differed according to baseline climate change concern, stratified analyses were conducted separately for participants concerned about climate change (n = 1,311) and those reporting “Moderate,” “Not concerned,” or “Don’t know” responses (n = 189). Stratified logistic regression models were adjusted for the same covariates as the main analysis: gender, age, region, health status, household size, the presence of a family member who regularly visits a doctor, the presence of a pregnant family member, educational attainment, occupation, household income, and religious affiliation. Confidence intervals (CI) for adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were examined to assess the consistency of associations across subgroups. All statistical analyses were performed with α = 0.05 as the level of statistical significance. Given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this study, which aims to identify potential correlates across multiple policy domains, p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. This approach was adopted to minimize the risk of Type II errors and to avoid overlooking potentially important associations in this relatively understudied field. 30

For dependent variables with three response categories (Support/Oppose/Don’t know), we combined “Oppose” and “Don’t know” responses into a single reference category. This was done based on similar response proportions and our theoretical interest in identifying factors associated with active policy support versus all other stances. This dichotomization strategy was adopted to identify factors specifically associated with a clear public mandate for sustainable healthcare transitions, as “Don’t know” responses often represent a lack of active endorsement required for policy implementation. 31 Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R 2 (R 2 ), a likelihood-based coefficient of determination adapted for discrete outcome models. 32 To ensure the robustness of this dichotomization, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multinomial logistic regression, which treated “Support,” “Oppose,” and “Don’t know” as three distinct outcome categories (see the Results for detailed rationale and findings). aORs with 95% CIs are reported. To further facilitate the interpretation of the logistic regression results, we additionally calculated the average marginal effects (AMEs) and predicted probabilities of policy support across different levels of health risk perception. 33 These results, which illustrate the estimated change in the probability of support as risk perception increases, are provided in Supplemental Table S3 and Figure S1.

Climate Change Perceptions and Policy Support

Most respondents expressed concern about climate change and perceived moderate to serious personal health impacts (Table 2). As reported in our preliminary status analysis, 22 approximately 63.1% of the population perceived these health impacts as “Serious” or “Very serious,” providing the baseline for the current association analysis. Regarding sustainable healthcare policy support, all six policies received majority support despite described potential inconvenience or costs (Table 2). Support was highest for Environmental Priority in Care (70.4%) followed by Green Vehicle Conversion (69.4%) and lowest for Telemedicine Services (59.9%). The remaining policies showed similar support levels: Eco-friendly Dietary Options (65.9%), Sustainable Treatments (64.3%), and Public Transportation Access (62.6%).

Multinomial Logistic Regression of Health Risk Perception on Policy Support

To further validate the robustness of the dichotomous model, a multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed (Table 4). The results revealed that higher health risk perception was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of supporting all six policies compared to opposing them, with aORs ranging from 1.29 (95% CI = 1.08–1.55) for Telemedicine Services to 1.82 (95% CI = 1.48–2.23) for Eco-friendly Dietary Options and Environmental Priority in Care. In contrast, health risk perception showed no significant association with “Don’t know” responses relative to the “Oppose” category across all items (aORs ranging from 1.05 to 1.16, all 95% CIs included 1.0). These findings support the primary dichotomous specification by demonstrating that the “Don’t know” and “Oppose” groups had similar associations with health risk perception, suggesting that higher health risk perception was associated specifically with active endorsement rather than uncertainty.

Subgroup Analysis by Climate Change Concern Level

We examined whether the associations between climate change health risk perception and support for sustainable healthcare policies varied by participants’ climate change concern level. Participants were stratified into two groups: those concerned about climate change (n = 1,311) and those who selected “Moderate,” “Not concerned,” or “Don’t know” as their baseline concern level (n = 189). Stratified logistic regression models were fitted separately for each subgroup, adjusting for the same covariates as the main analysis.

As shown in Table 5, the positive association between perceived health risks of climate change and support for sustainable healthcare policies remained consistent across concern levels. Among respondents concerned about climate change, higher perceived health risk was significantly associated with greater support for all six policy measures, with adjusted odds ratios ranging from 1.31 to 1.74 (all p < 0.01).

A similar pattern of positive associations was observed among participants in the “Moderate,” “Not concerned,” or “Don’t know” categories. However, only the associations with Eco-friendly Dietary Options (p < 0.01) and Environmental Priority in Care (p < 0.05) were statistically significant; estimates for the other policies were imprecise, with wider confidence intervals in the smaller subgroup.

The stratified models yielded R 2 values between 0.031 and 0.092 for the concerned group, and between 0.065 and 0.127 for the less concerned group. Although these values are modest, they indicate that the positive association between health risk perception and policy support was observed across different levels of baseline climate change concern.

Overall, these results suggest that the direction of association persists even among those with lower climate concern, though the strength and significance may vary by policy type.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional survey of 1,500 South Korean adults, support for the six sustainable healthcare policies ranged from 59.9% to 70.4%. Higher perceived health risk from climate change was independently associated with support for all six policies after covariate adjustment. The strongest association was observed for Eco-friendly Dietary Options, whereas the weakest association was observed for Telemedicine Services.

To assess the robustness of these findings, we examined model fit and conducted multiple sensitivity analyses. Although the Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) tests suggested a statistical lack of fit (Supplemental Table S5), the R 2 values indicated that the models possess a modest level of explanatory power for public health policy support in a large-scale population study. This modest goodness-of-fit likely reflects the multi-dimensional complexities inherent in policy support, which may not be fully captured by the current set of independent variables. Nevertheless, the stability of these associations is supported by our supplementary analyses. Specifically, the multinomial analysis showed that higher risk perception was associated with active endorsement rather than general engagement or uncertainty. Furthermore, subgroup analysis demonstrated the stability of this relationship; the positive direction of the association between health risk perception and policy support remained consistent even among respondents with lower climate concern. This consistency across diverse analytical frameworks suggests that the observed relationship between health risk perception and policy support is substantively robust, even if the models do not account for all variance in the data. Together, these results highlight the distinct role of health-based perceptions in broadening public engagement for sustainable healthcare transitions.

Public support for sustainable healthcare policies in this study was consistently high, ranging from 59.9% for telemedicine services to 70.4% for environmental prioritization in care. These levels are comparable to or higher than those observed in the United Kingdom, where a 2021 national survey reported majority support for green health initiatives. 23 Korean respondents demonstrated relatively higher support across most policy domains, suggesting a favorable sociocultural context for advancing healthcare sustainability. This support may be partly explained by the Korean public’s consistently high level of concern about climate change. A 2024 global survey found that 76% of Koreans expressed climate concern, 37 and longitudinal data from 2022 to 2025 showed an increase from 74% to 85%, placing Korea among the highest of 32 countries surveyed. 38

Regarding perceived impact, our study found that 63.1% of participants viewed climate change as seriously affecting their personal health—higher than the 55% reported in the UK in 2021. 23 While few recent cross-national data exist on health-specific climate perceptions, a 2024 national environmental awareness survey in Korea asked whether climate change has a serious impact on individuals themselves, and 66.5% of respondents agreed. In contrast, 88.6% perceived climate change as having a serious impact on society overall. This discrepancy suggests that although climate change is widely recognized as a societal threat, it may not yet be fully internalized as a personal or health risk perception. 39 While the Korean survey item did not explicitly reference health, its focus on perceived individual-level consequences of climate change may reflect an adjacent domain of personalized risk perception. Prior studies have shown that perceived personal relevance of climate change—whether defined in terms of lifestyle, environment, or general well-being—is associated with health risk perception and policy support. 19 This conceptual overlap may help contextualize the policy support observed among Korean respondents, by highlighting the role of personalized climate risk perception.

Taken together, our findings suggest that public support for sustainable healthcare policies in Korea may be influenced by a combination of persistent climate concern, elevated health risk perception, and increased exposure to decarbonization discourse. These associations could potentially create a more favorable context for advancing eco-friendly transitions in healthcare. The high levels of support for the Environmental Priority in Care and Eco-friendly Dietary Options policies suggest that these may be priority areas for early implementation. Our stratified analysis (Table 5) provides empirical weight to this, as these two policies alone remained significantly associated with health risk perception even among the less climate-concerned subgroup.

This resilience may be attributed to the fact that sustainable diets and environmental prioritization in care are perceived to have a more direct and tangible link to immediate personal health outcomes—such as improved nutrition and a safer, cleaner clinical environment. Unlike infrastructure-based initiatives like green vehicle conversion or public transport access, which may feel more distal to an individual’s wellbeing, these two policies resonate with the public’s desire for personal health protection. Recent reports emphasize the importance of shifting towards sustainable diets—such as adopting more plant-based food patterns—and promoting environmental priority in care to achieve both climate mitigation and health co-benefits. 2 Furthermore, survey studies have consistently found high public acceptance for measures targeting environmental priority in care and sustainable diets, indicating these policy areas are not only important but also resilient to variations in general climate change concern, making them socially feasible as early priorities for implementation. 40 By contrast, the comparatively weaker association with support for Telemedicine Services may reflect well-documented barriers to telemedicine adoption—such as diagnostic limitations, concerns about clinical quality and continuity of care, and challenges in technology access 41 —that may reduce public willingness to support this measure despite its sustainability benefits.

Associations between climate change health risk perception and policy support align with behavioral health theories suggesting that personal health risk perceptions may serve as potential correlates for policy support. 18 The finding that health risk perceptions are associated with policy support beyond general climate change concerns 42 suggests that health-focused communication strategies could potentially be linked to higher public acceptance of healthcare decarbonization initiatives. The patterns observed in this study are in line with the Health Belief Model (HBM)’s ‘perceived severity’ construct, which suggests that individuals who view health consequences as more serious are more likely to support protective actions. 43 This theoretical framework helps explain why health risk perception emerges as a distinct variable associated with policy support, separate from general environmental concerns. The robust associations observed across diverse policy domains indicate broad public willingness to accept healthcare sustainability measures when health risks are salient. These findings suggest that framing healthcare sustainability in terms of health protection could be further explored as a potential communication strategy, though its actual effectiveness remains to be validated as a hypothesis through longitudinal or experimental designs.

The persistence of the association after adjustment for general climate change concern suggests that health-specific risk perception may capture an aspect of policy support not fully represented by general environmental concern. These findings are consistent with recent international research demonstrating links between health risk perception and environmental policy support. 44 Building on this, it is important to consider the specific communication needs of national contexts such as Korea, where public awareness of both climate change and its health implications is already high. In such contexts, policy communication strategies may need to move beyond simply raising awareness and instead focus on promoting practical engagement and behavioral change. 45

Importantly, the effectiveness of health-focused communication may be further enhanced by tailoring strategies to cultural values and generational preferences.46,47 For instance, younger generations in Korea tend to respond more positively to sustainability initiatives that are presented not only as responsible or protective, but also as aspirational, modern, and aligned with desirable lifestyles. 48 Messages that highlight personal health effects could resonate with younger and future-oriented audiences, particularly when they are combined with aspirational, modern, or lifestyle-oriented contexts. Thus, culturally and generationally tailored communication may warrant evaluation as a means of strengthening engagement with sustainable healthcare policies.49,50

While healthcare sustainability transitions may rely heavily on top-down governance, 51 our findings suggest that individual-level awareness and receptivity may serve as essential preconditions for broader system-wide implementation. In Korea, where healthcare service utilization is high and policy is centrally coordinated, sustainability efforts have largely been concentrated in large, urban tertiary hospitals, with insufficient attention to the broader network of primary care and small/medium-sized clinics. 10 Public awareness and demand for eco-friendly healthcare policies among users (patients and citizens) are likely to be key for accelerating adoption of these practices system-wide and closing the implementation gap between large and small institutions.12,15 Recent global editorials have declared climate and nature crises as a unified global health emergency, emphasizing the need for systemic change in health governance and practice. In this context, aligning sustainability efforts with both top-down governance and public receptivity is not only desirable but necessary. 13 Aligning institutional ESG innovation with patient-centered values—such as health and climate change co-benefits—and with communication that emphasizes health impacts may help increase responsiveness and legitimacy in healthcare sustainability efforts.10,52

These findings contribute primarily to theory but may also have implications for policy design in appropriate contexts. The consistent association between health risk perception and policy support across different healthcare domains supports further evaluation of health-based framing as a potential communication strategy for promoting sustainable healthcare practices. 53 This interpretation is consistent with health behavior theories that emphasize the potential role of perceived personal health threats in shaping policy support. 43

This study has several strengths. It used a large national sample aligned with the South Korean adult population by gender, age, and region. It also explored a range of healthcare sustainability policies and provided rare observational evidence on the association between climate-related health perceptions and support for domain-specific healthcare policies. The application of behavioral health theories, such as the HBM, enhances the explanatory power of our findings. Furthermore, by integrating cross-national comparisons and considering public receptivity within a top-down policy environment, this study contributes novel insights to both theory and practice.

Several limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. 54 It is plausible that individuals with pre-existing pro-environmental or pro-health attitudes reported higher health risk perceptions to justify their policy preferences.55,56 This potential reverse causality could inflate the observed associations, a phenomenon discussed in prior psychological and policy research. 54 Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to better establish causal pathways between perceived health risks and policy support.57,58

Second, our modeling approach may be subject to both over-adjustment and unmeasured confounding, as the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference. 59 While we conceptualized general climate change concern as a baseline factor (X in Supplemental Figure S3), 60 it is possible that health risk perceptions are associated with broader environmental concerns. 61 Given the directional path from X to M specified in our Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) (Figure S3), 62 adjusting for X may isolate the health-specific incremental association but could also lead to an underestimation of the total association. 59 Consequently, the reported aORs are interpreted as conservative, lower-bound estimates of the relationship between health risk perception (M) and policy support (Y).

This adjustment, along with the modest goodness-of-fit observed in the R 2 values and HL tests, suggests that our variables may not fully capture the multi-dimensional complexities of policy support as previously noted. Furthermore, certain relevant covariates—such as environmental values, media exposure, and political ideology—were not measured.19,26-29,63 While the influence of these unmeasured confounders cannot be entirely ruled out, our sensitivity analysis suggested the observed associations are relatively robust. Specifically, the E-values for the point estimates ranged from 1.83 to 2.73 (Supplemental Table S4). 64 Depending on the policy outcome, an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both health risk perception and policy support by a risk ratio of at least 1.83 to 2.73, conditional on the measured covariates, to fully explain the observed association.

Third, measurement and survey-related biases must be acknowledged. 65 Both independent and dependent variables were assessed using single-item measures to facilitate direct comparison with existing international studies. Although this approach precludes the calculation of internal consistency metrics (e.g., Cronbach’s α), our focus was on ensuring high face validity and comparability with established global standards. However, this approach may not fully capture the multi-dimensional complexity of constructs like health risk perception, potentially affecting measurement reliability and construct validity. Because both independent and dependent variables were self-reported within a single survey, common-method bias (CMB) could have artificially inflated correlations by 20–50%. 66 This risk is particularly relevant in behavioral research relying on attitudinal self-reports. 67 Additionally, social desirability bias—a tendency to provide normatively favorable responses—may have led participants to overstate both their health risk perception and policy support, particularly in the context of climate and health issues that carry moral and social significance.68-70 Future studies should consider using validated multi-item scales, indirect questioning techniques, behavioral indicators, or multi-source data to mitigate these biases. 71

Fourth, the dichotomization of policy support responses—combining “Oppose” and “Don’t know” into a single reference category—was necessary for model parsimony and to focus on associations of active policy support. While this approach improves parameter estimation accuracy 72 and maintains high correlations (>0.90) with original scales, 73 we acknowledge that simplifying ordinal data into binary categories may have reduced statistical power and attenuated observed effect sizes. 74 Nevertheless, this grouping aligns with empirical patterns where ‘Don’t know’ often mirrors ‘no’, 75 and focuses on active endorsement, which may provide an important motivational basis for sustained policy acceptance.31,76 Furthermore, as this study examined associations across six different policy items, the issue of multiple testing must be acknowledged. However, we prioritized identifying a broad range of potential associations for healthcare sustainability support. By retaining an unadjusted alpha level, we aimed to identify potentially important associations in an exploratory, hypothesis-generating analysis.

Fifth, collection of the data through an online panel during a major COVID-19 wave may have contributed to overestimation of health risk perceptions and policy support. During this period, heightened public sensitivity to health-related crises may have inflated both health risk perceptions and pro-policy sentiments beyond baseline levels.77,78 Consequently, the associations observed in this study might be more pronounced than those in non-emergency periods, and the results should be interpreted with this unique situational context in mind.

Finally, the study’s generalizability is limited. The findings reflect the unique sociocultural and healthcare context of South Korea, characterized by high healthcare utilization and centralized policy structures.2,10 The relationships between climate risk perception and policy support may differ in societies with varying political ideologies, healthcare accessibility, or climate communication contexts. 19

Taken together, these limitations may have affected the magnitude of the estimated associations. However, the consistent directions of the findings across the binary, multinomial, and stratified analyses support the robustness of the overall conclusion that higher health risk perception is associated with greater policy support. The E-value analysis further suggests that unmeasured confounding of a nontrivial magnitude would be required to fully explain away the observed associations. Thus, although the precise magnitude of the associations remains uncertain, their overall presence and direction appear reasonably robust.

Given the cross-sectional nature of these data, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing definitive causal pathways. While our results indicate consistent positive associations, future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether health risk perception functions as a causal driver in increasing policy support.

Conclusion

Despite these limitations, this study provides important empirical evidence that perceived health risks from climate change are independently associated with public support for sustainable healthcare policies in South Korea. The results align with behavioral health theories, such as the HBM, by demonstrating that health risk perceptions are independently associated with pro-environmental and pro-health policy support, suggesting their potential relevance in understanding public acceptance.18,43

From a policy standpoint, these findings suggest the potential value of exploring sustainability and decarbonization initiatives in health-protective terms, rather than presenting them solely as environmental or economic measures.40,44,79 Communicating the tangible health co-benefits of sustainability is associated with higher public engagement and could be further investigated for its potential to foster acceptance of healthcare decarbonization policies. While our findings are hypothesis-generating, policymakers should consider exploring the integration of health risk communication strategies into climate policy design—particularly focusing on domains such as sustainable diets and environmental priority in care, which showed the strongest adjusted associations with health risk perception. Broader engagement beyond tertiary hospitals, including primary care and community health settings, is essential to ensure equitable and system-wide adoption of green healthcare practices.12,53 In high-utilization systems like South Korea’s, such user-driven support can help complement top-down policies and accelerate the shift toward resilient and people-centered health systems.

Future research should build on these findings by addressing the key limitations of this study. Given the cross-sectional nature of this research, a next step is to move beyond single-point surveys toward longitudinal panel studies and experiments. This would not only help establish causal pathways but also allow for the inclusion of key psychological and political covariates—such as environmental values and political ideology—to address the issue of unmeasured confounding.57,58 Furthermore, our previous analysis of the same survey data identified significant age-group differences in support for sustainable healthcare policies. 22 These emerging generational insights suggest that support for sustainable healthcare policies may vary by age, a topic currently being explored in a follow-up study. Establishing a program of regular, repeated surveys is essential for creating a robust baseline to isolate the effects of acute events like the COVID-19 pandemic. Because our data collection coincided with a period of heightened health sensitivity, these results should be interpreted cautiously as potential overestimations of baseline sentiments. Future replication studies in normal, non-emergency periods are important to confirm these associations and assess whether support for sustainable healthcare persists beyond global health crises. Such longitudinal tracking will also be critical for evaluating the long-term effectiveness of health-based communication campaigns.77,78 Finally, the scope of inquiry should be expanded through cross-cultural comparisons and by investigating the crucial role of healthcare providers in translating public support into practice.

The Korean case also offers insights for other countries with centralized health systems—such as those in East Asia or emerging economies—where aligning top-down climate mandates with public receptivity may be essential for durable, equitable, and socially accepted transitions. In such contexts, enhancing personal relevance through climate communication that emphasizes health impacts may bridge the gap between policy directives and patient experiences, increasing both institutional responsiveness and the social legitimacy of healthcare sustainability reforms. Also, in societies where climate awareness is already high, such as South Korea, public engagement efforts might potentially be more impactful when they emphasize actionable steps beyond policy, personalized health gains, and culturally resonant narratives. Framing sustainable action not only as a moral duty but also as an element of modern, aspirational living—particularly for younger generations—may help translate awareness into sustained behavioral engagement. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously and adapted to specific policy contexts, considering local political, institutional, and cultural factors. The observed associations provide a starting point for future research to confirm whether health-based narratives can serve as a contributing factor for sustained behavioral and policy change.
