Network and Bayesian DAG Analysis of Healthy Lifestyle Beliefs, Educational Stress, and Family Relationships Among Adolescents
Authors: Mohammed A. Mamun, Maysa Mohamed Rabea Abdelall, Firoj Al‐Mamun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealthy lifestyle beliefs are important for adolescent health promotion, yet they are embedded within broader psychosocial contexts involving family relationships and educational stress. Although these domains are likely to interact, little is known about how their specific components are organized within a broader psychosocial system. This study examined the interrelationships among healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship quality among Bangladeshi adolescents using network analysis and directed acyclic graph (DAG) modeling.MethodsData from 501 college‐attending adolescents in Bangladesh were analyzed using the Healthy Lifestyle Beliefs Scale, the Educational Stress Scale for Adolescents, and the Brief Family Relationship Scale. An undirected Gaussian graphical model was estimated to examine network structure, centrality, bridge centrality, predictability, and stability. Gender differences were assessed using the Network Comparison Test. Directed acyclic graph analyses were conducted at the subscale level to explore statistical dependency structures and edge orientations among domains.ResultsThe strongest positive edge in the network was observed between family cohesion and family expressiveness (0.520), whereas the strongest negative edge was observed between family cohesion and family conflict (−0.232). Confidence in handling problems effectively emerged as the most central node (strength = 1.325). The strongest bridge node was belief in family support for achieving goals (0.522), followed by belief in achieving personal goals and family expressiveness. Network stability was good (CS‐coefficient = 0.75). Gender comparison indicated a significant difference in network structure (p = 0.003), but not in global strength (p = 0.511). DAG analyses identified dependency structures linking family relationships, educational stress, and healthy lifestyle beliefs; edge orientations were interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of temporal or causal ordering.ConclusionsHealthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship quality were interconnected within a broader psychosocial system among adolescents. Coping‐related healthy lifestyle beliefs appear central within this system, while family relational processes and academic stress showed meaningful connections with health‐related beliefs. These findings may inform future longitudinal and preventive research examining coping‐related beliefs alongside family communication, support, and academic stress.

Introduction

Social Cognitive Theory posits that health‐related behaviors and cognitions develop through reciprocal interactions between individuals and their surrounding environments (Bandura 1991, 2004). Within this framework, beliefs about one's capacity for self‐regulation influence whether individuals initiate health‐promoting behaviors, maintain them over time, and adapt when facing stress or competing demands. During adolescence, a developmental period marked by increasing autonomy and expanding social contexts, such beliefs may be especially important in shaping long‐term health trajectories (Dick and Ferguson 2015; Sawyer et al. 2012). From a broader developmental perspective, adolescents' health‐related beliefs are also embedded within interconnected ecological contexts, particularly family and school environments, that jointly influence developmental and behavioral outcomes (Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2006). One construct reflecting these processes is healthy lifestyle beliefs, defined as adolescents' confidence in their ability to engage in and sustain health‐promoting behaviors while coping effectively with everyday challenges (Melnyk et al. 2021). These beliefs encompass both confidence in physical health practices and confidence in using coping and relational resources that support healthy living. Because these beliefs also encompass coping, problem management, and perceived family support, they are relevant not only to health behavior but also to adolescents' psychological adaptation (Kelly et al. 2011; Melnyk et al. 2021; Melnyk et al. 2016). Understanding the psychosocial contexts in which such beliefs are embedded is therefore important.

Family relationship quality and educational stress are two psychosocial contexts that may be particularly relevant to healthy lifestyle beliefs during adolescence. Supportive, cohesive, and communicative family environments have been associated with better psychological and behavioral outcomes, whereas conflictual family dynamics may heighten stress vulnerability and undermine adaptive coping processes (Compas et al. 2017; Repetti et al. 2002; van Eickels et al. 2022). Family processes are also relevant to behavioral health more specifically; a meta‐analysis found that parental socialization behaviors such as encouragement, involvement, and support are positively associated with physical activity in children and adolescents (Pugliese and Tinsley 2007). Educational stress is likewise a prominent feature of adolescent life in academic settings characterized by examination pressure, heavy workloads, and strong expectations for achievement (Deb et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2011), and higher levels of such stress have been linked to psychological distress and difficulties in maintaining adaptive health behaviors (Moreno and Jurado 2024; Pascoe et al. 2020). From social‐cognitive and ecological perspectives, family relationships, educational stress, and healthy lifestyle beliefs therefore represent distinct but potentially interconnected aspects of adolescent functioning (Bandura 2004; Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2006). However, how their specific components are conditionally interconnected remains insufficiently understood.

Empirical findings further support the relevance of these domains. Stronger healthy lifestyle beliefs have been associated with more favorable health attitudes, greater engagement in physical activity, and healthier lifestyle behaviors among adolescents and young adults (Kaynak et al. 2025; Kelly et al. 2011; Kudubes et al. 2022). Higher stress has been associated with less favorable health‐related behaviors and lifestyle patterns in adolescent and university students (Badger et al. 2019; Moreno and Jurado 2024; Olson et al. 2025). Supportive family environments have also been shown to foster adaptive coping, emotional security, and greater perceived competence among adolescents (Compas et al. 2017; Peng and Zhang 2025; Pinquart 2017). However, most previous studies have relied on conventional variable‐centered approaches that treat these constructs as separate predictors or outcomes. Although useful for estimating prespecified associations between variables or broader constructs, such approaches may overlook how specific components of family relationship quality, educational stress, and healthy lifestyle beliefs are linked within a broader psychosocial system. Consequently, it remains unclear whether particular components are especially prominent within this system or serve as connections between otherwise distinct individual, family, and educational domains.

Addressing these complexities requires analytic approaches capable of modeling psychological phenomena as interconnected systems. Network analysis complements conventional regression and structural equation approaches by examining conditional associations among multiple components simultaneously. It conceptualizes constructs as systems of interconnected components and their conditional associations, thereby enabling the identification of central elements within a system and bridge nodes linking distinct conceptual domains (Borsboom 2017; Epskamp et al. 2018). In the present context, this approach can clarify how specific healthy lifestyle belief items, educational stress dimensions, and family relationship dimensions are conditionally linked within a broader psychosocial system. Centrality summarizes the extent to which components are comparatively strongly connected within the estimated network, whereas bridge centrality highlights components with relatively strong connections across conceptual domains. The latter is theoretically relevant because it may indicate where individual health‐related beliefs intersect with family and academic contexts. Importantly, central or bridge status reflects structural prominence within the estimated network and should not be interpreted, by itself, as evidence of causal importance or intervention effectiveness (Bringmann et al. 2019; Mcnally 2021). However, undirected network models do not indicate the orientation of these conditional associations. Bayesian directed acyclic graph (DAG) modeling offers a complementary probabilistic framework for exploring dependency structures and edge orientations that are statistically compatible with conditional independence structures (Scutari 2010). With cross‐sectional observational data, however, directed edges cannot by themselves establish temporal precedence or causal effects, and multiple Markov‐equivalent DAGs may represent the same conditional independence structure (Pearl 2009; Scutari and Denis 2014). Accordingly, the present DAG analyses were used to explore statistically plausible dependency structures rather than causal pathways. The unconstrained DAG provided exploratory structure learning, whereas the theory‐informed DAG incorporated prespecified directional restrictions; orientations in the latter were therefore interpreted as conditional on those theoretical assumptions. Combining these methods may therefore provide complementary information about how family relational context, educational stress, and healthy lifestyle beliefs are jointly organized.

Such analyses may be particularly informative in Bangladesh, where studies has documented substantial psychological distress among adolescents and highlighted academic pressure and school‐related demands as relevant concerns (Al Mamun et al. 2021; Al‐Mamun et al. 2024; Anjum et al. 2022; Ria et al. 2024; Roy et al. 2021). Against this background, examining healthy lifestyle beliefs within a broader psychosocial framework may help clarify how health‐related and coping beliefs are interconnected with relational and academic experiences. Although healthy lifestyle beliefs are not psychiatric symptoms, their coping, self‐regulatory, and family‐support components are relevant to adolescent psychological adaptation and preventive mental health research. Examining how these components connect with family relationships and educational stress may also generate hypotheses for future longitudinal and intervention studies. Accordingly, the present study investigated the interrelationships among healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship quality among college‐attending adolescents in Bangladesh by integrating undirected network analysis with directed acyclic graph modeling. Specifically, the study aimed to: (i) estimate the network structure linking these domains; (ii) identify the most central components and the bridge components connecting domains; (iii) evaluate the predictability and stability of the estimated network; (iv) examine gender differences in network structure and overall connectivity; and (v) explore statistically plausible dependency structures among the domains using unconstrained and theory‐informed DAG models.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This study employed a cross‐sectional design. Data were collected between May 2025 and August 2025 from students enrolled at Birshreshtha Noor Mohammad Public College and Birshreshtha Munshi Abdur Rouf Public College, two public colleges in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Participants were recruited using classroom‐based convenience sampling. Students attending the selected colleges during the data collection period were approached and invited to participate. Approval to conduct the survey was obtained from the relevant college authorities before recruitment began. Data collection was conducted in classroom settings using a standardized administration procedure to ensure consistency across groups.

Data were collected using a paper‐based, self‐administered questionnaire that took approximately 30–40 minutes to complete. Before participation, students were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the study procedures, and the measures taken to ensure confidentiality. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For respondents aged under 18 years, participation followed the assent procedures specified in the approved ethics protocol. Students were also informed that they could decline participation or withdraw at any stage without any academic or personal consequences. No identifying information was recorded, and anonymity was maintained throughout the study.

Initially, 503 questionnaires were returned. After data screening, two questionnaires were excluded because they contained substantial missing data on key study measures. The final analytic sample, therefore, consisted of 501 adolescents. Questionnaires were checked for completeness during collection to minimize missingness. The variables used in the present analyses had no missing values in the final dataset, and all statistical models were therefore estimated using the full sample (N = 501).

Measures

Sociodemographic Characteristics

The questionnaire collected information on sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, academic group, father's education, and mother's education.

Family Relationship

Family relationship quality was assessed using the Brief Family Relationship Scale, derived from the Relationship dimension of the Family Environment Scale (Fok et al. 2014). The instrument contains 16 items rated on a 3‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 3 (a lot). It assesses three components of family relationship functioning: Cohesion (7 items), Expressiveness (3 items), and Conflict (6 items). Subscale scores were derived according to the recommended scoring procedures, and an overall family relationship score was also computed. In the present sample, the total scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.86).

Educational Stress

Educational stress was measured using the Educational Stress Scale for Adolescents (ESSA) (Sun et al. 2011). This scale includes 16 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater educational stress. The ESSA assesses five dimensions: self‐expectation stress, workload, despondency, pressure from study, and worry about grades. A total educational stress score was obtained by summing all items. The original instrument demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.81). In the current study, the total ESSA score showed strong internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.88).

Healthy Lifestyle Beliefs

Healthy lifestyle beliefs were assessed using the Healthy Lifestyle Beliefs Scale (HLBS) (Melnyk et al. 2021). The scale comprises 16 items, each rated on a 5‐point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate stronger confidence in one's ability to engage in healthy lifestyle behaviors. The HLBS includes two subdimensions: physically healthy lifestyle beliefs (9 items) and coping/family support beliefs (7 items). Subscale scores were calculated from the relevant item sets, and a total score was generated by summing all 16 items in accordance with the original scoring approach. In the present sample, the total HLBS demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.916). Reliability was also strong for the physically healthy lifestyle beliefs subscale (α = 0.904) and the coping/family support beliefs subscale (α = 0.875).

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board of the CHINTA Research Bangladesh [Ref: CHINTA/IRB/03‐2025/3(4)], and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. In addition, administrative permission was obtained from the principals of the participating colleges before the survey was conducted. Participation was entirely voluntary. Before data collection, students were informed about the purpose of the study, the procedures involved, possible risks and benefits, and their right to refuse participation or withdraw at any point without any academic or personal consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For respondents younger than 18 years, assent procedures were followed in line with the approved ethical protocol. To protect privacy, no personally identifying information was collected, and all responses were treated confidentially.

Statistical Analysis

Network Analysis

Network analysis was conducted to examine conditional associations among healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship indicators. Analyses were performed on the full dataset (N = 501) and included 24 observed variables: 16 healthy lifestyle belief items (HLB_1–HLB_16), five educational stress indicators representing the subdomains of self‐expectation stress, workload, despondency, study pressure, and grade worry, and three family relationship indicators (cohesion, expressiveness, and conflict). The analyzed node structure, including variable codes, item membership, and construct descriptions, is presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S1. Healthy lifestyle beliefs were retained at the item level because identifying specific beliefs was a primary focus of the study, whereas educational stress and family relationships were represented by their established subscale scores to preserve interpretable contextual dimensions and limit network dimensionality. This mixed‐level specification was considered when interpreting centrality and bridge estimates across domains. Variables were converted to numeric format prior to analysis.

An undirected Gaussian graphical model (GGM) was estimated, in which edges represent regularized partial correlations between variables after conditioning on all other nodes. Network estimation used the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator with Extended Bayesian Information Criterion model selection (γ = 0.50), implemented using the EBICglasso algorithm. Correlations were estimated using cor_auto, and positive‐definite adjustments were applied when necessary to improve numerical stability during estimation and resampling. Diagonal elements of the adjacency matrix were fixed at zero. Networks were visualized using the Fruchterman‐Reingold spring layout in the qgraph package. Nodes were colored according to predefined conceptual domains, and edge thickness reflected the magnitude of the regularized partial correlations. A heatmap of the weighted adjacency matrix was also generated to display the magnitude and direction of the estimated associations.

Node‐level connectivity was characterized using strength centrality and expected influence (one‐step and two‐step). A descriptive nodewise predictability proxy was computed as the sum of squared adjacent edge weights. Although reported alongside centrality indices for descriptive purposes, this measure should not be interpreted as conventional regression‐based R 2 or formal nodewise explained variance. To identify nodes linking conceptual domains, bridge centrality was computed using the bridge strength metric, with communities defined a priori as HLB_Physical, HLB_Coping/Family, EduStress, and FamilyRel.

Network accuracy and stability were evaluated using bootstrap procedures implemented in the bootnet package. Edge‐weight accuracy was assessed using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 iterations, and centrality stability was examined using case‐dropping bootstrapping with 1000 iterations, summarized by the correlation stability (CS) coefficient. Gender differences in network structure were examined using the Network Comparison Test (NCT) with 1000 permutations. The NCT evaluated network structure invariance, global strength invariance, edge‐wise differences, and node‐level strength centrality differences. Because multiple edge‐wise comparisons were performed, p‐values for edge‐specific tests were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Directed Acyclic Graph Analysis

Bayesian directed acyclic graph analyses were conducted at the subscale level to explore statistical dependency structures and edge orientations among healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship indicators. Two healthy lifestyle belief subscales were derived by averaging item scores: HLB_Physical (items 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14, and 15) and HLB_Coping/Family (items 4, 5, 6, 11, 12, 13, and 16). These two subscales were analyzed together with the five educational stress indicators and the three family relationship indicators, yielding a dataset of 10 nodes. The composition of these nodes is summarized in Supporting Information S1: Table S1. Because the data were cross‐sectional, directed edges were not interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal ordering, and alternative Markov‐equivalent DAGs may be compatible with the same conditional independence structure (Pearl 2009; Scutari 2010; Scutari and Denis 2014).

DAG structure learning was performed using hill‐climbing search with a Gaussian BIC scoring function, implemented in the bnlearn package. To assess robustness, bootstrap structure learning was conducted using 1000 resamples, yielding estimates of arc strength, defined as the proportion of bootstrap samples in which an arc was present, and direction probability, defined as the conditional probability of a given direction given that the arc was present. Bootstrap‐averaged DAGs were constructed by retaining arcs with an arc‐strength threshold of 0.50.

Two DAG models were estimated. The unconstrained DAG imposed no directional restrictions and was used for exploratory structure learning. The theory‐informed DAG incorporated prespecified directional constraints via a blacklist. Specifically, paths from the healthy lifestyle belief subscales to educational stress variables were prohibited, and paths from healthy lifestyle beliefs or educational stress variables to family relationship indicators were also prohibited. Accordingly, edge orientations in the theory‐informed DAG were interpreted as conditional on these imposed theoretical constraints rather than as empirical evidence of causal precedence. Both bootstrap‐averaged DAGs were visualized using Graphviz.

Software

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Data management was performed using readr and dplyr. Network estimation and bootstrap procedures were conducted using bootnet, qgraph, networktools, and NetworkComparisonTest. Directed acyclic graph analyses were performed using bnlearn, and DAG visualizations were generated using Rgraphviz. Heatmaps were produced using ggplot2, and positive‐definite matrix adjustments were handled using the Matrix package.

Undirected Network Structure

The undirected network was estimated in the full analytic sample of 501 adolescents and comprised 24 nodes, including 16 healthy lifestyle belief items, five educational stress dimensions, and three family relationship dimensions. The network showed clear domain‐based clustering, with healthy lifestyle belief items forming physical and coping/family‐related clusters, educational stress indicators forming a compact stress cluster, and family relationship variables showing strong internal coherence (Figure 1). Edge weights ranged from −0.232 to 0.520, and most associations were positive.

Undirected network of healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship variables. Nodes represent individual healthy lifestyle belief items and subscale‐level educational stress and family relationship indicators. Edges represent regularized partial correlations estimated using EBICglasso, with thicker edges indicating stronger conditional associations. Node colors denote conceptual communities, and red‐bordered nodes indicate the strongest bridge nodes.

The strongest positive edge was observed between FRel_Coh (family cohesion) and FRel_Exp (family expressiveness) (0.520), whereas the strongest negative edge was observed between FRel_Coh (family cohesion) and FRel_Con (family conflict) (−0.232). Within the healthy lifestyle belief domain, the strongest edges were observed between HLB_6 (confidence in handling problems effectively) and HLB_11 (positive coping with pressure) (0.464), between HLB_5 (belief in achieving personal goals) and HLB_6 (confidence in handling problems effectively) (0.406), and between HLB_14 (expectation of feeling better through regular exercise) and HLB_15 (belief that being physically active is enjoyable) (0.375). Within the educational stress domain, the strongest edges were observed between EduS_SE (self‐expectation stress) and EduS_GW (worry about grades) (0.302), between EduS_De (despondency) and EduS_SP (study pressure) (0.288), and between EduS_SP (study pressure) and EduS_GW (worry about grades) (0.241). The heatmap showed the same overall pattern, with the strongest associations concentrated mainly within conceptual domains and fewer cross‐domain connections (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1 and Table S2).

Centrality and Predictability

Centrality analysis indicated that several healthy lifestyle belief items, particularly those related to coping, self‐regulation, and behavioral confidence, showed comparatively high centrality values in the network (Figure 2; Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Strength centrality ranged from 0.352 to 1.325, with the highest value observed for HLB_6 (confidence in handling problems effectively) (1.325). This was followed by HLB_7 (confidence in being more physically active) (1.149), HLB_3 (confidence in making healthy food choices) (1.088), EduS_SP (study pressure) (1.087), and HLB_13 (belief in family support for achieving goals) (1.087). Among the family relationship dimensions, FRel_Coh (family cohesion) had the highest strength (1.022), whereas FRel_Con (family conflict) had the lowest (0.352).

Centrality and predictability indices of the network. Panels display node strength, expected influence (1‐step and 2‐step), and node‐wise predictability. Higher values indicate structural connectivity or stronger embedding within the estimated network.

Expected influence showed a similar pattern, with the highest one‐step expected influence again observed for HLB_6 (confidence in handling problems effectively) (1.207). The descriptive predictability proxy values ranged from 0.059 to 0.443, with the highest value observed for HLB_6 (confidence in handling problems effectively) (0.443), followed by FRel_Coh (family cohesion) (0.366), HLB_11 (positive coping with pressure) (0.332), and FRel_Exp (family expressiveness) (0.310). Overall, the highest centrality and descriptive predictability‐proxy values were concentrated in coping‐related healthy lifestyle belief items and positive family relationship indicators.

Bridge Centrality

Bridge centrality analysis identified the nodes with the strongest cross‐domain connections (Supporting Information S1: Table S4). Bridge strength ranged from 0.051 to 0.522. The highest bridge strength was observed for HLB_13 (belief in family support for achieving goals) (0.522), followed by HLB_5 (belief in achieving personal goals) (0.414), FRel_Exp (family expressiveness) (0.339), HLB_15 (belief that being physically active is enjoyable) (0.315), and HLB_4 (healthy coping with bothersome or upsetting situations) (0.301). Among the educational stress indicators, EduS_GW (worry about grades) (0.171) and EduS_SP (study pressure) (0.164) showed the highest bridge strength. These findings suggest that goal‐oriented and family‐supported healthy lifestyle beliefs, together with family expressiveness, showed comparatively strong cross‐domain connectivity within the estimated network.

Accuracy and Stability of the Network

Network accuracy and stability were evaluated using 1000 nonparametric bootstrap samples for edge‐weight accuracy and 1000 case‐dropping bootstrap samples for centrality stability. Bootstrap confidence intervals indicated that stronger edges were estimated more precisely than weaker edges, as reflected by their comparatively narrower intervals (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). The edge‐difference plot showed that several of the strongest edges were distinguishable from many weaker edges (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). Case‐dropping bootstrap analysis showed that correlations between original‐sample and subset‐based strength estimates remained high across increasing proportions of case removal (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). The correlation stability coefficient for strength was 0.75, indicating good stability of the strength metric.

Network Comparison Test by Gender

Gender differences were examined using the Network Comparison Test, and the male and female networks are displayed in Figure 3. The network structure invariance test indicated a statistically significant difference in overall structure between male and female adolescents (M = 0.39, p = 0.003). However, the global strength invariance test was not significant (p = 0.511), indicating no significant difference in overall connectivity between the two networks. The estimated global strength values were similar for males (10.693) and females (10.314) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5 and Table S5). Edgewise comparison analyses showed that no individual edge remained significant after false discovery rate correction. Overall, these findings suggest that the configuration of associations differed by gender, whereas the overall level of connectivity was comparable across the two networks.

Directed Acyclic Graph Analyses

DAG analyses were conducted at the subscale level using a 10‐node dataset comprising HLB_Physical (physical healthy lifestyle beliefs), HLB_Coping/family (coping/family healthy lifestyle beliefs), five educational stress dimensions, and three family relationship dimensions. Bootstrap structure learning was performed using 1000 resamples, and arcs retained in at least 50% of bootstrap samples were included in the averaged DAGs.

In the unconstrained DAG, several arcs were retained in the bootstrap‐averaged structure. These included HLB_Coping/family (coping/family healthy lifestyle beliefs) → HLB_Physical (physical healthy lifestyle beliefs), EduS_SP (study pressure) → EduS_De (despondency), EduS_GW (worry about grades) → EduS_SE (self‐expectation stress), and FRel_Coh (family cohesion) → FRel_Con (family conflict). Additional cross‐domain arcs connected HLB_Coping/family (coping/family healthy lifestyle beliefs) with FRel_Exp (family expressiveness) and FRel_Coh (family cohesion). Overall, HLB_Coping/family was connected with both family relationship indicators and physical healthy lifestyle beliefs in the averaged structure; however, these edge orientations represent the learned dependency structure and should not be interpreted as causal ordering (Figure 4; Supporting Information S1: Table S6).

Directed edges represent edge orientations in the averaged dependency structure retained in at least 50% of bootstrap samples across 1000 iterations.

In the theory‐informed DAG, estimated under the prespecified directional constraints, FRel_Exp (family expressiveness) and FRel_Coh (family cohesion) showed directed arcs to HLB_Coping (coping/family healthy lifestyle beliefs). EduS_SP (study pressure) also showed directed arcs to educational stress dimensions and to HLB_Physical (physical healthy lifestyle beliefs) (Figure 5; Supporting Information S1: Table S7). Because reverse directions between these domains were prohibited by the blacklist, these orientations represent the dependency structure obtained under the imposed theoretical constraints rather than evidence of temporal or causal precedence.

Discussion

The present study offers a systems‐level examination of the interrelationships among healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship quality in a sample of Bangladeshi adolescents using an integrated network and Bayesian directed acyclic graph framework. Rather than treating these domains as separate constructs, the findings indicate that they are embedded within an interconnected psychosocial structure. More specifically, coping‐related healthy lifestyle beliefs, particularly confidence in handling problems effectively, emerged as the most central components of the network, whereas family expressiveness, family‐supported goal attainment, and study‐related stress showed comparatively strong cross‐domain connectivity. The DAG analyses further provided complementary information about dependency structures among these domains, although their edge orientations should not be interpreted as temporal or causal ordering.

Centrality analyses consistently identified coping‐related healthy lifestyle belief items, especially confidence in handling problems effectively, as the most structurally central components of the network. This finding highlights the importance of perceived self‐regulatory capacity in adolescents' health‐related cognition. According to Social Cognitive Theory, self‐efficacy beliefs are foundational for initiating and maintaining health‐promoting behaviors, particularly under conditions of stress or environmental constraint (Bandura 1991, 2004). Notably, the most central nodes were not confined to physically oriented beliefs alone, but were concentrated in beliefs reflecting problem management, coping, and confidence in one's ability to act effectively. This pattern is consistent with evidence that stronger healthy lifestyle cognitive beliefs are associated with physical activity and healthier dietary behaviors among adolescents (Kelly et al. 2011; Melnyk et al. 2021). Moreover, recent studies have shown that self‐regulatory beliefs are closely linked to resilience and psychological well‐being in adolescence, acting as protective factors against stress‐related maladjustment (Compas et al. 2017; Zimmer‐Gembeck et al. 2023). Within the present network, these findings indicate that coping‐oriented beliefs were particularly strongly connected with other components of adolescent psychosocial functioning; they should not, however, be interpreted as causal determinants solely on the basis of centrality.

Bridge centrality analyses further revealed that belief in achieving personal goals, perceived family support for those goals, and family expressiveness showed comparatively strong connections across conceptual domains. These bridge nodes highlight points at which personal agency, health‐related beliefs, and family relational processes intersect within the estimated network. In particular, belief in goal attainment may connect individual confidence with broader coping‐related beliefs, whereas family expressiveness may connect the emotional climate of the home with adolescents' ability to manage stress and maintain healthy attitudes. Previous research has consistently shown that supportive family environments characterized by cohesion, communication, and emotional openness are associated with better psychological adjustment, stronger self‐efficacy, and healthier behavioral patterns (Pinquart 2017; Repetti et al. 2002; Senguttuvan et al. 2014). In addition, parental support and family involvement have been shown to buffer the negative effects of stress and promote adaptive coping and goal‐directed behavior among adolescents (Wang and Eccles 2012; Zimmer‐Gembeck et al. 2023). The present findings add to this literature by showing that family‐related factors also occupy cross‐domain positions within the network of beliefs, stress, and relationships.

Within the educational stress domain, study pressure and worry about grades emerged as the most structurally prominent and bridge‐relevant components. This suggests that not all aspects of educational stress are equally connected within the estimated network; rather, stressors related to performance evaluation, pressure to achieve, and fear of academic consequences may be especially influential. Prior studies have documented that academic pressure, competition, and parental expectations are among the most prominent stressors experienced by adolescents, particularly in high‐achievement‐oriented educational settings (Deb et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2011). These stressors have been linked to a range of adverse outcomes, including psychological distress, reduced physical activity, and poorer health behaviors (Badger et al. 2019; Pascoe et al. 2020). Recent evidence also suggests that academic stress may undermine self‐regulatory processes and make it more difficult for adolescents to maintain adaptive lifestyle behaviors (Moreno and Jurado 2024; Olson et al. 2025). Viewed together, the present findings indicate that academic stress is not only internally coherent but also meaningfully interwoven with health‐related beliefs and family relational processes.

The family relationship variables also showed strong internal organization, with family cohesion and expressiveness displaying the strongest positive association, and cohesion showing a strong negative association with conflict. This pattern suggests that the family domain was organized around a clear polarity between supportive relational functioning and conflictual family dynamics. Such a structure is consistent with research on family relational functioning demonstrating that warm, cohesive, and communicative family environments are associated with lower conflict and better psychosocial outcomes among adolescents (Repetti et al. 2002; van Eickels et al. 2022). Importantly, these family dimensions were also linked to key healthy lifestyle belief nodes, indicating that adolescents' perceptions of their family environment are interconnected with their confidence in engaging in health‐promoting behaviors and managing everyday stressors. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have reported that family support is positively associated with physical activity, healthy eating, and psychological well‐being in adolescents (da‐Silva‐Domingues et al. 2024; Pugliese and Tinsley 2007). The present findings, therefore, position family relationship quality as an important contextual component within the broader network of adolescent health‐related beliefs and stress.

The DAG analyses provided a complementary view of the statistical dependency structure among the study domains. In the unconstrained model, coping/family healthy lifestyle beliefs were connected with both family relationship indicators and physical healthy lifestyle beliefs, although the orientations of these edges do not establish temporal or causal direction. In the theory‐informed DAG, family cohesion and expressiveness were connected to coping‐related healthy lifestyle beliefs, while study pressure was connected to educational stress dimensions and physical healthy lifestyle beliefs under the prespecified directional constraints. These patterns are compatible with social‐cognitive and ecological perspectives emphasizing the interconnection of individual beliefs with family and school contexts (Bandura 2004; Bronfenbrenner and Morris 2006). However, the constrained edge orientations partly reflect the imposed blacklist, and alternative Markov‐equivalent structures may also be statistically compatible with the cross‐sectional data. The DAG findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory dependency structures rather than evidence that family or educational factors causally precede healthy lifestyle beliefs.

Gender differences in network structure were also observed. Although the overall level of connectivity did not differ significantly between male and female adolescents, the configuration of relationships among variables did. In other words, the two groups showed a similar overall degree of network connectedness, but the patterning of specific inter‐node associations differed at the global network‐structure level. Such findings are consistent with previous research showing gender differences in stress perception, coping strategies, and socialization processes during adolescence (Compas et al. 2017; Matud 2004). For example, some adolescent studies have reported higher levels of academic stress among female than male students (Nolen‐Hoeksema and Girgus 1994; Singh et al. 2023). However, because no individual edge remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons, specific gender‐related network differences should be interpreted cautiously.

The findings of this study have important implications for future preventive mental‐health and health‐promotion research targeting adolescent populations. Although healthy lifestyle beliefs are not psychiatric symptoms, several prominent nodes reflected coping confidence, problem management, self‐regulation, and perceived family support, which are relevant to adolescents' psychological adaptation. The central role of coping‐related healthy lifestyle beliefs, particularly adolescents' confidence in handling problems effectively, suggests that these components may warrant further investigation as potential targets in longitudinal and intervention studies. Because several of the most central nodes were related not only to physical health practices but also to problem management and positive coping, interventions focused solely on lifestyle education may be insufficient unless they also address the psychological resources needed to sustain healthy behaviors under stress. At the same time, the identification of family expressiveness and family‐supported goal attainment as key bridge nodes highlights the potential relevance of family‐based approaches. These findings suggest that open communication and perceived support from parents and family members may help connect adolescents' coping‐related beliefs with broader health‐promoting attitudes and behaviors. In addition, the prominence of study pressure and worry about grades within the educational stress domain indicates that academic stress may warrant attention in future school‐based prevention research. For clinicians and school‐based mental‐health professionals, the findings highlight the potential value of considering adolescents' family and academic contexts alongside individual coping beliefs. However, central and bridge nodes should not be interpreted as established intervention targets based on the present cross‐sectional analysis. This systems‐based perspective aligns with broader adolescent‐health frameworks emphasizing the importance of addressing both individual and contextual determinants of health (Patton et al. 2016; Sawyer et al. 2012).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the DAGs represent statistically plausible dependency structures rather than definitive temporal or causal pathways. In addition, edge orientations in the theory‐informed DAG were conditional on prespecified directional restrictions, and alternative Markov‐equivalent structures may be compatible with the observed data. Second, the use of convenience sampling from two public colleges in Dhaka limits the generalizability of the findings. The sample may not represent adolescents attending private or vocational institutions, those living in rural or other regional settings, or adolescents outside formal education. The educational and family expectations characteristic of the Bangladeshi context may also influence the observed network structure; replication across different cultural and educational settings is therefore needed. Third, all variables were assessed using self‐report measures, which may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias. Fourth, the analytical model combined item‐level healthy lifestyle beliefs with subscale‐level educational stress and family relationship indicators. Although this specification preserved detailed information on the focal healthy lifestyle belief construct, differences in node granularity may influence network topology and centrality or bridge estimates. Future studies should examine whether the findings replicate using uniformly item‐level or uniformly subscale‐level representations across constructs. Notwithstanding these limitations, the study has several notable strengths. It employed validated instruments, included a relatively large adolescent sample, and applied complementary analytical approaches that enabled conditional associations and exploratory dependency structures to be examined within a single framework. By integrating network analysis with DAG modeling, the study provides a more nuanced understanding of how healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship quality are interconnected within adolescent psychosocial functioning.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that healthy lifestyle beliefs, educational stress, and family relationship quality are closely interconnected within a broader psychosocial system among adolescents. Coping‐related healthy lifestyle beliefs, particularly confidence in handling problems effectively, emerged as central components, while family expressiveness, family‐supported goal attainment, and academic study pressure showed comparatively strong cross‐domain connectivity. The findings suggest that adolescents' health‐related beliefs are not shaped in isolation, but are closely interconnected with relational and academic contexts. Although causal conclusions cannot be drawn, the combined network and DAG analyses identified complementary patterns of conditional association and statistical dependency across these domains. The DAG findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory dependency structures rather than evidence that family relationship processes or educational stress causally precede healthy lifestyle beliefs. These results highlight the importance of adopting a systems‐based perspective in adolescent health research. The identified coping‐, family‐, and academic‐stress‐related components may warrant further investigation in longitudinal and intervention studies. Future preventive approaches may benefit from considering these interconnected individual, family, and academic contexts rather than focusing on isolated behaviors alone.
