Hollow polyaniline/pyrrole-chitosan supported Pt-Cu0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 nanoparticles as an efficient electrocatalyst for enhancement of the methanol oxidation reaction
Authors: Mehri-Saddat Ekrami-Kakhki, Nahid Farzaneh, Parvin Asen, Taha Safari, Mohammadreza Taheri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this work, the synthesized hollow poly aniline/pyrrole (HP) combined with the biopolymer chitosan (CH) was employed as a robust support material (HP-CH) for nanoparticle deposition. Cu0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 (CZFO) nanoparticles were synthesized and characterized by FT-IR, FESEM, XRD, EDX, and mapping analyses. The novel Pt-CZFO/HP-CH nanocatalyst was then fabricated by chemically reducing hexachloroplatinic acid on the HP-CH support embedded with CZFO nanoparticles, followed by comprehensive characterization. Incorporating CZFO nanoparticles has a positive effect on the performance of Pt nanoparticles, resulting in improved activity, increased stability, and reduced CO poisoning of the catalyst during methanol oxidation, when compared with the Pt-ZFO/HP-CH, Pt-CFO/HP-CH, Pt/HP-CH, and Pt/CH catalysts. The findings underscore the value of integrating mixed metal oxides with Pt-based catalysts and highlight the role of support materials in developing advanced, efficient catalysts for fuel cell applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32651-1.

Introduction

Among the different types of fuel cells, direct methanol fuel cells (DMFC), as efficient and clean energy sources, have become the focus of research due to the low start-up temperature, low pollutant production, and high efficiency. DMFCs are devices that convert the chemical energy into electrical energy through the electro-oxidation of methanol fuel1,2. Since methanol can be obtained from various sources such as coal, natural gas, and biomass, which are more abundant sources than crude oil, it seems that in the not-so-distant future, methanol will be considered one of the new energy carriers. Platinum (Pt) is the commonly used catalyst for the methanol oxidation reaction (MOR) in DMFCs and has the strong ability to cleavage the C-H bond in MOR. Whereas, its high price, low stability under extreme electrochemical conditions, scarce resources, and strong affinity for carbon monoxide (CO) species, as byproducts of methanol oxidation, limit its large-scale application2,3. Therefore, the preparation of low-cost catalysts with higher CO tolerance and high performance for methanol oxidation is essential for the development of DMFCs. Today, it is known that mixed metal oxides such as perovskite and spinel catalysts are very suitable candidates for various applications such as photocatalytic activities and methanol oxidation2,4,5. Spinel ferrites with the general formula MFe2O4 (M = Cu, Ni, Mn, Co, Zn) have significant features, including high electrocatalytic/photocatalytic properties, recyclability, less environmental damage, chemical and thermal stability, and unique magnetic, electrical, optical and physical properties6–8. The use of Cu2+ and Ni2+ ions in the ferrite lattice can enhance its saturation magnetization, and redox ability. Nowadays, spinel ferrite nanoparticles have attracted considerable attention and research efforts due to their wide applications as gas sensors, rechargeable lithium batteries, solar energy conversion, and catalysts for several processes such as alkylation reaction, alcohol oxidation, and treatment of gases exhausted from automobiles, etc9–11. The suitable support for Pt nanoparticles plays a crucial role in improving their catalytic efficiency. The proper support materials help to disperse the Pt nanoparticles uniformly, increasing their surface area and exposure of active sites for catalytic reactions, and preventing aggregation of Pt nanoparticles. The choice of support materials can also influence the electron transfer between the Pt nanoparticles and the reactants, and help protect Pt nanoparticles from poisoning by reaction intermediates. Thus, selecting the right support enhances the overall catalytic performance of Pt nanoparticles by boosting their dispersion, stability, electron transfer, and resistance to poisoning12,13. Polymers can serve as excellent supports for Pt nanoparticles in methanol oxidation reactions due to their high surface area, tunable properties, and stability. The interaction between the polymers and the Pt nanoparticles can enhance the catalytic activity and stability of the nanoparticles during the methanol oxidation process14,15. Some of the commonly employed polymers include polyvinylpyrrolidine (PVP), poly(N-vinyl-2-pyrrolidone) (PVP-co-VP), polyethyleneimine (PEI), polyaniline (PANI), chitosan (CH), and polydopamine (PDA)16–20. These materials provide diverse characteristics and functional groups that can be adjusted to enhance the activity of Pt nanoparticles in methanol oxidation. Researchers continue to explore new polymer supports to further enhance the catalytic activity, stability, and selectivity of Pt nanoparticles in this important reaction. PANI is a conducting polymer that can provide both structural support and electronic conductivity for Pt nanoparticles. Its conductivity can facilitate the transfer of electrons during the methanol oxidation reaction, leading to the improved catalytic activity. PANI is known for its chemical stability, which can help in maintaining the stability of the Pt nanoparticles during the methanol oxidation process and prevent the aggregation of Pt nanoparticles, leading to the improved catalytic durability. The interaction between PANI and Pt nanoparticles often results in synergistic effects that enhance the overall catalytic performance of the catalyst system. PANI can act as a stabilizing agent for the Pt nanoparticles, improving their dispersion and promoting efficient catalytic activity21,22. Polypyrrole (PPy) can be also utilized as support material for platinum nanoparticles in MOR. The conductive and stable nature of polypyrrole makes it an excellent choice for anchoring and stabilizing the platinum nanoparticles, enhancing the catalytic activity and durability of the catalyst in MOR23. Chitosan, obtained from chitin, is a natural polymer that provides benefits including a large surface area, biocompatibility, and tunable surface chemistry24. When used as support for Pt nanoparticles, chitosan can enhance the catalytic performance, stability, and selectivity of the catalyst for methanol oxidation25.

In this work, Cu0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 (CZFO) nanoparticles were successfully synthesized and their catalytic activity was examined for methanol oxidation for the first time. Hollow poly aniline/pyrrole (HP) accompanied by the chitosan (CH) biopolymer, was utilized as a suitable support (HP-CH) for nanoparticles. The novel Pt-CZFO/HP-CH nanocatalyst was obtained in a single-step process by chemical reduction of H2PtCl6 and uniformly dispersion of Pt and CZFO nanoparticles on the HP-CH support. ZnFe2O4 (ZFO), and CuFe2O4 (CFO) nanoparticles were also synthesized. The electrocatalytic performance of Pt-CZFO/HP-CH nanocatalyst for MOR was assessed compared to the Pt-ZFO/HP-CH, Pt-CFO/HP-CH, Pt/HP-CH, and Pt/CH catalysts through several electrochemical techniques performed by the Autolab apparatus. This work differs from previous efforts26–28 by integrating a multicomponent spinel ferrite (Cu0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4) with Pt and supporting it on a composite of conducting (polyaniline, pyrrole) and biopolymer (chitosan) in a hollow nanostructure. While other research has used individual polymers like polyaniline or polypyrrole, or simple ferrite supports, this work distinctly uses both mixed conducting polymers and biopolymer, which may enhance charge transport, and mechanical properties. The introduction of a hollow structure in the catalyst can significantly increase the accessible surface area, optimize the mass transport, and expose more active sites for the methanol oxidation reaction. Although hollow Pt-based nanoparticles and some multimetallic hollow nanospheres have been previously reported, the construction of a hollow polyaniline/pyrrole-chitosan matrix hosting Pt-Cu0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 nanoparticles is a rare, highly integrated design29. This research combines Zn and Cu into the spinel structure which theoretically could bring synergistic effects from various metal centers to promote better electronic conductivity, improved catalyst stability, and enhanced catalytic activity. Such a mix is not commonly found in previous Pt/polymer/ferrite studies and may yield performance advantages. Although polyaniline and polypyrrole have both been used as supports for Pt and ferrite-based electrocatalysts, the use of a hybrid polymer matrix of polyaniline, pyrrole, and chitosan is novel26,28.

Results and discussion

Characterization

The CZFO nanocatalyst was successfully synthesized by co-precipitation method and identified by EDX and FT-IR methods. The morphology and elemental distribution of the CZFO nanocatalyst, determined by FESEM and mapping analysis, are shown in Fig. S1 and Table S1. The EDX analysis also confirms the formation of CZFO trimetallic mixed oxide. The details of CZFO composition is presented in Fig. S1. The FT-IR spectra of the samples are shown in Fig. S2. The octanoic acid spectrum is exhibited in Fig. S2a. The broad peak observed in the 2650–3480 cm− 1 range is related to the stretching vibration of the O-H bond. The peak corresponding to the carbonyl group is located at 1718 cm− 1. Bending vibrations of the CH3 and CH2 groups appear at 1381 cm− 1 and 1461 cm− 1, respectively. The C-O stretching vibrations are observed at 1220–1330 cm− 1, while the out-of-plane bending of the O-H group is shown at 941 cm− 1. The FT-IR spectrum of CZFO nanoparticles is presented in Fig. S2c. In the FTIR spectrum of the CZFO nanoparticles, two characteristic absorption bands are observed. The peak at 1011 cm− 1 is attributed to the M–O stretching vibration at the tetrahedral sites, while the peak at 544 cm− 1 corresponds to the O–M–O bending vibration at the octahedral sites of the spinel lattice. The appearance of these two bands confirms the formation of a spinel-type Cu–Zn ferrite structure, where the metal cations occupy both tetrahedral and octahedral positions30,31. Pt-CZFO/HP-CH was prepared by first dispersing CZFO nanoparticles along with hexachloroplatinic acid within the HP-CH matrix, followed the chemical reduction of the platinum precursor with sodium tetrahydroborate. Fig. S3 presents the FESEM image and the elemental distribution map of the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalysts. The successful formation of the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH nanocatalyst and its elemental composition are confirmed through EDX analysis, as illustrated in Fig. S3 and Table S2. The morphology, size, and dispersion of Pt and CZFO nanoparticles in the synthesized catalysts are well demonstrated using TEM images (Fig. 1). HP nanoparticles and their hollow structure are clearly indicated in Fig. 1A. Uniform hollow spheres with an outer diameter of about 100 nm and an inner diameter of about 60 nm are observed. The TEM image of the Pt/HP-CH nanocatalyst is displayed in Fig. 1B, where the platinum nanoparticles appear as dark, spherical particles averaging around 5 nm in size. These Pt nanoparticles are well dispersed throughout the chitosan matrix and surrounding the HP nanoparticles. The TEM image of the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH nanocatalyst is shown in Fig. 1C. As observed, platinum nanoparticles with an average size of about 5 nm are homogeneously distributed within the chitosan substrate, around the HP particles, and on the surfaces of CZFO nanoparticles. The spherical CZFO nanoparticles with an average particle size of 30 nm are uniformly embedded within the HP-CH matrix. The functional groups in chitosan and polypyrrole/aniline are positively charged in an acidic medium, and the electrostatic attraction between the positively charged functional groups and the negatively charged PtCl62− causes a more uniform dispersion of platinum nanoparticles and their better catalytic performance for the methanol oxidation reaction. The formation of CZFO nanocatalyst was confirmed by XRD (Fig. 1D). In the XRD pattern of CZFO, the characteristic diffraction peaks observed around 2θ values 30.3°, 35.6°, 37.5°, 43.3°, 53.6°, 57.1°, and 62.6° are attributed to the crystal planes of (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), and (440), respectively. The XRD pattern shows high crystallinity of the Cu0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 catalyst with a cubic structure, which is in accordance with the standard (JCPDS 82-1042)32. Also, no additional peaks were observed in the XRD pattern, which indicates the purity of the synthesized catalyst. The average crystallite size was calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation (explained in supporting information).

Electrochemical characterizations

The electrochemical performance of various electrodes-namely Pt-CZFO/HP-CH, Pt-ZFO/HP-CH, Pt-CFO/HP-CH, Pt/HP-CH and Pt/CH nanocatalysts-was surveyed by CV curves in N2-saturated H2SO4 (0.5 M) solution (Fig. 2A). As demonstrated, the two cathodic and anodic peaks observed in the potential range around − 0.26 to 0.2 V are related to the adsorption (Pt-H formation) and desorption (Pt-H reduction) of hydrogen on the surface of the electrodes. The hydrogen adsorption peaks were observed from − 0.3293 to 0.0979 V and hydrogen desorption peaks were observed from − 0.2829 to 0.1729 V. It is well-known that the activity of an electro-catalyst is controlled not only by the electronic and chemical properties but also by the electrochemical surface area (ECSA). The electrochemical activity of the catalysts for MOR was examined by CV technique in sulfuric acid (0.5 M) and methanol (1.0 M) solution (Fig. 2B) at the scan rate of 100 mVs− 1. Two oxidation peaks are observed for methanol oxidation in the CV curves of all catalysts. The forward sweep’s anodic peak (jf) corresponds to methanol electrooxidation, while the reverse sweep’s cathodic peak (jb) corresponds to the removal of carbon species produced during methanol oxidation36. The Pt-CZFO/HP-CH demonstrated a higher mass activity (3859.925 mA mg− 1Pt) for methanol electrooxidation compared to the Pt-ZFO/HP-CH (3532.209 mA mg− 1Pt), Pt-CFO/HP-CH (3128.488 mA mg− 1Pt), Pt/HP-CH (2935.051 mA mg− 1Pt) and Pt/CH (1519.05 mA mg− 1Pt) catalysts (Fig. 2C). The enhanced catalytic performance of Pt/HP-CH in comparison with Pt/CH is attributed to the use of hollow poly aniline/pyrrole particles with chitosan as a nanoparticle substrate, resulting in a more uniform nanoparticle dispersion. The superior catalytic performance of Pt-CZFO/HP-CH for methanol oxidation, compared to Pt/HP-CH and Pt/CH is mainly due to the incorporation of CZFO nanoparticles alongside platinum and their even distribution within the HP-CH matrix. The trimetallic mixed metal oxides CZFO nanoparticles contribute additional hydroxyl groups, which are essential for facilitating the complete oxidation of methanol. Chitosan and hollow poly aniline/pyrrole contain -NH2 and -OH functional groups in their structures which are positively charged in acidic environments. The electrostatic attraction of these positively charged groups with the negatively charged PtCl62−, the platinum precursor, made the uniform dispersion of Pt nanoparticles throughout the HP-CH support37. Pt nanoparticles serve as highly active catalysts for methanol oxidation, but they are prone to poisoning by CO intermediates, which block active sites and hinder efficiency. The use of CZFO nanoparticles forms a composite catalyst (Pt-CZFO/HP-CH) and mitigates this issue. The first step is adsorption and dehydrogenation of methanol on the Pt nanoparticles (Eq. 3). Methanol adsorbs onto Pt surfaces in acidic or aqueous solutions and undergoes stepwise dehydrogenation, breaking C-H and O-H bonds to form intermediates, ultimately leading to adsorbed CO (COads). CO binds strongly to Pt, occupying sites and impeding further methanol oxidation. To regenerate Pt sites, COads must react with oxygen-containing species (e.g., OHads from water dissociation) to form CO₂ (Eqs. 4 and 5). The overall goal is to convert methanol into CO₂ (Eq. 6), but this doesn’t happen directly; instead, it proceeds through intermediates. CZFO enhances this by accelerating hydroxyl species production through its oxide surface, which stabilizes intermediates and facilitates water adsorption/dissociation. This operates via a bifunctional mechanism: Pt manages methanol dehydrogenation and CO formation, while CZFO supplies OHads for CO oxidation, preventing poisoning (Eqs. 7 and 8) 39,40.

To assess the methanol oxidation performance of Pt-CZFO/HP-CH, the effect of varying methanol concentrations on the anodic peak mass activity was investigated. The cyclic voltammograms of the electrodes immersed in H2SO4 (0.5 M) solution containing different methanol concentrations are shown in Fig. 3A. As observed in Fig. 3A and B, the anodic mass activity of MOR at the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalyst increases with the increase in methanol concentration up to 1.0 M. At higher methanol concentration, the anodic mass activity is almost constant. This phenomenon is probably due to the saturation of the active surface of catalyst and the poisoning of the Pt surface25. Therefore, the optimum concentration of methanol is 1.0 M. This is enough methanol to maintain high activity, but below the range where the catalyst surface becomes saturated. The use of methanol concentrations higher than 1.0 M does not yield additional electrochemical benefit. This suggests that electrochemical measurements at the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalyst should be conducted at this methanol concentration. The stability of the synthesized catalysts for MOR was examined by continuous potential cycling in H2SO4 (0.5 M) + MeOH (1.0 M) solution at the scan rate of 100 mV s− 1. The CV curves of the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH over the 200 consecutive cycles are exhibited in Fig. 3C. As shown, the anodic peak mass activity of MOR gradually declines with repeated cycling. This decrease can be explained with two basic reasons: firstly, the partial depletion of MeOH within the diffusion layer during the successive potential scanning, and secondly, the catalysts poisoning with the CO species generated during the reaction41. According to the information obtained from the voltammograms and the bar graphs of MOR after 200 consecutive cycles (Fig. 3D), the anodic peak mass activities of the Pt/CH, Pt/HP-CH, Pt-CFO/HP-CH, Pt-ZFO/HP-CH, and Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalysts reach almost a constant value after 200 cycles and show a decline of 38.52, 56.19, 38.24, 36.75, and 34.03%, respectively, by the 200th cycle compared to their initial values. Among these, the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalyst has the lowest drop in the anodic peak mass activity, highlighting its superior durability and stability for methanol electro-oxidation relative to the other catalysts.

To further assess the electrocatalytic performance and stability of the catalysts, chronoamperometry (CA) tests were carried out for 1000 s at 0.7 V in a solution of H2SO4 (0.5 M) containing MeOH (1.0 M). According to the CA curves (Fig. 4A) and the bar graph (Fig. 4B) of the catalysts after 1000s, generally all the CA curves presented original quick decline in the mass activity. Presumably, the decline was attributed to the catalyst poisoning by the as-formed intermediates and some poisoning species such as CO-like species on the catalyst surface during MeOH oxidation. Among the tested materials, Pt-CZFO/HP-CH displayed the highest initial mass activity (4020.076 mAmg− 1Pt), outperforming Pt-ZFO/HP-CH (3697.698 mAmg− 1Pt), Pt-CFO/HP-CH (2959.598 mAmg− 1Pt), Pt/HP-CH (2724.731 mAmg− 1Pt) and Pt/CH (1294.247 mAmg− 1Pt). After 1000 s, Pt-CZFO/HP-CH maintained the greatest residual mass activity (780.490 mAmg− 1Pt) with the smallest decay rate (80.59%), compared to Pt-ZFO/HP-CH (510.256 mAmg− 1Pt, 86.20% decay), Pt-CFO/HP-CH (385.177 mAmg− 1Pt, 86.98% decay), Pt/HP-CH (291.146 mAmg− 1Pt, 89.31% decay), and Pt/CH (139.049 mAmg− 1Pt, 89.26% decay). The improved retention of activity in catalysts containing CZFO suggests that CZFO helps oxidize the adsorbed CO-like species, reducing catalyst poisoning42. These results highlight the promising potential of Pt-CZFO/HP-CH in DMFCs application. The electrochemical behavior of the catalysts for MOR was assessed by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), with Nyquist plots recorded in a solution of H2SO4 (0.5 M) and methanol (1.0 M) (Fig. 4C). EIS measurements were carried out over a range from 104 to 10− 2 Hz at open circuit potential (OCP), and the data were analyzed using a transmission line model fitted to the Nyquist plots. In each Nyquist curve, a small semicircle is located at high frequency region, related to the charge transfer process at the electrode/electrolyte interface. The presence of a straight line at low frequency region is attributed to the ion diffusion features of the catalysts during MOR. Among all catalysts tested, Pt-CZFO/HP-CH exhibited the smallest semicircle diameter, indicating the most efficient charge transfer kinetics for methanol oxidation. Moreover, the more vertical line in the low frequency region indicates the enhanced ion diffusion properties43,44. The equivalent electrical circuit used to model the EIS data is exhibited in the inset of Fig. 4C. As shown, Rs is due to the solution resistance, the constant-phase element (CPE) models the double-layer capacitance. R0 and C0 are due to the resistance and capacitance of the CO intermediates produced in MOR, respectively45. The Quantitative values for EIS circuits of the catalysts are expressed Table S4 in Supporting Information. The influence of scan rate on the performance of the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalyst was investigated in the H2SO4 (0.5 M) and methanol (1.0 M) solution at various scan rates from 30 to 190 mVs− 1 (Fig. 4D). As shown, the increase in the scanning speed causes an increase in the anodic peak mass activity of methanol oxidation. Furthermore, plotting the anodic peak mass activity against the square root of the scan rate produced a linear correlation, revealing that the electrochemical oxidation of methanol on the Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalyst is under the control of mass transfer. Additionally, the relationship between the peak potential (Ep) and the logarithm of the scan rate (log υ) demonstrated a linear trend, confirming that the methanol oxidation involves an irreversible charge transfer mechanism46. Although the anodic peak mass activity of methanol oxidation increases with increasing the scan rate, excessively high scan rates may change the peaks shape or prevent the steady-state conditions. Hence, the optimum scan rate is usually around moderate scan rates (100 mVs− 1), which is fast enough to increase the mass activity but slow enough to maintain the precise shape of the methanol oxidation peaks.

Conclusions

In summary, a novel Pt-CZFO/HP-CH catalyst exhibiting outstanding catalytic activity for methanol oxidation was successfully developed. The integration of CZFO nanoparticles accompanied by Pt particles, uniformly distributed on a hollow poly aniline/pyrrole-chitosan matrix, resulted in significantly enhanced anodic mass activity, improved stability, increased active surface area, and reduced catalyst poisoning compared to Pt/HP-CH and Pt/CH counterparts. These findings highlight the beneficial impact of incorporating mixed metal oxides and employing advanced support materials in Pt-based catalysts, paving the way for the design of highly efficient and durable catalysts for MOR in DMFCs technologies.
