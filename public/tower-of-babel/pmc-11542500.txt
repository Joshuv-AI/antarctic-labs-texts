First Isolation and Characterization of Feline Panleukopenia Virus From Wild Raccoon Dogs in the Residential Area of Shanghai, China
Authors: Chen Chen, Jin Tao, Ling Tang, Tong Sun, Zhuyun Sun, Huan Xu, Shuaitao Deng, Cheng Xue, Xing Ji, Rui Wang, Hongjun Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCarnivore parvovirus is commonly detected in canines and felines and results in a range of symptoms, including diarrhoea, with a high lethality rate. In recent years, the gradual restoration of urban ecosystems coupled with a lack of natural predators means that wild raccoon dogs have gradually entered residential neighbourhoods in Shanghai, and the carnivore parvovirus carried by such canines may seriously affect their health and that of dogs and cats in these neighbourhoods.ObjectivesTherefore, it is essential to monitor wild raccoon dogs for carnivore parvovirus.MethodsThe wild raccoon samples were collected from the residential area of Shanghai and screened for parvovirus, followed by virus isolation and sequence comparison analysis.ResultsIn this study, three strains of parvovirus, YSH1‐2022, YSH2‐2022 and GSW1‐2023, were isolated from anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures of 42 live raccoon dogs and two dead raccoon dogs in Shanghai, China. After alignment, it was found that the three strains of parvovirus were all located in the same branch as feline panleukopenia virus (FPV), and the three VP2 genes had 100% homology with FPV VP2 genes isolated from Shanghai and Yangzhou, Jiangsu Province. The homology of the three NS1 genes with FPV NS1 isolated from Shanghai and other places was 99.9%–100%.ConclusionThis is the first report of FPV isolated from raccoon dogs. In this study, the host range and risk of infection of parvovirus were evaluated, providing data to support wildlife management and policy formulation in Shanghai.Keywords: feline panleukopenia virus, isolation, phylogenetic analysis, residential area, risk analysis, wild raccoon dogs

Introduction

Raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides) belong to the Mammalia, Carnivora, Canidae and Nyctereutes Temminck and are second‐class key protected wild animals in China. As a native species of Shanghai, raccoon dogs were once widely distributed in the city's suburban ecosystems, but rapid urbanization caused them to disappear. With the continuous promotion of urban ecological construction in recent years, raccoon dogs have returned to the city, and their number has increased rapidly, with their distribution expanding to more than 270 residential areas in Shanghai, causing great public concern (Zhao et al. 2022). The population of raccoon dogs in Shanghai is estimated to be between 3000 and 5000, with the highest distribution found in Songjiang, Qingpu and Minhang Districts. As the population of wild raccoon dogs increases, the risk of infection and transmission of various infectious diseases, such as carnivore parvovirus, is also gradually rising.

Carnivore parvovirus is a single‐stranded DNA virus of the Parvoviridae family, which is widely distributed in nature and causes an acute infectious disease characterized by vomiting, severe diarrhoea and decreased white blood cell counts in animals such as canines and feline. The virus has a genome of approximately 5 kb, and among its main structural proteins are the capsid proteins VP1 and VP2, with VP2 constituting 90% of the capsid proteins and serving as a primary determinant of the host range (Truyen, Agbandje, and Parrish 1994; Yuan and Parrish 2000). The non‐structural (NS) protein plays a protective and regulatory role in the gene expression and replication processes of the virus (Niskanen et al. 2010). In China, there have been reports of carnivore parvovirus infections in both domestic and wild animals (Steinel et al. 2001); parvovirus has been isolated from animals such as cats, dogs, raccoons, minks, tigers, blue foxes and monkeys (Diao et al. 2017; Steinel et al. 2001; Yang et al. 2010; Yu et al. 2018). Research shows that many carnivore parvoviruses, including canine parvovirus, mink enteritis virus, raccoon dog parvovirus and raccoon parvovirus, evolved from feline panleukopenia virus (FPV) through genetic mutations, thereby gaining the ability to infect other animals. Therefore, FPV is considered the ancestor of these viruses (Parker and Parrish 1997; Parrish 1990; Yang et al. 2010). There have been few reports of the isolation of raccoon dog parvovirus, and all of these were canine parvovirus.

In this study, three strains of FPV were isolated from anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures of wild raccoon dogs in Shanghai. The VP2 and NS1 gene sequences were genetically analysed to evaluate the infected host range and risk, which will provide data to support wildlife management and policy formulation in Shanghai, China.

Materials and Methods

Samples

From July 2022 to August 2023, 44 anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures were collected from 42 live wild raccoon dogs and two dead raccoon dogs captured from apartments in Songjiang, Qingpu, Minhang and Pudong administrative districts in Shanghai (Figure 1). Twenty‐two samples were collected in 2022 and 2023, respectively. The samples were stored at −20°C for transportation to the laboratory and later preserved at −80°C for long‐term storage until further use. The study was approved by the Shanghai Forestry Bureau.

Sampling distribution. Study area in Shanghai, located in the Yangtze River Delta Urban Agglomeration (YRDUA). MH, Minhang district; PD, Pudong district; QP, Qingpu district; SJ, Songjiang district.

Virus Identification

DNA was obtained from the collected anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures using the EasyPure Genomic DNA kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The samples were detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the primers VP2‐P1: 5′‐GAATCTGCTACTCAGCCACCAAC‐3′ and VP2‐P2: 5′‐GTGCACTATAACCAACCTCAGC‐3′. The PCR conditions are available upon request.

Virus Isolation

A 1‐mL sample of each anal and pharyngeal swab mixture with a positive PCR result was aspirated and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was retained. Subsequently, a mixture of penicillin and streptomycin was added to the supernatant and mixed well, and the bacteria were removed by filtration using a 0.22‐µm filter (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) and stored at −80°C for later use. The Crandell feline kidney (CRFK) cell culture medium was changed to Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (BI, China) culture medium containing 1% foetal bovine serum (FBS, PAN Biotech, Germany) and 1% antibiotics for maintenance culture. The treated sample of 200 µL was aspirated and added to the CRFK cells, and the mixture was cultured in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C. The morphological changes in the cells were observed using an electron microscope.

Nucleotide Sequencing and Phylogenetic Analysis

PCR was used to amplify a 1752‐bp segment of the VP2 gene without the stop codon using the primers VP2‐F: 5′‐ATGAGTGATGGAGCAGTTCAACCAG‐3′ and VP2‐R: 5′‐ATATAATTTTCTAGGTGCTAGTTGAGAT‐3′ and a 2007‐bp segment of the entire NS1 gene using the primers NS1‐F: 5′‐ ATGTCTGGCAACCAGTATACTGAG‐3′ and NS1‐R 5′‐TTAATCCAAGTCGTCTCGAAAATC‐3′. The PCR conditions are available upon request. The target fragment was connected to the T vector by T4 DNA ligase overnight at 16°C, and the connected product was transferred to receptor DH5α cells, which were cultured at 37°C for 16 h on luria‐bertani (LB) plates containing antibiotics. Three positive clones were selected, and each clone was sequenced three times to ensure accuracy. The VP2 and NS1 gene sequences of the three strains were submitted to GenBank (accession Nos. PP355844 to PP355849).

Each sequence was subjected to BLAST analysis (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ Blast. cgi) to identify relevant reference viruses, and the DNA sequence was translated using SnapGene sequence analysis software. Using CLUSTAL W, multiple alignments of sequences were performed. MEGA6.0 software was applied to construct the phylogenetic tree of the nucleotide sequence of the target gene using the neighbour‐joining method and a Bootstrap value of 1000.

Results

Eight Positive Samples Were Detected From Raccoon Dogs

In this study, 8 positive samples were detected from 44 anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures, amplified to a fragment of 559‐bp, which was consistent with expectations (Figure 2). In 2022, there were three positive samples all from Yushanghai (YSH) residential area in Songjiang District (shown in red in Figure 1), with a positive rate of 13.6% (95% confidence interval (CI): 2.9%–34.9%). In 2023, there were five positive samples, two of which were from Yushanghai (YSH) residential area in Songjiang District (shown in red in Figure 1), and three were from Gushuiwan (GSW) residential area in Songjiang District (shown in orange in Figure 1), with a positive rate of 22.7% (95%CI: 7.8%–45.4%).

PCR results of parvovirus. (A) Anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures collected in 2022; (B) anal and pharyngeal swab mixtures collected in 2023. M, marker; +, positive control; −, negative control.

Three Parvoviruses Isolated From Raccoon Dogs

Three parvovirus strains were isolated from the eight samples, two of which were collected from the Yushanghai (YSH) residential area in Songjiang District, shown in red in Figure 1, and one that was collected from the Gushuiwan (GSW) residential area in Songjiang District, shown in orange in Figure 1. The strains were named YSH1‐2022, YSH2‐2022 and GSW1‐2023 according to the samplings area and time. CRFK cells were transmitted blind for second generations for 96 h, typical cytopathic effects (CPEs) were observed in all CRFK cells. Compared with normal CRFK cells, pathological cells showed shedding, suspension, rounding and a larger cell space (Figure 3). The cell culture medium was frozen and thawed three times, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant, then passaged twice. PCR tests for all passages showed positive results.

Typical cytopathic effects (CPEs) of CRKF cells (10×). (A) Normal CRFK cells; (B) CRFK cells infected with YSH1‐2022; (C) CRFK cells infected with YSH2‐2022 and (D) CRFK cells infected with GSW1‐2023. CRFK, Crandell feline kidney.

Phylogenetic Analysis of the VP2 Gene

The VP2 gene of the three strains is in the same branch as FPV (Figure 4A). The parvovirus isolated from the Gushuiwan residential area in Songjiang District in 2023 was named GSW1‐2023 (PP355848). The full‐length VP2 sequence was aligned by NCBI and was found to be highly similar to nearly 100 FPV VP2 genes uploaded from China, with 99.83%–100% homology. The nucleotide sequence of the FPV VP2 gene was completely consistent with that of three strains isolated from Shanghai (MW811187.1, MW659466.1 and MW650831.1), three strains isolated from Jiangsu Yangzhou (OQ863615.1, OQ863617.1 and OQ863618.1) and five strains from other provinces in China (OL547736.1, OL547735.1 and OL547732.1). The parvoviruses isolated from the Yushanghai residential area in Songjiang district in 2022 were named YSH1‐2022 (PP355844) and YSH2‐2022 (PP355846). The nucleotide homology of the VP2 gene of these two strains was 100%. Compared with GSW1‐2023, there were three stable nucleotide mutations: Base A at 384 was mutated into base G, base T at 537 was mutated into base C, and base A at 756 was mutated into base G. Homology was 99.8%, but the amino acid encoded by the nucleotides was not changed, meaning that the VP2 amino acid homology of the three strains was 100%.

Phylogenetic tree of VP2、NS1 gene sequences. (A) VP2 gene; (B) NS1 gene. FPV, feline panleukopenia virus.

Phylogenetic Analysis of the NS1 Gene

The NS1 gene of the three strains is in the same branch as FPV (Figure 4B). The nucleotide homology of the NS1 genes of YSH1‐2022 (PP355845) and YSH2‐2022 (PP355847) was 100%. Furthermore, the nucleotide sequences were identical to three FPV NS1 genes isolated in Shanghai (MW811187.1, MW659466.1 and MW650831.1) as well as two FPV NS1 genes isolated from other provinces in China (OL547737.1, MZ836428.1). Compared with YSH1‐2022 and YSH2‐2022, GSW1‐2023 (PP355849) showed 99.9% nucleotide homology, and only one stable nucleotide mutation was observed. Specifically, base G at position 1954 was mutated to base A, resulting in mutation of Ala at position 652 to Thr.

Discussion

The FPV VP2 gene isolated from GSW1‐2023 showed 100% nucleotide homology with the FPV VP2 gene isolated from Shanghai and Yangzhou, Jiangsu Province. Compared with GSW1‐2023, although YSH1‐2022 and YSH2‐2022 had three nucleotide inconsistencies, all were synonymous mutations. Thus, the amino acid homology of the VP2 gene among the three isolated strains of the virus was 100%, and they shared 100% amino acid homology with FPV VP2 genes isolated from locations such as Shanghai and Yangzhou, Jiangsu Province.

The presence of a unique glycosylation site for the transferrin receptor (TFR)on canine cells results in resistance to FPV; thus, FPV itself could not infect canines (Allison et al. 2012; Chang, Sgro, and Parrish 1992; Hueffer et al. 2003; Palermo, Hafenstein, and Parrish 2006; Parker et al. 2001). However, when the amino acid residues 93 (Lys‐Asn) and 323 (Asp‐Asn) on the FPV VP2 protein were altered, the FPV VP2 protein could bind to the canine transferrin receptor TFR and infect canine cells (Govindasamy et al. 2003). The amino acid residues 80, 93, 103, 323, 564 and 568 on the VP2 gene that determine host range remained unaltered. Because there are a large number of stray cats in several residential areas in Shanghai and residents report many fights between wild raccoon dogs and stray cats over food, it seems possible that wild raccoon dogs, a type of canine, could carry FPV, but FPV may not replicate in wild raccoon dogs. On the basis of this view, this study speculated that the isolated strains would not spread among raccoon dogs. To further validate this issue, anal and pharyngeal swabs should be collected and tested separately in the future.

Carnivore parvovirus, although a DNA virus, exhibits a high propensity for genetic evolution, with a mutation rate comparable to RNA viruses. This evolution primarily occurs in the capsid gene VP2 (Hoelzer et al. 2008; Shackelton et al. 2005), which is a determinant of host range for carnivore parvovirus (Truyen, Agbandje, and Parrish 1994; Yuan and Parrish 2000). For instance, shortly after the initial emergence of canine parvovirus (CPV)‐2, mutations in the capsid protein VP2 involving changes in five–six amino acids (Decaro et al. 2008) led to its replacement by variant strains CPV‐2a and CPV‐2b within 3 years, followed by it spreading globally among dog populations (Parrish 1994; Truyen 1999). In addition, when the pathogen transfers to a new host, mutation and evolution of the virus are likely to occur, producing evolutionary intermediates (Allison et al. 2012; Truyen et al. 1996). Canine parvovirus CPV‐2 crossed over to raccoons, resulting in an evolutionary intermediate between CPV‐2 and CPV‐2a (Allison et al. 2012). Shandong Agricultural University collected samples from raccoon dog farms and analysed the samples positive for parvovirus and found that it produced a novel canine parvovirus 2, whose VP2 gene is between CPV‐2a and CPV‐2c (Yu et al. 2018). The only available isolate from a farmed Arctic fox from Finland revealed a DNA sequence intermediate between the FPV and CPV viruses, although the corresponding amino acid sequence clearly grouped the virus as an FPV type virus (Truyen and Parrish 1995). When FPV is transferred to wild raccoon dogs, there is a risk of mutation, resulting in evolutionary intermediates.

The emergence of evolutionary intermediates or new variants is not only the result of the virus adapting to a new host but also indicates a wider host range. CPV is believed to have evolved from FPV (Agbandje et al. 1993; Chang, Sgro, and Parrish 1992; Parker and Parrish 1997) and is currently divided into four subtypes: CPV‐2, CPV‐2a, CPV‐2b and CPV‐2c. The first subtype that appeared, CPV‐2, did not infect cats initially but then evolved into CPV‐2a, CPV‐2b and CPV‐2c, which infected not only dogs but also cats (Allison et al. 2012; Hoelzer et al. 2008; Parrish et al. 1988; Truyen 1999). CPV‐2a has a stronger adaptation to dogs than CPV‐2, and variants CPV‐2a and CPV‐2b are prevalent in dogs around the world (Hafenstein et al. 2009; Palermo, Hafenstein, and Parrish 2006; Parrish et al. 1985; Truyen and Parrish 1992). When FPV evolves into a new strain adapted to raccoon dogs, there is a risk that the new strain will become prevalent in raccoon dogs and other populations. The structure of parvovirus is compact, the physical and chemical properties are more stable than other viruses, and the virus can remain active in the external environment for more than 1 year (Hendricks et al. 2014). There are a large number of stray cats in many communities in Shanghai, and even if cats or dogs infected with FPV and CPV are clinically normal, they can excrete virus in their faeces for a long time (Battilani et al. 2013), which leads to the possible evolution of parvovirus into new mutant strains adapted to raccoon dog hosts.

Conclusion

In summary, the first investigation of viral pathogens carried by wild raccoons in residential areas of Shanghai was conducted, and FPV was found for the first time among them. Although it has been speculated that feline parvovirus does not infect raccoon dogs, the virus has a high mutation rate, and new mutant strains can easily be produced when transferred to new hosts. The emergence of new mutant strains is not only the result of virus adaptation to new hosts but also may indicate a wider host range. In addition, given the large number of stray cats in residential areas in Shanghai, cats infected with parvovirus may be excrete virus in their faeces for a long time. Moreover, feline parvovirus has strong resistance and can remain active in the environment for a long time, which provides a possibility for feline parvovirus to evolve into a variant strain adapted to raccoon dog hosts. Therefore, it is recommended to continue monitoring, especially in communities with a high density of raccoon dogs and stray cats, and to strengthen the management of stray animals, improve the management of refuse in communities, and reduce the feeding of stray and wild animals to prevent the emergence of mutant strains and their prevalence in raccoon dogs or other animals.
