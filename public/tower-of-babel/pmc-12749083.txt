Sea surface suspended concentrations prediction based on pre-trained transformer
Authors: Tao Su, Leilei Wang, Yu Song, Zhongli Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSea surface suspended sediments (SSSC) monitoring is an important branch of marine engineering, which plays an important role in port transportation, marine habitat ecology, and the environment. However, understanding and monitoring the dynamics of suspended sediments remain challenging due to complex natural and anthropogenic interactions. Existing approaches, such as using suit data or satellite data to construct numerical models, face limitations in sampling irregular spaces or lacking generalization capabilities. Besides, unlike natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV), the amount of suit data or satellite data is small, which make it difficult for some data-driven deep models to achieve excellent performance. To address these challenges, we propose a pre-trained transformer model that leverages an NLP pre-trained model trained on billions of tokens for SSSC reconstruction. Specifically, we design input embedding layers to project remote sensing image data into dimensions required by a specific pre-trained model. During the fine-tuning phase, we freeze the self-attention and feed-forward layers of the residual block in the pre-trained model, allowing knowledge transfer to the SSSC task. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed pre-trained transformer models achieve state-of-the-art performance on the SSSC task. This result may indicate that parameters trained on massive data in large models have the potential to enhance performance on other tasks.Keywords: Deep learning, Sea surface suspended sediments, TransformerSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Computer science

Introduction

Seabed sediment concentration prediction is an important field and plays an important role in seabed engineering1,2, global water hazards3, and ecological environment monitoring4. The main component of seabed sediment is sand, which is mainly produced by tides and waves, but is also produced by trawling and marine development5–7, such as the extraction of oil and natural gas, the installation of submarine cables, tunnels, offshore power generation, etc. Suspended sediment in the water body absorbs heavy metals, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and other pollutants8,9. This suspended sediment not only affects the optical properties of the water body, such as transparency, turbidity, and color, but also influences the reproduction and growth of aquatic life, especially algae10,11. Furthermore, the accumulation of sediment in large quantities can lead to water hazards and have adverse effects on river management, biological habitats, and the ecological environment12. Therefore, it is crucial to predict the accumulation of sea surface suspended sediment13,14.

In past decades, researchers have traditionally relied on turbidity meters to collect suit samples for sea surface suspended sediments concentration (SSSC) in coastal and estuarine environments. However, this approach is time-consuming and limited in spatial resolution. With the development of the satellite remote sensing technology, satellite remote sensing has emerged as a valuable tool for monitoring SSSC. Satellite remote sensing offers numerous advantages over traditional suit sampling methods, including extensive spatial coverage, high spatial resolution, and cost-effectiveness. The ocean color satellite sensors such as SeaWiFS, MODIS, MERIS, and GOCI are specifically designed with spectral bands optimized for observing bio-optical properties in aquatic environments. Notably, these sensors with higher spatial resolution15 can effectively monitor suspended particulate matter (SPM) concentrations in areas characterized by higher SSSC variability.

In recent years, an increasing number of researchers have turned to remote sensing satellite data for their studies16–18. For example, the Landsat series of remote sensing satellite data sets are widely used due to their public accessibility, high spatial resolution (30 meters) and spanning several decades (from the 1970s to the present). Researchers focus on reconstructing of sea surface suspended sediments concentration (SSSC) using irregular spatiotemporal and potentially scarce ocean remote sensing data. They have studied some approaches to evaluate the sediment dynamics in coastal areas. These methods can be categorized into two types: traditional mathematical modeling approaches19–22 and data-driven algorithmic models23–25. The traditional SSSC prediction method mainly involves point sampling to obtain the sediment concentration from the continental source to the continental margin and establish a suitable numerical model to predict it. For example, Qiu et al.19 proposed an empirical model for estimating SSSC in the Yellow River estuary, which utilized Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (L8/OLI) band ratio B4/B3 to tune the SSSC retrieval mode. According to the study conducted by Li et al.20, it was observed that low turbidity water bodies exhibit a strong positive correlation between SPM and Remote Sensing Reflectance (Rrs) in the green and red bands. Additionally, in high turbidity water bodies, the correlation between SPM and Rrs in the Near-Infrared (NIR) band was found to be further enhanced. Building upon these findings, Li et al. developed a three-band SSSC retrieval model. Zhou et al.21 utilized the backscattering coefficient (bb532) from satellite Remote Sensing Reflectance (RSR) to represent the turbidity and obtained the final concentration of surface suspended sediments. These mathematical statistical models used for remote sensing monitoring of SSSC typically assume a specific functional relationship between the uncertainty of water body information and remote sensing information. Then, these models estimate the parameters by transforming them into multivariate linear functions22. However, these approaches often require the sample population to conform to specific distribution characteristics, such as normal distribution. In practice, the spatiotemporal autocorrelation of water parameters may not necessarily meet these assumptions, which may result in lower prediction accuracy, poor generalization performance of the model and limited application.

Nowadays, deep learning methods have shown remarkable success in computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP). As a result, some researchers have started embracing deep learning techniques in the field of satellite remote sensing. By leveraging the power of deep learning, they aim to enhance the analysis and interpretation of satellite remote sensing data, enabling more accurate and efficient extraction of valuable information from these datasets. The application of deep learning in satellite remote sensing has the potential to significantly improve various tasks23–29, including image classification, object detection, land cover mapping, and change detection. For instance, Wei et al.30 employ a quantum bee colony algorithm based on Boltzmann’s selection and dimensional dynamic update strategy to optimize artificial intelligence (AI) models (BDQABC-ANN) and exhibited stronger predictive capabilities for SSSC. Xiaoming et al.31 utilized generative adversarial network (GAN) to generate more samples and employed deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to predict seabed sediment classification. In the study conducted by Wei et al.32, it was observed that the shallow CNNs were often more effective in extracting relevant features and achieving accurate predictions and outperformed the deep CNN architecture when dealing with small datasets. Although these deep learning methods have achieved excellent performance on satellite remote sensing data, however, we can find that due to the small sample size of the dataset, it is difficult to train an effective model to complete the corresponding task.

To address these issues, this paper proposes a pre-trained Transformer model, which utilizes parameters in the NLP pre-trained Transformer model from billions of tokens to facilitate model training for downstream tasks, especially in few shot tasks for SSSC. Specifically, we design input embedding layers to project remote sensing image data into dimensions required by a specific pre-trained model. During the fine-tuning phase, we freeze the self-attention and feed-forward layers of the residual block in the pre-trained model, allowing knowledge transfer to the SSSC task. Then, we conduct experimental verification using the Bay of Biscay dataset, which involves natural movements and artificial operations of sediment generation and can evaluate the performance of the SSSC model effectively. Finally, the results demonstrate that our proposed methods can achieve state-of-the-art performance on the SSSC task. The layout of this paper is arranged as follows. In section II, we introduce the Bay of Biscay dataset. Section III describe the proposed pre-trained Transformer framework in detail. In section IV, we explain the experiment results. Section V analyzes and discusses the experiment result. This paper is concluded in Section VI.

Materials

The study aimed to predict the sea surface suspended sediments concentration for small dataset. In particular, the Bay of Biscay is selected as the areas to investigate SSSC. The Bay of Biscay is located in the northeastern part of the North Atlantic Ocean, bordering France to the east and Spain to the south. It covers an area of 194,000 km, a total volume of about 332,000 cubic kilometers, an average depth of 1715 m, and a maximum depth of 5120 m. Sediment fluxes at the Bay of Biscay are often affected by natural and anthropogenic interactions such as sea breezes, tides, trawling and fishing. Sediments mainly include three different types, which can be classified according to their mean grain size, including mud (m ), sand (from 63 to m), and gravel (mm ). In this work, we mainly consider the first two, adding the concentrations of mud and sand to obtain the total suspended sediment concentration at the sea surface (in kg m ).

The suit data were collected using a Nortek profiler and turbidity meter installed on a bottom mooring at a depth of 23 m33. The site is located near the southern coast of Brittany (at one station of latitude and longitude ), close to the city of ”Le Croisic”. The measurements were conducted from November 25, 2007, to January 31, 2008. Additionally, simultaneous SSC measurements were performed by the AWAC profiler, covering the entire water column over a 20-meter range. The AWAC profiler was calibrated with a turbidity meter. The satellite data34 were derived from the MERIS satellite sensor dataset available from 2007 to 2011 and daily sampled.

To optimize the utilization of the dataset, we have followed the approach employed in previous studies35 and utilized the MARS-MUSTANG model to simulate oceanic sedimentary fluxes. As shown in Fig. 1, we introduce the relationship between the bathymetry and the suspended sediment dynamics. Specifically, Fig. 1a illustrates the isobaths of the Bay of Biscay, providing a visual representation of the depth contours in the study area. This information is essential as bathymetry plays a crucial role in influencing the circulation patterns and sediment transport processes within the bay. In particular, the remote sensor can only work in the near visible range, and it is impossible to observe the sea surface under cloudy conditions, which will result in a part of the data being missing. To better reconstruct the sea surface field based on observation data, we use the Observing System Simulation Experiment (OSSE)36 as a benchmark experimental framework. This remote sensor dataset contains 1436 samples, and some data are partially missing due to weather conditions. After data preprocessing, the total number of the training set are 895, and the number of the valid set are 1, and the number of the test data set are 100.

Methods

Deep learning (DL) techniques have gained wide popularity in CV and NLP due to their impressive performance, especially the large language model (LLM), like GPT237, SAM38. Researchers have also started using DL methods to tackle the problem of Sea Surface Suspended Concentration (SSSC) and have achieved remarkable results. Unlike CV and NLP, where abundant data is available to train complex models capable of performing various tasks, SSSC datasets are limited, making it challenging to achieve excellent performance. To overcome this challenge, we leverage a pre-trained Transformer model (GPT2), originally trained on billions of NLP tokens, and adapt it for SSSC tasks. Specifically, we utilize its NLP-derived parameters without retraining on new tokens, focusing instead on fine-tuning with our small dataset. This approach, termed Pretrained Transformer, transfers knowledge across domains efficiently.

By fine-tuning these layers, the model can better capture the positional and contextual information relevant to the SSSC prediction.

Pretrained transformer

Building complex models with excellent performance often requires a large amount of training data to mitigate problems such as underfitting or overfitting. However, compared to domains like CV or NLP, the availability of data is often limited in the field of remote sensing. When dealing with small remote sensing datasets, training complex models with a large number of parameters can be challenging. The limited data may not sufficiently capture the underlying patterns or provide enough variation for the model to generalize well. As a result, the performance of these models may suffer.

To address this issue, this paper employs transfer learning strategy. Transfer learning involves leveraging pre-trained models on larger datasets from related domains (e.g., CV or NLP) and fine-tuning them on the remote sensing data. This approach allows the model to benefit from the knowledge learned from abundant data sources. Hence, we propose a pretrained transformer model to deal with SSSC prediction. Specifically, to effectively utilize transformer models in remote sensing data, several steps can be taken, including Input Embedding, Normalization, Patching, Fine-tuning.

1. Input embedding

When applying NLP pre-trained models to remote sensing tasks, it becomes necessary to redesign and train the input embedding layer to transform the raw remote sensing data into a suitable input format for the transformer model. This step ensures that the model can effectively process and comprehend the unique characteristics of remote sensing data. To project remote sensing image data into the dimensions required by a specific pre-trained model, linear probing can be used. Linear probing involves adding a linear layer on top of the pre-trained model’s embedding layer. This linear layer reduces the dimensionality of the remote sensing image data and aligns it with the dimensions expected by the pre-trained model. By using linear probing, the number of parameters required for training is reduced. This reduction is particularly advantageous when working with limited remote sensing data, as it helps mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhances the model’s ability to generalize to unseen examples.

The sea surface field dataset we used includes 1430 days of image data with a size of 6464. The data are first normalized using instance normalization to standardize scales across features. Patching is applied to divide each 6464 sample into 16 patches of size 1616, converting spatial data into a sequence of patches. This is inspired by Vision Transformers (ViT) to retain local spatial information. We use Token Embedding, Positional Embedding, and Temporal Embedding to embed patch features, patch positions, and times, respectively, ensuring that the output feature dimensions conform to the 768 embedding dimension expected by the GPT-2 model. The output is a sequence of embedding vectors fed directly into the frozen Transformer blocks. This process ensures compatibility with the pre-trained architecture while minimizing trainable parameters.

2. Normalization

The data normalization block is a valuable step in ensuring consistent and standardized scales in the input data for remote sensing tasks. This normalization block plays a crucial role in facilitating knowledge transfer and improving the model’s ability to learn effectively. The normalization process involves computing the mean and variance of the input time series data. By normalizing the data using these statistics, the different features or channels of the remote sensing data are treated equally during training. This ensures that no particular feature dominates the learning process due to differences in scale or magnitude. Normalizing the data helps to mitigate issues related to varying scales, improves convergence during training, and allows the model to learn more efficiently. Moreover, it aids in reducing the impact of outliers and noise in the data, ultimately enhancing the robustness of the model.

3. Patching

Vision Transformer (VIT)40 employs patching methods to capture local spatial information within images effectively and achieves excellent performance. Hence, we also incorporate patching to convert spatial remote sensing data into sequence patches, enabling the transformer model to operate on sequential inputs and retain spatial information. Patching allows the transformer model to handle larger input history timeframes while maintaining a consistent token length. By dividing the input image into smaller patches, the model can capture local details and spatial relationships effectively. Patching helps reduce information redundancy by allowing the model to process smaller spatial regions individually, reducing computational complexity and memory requirements.

4. Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning on specific remote sensing data follows an initial pre-training or transfer learning phase using large datasets from related fields. This step involves training a transformer model on a remote sensing dataset to adapt it to the specific features and patterns present in the data. Fine-tuning helps model specialization and achieve better performance on remote sensing tasks. Specially, we utilize two strategies to help model to transfer learning in new remote sensing data.

Frozen pretrained block: we choose to retain the position embedding layer and self-attention block in the pre-trained model in our architecture. These components contain extensive knowledge learned from pretrained language models. To preserve this knowledge and prevent it from being overwritten during fine-tuning, you decide to freeze the self-attention block. This approach ensures that the pre-trained knowledge captured in these blocks remains intact and the model can effectively utilize the learned attention mechanism. Freezing the self-attention module while fine-tuning helps to strike a balance between leveraging pre-trained knowledge and allowing the model to be specialized for remote sensing tasks. It enables the model to benefit from the rich representation learned in the pre-training stage while adapting to the specific characteristics of remote sensing data during fine-tuning.

Positional embeddings and layer normalization: we choose to fine-tune the position embedding and layer normalization layers during the fine-tuning process, which allows these components to adapt to specific downstream tasks and new patterns present in the data. By fine-tuning the position embedding layer, the model can learn task-specific position information relevant to the target task. This is especially important in situations where the position of an element in the input sequence is important to the current task. Similarly, fine-tuning layers and normalization layers allow the model to adapt the normalization parameters to the specific data distributions and patterns observed in downstream tasks. This fine-tuning process enables the model to better capture and exploit the statistical properties of the data during training. By combining fine-tuning of position embeddings and layer normalization layers, pre-trained knowledge can be effectively exploited while allowing these components to adapt to the specific requirements and patterns of the target task. This approach has the potential to enhance model performance on downstream tasks by tailoring these components to the specific data distributions and patterns encountered in new tasks.

Evaluation metric

In this work, to evaluate the performance of different methods, we adopt global root mean square error (RMSE) and global reconstruction score (R-score). These metrics provide insights into the method’s capture of the real data and variance rate. Descriptions of these evaluation metrics are as follows:

Root mean square error (RMSE): RMSE is a widely used metric to evaluate the accuracy of interpolation methods. It measures the square root of the average squared difference between the predicted and true values. A lower RMSE indicates higher accuracy because it represents the overall difference between the predicted and actual values.

Results

Our experimental results are summarized as follows. Specifically, we verified it with different methods on the same dataset to demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed method.

Computational setup

The configuration of the workstation we employ in these experiments are as follows: CPU: i5-12600KF, RAM: 64G, GPU: RTX 4070s 12G. We utilized the python language to run all programs, and pytorch framework was used to run all deep learning methods.

We use the pre-trained model GPT2(3)41 for experiments, where 3 represents the use of the first 3 layers. The patch size is set to 16, so that each 6464 sample can be divided into 16. The learning rate is set to 0.0001 and the batch size is set to 48. Dropout is set to 0.2, and L2 regularization is set to 0.005 to avoid overfitting. Specifically, the early stopping strategy is used to stop the training phase and avoid overfitting, where a counter is used to stop the training process after ten epochs when no loss degradation is observed on the valid set.

Metrics and evaluation

To effectively evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method, different methods for SSSC prediction are also compared in this study, including optimal interpolation (OI), EOF-based interpolation (DinEOF), analog data assimilation (AnDA) and variation-based Neural network based on the sub-formula (4DVarNet). Here’s a brief overview of these methods:

Optimal interpolation (OI)42: optimal interpolation is a widely used data interpolation technique that combines observations with a background field to produce an optimal analysis. It minimizes the difference between observations and the analysis while considering the background error covariance. OI is often employed for spatial interpolation of data, including SSSC.

EOF-based interpolation (DinEOF)43: EOF-based interpolation utilizes Empirical Orthogonal Functions (EOFs) to represent the dominant modes of spatial variability in the data. By projecting the data onto the EOFs, missing or sparse data can be interpolated based on the spatial patterns captured by these modes.

Analog data assimilation (AnDA)44: simulated data assimilation refers to the process of assimilating simulated or synthetic observations into a model to improve its state estimation. This approach allows for assessing the performance and sensitivity of data assimilation methods under controlled conditions.

4DVarNet35: 4DVarNet is a variation-based neural network method that combines the principles of four-dimensional variational data assimilation (4DVar) and deep learning. It formulates the data interpolation problem as an optimization problem and utilizes neural networks to learn the mapping between the observations and the analyzed state.

Each method has its own approach and assumptions and comparing them helps to assess the strengths and limitations of different interpolation and data assimilation techniques. The different interpolation methods are evaluated according to two standard metrics: the global root mean square error (RMSE) and a global reconstruction score (R-score), in terms of rate of variance of the true data captured by the interpolation.

Discussion

The study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of different models for retrieving gap-free series of SSSC fields from irregularly sampled satellite measurements. From these results may indicate that deep learning methods have significant improvements compared to traditional methods. This may be because traditional methods rely on EOF decomposition to study SSS dynamics, resulting in relatively poor retrieval capabilities. Deep learning methods, using a data-driven approach, have better data representation capabilities, thereby achieving better performance. Moreover, compared with different methods, our proposed pre-trained transformer GPT2(3) method achieve state-of-the-art performance on the SSSC task. It can be found from the results that compared with the traditional OI method, the average relative gain of the log10(SSSC) value of our proposed pre-trained transformer model is 70% in terms of RMSE. These findings provide evidence for the potential of leveraging parameters from large models trained on billions of tokens to aid in various tasks. Furthermore, the employed freezing strategy demonstrates its efficacy in harnessing the capabilities of the large model to address related tasks. Simultaneously, fine-tuning enables the model to acquire knowledge about the specific characteristics of remote sensing data.

Despite overall strong performance, the model struggles in two key scenarios: (1) regions with extreme sediment concentrations (e.g., sudden spikes from storm events), where the model tends to underestimate peaks due to smoothing effects; and (2) areas with prolonged cloud cover, where missing data leads to increased uncertainty in reconstruction. These cases highlight the need for incorporating meteorological data and robust gap-filling techniques in future work.

Conclusion

In this study, we address the problem of data-driven interpolation of satellite-derived SSSC fields. The OSSE setting in the Bay of Biscay is employed to valid our proposed pre-training transformer GPT2(3). Extensive experiment results demonstrate that our proposed method can significantly improve the reconstruction of SSSC dynamics when compared to the traditional Optimal Interpolation (OI) method. Specifically, we achieve a relative gain of up to 70% in terms of root mean square error (RMSE). Besides, this research also reveals the superior performance of large models in various downstream tasks, highlighting the versatility of Transformer models. In the future, building on these findings, we aim to further enhance the performance of our method by leveraging parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, thereby enabling its more effective utilization in SSSC-related tasks.
