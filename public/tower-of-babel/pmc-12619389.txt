Retention of Co-Cr bars supporting mandibular implant overdenture fabricated with two techniques
Authors: Sara Zaky, Eman A. Tawfik, Mostafa Mahmoud, Reham Abdelfatah, Ahmed Mohamed Shoeib, Yousra Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe goal of this study was to see how laser-sintered and regular cobalt-chromium bars affect how well they hold in place and how rough their surfaces are in lower jaw implant overdentures.Materials and MethodsThis study utilized a completely edentulous mandible model that was fabricated from epoxy resin. Implants were imbedded in the canine region in the model, then implant abutment were screwed on each implant. Scan bodies were inserted, then scanning of the 3D model was done using a laboratory scanner. CO-CR bars were divided into: Group A: Twelve bars that were fabricated using the laser sintering method. Group B: twelve bars were designed using CAD/CAM for the fabrication of a wax model, and then the bar was fabricated using a conventional lost wax-casting technique. Each bar and implant were loaded with a conventional acrylic resin denture. A universal testing machine was used to apply tensile load. Surface roughness was estimated by using a digital microscope with digital software. The data was collected and analyzed using statistical analysis.ResultsThere was a significant difference between group A and group B regarding the technique of fabrication of the implant bar in both retention and surface roughness.Conclusionchanging the technique for fabricating the implant bars has improved the retention and surface roughness of the implant-supported overdenture.

Introduction

Complete dentures have been the preferred therapy for edentulous people. However, patients often express dissatisfaction with the stability and retention of the mandibular denture [1].

To mitigate these issues, implant-supported overdentures (ISODs) have been used as a more stable option, particularly for the mandible. Utilizing splinting implants with bar attachments has shown enhanced support and functional efficacy by more uniformly dispersing occlusal pressures. Bar attachments differ in mechanical design and retention mechanisms, and their clinical efficacy is determined by the accuracy of their fit and the characteristics of the materials used [2, 3].

formerly, implant bars have been produced by casting methods, often utilizing cobalt–chromium (Co–Cr) alloys for their strength and longevity. Nevertheless, casting may result in mistakes, including shrinkage, contamination, and the need for intricate post-processing procedures like soldering or welding, which may undermine the fit and durability of the prosthesis. On the contrary, computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies provide enhanced accuracy, diminished fabrication faults, and heightened mechanical reliability [4].

Various attachment techniques attach implants to overdentures. The primary objective of attachment methods was to enhance the retention and stability of tooth-supported overdentures. The systems include either non-splinted attachments that are directly fixed to the implant (like magnets, balls, locators, or telescopic crowns) or splinted attachments that use a bar and clip system to join the implants [5].

Among CAD/CAM alternatives, laser sintering—a kind of additive manufacturing has attracted interest for its capacity to create intricately detailed components with uniform structural integrity. This technique uses a high-intensity laser to fuse tiny metal powders incrementally, facilitating the production of intricate geometries straight from digital designs. In comparison to traditional casting, laser sintering is thought to produce bars with superior dimensional precision and enhanced surface stability [6, 7].

On the other hand, CAD/CAM techniques provide exceptional stability, an optimal fit, compatibility with various implant systems, adaptability in therapeutic and design options, superior precision, and a lower risk of complications such as joint fractures, deformations, screw breakage, or loosening [8, 9].

Denture retention is how well a denture stays in place against the forces trying to pull it out, which is important for keeping it secure when it’s put in. For optimal denture retention, the denture base must be precisely adapted to the mucosa. The sulci are occluded by extending the denture flanges to establish a border seal [10].

The preservation of dentures is undoubtedly one of the most challenging issues encountered by prosthodontists. The capacity to endure occlusal stress, retention, and stability are critical elements in effective denture treatment. Complete denture retention is the primary determinant of patient satisfaction and the enduring success of dentures. Denture retention is affected by the shape and size of the denture base, the amount and quality of saliva, the stickiness between surfaces, the tension at the surface, the presence and depth of bone undercuts, and the height, width, and shape of the remaining jawbone ridge [11, 12].

Surface roughness measures surface irregularities, related to uneven elevation values, and is also known as landscape, terrain, or topographic roughness in geomorphology [13].

Roughness influences an object’s interaction with its environment, with rough surfaces typically exhibiting faster wear and higher friction coefficients. Roughness predicts mechanical component performance, as irregularities may cause cracks or corrosion nucleation sites, while surface roughness may promote adhesion. This information is crucial in understanding friction, lubrication, and wear phenomena [14].

Oral biofilms, formed by microorganisms colonizing teeth and artificial surfaces, are responsible for dental pathologies like caries, periodontitis, denture stomatitis, and candidiasis. The initial attachment of single microbes is essential for biofilm formation [15].

Despite increasing interest in digital fabrication techniques, there is a lack of comparative data assessing how manufacturing methods—specifically laser sintering vs. traditional casting—impact both retentive force and surface roughness of bar attachments for mandibular ISODs. Prior research has either concentrated on mechanical qualities in isolation or failed to provide direct comparisons under standardized settings.

This research aims at studying the retention properties and surface roughness alterations of laser-sintered and traditionally cast bar attachments used in mandibular implant-supported overdentures. The study aims to determine whether additive manufacturing offers quantifiable therapeutic benefits regarding prosthesis function and long-term stability.

Null Hypotheses (H₀):

There is no significant difference in the retention of mandibular implant-supported overdentures between cobalt-chromium bars fabricated using the laser sintering technique and those fabricated using the conventional lost wax-casting technique.

There is no significant difference in the surface roughness of cobalt-chromium bars fabricated using the laser sintering technique compared to those fabricated using the conventional lost wax-casting technique.

Methodology

Sample grouping

The sample size for this study was calculated according to Botega and Mesquita et al.,2004 (16). A power analysis was performed using G*Power software (v3.1.9.2) (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany) to calculate the sample size. The power value was 95%, and the α level of significance was 0.05. The results showed an effect size of ƒ = 2.252 and the calculated sample size was twelve for each group. The samples are classified into two groups: -.

Group A

Twelve bar attachments were fabricated using direct metal laser sintering (DMLS) technology with a VULCANTEC VM120 laser sintering machine. The STL files were uploaded to the laser sintering system, which employed a high-powered laser to fuse layers of Co-Cr alloy powder.

Group B

Twelve bar attachments were fabricated using the lost-wax casting technique, following CAD design generation. A Roland DGSHAPE DWX-52D milling machine was used to mill wax patterns based on identical STL files used in Group A to ensure design consistency.

Preliminary smoothing with rotating carbide burs with water cooling.

Careful polishing using a succession of silicone polishers, followed by high-luster buffing utilizing pumice and universal polishing paste. Polishing was conducted via a micromotor at 10,000–15,000 rpm with felt wheels under little pressure to maintain surface integrity.

Prior to mechanical testing, all completed bars in both groups were compared to the original CAD dimensions and subjected to dimensional verification using a digital calliper with an accuracy of ± 0.01 mm. To ensure uniformity in geometry among groups, bars that were found to surpass a tolerance of ± 0.1 mm were either eliminated or remade.

Fabrication of models with implants

A 3D printer was used to create a 3D model of a human edentulous mandibular jaw, with a 2-mm silicone lining layer applied, and two implant holes made. Figures (1) & [2].

The implants 4.2 mm (width), 10 mm (length) were positioned bilaterally in the canine regions of the epoxy resin mandibular model, with an inter-implant distance of approximately 22 mm, following anatomical norms for mandibular overdenture cases. Each implant was placed perpendicular to the occlusal plane (0° angulation) to eliminate the influence of implant angulation on bar adaptation, retention, or stress distribution.

The Instron Universal Testing system, specifically Bluehill Lite software, was utilized to measure the retention of overdentures by gradually increasing vertical load until total framework dislodgment and decrease in load-displacement curve were recorded. Figure 8.

Protocol for mechanical chewing simulation

All specimens underwent mechanical chewing simulation using a chewing simulator capable of applying both vertical loading and horizontal sliding movements under thermocycle conditions to assess the fatigue performance of the bar attachments. Loading force: 50 N (1,460 cycles—roughly 12 months of clinical activity based on average daily mastication rates [16].

Every overdenture-bar assembly was set in a custom-built jig to guarantee uniform alignment and force application.

Conditions for thermocycling

Indeed, a thermocycle protocol was used concurrently with the chewing simulation, as noted in the paper. The chewing chamber was integrated with thermal cycling to mimic changes in intraoral temperature. 5 °C to 55 °C.

Measuring surface roughness

A 3D surface analyzer system optical profilometer was used to assess surface roughness for both tested groups. The test involved a USB digital microscope with a built-in camera and 8 LED lamps. Images were taken at maximum resolution and cropped to 350 × 400 pixels. 3D images of the specimens’ surface profile were created and collected and analyzed using WSxM software. Average heights (Ra) were calculated using WSxM software. All measurements were taken before and after being subjected to a chewing simulator device. Statical analysis

The data distribution of normality was done by using Shapiro Wilk test which showed normal distribution of data. The results of the two groups are compared with independent t-test (p < 0.05). Meanwhile comparison of surface roughness before and after chewing stimulation in each group was done using paired t-test. The Data were analyzed using a commercial SPSS© program (Chicago, IL, USA version 20 for windows).

Discussion

Using a bar attachment improves the retention of overdentures, supports them by distributing forces evenly, and thereby reduces the stress experienced by the implants [18].

Using bar attachments made by two methods—selective laser melting (SLM) and conventional casting—this study assessed and contrasted the retention force and surface roughness of mandibular implant-supported overdentures. The results showed that the laser-sintered bars had statistically significantly greater retention values and lower surface roughness than their cast equivalents, therefore supporting the goal of the study to evaluate the influence of manufacturing technique on these clinical parameters.

The improved retention seen in the SLM group could be ascribed to the better surface finish obtained by additive manufacturing, which provides more consistent structural integrity and higher accuracy than the traditional lost-wax method. These findings agree with previous research indicating that conventional cast bars may show surface irregularities including porosities and anomalies caused by wax distortion, investment material expansion, and metal solidification contraction. Such flaws could affect the retention and fitness of the last prosthesis [19, 20].

It is important to remember, though, that although the variations were statistically significant, the actual values of the variations in retention and surface roughness were rather low and might not have much clinical relevance. Patients perceive different retention thresholds, and a small numerical improvement might not automatically imply higher patient satisfaction. Especially for surface roughness, while the SLM group displayed smoother surfaces, both groups kept roughness averages close to the clinically acceptable threshold (Ra ± 0.2 μm), below which bacterial adherence is low [21]. This implies that, if suitably completed, both manufacturing techniques could still offer biologically acceptable surfaces over a 12-month simulated period.

The observed improvement in retention and surface roughness in laser-sintered cobalt-chromium bars can be attributed to the unique microstructural features inherent to the additive manufacturing process. Specifically, laser sintering produces a distinct “fish-scale” or layered morphology, which results from the rapid melting and solidification of metal powders layer by layer. This process leads to increased surface irregularities and micro-retentive features compared to the smoother surfaces achieved through conventional casting. These microscale surface irregularities likely enhance mechanical interlocking between the bar and the overlying denture resin, thereby contributing to increased retention. In contrast, the lost-wax casting technique tends to yield smoother and more homogenous surfaces due to the complete melting and controlled cooling of the alloy, resulting in lower surface roughness and potentially less mechanical interlocking [22].

Revilla-León et al. reported similar improvements in surface texture and adaptation with laser-sintered and other additive manufacturing techniques, which directly influenced the mechanical performance and retention characteristics of implant bars. Additionally, Çelik et al. (2021) demonstrated that additive manufacturing techniques, including selective laser melting (SLM), result in higher initial retention forces due to increased surface energy and micro-roughness, which enhance the bonding between the bar and acrylic resin. Our findings align with these studies, reinforcing the conclusion that fabrication technique significantly influences both the functional and physical characteristics of implant-supported prostheses [23].

Another study stated that the design and fitness of conventional bars might be less precise, causing increased stress and failure of the retentive components. Milled bars exhibit greater precision compared to conventional bars [22].

Researchers have observed a wide range of retention forces for various attachment techniques, both under static and simulated conditions. This discrepancy could result from various aspects of research. Several investigations indicated significant variability in the retention of similar attachment methods. One reason for the loss of retention with cast Co-Cr frameworks attached to implant-supported overdentures was changes in the surface and size of the attachments. The fit of the cast Co-Cr frameworks could be affected by mistakes in the wax blocking and duplication process, differences in how the refractory material expands, and the methods used for fitting and polishing the metal frameworks [23].

Another reason maybe that the conventional processing (lost-wax technique) used for the fabrication of cast Co-Cr alloys would result in fit deficiencies due to high casting temperatures and easy oxidation. The high modulus of elasticity decreases during conventional processing, and the adjustment of the retention force becomes more difficult. Cast Co-Cr alloys are subjected to high contraction cooling, which would influence the accuracy and fit, leading to decreased retention [24].

These results coincide with previous studies which stated that the reason for the significantly greater retention values initially in the milled Co-Cr frameworks over the cast Co-Cr frameworks may be explained by the elimination of the interdimeric microporosity, errors in wax blocking, and distortions that occur at the level of secondary framework surfaces during the casting procedures, subsequently increasing retention [25].

This study utilized optical microscope observations to examine the microstructure of both conventionally cast and Selective Laser Melting (SLM) Co-Cr specimens. Conventional cast specimens had a dendritic solidification structure, while SLM samples had a “fish scales” macrostructure. The study found that fine lamellar structures extend in the direction of the building when examined in transverse cross-section. Process parameters like laser power, scan rate, and scan line spacing affect the microstructure. The way Co-Cr structures are made greatly affects their microstructure, and SLM creates a more even microstructure because it melts the material in specific areas and cools it quickly [26, 27].

These study results agree with other findings that indicate SLM has greater hardness than casting, which exhibits lower scratch resistance and slightly rougher surfaces compared to SLM [28].

Several studies have looked at the retention force of implant overdenture attachments made utilizing several additive manufacturing (AM) technologies, including Selective Laser Melting (SLM) and Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS). Winkler et al. (2020), for instance, found that bars manufactured with DMLS showed greater initial retention and improved long-term retentive stability than those formed using conventional casting, consistent with the results of the current investigation. Likewise, Klein et al. (2018) discovered that whereas cast bars fell below clinically acceptable levels (10–12 N) under the same circumstances, retention forces of SLM-fabricated bars remained above 20 N after 1,500 cycles [29]. (30) [31].

Retention forces of CAD/CAM milled bars were much higher than those of conventionally cast bars in a comparative study by Abdallah et al. (2019), but somewhat lower than those of SLM-fabricated bars, suggesting that the additive layer-by-layer buildup in SLM may allow for better adaptation and retention than subtractive milling or traditional wax casting. Improved dimensional precision and better engagement surfaces for clip retention most likely explain this [32].

Moreover, Ghaffari et al. (2021) showed that SLM-produced bars maintained mechanical stability and consistent retention values during thermomechanical cycling because of their dense and homogenous microstructure. These results support the outcomes of the current investigation, which indicated a statistically significant—but maybe clinically modest—improvement in retention and surface roughness for laser-sintered bars in comparison to cast ones.(33).

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this research, laser-sintered bar attachments used in implant-supported overdentures exhibited enhanced retentive performance and increased surface stability (i.e., reduced roughness change) in comparison to conventionally cast bar attachments. These results indicate that additive manufacturing by laser sintering may provide a more robust and retentive alternative for bar-supported implant overdenture prostheses.

Limitation of study

For mandibular edentulism, implant-supported overdentures with bar attachments continue to be a consistent treatment option. Fabrication method selection could affect mechanical performance; SLM might have benefits in retention and surface qualities. Clinical decision-making, on the other hand, should consider cost, availability, and clinician knowledge as well. Future research should validate and broaden these results by including longer-term simulations, several attachment designs, and patient-centered outcomes.
