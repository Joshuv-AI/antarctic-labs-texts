Global, regional and national estimates of obstetric fistula prevalence
Authors: Saifuddin Ahmed, Rene Genadry, Bridget Asiamah, Mengjia Liang, Vandana Tripathi, Erin Anastasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe UN General Assembly in 2018 passed a resolution to eliminate obstetric fistula within a decade. Because the prevalence of obstetric fistula is not known, it is difficult to develop appropriate national and global health strategies to prevent, treat and eliminate the condition. We estimate the prevalence of obstetric fistula in 55 countries targeted by the UNFPA-led global Campaign to End Fistula Programme.MethodsWe used data on reported symptoms of obstetric fistula from 31 Demographic and Health Surveys conducted in 22 countries and applied a predictive model to estimate the global, regional and country-level prevalences of current obstetric fistula cases in target countries. Self-reported fistula symptoms are subject to over-reporting and misclassification biases. Using known estimates of clinically diagnostic validation from large population studies, we additionally corrected self-reported obstetric fistula estimates for misclassification errors.ResultsWe estimate the global fistula prevalence of about 457 000 (95% CI 303 000 to 709 000) among women aged 15–64 and about 386 000 (95% CI 256 000 to 599 000) among women of reproductive age (15–49). The obstetric fistula prevalence rate was almost two times higher in the sub-Saharan region (71 per 100 000 women) compared with Asia (36 per 100 000 women).ConclusionThe estimated prevalence of obstetric fistula is substantially lower than the previously cited numbers of 2 million women. The prevalence and incidence rates of obstetric fistula have likely declined over time due to a number of factors. Skilled birth attendance, access to emergency obstetric care and caesarean section deliveries have substantially increased in low- and middle-income countries with a concurrent decline in maternal mortality during the last two decades. The global Campaign to End Fistula, launched in 2003, together with other international efforts, has also targeted improving prevention and treatment of obstetric fistula during this period. This is a preventable and treatable condition, and the global community must mobilise to eliminate this devastating and tragic condition by ensuring that women have access to timely and quality emergency obstetric care, including safe surgical care for caesarean section.

Introduction

Substantial interest has emerged to estimate maternal morbidities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), recognising that many of them are also the causes of maternal and perinatal mortality.13 The WHO defines maternal morbidity as “any health condition attributed to and/or aggravated by pregnancy and childbirth that has a negative impact on the woman’s wellbeing”.4 Among all maternal morbidity conditions, obstetric fistula is the most devastating and debilitating5 and is considered a public health and human rights problem.6

Obstetric fistula, often referred to as female genital fistula, develops after prolonged and obstructed labour. In this condition, an abnormal opening develops between the vagina and the urinary bladder and/or rectum, clinically known as vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) and rectovaginal fistula, respectively, leading to continuous urinary and/or faecal incontinence. When the progression of labour is protracted and becomes obstructed, the soft tissues of a woman’s bladder, vagina and rectum are compressed between the fetal head and maternal pelvic bones due to the sustained contractions of the uterus. Consequently, the blood supply to the woman’s genitourinary organs is progressively reduced, resulting in ischaemia and tissue necrosis. Almost 90% of such deliveries result in fetal deaths.7 Often, obstetric fistula results in extensive tissue loss and dense scarring, which makes the surgical operation difficult, often requiring multiple surgeries. Vaginal fistula may also occur from accidental injury during pelvic surgery (iatrogenic fistula), including caesarean section (CS) and hysterectomy, or by violence (traumatic fistula). For the purpose of this study, we include fistula developed after childbirth—a fistula caused by pressure necrosis and could be an iatrogenic fistula—within the definition of obstetric fistula.

Women and girls suffering from obstetric fistula are highly stigmatised and often become social outcasts. Arrowsmith and colleagues coined the term ‘obstructed labour injury complex’ to encompass the extent of physical and social injuries caused by fistula.8 In numerous ways, fistula adversely affects women and their families socially, emotionally and economically.9 10

In 2003, the UNFPA and its partners launched the ‘Campaign to End Fistula’ to make obstetric fistula as rare in LMICs as it is in the high-income world and in 2022, the UN Secretary-General called for greater investment to accelerate the action to end fistula within a decade.11 Since then, significant interest has emerged in assessing the burden of obstetric fistula globally and at the country level. It has been estimated that 1–3 million women and girls are suffering from obstetric fistula globally.6 12 However, the true prevalence level is not known.1315

The most commonly quoted estimates of obstetric fistula globally are 2 million prevalent cases and 50 000–100 000 incident cases annually.13 These estimates are often referred to as the WHO estimation of obstetric fistula. However, the original citation for these estimates is a 1993 paper by Waaldijk and Armiya’u in which it is stated:

From the authors’ experience in Northern Nigeria, where even 700 repairs a year are not sufficient to cope with all the VVF patients presenting, the incidence rate can be calculated at 1–2 per 1000 deliveries where the mother survives in situations where there is no easy access to a functioning obstetric unit.16

Clearly, the basis of the estimation was not empirical data but, rather, anecdotal. Wall subsequently suggested that about 3 million women are suffering from obstetric fistula; however, no data source or basis was provided for the statistics.6 Overall, a systematic literature review concluded that obstetric fistula prevalence and incidence are understudied and biased.17

Using meta-analytic techniques, Adler and her colleagues estimated a pooled obstetric fistula prevalence rate of 0.29 (95% CI 0.0 to 1.07) per 1000 women of reproductive age globally; 1.60 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.10) per 1000 women aged 15–49 in sub-Saharan Africa and 1.20 per 1000 women aged 15–49 in South Asia.12 The study pooled the results from 10 community-based studies and three hospital studies. Of the 10 community-based studies, 6 studies had estimated a prevalence level of 0%. All these studies were conducted in the Arab States region and relatively economically advantaged countries (Iran, Turkey, Jordan, Lebanon, Oman and Egypt). The study estimates that just over one million women may have an obstetric fistula in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

The Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) began to collect data on incontinence symptoms in 2005 in selected countries of high risk of obstetric fistula, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa. However, the DHS questionnaire on fistula was never validated for the true diagnosis of obstetric fistula. The reported incontinence rates ranged from a high of about 5% to a low of 0%. There were significant concerns that the tool had low sensitivity and specificity and was, therefore, likely to grossly over-report the prevalence of obstetric fistula. Maheu-Giroux and colleagues re-estimated the prevalence of obstetric fistula in a number of sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries after correcting for misclassification in self-reported symptoms in DHS and Multiple Indicators Cluster Surveys, using priors of a set of ranges in sensitivity and specificity values through a Bayesian analytical method.18 The study estimated a lifetime obstetric fistula prevalence rate of three cases per 1000 women of reproductive age and the current prevalence rate of one case per 1000 women of reproductive age.

There are, however, some concerns that the prevalence rates from DHS are higher than the local population level study suggests, and the rate may reflect the estimate of urinary incontinence symptoms rather than the true fistula prevalence rate.19 Another limitation of the study was that the estimates were limited to women of reproductive age. Many women remain untreated for years and even decades, and several studies suggest the presence of a high number of older women as obstetric fistula patients. Moreover, the estimates are limited to 19 countries only; no information was available for countries where DHS data are not available.

No reliable estimates are currently available on obstetric fistula at the global and country levels. To address the substantial data gaps in obstetric fistula, we estimated its global, regional and national prevalences using empirical sources.

Methods

We have re-examined the utility of the DHS data and exploited the availability of auxiliary information in the survey on the possible cause of fistula, timing of fistula formation in relation to pregnancy or adverse birth outcomes, the lag period between delivery and fistula symptoms development, results of treatment (if any) and current incontinence status to correct self-reporting bias in obstetric fistula prevalence estimation. In addition, we have used the recently available empirical data for correcting misclassification bias in survey instruments on the diagnostic validity of obstetric fistula reporting that used confirmation of cases through clinical examination by expert physicians.20 21 Finally, we used predictive modelling for estimating obstetric fistula in all 55 countries in the world that are considered to have non-negligible levels of obstetric fistula to provide national, regional and global estimates of obstetric fistula following a childbirth. Although vaginal fistula may occur from accidental injury during pelvic surgery or by violence, we focused on obstetric fistula only to reduce misclassification errors. The countries selected were identified and supported by the UNFPA-led global Campaign to End Fistula.

Data

The study uses data from four sources. First, we used 31 DHS data from 22 countries that collected self-reported information on the experience of urinary and/or faecal incontinence from women of reproductive age (https://dhsprogram.com/). Second, we used the WHO/Maternal Mortality Estimation Inter-Agency Group’s (MMEIG’s) 2020 maternal mortality estimation database which estimates global, regional and country-level maternal mortality.22 The database was compiled by the MMEIG. The data on maternal mortality ratio (MMR) were used in the predictive model for estimating obstetric fistula for the countries where DHS data were not available. Third, we used two published reports of validation of the self-reported obstetric fistula symptoms with clinical examinations that used population-level sampling. These two studies were conducted in Bangladesh21 and Ethiopia.20 Bangladesh Maternal Morbidity Validation Survey 2016 was conducted among 65 740 women in two geographical areas where experienced female physicians conducted a clinical examination and dye test with methylene blue to confirm fistula.21 The study in Ethiopia was based on a randomly selected 23 023 women of reproductive age (15–49 years) from 113 villages. The reported suspected cases based on self-reporting of incontinence symptoms were confirmed by a clinical examination and dye test with methylene blue by a clinical member of the research team or a clinician in the health centre. Diagnostic measures of accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV)) from these two studies were used to correct misclassification of self-reporting of obstetric fistula symptoms. A few other studies in Nepal,23 Rwanda,24 Pakistan25 and Nigeria26 also examined the validation of self-reporting with a clinical examination. However, these studies were based on a case-control design or small sample sizes or the patients were identified through outreach efforts, and they were not suitable for population-level diagnostic accuracy estimates. Fourth, we used data from the female population size aged 15–64 years from the United Nations World Population Prospects database, 2020 (the corresponding year of the maternal mortality estimates used for the analysis (https://population.un.org/wpp/).

Statistical analysis method

We estimated the current prevalence of obstetric fistula in five steps. First, we examined the self-reported positive response to urinary and/or faecal incontinence symptoms as reported by the respondents in the DHS. Second, we attempted to correct misclassification in self-reporting from other auxiliary information available in the DHS. We consider valid obstetric fistula cases to be those who reported the symptoms to start after a child delivery or difficult labour or after a stillbirth. An obstetric fistula usually starts within 10 days of delivery (after hypoxic necrotic tissue starts sloughing). In a few countries, an additional question was added on the timing of fistula development after the delivery. Any report that suggested fistula developed after 30 days was considered a misclassification; we conservatively used 30 days as the cut-off period, considering the low literacy of the study respondents and concerns of recall bias. Third, for the estimation of the current prevalence of obstetric fistula, we considered the women who reported that they had received a (surgical) treatment and their condition had been cured as non-cases. Fourth, we used a predictive model to estimate the obstetric fistula prevalence rate, primarily because several countries have no data available on obstetric fistula.

For setting the predictive model, we hypothesise that the prevalence of fistula rate in a country is proportional to the level of MMR, skilled attendance at birth (SAB) rate, general fertility rate (GFR) and CS rate:

MMR reflects the country’s mortality risk per birth, and GFR reflects each woman’s exposure to such risks in a calendar period. Higher obstetric fistula is expected in countries where MMR and GFR are high. In contrast, where SAB and CS rates are high, at least theoretically, obstetric fistula rates are expected to be low. However, fitting such a model empirically is challenging. The MMRs for LMICs are model-based estimates from GFR and SAB (including per-capita gross domestic product to capture developmental status) and are likely to be highly correlated. Despite the WHO guidance that CS levels of about 15% are not associated with further reductions in maternal mortality, the exact relationship between the level of CS and maternal morbidity is not known. Moreover, in countries with low surgical competency, CS may increase the risk of iatrogenic fistula and it is incredibly challenging to differentiate the cause due to iatrogenic or obstructed labour among women receiving CS from survey data and/or self-report alone. Another challenge is the very low sample size of the countries with data on obstetric fistula that may not allow them to fit multiple covariates in a regression-based prediction model. Because MMR captures overall risks of mortality from maternal causes and is estimated from the level of GFR and SAB, we used the MMR variable in the prediction model, including regional classification. As a part of sensitivity analysis, we also included SAB in the model but found the model fit deteriorated based on Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion statistics. Because obstetric fistula rates are expected to vary across geographical regions, we used the UN Population subregion classification of the countries as a controlling variable in the prediction model. We used a fractional logistic model to predict the (expected) proportion of obstetric fistula in each country. This model specification prevents predicting negative values, which may occur with a linear regression model, specifically with very low prevalence level data. Because we used the data from the WHO/MMEIG 2020 maternal mortality estimates, the results in this analysis represent the obstetric fistula estimates for 2020. All DHS that included the fistula module were conducted prior to the publication of the MMEIG’s 2020 MMR estimates. The results are based on the predicted estimates from the model, which allowed us to present the estimates for countries with and without DHS data.

Finally, we applied a statistical method to correct the misclassification of the obstetric fistula survey instrument. A number of studies have suggested that self-reporting of obstetric fistula overestimates the obstetric fistula prevalence rate. A large study of 23 023 women in Ethiopia found 35 women reported fistula symptoms, but a clinical examination confirmed only 13 women as true obstetric fistula cases (37.1%).20 Another study of 17 893 women in Nepal found only two confirmed cases of fistula among 55 symptomatic patients.23 A study of 65 740 women in Bangladesh found only 19 confirmed cases among 57 (33.3%) symptomatic patients.21 None of these studies found false negative cases.

Women develop frank incontinence in the case of obstetric fistula, and it is unlikely not to report incontinence if present. Conservatively, however, we considered NPV=0.99999, as a few ostracised women with obstetric fistula may be excluded in the household surveys and not counted. This assumption is likely to slightly increase the prevalence estimation.

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

We present the corrected prevalence rates per 100 000 women, as these estimates are reasonably low and easier to express in integer numbers. India, the second largest populous country in the world with 1.3 billion population, has an obstetric fistula prevalence rate of 33 per 100 000 women. Although the country has a much lower obstetric fistula prevalence rate compared with the global rate of 50 per 100 000 women, India represents the highest burden of obstetric fistula cases globally because of its enormous population size: 121 720 (81 346 to 183 695) women of reproductive age and 151 402 (101 004 to 228 087) women aged 15–64. Afghanistan has the highest obstetric fistula prevalence rates in the world (145 per 100 000 women). It is estimated that there are about 13 360 women aged 15–49 and 15 110 women aged 15–64 suffering from obstetric fistula in Afghanistan. Pakistan has the third highest level of obstetric fistula cases globally: 20 635 (17 730 to 24 030) women aged 15–49 and 24 399 (20 964 to 28 414) women aged 15–64.

In sub-Saharan Africa, West and Central African countries have the highest prevalence rates (81 per 100 000 women) with an estimated prevalence of 178 769. Discussion

A critical need for reliable data on the regional and global burden of obstetric fistula led us to use a novel methodology using the best available national-level empirical data to date. We believe that this study represents the optimal utilisation of self-reported national data on obstetric fistula and statistical methodology to provide acceptable national, regional and global estimates. The estimated numbers are substantially lower than the previously cited numbers of 2 million, which were first suggested in the early 1990s, and are still frequently cited.

We recognise several limitations of our study. In the absence of validated data on obstetric fistula prevalence at the national level, we used self-reported data, which are subject to gross misclassification. We have used the validation data that employed confirmation of cases through clinical examination by expert physicians or clinical professionals to correct any misclassification bias in survey instruments on the diagnostic validity of obstetric fistula. The DHS began to collect data on incontinence symptoms in 2005. There are two major challenges to using the DHS data for reliably estimating and tracking changes in obstetric fistula prevalence. The DHS questionnaires were not consistent across all the survey rounds and countries. They have often been modified over the DHS cycles and were never validated for checking self-reported responses to correct clinical diagnosis. Such information is critical for true estimation of the obstetric fistula prevalence and correcting misclassification in reporting. Maheu-Giroux and colleagues have used the DHS data for estimating obstetric fistula in 19 SSA countries, which used theoretical sensitivity and specificity levels for correcting the prevalence level.18 We rather used empirical data from two large validation studies from two countries, Bangladesh and Ethiopia, to correct the self-reported DHS data and used auxiliary data on obstetric contexts of the index pregnancy for addressing reporting biases. We recognise the limitations of this approach. The two countries may not represent the two distinct geographical regions. Nevertheless, we consider that our model-based results are comparable to many published results. The study in rural Ethiopia, based on data collected in 2014, estimated a prevalence rate of 60 (95% CI 30 to 100) per 100 000 women, and the current study for Ethiopia estimates 67 (95% CI 44 to 103) per 100 000 women. Bangladesh Mortality and Health Care Survey 2016 estimated a prevalence rate of 37 per 1 000 000 women; the current study estimated a prevalence rate of 35 (95% CI 26 to 47) per 100 000 women. We tried three different methods of estimation to check the robustness of the results under different assumptions, and the results from these methods are comparable. Another problem of our study was that due to the small sample size of countries for which DHS data were available on obstetric fistula, we used only two covariates in the predictive models. The small sample size and large variability have likely contributed to wide uncertainty in our estimates. We used the WHO/MMEIG estimated MMR to predict obstetric fistula for the countries. However, MMR estimates are also predicted from model-based methods and are subject to large uncertainty. Our results are affected by the accuracy level of MMR estimates. It is likely that the uncertainty estimates of obstetric fistula shown in this study are in the low range. The DHS implemented the fistula module questions predominantly in SSA countries, with only two in Southeast Asia and one in North Africa. Disproportionate representations of the countries in the dataset may also affect our estimates. Moreover, this study focused on obstetric fistula estimation and did not capture urogenital fistulas unrelated to childbirth.

Most studies on obstetric fistula prevalence are based on self-reported symptoms of urinary and faecal incontinence. Our results may seem lower than expected. Because these symptoms, especially urinary incontinence, may occur for other reasons, including postpartum incontinence, weakened pelvic floor muscles and prolapse, self-reported symptoms may be misclassified as obstetric fistula. Although not generalisable, situational analysis in southern Sudan using the key informant method with clinical examination revealed lower estimates with the prevalence of 30/100 000 women aged 15–49, validating the use of the key informant method and the benefit of clinical assessment.30 Indeed, in studies where a clinical examination was carried out, the frequency of obstetric fistula was substantially lower than from self-report estimates; in a Pakistan study, only 4.3% were confirmed obstetric fistula cases among the women who reported urinary incontinence.25

In a systematic review of obstetric fistula in low-resource countries published between 1995 and 2014, Cowgill et al also noted the wide variability of the incidence and prevalence estimates of obstetric fistula in South Asia and Africa, respectively, from 0 to 4.09 obstetric fistula cases/1000 deliveries and 0–81 cases/1000 deliveries.17 As the published estimates are unreliable and unsuitable for a useful meta-analysis, Tuncalp et al suggested a novel approach involving community outreach followed by physical examinations by nurse-midwives at lower-level facilities closer to where the women live.26 The authors reported a PPV of 47% and a 98% NPV of self-report of fistula-like symptoms based on a DHS fistula questionnaire module. However, the authors opined that these estimates of positive and negative predictive value do not represent the validity of the DHS fistula module questions, as the sample selection was based on self-selected screened patients and not a population sample of women of reproductive age. Using self-reports of constant urine leakage with clinical assessment for confirmation, Mocumbi et al report an incidence of 1.1/1000 recently pregnant women in a population-based study in a high-density health facility area in southern Mozambique.31 Except for the Bangladesh study, reliable national-level obstetric fistula estimation is currently not available. This study attempts to address the data gaps.

Our study suggests that it is likely that the prevalence of obstetric fistula has substantially declined from the earlier prevalence level. The frequently cited figure of 2 million obstetric fistula cases emerged in the early 1990s. Maternal mortality declined by about 45% between 1990 and 2020 and CS has increased from 7% in 1990 to 21% by 2018 globally.32 Studies also suggest that the maternal deaths due to obstructed labour, the primary cause of obstetric fistula, have reduced from 8% in the 1990s33 to 1.9% (80% UI 1.4 to 2.9)34 by 2009–2020. Nevertheless, many may find it surprising that fewer than 500 000 cases of obstetric fistula are prevalent globally. Consider the case of India, where obstetric fistula prevalence is estimated to be the highest in the world, MMR has declined about 81.6% (560–103 between 1990 and 2020) and CS increased substantially from 2.5% in the 1990s to 8.5% in 2005–2006 to 21.5% from 2019 to 2021. Skilled birth attendance increased from 31.5% to ~90% between 1990 and 2019–2020. Our analysis suggests India has the highest estimated number of obstetric fistula cases (~150 000, almost one-third of global obstetric fistula estimates). So, many may consider the present analysis overestimating India’s obstetric fistula prevalence level. Overall, we consider that our estimates of less than 500 000 cases globally seem reasonable and may be used by country officials, planners and developmental partners as a guide for planning and developing strategies to eliminate obstetric fistula.

A large number of women received surgical treatment for obstetric fistula repair through the UNFPA-led global Campaign to End Fistula and international development and humanitarian agencies, like USAID, FIGO and Fistula Foundation, during the last two decades. However, there is still an unmet need for treatment in many countries. More women now have access to emergency obstetric and neonatal care, including CS. However, there are concerns that an increasing trend in CS rates in low-resource settings with low surgical capacity may also increase the incidence of iatrogenic fistula. A retrospective review in nine sub-Saharan countries found that 26.8% of women who developed fistula after a CS were caused by surgery rather than pressure necrosis from obstructed labour,35 and a number of individual country studies in sub-Saharan Africa have similar findings.36 37 However, in South Asia, some studies have implicated other surgeries, notably hysterectomy, in contributing to a significant proportion of iatrogenic fistula.38 39 It is critical to address this challenge to end fistula by 2030. This aetiological shift from ‘poor access’ to ‘poor obstetrical and gynaecological care’ will require vigilance, accountability and regionally specific interventions directed at healthcare facilities and systems that address optimal training and equipment needs for conducting CSs and pelvic surgery.

Conclusion

Although the number of obstetric fistula cases is less than previously anticipated, the current prevalence of about 500 000 cases still poses a huge challenge to ensure surgical access to these women during their lifetime at the current rate of repair. Obstetric fistula is preventable and treatable: these estimates should guide the development and application of much-needed preventive and curative programmes for the eradication of this unacceptable condition.40 The global community must unite to eliminate this devastating and tragic condition by ensuring that all women have access to timely and high-quality emergency obstetric and safe surgical care through strengthened health systems. This effort must also address the social determinants that impact the well-being of women and girls, foster meaningful community engagement and build robust partnerships. Through evidence-based, country-driven, sustainable, quality maternal/newborn healthcare—including in humanitarian settings—it is possible to achieve the promise of eliminating obstetric fistula within a decade.
