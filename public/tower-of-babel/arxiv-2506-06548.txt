Evolution of a twisted electron wave packet perturbed by an inhomogeneous electric field
A. Kudlis, I. A. Aleksandrov, N. N. Rosanov

Abstract
Laguerre-Gaussian (LG) wave packets, known for their vortex structure and nonzero orbital angular momentum (OAM), are of great interest in various scientific fields. Here we study the nonrelativistic dynamics of a spatially-localized electron LG wave packet interacting with an inhomogeneous external electric field that violates the axial symmetry of the initial wave function. We focus on the analysis of the electron density and demonstrate how it is affected by the external field. Within the first order of perturbation theory, we calculate the electron wave function and reveal that the electric field may significantly alter the wave packet’s structure and distort its qualitative form. We demonstrate that due to the interaction with the external field, the degenerate zeros of the initial wave function located on the z z axis split into multiple nondegenerate nodes in the transverse plane representing separate single-charge vortices. This mechanism resembles the analogous effects known in topological optics. These findings provide new insights into controlling and manipulating twisted matter beams and into their possible instabilities.



I
Introduction

Singular optics and its generalization, topological optics
Soskin and Vasnetsov 2001
;
Berry and Dennis 2001
;
Freund 2002
;
Ruchi et al. 2020
;
Simon 2021
, which involve the analysis of properties and transformations of sets of points where various characteristics of optical radiation cannot be defined, have found numerous applications in the formation of optical traps, manipulation of microparticle positions, laser beam transport, and encoding of information carried by such beams
Gahagan and Schwartzlander 1996
;
Friese et al. 1998
;
O’Neil and Padgett 2000
;
Ruchi and Senthilkumaran 2020
;
Angelsky et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
. An important example of such singularity is optical vortices. At their center, the intensity of radiation falls to zero and, accordingly, the phase is undefined. Moreover, upon encircling the center along a closed contour in the plane orthogonal to the main propagation direction of the beam, the wave phase changes by
\pm 2\pi m
, where
\pm m
(
m=0,1,2,\dots
) is the integer
topological charge
.

A common example of a vortex beam is the optical Laguerre-Gaussian (LG) beams characteristic of laser radiation
Siegman 1986
. It is well known that in a homogeneous linear medium, singularities with higher topological charges of these and other vortex optical beams split into singularities with unit charges (
m=1
) under perturbations
Baranova and Zeldovich 1981
. The same splitting occurs in a homogeneous medium with Kerr nonlinearity
Kruglov et al. 1992
. At the same time, in a homogeneous medium with dissipative nonlinearity, vortex beams with higher charges are stable
Fedorov et al. 2003
.

Deep analogies between optics and quantum mechanics allow many concepts and conclusions to be effectively transferred between these branches of physics. In the present investigation, we examine the stability and dynamics of a vortex (twisted)
electron
wave packet interacting with an inhomogeneous electric field. Our goal is to explore how external perturbations affect the vortex structure of the initial LG wave packet. In particular, we will see whether the singularities of the electron wave function split due to the interaction, i.e., we will compare the electron dynamics with the above mentioned mechanisms revealed in optical setups.

The study of LG optical beams, characterized by their unique vortex structure and ability to carry a nonzero orbital angular momentum (OAM), has garnered significant interest in quantum optics, accelerator physics, and other fields of study
Allen et al. 1992
;
Beijersbergen et al. 1993
;
Sasaki and McNulty 2008
;
Verbeeck et al. 2010
;
Jentschura and Serbo 2011
;
Katoh et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Karlovets 2021
;
Ivanov 2022
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Bégin et al. 2025
. These modes offer a rich framework for exploring light-matter interactions and potential applications in optical tweezers, quantum information processing, and high-resolution microscopy
Torres and Torner 2011
;
Padgett and Bowman 2011
(see also reviews
Yao and Padgett 2011
;
Knyazev and Serbo 2018
;
Erhard et al. 2018
;
Shen et al. 2019
, where various fundamental aspects of OAM in light are discussed).

Although the field initially focused on the study of photons, it soon became evident that twisted states of massive particles could be generated as well. It was found that not only photons but also particles like electrons could carry OAM
Leary et al. 2008
;
Bliokh et al. 2011
;
Bliokh et al. 2017
;
Lloyd et al. 2017
). A notable aspect of twisted (vortex) electrons for practical applications is that, in contrast to photons, they have a magnetic moment directly proportional to their OAM, which can experimentally reach values in the hundreds of
\hbar

Mafakheri et al. 2017
.
Subsequent investigations have shown that vortex electrons are sensitive to various external fields
Silenko et al. 2017
;
Aleksandrov et al. 2022
;
Ababekri et al. 2024
;
Sizykh et al. 2024
(see also Refs.
Ivanov 2011
;
Maruyama et al. 2019
;
Neha et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2025
for the discussion of various setups involving twisted particles).

Despite the advances in generating and manipulating twisted electron states, a gap remains in understanding how external perturbations that violate the cylindrical symmetry affect the stability of such beams. In this paper, we analyze an electron LG wave packet with nonzero OAM that interacts with a static inhomogeneous electric field. As a specific examples, we consider a fully-localized external field described by a delta-function and an electric field oriented along the transverse
x
axis. These configurations intentionally break the rotational symmetry about the initial beam axis
z
and thus provide a simple models for examining how external perturbations affect the stability of vortex electrons. More specifically, we investigate the time evolution of the electron density, focusing on cross-sectional profiles of the wave packet. By employing a perturbative approach, we derive the first-order corrections to the wave function and evaluate how the probability density profile deforms under the influence of the external potential. Our results uncover notable OAM mixing and splitting of the central minimum in the electron density distribution, illustrating the importance of small external perturbations. These findings advance our fundamental understanding of structured beams and set the stage for future experimental and theoretical studies aimed at controlling and exploiting vortex electrons in real applications.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce the basic setup and describe the system under consideration. In Sec.
III
, we outline the theoretical method and develop a perturbative framework used to analyze the interaction of a twisted wave packet with an external electric field. Next, in Sec.
IV
, we present and discuss our numerical results, highlighting the key effects of the inhomogeneous electric field. Finally, in Sec.
V
, we provide a conclusion.

II
Setup

We assume that the initial nonrelativistic twisted wave packet at
t=0
is described by the following wave function in coordinate space:

\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{\pi^{3/4}\sqrt{l!}}\,(\sigma\rho)^{l}\mathrm{e}^{i\bar{p}z/\hbar}\,\mathrm{e}^{il\varphi}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\,(z^{2}+\rho^{2})\bigg],

(1)

where
\rho
and
\varphi
are the polar coordinates in the
xy
plane. Here
\sigma
is the characteristic width of the wave packet in momentum space,
\bar{p}
is the central momentum along the
z
axis, and
l
is the
z
projection of the electron OAM in units of
\hbar
(we assume
l>0
). The wave function (
1
) is localized in all of the three spatial directions and has a unit
L^{2}
norm. A more general form of a Laguerre-Gaussian wave packet can involve associated Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{|l|}

Karlovets 2021
, but here we will focus on
n=0
and positive
l
.

Consider the interaction of the electron wave packet with an inhomogeneous external electric field described by a static scalar potential
\phi(\mathbf{r})
. The full time‐dependent Schrödinger equation reads

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})=\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta+e\phi(\mathbf{r})\right]\Psi(t,\mathbf{r}),

(2)

where
m
and
e
are the electron mass and charge, respectively. The initial condition is given by
\Psi(0,\mathbf{r})=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{r})
. Since we are interested in investigating rather weak perturbations, our main theoretical tool will be perturbation theory with respect to the external potential
\phi(\mathbf{r})
. Let us rewrite the Schrödinger equation in the following form:

\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta\right)\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})=e\phi(\mathbf{r})\Psi(t,\mathbf{r}).

(3)

To solve this equation by means of perturbation theory, let us introduce the Green’s function,

\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta\right)G(t,\mathbf{r})=i\hbar\delta(t)\delta(\mathbf{r}).

(4)

This Green’s function is known in an explicit form,

G(t,\mathbf{r})=\theta(t)\bigg(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar t}\bigg)^{3/2}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg(\frac{im\mathbf{r}^{2}}{2\hbar t}\bigg).

(5)

The first-order correction can then be calculated via

\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!dt^{\prime}\int\!d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,G(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})e\phi(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\Psi^{(0)}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(6)

Next, let us specify the external electric field that provides a perturbation of the electron wave packet. We will consider two different setups. First, we will discuss a fully-localized external field in the following very simple form:

\phi(\mathbf{r})=\lambda\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}).

(7)

This model describes a static external field localized at the position
\mathbf{r}_{0}
.

Second, we will consider an external electric field directed along the
x
axis, so that it violates the axial symmetry of the initial state. It turns out that a homogeneous time-dependent external field
\mathbf{E}=E(t)\mathbf{e}_{x}
does not affect the structure of the electron state and only provide an acceleration of the whole wave packet (we prove this fact in the Appendix). In order to analyze a nontrivial space-dependent perturbation, we assume that the external field has a static localized profile in the transverse
x
direction and is described by the following scalar potential:

\phi(\mathbf{r})=\phi(x)=-E_{0}df\bigg(\frac{x-a}{d}\bigg),

(8)

where
E_{0}
is the electric field strength,
a
is its central position, and
d
is its spatial width. The dimensionless function
f(\xi)
obeys
f(\xi<-1)=-1
and
f(\xi>1)=1
. It can be represented as

f(\xi)=2\theta(\xi)-1+g(\xi),

(9)

where
\theta(\xi)
is a Heaviside step function and
g(\xi)
vanishes for
|\xi|>1
. In what follows, the representation (
9
) will be particularly useful within our analytical treatment of the problem in momentum space.

The field configuration (
8
) is considered as a toy model as it is sufficiently simple to perform analytical and numerical calculations. However, an
x
-dependent potential can be implemented, for example, with a pair of parallel plate electrodes whose length greatly exceeds the transverse size of the electron beam and whose separation varies slowly along
z
. By shaping the electrode edges (using razor-blade masks or lithographically patterned conductors) one prescribes the voltage drop
V(x)
and hence the static field
E_{x}(x)=-dV/dx
. Because the plates extend uniformly along
y
and parallel to the beam axis
z
, the resulting field is effectively homogeneous in those directions within the interaction region, matching the one-dimensional profile adopted in Eq. (
8
). The essential feature of this setup is the fact that it violates the axial symmetry of the initial state (
1
). The effects of this violation are our main focus.

In what follows, we will analyze the evolution of the twisted wave packet taking into account the leading-order contribution of the interaction term
e\phi(\mathbf{r})
. We will be primarily interested in how the structure of the electron wave function is modified due to the external perturbation.

III
Theoretical description

Here we will outline our theoretical approach based on the perturbative expansion with respect to the external electric field and perform necessary calculations for the two specific setups described above.

III.1
Zeroth-order solution

If one disregards the external potential
\phi(\mathbf{r})
, then the Schrödinger equation with the initial condition (
1
) can be solved analytically. This can be done, for instance, in the momentum representation, where the initial wave function is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\!d\mathbf{r}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{p}\mathbf{r}}\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{2^{3/2}\pi^{3/4}}{i^{l}\sqrt{l!}}\frac{1}{\sigma^{3/2}}\left(\frac{p_{\perp}}{\sigma\hbar}\right)^{l}\mathrm{e}^{il\varphi_{p}}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{(p_{z}-\bar{p})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\hbar^{2}}-\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\hbar^{2}}\bigg].

(10)

Here the polar radius and angle of the momentum vector are denoted by
p_{\perp}
and
\varphi_{p}
, respectively. The wave function
\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})
can be represented in the following form:

\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})=\int\!\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{p}\mathbf{r}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\,\Phi(t,\mathbf{p}).

(11)

The zeroth-order contribution to the wave function
\Phi(t,\mathbf{p})
in the momentum representation is stationary and reads

\Phi^{(0)}(t,\mathbf{p})=\Phi^{(0)}(0,\mathbf{p})=\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{p}).

(12)

This obviously describes the ordinary spreading of the wave packet in coordinate space:

\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}(t,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{3/4}\sqrt{l!}}\,\frac{\rho^{l}}{[\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{l+3/2}}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg(-\frac{i\bar{p}^{2}t}{2m\hbar}\bigg)\mathrm{e}^{i\bar{p}z/\hbar}\,\mathrm{e}^{il\varphi}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg\{\!-i(l+3/2)\arctan(t/t_\text{d})

\displaystyle-\frac{1-it/t_{\text{d}}}{2[\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{2}}\big[\rho^{2}+(z-\bar{p}t/m)^{2}\big]\bigg\},

(13)

where
t_{\text{d}}=m/(\sigma^{2}\hbar)
and
\sigma_{\perp}(t)=(1/\sigma)\sqrt{1+t^{2}/t_{\text{d}}^{2}}
.

III.2
Fully-localized external field

In the simplest case involving the external field in the form (
7
), the calculations can be performed immediately. According to Eq. (
6
), the first-order correction can be calculated via

\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})=-\frac{ie\lambda}{\hbar}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!dt^{\prime}\,G(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0})\Psi^{(0)}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{0}).

(14)

By taking into account Eq. (
13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})=

\displaystyle-\frac{ie\lambda/\hbar}{\pi^{3/4}\sqrt{l!}}\,\bigg(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar}\bigg)^{3/2}\rho_{0}^{l}\,\mathrm{e}^{i\bar{p}z_{0}/\hbar}\,\mathrm{e}^{il\varphi_{0}}

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\frac{im(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0})^{2}}{2\hbar(t-t^{\prime})}-\frac{i\bar{p}^{2}t^{\prime}}{2m\hbar}\bigg]

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg\{\!-i(l+3/2)\arctan(t'/t_\text{d})

\displaystyle-\frac{1-it^{\prime}/t_{\text{d}}}{2[\sigma_{\perp}(t^{\prime})]^{2}}\big[\rho_{0}^{2}+(z_{0}-\bar{p}t^{\prime}/m)^{2}\big]\bigg\},

(15)

where
\rho_{0}
,
\varphi_{0}
, and
z_{0}
are cylindrical coordinates of
\mathbf{r}_{0}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\varphi_{0}=0
. The azimuthal dependence of this correction is governed by the term
-2\rho\rho_{0}\cos\varphi
in
(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0})^{2}
in the second line of Eq. (
15
). We immediately observe that in the vicinity of the origin
\rho=0
, this term is suppressed, so
\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})
is approximately independent of
\varphi
, i.e., it represents a vortex contribution with a zero OAM. The numerical analysis of the solution (
15
) will be carried out in Sec.
IV
.

III.3
Electric field along the
x
axis

Here we will turn to the discussion of a more involved field configuration given by Eq. (
8
). Although one can directly employ the prescription (
6
), we will proceed in a different way by considering the problem in the momentum representation.

As was already seen in Sec.
III.1
, considering the evolution of the wave packet in momentum space is advantageous due to the very simple form of the unperturbed Hamiltonian in this representation. Let us first define the one-dimensional Fourier transform of the external potential:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}(q)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dx\,\mathrm{e}^{-iqx/\hbar}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=-E_{0}d^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-iqa/\hbar}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\xi\,\mathrm{e}^{-iqd\xi/\hbar}f(\xi).

(16)

The Fourier transform in the second line contains a generalized function arising from
2\theta(\xi)-1
in Eq. (
9
) and an ordinary function that represents the Fourier transform of
g(\xi)
. Namely,

\hat{\phi}(q)=-E_{0}d^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-iqa/\hbar}\bigg[\hat{g}(qd/\hbar)-\frac{2i\hbar}{d}\mathcal{P}\,\frac{1}{q}\bigg].

(17)

Here
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to the principal value integral and

\hat{g}(\eta)\equiv\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\!d\xi\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\eta\xi}g(\xi).

(18)

The function
g(\xi)
can be chosen in different ways and will be specified below.

The full time-dependent wave function
\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})
is represented in the form (
11
). The time-dependent exponential factor is introduced in order to simplify the corresponding equation for
\Phi(t,\mathbf{p})
(this is nothing but a transition to the interaction picture):

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi(t,\mathbf{p})=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\frac{dp_{x}^{\prime}}{2\pi\hbar}\,e\hat{\phi}(p_{x}-p^{\prime}_{x})\\
\times\mathrm{e}^{\frac{it}{2m\hbar}(p_{x}^{2}-p_{x}^{\prime 2})}\,\Phi(t,p^{\prime}_{x},p_{y},p_{z}).

(19)

The zeroth-order contribution (
12
) is explicitly given by Eq. (
10
). The first-order correction to the wave function in momentum space has now the following form:

\Phi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{p})=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\frac{dp_{x}^{\prime}}{2\pi\hbar}\,e\hat{\phi}(p_{x}-p^{\prime}_{x})\\
\times\mathrm{e}^{\frac{it^{\prime}}{2m\hbar}(p_{x}^{2}-p_{x}^{\prime 2})}\,\Phi_{0}(p^{\prime}_{x},p_{y},p_{z}).

(20)

Although the integral over
t^{\prime}
can be evaluated immediately, we will retain this integral. Since we are interested in computing the position-space wave function, let us directly analyze the following first-order contribution:

\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})=\int\!\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{p}\mathbf{r}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\,\Phi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{p}).

(21)

We first substitute Eq. (
20
) into Eq. (
21
), interchange the integration variables
p_{x}
and
p_{x}^{\prime}
, and then introduce dimensionless
\xi=(p_{x}^{\prime}-p_{x})d/\hbar
instead of
p_{x}^{\prime}
:

\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})=-\frac{i}{2\pi\hbar d}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\xi\int\!\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{p}\mathbf{r}}\mathrm{e}^{i\xi x/d}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\xi}{2md}\,(2p_{x}+\hbar\xi/d)(t-t^{\prime})}e\hat{\phi}(\hbar\xi/d)\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{p}).

(22)

Next, we will analytically calculate the three-dimensional integral over
\mathbf{p}
. The integral over
p_{z}
reads

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\frac{dp_{z}}{2\pi\hbar}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{z}z}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{ip_{z}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{(p_{z}-\bar{p})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\hbar^{2}}\bigg]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{\perp}(t)}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\bar{p}z}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\bar{p}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\\
{}\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg\{\!-(i/2)\arctan(t/t_\text{d})-\frac{1-it/t_{\text{d}}}{2[\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{2}}\,(z-\bar{p}t/m)^{2}\bigg\}\equiv\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,Q(t,z),

(23)

It is also possible to directly perform the
p_{x}
integration:

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\frac{dp_{x}}{2\pi\hbar}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{x}x}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{ip_{x}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\xi p_{x}}{md}\,(t-t^{\prime})}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\hbar^{2}}}\,(p_{x}+ip_{y})^{l}=\frac{i^{l}}{2\pi\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta^{2}/(4\alpha)}}{\alpha^{l}}\,F_{l}\bigg(\alpha,p_{y}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\bigg),

(24)

where

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1+it/t_{\text{d}}}{2\sigma^{2}\hbar^{2}},\qquad\beta\equiv\frac{x}{\hbar}-\frac{\xi(t-t^{\prime})}{md},

(25)

\displaystyle F_{l}(\alpha,w)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{[l/2]}(-1)^{n}C_{l}^{2n}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{2^{n}}\,\alpha^{l-n}w^{l-2n}.

(26)

Note that
\beta
here is a function of
\xi
(and also
t
and
x
), and
p_{y}
is involved in the argument of
F_{l}
. Next, let us calculate the following integral with a monomial
[p_{y}+\beta/(2\alpha)]^{k}
:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\frac{dp_{y}}{2\pi\hbar}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{y}y}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{ip_{y}^{2}}{2m\hbar}t}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{p_{y}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\hbar^{2}}}\,\bigg(p_{y}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-y^{2}/(4\alpha\hbar^{2})}}{\alpha^{k}}\,G_{k}\bigg(\alpha,\frac{iy}{2\alpha\hbar}+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\bigg),

(27)

\displaystyle G_{k}(\alpha,u)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{[k/2]}C_{k}^{2n}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{2^{n}}\,\alpha^{k-n}u^{k-2n}.

(28)

By combining our results, we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2\hbar d}\frac{1}{\pi^{7/4}\sqrt{l!}}\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{[\sigma\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{2l+2}}\,\bigg(1-\frac{it}{t_{\text{d}}}\bigg)^{l+1}Q(t,z)\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\!-\frac{y^{2}(1-it/t_{\text{d}})}{2[\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!dt^{\prime}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\xi\,\Xi_{l}(t,t^{\prime},x,y,\xi)e\hat{\phi}(\hbar\xi/d)\mathrm{e}^{i\xi x/d}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i\hbar\xi^{2}(t-t^{\prime})}{2md^{2}}},

(29)

where
Q(t,z)
is defined in Eq. (
23
) and

\displaystyle\Xi_{l}(t,t^{\prime},x,y,\xi)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{l}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta^{2}/(4\alpha)}\sum_{n=0}^{[l/2]}(-1)^{n}C_{l}^{2n}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{2^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{[l/2]-n}C_{l-2n}^{2k}\frac{(2k-1)!!}{2^{k}}(4\hbar^{2}\alpha)^{n+k}(iy+\beta\hbar)^{l-2n-2k}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{l}(iy+\beta\hbar)^{l}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta^{2}/(4\alpha)}.

(30)

Here
\alpha
and
\beta
involve
t
,
t^{\prime}
,
x
and
\xi
and are given by Eqs. (
25
). It turns out that only the term with
n=k=0
contributes, which can be easily shown by fixing

(

) and summing over
n=0
,
1
,
\ldots j
. The latter sum vanishes unless
j=0
. Let us now represent Eq. (
29
) in the following final form:

\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})=-\frac{i}{2\hbar d}\frac{1}{\pi^{7/4}\sqrt{l!}}\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{[\sigma\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{2l+2}}\,\bigg(1-\frac{it}{t_{\text{d}}}\bigg)^{l+1}Q(t,z)\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\!-\frac{\rho^{2}(1-it/t_{\text{d}})}{2[\sigma_{\perp}(t)]^{2}}\bigg]\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\xi\,\mathcal{I}_{l}(t,x,y,\xi)e\hat{\phi}(\hbar\xi/d)\mathrm{e}^{i\xi x/d},

(31)

where

\mathcal{I}_{l}(t,x,y,\xi)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!d\tau\,\mathrm{exp}\bigg[-\frac{\xi^{2}}{4\alpha m^{2}d^{2}}\,\tau^{2}+\frac{\xi}{2md}\bigg(\frac{x}{\alpha\hbar}-\frac{i\hbar\xi}{d}\bigg)\tau\bigg]\bigg(x+iy-\frac{\hbar\xi}{md}\,\tau\bigg)^{l}.

(32)

The integrations over
\xi
and
\tau
in Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
) will be performed numerically. Although the
z
dependence of
\Psi^{(1)}(t,\mathbf{r})
is given by the same profile
Q(t,z)
as that revealed in the zeroth-order wave function (
13
), the first-order correction (
31
) is highly nontrivial as a function of the transverse coordinates
x
and
y
.

Let us now discuss how one can perform the principal-value integration over
\xi
given the explicit form of
\hat{\phi}
in Eq. (
17
). We will choose the potential profile
f(\xi)
in the following form

f(\xi)=\begin{cases}1,&\xi\geqslant 1,\\
\sin\,(\pi\xi/2),&-1<\xi<1,\\
-1,&\xi\leqslant-1.\end{cases}

(33)

In this case,

\hat{g}(\eta)=\frac{2i}{\eta}\frac{4\eta^{2}-\pi^{2}+\pi^{2}\cos{\eta}}{4\eta^{2}-\pi^{2}}.

(34)

This is a smooth odd function which exhibits a
1/\eta
behavior for large
|\eta|
. It vanishes rather slowly since the function
g(\xi)
has discontinuities at
\xi=\pm 1
no matter how smooth the profile
f(\xi)
is. We now obtain

e\hat{\phi}(\hbar\xi/d)=-eE_{0}d^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-ia\xi/d}\bigg[\hat{g}(\xi)-2i\mathcal{P}\,\frac{1}{\xi}\bigg],

(35)

which is to be plugged into Eq. (
31
). To evaluate an integral of the form

I=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\xi\,h(\xi)\bigg[\hat{g}(\xi)-2i\mathcal{P}\,\frac{1}{\xi}\bigg],

(36)

one can use the following simple prescription:

I=2i\pi^{2}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\xi\,[h(\xi)-h(0)]\,\frac{\cos\xi}{\xi(4\xi^{2}-\pi^{2})}.

(37)

This is an ordinary Riemann integral which does not require any regularizations. Moreover, in our setup,
h(\xi)
rapidly vanishes for
|\xi|\to\infty
due to the exponential factor in Eq. (
32
), so the numerical convergence for large
|\xi|
is quite fast.

IV.1.2
Splitting of the density zeros

It is a well-known effect in the context of optical beams with high topological numbers
l
that the initial zero of the local intensity at
x=y=0
with multiplicity
l
can split into several nondegenerate zeros if the beam gains additional corrections with lower topological charges (see, e.g., Refs.
Kruglov et al. 1992
;
Baranova and Zeldovich 1981
;
Soskin and Vasnetsov 2001
;
Rosanov 2023
). In a purely linear and homogeneous medium, the initial singularity splits into multiple single‐charge vortices, typically arranged on a circle whose radius grows along the propagation direction. This phenomenon can be viewed as an
instability
of higher‐order vortices against symmetry‐breaking perturbations: because topological charges greater than unity are effectively superpositions of single‐charge vortices, any perturbation that breaks perfect azimuthal symmetry can induce their spatial separation.

In the case of twisted electrons, the initial state (
1
) contains
\rho^{l}
which indicates the presence of a degenerate zero at the origin of the transverse
xy
plane. In this section, we will demonstrate that, similarly to the optical effect, this zero, in fact, fully splits into separate first‐order zeros due to the interaction with the external symmetry-breaking electric background. Although the physical system here is governed by the nonrelativistic Schrödinger equation rather than the paraxial wave equation of classical optics, the mathematical analogies between the two formalisms lead to closely related outcomes.

In Fig.
3
we present the zero contours of
\mathrm{Re}\,\Psi
and
\mathrm{Im}\,\Psi
for
l=1
,
3
,
5
, and
7
. In the left column we, the unperturbed wave function contains a degenerate singularity at
x=y=0
representing a common intersection point of
2l
contour lines. The localized external perturbation breaks the azimuthal symmetry, so that each high-order vortex splits into
l
first-order vortices that migrate off axis and settle at equal azimuthal angles (right column in Fig.
3
). in Fig.
4

l
. We observe that for any given
l
, the new (nondegenerate) zeros lie on a circle centered at the origin and form a regular polygon with
l
vertices. Note that no residual central zero survives, indicating a full redistribution of the singularities.

Our results demonstrate that the well-known instability of higher-order optical vortices has an evident analog in the context of electron twisted states. Next, we will turn to the analysis of the transverse external field (
8
), which represents a more involved scenario. We will show that the main qualitative patterns remain in this case, whereas the structure of the resulting wave packet turns out to be not that regular.

Overall, these results serve as a bridge between the extensive knowledge of optical vortex splitting in linear media and the emergent field of twisted electron beams. They also suggest that the stability of high‐order electron vortices may be an even subtler issue, as additional factors (e.g., potential gradients and relativistic corrections at higher energies) can further influence or catalyze the splitting process.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated how a twisted electron wave packet with nonzero orbital angular momentum
l
evolves under the influence of a static yet inhomogeneous electric field that breaks the rotational symmetry about the
z
axis. By using a perturbative formalism, we derived the first-order corrections to the electron wave function and found a pronounced sensitivity of the density distribution with respect to the external field, including noticeable modifications of the vortex structure and splitting of the central zero. These findings are in agreement with those for optical beams and highlight the importance of external perturbations in realistic setups, where even small deviations from perfect cylindrical symmetry can induce OAM mixing. Our results thus underscore the need for careful control of ambient fields when manipulating twisted electron beams, and they open avenues for using such beams as probes of field inhomogeneities. Future studies could extend the analysis to more complex field configurations or higher-order Laguerre-Gaussian states to deepen our understanding of structured beams in both fundamental and applied contexts.

Appendix A
Wave packet dynamics in a homogeneous electric field

Here we will demonstrate that the interaction with a spatially homogeneous electric field leads only to a trivial uniform shift of the probability density. We will not invoke here perturbation theory and explicitly show that this result is valid to all orders.

Let us consider a general case where the electric field has an arbitrary temporal dependence:

\phi(t,x)=-E(t)x.

(38)

To get rid of the
x
dependence of the Hamiltonian, we perform a gauge transformation

\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{e}^{-ieA(t)x/\hbar}\,\Psi_{1}(t,\mathbf{r}),

(39)

where the
x
projection of the vector potential is given by

A(t)=-\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!E(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(40)

The Schrödinger equation in terms of the yet-unknown function
\Psi_{1}(t,\mathbf{r})
reads

i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi_{1}(t,\mathbf{r})=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg\{\Delta_{yz}+\Big[\partial_{x}-\frac{ie}{\hbar}A(t)\Big]^{2}\bigg\}\Psi_{1}(t,\mathbf{r}).

(41)

Next, we absorb the
A^{2}
term by means of the following phase factor:

\Psi_{1}(t,\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{exp}\Bigg[-\frac{ie^{2}}{2m\hbar}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!A^{2}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\Bigg]\,\Psi_{2}(t,\mathbf{r}).

(42)

Then the function
\Psi_{2}(t,\mathbf{r})
obeys

i\hbar\partial_{t}\Psi_{2}(t,\mathbf{r})=\bigg[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta+\frac{ie\hbar}{m}A(t)\partial_{x}\bigg]\Psi_{2}(t,\mathbf{r}).

(43)

If, at
t=0
, a classical electron at rest starts to interact with the external field
E(t)
, then by the time instant
t
it travels a distance

s(t)=-\frac{e}{m}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\!A(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(44)

This suggests the following substitution:

\Psi_{2}(t,\mathbf{r})=\Psi_{3}(t,x-s(t),y,z).

(45)

We find now that
\Psi_{3}(t,\mathbf{r})
satisfies a free-particle Schrödinger equation, i.e., it does not involve the external field at all. Furthermore, at the initial time instant
t=0
, the function
\Psi_{3}(0,\mathbf{r})
coincides with
\Psi(0,\mathbf{r})=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{r})
. This means that it is nothing but the unperturbed solution,

\Psi_{3}(t,\mathbf{r})=\Psi^{(0)}(t,\mathbf{r}).

(46)

Finally, in terms of the probability density, we obtain

|\Psi(t,\mathbf{r})|^{2}=|\Psi^{(0)}(t,x-s(t),y,z)|^{2}.

(47)

Obviously, in the initial cylindrical coordinates
\rho
,
\varphi
,
z
, this shift violates the axial symmetry and gives rise to a combination of various projections
l
of the angular momentum, but this does not affect the structure of the wave packet: for any given
t
, it is only necessary to properly shift the origin along the
x
axis to recover the unperturbed probability density. Nevertheless, the effect of an
inhomogeneous
field cannot be described by such a shift as clearly seen in the graphs presented in Sec.
IV
.
