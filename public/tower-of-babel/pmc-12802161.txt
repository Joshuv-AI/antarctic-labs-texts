Effects of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain functional network connectivity and cognitive function in adult patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm
Authors: Fengmei Jiao, Guiting Li, Wen Bian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia were significant complications in patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery. The impact of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain function and cognitive outcomes in this patient population was an area of increasing interest. This study aimed to investigate the effects of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain functional network connectivity and cognitive function in adult patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm.MethodsAdult patients who underwent Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery were included in this retrospective cohort study and were divided into the No Hypercapnia group (PaCO2 = 35–45 mmHg, n = 65) and the Hypercapnia group (PaCO2>45 mmHg, n = 68) based on the occurrence of intraoperative hypercapnia. Various parameters including baseline characteristics, intraoperative parameters, anesthetic doses, postoperative brain functional network connectivity, and cognitive function were compared between the two groups.ResultsBaseline characteristics revealed no significant differences between the two groups. Anesthetic doses did not differ significantly between the groups. Postoperative brain functional network connectivity showed significant differences between the groups, with lower connectivity in the Hypercapnia group. Postoperative Mini-Mental State Examination scores were significantly higher in the Hypercapnia group.ConclusionThe study provides novel insights into the effects of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain functional network connectivity and cognitive function in adult patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Intraoperative hypercapnia, Postoperative brain functional network connectivity, Cognitive function, Adult, Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm

Introduction

Cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia were severe complications, such as hypercapnia, observed in a significant portion of patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Hypercapnia, characterized by elevated levels of carbon dioxide (CO2) in the blood, was a physiological disturbance that has been associated with various effects on cerebral physiology [3]. Studies have reported the potential for hypercapnia to induce cerebral vasodilation, leading to increased cerebral blood flow and altered brain function [4]. Also, hypercapnia can alter the brain functional connectivity network, including decreases in default mode and executive control network, evaluated by MRI [5]. While hypercapnia was traditionally regarded as detrimental to cerebral autoregulation and brain function, the complex interplay between CO2 levels and neurophysiology warrants thorough investigation, particularly in the context of neurosurgical procedures [6]. Nevertheless, the specific influence of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain function and cognitive outcomes in neurosurgical patients remains relatively underexplored.

The impact of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain function and cognitive outcomes has been a subject of increasing interest and clinical relevance [7, 8]. Understanding the potential implications of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative outcomes may provide valuable insights for optimizing perioperative care and improving patient outcomes in neurosurgical settings. As such, our study sought to fill this knowledge gap by investigating the potential correlations between intraoperative hypercapnia and postoperative brain functional network connectivity and cognitive function, contributing to the growing body of literature on perioperative care in neurosurgical settings.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study received approval from the Hospital Review Board and Ethics Committee of Shandong Second Provincial General Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No. 2024-038-01). Informed consent for this retrospective study was waived due to the use of de-identified patient data, posing no potential harm or impact on patient care. This study was conducted as a retrospective cohort study including patients who underwent Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery at our institution between January 2023 and June 2023. The criteria for intraoperative hypercapnia were as follows: normal arterial PaCO2 ranged from 35 to 45 mmHg, and intraoperative PaCO2 > 45 mmHg indicated intraoperative hypercapnia[9]. According to it, patients were divided into No Hypercapnia group (PaCO2 = 35–45 mmHg, n = 65) and the Hypercapnia group (PaCO2 > 45 mmHg, n = 68).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included as follows: patients with intracranial aneurysms confirmed by preoperative CTA or DSA who underwent Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery [10]; patients who showed onset of intracranial aneurysms within < 3 days; patients aged 18–75 years; patients who showed the score of ASA I or II; patients with Hunt-Hess classification ranging from Grade I- III; patients with complete medical records.

Patients were excluded as follows: patients with cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, or pulmonary diseases; patients with malignant tumors; patients who were discharged postoperatively and did not undergo regular follow-up CT scans; patients with concurrent other cerebrovascular diseases; patients with history of long-term sedative or analgesic drug use; patients with the diseases that might affect brain metabolism (such as metabolic syndrome); patients with recurrent intracranial aneurysms; patients with traumatic brain injury, intracranial infection, epilepsy, etc.; patients with neurological and psychiatric disorders such as severe cognitive impairment, epilepsy, hemiplegia, and aphasia prior to admission; patients with hypocapnia during surgery.

Treatment methods

Upon entering the operating room, patients underwent immediate blood pressure, heart rate, electrocardiography, oxygen saturation, bispectral index, and invasive continuous arterial pressure monitoring, with the routine establishment of right upper limb venous access. Before anesthesia induction, intravenous infusion of dexmedetomidine (Yichang Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Drug Approval H20183390, specification 2 ml/200µg) was administered at a dose of 1 µg/kg, completed within 20 min, with a maintenance dose of 0.4 µg/(kg·h) up to 30 min prior to extubation [11]. Anesthesia induction included the administration of sufentanil (Yichang Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Drug Approval H20054171, specification 1 ml/50µg) at 0.5 µg/kg, propofol (Xi’an Libang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Drug Approval H20123318, specification 20mL/0.2 g) at 2 mg/kg, or remimazolam (Yichang Renfu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Drug Approval H20200006, specification: 25 mg) at 0.2 mg/kg. After 3 min of mask-assisted ventilation, an endotracheal tube was inserted, and mechanical ventilation was initiated. Anesthesia maintenance involved intravenous infusion of propofol and sufentanil, with the goal of maintaining the bispectral index between 40 and 60. Patient vital signs were monitored, and medication doses were adjusted as necessary.

Measurement methods

Patient data

General data and intraoperative parameters of patients were retrieved through a systematic review of medical records. General patient data primarily included age, gender, and BMI, while intraoperative parameters encompassed surgical duration, end-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure (ETCO2), mean arterial pressure (MAP), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) levels, and doses of anesthetic agents. PaCO2 was detected immediately after anesthesia catheterization (T1), before aneurysm treatment (T2), immediately after aneurysm treatment (T3), and at the end of surgery (T4), respectively. Intraoperative PaCO2 was defined as the PaCO2 value measured at T4.

Brain functional network connectivity

Patients underwent nuclear magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination using a superconducting magnetic resonance imager (Magnetom Trio 3.0T, SIEMENS, Germany) [12]. MRI scanning was performed 1 week postoperatively. The resting brain structure images and fMRI sequences were obtained through MRI scanning. To identify default mode network connectivity, we overlaid fMRI data onto structural images and aggregated them. Then, artifacts were removed from the aggregated fMRI data, and the fMRI data were separated into independent components. The executive control network was identified as described in a previous study [13, 14]. During data acquisition, the subjects’ heads were fixed with a sponge, and they were instructed to relax, close their eyes, and remain awake. High-resolution transverse T1-FLASH sequences were used to acquire whole-brain structural images, with the following scan parameters: TR 350 ms, TE 2.46 ms, field of view 240 mm × 240 mm, matrix 320 × 256, slice thickness 4 mm, interslice gap 0.4 mm, 30 layers, and a scan parallel to the anterior-posterior commissure. T1-FLAIR data were acquired to exclude any brain parenchymal lesions, with the following scan parameters: TR 9000 ms, TE 93 ms, field of view 220 mm × 220 mm, slice thickness 4 mm, 25 layers, flip angle 130°, and a scan parallel to the anterior-posterior commissure. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance data were acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (GRE-EPI) sequence, with the following scan parameters: TR 2000 ms, TE 30 ms, flip angle 90°, field of view 240 mm × 240 mm, matrix 64 × 64, slice thickness 4 mm, interslice gap 0.4 mm, 250 time points were acquired over 500 s [15].

The imaging data were processed using the DPABI software based on the MATLAB platform. The processing steps included discarding the first 10 time points, time correction, motion correction, the removal of data affected by head motion exceeding 2 mm translation or 2° rotation, realigning all eligible resting-state functional images to the T1 image, spatial normalization, standardization of functional images (voxel size: 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm), 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian smoothing, band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) and regression of nuisance signals, which included brain white matter signal, cerebrospinal fluid signal, and six head motion parameters, to eliminate the influence of physiological and motion-related noise signals, and addressing linear drift. The Gretna software was used for graph theory network analysis to construct the brain functional network and analyze network topological properties. The entire brain was divided into 90 cortical and subcortical regions using an automatic anatomical labeling (AAL) template, with each brain region representing a node in the network. The average time series for each brain region were extracted and subjected to Pearson correlation analysis, resulting in a 90 × 90 correlation matrix. The sparsity of the connection matrix was used to set the threshold, with a range of 0.10–0.34 and a step size of 0.01, to construct the functional connectivity network of the brain [16, 17].

Cognitive function

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to assess the cognitive function of all participants before and after the procedure [18, 19] at the day before surgery and 7 days after surgery, evaluated by attending physicians who have received professional training. The score was completed independently by two researchers and then averaged. An unauthorized version of the Chinese MMSE was used by the study team without permission, however this has now been rectified with PAR. The MMSE is a copyrighted instrument and may not be used or reproduced in whole or in part, in any form or language, or by any means without written permission of PAR(www.parinc.com). Cognitive function encompasses various aspects such as memory, orientation, attention, language, visuospatial abilities, and executive function. The total score was 30 points, with the highest possible scores for memory (9 points), orientation (10 points), attention (3 points), language (3 points), and visual-constructional abilities (5 points). A total score between 27 and 30 points indicated normal cognitive function, while a score of 21–26 points indicated mild cognitive impairment; 10–20 points indicated moderate cognitive impairment; 0–9 points indicated severe cognitive impairment. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was higher than 0.7 [20].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data were presented as [n (%)] format. For sample sizes ≥ 40 and theoretical frequency T ≥ 5, the chi-squared test was applied using the basic formula; for sample sizes ≥ 40 but 1 ≤ T<5, a corrected formula chi-squared test was used. For sample sizes < 40 or theoretical frequency T < 1, statistical analysis was conducted using Fisher’s exact probability method. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, results were presented as mean ± SD and the corrected variance t-test was used. Non-normally distributed data were presented in the form of median (25th percentile, 75th percentile), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences.

Intraoperative parameters

The comparison of intraoperative parameters between the No Hypercapnia group and the Hypercapnia group were presented in Table 2, indicating that the average duration of surgery was similar in both groups (2.65 ± 0.68 vs. 2.72 ± 0.76 h, t = 0.536, P = 0.593). However, statistically significant differences were observed in intraoperative end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2) levels (39.72 ± 2.91 vs. 41.28 ± 4.12 mmHg, t = 2.534, P = 0.013) and intraoperative partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) levels (39.85 ± 3.14 vs. 49.43 ± 5.25 mmHg, t = 14.730, P = 0.000) between the two groups. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were found in intraoperative MAP (95.34 ± 6.52 vs. 97.21 ± 8.33 mmHg, t = 1.447, P = 0.150) and intraoperative partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2) levels (98.56 ± 7.32 vs. 96.78 ± 8.21 mmHg, t = 1.326, P = 0.187). These findings suggest that there were notable differences in intraoperative ETCO2 and PaCO2 levels between the two groups, while other parameters did not exhibit significant disparities.

Postoperative brain functional network connectivity

The comparison of postoperative brain functional network connectivity between the No Hypercapnia group and the Hypercapnia group was presented in Table 4. The analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences in resting-state functional MRI connectivity (0.82 ± 0.07 vs. 0.79 ± 0.09, t = 2.419, P = 0.017), default mode network connectivity (0.85 ± 0.05 vs. 0.82 ± 0.06, t = 3.172, P = 0.002), executive control network connectivity (0.83 ± 0.06 vs. 0.80 ± 0.08, t = 2.838, P = 0.005), salience network connectivity (0.81 ± 0.07 vs. 0.78 ± 0.08, t = 2.060, P = 0.041), and sensorimotor network connectivity (0.82 ± 0.06 vs. 0.79 ± 0.07, t = 2.855, P = 0.005) between the two groups. These results indicate significant differences in postoperative brain functional network connectivity, suggesting potential implications for the impact of intraoperative hypercapnia on brain function in patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm.

MMSE score (cognitive function)

The comparison of MMSE scores (a measure of cognitive function) between the No Hypercapnia group and the Hypercapnia group was presented in Fig. 1. The analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in preoperative MMSE scores (24.06 ± 3.41 vs. 24.17 ± 3.21, t = 0.187, P = 0.852) between the two groups. However, a significant difference was observed in postoperative MMSE scores, with higher scores in the Hypercapnia group (29.86 ± 3.72) compared to the No Hypercapnia group (26.51 ± 3.76), (t = 4.806, P = 0.000). These results suggest that there was no significant difference in preoperative MMSE scores between the two groups, but postoperative MMSE scores were higher in the Hypercapnia group, indicating a potential association between intraoperative hypercapnia and improved postoperative cognitive function in patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm.

Discussion

One of the significant findings from our study was the association between intraoperative hypercapnia and postoperative alterations in brain functional network connectivity. In the present study, patients were divided into a no hypercapnia group and a hypercapnia group based on PaCO₂, the gold standard. Since PaCO₂ could not provide a continuous monitoring of CO2, we subsequently utilized EtCO₂ to monitor CO₂ levels continuously. The results showed hypercapnia group exhibited the higher EtCO2 as compared to no hypercapnia group, suggesting the higher level of CO2 in hypercapnia group. We then compared postoperative functional brain network connectivity between the two groups. Our results indicated a notable decrease in resting-state functional MRI connectivity, default mode network connectivity, executive control network connectivity, salience network connectivity, and sensorimotor network connectivity in patients who experienced intraoperative hypercapnia. These findings suggest that higher intraoperative CO2 levels can cause significant effects on lower postoperative brain functional network connectivity. The impact of hypercapnia on brain function has been a subject of interest in various medical disciplines [21]. Hypercapnia has been shown to induce cerebral vasodilation, leading to increased cerebral blood flow and potentially altering brain function [22]. Hypercapnia may lead to synchronization of certain brain regions that are part of the brain functional connectivity group, thereby resulting in changes in brain functional connectivity [23]. The functional connectivity network of the brain is closely related to cognitive function [24]. Therefore, decreases in resting-state functional MRI connectivity, default mode network connectivity, executive control network connectivity, salience network connectivity, and sensorimotor network connectivity could cause the altered con cognitive function. Our findings emphasized the need for vigilant monitoring and management of CO2 levels during intraoperative care to mitigate potential adverse effects on postoperative brain function [25].

Another significant finding of our study was the potential impact of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative cognitive function according to MMSE. We observed both the no-hypercapnia and hypercapnia group showed increased cognitive function after surgery, but the hypercapnia group had cognitive function within the normal range, while the No hypercapnia group had slightly lower cognitive function compared to the normal level, indicating that hypercapnia might improve the cognitive function of patients. This unexpected finding prompts further exploration into the underlying mechanisms and implications for patient care and outcomes. The relationship between CO2 levels and cognitive function was multifaceted, with both hypercapnia and hypocapnia potentially influencing cognitive performance [26]. While traditionally viewed as detrimental to cognitive function, the effects of hypercapnia on brain metabolism and neuronal activity have been a subject of ongoing research [27–29]. Studies have reported the potential cognitive-enhancing effects of hypercapnia, particularly in the context of enhancing cerebral perfusion and oxygen delivery to the brain [30]. High levels of CO2 might cause vasodilation, thereby increasing cerebral vascular reactivity; in addition, high level of CO2 also led to BOLD activation that could promoted cognitive function [31, 32]. Our findings contribute to this discourse by suggesting a potential association between intraoperative hypercapnia and improved postoperative cognitive function, challenging conventional assumptions and warranting further investigation into the complex interplay between CO2 levels and cognitive outcomes in neurosurgical patients.

The implications of our findings for clinical practice and perioperative care were significant. Effective perioperative management of CO2 levels, careful monitoring, and prompt intervention to maintain appropriate CO2 levels may contribute to optimizing postoperative brain function and cognitive outcomes. By recognizing the potential impact of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain connectivity and cognitive function, anesthesiologists and surgical teams can tailor their care strategies to minimize the occurrence and mitigate the effects of hypercapnia, ultimately promoting better patient outcomes.

While our study contributes valuable insights into the potential impact of intraoperative hypercapnia on postoperative brain function and cognitive outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study and a limited follow-up duration are the limitation of the study. Next studies with long follow-up duration is need to be performed. Although some inclusion and exclusion criteria were used to exclude the impact of factors except hyperuricemia on the cognitive function in this work, the generalizability of our findings may be influenced by the specific patient population and surgical procedures included in the study. Thus, it is necessary to perform a prospective study to validate our findings. Also, the inclusion and exclusion criteria in our study limited the scope of the study to adult patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery, and the findings may not directly translate to other surgical populations or procedures. Future studies incorporating diverse patient populations and surgical interventions would further enhance the applicability of the findings to broader clinical settings. The lack of a more comprehensive cognitive battery beyond the MMSE is a limitation that needs to be addressed in future studies. CO2 management during surgery is only a short phase of subarachnoid hemorrhage treatment. However, CO2 measurement at the following time after surgery is not included in this study, which is a limitation of this research. Also, interventional embolization or pipeline is more and more used in ruptured aneurysm treatment, and however, this work included only aneurysms with clipping, which is the limitation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides novel the insight that intraoperative hypercapnia may be associated with postoperative cognitive function in adult patients undergoing Clipping Intracranial Aneurysm surgery. The observed associations highlight the intricate relationship between CO2 levels, brain function, and cognitive outcomes, underscoring the importance of vigilant intraoperative CO2 management. These findings offer valuable contributions to the ongoing discourse on perioperative care and patient outcomes, calling for continued investigation and tailored perioperative strategies to optimize patient care and postoperative neurocognitive function.
