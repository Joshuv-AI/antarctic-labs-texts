Tau T205 phosphorylation modulates engram cell recruitment and remote memory in mice
Authors: Renée Kosonen, Kristie Stefanoska, Yijun Lin, Samantha Edwards, Emmanuel Prikas, Josefine Bertz, Anne Poljak, Lars M Ittner, Arne Ittner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMemory loss is linked to failure of the engram, the physical substrate associated with a specific memory. Tau is a microtubule associated protein best known for its central role in neurodegenerative dementias, yet its physiological function in memory formation remains poorly understood. In this work, we show that tau is crucial for forming long lasting memories in mice. Site-specific tau phosphorylation at threonine-205 (T205) is induced by memory encoding and is required for remote memory formation. Tau T205 phosphorylation regulates engram cell, physical unit of memory storage, recruitment and constrains extraneous local cell activation, thereby promoting efficient memory recall. Tau selectively links sensory cue presentations to the encoding ensemble, as optogenetic retrieval of remote memory is tau-independent. Pathogenic tau within active cell ensembles induces aberrant local activation and causes anterograde or retrograde amnesia when present during an encoding or recall time window, respectively. Together, these findings identify a direct physiological role for tau in engram ensemble regulation and establish a mechanistic link between tau dysfunction and memory loss.Subject terms: Long-term memory, Molecular neuroscience, Alzheimer's disease

Introduction

The microtubule-associated neuronal protein tau, expressed from the Mapt gene, is implicated in several neurological disorders, termed tauopathies1–3. Progressive loss of memory in primary and secondary tauopathies, such as frontotemporal dementia4,5 and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)2,6, strongly correlates with accumulation of intracellular aggregates containing abnormally phosphorylated forms of tau1,4,7–9. Memory loss in tauopathies involves anterograde and retrograde amnesia, including loss of recent (hours/days) and remote memories (weeks/years)5,6,10–12. Despite tau’s association with memory loss in human disease context, a direct role of tau in physiologic memory processes remains elusive.

Mouse models with neuronal transgenes that express tau with dominant pathogenic mutations from primary tauopathies present with mild progressive impairment in memory paradigms13–16. Mouse models of tau deficiency suggest that tau is not required for memory formation and recall, as adult tau knockout mice showed no or minimal changes in memory across several forms of learning17–26. Endogenous tau regulates neuronal activity, in the hippocampus and cortex, a central region for memory formation and storage, yet this was addressed in models of hyperexcitation, amyloid toxicity and seizures17,19,24,27–29, and it remains to be resolved how this potentially relates to functions in memory. Tau knockout models, however, can reveal non-redundant functions of tau in the context of learning and memory, as tau reduction prevents cognitive deficits in mouse models of AD17,19,20, autism30, and epilepsy29, showing that unique functions of tau modulate memory performance in these models.

Tau is a phosphoprotein subject to extensive regulation through phosphorylation of serine and threonine residues, with dynamic phosphorylation occurring under physiological conditions in the adult brain1,4. While excessive, multi-site phosphorylation defines pathological tau states, increasing evidence indicates that phosphorylation of specific residues modulates tau function in a context-dependent manner at the molecular and subcellular level1,31. Such site-specific modifications have been implicated in regulating tau localization, protein interactions, and responses to neuronal activity, extending beyond structural roles and suggesting broader functional relevance3. Accordingly, individual tau phosphorylation sites can influence memory performance in mouse models, indicating that tau’s phosphorylation status may participate in cognitive processes23,24. Whether physiologically occurring tau phosphorylation events are engaged during learning and contribute directly to memory-related mechanisms under homoeostatic conditions remains unknown.

Memory is intrinsically linked to the engram, the physical unit of cognitive information storage32–35. For memory to form, an experience or learning event must induce enduring molecular and subcellular changes in a sparse ensemble of neurons recruited to the engram33,35,36, and reactivation of these engram cells is sufficient to drive memory recall36. Engram cells are preferentially allocated during learning based on their relatively higher intrinsic excitability at the time of experience34,37,38, and coordinated, sparse activation of this neuronal population within the broader network is required for the formation of stable, long-lasting memories39. These network activation patterns partially reinstate the original encoding ensemble, and overlap between network activation and the encoding engram ensemble is critical for accurate memory retrieval40–42. Consistent with the concept of an encoding ensemble, direct optogenetic activation of opsin-labelled engram neurons in mice is sufficient to elicit memory retrieval40, even under conditions of experimentally induced retrograde amnesia36 or in mouse models of AD43,44. Thus, ensemble allocation and sparse coordinated activity define the engram as the functional basis of memory. Molecules and mechanisms required to encode the engram and permit recall of its cognitive information remain largely unknown, and their loss of function may contribute to memory impairment.

Since its discovery as a microtubule-associated protein45 and later identification in paired helical filaments in AD46, tau has been studied predominantly in pathological contexts. Although tau research established a strong association between tau alterations and cognitive dysfunction, potential roles of tau in memory under physiological conditions have remained unclear. Here, we re-examined tau function within the framework of the memory engram, extending beyond conventional assays that emphasize recent recall after learning. We show that tau is required during memory encoding to support efficient remote recall in mice. This function depends on activity-induced phosphorylation of tau at threonine-205 and involves regulation of engram ensemble recruitment and local network selectivity during encoding. Tau promotes the coupling of sensory cues to the encoding ensemble, while direct optogenetic reactivation of remote engrams occurs independently of tau. Disruption of tau function within active ensembles impairs memory in a temporally specific manner. These findings identify tau as a physiological regulator of engram ensemble organization and link tau function to the molecular basis of remote memory.

Results

Remote memory is impaired in tau-deficient mice

Tau is dispensable for learning and recent memory recall, defined as hours-to-days post-encoding17,21,23–26. We asked whether tau is involved in remote memory and employed standardised paradigms for associative or spatial reference learning47–49, with recall at recent and remote timepoints (Fig. 1A). We used tau−/− mice that lack all forms of tau and exhibit normal learning and recent recall26,50.

We first assessed remote memory in three month-old tau+/+ and tau−/− mice using cued fear conditioning (CFC), which encodes robust aversive cue-response associations measured by freezing response as recall proxy36,48. Fear memory was tested in recent recall on day 1 and remote recall 21 days and 42 days post-conditioning. Tau+/+ mice displayed robust recent and remote recall (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Tau−/− mice exhibited efficient encoding and recent recall but impaired remote recall when compared with tau+/+ (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 1A), indicating that tau is specifically required for remote memory. Stimulus exposure during recent recall confers fear memory reconsolidation51,52. Lower remote recall in tau−/− was not a consequence of reduced reconsolidation (Supplementary Fig. 1B) or enhanced extinction due to lack of tau (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D), precluding a role of tau in reconsolidation or active memory degradation and substantiating a role of tau within the encoding process. A strong aversive experience engages anxiety-related circuits38. To control for maladaptive fear responses as cause of reduced freezing in tau−/− during remote recall, spontaneous locomotion and freezing behaviour were assessed. Both tau+/+ and tau−/− mice showed similar locomotor activity (Supplementary Fig. 1E) and immediate freezing responses to the aversive sensation (Fig. 1B ‘baseline’). Open-field (Supplementary Fig. 1F) and elevated plus maze (Supplementary Fig. 1G) corroborated normal anxiety levels in tau−/− mice25,50 and that remote recall deficits in tau−/− are not due to effects on anxiety-related behaviour. Together, these results show that tau is required for remote memory recall upon associative learning with an aversive reinforcer.

We next determined whether remote recall attenuation in tau−/− mice applied to memory from appetitive learning in a pairwise discrimination (PD) paradigm on a touch-screen operant platform, which relies primarily on positively reinforced instrumental learning49. Tau+/+ and tau−/− mice both reached PD task acquisition criterion with comparable efficiency (Fig. 1C). Recent memory in the PD task was unaffected in tau−/− mice. Upon pairwise stimulus exposure 2 months after training, tau−/− showed markedly lower remote recall compared with tau+/+ mice (Fig. 1C), which translated into delayed stimulus-reward reacquisition (Supplementary Fig. 1H). Thus, results of touchscreen-based appetitive learning corroborate requirement of tau for remote memory recall.

We assessed remote spatial reference memory in an optimized Morris water maze (MWM). A rigorous learning criterion ensured robust recent (24 h) and remote (4mo) recall. This was followed by spatial reacquisition and reversal learning and visual acuity testing, to assess support of task relearning by residual memory, cognitive flexibility, and visuo-sensory competence, respectively. We compared this spatial acquisition protocol with non-spatial controls to distinguish from chance solution (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Tau−/− and tau+/+ mice have similar rates of learning (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 2A) and recent recall (Fig. 1D), consistent with previous work22,25,31. Swim path analysis excluded compensatory mechanisms in tau−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Tau−/− had significantly impaired remote recall efficiency as compared with tau+/+ (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 2C). Consequently, spatial reacquisition in tau−/− mice was slower, yet not reversal learning (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 2A). This demonstrates that tau is required for enduring memory and efficient remote recall of spatial information. Overall, results from aversive associative, appetitive, and spatial learning paradigms indicate that different mnemic processes all require tau for adequate remote recall.

Neuronal tau expression around the time of memory encoding is necessary and sufficient for robust remote recall

Remote recall deficits in tau−/− were not a result of altered extinction, reconsolidation or learning strategies, suggesting that successful remote recall of a memory may require tau during the initial encoding process. We hence hypothesized that conditionally expressing tau in tau−/− mice within different time windows along the memory process would define a temporal role of tau in formation of remote memory. To test this, we created adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based vectors for controlled neuronal expression (neuron-specific, activity-independent synapsin-1 promoter) in the absence of doxycycline (d2tTA-TRE; DOX-Off system) of 2N4R tau (AAV syn1-d2tTA-tau) or enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP; AAV syn1-d2tTA-eGFP) expression in the murine hippocampus within different temporal windows encompassing memory stages (e.g., habituation, encoding, recent and remote recall) to determine the effect of timed tau expression on memory performance (Fig. 1E). Both remote fear memory and spatial memory are hippocampal-dependent53–55. Transgene expression was suppressed by providing doxycycline (DOX)-containing feed and permitted by relieving the DOX block56 (Fig. 1E). To characterise resulting tau re-expression levels, AAVs were hippocampally delivered to tau−/− mice undergoing a DOX feed schedule with DOX removal before and DOX reinstatement after fear conditioning, and assessed viral transgene expression 24 h before (+DOX), 2 h before and after fear conditioning (−DOX) as well as 24 h or 22 days later (+DOX) (Fig. 1E). Immunoblots of hippocampal lysates show both eGFP and tau transgenes were expressed with peak expression 2 h post-CFC, and minimal expression 2 h before CFC. Both were below detection limit before DOX removal and 24 h after refeeding DOX, suggesting that AAV syn1-d2tTA-systems combined with a DOX feed schedule permit expression with rapid induction and repression (Fig. 1F). Low expression 2 h before memory encoding reflects limitations in temporal resolution of DOX-regulated expression systems57,58, yet are minimised by the d2tTA enhanced tetracycline transactivator59.

To examine if neuronal tau expression within a time window surrounding memory encoding affects fear memory recall, recent (24 h) and remote recall (21 d and 42 d) were measured in tau−/− mice injected with AAVs syn1-d2tTA-tau or -eGFP. Expression was limited to an encoding window. Both groups displayed similar basal freezing levels, encoding and recent recall of tone-shock association (Fig. 1G), while eGFP-injected mice had lower remote recall (Fig. 1G; Supplementary Fig. 3B). Controlled expression of tau restricted to habituation or remote recall windows did not affect remote recall in tau−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 3C, E), further supporting a critical window for tau’s contribution to remote memory around the time of encoding. Remote memory recall in tau−/− mice was partially improved when tau expression was limited to immediately following CFC encoding (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Assessing potential impact of AAV-mediated protein expression, we showed that neither AAV-syn1-d2tTA-tau nor -eGFP affected memory performance in injected tau+/+ mice within the experimental constraints of the fear conditioning protocol used (Supplementary Fig. 3F).

We next restricted neuronal hippocampal tau expression to a time window covering the early stage of spatial memory formation (d1-3) followed by DOX-mediated suppression for 4 months during latency, remote recall and spatial reacquisition. Immunofluorescence confirmed hippocampal expression with induction and suppression by DOX (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Fig. 4A). Immunoblots to characterise expression before during and after DOX block removal confirmed increases in expression over acquisition d1 and d2 and suppression upon DOX reinstatement (Supplementary Fig. 4B), coupling tau synthesis to the most dynamic period of spatial memory acquisition60. Both tau−/− mice with induced expression within a spatial encoding window from AAV syn1-d2tTA-tau or eGFP control showed comparable learning and recent recall (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Fig. 4C). Remote recall (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Fig. 4D) and reacquisition (Fig. 1I) were significantly better in tau−/− mice expressing tau during the encoding window as compared with mice expressing eGFP at encoding 4 months prior. Tau expression during spatial acquisition did not modulate reversal learning (Fig. 1I).

Taken together, neuronal tau expression in a time window encompassing memory encoding is necessary and sufficient to support formation of robust remote memory, while being dispensable for recent memory.

Site-specific T205 tau phosphorylation enhances remote memory

Specific phosphorylation of tau modulates memory deficits in rodent models23,24,61. A physiologic role of tau phosphorylation in memory formation is unknown. To characterise tau phosphorylation associated with memory formation, we first analysed specific phosphorylation sites on syn1-d2tTA-expressed tau during spatial acquisition in tau−/− mice using phospho-peptide mass spectrometry24,61, which detected, in order of decreasing abundance, phosphorylation at threonine-205 (T205), T181, serine-396 (S396), S404, T217, T231, and S202 (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Data 1). Next, we probed detection of individual phospho-epitopes of tau expressed in tau−/− hippocampus in a time window around fear memory encoding at different timepoints using immunoblot with site-specific phospho-antibodies (Fig. 2B), confirming phosphorylation at T181, S202, T205, T231, S396 and S404 (Fig. 2C). Phosphorylated wildtype and phospho-deficient tau variants served as immunoblot controls61. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed T205 phosphorylation in ~47% of tau+ DG granule cells in tau−/−. AVV syn1-d2tTA-tau mice at day 1 of spatial acquisition (Fig. 2D). To test whether detectable phosphorylation sites were associated with fear memory formation, we compared lysates from tau−/− mice expressing tau during a fear memory encoding window with tau−/− controls expressing tau under home cage conditions. Assessing the abundance of individual tau phospho-sites, T205 showed higher levels associated with memory encoding (Fig. 2E). Together, these results indicate that specific phosphorylation is induced in tau expressed in hippocampal neurons during memory formation.

We investigated the role of specific tau phospho-sites on remote memory and focussed on T205 due to its induction with memory formation. T205 regulates post-synaptic complexes of tau involved in learning deficits24,62. Yet, the physiologic relevance of tau T205 phosphorylation is currently unknown. We previously generated mice with CRISPR-edited T205 codon in the endogenous Mapt gene, in which phosphorylation is precluded by a T → Alanine (A) codon exchange (tauT205A)62 (Fig. 2F). We next analysed hippocampal tau phosphorylation 10 min after fear memory encoding compared with mice exposed to context only (Fig. 2G). Consistent with a previous study63, wildtype mice (tauT205T/T) undergoing fear conditioning showed significantly higher T205 and S235 phosphorylation of murine tau compared with tauT205T/T context controls (Fig. 2H). Besides absence of T205, tauT205A/A mice had lower T212, T217, and S235 levels following fear conditioning (Fig. 2H). Thus, tauT205A/A mice represent the immediate molecular effects of removing tau T205 phosphorylation during fear conditioning.

TauT205A/A mice showed normal spontaneous fear response or recent recall of fear memory, however impaired remote recall (Fig. 2I). Extinction of fear memory was similar in tauT205T/T and tauT205A/A mice, precluding a role of T205 tau phosphorylation in active uncoupling of the fear response (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Ablating T205 had no impact on generalised anxiety-related behaviour (Supplementary Fig. 5B). TauT205T/T and tauT205A/A mice showed comparable acquisition and recent recall of spatial reference memory (Fig. 2J), as in prior studies62. TauT205A/A had diminished remote recall compared with wildtype tauT205T/T mice (Fig. 2J; Supplementary Fig. 5C–E). Mice lacking Mapk12 (p38γ−/−), which encodes post-synaptic T205 tau kinase p38γ24, demonstrated a similar remote recall phenotype, while initial spatial acquisition and recent recall were comparable to p38γ+/+ controls24 (Supplementary Fig. 5F). Overall, these results define phosphorylation at Threonine-205 as key mechanistic event in tau-dependent remote memory formation.

Tau T205 phosphorylation restricts activated cell recruitment to the engram

During encoding, an ensemble of cells co-activates to form an engram of learning-induced changes33–35. Encoding of lasting hippocampal-dependent memories requires selective and precise co-activation of cells within a local neuronal population, giving rise to a sparse engram ensemble with high precision64–66. Precision of reactivation of this sparse engram underlies sustained memory persistence40,67. To test whether sparse activation and precise encoding require tau, we quantified local cell activation and its overlap with the fear memory ensemble in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice (Fig. 3A, B). We labelled the ensemble with eGFP driven by robust activation marking (RAM) promoter, an activity-dependent expression element of enhanced sensitivity and selectivity59, combined with the d2tTA-TRE system for temporal control59,68. We immunolabelled the hippocampus for c-Fos, a robust cell activation marker69,70, 30 min before and 90 or 240 min after fear memory encoding and in comparison with home cage control mice (90 min time point) (Fig. 3B, C). Recruitment of dentate gyrus (DG) granule cells to the engram (GFP+) was significantly induced by fear conditioning and comparable in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice (Fig. 3C). Low degree of GFP labelling in home cage controls at −30 and 90 min reflects baseline neural activity40,71. Precision of robust activation relative to the engram ensemble was lower in tau−/−, with excessive c-Fos+ and low proportion of double positive GFP+c-Fos+ cells at 90 min, which persisted at 240 min timepoints (Fig. 3C). To assess ensemble reactivation upon remote recall, we employed a dual-AAV vector strategy to stably tag fear memory ensembles with YFP at encoding in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice. Upon remote recall (22 days) of the fear memory, we immunolabelled reactivated cells for c-Fos (Fig. 3D). Tau−/− at remote recall had more c-Fos+ cells and limited overlap with the original encoding ensemble with c-Fos+ cells as compared with tau+/+ mice, suggesting that local reactivation of the initial engram ensemble remains imprecise at remote recall in tau−/− (Fig. 3D). This data shows that tau enhances specificity of cell activation of the engram ensemble, and lack of tau persistently impairs precise recruitment of active cells rendering encoding and hence recall impaired.

To assess the relation between encoding-associated tau and local cell activation, we immunolabelled c-Fos in tau−/− mice expressing tau in hippocampal neurons within a fear memory encoding window. Tau−/− mice expressing tau from AAV syn1-d2tTA-tau during an encoding window had sparser c-Fos labelling as compared with eGFP-expressing controls 90 min and 240 min post fear conditioning (Supplementary Fig. 6A–D), despite comparable overall hippocampal network activity on telemetric electroencephalography (EEG) recordings (Supplementary Fig. 7). c-Fos+ cells were comparable to eGFP controls when neuronal tau was expressed in tau−/− mice during a remote recall window (Supplementary Fig. 6E). Together, these data suggest that tau influences remote memory by modulating encoding-associated neuronal activation in the hippocampus during memory formation, rather than recall-related activation.

To test whether specific tau phosphorylation at T205 was required for precision of cell activation in the fear ensemble, we identified c-Fos+ cells and labelled the hippocampal ensemble with eGFP in tauT205A/A and tauT205T/T mice upon CFC encoding or in home cage controls (Fig. 3E). More granule cells were labelled during FC (eGFP+) than in home cage controls to a similar extent in both tauT205A/A and tauT205T/T mice (Fig. 3E). c-Fos+ cells were more abundant in tauT205A/A and fewer c-Fos+ neurons coincided with GFP+ engram cells at both 90 and 240 min (Fig. 3E), indicating that T205 tau phosphorylation is required to restrict local cell activation to the memory ensemble around encoding. These changes in tauT205A/A were specific to fear memory encoding as baseline GFP-labelling, c-Fos+ cells and double-positive cells were substantially lower and comparable to tauT205T/T in home cage controls (Fig. 3E). Together, these results show that tau directs cell activation preferentially to the engram ensemble and limits activation of non-engram cells, which supports sparse hippocampal cell activity during remote memory encoding65,66. This mechanism critically requires phosphorylation of tau at T205.

Specific tau phosphorylation in the engram supports remote memory

To directly probe whether control of local cell activation by T205 phosphorylation in the encoding ensemble translates to memory function, we developed a vector system to express tau in the active ensemble. We modified a vector based on the synthetic RAM promoter and d2tTA-TRE system59,68 to express wild-type tau (tau) or phospho-T205-defective tauT205A in memory ensembles (Fig. 3F; Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). Hippocampal AAV delivery to tau−/− mice and DOX removal during a fear memory encoding window targeted ensemble cells to express either eGFP, tau or tauT205A (Fig. 3G; Supplementary Fig. 8A–C), resulting in ~5% positive cells of the dentate granule cell layer (Supplementary Fig. 8B). In addition, tau or eGFP expressed from the RAM-d2tTA system was detectable in a variable proportion of DG hilus cells (Supplementary Fig. 8A, C). T205 colabelling was more frequent than T181, S396, or S404 in granule cells expressing activitytargeted tau (Supplementary Fig. 8D, E). Isolated tauT205A had lower overall detection of phospho-epitopes in phospho-peptide mapping by mass spectrometry than tau (Supplementary Fig. 8F; Supplementary Data 2). These results indicate that T205 is a key site to regulate phosphorylation status of tau expressed in engram cells.

To functionally test the role of T205 tau in memory-associated cells, we induced wildtype tau or phospho-defective tauT205A expression in the active ensemble during a spatial reference learning window. Both groups of mice showed normal learning (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Fig. 9A), recent recall (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Fig. 9B), and reversal learning (Fig. 3H). However, tauT205A expression led to insufficient remote recall and thus, delayed spatial reacquisition as compared with tau (Fig. 3H; Supplementary Fig. 9C). This demonstrates control of robust encoding of remote spatial memory by site-specific T205 phosphorylation in engram ensemble cells.

We next addressed the impact of tau T205 phosphorylation on the active ensemble of fear memory. Active ensemble-targeted tau expression during a fear memory encoding window limited hippocampal c-Fos+ reactivity in tau−/− hippocampus, an effect that required the T205 phosphorylation site (Fig. 3I). To test whether this would translate to differences in memory, we tested fear memory encoding, recent and remote recall in these mice. Engram ensemble-specific tauT205A-expression in tau−/− mice resulted in normal learning and recent recall compared to tau expression in engram cells (Fig. 3I). Lack of T205 phosphorylation in engram-specific tau during the encoding window was accompanied by lower remote memory retrieval while tau expression supported sufficient remote recall (Fig. 3I). To control for generalised effects of engram-targeted tau expression on memory, we used the same vectors in tau+/+ mice to target tau to active ensemble in an encoding window, which resulted in similar remote memory compared to GFP-expressing tau+/+ mice (Supplementary Fig. 9D). Active ensemble-targeting of tau lowered the number of c-Fos+ cells among tau−/− DG granules cells, while T205A tau variant had no effect compared with GFP controls (Fig. 3J). Thus, active cells engage tau T205 phosphorylation to limit cell activation to a sparse local ensemble and support formation of remote memory formation.

Remote memory is amenable to direct ensemble activation in absence of tau

One possibility is that tau action in the encoding ensemble underlies the association of a cue with a selective and coordinated neuronal reactivation during expression of remote memory. Alternatively, tau-dependent remote memory substrates may be uncoupled from ensemble reactivation. We therefore asked whether tau is required for remote memory expression upon direct ensemble activation or whether tau specifically makes physiologic recall permissive to natural cues. To test these possibilities, we turned to optogenetic stimulation of engram ensembles in presence or absence of tau. To make ensembles amenable to stimulation, a fear engram was labelled with ChR2-YFP in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice and light cannulas implanted (Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). Mice were then probed for recent and remote recall in context A, followed by light-induced reactivation in an unfamiliar context (-CS in context B) (Fig. 4C). Both natural stimulus (+CS in context A) and optogenetic stimulation led to robust recent responses in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. 10C). Remote recall in response to natural stimulus was impaired in tau−/− mice (Fig. 4C). Optogenetic ensemble activation on day 22 induced memory expression in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. 10C). Thus, remote memory is amenable to direct ensemble activation in the absence of tau, indicating that tau renders stimulus-induced recall permissible and distinguishes remote recall of the learned association from mere neuronal activation.

Pathologic tau directly impairs the engram causing antero- and retrograde amnesia

To link a direct role of tau in engram cells to pathologic processes, we tested whether pathogenic tau affects memory when targeted to the memory ensemble. FTD mutation P301S is associated with rapid-onset memory impairment72,73. We targeted expression of human tauP301S or tau to the fear memory ensemble in tau-competent tau+/+ mice to test for dominant effects over endogenous tau (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. 11A). TauP301S expressed in an active cells around the time of encoding was sufficient to cause anterograde amnesia with impaired recent and remote recall, when compared with fear conditioned naïve tau+/+ and context exposure controls with the same schedule of tau or tauP301S expression (Fig. 4E). We next tested the impact of tauP301S on existing remote memory. Expression of tauP301S within a 30 h time window including remote recall 22 days after fear memory encoding specifically in active cells resulted in retrograde amnesia of remote memory (Fig. 4F). Testing whether a contextual fear engram was interfering with remote recall upon tauP301S expression, context exposure before showed that tauP301S neither within a time window of encoding nor of remote recall had impact on context-induced response just before conditioned cue presentation at remote recall (Supplementary Fig. 11B, C). In contrast to encoding ensemble-restricted tauP301S or tau, widespread neuronal expression of human tau74 did not impact fear memory (Supplementary Fig. 11D), and tau P301S transgenic mice have minimal effects on fear memory15,75, suggesting that non-selective, longitudinal neuronal expression of human tau does not immediately affect the engram. In summary, these results show that tauP301S can induce anterograde or retrograde amnesia when present in active cells within the encoding or remote recall window, respectively, indicating direct disruption of the memory trace by pathogenic tau.

To assess how presence of tauP301S in active cell ensembles affects cell activation in the local network environments upon encoding or remote recall, we determined numbers of c-Fos+ DG granule cells in mice expressing eGFP, tau or tauP301S. Moreover, because hippocampal engram ensembles coordinate cortical activation for remote memory, including in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), we examined ACC activation76,77(Supplementary Fig. 11E). TauP301S expression in neurons active during an encoding window produced significantly more c‑Fos⁺ DG granule cells than tau in both tau+/+ and tau−/− mice (Fig. 4G), indicating that tauP301S drives excess activation in the presence of tau and fails to compensate for the function of endogenous tau in suppression of surplus activation. Upon remote recall, presence of tauP301S in active cells during an encoding window resulted in more c‑Fos⁺ DG granule cells compared with tau, eGFP and context controls in tau+/+ hippocampus, despite virtual absence of tau transgene at this time point (Fig. 4H). Tau+/+ mice expressing activity‑targeted tauP301S during encoding showed increased ACC activation at remote recall (Supplementary Fig. 11F). Tau+/+ with presence of tauP301S during a remote recall window showed elevated granule cell activation upon remote recall as compared with tau and eGFP controls (Fig. 4I), yet normal ACC activation (Supplementary Fig. 11G). Together, these findings indicate that tauP301S lowers local activation thresholds, fails to suppress neuronal hyperactivation, and, if present around encoding, disrupts coordinated hippocampal-cortical recruitment, contributing to excess cell activation and memory impairment in models of active-ensemble targeted expression of pathogenic tau.

Discussion

Our study describes a role of tau during memory formation that is critical for remote memory. Previous memory studies from us and others focussed on working or recent memory, showing no or minor effects of the absence of tau17,22,24–26,31,78, varying by gene targeting strategy and genetic background79. Presence of tau around the time of memory encoding is sufficient to promote remote recall in multiple forms of learning. Tau enabled remote recall when expression is restricted to a time window encompassing memory encoding in tau-deficient mice despite complete suppression during latency. Moreover, tau expression in tau−/− mice around habituation or remote recall phase was insufficient to modulate remote memory. This indicates that tau governs long-lasting memories around the time of encoding. Partial restoration of remote memory in tau−/− was achieved by presence of tau directly following fear conditioning, indicating that tau may – to some extent – engage with processes of memory consolidation33. Temporally controlled expression of tau in presence of endogenous murine tau had no impact on fear memory, supporting specificity of tau’s role in memory. While defining a critical role of tau during memory formation, these targeted re-expression experiments are subject to limitations in temporal resolution of the DOX-d2tTA expression system and do not entirely exclude a role of tau expression in remote memory at other times e.g., longitudinally during latency. However, when tau was present around encoding in tau−/− mice, tau was not required during latency period for efficient remote recall indicating that tau is not involved in preventing memory degradation over time.

Different mechanisms underlie persistent mnemonic signals over months or years than over hours or days32. We show that tau’s function in this context is inherently linked to tau phosphorylation. We show a functionally critical site on tau is T205, which is induced by fear conditioning in the hippocampus and is required for remote memory. Experiments in tauT205A/A mice lacking this phospho-site in tau and with engram-targeted tauT205A version corroborate the critical function of this site in tau. T205 phosphorylation may still act in conjunction with tau modification at other sites as we observed low level phosphorylation at other epitopes, in endogenous tau or hippocampal AAV tau, and T205 and other sites are known to be biochemically linked61. Mechanistically, specific tau phosphorylation at T205 in cells of the encoding ensemble is critical for limiting extraneous local cell activation, measured by marker c-Fos, thereby increasing precision of neuronal allocation to the engram, in accordance with the need for sparsity and competitive inhibitory control for lasting memory65,66. Suppression of non-engram cell activation fails in absence of tau (tau−/−) and when tauP301S is present (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating convergent or overlapping mechanisms of control of network activity by loss- and gain-of-function models of tau action.

Within the hippocampal formation, diverse neuron types contribute to encoding ensembles80. Different cell types make functional contributions to circuits of remote memory and recall81. Viral vector transduction and expression cassettes, for neuronal (syn1 minimal promoter82) or active ensemble targeting (RAM promoter59), used in our study do not distinguish between these subtypes. Targeting tau to the dentate gyrus resulted in transgene expression in granule cells, which are critical for sparse hippocampal engrams65,83, and – to some extent – in hilus cells, which form part of memory circuits81,84. Modulation of tau expression or absence of T205 in the dentate gyrus correlates with excess local granule cell activation and impaired remote memory. Endogenous tau is present across dentate subfields, but whether tau acts in specific cell types to support persistent memory remains to be determined. It is therefore possible that tau coordinates memory circuits through action in several cell types.

Episodic and visuospatial memory are affected early in progressive tauopathies such as AD or Lewy body dementia85,86, and amnesia in dementia tauopathies is presently irreversible87. The current tenet holds that tau pathology impairs cellular and circuit functions in limbic regions subserving episodic memory1,88,89, leading to an early degradation of episodic recollection85,90 and visuospatial abilities91. Direct optogenetic activation of the engram ensemble facilitates remote recall in absence of tau, indicating that tau – at encoding – renders memory permissible to natural remote recall specifically, suggesting that tau is required to couple environmental stimuli to engram activation but not for engram reactivation itself. This role of tau in the engram may reconcile concepts of impaired encoding85,92 and impaired retrieval44 of memories in tau-dependent memory loss. Our optogenetics experiments indicate functional overlap of encoding, recent, and remote memory ensembles in tau+/+ and tau−/− mice, and show that bypassing extraneously active non-engram cells restores memory. However, the exact degree of overlap and identity of neurons recruited to these ensembles, and how it may be affected by tau, remains to be determined with methods that can distinguish ensembles at different stages. Experiments in mice with memory impairments suggested that the target engram is not amenable to retrieval due to extra neuronal ensemble activity disrupting recall43. Consistent with this concept, excess DG cell activation in the absence of tau, notably in a manner that is associated with memory formation as compared with context controls, gives rise to extra neural ensemble activity, potentially reflecting competing or non-specific ensemble activity that interferes with engram precision and recall35. Pathogenic tau has been shown to alter excitability of neurons28. In normal learning and memory processes, tau phosphorylation, induced by encoding stimuli, may limit extraneous activity of non-engram cells thereby linking mechanistic association of stimulus representation to precise memory content.

We found that pathogenic tau, previously linked to progressive memory impairment72,73, can induce anterograde or retrograde amnesia when present in active neurons within encoding or remote recall windows, disrupting remote memory formation or retrieval, respectively. Concomitantly, tauP301S increased c-Fos induction, as proxy of local network engagement after fear conditioning, indicating exaggerated neuronal activation. While not a direct measure of engram reactivation, this heightened activation likely contributes to loss of discrete memory traces: pathogenic tau in the encoding ensemble directly impairs memory mechanisms and drives aberrant activation extending into cortical regions such as the ACC, which support consolidation41,76. When tauP301S is present during the remote recall window, memory impairment occurs without ACC hyperactivation, consistent with disruption of an already-consolidated hippocampal indexing ensemble41,76,93. Some effects during the recall window may also reflect tauP301S present shortly before recall. Notably, longitudinal forebrain expression of tauP301S causes minimal fear-memory deficits15,75, possibly because ensemble-targeted tauP301S uniquely interferes with normal tau’s function in neurons allocating to a specific memory trace. Alternatively, prolonged expression may allow network-level compensation. The active cell-targeted tau models presented here offer a way to further dissect failure of specific memory traces.

Overall, the herein identified role of tau in the engram is critical to advance our understanding of both the molecular nature of long-lasting memories and the basis of memory loss in tauopathy dementia. AD pathology presents with a high load of phosphorylated tau, including at T20588,94, potentially interfering with physiologic, memory-associated tau modifications. Our results align with the concept of lowering tau for therapy because of pathologically elevated tau levels in disease95,96. It will therefore be critical to reduce tau in disease to levels, at which tau can fulfil its physiologic function in engram-associated ensembles. In addition, pending feasibility of modulation within critical time windows for learning, future therapeutic strategies could indirectly tune the function of tau in memory-encoding cells, including through phospho-tau-targeting therapeutic antibodies.

Methods

Animals

All mouse experimentation was approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Flinders University (AWC approval numbers 2949/5979/6415) and conformed to the Australian Code for the care and use of animals for scientific purposes as stipulated by the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia. Mice were sourced from a breeding colony at Flinders University, including mice with targeted alleles for p38γ (Mapk12) [p38γ−; B6.129S4(Cg)-Mapk12tm1.2Lex]24,97, tau-deficient mice [tauEx1; B6.129S4(Cg)-Mapt]50, and tauT205A mice with a codon exchange in the Mapt gene at T194 (i.e., T205 in human tau)62. All mouse lines were maintained on a C57Bl/6 background. Mice were housed at 22 °C (+/−1 °C) and 50 % (+/-3%) relative humidity in individually vented cage systems (Tecniplast) in groups of up to five/cage and maintained on a standard chow diet (Gordon Specialty Feeds) and 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Only animals with normal health parameters (e.g., body weight, coat condition) were included in experiments. Experimental groups were age-matched by 2 weeks within an age of 8–16 weeks old at experiment start. Testing and data analyses were done by researchers blinded to genotypes and treatments. Mice were accustomed to handling for up to 5 consecutive days for 1–5 min. Both male and female mice were included in all experiments in equal proportions. Sex-stratified analyses conducted at the outset of the study revealed no significant effects of sex on the measured phenotypes. Accordingly, data from male and female mice were combined for subsequent analyses. All mice were transcardially perfused with 1x PBS (pH7.4). Mice were genotyped by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using isopropanol-precipitated genomic DNA and DNA polymerase (M0480, New England Biolabs, (NEB); 04728858001, Roche). TauT205A mice were genotyped by tetra-primer amplification-refractory mutation system (4ARMS) PCR62. Genotyping oligonucleotide primers are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Memory and behaviour testing in mice

Cued fear conditioning (CFC)

CFC was performed on a system with automated load cell detection, shock grid floor and video monitoring (PanLab, Spain). The fear conditioning chambers were enclosed in sound-insulated boxes holding one testing unit each. The fear conditioning system was in a dedicated behavioural testing room. The chamber floor consisted of metal parallel grid bars connected to the load cell coupler and shock generator. A camera was custom built into the ceiling of the fear chamber for visualisation and analysis. Chamber floor and walls were wiped with 70% ethanol before and after each use to neutralize scents in the testing environment for the next subject. The standard chamber context (Context A) included square arena (35 cm), blank wall and indirect blue light (LED). Context B was white methacrylate walls, solid silver floor and red light.

Habituation

Mice (8–16 weeks old) were habituated to the conditioning chamber for 3 min. For experiments with subsequent biochemical analysis, mice were habituated on 3 consecutive days for 5 - 10 min63. During habituation, percent activity and freezing was measured and mice that displayed spontaneous freezing behaviour were excluded from further experimentation.

Encoding of fear memory

Encoding sessions consisted of 180 s habituation, followed by two auditory cue (CS) presentations (20 s, 2 kHz, 75 decibel (dB)) delivered at 180 s and 260 s and co-terminated with a 2 s 0.6 mA foot shock (US) with a 60 s intertrial interval. Following encoding mice were returned to their home cage. Home cage controls were sacrificed at equal time points59. Context controls were exposed to context A for the same duration as mice undergoing fear conditioning.

Recall testing

All recall test sessions were 420 s in duration and consisted of 180 s habituation followed by two tone presentations (2 kHz, 75 dB) of 20 s duration with no foot shock (-US).

Extinction trials

Fear memory extinction experiments were performed based on a 2-day protocol with 5 consecutive extinction trials per day98. Mice followed the CFC protocol as above. 24 h post-conditioning, mice were tested for recent recall as above, followed 24 h later by presentation with 5 consecutive Unpaired CS (120 s, 2 kHz, 74 dB) within 10 min, which was repeated 24 h later.

Quantification of freezing behaviour

Data was automatically recorded by load cell coupling and quantified using 2 s freeze detection and breathing filter (PanLab) and confirmed by video tracking (AnyMaze, Stoelting). Average freezing response maps were generated with Rtrack99 in R (v2023.06.0 + 421).

Touchscreen operant testing

Pairwise visual discrimination (PD) training was performed on a touchscreen operant chamber (Campden) with four operant learning units each with a trapezoid chamber with the touchscreen on the long side and the food reward tray on the parallel short side. Each of the four testing chambers was enclosed in a sound-insulated light-proof box. The apparatus was in a dedicated behavioural testing room. Appetitive motivation was provided as liquid reward (Strawberry Iced Milk, Nippy’s). Priming of sessions at the start of each session provided an initial reward delivery (20 µl). The chamber was wiped with 70% ethanol between mice to neutralize scents in the testing environment for the next subject. The PD training and pre-training modules were based on the developer’s protocols49.

Mice (8–16 weeks old) were placed on food restriction (2 g chow/10 g bodyweight per day). Mice with >10% body weight loss were excluded. Mice were habituated to the testing chamber for 10 min on 3 consecutive days and subsequently underwent 4 stages of pre-training49. For PD training, mice were presented with two stimuli (CS+ and CS-) with randomized reward association and pseudo-randomised location of stimuli. Correct (CS+) responses resulted in reward delivery (20 µl) and tone (1 s, 3 kHz), while incorrect choices (CS−) were followed by an inter-trial interval (ITI, 20 s) with activated house light (5 s) and a correction trial (CT) with the same stimuli presentation. Mice were administered 1 h of training daily at the same time of day with a maximum of 36 trials per session to a criterion of >80% correct trials in the last 2 consecutive acquisition sessions. Response efficiency was defined as correct choice count over the total session trials/time to complete the session trials. For remote memory testing, mice were reintroduced into the operant chamber for a 1-day habituation session followed by reassessment of operant behaviour for 4 consecutive sessions, prior to reacquisition of PD paradigm using the same CS+ and CS- pairings. Recall efficiency was defined as average response efficiency for sessions 5–10.

Morris water maze

Spatial learning and memory was assessed in the Morris water maze based on a standard protocol47 with modifications to optimize for assessment of remote memory. The water maze tank (145 cm diameter) with white opaque high-density polyethylene walls (Acrilix, Australia) was centred in a dedicated behavioural testing room with minimal peripheral features on white walls and ceiling, and a digital camera fixed centrally on the ceiling. The tank was filled with opacified room temperature (22 °C) water until 30 cm from top edge and was surrounded equidistantly by off-white walls and partitions in an environment without high-contrast features and illuminated indirectly with low light. A hidden platform (12.6 cm) was located 1 cm below the water surface in the quadrant opposite entry point. Four off-white cue boards with salient visual cues were affixed at equidistant poles along the maze perimeter. Activity and movement were recorded with a ceiling-mounted digital camera (The Imaging Source) and analysed using automated tracking (ANY-maze, v7.0, Stoelting). Mice (8 weeks old) were habituated to the testing room and to the experimenter handling for 1 h/day for 2–3 consecutive days prior to onset of spatial training. Entry points were semi-randomized. The initial acquisition phase (four 60 s-trials per day) was performed until mice reached spatial criterion (<=15 s on average for the entire cohort, <=20 s average of 4 trials). Mice that did not reach criterion were excluded. Spatial acquisition strategies were categorized100. For non-spatial acquisition, the escape platform location was randomised between trials. For spatial recall, two 30 s-probe trials were performed. Recent memory trials were administered 24 h after the last reacquisition day to avoid the robust extinction effect on memory by void solution (i.e., probe) trials47,101,102. Recall trials were analysed (a) as crossings of quadrant annuli103, (b) by cumulative time spent in the target quadrant47, and (c) as occupancy map. 24 h following remote recall trials, mice were subjected to spatial reacquisition of the initial platform position until they reached criterion (3 1-min trials per session), followed by a reversal phase. For visual cued trials, a marker was attached to the hidden platform.

Open-field test

Adult mice (8–16 weeks old) were tested during the light phase of the light–dark cycle in a dedicated behavioural testing room. Animals were habituated to the testing room for at least 30 min prior to testing. All experiments were conducted under dim illumination and with constant ambient noise.

The open-field arena consisted of a square, opaque grey Perspex box (40 × 40 × 40 cm unless otherwise stated). The floor was virtually divided into a central zone (20 × 20 cm) and a peripheral zone for analysis. At the start of each trial, mice were placed gently in the centre of the arena and allowed to freely explore for 15 min. Animal behaviour was recorded using an overhead camera positioned directly above the arena centre. Locomotor activity was quantified using automated video-tracking software (ANY-maze, v7.0, Stoelting), with experimenters blinded to genotype or treatment during analysis. Parameters extracted included total distance travelled, mean velocity, time spent in the centre zone, number of centre entries, and percentage of time spent moving. Immobility was defined as the absence of detectable movement for ≥1 s. Between trials, the arena was wiped with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry to eliminate olfactory cues. Each animal was tested once.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The elevated plus maze (EPM) was used to assess anxiety-like behaviour in mice. Adult mice (8–16 weeks old) were tested during the light phase of the light–dark cycle under the same environmental conditions as the open-field test. Animals were habituated to the testing room for at least 30 min prior to testing. The EPM apparatus consisted of four opaque Perspex arms (two open arms and two closed arms) extending from a central platform, elevated 50 cm above the floor. Each arm measured 30 × 5 cm; closed arms were enclosed by opaque walls 15 cm in height. The apparatus was constructed from non-reflective, grey Perspex. At the start of each trial, mice were placed gently on the central platform facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. Behaviour was recorded using a camera fixed centrally on the ceiling above the EPM apparatus. Recordings were analysed using automated video-tracking software (ANY-maze, v7.0, Stoelting), with the experimenter blinded to genotype or treatment during analysis. Primary outcome measures included time spent in the open and closed arms, number of entries into each arm, and total distance travelled. An arm entry was defined as all four paws entering an arm. The EPM apparatus was wiped with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry to eliminate olfactory cues. Each animal was tested once.

Stereotaxic delivery to the hippocampus

Mice (8–16 weeks old) were anaesthetized using ketamine/xylazine (80/8 mg/kg of body weight, i.p.) and general anaesthesia was confirmed. The scalp was shaved and disinfected using alcoholic chlorhexidine. A midline incision was made, skull surface cleared using a retractor and mouse mounted onto the stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, USA, catalogue nos. 940 and 926) with a temperature controller (TCAT-2, Physitemp, Clifton, USA). Stereotaxic coordinates for bilateral hippocampal infusion to the dorsal and ventral dentate gyrus were anterior-posterior (AP). −2.0 mm; lateral (L), ±1.85 mm; dorso-ventral (DV), −2.0 mm, and AP, −3.6 mm; ±2.5 mm; DV, −3.2 mm from bregma. Bur holes were made using a microdrill (Stoelting, 58610 v) fitted with a 0.05mm-diameter tungsten carbide drill head (Fine Science Tools, Meisinger 310104001). Solution was infused using a 34-gauge needle (NF34BV-2, WPI) connected to a 10 µL Nanofil Syringe (NANOFIL, WPI) and precision nanolitre pump (UMP3, WPI) with controller (Micro4, WPI). The needle remained for 2 min before infusion at 200 nl/min and for a further 5 min post volume delivery before retraction. After repeating injections at all mark coordinates, the skin incision was closed with skin staples and adhesive (Vetbond, 3 M). Mice were allowed to recover for 1-week before subsequent experimentation.

Doxycycline feed schedule for engram labelling

Mice were placed on doxycycline-containing diet (40 mg/kg, Specialty Feeds) 5–7 days prior to stereotaxic injection. For DOX reinstatement, mice were placed back on DOX-containing diet (200 mg/kg, Specialty Feeds). For detailed information on DOX removal windows see respective figures and schematics.

Telemetric optogenetic stimulation of fear engram

Wireless optogenetic targeting and stimulation was used to selectively reactivate neuronal fear memory ensembles. Mice (8–16 weeks old) were implanted with a wireless light delivery device104, before tagging a fear memory ensemble with excitatory opsin hChR2(H134R) and subsequent stimulation with blue light to reactivate the fear ensemble40,44. All procedures were conducted during the light phase of the light/dark cycle in a dedicated procedure room.

Light cannula implantation

For cannula implantation, mice were anaesthetized using ketamine/xylazine (80/8 mg/kg of body weight, i.p.) and general anaesthesia was confirmed. The scalp was shaved and disinfected using alcoholic chlorhexidine. A midline incision was made, skull surface cleared using a retractor and mouse mounted onto the stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, USA, catalogue nos. 940 and 926) with a temperature controller (TCAT-2, Physitemp, Clifton, USA). Stereotaxic coordinates for bilateral hippocampal infusion to the dorsal and ventral dentate gyrus were anterior-posterior (AP). −2.0 mm; lateral (L), ±1.85 mm; dorso-ventral (DV), −2.0 mm from bregma. Bur holes were made using a microdrill (Stoelting, 58610 v) fitted with a 0.05 mm-diameter tungsten carbide drill head (Fine Science Tools, Meisinger 310104001). The telemetric optogenetic emitters (TeleR-2-P, TeleOpto) were equipped with bilateral LED cannulae (TeleLCD-B, TeleOpto; fibre diameter, 500 µm; fibre length, 1.8 mm; bilateral 3.8 mm distance, 473 nm) customized to target the mouse dentate gyrus. LED cannulae were implanted in a stereotaxic procedure as described above. Prior to LED cannula implantation, hippocampus was dually infused with 50 nl opsin-encoding AAV EF1a-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-EYFP-WPRE-HGHpA (1.14 × 1014 viral genomes (vg)/ml) combined with 50 nl AAV RAM-d2tTA-Cre-WPRE-pA (8.14 × 1012 vg/ml) bilaterally. pAAV-EF1a-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-EYFP-WPRE-HGHpA was a gift from Karl Deisseroth (20298, Addgene)105. Solution was infused using a 34-gauge needle (NF34BV-2, WPI) connected to a 10 µL Nanofil Syringe (NANOFIL, WPI) and precision nanolitre pump (UMP3, WPI) with controller (Micro4, WPI). The needle remained for 2 min before infusion at 200 nl/min and for a further 5 min post volume delivery before retraction. After repeating injections for both bilateral coordinates, the implant was lowered to the stereotaxic coordinates AP −2.0 mm, L ± 1.9 mm, DV −1.8 mm and secured with bone anchors and dental cement (SpeedCEM, Ivoclar). The scalp was closed, disinfected and the implant cap edges were covered with silicone cure (KwikSil, WPI). After 7 days of recovery, mice were habituated to a dummy emitter (TeleOpto) for 2–3 days for 1 h/day.

Stimulation protocol

Memory encoding, recent and remote recall with natural stimulus were all performed in the standard context (context A) as described for cued fear conditioning. Light stimulation to reactivate ChR2-expressing memory ensembles was performed in context B. Freezing response was recorded with and without light (473 nm) stimulation (20 Hz, 25 s, at 0.9 - 1.0 mW) based on comparable protocols for hippocampal ensemble stimulation44. Light intensity at the cannula tip was confirmed pre- and post- experiments using a light power metre (LPM-100, TeleOpto).

Telemetric electroencephalography

Telemetry implantation

For implantation of electrodes and emitters for telemetric electroencephalography (EEG), mice (8–16 weeks old) were anaesthetized using ketamine/xylazine (80/8 mg/kg of body weight, i.p.) and general anaesthesia was confirmed. The scalp was shaved and disinfected using alcoholic chlorhexidine. A midline incision was made, skull surface cleared using a retractor and mouse mounted onto the stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, USA, catalogue nos. 940 and 926) with a temperature controller (TCAT-2, Physitemp, Clifton, USA). In a stereotaxic procedure, recording electrodes (0.05 mm; DSI) were inserted bilaterally into the hippocampus (−2.0 mm AP, ±1.85 mm ML, −2.0 mm DV from bregma) with reference electrode above the cerebellum (−6.0 mm AP, 0 mm ML, 0 mm DV). Electrodes were fixed in place by polyacrylate and dental cement. The telemetric emitter (ETA-F10, DSI) was positions in a subcutaneous pocket under the dorsal skin. After complete recovery for 7 days, mice were habituated to cages housed in dedicated recording room placed on receiver platforms for 7 days before recordings could proceed. Correct placement of electrodes was confirmed by serial sections of paraffin embedded brain tissue with hematoxylin-eosin staining. Only recordings from mice with correct electrode placement were included in analysis.

EEG recordings

Local field potentials (LFP) were recorded at 1000 Hz sampling rate on amplifier matrices using Ponemah software (DSI). LFP data were pre-processed offline using custom scripts in MATLAB (MathWorks) or equivalent analysis software. Signals were band-pass filtered to remove slow drift (<1 Hz) and high-frequency noise (>300 Hz). Artefact-contaminated segments (movement artefacts or amplifier saturation) were excluded prior to further analysis. Only epochs corresponding to active wakefulness (Sleep scoring module, Neuroscore, DSI), defined by the presence of continuous theta oscillations and corroborated by behavioural monitoring, were included in the analysis. Raw LFP signal was exported for further analysis.

Spectral power analysis

Spectrogram analysis was performed in MATLAB (R2021a) using 60 s LFP data bins in active-wake state. All analyses were performed with experimenters blinded to genotype or treatment. Animals with insufficient theta power or unstable EEG recordings were excluded prior to unblinding. Spectral power analysis was performed on hippocampal EEG recordings to quantify oscillatory activity across defined frequency bands. Pre-processed EEG signals (see above) were segmented into artefact‑free, non‑overlapping epochs of 2 s duration corresponding to active wakefulness. Power spectral density (PSD) was estimated for each epoch using a mean periodogram‑based approach (Fast Fourier Transform with Hamming window). Power spectra were expressed as power per frequency (µV²/Hz) and, where indicated, log‑transformed prior to statistical analysis. Band‑limited power was calculated by integrating the PSD over predefined frequency ranges (Delta: 0 - 4 Hz; Theta: 4 - 12 Hz; Gamma: 40 - 100 Hz). Absolute band power values were extracted from the averaged periodogram for each animal.

Cross-frequency coupling analysis

Cross-frequency coupling was analysed in MATLAB (R2021b) based on the method developed by Tort et al.106. Cross-frequency coupling analysis was performed on 90 s bins of active-wake LFP and was quantified as phase-amplitude coupling of the gamma amplitude with the theta phase (4 - 12 Hz) and. Theta and gamma components were extracted using zero‑phase finite impulse response band‑pass filters. Instantaneous theta phase and gamma amplitude envelopes were derived using the Hilbert transform. Gamma amplitude was computed across a range of frequencies (e.g. 40 - 100 Hz in 5 - 10 Hz steps) to generate comodulograms. For non-overlapping two-second epochs, gamma amplitude values were binned according to theta phase (18 - 20 phase bins spanning −π to π), and the mean gamma amplitude per phase bin was calculated. Phase-amplitude coupling strength was quantified using the modulation index (MI), which measures the deviation of the observed amplitude‑by‑phase distribution from a uniform distribution. MI values were calculated for each frequency pair and averaged across epochs for each animal. Analysis was performed blinded to genotype or treatment. Animals with insufficient theta power or unstable EEG recordings were excluded prior to unblinding.

Molecular cloning

Sequences for eGFP, eYFP or tau (441 amino acids) as wildtype or T205A variant were amplified by PCR (M0492, NEB) from peGFP-C1 (Clontech, #6084-1), pAAV-Ef1a-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-EYFP-WPRE-hGHpA (Addgene #20298), and pcDNA3.2/V5-DEST-htau4024, respectively. PCR products were cloned into target constructs using HiFi Master mix (E2621, NEB). Tau and tau variants S202A, T205A, T217A, S235A, S396A and S404A in pcDNA3.2/V5-DEST (12489, Invitrogen) were previous plasmid constructs24,61,107. Variants T181A, T212A, and T231A were subcloned into pcDNA3.2/V5-DEST-htau40 using BsiWI and NheI. Addgene plasmids: Plasmids pAAV-RAM-d2TTA::TRE-MCS-WPRE-pA (63931) and pAAV-RAM-d2TTA::TRE-eGFP-WPRE-pA (84469) were a kind gift from Yingxi Lin. The RAM promoter was replaced with a human synapsin-1 promoter to generate pAAV-syn1-d2tTA::TRE-MCS-WPRE-pA. All constructs were verified by dideoxynucleotide sequencing (Macrogen), propagated in NEB Stable Competent E.coli (C3040, NEB) and isolated using plasmid purification kits (T1010, NEB; K210004, Invitrogen). Cloning oligonucleotide primers are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Production of recombinant adeno-associated viral vectors (rAAVs)

293T cells (70–80% confluence) were seeded in complete DMEM (11965, Gibco), 10% FBS (SH30084.03IR30-40, Cytiva), 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1 mM sodium pyruvate). IMDM (I3390, Sigma-Aldrich), 2% FBS was replaced 16 h later. Cells were transfected 3 h later with transfer plasmid, pHelper, and AAV-PHP.B108 using PEI-Max (24765, Polysciences; DNA:PEI ratio 3:1). Complete DMEM with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) was replaced 16 h later. Seventy-two hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and resuspended in 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, 2 mM MgCl₂, pH 8.0. Supernatants were clarified at 8% PEG 8000/0.5 M NaCl at 4 °C for 16 h, centrifuged at 2000 × g for 30 min and the pellet was combined with the cell resuspension. After treatment with 0.5% sodium deoxycholate and benzonase (5 U/µL) at 37 °C for 40 min, supplementation with NaCl to 1.1 M, incubation at 37 °C for 40 min, and 3 freeze–thaw cycles, the resulting mixture was centrifuged (5000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation (475,900 × g, 2 h, 18 °C), concentrated (100 kDa MWCO) and buffer exchanged (100 mM sodium citrate, 10 mM Tris pH 8, 0.001% Pluronic F-68). Titers were determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using Luna qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) on a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) with WPRE primers (forward: 5′-GGCTGTTGGGCACTGACAAT-3’, reverse: 5′- CCGAAGGGACGTAGCAGAAG-3’57). Viral titres (expressed in viral genomes/mL): AAV-syn1-d2tTA-eGFP (6.40 × 1013), AAV-syn1-d2tTA-tau (5.59 × 1013), AAV-RAM-d2tTA-eGFP (9.69 × 1013), AAV-RAM-d2tTA-eYFP (2.99 × 1013), AAV-RAM-d2tTA-tau (4.49 × 1013), AAV-RAM-d2tTA-tauT205A (2.48 × 1013), AAV-RAM-d2tTA-tauP301S (4.89 × 1013), AAV-RAM-d2tTA-Cre (8.14 × 1012), AAV-EF1a-double floxed-hChR2(H134R)-EYFP-WPRE-HGHpA (1.14 × 1014).

Tissue and cell lysates

Hippocampal brain tissue was extracted upon transcardial perfusion and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −80 °C. Ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris pH8.0, 1 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM activated sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4), 10 mM NaF, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate (Na2H2P2O7), 1% IGEPAL (I3201, Sigma-Aldrich), 1x Pierce phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (A32959, Thermo Scientific)) was added at a ratio of 10 μl of buffer per milligram of hippocampal tissue weight, and tissue was homogenized with a dounce homogenizer (Heidolph) with 10 strokes at 650 rounds-per-minute on ice. 293T cells transfected with plasmids encoding tau, tau variants or empty vector were harvested 48 h later in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 5 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM NaF, 1 mM Na4P2O7, 1 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1% IGEPAL, 1× phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (A32961, Thermo-Fisher Scientific)). Lysates were sonicated twice for 2 s at 20% amplitude with a tip-probe sonicator (Qsonica) and centrifuged (16,000 × g, 10 min). Protein concentrations were determined by Bio-Rad Protein DC assay (5000111) and normalized to 1 mg/mL in 1× sample buffer (10% Glycerol, 2% SDS, 50 mM Tris pH6.8, 0.05% Bromophenol blue, 0.2% β-mercaptoethanol).

Phospho-peptide mass spectrometry

Tau protein was immunoprecipitated from hippocampal tissue extracts adjusted to a total protein concentration of 6 µg/µl and a total protein amount of 500 μg. Extracts were incubated with 60 μl Pierce Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (no. 88836, Thermo Scientific) per sample for 4 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed three times with RIPA buffer for 5 min with rotation at 4 °C. After one rinse and one wash with 20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.4, 2 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, beads were transferred to a new tube to eliminate trace detergent and protease inhibitor. Beads were washed twice with 20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.4, 2 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, three times with freshly prepared 20 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 150 mM NaCl, before resuspension in 1× trypsin buffer (V5111, Promega). Enriched protein was reduced with 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) at 56 °C for 10 min, followed by alkylation using 10 mM iodoacetamide (I1149, Sigma-Aldrich) at ambient temperature for 30 min and on-beads digestion [1:25 (w/w) trypsin:protein ratio for 16 h at 37 °C using sequencing-grade trypsin (V5111, Promega) and ProteaseMAX (V2072, Promega). Phospho-peptides were enriched on TiO2 Spin tips (GL Sciences) according the manufacturer’s instructions. Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added to a 0.1% final concentration to the enriched phospho-peptides. Peptides were desalted using tC18 cartridges (WAT036805, Waters) that were conditioned with steps of 100% acetonitrile, 50% acetonitrile with 0.5% acetic acid and 0.1% TFA. tC18 cartridges were washed after samples binding with steps of 0.1% TFA and 0.5% acetic acid. Phospho-peptides were eluted into 80% acetonitrile with 0.5% acetic acid. Samples were dried in a vacuum centrifuge (Eppendorf) for 45 min at 45 °C, resuspended in 0.2% heptafluorobutyric acid containing 1% formic acid and stored at −80 °C before analysis. Phospho-peptides were analysed by LC-MS/MS (ESI-QUAD-TOF type) on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) with FAIMS pro duo source attachment and Vanquish Neo UHPLC (Thermo Electron). Peptides (3–6 mg in 1–7 μl injected) were captured on a C18 cartridge (PepMap Neo C18, 300 mm, 5μm, Thermo Scientific), then C18 reverse phase column (~25 cm; Reprosil-Pur, 1.9 μm, 200 Å, Dr. Maisch GmbH), supported within a column heater (45 °C; Sonation). Peptides were eluted on a 40-min water:acetonitrile gradient with 0.1% formic acid at 200 nl/min. Detailed LC and MS parameters are provided in Supplementary Data 6. Peak lists were generated and searched using MASCOT Distiller and search engine (MASCOT search engine versions v3.0.0 and v3.1.0, Matrix Science) against SwissProt database (19/1/24, 570,420 sequences; 206,321,560 residues) using mammalia taxonomy. Search parameters included MS/MS ion search type, semi-trypsin cleavage with a maximum of 3 missed cleavages. All data were searched with acetylation of Asparagine, oxidation of Methionine, phosphorylation of Serine, Threonine and Tyrosine set as variable modifications, and carbamidomethyl of Cysteine set as fixed modification. Mass tolerance for precursor and fragment ions were ±5 ppm and ±0.05 Da respectively. The mascot default minimum peptide length was used (i.e., 7 residues). Peptides were accepted with Mascot peptide score ≥20 and peptide spectral matches, peptide and false discovery rate (FDR) set to 1%. Phospho-site localisation was supported by site-determining fragment ions. Normalised, semi-quantitative determination of relative phospho-epitope abundance was performed using peptide spectral count analysis. For each phospho-epitope, abundance was operationally defined as the total number of MS/MS spectra assigned to peptides containing the modified site across the corresponding LC–MS/MS run. To account for differences in sampling depth and total ion acquisition between runs, raw spectral counts were summed prior to normalization to the total number of identified MS/MS spectra per sample, yielding normalized spectral counts that allow comparison across biological conditions.

Immunoblots

Tissue lysates (5–10 µg for AAV-syn1-d2tTA; 0.5 µg for controls) were separated by SDS-PAGE (8% bis-tris gels) in Tris SDS running buffer at 100 V for 2 h at ambient temperature. Protein was transferred to 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membranes (catalogue no. 10600002, Cytiva) in Tris SDS Transfer buffer with 20% methanol for 45 min at 20 V at ambient temperature. Membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at ambient temperature with gentle shaking. Primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary Table 1), diluted in TBS-T containing 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA; BSAS0.1, Bovogen) and 0.05% sodium azide (NaN3) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Membranes were washed three times for 5 min in TBS-T. Membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-coupled secondary antibodies (Bio-Rrad) for 1 h (goat–anti-rabbit, 1:8000, 1705046; goat–anti-mouse, 1:8000, 1705047) and washed three times with TBST. Proteins were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (1705061/062, Bio-Rad) and imaged on an ImageQuant LAS-4000 biomolecular imager (Fujifilm). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ 2.0.0-rc-49/1.51d. Band intensities were measured within identical lane frames, background subtracted, normalized to loading control (GAPDH) and/or total tau, and expressed relative to control conditions. Uncropped images of immunoblots are included in the Supplementary Information.

Immunofluorescence

Mice were humanely killed and transcardially perfused with 1× PBS, immediately followed by 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, and brains were extracted and incubated in 4% (w/v) PFA overnight at 4 °C. Brains were processed in a HistoCore PEARL processor (Leica) for paraffin embedding. Sections (5 µm) were rehydrated and equilibrated in PBS pH7.4 before immunostaining. For cryosections, brains were transferred from PFA to PBS, followed by consecutive steps of 15% and 30% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C for 8 h or overnight with gentle rotation. Tissue was embedded in Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek USA Inc.), frozen in isopentane and stored at –80 °C. Cryosections were prepared at 30–40 μm thickness and stored in antigen preserve solution (50% ethylene glycol, 1% Polyvinyl pyrrolidone, 0.01% NaN3 in PBS). Rehydrated paraffin sections or floating cryosections were blocked with 2% BSA in PBS, then incubated at 4 °C for 16 h with primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary Table 2) diluted in 2% BSA. After PBS washes then 1 h incubation at RT with Alexa Fluor-conjugated goat or donkey secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) diluted 1:1000 in 2% BSA, sections were washed and mounted with Diamond Antifade (S36968, Invitrogen). 4′,6-diamidino-2′-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI; 1:2000; D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to visualize nuclei. Immunofluorescence was imaged digitally using a slide scanner VS-200 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) or a Nikon Eclipse Ci-S epifluorescence microscope with a Digital Sight-10 camera (Nikon). Percentage of labelled hippocampal cells were based on number of c-Fos+ or Egr-1+ cells counted in ImageJ (v1.53q) and normalized to DAPI+ nuclei in the dentate granule cell layer or hilus subfield. All cell counting was conducted blind to experimental group. For ACC analysis, fluorescent signals were thresholded to identify Egr-1⁺ and DAPI⁺ cells. Overlapping signals were determined using the ImageJ Calculator function, and the proportion of Egr-1⁺ cells as the number of nucleus-sized overlaps with circularity ≥0.4, divided by the total number of DAPI⁺ nuclei.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.2.2. Data normality was analysed with Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances with Bartlett’s and Brown–Forsythe tests. For comparisons between two independent groups, Unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test was applied. If variances were unequal, Welch’s t test was used. For non-parametric distributions, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied. For comparisons of two groups or more, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by Holm–Šidák post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. If variances were unequal, Welch’s ANOVA was performed, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg–Yekutieli correction. Data are presented as mean ± SEM for bar and line graphs, and 25th to 75th percentile including median with 10th to 90th percentile whiskers for box plots. Statistical test details are provided in Supplementary Data 3, 4. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05.
