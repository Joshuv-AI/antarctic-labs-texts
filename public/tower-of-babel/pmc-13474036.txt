Comparative Risk of Hyponatremia Among Patients Treated With Second-Generation Antipsychotics
Authors: Masakazu Hatano, Takenao Koseki, Takeo Saito, Shigeki Yamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDo risks of hyponatremia differ among individual second-generation antipsychotics?FindingsThis cohort study of 55 394 patients found that aripiprazole was associated with a lower risk of hyponatremia than is olanzapine.MeaningThese findings suggest that the risk of hyponatremia may differ among second-generation antipsychotics, potentially facilitating antipsychotic selection for patients at higher risk.

Introduction

Antipsychotics are the primary treatment for schizophrenia, as recommended in clinical guidelines.1 They also play a crucial role in maintenance therapy to prevent relapse.2 In addition to preventing relapse in patients with schizophrenia, these medications are commonly used for treating those with bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder.3,4 However, safety concerns have been raised, particularly regarding hyponatremia.5 Even mild hyponatremia has been associated with prolonged hospitalization, readmission, and mortality.6 Although previous studies have suggested an association between second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) and a lower risk of hyponatremia compared with first-generation antipsychotics,7 it remains unclear how this risk varies among SGAs.

Antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia occurs in 3% to 6% of patients.8,9 However, it remains difficult to evaluate between-drug differences in randomized clinical trials, as head-to-head studies of SGAs are limited and existing trials are typically underpowered to assess comparative safety outcomes. Furthermore, the occurrence of hyponatremia is influenced by multiple factors, complicating causal inferences regarding the risks associated with individual antipsychotics. For example, comorbidities, such as cerebrovascular disease, pulmonary disorders, and malignant neoplasm, are known to be associated with risk of hyponatremia.10 In addition, several medications—including antidepressants and antiepileptics—have also been reported to induce hyponatremia.11,12

To address these limitations, we used 2 large-scale, general-population databases to assess drug safety in clinical practice. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), a spontaneous reporting system (SRS) for adverse events, provides rapid screening with low computational burden and detects potential safety signals, particularly for rare outcomes. However, because of inherent reporting biases and the absence of denominator data, SRS findings cannot quantify risks and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.13,14 In contrast, claims databases systematically capture patient demographics, diagnoses, procedures, and prescription records longitudinally.15 They enable population-based assessment of drug safety, incidence rate estimation, and adjustment for key clinical characteristics. Therefore, claims databases are suited for conducting more robust analyses of clinical practice outcomes and evaluating preliminary findings from the SRS.16 Thus, SRS and claims databases serve complementary roles in pharmacovigilance. This study aimed to generate hypotheses regarding differences in the risk of hyponatremia among individual SGAs using the FAERS and to evaluate these associations in a large-scale Japanese claims database.

Methods

Study Design

This cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Reporting of a Disproportionality Analysis for Drug Safety Signal Detection Using Individual Case Safety Reports in Pharmacovigilance (READUS-PV)17 and Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data Statement for Pharmacoepidemiology (RECORD-PE)18 guidelines. As this study used anonymized personal information that had already been created, ethical approval and informed consent were not required because it was outside the scope of the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan. The studies were conducted in accordance with the local legislation and institutional requirements.

This study was conducted in 2 stages to investigate antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia. Hypotheses were generated using the FAERS database to assess the disproportionality of hyponatremia reports among individual antipsychotics. This was followed by hypothesis testing using a Japanese claims database with a retrospective cohort design. The following SGAs approved in Japan were analyzed: aripiprazole, asenapine, blonanserin, brexpiprazole, lurasidone, olanzapine, paliperidone, perospirone, quetiapine, and risperidone.

FAERS Database

Signal detection for antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia was evaluated using the Japan Pharmaceutical Information Center Adverse Reporting System, which is a curated FAERS-based database, in which data are systematically processed by the Japan Pharmaceutical Information Center. Preprocessing included the removal of duplicate case reports, the standardization of drug names using the World Health Organization Drug Dictionary and Drugs@FDA, and the updating of adverse event terms to the latest Japanese version of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities/Japanese. A relational database based on PRIMARYID was constructed, comprising 4 data tables: patient demographic information, drug information, adverse events, and indications. This study included adverse event case reports from the FAERS database from quarter 4 1997 to quarter 3 2023.

Adverse events were coded using preferred terms according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities/Japanese, version 28.0. Hyponatremia was defined on the basis of the narrow scope of the Standardized MedDRA Query for hyponatremia and/or syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (Standardized MedDRA Query code 20000141), which included 15 preferred terms (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

A disproportionality analysis was performed to detect antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia signals. Among the antipsychotics analyzed, olanzapine was selected as the reference drug since it has the largest number of hyponatremia reports in the FAERS, enables stable estimation of the reporting odds ratios (ORs), and is widely used in clinical practice. Reporting ORs and 95% CIs were calculated for each antipsychotic relative to olanzapine using 2 × 2 contingency tables constructed from the number of drug-event reports (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).13 Disproportionality was considered present when the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeded 1. Analyses were restricted to drug-event combinations with at least 3 reports to ensure stability of the estimates.19 To compare the disproportionality across individual antipsychotics, the analyses were restricted to reports involving antipsychotic monotherapy, excluding cases of concomitant use of multiple antipsychotics.

Japanese Claims Database

Hypotheses regarding antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia derived from the FAERS-based analysis were evaluated using a large-scale, hospital-based administrative claims database (Medical Data Vision Co, Ltd [MDV]) in Japan. As of April 2024, the MDV database contains administrative claims data for approximately 48 million patients from 515 hospitals participating in Japan’s Diagnosis Procedure Combination system. The Diagnosis Procedure Combination is a national case-mix classification and reimbursement framework for acute care hospitals in Japan. The diagnoses were coded according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10).

A new-user design was adopted in this study. Patients initiated on routinely prescribed antipsychotics (excluding as-needed prescriptions) between April 2008 and April 2024 were included in the analysis. The index date was defined as the date of the first antipsychotic prescription. A 180-day baseline observation period was required prior to the index date to ensure adequate continuous enrollment in the database. The inclusion criteria were an age of 18 to 69 years, at least 180 days of an active medical history preceding the index date, and no history of antipsychotic use or hyponatremia during the 180 days preceding the index date. The exclusion criteria included the prescription of multiple antipsychotics at the index date, treatment for hyponatremia within 180 days preceding the index date, a diagnosis of delirium during hospitalization that included the index date, or the use of antineoplastic agents or chemotherapy-related supportive medications within 180 days preceding the index date (to exclude patients receiving olanzapine as an antiemetic for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting). Detailed definitions of the inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in eTables 2 and 3 in Supplement 1. The study design diagram is illustrated in eFigure 2 in Supplement 1.20

The primary outcome was the incidence of hyponatremia, defined as any of the following: serum sodium level less than 135 mEq/L, syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ICD-10 code E22.2), or hypo-osmolality and hyponatremia (ICD-10 code E87.1). Patients were monitored for up to 180 days after antipsychotic treatment initiation (index date). This follow-up period was selected a priori to provide a clinically relevant observation window after treatment initiation, while limiting the influence of longer-term changes or switching of antipsychotic treatments. Patients were censored at the earliest occurrence of any of the following events: development of the study outcome, treatment discontinuation of the index antipsychotic, death, loss to follow-up (eg, transfer to another hospital or admission to a care facility), or completion of the 180-day follow-up window. Treatment discontinuation was defined as the absence of a subsequent prescription within a 14-day grace period after the estimated end date of the preceding prescription. Overlapping prescription durations were incorporated to account for potential stockpiling by extending the supply period for subsequent prescriptions.

Covariates were selected according to their clinical relevance and previously reported risk factors for hyponatremia.10,11,12,21 Demographic characteristics (age and sex) were assessed at the index date. Comorbidities and concomitant medications were identified using diagnostic and prescription records, respectively, within the 180-day covariate assessment window before the index date. Detailed definitions of the comorbidities and concomitant medications are provided in eTables 4 and 5 in Supplement 1, respectively. The care setting at the index date (inpatient or outpatient) was included to account for differences in the baseline clinical context that could influence antipsychotic selection. In addition, the number of serum sodium measurements within the same 180-day covariate assessment window was included as a proxy for baseline laboratory monitoring intensity, which may have affected the likelihood of hyponatremia detection.

Statistical Analysis

For the retrospective cohort study using the MDV database, to ensure statistical robustness, only antipsychotics with at least 1000 patients were included in the analysis. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as all eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included. The incidence of hyponatremia was calculated for each antipsychotic by dividing the number of events by the total person-years of follow-up. Incidence rates were expressed per 1000 person-years, with 95% CIs estimated assuming a Poisson distribution.

Pairwise comparisons with olanzapine were conducted for each antipsychotic. To account for potential confounding due to differences in baseline clinical characteristics across treatment groups, we applied propensity score methods. Propensity scores were estimated using separate logistic regression models for each pairwise comparison with olanzapine, including all measured baseline covariates. Continuous variables were modeled linearly, and categorical variables were included as indicator variables. The average treatment effect was estimated using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting.22 Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences, with an absolute standardized mean difference less than 0.1 indicating acceptable balance.23 Weight trimming or truncation of stabilized weights was reserved only for cases with extreme weight instability.

Cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) for the association between antipsychotics and hyponatremia. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Crude HRs were calculated without weighting, whereas adjusted HRs were estimated using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting, with add-on use of another antipsychotic modeled as a time-varying covariate. Robust SEs were used to calculate the 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as a 95% CI that did not include the null value of 1.

Stratified analyses were performed according to whether antipsychotic treatment was initiated in an inpatient or outpatient setting to assess whether the findings were consistent across care settings. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of primary outcomes. To capture severe hyponatremia, the plasma sodium threshold was lowered from less than 135 mEq/L to less than 130 mEq/L. In addition, the definition of treatment discontinuation was varied by altering the grace period from 14 to 7 or 28 days. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.5.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Disproportionality Analysis

A total of 17 805 418 reports were recorded in the FAERS database, of which 458 745 involved monotherapy with an SGA. The antipsychotics most frequently associated with hyponatremia were olanzapine (1421 reports), quetiapine (1274 reports), and risperidone (1231 reports). Compared with olanzapine, the 7 antipsychotics showed significantly lower reporting ORs (RORs) for hyponatremia; blonanserin and perospirone could not be evaluated due to fewer than 3 reports. Among them, brexpiprazole (ROR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.08-0.19) and lurasidone (ROR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.14-0.24) showed the lowest RORs. Retrospective Cohort Study

The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 2. Antipsychotics were routinely prescribed to 961 010 patients, 88 741 of whom met the inclusion criteria. After applying the exclusion criteria, 55 394 patients were included in the final cohort (Figure 1). The mean (SD) age was 50.4 (14.3) years, 25 839 (46.6%) were male, and 29 555 (53.4%) were female. Of these, 31 723 (57.2%) were inpatients. Schizophrenia was the most prevalent comorbidity (20 695 patients [37.4%]), followed by major depressive disorder (10 859 patients [19.6%]). Among the concomitant medications, proton pump inhibitors were the most frequently prescribed (15 839 patients [28.6%]), followed by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (14 611 patients [26.4%]). The mean (SD) follow-up duration was 60.2 (58.9) days. End of follow-up was most commonly due to discontinuation of the index antipsychotic (42 028 patients [75.9%]), followed by the end of the 180-day observation period (8041 patients [14.5%]) and transfer to another facility (3789 patients [6.8%]). Death occurred in 1170 patients (2.1%). Hyponatremia occurred in 366 patients (0.7%) and was defined as the study outcome.

Incidence Rates of Hyponatremia

Pairwise comparisons with olanzapine were conducted for aripiprazole, blonanserin, brexpiprazole, perospirone, quetiapine, and risperidone. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated across all pairwise comparisons using Schoenfeld residuals. No evidence of violation was observed in any model. The stabilized weights had mean values close to 1.00 across all comparison groups (range, 0.995-1.001). The 99th percentile of the weights ranged from 1.61 to 3.32, whereas the maximum weight ranged from 3.84 to 19.22 across comparisons, suggesting limited influence of extreme weights. The effective sample sizes ranged from 73.4% to 94.4% of the original sample sizes. Therefore, weight trimming or truncation was not applied. Covariate balance was achieved across comparisons, with absolute standardized mean differences generally below 0.1. Love plots showing covariate balance before and after weighting are presented in eFigure 3 in Supplement 1. In the unweighted analysis, the incidence of hyponatremia was significantly lower with brexpiprazole (crude HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.11-0.85) and aripiprazole (crude HR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.23-0.48) than with olanzapine (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1).

After weighting, aripiprazole remained significantly associated with a lower risk of hyponatremia (adjusted HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.35-0.76), whereas brexpiprazole no longer showed a significant difference (adjusted HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.13-1.09). The remaining antipsychotic drugs did not significantly differ from olanzapine (Figure 2). The primary findings were generally consistent across various sensitivity analyses. In stratified analyses according to whether antipsychotic treatment was initiated in an inpatient or outpatient setting, similar associations were observed in both strata, although statistical significance was reached only in the inpatient stratum (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Consistent findings were also observed under alternative plasma sodium thresholds and grace periods for treatment discontinuation (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this cohort study, we investigated antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia using complementary evidence from an SRS (FAERS) and a large-scale Japanese claims database. We observed lower RORs for hyponatremia among several antipsychotics compared with olanzapine. These findings are hypothesis-generating owing to the limitations of the SRS data; however, they provided a rationale for subsequent hypothesis testing in a claims-based cohort study.

In a retrospective analysis using the MDV database, which used a new-user design and stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting to minimize confounding, only aripiprazole showed a significantly lower risk of hyponatremia, a finding that was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. The lack of significant differences among other antipsychotics in the claims database may reflect improved control for measured confounders compared with SRS; however, smaller sample sizes for individual agents may have reduced statistical power, potentially obscuring smaller differences. Collectively, although differences in other agents could not be detected, the results suggest an association between aripiprazole and a lower risk of hyponatremia compared with olanzapine in routine clinical practice. These findings can facilitate the selection of antipsychotics in patients with risk factors for hyponatremia, such as underlying conditions predisposing to hyponatremia or concomitant use of medications associated with sodium imbalance.

Pharmacovigilance and observational studies indicate that antipsychotics may be linked to a higher risk of hyponatremia, although evidence of differences among specific drugs remains limited. A VigiBase case-noncase study reported a significant pharmacovigilance signal for hyponatremia with antipsychotics overall, supporting the likelihood of an association.24 Population-based cohort studies from Canada and Taiwan have also reported high risks of hyponatremia among antipsychotic users, further supporting a class-level association.25,26 Meanwhile, a Swedish population-based case-control study reported no significant increase in risk for aripiprazole, consistent with our finding of a lower relative risk compared with olanzapine.27

Lurasidone also showed a relatively low ROR in the FAERS analysis; however, it was not included in the claims database analysis since the number of eligible patients did not reach a predefined threshold. Therefore, whether the lower disproportionality observed in the FAERS reflects a true difference in risk could not be evaluated in the cohort analysis.

In contrast, blonanserin and perospirone had insufficient reporting in the FAERS. These agents are not approved in the US and are primarily used in Japan and a few Asian countries; therefore, disproportionality for these agents could not be reliably evaluated.

Although the mechanisms underlying antipsychotic-associated hyponatremia remain unclear, previous studies have proposed the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion as a potential mechanism.5,11 Antipsychotics may promote excess antidiuretic hormone release through dopamine D2 receptor hypersensitivity, and serotonin-mediated effects.11,21 Olanzapine exhibits broad antagonism at multiple neurotransmitter receptors, including dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2 receptors,28,29 both of which have been implicated in the regulation of antidiuretic hormone secretion. In addition, the anticholinergic effects of psychotropic medications, including olanzapine, may indirectly contribute to increased free water intake as a result of dry mouth.30,31,32 In contrast, aripiprazole, a partial D2 receptor agonist,33 may exert a smaller effect on this pathway, thereby partly explaining the lower risk of hyponatremia observed in our analysis. These pharmacological considerations may account for the observed differences in hyponatremia risk among antipsychotics; however, these hypotheses remain speculative and were not evaluated in this study.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, analyses using FAERS data are subject to limitations inherent to the SRS. The FAERS does not provide denominator data, precluding the estimation of incidence or absolute risk.14 Patient characteristics (eg, age and sex) and concomitant medications are often incomplete and comorbidities are generally unavailable, limiting the ability to adjust for confounding factors. Second, although claims databases can overcome some limitations of spontaneous reporting systems, several limitations remain. The outcomes were defined using ICD-10 codes rather than clinical assessments, which may have led to misclassification. Hyponatremia events may not have been fully captured without routine laboratory testing, potentially underestimating incidence. Claims data also lack information on key clinical factors, such as fluid intake, psychogenic polydipsia, disease severity, and medication adherence. In addition, confounding by indication may persist because of differences in baseline severity and treatment selection. Outcome ascertainment may also differ across treatment groups because of differences in health care utilization and monitoring intensity. Consequently, residual confounding and detection bias may have contributed to the observed association in favor of aripiprazole, which should, therefore, be interpreted with caution. Third, although a new-user design was applied using available claims history, prior antipsychotic exposure outside participating hospitals could not be fully ascertained. Therefore, some patients classified as incident users may in fact represent prevalent users, potentially leading to exposure misclassification.34 Fourth, the findings may also have limited generalizability, as the MDV database primarily captures data from acute-care hospitals in Japan. Fifth, multiple pairwise comparisons against olanzapine were conducted without formal adjustment for multiplicity, which may have increased the family-wise type I error rate. Sixth, since these findings were derived from observational data, causal relationships could not be established.

Conclusions

In this pharmacovigilance and retrospective cohort study of patients treated with antipsychotics, we observed a differential risk of hyponatremia among individual antipsychotics using 2 independent data sources: a pharmacovigilance database and a general-population Japanese claims database. After adjustment for measured confounders, aripiprazole was associated with a lower risk of hyponatremia than was olanzapine in multiple analyses. However, residual confounding and differential outcome ascertainment should be considered, and further studies are warranted to clarify the association between individual antipsychotics and hyponatremia. These findings may help inform clinical decision-making for patients at higher risk of hyponatremia.
