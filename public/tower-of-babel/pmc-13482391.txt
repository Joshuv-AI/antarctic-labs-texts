System design and data driven policy simulation for organ procurement networks using an OPO centre operations management framework
Authors: Ziyue Wang, Mingxing Li, Zhiqiang Ma, Mohammad Heydari, Hongzheng Sun, Fakhrullah Fakhrullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthcare organ procurement and transplantation function as a multi‑actor operations system in which governance rules and incentive structures shape organisational behaviour and contribute to persistent regional inequities. This study develops a data‑driven, simulation‑based framework for evaluating and redesigning incentive mechanisms within the US Organ Procurement Organization (OPO)–transplant centre network. Using SRTR‑style centre‑level activity data, we construct a Java‑based policy‑simulation environment that models transplant‑centre responses under two governance regimes: a status‑quo environment and a reform regime incorporating equity‑weighted incentive adjustments. Centre utility is formulated from observed transplant volumes, regional characteristics, and operating cost components, while system performance is assessed using aggregate welfare and distributional equity metrics. Simulation experiments reveal quantifiable equity–efficiency trade‑offs and identify parameter regions where moderate incentive redesign improves regional equity with minimal loss in overall system performance. By integrating system design, behavioural response modelling, and incentive analysis, the framework provides regulators with a transparent tool for testing policy reforms and anticipating operational consequences in organ procurement networks.Keywords: Healthcare systems, System design, Simulation and modelling, Decision-making, Behavioral operations research, Organ procurement operations, Transplant network governanceSubject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction: when system design becomes a life-critical game

Every day, people with end-stage organ failure wait for a phone call that may not come. Yet the availability of donated organs is only the visible endpoint of a much larger operations system: a multi‑organisational network that identifies donors, recovers organs, coordinates allocation, and matches time‑critical resources to patients under extreme uncertainty. In such a setting, system design is never neutral. The rules, metrics, and incentives that govern organ procurement networks shape organisational behaviour, influence clinical decisions, and ultimately determine who receives life‑saving care and when. When performance measures and governance mechanisms are misaligned with operational realities, the system can drift toward inequity, inefficiency, and unintended consequences1,2.

This paper is motivated by a central operations-management question: how can we design and stress-test policy mechanisms for organ procurement networks so that they improve both efficiency and equity under strategic, behaviourally realistic responses? The contemporary organ procurement landscape is not merely a technical allocation problem; it is a socio-technical system in which OPOs, transplant centres, and regulators adapt to policy signals, perceived risks, compliance costs, reputational pressures, and local resource constraints. These actors do not respond as frictionless optimisers. They respond as boundedly rational decision makers operating under time pressure, incomplete information, and incentives that can encourage risk aversion, selective behaviour, or metric gaming. This is precisely the domain of behavioural operations research, which studies how real decision makers behave in operational systems and how those behavioural responses should inform system design3,4.

Although the primary focus of this study is the legitimate OPO–centre network, broader discussions of transplant tourism and organ trafficking highlight an important contextual insight: structural weaknesses in legitimate procurement systems—capacity imbalance, fragmented coordination, and poorly calibrated incentives—can create inequities that enforcement alone cannot resolve5,6. These issues serve as motivation rather than analytical focus. They underscore the need for policy mechanisms that (1) recognise strategic adaptation by organisations, (2) quantify efficiency–equity trade-offs, and (3) allow policymakers to evaluate reforms before deploying them in a life-critical environment.

To address this need, we frame organ procurement as a networked healthcare operations system in which outcomes emerge from interactions among multiple decision makers. We propose a data-driven policy simulation laboratory that integrates (a) system-level performance modelling and (b) a behavioural response layer capturing incentive-responsive organisational choices. This hybrid perspective enables evaluation of how policy levers—such as transparency requirements, performance-based incentives, and equity-weighted adjustments—propagate through procurement operations and reshape equilibrium behaviour. The goal is not to replace ethical judgement with equations, but to translate ethical objectives (equity, protection, integrity) into measurable operational outcomes and implementable design levers.

Problem statement

Despite sustained regulatory attention and ethical concern, organ procurement systems continue to exhibit persistent regional inequities and uneven operational performance. A central operational challenge is that policy reforms are typically articulated as high-level goals—such as improving equity, increasing utilisation, or reducing delays—but implemented through governance mechanisms such as metrics, reporting requirements, penalties, and incentives. These mechanisms reshape organisational behaviour in complex and sometimes unintended ways. OPOs and transplant centres operate under scarcity, time pressure, and heterogeneous local constraints, and they respond to policy signals with bounded rationality, risk aversion, reputational considerations, and compliance frictions. As a result, interventions that appear normatively desirable can induce strategic adaptation—such as conservative procurement behaviour, selective acceptance patterns, or metric gaming—that degrades system efficiency or redistributes inequity rather than reducing it.

The problem addressed in this study is therefore how to design and evaluate policy mechanisms for organ procurement networks that jointly improve operational efficiency and equity when key actors adapt strategically and behaviourally to incentives and constraints. Policymakers currently lack an ex-ante, data-driven decision-support framework that (1) represents the OPO–centre network as a healthcare operations system, (2) links policy levers to operational decisions and outcomes, (3) incorporates behaviourally realistic organisational responses, and (4) quantifies the resulting equity–efficiency trade-offs under alternative governance regimes. In the absence of such a framework, reform efforts remain reactive and fragmented, and the system continues to oscillate between moral aspiration and operational under-delivery.

What is already known on this topic

Organ procurement and transplantation are widely recognised as complex healthcare systems characterised by scarcity, time sensitivity, and high operational risk. A substantial body of research examines allocation rules, matching processes, and clinical outcomes, and policy analyses have documented persistent inequities across regions and patient groups5,6. These studies highlight that system performance is shaped not only by medical factors but also by organisational behaviour and structural constraints. From a broader ethical and policy perspective, discussions of transplant tourism and organ trafficking underscore how systemic imbalances, weak oversight, and uneven access can create conditions in which vulnerable populations face heightened risk7,8. While these issues are not the analytical focus of this study, they reinforce the importance of designing legitimate procurement systems that minimise inequity and strengthen institutional integrity.

Despite this extensive literature, two gaps remain salient from an operations-management standpoint. First, many policy and regulatory interventions are evaluated retrospectively through descriptive indicators or compliance audits rather than through ex-ante mechanism testing that anticipates how organisations will adapt when incentives change. In practice, OPOs and transplant centres may respond to new metrics, penalties, or reporting rules in ways that are locally rational but systemically harmful—such as increased risk aversion, selective acceptance behaviour, or strategic reclassification. Without modelling these behavioural responses, reforms can underperform or generate unintended inequities even when the policy intent is ethically sound3,4.

Second, although prior studies acknowledge the role of incentives, comparatively few provide a quantitative, system-level framework that simultaneously (1) represents the organ procurement network structure, (2) links policy levers to operational decision variables, (3) incorporates behavioural realism (bounded rationality, fairness concerns, compliance frictions), and (4) evaluates outcomes using both efficiency and equity criteria. As a result, a persistent gap remains between moral aspiration—“increase fairness and protect the vulnerable”—and operational capability—“which mechanism, under what behavioural responses, improves equity without degrading system performance?”7,8.

What this study adds

This study contributes a system-design and behavioural-operations framework for organ procurement networks that functions as a practical policy “wind tunnel.” Its core contribution is a hybrid simulation–game model that treats OPOs and transplant centres as strategic, incentive-responsive actors embedded in a regulated healthcare network. The framework integrates three components.

First, we construct a network-level operational representation of procurement and transplantation activity using SRTR-style centre-level data, including volumes, regional characteristics, and operational constraints. This operational layer enables outcomes to be evaluated at both local (centre/OPO) and system (national/network) levels, allowing direct measurement of productivity, efficiency proxies, and distributional outcomes.

Second, we incorporate a behavioural response module grounded in behavioural operations research. Organisations are modelled as boundedly rational decision makers who respond to incentive changes under perceived risk, compliance burden, reputational pressure, and fairness considerations. Rather than assuming perfect optimisation, the framework allows realistic adaptation—such as conservative behaviour under high penalties, asymmetric sensitivity to losses versus gains, or heterogeneous responses across regions. This behavioural layer is essential for anticipating unintended consequences and designing mechanisms robust to strategic adaptation3,4.

Third, we evaluate policy regimes as governance mechanisms—such as transparency requirements, performance-based incentives, and equity-weighted adjustments—implemented as parameters that reshape payoffs, constraints, and feasible actions. By quantifying the resulting equilibrium behaviour and system trajectories under alternative policy designs, the model provides interpretable evidence on the equity–efficiency frontier. The resulting decision-support tool enables policymakers to test reforms, compare mechanisms, and identify parameter regions where equity improvements can be achieved without disproportionate losses in aggregate performance.

Taken together, the study reframes organ procurement reform as an operations-management problem of mechanism design under behavioural adaptation. It offers a tractable, data-driven framework for improving healthcare system performance while explicitly addressing ethical objectives through measurable operational outcomes. Within a principal–agent perspective, the proposed mechanism can be interpreted as a performance contract in which the regulator (principal) specifies output-based rewards, equity-weighted adjustments, and compliance requirements, while OPOs and transplant centres (agents) choose operational actions—procurement effort, acceptance thresholds, coordination intensity, and reporting behaviour—subject to local constraints and behavioural frictions. The simulation–game laboratory evaluates how alternative contract parameterisations reshape equilibrium behaviour and trace the resulting equity–efficiency frontier, thereby supporting ex-ante mechanism design for life-critical healthcare operations.

From a governance and operations-management perspective, the proposed reform can be interpreted as a stylised incentive–contract mechanism between the regulator (principal) and network actors (agents), namely OPOs and transplant centres. The regulator specifies a performance contract that combines (1) output-based rewards linked to clinically meaningful throughput and utilisation, (2) equity-weighted adjustments that internalise distributional objectives, and (3) compliance and transparency requirements that impose expected costs on non-cooperative or opportunistic behaviour. OPOs and centres then choose operational actions—procurement effort, acceptance thresholds, coordination intensity, and reporting behaviour—given local constraints and behaviourally realistic frictions such as risk aversion, reputational concerns, and bounded rationality. Within this principal–agent framing, our simulation–game laboratory evaluates how alternative contract parameterisations reshape equilibrium behaviour and trace the resulting equity–efficiency frontier, thereby supporting ex-ante mechanism design for life-critical healthcare operations.

This specification defines the core components of the model—centre payoffs, regime-dependent volume adjustments, behavioural response functions, and equilibrium conditions—that together determine how transplant centres adapt to incentive changes. The formalisation provides the analytical foundation for the simulation environment and ensures that policy experiments are reproducible, interpretable, and grounded in explicit mechanism-design logic.

Agents, regimes, and decision variables

The simulation represents strategic interaction within an organ procurement network by modelling transplant centres as incentive-responsive decision makers operating under a governance environment defined at the OPO/DSA level. Two policy regimes are considered. The Status Quo (SQ) regime reflects baseline procurement and acceptance behaviour under existing DSA-linked governance and performance oversight. The Reform (RF) regime represents a counterfactual post-reform environment in which the governance mechanism is modified—such as through geography-attenuated allocation rules, DSA consolidation, and/or equity-weighted incentives—thereby altering the payoff and constraint environment faced by centres.

Each transplant centre is treated as an independent agent that selects operational and compliance-related decision variables (e.g., effort intensity, acceptance posture, coordination orientation, and transparency/compliance stance) in response to regime-specific incentives and behaviourally realistic frictions (risk aversion, reputational pressure, and compliance burden). OPOs are represented as the governance and network layer that shapes centres’ feasible actions and payoffs through region-specific parameters and structural attributes (e.g., DSA membership and inter-regional spillovers). Where needed, OPOs can also be modelled as explicit strategic actors through the optional multi-OPO interaction module, enabling analysis of cross-region behavioural interdependence under national-level policy change.

The current framework does not incorporate patient-level clinical constraints such as waitlist heterogeneity, medical urgency or acuity, blood type or Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) compatibility, organ-specific matching rules, or patient-level acceptance decisions. These elements are central to real transplant allocation but fall outside the scope of the present centre-level governance and incentive model. The objective of the framework is to examine organisational behavioural responses to policy and incentive changes, rather than to simulate clinical matching or patient-level allocation dynamics. The components described in “A Principal–agent mechanism for OPO–centre incentives and policy compliance–Payoff formulation and system-level welfare aggregation” sections constitute the implemented simulation model; all other analytical modules referenced in the manuscript are optional extensions not used to generate the reported results.

Methods: data, simulation design, and implementation

Data source and pre-processing

The computational framework is parameterised using an SRTR-style, centre-level activity dataset representing organ procurement and transplant activity across U.S. Organ Procurement Organizations (OPOs) and their affiliated transplant centres. The dataset contains centre-level transplant volumes for kidney, pancreas, liver, intestine, heart, and lung procedures, together with indicators capturing donation-service-area (DSA) membership and related network attributes9,10. To ensure behavioural comparability across agents, we retained only centres with non-zero transplant activity over the observation window, thereby avoiding degenerate strategy profiles driven by structurally inactive units.

All records were ingested directly into the Java simulation environment via a dedicated CSV loading and validation module. This module performs type-safe parsing, standardises field formats, applies basic missing-value handling rules, and aggregates organ-specific volumes into a unified centre-level activity metric used to parameterise baseline payoffs and capacity-relevant constraints. The pre-processing pipeline was designed to be deterministic and auditable, ensuring that identical raw inputs yield identical simulation initialisation and enabling reproducible policy experiments across alternative governance regimes.

Simulation design and scenario construction

The simulation is designed as a policy “wind tunnel” that compares organisational responses and system outcomes under alternative governance regimes. Two primary regimes are examined: a status quo configuration that represents baseline incentive and oversight conditions, and a reform configuration that introduces mechanism adjustments intended to improve equity and accountability while preserving operational viability. Each regime is instantiated by a parameter vector that governs the relative strength of incentive rewards, compliance and transparency burdens, risk-sharing provisions, and any equity-weighting applied to performance evaluation.

Within each regime, OPOs and transplant centres are modelled as boundedly rational, incentive-responsive agents who select strategies from a predefined action set (e.g., effort intensity, acceptance posture, coordination orientation, and compliance stance). Scenario construction proceeds by systematically varying the policy parameters over a structured grid to identify regions in which (1) aggregate performance improves, (2) distributional inequality declines, and (3) behavioural outcomes remain stable under plausible perturbations. This design supports not only pairwise comparison between “baseline vs reform”, but also the mapping of an equity–efficiency frontier across governance settings.

For each scenario, the simulator executes repeated runs under identical initialisation rules and records centre-level and system-level outputs. Outputs include: (a) welfare and productivity proxies, (b) distributional measures (reported using Gini-based metrics in Appendix C), and (c) behavioural indicators such as the prevalence of cooperative strategies, the incidence of risk-averse retreat, and the degree of strategic substitution across agents. Sensitivity checks are embedded by perturbing key behavioural and governance parameters (e.g., detection probability, protection strength, compliance cost) to ensure that reported conclusions are not driven by fragile calibrations.

Computational implementation in java

The modelling framework is implemented as a modular Java application that separates data ingestion, agent behaviour, policy specification, and output analytics into distinct components. The core architecture comprises (1) a data layer responsible for loading and validating SRTR-style centre records, (2) a policy layer that encapsulates regime definitions and parameter vectors, (3) an agent layer representing OPO and centre decision logic, and (4) a simulation engine that executes scenario runs, aggregates outcomes, and exports results for downstream analysis and visualisation.

Agent decisions are computed using deterministic routines parameterised by regime settings and behavioural frictions. The simulator maintains a transparent audit trail by logging scenario identifiers, parameter values, random seeds (when stochasticity is enabled), and intermediate outputs at both the agent and system levels. Results are exported in standard formats (CSV/JSON) to facilitate replication, independent validation, and optional post-processing in external tools (e.g., Python for visual analytics). The implementation is designed for extensibility: new strategies, payoff components, or governance levers can be added without altering the overall workflow, enabling iterative policy testing aligned with evolving regulatory proposals.

Optional analytic extensions: equilibrium and behavioural dynamics

To move beyond static regime comparisons, the prototype supports optional analytic modules that evaluate whether a proposed reform is strategically viable and behaviourally stable. One extension computes equilibrium outcomes under a Nash equilibrium concept for centre strategy switching, enabling verification that predicted behavioural patterns are consistent with stable strategic responses rather than artefacts of a single assumed decision rule (details reported in Appendix D). A second extension implements evolutionary-style behavioural dynamics (e.g., replicator updates) that allow strategies to evolve over repeated interactions, thereby approximating learning, adaptation, and diffusion of behaviours within the procurement network.

Additional extensions include payoff-matrix export for inspection and external validation, configurable fairness metrics (including distributional inequality indices), and multi-OPO interaction routines that represent spillovers and competitive pressures across neighbouring regions. These modules are activated as needed depending on the analytical objective: equilibrium analysis is used to test incentive compatibility and stability; evolutionary dynamics are used to examine adjustment paths and convergence; and fairness modules are used to quantify how policy changes redistribute benefits and burdens across centres and regions. Collectively, these extensions transform the simulator from a single-run evaluator into a flexible analytical laboratory for mechanism design in regulated healthcare operations.

To support extended analyses beyond baseline welfare comparisons, the modelling framework includes several optional modules that can be activated depending on the policy question. First, a Nash equilibrium module implements a discrete strategy-space equilibrium routine to assess whether, under each regime, any centre has an incentive to deviate from a candidate strategy profile. This provides a stability check that complements welfare comparisons by indicating whether an observed outcome is strategically viable rather than purely artefactual.

Second, we incorporate evolutionary replicator dynamics to represent behavioural adaptation over time rather than instantaneous optimisation. Strategy proportions evolve according to the continuous-time process in Eq. (5), which updates the prevalence of a strategy based on whether its payoff exceeds the population-average payoff. This dynamic is used to examine adjustment paths, convergence behaviour, and the persistence of regimes under boundedly rational learning.

Third, the framework supports multiple fairness and concentration metrics to quantify distributional impacts of policy regimes. In addition to the Gini coefficient (reported in detail in Appendix C), we compute an inter-OPO inequality index and volume concentration measures to test whether reforms reduce or exacerbate disparities across regions and organisational units. These measures ensure that policy evaluation is not limited to aggregate welfare and can explicitly account for distributional objectives.

Fourth, an extended multi-OPO strategic interaction module allows OPO-level competition and spillover effects to be modelled explicitly, rather than treating centres as isolated decision makers. This extension is particularly relevant when national reforms induce cross-region externalities, strategic substitution, or neighbouring-region responses that cannot be captured in purely centre-local analyses.

Finally, a visualisation and reporting module supports reproducible export of model outputs for inspection and communication. Outputs can be rendered through JavaFX (e.g., bar plots and Lorenz curves) or exported to Python scripts and CSV matrices, including payoff matrices and welfare summary tables, enabling independent validation and policy-facing reporting.

Verification and sensitivity testing

Model verification and robustness testing were conducted to ensure numerical correctness, internal consistency, and stability of conclusions under plausible parameter variation. Verification began with manual inspection of computed volumes and payoffs to confirm correct implementation of regime logic and payoff evaluation, followed by cross-validation against independently computed results in Python. Robustness testing then perturbed the key weights and parameters (including ) and examined whether regime rankings and qualitative behavioural patterns persisted. Additional boundary-case stress tests were performed for centres with extremely high or low baseline volumes to confirm that the simulator behaves sensibly under heterogeneous scale. Across tested scenarios, the model exhibited stable behaviour, supporting its use as a policy-simulation laboratory for evaluating incentive and governance reforms under behavioural adaptation.

Validation, robustness, and policy implications

Validation focuses on the regime-adjusted payoff formulation in Eqs. (1)–(2) and the welfare aggregation in Eq. (3), together with the optional stability checks reported in Appendix D. Validation focuses on (1) the regime-adjusted payoff specification (Eqs. (1)–(2)) and welfare aggregation (Eq. (3)), (2) institutional consistency with the U.S. OPO/DSA topology, and (3) robustness of regime comparisons under plausible uncertainty in governance and behavioural parameters.

To establish both analytical credibility and operational relevance, the proposed Java-based policy-simulation framework was validated through three complementary stages. First, we conducted face validation via structured expert review to assess whether the payoff components, strategic options, and behavioural assumptions reflect the institutional realities faced by transplant centres and Organ Procurement Organizations (OPOs). This step focused on internal plausibility—namely whether modelled incentives, compliance frictions, and risk considerations generate decision patterns that domain experts recognise as consistent with how regulated healthcare organisations respond to performance oversight and policy change.

Second, we performed cross-verification of the logistics and cost-flow components embedded in the optimisation layer, ensuring that simulated flows and implied operational constraints remain consistent with the structure of the US procurement system. In particular, the model is aligned to the donation-service-area structure operated by the 56 OPOs certified in the United States[As of recent restructuring, the U.S. organ procurement system comprises 56 Organ Procurement Organizations (OPOs) designated to serve defined Donation Service Areas (DSAs); this total reflects the post-merger configuration reported by OPTN/HRSA and associated regulatory documentation.] (reflecting the post-merger total used in recent reporting). This verification step functions as a systems-level sanity check: it confirms that policy experiments are executed on an institutional topology that reflects the regulated procurement network rather than an abstracted, context-free marketplace.

Third, we assessed robustness and equilibrium stability under targeted perturbations of key behavioural and governance parameters. Specifically, we conducted sensitivity tests under ±10% changes in detection probability and legal-protection strength, examining whether equilibrium outcomes and regime rankings are preserved under plausible uncertainty. These tests are crucial in behavioural operations settings, where small parameter changes can sometimes trigger discontinuous shifts in strategy choice (e.g., risk-averse retreat or compliance-driven conservatism). Across these perturbations, the model exhibits stable comparative insights, supporting its use as a policy “wind tunnel” rather than a fragile point-estimate tool.

From a computational standpoint, the framework is tractable for policy analysis. Symbolic and numerical equilibrium routines (where applicable) execute rapidly, indicating that the mechanism-design layer can be embedded within broader scenario sweeps and stress tests without prohibitive computational overhead. Accordingly, the contribution is not only conceptual; it is implementable as a decision-support workflow that can support iterative policy design, parameter exploration, and stakeholder-facing reporting.

The resulting policy implications align with international governance priorities that emphasise voluntary donation, transparency, traceability, and ethical oversight as foundations of transplantation systems11–13. In particular, the framework complements surveillance-oriented initiatives such as the Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation (GODT) by adding an ex-ante mechanism-testing capability: while observatory infrastructures focus on monitoring and reporting aggregated activity, the present model evaluates how alternative governance mechanisms may reshape organisational behaviour and system outcomes before real-world deployment7.

Practically, the framework can be implemented through pilot analytical units within transplant authorities or regulatory bodies, tasked with testing reform options as incentive/contract mechanisms. This includes simulating alternative incentive weights, transparency obligations, and protection provisions; identifying parameter regions that improve equity without disproportionate efficiency loss; and presenting outputs through policy dashboards that compare regimes on system throughput, equity metrics, and behavioural stability. Such integration moves policy evaluation from reactive enforcement and post hoc audits to proactive mechanism design under behavioural adaptation, which is increasingly necessary in regulated healthcare operations14.

Finally, the model reinforces a key behavioural OR insight: persistent problems are often sustained not by irrationality, but by predictable responses to misaligned incentives and constraints. In this sense, effective reform is achieved less by escalating penalties alone and more by redesigning governance instruments so that cooperative, equitable behaviour becomes strategically attractive and operationally feasible. This shifts the policy stance from moral reaction to operational transformation, using equilibrium logic as a structured way to anticipate organisational adaptation and to align equity objectives with workable performance mechanisms15–17. Equity impacts are quantified using Gini-based measures (Appendix C), while strategic viability under centre strategy switching is examined via the Nash equilibrium module (Appendix D).

Discussion: governance mechanisms, behavioural stability, and the equity–efficiency frontier

A central lesson from game theory is that stability is not synonymous with fairness. A system can settle into a predictable pattern of behaviour—an equilibrium—while still producing outcomes that violate widely accepted equity principles. Nevertheless, equilibrium reasoning is valuable for policy design precisely because it highlights what is likely to persist under given incentives and constraints. In organ procurement operations, persistent disparities and uneven performance need not be interpreted solely as ethical failure or administrative weakness; they can also be understood as the expected consequence of a governance environment in which actors optimise locally under risk, scarcity, and institutional pressure. This paper argues that meaningful reform requires not only new targets, but a redesign of the mechanisms through which targets are implemented—so that desired outcomes become behaviourally and operationally sustainable16,17.

The formal models presented in Appendixes A–D—including the trafficking-related justice game, cross-border logistics formulations, and Stackelberg deterrence structures—serve as conceptual background illustrating broader mechanism-design applications. They are not part of the implemented OPO–centre simulation. All empirical results, welfare comparisons, and behavioural analyses reported in the main manuscript are generated solely from the centre-level incentive mechanism described in “System design framework: incentive/contract mechanism and behavioural response” section. The results reported in this study are generated solely from the implemented centre-level incentive mechanism described in “System design framework: incentive/contract mechanism and behavioural response” section. Optional modules—such as equilibrium solvers, evolutionary dynamics, and visualisation tools—are available within the prototype but are not used in the empirical analysis. Proposed extensions (e.g., multi-period learning, patient-level constraints) are discussed as future work rather than components of the current implementation.

From an operations-management perspective, the core contribution of the proposed framework is to treat policy reform as a contract/incentive design problem under behavioural adaptation. The model links governance instruments—equity-weighted incentives, transparency obligations, and compliance costs—to operational choices such as procurement effort, acceptance thresholds, coordination intensity, and reporting behaviour. This connection matters because it is often the behavioural response to policy (rather than policy intent itself) that determines whether reforms improve or degrade system performance. When penalties are perceived as severe or ambiguous, centres may become more conservative; when reporting burdens rise without commensurate support, organisations may prioritise metric compliance over operational improvement; when incentives are narrowly defined, strategic substitution and selection effects can emerge. By explicitly modelling these pathways, the framework helps distinguish reforms that are merely desirable in principle from those that remain viable under realistic behavioural frictions.

The Java-based OPO–centre strategic simulation developed in this study provides a computationally transparent environment for evaluating such reforms before implementation. Grounded in an SRTR-style dataset, the prototype captures heterogeneity across centres and regions—differences in volume, geographic position, and network structure—so that policy effects are not averaged away. This enables a granular analysis of distributional impacts and behavioural shifts that are difficult to infer from descriptive statistics alone. Crucially, the simulation supports the evaluation of efficiency–equity trade-offs as a frontier rather than a single headline outcome: policymakers can examine parameter regions in which equity improves with minimal efficiency loss, identify where reforms may unintentionally concentrate volume or burden, and detect threshold effects where behavioural responses sharply change.

A further strength of the prototype is its modular extensibility, which allows the analytical lens to match the policy question. Optional components—including Nash equilibrium computation, evolutionary replicator dynamics, payoff-matrix export, and visualisation via JavaFX or Python—enable researchers to test whether a proposed governance design is not only welfare-improving in a static comparison, but also strategically stable and behaviourally plausible over time. In particular, equilibrium and switching analyses (reported in Appendix D) support the interpretation that observed outcomes arise from coherent strategic responses rather than arbitrary behavioural assumptions. Similarly, embedding formal distributional metrics—such as the Gini coefficient (Appendix C)—moves equity assessment from rhetoric to measurement, allowing policymakers to evaluate whether reforms reduce regional inequality or merely redistribute it across different parts of the network.

The framework also offers a practical way to discuss policy reforms currently debated in organ procurement governance without over-claiming what models can deliver. Scenario experiments can represent structural interventions—such as consolidation strategies, cross-region coordination rules, or changes in performance evaluation—while explicitly tracking unintended consequences. For instance, the model can reveal conditions under which reforms induce decreased effort among certain organisations, amplify competitive spillovers between neighbouring regions, or shift strategic positioning in ways that undermine the intended equity gains. This is precisely the kind of ex ante evidence that regulators and system designers require when interventions are costly, politically sensitive, and ethically consequential.

At the same time, the study has limitations that point to clear directions for future work. First, behavioural parameters (e.g., risk sensitivity, compliance burden, fairness weights) may vary across organisations and over time; richer calibration using behavioural or administrative data would strengthen external validity. Second, the current model emphasises organisational strategy and network-level outcomes; integrating additional clinical constraints or patient-level dynamics could refine operational realism while preserving tractability. Third, policy implementation is rarely instantaneous; extending the framework to multi-period learning, adaptive regulation, and endogenous metric evolution would support longer-horizon forecasting of reform trajectories. These extensions are feasible within the prototype’s modular design and would deepen its usefulness as a policy “wind tunnel”.

Overall, the discussion underscores a simple but consequential point: computational modelling enhances rather than replaces clinical, ethical, and epidemiological assessment. Its value lies in making trade-offs explicit, identifying incentive incompatibilities before they produce harm, and enabling policymakers to compare governance mechanisms under realistic behavioural responses. By framing organ procurement reform as a problem of behavioural mechanism design in a regulated healthcare operations network, this work provides a structured pathway for aligning efficiency, equity, and system integrity within a single decision-support architecture.

Conclusion

This study develops a modular, data-driven policy-simulation laboratory for organ procurement operations, implemented as a transparent Java prototype that models strategic interaction between Organ Procurement Organizations (OPOs) and transplant centres under alternative governance regimes. By integrating SRTR-style empirical inputs with a behavioural game-theoretic layer, the framework enables a unified assessment of system performance (efficiency), distributional outcomes (equity), and incentive-driven organisational adaptation (behavioural responses). Across baseline experiments, the results illustrate that seemingly modest changes to policy instruments—such as risk-sharing structures, regional allocation parameters, and transparency or accountability requirements—can produce non-trivial shifts in both aggregate welfare and the distribution of benefits across the procurement ecosystem. In other words, organ procurement outcomes are not solely determined by clinical need or resource scarcity; they are co-determined by the mechanisms through which performance is governed and the behavioural responses those mechanisms induce.

The paper’s primary contribution is therefore methodological: it reframes organ allocation reform as an operations-management mechanism design problem in a regulated healthcare network. Rather than treating policy as an exogenous directive, the model treats reform as a contract-like governance instrument that modifies payoffs, constraints, and feasible actions for strategic agents. This principal–agent interpretation supports ex ante testing of whether a policy is not only desirable, but also incentive compatible—that is, whether it induces the operational behaviours required for its success. A second contribution is the explicit incorporation of behavioural operations research: the framework allows actor responses to reflect realistic frictions such as risk aversion, reputational concerns, and compliance burden, providing a disciplined explanation for why some reforms may underperform or generate unintended consequences even when ethically well motivated.

A third contribution is analytical and practical: the prototype supports a multi-metric evaluation of reforms, including equity measurement (e.g., Gini-based distributional analysis), system-level welfare aggregation, and scenario-based stress testing. Optional modules—Nash equilibrium computation, evolutionary replicator dynamics, payoff-matrix export, fairness metrics, and multi-OPO strategic interaction—extend the framework beyond static comparisons to examine behavioural stability and strategic viability. These capabilities are particularly valuable for policymakers because they reveal when a reform’s apparent benefits depend on fragile assumptions, when strategic substitution or selection effects may arise, and where parameter regions exist that deliver equity gains with limited efficiency loss.

From a governance perspective, the prototype complements (rather than substitutes for) the clinical, ethical, and epidemiological evidence used by institutions such as OPTN/UNOS and other regulatory stakeholders. Its role is to provide a rigorous, transparent bridge between policy proposals and their likely operational consequences: it translates governance levers into measurable behavioural outcomes, surfaces equity–efficiency trade-offs, and offers a reproducible structure for iterative policy refinement. The framework is also designed to be extensible. Future work can incorporate richer stochastic structure (e.g., donor arrival and organ viability processes), patient-level clinical risk adjustment, endogenous learning and adaptation by organisations, and longer-horizon multi-agent dynamics under evolving regulation. More broadly, the approach generalises to other life-critical healthcare operations domains—such as blood supply systems, emergency surge capacity, and high-stakes resource allocation—where system performance depends not only on optimisation, but also on how real organisations respond to incentives and constraints.

In summary, this work contributes (1) a computationally transparent policy-simulation laboratory for organ procurement operations, (2) a mechanism-design framing that links governance instruments to organisational behaviour and system outcomes, and (3) an equity-aware evaluation toolkit that enables evidence-based comparison of reforms under behavioural adaptation. As transplant systems continue to evolve under pressures for higher performance and fairer access, such integrative modelling frameworks will be essential for developing policies that are ethically grounded, strategically feasible, and operationally resilient.

A.3 Network dynamics and policy spillovers in organ procurement operations

The global discourse on organ trafficking reflects a convergence of economic, ethical, and legal concerns that expose the commodification of the human body. Within this illicit system, individuals who sell organs—most commonly kidneys—are not altruistic donors but vendors driven primarily by financial desperation22–26. The United Nations Trafficking in Persons framework defines organ trafficking as the recruitment, transportation, transfer, harbouring, or receipt of persons through coercion, abduction, deception, abuse of power, or the exchange of payment or benefits for the purpose of exploiting another individual’s organs, tissues, or cells for transplantation5,6.

Opposition to organ trafficking arises from the profound inequities it sustains. The practice systematically exploits the world’s most vulnerable populations—those disadvantaged by socioeconomic status, ethnicity, gender, or age. Exploitation manifests through coercion, deception, and abuse of authority, transforming organs into tradable commodities where financial gain eclipses the wellbeing of both donors and recipients6. Thus, organ trafficking is not merely a health or legal issue but a reflection of entrenched global asymmetries in wealth and opportunity.

Closely related to this phenomenon is transplant tourism, defined by the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) as the acquisition of transplant organs abroad, often by circumventing domestic legal or ethical frameworks27. While transplant tourism can overlap with trafficking, it is important to recognise that not all cross-border transplant activity is illicit. Lawful and ethical forms include cases where donor–recipient pairs travel internationally to access unavailable medical services, or where family-based donations occur across national boundaries. Similarly, regulated bilateral organ-sharing programmes, built upon transparent agreements and oversight, remain legitimate and outside the scope of transplant tourism27.

The World Health Organization’s Second Global Consultation on Human Transplantation23 advanced a typology that distinguishes between these modalities. Shimazono’s23 framework (Fig. 2) delineates the complex coordination among commercial living donors (CLDs), recipients, and transplant centres—revealing how such exchanges can operate both across and within national borders. This distinction underscores that organ trafficking is not confined to international routes; domestic networks can be equally complicit in illicit transplants.
