Aerial monitoring of turkey behavior using unmanned aerial vehicles and computer vision
Authors: Giulio Calderone, Mireia Molins, Pietro Catania, John Boney, Enrico Casella
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonitoring animal health and welfare in large commercial poultry houses is an essential aspect of management and productivity, but it is a time-consuming and labor-intensive task. Additionally, high employee turnover increases training costs and compromises effective and efficient operations. To address these challenges, this study explores the combined use of an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) and computer vision technology as an innovative, non-invasive approach to support continuous behavioral monitoring and staff workload. Using a DJI Neo UAV equipped with a color camera, overhead videos of 160 Nicholas Select turkey toms from age 5 d to 32 d were acquired. Video frames were sampled and manually annotated to create a dataset for a You Only Look Once (YOLO) computer vision model capable of detecting and classifying eight key behaviors: feeding, drinking, sitting, standing, perching, huddling, wing flapping, and dead. The annotated dataset of 2388 images was divided into 70 % training, 15 % validation and 15 % testing. Among the tested configurations, the best model was selected based on the Mean Average Precision 50–95 (mAP50-95) performance on the validation set, corresponding to version 11 of the large YOLO model (YOLOv11-l) with an image resolution of 1280 pixels per side. Performance metrics for this model on the independent testing set were: precision = 0.90, recall = 0.87, F1-score = 0.89, and Mean Average Precision 50 (mAP50) = 0.89 using confidence threshold of 0.20. These results suggest that UAV-integrated computer vision could be applied to accurately classify turkey behaviors in commercial environments, improving welfare monitoring and management efficiency.Keywords: UAV, Turkey, Poultry, Computer vision, Behavioral monitoring

Introduction

Monitoring behavior and performing daily checks of poultry animals in commercial farms is crucial for productivity and animal welfare. Yet it remains a costly, time-consuming, and labor-intensive task, particularly in large densely populated facilities where continuous human observation is difficult to maintain (Li et al., 2020; Ojo et al., 2022; Olejnik et al., 2022). The poultry industry also faces persistently high employee turnover, which undermines operational stability and drives up recruitment and training costs. Frequent staff changes lower productivity and weaken competitiveness, while forcing companies to invest heavily in selecting and preparing replacements so they can quickly perform essential tasks (Alaghbari et al., 2024). In recent years, precision livestock farming (PLF) technologies have become pivotal for enhancing the efficiency and productivity of animal production systems (Tuyttens et al., 2022). PLF relies on continuous monitoring of animal behavior, with data collected either directly from the animals via sensors, e.g., temperature loggers (Gómez et al., 2021), GPS (Bernabucci et al., 2025), accelerometers (Cantor et al., 2022; Casella et al., 2023) or through image and sound-based analysis. The latter approaches offer key advantages: they avoid physical contact, reduce the risk of infection or behavioral disturbance, eliminate the need to retrieve sensors from live animals, and lower operating costs (Berckmans, 2017). In poultry production, these technologies are transforming management by enabling continuous, non-invasive tracking of bird health, behavior, and environmental conditions (Ojo et al., 2022). Okinda et al. (2020) reviewed the state of the art of computer vision applications for PLF in poultry, highlighting their ability to provide objective continuous welfare assessments. Computer vision not only records data for retrospective analysis but also reduces reliance on labor-intensive human monitoring, thereby simplifying day-to-day management.

Within computer vision, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are among the most established deep-learning architectures for image processing and have achieved proven results in numerous agricultural and livestock applications (Borges Oliveira et al., 2021; Bumbálek et al., 2025a; Fernandes et al., 2020; Ferreira and Dórea, 2025; Rahman et al., 2025). CNNs are artificial neural networks designed to analyze grid-structured data such as images and videos, inspired by the organization of animals’ visual cortex (Fukushima, 1980; Hubel and Wiesel, 1959). A typical CNN consists of three main components: convolutional layers, which extract local features (edges, corners, textures) to generate feature maps; pooling layers, which reduce dimensionality and computational cost while helping to reduce overfitting; fully connected layers, which combine the extracted features to produce the final output, such as a classification. This hierarchical architecture enables automatic learning of features, from low-level details to complex patterns, making CNNs particularly suited for recognition, monitoring, and automatic tracking tasks (Yamashita et al., 2018). In poultry production, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models, across all its versions, has been widely used for its high efficiency and excellent object detection accuracy, making it as a promising tool to enhance management and welfare practices (Badgujar et al., 2024; Umurungi et al., 2025). Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of computer vision systems for monitoring key activities and behaviors in poultry farming. Subedi et al. (2025) developed and compared three YOLO models specifically adapted for the automatic detection of behaviors (feeding, drinking, perching, preening, dust bathing, pecking, and foraging) in cage-free laying hens, achieving strong performance Mean Average Precision (mAP50) = 0.753. Bumbálek et al. (2025) evaluated different YOLO models for carcass detection in cage-free flocks and reported that YOLOv9c achieved the highest accuracy (mAP50 = 0.983). Similarly extending YOLO’s application to mortality monitoring Li et al. (2025) proposed MIF-YOLO, which combines thermal and visible imaging to detect dead birds in multi-tier systems with very high precision. Bodempudi et al. (2025) developed a behavior detection framework capable of automatically identifying mating behavior in group-housed broiler breeders while (Ke et al., 2024) designed an automatic beak-opening detection system for broilers as an early indicator of heat stress or health issues.

These studies demonstrate that camera networks integrated with YOLO can effectively identify animals in images, monitoring their activity and recognize key behaviors. However, such analysis are limited to the fixed field of view of cameras, which can be a constraint in large and complex farm environments (Rossi et al., 2025). In contrast, the use of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV) can overcome this limitation by providing flexible, wide-area coverage and potentially replacing the need for multiple fixed cameras to monitor the entire pen, thereby reducing both installation complexity and overall costs. Moreover, UAV allows the adjustment of altitude and viewing angle to capture animal postures more effectively, and can be easily moved between pens or facilities, increasing their versatility for large-scale monitoring applications. In this context, UAV represents a valuable alternative. In recent years, their use in agriculture has expanded rapidly, encompassing a wide range of applications including crop growth assessment, irrigation management, seed planting, crop monitoring, crop height estimation, pesticide spraying, soil and field analysis, and even livestock farming (Calderone et al., 2025a, 2025b; Canicattì and Vallone, 2024; Catania et al., 2023; Greco et al., 2025; Tzanidakis et al., 2023). Nevertheless, UAV applications in poultry production remain limited and the few available studies focus mainly on evaluating birds’ behavioral responses to UAV flights rather than on continuous monitoring systems. Parajuli et al. (2020) investigated the avoidance distance and fleeing speed of 4-8-week-old broilers exposed to two robotic systems: an overhead rail with a dummy arm, and a UAV. Their observations showed that broilers retreated from the aerial systems without any measurable negative effects on performance or welfare. They also noted that aerial platforms can stimulate locomotor activity, which may help prevent or alleviate skeletal problems in fast-growing birds. Daley et al. (2024) evaluated the combined use of a UAV and an autonomous ground robot in a commercial broiler house across a full 49-day production cycle. Monitoring feed conversion ratio, water intake, mortality, and behavioral recovery times, they found no significant differences in bird performance or welfare compared with control houses. Consistent with earlier reports, the findings indicate that the operation of UAV and autonomous ground vehicles in poultry facilities does not increase stress relative to human presence and can support efficient high-frequency routine house management without adverse effects on poultry welfare.

To our knowledge, this study provides the first integration of UAV-captured videos with a YOLO-based framework for automated recognition of turkey behaviors. The objective was to develop and validate a UAV-based computer vision method for detecting and classifying key behaviors and to assess its performance. High-resolution aerial footage was collected and used to train and validate the YOLO model. This approach demonstrates the potential of combining UAV imagery and computer vision for continuous, non-invasive monitoring of turkey welfare under commercial conditions.

Materials and methods

Animal housing

The study was conducted at the Poultry Education and Research Center (PERC) of The Pennsylvania State University, located in University Park, PA, USA (40.814309, -77.870575). A total of 160 Nicholas Select turkey toms were enrolled in the trial starting from day 0. Birds were reared in a pen measuring 4.16 m in length, 6.86 m in width, and 3.0 m in height (13 ft 8 in x 22 ft 6 in x 8 ft), yielding a stocking density of approximately 5.6 turkey/m2 (0.52 turkey/ft2) which complies with the Ag Guide (Federation of Animal Science Societies (FASS), 2020). Before placement, the floor was uniformly covered with 7.6 cm (3′′) depth of wood shavings.

Turkeys had ad libitum access to commercial feed and fresh water throughout the entire study. Lighting schedules, flock management, and nutritional regimes followed standard commercial practices. Environmental parameters, including air temperature, humidity, and photoperiod, were automatically regulated through a climate control system. Specifically, the artificial lighting program provided 24 h of light per day from day 0 to day 3, and 20 h of light with 4 h of darkness per day from day 4 to day 32. Ventilation was managed by a Phason AEC-2 climate controller (Phason Manufacturing Inc., Langley, BC, Canada), ensuring optimal air exchange and temperature regulation within the rearing facility.

Experimental design

The experiment was designed to evaluate the feasibility of UAV-based monitoring for automated behavioral recognition in 160 Nicholas Select turkey toms from aged 5 d to 32 days. A DJI Neo UAV (technical specifications in Section 2.3) equipped with a color camera. The recorded aerial video sequences were used to develop and validate a YOLO-based model for detecting and classifying eight key behaviors: feeding, drinking, sitting, standing, perching, huddling, wing flapping, and dead (mortality event).

To minimize potential disturbance caused by UAV presence, a habituation protocol was implemented from day 0 to day 4. During this phase, a GOgroove SonaVERSE BX speaker (Accessory Power, Westlake Village, CA, USA) positioned outside the pen continuously broadcasted pre-recorded UAV sounds at a maximum intensity of 87 dB, measured at a distance corresponding to the UAV’s flight height (2 m) to replicate the peak acoustic level generated by the UAV. The sound exposure was maintained for the entire study period from day 0 to day 32 and was broadcast for most of the day, typically over 12 h, in accordance with the approved IACUC protocol. Animals were inspected several times per day by trained researchers and caretakers to detect any signs of stress or abnormal activity. In addition, an Insta360 action camera (Insta360, Arashi Vision Inc., Shenzhen, China) was mounted inside the turkey pen to provide continuous 360° video recording throughout all UAV operations. While the footage enabled observation of turkey behavior before, during, and after each flight, in this study it was used exclusively to verify animal welfare. A comprehensive behavioral analysis will be reported in a separate publication.

From day 5 to day 32, aerial observations were conducted in compliance with Penn State institutional UAV safety regulations. The UAV was operated at a constant altitude of 2 m above ground level, and a forward speed of approximately 1 m/s.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the UAV flight trajectories were designed to cover the entire pen area during each flight, following predefined linear paths to ensure standardized spatial coverage.

Schematic illustration of the Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV) operation inside the turkey pen.

Video recording was performed with the camera gimbal set at a −45° tilt angle, this perspective provided a wide field of view and allowed for the simultaneous observation of a large number of birds, as well as enabling the identification of different postures and specific behaviors.

Each flight was carried out by a certified UAV pilot assisted by an observer, as mandated by institutional requirements. From day 5 to day 32, a total of four UAV flights were performed daily (two in the morning and two in the afternoon), each lasting approximately 1 min, with 5 min intervals between consecutive flights. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol number PROTO202502935.

UAV system and data acquisition

The video footage was acquired using a four-rotor DJI Neo UAV equipped with propeller guards (DJI, SZ DJI Technology Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China). The UAV has a total take-off weight of 135 g and a maximum hovering time of approximately 18 min under standard conditions. It carries an integrated camera with a 1/2-inch CMOS sensor and an effective resolution of 12 megapixels (4000 × 3000 px, 4:3 format), capable of capturing high-resolution still images and 4 K video. The lens provides a 117.6° field of view (FOV) with a fixed aperture of f/2.8 and a 13 mm equivalent focal length. The camera is mounted on a single-axis mechanical gimbal, offering a controllable range from −90° to +60°, with a stabilization accuracy of ±0.01°. Video can be recorded at a maximum resolution of 3840 × 2160 pixels (4 K UHD) at 30 frames per second (fps) in 16:9 format, or at 3840 × 2880 pixels (4 K in 4:3) also at 30 fps when electronic image stabilization (EIS) is disabled. The UAV was operated using the DJI Fly mobile application (DJI Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), which provides real-time flight control, live video preview, and access to camera and stabilization settings. Image annotation

Annotation was carried out by two trained annotators using the Roboflow platform (Roboflow, Inc., Des Moines, IA, USA), which enabled precise placement of bounding boxes around individual behavioral instances within each frame (Fig. 2).

Example of annotated images used for object detection of the turkey class.

Video recordings were converted into still frames by extracting one frame per second, providing sufficient temporal resolution to capture relevant behavioral changes while minimizing data redundancy. From this point onward, the term “images” refers to frames containing multiple birds within the field of view, each individually annotated according to its behavioral category.

Given the manual nature of the process, which required labeling multiple turkeys per frame, images were selected at regular temporal intervals throughout the experimental period to ensure representative temporal coverage. The number of annotated frames was gradually increased until the model’s performance metrics reached a stable plateau.

To develop a reliable detection and classification system for turkey behaviors, eight behavioral categories were annotated: feeding, drinking, huddling, wing flapping, standing, sitting, perching, and dead. These categories were defined in advance based on an ethogram (Table 2).

Ethogram of turkey behaviors.

Each turkey exhibiting a specific behavior was annotated with a distinct bounding box, except for huddle, where the bounding box encompassed groups of at least three birds.

The annotation process yielded a total of 628 labeled images collected from turkeys aged 5 to 32 days. The resulting dataset was divided into 439 images for training (70 %), 94 for validation (15 %), and 94 for testing (15 %), following standard computer vision practices.

To increase dataset variability and improve model generalization, a range of data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set. The augmentation pipeline included: horizontal flip, random crop with a minimum zoom of 0 % and a maximum zoom of 25 %, rotation from −15° to +15°, saturation adjustment between −25 % and +25 %, brightness adjustment between −15 % and +15 %, exposure adjustment between −10 % and +10 % and gaussian blur up to 1 px.

These steps expanded the training set to 2,200 images while keeping validation and test data unchanged, preserving an unbiased evaluation.

The final dataset comprised of 2388 images, exported in two separate versions to evaluate the effect of input resolution: one resized to 640 × 640 pixels and one resized to 1280 × 1280 pixels. Since YOLO requires a 1:1 aspect ratio, the frames were fitted with black edges to preserve the original aspect ratio of the recorded videos.

The final dataset followed the standard YOLO format, with separate directories for images and labels (containing the annotated behaviors). Each image’s annotation was saved in a .txt files, containing class identifiers and normalized bounding box coordinates, ensuring compatibility with the YOLO training pipeline. In addition, a dedicated .yaml configuration file specified the dataset structure for the YOLO framework, including the paths to the training and validation sets, the total number of behavior classes, and the corresponding class names. This setup ensured correct linkage between the annotated labels and the model during training and validation (Dhelia et al., 2024).

Model selection and training

All coding and algorithm development were performed in Python (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) using open-source libraries for data handling and processing. In particular, for object detection, the Ultralytics YOLOv11 library (v8.3.191) was employed. Among the different architectures, the YOLOv11 model showed the highest inference speed, making it the most suitable for real-time applications (Bumbálek et al., 2025b). This choice is in line with the future direction of our research, which aims to enable real-time monitoring of turkey behavior using UAV.

All models were trained and evaluated on the Pennsylvania State University Institute for Computational and Data Sciences (ICDS) Roar high-performance computing system, using an allocation equipped with 48 Intel® Xeon® Cascade Lake Gold 6248R CPU cores, one NVIDIA A100 GPU, and 384 GB of RAM. The implementation was based on PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.8, running on Python 3.12.9.

Ten different YOLOv11-based architectures were tested by combining five model sizes (nano n, small s, medium m, large l, and extra-large x) and two input image resolutions (640 and 1280 pixels). A batch size of 16 was used for eight out of the ten models. Due to GPU memory limitations, the large and extra-large models at 1280 pixel resolution could only be trained with a reduced batch size of 8. All models were trained for a maximum of 300 epochs with an early stopping criterion (patience = 100) that halted training if performance did not improve over 100 consecutive epochs. Training was conducted using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate (lr0) of 0.001, a learning rate multiplier (lrf) of 0.01, a momentum value of 0.9, and a weight decay coefficient of 0.005. The AdamW optimizer was selected because of its stable convergence behavior and strong generalization capabilities, particularly in datasets characterized by uneven annotation density and class imbalance (Kunstner et al., 2024). The learning rate was automatically adjusted during training according to the default scheduling implemented in the Ultralytics framework, which gradually decreases the learning rate to stabilize convergence. The early stopping patience (100 epochs) was chosen to allow sufficient time for late improvements in validation metrics while avoiding redundant computation once model performance plateaued. The testing set performance was evaluated across nine confidence thresholds ranging from 0.10 to 0.90 in increments of 0.10.

Model evaluation

Evaluation metrics

To objectively compare the performance of the tested object detection models, we used a set of widely adopted evaluation metrics and visualization tools that quantify both detection accuracy and class-level errors (Henderson and Ferrari, 2017; Murat and Kiran, 2025; Padilla et al., 2020; Subedi et al., 2025). Model performance was primarily assessed using precision, recall, and mean average precision (mAP). Precision measures the proportion of predicted positive detections that are correct, i.e., the fraction of true positives among all positive predictions (Eq. (1)). Recall quantifies the model’s ability to find all relevant objects, i.e., the fraction of true positives among all ground-truth positive instances (Eq. (2)). Both metrics range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating stronger predictive performance.

F1-score provides a single indicator of the balance between precision and recall. It is defined as the harmonic mean of the two metrics and is particularly informative when an even trade-off between false positives and false negatives is desired (Eq. (3)).

Mean average precision (mAP) (Eq. (4)) provides a comprehensive measure of detection accuracy across all classes by integrating precision over the full range of recall levels. In the following, we report mAP50, which measures detection accuracy at a fixed Intersection Over-Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5, that is, a predicted bounding box is considered correct only if it overlaps the ground-truth box by at least 50 %. We also report mAP50-95, which averages the average-precision scores over IoU thresholds ranging from 0.50 to 0.95 in 0.05 increments, providing a more stringent evaluation of localization accuracy and overall model robustness.

Where TP are true positives, FP false positives, FN false negatives, N the total number of object classes, and APi the average precision for one of the N object classes.

Confusion matrix

To gain deeper insight into class-specific behavior, normalized confusion matrices were generated for every trained model. These matrices provide a detailed breakdown of prediction outcomes (true positives, false positives, and false negatives) on a per-class basis, allowing the identification of systematic misclassification patterns (e.g., occasional confusion between visually similar behaviors or between animals and background). Unlike aggregated metrics such as mAP50, confusion matrices reveal which classes are easiest or hardest for the detector to separate (Adil Raja et al., 2025).

Results and discussion

Training set

The best-performing configuration corresponded to the YOLOv11-l model (the large variant of the YOLOv11 object detector). This model used an input resolution of 1280 pixels, and a batch size of 8. On the validation set it achieved a precision of 0.90, recall of 0.84, and an F1-score of 0.87. The highest performance occurred at epoch 81, with a mAP50 of 0.91 and mAP50-95 of 0.61.

Similar results were reported by Wang et al. (2020), who developed a behavior detection system for breeder chickens based on YOLOv3 using fixed cameras. The annotated behaviors included feeding, standing, fighting, wing flapping, mating, and drinking. The model achieved an overall mAP of 0.920 on the validation set using a low-density population of about 20-21 birds. When comparing stocking densities, the authors observed that the lower-density system produced fewer occlusions and overlaps, yielding clearer body contours and slightly higher metrics (mAP and precision) than the high-density condition, where close proximity increased visual obstruction and the likelihood of false or missed detections. Bodempudi et al. (2025) worked in a similar way with groups of around 20 broiler chicken, developing a deep learning framework that combined a customized YOLOv8-l detector with temporal logic to identify mating events. Their system achieved a mAP50 of around 0.995 in the detection task.

Fig. 3 shows the evolution of the main training and validation metrics across epochs. The training curves for the bounding box regression (train/box_loss), the classification (train/cls_loss), and the distributional focal (train/dfl_loss) losses decrease smoothly and monotonically before reaching a plateau, indicating stable convergence. As training progresses, the model becomes increasingly accurate in both object localization (lower box and dfl losses) and behavioral classification (lower cls loss).

Training metrics of the object detection model for turkeys behavior detection.

On the validation set, the same losses initially follow a similar downward trend, confirming that the model quickly learns features that generalize beyond the training data. After this early phase, they tend to stabilize rather than continue declining. A slight upward drift is visible, more pronounced in the distributional focal loss (val/dfl_loss), while the bounding-box and classification losses remain largely stable, fluctuating within a narrow range after their initial decrease.

This behaviour does not indicate severe overfitting or numerical instability but rather reflects two complementary effects: a progressive fine-tuning of highly specific spatial details in the training images-the model continues to refine bounding-box boundaries with increasing precision, yet these micro-optimizations do not always translate into lower error on the validation set, where object geometries and poses differ slightly; furthermore, the inherent variability across validation batches, including differences in animal positioning, body overlap, occlusion, and viewing angle. Once the overall loss has reached a low magnitude, even minor variations of this kind can cause small fluctuations in validation losses without implying a genuine degradation of the model’s generalization capability.

Importantly, this mild separation between training and validation losses does not correspond to a degradation of detection quality. After approximately 40–80 epochs, the core detection metrics on the validation set remain high and stable with only minor oscillations through late epochs. This indicates that the model continues to correctly detect and classify most behavior events in previously unseen images. While the validation loss terms become slightly less smooth at later epochs, the end-task performance on validation data does not collapse. The model therefore preserves practical generalization ability, rather than memorizing the training data at the expense of validation performance. In addition, the class imbalance in the dataset, that reflects the natural behavioral of turkeys under normal rearing conditions, may accentuate small oscillations in the validation losses, since rare behaviors contribute unevenly to the loss computation. This imbalance explains part of the late-epoch variability without implying unstable learning dynamics.

Fig. 4 presents the normalized confusion matrix obtained on the training set, showing how the YOLOv11-l model distinguishes the different behavioral categories.

Normalized confusion matrix illustrates per-class prediction performance of the YOLOv11-l model on the training set.

In a confusion matrix, diagonal cells represent correctly classified instances, whereas off-diagonal cells correspond to misclassifications, indicating the proportion of samples incorrectly predicted as another class. The diagonal values indicate that most classes are recognized with high percentages: perching and huddle reach 98 %, dead 96 %, feeding 92 %, sitting 92 %, and standing 88 %. Lower performance is observed for drinking (81 %) and especially wing flapping (67 %), which represent the most challenging categories, probably due to their sporadic and brief nature during the UAV flights. Off-diagonal cells highlight the main patterns of confusion: 29 % of background instances are predicted as standing, 13 % as feeding, and 45 % as sitting, while 33 % of drinking instances are predicted as feeding. Small percentages of false positives toward background (2-9 %) also appear across nearly all classes. Overall, the matrix indicates that the model effectively learned the distinctive features of most categories in the training set, while leaving room for improvement in classes that are harder to distinguish, particularly wing flapping, drinking, and the distinction between sitting and background.

Testing set

The final performance assessment of the object detection framework was carried out on an independent testing set that, like the validation set, was kept separate from the training data to provide an unbiased measure of generalization. This evaluation step ensures that the reported metrics reflect the model’s true ability to detect and classify new, unseen images rather than its capacity to memorize the training examples.

Several studies have explored deep-learning models based on YOLO for behavior detection. The results obtained in the present study with the YOLOv11-l model performed better than those reported by Subedi et al. (2025), who developed and tested three YOLO networks: YOLOv5s_BH, YOLOv5x_BH e YOLOv7_BH, for the classification of seven behaviors in cage-free laying hens (feeding, drinking, perching, preening, dust bathing, pecking, and foraging). Their best model, YOLOv5s_BH, achieved a precision of 0.781, recall of 0.717, and mAP50 of 0.753. Identifying multiple behaviors is a difficult task, and is further complicated by bird overcrowding, which caused overlaps between turkeys and, consequently, between different behaviors.

Research on the simultaneous detection of multiple behaviors remains limited, several investigations have focused on identifying only a few or single behaviors. The study by Bumbálek et al. (2025b) evaluated YOLOv8n, YOLOv9c, YOLOv10n and YOLOv11n models for the automatic detection of dead chickens in cage-free flocks. To compensate for the limited availability of real cases, the authors created a synthetic dataset of 3,413 images by combining photographs of live and dead chickens with realistic barn backgrounds. For the single-class detection task (dead birds), the YOLOv9c model provided the best overall performance, achieving precision 0.992, recall 0.962, and mAP50 0.983. Similarly extending YOLO’s application to mortality monitoring, Li et al. (2025) proposed MIF-YOLO, a model for the automatic detection of dead chickens in multi-tier cage farms that integrates both thermal and visible images. The model achieved a precision of 0.992 and a mAP50 of 0.989. However, the authors note that their work focused primarily on identifying dead birds at a specific growth stage and did not cover the entire broiler life cycle, stating that more powerful learning models will be required to accurately detect mortality across all stages. Clearly, the results of these works show that computer vision is able to distinguish between two drastically different classes, such as live and dead bird, especially if a thermal camera can provide information on the drastically different temperature of the animal. While detecting more activities and behaviors makes the task much more complex, the overall F1-score of 0.88 achieved by this work is a meaningful result.

Precision-recall and F1-confidence curves

The Precision-Recall (PR) curves in Fig. 6 illustrate the trade-off between precision and recall across the full range of detection thresholds for each behavior class.

Precision-Recall curves for the You Only Look Once (YOLO) v11-l model on the independent testing set. The curves illustrate the balance between precision and recall across confidence thresholds for each behavior class, with the thick blue line indicating the overall Mean Average Precision (mAP50) = 0.873.

The generally flat, near-plateau profiles of most curves indicate that the YOLOv11-l model maintains high precision even as recall increases, demonstrating robust detection performance under varying confidence settings. The class wing flapping shows the lowest area under the curve (AP = 0.439), confirming it as the most challenging category, whereas all other behaviors achieve AP values above 0.87. A similar pattern of class-specific difficulty was reported by Subedi et al. (2025) in their cage-free laying-hen study: among the seven annotated behaviors the lowest metrics occurred for preening (mAP50 = 0.584) and foraging (mAP50 = 0.668), highlighting how subtle or overlapping movements within dense flocks can reduce model performance, especially when limited training data are available for those behaviors. Consistent with this observation, Sozzi et al. (2023) evaluated YOLOv4 and YOLOv4-tiny to monitor in real time both the number of laying hens present on the floor and those engaged in dust bathing, a key welfare behavior. YOLOv4-tiny achieved the best validation results for the hens on floor class (mAP50 = 0.945), whereas detection of dust bathing remained modest (mAP50 of 0.282 with YOLOv4-tiny and 0.316 with YOLOv4).

Fig. 7 presents the F1-Confidence curves, which describe how the harmonic mean of precision and recall changes with the model’s confidence threshold.

F1-Confidence curves showing how the F1-score varies with the detection confidence threshold. The thick blue line represents the aggregate performance across all classes, which peaks at F1-score 0.86 and confidence 0.22, highlighting the optimal trade-off between precision and recall.

Here the confidence threshold represents the minimum probability required for a detection to be accepted. Lower thresholds make the detector more permissive, potentially capturing more true positives at the cost of additional false positives, whereas higher thresholds ensure only highly certain detections but may miss some objects. The aggregate curve (blue) peaks at an F1-score of 0.86 around a confidence of 0.22, indicating that this operating point offers the best balance between detection quality and decision certainty for deployment in behavior-monitoring applications. Accordingly, a confidence threshold of 0.20 was selected for reporting the final results, as it corresponds to the optimal balance between precision and recall. At this threshold, the model achieved a precision of 0.90, recall of 0.87, mAP50 of 0.89, mAP50-95 of 0.60 and an overall F1-score of 0.88.

Confusion matrix

Fig. 8 reports the normalized confusion matrix computed on the independent testing set for the YOLOv11-l model.

Normalized confusion matrix showing per-class prediction performance of the YOLOv11-l model on the independent testing set.

The diagonal values indicate that most categories were predicted with high accuracy: perching 90 %, dead 96 %, drinking 87 %, feeding 92 %, huddle 93 %, sitting 93 %, and standing 89 %. Wing flapping shows the lowest accuracy (17 %). The most evident misclassifications occur for wing flapping, with 33 % of its instances predicted as standing, 33 % as background, and 17 % as perching. Background images are frequently mistaken for static behaviors, with 44 % of background instances predicted as sitting. Smaller but consistent off-diagonal values indicate residual confusion toward background across nearly all classes (typically 2–6 %). Overall, these results confirm that the model maintains strong discriminatory ability on unseen data, while highlighting the need for further refinement in the separation of certain classes and in the reduction of background-related classification errors.

Conclusion

This study represents the first experimental integration of UAV imagery and YOLO-based computer vision for multi-behavior detection in turkeys and demonstrates the feasibility of using these combined technologies to improve automated monitoring of poultry behavior. Using overhead footage acquired by a DJI Neo UAV, an annotated dataset was split into 70 % training, 15 % validation, and 15 % testing, to train a YOLOv11 detection model for eight turkey behaviors: feeding, drinking, sitting, standing, perching, huddling, wing flapping, and dead. Of the tested configurations, the YOLOv11-l model with an input resolution of 1280 pixels and a confidence threshold of 0.20 achieved the best performance, with precision = 0.903, recall = 0.868, F1-score = 0.885, and mAP50 = 0.895 on the independent test set, with wing flapping and drinking identified as the most difficult classes.

Although these results demonstrate that UAV-integrated computer vision can effectively identify multiple turkey behaviors, the experiment was conducted under controlled research conditions and at a moderate stocking density lower than that typically found in commercial farms. Future studies should therefore assess model performance under real farm conditions, where higher bird densities may increase occlusion and reduce detection accuracy.

In our experimental setup, ventilation fans did not interfere with UAV operations, and no flight instability was observed despite the use of one of the smallest commercially available drones. Nevertheless, for large-scale applications or when employing different UAV platforms, the potential influence of ventilation airflow on flight stability and overall performance should be carefully evaluated.

Similarly, although flight endurance was not a limiting factor in our experiment, it should be considered in future applications, as battery capacity may constrain data collection duration depending on the UAV model and mission requirements. Nevertheless, most UAVs are equipped with interchangeable battery packs, allowing rapid replacement and minimizing data collection interruptions.

Another operational aspect to consider is the potential increase in noise level associated with different UAV models. In our case, a single loudspeaker was sufficient to provide adequate sound intensity within the enclosure, but for larger facilities, the use of multiple loudspeakers would be advisable to ensure homogeneous acoustic coverage.

Further research should also aim to expand and diversify the dataset and test real-time implementation on edge devices.

We acknowledge that there is still room for improvement, particularly in reducing confusion between visually similar behaviors and improving separation between animals and background elements. These limitations, however, reflect the inherent visual complexity of dense flocks and the ambitious goal of detecting eight distinct behaviors simultaneously. The present work should therefore be considered an exploratory and non-definitive step, forming the foundation for more refined and scalable UAV-based monitoring systems.

Despite these limitations, this work provides a proof-of-concept demonstration that UAV-based computer vision systems can serve as a non-invasive and efficient welfare monitoring tool, potentially reducing the labor intensity and staff turnover associated with continuous human observation in intensive poultry production.
