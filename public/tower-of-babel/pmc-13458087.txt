Bioengineered Zn2TiO4 nanomaterial and its effective electrochemical, antibacterial and photocatalytic responses
Authors: A. Fall, K. K. Masibi, N. L. Botha, KJ. Cloete, R. Ahmed, M. Maaza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bioengineering of bimetallic oxides is a significant requirement for both social and environmental applications. In this work, bimetal oxide nanomaterials, Zinc orthotitanate Zn2TiO4 was produced using a green process which utilizes natural extract of Hibiscus sabdariffa. More accurately, natural extract/phyto-compounds of Hibiscus sabdariffa was validated as an effective chelating agent at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. A variety of techniques were applied to analyse their physicochemical properties and verify the production of the Zn2TiO4 nanoparticles. The XRD analysis illustrated the crystalline structure of the Zn2TiO4 nanocomposites. The SEM analysis showed the morphology of the compounds Zn2TiO4 to be highly porous. The EDS analysis validated the presence of elements such as Ti, and Zn while FTIR analysis indicated the existence of multiple functional groups, including O–H, C–H, C=O, Ti–O, and Zn–O bonds in the compound. The PL peaks identified at 240 and 450 nm are attributed to the charge transfer characteristics present in the surface state of the resulting product. The single phase crystals of the bio-engineered Zn2TiO4 were found to be within the nanoscale while exhibiting a significantly elevated photocatalytic efficacy in decomposing Methylene Blue reaching a threshold of about 94.1% efficacy at about 120 min. From the electrochemistry viewpoint, the bioengineered Zn2TiO4 nanomaterial demonstrated enhanced redox activity and stability, exhibiting 94.2% current retention after 20 cycles. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) studies confirmed a diffusion-controlled process, while electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) revealed a 10-fold reduction in charge transfer resistance ®ct), from 5.48 to 0.53 KΩ. This significant acceleration in electron transfer kinetics is corroborated by a nearly 2-fold increase in interfacial admittance (Yo), indicating an expanded electroactive surface area. These results demonstrate the applicability and potential for Zn2TiO4 nanomaterial in advanced electrochemical applications. From the antibacterial perspective, Zn₂TiO₄ nanomaterial showed limited activity in the agar-disc diffusion assay, with no meaningful inhibition against most tested Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains and only a marginal response against K. pneumoniae. These results suggest that any antibacterial potential of the Zn₂TiO₄ nanomaterial may likely to be strongly dependent on assay format, nanoparticle dispersion, contact conditions, and possible light-driven ROS generation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54507-y.

Introduction

Nanoscaled metal oxides have been widely used in many technical fields, such as photonics, energy storage, gas sensing, environmental decontamination and water recovery, nano-medicine, micro-electronics, and catalysis1–4. As nanoscale ZnO and TiO2 have been identified as the most multifunctional oxides within this family5. Aside from the cosmetics industry, their primary applications are focused on the environment and energy because to their inherent opto-electronic characteristics and very similar band-gap values within the 3.2 eV region. They are employed in the Energy focus for oxidation/reduction, H2O water splitting, and solar energy conversion. Among its applications in the environmental area are air purification, water decontamination, agriculture, and cosmetics6,7.

ZnO is actually one of the most popular optically transparent conducting oxides in the photonics industry8. Smart windows with features like gas detection, solar cells, flat panel displays, and electrochromic mirrors are made possible by its special capacity to reflect infrared light9. Because of their low emissivity, optically transparent conductors are currently most frequently used in smart window applications10,11. ZnO’s excitonic UV emission has sparked increased interest in opto-electronics due to its verified application in solid-state lighting systems, such as light-emitting diodes and laser diodes10,11. ZnO is basically a semiconductor at room temperature, having a wide, straight band-gap of “~3.7 eV.”

Due to its wurtzite crystal structure and optical properties that are similar to those of GaN, it is presently being investigated and confirmed as a potential candidate for blue-UV light emitting diodes. In contrast, ZnO can tolerate high temperature treatments related to doping and the formation of ohmic connections since it is sufficiently stable with a melting point of 2248 K. In a similar vein, ZnO is a strong candidate to be the material of choice for short-wavelength optoelectronic and photonic devices due to its intrinsic properties. These features are most notable for their high excitonic binding energy of 60 meV, which allows devices to function effectively at room temperature and above12.

One important multifunctional oxide, particularly in the photocatalysts family, is standard titania (TiO2)1. In fact, these later materials are a class of photoactive substances that, when exposed to light, produce excitonic pairs, which in turn produce extremely reactive superoxides and/or hydroxyl radicals. Among other phenomena, these quick chemical reactions are effective for air purification, H2O splitting, and the breakdown of pollutants in waste H2O13,14.

Under typical pressure and temperature settings, TiO2 exhibits three primary crystallographic phases: rutile, brookite, and anatase. These phases correspond to different bandgap values of 3.4 eV for brookite, 3.0 eV for rutile, and 3.2 eV for anatase. The bandgap further validates the oxygen excess characteristic of the samples15. According to the extensive literature, TiO2 nano-scaled is without a doubt the preferred photocatalyst13–16, particularly when it comes to multi-sectorial functioning as environment, and energy sectors. However, from a catalytic point of view, its usefulness is limited by the poor quantum yield caused by the rapid excitonic recombination of charge carriers (e_/h+) and the need for UV irradiation for its photo-activation. It has been demonstrated that nitrogen doping can get over this restriction17,18.

Both nanoscale ZnO and TiO2 are valuable as opto-electronics compounds, despite their differences from a crystallographic standpoint. At ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure, ZnO crystallises in the hexagonal wurtzite phase. As previously established, TiO2 exhibits three primary phases (anatase, rutile, and brookite).

Combining the inherent properties of ZnO and TiO2 could result in an enhanced zinc titanate. ZnTiO3 (Hexagonal), Zn2TiO4 (Cubic), and Zn2Ti3O8 (Cubic) can be distinguished within this family. These binary type oxides are efficient electroceramics with a variety of technological uses, such as light-emitting materials, photocatalysts, sensors, dielectric resonator materials, filters, white pigment, etc.19–24.

Zinc titanate have been synthesized through a variety of methods, including sol-gel, hydrothermal processes, magnetron reactive sputtering, pulsed laser ablation, and solid-state reactions24–31. For the synthesis of single-phase Zn2TiO4, the predominant crystallographic structure and key challenges have been identified as amorphous with insufficient high crystallinity20, multi-phase and biphase systems24, significantly high crystallinity without complete epitaxial growth26, the disparity in melting points between ZnO (975 °C) and TiO2 (1830 °C) phases27–29,32,33, and a high degree of crystallinity coupled with enhanced chemical homogeneity29. In this research, green biosynthesis was utilized to produce Zn2TiO4 with a high degree of crystallinity, improved chemical homogeneity, and nanoscale dimensions.

The confirmation of the synthesis of single phase Zn2TiO4 nanoparticles utilising a totally green approach is presented for the first time in this contribution. More specifically, Hibiscus-sabdarifa natural extract was employed as an efficient chelating agent in place of harsh reducing agents. The process of creating nanoparticles through green synthesis entails the reduction of metal ions present in the precursor solution by means of biologically active compounds derived from Hibiscus extracts. The mechanisms involved vary according to the type of nanoparticles produced, the reaction conditions, and the composition of the plant extract34. The biomolecules found in the Hibiscus extract, which include proteins, polysaccharides, and secondary metabolites such as phenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids, interact with metal ions, aiding in their stabilization and reduction. These biomolecules serve as stabilizing, reducing, capping, and reducing agents, thereby affecting the size, shape, and properties of the nanoparticles that are synthesized35.

Experimental section and methods

Materials

Zn(NO3)2·6H2O and Titanium (IV) bis (ammonium lactato) dihydroxide ([CH3CH(O-)CO2NH4]2Ti(OH)2) (both > 98% in purity/quality were obtained from Sigma Aldrich and used without further purification. The biomass Hibiscus sabdariffa was sourced from the local market in Dakar-Senegal. Deionized water with a conductivity lower than 10− 6 S cm− 1 was employed in the experimental procedures.

Synthesis

(i)Preparation of the extract.Initially, to create the Hibiscus sabdariffa extract, 6 g of the leaves were added to 100 mL of deionized water at room temperature for a duration of one day. The following day, the extract was filtered to achieve the clearest possible solution. The extract was then stored in the refrigerator for future use in the production of Zn2TiO4.(ii)Production of the Zn2TiO4.For preparation of the Zn2TiO4 nanoparticles, 0.02 molar of [CH3CH(O)CO2NH4]2Ti (OH)2 solution 50 wt%, and 5.95 g of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O were dissolved in 10 mL deionized H2O in separate 50 mL beakers. Both solutions were then added to 50 mL of the extract one by one while stirring at room temperature. This mixture was allowed to react under stirring for 24 h. The obtained precipitated was centrifuged out and washed 3 times with deionized H2O. Subsequently, the precipitate was dried in the oven at 100 °C. The final powder was annealed at 500 °C for 2 h in view of inducing its crystallization. Thereafter, it was stored for further characterizations.

Production of the Zn2TiO4.

For preparation of the Zn2TiO4 nanoparticles, 0.02 molar of [CH3CH(O)CO2NH4]2Ti (OH)2 solution 50 wt%, and 5.95 g of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O were dissolved in 10 mL deionized H2O in separate 50 mL beakers. Both solutions were then added to 50 mL of the extract one by one while stirring at room temperature. This mixture was allowed to react under stirring for 24 h. The obtained precipitated was centrifuged out and washed 3 times with deionized H2O. Subsequently, the precipitate was dried in the oven at 100 °C. The final powder was annealed at 500 °C for 2 h in view of inducing its crystallization. Thereafter, it was stored for further characterizations.

Characterization devices

The morphology of the material was explored via SEM analysis. The composites elements in the mixture were identified by using EDX and RBS techniques. Rutherford backscattering spectrometry (RBS) analysis was utilized to identify the elemental depth distribution. The crystalline form of the nanocompounds Zn2TiO4 was investigated by utilizing XRD analysis. FTIR spectroscopy was using to determinate the functional groups in the compounds and The Raman vibration analysis was utilized to identify the vibration modes. The absorbance and the diffuse reflectance were estimated by using UV-Vis spectroscopy. The luminescence was performed via PL spectroscopy measurement.

Photocatalytic investigations

Before performing the photodegradation analysis, 0.1 g of the nanocomposite was combined with 100 mL of methylene blue solution and agitated in the dark for half an hour. The solution was then exposed to a light source, and for a total of 120 min, we collected 2 mL every 15 min to analyse absorption using UV–visible spectroscopy.

Electrochemical investigations

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) were used to examine the electrochemical characteristics of the Zn2TiO4 nanocomposite material. All electrochemical measurements were carried out using Metrohm Autolab PGSTA302N potentiostat controlled by NOVA version 2.1 electrochemical software (Metohm Autolab NOVA Software). A standard three-electrode setup was utilized, comprising bare and modified glassy carbon electrode (GCE) as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl in saturated KCl as the reference electrode.

Electrode modification: Prior to modification, the GCE surface was gently polished to a mirror-like finish using alumina slurries (1.0, 0.5, and 0.3 μm) on a polishing pad. To ensure the removal of residual alumina, the electrode was sequentially sonicated in ethanol and deionized water for 5 min each. To prepare the modified electrode, 0.04 g of Zn2TiO4 nanocomposite was mixed with 0.006 g of carbon black, and 0.006 g of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF). This mixture was homogenized using a mortar and pestle with the addition of N-methly-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) as a solvent. The resulting mixture was sonicated for 2 h to obtain a uniform slurry. Finally, a few microlitres of the resulting nanocomposite paste were drop-cast onto the surface and dried in an oven at 35 °C for 2 min to yielding the modified electrode.

Antibacterial investigations

The antibacterial activity of Zn2TiO4 nanoparticles was assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion susceptibility test. This evaluation targeted Gram-positive bacteria, including Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), as well as Gram-negative bacteria, namely Escherichia coli (E. coli), Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), in accordance with a previously established protocol (BAUER, 1996). The bacterial strains, obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) in Manassas, USA, were cultured on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) from Merck, Germany, as the growth medium. A fresh 24-hour stock culture was used to create a bacterial suspension in sterile saline, which was then adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. After that, 50 µl of the suspension was applied to the MH agar plates, which were there after allowed to air dry at room temperature while being kept sterile. Fifty microlitres of the test solutions and controls were placed on sterile 6 mm discs. Zn2TiO4 nanoparticles were present in discs with the labels C1 through C4 at a concentration of 1000 µg/mL. As positive controls, discs C5 and C6 contained 100 µg/ml and 10 µg/mL of ciprofloxacin, respectively. The negative control was sterile saline. After placing the discs on agar plates, they were incubated for twenty-four hours at 37 °C.

Vibrational spectroscopy investigations: ATR-FTIR and Raman studies

Attenuated total reflection (ATR) Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy of the ZnxTiyOz sample annealed at 500 °C is shown in Fig. 4a. Different functional groups can be distinguished. The O–H functional group is responsible for the vibrational mode near 3390 cm−1. The C–H functional group is associated with the strong mode at 2885 cm−1, whereas the C–H bending vibrations in di-alkyl or tri-alkyl alkenes are probably responsible for the mode near 1410 cm−1. In addition to the modes mentioned above, the stretching vibrations of the Ti–O groups are responsible for the mode at 585 cm−128, and Zn–O vibrations are responsible for the mode at 1435 cm−132–37. As a preliminary conclusion, the measured vibrational modes, particularly those of 585 cm−1 and 1635 cm−1, support Zn2TiO4’s single phase nature, as reported by Sharma et al.31. Zn2TiO4 is an inverse spinel with a space group Fd-3 m and typically described by a 56-ato m unit cell. In its unit cell, 32 oxygen atoms comprise an fcc lattice, with associated interstitial tetrahedral (T) and octahedral (O) sites. The T sites are occupied by half of the divalent Zn cations and the O sites are filled by a stoichiometric mixing of the divalent Zn and tetravalent Ti cations randomly.

Figure 4.b displays the Raman spectrum of the ZnxTiyOz sample annealed at 500 °C. The spectrum exhibits several Raman active modes within the spectral range up to 3000 cm−1. It consists a priori of 2 separate regions; the Zn2TiO4 modes and that governed by TiO2or/and ZnO mides. More precisely, the modes are centered at the following spectral positions 139,272(F2g), 371(E-2 H),744 cm−1 (A1g) for the Zn2 TiO4 and 1994, 2482 and 2806 cm−1 for the ZnO/TiO2 labelled region. In the case of the labelled Zn2TiO4 region, the corresponding modes are likely to be attributed to the A1g, F2g and E-2Hg modes of the spinel structure of the Zn2TiO4 perovskite as observed by Li et al.29 and Matsumuto et al.38 in bulk crystalline Zn2TiO4 perovskite. However, there is a significant spectral differences. This later difference is likely to be attributed to size/shape effect of the Zn2TiO4 nano-crystals39,40.

Optical investigations: absorbance and photoluminescence studies

Figure 5a reports the Diffuse reflectivity of the Zn2TiO4 sample annealed at 500 °C. The UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectrum shows a sharp absorption edge at λ_cut-off ≈ 271 nm, which lies in the UV region. The spectrum shows reflectance of approximately 80% across the visible range (400–1100 nm), indicating that the material is highly reflective. The decrease in the wavelength range of 374–413 nm for the sample is due to the intrinsic absorption of Zn2TiO4 as a direct semiconductor31. This shows that this material is a wide bandgap oxide semiconductor. The absorption edge wavelength was used to calculate energy band gap to be 4.58 eV using Planck equation. This shows a blue shift when compared to the TiO2 bulk energy band gap (3.0–3.2 eV)41. However, this agrees with the values published within the literature42 in line with band structure of the Zn2TiO4 perovskite structure, which confirms the results obtained from the XRD showing the formation of Zn2TiO4.

Figure 5b reports the room temperature photoluminescence of the ZnxTiyOz sample annealed at 500 °C. The spectrum consists of 2 emissions centered at 428.2 and 648.5 nm. The near band-edge emission is responsible for the comparatively narrow peak in the UV range that is centred at 384 nm41–44.

The dominant emission at 428.2 nm reflects radiative recombination through shallow trap states, likely associated with slightly oxygen-deficient TiO2 units or Zn–O surface defects43. The sharpness of this peak suggests a relatively ordered defect structure, which is favourable for reproducible photocatalytic behaviour44. This also explains the 94.1% degradation of methylene blue by this material with 4.58 eV using white light is due to some surface defects45,46.

Photocatalytic response investigations

The absorbance variation and the time photo-degradation efficiency of a typical water solution polluted with Methylene Blue under xenon lamp UV light at different times, ranging from 0 to 120 min, are shown in Fig. 6. Considerable focus has been placed on the use of bimetal oxides for the remediation of polluted water, with significant treatment efficiencies reported through the photocatalysis process47. Typically, the photocatalytic process involving semiconductors includes several critical reactions, such as the generation of charge carriers, their relaxation, separation, transfer, capture, transport, and recombination. The catalytic characteristics of these bimetal oxides are affected by various factors, including structure, size, shape, composition, and surface properties, all of which are influenced by the different methodologies employed48. The techniques commonly used for synthesizing bimetal oxides encompass both chemical and physical methods49. The efficacy of the Zn2TiO4 photocatalyst in degrading MB dye was assessed in relation to existing studies on photocatalysts. More specifically, it presents the absorbance profiles recorded at 0.0, 30.0, and 90.0 min. One can distinctly observe the steady decline in the intensity of the intrinsic methylene blue absorbance peak within the wavelength range of 500–700 nm. Electrochemical response investigations

The electrochemical properties of the Zn2TiO4 nanocomposite were investigated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV). CV is a widely utilized electroanalytical technique known to provide intuitive insights into electrochemical reactions, by rapidly determining the redox potentials of electroactive species. It offers valuable information on the thermodynamics of redox reactions, kinetics of heterogeneous electron transfer, as well as the examination of linked electrochemical reactions or adsorption phenomena47.

CV measurements were performed in 1 M NaOH electrolyte over a potential range − 2.0 to 0.5 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. As shown in Fig. 7a, the bare GCE exhibits a relatively low current response, whereas the Zn2TiO4-modified GCE displays enhanced current response with distinct cathodic peaks at −1.6 V, −0.46 V, and − 0.12 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), alongside corresponding anodic features. This increase in current response suggests improved electrochemical activity and enhanced electron transfer at the modified electrode surface. The stability of the modified electrode was assessed over 20 consecutive CV cycles (Fig. 7b). The anodic peak current exhibited excellent retention (94.2%), with only ∼5.8% current loss between the 1 st and 20th scans, demonstrating outstanding stability to electrode fouling.

The effect of scan rate (50–1000 mV/s) was investigated by CV in 1 M NaOH at the Zn2TiO4 modified GCE. Figure 7c depicts the results obtained, and it was observed that both the anodic and cathodic peak currents increased with increasing scan rate. Furthermore, a linear correlation was observed between peak currents (Ip.a. and Ipc) and the square root of the scan rate (v1/2) (Fig. 7d), suggesting that the redox process is primarily diffusion-controlled, in accordance with the Randles-Sevcik model61.

The interfacial electron transfer kinetics were further evaluated by EIS in a 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− solution prepared in 0.1 M KCl over a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 10 kHz. The equivalent circuit for the Nyquist plots of the asymmetric supercapacitor device typically exhibits a semicircular shape in the mid-frequency range, a spike in the low-frequency range, and a characteristic equivalent series resistance in the high-frequency range62. The resulting Nyquist plots (Fig. 7e) display a semicircular region at high frequencies corresponding to charge transfer resistance (Rct), followed by a linear tail at low frequencies associated with diffusion-controlled processes. The experimental data were fitted using a modified Randles equivalent circuit (Fig. 7f) comprising solution resistance (Rs), Rct, a constant phase element (Q), and a Warburg impedance element (W). As summarised in Table 2, the bare GCE exhibited an Rct value of 5.48 kΩ, which decreased significantly to 0.53 kΩ upon modification with Zn2TiO4. This nearly 10-fold reduction in Rct indicates that the bioengineered Zn2TiO4 nanomaterial facilitates faster electron transfer kinetics. The constant phase element (CPE) exponent (n ≈ 0.73) suggests a porous and heterogeneous surface morphology, typical of bioengineered nanomaterials. Furthermore, the Zn2TiO4 modified GCE displayed a higher admittance (Yo = 3.94 µΩ−1sn) compared to bare GCE (2.02 µΩ−1sn), suggesting an increase in electroactive surface area. The decrease in the Warburg parameter (from 351.1 to 250.42 µMho) reflects changes in diffusion-related impedance at the electrode interface. These findings demonstrate the superior conductivity and charge-transfer properties of Zn2TiO4 nanomaterial, highlighting their potential in advanced electrochemical applications.

Antibacterial response investigations

The inhibition zone measurements are summarized in Fig. 8 and Table 3. After 24 h of incubation, the Zn₂TiO₄ nanocomposite did not produce measurable inhibition zones against the Gram-positive strains tested, namely S. aureus and MRSA, nor against most of the Gram-negative strains, including A. baumannii, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa. A very small zone was observed only for K. pneumoniae, with mean inhibition values of 6.85 ± 0.07, 6.95 ± 0.21, 7.08 ± 0.28, and 7.10 ± 0.22 mm for samples C1–C4, respectively. Since the sterile disc diameter was approximately 6 mm, these values indicate only marginal inhibition beyond the immediate disc area and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. By contrast, ciprofloxacin produced clear and concentration-dependent inhibition zones across all bacterial strains, confirming the validity of the assay conditions and the responsiveness of the bacterial panel.

The antibacterial action of metal-oxide nanomaterials is commonly associated with three interrelated processes: reactive oxygen species generation, disruption of the bacterial membrane envelope, and subsequent loss of cellular viability. In semiconductor oxides such as ZnO-, TiO₂-, and titanate-based systems, photon absorption or surface redox reactions can promote electron–hole separation. The photogenerated holes may react with adsorbed water or hydroxide ions to form hydroxyl radicals, while electrons can reduce dissolved oxygen to superoxide radicals. These reactive oxygen species, including ·OH, O₂·⁻, and H₂O₂, can oxidize membrane lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. At the bacterial surface, this oxidative stress weakens the structural integrity of the cell envelope, increases membrane permeability, disturbs ion homeostasis, and may lead to leakage of intracellular contents. Once membrane damage exceeds the bacterium’s repair capacity, respiratory activity, nutrient transport, and metabolic regulation are compromised, resulting in growth inhibition or cell death.

For Zn₂TiO₄ specifically, such ROS-mediated pathways are mechanistically plausible because the material showed strong photocatalytic activity toward methylene blue degradation, indicating that the nanocomposite can participate in light-driven oxidative reactions. However, the antibacterial experiment was performed using a disc-diffusion format under standard incubation conditions. ROS-driven antibacterial effects usually require close contact between the nanoparticle surface and bacterial cells, sufficient oxygen and water at the interface, and, in many titanate or Ti-containing oxides, suitable light activation. In the present agar-diffusion assay, Zn₂TiO₄ particles are unlikely to diffuse efficiently through agar in the same way as a small-molecule antibiotic. Therefore, even if ROS are generated locally at the nanoparticle surface, the oxidative species may remain confined near the application site, decay rapidly, or fail to reach bacteria located at the expanding growth front59. This may explain why the nanocomposite produced little to no measurable inhibition zone despite its demonstrated photocatalytic functionality.

The relationship between ROS production, membrane damage, and bacterial inactivation may therefore be understood as a sequential process: Zn₂TiO₄ surface activation may generate ROS; ROS can oxidatively attack bacterial envelopes; membrane oxidation can increase permeability and cause intracellular leakage; and these events can culminate in bacterial inactivation. Nevertheless, under the present experimental conditions, this sequence was not sufficiently expressed as a clear diffusion-based inhibition zone. The negligible activity against S. aureus, MRSA, A. baumannii, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa suggests that ROS generation, ion release, or direct particle–cell contact was insufficient in the agar environment to produce observable bactericidal effects. The marginal response against K. pneumoniae may reflect limited local contact-dependent stress at the disc boundary rather than strong diffusible antibacterial activity.

Several factors may have limited the expression of antibacterial activity. First, particle agglomeration can reduce the available reactive surface area and decrease the effective contact frequency between Zn₂TiO₄ and bacterial cells. Second, limited release of Zn²⁺ species from the titanate matrix may reduce metal-ion-mediated stress63,64. Third, incubation in the absence of controlled illumination may suppress photocatalytic ROS pathways associated with Ti-containing oxides65. Fourth, Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria differ in cell-envelope architecture, which influences nanoparticle attachment, ROS susceptibility, and permeability responses. The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria may restrict nanoparticle access, while the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria can also act as a physical barrier to oxidative or contact-mediated damage.

Accordingly, the present findings indicate that Zn₂TiO₄, as formulated and tested here, does not behave as an effective diffusible antibacterial agent in an agar-disc diffusion assay. However, the absence of large inhibition zones should not be taken as definitive evidence that the material lacks antibacterial potential under all conditions. Rather, it suggests that the antibacterial mechanism, if present, is more likely to be contact-, surface-, dose-, dispersion-, or illumination-dependent. Future work should therefore quantify ROS generation directly under antibacterial assay conditions, for example using ROS-sensitive probes, and should correlate ROS levels with membrane damage assays such as propidium iodide uptake, protein/nucleic acid leakage, lipid peroxidation, or electron microscopy of bacterial envelope disruption. In addition, broth microdilution, time-kill kinetics, illuminated versus dark incubation, nanoparticle-coated surface assays, and Zn²⁺ release measurements would help determine whether Zn₂TiO₄ can induce ROS-mediated membrane damage and bacterial inactivation under conditions better aligned with nanoparticle mechanisms. Importantly, the negligible activity observed in the agar-diffusion assay also explains why additional antibacterial assays, such as broth microdilution, MIC/MBC determination, and time-kill studies, were not pursued within the present work. The initial disc-diffusion test was used as a first-line screening method to establish whether Zn₂TiO₄ produced a measurable antibacterial response under standard conditions. Since the material showed no meaningful inhibition zones for most organisms, and only a near-baseline response against K. pneumoniae, subsequent antibacterial assays were considered beyond the immediate scope of this study and are now identified as essential follow-up investigations rather than confirmatory tests. At the same time, we acknowledge that the absence of activity cannot be fully interpreted without quantitative physicochemical information under biological test conditions. In particular, nanoparticle dispersion state, hydrodynamic size, ζ-potential, agglomeration behaviour in Mueller–Hinton medium, and Zn²⁺ ion release could strongly influence bacterial contact, ROS generation, and membrane interaction66. These parameters were not measured during the antibacterial assay and therefore could not be quantitatively correlated with the observed lack of inhibition. Similarly, because the antibacterial experiments were performed under standard incubation conditions rather than controlled illumination, the possible photocatalytic bactericidal effect of Zn₂TiO₄ was not directly assessed. This is a relevant limitation, especially given the strong photocatalytic activity observed for methylene blue degradation in this study. In sum, future antibacterial work should therefore be specifically designed to compare dark and illuminated conditions, quantify ROS and Zn²⁺ release in the assay medium, evaluate colloidal stability and ζ-potential, and determine MIC/MBC and time-kill profiles under conditions that better reflect the likely surface-contact and light-activated mechanisms of Zn₂TiO₄.

Overall, the antibacterial results should be interpreted conservatively (Table 3). The Zn₂TiO₄ nanocomposite showed no meaningful inhibition against most tested organisms in the disc-diffusion format, with only a minimal response against K. pneumoniae. Mechanistically, ROS-mediated oxidative membrane damage remains a plausible pathway for Zn₂TiO₄-based antibacterial action, particularly under light-activated or contact-rich conditions, but the present assay did not provide direct evidence of sufficient ROS generation, membrane disruption, or bacterial inactivation to support a strong antibacterial claim.

Conclusion

The bio-inspired synthesis of single phase Zinc Orthotitanate (Zn2TiO4) is validated in this work. More precisely, Hibiscus sabdariffa’s natural extract and phytocompounds were shown to be an efficient chelating agent at room temperature and atmospheric pressure.
