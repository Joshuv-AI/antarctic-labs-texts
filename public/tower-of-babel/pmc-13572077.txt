Reversal of aqueous flow in myopic, vitrectomized eyes with scleral-fixated intraocular lenses: A proposed mechanism underlying reverse pupillary block
Authors: Steven G. Safran, Brian M. Smith, Minas T. Coroneo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReverse pupillary block (RPB) is an increasingly recognized complication following secondary scleral-fixated intraocular lens (IOL) implantation, particularly in vitrectomized eyes with high axial myopia. Although its clinical manifestations are well described, the underlying hydrodynamic mechanism remains poorly understood. We sought to visualize aqueous humour flow in vivo and determine whether altered intraocular fluid dynamics could explain the development of RPB.SettingPrivate practice with a single surgeonDesignRetrospective chart review and observational studyMethodsPatients who underwent IOL exchange with intrascleral haptic fixation (ISHF) by Yamane technique by a single surgeon underwent slit lamp video microscopy postoperatively using a Haag-Streit BQ900 slit lamp with a IM900 imaging system.ResultsSix eyes of six patients (mean axial length 27.26 mm) demonstrated reproducible movement of suspended particles from the anterior chamber into the posterior segment through the iridolenticular channel. All six eyes underwent concurrent pars plana vitrectomy, although two had previously been vitrectomized. All eyes had one or more prophylactic peripheral iridotomies and none demonstrated active reverse pupillary block or elevated intraocular pressure at the time of observation. However, the observed flow pattern provides a physiological explanation for the reverse pupillary block that had occurred previously or was known to occur intermittently in these anatomically susceptible eyes.ConclusionIn vitrectomized eyes with scleral-fixated IOLs, aqueous humour may preferentially flow from the anterior chamber toward the posterior segment. We propose that this reversed pressure gradient is driven by enhanced posterior aqueous outflow through the retinal pigment epithelium pump in eyes lacking the normal lens-zonule-vitreous barrier. This altered fluid dynamics provides a mechanistic explanation for reverse pupillary block and its relief following peripheral iridotomy.

Introduction

With the increasing use and popularity of secondary scleral-fixated posterior chamber intraocular lenses (IOLs), particularly intrascleral haptic fixation (ISHF) techniques such as Yamane ISHF for lens dislocation in pseudophakic capsular bag insufficiency,1,2 reverse pupillary block (RPB) has become increasingly recognized as an important postoperative complication associated with iris chafing, uveitis-glaucoma-hyphema (UGH) syndrome and optic capture by the iris.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Although commonly described following Yamane fixation, similar clinical findings have also been reported after other scleral-fixation techniques, suggesting that disruption of the iris-lens diaphragm, rather than the fixation method itself, is the fundamental predisposing factor.7, 8, 9, 10 A recent review4 reported RPB in approximately one-third of eyes undergoing scleral-fixated IOL implantation without peripheral iridectomy using a variety of fixation techniques, including Yamane, Carlevale, Gore-Tex-sutured, four-flanged and Scharioth fixation.4,10,11

RPB can be thought of as a compartment syndrome in which posterior bowing of the iris with extensive apposition of the pupil to the anterior IOL surface, consequent pigment dispersion, inflammation and haemorrhage may contribute to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP).9 RPB is a significant complication with important acute and chronic consequences. Clinically, however, RPB differs fundamentally from iris bombé. Rather than anterior bowing of the iris with angle closure, eyes with RPB demonstrate a markedly deep anterior chamber, a wide-open angle, and posterior iris concavity with circumferential contact of the pupil to the IOL surface. Elevated IOP is frequently absent, particularly early in the disease process, and when glaucoma develops it more commonly reflects chronic pigment dispersion and possibly red blood cell deposition associated with UGH syndrome and progressive trabecular compromise rather than an acute pressure crisis caused by the RPB anatomic configuration.

While RPB is well recognized clinically, an understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanism has yet to emerge. Although the term reverse pupillary block is well established in the literature, it may not fully describe the underlying physiology, as the condition is frequently intermittent and IOP is often normal. Nevertheless, for consistency with the existing literature, we retain the accepted terminology throughout this manuscript while proposing that RPB reflects an alteration in intraocular hydrodynamics resulting in reversal of the normal aqueous pressure gradient. The alteration of the mechanism which directs and regulates aqueous flow between the anterior and posterior segments is unmasked in RPB and illustrated in our findings.

The traditional view that aqueous circulation in the anterior segment of the eye is predominantly posterior to anterior and is based on aqueous production by the ciliary body processes, drainage through the trabecular meshwork and uveoscleral outflow, together with convection currents generated by corneal cooling,12,13 is difficult to reconcile with the development of RPB. The presence of RPB suggests a compartment syndrome in which pressure within the anterior chamber exceeds that behind the iris, contrary to the posterior-to-anterior flow pattern that has generally been accepted as ubiquitous. However, the absence of readily available techniques to visualize aqueous humour currents in vivo12 has limited our understanding of altered perilenticular and trans-vitreal aqueous humour flow in both physiological and pathological states. Consequently, little is known about how surgical procedures such as glaucoma drainage interventions, cataract extraction with IOL implantation, pars plana vitrectomy and secondary IOL fixation alter intraocular aqueous dynamics.

We initially noted that when a laser peripheral iridotomy (PI) is performed in an eye with active RPB and a posteriorly bowed iris, a visible gush of fluid travels posteriorly through the PI into the posterior chamber.14 This was particularly evident in myopic patients who had undergone Yamane ISHF and vitrectomy and were in active reverse pupillary block at the time the iridotomy was performed. In some eyes, RPB may be intermittent. In a single-surgeon series of patients who underwent IOL exchange with ISHF using the Yamane technique, the presence of postoperative cellular and particulate material within the aqueous enabled us to observe an apparent reversal of aqueous flow from the anterior chamber to the posterior segment. These observations are consistent with recent research showing that the retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) “pump” may transport as much aqueous humour posteriorly as flows anteriorly,16 particularly in eyes with an enlarged myopic retinal surface area, following vitrectomy, and in the presence of compromised trabecular outflow. The direct observation of this reversed aqueous current provides a physiological explanation for the development of reverse pupillary block in anatomically susceptible eyes.

Methods

Patients who underwent IOL exchange with ISHF using the Yamane technique by a single surgeon over a six-month period (Table 1) underwent slit-lamp videomicroscopy using a Haag-Streit BQ900 slit lamp equipped with an IM900 imaging system. In all cases, the haptics were externalized 2.3 mm posterior to the limbus at approximately the 11 and 5 o'clock meridians, consistent with our standard Yamane technique. Four of the six patients underwent four-port pars plana vitrectomy at the time of IOL exchange for posterior dislocation of the lens-bag complex using chandelier endoillumination system. The remaining two patients had previously undergone pars plana vitrectomy and underwent additional two- or three-port pars plana vitrectomy at the time of Yamane ISHF, with an infusion line placed through the pars plana as part of the standard surgical technique. One or more prophylactic peripheral iridotomies placed temporally and/or inferiorly were created intraoperatively in every eye using the vitreous cutter to reduce the risk of postoperative reverse pupillary block. Consequently, none of the eyes demonstrated active reverse pupillary block during postoperative videomicroscopy. Instead, postoperative pigment, inflammatory cells and particulate material suspended within the aqueous humour served as physiological tracers, allowing visualization of aqueous humour flow following decompression of the anterior-posterior pressure gradient by the prophylactic peripheral iridotomies. Results

A series of six eyes from six patients who underwent IOL exchange with ISHF using the Yamane technique by a single surgeon were included in the study. Patients ranged in age from 58 to 77 years, with three males and three females. All patients had axial lengths within the myopic range (mean axial length 27.26 mm), although one patient had only mild axial myopia (24.63 mm) following previous photorefractive keratectomy. All patients were observed at least one week postoperatively.

At the time of slit-lamp videomicroscopy, all eyes had patent peripheral iridotomies, normal intraocular pressure, and no evidence of active reverse pupillary block. This likely reflected successful decompression of the anterior-posterior pressure gradient by the prophylactic iridotomies performed at surgery. Several eyes, however, had previously demonstrated reverse pupillary block or were considered anatomically predisposed to intermittent episodes based on their postoperative anatomy and clinical course (Table 1).

The interval between IOL exchange and video observation ranged from one week to two years. In all cases, aqueous humour particles were observed to move from the anterior chamber to the posterior segment (Videos 1 and 2). Both larger pigment aggregates and smaller inflammatory particles followed the same anterior-to-posterior trajectory, providing direct in vivo visualization of a consistent posteriorly directed aqueous current despite differences in surgical history and postoperative interval. The direction of flow was consistent in every patient.

Discussion

Reverse pupillary block (RPB) can be considered as a form of intraocular compartment syndrome. It has been observed particularly in cases following scleral fixation of intraocular lenses (IOLs), where the iris is displaced posteriorly onto the IOL surface. This anatomical change may lead to iris chafing, pigment dispersion, secondary glaucoma, recurrent bleeding and uveitis—collectively known as UGH syndrome (Fig. 4).7 Although our series utilized the Yamane technique, the underlying mechanism is likely to apply to other methods of scleral-fixated IOL implantation in which disruption of the iris-lens diaphragm results in similar alterations in intraocular fluid dynamics.4,7, 8, 9, 10.

Clinically, RPB presents as a posteriorly concave iris with a markedly deep anterior chamber and 360-degree apposition of the pupil margin to the IOL surface. This configuration may be constant or intermittent, with patients cycling in and out of RPB during slit-lamp examination (Fig. 2). Iris capture of the IOL optic is commonly seen and may temporarily relieve the block (Fig. 3), although it frequently recurs when the lens is repositioned. Importantly, RPB should be distinguished from iris bombé. Rather than anterior bowing of the iris with angle closure, eyes with reverse pupillary block (RPB) demonstrate a markedly deep anterior chamber, a wide-open angle, and posterior iris concavity with circumferential contact of the pupil to the anterior surface of the intraocular lens. Intraocular pressure is frequently normal, particularly early in the disease process, and is not an essential feature of RPB. Consistent with our observations, Schranz et al. found no significant difference in IOP between eyes with and without RPB, nor before and after YAG peripheral iridotomy.4 Pressure elevation therefore appears to be a secondary consequence of chronic iris chafing, pigment dispersion and progressive trabecular compromise rather than the defining feature of the condition.Fig. 2Schematic representation of intraocular aqueous humour fluid dynamics.A: In the normal phakic eye, after secretion by the ciliary body, aqueous humour exits both anteriorly through the relatively low-resistance trabecular meshwork pathway (“pull” mechanism) and posteriorly via active transport across the retinal pigment epithelium (“push” mechanism). Peripheral iridotomy (PI) remains the most effective intervention for resolving RPB. When a YAG laser PI is performed during an active episode, a sudden posterior gush of aqueous and pigment through the iridotomy into the posterior chamber is typically observed, supporting the presence of a pressure differential with higher pressure in the anterior chamber.4,14 These observations are supported by our slit-lamp observations of aqueous currents, visualized by tracking suspended pigment and particulate material in vitrectomized eyes with scleral-fixated IOLs. During active reverse pupillary block the posterior aqueous current is temporarily obstructed by circumferential iridolenticular contact, which acts as a physiological baffle preventing manifestation of the posterior current. It is only when this barrier is breached by peripheral iridotomy that the accumulated pressure gradient is released, producing the characteristic posterior gush of aqueous and subsequently allowing the reversed aqueous current to be visualized. Our observations build upon those of Schranz et al. Whereas they demonstrated the characteristic anatomical configuration of RPB using anterior segment OCT and showed that peripheral iridotomy restores normal iris configuration without significantly altering IOP,4 our direct visualization of aqueous currents provides a clinical and physiological explanation for these observations. While PI equalizes pressure across the iris and relieves the compartment syndrome, it does not appear to alter the predominant direction of aqueous flow, which remains from the anterior chamber toward the posterior segment. Clinically, we have also observed that following successful PI the peripheral iris often assumes a slightly more anterior position, narrowing the peripheral angle while reducing iridolenticular apposition.

In-the-bag IOL implantation largely preserves the compartmental anatomy and fluid dynamics of the phakic eye (Fig. 1). By contrast, scleral fixation techniques are increasingly employed in eyes with insufficient capsular support due to lens subluxation or dislocation.1,2 These procedures fundamentally alter normal compartmental anatomy with important physiological consequences.

In vitrectomized eyes with scleral-fixated IOLs, normal barriers including the lens capsule, zonules and vitreous are removed, effectively transforming the eye into a largely unicameral system. This anatomical alteration disrupts the normal iris-lens diaphragm, which functions as a physiological baffle regulating aqueous movement between the anterior and posterior segments, thereby facilitating unimpeded posterior flow. All eyes in the present series had undergone complete pars plana vitrectomy, which may further enhance posterior aqueous transport by removing the vitreous barrier and exposing a greater retinal surface area over which retinal pigment epithelial pump activity may contribute to posterior aqueous transport. Whether complete vitrectomy predisposes to greater posterior aqueous transport than limited anterior vitrectomy deserves further investigation. Evidence suggests that the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) actively contributes to aqueous outflow through a pump mechanism and, in the absence of normal anterior resistance, posterior aqueous transport may become exaggerated.12,13,16, 17, 18 Increased posterior aqueous outflow has also been implicated in Iris Retraction Syndrome (IRS), which occurs in association with retinal detachment and following retinal detachment repair.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 IRS may present with hypotony or elevated IOP together with pronounced posterior bowing of the iris and pupillary seclusion. Like RPB, IRS is relieved by peripheral iridotomy and appears to result from disruption of anterior-posterior pressure homeostasis.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 The two conditions differ clinically in that IRS is associated with retinal pathology and often hypotony, whereas RPB occurs in anatomically altered pseudophakic eyes, usually with normal IOP and without retinal detachment. Nevertheless, both conditions may lie within a spectrum of disorders characterized by increased posterior aqueous outflow and reversal of the normal aqueous pressure gradient.

Physiologically, the crystalline lens and its associated structures—the zonules and ciliary processes—act as a one-way valve or baffle, directing aqueous flow from the ciliary body towards the anterior chamber25 (Fig. 1). Removal of these structures, as occurs following vitrectomy with secondary IOL implantation, particularly using ISHF, induces involutional changes in the ciliary body secondary to loss of zonular tension26 that may impair trabecular outflow27 and further favour posterior redistribution of aqueous.

Historically, the contribution of posterior aqueous outflow via the RPE, choriocapillaris and vortex veins has been underappreciated.17,18 Recent reviews suggest that posterior aqueous transport in humans may approach anterior outflow.16 This posterior shift is likely to be accentuated in larger myopic eyes owing to increased retinal surface area, reduced vitreous resistance28,29 and loss of the normal lens-iris diaphragm, creating a “perfect storm” for RPB. Many eyes requiring scleral-fixated IOLs also have compromised trabecular function or pre-existing glaucoma. Vitrectomy has been implicated in oxidative damage to the trabecular meshwork, further increasing glaucoma risk.30 Additional contributing factors may include altered aqueous transport by the iris31 and mechanical changes within the ciliary body following zonular disruption.26,27

Clinical experience also suggests that individual anatomical characteristics may influence susceptibility to RPB. Eyes with thin or atrophic irides, posterior iris insertion, marked iridodonesis and pseudophakodonesis appear particularly prone to excessive iridolenticular contact, analogous to mechanisms implicated in pigment dispersion syndrome. Whether these anatomical factors contribute directly to altered aqueous dynamics warrants further investigation.

The risk of RPB in myopic, vitrectomized pseudophakic eyes may be reduced by prophylactic peripheral iridotomy.4,10,14 However, the optimal number, size and position of iridotomies remain uncertain and are likely to depend upon individual ocular anatomy and fluid dynamics.14,32 In some eyes a single iridotomy appears insufficient, possibly because the rate of anterior-to-posterior aqueous flow exceeds the drainage capacity of one opening. We have observed quadrant-specific and position-dependent flow patterns in some patients. In one case, posterior-to-anterior flow occurred superiorly while simultaneous anterior-to-posterior flow was observed in other quadrants despite multiple patent iridotomies. The influence of haptic position on these fluid dynamics also deserves further study.

In this observational series we utilized naturally occurring pigment, inflammatory cells and particulate material as physiological tracers, allowing direct visualization of aqueous currents at the slit lamp without the need for specialized imaging techniques. Our observations support the concept that RPB represents a compartmental imbalance in which anterior chamber pressure transiently exceeds posterior chamber pressure as a consequence of altered aqueous dynamics rather than true pupillary obstruction.

Future studies employing real-time imaging and tracer techniques may further define aqueous flow patterns in eyes with altered compartmental anatomy, including those undergoing secondary IOL fixation and glaucoma surgery. The direct observation of aqueous particles moving from the anterior chamber toward the posterior segment provides clinical support for a reversal of the normal aqueous pressure gradient in vitrectomized eyes with scleral-fixated IOLs. Recognition of this altered physiology provides a rational explanation for reverse pupillary block and for the effectiveness of peripheral iridotomy in its prevention and treatment.
