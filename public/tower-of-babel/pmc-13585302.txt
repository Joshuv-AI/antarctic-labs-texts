Mild alkaline phosphatase elevation associates with increased mortality and major adverse liver outcomes in MASLD
Authors: Jingyi Hu, Christopher Okarski, Pooja Rangan, Blake Czapla, Kira Zhao, Ethan Grey, Suranjan Bantupalli, Leanne Nasser, Majd Aboona, Karn Wijarnpreecha, Vincent L. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) elevation is commonly observed in patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), but its clinical implications in MASLD are unclear. We evaluated whether longitudinal patterns of mild ALP elevation are associated with mortality and major adverse liver outcomes (MALO).Methods:We performed a multicenter retrospective cohort study of adults with MASLD from Michigan Medicine and the Banner Health system. We included patients with MASLD defined as hepatic steatosis on imaging and at least one cardiometabolic comorbidity, with at least 1 ALP measurement within 1 year of the imaging date. Mild ALP elevation was defined as >1 to ≤1.5× the upper limit of normal and categorized as persistently normal, transiently elevated, or persistently elevated. Primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and MALO. Mortality was modeled as a Cox proportional hazards model, and MALO as Fine–Gray competing risk models.Results:Among 32,753 patients, persistently elevated ALP was present in ~10% of both cohorts. After multivariable adjustment, persistently elevated ALP was associated with increased mortality and MALO in both Michigan (mortality HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.20–1.89; MALO SHR 2.45, 95% CI 1.55–3.88) and Banner (mortality HR 1.81, 95% CI 1.19–2.75; MALO SHR 2.13, 95% CI 1.55–2.94). Associations were most consistent in low-risk and intermediate-risk FIB-4 groups. Associations remained significant in sensitivity analyses excluding baseline cirrhosis and restricting to patients with at least 2 ALP measurements.Conclusions:Persistent ALP elevation was associated with increased risk of mortality and MALO in MASLD, particularly among patients with low-risk and intermediate-risk FIB-4 scores. These findings suggest that ALP may serve as a clinically meaningful biomarker in early MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a leading cause of advanced liver disease and major adverse liver outcomes (MALO), including hepatocellular carcinoma and hepatic decompensation. Current risk stratification in MASLD relies largely on noninvasive fibrosis markers such as FIB-4 and transient elastography.1,2 While these tools are useful for fibrosis assessment, additional widely available biomarkers may further refine prognostic assessment in MASLD.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is routinely measured in clinical practice and is traditionally interpreted in the setting of biliary obstruction, cholestatic liver disease, or extrahepatic processes such as bone turnover. 3 Higher ALP levels—even if within the “normal” range—have been associated with increased incidence of overall mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events;4,5 the mechanisms underlying these associations remain unclear and may involve vascular calcification or systemic inflammation. However, whether ALP elevations are associated with incident MALO in MASLD (in the absence of concomitant overt cholestatic liver disease) is not well-described. One multicenter study of 685 patients with MASLD and cirrhosis found that patients with ALP >1.5× the upper limit of normal (ULN) were more likely to have experienced hepatic decompensation. 6 Incidence of decompensation was not reported. Another study in MASLD found that a cholestatic liver enzyme pattern (defined by R ratio <2) was associated with increased incidence of MALO among 228 patients with stages 3–4 fibrosis; this study did not strictly assess ALP levels, however, but rather relative levels of ALT and ALP, and the median ALP level in the cholestatic group was only 100 (presumably normal). 7 The clinical implications of low-grade ALP elevations (>1.0 to ≤1.5× ULN) in MASLD are not known, especially in the absence of cirrhosis, which is itself commonly associated with cholestasis. Data specifically examining mild and longitudinal patterns of ALP elevation in MASLD are limited. It is unclear whether mild or persistent ALP elevation is associated with adverse clinical outcomes in MASLD.

In this study, we evaluated whether mild ALP elevation (defined as >1.0 to ≤1.5× ULN) and patterns of ALP elevation (transient or persistent) are associated with mortality and MALO in patients with MASLD.

METHODS

Ethics

The Institutional Review Boards of the University of Michigan (Ann Arbor, MI) and Banner University Medical Center—Phoenix approved all research conducted in this study. Written consent was deemed not to be required because this study met criteria for secondary use analysis. All study procedures complied with the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Cohorts

We conducted a longitudinal retrospective cohort study of adult patients with MASLD from 2 independent health systems: Michigan Medicine and the Banner Health system, which represents primary and tertiary care centers in Arizona, Colorado, and California. The cohorts have been previously described.8–10 In brief, Michigan Medicine patients were identified based on a natural language processing algorithm, which searched imaging reports for evidence of hepatic steatosis. Banner patients were identified based on ICD codes for MASLD (571.6, K76.0, or K75.81), which have been well validated.11,12 In both cohorts, we required at least one cardiometabolic risk factor consistent with MASLD, 13 in the absence of alternative causes of liver disease, including cholestatic conditions such as primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis (Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480). In addition, because the focus of this manuscript was ALP, we excluded participants with non-liver conditions that could affect ALP levels during the study period, such as pregnancy, chronic bone disease, and severe pancreaticobiliary pathology (Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480). We also excluded patients with baseline malignancy since cancer staging is a common indication for diagnostic imaging 14 and may result in a biased cohort with increased risk of biliary obstruction or bone metastasis, which could also result in ALP elevations.

The index date was defined as the date of the first qualifying imaging study on record demonstrating hepatic steatosis (Michigan Medicine) or ICD codes for hepatic steatosis (Banner). Demographic, laboratory, diagnosis, procedure, and vital status were extracted from the electronic medical record.

Predictors and outcomes

We defined the primary exposure as the ALP elevation pattern based on all available ALP measurements within 1 year before or after the index date. Values were normalized to the Michigan Medicine ULN (115 IU/L), with elevated ALP defined as >1 to ≤1.5×ULN. Patients were then categorized into 3 groups: persistently normal (all values ≤1×ULN), transiently elevated (at least one value >1 to ≤1.5×ULN, but a median ≤1×ULN), and persistently elevated (median >1 to ≤1.5×ULN). Persistently normal patients served as the reference in subsequent analyses. To maintain a focused study population and minimize confounding from more advanced or alternative pathologies, we excluded patients with any ALP value exceeding 1.5×ULN from the primary analysis. This threshold was chosen to isolate the impact of mild ALP elevations and ensure a more clinically homogeneous cohort.

The primary outcomes were overall mortality and MALO. Mortality was defined as death from any cause during follow-up. MALO was defined as the occurrence of hepatic decompensation events, including ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, and hepatorenal syndrome, identified using diagnostic codes (Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480). Secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), cardiovascular (CV) death, and liver-related death. MACE was identified using diagnostic and procedural codes for major cardiovascular outcomes, including myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular interventions. Cause-specific mortality analyses were only available in the Michigan cohort due to the lack of linkage to a national cause-of-death database in the Banner cohort. We used a landmark of 365 days following the index date to reduce the risk of lead-time bias. For mortality analyses, follow-up continued until death or the last clinical encounter. For MALO and MACE, follow-up continued until the outcome of interest, death, or last clinical encounter, whichever occurred first; death prior to the outcome of interest was treated as a competing event. In the Michigan cohort, all patients with potential MALO events underwent manual chart review to confirm MASLD diagnosis, exclusion criteria, and MALO occurrence.

Statistics

Baseline characteristics of both cohorts were summarized and compared across ALP elevation groups. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

To examine the relationship between ALP elevation pattern and clinical outcomes, we used time-to-event analyses suitable for each outcome type. All-cause mortality was modeled using Cox proportional hazards regression with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Fine–Gray competing risk regression was used for MALO and MACE, treating death as a competing event with results reported as subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For each outcome, we first ran unadjusted models, then built multivariable models adjusting for clinically relevant baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index, and comorbid conditions: diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed stratified by FIB-4 categories as a surrogate of fibrosis stage (<1.3, 1.3–2.67, and >2.67) and by diabetes status. Sensitivity analyses excluding patients with baseline cirrhosis and patients with only a single ALP measurement within ±1 year of the index date were also performed.

In addition, we evaluated the association between ALP and outcomes using restricted cubic spline models with 3 knots. ALP was analyzed as a continuous variable. Overall mortality and MALO were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models for both cohorts.

A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Cohort description

A total of 32,753 patients were analyzed, including 13,046 from the University of Michigan cohort and 19,707 from the Banner Health cohort. Baseline characteristics stratified by ALP elevation pattern are presented in Table 1. Most patients had persistently normal ALP (85% at Michigan, 81% at Banner), and those with transiently (5% at Michigan, 9% at Banner) or persistently elevated ALP >1.0 to ≤1.5× ULN (10% at both sites) were more likely to be female and older. Metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, class 3 obesity, and hypertension, were more common in patients with transiently or persistently elevated ALP. ALT and AST were higher among the participants with ALP elevations, as were FIB-4 scores.

Event rates: Analysis of raw event rates (Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480) showed a progressive increase in both mortality and MALO as ALP levels rose relative to the ULN (115 U/L). In the Michigan cohort, mortality rates nearly doubled from 6.9 per 1000 person-years (PY) in the lowest ALP category (<0.5× ULN) to a peak of 13.1 per 1000 PY in the highest category (1.4–1.5× ULN). Similarly, Michigan’s MALO rates exhibited a steady upward trajectory, rising from 1.3 per 1000 PY in the lowest group to 6.3 per 1000 PY as ALP levels approached 1.4× ULN. The Banner cohort had similar findings, with mortality rates also nearly doubling from the lowest ALP group to the highest. The increase in MALO rates began once ALP levels exceeded the ULN. For both groups, the relative increase in event rates was more pronounced for MALO compared with mortality. MACE event rates showed less consistent trends across ALP strata compared with mortality and MALO, particularly in the Banner cohort (Supplemental Table S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480).

Regression models

Given these observed trends in raw event rates, we next performed survival analyses to determine whether these longitudinal ALP patterns remained independently associated with clinical outcomes (Table 2 and Figure 1). After multivariable adjustment, persistently elevated ALP remained significantly associated with both mortality and MALO in both cohorts. In the Michigan cohort, persistently elevated ALP was associated with a 51% increased risk of mortality (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.20–1.89) and more than a twofold increased risk of MALO (SHR 2.45, 95% CI 1.55–3.88) after adjustment. Similarly, in the Banner cohort, persistently elevated ALP was associated with increased risks of mortality (HR 1.81, 95% CI 1.19–2.75) and MALO (SHR 2.13, 95% CI 1.55–2.94). In secondary analyses from the Michigan cohort, persistently elevated ALP was also associated with increased risks of cardiovascular death (adjusted HR 1.79, 95% CI 1.35–2.38) and liver-related death (adjusted HR 2.91, 95% CI 1.12–7.53). In contrast, no significant association was observed between ALP elevation and MACE after adjustment in either cohort (Supplemental Table S6, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480).

Results were less consistent for the transient elevation group. In the Michigan cohort, transient elevation was not significantly associated with either outcome after adjustment, although it remained associated with cardiovascular death. In Banner, transient elevation remained associated with increased mortality risk but was not significantly associated with MALO. Given these findings, we focused on persistent ALP elevation versus persistently normal ALP for the remainder of our analyses.

For mortality, risk showed a modest increase near but below the ULN, with a more progressive rise once exceeding the ULN. For MALO, the Michigan cohort exhibited a threshold-like rise mostly above the ULN, whereas Banner showed a more gradual increase in risk as ALP increases.

Sensitivity analyses

We next conducted subgroup analyses based on liver disease severity measured by FIB-4 score and diabetes, which is a major risk factor for adverse outcomes in MASLD 15 (Table 3). In patients with diabetes, persistent ALP elevation continued to signal a higher risk. While this association remained significant for both outcomes in the Michigan cohort, it was limited to MALO in the Banner cohort. When stratified by fibrosis estimated by FIB-4, persistent ALP elevation was associated with increased risk of mortality and MALO in both the Michigan and Banner cohorts, in the low FIB-4 categories. In intermediate FIB-4 (1.3–2.67) patients, ALP elevation was associated with increased mortality in both cohorts, but with MALO only in the Michigan cohort. Associations between ALP elevation and mortality or MALO were mostly not significant in the high FIB-4 (>2.67) category, likely due to low numbers of patients. Notably, in both cohorts, patients with low FIB-4 (<1.3) but persistently elevated ALP experienced an incidence rate of MALO comparable to that of intermediate FIB-4 (1.3–2.67) and persistently normal ALP (Supplemental Table S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480).

To further assess whether the observed risks were reflective of cirrhosis, which can be associated with mild ALP elevations, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding all patients with cirrhosis at baseline (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480). The associations were all significant and consistent with the primary analysis.

Finally, we performed sensitivity analyses limited to patients with at least 2 ALP measurements within ±1 year of the index date to better capture longitudinal ALP patterns rather than relying on a single value that may reflect an acute or transient state (Supplemental Table S4, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480). Associations between mild ALP elevation and both MALO and mortality were significant in the Michigan cohort, and the MALO association remained significant in the Banner cohort.

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study, we found that mild ALP elevation was associated with increased risk of mortality and major adverse liver outcomes in patients with MASLD. These associations were reproducible across 2 independent health systems and remained significant after adjustment for demographic and cardiometabolic risk factors and several sensitivity analyses. This study adds to the literature by demonstrating associations between ALP and MALO in a cohort size >10 times larger than in previous studies and with a low prevalence of cirrhosis.

Persistent ALP elevation was associated with modestly increased overall mortality and markedly increased MALO incidence. The association between MALO and ALP was largely consistent across FIB-4 categories in both cohorts. Of note, patients with low FIB-4 (<1.3) and persistently elevated ALP had a similar MALO incidence rate as those with intermediate FIB-4 (1.3–2.67) and persistently normal ALP (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C480). These findings suggest that ALP elevation may increase disease risk to a level that may require, at a minimum, closer FIB-4 monitoring, or even second-line testing such as transient elastography. This is akin to current practice guidance, which recommends more frequent FIB-4 monitoring in patients with diabetes or at least 2 cardiometabolic risk factors than in those with only 1 non-diabetes cardiometabolic risk factor. 1 Persistent ALP elevation was also associated with cardiovascular and liver-related death in the Michigan cohort, despite no significant association with MACE. These findings may suggest that mild ALP elevation reflects broader systemic and hepatic disease severity rather than simply increased incidence of discrete cardiovascular events.

Mechanisms underlying the association between mild ALP elevation and overall mortality or MALO remain unclear. Some of the earliest associations between ALP and overall mortality were in patients undergoing hemodialysis,16,17 suggesting that the associations may have been due to abnormal bone metabolism (common in end-stage renal disease), which has itself been linked to overall mortality. 18 ALP also inactivates inorganic pyrophosphate (reduced in chronic kidney disease), which is normally protective against vascular calcification; thus, ALP elevations may result in increased atherogenesis exacerbated by pyrophosphate deficiency in chronic kidney disease.19,20 However, more recent studies showing associations between ALP and both overall and cardiovascular mortality in the general population suggest that either these mechanisms are not exclusive to chronic kidney disease or other mechanisms are responsible. The association between mild ALP elevation and MALO—in a population with a low prevalence of chronic kidney disease—is also of uncertain cause. Beyond chronic kidney disease, associations between ALP and outcomes have been investigated in other chronic liver diseases and metabolic populations, although the direction and magnitude of these associations vary depending on the disease. In chronic hepatitis C, lower ALP levels are associated with an increased risk of virologic relapse before, during, and after pegylated interferon-α and ribavirin treatment. These findings demonstrated a potential link between ALP and residual hepatic disease activity or underlying fibrosis. 21 However, in chronic hepatitis B, elevated ALP has been reported to correlate with significant fibrosis in untreated patients. 22 In addition, from a study of a large US general population cohort, we know that elevated ALP is independently associated with increased mortality after adjusting for metabolic syndrome, meaning the prognostic value of ALP is not fully explained by traditional metabolic risk factors. 5 Notably, there is an extensive literature on bile acid derangements in MASLD: MASLD is associated with increased serum bile acid levels and an increased proportion of primary conjugated (relative to unconjugated) bile acids. 23 Activation of farnesoid X receptor reduces hepatic lipids and also results in decreased intestinal bile acid levels, resulting in less intestinal lipid absorption. 24 Thus, elevated ALP may reflect lower levels of FXR activation, which may contribute to a higher risk of MALO. 25 Future mechanistic studies will be required to determine the reason for the associations between mild ALP elevation and MALO.

The persistence of these associations after exclusion of patients with baseline cirrhosis suggests that the findings were not solely driven by advanced liver disease. In subgroup analyses, the strongest and most consistent associations were observed in low-risk and intermediate-risk FIB-4 populations. Together, these findings suggest that persistent mild ALP elevation may identify higher-risk MASLD patients in the early disease stage, before advanced fibrosis becomes clinically evident.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, ~25% of patients had only a single ALP measurement within the analysis window, which may limit certainty in classifying persistent versus transient elevation. However, sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with at least 2 ALP measurements support the stability of the observed associations. Second, patients who died within 1 year of the index date were excluded to ensure meaningful risk prediction. This exclusion had a greater impact in the Banner cohort, where some patients appeared to receive MASLD diagnoses during acute illness shortly before death, potentially contributing to differences in early survival curve separation between cohorts. Third, ALP is a nonspecific biomarker that may reflect hepatic, biliary, bone, and systemic processes. Although extensive exclusion criteria were applied to minimize alternative causes of ALP elevation, residual confounding from unrecognized conditions cannot be completely excluded. However, around 10% of patients demonstrated persistently elevated ALP, making it unlikely that the observed associations were primarily driven by undiagnosed cholestatic liver diseases alone, given that the prevalence of conditions such as primary biliary cholangitis or primary sclerosing cholangitis is under 1 per 1000 adults.26–29 Lastly, the lack of significant association between ALP elevation and MACE should be interpreted with caution, as MACE was identified using ICD-code and procedure-code instead of adjudicated cardiovascular hospitalizations, which may have reduced sensitivity for detecting MACE events during follow-up.

In conclusion, persistent mild ALP elevation was independently associated with increased risks of mortality and major adverse liver outcomes in MASLD across 2 independent cohorts. These associations were numerically greatest for MALO and remained evident in noncirrhotic and lower-risk fibrosis populations. Persistent mild ALP elevation may therefore represent a widely available biomarker for identifying higher-risk MASLD patients earlier in the disease course; in particular, patients with MASLD and low FIB-4 but persistently elevated ALP may warrant more aggressive monitoring, similar to what is already done for those with low FIB-4 but diabetes.
