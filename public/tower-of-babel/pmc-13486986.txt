The Modular Cup-in-Cup Construct in Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Ryan C Palmer, McKenzie Culler, Pranit Kumaran, Donald B Longjohn, Daniel A Oakes, Nathanael D Heckmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe modular cup-in-cup technique, in which a modular primary acetabular component is cemented into a highly porous monobloc tantalum revision shell, provides the intraoperative flexibility to change bearings if subsequent re-revision is required. This study seeks to characterize the early outcomes of this technique.MethodsA single-center retrospective review was performed of all patients who underwent revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) utilizing a modular acetabular component cemented into a highly porous monobloc tantalum revision shell. Preoperative and postoperative radiographs were assessed to quantify bone loss, cup position, and aseptic loosening. All-cause reoperation, revision for aseptic loosening, and revision for instability were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsIn total, 27 hips were identified with a mean follow-up of 21.6 months. The most common indication for revision was aseptic loosening (48.1%). Preoperatively, 16 (59.3%) hips had Paprosky 3A defects, 7 (25.9%) had Paprosky 3B, 2 (7.4%) had Paprosky 2C defects, and 1 (3.7%) had a Paprosky 2A defect. In total, 4 (14.8%) patients underwent reoperation, 3 (11.1%) for instability and 1 (3.7%) for irrigation and debridement. All patients revised for instability were converted from a dual mobility articulation to a constrained liner. No radiographic loosening was observed at final follow-up. Estimated 3-year survivorships for all-cause reoperation and revision for instability were 84.6% (95% confidence interval: 46.6-95.6%) and 88.5% (95% confidence interval, 48.8-97.6%), respectively.ConclusionsThe modular cup-in-cup technique is a viable option for rTHA patients at increased risk of instability. This technique provides surgeons the flexibility to exchange the articulation if recurrent instability occurs.Keywords: Revision total hip arthroplasty, Acetabular bone loss, Instability, Modular cup-in-cup technique, Dual-mobility articulation, Porous tantalum

Introduction

Instability following revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) remains a persistent challenge for arthroplasty surgeons, particularly in patients with a history of dislocation or significant acetabular bone loss. [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]] Various reconstructive strategies are frequently employed to enhance hip stability, including restoration of leg length and offset [2,6,7], use of large-diameter femoral heads [8,9], constrained liners [10], dual-mobility (DM) articulations [11], and finally abductor mechanism reconstruction. [12]

In high-risk patients, DM constructs are frequently employed given their ability to increase jump distance, improve the head-to-neck ratio, and reduce the risk of dislocation. [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]] However, in situations where a DM liner cannot be accommodated due to locking mechanism damage or incompatibility with an existing acetabular shell, cementation of a monobloc DM cup into a well-fixed acetabular shell has emerged as a viable reconstructive strategy. [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]] This approach maximizes stability without introducing additional constraint at the bone–implant interface. Chalmers et al. [20]were among the first to describe this technique in a series of 18 patients, reporting a 17% dislocation rate at a mean 3-year follow-up. While later series have reported similarly encouraging outcomes, concerns persist regarding the potential for recurrent dislocation, dissociation at the cement–cup interface, and the limited ability for articulation exchange if subsequent revision is required. [25,26]

At our institution, cementation of a modular acetabular component into a highly porous monobloc revision shell has been successfully employed in high-risk patients undergoing rTHA with severe acetabular defects and an increased risk of instability. This modular cup-in-cup technique enables independent optimization of shell position and liner orientation, while preserving the ability to exchange the modular DM articulation or increase constraint if subsequent acetabular-sided revision is required. Despite these advantages, the efficacy and longevity of these modular cup-in-cup constructs have yet to be determined. As such, this study sought to evaluate the early survivability of the modular cup-in-cup construct used in rTHA by assessing all-cause reoperation, revision for instability, and radiographic evidence of acetabular construct loosening.

Material and methods

Following Institutional Review Board approval (IRB HS-22-00553), a single-institution retrospective observational study was conducted between January 2017 and May 2025. Adult patients (≥18 years) were identified through our institutional total joint registry by first screening for rTHA cases in which a monobloc Trabecular Metal Acetabular Revision Shell (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) was utilized. Among these cases, patients in whom a modular primary acetabular component was cemented into a monobloc tantalum revision shell were included.

Radiographic review

Acetabular bone loss was evaluated based on preoperative radiographs, utilizing the Paprosky classification of acetabular bone loss [27]. Operative reports and implant records were reviewed to record operative details and implant sizes. If intraoperative computer navigation was utilized, the recorded values for inclination and anteversion of both the primary modular shell and the monobloc porous tantalum revision shell were extracted from the operative report. In cases where this information was absent or computer navigation was not utilized, inclination and anteversion were estimated on postoperative radiographs using TraumaCad Digital Orthopaedic Templating software (Brainlab AG; Munich, Germany) (Fig 1).

Postoperative measurements of cup inclination and anteversion for both the porous tantalum shell and inner modular component on a low anteroposterior radiograph of the pelvis, performed using TraumaCad Digital Orthopaedic Templating software (Brainlab AG; Munich, Germany).

Modular cup-in-cup indications and intraoperative technique

After bony acetabular preparation, the monobloc porous tantalum revision shell was placed. A nonmodular porous tantalum acetabular revision shell was selected for all cases because of its up to 80% porosity [28], modulus of elasticity similar to cancellous bone [29], and elliptical geometry, which facilitate host–bone contact and osseointegration. [30] The shell may also be modified to accommodate adjunctive locking screw fixation. (Fig 2) Cancellous screw fixation was used in all cases, and locking screw fixation was used in 25 (92.6%) cases. For locking screw fixation, the monobloc revision shell was predrilled with a 2.5-mm drill bit and countersunk on power to allow the heads of the 3.5-mm locking screws (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA) to gain purchase within the highly porous tantalum shell, creating a fixed-angle construct. In cases of extensive bone loss or inadequate acetabular component fixation, cages or augments were used for adjunct fixation. After placement of the monobloc revision shell, a modular primary acetabular component, at least 10-12 mm smaller than the outer porous tantalum acetabular revision component, was cemented into place. The outer monobloc shell was placed to optimize host–bone contact, while the inner modular primary component was placed in an orientation to optimize hip stability. Final bearing selection was determined after trialing, allowing the final articulation choice to occur after cementation.

(d) Cementation of the modular 58 mm multihole cup into the porous tantalum revision shell.

Clinical outcomes

Survival was assessed using all-cause reoperation, instability-related revision, and radiographic evidence of acetabular component loosening as endpoints. All-cause reoperation was defined as any subsequent ipsilateral hip surgery, including irrigation and debridement, modular component exchange, or revision of the femoral or acetabular components. Instability-related revision was defined as any surgical intervention meant to address dislocation or recurrent instability, including the addition or exchange of a DM articulation, or conversion to a constrained liner. Patients were classified as radiographically loose if serial radiographs demonstrated progressive radiolucent lines around the acetabular component or component migration on serial radiographs. Radiographs were evaluated in the anteroposterior and lateral planes to assess the bone–implant interface of the overlying monobloc revision shell. Two reviewers (R.C.P. and N.D.H.) independently assessed all radiographs, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Data analyses

One and 3-year survivorship estimates for all-cause reoperation, revision for instability, and aseptic loosening were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the Kaplan–Meier survivorship estimates were derived using the beta-product confidence procedure, given the relatively small sample size and substantial censoring in this cohort. [31,32] Means, medians, and ranges were reported for patient demographics, surgical/implant characteristics, and radiographic parameters, where appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Implant characteristics

Modular cup-in-cup constructs consisted of an outer porous tantalum acetabular revision shell (outer diameter range: 60–80 mm) with a modular primary acetabular component cemented into the outer shell (inner diameter range: 48–62 mm), all from a single manufacturer (Zimmer Biomet). Eighteen (66.7%) hips received a DM articulation (Zimmer Biomet G7), 7 (25.9%) received a constrained liner (Zimmer Biomet G7 Freedom Constrained, Warsaw, IN), and 2 (7.4%) received a standard solid bearing liner (Zimmer Biomet G7, Warsaw, IN). (Table 4) Porous tantalum augments were utilized in 11 (40.7%) cases, and cages were utilized in 4 (14.8%) cases.

Complications

Six patients (22.2%) experienced at least 1 postoperative complication. (Table 5) Four patients (14.8%) underwent reoperation at a mean of 64.5 days postoperatively (range, 51–78 days). Indications for reoperation included recurrent dislocation of a DM articulation requiring conversion to a constrained liner (n = 3) and superficial wound dehiscence managed with irrigation and debridement (n = 1). (Fig 3) The mean number of prior ipsilateral hip surgeries was 7 among patients who dislocated, compared with 3 among those who did not experience postoperative instability. One patient developed a postoperative sciatic nerve palsy and 1 sustained an intraoperative periprosthetic femur fracture managed with cerclage wire fixation.

Construct survivorship

Estimated survivorship free from all-cause reoperation was 84.62% (95% CI, 61.3-95.6%) at 1 year and 84.62% (95% CI, 46.6-95.6%) at 3 years, while survival free from instability-related revision was 88.5% (95% CI, 65.1–97.6%) at 1 year and 88.5% (95% CI, 48.8–97.6%) at 3 years. Discussion

In this retrospective case series of 27 revision hips performed using the modular cup-in-cup technique, the estimated 3-year survivorship free from all-cause reoperation was 84.62% (95% CI, 46.6-95.6%), while survivorship free from instability-related revision was 88.5% (95% CI, 48.8–97.6%). Four patients (14.8%) required reoperation, including three for recurrent instability necessitating conversion from a DM articulation to a constrained liner. Given that all reoperations occurred within 90 days of the index procedure, these events should be interpreted within the context of the complex revision setting and patient-specific risk factors rather than isolated evidence of early modular construct failure. The majority of cases were performed in the setting of significant acetabular bone loss, with 85.2% of hips classified as Paprosky 3A or 3B. Despite the complexity of achieving a stable hip and durable fixation in this multiply operated cohort, no failures occurred due to aseptic loosening, underscoring the potential durability of this revision acetabular construct. Although longer-term follow-up is needed to better define the longevity of this modular construct, these findings support the modular cup-in-cup technique as a viable fixation strategy in multiply revised patients at increased risk of instability with significant acetabular bone loss.

Compared to conventional single-bearing constructs, DM implants have consistently demonstrated lower postoperative dislocation rates in both primary and rTHA. In a systematic review of 17,908 THAs with DM articulations from 59 studies, De Martino et al. [11] reported a pooled dislocation rate of 0.9% following primary THA and 3.0% following rTHA, substantially lower than the 7% to 14% rates reported for conventional bearings. In contrast to constrained liners, which reduce instability through capture of the femoral head, DM articulations preserve a greater arc of motion and potentially decrease forces across the bone–implant interface, mitigating the risk of mechanical failure [33]. These advantages have contributed to the expanding use of DM components in complex revision scenarios involving instability, severe acetabular bone loss, abductor deficiency, and in cases when adjunctive fixation, such as augments, bone grafting, or cup–cage constructs, is needed [11,[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Early efforts involved scoring the backside of a cobalt–chromium DM liner with a high-speed burr and cementing it into a retained acetabular shell or monobloc component. This approach provided a practical means of obtaining a DM articulation; however, early experience demonstrated limited durability. Plummer et al. [38] reported 2 failures within 90 days in which modular DM liners dissociated at the liner-cement interface, prompting abandonment of the technique in favor of cementing a monobloc DM shell instead.

Cementation of a monobloc DM cup into a well-fixed acetabular component subsequently emerged as a viable technique to enhance stability and mitigate bone loss that would otherwise occur with the removal of a well-fixed shell [39,40]. Several studies have demonstrated favorable results using this approach, both in well-fixed shells and newly implanted acetabular components [22,23,[41], [42], [43], [44]]. Chalmers et al. [20] reported three dislocations among 18 hips at a 3-year mean follow-up, including 1 patient who required revision to a constrained liner and 2 who underwent open reduction with retention of the DM construct. Wilson et al. [25] reported a 92% survivorship free from both aseptic revision and dislocation at 2 years among 65 hips, with 9 revisions performed: three for periprosthetic joint infection, three for acetabular loosening, 2 for dislocation, and 1 for a broken cup–cage construct. Gabor et al. reported a 2.6% dislocation rate at a median follow-up of 7 months among 38 cases, with no instances of construct failure or cement–implant interface dissociation. [22] Stambough et al. [45] similarly described a cup-in-cup technique in 13 hips revised for recurrent instability with a retained well-fixed acetabular component, reporting 85% survivorship at minimum 2-year follow-up and no revisions for loosening at the cup–cup cement interface. Compared to these series, our cohort demonstrated similar results. At final follow-up, no radiographic loosening was observed at a mean 21.6-month follow-up, and the estimated 3-year survivorship free from instability-related revision was 88.5%. These findings are notable given that our series comprised a multiply revised cohort in which 85.2% of hips were classified as Paprosky 3A or 3B defects. Moreover, in 3 patients with recurrent instability, conversion to a constrained liner was successfully performed without removal of the well-fixed outer shell, thereby limiting operative complexity and potential morbidity related to re-revision surgery.

As with cementation of a monobloc DM cup, the modular cup-in-cup technique enables independent control of the porous outer shell and inner modular articulation. The outer porous tantalum component can be positioned to optimize host bone contact, while the inner cup can be oriented to maximize impingement-free range of motion and optimize hip stability. Liner modularity also allows adjustment of offset, elevated rim options, and face-changing liners. In the event of recurrent instability, this modularity preserves the ability to exchange the inner articulation without disrupting the well-fixed outer shell or requiring meticulous removal of a retained cemented liner or monobloc DM cup. [40] While this functional advantage distinguishes the modular cup-in-cup construct from monobloc alternatives, dedicated biomechanical and comparative clinical studies are needed to determine whether this translates into improved fixation strength or implant survivorship. Modular compatibility of the construct further enables a potential staged approach in which stable osseointegration can first be achieved before sequentially introducing higher levels of constraint in the event of recurrent instability. Historically, while constrained liners have been associated with all-cause reoperation rates approaching 50% at 10-year follow-up [46], recent studies of contemporary constrained systems have demonstrated survivorship exceeding 95%, with lower rates of mechanical failure, dissociation, and aseptic loosening than historical constrained designs [38,[47], [48], [49]]. In addition to these mechanical advantages, the modular construct aligns with registry-based evidence demonstrating that DM implants are cost-effective in revision THA, particularly among high-risk patients and those revised for instability [50]. Collectively, these findings support the modular cup-in-cup technique as an innovative, adaptable, and potentially economically viable strategy for addressing instability and extensive acetabular bone loss in complex rTHA.

This study has several potential limitations. First, the relatively short duration of follow-up limits our ability to assess long-term implant survivorship. Accordingly, survivorship was reported at 1 and 3 years to better reflect the available follow-up in this cohort and minimize overinterpretation of later time points where few patients remained at risk. In addition, 95% CIs were calculated using the beta-product confidence procedure to more appropriately reflect uncertainty in the setting of substantial censoring. Second, this was a retrospective, single-center study without a comparator group. While this limits direct comparisons to alternative reconstruction strategies, patients selected for this technique typically had severe acetabular bone loss and were at increased risk of instability, and therefore would not have been appropriate candidates for standard revision techniques. Furthermore, a comparator group was not available as this technique was preferentially employed for patients at increased risk of instability whenever a 60-mm or larger monobloc porous tantalum acetabular revision component was used. Finally, all procedures were performed by high-volume, fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons at a single high-volume tertiary academic center, which may limit generalizability. While this consistency in operative technique and implant selection minimizes confounding from intersurgeon variability and may provide a best-case assessment of construct performance, the learning curve and reproducibility of the modular cup-in-cup technique in lower-volume settings remain unknown.

This study has several notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the largest case series to date focused specifically on the modular cup-in-cup technique in rTHA [45,51,52]. Second, a detailed radiographic analysis was performed using both intraoperative records and standardized templating software to characterize component positioning and confirm the absence of radiographic loosening. Third, survivorship was rigorously assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method, enabling time-dependent interpretation of outcomes in a cohort with variable follow-up.

Conclusions

The modular cup-in-cup technique represents a promising reconstructive strategy for patients undergoing rTHA in the setting of severe acetabular bone loss and instability. In this series, no cases of aseptic loosening were observed, and the estimated 3-year survivorship free from instability-related revision was 88.5%, supporting the potential stability afforded by this construct in high-risk, multiply operated patients. If a subsequent revision is required, this modular approach preserves intraoperative flexibility, allowing articulation exchange or conversion to a constrained liner without the need to revise a well-fixed acetabular shell or remove bone cement. While early outcomes are favorable, additional studies with longer follow-up and larger cohorts are needed to further define optimal patient selection, cost-effectiveness, and implant durability.
