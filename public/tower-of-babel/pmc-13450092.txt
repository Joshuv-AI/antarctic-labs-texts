Associations between physical literacy and physical activity and sedentary behavior among Taiwanese older adults: a nationwide survey
Authors: Chien-Yu Liu, Yung Liao, Ching-Ping Lin, Ching-Wei Chang, Hsin-Heng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePhysical literacy (PL) is increasingly viewed as a modifiable determinant of movement behaviors in later life. This study examined the associations of PL with meeting physical activity (PA) recommendations and with sedentary behavior (SB) among community-dwelling older adults in Taiwan.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted using computer-assisted telephone interviewing with stratified random sampling across four geographic regions (N = 1,078; ≥ 65 years). PL was assessed using the Senior Perceived Physical Literacy Instrument, and PA and SB were measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form. Binary logistic regression models were estimated with adjustment for demographic and health-related covariates.ResultsHigher PL was associated with greater odds of meeting recommended PA levels (adjusted OR = 1.08, 95% CI [1.05, 1.12]) and lower odds of prolonged SB (adjusted OR = 0.96, 95% CI [0.94, 0.99]).ConclusionPL was a robust factor of both higher PA and lower SB among older adults. PL-informed, community-based strategies that build motivation, confidence, and functional capability may support more active daily routines and help reduce disparities in movement opportunities across diverse living contexts.

Background

Physical activity (PA) is critical for preserving health, functional independence, and quality of life in later adulthood. A substantial body of evidence has demonstrated that regular PA is associated with reduced risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, functional decline, and disability, while also contributing to improved mental health and well-being among older adults [9, 14, 28], Izquierdo Mikel et al., 2021). As the global population ages, healthy aging has become a pressing public health priority, with increasing emphasis on maintaining functional ability and promoting active lifestyles across the lifespan [14]. The World Health Organization [18] estimates that by 2030, one in six individuals worldwide will be 60 years or older. Regular PA contributes substantially to both physical and psychological well-being, reducing the risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, certain cancers, and falls, while enhancing cognitive function, mental health, sleep quality, and body composition [14]. Accordingly, the WHO recommends that older adults accumulate 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination per week. In Taiwan, a recent survey showed that 68.3% of older adults met these recommendations, however, 15.7% were classified as physically inactive (Tsai et al., 2024). Despite this encouraging finding, understanding why some Taiwanese older adults meet PA recommendations while others remain inactive is critical for tailoring effective health promotion strategies.

In addition to PA, sedentary behavior (SB) has been identified as an independent behavioral risk factor. A study of sedentary patterns among older adults in Taiwan reported that over one-third accumulated more than six hours of sedentary time per day [12]. SB is defined as any waking behavior characterized by an energy expenditure of ≤ 1.5 METs while in a sitting, reclining, or lying posture and is conceptually distinct from physical inactivity (Tremblay et al., 2017). Increasing evidence indicates that prolonged SB adversely affects metabolic health—contributing to metabolic syndrome, obesity, and impaired glycemic regulation—and is associated with cognitive decline and mental health disorders [9, 14, 18]. These risks may be further exacerbated by age-related declines in mobility, limited awareness of SB-related health implications, and entrenched sedentary routines [8], McGowan et al., 2019). Taken together, insufficient PA and prolonged SB pose significant threats to older adults’ health, highlighting the urgent need to identify effective strategies to promote more active lifestyles and reduce sedentary time.

Physical literacy (PL) has emerged as a promising framework for addressing physical inactivity and sedentary behavior while supporting healthy aging. Whitehead [18] conceptualized PL as “the motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge, and understanding to value and take responsibility for engaging in PA for life,” emphasizing that both capability and disposition are essential for sustained engagement in movement. Building on this perspective, Cairney (2019) positioned PL as an upstream determinant of health through its influence on PA participation. By integrating motor competence, motivation, confidence, affective engagement, and knowledge, PL facilitates sustained PA involvement and generates both physiological and psychological benefits that reduce the risk of chronic disease. Conversely, lower levels of PL have been consistently associated with inactivity, suggesting that PL may shape long-term behavioral patterns that either promote or hinder healthy aging [18].

Despite growing scholarly interest, empirical evidence examining the concurrent associations between PL, PA, and SB in older adults remains limited. Existing PL research has predominantly focused on children, adolescents, and general adult populations, with comparatively little attention given to older adults [5, 6, 18]. This gap is particularly important given that older adults often experience distinct functional limitations, chronic health conditions, and environmental constraints that may influence their engagement in PA and related behaviors [7, 14, 18].

To address this gap, the present study investigates the relationships between PL and PA, as well as between PL and SB, among older adults in Taiwan. By simultaneously examining these associations, this research aims to advance the empirical understanding of PL as a behavioral determinant in later life and to inform more context-sensitive strategies for promoting active and healthy aging.

Methods

Study design and sample

A nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted in Taiwan using computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI). Eligible participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years as of December 2024. The sampling frame was derived from the 2023 national residential telephone directory, which includes household telephone numbers across all 22 administrative regions. To ensure that individuals with unlisted numbers had an equal probability of selection, random digit dialing (RDD) was applied by randomly generating the last two digits of telephone numbers.

A stratified random sampling approach was employed based on four geographic regions of Taiwan (Northern, Central, Southern, and Eastern Taiwan and Offshore Islands), with proportional allocation according to national population statistics (reference month: November 2023) reported by the Ministry of the Interior. The target sample size was 1,067 participants, including 482 from Northern Taiwan, 253 from Central Taiwan, 301 from Southern Taiwan, and 32 from Eastern Taiwan and Offshore Islands (Table 1). This sample size was determined to achieve a sampling error of less than 3% at a 95% confidence level. Data collection was conducted using CATI, a method widely adopted in large-scale population surveys due to its efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and ability to reach geographically diverse populations. The standardized interview procedures help reduce interviewer-related bias and improve data quality.

The data collection procedure consisted of three stages. First, experienced interviewers underwent at least 8 h of standardized training to ensure consistency in survey administration. Second, formal telephone interviews were conducted, with ongoing supervision and monitoring to maintain data quality. Third, all collected data were systematically checked, including data entry verification and error screening, prior to statistical analysis.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted between January and March 2024 by trained interviewers from a professional telephone survey center using a standardized and structured questionnaire. Before the interviews, the interviewers received at least 8 h of training on survey administration, and a pre-interview checklist was used to confirm participants’ ability to comprehend and respond logically; if a respondent could not answer specific questions even after explanation, or if the interview was disrupted (e.g., due to technical interference), the case was excluded.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from an institutional review board. All participants provided verbal informed consent prior to the interview.

Measures

Senior perceived physical literacy instrument

The SPPLI, developed by Liu et al. (2022), is an 11-item self-report scale designed to assess older adults’ perceived PL across three dimensions: attitude, ability, and sociality. Participants were asked to rate each item based on their self-perceptions using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). The attitude dimension reflects lifelong exercise habits, motivation, and awareness of the health benefits of PA; the ability dimension captures perceived movement competence and the transferability of movement-related knowledge and skills; and the sociality dimension assesses perceived social interaction and communication through engagement in PA. The SPPLI has demonstrated good internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90 (Liu et al., 2022).

The total SPPLI score was calculated by summing the responses across all 11 items, with higher scores indicating higher levels of perceived PL.

International physical activity questionnaire–short form (IPAQ-SF), Taiwanese Version

The authorized Taiwanese version of the IPAQ-SF (authorized local-language version) consists of seven items that assess the frequency and duration of participants’ engagement in vigorous-intensity activity, moderate-intensity activity, walking (classified as moderate-intensity activity), and sitting behavior during the previous seven days. The questionnaire categorizes PA into moderate-intensity and vigorous-intensity activity. Therefore, the overall PA was determined by summing the number of minutes spent engaging in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. The test–retest reliability was r = 0.78, and the criterion validity ranged from r = 0.31 to 0.41 (Liu, 2004). Sedentary behavior was operationalized as a binary variable using a threshold of ≥ 7 h/day based on epidemiological evidence [1, 10].

The interview consisted of 18 items and was estimated to require approximately 20 min to complete.

Statistical analysis

The interview responses were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and percentages) were computed to summarize demographic characteristics and key variables.

Two binary logistic regression models were constructed to examine the associations between total physical literacy (PL) scores and (1) meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) physical activity (PA) recommendations for older adults, and (2) sedentary behavior (SB). The total PL score was included as the primary independent variable in all analyses.

Covariates included demographic and health-related variables collected via standardized self-report questionnaires, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), educational level, living arrangement, retirement status, and history of chronic disease. Educational level was categorized into three groups (elementary or below, junior/senior high, and college or above), living arrangement was classified as living with family or living alone, and history of chronic disease was assessed as a binary variable (yes/no) based on self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions.

All covariates were simultaneously entered into the adjusted models to account for potential confounding effects. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model diagnostics, including pseudo R2 values, were examined to assess model fit.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The target sample size was determined based on the population statistics reported by the Ministry of the Interior, Taiwan (November 2023), using a stratified proportional sampling approach to reflect regional population distributions. Specifically, the planned sample included 482 participants from northern Taiwan, 253 from central Taiwan, 301 from southern Taiwan, and 32 from eastern and offshore regions, yielding a total target sample of 1,067 participants. This sample size was considered sufficient to achieve a sampling error of less than 3% at a 95% confidence level, which is generally acceptable for population-based surveys.

Discussion

The findings demonstrate that higher overall PL was significantly associated with a greater likelihood of meeting the WHO’s recommended PA levels for older adults. Additionally, these findings are consistent with our hypotheses. Specifically, each one-point increase in PL score was associated with 8% higher odds of achieving sufficient PA, and this association remained robust after adjusting for key demographic and health-related covariates. These results suggest that physical literacy (PL) is consistently and meaningfully associated with adequate physical activity (PA) engagement in later life, independent of age, sex, body composition, residential context, and health status.

This finding aligns with prior research conducted predominantly in children, adolescents, and general adult populations, which consistently reported positive associations between PL and PA participation [5, 6]. The present study extends this evidence to older adults—a population for which empirical physical literacy research remains comparatively limited, with existing evidence described as fragmented and insufficiently synthesized (Galán-Arroyo et al., 2024,Yu et al., 2025)—confirming that PL continues to operate as a relevant behavioral factor in later life.

The observed association between PL and PA may be interpreted in light of the integrative nature of physical literacy, which encompasses movement competence, motivation, confidence, and knowledge. Rather than attributing specific effects to individual components, it is more appropriate to consider PL as a multidimensional construct that may collectively support engagement in physical activity. Older adults with higher PL may therefore be more likely to engage in PA, potentially reflecting a combination of functional capacity, confidence, and motivation in the context of aging-related challenges [5, 28]. At the functional level, enhanced movement competence—such as balance, gait stability, and strength—reduces perceived physical vulnerability and fall-related concerns, which are commonly cited as barriers to PA in later life [14]. Beyond physical capability, motivational and confidence-related components of PL appear particularly salient for sustaining PA among older adults, who are often driven by goals related to functional independence, well-being, and quality of life rather than performance or competition [14].

In addition to its positive association with PA, the present study also identified a significant inverse relationship between PL and prolonged SB among older adults: each one-point increase in PL score was associated with an approximately 4% reduction in the likelihood of engaging in prolonged SB, even after controlling for relevant covariates. By conceptualizing SB as a health-related behavior distinct from PA, this study contributes novel evidence to an area that has been comparatively underexplored within the PL literature, particularly in older populations [3, 14].

The inverse association between PL and SB observed in this research should not be interpreted as a simple by-product of higher PA participation in individuals with higher PL. This is because, in later life, SB often reflects behavioral withdrawal rather than insufficient energy expenditure. Studies indicate that, in older adults, SB is shaped by psychological and health-related factors such as fear of falling, pain, fatigue, and uncertainty regarding physical capability [7],fear of falling is particularly prevalent and has been consistently associated with activity restriction and avoidance, even in the absence of actual falls (Scheffer et al., 2008; Schoene et al., 2019; [18]. In this context, higher PL—which is characterized by greater movement confidence, higher perceived movement competence, and more positive embodied movement-related experiences—may mitigate PA withdrawal in older adults by enabling them to engage in frequent low-intensity posture transitions and interrupt prolonged sitting in daily life.

International guidelines recognize SB as a health risk independent of PA, emphasizing that reducing and breaking up sedentary time can confer health benefits even when PA recommendations are not met [14], Bull et al., 2020). These studies, together with the present findings, suggest that PL attenuates SB primarily through enhanced behavioral self-regulation and reduced reliance on static postures rather than through increased engagement in structured exercise alone. The present results therefore reinforce PL as a key behavioral determinant influencing active and sedentary patterns in later life and underscore the value of PL-oriented approaches for promoting active and healthy ageing.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation, and caution is therefore warranted when inferring directionality among variables [14]. Second, as the sample consisted solely of community-dwelling older adults, the findings may not be generalizable to institutionalized or medically complex older populations [14]. Third, although standardized interviewer-administered telephone interviews were used, data validity may have been influenced by potential auditory, cognitive, or communication barriers, as well as self-selection bias. Additional limitations include potential recall bias and social desirability bias due to self-reported measures, as well as possible exclusion of more vulnerable populations resulting from the use of CATI methodology.

Furthermore, the use of self-reported instruments (SPPLI and IPAQ-SF) introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability bias [14]. Moreover, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as several relevant variables that may have shaped the observed associations—such as functional capacity, burden of chronic disease, socioeconomic disadvantage, and social support—were not captured.

Additionally, environmental characteristics were not directly measured. Although geographic stratification was applied, this proxy may insufficiently reflect neighborhood walkability, access to facilities, or built-environment determinants of PA and SB. Lastly, the relatively long survey length (~ 20 min) may have induced respondent fatigue, potentially affecting data completeness and precision.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PL is a crucial factor of movement behaviors in later life, with higher PL being associated with greater participation in PA and lower sedentary time, even after adjusting for sociodemographic and health-related factors. This suggests that, as a modifiable life-course construct, PL presents a strategic lever for promoting active and healthy aging. Therefore, integrating PL into community health programming, preventive services, and age-friendly environmental initiatives may offer scalable and equitable benefits for older adults. To extend these findings, longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify causal pathways, evaluate population-level impacts, and determine the feasibility, scalability, and sustainability of PL-focused interventions in real-world settings.

From a practical perspective, the present findings highlight the importance of developing targeted strategies to enhance physical literacy (PL) among older adults. Interventions should adopt a holistic approach that addresses not only movement competence but also motivation, confidence, and knowledge, which are central components of PL. In particular, programs designed for older adults should emphasize functional movement skills (e.g., balance, mobility, and strength), as well as build confidence in performing everyday physical activities.

In addition, community-based and accessible programs may play a critical role in promoting PL in later life, especially when they are tailored to individuals’ functional capacities and health conditions. Creating supportive environments that reduce barriers to movement—such as fear of falling, lack of access to facilities, or low perceived competence—may further facilitate engagement in both physical activity and reduced sedentary behavior.

Future research should continue to explore effective PL-based intervention strategies and evaluate their impact on long-term behavioral and health outcomes in older populations.
