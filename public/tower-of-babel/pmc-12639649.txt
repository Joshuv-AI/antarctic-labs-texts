l-Glycine and l-leucine derived multifunctional ionic liquids for potential antibacterial applications: in-vitro and in-silico studies
Authors: Amina Arshad, Hira Akram, Habib Ullah, Saadia Andleeb, Muhammad Shafiq, Zaheer Ul-Haq, Rawaiz Khan, Ali Bahadar, Azhar Mahmood, Mudassir Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIonic liquids (ILs) have gained significant attention among material scientists for their versatile properties. Amino acid-based ionic liquids (AAILs) have the potential to be tailored for desired physicochemical properties due to their structural adaptability and unique attributes. Twenty-four distinct ILs were synthesized, containing multifunctional cations viz. 1-octyl pyridinium, 1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium, 1,3-dioctylimidazolium, Di-octyldi-butylammonium, Tetraoctylphosphonium, N, N, N', N'-Tetrakis-(2-hydroxyethyl)dioctyldiammonium, 1H-1,2,3-triazole, and 1-octyl-1,8-diazabicyclo(5.4.0)undec-7-enium, each with varied chain lengths. Whereas, bromide, glycinate, and leucinate were incorporated as counter-anionic species. These distinct ILs were characterized by FT-IR, 1H, and 13C-NMR spectroscopies and were systematically evaluated for anti-bacterial efficacy by agar well diffusion method against six potent bacterial strains. Both gram-positive as well as gram-negative types, namely Shigella dysenteria, Shigella boyydii, Staphylococcus aureus, Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter baumannii, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumonia were tested. Remarkably strong antibacterial performance was observed across most of the synthesized ILs, particularly notable against gram-negative strains, signifying their potential as antibacterial agents. The good antibacterial performance of ILs was also validated by molecular docking, and a good agreement was found between computational and experimental studies. These findings open new avenues for the development of effective antibacterial agents based on ILs in infection control for individuals with disabilities.Keywords: Multifunctional, Cations, Glycine, Leucine, Ionic liquids, Antibacterial, Rehabilitation, Wound healing

Introduction

The synthesis of novel materials with tailored properties has become a prominent focus across various scientific disciplines. Due to their exceptional chemical versatility, ionic liquids (ILs) have been extensively explored in recent decades, demonstrating significant potential in biomedical applications [1, 2]. ILs are a kind of salt that is liquid at room temperature [3, 4]. However, it is critical to understand that not all potential combinations of cations and anions yield ILs with the appropriate physicochemical characteristics for certain applications [5, 6]. The challenge lies in the complexity of ion types that possess the necessary attributes, such as non-volatility, thermal stability, low melting and high decomposition temperatures, low viscosity, and lack of toxicity [7, 8].

Synthesizing universally ideal ILs that incorporate all these physical properties becomes a challenging task [9]. The promising field of ILs has expanded significantly, with a strong emphasis on the synthesis of distinct amino acid-based derivatives with potential biological applications. Amino acids, known for their dual carboxylic acid and amino groups in a single molecule, provide a unique pathway for versatile IL design [10]. Their special structure enables them to act as either anions or cations, offering adaptability, versatility, and biocompatibility to the resulting ILs [11]. Additionally, the ability of amino acids to introduce various functional groups enhances the potential for tailoring IL properties for biological and biocompatible applications [12].

Furthermore, incorporating amino acids into ILs renders them eco-friendly [13], as the anions are biodegradable [14], biocompatible [15], and possess antimicrobial properties [16, 17]. The study suggests that the antimicrobial activity and cytotoxicity of this ionic liquid (IL) are primarily attributed to the imidazolium cation. Interestingly, the biocompatibility of ILs can be modified by incorporating different anions without compromising their antibacterial efficacy. In the case of [EMIM][HSO4], this IL maintains its ability to kill bacteria while demonstrating enhanced biocompatibility, making it suitable for various biological rehabilitation and recovery applications [18].

AAILs offer a promising solution for microbial control, merging the attributes of amino acids and ILs [19]. With customizable properties, AAILs exhibit diverse antimicrobial mechanisms, from disrupting cell membranes to inhibiting enzymatic activity [20]. Their biocompatibility, reduced toxicity, and eco-friendliness make them versatile candidates for various applications, while challenges in optimization and production need further exploration [21]. By combining ILs with growth factors or stem cells, the regeneration of tissues can be enhanced, which makes them suitable for chronic wound rehabilitation [22, 23] The imidazolium-based ILs, along with the sericin-based hydrogels, have been used for diabetic wound [24] recovery that integrates phenylboronic acid for bacterial immobilization and imidazole functionalized ILs to help in bacterial eradication. This system is mainly used for Staphylococcus aureus. By accelerating healing and minimizing complications, SIP contributes to improved recovery outcomes, making it a valuable tool in the rehabilitation of chronic diabetic wounds [25].

Recent research has led to a deeper understanding of AAILs, illuminating their potential in numerous scientific fields. The new synthetic approach and physicochemical properties were studied for various choline AAILs [26, 27] and ammonium and phosphonium-based AAILs [28], expanding the horizons of potential applications across diverse fields. Moreover, a detailed study revealed that various parameters affect the antimicrobial properties of ILs, which include alkyl chain length and choice of cations/anions [2, 29, 30], especially where the prolonged microbial infections can cause functional disability. It was investigated that the unique properties of AAILs make them promising candidates for various biomedical applications, including regenerative medicine, and glucose biosensing, that is the best performance towards glucose was achieved by [C12mim]Asp/GOx/Nafion/GCE at a working potential of −0.33 V [31]. In a previous study, they revealed that DILs, particularly those with bis-ammonium and bis-phosphonium cations and an alkyl linker, stimulated root growth significantly. DILs with bis-phosphonium cation and alkyl linker outperformed bis-ammonium counterparts, regardless of the anion. However, DILs containing ester bonds in the linkers increased phytotoxicity, inhibiting root growth [32]. They developed new ILs containing bis-ammonium or bis-phosphonium cations and l-histidine or l-prolinate anions. Long alkyl chains and ester bonds have a significant impact on biological activity and provide insight into structure–activity relationships for improved rehabilitation.

In recent years, researchers have fine-tuned the properties of AAILs by selecting amino acids and modifying their chemical structures for enhanced biocompatibility and biodegradability. When A. Valls et al. synthesized imidazole [25] and imidazolium salts (imidazole attached with l-valine and l-phenylalanine derivatives containing hydrophobic groups that is long dodecyl chains and tertiary alkyl groups at one side and benzyl bromide at other side of the imidazole) and evaluated antibacterial activity against B. subtilis than E. coli, these compounds showed strong antibacterial activity and MBC values corresponding to 16 μg/mL or lower. [33]. A. Jordan et al. synthesized ILs pyridinium, pyrolidinium, imidazolium, and ammonium with l-phenylalanine and l-tyrosine, and their antimicrobial toxicity evaluation was conducted, where moderate antimicrobial activity was observed. While the pyridinium IL containing the same amino acid derivatives showed promising results in terms of green chemistry metrics and biodegradation [34]. However, creating cost-effective AAILs with enhanced antibacterial properties remains a formidable task [35].

In the present study, the selection of glycine and leucine as counter-anions is based on their well-documented biological and physicochemical properties. Glycine’s polar nature enhances aqueous solubility and facilitates hydrogen bonding, improving bioavailability and interaction with microbial membranes [36]. Leucine, in contrast, contains a hydrophobic side chain that helps in membrane disruption, a critical mechanism for antibacterial efficacy [37]. Moreover, these anions were selected for their natural origin, proven biocompatibility in antimicrobial IL systems, making them ideal candidates for developing eco-friendly antibacterial agents [38, 39]. In contrast to halide-based ILs, which often exhibit higher toxicity and poor biodegradability, our glycinate- and leucinate-based ILs demonstrated superior antibacterial activity alongside improved biocompatibility and environmental safety. They offer great potential for pharmaceutical and medical applications in wound healing and rehabilitation. This paradigm of bio-based AAILs not only maintains the efficacy of the antibacterial effect but also significantly reduces toxicity and environmental impact, making them a promising choice for future developments in these fields. Guo et al. demonstrated that membranes based on AAILs exhibit strong antibacterial activity alongside enhanced biocompatibility, positioning them as safer alternatives to conventional ILs, particularly halide-based systems. In vitro cytotoxicity assays further confirmed their low toxicity toward mammalian skin fibroblast cells, underscoring their potential for biomedical applications involving direct skin contact, such as wound healing. Similarly, Esposito et al. also demonstrated in their study that AAILs are less hazardous towards the environment and have improved biocompatibility when the anionic parts are amino acid based. While conventional 1-alkyl-3-methylimidazolium ILs have raised concerns regarding their sustainability, biodegradability, and toxicity [40], the present study employs AAILs, which are structurally distinct and intentionally designed to be more biocompatible and environmentally benign. AAILs incorporate naturally occurring amino acid moieties, which contribute to improved biodegradability and reduced cytotoxicity, as demonstrated by in vitro assays showing low toxicity to mammalian skin fibroblasts. Thus, AAILs represent a promising class of sustainable ILs suitable for biomedical applications, including wound healing. ILs have been used in advanced wound dressings [41] for diabetic patients, which reduces the risk of infection and limits prolonged infection-induced disability by promoting a faster rehabilitation process [42].

In this work, we synthesized a series of imidazolium, pyridinium, triazolium, quaternary ammonium, and phosphonium ionic liquids by N-alkylation with an octyl chain followed by anion exchange with l-glycine and l-leucine salts, and to the best of our knowledge these specific nitrogen- and phosphorus-based ILs with amino acid anions have not been reported previously.

Experimental details

Materials

Chemicals used were 1-methyl imidazole (DAE JUNG, > 99%), pyridine (Sigma-Aldrich, ~ 99%), tri-octyl phosphine (MACKLIN, 92.23%), diethyl amine (Sigma-Aldrich ≥ 99.5%), imidazole Sigma Alrich > = 99.0%), N, N, N', N'-Tetrakis-(2-hydroxyethyl)dioctyl diamine (Sigma-Alrich 98%), 1,8- Di-aza-bicyclo [5.4.0] undec-7-ene, 1H-1,2,3-triazole (Sigma-Aldrich ≥ 99.0%, butyl bromide (Sigma-Aldrich ≥ 99.5%), Octyl bromide (MACKLIN, > 95%), sodium hydroxide (Sigma-Aldrich ≥ 98%, pellets; anhydrous), l-glycine (Sigma-Aldrich, reagent, ≥ 98.5%), l-leucine (Sigma-Aldrich reagent grade, ≥ 98%.) All these were purchased from registered lab chemical suppliers and used as received. Ethyl acetate, hexane, acetonitrile, methanol, and chloroform were used as solvents. As per standard methods, all these liquids were dried and kept on molecular sieves for storage. FTIR analysis was conducted using the Brucker ATR-alpha FTIR instrument over the spectral range of 4000–550 cm⁻1 to characterize the functional groups within the synthesized ILs. Additionally, NMR analysis was carried out on the Brucker NMR (500 MHz) instrument employing CDCl₃ as the solvent.

Synthesis of ionic liquids

Synthesis of salts of amino acids

Preparation of sodium glycinate [NH2CH2COONa]

l-Glycine (1 g = 10 mmol) was dissolved in 5 mL of distilled water at room temperature and got immediate dissolution. Equivalent moles (0.4 g = 10 mmol) of sodium hydroxide to the l-glycine were added to this solution. The mixture was then kept in a hot water bath; set at 60 °C temperature and slowly warmed for 60–70 min. The mixture was then vacuum dried in the oven for a few hours to remove moisture from the product and a solid powdery white product was obtained.

Preparation of sodium leucinate [C6H12NO2Na]

1 g (10 mmol) of l-leucine was added in 5 mL of distilled water. The mixture was stirred at 40 °C to get complete dissolution. Equivalent moles (0.4 g = 10 mmol) of sodium hydroxide were incorporated into the already prepared solution of leucine. This combined mixture underwent a mild heating procedure in a hot water bath set at 60 °C for 60 to 80 min. The obtained product was then subjected to vacuum drying for several hours.

Synthesis of bromide-based ILs and their exchange with sodium glycinate and sodium leucinate

1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide [C8mim][Br] (I)

In a flask with 60 mL of acetonitrile, octyl bromide (11.74 g) and methyl-imidazole (5 g) were combined. This reaction produced 1-methyl-3-octyl imidazolium bromide (Fig. 1). The mixture underwent 48 h of refluxing after decanting to ensure completion, which was verified by thin-layer chromatography. Rotary evaporation was used to remove the solvent, and the remaining yellow, oily liquid was vacuum dried for 12 h at 50 °C to provide 95% yield. Leucinate and glycinate exchanged products were synthesized by adding sodium leucinate and glycinate in 40 mL acetonitrile, stirred overnight at 60–70 °C temperature. Once the exchange reaction was completed, NaBr was precipitated by a solvent extraction technique and subsequently subjected to filtration.

1,3-dioctylimidazolium bromide [C88im][Br] (IV)

In acetonitrile (30 mL), imidazole (5 g), octyl bromide (10.21 g), and sodium carbonate (3.9 g) were added to produce 1-octyl imidazolium bromide by stirring it overnight at room temperature. After confirmation of the first product being synthesized, the reaction mixture was purified by washing it with ethyl acetate. To synthesize 1,3-dioctyl imidazolium bromide, another 10.21 g of octyl bromide was added to the acetonitrile solution and 1-octyl imidazolium bromide solution. TLC was used to confirm that the reaction mixture had been completed after 48 h of reflux. After the solvent was removed by rotary evaporation, a yellow, oily liquid with a 95% yield was produced. The product was then dried in a vacuum oven for 12 h at 50 °C. Leucinate and glycinate exchanged products were synthesized by adding sodium leucinate and glycinate in 40 mL acetonitrile, stirred overnight at 60–70 °C temperature. Once the exchange reaction was completed, NaBr was precipitated by a solvent extraction technique and subsequently subjected to filtration.

Di-octyl di-butylammonium bromide [N4488] [Br] (VII)

After the confirmation of the product of mono addition of octyl bromide, the product was purified using ethyl acetate. Now the second equivalent of octyl bromide was added. The mixture underwent 48 h of refluxing after decanting to ensure completion, which was verified by thin-layer chromatography. Rotary evaporation was used to remove the solvent, and the remaining yellow, oily liquid was vacuum dried for 12 h at 50 °C which furnished 95% yield. Leucinate and glycinate exchanged products were synthesized by adding sodium leucinate and glycinate in 40 mL acetonitrile, stirred overnight at 60–70 °C temperature. Once the exchange reaction was completed, NaBr was precipitated by a solvent extraction technique and subsequently subjected to filtration.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of 24 samples was tested against six bacterial pathogens Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Bacillus subtilis (Local Isolate), Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC 700603), Shigella dysenteriae (Clinical Isolate), Shigella boydii (Clinical Isolate) primarily listed among the top ten pathogens prioritized by the Centre for Disease Control (CDC), US. A fresh 24-h bacterial culture of planktonic free cells (O.D 0.5, 600 λ) was employed to create the lawn on nutrient agar in Petri dishes in triplicate [32]. Each equalized bacterial sample was spread across around 50 μL of space on each Petri plate [44]. Three-millimeter wells were drilled at equal intervals using a sterilized borer. Equal volumes of both positive and negative controls were utilized on each plate. The negative control was normal saline water while the positive control was an antibiotic named Ceftriaxone. Each sample solution was poured into a well in a quantity of around 25 μL, and it was then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Most ionic solutions demonstrated antibacterial activity against the targeted microorganisms.

Computational studies

Based on cationic moieties, the entire range of ILs was classified into eight distinct groups. The ILs with promising antibacterial activities from each group namely; (I), (V), (VII), (X), (XIII), (XVII), (XIX), and (XXII) were selected for further exploration of binding mode to unravel their inhibitory action [45]. The β-lactamase enzyme was selected as the target protein, which is one of the most clinically relevant resistance-related enzymes in bacteria. This protein target was selected for the evaluation of the antibiotic activity of the ILs to check whether our synthesized ILs could interact with or potentially inhibit such resistance-related targets. In this connection, the crystal structures for the β-lactamase from S. aureus (6WGR), K. pneumonia (6ZRJ), and E. coli (5A92) were retrieved from the RCSB protein data bank. The initial processing steps included the deletion of extra protein chains, water molecules, and unwanted co-crystallized ligands. The clean structures were subjected to structure correction, protonation, and the application of partial charges. All the operations for molecular docking were performed in the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE 2019.01). The process started with an initial search of conformations followed by the generation of docking poses through the Triangle Matcher algorithm. A total of 15 docking poses were generated for each IL against β-lactamase proteins, which were then scored by London dG. Subsequently, the five best-scored poses were retained using the GBVI/WSA dG scoring function in the refinement step. UCSF Chimaera 1.15 was used to create images as the arrangement of molecules with the highest binding affinity was investigated for the intermolecular interaction pattern.

Results and discussions

Using anions (bromide, glycinate, and leucinate), ILs based on pyridinium, Imidazolium, phosphonium, quaternary ammonium, and triazolium were synthesized. TLC, FTIR and NMR were used for characterization. These synthetic ILs were assayed against six distinct pathogenic bacterial strains.

Synthesis of IL

Imidazolium-based ILs

1-Methyl-3-octyl Imidazolium IL

In a 48-h reflux reaction, methyl imidazole and octyl bromide in the solvent acetonitrile produced product (I). The successful completion of synthesis (I) was shown by the observation that the aromatic proton (NCHN) peak changed from 7.91 ppm to 10.31 ppm in the final product. The fact that the -CH2 peak in product (I) changed from 3.91 ppm in octyl bromide to 4.42 ppm was evidence that (I) had undergone quaternization. Octyl bromide and methyl imidazole have been quaternarized, according to the FTIR of (I). Different bands were observed containing stretching vibrations at 2924 cm−1 and 2854 cm−1, which correspond to aliphatic sp3 CH2 and CH3 groups, in the FTIR analysis of 1-Methyl-3-Octylimidazolium Bromide. At 3120 cm−1, aromatic CH stretching is visible in the spectrum, while at 1650 cm−1, 1569 cm−1, and 1463 cm−1, imidazolium ring vibrations are present. Imidazolium ring stretching is also indicated by a distinct signal at 1165 cm−1. For 1-Methyl-3-octylimidazolium glycinate (II) and leucinate (III), N–H stretches at 3300 to 3400 cm−1, C-O stretches at 1025 cm−1, and carbonyl stretches at 1600 cm−1 showed bromide exchange with glycinate and leucinate [17, 18]. NMR analysis confirmed the successful formation of glycinate and leucinate-based ILs, denoted as (II) and (III). This confirmation was evident through the observation of distinct methylene peaks at 2.34 ppm in (II), as well as three characteristic iso-butyl peaks in (III) at 0.91, 1.72, and 3.71 ppm.

The spectra also indicated aromatic CH stretching and prominent imidazolium ring vibrations. Antibacterial activity

The alarming threats of antimicrobial resistance developed to almost all the classes and generations of antibiotics, prompting researchers to explore the antibacterial properties of ILs as an alternate option to the current antibiotic-based therapeutics, the accelerated growth cycle of bacteria, being another option to test these ILs [49, 50]. The toxicity of ILs, particularly those based on pyridinium, phosphonium, and imidazolium, was found to escalate with the elongation of alkyl chains in their quaternary ammonium salts and due to the presence of distinct structures of amino acids [51] that also inhibit bacterial growth by disrupting bacterial cell membranes, inhibit enzymes and altering pH [52]. The nature of their cationic groups significantly influences the bactericidal efficacy of ILs. These cations Tetraoctylphosphonium, 1-octyl pyridinium, 1,3-dioctyl imidazolium, Di-octyl di-butyl ammonium, 1-octyl-1,8-Diazabicyclo(5.4.0)undec-7-enium), N, N, N', N'-Tetrakis-(2-hydroxyethyl) dioctyl diammonium, 1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium, 1H-1,2,3-triazole and form strong electrostatic bonds with the membranes of bacterial cells, facilitating their incorporation into the lipid layers. The disturbance causes an increase in permeability and fluidity when there is interaction with cell wall. Such alterations result in the leakage of critical intracellular substances and a decrease in membrane potential, disrupting the cell's equilibrium and leading to lysis and death. Moreover, the cations directly impact bacterial DNA by slotting themselves between base pairs, hindering essential biological functions like replication and transcription. The disturbances cause an increase in permeability and fluidity when there is interaction with the cell wall [18, 53].

The potential of ILs against different classes of Bacteria is also influenced by the length of their alkyl chains, which directly correlates with the hydrophobic nature of the compound and its ability to disrupt bacterial cell membranes. As the alkyl chain lengthens, ILs exhibit stronger hydrophobic interactions with the lipid bilayer, integrating more deeply into the membrane's hydrophobic core [45]. This deeper integration leads to greater disruption of lipid packing and increased membrane permeability, resulting in extensive leakage of intracellular contents, further loss of membrane potential, and ultimately, more effective bacterial lysis. Studies have revealed that ILs with longer alkyl chains are more potent than those having a shorter chain in antimicrobial activity, and it has been reported consistently [17, 54].

The study of various cations for their antibacterial activity in ILs has revealed a distinct hierarchy in terms of antibacterial effectiveness in infection control for Individuals with disabilities. ILs formulated with imidazolium-based cations were found to be the most effective, followed by those containing pyridinium and quaternary ammonium cations, which exhibited comparable effectiveness. Subsequently, ILs featuring triazolium-based cations showed lesser efficacy, and those with phosphonium-based cations were the least effective [43, 55]. This hierarchy underscores the pivotal influence of cationic structural attributes on the antibacterial activity of ILs, providing valuable insights for the design of more potent antimicrobial agents by elucidating the relevance of the cationic mode of action in determining antibacterial efficiency [56].

The choice of the counter anion in an ionic liquid is also an important parameter for best antibacterial efficiency and reduced toxicity. AAILs belong to a fairly new class showing enhanced biocompatibility and antibacterial activity; thus, they may find applications in drug formulation and biomedical uses. In comparison to conventional ILs based on chlorine or bromine, the AAILs from natural amino acids provide an alternative that is safe and effective [57].

Therefore, in this study, 24 different ILs were synthesized to evaluate their potential antibacterial toxicity evaluation against gram-positive and gram-negative strains, including Shigella dysenteria, Shigella boydii, Staphylococcus aureus, Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter baumannii, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. The diameter of the inhibition zone on agar plates, which denotes the area where bacterial growth was inhibited, was measured to determine the antibacterial effectiveness of these synthesized ILs [32]. Normal saline water was used as a negative control. The maximum antibacterial efficacy against all bacterial strains was seen among the ILs made from octyl-imidazolium, with certain ILs even outperforming even the positive control. Shigella dysenteria was detected in the ILs (X), (I), (II), (XXII), and (V), respectively, with maximum zones of inhibition of 36, 34, 32, 32, and 30 mm. For Staphylococcus aureus, the maximum zone of inhibition was measured in the compounds (I), (V), and (XXII) which were 30, 34, and 32 mm. The zone of inhibition in the case of bacterial strain Carbapenum-resistant Acinobacter baumannii is the zone of inhibition of ILs (I), (X), and (XXIV) maximum which is 35, 30, and 34 mm. For Escherichia coli, the maximum zone of inhibition was measured in the compounds (II), (V), and (X) which were 35, 32, and 31 mm. In the case of Shigella boydii, the maximum zone of inhibition was measured in the case of (I), (II), and (X), which are 35, 31, and 32 mm. The zone of inhibition is 30 mm for ILs (IV), (V), and (VIII) in the case of Klebsiella pneumonia as shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. It has been observed that Long-chain alkyl groups and bromide anions are effective antibacterial agents [58] due to their disruptive interactions with bacterial cell membranes that interfere with the essential cellular processes of bacteria [14]. Long alkyl chains can destabilize membranes due to their hydrophobic nature and disrupt the arrangement of lipids and proteins in the bacterial membrane, leading to its destabilization, while bromide anions hinder essential microbial functions, collectively impeding bacterial growth and viability [59]. The unique combination of distinct cations with long alkyl chains and anions shows remarkable potency of toxicity against bacterial activity. The toxicity of ILs depends on the length of an alkyl chain, the nature of cations and the nature of anions as well. According to studies reported, cations have a major effect due to their lipophilicity making them toxic against bacterial activity whereas long alkyl chains attachment to it increases this lipophilic and hydrophobic effect and potentially works to disrupt the cell membrane of bacteria leading to the death of cells. Secondly, the nature of anion also plays an important role in interaction with the cell membrane of bacteria which helps in penetration of it in the cell and killing the bacterial cell. So, amino-acid-based ILs are used in which unique combinations of anions and cations are synthesized to study the comparative effect of ILs on bacterial activity [60].

Conclusion

In summary, this study has investigated 24 different ILs containing various cations (1-octyl pyridinium, 1-octyl-3-methylimidazolium, 1,3-dioctyl imidazolium, Di-octyl di-butyl ammonium, Tetraoctylphosphonium, N, N, N', N'-Tetrakis-(2-hydroxyethyl)dioctyl diammonium, 1H-1,2,3-triazole and 1-octyl-1,8-Diazabicyclo(5.4.0)undec-7-enium) and anions (bromide, glycinate, and leucinate) to explore their antibacterial properties in wound healing. These ILs were analyzed using advanced techniques like FTIR and NMR, and their antibacterial activity was tested against several bacterial strains, including Shigella dysenteria, Shigella boyydii, Staphylococcus aureus, Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter baumannii, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumonia. Our findings revealed that imidazolium-based ILs exhibited robust inhibitory effects, surpassing the control antibiotic, CTX. Specifically, ILs (I), (V), (X), and (XXII) displayed superior efficacy against the tested bacterial strains, while IL (XIII) showed no inhibitory activity. Interestingly, phosphonium-based ILs exhibited distinct behavior against all bacterial strains. When it comes to effectiveness, the hierarchy observed was as follows: ILs (Imidazolium) > ILs (Pyridinium) ~ ILs (Quaternary ammonium) > ILs (Triazolium) > ILs (Phosphonium). Molecular docking studies of the ILs having the highest antibacterial activity were performed which provided further support for our experimental results, emphasizing the potential of these ILs as antibacterial agents. In conclusion, imidazolium-based ILs, particularly those with extended alkyl chains and bromide anions, have demonstrated potent antimicrobial properties, highlighting their promising role in combating bacterial pathogens of high priority as declared by the Centre for Disease Control (CDC). These findings open new avenues for the development of effective antibacterial agents based on ILs in infection control for individuals with disabilities.
