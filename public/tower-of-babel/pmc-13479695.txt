Targeting cancer stem cells enhances multikinase inhibitor therapy in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease-related hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yasi Pan, Xiang Zhang, Chi Chun Wong, Huarong Chen, Stephanie Ma, Terence Kin-Wah Lee, Kai Yuan, Cong Liang, Xingyu Zhou, Harry Cheuk Hay Lau, Pingmei Huang, Danyu Chen, Lina Wang, Yanqiang Ding, Qinyao Wei, Alvin Ho Kwan Cheung, Ka Fai To, Jun Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease-related hepatocellular carcinoma (MASLD-HCC) is an emerging malignancy with limited therapeutic options. The identity and function of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in MASLD-HCC remain poorly understood. In this study, we characterised CSCs in MASLD-HCC and investigated their contribution to MASLD-HCC tumourigenesis and therapy response.DesignWe performed expression profiling in human MASLD-HCC samples (n=29 pairs of tumour and adjacent normal tissues). Advanced in vivo genetic lineage tracing coupled with single-cell RNA sequencing was used to characterise CD133+ CSCs in preclinical models. To establish causality, we developed a hepatocyte-specific CD133-overexpressing mouse model of MASLD-HCC. We identified CD133 protein interactors by mass spectrometry. A novel strategy combining CD133-targeted small interfering RNA (siRNA) nanoparticles with first-line therapy was assessed in clinically relevant MASLD-HCC models.ResultsCD133+ CSCs were significantly enriched in human MASLD-HCC tumours and positively correlated with established markers of malignancy. In vivo genetic lineage tracing in mice revealed that CD133+ cells exhibit hallmark CSC properties, including self-renewal, tumour-initiating capacity and multipotent differentiation, as compared with CD133− counterparts. Hepatocyte-specific CD133 overexpression in mice accelerated MASLD-HCC tumourigenesis. Mechanistically, CD133 interacts with myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9) to stabilise active β-catenin, thereby propagating Wnt/β-catenin signalling that drives CSC phenotypes and tumourigenic potential. Therapeutically, genetic ablation of CD133+ cells or systemic delivery of CD133-siRNA nanoparticles potently sensitised MASLD-HCC to sorafenib and lenvatinib, significantly improving outcomes in MASLD-HCC.ConclusionThis study established CD133+ CSCs as critical mediators through the CD133-MYH9/β-catenin axis in MASLD-HCC. Targeting CD133 enhances multikinase inhibitor efficacy, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for MASLD-HCC.Keywords: STEM CELLS, NONALCOHOLIC STEATOHEPATITIS, HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA, GENE TARGETING

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) includes a range of liver disorders, characterised by hepatic steatosis and accompanied by overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes or metabolic dysregulation. MASLD affects over a quarter of the world’s adult population, and MASLD is fast becoming an emergent risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1–7 A majority of patients with MASLD-HCC present with comorbidities due to metabolic syndrome and are frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage.5 As a consequence, the response to systemic therapies in these patients is generally unsatisfactory. Hence, understanding the underlying mechanism and identifying therapeutic targets for MASLD-HCC is an unmet clinical need.

Tumours are sustained by cancer stem cells (CSCs) that possess characteristics of progenitor cells, including self-renewal and ability to differentiate into multiple cell types.8 CSCs are clinically important as they drive tumour heterogeneity, metastasis and therapy resistance in multiple cancers. CD133 is considered as a CSC marker for several cancer types,9–12 and CD133+ cells are associated with tumourigenic potential.13–16 However, the presence and functional role of CSCs, particularly CD133+ subsets, remains poorly defined in MASLD-driven HCC. Given the distinct aetiology of MASLD-HCC, rooted in the accompanying metabolic dysregulation, chronic inflammation and a tumour microenvironment (TME) that diverges markedly from that of virus-associated HCC, significant gaps remain in our understanding of the existence of CSCs in MASLD-HCC. Moreover, MASLD demonstrated markedly differential response to therapies. For instance, immune checkpoint blockade therapies have shown promise for viral-HCC, but they paradoxically shorten survival in patients with MASLD-HCC,17–19 whereas obesity impairs the efficacy of bevacizumab, an angiogenesis inhibitor.20 In this connection, whether CSCs in MASLD-HCC impact therapy response to first-line advanced HCC therapy is largely unknown.

In this study, we investigated the role of CD133+ HCC cells in the initiation and progression of MASLD-HCC using CD133 lineage-tracing mice and established CD133+ HCC cells as potential tumour-initiating cells harbouring CSC features. Further characterisation of CD133+ and CD133− tumour organoids and cell lines derived from MASLD-HCC mice demonstrated that the CD133+ cell subset exhibited increased oncogenic potential both in vitro and in vivo, as compared with CD133− counterparts. Mechanistically, we showed that CD133 directly conferred CSC characteristics by interacting with myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9) to activate Wnt/β-catenin signalling, which was confirmed by the exacerbated tumourigenesis of MASLD-HCC in liver-specific CD133 overexpression mice. Importantly, targeting CD133+ cells in vivo overcame therapy resistance to multikinase inhibitors in MASLD-HCC, validating CD133 as a therapeutic target to improve treatment outcomes in MASLD-HCC.

Methods

Clinical samples

Human MASLD-HCC tumour tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected in Prince of Wales Hospital, the Chinese University of Hong Kong, from biopsy-proven MASLD-HCC (n=17). In the independent validation cohort, human MASLD-HCC samples (12 paired) were collected in Queen Mary Hospital of the University of Hong Kong. Population characteristics of the cohort are shown in online supplemental table S1.

Statistical analysis

All results were presented as mean±SD unless otherwise specified. Statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism V.8.3 software. Mann-Whitney U test or unpaired Student’s t-test was performed to compare the variables of the two sample groups where appropriate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the differences among multiple groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation between two genes’ expression. The tumour incidence was analysed using Fisher’s exact test. Overall survival was assessed by the Kaplan-Meier survival curve and the log-rank test. The difference in cell viability was determined by repeated-measures ANOVA. A probability value of 0.05 or less was considered significant.

Additional methods are provided in supplementary information. Primers used for validation are listed in online supplemental table S2. The antibodies used in this study were listed in online supplemental table S3.

Results

CD133 is overexpressed in MASLD-HCC in humans and mice

To determine whether CSCs exist in MASLD-HCC, we collected samples from patients with MASLD-HCC and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (figure 1A). Stemness markers (CD133, LGR5, CD44, EPCAM, SOX2, NANOG and CD24) correlated positively with MASLD-associated genes (AKR1B10, ADAMTSL2, CFHR4, TREM2)21 and malignant markers (VIM, SOX9, GPC3) (figure 1B), suggesting an association between CSCs and MASLD-HCC. Notably, stemness signatures were enriched in CD133high MASLD-HCC compared with CD133low counterparts (figure 1C), which indicates CD133 as a marker for CSCs in MASLD-HCC. Independent validation cohort (n=12) demonstrated CD133 as the dominant CSC marker in MASLD-HCC, with overexpression in tumour tissue (figure 1D–G). We further analysed CD133 expression in spontaneous MASLD-HCC tumourigenesis mouse models induced by high-fat/high-cholesterol diet (HFHC) diet (figure 1H–M) or diethylnitrosamine (DEN) plus choline-deficient high-fat (CDHF) diet model (figure 1N–P). In both models, CD133 expression is significantly upregulated in MASLD-HCC tumours as compared with adjacent normal tissues (figure 1L–P). Moreover, CD133 messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein expression were progressively increased during HFHC diet-induced MASLD to HCC progression (figure 1I,J). Taken together, CD133 may serve as a marker for CSCs in MASLD-HCC and may be functionally involved in tumour initiation and progression.

Lineage tracing of CD133+ cells indicates that CD133+ cells drive MASLD-HCC progression

To trace the fate of CD133+ cells in the context of MASLD-HCC, we established a transgenic mouse model for lineage tracing of CD133+ cells by crossing Cd133-CreERT2 mice to Rosa26-lsl-tdTomato mice to give Cd133-CreERT2Rosa26-lsl-tdTomato mice (figure 2A). Mice were then subjected to normal chow (NC) or HFHC diet plus high-fructose22 23 in drinking water to promote MASLD-HCC (figure 2B). HFHC plus fructose fed-mice showed increased body weight as compared with mice fed NC (figure 2C). Serum α-fetoprotein (AFP, a marker for HCC) was elevated after 13 months of diet treatment, suggesting tumour initiation in HFHC plus fructose group (figure 2C). We therefore injected a single dose of tamoxifen (TAM) (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) to initiate the lineage tracing of CD133+ cells (figure 2D). Subsequently, mice were harvested at 3-day, 10-day and 30-day post-TAM injection (figure 2D). Compared with vehicle-treated Cd133-CreERT2Rosa26-lsl-tdTomato mice, significant tdTomato+ve cells were detected in the tumours of TAM-treated counterparts across different time points (online supplemental figure S1A). Histological evaluation confirmed the MASLD-HCC formation in the HFHC plus fructose group (figure 2E), with an enhanced tumour burden at Day 30 compared with Day 3, whereas no tumours were observed in the NC group (figure 2F). Consistent with this, Ki-67 staining demonstrated increased cell proliferation in the HFHC plus fructose group as compared with NC group (figure 2G). We deciphered patterns of CD133+-derived lineage (tdTomato+ cells) across 3-day, 10-day and 30-day time points post-TAM injection. Proportions of tdTomato+ cells were low (<2%) in normal liver tissues from NC-fed mice and largely unchanged across all the time points. On the contrary, tdTomato+ cells showed an elevated abundance in MASLD-HCC tumour tissues with significant enrichment at 30-day in HFHC plus fructose fed mice (figure 2H). Concordantly, immunofluorescence of MASLD-HCC tissues revealed the progressive expansion of tdTomato+ cells from Day 3 to Day 30, supporting the clonal expansion capacity of CD133+ cells (figure 2I). Collectively, lineage tracing of CD133+ cells revealed their clonal expansion during MASLD-HCC progression, suggesting that the expression of CD133 could mark cells with CSC characteristics in MASLD-HCC.

CD133+ cells in MASLD-HCC give rise to progeny with EMT and proliferative phenotypes

Given that CD133+ cells undergo amplification and clonal expansion during MASLD-HCC progression, we questioned whether the growth of primary MASLD-HCC is hierarchically organised and propagated by CD133+ cells. To this end, we developed an inducible genetic lineage-tracing approach based on the Rosa26-lsl-egfp allele, which enables the expression of green colour, and the TAM-inducible CreERT2 allele under the control of Cd133 promoter (figure 3A). Rosa26-egfplslCd133-CreERT2 mice were injected with DEN and administered CDHF diet. At 22 weeks post-DEN injection, serum AFP levels were elevated in CDHF-treated mice compared with NC group (figure 3B), suggesting the development of MASLD-HCC. Mice were injected with a single dose of TAM, and tumours were collected after 3 and 30 days, respectively (figure 3C). Histological evaluation confirmed the initiation (Day 3) and progression (Day 30) of MASLD-HCC in these mice (figure 3D). We harvested MASLD-HCC tumours, isolated all viable eGFP+ resident cells by flow cytometry and performed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) to trace the fate of CD133+ HCC cells (figure 3E).

We identified six major eGFP+ cell clusters according to the expression of known lineage markers, including stem cell-like dormant cells (c0: Prom1, Epcam, Cdkn1a+), stem cell-like active cells (c1: Prom1, Epcam, Cdkn1a-), epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-like cells (c2: Vim, Col17a1), proliferative cells (c3: Ki-67), metabolism-associated cells (c4: Fabp1, Apoc2, Hmgcl) and differentiated cells (c5: Hnf4a, Foxa3, Krt20) (figure 3F,G). The cell clusters on Day 3 (c0, c1) predominantly expressed stemness markers, consistent with their identity as CSCs. Meanwhile, the major cell clusters (c2 to c5) on Day 30 exhibited considerable heterogeneity, including signatures of EMT, cell proliferation, metabolism and differentiation (figure 3H). A smaller proportion of cells with stemness (c0, c1) was retained on Day 30, indicating that CD133+ cells could self-renew as well as differentiate into various cell types that comprise the tumour at large. The relative abundance of six major cell populations was summarised in figure 3I. In line with these observations, Cd133 was highly expressed at tumour initiation stage (Day 3), while the expression of Krt20, a marker of differentiated malignant cells, was increased during tumour progression (Day 30) (figure 3J). Pseudotime analysis further traced the cell fate of CD133 cells (figure 3K). Collectively, CD133+ cells are the key cells in propagating MASLD-HCC tumours via self-renewal and differentiation into multiple cell subtypes.

CD133+ cells from MASLD-HCC tumours exhibit CSC-like phenotypes

To prove that CD133+ cells from MASLD-HCC are indeed CSCs, we harvested MASLD-HCC tissues from DEN-injected, CDHF diet-fed mice at 24 weeks of age and performed flow cytometry to sort out CD133+ and CD133− tumour cell populations (figure 4A). CD133+ and CD133− cells were respectively cultured in Matrigel to establish MASLD-HCC tumour organoids (figure 4A). CD133+ cells showed markedly higher ability to form tumour organoids in 3D Matrigel (19.7%) as compared with CD133− cells (0.0066%) (figure 4B), an observation confirmed by in vitro limiting dilution assay (figure 4C). A hallmark of CSCs is therapeutic resistance. To test this notion, we treated CD133+ and CD133− MASLD-HCC organoids with a panel of chemotherapeutic agents and molecular targeted therapies. We found that CD133+ tumour organoids were significantly resistant to sorafenib and lenvatinib, two first-line multikinase inhibitors for advanced HCC, as well as to 5-FU and cisplatin (figure 4D and online supplemental figure S2A). We performed in vivo limiting dilution assay by orthotopically injecting CD133+ and CD133− MASLD-HCC cells in NSG mice (figure 4E). Consistently, CD133+ cells demonstrated much superior tumour-initiating potential as compared with their CD133− counterparts (figure 4E). Accordingly, mice injected with CD133+ MASLD-HCC cells suffered from worse survival (figure 4F). Moreover, ectopic expression of CD133 in human MASLD-HCC cells (HKCI2 and HKCI10) conferred increased tumourigenic potential as determined by in vitro limiting dilution assay (online supplemental figure S2B and C). Collectively, CD133+ cells from MASLD-HCC exhibited characteristics of CSCs, including self-renewal, chemotherapy resistance and high tumourigenic potential.

CD133+ cells from MASLD-HCC exhibit the hyperactivation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling

CD133+ cells-derived tumour organoids have increased expression of CSC markers, such as CD133, CD44, EPCAM, NANOG, OCT4 and SOX2, reflective of increased stemness traits (figure 5A, online supplemental table S2). To identify the molecular mechanism underlying increased stemness in CD133+ cells, we performed bulk RNA-seq of CD133+ and CD133− MASLD-HCC organoids. RNA-seq analysis demonstrated Wnt/β-catenin signalling as the top pathway enriched in CD133+ MASLD-HCC organoids compared with CD133− counterparts (figure 5B,C). Concordantly, mRNA expression of multiple β-catenin targets was markedly up-regulated in CD133+ MASLD-HCC organoids (online supplemental figure S2D). Confirming this, CD133+ MASLD-HCC organoids had elevated protein expression of active β-catenin and phospho-GSK-3β (inactive), together with downregulation of total GSK-3β by western blot (figure 5D). Hence, CD133+ MASLD-HCC organoids have hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin signalling, a critical signalling nodule for hepatocarcinogenesis.

CD133 exerts protumourigenic effect in MASLD-HCC via Wnt/β-catenin activation

We studied the role of CD133 in the tumourigenicity of MASLD-HCC. We overexpressed CD133 in HKCI2 and HKCI10 MASLD-HCC cells with low endogenous CD133. Both HKCI2 and HKCI10 cells overexpressing CD133 showed increased cell growth and colony formation ability (online supplemental figure S2E and F), accompanied by increased active β-catenin expression (online supplemental figure S2G). A selective Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor, MSAB, abolished the effect of CD133 overexpression on cell growth, implying that the protumourigenic effect of CD133 depends on Wnt/β-catenin signalling (online supplemental figure S2H). Knockout of CD133 in CD133+ MASLD-HCC organoids led to a reduction in the expression of active β-catenin by immunofluorescence (figure 5E). We used AAV-TBG-Cd133 to selectively overexpress CD133 in the mouse liver to evaluate if CD133 promotes self-renewal and tumourigenesis in MASLD-HCC in vivo. Mice were treated with DEN plus CDHF to induce MASLD-HCC (figure 5F). At 8 weeks of age, they received a single dose of either pAAV-TBG-Cd133 or control pAAV-TBG-null (3×1011 v.g./mice) via tail vein. On sacrifice, we observed a significant increase in both the tumour number (p=0.0065) and tumour size (p=0.0008) in hepatocyte-specific CD133 overexpressing mice as compared with control mice (figure 5G). Histological assessment (H&E) confirmed enhanced MASLD-HCC formation in the CD133 overexpression group compared with the control group (figure 5G). Hepatocyte-specific CD133 overexpression increased cell proliferation by Ki-67 staining (figure 5G). Tumour organoids derived from primary tumours of hepatocyte-specific CD133 overexpressing mice exhibited rapid growth in vitro compared with their control counterparts (figure 5H). Consistent with increased tumourigenic potential, Wnt/β-catenin signalling was upregulated in tumours from hepatocyte-specific CD133 overexpressing mice as compared with those from control mice (figure 5I,J). Moreover, we observed a positive correlation between CD133 and active β-catenin protein expression in paired human MASLD-HCC tissues (figure 5K). Hence, our results indicated that CD133 functions as an oncogenic driver in CSC-associated tumourigenesis in MASLD-HCC by activating Wnt/β-catenin signalling.

CD133 interacts with MYH9 to boost Wnt/β-catenin signalling and CSC phenotypes

To explore the molecular mechanism(s) by which CD133 promotes CSC characteristics in MASLD-HCC, we performed pulldown assays to screen for CD133-interacting proteins in HKCI2 and HKCI10 cell lysates (figure 6A,B). Mass spectrometry revealed MYH9 as a top protein partner of CD133 (figure 6C, online supplemental figure S3A and B). We confirmed the interaction between CD133 and MYH9 in CD133+ MASLD-HCC mouse organoid by anti-CD133 pulldown and mass spectrometry (figure 6D). We then validated CD133-MYH9 interplay by co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay using anti-MYH9 antibody in MASLD-HCC cells (figure 6E). Immunofluorescence costaining of CD133 and MYH9 indicated the colocalisation in MASLD-HCC organoids (figure 6F). Furthermore, the direct interaction between CD133 and MYH9 was confirmed by microscale thermophoresis assay, with a binding affinity (Kd) of 0.45 µM (figure 6G). Cross-linking mass spectrometry in conjunction with molecular docking further elucidated the amino acid residues involved in CD133-MYH9 interaction (figure 6H,I). Computational modelling and co-IP assays confirmed a direct interaction between CD133 and MYH9, mapping the binding domain to Gln286, Ser289, Ser290, Glu689, Gln690 and Asn646 of CD133 (online supplemental figure S3C). Mutation of these residues (Flag-CD133 Mut) abolished the interaction between CD133 and MYH9 (online supplemental figure S3D). Therefore, CD133 physically interacts with MYH9 in MASLD-HCC cells and tumour organoids.

MYH9 has been reported to promote Wnt/β-catenin signalling24 and therapy resistance.25 We tested whether the effect of CD133 on Wnt/β-catenin signalling cascade depends on MYH9. MYH9 knockdown using small interfering RNA (siRNA) in HKCI2 and HKCI10 cells abrogated hyperactivation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling evidenced by decreased protein expression of phospho-GSK-3β (inactive) and active β-catenin (figure 6J). CD133 overexpression in HKCI2 and HKCI10 cells induced TOPflash activity; such an effect was abolished by knockdown of MYH9 (figure 6K). Consistently, MYH9 knockdown eliminated nuclear expression of active β-catenin in CD133-expressing HKCI2 and HKCI10 cells, while having no effect on control cells (figure 6L). MYH9 has been shown to boost Wnt/β-catenin signalling by binding to and inactivating GSK-3β.26 In line with this, CD133 overexpression in HKCI2 cells induced a strong interaction between MYH9 and phospho-GSK-3β (figure 6M), thereby potentially relieving GSK-3β-mediated blockade of β-catenin activation. As a consequence, MYH9 knockdown largely attenuated CD133 overexpression-induced tumour sphere formation in HKCI2 and HKCI10 cells as determined by limiting dilution assays (figure 6N). Together, our findings underscore the significance of CD133-MYH9 interaction in boosting Wnt/β-catenin signalling activation and CSC characteristics in MASLD-HCC (figure 6O).

Targeted depletion of CD133+ cells sensitises MASLD-HCC to multikinase inhibitors

Ectopic expression of CD133 in HKCI2 and HKCI10 cells alleviated sorafenib-induced apoptosis (online supplemental figure S4A and B), suggesting that CD133+ MASLD-HCC cells were potential culprits for sorafenib resistance. To evaluate selective depletion of CD133+ cells in the sensitisation to multikinase inhibitors in MASLD-HCC tumourigenesis in mice, we generated CD133-CreERT2Rosa26-lsl-iDTR-EGFP mice, which express diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR) in CD133+ cells on TAM injection (figure 7A). These mice were given DEN plus HFHC diet to induce MASLD-HCC development. When MASLD-HCC was established (based on AFP value), these mice were given TAM to activate iDTR expression in CD133+ cells, followed by treatment with diphtheria toxin (DT) to ablate CD133+ cells (figure 7B, online supplemental figure S4C). CD133-CreERT2 Rosa26-lsl-iDTR-EGFP mice with or without DT treatment were then randomised to receive sorafenib or vehicle (figure 7B). We found that sorafenib alone failed to significantly suppress MASLD-HCC growth, whereas CD133+ cell depletion had a moderate inhibitory effect (figure 7C). Importantly, the combination of CD133+ cell ablation plus sorafenib synergistically inhibited MASLD-HCC (figure 7C) and cell proliferation in tumours (figure 7D). Ablation of CD133+ cells was confirmed by immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses (figure 7E and online supplemental figure S4D). CD133+ cell ablation also synergised with lenvatinib, a second-generation multikinase inhibitor, to strongly reduce the tumour burden of MASLD-HCC in DEN plus CDHF diet model (figure 7F), as evidenced by MRI, histological analysis (figure 7G,H) and Ki-67-positive proliferating cells (figure 7I), as compared with lenvatinib treatment alone. This indicates that targeting CD133+ cells potentiates multikinase inhibitor efficacy in MASLD-HCC.

Targeting CD133 with VNP-siCD133 reduces tumour burden and enhances efficacy of multikinase inhibitor in MASLD-HCC

Since inhibitors of CD133 are not available, we used a Food and Drug Administration-approved formulation for in vivo delivery of siRNA targeting CD133 (figure 8A). Pyrimidine base (C/U) in siRNAs was modified with 2’-O-methylation to improve its stability. We established human MASLD-HCC (HKCI2) xenografts in NSG mice. After tumour establishment, mice were randomised to receive vesicle-like nanoparticle-siNC (VNP-siNC) or VNP-siCD133, with or without sorafenib treatment (figure 8B). Remarkably, VNP-siCD133 plus sorafenib demonstrated synergistic tumour suppression throughout the treatment regimen as compared with control (p<0.0001), VNP-siCD133 (p<0.05) and sorafenib (p=0.001) groups (figure 8B,C), achieving >90% reduction in tumour weight (figure 8D). Along with reduced tumour growth, VNP-siCD133 plus sorafenib inhibited cell proliferation by Ki-67 staining and induced apoptosis by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay more effectively than either treatment alone, while sorafenib alone showed no effect (figure 8E). CD133 protein was markedly reduced by VNP-siCD133, and the combination treatment with sorafenib synergistically downregulated phospho-GSK-3β and active β-catenin expression by western blot (figure 8F), indicative of repressed Wnt/β-catenin signalling cascade. We further established orthotopic HKCI2 tumours in NSG mice. After tumour establishment (Day 9), the mice were randomised to receive nanoparticle-siNC or nanoparticle-siCD133, with or without sorafenib (online supplemental figure S5A). Nanoparticle-siCD133 plus sorafenib exerted the strongest inhibitory effect on tumour growth in vivo (online supplemental figure S5B-D), consistent with our previous observations.

We further verified this therapeutic effect in an orthotopic MASLD-HCC model established using CD133-high mouse MASLD-HCC organoids (figure 8G). Single VNP-siCD133 (p=0.0002) or sorafenib (p=0.004) partly suppressed tumour growth, whereas the combination of VNP-siCD133 and sorafenib exerted a synergistic effect against MASLD-HCC growth as compared with control (p<0.0001) or sorafenib alone (p=0.02) (figure 8H,I). Accordingly, VNP-siCD133 plus sorafenib most strongly inhibited tumour cell proliferation (figure 8J), induced cell apoptosis (figure 8J) and downregulated the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (figure 8K). Neither VNP-siCD133, sorafenib, nor their combination caused serious adverse events or changes in body weight, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase for liver damage or blood urea nitrogen for renal function throughout the entire study duration (online supplemental figure S5E and F). Taken together, targeting CD133 in combination with sorafenib holds promise for the safe and effective treatment of MASLD-HCC.

Discussion

The escalating global burden of MASLD-HCC underscores the need for a deeper understanding of its molecular drivers, particularly the identity of CSCs, a subpopulation responsible for sustained tumour progression and therapeutic resistance.27 Although CD133 has been implicated as a CSC marker in several cancers, its functional role and therapeutic relevance in MASLD-HCC remains poorly defined. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that CD133 positivity identifies CSCs with enhanced self-renewal and tumourigenic capacities in MASLD-HCC. Our findings not only establish CD133+ cells as bona fide CSCs in MASLD-HCC, but also the direct oncogenic mechanism mediated by CD133 overexpression and validate CD133-targeted therapy as a potential promising strategy to overcome treatment resistance.

Expression of CD133 is significantly increased in tumour tissues of patients with MASLD-HCC and mice with diet-induced MASLD-HCC. Genetic lineage-tracing in mice demonstrated clonal expansion capacity of CD133+ MASLD-HCC cells in vivo. Mapping of the hierarchical organisation of MASLD-HCC progression at the single cell level revealed that CD133+ MASLD-HCC cells function as progenitors that give rise to multilineage cancer cells, and also exhibit drug resistance phenotypes. Our results offer insights into CSCs in MASLD-HCC and their contributions to tumour heterogeneity and therapy response.

Here, by lineage tracing, we identified the rapid clonal expansion of CD133+ cells to form MASLD-HCC tumour nodules in mice. To further delineate lineage characteristics, we performed scRNA-seq to trace eGFP-labelled CD133+ cells in mouse MASLD-HCC tumours. Compared with the earlier time point (Day 3) where the tumours are enriched with stem cell-like cells, tumours at the later time point (Day 30) predominantly comprise cells that express markers of metabolism, proliferation, differentiation and EMT. These results are in line with previous studies showing the multipotent potential of CD133+ cancer cells28 and their ability to differentiate into diverse cell lineages.29

To evaluate the functionality of CD133+ cells, we isolated and sorted CD133+ cells from murine MASLD-HCC tumours. CD133+ cells have greater clonogenic and tumour-initiating abilities than their CD133− counterparts and are highly resistant to sorafenib, the first-line treatment of advanced HCC. CD133+ cells also markedly reduced survival in mice with orthotopic MASLD-HCC tumours. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that CD133+ cells function as CSCs in MASLD-HCC, as evidenced by protumourigenic and multipotent potential, and resistance to therapy, all of which are fundamental features of CSCs.30 In agreement with this, previous studies have reported that CD133+ cells are associated with chemoresistance31 32 and the poor prognosis of HCC.33 Although the clinicopathological and molecular characteristics of MASLD-HCC are distinct from viral-associated HCC, our study suggests that CD133 positivity is a common feature of HCC CSCs.

We uncovered a direct mechanistic link between CD133 and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, a central regulator of cancer stemness.34 35 We revealed that CD133 binds to MYH9 to promote the phosphorylation and inactivation of GSK-3β, leading to β-catenin stabilisation and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Hence, CD133 not only serves as a surface marker for CSC identification but also as an active enhancer of stemness properties. Aberrant Wnt/β-catenin activation in cancer commonly arises from Wnt ligand binding or loss-of-function mutations in repressor genes such as APC and AXIN.36 Our identified CD133-MYH9 axis presents an alternative, protein interaction-driven mechanism that inactivates GSK-3β to suppress β-catenin degradation. This mechanism is particularly relevant in MASLD-HCC with a high prevalence of aberrant WNT activation.37 Our findings suggest that CD133 directly activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CSCs, providing a complementary mechanism in addition to CTNNB1 activating mutations.37 Hence, these findings indicate that CD133 is an oncogenic driver by binding to MYH9 on MASLD-HCC cells to activate Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway. Beyond intrinsic cellular mechanisms that upregulate CD133 in CSCs, recent studies have implied that CD133 expression is modulated by the TME. MASLD-HCC TME comprises a complex milieu of chronic inflammation, fibrosis, hypoxia and metabolic stress, likely providing extrinsic cues that drive the selection and expansion of CD133+ cells. For instance, oxygen,38 glucose39 and lactate40 levels in the TME have been reported to influence CD133 expression in tumour cells. Soluble factors derived from intratumoral immune and stromal components, such as cytokines, growth factors and hypoxic signals, could also activate transcription factors that trigger stem cell-like properties.41 42 In MASLD-HCC CSCs, CD133 overexpression activates Wnt/β-catenin signalling, promoting cell proliferation, self-renewal, plasticity and immune evasion. These changes enable cells to adapt and thrive under metabolic and genotoxic stress, ultimately promoting tumours initiation and progression. Consistently, our observation of progressive CD133 upregulation during MASLD-HCC development supports the notion that CD133 offers an adaptive advantage facilitating tumour expansion.

The identification of CD133 as an active oncogenic driver has important therapeutic implications. We demonstrated that targeted ablation of CD133+ cells, using either a genetic model (DTR system) or a translational approach (VNP-siCD133), markedly sensitises MASLD-HCC tumours to multikinase inhibitors. The synergistic effect of VNP-siCD133 and sorafenib, observed without severe adverse events in our mouse models, provides a compelling preclinical rationale for this combinatorial strategy. The inherent liver tropism of the VNP delivery system further supports its potential for clinical translation in HCC. However, the therapeutic targeting of CD133 is not without potential caveats. The main challenge arises from its abundant expression on stem and progenitor cells in various normal tissues.43 This may lead to adverse effects, such as impaired tissue regeneration or organ dysfunction. Hence, clinical translation of CD133-directed therapy will require rigorous safety evaluation. In this connection, using nanoparticles as a carrier for siCD133 offers the potential advantage of maximising the therapeutic window by leveraging the enhanced permeability and retention effect of nanoparticles in tumours, as well as their preferential accumulation of nanoparticles in the liver. Future strategies that incorporate a second tumour-specific antigen to guide nanoparticle homing may further improve the specificity of CD133 targeting in tumour tissues.

In conclusion, we for the first time identify that CD133+ cells harbour CSC phenotypes in MASLD-HCC, including self-renewal, multipotency and drug resistance. CD133 confers robust oncogenic function by interacting with MYH9 to activate Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Moreover, targeted ablation of CD133+ CSCs markedly increases the sensitivity of tumour cells to multikinase inhibitors in MASLD-HCC. Altogether, our study comprehensively reveals the functionality and protumourigenic mechanism of CD133+ CSCs, emphasising CD133 as a potential and promising therapeutic target against MASLD-HCC.
