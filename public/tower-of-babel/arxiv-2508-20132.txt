Delta-system method: a survey
Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract




0.1
Introduction

In 1960 Erdős and Rado
ER
published a paper that, in retrospect, became one of the most influential papers in extremal set theory. (It was also probably the second paper on the subject, after the famous paper of Sperner.) They proved a result of Ramsey theoretic flavour, stating that in any sufficiently large family of sets of bounded size there is a homogeneous substructure, called a
\Delta
-system
(also known under the name of a
sunflower
). For many qualitative results in Discrete Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science, this has become a very powerful tool to analyze complex set families. Extremal set theory flourished in the 1970’s–80’s, and many exciting developments happened then. One of them was the development of the
\Delta
-system method
in the works of Frankl and Füredi. In this survey, we try to give a concise picture of this method starting from its early stages and to the modern day. We also tried to present the proofs of most of the key results. On top of this, we survey the literature on the problems that the Delta-systems was applied to.

We should also note that two other excellent sources on the Delta-system method are the survey of Füredi
Fur91
and the book of Frankl and Tokushige
FT
. The content of this survey turned out to have quite significant intersections with these two sources. We decided to leave it as it is, partly because we aimed for completeness, and partly because presenting proofs allowed us to elaborate on some of the aspects of the method.

X
, all
\leqslant k
-element subsets of
X

0.1.2
\Delta
-systems and the result of Erdős and Rado

A family
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}\}
of sets is a
\Delta(s)
-system
, or a
sunflower
if
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\bigcap_{i\in[s]}A_{i}
for all
i\neq j\in[s]
. If we want to specify the size, the we say that it is a
\Delta(s)
-system or an
s
-sunflower. The set
\bigcap_{i\in[s]}A_{i}
is called the
core
(or
kernel
) of the
\Delta
-system. Erdős and Rado introduced
\Delta
-systems in 1960 in the paper
ER
and proved the following result.

Theorem 1
(Erdős and Rado
ER
)

If
\mathcal{F}
is a family of sets of size
\leqslant k
and
|\mathcal{F}|>k!(s-1)^{k}
, then
\mathcal{F}
contains a
\Delta(s)
-system.

Proof

The proof is by induction on
k
. The result is evident for
k=1
. Assuming that the result holds for
k-1
, let us prove it for
k
. Take the largest matching
M_{1},\ldots,M_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}
. If
\ell\geqslant s
, then
\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}\}
form a
\Delta(s)
-system, and thus the theorem is proved. If
\ell\leqslant s-1
, then
M:=\bigcup_{i\in[\ell]}M_{i}
has size at most
(s-1)k
. By maximality of the chosen matching, any set from
\mathcal{F}
intersects
M
. Thus, there is an element
x\in M
such that

\mathcal{F}(x):=\{F\setminus\{x\}:x\in F,F\in\mathcal{F}\}

has size at least
\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{(s-1)k}>(k-1)!(s-1)^{k-1}
. The sets in
\mathcal{F}(x)
have size at most
(k-1)
, and
|\mathcal{F}(x)|>(k-1)!(s-1)^{k-1}
. Thus we may apply the inductive assumption to
\mathcal{F}(x)
and find a
\Delta(s)
-system in
\mathcal{F}(x)
. If we append
x
to every set in that
\Delta(s)
-system, we get a
\Delta(s)
-system in
\mathcal{F}
, as claimed.

Let us denote

\phi(k,s):=\max\big\{|\mathcal{F}|:\mathcal{F}\text{ consists of sets of size }\leqslant k\\
\text{ and }\mathcal{F}\text{ contains no }\Delta(s+1)-\text{system}\big\}.

(1)

In these terms, Theorem
1
states that
\phi(k,s)\leqslant k!s^{k}
. Also note that the maximum is attained on a family of sets of size
k
: we can always complement sets of size
<k
by new elements of the ground set that are all pairwise distinct. Clearly, this will not create new
\Delta
-systems. Abbot, Hanson and Sauer
AHS
in 1972, and then Spencer
S
in 1977 improved upper bounds on
\phi(k,s)
. The result of Spencer states that for any fixed
s
and
\epsilon>0
there exists
C
such that
\phi(k,s)\leqslant Ck!(1+\epsilon)^{k}
. Kostochka
Kost
improved this bound for
\Delta(s)
-free systems to
\phi(k,s)\leqslant C(s,\alpha)k!\Big(\frac{(\log\log\log k)^{2}}{\alpha\log\log k}\Big)^{k}
for any
\alpha>1
and a certain function
C(s,\alpha)
. For fixed
k
and large
s
Kostochka, Rödl and Talysheva
KRT
showed that
\phi(k,s)=(1+o(1))k^{s}
.

One natural example for a
\Delta(s+1)
-free
k
-uniform family is a
k
-partite hypergraph with parts of size
s
. It has
s^{k}
sets. There are, however, examples that are exponentially bigger. The authors of
AHS
showed that
\phi(2,s)=s(s+1)
for even
s
and
s^{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}
for odd
s
. The following observation allows us to extend the graph bound to higher uniformities.

Observation 2

We have
\phi(a+b,s)\geqslant\phi(a,s)\phi(b,s)
.

Proof

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a family of
a
-element sets with no
\Delta(s)
-system and of size
\phi(a,s)
. Define
\mathcal{B}
,
|\mathcal{B}|=\phi(b,s)
similarly. Assume that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
live on disjoint ground sets
X
and
Y
, respectively. Consider a family
\mathcal{C}:=\{A\sqcup B:A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}\}
. It is easy to see that
|\mathcal{C}|=\phi(a,s)+\phi(b,s)
and that it is a
\Delta(s+1)
-system free.

Abbott, Hanson and Sauer also showed a lower bound
\phi(k,3)\geqslant 10^{(k/2)-c\log k}
with some positive constant
c
, which is exponentially better than
3^{k}
which follows from the construction above. They used a construction of a
3
-uniform family of size
10
and with no
\Delta(3)
-system, and then leveraged it to any uniformity using an iterated product construction, which gives a recursion
\psi(ab)\geqslant\psi(a)\psi(b)^{a}
, where
\psi(a)
is the size of their iterated construction of uniformity
a
.
The celebrated conjecture of Erdős and Rado, mentioned in their paper
ER
, suggests that we cannot improve the lower bound given by a complete
k
-partite hypergraph too much further. It states that the dependence on
k
is exponential:
\phi(k,s)\leqslant(Cs)^{k}
for some absolute constant
C
.

For a more detailed survey on the Erdős–Rado question and related questions (weak
\Delta
-systems, as well as the Erdős–Szémeredi question on
\Delta
-systems for a restricted ground set), we refer to a survey of Kostochka
Kost00
.

0.1.3
How
\Delta
-systems changed to sunflowers?

The original name of the object from the paper of Erdős and Rado is a
\Delta
-system
, and it was called this way for the next 20 years.

The first occurrence of the name
sunflower
I could trace is in the name of the paper of Deza and Frankl
DF
from 1981 “Every large set of equidistant
(0,+1,-1)
-vectors form a sunflower,” which can be seen as a follow-up to the paper of Deza
D0
that we discuss below. Funnily, the word “sunflower” does not appear in the body of the paper, only in the title. Moreover, the conclusion of the main theorem of that paper guarantees a structure which is not exactly a sunflower, but a (one possible)
(-1,0,1)
-generalization of it. Peter Frankl (private communication) confirmed that he came up with the term while working with Michel Deza on the paper
DF
.

The second occurrence of the term I could trace (and the first one where it is actually used in the paper and gets its present meaning) is the influential paper of Alon, Frankl and Lovász
AFL
from 1985 on the chromatic number of Kneser hypergraphs. Two years later, in 1987, Alon and Boppana
AB
write another influential paper “Monotone circuit complexity of Boolean functions”, where they use structures akin to sunflowers for proving certain complexity lower bounds. Their result extended a breakthrough result of Razborov
Raz
, who also used similar structures in the argument. My guess is that it is at this point, when the term entered TCS community, that it solidified. Later, it appeared in different other complexity lower bounds papers, in Jukna’s book
Juk
, and ultimately took over the
\Delta
-system terminology.

In combinatorics community,
\Delta
-system terminology prevailed for a while. But the object went under different names, notably, it was called an
s
-star
in some of the most important papers on the subject of the
\Delta
-system method. This term was introduced by Chung and, in particular, used by Füredi in his paper
Fu1
and by Frankl and Füredi in
FF1
. Peter Frankl (private communication) pointed out that the
s
-star term came as a generalization of graph stars, which are
2
-uniform
\Delta
-systems with kernel of size
1
. In another influential paper
FF2
, however, Frankl and Füredi used the sunflower terminology.

0.1.4
Recent progress on the Erdős–Rado conjecture

A breakthrough in the study of the Erdős–Rado Delta-system question came several years ago in a work of Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang
Alw
. They managed to reduce the dependence on
k
in Theorem
1
from
k^{k}
to essentially
(\log k)^{k}
. It spurred a lot of research. Several follow-up papers slightly improved upon the bound and presented the proof quite differently. Rao
Rao
gave a code-theoretic proof with a better bound; Bell, Chueluecha and Warnke
BCW
improved upon some of the arguments of the original proof, which lead to the bound
\phi(s,k)\leqslant(Cs\log k)^{k}
; Tao
Tao
gave a proof based on entropy, which, however, contained a mistake; Lu
Hu
and then Stoeckl
Sto
gave another entropy proof, which gave the bound
\phi(s,k)\leqslant(64s\log k)^{k}
; Mossel, Niles-Weed, Sun, and Zadik
MNSZ
gave a second moment proof; finally, Rao
Rao2
gave the same bound with a very short combinatorial proof in the spirit of the original paper
Alw
.

The proof is based on a result, which is called the spread lemma. Given a real number
r>1
, we say that a family
\mathcal{F}
of sets is
r
-spread
if for each set
X
we have
|\mathcal{F}(X)|<r^{-|X|}|\mathcal{F}|
. We say that
W
is a
p
-random subset
of
[n]
if each element of
[n]
is included in
W
with probability
p
and independently of others.

Theorem 3
(The spread lemma,
Alw
, a sharpening due to
Sto
)

If for some
n,k,r\geqslant 1
a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose\leqslant k}
is
r
-spread and
W
is a
(\beta\delta)
-random subset of
[n]
, then

\Pr[\exists F\in\mathcal{F}\ :\ F\subset W]>1-\Big(\frac{2}{\log_{2}(r\delta)}\Big)^{\beta}k.

In order to prove the aforementioned bound on
\phi(k,s)
, one needs, first, to find a spread subfamily
\mathcal{F}(X)
in a large family
\mathcal{F}
. (Choose
X
to be a maximal set that violates the
r
-spreadness condition.) Then, using the spread lemma, one sees that, if the parameters are chosen right (say,
r=32s\log_{2}(2k),\delta=1/(2s\log_{2}(2k)),\beta=\log_{2}(2k)
), a random
\frac{1}{2s}
-subset contains a set from
\mathcal{F}(X)
with probability at least
1/2
. Then, in a random
2s
-coloring there are in expectation at least
s
colors that contain a set from the family. These sets form a matching in
\mathcal{F}(X)
, which gives a
\Delta(s)
-system with kernel
X
in
\mathcal{F}
.

The paper of Rao
Rao2
gives a broad view on different applications of sunflowers.

0.1.5
Structure of the survey

Roughly speaking, the survey consists of three parts. In the first part (Sections
0.2
-
0.5
), we discuss the development of the method in the 1970’s and 1980’s. In the second part (Sections
0.6
-
0.8
), we discuss aspects of the method and some relevant recent developments. In the third part (Section
0.9
), I tried to make a comprehensive survey of the literature that deals with the problems from the earlier parts.

Section
0.2
contains the early manifestations of the method. It starts with the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, then it gives the result of Deza on
\Delta
-systems and families with one intersection size, and then presents the theorem of Deza, Erdős and Frankl on
(n,k,L)
-systems. The latter is considered to be the starting point of the method. Then we discuss the development of the method by Peter Frankl up until 1983. One thing that I realized while going through the literature is that Peter Frankl developed a (lesser known) approach that can be considered as an ‘intermediate’ approach between the earlier ‘base’ variant of the method and the ‘homogeneous families’ variant.

Section
0.3
is devoted to Füredi’s structural theorem on homogeneous families, which underlies further development of the method, and its early applications. In particular, we present the results of Füredi on families with three intersection sizes.

In Section
0.4
, we discuss the ‘Forbidding one intersection’ paper of Frankl and Füredi, which to a large extent shaped the
\Delta
-system method. The result of Füredi meets the result of Frankl and Katona on families with many intersection conditions, and then they develop the shadow/stability machinery to get exact results.

Section
0.5
is devoted to the paper ‘Exact solutions of Turán-type problems’, in which Frankl and Füredi applied the method to a variety of Turán-type questions. Although most of the key
\Delta
-system-related ideas were already present in the previous paper, here they harvest the fruits of their previous findings, but also delineate the scope and the limitations of the method in terms of classes of hypergraphs that it works for.

In Section
0.6
, we discuss stability and supersaturation that could be achieved using Delta-systems. Stability via Delta-systems is an aspect that is often overlooked in the literature, and we go through the proof of Frankl and Füredi
FF1
and derive an efficient stability result. We present some further ideas on stability via Delta-systems for harder problems. We also discuss a recent result of Jiang and Longbrake that gives a supersaturation result for
2
-reducible trees via
\Delta
-systems.

In Section
0.7
, we discuss different approaches to base constructions. This is a key part of the earlier variant of the method, and can be used in conjunction with other approaches. We go over different approaches that appeared in the literature and present the peeling/simplification procedure, which is an important part of the spread approximation method.

In Section
0.8
, we briefly discuss other general methods that were developed recently to tackle Túran-type problems: junta approximations, spread approximations and hypercontractivity. We do not go in depth on the methods, but rather underline the different regimes, in which these methods work. In particular, we show that, as it stands now, each of the
\Delta
-system method, hypercontractivity method and spread approximation method allows to solve Turán-type problems in incompatible regimes of parameters.

In Section
0.9
, we survey literature concerning the topics of the previous sections, and especially further studies on the topics of the papers
DEF
,
FF1
,
FF2
. We cover forbidden one intersection problem;
(n,k,L)
-systems; forbidden sunflowers and unavoidable hypergraphs; forbidden simplices and clusters; forbidden hypertrees and expanded hypergraphs.

0.2
Early days of the Delta-system method

0.2.1
(n,k,L)
-systems of Deza, Erdős and Frankl, the precursor of the Delta-system method and the proof of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem for large
n

\Delta
-system method was introduced in the paper of Deza, Erdős and Frankl
DEF
. They introduced and studied the following question, along with its several generalizations. Given positive integers
n,k
and a set
L\subset[0,k-1]
, we say that
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is an
(n,k,L)
-system
if for any
A\neq B\in\mathcal{F}
we have
|A\cap B|\in L
. The question is as follows:

what is the largest size
m(n,k,L)
of an
(n,k,L)
-system, for different values of
n,k,L
?
1
1

1

In the paper
FF1
, Frankl and Füredi attribute the idea of using Delta-systems in this context to Deza.

Before discussing the result of Deza, Erdős and Frankl and the
\Delta
-system method, we want to put the result and the method into context. Another cornerstone result (and probably the third paper on the topic of extremal set theory) in extremal set theory is the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
EKR
. It determines for all
n,k
the size of the largest
(n,k,\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\})
-system, and moreover, the size of the largest
(n,k,\{t,t+1,\ldots,k-1\})
-system for any
t\in[1,k-1]
, provided
n\geqslant n_{0}(k)
. (Such families are called
intersecting
and
t
-intersecting
, respectively.) It is instructive to discuss the proof of the EKR theorem.

Theorem 4
(Erdős, Ko and Rado
EKR
)

Let
n\geqslant k\geqslant t
be positive integers and suppose that
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is
t
-intersecting, that is,
|A\cap B|\geqslant t
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
. Then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant{n-t\choose k-t}
for
n>n_{0}(k)
.

Here, we sketch a variant of their proof. In the proof of Erdős, Ko and Rado,
n_{0}(k)
is roughly
{k\choose t}^{3}
.

Proof

Take a maximal
t
-intersecting family
\mathcal{F}
. Maximality implies that there are
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
with
|A\cap B|=t
. There are two cases to consider. If
|\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A|\geqslant t
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant{n-t\choose k-t}
and we are done. If
|\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A|<t
then take two sets
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
such that
|A\cap B|=t
and add a third set
C
such that
C\not\supset A\cap B
(we leave the verification of the existence of such
A,B,C
as an exercise). Then
|A\cap B\cap C|<t
. Any set
F\in\mathcal{F}
intersects each of
A,B,C
in
\geqslant t
elements, and thus it must intersect
A\cup B\cup C
in at least
t+1
elements. Therefore,
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant{|A\cup B\cup C|\choose t+1}{n-t-1\choose k-t-1}\leqslant{3k\choose t+1}{n-t-1\choose k-t-1}
. For
n>n_{0}(k)
this is smaller than
{n-t\choose k-t}
.

Summarizing, the ‘trivial
t
-intersecting’ families are covered by one set of size
t
(that is, all sets from the family contain a given set of size
t
), while the non-trivial families are covered by
const(k,t)
sets of size
t+1
, and for large
n
the latter gives smaller sizes since the number of ways to extend sets of size
t+1
to sets of size
k
is roughly
n
times smaller than the number of ways to extend sets of size
t
. The important common point between the
\Delta
-system method and the proof above is that in both we compare contributions of ‘cover sets’ of different sizes. The
\Delta
-system method, however, finds and deals with these covers more efficiently.

0.2.2
Families of sets with only one intersection size

One result that precedes the general question of Deza, Erdős, and Frankl is due to Deza and deals with the case when
L=\{\ell\}
, that is, all pairwise intersections in the family have size
\ell
. According to the paper of Deza
D1
, the following conjecture was posed by Erdős on a seminar in Paris in 1973.

Let
k>\ell
be positive integers and consider a family
\mathcal{F}
of at least
k^{2}-k+2
sets, each of size
k
, such that for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
we have
|A\cap B|=\ell
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is a
\Delta
-system.

The bound
k^{2}-k+2
is best possible: for
k-1
being a prime power, one can consider a projective plane of order
k-1
. It contains exactly
k^{2}-k+1
lines, each two intersecting in exactly
1
point and is obviously not a sunflower.

Deza
D1
noticed that this conjecture is true because it immediately reduces to his result from the recently published paper
D0
, in which he studied one-distance codes. The main result of
D0
has an elegant proof with geometric flavour, and we present it here. (For the rest of this subsection,
u,v,w
will exceptionally stand for vectors.) Recall that, given two vectors
v,w
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
, the
Hamming distance
between
v
and
w
is the number of coordinates
i
such that
v_{i}\neq w_{i}
. Also recall that there is a natural correspondence between vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
and subsets of
[n]
: set
\leftrightarrow
characteristic vector.

Theorem 5
(Deza
D0
)

Let
m,q
be integers and
\mathcal{F}
be a collection of vectors
v^{0},v^{1},\ldots v^{m}\in\mathcal{\{}0,1\}^{n}
such that the Hamming distance between any two vectors is
2q
. If
m>q^{2}+q+1
then the code is trivial: for each coordinate
i\in[n]
, either
\sum_{j\in[m]}v_{i}^{j}\leqslant 1
or
\sum_{j\in[m]}v_{i}^{j}\geqslant m-1
.

This theorem allows us to deduce the conjecture of Erdős and Lovász.

Corollary 6
(Deza
D1
)

Let
k>\ell
be positive integers and consider a family
\mathcal{F}
of at least
k^{2}-k+2
sets, each of size
k
, such that for any
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
we have
|A\cap B|=\ell
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is a
\Delta
-system.

Proof

Consider a collection of
m
sets, each of size
k
and with pairwise intersections of size
\ell\geqslant 1
. Note that, seen as vectors
v^{1},\ldots,v^{m}
, they have pairwise Hamming distance
2(k-\ell)
. We want to transform them into vectors with pairwise Hamming distance equal to their Hamming weight. For this, add to each vector
\ell-1
new coordinates with ones, such that these
\ell-1
coordinates for different vectors are pairwise distinct, as well as
(k-\ell-1)
new coordinates where all vectors will have ones. It is easy to check that now each vector has Hamming weight

In set terms, what does it mean for the corresponding code to be trivial? If two vectors have a common

1
there, which means that any element common for two sets is common for all sets. This is exactly the definition of a
\Delta
-system.

Lemma 7
(Lemma on cross-variance)

Consider two independent random vectors

(\mathbf{X_{2}},\mathbf{Y_{2}})

(2)

where the norm is the Euclidean norm.

We will need the case when the random vectors are uniformly distributed on sets of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}

Proof

We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[(\mathbf{X}_{1}-\mathbf{X}_{2})^{2}]

\displaystyle=2\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{X}^{2}]-2\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{X}]^{2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[(\mathbf{Y}_{1}-\mathbf{Y}_{2})^{2}]

\displaystyle=2\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{Y}^{2}]-2\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{Y}]^{2}

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{X}^{2}]+\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{Y}^{2}]-2\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{X}]\mathsf{E}[\mathbf{Y}].

The statement now follows from

(ii)

(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})|(\ell_{3}-\ell_{2})|\ldots|(\ell_{r}-\ell_{r-1})|(k-\ell_{r}).

Below we sketch the original proof of Deza, Erdős and Frankl and discuss the

-system part in detail. The proof of the theorem is by induction on

We first treat the case

(n-1,k-1,\{\ell_{2}-1,\ldots,\ell_{r}-1\})

It remains to show that
\ell_{2}|(\ell_{3}-\ell_{2})

for different

\mathcal{F}
may end up in
\mathcal{F}_{I}
, and thus
X
will not become such a center.

Now, for each
I\subset[k]
and
X
such that
\pi(X)=I
and
\mathcal{F}_{I}[X]
is non-empty, we take a maximal
\Delta
-system
F_{1},\ldots,F_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
with kernel
X
, where
\ell<s
. Any other set in
\mathcal{F}_{I}[X]
(the sets in
\mathcal{F}_{I}
containing X) must intersect one of
F_{i}\setminus X
, so we may take the element
v_{X}\in\cup F_{i}\setminus X
that is contained in at least a
1/\ell k
-proportion of sets from
\mathcal{F}_{I}[X]
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}
is
k
-partite, and thus
\mathcal{F}[X_{1}]
,
\mathcal{F}[X_{2}]
are disjoint for distinct

such that
\pi(X_{i})=I
. Now put

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{I}:=\bigsqcup_{X:\pi(X)=I}\mathcal{F}_{I}[X\cup\{v_{X}\}].

We note that, first,
|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{I}|\geqslant\frac{1}{(s-1)k}|\mathcal{F}_{I}|
and, second,
I\notin\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{I})
.

Finally, we take as
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
the largest family out of
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\{\mathcal{F}_{I}^{\prime}\}

\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\{\mathcal{F}_{I}^{\prime}\}

\mathcal{F}

\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{1+s(k-1)|\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F})|}

After each application of Lemma

we either get a subfamily with desired properties, or pass from
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}

0.3.2
First applications:
(n,k,L)
-systems

In the paper

(n,k,L)

Theorem 25

We have

if and only if there is no family
\mathcal{M}\subset 2^{[k]}
that is closed under intersection, and such that
\cup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}M=[k]

for all

That is, he generalizes several previous results, including the result of Frankl
Fra83
for
|L|=3
. In order to see that the ‘if’ direction of the theorem is valid, take any
(n,k,L)
-family
\mathcal{F}
and find a
(k,k+1)
-homogeneous structure
\mathcal{F}^{*}\subset\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}^{*})

The second theorem is the following reduction theorem.

Theorem 26

Assume that
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r},k

The
\Omega_{k}

\big(\lfloor\frac{n}{d}\rfloor,\frac{k}{d},\{\frac{\ell_{1}}{d},\ldots,\frac{\ell_{r}}{d}\}\big)

\mathcal{F}
and then replace each element of the ground set with
d

(n,k,\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}\})
-system of the same size. The

21
. Take the largest
(n,k,\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}\})
-system
\mathcal{F}
and select a
(k,k+1)
-homogeneous structure
\mathcal{F}^{*}\subset\mathcal{F}
.

Consider
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}^{*})
and define its

P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
, with some integer

. There are disjoint subsets of

defined by the property that the elements of each

Next, we use the intersection structure

Families with

In the paper

10

\ell_{1}|\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}|\ell_{3}-\ell_{2}|k-\ell_{3}

. Frankl and Füredi explored the range between
n
and

and showed that different non-integral exponents are possible.
Using that
m(n,k,\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\})=\Theta_{k}(m(n-\ell_{1},k-\ell_{1},\{0,\ell_{2}-\ell_{1},\ell_{3}-\ell_{1}\}))

.

In the paper that we discussed in Section

, Frankl

. At the same time, if we are not in the situation of Theorem

m(n,k,L)=\Omega(n^{k/(k-1)})

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset 2^{[k]}
be the prospective intersection structure,
\mathcal{M}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{f},

B_{1},\ldots,B_{g}\}
, where
A_{i}
and
B_{j}
are the prospective intersections of sizes
a
and
b
, respectively. We of course assume that
\mathcal{M}
is closed under intersection. Let
I=\{i\in[f]:A_{i}\subset B_{j}\text{ for at least two $B_{j}$'s}\}
. Let
B_{j}^{\prime}:=\{i\in I:A_{i}\subset B_{j}\}
encode the indices of sets
A_{i}
that are contained in
B_{j}
. Finally, form a family out of these sets: put
\mathcal{C}:=\{B_{j}^{\prime}:j\in[g]\}
.
Consider the following condition.

There exists
\mathcal{M}
as above and such that every pair of elements of
I
is contained in exactly one set in
\mathcal{C}
. That is,
\mathcal{C}
is a
2
-design on

.

Note that any pair of elements of
I
is contained in at most one set in
\mathcal{C}
, otherwise, two sets of size
b

a

for some values of parameters.

Let us give some proof ideas. First, he passes to the
(k,k+1)
-homogeneous structure
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. As we assumed,
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}^{*})

and define
\mathcal{B}
analogously for intersection size

. E.g., sets in

are pairwise disjoint. Then, using an argument similar to Proposition

. (This is again the argument as in proving that a graph with many edges has a subgraph with high minimum degree.) Abusing notation, we use
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{F}^{*}
for these ‘cleaned’ families. In this process, we removed
O_{k}(n^{4/3})
sets. Consider the family
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}
of sets in
\mathcal{A}
that are contained in at least
n^{1/3}
sets from
\mathcal{B}
. A simple double-counting argument, together with the fact that sets in
\mathcal{A}
are disjoint and
|\mathcal{B}|=O_{k}(n)
, shows that
|\mathcal{A}_{0}|=O_{k}(n^{2/3})
.

Finally, we use the fact that the property is violated. Take an arbitrary
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
and two sets
A_{1},A_{2}\subset F
that satisfy the following: both are contained in at least two sets from
\mathcal{B}
, but there is no set
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\subset B
. He then shows that, first,
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

8

8

For this we need the fact that each
A_{i}
is contained in at least two

Second, he shows that

is not contained in any intersection of sets from

(in the terminology of the next section, we may call it an

|\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant{|\mathcal{A}_{0}|\choose 2}=O_{k}(n^{4/3})

0.4
Forbidding just one intersection

One of the first and most prominent applications of Theorem
21
is the paper of Frankl and Füredi
FF1
, in which they solve for
n>n_{0}(k)
the ‘forbidden one intersection problem’. Recall that the question is to determine the largest size of a family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
such that
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|\neq\ell
. See the problem collection of Erdős
(
Erd75
, page 18)
. For
\ell=1
this problem was proposed by Erdős and Sós. Then Erdős in
Erd75
suggested the generalization of this problem for all values of
\ell
. He wrote:

I just thought of (2)
while writing these lines and thus would not be surprised if
it would be completely false or at best not completely
accurate.

The equation (2) above stands for the two prospective extremal families. One is the family of all sets containing a given
(\ell+1)
-element set, and the other one is a (partial) Steiner system/design: the largest collection of
k
-element sets such that any two intersect in at most
\ell-1
elements. The bound was indeed not completely accurate, but not completely false, as we shall see in Section
0.4.1
below.

We already mentioned the paper of Frankl
Fra80a
, in which he managed to show that for
n\geqslant n_{0}(k)
,
k\geqslant 3\ell
the largest
\ell
-avoiding family has size at most
c(k){n-\ell-1\choose k-\ell-1}
. Using Theorem
21
, Frankl and Füredi managed to obtain great progress on this problem. In this section, we discuss the approximate result for all
\ell
, and in the next section we discuss the exact result for
k\geqslant 2\ell+2
.

Theorem 27
(Frankl and Füredi
FF1
)

If
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is an
(n,[0,k-1]\setminus\{\ell\},k)
-system, then

|\mathcal{F}|=O_{k}(n^{\max\{\ell,k-\ell-1\}}).

0.4.1
Examples

Let us explain why the maximum in the exponent is for a good reason. This problem has very different prospective extremal examples depending on whether
k>2\ell+1
or
k\leqslant 2\ell+1
.

In the former case, it is of the Erdős–Ko–Rado type: the family of all sets containing a given
(\ell+1)
-element set. That is, it avoids all intersections smaller than
\ell
. It has size
{n-\ell-1\choose k-\ell-1}
.

In the latter case, it is based on a partial
design
(partial
Steiner system
). Consider the following partial design: a collection of
(2k-\ell-1)
-element sets such that any
\ell
-element set is contained in at most one of them. A design is
complete
if each
\ell
-element set is contained in exactly one set. Any such family has size at most
{n\choose\ell}/{2k-\ell-1\choose\ell}
, and Rödl
Ro
proved that such families of size
(1+o(1)){n\choose\ell}/{2k-\ell-1\choose\ell}
can be found using a greedy/random procedure called Rödl Nibble Method. Keevash
Kee
proved that (complete) designs exist for large
n
, provided that natural divisibility conditions hold. Back to the construction, take the
k
-shadow of such family, that is, all
k
-element sets contained in one of the
(2k-\ell-1)
-element ‘blocks’. Note that any two
k
-sets inside one
(2k-\ell-1)
-element block intersect in at least
\ell+1
elements, while sets from different blocks intersect in at most
\ell-1
elements. The size of such construction is
(1+o(1)){n\choose\ell}{2k-\ell-1\choose k}/{2k-\ell-1\choose\ell}.

9
9

9

We may compare it with the example suggested by Erdős. The latter has size (at most)
{n\choose\ell}/{k\choose\ell}
, and the ratio of the sizes is
\frac{1}{(k-\ell)!}\prod_{i=1}^{k-\ell}(2k-2\ell-i)
, which is strictly bigger than
1
for
\ell\leqslant k-2
. For
\ell=k-1
the examples coincide.

Let us compare the two constructions when
\ell=k-\ell-1
, i.e., when
k=2\ell+1
. The size of the latter one is
(1+o(1)){n\choose\ell}{k+\ell\choose k}/{k+\ell\choose\ell}=(1+o(1)){n\choose\ell}
, while the size of the former one is
{n-\ell-1\choose\ell}
. That is, the two constructions are roughly of the same size, but the design-based construction is potentially slightly bigger, at least when complete designs exist.

The conjecture of Erdős
Erd75
, corrected by Frankl
Fra83b
, is as follows.

Conjecture 1
(Erdős

, Frankl
Fra83b
)

Assume that
n\geqslant n_{0}(k)
and
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is avoids intersection
\ell
.

•

If

then

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant{n-\ell-1\choose k-\ell-1}.

•

If

Actually, Frankl

conjectured that the second part holds without the

assumption. He proved it for

0.4.2
Proof of Theorem
27

In this section, we present the proof of Theorem
27
. In the theorem, we assume that
k
is constant. Thus, we may apply Theorem
21
and select
\mathcal{F}^{*}\subset\mathcal{F}
that is a
(k,k+1)
-homogeneous structure. In what follows, we bound the size of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, which then implies the bound on
|\mathcal{F}|
. Informally, passing to
\mathcal{F}^{*}
allows us to work with the intersection structure
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F}^{*})
instead of the family
\mathcal{F}
itself. Let us say that the
rank

r(\mathcal{M})
of
\mathcal{M}
is the size of the smallest set in
2^{[k]}
not contained in any set from
\mathcal{M}
. The next lemma contains the information we need concerning the structure of
\mathcal{M}
.

Lemma 28

We have
r(\mathcal{M})\leqslant\max\{\ell,k-\ell-1\}
.

Given this lemma, the proof of Theorem
27
is easy to complete. Indeed, recall that
\mathcal{M}=\pi(\mathcal{F}^{*}|_{F})
for any set
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
. By Lemma
28
, the set
F
contains a subset
X(F)
of size
m:=r(\mathcal{M})\leqslant\max\{\ell,k-\ell-1\}
that is not contained in any other
B\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
. (We call it an
own subset
.) Otherwise,
B\cap F\supset X(F)
, which contradicts the definition of the rank. Therefore, there is an injection from
\mathcal{F}^{*}
to the sets of size
m
, and thus
|\mathcal{F}^{*}|\leqslant{n\choose m}
. Consequently,
|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant c(k,k+1)^{-1}{n\choose m}\leqslant c(k,k+1)^{-1}n^{m}
. We are left to prove Lemma
28
. The proof of the lemma relies on a result of Frankl and Katona
FrKa
on ‘sets with many intersection conditions’. As Frankl and Katona write in their paper, this problem arose in the context of a problem on database systems.

Theorem 29
(Frankl and Katona
FrKa
)

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be not necessarily distinct subsets of
[n]
and assume that
|\cap_{j=1}^{t}A_{i_{j}}|\neq t-1
for any distinct
i_{1},\ldots,i_{t}
. Then
m\leqslant n
.

At the first glance, the theorem has an unusual condition for an extremal set theoretic question. But, actually, it is kind of a Hall’s condition in disguise. We shall comment on this in the proof.

The theorem is sharp: the bound is attained on
A_{1}=\ldots=A_{m}=[n]
, in which case all intersections are larger than the right hand side of the condition. This is, however, not the only possibility. In
FF1
Frankl and Füredi discuss well-intersection designs: other families that could attain equality in Theorem
29
.

Proof

It is convenient for us to think of a (containment) bipartite graph
G
between sets
A_{i}
on one side and elements of the ground set
[n]
on the other side. For an element
x\in[n]
, let us denote its neighborhood in
G
by
N(x)=\{i:x\in A_{i}\}
and its degree by
d(x)=|N(x)|
. We essentially want to show is there is a matching covering the set part. In order to do that, we may try to verify Hall’s conditions. The condition in the theorem for
t=1
gives that all sets are non-empty. For
t=2
, it prevents two sets to intersect in exactly
1
element, which excludes the only possible violation of Hall’s condition for
2
sets: that both sets consist of the same singleton. Continuing further this way, however, becomes complicated.

It is convenient to prove the theorem by induction on
m
. For any
m^{\prime}<m
, we can apply inductive assumption to any subcollection of
m^{\prime}
sets, and it then implies that the union of any
m^{\prime}
sets
A_{i}
has size at least
m^{\prime}
. That is, Hall’s conditions for all proper subsets of
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\}
are verified. Arguing indirectly, assume that
m>n
. By induction for
m^{\prime}=m-1
, the union of the first
m-1
sets has size at least
m-1
, but it is at most
n
, and thus
m=n+1
. Then there is a matching covering the element side in
G
and, say
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
. We may w.l.o.g. assume that
i
is matched to
A_{i}
.

Below we show that for each
A_{i}
and
x\in A_{i}
we have
d(x)\leqslant|A_{i}|
. This concludes the proof, since, double counting the edges of
G
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|=\sum_{x=1}^{n}d(x)
, but
d(i)\leqslant|A_{i}|
for all
i\in[n]
. It implies that
|A_{m}|=0
, which is impossible.

In order to prove
d(x)\leqslant|A_{i}|
, we again apply induction, but this time we change the sets to essentially their intersections with
A_{i}
. Take distinct
j_{1},\ldots,j_{t}\in N(x)\setminus\{i\}
and observe that

\Big|A_{i}\cap\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{t}(A_{i}\cap A_{j_{\ell}})\Big|\neq t

by assumption. Removing
A_{i}
from the list and
x
from the sets, we get

\Big|\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{t}(A_{i}\cap A_{j_{\ell}}\setminus\{x\})\Big|\neq t-1.

We see that the sets
A_{i}\cap A_{j}\setminus\{x\}
for
j\in N(x)\setminus\{i\}
satisfy the condition of the theorem. Now apply inductive hypothesis to these sets and get that
|N(x)\setminus\{i\}|\leqslant|A_{i}\setminus\{x\}|
, which is equivalent to
d(x)\leqslant|A_{i}|
.

The proof we presented is essentially the proof of Frankl and Katona. Frankl and Füredi
FF1
give a different proof.

Corollary 30

Let
r\geqslant 1
be an integer and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
be not necessarily distinct subsets of
[n]
. Assume that
|\cap_{j=1}^{t}A_{i_{j}}|\neq t-1-r
for any distinct
i_{1},\ldots,i_{t}
. Then
m\leqslant n+r
.

The corollary is again tight: again, take all
A_{i}
to be equal to
[n]
. Interestingly, in contrast to
r=0
case, for
r\geqslant 1
this is the only extremal example. We shall need this fact later.

Proof

We modify the sets: consider
(\cap_{i=1}^{r}A_{i})\cap A_{j}
for
j=r+1,\ldots,m
. These sets satisfy the assumption of Theorem

0.5
Exact solution of some Turán-type problems

The next joint paper of Frankl and Füredi
FF2

\Delta
-system method. From the
\Delta
-system method point of view, it is in a sense harvesting the fruits of their previous work
FF1

\Delta
-system method, it contains many influential ideas that affected the course of a big part of extremal set theory. In the paper, they solve (for

) several hard and general extremal set theoretic (or Turán-type, as the name of the paper suggests) problems. In this section, we go over some of the contributions of the paper that are related to the
\Delta
-system method.

0.5.1
\Delta
-systems with a fixed kernel

The following question was asked by Duke and Erdős

of the family
\mathcal{F}\subset{[n]\choose k}
that does not contain a
\Delta(s+1)
-system with kernel of size
\ell

this is the forbidden one intersection problem that we discussed before, and for
\ell=0
this is the famous Erdős Matching Conjecture

(see
FK21

f(n,k,\ell,s)=O_{k,s}(f(n,k,\ell,1))

An observation of Füredi from
Fu1
is that Theorem
21
immediately implies that. Take any family
\mathcal{F}
that does not contain a
\Delta(s+1)
-system with kernel of size
\ell
and select a
(k,s+1)
-homogeneous system
\mathcal{F}^{*}
in
\mathcal{F}
. If
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=\ell

\Delta(s+1)
-system with kernel

That is, in view of Theorem

(Frankl and Füredi

The second example is as follows: Take a partial Steiner system of

The following definition generalizes the notion of a triangle in a graph.

0.6
Stability, structure, and supersaturation via Delta-systems

0.6.1
Stability

A powerful and very popular method of proving results in extremal combinatorics is via stability results. In a large sense, it can be any ‘bootstrapping’ strategy. We start by discerning some structure in all large/nearly-optimal examples for a particular problem. This structure, sometimes together with more restrictive assumptions on how close is the size of the structure to extremal, allows us to discern even more structure. We may repeat this process several times until eventually getting the exact extremal object. For results in extremal set theory, typically, there are at most
3
steps in such proof strategy: (i) a coarse structural result, which in some way allows to compactly describe the structure of most of the object; (ii) a
99\%
-stability result, which states that almost all nearly-extremal families fall inside the expected extremal example; (iii) proof of the exact result.

Stability approach has rich history, going back to works of Erdős and Simonovits (see
Simo
). Their method is discussed in the survey of Keevash
Kee2
. For the problems discussed in this survey, stability was pushed forward, extensively used and popularized in the works of Mubayi and coauthors, see
MV
. In these works, stability results are made into a separate statement, which since then became a common practice. Stability results for extremal questions in a different parameter regime were also developed in the Boolean Analysis perspective in the works of Keller, Lifshitz and coauthors (see
KL
,
EKL
) and by Zakharov and the author
KuZa
. (See Section
0.8
for a brief discussion of the last two methods.) In this survey, I wanted to point out that stability results are a natural part of the Delta-system method and, as such, stability method was used from the early days of the Delta-system method.

The earlier variant of the Delta-system method decomposition (such as, say, in Theorem
10
) by its nature gives a low-uniformity family that contains most of the family. I.e., if the
(n,k,L)
-family in Theorem
10
in the Delta-system decomposition does not have a set of uniformity
\ell_{1}
, then it has size
O_{k}(n^{|L|-1})
. Next, all but
O_{k}(n^{|L|-2})
sets of the family are covered by sets of sizes

etc. This opens up possibilities for a series of coarse structural results. We also refer the reader to Section
0.7
, where we touch upon decompositions as in the early variant of the Delta-system method, as well as to the paper of Kostochka and Mubayi
KM2

I wanted to focus on post-Theorem

0.6.2
A more quantitative view, structure, and supersaturation

In a series of papers

FJ15

Approaches to constructing bases

We have given a description of the ‘original’ sunflower base construction in Lemma

•

\mathcal{B}
contains the family
\mathcal{F}

•

\mathcal{B}

The bounds on

-system. The proof is what we could call a ‘bottom to top construction, top to bottom argument’: when constructing the base family, we start from finding smaller kernels and then go to larger kernels. When seeking for contradiction, to the contrary, we start with a larger
\Delta
-system with bigger kernels and get a smaller
\Delta
-system with a smaller kernel.

A more efficient approach is to run a ‘top to bottom construction, bottom to top argument’. Let us describe a possible procedure. Start by including in
\mathcal{B}
the sets from the original family. Then, we simplify

as follows: if some sets in the current family form a
\Delta(k+1)
-system, then replace them by the kernel of the
\Delta
-system. We perform this exchange as long as it is possible. Clearly, the resulting family has no

-systems (a much better restriction than no

0.9
Survey of future works

0.9.1

0.9.2

Let

The problem of determining

In the paper

-system is very rich. Namely, he showed that any rational number

. He used Delta-systems to show the upper bound. Continuing the work of Frankl

We have discussed several reductions between

with different parameters throughout this survey. As we have seen in the proof of Theorem

m(n,k,\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}\})\sim m(n,k-\ell_{1},\{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}-\ell_{1}\}).

Another reduction is given in Theorem

Frankl and Wilson

Theorem 62

Fix

-system method with the linear-algebraic method. We recite it here.

Theorem 63

Fix

Proof

Pass from

Arguing indirectly, assume that the intersection structure

0.9.4
Simplices, clusters

As we have discussed in Section
0.5.3
, Erdős

Actually, there are two very different types of simplices that the aforementioned works dealt with. In Section

, we have discussed the very structured

The second type of simplices that was considered are much less structured and are called

. Let us first define clusters. A
d

is a collection of

distinct sets in

with empty common intersection and with union of size at most

-cluster is just two disjoint sets, and thus

-cluster-free families are the same as intersecting families. As another possible generalization of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, Katona conjectured that the largest

-cluster-free family in
{[n]\choose k}

(see

). Frankl and Füredi

proved Katona’s conjecture for

.
Mubayi

proved the conjecture for

{[n]\choose k}

for all

, and conjectured that the same should hold for any
d\geqslant 4
. In a follow-up paper, he proved the asymptotic upper bound
(1+o(1)){n-1\choose k-1},
as well as resolved the case

and

applies to
(d+1)
-cluster-free sets as well. Then Mubayi and Ramadurai

and

large enough. The case

(where the union condition in the definition of a cluster is automatically fulfilled) was resolved by Frankl
Fra76
. Finally, Currier

made impressive progress and proved Mubayi’s conjecture for the whole range of parameters:
d\leqslant k
and
n\geqslant dk/(d-1)
.

His work on Chvátal’s conjecture builds upon this work.
The latter condition is needed so that there are

For

-cluster-free family

must have size at most

. First, note that for any

there must be a subset
X(F)

that is not contained in any other set

. Otherwise, if

and

and
|F\cup F_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F_{k}|\leqslant 2k

(A_{F},B_{F}):=(X(F),[n]\setminus F)
,

A_{F}\cap B_{F^{\prime}}=\emptyset

|\mathcal{F}|\leqslant{|A_{F}|+|B_{F}|\choose A_{F}}={n-1\choose k-1}
. We should also note that if in the definition of a

-partite hypergraph has no such substructure. Frankl and Füredi

,
n>n_{0}
. A related notion for a

Merging the two definitions, that of a

-simplex-cluster. Keevash and Mubayi made, quoting, ‘ambitious conjecture’ that for

avoiding a

Let us now discuss the variants of these questions that arose in the literature while studying simplices and clusters. A triangle, and, more generally, a
d
-simplex are the simplest examples of non-trivial intersecting families, that is, intersecting families with covering number
2

MV

n\geqslant(d+1)k/d

, provided

(\vec{a},p)
-
\Delta
-system. If the dimension of
\vec{a}
is
d

(\vec{a},p)
-

-system
is any family of the following type: start with a partition of a host set

into
d

. Let
B_{i}:=F_{0}\setminus A_{i}
. Now extend each
B_{i}
into
b_{i}>0
pairwise disjoint

-sets, so that, first, all extensions of all
B_{i}
’s are pairwise disjoint and, second,
\sum b_{i}=p
. This construction actually generalized the construction of Füredi and Özkahya

d

, in which case each

has a unique extension

, as well as they do not have a common intersection, so they indeed form a

Keevash and Mubayi

0.9.5
Hypertrees, expanded hypergraphs

In Section
0.5.2
we discussed a general result of Frankl and Füredi on expanded hypergraphs, as well as introduced an important notion of a cross-cut. Let us give a variant of the definition of an expanded hypergraph.

Definition 64
(
t
-contractible hypergraphs)

For a positive integer
t\leqslant k-1
, a
k
-uniform family
\mathcal{H}
is
t
-contractible if each set of
\mathcal{H}
contains
t

1

t

\mathcal{H}
is the
(k-t)
-uniform multi-family obtained by deleting
t

1

\mathcal{H}

The notion of

-contractible hypergraphs is slightly is more general than that of the
k
-expanded hypergraphs, see Definition

The following general result was proved by Füredi and Jiang

using
\Delta
-systems.

Theorem 65
(Jiang and Füredi,
FJ15
)

Let

be an integer. For any

-graph
\mathcal{H}
that is a subgraph of a

(We remind the reader that

-crosscut minus

This theorem extended several previous results on the subject, which we discuss below. First, we should note that for

A
