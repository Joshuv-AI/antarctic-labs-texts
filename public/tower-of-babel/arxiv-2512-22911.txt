Covering in Hamming and Grassmann Spaces: New Bounds and Reed–Solomon-Based Constructions
Samin Riasat, Hessam Mahdavifar

Abstract
We study covering problems in Hamming and Grassmann spaces through a unified coding-theoretic and information-theoretic framework. Viewing covering as a form of quantization in general metric spaces, we introduce the notion of the average covering radius as a natural measure of average distortion, complementing the classical worst-case covering radius. By leveraging tools from one-shot rate-distortion theory, we derive explicit non-asymptotic random-coding bounds on the average covering radius in both spaces, which serve as fundamental performance benchmarks. On the construction side, we develop efficient puncturing-based covering algorithms for generalized Reed–Solomon (GRS) codes in the Hamming space and extend them to a new family of subspace codes, termed character-Reed–Solomon (CRS) codes, for Grassmannian quantization under the chordal distance. Our results reveal that, despite poor worst-case covering guarantees, these structured codes exhibit strong average covering performance. In particular, numerical results in the Hamming space demonstrate that RS-based constructions often outperform random codebooks in terms of average covering radius. In the one-dimensional Grassmann space, we numerically show that CRS codes over prime fields asymptotically achieve average covering radii within a constant factor of the random-coding bound in the high-rate regime. Together, these results provide new insights into the role of algebraic structure in covering problems and high-dimensional quantization.



I
Introduction

Covering problems lie at the heart of several questions in coding theory, information theory, and signal processing, where one seeks structured families of objects that approximate the ambient space with minimal distortion.
They are mathematically appealing in their own right, but have also found a wide range of technical applications in data compression, signal processing, clustering, sampling, and robust system design, especially in high-dimensional spaces, to name a few
[
35
,
36
]
. In discrete settings, such as the Hamming space, the covering radius of a code captures its ability to represent arbitrary vectors, while in geometric settings like the Grassmann space, analogous notions arise through metrics such as the chordal distance on subspaces. Despite their shared conceptual foundation, these two domains have typically been treated with separate analytic tools and constructions. This work develops a unified perspective on covering in Hamming and Grassmann spaces, focusing on both the algorithmic task of finding nearby codewords and the quantitative task of understanding the distortion inherent to different code families.

The notion of subspace codes and their application in non-coherent communication was first developed in the seminal work of Koetter and Kschischang
[
17
]
in the context of randomized network coding. Specifically, they developed a coding theory framework for subspace coding over finite fields. Inspired by this, a parallel framework in the field of real/complex numbers was later developed by Soleymani and Mahdavifar
[
34
]
. They introduced a new algebraic construction for one-dimensional complex subspace codes called
character-polynomial (CP) codes
and later extended it to higher dimensions
[
33
]
.

In the Hamming space, we study the covering problem through the lens of Reed–Solomon (RS) codes
[
25
]
. This well-known family of codes is not only theoretically rich but also widely used in practice, ranging from hard-disk drives to distributed storage and computing.
Although various properties of RS codes have been studied extensively in the literature, they continue to inspire the development of new codes to this day. This includes the aforementioned CP codes
[
34
]
, which are essentially the concatenation of a subcode of
generalized
Reed–Solomon (GRS) code, where a certain subset of the coefficients of the message polynomial is set to zeros, with a character function mapping the codeword coordinates to the complex unit circle. In a recent work, Gooty et al.
[
13
]
used CP codes to construct new precoding designs for multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems with limited feedback, an important problem in wireless communications.

I-A

Problem Motivation

Covering can be viewed as a form of quantization in a general metric space, which is central to problems involving the approximation or representation of continuous (or large discrete) spaces using structured, finite subsets. In a canonical covering problem, one seeks to cover a metric space with the minimum number of spheres of a prescribed radius, referred to as the covering radius. This guarantees that any point in the space can be approximated by mapping it to the center of a nearby sphere with the covering radius.

A natural approach to the covering problem is to leverage well-established code families in the underlying metric space that possess strong minimum-distance properties, while shifting attention to the algorithmic challenge of quantization. Specifically, the goal is to design efficient procedures that map an arbitrary element of the space to a nearby codeword, without requiring optimal (nearest-neighbor) decoding. In the Hamming space, GRS codes are known to meet the redundancy bound
[
16
, Corollary 11.1.3]
, implying that their worst-case covering radius is large and therefore unattractive from a classical covering perspective. To the best of our knowledge, this remains the only general covering result available for RS codes.

Despite this unfavorable worst-case guarantee, we show in this paper that the average covering radius of GRS codes is significantly smaller than the redundancy bound. This observation highlights a fundamental gap between worst-case and typical covering behavior and provides strong motivation for developing
efficient covering algorithms
for RS-based constructions. Moreover, since subspace codes can be obtained by mapping RS codes to the complex domain, these insights naturally extend to covering problems in Grassmann spaces, with direct relevance to applications such as limited-feedback wireless communication systems.

Despite the extensive early literature and the recent renewed interest in determining the covering radius of specific code families, the design and analysis of

I-C

Our Contribution

In this paper, we develop a unified algorithmic and information-theoretic framework for covering in both Hamming and Grassmann spaces. On the algorithmic side, we propose an efficient puncturing-based covering algorithm for GRS codes. The algorithm operates by successively puncturing the code and invoking an off-the-shelf decoder on the resulting shortened codes, until a nearby codeword is identified. We show that this procedure efficiently, i.e., with polynomial-time complexity, finds, for any input vector, a codeword within the covering radius of the input vector. We analyze the average-case performance of the proposed algorithm by characterizing the average number of punctures required for success and by estimating the resulting average covering radius, defined as the expected distance between the input vector and the selected codeword. In the Hamming space, we derive theoretical bounds on the average number of punctures and partially resolve a conjecture posed in the preliminary version of this work
[
27
, Conjecture 1]
. These results demonstrate that, despite their poor worst-case covering guarantees, GRS codes exhibit favorable average covering behavior.

To extend these ideas to subspace covering in the complex domain, we introduce character-Reed–Solomon (CRS) codes, which generalize CP codes by removing structural constraints tailored to packing. We show how the proposed puncturing-based algorithm can be adapted to CRS codes, yielding an efficient covering method for one-dimensional Grassmannian spaces over complex numbers under the chordal distance.

From an information-theoretic perspective, we formalize the notion of average covering radius as a natural measure of average distortion when a code is used for quantization. Building on one-shot rate-distortion results of Elkayam and Feder
[
10
]
, we derive explicit non-asymptotic random-coding bounds on the average covering radius in both Hamming and Grassmann spaces. A numerical analysis of CRS codes over prime fields suggests that, at extreme high rates, these algebraic constructions asymptotically achieve average covering radii within a constant factor of the random-coding bound in the one-dimensional Grassmann space. Moreover, numerical results in the Hamming space demonstrate that RS-based codes frequently outperform random codebooks with respect to the average covering radius.

Finally, we conclude the paper in

III
Covering Radius as a Measure of Distortion

III-A

Average Covering Radius

The
(Hamming) covering radius
of a block code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
is defined as follows
[
16
]

\displaystyle\rho_{{\rm H}}({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle:=\max_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}}\min_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}}d_{{\rm H}}(y,c),

(6)

where
d_{{\rm H}}
denotes Hamming distance.
For instance, it is well known that
\rho_{{\rm H}}(\GRS)=d-1
.
This means, in particular, that any
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
is at distance at most
d-1
from a
\GRS
codeword.

Given a vector
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
and a code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, the
covering problem
asks to find a codeword
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
d_{{\rm H}}(y,c)\leq\rho_{{\rm H}}({\mathcal{C}})
.
An algorithm that solves the covering problem for
{\mathcal{C}}
is called a
covering algorithm
for
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Generally speaking, the covering radius of a code represents the maximum error when the code is used for the quantization of its ambient space. This is because the covering radius characterizes the worst-case scenario of the quantization process. In practice, however, given a certain distribution over the space, the average quantization error becomes more relevant. This motivates us to modify (
6
) to the following definition.

Definition 4

(Average Covering Radius)
.

For a block code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
and a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, the
average (Hamming) covering radius
of
{\mathcal{C}}
over
{\mathcal{D}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{{\rm H}}({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle:=\expec_{X\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\min_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}}d_{{\rm H}}(X,c)\right].

(7)

Similarly, for a subspace code
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq G_{m,n}({\mathbb{C}})
and a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
G_{m,n}({\mathbb{C}})
, the
average (chordal) covering radius
of
{\mathcal{C}}
over
{\mathcal{D}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{{\rm ch}}({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle:=\expec_{U\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\min_{V\in{\mathcal{C}}}d_{{\rm ch}}(U,V)\right].

(8)

Note that
Definition 4
can be generalized naturally to codes over other metric spaces, e.g., rank-metric codes. Henceforth,
covering radius
will be understood to mean
Hamming covering radius
for the Hamming space, and
chordal covering radius
for the Grassmann space.

To obtain the analog of
Theorem 1
for the Grassmann space
{\mathcal{Y}}=G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})
,
we first introduce the following notation.
For
V=\langle v\rangle\in{\mathcal{Y}}
with
\left\|{v}\right\|=1
, the orthogonal projection operator onto
V
is denoted by
P_{V}:=vv^{\dagger}
.
Some key observations are:

•

For any
x,y\in G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})
,

\displaystyle d_{{\rm ch}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\tr(P_{x}P_{y})}.

(22)

•

p_{x,y,U}=\Pr[d_{{\rm ch}}(x,Y)<d_{{\rm ch}}(x,y)]
for any continuous distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
by (
12
), which is independent of
U
, and

•

\tr(P_{x}P_{Y}){}=\tr(P_{Qx}P_{QY})=|\hat{Y}_{1}|^{2}\sim{\rm Beta}(1,n-1)
by unitary invariance, for any fixed
x:=\langle\hat{x}\rangle\in{\mathcal{Y}}
,
Y
Haar-uniform on
{\mathcal{Y}}
, and unitary
Q
with
Q\hat{x}=\hat{e}_{1}
,
so that the distributions of
Y
and
QY:=\langle\hat{Y}\rangle
are equal
[
18
]
.

We then have the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
denote the uniform distribution on the Grassmann space
{\mathcal{Y}}=G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Then, the expected average covering radius of a random code
{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
consisting of
M
codewords drawn independently from
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
is given by:

\expec_{{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}[\overline{\rho}_{{\rm ch}}({\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}})]=\\
\frac{2(n-1)}{2n-1}M(M-1)\betaf\left(1+\frac{2n-1}{2(n-1)},M-1\right),

(23)

where
\betaf(\cdot,\cdot)
is the beta function.

Proof.

For
(X,Y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\times{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}
is any distribution on
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{Y}}
, let
Z:=\tr(P_{X}P_{Y}){}
.
Then, for any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, we have
Z\mid X=x\sim{\rm Beta}(1,n-1)
,
so that
Z\sim{\rm Beta}(1,n-1)
. Therefore,
by (
22
),

\displaystyle p_{x,y,U}

\displaystyle=\Pr[d_{{\rm ch}}(X,Y)<t]

\displaystyle=\Pr[Z>1-t^{2}]

\displaystyle=t^{2(n-1)},

(24)

where
t=d_{{\rm ch}}(x,y)
.
Letting
T:=d_{{\rm ch}}(X,Y){}
therefore gives:

\displaystyle\tilde{D}(w,{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w}\expec[d_{{\rm ch}}(X,Y)\cdot{\bf 1}_{\{p_{X,Y,U}\leq w\}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w}\expec[T\cdot{\bf 1}_{\{T^{2(n-1)}\leq w\}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w}\int_{0}^{w^{\frac{1}{2(n-1)}}}tf_{T}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-1)}{w}\int_{0}^{w^{\frac{1}{2(n-1)}}}t^{2n-2}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-1)}{2n-1}w^{\frac{1}{2(n-1)}},

(25)

which follows using (
24
).
Thus, by (
10
),

\displaystyle\expec_{{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}[\overline{\rho}_{{\rm ch}}({\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}})]=\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{D}(w,{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}})G^{\prime}(w)dw

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-1)}{2n-1}M(M-1)\int_{0}^{1}w^{1+\frac{1}{2(n-1)}}(1-w)^{M-2}dw

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-1)}{2n-1}M(M-1)\betaf\left(1+\frac{2n-1}{2(n-1)},M-1\right),

which follows using (
13
) and (

), as desired.
∎

It is important to note that the conclusions of
Theorem 1
and

do not depend on the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}
over which the average covering radius of the random code
{\mathcal{C}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
is defined.

IV
Covering Using GRS Codes

Decoding algorithms have been the subject of study for many decades, and several decoding algorithms for GRS codes are known
[
16
,
15
]
.
In contrast, to the best of our knowledge, no explicit covering algorithms are known for GRS codes.
Note that covering involves solving a more intricate minimax problem, which is computationally harder due to the need to analyze the worst-case scenario over the entire space.
And, although one might use decoding algorithms for covering, their performance is bound to be subpar as spheres of radius
\tau<d/2
do not cover the space well.
Therefore, as the first algorithm of its kind to the best of our knowledge, we propose
Algorithm 1
and analyze its performance in this section.

IV-A

Algorithm

Given an
[n,k,d]
GRS code
{\mathcal{C}}
and an input vector
y
in its ambient space, the algorithm first tries to decode
y
. If the decoder is successful, then we have found the closest codeword to
y
. If not, it repeatedly (up to
d-1
times) punctures the code at the last coordinate and tries to decode the corresponding substring of
y
until the decoder is successful,
when it finally returns the re-encoding of the decoder output in the original code.
Note that in an actual implementation of the algorithm, it may be desirable to have the message polynomial returned directly instead of its re-encoding, as we will see in
Section V-B
.

Algorithm 1

\GRScover({\mathcal{C}},y)

1:

[n,k,d]_{q}

\GRS
code
{\mathcal{C}}
, arbitrary vector
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}

2:

A codeword
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
d_{{\rm H}}(y,c)\leq d-1

Available Functions:

\GRSdecode
: a
\GRS
decoder

3:

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\leftarrow{\mathcal{C}}

4:

for

i=1,\dots,d-1

do

5:

f\leftarrow\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}},y)

6:

if

f
is not null
then

7:

return

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(f)

8:

else

9:

puncture
{\mathcal{C}}
at coordinate
n-i+1

10:

y\leftarrow y[1..n-i]

11:

end

if

12:

end

for

Theorem 3
below proves the correctness of
Algorithm 1
.

Theorem 3
.

Given an
[n,k,d]_{q}
GRS code
{\mathcal{C}}
and
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
,
\GRScover({\mathcal{C}},y)
returns a codeword
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
d_{{\rm H}}(y,c)\leq d-1
.

Proof.

Puncturing an
[n,k]
GRS code at any coordinate gives an
[n-1,k]
GRS code.
Denote by
y^{(i)}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
the values of
y
and
{\mathcal{C}}
on lines
10
and
9
, respectively, at step
i
. Plainly,
for any
i
,

\displaystyle d_{{\rm H}}(y^{(i+1)},{\mathcal{C}}_{i+1}(f))

\displaystyle\leq d_{{\rm H}}(y^{(i)},{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(f)).

(26)

If
\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}_{i},y^{(i)})
is successful, then
by repeated application of (
26
),

\displaystyle d_{{\rm H}}(y^{(i)},{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(f))\leq d_{{\rm H}}(y,{\mathcal{C}}(f))\leq d-1.

It remains to show that
\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}_{i},y^{(i)})
does indeed succeed for some
i<d
.
To see this, note that,
for each
i
,

\displaystyle d_{\min}({\mathcal{C}}_{i+1})=d_{\min}({\mathcal{C}}_{i})-1.

(27)

Hence,
\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}_{i},y^{(i)})
will succeed in at most
d-1
steps, since
d_{\min}({\mathcal{C}}_{i})=1
when
i=d-1
, at which point any
y^{(i)}
becomes a valid codeword.
∎

We emphasize that
Theorem 3
does not claim that
Algorithm 1
returns the closest codeword to the input vector, but rather guarantees returning of a codeword within the covering radius of the code (there might be multiple codewords within the covering radius of the input vector). This distinction is essential: finding the closest codeword (i.e., solving the minimum-distance decoding problem) is NP-hard in general, whereas the proposed covering algorithm achieves its guarantee with polynomial-time complexity. A detailed analysis of the complexity of
Algorithm 1
will be provided later in this section.

IV-B

Improvement Using List Decoding

Consider the following modification of
Algorithm 1
, where
\GRSdecode
is the Guruswami–Sudan list decoder (GS)
[
15
]
and returns the best candidate in the list (i.e., the codeword with the shortest Hamming distance from
y
).
There are several advantages of doing this.
First, it was shown by McEliece
[
21
,
22
]
that GS almost always returns a list of size
1
, whereby implying that it is essentially a unique decoder.
Next, the decoding radius of GS is given by:

\displaystyle\tau_{\GS}

\displaystyle:=n-1-\lfloor\sqrt{(k-1)n}\rfloor.

(28)

This implies, in particular, that
Algorithm 1
will now return a
\GRS
codeword
c
within distance
\tau_{\GS}
of
y
, i.e., with
d_{{\rm H}}(y,c)\leq\tau_{\GS}
, if such a codeword exists
[
22
]
.
This potentially reduces the average number of punctures, thereby improving the average covering radius.

IV-C

Complexity Analysis

Plainly, the worst-case running time of
Algorithm 1
is
(n-k)\cdot T(n)
, where
T(n)
is the running time of
\GRSdecode
.

•

For unique decoding, algorithms like Berlekamp–Welch (BW) are known with
T(n)=O(n^{3})

[
14
, Theorem 15.1.4]
as well as more efficient ones with
T(n)=O(n^{2})
and
T(n)=O(n\polylog(n))

[
14
, § 15.4]
.

•

For list decoding, there are known implementations of
\GS
with
T(n)=O(s^{4}n^{2})

[
21
, § VII]
,
[
21
, § VII]
,
[
11
]
,
[
28
, § V]
,
where
s>0
is an integer parameter known as the
interpolation multiplicity
.

IV-D

Average Number of Punctures

When running simulations, we are generally more interested in the average-case running time of an algorithm rather than the worst-case. Note that the average-case running time of
Algorithm 1
depends on the average number of punctures needed for
\GRSdecode
to succeed. Based on the data in
Table I
, we made the following conjecture in an earlier version of this work
[
27
]
.

Conjecture 1
.

For an
[n,k,d]_{q}
GRS code, the average number of punctures needed for
Algorithm 1
to succeed in returning a codeword within the covering radius is less than

•

c_{1}(d-1)
for
\GRSdecode
a unique decoder, and

•

c_{2}
for
\GRSdecode
the
\GS
list decoder,

for some positive constants
c_{1}<1
and
c_{2}
.

However, it turns out that the correct behavior of
Algorithm 1
is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
\GRSdecode
in
Algorithm 1
is a list decoder that returns all codewords within its decoding radius. Then, uniformly averaged on all inputs
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, the number
N_{{\rm punc}}
of punctures needed for
\GRScover({\mathcal{C}},y)
to succeed for an
[n,k,d]_{q}
GRS code
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies the following inequalities:

d-1-\frac{1}{q^{d-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}q^{i}\Vol_{q}(\tau_{i},n-i)\leq\overline{N}_{{\rm punc}}\\
\leq d-1-\frac{1}{q^{d-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\frac{q^{i}\Vol_{q}(\tau_{i},n-i)}{L_{i}},

(29)

where

\tau_{i}
and
L_{i}
are respectively the decoding radius and the maximum list size of
\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}_{i},\cdot)
.

Proof.

Using the notation from the proof of
Theorem 3
, let

\displaystyle S_{i}

\displaystyle:=\{(y,c)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}\times{\mathcal{C}}_{i}:d_{{\rm H}}(y^{(i)},c)\leq\tau_{i}\},

(30)

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(y)

\displaystyle:=\#\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}:d_{{\rm H}}(y^{(i)},c)\leq\tau_{i}\}.

(31)

In other words,
S_{i}
is the set of all pairs
(y,c)
such that the output list of
\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}_{i},y^{(i)})
contains a polynomial
f
with
c={\mathcal{C}}_{i}(f)
, and
\ell_{i}(y)
is the size of the list.
Then, the number of vectors
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
for which
Algorithm 1
succeeds within
i
punctures is given by:

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle:=\#\{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}:\ell_{i}(y)\geq 1\}.

(32)

In particular, the average number of punctures
\overline{N}_{{\rm punc}}
needed for
Algorithm 1
to succeed is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}i(V_{i}-V_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\left((d-1)V_{d-1}-\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}V_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=d-1-\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}V_{i},

(33)

since
V_{d-1}=q^{n}
.
Now, by (
30
) and (
31
),

\displaystyle|S_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}}\ell_{i}(y)=\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}q^{i}|B(c,\tau_{i})|

\displaystyle=q^{k+i}\Vol_{q}(\tau_{i},n-i).

(34)

Since
L_{i}
is the maximum list size of
\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}_{i},\cdot)
,

\displaystyle 1_{\{\ell_{i}(y)\geq 1\}}

\displaystyle\leq\ell_{i}(y)\leq 1_{\{\ell_{i}(y)\geq 1\}}L_{i}.

(35)

Summing (
35
) over
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
using (
32
) and (
34
)
gives

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle\leq|S_{i}|\leq V_{i}L_{i},

(36)

i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{|S_{i}|}{L_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq V_{i}\leq|S_{i}|.

(37)

Finally, (
29
) follows from (
IV-D
), (
34
) and (
37
).
∎

If
\GRSdecode
is a unique decoder, we can substitute

for all

into

and obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that
\GRSdecode
in
Algorithm 1
is a unique decoder with maximum decoding radius. Then, averaged over all inputs
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, the number
N_{{\rm punc}}
of punctures needed for
\GRScover({\mathcal{C}},y)
to succeed for an
[n,k,d]_{q}
GRS code
{\mathcal{C}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\overline{N}_{{\rm punc}}

\displaystyle=d-1-\frac{1}{q^{d-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}q^{i}\Vol_{q}(\tfrac{n-k-i}{2},n-i).

(38)

In particular,

\displaystyle(d-1)\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{N}_{{\rm punc}}\leq d-1,

(39)

i.e.,
\overline{N}_{{\rm punc}}/(d-1)\to 1
as

.

Proof.

Taking
\tau_{i}=(n-k-i)/2
and
L_{i}=1
for all

in (

) gives (

), and in (

) gives,
by (

),

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=|S_{i}|=q^{k+i}\Vol_{q}(\tau_{i},n-i).

(40)

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}V_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(d-1)V_{d-2}}{q^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(d-1)q^{k+d-2}\Vol_{q}(\tau_{d-2},n-d+2)}{q^{n}}

(41)

since the

IV-E

Coverage Fraction

Next, we provide a lower bound on the fraction of space covered by Hamming spheres of a given radius centered at the codewords of an MDS code in terms of its weight and intersection distribution.
The key observation is that one can explicitly compute the sizes of the Hamming spheres and their pairwise intersections, as
Theorem 5
below demonstrates.

Theorem 5
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
MDS code. Then, the fraction of the space
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
covered by Hamming spheres of radius
\tau
associated with
{\mathcal{C}}
is lower bounded as follows:

\frac{1}{q^{n}}{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\bigcup_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}}B(c,\tau)}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}\\
\geq\frac{1}{q^{d-1}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\tau}\binom{n}{i}(q-1)^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=d}^{2\tau}A_{w}I(w,\tau)\right),

(42)

where the
weight distribution

A_{w}
is given by:

\displaystyle A_{w}:=\binom{n}{w}\sum_{j=0}^{w-d}(-1)^{j}\binom{w}{j}(q^{w-d+1-j}-1),

(43)

and the
intersection distribution

I(w,\tau)
is given by:

\displaystyle I(w,\tau):=

(44)

\displaystyle\sum_{z=0}^{n-w}\binom{n-w}{z}(q-1)^{n-w-z}\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n-\tau-z\leq u,v\leq\tau\\
u+v\leq w\end{subarray}}\binom{w}{u}\binom{w-u}{v}(q-2)^{w-u-v}
.

Proof.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle,

{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\bigcup_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}}B(c,\tau)}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}\geq\sum_{c\in{\mathcal{C}}}|B(c,\tau)|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c_{1}\neq c_{2}}|B(c_{1},\tau)\cap B(c_{2},\tau)|\\
=|{\mathcal{C}}|\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\tau}\binom{n}{i}(q-1)^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=d}^{2\tau}A_{w}I(w,\tau)\right),

(45)

where
A_{w}
is the number of codewords
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
of weight
w
, and
I(w,\tau):=|B(c_{1},\tau)\cap B(c_{2},\tau)|
for
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
d_{{\rm H}}(c_{1},c_{2})=w
.
Now, (
43
) is known to hold for
d\leq w\leq n
(e.g., see
[
19
]
), and (
44
) follows by a counting argument, where
u
(resp.
v
) represents the number of indices in
\{1,\dots,n\}
where
y
and
c_{1}
(resp.
c_{2}
) agree, and
z
represents the number of indices where
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
y
agree.
Substituting
|{\mathcal{C}}|=q^{k}=q^{n-d+1}
into (
45
) gives (
42
).
∎

Note that
Theorem 5
is sharp when overlaps of three or more Hamming spheres are negligible, which is the case when
\tau
is sufficiently small, and, in particular, expected to be the case for the smallest
\tau
such that the union of Hamming spheres of radius
\tau

We observe numerically in

V
Character-Reed–Solomon Code

Recall from
Definition 3
that the message space
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(k,q)
of CP codes consists of polynomials
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)
with
f_{jp}=0
for all integers
j\geq 0
.
This condition was used to establish minimum distance properties of CP codes
[
34
]
. Since our focus in this work is on covering rather than packing, we utilize the full message space
{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)
and generalize the definition of CP codes below to what we call
character-Reed–Solomon (CRS) codes
.

Definition 5

(CRS Code)
.

Fix
k\leq n<q
, a non-trivial character
\chi_{\beta}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and distinct evaluation points
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Then, the encoding of
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)
in the CRS code
\CRS:=\CRS_{n,\beta}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))\subseteq G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})
is given by:

\displaystyle\CRS(f)

\displaystyle:=(\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{1})),\dots,\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{n}))),

(46)

where we identify
\CRS(f)
with the one-dimensional subspace
\langle\CRS(f)\rangle
.

We now derive some theoretical properties of CRS codes, including bounds on the code size.

V-A

Theoretical Properties and Bounds on Code Size

Note that

\CRS_{n,{\beta}}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
is essentially the concatenation of a RS code with the character map
\chi_{\beta}:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\to U
, and therefore can be regarded as a RS-coded modulation.
In particular,
\CRS_{n,\beta}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
prior to concatenation by
\chi_{\beta}
is simply expressed as
\RS_{n}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
using (
1
).
Hence,

\displaystyle|\CRS|

\displaystyle\leq|\RS|=|{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)|=q^{k}.

(47)

Equality holds if and only if the encoding map (
46
) is injective.
To see when this is the case, observe that
\CRS(f)=\CRS(g)
for
f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)
if and only if
\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{j}))=\chi_{\beta}(g(\alpha_{j}))
for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, which holds if and only if
\Tr(\beta h(\alpha_{j}))=0
for each
j
by (
4
),
where
h=f-g\in{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)
.
The following lemma characterizes the zeros of
\Tr
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Then,
\Tr(\alpha)=0
if and only if

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\beta^{p}-\beta,

(48)

for some
\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
If
\beta=\beta_{0}
is a solution to (
48
), then all solutions are given by
\beta_{\lambda}=\beta_{0}+\lambda
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

Proof.

Consider the map:

\displaystyle\phi:{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q},\quad\beta\mapsto\beta^{p}-\beta.

(49)

Note that
\phi
is
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-linear with
\ker(\phi)={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
by Fermat’s little theorem. Hence,
|\phi({\mathbb{F}}_{q})|=q/p
by the first isomorphism theorem. But
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\supsetneq\ker(\Tr)\supseteq\phi({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
, whence
\ker(\Tr)=\phi({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.
Thus, if
\alpha\in\ker(\Tr)
, then
\alpha=\phi(\beta_{\lambda})
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
∎

Therefore,

\beta h(\alpha_{j})=\beta_{j}^{p}-\beta_{j}

for some
\beta_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, for each
j
. Interpolating
u\in{{\mathcal{F}}(n,q)}
over
\{(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}):1\leq j\leq n\}
implies that
u^{p}-u-\beta h

vanishes on the set
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
.
Furthermore, by
Lemma 1
, there are exactly
p^{n}
such
u
, each corresponding to a realization of
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})

We thus obtain the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\CRS_{n,{\beta}}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
with evaluation points
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
.
Then,
{\mathcal{C}}(f)={\mathcal{C}}(g)
if and only if

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle(f-g)\equiv u^{p}-u{}\pmod{(X-\alpha_{1})\cdots(X-\alpha_{n})},

(50)

for some
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(n,q)
.
In particular, given the
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-linear map

\displaystyle T:{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)\to{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n},\quad f\mapsto(\Tr(\beta f(\alpha_{j})){})_{j=1}^{n},

(51)

we have

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{C}}|

\displaystyle=|\Image(T)|=p^{\rank(T)}.

(52)

Furthermore,
if there exists a
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(n,q)
satisfying (
50
), then there are exactly
p^{n}
such
u
.
Finally, if (
50
) is an equality, i.e.,

\displaystyle\beta(f-g)

\displaystyle=u^{p}-u

(53)

for some
u\in{\mathcal{F}}(k/p,q)
,
then (
53
) holds for exactly
p
such
u
. Otherwise,
\deg(u)\geq n/p
for all
u
satisfying (
50
).

Proof.

Only the claims regarding (
53
) remain to be proved.

Since
\deg(f-g)<k<n
, it follows from (
50
) that
\deg(u)\geq n/p
or
\deg(u)<k/p
, with (
50
) taking the form of an equality precisely in the latter case.

Consider the map
\phi
in (
49
) extended to
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]
. Then
\phi
is
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
-linear with
\ker(\phi)={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
Therefore, if
h\in\phi({\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X])
, i.e.,
h=u^{p}-u
for some
u\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]
, then there are exactly
p
such
u\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]
, given by
u+\lambda
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
∎

Observe that (
50
) cannot be satisfied for
f\neq g
when
k
is sufficiently small. This is made precise in the corollary below.

Corollary 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\CRS_{n,{\beta}}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
. If
k<1+np/q
, then
|{\mathcal{C}}|=q^{k}
.

Proof.

We claim that, under the given hypothesis, the map
T
given by (
51
) is injective. To see this, note that if
T(f)=0
, then the polynomial
\Tr\circ\beta f\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]
has zeros at the
n
evaluation points of
{\mathcal{C}}
. But

\displaystyle\deg(\Tr\circ\beta f)

\displaystyle=\deg(\Tr)\deg(\beta f)\leq(k-1)q/p<n,

which forces
\Tr\circ\beta f=0
, i.e.
f=0
, thereby proving the claim.
Thus,
|{\mathcal{C}}|=p^{\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}{\mathcal{F}}(k,q)}=q^{k}
by (
52
), as desired.
∎

In general,
Theorem 6
implies that
|{\mathcal{C}}|
must be equal to a power of
p
less than or equal to
p^{n}
. Combined with (
47
), this gives the following bounds.

Theorem 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\CRS_{n,{\beta}}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
.
Then
|{\mathcal{C}}|
is a power of
p
such that

\displaystyle\frac{p^{n}}{q^{n-k}}

\displaystyle\leq|{\mathcal{C}}|\leq\min\{p^{n},q^{k}\}.

(54)

Equality holds on both sides of (
54
) when
k=n
or
q=p
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be the evaluation points of
{\mathcal{C}}
,
and put
w(X)=(X-\alpha_{1})\cdots(X-\alpha_{n})
. Let
R:={\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]/(w)
and consider the map
\phi
in (
49
) extended to
R
. By the Chinese remainder theorem,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]/(X-\alpha_{i})\cong{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n},

(55)

so that
\ker(\phi)\cong{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n}
by
Lemma 1
.
Moreover, by
Theorem 6
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}(f)={\mathcal{C}}(g)

\displaystyle\iff\beta(f-g)\in S\cap\phi(R),

where
S={\mathcal{F}}(k,q)/(w)\subseteq R
. Therefore, by the isomorphism theorems,

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{C}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{|S|}{|S\cap\phi(R)|}\geq\frac{|S|}{|\phi(R)|}=\frac{q^{k}}{(q/p)^{n}},

(56)

and

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{C}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{|S+\phi(R)|}{|\phi(R)|}\leq\frac{|R|}{|\phi(R)|}=|\ker(\phi)|=p^{n}.

(57)

Thus, (
54
) follows from (
56
), (
57
) and (
47
).
Equality holds in (
56
) if and only if
\phi(R)\subseteq S
,
which is satisfied for
k=n
or
q=p
(the latter since
u(\alpha_{i})^{q}=u(\alpha_{i})
for all
i
).
Likewise, equality holds in (
57
) if and only if
S+\phi(R)=R
,
which is satisfied for sufficiently large
k
, in particular, for
k=n
.
∎

Note that if
k<n
, it is possible that neither
\phi(R)\subseteq S
nor
S+\phi(R)=R
holds. On the other hand, if
q=p
, then
\phi(R)=\{0\}
. Hence,
S+\phi(R)=R
holds if and only if
k=n
.

V-B

Covering Algorithm

As mentioned in the introduction, CP codes have recently been used for the quantization problem in MISO systems with limited feedback
[
13
]
,
and the quantization error was characterized using the covering radius of CP codes.
Moreover, a decoding algorithm for CP codes over prime fields was recently proposed
[
26
]
.
However, as discussed at the beginning of
Section IV
, decoding algorithms do not produce good coverage due to the decoding radius being much smaller than the covering radius. Therefore, we propose
Algorithm 2
below for covering lines in
G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})
using CRS codes.
To describe the idea behind the algorithm, consider the map
\psi_{\beta}
defined in (
58
),
which can be regarded as an extension of the
1
-to-
q/p
map
\chi_{\beta}^{-1}
to all of
{\mathbb{C}}
, obtained by first mapping any
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
to its closest point (in euclidean distance) in
\chi_{\beta}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
then taking its pre-image:

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}:{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\to\binom{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}{q/p}

(58)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto\chi_{\beta}^{-1}\left(\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{p}\left\lfloor\frac{p\arg(z)}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\right).

Here,
\binom{S}{t}
conventionally denotes the set of all subsets of
S
of size
t
.
The given subspace
\langle y\rangle\in G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})
is first transformed into a
\RS
codeword by applying
\psi_{\beta}
to each
y_{i}
and picking a random element in
\chi_{\beta}^{-1}(y_{i})
, which is then followed by a call to
\GRScover
on the resulting vector. Finally, the message polynomial corresponding to the output is encoded in the CRS code and returned by the algorithm.

Algorithm 2

\CRScover({\mathcal{C}},y)

1:

CRS code
{\mathcal{C}}=\CRS_{n,{\beta}}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))
, arbitrary subspace
\langle y\rangle\in G_{1,n}({\mathbb{C}})

2:

A codeword
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
d_{{\rm ch}}(\langle y\rangle,\langle c\rangle)\leq\rho_{{\rm ch}}({\mathcal{C}})

Available Functions:

\psi_{\beta}
,
\GRScover
,
\GRSdecode

3:

{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\leftarrow\RS_{n}({\mathcal{F}}(k,q))

4:

for

i=1,\dots,n

do

5:

u_{i}\leftarrow\hbox{random element in }\psi_{\beta}(y_{i})

6:

end

for

7:

c^{\prime}\leftarrow\GRScover({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime},u)

8:

f\leftarrow\GRSdecode({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime},c^{\prime})

9:

return

{\mathcal{C}}(f)

In a practical implementation of
Algorithm 2
, one may avoid
Step 8
altogether by modifying
\GRScover
in
Algorithm 1
to directly return the message polynomial in
Step 7
rather than its re-encoding. Moreover, the pre-images
u_{j}\in\psi_{\beta}(y_{j})
in
Step 5
under the character map
\chi_{\beta}
may be chosen using a “best-of-
N
” approach for some fixed
N
. In other words, out of
N
random assignments of the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to the
u_{i}
under
\psi_{\beta}
, the one giving the smallest chordal distance of the
\CRS
codeword from
y
is chosen.
This is because, in general, each point in
\chi_{\beta}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
has
q/p
distinct pre-images in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so a brute-force approach would need to test
(q/p)^{n}
assignments to find the optimal one.

V-C

Improved
Upper Bound on Average Covering Radius

Let
\Psi=\chi_{\beta}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Then
\Psi
consists of the
p
-th roots of unity. Given the input vector
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, let
\theta_{j}=\arg(y_{j})\in[0,2\pi)
and, for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, define

\displaystyle\check{y}_{j}

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{p}\left\lfloor\frac{p\theta_{j}}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor\right).

(59)

In other words,
\check{y}_{j}

is the closest point to
y_{j}

in
\Psi
(in Euclidean distance).
The following theorem improves the upper bound on the average chordal covering radius of

\CCP_{n,\beta}({\mathcal{F}}_{p}(k,p))=\CRS_{n,{\beta}}({\mathcal{F}}(k,p))
by Gooty et al.
[
13
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 8
.

Consider a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, where the amplitudes are i.i.d. with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, and are independent of the phases.
Then, over
{\mathcal{D}}
,
the average covering radius of
{\mathcal{C}}:=\CRS_{n,\beta}({\mathcal{F}}(k,p))
of rate
R:=k/n
such that

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{c+1}+\frac{(1-c)\sigma^{2}}{2(1+c)n\mu^{2}},

(60)

where
c:=\sqrt{\cos(2\pi/p)}
, is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{{\rm ch}}({\mathcal{C}})\leq\min\bigg\{\sqrt{1-\dfrac{(cR+R-1)^{2}\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}}},

(61)

\displaystyle\resizebox{21479355}{}{$\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\dfrac{cR+R-1}{c}\cdot\dfrac{(cR+R-1)^{2}\mu^{2}+(c^{2}R-R+1)\sigma^{2}/n}{\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}}}$}\bigg\}.

Proof.

The first expression inside the
\min
in (
61
) is the square root of the bound on the mean-squared quantization error given by Gooty et al.
[
13
, Theorem 1]
.
To obtain the improved bound, let
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(k,p)
denote the output of
\GRSdecode
in
Step 8
of
\CRScover({\mathcal{C}},y)
. Then,
by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|\langle y,{\mathcal{C}}(f)\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}y_{i}^{*}\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{i}))}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}-{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}y_{i}^{*}\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{i}))}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}

\displaystyle\geq{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}y_{i}^{*}\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{i}))}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{i}|,

(62)

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{A}}&=\{1\leq i\leq n:\check{y}_{i}=\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{i}))\},\\
{\mathcal{B}}&=\{1\leq i\leq n:\check{y}_{i}\neq\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{i}))\}.\end{aligned}

To estimate the first term, note that
\check{y}_{j}=e^{i\delta_{j}}y_{j}/{|y_{j}|}

for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
by (
59
),
where

\displaystyle\delta_{j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{2\pi}{p}\left\lfloor\frac{p\theta_{j}}{2\pi}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor-\theta_{j}\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{p},\frac{\pi}{p}\right),

(63)

so that
for
j\in{\mathcal{A}}
,

\displaystyle y_{j}^{*}\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{j}))

\displaystyle=y_{j}^{*}\check{y}_{j}=|y_{j}|e^{i\delta_{j}}.

(64)

Thus,
by (
63
) and (
64
),

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt{\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}y_{j}^{*}\chi_{\beta}(f(\alpha_{j}))}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}={\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggl\lvert$}}{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{j}|e^{i\delta_{j}}}\raisebox{-0.25pt}{$\biggr\rvert$}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{j}|^{2}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\in{\mathcal{A}}\\
j<\ell\end{subarray}}|y_{j}||y_{\ell}|\cos(\delta_{j}-\delta_{\ell})}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{j}|^{2}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,\ell\in{\mathcal{A}}\\
j<\ell\end{subarray}}|y_{j}||y_{\ell}|\cos(2\pi/p)}

\displaystyle\geq c\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{j}|.

Let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{y\in{\mathcal{D}}:c\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{j}|\geq\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{j}|\bigg\}.

By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr[{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle=\Pr\bigg[c\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|\geq(c+1)\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{j}|\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\expec\left[\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{j}|/\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|\right]}{c/(c+1)}.

(65)

Letting

A:=|{\mathcal{A}}|
,
B:=|{\mathcal{B}}|
,

Y:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|
,
and using the fact that the
|y_{j}|
are i.i.d., we have

\displaystyle\expec\left[\frac{\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{j}|}{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=\expec\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{|y_{j}|}{Y}{\bf 1}_{\{j\in{\mathcal{B}}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\expec\left[\frac{|y_{j}|}{Y}\right]\expec[{\bf 1}_{\{j\in{\mathcal{B}}\}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\expec[B]}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-k}{n}

\displaystyle=1-R,

(66)

which follows by linearity and independence of the amplitudes and phases.
Here we have used

\displaystyle B=n-A\leq\rho_{{\rm H}}({\mathcal{C}})=\rho_{{\rm H}}(\RS_{n}({\mathcal{F}}(k,p))=n-k,

(67)

which follows from the definition of
\rho_{{\rm H}}
(see (
6
) and its associated remarks) and the injectivity of
\chi_{\beta}:{\mathbb{F}}_{p}\to{\mathbb{C}}
.
Thus, by the law of total expectation,

\displaystyle\expec[|\langle y,{\mathcal{C}}(f)\rangle|^{2}]\geq\expec[|\langle y,{\mathcal{C}}(f)\rangle|^{2}|{\mathcal{E}}]\Pr[{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt\geq\left(1-\frac{1-R}{c/(c+1)}\right)\cdot\expec\left[\bigg(c\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{i}|-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{i}|\bigg)^{2}\bigg|{\mathcal{E}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt=\frac{cR+R-1}{c}\cdot\expec\left[\expec\left[\bigg(c\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}|y_{i}|-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}|y_{i}|\bigg)^{2}\bigg|{\mathcal{A}}\right]\bigg|{\mathcal{E}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt=\frac{cR+R-1}{c}\cdot\expec[(cA-B)^{2}\mu^{2}+(c^{2}A+B)\sigma^{2}|{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt\geq\frac{cR+R-1}{c}\cdot[(ck+k-n)^{2}\mu^{2}+(c^{2}k+n-k)\sigma^{2}],

(68)

which follows from (
62
), (
65
) and (
66
) upon noting that

•

(cA-B)^{2}\mu^{2}+(c^{2}A+B)\sigma^{2}
is increasing in
A
for

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{1+c}+\frac{(1-c)\sigma^{2}}{2(1+c)\mu^{2}}.

(69)

•

(
69
) is implied by (
60
), since
A\geq k=nR

by (

).

Applying Jensen’s inequality twice
followed by (

) gives:

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{{\rm ch}}({\mathcal{C}})=\expec\left[\sqrt{1-\frac{|\langle y,{\mathcal{C}}(f)\rangle|^{2}}{n\left\|{y}\right\|^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\expec\bigg[\frac{|\langle y,{\mathcal{C}}(f)\rangle|^{2}}{n\left\|{y}\right\|^{2}}\bigg]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{cR+R-1}{c}\cdot\frac{(ck+k-n)^{2}\mu^{2}+(c^{2}k+n-k)\sigma^{2}}{n\expec[\left\|{y}\right\|^{2}]}}.

Substituting

yields the conclusion.
∎

Theorem 8
provides sufficient SNR and rate conditions under which CRS codes achieve a small average covering radius. Comparing this bound with the random-coding expectation of
Theorem 2
yields the following corollary, which shows that CRS codes asymptotically achieve the average covering radius of random codes at high rate and high SNR.

Corollary 3
.

Consider a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, where the amplitudes are i.i.d. with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}{}
, and are independent of the phases. Then, over
{\mathcal{D}}
, the average covering radius of a
\CRS
code of rate
R
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\geq\max\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1-c}{1-c+2n(cR+R-1)},

The behavior observed in

highlights an important distinction between covering in the Hamming and Grassmann spaces. While Reed–Solomon-based constructions can outperform random codes in the Hamming space under the average covering radius metric, the corresponding CRS constructions fall short of random codebooks in the Grassmann space. This performance gap can be attributed to a structural constraint inherent to CRS codes: all coordinates of the basis vector have equal amplitude, as defined in (
46
). Although this constant-modulus property is highly desirable in wireless communication applications, where it enables power-efficient transmission and simplifies hardware implementation, it restricts the geometric flexibility of the codebook in the Grassmann space. In contrast, random codebooks implicitly exploit variations in coordinate amplitudes, which allows for more effective space covering. We believe that achieving or surpassing the performance of non-asymptotic random codes in the Grassmann space requires the design of structured codes with non-uniform amplitude profiles across coordinates. The exploration of such amplitude-adaptive constructions, while preserving practical constraints, is left for future work.

VI
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this work, we developed efficient covering algorithms for both block and subspace codes based on RS codes. Central to our approach is a puncturing-based covering algorithm that leverages existing decoding techniques and enables efficient identification of codewords within the covering radius of the code. For block codes, we showed that when combined with the Guruswami–Sudan list decoder, the proposed algorithm achieves an average covering radius that closely approaches the optimal performance of the MAP decoder, despite operating with polynomial-time complexity.

From an information-theoretic perspective, we introduced the notion of the average covering radius for both block and subspace codes as a meaningful measure of average distortion in quantization problems. Using tools from one-shot compression and rate–distortion theory, we derived explicit random-coding bounds on the average covering radius in both Hamming and Grassmann spaces. These bounds provide fundamental benchmarks against which structured code constructions can be evaluated. In particular, our numerical results demonstrated that the RS-based constructions outperform random codebooks in various fixed block-length scenarios. The analysis provides new insights into the role of algebraic structures in covering problems and applications.

Several directions for future work naturally emerge from this study. First, it would be of interest to extend the present analysis to CP/CRS codes over arbitrary finite fields, as well as to more general Grassmannian code constructions. Second, the performance of the CRS covering algorithm may be further improved by refining the mapping step in
Step 5
of
Algorithm 2
, potentially through more deterministic or structure-aware choices. Additionally, the upper bound in
Theorem 8
could be tightened by replacing Jensen’s inequality with sharper concentration bounds or by incorporating additional sources of randomness into the analysis.

A notable limitation of CRS codes is the constant-modulus constraint on codeword coordinates. This, while advantageous for practical wireless implementations, restricts geometric flexibility and likely contributes to their inferior performance relative to random codes in short-length regimes. This observation motivates the exploration of multi-level CRS codes with variable coordinate amplitudes, and a systematic study of their impact on average covering performance.

Finally, many of the concepts and techniques developed in this work extend naturally beyond the Hamming and Grassmann spaces. In particular, investigating average covering properties and efficient covering algorithms for codes in other metric spaces, such as rank-metric codes, is another promising avenue for future research.
