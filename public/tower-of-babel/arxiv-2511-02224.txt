Computational Hardness of Static Distributionally Robust Markov Decision Processes
Yan Li

Abstract
We present some hardness results on finding the optimal policy for the static formulation of distributionally robust Markov decision processes. We construct problem instances such that when the considered policy class is Markovian and non-randomized, finding the optimal policy is NP-hard, and when the considered policy class is Markovian and randomized, the robust value function possesses sub-optimal strict local minimizers. The considered instances involve an ambiguity set with only two transition kernels.

Introduction

Consider a finite-horizon distributionally robust Markov decision process
(\left\{{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T},\left\{\mathcal{A}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T},\mathcal{P},\left\{c_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T})
.
Here
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
and
\mathcal{A}_{t}
denote the finite state and action space at stage
t
,

\mathcal{P}
denotes a compact set of well-defined transition kernels,
and
c_{t}:{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\times\mathcal{A}_{t}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the cost function at stage
t
.
For any policy
\pi
, its corresponding value function associated with a kernel
P\in\mathcal{P}
is given by

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{P}(s_{1})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi,P}\left[\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(S_{t},A_{t})|S_{1}=s_{1}\right],

(1.1)

where
\mathbb{E}^{\pi,P}\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the expectation with respect to the law of stochastic process
\left\{(S_{t},A_{t})\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
induced by
(\pi,P)
(cf. Ionescu Tulcea Theorem
[
9
]
).
The static formulation of distributionally robust Markov decision process, also known as robust MDP
[
10
,
8
,
6
,
3
]
, defines the robust value function as

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{r}(s_{1})=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}V^{\pi}_{P}(s_{1}),

(1.2)

for a given initial state
s_{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
.
Correspondingly the policy optimization problem considers

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi}V^{\pi}_{r}(s_{1})=\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}V^{\pi}_{P}(s_{1}),

(1.3)

where
\Pi
is a given set of Markovian policies.

It is worth mentioning here that optimal policies for the static formulation (
1.3
) can be history-dependent.
We restrict our attention to Markovian policies here as history-dependent policies take a space complexity that can grow exponentially with respect to the horizon length.

In this manuscript we will focus on a subclass of (
1.3
), where
\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{(1)},P_{(2)}\right\}
consists of two distinct transition kernels.
Clearly, (
1.3
) reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\max\left\{V^{\pi}_{P_{(1)}}(s_{1}),V^{\pi}_{P_{(2)}}(s_{1})\right\}.

(1.4)

Note that
\mathcal{P}
is non-rectangular
[
4
,
10
]
unless
P_{(1)}
and
P_{(2)}
differs at only one state.

Remark 1.1
.

It is worth mentioning here that despite
\mathcal{P}
being non-rectangular, the corresponding robust policy evaluation problem (
1.2
) involves solving two linear systems and hence can be solved in polynomial time.
This should be contrasted with the NP-hardness result for computing
V^{\pi}_{r}
with general
\mathcal{P}

[
10
]
.

Let us use
\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}
to denote the set of non-randomized Markovian policies,
and use
\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
to denote the set of randomized Markovian policies.
We will proceed to show that finding the optimal policy for (
1.4
) is difficult for both
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}
and
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
.
To facilitate our discussion, for any integer
n>0
, let us denote
[n]=\left\{0,\ldots,n-1\right\}
.
We reserve
\lVert\cdot\rVert
for the standard Euclidean norm.

2
Computational Hardness

We begin by showing that finding the optimal non-randomized Markovian policy is NP-hard.

Theorem 2.1
.

The robust MDP (
1.4
) is NP-hard
when
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}
.

Proof.

Given any
\mathcal{W}=\left\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
, consider the following construction of a robust MDP instance.
For any
1\leq t\leq n
, define

{\mathcal{S}}_{2t-1}=\left\{2t-1\right\}
,

{\mathcal{S}}_{2t}=\left\{0,1\right\}
,

\mathcal{A}_{2t-1}=\left\{0,1\right\}
,

\mathcal{A}_{2t}=\emptyset
.
Let cost function be defined as

\displaystyle c_{2t-1}(\cdot)=0,c_{2t}(0)=w_{t},~c_{2t}(1)=0.

Consider two transition kernels defined by

\displaystyle P_{(1)}(S_{2t}=0|S_{2t-1}=2t-1,A_{2t-1}=0)=1,~P_{(1)}(S_{2t}=1|S_{2t-1}=2t-1,A_{2t-1}=1)=1,

\displaystyle P_{(2)}(S_{2t}=1|S_{2t-1}=2t-1,A_{2t-1}=0)=1,~P_{(2)}(S_{2t}=0|S_{2t-1}=2t-1,A_{2t-1}=1)=1,

and
P_{(1)}(S_{2t+1}=2t+1|S_{2t})=P_{(2)}(S_{2t+1}=2t+1|S_{2t})=1
.
Given any non-randomized policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}
, let

{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}=\left\{t:\pi(S_{2t-1})=0,1\leq t\leq n\right\}
. We have

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{P_{(1)}}(s_{1})=\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{t\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}}w_{t},~V^{\pi}_{P_{(2)}}(s_{1})=\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{t\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}}w_{t},

which implies

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{r}(s_{1})=\max\left\{\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{t\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}}w_{t},\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{t\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}}w_{t}\right\}.

It is then clear that

\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}}V^{\pi}_{r}(s_{1})\geq\tfrac{\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{1\leq t\leq n}w_{t}}{2}
,
and equality holds if and only if there exists

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{W}
, such that

\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{w\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}w=\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{w\in{\mathcal{W}\setminus W^{\prime}}}w
.
The latter is the set partition problem and is NP-complete.
∎

We then construct problem instances for which (
1.4
) possesses a non-optimal local minimizer among the set of randomized Markovian policies.

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists a robust MDP instance such that (
1.4
) has a sub-optimal strict local minimizer
when
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
.

Proof.

Given integer
n>0
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
consider a three-stage Markov decision process with
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{1}={\mathcal{S}}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{2}=[n]
,
{\mathcal{S}}_{3}=[n]\times[n]
,
\mathcal{A}_{3}=\emptyset
.
The per-stage cost function is given by
c_{1}(\cdot)=c_{2}(\cdot)=0
, and
c_{3}(i,j)=A_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
.
Consider transition kernel
P_{(1)}
defined as

\displaystyle P_{(1)}(S_{2}=i|S_{1}=s_{1},A_{1}=i)=1,~P_{(1)}(S_{3}=(i,j)|S_{2}=i,A_{2}=j)=1,~\forall i,j\in[n].

That is, starting from
s
, with action
A_{1}=i
, the state transits to
S_{2}=i
. Upon making
A_{2}=j
, the state transits to
S_{3}=(i,j)
.
In addition, the transition kernel
P_{(2)}
is defined as

\displaystyle P_{(2)}(S_{2}=n-i|S_{1}=s_{1},A_{1}=i)=1,~P_{(2)}(S_{3}=(n-i,j)|S_{2}=i,A_{2}=j)=1,~\forall i,j\in[n].

That is, starting from
s
, with action
A_{1}=i
, the state transits to
S_{2}=n-i
. Upon making
A_{2}=j
, the state transits to
S_{3}=(i,j)
.

For notational simplicity, given a randomized policy
\pi=(\pi^{1},\pi^{2})
with
\pi^{t}:{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{A}_{t}}
, let us denote

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{i}=\pi^{1}(A_{1}=i|S_{1}=s_{1}),\pi^{2}_{ij}=\pi^{2}(A_{2}=j|S_{2}=i),~\forall i,j\in[n].

Clearly, the value function
V^{\pi}_{(1)}
associated with kernel
P_{(1)}
is given by

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{(1)}(s_{1})=\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\pi^{1}_{i}\left(\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{j\in[n]}\pi^{2}_{ij}A_{ij}\right),

and the value function
V^{\pi}_{(2)}
associated with kernel
P_{(2)}
is

\displaystyle V^{\pi}_{(2)}(s_{1})=\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\pi^{1}_{i}\left(\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{j\in[n]}\pi^{2}_{(n-i)j}A_{ij}\right).

Hence the robust value function is defined as

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(s_{1})=\max\left\{\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\pi_{i}^{1}\left(\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{j\in[n]}\pi_{ij}^{2}A_{ij}\right),\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\pi_{i}^{1}\left(\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{j\in[n]}\pi_{(n-i)j}^{2}A_{ij}\right)\right\}.

Now consider
n=1
,
and

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently we have

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(s_{1})=\max\left\{\pi^{1}_{0}\pi^{2}_{00}+\pi^{1}_{1}\left(-\pi^{2}_{10}+\pi^{2}_{11}\right),~\pi^{1}_{0}\pi^{2}_{10}+\pi^{1}_{1}\left(-\pi^{2}_{00}+\pi^{2}_{01}\right)\right\}.

(2.1)

Let us define

\displaystyle f(\pi^{1})=\min_{\pi^{2}}V^{\pi}(s_{1})=\min_{\pi^{2}_{00},\pi^{2}_{10}\in[0,1]}g_{\pi^{1}}(\pi^{2}_{00},\pi^{2}_{10}),

(2.2)

where

g_{\pi^{1}}(a,b)=\max\left\{\pi^{1}_{0}a+\pi^{1}_{1}\left(1-2b\right),\pi^{1}_{0}b+\pi^{1}_{1}\left(1-2a\right)\right\}
.
It is clear that
g_{\pi^{1}}(\cdot)
is convex and symmetric, i.e.,
g_{\pi^{1}}(a,b)=g_{\pi^{1}}(b,a)
.
Hence there must exists an optimal solution
(a^{*},b^{*})
of (
2.2
) satisfying
a^{*}=b^{*}
, and

\displaystyle f(\pi^{1})=\min_{\pi^{2}}V^{\pi}(s_{1})=\min_{a\in[0,1]}\pi_{0}^{1}a+\pi_{1}^{1}(1-2a)=\min\left\{\pi^{1}_{1},\pi^{1}_{0}-\pi^{1}_{1}\right\}.

(2.3)

From the above observation, it is then clear that
\overline{\pi}^{1}=(1,0)
is a strict local minimizer of
f(\cdot)
.
That is, there exists
\delta>0
, such that

f(\pi^{1})>f(\overline{\pi}^{1})
for any
\lVert\pi^{1}-\overline{\pi}^{1}\rVert\leq\delta
and
\pi^{1}\neq\overline{\pi}^{1}
.
In addition,
\overline{\pi}^{1}
is suboptimal for
f(\cdot)
as

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}V^{\pi}(s_{1})=\min_{\pi^{1}}f(\pi^{1})=-1<0=f(\overline{\pi}^{1}).

Let
\overline{\pi}^{2}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Argmin}}_{\pi^{2}}V^{\overline{\pi}^{2},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})
.
That is,

f(\overline{\pi}^{1})=V^{\overline{\pi}^{1},\overline{\pi}^{2}}(s_{1})
.
Note that from (
2.2
), the definition of
g_{\overline{\pi}^{1}}(\cdot)
and the choice of
\overline{\pi}^{1}
, such choice of
\overline{\pi}^{2}
is unique.
Let us write
\overline{\pi}\coloneqq(\overline{\pi}^{1},\overline{\pi}^{2})
.

It is clear that
(\overline{\pi}^{1},\overline{\pi}^{2})
is a strict local minimizer of
V^{\pi}(s_{1})
but not a global minimizer.
Indeed, consider any
\pi\coloneqq(\pi^{1},\pi^{2})
such that
\lVert\pi-\overline{\pi}\rVert\leq\delta
and
\pi\neq\overline{\pi}
.
If
\pi^{1}\neq\overline{\pi}^{1}
, then

\displaystyle V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})\overset{(a)}{\geq}f(\pi^{1})\overset{(b)}{>}f(\overline{\pi}^{1})\overset{(c)}{=}V^{\overline{\pi}^{1},\overline{\pi}^{2}}(s_{1})\overset{(d)}{>}\min_{\pi}V^{\pi}(s_{1}),

where
(a)
follows from definition of
f(\cdot)
in (
2.2
),

(b)
follows from that
\overline{\pi}
is a strict local minimizer of
f(\cdot)
in
\left\{\pi^{1}:\lVert\pi^{1}-\overline{\pi}^{1}\rVert\leq\delta\right\}
,

(c)
follows from the definition of
\overline{\pi}^{2}
, and
(d)
follows from the sub-optimality of
\overline{\pi}^{1}
for
f(\cdot)
.
On the other hand, if
\pi^{1}=\overline{\pi}^{1}
, then we must have
\pi^{2}\neq\overline{\pi}^{2}
, and hence

\displaystyle V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})\overset{(e)}{>}f(\pi^{1})=f(\overline{\pi}^{1})=V^{\overline{\pi}^{1},\overline{\pi}^{2}}(s_{1})>\min_{\pi}V^{\pi}(s_{1}),

where
(e)
follows from that
\overline{\pi}^{2}
is the unique minimizer of
\min_{\pi^{2}}V^{\overline{\pi}^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})
.
The above two observations jointly implies

V^{\pi^{1},\pi^{2}}(s_{1})>V^{\overline{\pi}^{1},\overline{\pi}^{2}}(s_{1})>\min_{\pi}V^{\pi}(s_{1}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

It could be interesting to note that with a fixed transition kernel, the value function
V^{\pi}_{P}
defined in (
1.1
) does not possess sub-optimal local minimizers when
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
(cf.
[
1
, Lemma 4.1]
).

When the maximization problem in (
1.4
) involves both ambiguous cost functions
\left\{c_{t}\right\}
and transition kernel
P
,
[
4
]
establishes the NP-completeness of (
1.4
) based on the reduction from Path with Forbidden Pairs
[
2
]
.
It appears to us that the reduction presented in
[
4
]
is not polynomial, as the transition kernel constructed therein needs to memorize the visitation history of the forbidden pairs and hence is non-Markovian.

Theorem
1.3
can also be strengthened if (
1.3
) involves
\mathcal{P}
consisting of
K
distinct kernels and the characterization of computational hardness is allowed to depend on
K
.
In this case,
[
4
]
shows that finding the history-dependent optimal policy for (
1.3
) is NP-hard.
Indeed, using the observations made in
[
7
]
for multi-model MDPs, the following simple proposition shows that finding the optimal (randomized) Markovian policy is also NP-hard.

Proposition 2.1
.

For the robust MDP (
1.3
) where
\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{(k)}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
consists of
K
distinct transition kernels,
finding the optimal policy within
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
is NP-hard.

Proof.

From
[
7
, Proposition 2]
, finding a non-randomized policy
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}
such that

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}V^{\pi^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)\leq 0

(2.4)

is NP-hard for non-negative cost functions.
If such a policy
\pi^{*}
exists, then
V^{\pi^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)=0
for any
1\leq k\leq K
and subsequently

\max_{1\leq k\leq K}V^{\pi^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)\leq 0
.
Conversely,
if there exists
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
such that
\max_{1\leq k\leq K}V^{\pi^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)\leq 0
,
then
\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}V^{\pi^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)=0
.
This implies

\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}}\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}V^{\pi}_{P_{(k)}}(s)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}}\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}V^{\pi}_{P_{(k)}}(s)\leq 0
,
where the equality follows from
[
7
, Proposition 1]
.
Consequently there exists
\overline{\pi}^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}
such that

\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}V^{\overline{\pi}^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)\leq 0
.
Hence certifying (
2.4
) is equivalent to finding a policy
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
such that

\max_{1\leq k\leq K}V^{\pi^{*}}_{P_{(k)}}(s)\leq 0
.
∎

3
Dynamic Formulation

It is clear that in the static formulation (
1.4
), the transition kernel
P\in\mathcal{P}
is selected before the realization of process
\left\{(S_{t},A_{t})\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and that the non-rectangularity of
\mathcal{P}
creates coupling between transition probabilities across states.
This unfortunately destroys the dynamic decomposition of the corresponding distributionally robust functional in (
1.4
), and hence the dynamic programming equations no longer exist.
This also constitutes the source of the hardness results presented in Section
2
.
We can instead consider a dynamic counterpart of the static formulation that allows an adversarial nature to adopt a policy that selects the transition kernel based on the process history
[
5
]
.
It is shown in
[
5
]
that one can obtain dynamic equations of the form

\displaystyle V_{t}(s)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\pi_{t}(a_{t}|s_{t})\left[c_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\textstyle\sum\nolimits_{s_{t+1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{t+1}}P(S_{t+1}=s_{t+1}|S_{t}=s_{t},A_{t}=a_{t})V_{t+1}(s_{t+1})\right]

for general compact
\mathcal{P}
,
and the above dynamic equations also characterize the value function for the static formulation (
1.3
) with the rectangularized version of
\mathcal{P}
.
It is clear that computing the above optimal value functions and policies are
R
-polynomial
for both

\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MR}}
and
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{MD}}

when
\mathcal{P}
is convex, compact, and equipped with a separating oracle.

4
Concluding Remarks

We conclude by noting that the hard instances constructed within the manuscript can be extended to the discounted infinite-horizon setting by taking union of the per-stage state spaces and introducing an additional sink state.
That is,
{\mathcal{S}}=\cup_{1\leq t\leq T}{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\cup{\mathcal{S}}_{T+1}
,
where
\left\{{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
is defined as before and
{\mathcal{S}}_{T+1}=\left\{s_{\mathrm{sink}}\right\}
denotes an additional sink state.
The transition kernel
P_{(i)}
in the discounted infinite-horizon setting is defined in the same way as in the original instances, with
P_{(i)}(S_{T+1}=s_{\mathrm{sink}}|S_{T})=1
for
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
.
It remains interesting to study whether similar results can be established for the infinite-horizon average-cost setting.
