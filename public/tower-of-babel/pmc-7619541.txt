An activator of tailless’ repressor function stimulates anti-neurodegenerative gene expression
Authors: Emily C. Hank, Romy Busch, Úrsula López-García, Till Kasch, Loris Knümann, Vasily Morozov, Georg Höfner, Julian A. Marschner, Daniel Merk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transcription factor tailless (TLX, NR2E1) is expressed in neuronal stem cells and retinal progenitor cells and thought to maintain their self-renewal by balancing proliferation and differentiation. It is considered as a master regulator of neurogenesis and as promising target in neurodegeneration. Here, we developed highly potent and selective activators of TLX’ transcriptional repressor activity by systematic extension of previously optimized fragment TLX ligands and tuning of the substitution pattern. An optimized TLX activator with nanomolar potency and pronounced selectivity was studied for its transcriptomic effects by RNAseq which demonstrated upregulation of genes associated with ATP production, lysosomal function, and vesicular transport. Moreover, the mitochondrial quality control associated PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) was induced and the amyloidogenic β-secretase (BACE) was downregulated supporting anti-neurodegenerative effects of TLX modulation on transcriptional level.

Introduction

The transcription factor tailless homologue (TLX, NR2E1) is an orphan nuclear receptor (NR) found in neuronal stem cells (NSC) and retinal progenitor cells (RPC).1,2 It mainly acts as a transcriptional repressor and controls expression of cell cycle regulators, such as p21 and the phosphatase and tensin homologue (PTEN), to maintain NSC in an undifferentiated, self-renewing state.1–7 TLX is essential for normal brain development8 and has been linked to behavior9–11, memory function11–13, and learning capacity7 in vitro and in vivo. The stimulation of TLX activity seems to promote neurogenesis and protect from neuroinflammation.7,14–17 Moreover, TLX is important for vision and retinal health suggesting its activation as beneficial in retinal dystrophies5,18–20. Several lines of evidence thus highlight TLX as a promising target in neurodegenerative diseases which has stimulated the search for TLX modulators21,22.

Early druggability evaluation by library screening in a thermal shift assay yielded the first three TLX ligands including ccrp2 (1, Figure 1) as the highest affinity entity (Kd 0.6 µM)23. Kandel et al.26 reported unsaturated fatty acids including oleic acid (2; Kd 7.3 µM) as relevant endogenous TLX ligands with oleic acid stimulating neurogenesis in vivo. Additionally, Griffett et al.27 discovered TLX modulation by natural and synthetic retinoids with all-(E)-retinal acting as inverse agonist and BMS453 (3) as activator of TLX’s repressor activity. Dueva et al.28 identified a number of chemically diverse TLX modulators (e.g., 4) with weaker potency by virtual screening and we employed a focused drug fragment screening to identify both TLX agonists (e.g. 5) and inverse agonists24,29,30. Recently, systematic SAR evaluation of the fragment screening hit 5 enabled marked optimization of potency and affinity up to sub-micromolar EC50 values in 625.

Here, we report the development of selective high-affinity TLX agonists from the most optimized fragment derivatives. Comparative evaluation of scaffold extension and systematic optimization of the substitution pattern resulted in TLX activators with double-digit nanomolar potency. A highly potent and selective representative was used to study the transcriptomic impact of the TLX repressor activation which revealed induction of respiratory chain components and ATP production genes as most prominent effect. Additionally, we observed altered expression of genes associated with neurodegeneration such as downregulation of β-secretase (BACE), which is linked to the amyloidogenic pathway in Alzheimer’s disease.

Results & Discussion

Systematic optimization of the screening hit 5 recently enabled the development of four potent fragment TLX agonists (6-9, Table 1) with low micromolar to sub-micromolar TLX agonist potency.25 To refine this collection for further optimization, we scanned the suitability of 6-9 for structural extension by introducing N-benzyl (10-13) and phenylacetamide (14-17) substituents. The benzyl motif (10-13) consistently disrupted TLX agonism of all lead fragments while the phenylacetamide (14-17) consistently enhanced potency. However, the impact of this extension varied markedly with the smallest gain in potency observed for the most active lead fragment 6 and the strongest increase for 9. Nevertheless, all phenylacetamide derivatives 14-17 displayed favorable TLX agonist potency with similar sub-micromolar EC50 values between 0.19 and 0.5 µM and hence appeared suitable as leads for further evaluation. We based our choice on the drug-like properties and prioritized 14 (logP 4.3531) and 16 (logP 5.08) for their lower lipophilicity compared to 15 (logP 5.16) and 17 (logP 5.41).

With the scaffolds of 6/14 and 8/16, we next explored structural extension from the aniline in more detail (Table 2). Shorter benzamide substituents (18, 19), reduction of the phenylacetamides (14, 16) to the corresponding secondary amines (20, 21), and phenethyl ethers (22, 23) were detrimental for TLX activation. Inversion of the amide group (24, 25) was tolerated but slightly diminished potency. Urea (26, 27) and urethane (28, 29) linkers also retained slightly reduced TLX agonism, suggesting that rigid groups were favored, but incorporation of a cyclopropyl motif (30, 31) for rigidification of the phenylacetamide was only tolerated in the dimethylpyridine-based scaffold (30). N-Methylation of the acetamide (32, 33) was tolerated but accompanied by a loss of TLX activation efficacy. Only a phenylmethanesulfonamide motif (34, 35) could enhance activity on TLX when combined with the pyrrolo[2,3-b]pyridine-based fragment 6. The resulting TLX agonist 35 displayed a remarkable EC50 value of 10 nM and emerged as first TLX ligand with low nanomolar potency. The inverted sulfonamides (36, 37) and the shorter benzenesulfonamides (38, 39) were less potent.

Within this strict SAR, the evaluation of structural extension from the aniline thus highlighted the phenylacetamide 16 and particularly the phenylmethanesulfonamide 35 as the most potent TLX agonist scaffolds for fine-grained optimization. We next continued structural tuning of 16 and studied the introduction of substituents on the phenylacetamide (Table 3). As the incorporation of a cyclopropane in the acetamide linker (30) was detrimental, we focused on substituents on the phenyl motif.

Scanning of methyl groups in all positions of the phenyl substituent (40-42) revealed no major activity difference and no improvement over 16 but a slight preference for 2-substitution (40). Fluorine (43), chlorine (44) and a methoxy group (45) were tolerated as alternative residues in 2-position, with fluorine (43) being favored in terms of efficacy but no modification provided an improvement in TLX agonist activity. Based on the preference for fluoro substitution in 2-position we tested the 2,6-difluoro analogue (46, Table 4) which indeed enhanced TLX agonist potency to a double-digit nanomolar EC50 value. The isomeric 2,3- (47), 2,4- (48) and 2,5- (49) difluoro derivatives were less favored. Evaluation of alternative, asymmetric 2,6-disubstitution patterns (50-53) revealed a distinctive SAR. Combined fluoro and chloro substitution (50) diminished potency compared to the difluoro analogue 46 while the 2-fluoro-6-trifluoromethyl (51) and 2-fluoro-6-methoxy (52) derivatives were equipotent to the 2,6-difluoro analogue 46. Combining these observations in a 2-fluoro-6-difluoromethoxy substitution pattern (53) was strongly favored and displayed an EC50 of 15 nM for TLX activation. With a 100-fold increase in activity over the fragment lead 8, 53 emerged as highly potent TLX agonist and appeared suitable for in-depth evaluation as chemical tool.

Using the SAR insights from 40-53, we moved our attention to the phenylmethanesulfonamide 35 and evaluated transfer of the favored substitution patterns (Table 5). As observed for 43, installation of a 2-fluoro substituent on the phenyl motif (54) was tolerated, but double fluorine substitution (55), which was favored in the phenylacetamide series (46), markedly decreased TLX agonism. Introduction of a bulkier trifluoromethyl motif in the 2-position (56) was detrimental, overall highlighting the unsubstituted phenylmethanesulfonamide 35 as favored. SAR transfer between the scaffolds of 16 and 35 was hence not productive which underscored the chemical diversity of both TLX agonists as valuable feature for their application as tools.

Our previous efforts in optimization of the fragment screening hit 525 had also yielded the moderately potent inverse agonist 57 (Table 6). Based on the remarkable improvement in TLX agonist potency that could be achieved via extension from the aniline motif (e.g., in 35 and 53), we explored whether this strategy could also boost inverse agonism (Table 6). Introduction of the 2-fluoro-6-difluoromethoxyphenylbenzamide motif of 53 to the inverse agonist scaffold of 57 was not tolerated (58), however, and even a small acetamide motif (59) disrupted inverse agonism and switched the profile to weak agonist activity. These results suggested that inverse TLX agonists did not follow a similar SAR pattern in this region and required individual systematic optimization.

35 and 53 emerged from the fragment extension approach with double digit nanomolar TLX agonist potency for further exploration and in-depth characterization. Both compounds displayed favorable selectivity for TLX in the nuclear receptor family (Figure 2a) with no relevant activity at 3 µM in a representative panel of NR1, NR3, NR4 and NR5 receptors. 35 retained selectivity also within the NR2 family, while 53 affected the activity of TR2, TR4 and PNR closely related to TLX. A slight off-target liability on RORα, which we have detected in previous studies on this TLX ligand chemotype32, was not relevant for 35 and 53 with >30-fold selectivity for TLX over RORα (Table 7, Figure S1). Additionally, in vitro metabolic stability evaluation using human liver microsomes (HLM) revealed a half-life >100 min for both 35 and 53 indicating potential suitability for in vivo evaluation (Figure S2, Table 7).

Affinity-selection-mass-spectrometry (ASMS) experiments confirmed binding of 35 and 53 to the recombinant TLX LBD (Figure 2b) validating TLX mediated effects. For preliminary mechanistic insights, we studied the compounds’ effects on TLX dimer formation, which has been found responsive to ligand binding29. In a homogenous time-resolved fluorescence resonance energy transfer (HTRF) based setting using Tb3+-cryptate labeled TLX LBD and sGFP-labeled TLX LBD, 35 and 53 promoted TLX dimerization (Figure 2c). In line with the mechanism of the nuclear receptor revERB (NR1D), which forms homodimers binding to the RORE to act as a repressor33,34, these results suggest that activation of TLX’ repressor activity by ligands involves stabilization of a TLX dimer.

With nanomolar TLX agonist potency, >30-fold selectivity, and validated binding, 35 appeared suitable as tool to study the transcriptomic impact of activating TLX’ repressor function. Therefore, we performed RNA sequencing (RNAseq) in TLX expressing retinal pigment epithelial cells (ARPE-19) after treatment with 35 (1 µM) for 16 h to detect direct effects. 35 modulated the expression of 327 genes (padj ≤ 0.05, log2foldchange ≥ |0.3|; Figure 3a) and caused uniform effects over the samples according to principal component (PCA, Figure 3b) and correlation analysis (Figure 3c).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA; Figure 3d) highlighted upregulation of cellular respiration, oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production as prominent transcriptomic outcomes. These effects centered mainly on components of the respiratory chain complexes I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) and V (ATP synthase). Additionally, 35 induced genes related to SNAREs and vesicular transport (e.g., synaptosome-associated protein of 23 kDa (SNAP23)) as well as lysosomal components (e.g., vacuolar-type H+-adenosine triphosphatase (V-ATPase), biogenesis of lysosome-related organelles complex-1 (BLOC1), acid ceramidase (ASAH1)). Overall, however, 35 mainly caused suppression of gene expression in line with its repressor-activating mode-of-action. Inflammatory signaling pathways (TNF, NFκB), apoptosis signaling, as well as cell cycle and DNA replication genes were downregulated. These transcriptomic effects of 35 pointed to a protective, energy-storing state upon activation of TLX’ repressor activity.

GSEA on pathology-related pathways linked the transcriptomic effects of 35 to several neurodegenerative diseases. Therein, the favorable upregulation of respiratory chain components, was accompanied by direct transcriptomic effects on prominent neurodegeneration-associated genes: For example, β-secretase (BACE), an amyloid precursor protein processing enzyme producing amyloid-β, was downregulated, while the PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), a key mitochondrial quality control factor, and LC3 genes, which are related to autophagy, were induced. Altogether, the transcriptomic effects of 35 add another clue for therapeutic potential of TLX modulation in neurodegeneration.

Conclusion

Several lines of evidence from preclinical studies designate the peculiar nuclear receptor TLX as master regulator of neuronal stem cell homeostasis and as promising target in degenerative diseases. However, the effects of pharmacological TLX modulation remain elusive due to the lack of high-quality ligands. Here, we developed a potent and selective activator of TLX’ repressor function (35) as chemical tool and explored its transcriptomic impact in RNAseq. 35 stimulated genes associated with ATP production, lysosome function, and vesicular transport while suppressing inflammatory and apoptotic pathways. Additionally, 35 affected the expression of prominent genes linked to neurodegenerative pathologies such as BACE and PINK1. These transcriptomic effects of activation of TLX’ repressor function further support the receptor’s potential as a target in degenerative diseases.

Chemistry

Compounds 10-17 (Scheme 1) were prepared by Suzuki-Miyaura reaction of the 3-bromo-5-(trifluoromethyl)aniline derivatives 60-62. The benzyl amine 60 was generated by reaction of SM1 with benzyl bromide (SM2), the phenylacetamide intermediate 61 was obtained from TCFH/NMI-mediated coupling35 of SM1 with phenylacetic acid (SM3), and the precursor 62 of the N-methyl derivatives 32 and 33 was prepared by methylation of 61 with MeI. Suzuki-Miyaura couplings were performed with commercially available boronic acids (SM4) or Bpin esters (SM5, SM6) and with the Bpin ester 63, which was prepared by borylation of 2-acetyl-4-bromothiophene (SM7).

Similarly, compounds 20-25 and 36-39 were prepared from the respective aryl bromides 64-68 by Suzuki-Miyaura coupling with (2,6-dimethylpyridin-4-yl)boronic acid (SM4) or (1-methyl-1H-pyrrolo[2,3-b]pyridin-5-yl)boronic acid pinacol ester (SM5, Scheme 2). The required precursors 64 and 65 were generated by nucleophilic substitution of 2-phenylethylbromid (SM8) with SM1 or 3-bromo-5-trifluoromethylphenol (SM9). 66 and 67 were obtained by reaction of 3-bromo-5- (trifluoromethyl)benzoic acid (SM10) or 3-bromo-5- (trifluoromethyl)benzenesulphonyl chloride (SM11) with benzylamine (SM12). 68 was available from the reaction of SM1 with benzenesulfonyl chloride (SM13).

Compounds 18, 19, 26-31, 34, 35, 40-56, 58 and 59 were synthesized from the respective aniline fragments 6, 8 and 57 (Scheme 3), whose preparation was described previously25. To obtain the amides 30, 31, 40-53 and 58, the respective carboxylic acids (SM15-SM29) were used for coupling with 6, 8 or 57 using SOCl2 or TCFH/NMI. 18, 19 and 60 were prepared by reacting 6 or 8 with benzoyl chloride (SM14) and 57 with acetyl chloride (SM30). The urea derivatives 26 and 27 were obtained by treating aniline 6 or 8 with phenyl isocyanate (SM31), while the carbamates 28 and 29 were formed by reaction of 6 or 8 with phenyl chloroformate (SM32). The sulfonamides 34, 35 and 54-56 were generated by coupling 6 or 8 with the sulfonyl chlorides SM33-SM36.

Experimental Procedures

Chemistry

General

All chemicals were of reagent grade, purchased from commercial sources (e.g., Sigma-Aldrich, TCI, BLDpharm) and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. All reactions were conducted under nitrogen (N2) or argon atmosphere and in absolute solvents purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Other solvents, especially for work-up procedures, were of reagent grade or purified by distillation (iso-hexane, cyclohexane, EtOAc, EtOH). Reactions were monitored by thin layer chromatography (TLC) on TLC Silica gel 60 F254 coated aluminum sheets by Merck and visualized under ultraviolet light (254 nm). Purification by column chromatography was performed on a puriFlash® XS520Plus system (Advion, Ithaca, NY, USA) using high performance spherical silica columns (SIHP, 30 or 50 µm) by Interchim and a gradient of iso-hexane or cyclohexane to EtOAc, reversed-phase (RP) column chromatography was performed on a puriFlash® 5.250 system (Advion) using C18HP columns (SIHP, 15 µm) by Interchim and a gradient of H2O with 10% MeCN to 100% MeCN (HPLC gradient grade). Melting points (mp) were determined with a Krüss M3000 melting point meter (A. Krüss Optronic GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). Mass spectra were obtained on a puriFlash®-CMS system (Advion) using atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were obtained with a Thermo Finnigan LTQ FT instrument for electron impact ionization (EI) or electrospray ionization (ESI). NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker Avance III HD 400 MHz or 500 MHz spectrometers equipped with a CryoProbeTM Prodigy broadband probe (Bruker). Chemical shifts are reported in δ values (ppm) relative to residual protium signals in the NMR solvent (1H-NMR: acetone-d6: δ = 2.05 ppm; DMSO-d6: δ = 2.50 ppm; methanol-d4: δ = 3.31 ppm; CD2Cl2: δ = 5.32 ppm, 13C-NMR: acetone-d6: δ = 206.26, 29.84 ppm; DMSO-d6: δ = 39.52 ppm; methanol-d4:δ = 49.0 ppm; CD2Cl2: δ = 53.84 ppm) and coupling constants (J) in hertz (Hz). The splitting of signals is indicated by s for singlet, d for doublet, t for triplet, q for quartet, dd for doublet of a doublet and m for multiplet. The purity of the compounds was determined by 1H NMR (qHNMR) according to the method described by Pauli et al.36 with internal calibration. To ensure accurate determination of peak area ratio, the qHNMR measurements were conducted under conditions allowing for complete relaxation. Maleic acid (LOT#BCCH2407, purity 99.85%) was used as internal standard in methanol-d4 or DMSO-d6. All compounds for biological testing had a purity >95% according to quantitative 1H NMR (qHNMR).

The synthesis of compounds 6-9 and 57 has been reported previously25.

General procedure A (Suzuki-coupling)

A 50-100 mL Schlenk flask was charged with a stir bar, the boronic acid/pinacol ester (1 eq.) and K3PO4 (3 eq.). The flask was evacuated and backfilled with nitrogen three times. The previously de-gassed (3 freeze thaw-cycles) solvent mixture (dioxane/water = 85:15, ~0.05M), together with the Pd catalyst (10 mol%) and the heteroaryl bromide (1.5-2 eq.) was added and the reaction mixture was stirred at 100°C under reflux and nitrogen atmosphere. After TLC indicated completion of the reaction, the solution was cooled down to room temperature (rt) and the solvents were evaporated. The residue was taken up in EtOAc and washed with water and brine. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4, concentrated in vacuo, and the product was purified by flash column chromatography.

General procedure B (amide coupling with SOCl2)

The carboxylic acid (1 eq.) was dissolved in SOCl2 (1-3 mL) and heated to reflux for 2-2.5 h. The mixture was cooled down to rt and the solvent was evaporated. To the prepared acyl chloride was added CHCl3 (~0.1 M), and the amine (1 – 2 eq.) at 0 °C. The reaction mixture was flushed with N2, allowed to warm up to rt and stirred for 12-72 h. After TLC indicated completion of the reaction, the solvent was evaporated and the residue was acidified with 5% aq. HCl. The mixture was extracted with EtOAc (3x), the combined organic layers were washed with 1 M NaOH, water and brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography and if necessary, by RP column chromatography to yield the desired amides.

General procedure C (amide coupling with TCFH)

The carboxylic acid (1-1.5 eq.), the amine (1-2 eq.) and N-methylimidazole (NMI) (2 eq.) were dissolved in ACN or DMF (~0.03M) and TCFH (1.2-1.5 eq.) was added under stirring. The mixture was heated to 80 °C for 16-24 h. After TLC indicated completion of the reaction, the solvent was evaporated, and the residue was acidified with 5% aq. HCl. The mixture was extracted with EtOAc (3x), the combined organic layers were washed with 1 M NaOH, water and brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography and if necessary, by RP column chromatography to yield the desired amides.

General procedure D (Sulfonamide coupling)

The amine (1-2 eq.) was dissolved in dry CH2Cl2 (~0.1M) and stirred at 0 °C. The sulfonyl chloride (1-2 eq.) and DIPEA (3 eq.) were added, the mixture was allowed to warm up to rt, stirred overnight, and then concentrated in vacuo. The residue was taken up in EtOAc and washed with 5% aq. HCl and brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column and if necessary, by RP column chromatography to yield the desired sulfonamides.

In vitro characterization

Hybrid Reporter Gene Assays

TLX modulation was determined in a Gal4 hybrid reporter gene assay in HEK293T cells (German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Culture GmbH, DSMZ) using pFR-Luc (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA; reporter), pRL-SV40 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; internal control), pECE-SV40-Gal4-VP1637 (#71728, Addgene, Watertown, MA), and pFA-CMV-hTLX-LBD24, coding for the hinge region and ligand binding domain of the canonical isoform of human TLX. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), high glucose supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), sodium pyruvate (1 mM), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and seeded in 96-well plates (3×104 cells/well). After 24 h, medium was changed to Opti-MEM without supplements and cells were transiently transfected using Lipofectamine LTX reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Five hours after transfection, cells were incubated with the test compounds in Opti-MEM supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL) and 0.1% DMSO for 16 h before luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol on a Tecan Spark luminometer (Tecan Deutschland GmbH, Crailsheim, Germany). Firefly luminescence was divided by Renilla luminescence and multiplied by 1000 resulting in relative light units (RLU) to normalize for transfection efficiency and cell growth. Fold activation was obtained by dividing the mean RLU of test compound by the mean RLU of the untreated control. All samples were tested in at least three biologically independent experiments in duplicates. For dose-response curve fitting and calculation of EC50 values and remaining activity (= “bottom”), the equation “[Agonist] vs. response -- Variable slope (four parameters)” was used in GraphPad Prism (version 7, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Selectivity profiling was performed with pFA-CMV-THRα-LBD38, pFA-CMV-RARα-LBD39, pFA-CMV-PPARα-LBD40, pFA-CMV-PPARγ-LBD40, pFA-CMV-PPARδ-LBD40, pFA-CMV-revERBα-LBD41, pFA-CMV-RORα-LBD42, pFA-CMV-VDR-LBD43, pFA-CMV-CAR-LBD43, pFA-CMV-PXR-LBD43, pFA-CMV-LXRα-LBD43, pFA-CMV-FXR-LBD43, pFA-CMV-HNF4α-LBD44, pFA-CMV-hRXRα-LBD45, pFA-CMV-hRXRβ-LBD45, pFA-CMV-hRXRγ-LBD45, pFA-CMV-TR2-LBD40, pFA-CMV-TR4-LBD40, pFA-CMV-PNR-LBD46, pFA-CMV-ERα-LBD, GAL-AR-1-50347 (#89093, Addgene), pFA-CMV-Nur77-LBD48, pFA-CMV-hNurr1-LBD48, pFA-CMV-hNOR1-LBD48, pFA-CMV-SF1-LBD41 and pFA-CMV-LRH1-LBD. The following reference ligands were used: T3 (1 µM, THRα), Tretinoin (1 µM, RARα), GW7674 (1 µM, PPARα), Pioglitazone (1 µM, PPARγ), L165,041 (1 µM, PPARδ), SR1001 (1 µM, revERBα and RORα), calcitriol (1 µM, VDR), SR12813 (1 µM, PXR), CITCO (10 µM, CAR), T0901317 (1 µM, LXRα), GW4064 (1µM, FXR), Bexarotene (1 µM, RXRα/β/γ), PR3 (1 µM, PNR), diethylstilbestrol (1 µM, ERα), spironolactone (10 µM, AR), compound 29 from Vietor et al.49 (0.3 µM, Nur77 and NOR1) and fluvastatin (10 µM, Nurr1).

Affinity Selection Mass Spectrometry (ASMS)

A mixture containing compounds 35 and 53 (1 µM final concentration each) was incubated (at 4 °C for 1h) with TLX-LBD protein (35 µM) in buffer (50 mM Tris, 200 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) containing 1% (v/v) DMSO in a total volume of 55 µL (N=7). In parallel, this procedure was performed for denatured (82 °C, 30 min) TLX-LBD (35 µM) under otherwise identical conditions (N=3). After equilibration of the PD SpinTrap G-25 columns containing Sephadex G-25 medium (Cytiva, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, 50 µL of each binding sample were transferred onto an SEC column followed by centrifugation (800 g, 2 min) to generate the eluates E1. In the same way the pure compound mixture (1 µM in buffer with 1% DMSO) without TLX incubation was subjected to SEC as further negative control (N=3). Next, aliquots of 40 µL of each E1 eluate were diluted to 400 µL with 0.1% HCOOH in H2O and 0.1% HCOOH in MeCN (50:50, v/v) containing 100 nM Terfenadine as internal standard for quantification by LC-MS. In the same way samples containing 10 nM test compounds and 100 nM Terfenadine, as well as blanks, were generated by dilution with 40 µL of pure buffer as LC-ESI-MS/MS control. Subsequently, the generated samples were sonicated for 5 min, vortexed and finally centrifuged (15.000 g, 5 min). After transfer of the samples to a 96 deep well plate LC-ESI-MS/MS was performed with an API5500 QTrap mass spectrometer (Sciex, Darmstadt, Germany) coupled to an Agilent 1260 infinity HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) equipped with a SIL-20AHT autosampler (Shimadzu, Duisburg, Germany) and a rack changer II (Shimadzu, Duisburg, Germany) controlled by the Analyst software 1.6.3. For chromatography, a Triart C18 column (3 µm, 50 mm x 2 mm, YMC-Europe, protected with a 0.5 µm and 0.2 µm frit) as stationary phase in combination with 0.1 % (v/v) HCOOH in H2O (A) and 0.1 % (v/v) HCOOH in MeCN (B) as mobile phase under gradient elution (0-2 min: 90%A/10%B, 2-11 min: 90%A/10%B → 5%A/95%B, 11-12 min: 5%A/95%B, 12-13 min: 5%A/95%B → 90%A/10%B, 13-16 min: 90%A/10%B) was used at a flow rate of 400 µL/min, injecting 50 µL per sample. MS data acquisition was performed under positive ESI conditions in the MRM mode recording 35 at m/z 446 → 291, 53 at m/z 469 → 293 and Terfenadine at m/z 472 → 436 from 4.7 to 7.0 min. Quantification of the test compounds was based on area ratios of analyte vs internal standard.

Evaluation of TLX homodimer formation (HTRF)

Modulation of TLX LBD homodimerization was investigated by titration of sGFP-labeled TLX LBD (FRET acceptor) against a fixed concentration of Tb3+-cryptate as streptavidin conjugate (Tb-SA, 610SATLF, Revvity; FRET donor) coupled to biotinylated TLX LBD protein29, in presence of a fixed concentration of test compounds (10 µM, solubilized with DMSO) or absence of compounds as a negative control (1% DMSO). Assay solutions were prepared in HTRF assay buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM KF, 5% (m/v) glycerol, 5 mM DTT and 0.1% (m/v) CHAPS). The FRET donor complex was formed from biotinylated TLX LBD (0.5 nM) and Tb-SA (1 nM). The FRET acceptor sGFP-TLX was titrated from 1.2 µM, while free sGFP was added to keep a constant concentration of sGFP at 1.2 µM. Samples were equilibrated at room temperature for 2 h and, after excitation at 340 nm, fluorescence intensities (FI) were recorded for the sGFP acceptor at 520 nm and for the Tb-SA donor at 620 nm on a Tecan SPARK plate reader (Tecan). FI250 was divided by FI620 and multiplied by 10,000 to give a dimensionless HTRF signal. The HTRF data were normalized to control samples containing only biotinylated TLX LBD and sGFP (ΔHTRF) and fit with a one-site binding model in GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Evaluation of Microsomal Stability

Metabolic stability of 35 and 53 (final concentration 10 µM) was assessed by incubation with pooled human liver microsomes (#M0317, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt; 0.5 mg/mL) in a 100 mM potassium phosphate based buffer at pH 7.4 containing 1 mM NADPH. Each compound was tested in three independent replicates. 100 µL samples were drawn from each replicate at timepoints 0, 30, 60 and 120 minutes after onset of incubation, microsomal activity was terminated by addition of 500 µL MeCN, samples were centrifuged at 10000 g for 5 minutes, and the supernatants were aspirated. Metaboliuc degradation of 7-ethoxycoumarin (7-EC) was evaluated equally for comparison. A reaction mixture containing heat inactivated microsomes (95 °C, 10 min) served as matrix control. Concentrations of remaining compound at each timepoint were determined by LC-MS/MS on an API-3200-QTrap (Sciex) coupled with an Agilent Technologies 1100 series setup including a binary pump (G1311A), a degasser (G1322A), and a Shimadzu SIL 20A HT autosampler under the control of Analyst 1.6 (Sciex). An XBridge C18 (3.5 µm, 150 mm x 3 mm, Waters, protected with a 0.5 µm frit) stationary phase was used under isocratic conditions with 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and MeCN (B) as mobile phase (50%:50% v/v = A:B). 10 µL of aspirated supernatant diluted in mobile phase was loaded onto the column, separated at a flow rate of 400 µL/min, and detected and quantified per area of MRM (multiple reaction monitoring). Compounds were identified and quantified according to the following m/z transitions: 7-ethoxycoumarin, m/z 190.990 → m/z 163.000 (quantifier), m/z 190.990 → m/z 107.100 (qualifier); 35, m/z 445.752 → m/z 291.000 (quantifier), m/z 445.752 → m/z 264,000 (qualifier); 53, m/z 468.782 → m/z 293.000 (quantifier), m/z 468.782 → m/z 239.100 (qualifier). % remaining compound data were fit to a one-phase decay (constraint: plateau = 0) in GraphPad Prism (version 7, GraphPad software).

transcriptomic analysis of retinal pigment epithelial cells (ARPE-19)

ARPE-19 human retinal pigment epithelial cells (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) were incubated under serum-reduced conditions with vehicle control (DMSO) or 35 (1 µM) for 16 h. Three independent biological replicates were generated per condition. Cells were harvested and processed for RNA isolation immediately after incubation. RNA was isolated from cell lysates using the Maxwell RSC 48 miRNA Tissue Kit (AS1460, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the isolated RNA was assessed using a Nanodrop ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a 2100 Bioanalyzer (RNA 6000 Nano, Agilent). Libraries were prepared with the CORALL mRNA-Seq V2 Kit (Lexogen) following the manufacturer’s guidelines and paired-end sequencing was carried out on Illumina NextSeq systems. Samples were sequenced across two technical runs; corresponding FASTQ files were concatenated per sample prior to downstream processing. Adapter and quality trimming was performed using fastp (20 threads). Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38 primary assembly) using STAR in two-pass mode. Genome indices were generated from the GRCh38 primary assembly FASTA and Ensembl gene annotation (release 115) with splice junction overhang set to 99. Alignments were written as coordinate-sorted BAM files and indexed using samtools. Sequencing and alignment quality were evaluated using FastQC, MultiQC, STAR alignment statistics and Qualimap. Across all samples, a mean of 16.7 million paired-end reads per sample were obtained (range 10.9–21.1 million). On average, 80.3% of reads aligned to the reference genome (range 75.0–85.7%), with 69.7% uniquely mapped reads (range 64.9–76.1%). Average mapped read length was approximately 100–102 bp and mismatch rates were low (~1.2–1.3%). Alignment metrics were consistent across biological replicates, and no technical outliers were observed. Gene-level counts were generated from aligned BAM files using featureCounts (Subread) in paired-end mode with strand-specific counting enabled (forward-stranded; -s 1), using the Ensembl GRCh38.115 annotation and 20 processing threads. Count matrices from separate sequencing runs were combined for downstream statistical analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed in R (version 4.3.3) using DESeq2. A negative binomial generalized linear model was fitted using the design formula ~ batch + condition, where batch corresponded to sequencing run and condition corresponded to treatment. Samples from DMSO treated cells were used as the reference level. Genes with fewer than 10 total reads across all samples were filtered prior to model fitting. Wald tests were used for inference and P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. For effect-size reporting and visualization, log2 fold changes were shrunk using apeglm (lfcShrink), while statistical significance was taken from the corresponding unshrunk DESeq2 results. For sample-level visualization, count data were transformed using the DESeq2 variance stabilizing transformation (VST). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on VST-transformed expression values. Sample-to-sample distances were computed as Euclidean distances between VST profiles and visualized as a clustered heatmap. To visualize transcriptional patterns across the DMSO and 35 groups, the top 100 most variable genes (ranked by variance across the selected subset) were extracted from the VST matrix, mean-centered per gene, and displayed as a heatmap. Volcano plots were generated using shrunken log2 fold changes on the x-axis and the corresponding unshrunk adjusted p values on the y-axis. Genes meeting adjusted p value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change ≥ |0.3| as the defining inclusion criteria were considered for differential expression analysis. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) implementedin the clusterProfiler package. Genes were ranked based on the DESeq2 Wald statistic derived from the differential expression model. Ensembl gene identifiers were mapped to Entrez identifiers, and where multiple genes mapped to the same Entrez ID, the statistic with the largest absolute value was retained. Ranked gene lists were analyzed against KEGG pathway gene sets for Homo sapiens (“hsa”). Enrichment scores were normalized (NES), and p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.
