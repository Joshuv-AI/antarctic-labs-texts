Marin Vatin, Rosa Sinaasappel, Renske Kamping, Chantal Valeriani, Emanuele Locatelli, Antoine Deblais

Abstract
Active polymer translocation through confined spaces is a key biological process underlying DNA transport through nuclear pores and actin filament dynamics in cell migration. Here, we use living polymer-like Tubifex tubifex worms as a model system to explore how contour length and activity—modulated via environmental temperature—affect translocation dynamics in confined geometries. Using 3D-printed chambers connected by a narrow bridge, we track worm conformation and motion under confinement. In contrast to passive polymers, contour length has no influence, while activity strongly modulates escape dynamics. At an intermediate temperature of 20 ∘ C, an optimal balance between directed propulsion and reorientation—characterized by the Péclet number—maximizes translocation efficiency. Our findings suggest that active filaments achieve optimal exploration of confined spaces at intermediate activity levels. We further validate our results through simulations of tangentially driven polymer models which quantitatively reproduce the experimental findings at ambient temperature.

To investigate the transport properties of self-propelled, flexible filaments under confinement, we use
T. tubifex
worms—biological filaments exhibiting active polymer-like behavior—as a model system. The worms are confined in a quasi-2D “two-state” geometry consisting of two 3D-printed circular chambers connected by a narrow bridge
Brückner et al. 2019
. This minimal setup enables the collection of a statistically significant dataset, allowing us to characterize their conformation, dynamics, and translocation properties.

We find that worms stochastically hop between cavities while efficiently reptating in the connecting bridge with occasional U-turns, due to reorientation. Trapping times in the cavities are not affected by the contour length, unlike in passive polymers driven by thermal fluctuations
Muthukumar 2003
;
Wong and Muthukumar 2008
;
Mohan et al. 2010
. These observations are well captured by a tangentially driven active polymer model with homogeneous active forces
Bianco et al. 2018
, once calibrated to reproduce the worms’ conformations in unconfined conditions. Both experiments and simulations reveal a repeated alternation between compact conformations in the chambers and elongated ones in the bridge, enhancing transport via shape modulation. Finally, tuning the worm activity through environmental temperature reveals a maximum in translocation efficiency at intermediate activity levels, where a balance between rotational and translational motion facilitates cavity escape and bridge crossing.

On a free surface,
T. tubifex

\tau_{D}\sim 10
s at room temperature (20
∘
C). Their motion is quasi-two-dimensional: being denser than water, worms sediment to the bottom surface, though segments occasionally lift out of plane.

To interpret the dynamics and locomotion of the worms, we use a minimal model of a tangentially driven active polymer
Bianco et al. 2018
;
Fazelzadeh et al. 2023b
;
Fazelzadeh et al. 2023a
. The filament is represented as a semi-flexible bead-spring chain, whose contour length is comparable to the average length of the worms, with bending stiffness
\kappa
capturing its flexibility. Each bead evolves via overdamped Langevin dynamics and experiences an active force
\mathbf{f}^{a}
of constant magnitude
f^{a}
, aligned with the local tangent. An effective thermal noise models stochasticity in the worm’s motion, while gravity is included as a downward force balanced by a planar wall. This allows us to account for the occasional out-of-plane excursions of the worm’s segments.
Three parameters define our model: the active force
f^{a}
, the bending rigidity
\kappa
, and the effective temperature

. We calibrate them by comparing the distributions of the end-to-end distance
R_{e}/\ell
and the radius of gyration
R_{g}/\ell
between simulations and experiments in unconfined environment. We select the parameter set that minimizes the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between experimental and simulated statistics (see Sup. Mat., Sec. III). The optimal set -
\kappa=0.4\,u_{E}
,
k_{B}T=0.1\,u_{E}
, and
f^{a}=0.5\,u_{E}/\sigma
,
u_{E}
and
\sigma
being the units of energy and length of the model - closely matches the experimental conformations and reproduces the qualitative dynamics of isolated worms without further tuning.

This approach allows us to capture the physical behavior without relying on direct parameter extraction, which is often impractical in living systems.
We then transfer the model under confinement, using the same parameters: simulations reproduce key features observed in
T. tubifex
worms, including partial translocations where the filament folds and reverses within the bridge [Fig.

Both worms and tangentially driven active polymers sample both cavities; the asymmetry in the distribution reflects a statistical memory effect caused by initially placing the worm in one of the two chambers.

We further examine the dynamics by analyzing the translocation time, defined as the duration required for the center of mass to move from one cavity to the other. This time can be decomposed into two components:
\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}
, the average time spent in either cavity, and
\tau_{\mathrm{cr}}
, the time spent in the connecting channel. Given that the bridge width
w_{b}
is comparable to the filament width
w
, extended conformations dominate during crossing. Therefore, we focus primarily on the trapping time
\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}
in the cavities. We define
\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}
as the average time during which the filament’s center of mass,
x_{cm}
, remains within a cavity for longer than the worm’s typical timescale of fluctuations, as measured by the decorrelation time of the end-to-end autocorrelation under confinement, approximately 6 s. This criterion excludes brief excursions and ensures that only partial or full translocation events are captured.
Indeed, as noted earlier, the filament may fold and re-enter the cavity, making partial crossings common.

Interestingly, the distribution of trapping times, both in experiments and simulations, follows an exponential law [Fig.
2
(a)], in agreement with previous studies on other living worms (
L. variegatus
)
Biswas and Kudrolli 2023
and with an Arrhenius-like process observed for bacteria and active filaments escaping a pore
Bhattacharjee and Datta 2019a
;
Bhattacharjee and Datta 2019b
;
Kurzthaler et al. 2021
. In this picture, the pore acts as an entropic trap, with the polymer’s activity playing a role analogous to the thermal fluctuations.
This suggests that both the conformation and activity of the active filament govern its trapping dynamics and motivates a closer examination of how two key parameters—contour length and activity—affect filament dynamics under confinement.

To investigate the influence of contour length on the dynamics and conformations of worms under confinement, we compute trapping times for worms with different contour lengths
\ell

2

Rubinstein and Colby 2003

Bianco et al. 2018

We rationalize this observation by plotting the characteristic trapping time in the cavity as a function of

At low activity, the worm lacks sufficient drive to escape; at high activity, excessive reorientation effectively traps the worm. These results highlight how active polymers can exhibit self-trapping and emphasize the nonlinear role of activity in shaping transport through confined environments.

In conclusion, our study reveals the unique translocation dynamics of active polymer-like
T. tubifex
worms in confined geometries, demonstrating that their transport properties differ fundamentally from those of passive polymers, where increasing temperature enhances diffusion by lowering trapping times. Our results show a non-monotonic dependence of translocation efficiency on activity: an optimal intermediate activity level maximizes exploration, while both lower and higher activities hinder translocation. This effect arises from the interplay between directed motion and rotational diffusion, which together dictate the efficiency of cavity escape and bridge crossing.

By systematically comparing our experimental findings with simulations of tangentially driven active polymers, we establish that homogeneously distributed active forces accurately capture the observed behaviors.
Additionally, our results indicate that contour length has a negligible impact on translocation, further emphasizing activity as the dominant control parameter.
This validates our approach and supports the use of tangentially active polymers to describe living worm dynamics and, in turn, to address universal aspects of active polymer transport in confinement with living worms.

Beyond characterizing the behaviour of active polymers in a two-state system, our findings have broader implications for understanding transport in confined environments. The existence of an optimal activity level suggests a general strategy for enhancing translocation efficiency in active polymer systems, which could inspire biomimetic designs for synthetic active filaments. Future studies could extend this work to more complex confinements, such as disordered environments or structured microfluidic networks, to further explore how activity-driven transport mechanisms are shaped by geometry, boundary interactions and under external potentials such as flow current or electrical field.
