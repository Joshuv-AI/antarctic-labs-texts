Achilles Tendon Injuries in Major League Soccer: A 10-year Analysis of Injury Rate, Return to Play, and Performance Metrics
Authors: Brian Forsythe, Catherine Hand, Francis Hand, Henry Eilen, Matthew Varano, Jared Sasaki, Camden Bohn, Anne Timmermann, Lauren Fertig, Eric Giza, Robert H Brophy, Bert Mandelbaum, Kirk McCullough
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Injuries to the Achilles tendon, such as tears or ruptures, are commonly observed among elite athletes. These injuries can be debilitating for professional soccer players, leading to prolonged recovery and potential performance declines.Purpose:To investigate the annual incidence, return-to-play (RTP) duration, and postinjury performance outcomes of Major League Soccer (MLS) players who sustained Achilles tendon injuries from 2010 to 2021.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:This retrospective matched-cohort study evaluated MLS players with Achilles tendon injuries from 2010 to 2021, identified through the MLS Injury Surveillance database, and compared outcomes, including RTP duration, between players with ruptured and nonruptured (soreness, contusion, inflammation, irritation, sprain, tendinosis, tendinitis) injuries. Uninjured controls were matched 2:1 by age, position, and MLS experience. Annual injury incidence, RTP duration, and performance metrics, including games played, minutes played, goals, assists, and defensive contributions, were compared between injured and control cohorts. Multivariate linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors of RTP duration, adjusting for age, player position, injury type, mechanism of onset, field type, weather condition, and injury period. Statistical analyses set significance at P≤ .05.Results:The mean RTP for ruptured Achilles injuries (n = 20) was 229 days, with no significant difference between 2010-2015 and 2016-2021 (P = .718). Nonruptured injuries (n = 352) had a significantly shorter RTP of 14.9 days versus rupture injuries with an RTP of 229 days (P < .001). Player age was significantly associated with RTP time, with younger players (≤26 years) exhibiting prolonged RTP durations (303.56 ± 112.97 days) compared to older players (>26 years) (168.09 ± 99.66 days; P = .017). There was no difference in RTP with field type (artificial turf vs grass, P = .59). Reinjury rates increased over time but were not statistically significant (P = .77). Following multivariate analysis, injury type was the only statistically significant factor affecting RTP duration, with ruptured Achilles injuries associated with significantly increased time missed compared to nonrupture injuries (95% CI, 171.15-264.11 days; P < .001). Forwards with ruptured Achilles injuries exhibited a decline in games played in the index year (−9.00 ± 8.49 vs 3.00 ± 1.73 games; P = .041). Midfielders with ruptured Achilles injuries demonstrated a significantly greater increase in goals than controls in the index year (1.00 ± 1.41 vs −0.43 ± 0.25 goals; P = .004) and a smaller decline in assists than controls (−0.50 ± 2.83 vs −1.00 ± 2.31 assists; P = .024) relative to preinjury performance. Among goalkeepers, rupture injuries were significantly associated with declines in games played in the first year postinjury (−20.50 ± 16.26 vs 1.00 ± 1.26 games; P = .050) and in the second year postinjury (−31.00 ± 4.24 vs −3.50 ± 5.85 games; P = .006). Rupture injuries were associated with a greater decline in clean sheets in the first year postinjury (−6.01 ± 0.71 vs −0.75 ± 2.99 clean sheets; P = .041) and second year postinjury (−5.98 ± 0.02 vs −2.50 ± 2.08 clean sheets; P = .024).Conclusion:Our study demonstrated that Achilles tendon ruptures in MLS players were associated with substantial recovery burden and long-term performance declines, particularly among goalkeepers. These findings suggest a potential need for position-specific rehabilitation strategies and structured RTP protocols aimed at optimizing recovery and performance retention in professional soccer players.Keywords: Achilles tendon rupture, return to play, Major League Soccer, performance metrics

The Achilles tendon, a vital component of the musculoskeletal system, connects the calf muscles to the calcaneus, playing a crucial role in activities that involve running, jumping, and other dynamic movements. Injuries to the Achilles tendon, such as tears or ruptures, are commonly observed among athletes and can significantly impair mobility and performance. The annual incidence of Achilles tendon injuries is notably higher in sports that demand sudden acceleration, deceleration, and frequent directional changes. 15 These injuries vary in severity from mild strains to complete ruptures, each presenting with their own recovery challenges.13,15,25

Achilles injuries most frequently occur via noncontact mechanisms, with explosive movements such as abrupt increases in ankle dorsiflexion commonly implicated. 21 Athletes who routinely perform such aggressive maneuvers are at elevated risk of sustaining Achilles injuries, particularly among sports including basketball, soccer, American football, and tennis.15,21,23 Such injuries not only threaten an athlete's immediate ability to participate in their sport but also pose long-term challenges to their career longevity and performance level.1,14,16 Additionally, athletes may develop compensatory movement patterns following injury, potentially increasing their risk of subsequent knee injuries and further complicating their rehabilitation. 27

Nonruptured Achilles tendon injuries, including tendinopathy, contusions, and strains, are prevalent among elite athletes and can significantly impair performance and limit training availability despite being less severe compared to ruptured tendon injuries. These injuries are often the result of cumulative mechanical overload, repetitive microtrauma, or acute strain and typically present with localized pain, stiffness, and impaired function.18,25 Diagnosis relies on clinical assessment supported by imaging, particularly ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging when structural pathology is suspected. Management is largely nonoperative, involving eccentric strengthening, load management, manual therapy, and activity modification. 18 Despite their noncatastrophic classification, nonruptured Achilles injuries can result in recurrent symptoms, missed competition time, and compromised performance if not properly addressed. In the context of professional soccer, where explosive movements, rapid accelerations, and frequent directional changes are routine, the Achilles tendon is subjected to considerable biomechanical stress, increasing susceptibility to such overuse injuries.4,12 Moreover, prolonged or poorly managed symptoms can predispose athletes to more severe pathologies, including tendon rupture, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and targeted intervention.5,12,19,20

The implications of Achilles tendon ruptures on return to play (RTP) are profound, especially for professional athletes whose careers and livelihoods depend on their physical performance.7,11 The competitive and financial pressures to return swiftly may contribute to premature RTP and a higher risk of injury recurrence. While there is substantial research on RTP and performance metrics in sports such as basketball and American football, data on soccer, particularly within Major League Soccer (MLS), remain sparse.3,14,16

As professional soccer continues to grow in the United States and globally, understanding the impact of Achilles tendon injuries on performance post-RTP becomes increasingly important. Given these circumstances, there are crucial questions regarding whether professional soccer players can restore their preinjury performance levels following an Achilles tendon injury. This study aims to fill this gap by comparing the timing of RTP and the performance metrics of elite soccer players post-RTP from Achilles tendon injuries with those of matched, uninjured controls. It is hypothesized that players who were able to return to match participation will exhibit comparable performance metrics to uninjured peers within 3 years postinjury.7,11,26

Methods

This retrospective matched-cohort study examined male soccer players who sustained an Achilles tendon injury while competing in MLS from 2010 to 2021. An Achilles tendon injury was defined as any incident involving the Achilles tendon that necessitated medical intervention. All injured players were identified via the MLS Injury Surveillance database, which tracks injuries within the MLS and is used by all MLS teams. Uninjured players were sourced from a publicly available database, mlssoccer.com. Injury and player performance data were manually entered and cross-verified by 3 authors. Players were separated into 2 cohorts: ruptured Achilles injury and nonruptured Achilles injury. Nonruptured Achilles injury included the following: soreness, contusion, inflammation, irritation, sprain, tendinosis, tendinitis, and tendinopathy. Nonruptured injuries were grouped to reflect how diagnoses were recorded in the MLS surveillance system, where further differentiation between tendinopathy and minor trauma may be inconsistent and not standardized. Information regarding operative or nonoperative treatment of Achilles tendon ruptures was not readily available in the MLS Injury Surveillance database. All cases were recorded as having been evaluated by team physicians, but specific treatment modalities, including surgical repair, were not available for analysis.

Player demographics, injury characteristics, and performance metrics were obtained from official MLS records. Demographic variables included age, playing position, team at the time of injury, and MLS experience. Injury details covered the year of injury, playing surface (natural grass vs artificial turf), injury context (match vs training), location (home vs away), player age at the time of injury, weather conditions during injury, and the RTP date. Performance metrics both pre- and post-injury were collected, including games played, games started, minutes played, goals scored, assists, pass accuracy, interceptions, clean sheets, and goals conceded, depending on the player's position.

RTP was defined as the date of first MLS match appearance following injury and represents a participation-based milestone rather than full functional recovery, medical clearance criteria, or biomechanical readiness. Reinjury rates were calculated as the percentage of players experiencing a second Achilles injury within 2 months of the initial injury, following the definition by Ekstrand. 6 These rates were analyzed for 2 periods: 2010-2015 and 2016-2021. Injury rates were determined by dividing the number of unique players with an Achilles injury each year by the total number of MLS players that year, with mean injury rates calculated for each cohort. Player-level training and match exposure hours were not available in the MLS Injury Surveillance dataset accessible for this study; therefore, annual injury proportions were calculated by dividing the number of unique rostered MLS players sustaining an Achilles tendon injury in a given season by the total number of rostered MLS players that season rather than athlete-exposure incidence rates (e.g., injuries per 1000 player-hours), consistent with prior studies.8-10 These proportions describe the seasonal burden of injury among players rather than exposure-adjusted injury risk. The average RTP durations were measured as the mean number of days missed due to injury for each cohort, and comparisons were made between the 2 time periods. A multivariate linear regression analysis was performed on the full cohort of players with ruptured and nonruptured Achilles injuries to evaluate the association between clinical and environmental factors and RTP duration, including age, player position, injury type, mechanism of onset, field type, weather condition, and injury period.

A matched-cohort analysis compared performance metrics between injured and uninjured players from 2010 to 2021. Uninjured players were matched to injured players in a 2:1 ratio based on position, age (±2 years), season (±1 year), and MLS experience (±1 year). Performance metrics at 1, 2, and 3 years after the index year (year of injury) were compared to metrics from 1 season prior to the index year. Changes in performance metrics were calculated as delta values for comparison. Player position was considered to account for varying roles in performance. Statistical comparisons of injury rates, RTP durations, and reinjury rates between the 2 periods (2010-2015 vs 2016-2021) were performed. Additionally, performance metrics for injured players and their matched controls were compared using independent t tests for continuous variables that were normally distributed. Injury rates and RTP durations were reported as means ± standard deviations. Statistical significance was established at P≤ .05 for all comparisons, and analyses were conducted using STATA (Stata Statistical Software: Release 17; StataCorp LLC, 2021).

Results

Return to Play Following Ruptured Achilles Injuries

RTP following ruptured Achilles tendon injuries (n = 20) averaged 218.2 ± 97.8 and 239.9 ± 158.6 days for 2010-2015 and 2016-2021, respectively. RTP duration did not differ significantly by year of injury, playing surface, or injury setting (P > .05, Table 1). Although positional differences were not statistically significant, forwards demonstrated a notable trend toward faster RTP (145.5 ± 105.8 days) relative to midfielders (265.0 ± 240.1 days) and defenders (254.2 ± 122.3 days) (P = .149). Player age was significantly associated with recovery duration, as younger athletes (≤26 years) required substantially longer RTP times compared to their older counterparts (303.6 ± 113.0 vs 168.1 ± 99.7 days; P = .017). Weather conditions at the time of injury were not significantly associated with RTP (Table 1).

Return to Play Following Nonruptured Achilles Injuries

RTP following nonruptured Achilles tendon injuries (n = 352) was substantially shorter than for ruptures, with a mean duration of 14.9 ± 65.9 days. RTP trended toward being longer in the later study period (2010-2015: 9.23 ± 28.1 days vs 2016-2021: 19.69 ± 78.4 days), although this did not reach statistical significance (P = .094). Field type, player position, injury setting, and player age were not significantly associated with RTP duration. However, weather conditions at the time of injury significantly influenced recovery, with injuries sustained in sunny, clear, or cloudy conditions resulting in longer RTP durations (14.86 ± 64.82 days) compared to those sustained in rainy or snowy conditions (4.00 ± 10.32 days; P = .019). Additionally, injury mechanism significantly influenced RTP, with players who sustained acute contact injuries returning significantly sooner (4.64 ± 10.68 days) than those with acute noncontact injuries (22.00 ± 50.63 days; P = .031). Chronic or gradual noncontact injuries were associated with intermediate RTP times (15.09 ± 72.01 days), although differences between these and acute contact injuries were not statistically significant (Table 1).

Injury and Reinjury Rates

The overall annual incidence of Achilles tendon injuries decreased over time, with a statistically significant decline observed for nonruptured injuries (5.99% in 2010-2015 vs 4.80% in 2016-2021; P = .002). Although the injury rate for ruptured Achilles injuries also declined (0.369% to 0.292%), this change was not statistically significant (P = .189). RTP durations were longer in the later cohort for both injury types, but these differences did not reach statistical significance.

RTP following a second Achilles injury showed a nonsignificant decrease for ruptured injuries between 2010-2015 and 2016-2021 (242.0 ± 11.3 vs 152.7 ± 81.3 days; P = .239). In contrast, RTP for nonruptured reinjuries remained stable across time periods (P = .973) (Table 2).

Multivariate Analysis

In the multivariate linear regression analysis examining factors associated with RTP duration, rupture injuries were significantly associated with increased time missed compared to nonrupture injuries. Specifically, rupture injuries were associated with an increase of 217.63 days missed (95% CI, 171.15-264.11 days, P < .001). No other variables were statistically significant predictors of RTP duration in this model (Table 3).

Performance Metrics Following Achilles Injuries

Forwards Analysis

For players who sustained ruptured Achilles injuries, a significant reduction in games played was observed in the index year compared to controls (−9.00 ± 8.49 vs 3.00 ± 1.73 games; P = .041). However, no significant differences were noted in the first year after injury (−7.00 ± 11.31 vs 7.00 ± 3.00 games; P = .066) or subsequent years. In contrast, players with nonruptured Achilles injuries did not demonstrate a significant difference in games played in the index year or subsequent years, except in the third year postinjury, where a significant decline was noted (−3.64 ± 12.25 vs 6.23 ± 5.83 games; P = .018).

Among forwards, neither ruptured nor nonruptured Achilles injuries were associated with statistically significant differences in minutes played, goals scored, or assists across any time point. Although trends toward reduced playing time were observed following ruptured injuries, particularly in the index and first postinjury years, these did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, minor declines in third-year playing time and assists were noted in both injury groups but without meaningful differences compared to controls (Table 4).

Midfielders Analysis

For ruptured Achilles injuries, no significant difference in games played was observed in the index year compared to controls (P = .556), and although a reduction was noted in the first postinjury year (−6.00), statistical analysis was not possible due to the small sample size. Among players with nonruptured injuries, games played were comparable to controls in the index and first postinjury years. However, in the second postinjury year, midfielders with nonruptured Achilles injuries played significantly more games relative to controls (2.52 ± 8.61 vs −1.89 ± 10.43 games; P = .046).

Ruptured Achilles injuries were not associated with significant differences in minutes played during the index year, and a reduction observed in the first postinjury year could not be statistically assessed due to limited sample size. In the nonruptured cohort, no significant differences in minutes played were observed across all time points.

Midfielders with ruptured Achilles injuries demonstrated a significantly greater change in goals scored relative to controls in the index year (1.00 ± 1.41 vs −0.43 ± 0.25 goals; P = .004). Although goal production declined in the first postinjury year, no statistical comparison was possible due to limited sample size. No significant differences in goals scored were observed at any time point among players with nonruptured injuries.

Midfielders with ruptured Achilles injuries demonstrated a significantly smaller decline in assists compared with controls in the index year (−0.50 ± 2.83 vs −1.00 ± 2.31 assists; P = .024). Although assist numbers declined further in the first postinjury year, due to sample size constraints, no statistical analysis was possible. For nonruptured injuries, no significant differences in assists were observed at any time point (Table 5).

Defenders Analysis

Among defenders with ruptured Achilles injuries, no statistically significant differences were observed in games played, minutes played, goals scored, or assists at any time point. A trend toward reduced participation persisted across all metrics, particularly in the first and third years postinjury, but none reached statistical significance. Similarly, nonruptured Achilles injuries were not associated with significant changes in games played, minutes, or goals scored. However, in the index year, injured defenders demonstrated a significantly greater increase in assists than controls (0.39 ± 2.10 vs 0.14 ± 1.51 assists; P = .050), although this difference did not persist in subsequent seasons (Table 6).

Goalkeepers Analysis

Goalkeepers with ruptured Achilles injuries experienced a significant reduction in games played in both the first (−20.50 ± 16.26 vs 1.00 ± 1.26 games; P = .050) and second (−31.00 ± 4.24 vs −3.50 ± 5.85 games; P = .006) years postinjury. This decline was no longer significant by the third year. Nonruptured Achilles injuries were not associated with any significant changes in games played across all time points.

Ruptured Achilles injuries showed a significant reduction in minutes played during both the first year (−1902.5 ± 1499.77 vs 92.25 ± 130.11 minutes; P = .049) and second year (−2790.00 ± 381.84 vs −316.00 ± 528.98 minutes; P = .007) following injury. While a decrease persisted into the third year, it did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, nonruptured Achilles injuries were not associated with any significant differences in minutes played across all observed time points.

Ruptured Achilles injuries in goalkeepers were associated with a marked decline in clean sheet performance during the first 2 years postinjury, with a significantly greater decline in clean sheets recorded in the first year postinjury (–6.01 ± 0.71 vs −0.75 ± 2.99 clean sheets; P = .041) and second year postinjury (−5.98 ± 0.02 vs −2.50 ± 2.08 clean sheets; P = .024) compared to controls. By the third year, clean sheet counts were comparable between groups. In contrast, nonruptured Achilles injuries did not result in any significant differences in clean sheets at any observed time point.

Goalkeepers with ruptured Achilles injuries experienced a significantly greater reduction in goal conceded in the second year postinjury compared to controls (−44.50 ± 8.48 vs 5.50 ± 10.71 goals; P = .009). No significant differences were observed in the index year, first year, or third year postinjury. Nonruptured Achilles injuries were not associated with any significant differences in goals conceded across all time points (Table 7).

Discussion

The major findings of this study demonstrate that Achilles tendon ruptures in MLS players are associated with significantly prolonged RTP (229 days) compared to nonrupture injuries (14.9 days). Injury type was the only independent predictor of RTP on multivariate analysis and was also associated with position-specific performance declines, particularly among forwards and goalkeepers. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of RTP outcomes following Achilles tendon injuries among MLS athletes. While previous research has extensively examined Achilles injuries in other professional sports, such as basketball and American football, limited data exist regarding their impact on soccer players, particularly within the MLS context.14,16 Our findings suggest that Achilles ruptures may have career implications, with notable position-specific differences in recovery and long-term performance outcomes.

Influence of Injury Mechanism on RTP and Recovery

Our findings highlight the impact of injury mechanism on RTP outcomes. Noncontact Achilles injuries required significantly longer RTP durations compared to contact injuries. This distinction likely reflects underlying pathophysiologic differences, where noncontact injuries may be associated with chronic tendon degeneration or greater structural disruption, necessitating prolonged recovery.13,15,25 These results underscore the need for targeted injury prevention strategies, particularly for players at high risk of noncontact Achilles injuries due to repetitive stress and load accumulation.

The lack of a statistically significant difference in RTP duration between the first (2010-2015) and second (2016-2021) halves of the study period suggests that the recovery timeline for Achilles injuries in professional soccer players has remained relatively stable. However, the decreasing annual incidence of nonruptured Achilles injuries over time suggests that injury prevention efforts may be mitigating certain Achilles-related pathologies, potentially through improved load management, training regimens, and sports science interventions.6,17,28 Although most variables did not independently influence RTP in the multivariate linear regression analysis, injury type (ruptured vs nonruptured) was a statistically significant predictor of RTP, as expected given the severity of the injury. In the absence of biomechanical, physiological, or workload data, the observed associations between RTP and contextual variables such as surface type, weather conditions, and injury onset should be interpreted descriptively and are not intended to imply causal relationships. Rather, they may reflect unmeasured contextual or clinical factors and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Return to Play and Performance Impacts

The results indicate that Achilles tendon ruptures impose a significant recovery burden, with a mean RTP duration of approximately 229 days. This aligns with prior research in professional athletes, where RTP timelines following Achilles tendon ruptures often range between 6 and 12 months.11,26 However, our data reveal positional disparities in postinjury performance, particularly in forwards and goalkeepers.

Forwards who sustained Achilles tendon ruptures exhibited a decline in games played per season the year they were injured. Forwards who had non-ruptured injuries showed a significant decline in games played per season 3 years postinjury, in contrast to matched controls who demonstrated improvements over time. This suggests that Achilles injuries may result in lingering deficits affecting play. 20 These findings are consistent with prior research in elite soccer and basketball players, in whom lower extremity injuries, particularly those affecting tendons, were associated with declines in performance.14,16,26

Goalkeepers demonstrated the most pronounced changes in performance following Achilles ruptures, as evidenced by significant reductions in games played, minutes played, and clean sheets recorded in the first and second years postinjury. Given the biomechanical demands of goalkeeping, requiring frequent explosive vertical and lateral movements, diving saves, and rapid directional changes, the Achilles tendon plays a crucial role in maintaining both agility and responsiveness. The observed decline in postinjury performance reinforces the notion that Achilles injuries may disproportionately affect goalkeepers compared to other positions.2,4

Similar trends have been reported in prior studies examining lower extremity injuries in professional soccer goalkeepers. Hoenig et al 12 noted that goalkeepers most commonly sustained Achilles injuries during explosive starting movements and landing, mechanisms associated with prolonged RTP durations. These findings may underscore the importance of tailored rehabilitation protocols that address the unique biomechanical demands placed on goalkeepers to optimize postinjury recovery and mitigate performance decline.

Impact of Ruptured Achilles Injuries in Midfielders

Although the sample size of midfielders with Achilles ruptures was limited, most of these players did not return to play beyond the index year of injury. This observation suggests that a ruptured Achilles tendon may represent a potentially devastating injury for midfielders. The high physical demands of this position, including frequent accelerations, decelerations, and sustained aerobic activity, may exacerbate the functional deficits following an Achilles rupture.2,22 Previous literature on Achilles tendon injuries in professional soccer players has demonstrated prolonged RTP times and variable performance outcomes postinjury.7,14 For instance, Trofa et al 26 reported that only 70.8% of professional soccer players returned to play after Achilles tendon repair, with nearly 30% unable to resume their preinjury game minutes. In contrast, Grassi et al 11 found a higher RTP rate of 96% among professional football players but noted that 18% did not return to their preinjury level of play within 2 seasons.

These findings align with research indicating that midfielders rely heavily on sustained endurance, which may be compromised following an Achilles rupture.4,22 Similar trends have been observed following other lower extremity injuries in MLS, where midfielders may have lower postinjury participation rates due to the high physiological demands of their role.2,9 The present study adds to this growing body of evidence by suggesting that Achilles tendon ruptures may similarly impair midfielders’ ability to sustain professional-level play, necessitating further investigation into rehabilitation strategies tailored to position-specific demands. 7

Clinical and Practical Implications

The findings of this study suggest implications for sports medicine professionals, athletic trainers, and team physicians. Given the potentially long-term impact of Achilles ruptures, particularly for midfielders and goalkeepers, rehabilitation protocols should emphasize tendon-loading optimization, eccentric strengthening, and position-specific functional retraining.20,24 The observed performance deficits in goalkeepers further suggest that postinjury rehabilitation programs should integrate sport-specific drills to restore explosiveness and agility, potentially mitigating long-term declines in goal-scoring ability.2,20,24

Moreover, the increasing reinjury rates, albeit not statistically significant, suggest the need for structured RTP criteria19,29 that extend beyond basic physical recovery to include biomechanical and neuromuscular assessments. Future research should focus on refining RTP guidelines to reduce reinjury risk and enhance long-term performance sustainability.

Limitations

This study has several notable limitations. First, its retrospective design relies on historical data through the search engine, which may introduce inaccuracies in injury reporting and RTP timelines. Although surface type, weather conditions, and injury mechanism were included as covariates in multivariable regression, the absence of biomechanical, physiological, and workload data in the database limits the ability to support mechanistic interpretation, and observed associations may reflect residual confounding or statistical artifacts. Additionally, the injury rate calculations did not account for variables such as player exposure hours or the number of games played per season, which may affect injury incidence. Functional recovery, reinjury clearance criteria, and medical clearance protocols were not available in the database and therefore could not be incorporated into RTP definitions or analyses. Athlete-exposure data (training and match player-hours) were unavailable, precluding calculation of exposure-adjusted incidence rates. Instead, player-based seasonal proportions were calculated, which quantify league-level injury burden but limit direct comparison with studies using player-hour denominators. Further, the nonruptured Achilles group included a heterogeneous set of clinically recorded diagnoses, which may contribute to variability in RTP; however, further subclassification was not feasible without introducing subjective reinterpretation of diagnosis labels.

The exclusive focus on MLS players may also limit the generalizability of these findings to other leagues or sports with different physical demands or injury management strategies. Despite efforts to minimize confounding through the matching process, unmeasured factors such as access to medical care, player fitness, and individualized rehabilitation programs may still influence the results. Due to limitations of the data source and the 2:1 matching methodology, factors such as team affiliation, starter vs substitute status, injury history, and minutes played preinjury were also unable to be controlled for, allowing for the potential of residual confounding. Because tactical role assignments, substitutions, match exposure, and team-level defensive or offensive quality were not available in the database, performance findings that appear inconsistent (eg, changes in goals but not assists or clean sheets vs goals conceded) cannot be mechanistically interpreted.

Subgroup analyses stratified by player position and injury type were limited by small sample sizes, particularly among players with ruptured Achilles injuries, where some positional categories included fewer than 5 athletes. These analyses are therefore underpowered for detecting modest effects, and any observed associations should be interpreted as descriptive rather than definitive.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that Achilles tendon ruptures in MLS players were associated with substantial recovery burden and long-term performance declines, particularly among goalkeepers. These findings suggest a potential need for position-specific rehabilitation strategies and structured RTP protocols aimed at optimizing recovery and performance retention in professional soccer players.
