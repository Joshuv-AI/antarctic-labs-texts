In the setting of tricuspid valve repair or replacement, should epicardial pacing leads be favoured over transvenous leads?
Authors: Lucy Manuel, Levi Bassin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A best evidence topic in cardiac surgery was written according to a structured protocol. The question addressed “In patients undergoing tricuspid valve repair or replacement, should epicardial pacing leads be favored over transvenous leads?” A total of 46 papers were identified, of which 9 represented the best available evidence, including observational cohort studies and registry analyses. Key outcomes included tricuspid regurgitation (TR) severity, lead failure, prosthetic dysfunction, need for reintervention, mortality and long-term device reliability, with TR severity and valve-related reinterventions considered the primary clinically meaningful endpoints. In patients undergoing tricuspid valve replacement, there was a trend towards increased prosthetic valve dysfunction with transvenous leads. Epicardial lead placement, despite lower long-term durability and higher lead thresholds, avoids direct leaflet interaction and therefore represents a potential strategy in selected patients undergoing tricuspid valve replacement, although current evidence is insufficient to support formal recommendations. Larger prospective studies are required to establish guideline-level recommendations.

Clinical scenario

A 68-year-old patient with torrential tricuspid regurgitation (TR) presented for elective tricuspid valve intervention. The patient has long-standing atrial fibrillation with difficult-to-manage tachy-brady syndrome that may require long-term permanent pacing. A colleague suggests placing epicardial pacing leads during the index procedure, particularly if a bioprosthesis is selected, to reduce the risk of TR and mechanical interference that may result from placing a transvenous lead. You resolve to review the scientific literature to inform your plan.

Three-part question

In (patients undergoing tricuspid valve repair or replacement), do (epicardial pacing leads) result in fewer long-term complications than (transvenous pacing leads)?

Search strategy

A literature search was performed in the PubMed database (from 1987 to the present) using the following terms: [tricuspid valve (MeSH Terms) OR tricuspid valve repair OR tricuspid valve replacement OR TVR OR TVr] AND (epicardial lead OR epicardial pacing OR myocardial pacing) AND (transvenous lead OR transvenous pacing OR pacemaker lead OR defibrillator lead) AND (tricuspid regurgitation OR valve dysfunction OR valve failure OR reintervention). Only papers published in English were reviewed. The reference lists of the initially identified papers were searched for relevant studies.

Search outcome

A total of 46 papers were found through the reported search. Of these, nine studies were deemed to provide the best evidence to answer the question. Results

The results of the nine papers (eight retrospective cohort studies and one retrospective comparative study) are summarized in Table 1. For this synthesis, TR severity and freedom from valve-related reintervention were considered the primary clinically meaningful endpoints, with mortality, lead performance and prosthetic dysfunction treated as secondary outcomes. The included studies are heterogeneous with respect to the patient population (repair versus replacement, functional versus structural TR, lead-induced versus pacemaker-associated TR), pacing indication, surgical era and follow-up duration. Direct pooling of results is not appropriate, and findings should be interpreted with this variability in mind.

Discussion

Mazine et al[2] performed a retrospective cohort study including 791 patients undergoing tricuspid valve repair for functional TR. Of these, 176 (22%) had transvenous pacing leads. During a mean follow-up of 5.8 ± 5.5 years, the presence of a transvenous pacing lead was an independent predictor of recurrent ≥ moderate 2 + TR [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.60, P = 0.008] and ≥ severe 3 + TR (HR = 1.47, P = 0.046). Leads were also associated with increased late mortality (HR = 2.12, P = 0.02). Although tricuspid reoperation rates were lower in the paced group (1.5% vs. 8%, P = 0.04), the authors attributed this to the prohibitively high operative risk of redo tricuspid valve surgery rather than to a lower burden of recurrent TR.

Pfannmueller et al[3] conducted a retrospective study including 116 patients undergoing tricuspid valve repair in the presence of permanent pacemaker leads, with a mean follow-up of 19.4 ± 20.3 months. Lead-related leaflet injury was identified intraoperatively in 8 patients (7%), highlighting the potential for pacing leads to interfere with valve function. The 30-day mortality was 14.6%, and the 5-year survival was 45%. Four patients required reoperation, resulting in freedom from reoperation of 93.4% at 5 years [95% confidence interval (CI): 88.2–97.7].

Ratschiller et al[4] performed a larger retrospective cohort study including 415 patients undergoing tricuspid valve annuloplasty for functional TR, of whom 112 (27%) had transvalvular pacemaker leads. Follow-up was 94% complete, with a mean duration of 24.4 months. The 30-day mortality was 7.5%. The grade of TR improved significantly from 2.47 ± 0.52 preoperatively to 0.70 ± 0.54 at the time of discharge (P < 0.001). Residual TR ≥2+ was present in 7.1% of patients. At 5 years, freedom from recurrent TR ≥2+ was 86.7% ± 3.2%, with freedom from reoperation of 98.1% ± 0.8%. The presence of a transvalvular pacemaker lead was not associated with recurrent TR, reoperation, or mortality on multivariate analysis.

Huang et al[5] performed a retrospective cohort study of 444 patients undergoing tricuspid valve surgery in the presence of transvenous pacing leads, with a median follow-up of 9.7 years for non-lead-induced TR and 8.5 years for lead-induced TR. Patients were classified into non-lead-induced TR (n = 174, 39%) and lead-induced TR (n = 270, 61%). With propensity matching, lead-induced TR was not associated with increased mortality (P = 0.73). However, tricuspid valve replacement (TVR) was associated with worse survival (HR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.13–2.25, P = 0.008). Five-year freedom from tricuspid valve reintervention was 100% in the non-lead-induced group compared with 92.3% in the lead-induced group (P = 0.005). TR improved significantly following surgery in both groups (P < 0.001), although in patients undergoing valve repair, regurgitation severity increased over time.

Eleid et al[6] performed a retrospective cohort study to determine the impact of transvalvular pacemaker leads on bioprosthetic tricuspid valve function. The study included 58 patients with bioprosthetic TVR who subsequently underwent lead insertion, compared with 265 control patients. During a mean follow-up of 25 months, significant bioprosthetic TR developed in 5 patients with transvalvular leads (9%) vs. 12 patients (5%) in the control group (P = 0.20). Significant prosthetic regurgitation occurred 2 years or later in 80% of patients with leads and 83% of control patients, demonstrating no significant association between transvenous leads and prosthetic TR.

Shafquat et al[7] conducted a retrospective comparative study including 46 patients undergoing bioprosthetic TVR, comparing transvenous and epicardial pacing strategies. During a mean follow-up of 5.5 years, prosthetic valve dysfunction, defined as moderate or severe TR or stenosis, occurred in 6 patients (30%) in the transvenous group vs. 5 patients (19.2%) in the epicardial group, although this did not reach statistical significance. Valve dysfunction was significantly more common in patients with sinus rhythm compared with atrial fibrillation (50% vs. 10%, P = 0.004). Epicardial pacing leads were associated with higher pacing thresholds.

Bazire et al[8] reviewed outcomes in 80 patients undergoing TVR with prophylactic epicardial lead placement, with a mean follow-up of 36 months. Of these, 28 patients underwent isolated TVR. Overall, 46% of patients required cardiac pacing, including 14% with pacing dependency, 17% with high-degree atrioventricular block, and 15% with a ventricular pacing burden of more than 80%. Epicardial pacemaker complications occurred in 2 patients (2.5%). A spontaneous heart rate of >70 (P = 0.02) and a narrow QRS (P = 0.03) were associated with a lower risk of pacing requirement.

Saran et al[9] performed a retrospective cohort study including 622 patients undergoing tricuspid valve surgery in the presence of permanent pacemakers. Patients were classified as pacemaker-associated TR (n = 349, 58%) and pacemaker-induced TR (n = 249, 42%). The most common mechanism of lead-induced TR was restricted leaflet mobility (41%), followed by leaflet adherence (37%), leaflet perforation (12%), scarring (8%), and chordal entrapment (7%). The 30-day mortality was significantly lower in the pacemaker-induced group (4.4% vs. 9.5%; P = 0.023). During a median follow-up of 7.92 years, pacemaker-induced TR was associated with improved survival (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.64– 0.98, P = 0.033), and TVR was more frequently performed in this group (58.6% vs. 38.4%; P < 0.001). TVR in this group was independently associated with increased mortality (HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.20–1.87, P < 0.001).

The conflicting findings across the included studies merit critical appraisal. Mazine et al[2] demonstrated that transvenous leads independently predicted recurrent TR and mortality following repair, whereas Ratschiller et al[4] found no such association in a similarly sized cohort. These discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in patient selection, repair technique, and the proportion of patients with pre-existing leads versus peri-procedural leads. The apparent survival benefit of lead-induced TR observed by Saran et al[9] is counterintuitive but may reflect that lead-induced TR represents a more discrete, potentially correctable pathology compared with the advanced biventricular remodeling underlying pacemaker-associated TR in their cohort, rather than a true causal relationship. Selection bias is also an important confounder throughout and should be acknowledged when applying the evidence to individual clinical decisions. Patients selected for epicardial leads at the time of surgery may represent a higher-risk group with greater anticipated pacing requirements than those managed without prophylactic epicardial systems.

Clinical bottom line

Direct comparative evidence between epicardial and transvenous pacing strategies in the setting of tricuspid valve intervention is limited to a single underpowered retrospective study, which represents the central gap identified. Findings across the broader literature are inconsistent, likely reflecting patient selection differences and confounding by indication rather than true causal effects. Transvenous leads may contribute to recurrent TR following repair and prosthetic dysfunction following replacement, particularly with bioprosthetic valves, but neither association is sufficiently robust to support a formal recommendation. Epicardial leads avoid direct leaflet interaction at the cost of higher pacing thresholds and inferior long-term lead durability, a trade-off that is clinically relevant in pacing-dependent patients. An individualized approach incorporating the valve type, anticipated pacing burden, and patient-specific anatomy is suggested. Prospective comparative studies with standardized endpoints are required before guideline-level recommendations can be made.
