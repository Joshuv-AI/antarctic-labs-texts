The Impossibility of Cohesion Without Fragmentation
Daisuke Hirota

Abstract
Why do division and cohesion so often intensify together? This paper provides a structural answer by developing a static theory of relation maintenance based on minimal positional constraints. We model social relations as subject to minimum conditions defined over an abstract position space. A bifurcation event—such as a vote, declaration, or institutional assignment— fixes agents’ positions and thereby determines which relations are compatible. Within this framework, we identify position-dependent gain axes as the key structural source of selective relational survival. The main result establishes a structural necessity: for any bifurcation event under a non-degenerate positional constraint, the post-event network necessarily exhibits either the simultaneous emergence of fragmentation and cohesion, or degenerate triviality. In the non-degenerate case, fragmentation and cohesion are not merely correlated, but arise as dual consequences of the same positional constraint. The analysis is purely structural. It abstracts from preferences, beliefs, incentives, and dynamic adjustment, and relies on no probabilistic or behavioral assumptions. By isolating the minimal conditions under which division and cohesion must be understood as coexisting and relative phenomena, the framework provides a common structural language for the analysis of polarized networks, institutional sorting, and identity-based alignment.

1
Introduction and Theoretical Background

1.1
Existing Paradigms and Their Limitations

In the social sciences, the formation of relations has long been dominated by a quantitative paradigm. Within this perspective, the existence of a relation is implicitly assumed to be feasible, and analytical attention is directed toward quantitative attributes such as strength, probability, or threshold distributions. Relations are thus treated as entities that can be formed, with variation arising only in their magnitude or likelihood.

This section briefly reviews two representative paradigms that exemplify this approach.

1.1.1
Utility-Based Paradigm

In network formation games, most prominently represented by Jackson and Wolinsky (1996)
[
7
]
, the formation of a relation is modeled as a direct function of utility. A connection is established whenever the associated payoff is positive, formally expressed as

u_{i}(R_{ij})>0\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{connection}.

Under this paradigm, relations are assumed to be malleable: as long as mutual benefits exist, a connection can be formed. Relations are therefore treated as outcomes of preferences and optimization rather than as objects subject to prior constraints.

1.1.2
Probabilistic Paradigm

A second influential line of research, exemplified by Watts and Strogatz (1998)
[
11
]
and Barabási and Albert (1999)
[
1
]
, models relation formation probabilistically. In these models, links emerge with a given probability
p
, and the primary analytical focus lies on the resulting macroscopic network structures.

Within this paradigm, relations are regarded as probabilistic events. The specific conditions under which a relation fails to materialize are not treated as an object of analysis, but are subsumed into stochastic assumptions.

1.1.3
From Quantity to Structure

A similar implicit assumption appears in the study of social division. Schelling’s (1971)
[
8
]
segregation model presupposes distributions of tolerance thresholds, while Granovetter’s (1973)
[
5
]
theory of weak ties focuses on variations in tie strength. Despite their differences, these approaches share a common underlying premise: relations are assumed to be formable, and analysis concerns only their quantitative differentiation.

Consequently, the very phenomenon of relations failing to form is not placed at the center of theoretical inquiry. The absence of relations is treated as an outcome to be explained, rather than as a structural condition to be examined.

1.2
Our Perspective: Relation Formation as Structural Constraint

In many real-world settings, relations fail to form even when agents derive positive utility, encounter one another with high probability, and mutually desire connection. Such phenomena cannot be fully captured within existing paradigms that take the feasibility of relation formation for granted.

This study, therefore, brackets the conventional question of
why
or
how
agents form relations. Instead, we focus on a more fundamental inquiry: the
boundary conditions
that determine whether a relation can exist at all.
Under these constraints, the conventional problem of designing universally cohesive relations is shown to be
ill-posed
, rather than merely difficult.

In the spirit of Simmel’s formal sociology
[
9
]

and Blau’s structural approach to social parameters
[
3
]
,
we treat relations as structurally constrained possibilities,
rather than as outcomes reducible to psychological or behavioral choice.

1.2.1
Explicit Scope: What This Theory Brackets

To describe the formation of relations as a purely structural phenomenon, this theory
intentionally abstracts away
from the following elements:

•

Quantitative attributes:
The magnitude of payoffs or the strength of ties.

•

Empirical truth:
Whether the required utility is ”actually” satisfied in a given instance.

•

Subjective factors:
The psychology, intentions, or motivations of the players.

•

Process and Dynamics:
The specific details of actions or the evolutionary mechanisms over time.

What this theory addresses is exclusively the
structure
: the minimal conditions under which a relation can be established, defined by
who
occupies
which
position.

Scope of the Impossibility Result.

The impossibility established in this paper applies to systems containing at least one
position-dependent minimum condition.
Position-independent conditions represent a degenerate limiting case of complete homogeneity,
which is theoretically extreme and empirically atypical.
Linguistic, institutional, organizational, ideological, and legal constraints are all
position-dependent in nature.
Thus, the structural limit identified here applies to a broad and empirically salient class of relational constraints.
While the impossibility result is logically conditional, its empirical scope is extremely broad,
as only this exceptional and highly uniform configuration escapes the structural constraint.

1.2.2
Redefinition as Logical Constraint

We redefine relation formation not as a function of utility or a probabilistic outcome, but as a problem of satisfying structural constraints. Under this view, the possibility of relation formation depends solely on the logical compatibility of agents’ positions within a given space.

Even if both parties seek a relation and encounter each other frequently, a relation cannot be established if their occupied positions are structurally incompatible. Within this framework, division is not interpreted here as a consequence of preference mismatch or lack of incentive, but as an inevitable logical output imposed by the structural constraints of the position space itself.

Structural Scope of the Analysis.

The present theory is intentionally static.
Our aim is not to model the psychological, strategic, or institutional
processes that generate a particular bifurcation event

E:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{L}
, nor do we specify the temporal
dynamics through which positions are chosen or revealed.
Instead, we identify the
structural consequences that must hold
regardless of the underlying dynamics
.

Once positions are fixed—by any mechanism, voluntary or coercive—
the feasibility of relations is determined entirely by positional compatibility.
Thus, abstracting from the generative process of
E
is not a limitation but a

requirement for generality
: any dynamic or behavioral model of
position formation must operate within the structural constraints proved here.
Our results therefore apply uniformly across diverse mechanisms of alignment,
commitment, signaling, or institutional assignment.

1.2.3
Contributions

The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

First, we reformulate the problem of relation formation from a question of
want
to a question of
can
. We demonstrate that positional compatibility, rather than utility or probability, constitutes the primary structural filter for relation formation.

Second, we show that fragmentation and cohesion arise as dual outcomes
of a single structural mechanism, and that no institutional or positional
reconfiguration can eliminate fragmentation without trivializing the
underlying constraints.

2
Design Principles and Theoretical Scope

This section specifies the analytical stance and foundational assumptions
that govern the construction of the theory.
The purpose is not to introduce results,
but to clarify what is taken as primitive,
how structural comparisons are performed,
and which simplifications are adopted at the foundational level.

2.1
Analytical Focus

The present theory treats relations not as events or processes,
but as structural data.
A relation is defined as a configuration that either satisfies
or fails to satisfy a set of structural conditions.

By adopting this perspective,
the theory avoids circular definitions in which relations are explained
by outcomes that presuppose their existence.
All subsequent analysis is therefore conducted at the level of
structural compatibility, rather than behavioral realization.

2.2
Methodology of Structural Comparison

Division and cohesion are described through a comparison
between two distinct structural states.
These states are denoted as follows:

•

State “
-
”: the structural configuration prior to a bifurcation event

•

State “
+
”: the structural configuration after the bifurcation event

The distinction between these states is logical rather than temporal.
They represent alternative configurations generated
by a change in imposed assumptions,
not successive moments along a time axis.

Accordingly, dynamics—including rates of change,
transition paths, or probabilistic evolution—are explicitly excluded
from the scope of the present analysis.

2.3
Reduction to Pairwise Relations

Postulate 2.1

(Reduction to Pairwise Relations)
.

All social relations are assumed to be reducible to pairwise
relations.

This reduction is adopted as a foundational
simplification. The theory does not deny the existence of
genuinely
N
-ary relations (e.g., triadic structures emphasized
in balance theory); such extensions are deferred to future work.

An
N
-player system is therefore represented as a collection
of
\binom{N}{2}
two-agent relations.

This assumption is justified on the following grounds:

•

Cognitive constraints:

Human agents primarily perceive, interpret,
and evaluate social relations in a pairwise manner,
reflecting basic limits of social cognition

[
6

4
]
.

•

Consistency with network theory:

Standard representations of social structure
model relations as dyadic edges between nodes,
providing a canonical and analytically tractable abstraction

[

,
2
]
.

•

Theoretical extensibility:

Genuinely
N
-ary relations are not denied,
but are deferred as a subject of future theoretical extension.
The present reduction is adopted as a minimal
and structurally transparent starting point.

3
Structural Theory of Relations: Classification Framework

This section establishes a purely classificatory theory of relations.
The purpose is to define relations as structural objects
and to fix the minimal descriptive framework
upon which subsequent theoretical developments will build.

3.1
Universal Primitives

Before defining individual relations, we specify the universal sets
that constitute the structural environment.

Definition 3.1

(Universal Player Set)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the universal set of players (agents).
The cardinality
|\mathcal{P}|
may be arbitrary (finite or infinite).

Definition 3.2

(Gain Axes)
.

Let

G:=\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\}

denote the set of gain axes.

Each gain axis represents a type of value or condition
that may be realized through a relation.
Examples include recognition, belonging, monetary transfer,
security, authority, or protection.
No assumption is made regarding completeness,
hierarchical ordering, or mutual exclusivity.
The set of gain axes is given as an axiomatic input to the theory.

3.2
Local Relational Structure

We now define the structure of a single dyadic relation within this environment.

Definition 3.3

(Dyadic Player Set)
.

For a specific pairwise relation, we define the local player set as a subset

P\subset\mathcal{P}\quad\text{with}\quad|P|=2.

Typically, we denote the elements of
P
as
\{i,j\}
.

Only dyadic relations are considered.
By Postulate
2.1
(reduction to pairwise relations),
an
N
-player system is represented
as a collection of such two-player relations.

Definition 3.4

(Minimum Condition Function)
.

The minimum condition function is defined locally for a relation on
P
as:

a:P\times G\to\{0,1\}.

Axiom 3.5

(Minimum Condition Axiom)
.

For any player
i\in P
and gain axis
g\in G
,

a(i,g)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{if gain axis }g\text{ were absent,
player }i\text{ would withdraw from the relation}.

Remark 3.6

(Structural Interpretation of Withdrawal)
.

The concept of withdrawal introduced in Axiom
3.5
is not a
behavioral or psychological notion. It functions as a structural
predicate that delimits the set of admissible configurations:

a(i,g)=1
specifies that configurations in which
g
fails
to hold are excluded from the domain of viable relations for
player
i
. No assumption is made about the process or intention
behind such exclusion.

The condition
a(i,g)=1
does
not
assert
that the gain axis
g
is actually satisfied.
Rather, it expresses a counterfactual necessity:
the relation cannot be maintained by player
i

in any configuration in which
g
fails to hold.
This statement concerns structural possibility,
not realized events.

Withdrawal is a primitive concept in the present theory.
It is not defined behaviorally or psychologically,
but is introduced axiomatically.
Accordingly,
a(i,g)=1
specifies
an axiomatic relation between structural conditions
and the admissibility of participation in a relation.

3.3
Relation as a Structure

Definition 3.7

(Basic Relation)
.

A basic relation is defined as the triple

R_{\text{basic}}:=(P,G,a),

where
P
is the set of players,

G
is the set of gain axes,
and
a:P\times G\to\{0,1\}

is the minimum condition function.

A relation is neither an event nor a process.
It is a piece of structural data
that specifies which conditions are minimally required
by which players.

Definition 3.8

(Active Gain Axes)
.

For a relation
R
,
the set of active gain axes is defined as

G_{\text{active}}(R):=\{g\in G\mid\exists i\in P\text{ such that }a(i,g)=1\}.

Only relations satisfying
|G_{\text{active}}|>0

are considered within the scope of the theory.
If
G_{\text{active}}=\emptyset
,
the relation is termed an
empty relation

and is excluded from further analysis.

Definition 3.9

(Relation Matrix)
.

For a relation
R
with
|G_{\text{active}}|>0
,
the relation matrix is defined as

M_{R}:=[a(i,g)]_{i\in P,\;g\in G_{\text{active}}}\in\{0,1\}^{2\times|G_{\text{active}}|}.

The matrix
M_{R}
provides a complete description
of the relation at the purely structural level,
prior to any consideration of positional dependence.

each column

of the relation matrix falls into exactly one of the following types:

Define

Summary of this section.

This section has established a purely classificatory
structural theory of relations.
No evaluative or behavioral content has been introduced.
The resulting framework serves as the descriptive foundation
for the positional and relational analysis developed in subsequent sections.

4
Extension to Position Space

The classificatory framework developed in Section 3
describes the internal structure of an individual relation.
However, the relation matrix alone is insufficient
for analyzing the simultaneous maintainability of multiple relations,
or for describing cases in which the satisfaction of a gain axis
depends not only on who is involved,
but also on the state an agent occupies.

This section introduces the notion of position
as a minimal extension required to address these limitations.

4.1
Motivation for Position

Two conceptual limitations arise
when relations are represented solely by their relation matrices.

Simultaneous maintenance of multiple relations.

Suppose that a player
i
participates in multiple relations

R_{1},R_{2},\dots
at the same time.
Each relation imposes its own set of minimum conditions.
The relation matrix
M_{R}
characterizes these conditions
for a single relation in isolation,
but provides no mechanism for determining
whether the combined requirements across relations
are mutually compatible.

State-dependent satisfaction of gain axes.

In many cases, whether a gain axis
g
is satisfied
depends not only on the identity of the counterpart,
but also on the state occupied by the player.
Such dependence cannot be expressed
within the relation matrix representation alone.

These limitations indicate the necessity
of introducing an additional descriptive layer.
Specifically, it becomes necessary
to represent the state occupied by a player.
This motivates the introduction of the concept of
position
.

4.2
Position Space

Definition 4.1

(Position Space)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of positions
that a player may occupy.

All possible states of a player—including physical location,
opinion, role, affiliation, or attribute—are represented
as coordinates in the position space.
Positions are treated as abstract variables;
no concrete interpretation is fixed at this stage.

No structural assumptions are imposed on
\mathcal{L}
.
In particular, no topology, metric, or ordering
is assumed unless explicitly stated later.

Definition 4.2

(Player Position)
.

The position of player
i
is denoted by

\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{L}.

When it is necessary to distinguish positions
before and after a bifurcation event,
we write

\ell_{i}^{-}\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{i}^{+},

respectively.
In general, pre-event positions need not be specified,
while post-event positions are fixed
by the structural constraint imposed by the event.

5
Position Axioms

Section 4 introduced the notion of position
as an abstract state occupied by each player,
in order to account for the simultaneous maintenance of relations
and the position-dependent satisfaction of gain axes.
The present section specifies the axiomatic constraints
that govern how positions may be assigned to players.

These axioms are ontological rather than temporal:
they restrict which configurations are admissible
within a single structural state,
independently of any process or dynamics.

5.1
Uniqueness of Position

Axiom 5.1

(Uniqueness of Position)
.

For every player
i\in\mathcal{P}
,
there exists exactly one position occupied by
i
:

\forall i\in\mathcal{P},\qquad\left|\{\ell_{i}\}\right|=1.

Remark 5.2

(Interpretation of Uniqueness)
.

Axiom
5.1
does not assert that agents possess only a single psychological or social identity.
Rather, it ensures that within a given structural snapshot, an agent cannot occupy two mutually
incompatible positions simultaneously.

The position space
\mathcal{L}
is deliberately left unconstrained:
it may be high-dimensional, contain composite attributes, or encode multiple identities internally.
The uniqueness axiom enforces consistency at the level of the structural configuration, not
simplicity at the level of human identity.

Each player occupies one and only one position
in a given structural state.
This axiom is not a temporal restriction:
it does not prohibit movement or change across states.
Rather, it is an ontological constraint stating that
a player cannot simultaneously occupy
multiple incompatible positions
within the same structural configuration.

5.2
Consequences of Uniqueness

Remark 5.3

(Dual Consequences of Uniqueness)
.

Axiom
5.1
has two complementary consequences:

1.

Basis of fragmentation:

a player cannot simultaneously occupy
two distinct positions
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
.

2.

Basis of cohesion:

players occupying the same position
\ell

are aligned with respect to that position.

These consequences follow from the same axiomatic source
and do not presuppose any evaluative or behavioral interpretation.

5.3
Incompatibility of Positions

Definition 5.4

(Incompatibility of Positions)
.

Two positions
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}

are said to be incompatible, denoted
\ell\perp\ell^{\prime}
,
if and only if

\ell\perp\ell^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\;\land\;\text{they cannot be simultaneously occupied}.

Corollary 5.5
.

From Axiom
5.1
,
all distinct positions are incompatible:

\forall\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L},\qquad\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\;\Longrightarrow\;\ell\perp\ell^{\prime}.

6
Dependency Types of Gain Axes

This section classifies gain axes
according to the structural conditions
under which they can be satisfied.
The objective is not to evaluate relations,
but to characterize the degree of positional dependence
in a precise and unified manner.

6.1
Compatibility Sets

Definition 6.1

(Universal Player Set)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

denote the set of all players
considered in the analysis.

By Postulate
2.1
(reduction to pairwise relations),
any relational system defined on

\mathcal{P}

is represented as a collection of dyadic relations.

Definition 6.2

(Compatibility Set)
.

For a gain axis
g

and a player
i\in\mathcal{P}
,
the compatibility set of
i
with respect to
g

is defined as

C_{i}(g):=\left\{j\in\mathcal{P}\;\middle|\;\text{gain axis }g\text{ is satisfiable between }i\text{ and }j\right\}.

Here, “satisfiable” means that
there exists at least one pair of positions

(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})\in\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}

for which the gain axis
g
can hold.

6.2

Definition 6.3

(Unified Classification of Dependency Types)
.

The dependency type of a gain axis
g

is determined by the cardinality
of the compatibility set
C_{i}(g)
.

(Generative Principle of the Classification)
.

This classification is derived
from a single structural parameter,
namely the cardinality of

.
Existence-dependent and exclusive-dependent gain axes

Conditions commonly described as “attribute-dependent”

Remark 6.5

(Local vs. Global Interpretation)
.

Definition

specifies a purely

heterogeneity of its compatibility set

.
At this stage, no network-level consequence follows from the
classification alone.

In particular, local heterogeneity of compatibility does
not

by itself imply fragmentation or cohesion at the level of the network.
The mechanism by which such local incompatibilities are necessarily
propagated to the macroscopic structure requires additional structural
ingredients.

These ingredients are introduced once positions are fixed by a

The resulting lifting of local incompatibility to network-level

,

.

6.3
Position-Dependent Conditions

Definition 6.6

(Compatibility Function)
.

For a position-dependent gain axis
g
,
the compatibility function is defined as

f_{g}:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}.

Remark 6.7

(Symmetry of Compatibility Functions)
.

The theory does not require the compatibility function
f_{g}
to
be symmetric. That is,
f_{g}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})\neq f_{g}(\ell_{j},\ell_{i})

is permitted. Asymmetric compatibility functions can model directed
relations such as authority, patronage, or asymmetric recognition.
Theorem 10.1 and its corollaries hold regardless of whether
f_{g}

is symmetric or asymmetric.

A gain axis
g
is satisfied
between players
i
and
j

if and only if

f_{g}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})=1.

For existence-dependent gain axes,
the compatibility function is constant:

\forall\ell_{i},\ell_{j}\in\mathcal{L},\qquad f_{g}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})=1.

Postulate 6.8

(Unified Position Space)
.

All gain axes are assumed
to reference a common position space
\mathcal{L}
.
For each position-dependent gain axis
g
,
there exists a compatibility function

f_{g}:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}
.

Proposition 6.9

(Position-Dependent Gain Axes and Exclusive Regions)
.

Let
g
be a position-dependent gain axis.
If the compatibility function
f_{g}

defines an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{L}
,
then the position space can be partitioned
into mutually exclusive regions

\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{k}\}
with
k\geq 2
,
such that

\ell_{i}\in L_{a},\;\ell_{j}\in L_{b},\;a\neq b\;\Longrightarrow\;f_{g}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})=0.

Proof.

If
f_{g}
defines an equivalence relation,
then
f_{g}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})=1

is equivalent to
\ell_{i}\sim\ell_{j}
.
Equivalence classes are mutually disjoint.
Therefore, positions belonging to different classes
cannot satisfy
f_{g}
.
∎

Remark 6.10

(Minimal Feasibility Structure)
.

The binary feasibility function
f_{g}:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}

is intentionally minimal.
It captures only whether a relation is structurally possible, not its strength, utility, or
probabilistic intensity.
Richer models may refine
f_{g}
into a graded or continuous measure, but such elaborations do not
affect the structural conclusions of the present theory.
The minimal binary form is chosen to isolate the essential constraints on relation viability.

7
Relations and Position Requirements

This section examines the consistency of positional requirements
imposed by multiple relations.
When combined with the axiom of positional uniqueness,
these requirements give rise to selective relational collapse
as a purely structural consequence.

7.1
Extended Definition of Relation

Definition 7.1

(Complete Definition of a Relation)
.

A relation with positional dependence is defined as the tuple

R:=(P,G,a,\mathcal{L},F),

where

•

P=\{0,1\}
is the set of players,

•

G
is the set of gain axes,

•

a:P\times G\to\{0,1\}
is the minimum condition function,

•

\mathcal{L}
is the position space,

•

F:=\{f_{g}\mid g\in G\text{ is position-dependent}\}

is the family of compatibility functions.

If a relation contains only existence-dependent gain axes,
then
F=\emptyset
,
and the above definition reduces to
the basic relation
R_{\text{basic}}

introduced in Definition
3.7
.

7.2
Position Requirements of Relations

Definition 7.2

(Positional Requirement of a Relation)
.

Let
R=(P,G,a,\mathcal{L},F)
be a relation.
The positional requirement imposed by
R

on player
i\in P
is defined as

\Lambda_{i}(R):=\left\{\ell\in\mathcal{L}\;\middle|\;\text{for all }g\in G\text{ with }a(i,g)=1,\;g\text{ is satisfied at }\ell\right\}.

A relation
R
can be maintained by player
i

only if the occupied position
\ell_{i}

belongs to
\Lambda_{i}(R)
.

7.3
Consistency of Multiple Relations

Definition 7.3

(Positional Consistency)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}=\{R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{m}\}

be the set of relations involving player
i
.
The set
\mathcal{R}_{i}
is said to be positionally consistent
if

\bigcap_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\Lambda_{i}(R)\neq\emptyset.

That is, there exists at least one position
that simultaneously satisfies
the positional requirements of all relations in
\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

Proposition 7.4

(Inconsistency and Selective Collapse)
.

If

\bigcap_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\Lambda_{i}(R)=\emptyset,

then there exists at least one relation

R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}

that cannot be maintained by player
i
.

Proof.

By Axiom
5.1
(uniqueness of position),
player
i
occupies exactly one position
within a given structural state.
If the intersection of positional requirements is empty,
then for any position
\ell\in\mathcal{L}

occupied by
i
,
there exists at least one relation

R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}

such that
\ell\notin\Lambda_{i}(R)
.
By Definition
7.2
,
the minimum conditions imposed by that relation are not satisfied.
By Axiom
3.5
,
a relation whose minimum conditions are not satisfied
cannot be maintained.
∎

Proposition 7.5

(Consistency and Simultaneous Maintenance)
.

If

\bigcap_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\Lambda_{i}(R)\neq\emptyset,

then there exists a position
\ell^{*}

such that all relations in
\mathcal{R}_{i}

can be maintained simultaneously.

Proof.

If the intersection is nonempty,
there exists
\ell^{*}\in\bigcap_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\Lambda_{i}(R)
.
If player
i
occupies this position,
then by Definition
7.2
,
the positional requirements of all relations
in
\mathcal{R}_{i}
are satisfied simultaneously.
∎

Remark 7.6

(Dual Structure of Positional Consistency)
.

Propositions
7.4

and
7.5

correspond to the emptiness and non-emptiness,
respectively,
of the same structural object:
the intersection of positional requirements

\bigcap_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\Lambda_{i}(R)
.
This duality anticipates
the later distinction between fragmentation and cohesion,
which will be defined as logical outputs
of structural compatibility.

8
Bifurcation Events

This section introduces bifurcation events,
which force the determination of positions for all players.
Such events generate fragmentation and cohesion simultaneously
as structural consequences.
This mechanism constitutes the core of Theorem 9.1.

8.1
Definition of Bifurcation

Definition 8.1

(Bifurcation Event)
.

A bifurcation event is a mapping

E:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{L}

that forces each player to occupy a determinate position.
After the occurrence of a bifurcation event,
the position of each player
i
is uniquely fixed as

\ell_{i}=E(i).

Remark 8.2

(Scope and Exogeneity)
.

The present theory does not model the generative dynamics of a bifurcation event
E
.
Rather,
E
is treated as an exogenous position-fixing operator, and the analysis
focuses exclusively on the structural consequences that necessarily follow once
positions are fixed.
This abstraction ensures that the results do not depend on the particular strategic,
cultural, institutional, or stochastic mechanisms that might generate
E
in
practice.
The impossibility result established below relies only on positional fixation
itself, not on the processes that produce it.

Typical examples of bifurcation events include: a vote that enforces a choice between alternatives, a loyalty test that fixes a belief position, a mandatory expression of opinion, active engagements on social media (such as posting, liking, or reposting) that manifest one’s stance, or any situation in which one is required to declare “which side one is on.”

Definition 8.3

(Partition Induced by a Bifurcation Event)
.

A bifurcation event
E
induces a partition
of the player set
\mathcal{P}
as

\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{P}_{L_{k}},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{L_{k}}:=\{\,i\mid E(i)\in L_{k}\,\}.

Here,
\{L_{k}\}
denotes an appropriate partition
of the position space
\mathcal{L}
,
for example the equivalence classes
when the corresponding compatibility function
defines an equivalence relation.

8.2
Structural Effects of Bifurcation

Lemma 8.4

(Dual Effects of Bifurcation)
.

Let
g
be a position-dependent gain axis
and let
E
be a bifurcation event.
For any pair of players
(i,j)
,
the following hold.

(a)

Separation effect.

If

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0,

then
g
does not hold between
i
and
j
.
If
a(i,g)=1
or
a(j,g)=1
,
the relation
R_{ij}
collapses.

(b)

Aggregation effect.

If

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=1,

then
g
is compatible between
i
and
j
.
If
a(i,g)=1
and
a(j,g)=1
,
the minimum conditions of the relation
R_{ij}

are satisfied.

Proof.

(a)
By Definition
6.6
,

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0

means that the gain axis
g

does not hold between
i
and
j
.
If
a(i,g)=1
(or
a(j,g)=1
),
then by Axiom
3.5
,
player
i
(or
j
) withdraws from the relation.
If either player withdraws,
the relation collapses.

(b)
By Definition
6.6
,

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=1

means that
g
is compatible
between
i
and
j
.
If
a(i,g)=1
and
a(j,g)=1
,
then
g
constitutes a shared minimum condition.
Its compatibility therefore provides
the structural basis for maintaining the relation.
∎

8.3
From Local Incompatibility to Network-Level Effects

The preceding sections characterized compatibility and incompatibility
at the level of individual dyadic relations.
This subsection makes explicit how such
local

incompatibilities are necessarily lifted to the level of the
network once positions are fixed by a bifurcation event.

Lemma 8.5

(Lifting of Local Incompatibility)
.

Let
g
be a position-dependent gain axis and
let
E:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{L}

be a bifurcation event.
If there exist players
i,j\in\mathcal{P}

such that

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0,

and if
a(i,g)=1
or
a(j,g)=1
,
then there exists no post-event network configuration
in which all relations involving gain axis
g

are simultaneously maintainable.

Proof.

By the bifurcation event
E
,
each player
k\in\mathcal{P}

occupies exactly one position

\ell_{k}=E(k)
.
By Axiom
5.1
,
no player can simultaneously occupy incompatible positions.

If
f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0
,
then gain axis
g
is unsatisfiable between
players
i
and
j
at the fixed positions.
If
a(i,g)=1
(or
a(j,g)=1
),
then by Axiom
3.5

the corresponding relation fails its positional requirement
(Definition
7.2
)
and cannot be maintained.

Because the positions of all players are fixed globally by
E
,
this failure is independent of other relations
and necessarily removes at least one edge
from the post-event network.
∎

Remark 8.6

(Core Insight)
.

The separation effect and the aggregation effect
are not distinct mechanisms.
They are simply the 0 and 1
outputs of the same compatibility function
f_{g}

under the same evaluation rule.
Whether a relation collapses or is maintained
depends solely on the value of

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))
,
and not on any algebraic properties of
f_{g}

such as whether it defines an equivalence relation.

9
Network-Level Description

This section bridges the micro-level structure of dyadic relations
to macro-level descriptions of fragmentation and cohesion.
By doing so, it provides operational network-level objects
that will be used in Theorem
10.2
.

9.1
Two Network States

Definition 9.1

(Initial Relation Set)
.

Let the set of relations that exist prior to a bifurcation event be

\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}:=\{R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{m}\}.

Each relation
R_{k}=(P_{k},G_{k},a_{k},\mathcal{L},F_{k})

connects a pair of players
P_{k}=\{i_{k},j_{k}\}
.

Definition 9.2

(Post-Event Maintenance of a Relation)
.

Let
E:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{L}

be a bifurcation event.
A relation
R
is said to be maintained after
E
if

\forall i\in\mathcal{P}_{R}:\quad\ell_{i}^{+}=E(i)\in\Lambda_{i}(R).

That is, every player in the relation satisfies
the positional requirements of the relation
at the position fixed by the bifurcation event.

Definition 9.3

(Maintained Relation Set)
.

Define the set of relations maintained under
E
as

\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{maintained}}(E):=\{\,R\in\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}\mid R\textnormal{ is maintained under }E\,\}.

9.2
Network Snapshots

Definition 9.4

(Pre-Event Network)
.

Define the pre-event network as

\mathcal{N}^{-}:=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{E}^{-}),

where

\mathcal{E}^{-}:=\{\,(i,j)\mid\exists R\in\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}:P_{R}=\{i,j\}\,\}.

Definition 9.5

(Post-Event Network)
.

Define the post-event network as

\mathcal{N}^{+}:=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{E}^{+}),

where

\mathcal{E}^{+}:=\{\,(i,j)\mid\exists R\in\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{maintained}}(E):P_{R}=\{i,j\}\,\}.

Definition 9.6

(Connected Components)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{E})
be a network.
A connected component of
\mathcal{N}
is a maximal subset

C\subseteq\mathcal{P}
such that:

•

for any
i,j\in C
, there exists a path from
i
to
j

using edges in
\mathcal{E}
, and

•

for any
i\in C
and
k\notin C
,

(i,k)\notin\mathcal{E}
.

Let
\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N})
denote the set of connected components of

\mathcal{N}
.

9.3
Operational Definitions of Fragmentation and Cohesion

Definition 9.7

(Fragmentation)
.

Fragmentation induced by a bifurcation event
E

is said to occur if at least one of the following holds:

1.

Increase in the number of connected components:

|\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N}^{+})|>|\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N}^{-})|.

2.

Loss of edges between distinct components:

there exist
C_{a},C_{b}\in\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N}^{-})

with
C_{a}\neq C_{b}
such that

|\mathcal{E}^{+}\cap(C_{a}\times C_{b})|<|\mathcal{E}^{-}\cap(C_{a}\times C_{b})|.

Definition 9.8

(Cohesion)
.

Cohesion induced by a bifurcation event
E

is said to occur if at least one of the following holds:

1.

Increase in within-component relation density:

there exists
C\in\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N}^{+})
such that

\frac{|\mathcal{E}^{+}\cap(C\times C)|}{|C|(|C|-1)/2}>\frac{|\mathcal{E}^{-}\cap(C\times C)|}{|C|(|C|-1)/2}.

2.

Decrease in the number of connected components:

|\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N}^{+})|<|\mathrm{CC}(\mathcal{N}^{-})|.

Remark 9.9

(Structural Nature of the Comparison)
.

Definitions
9.7
and
9.8

compare two network structures,

\mathcal{N}^{-}
and
\mathcal{N}^{+}
.
They do not represent temporal evolution,
but rather a response of the relational structure
to the structural intervention induced by the bifurcation event
E
.

10
Main Theorem: Structural Necessity of Fragmentation

Definition 10.1

(
g
-relevant edges and degeneracy under
E
)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
be a set of dyadic relations on
\mathcal{P}
.
For a gain axis
g
, define the set of
g
-relevant initial edges by

\mathcal{E}_{g}^{-}:=\{\,(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\mid R_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}},\ \bigl(a(i,g)=1\lor a(j,g)=1\bigr)\,\}.

We say that
g
is
degenerate under
E
(relative to
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
)

if the map
(i,j)\mapsto f_{g}(E(i),E(j))
is constant on
\mathcal{E}_{g}^{-}
.

Theorem 10.2

(Structural Necessity of Fragmentation)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
be a nonempty set of relations and
E:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{L}
be a bifurcation event.
Consider a gain axis
g
relevant to
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
.

The structural outcome is characterized by the following dichotomy:

1.

The Trivial Case (Degeneracy):

The gain axis
g
is effectively position-independent under
E
.
Mathematically, the compatibility map
(i,j)\mapsto f_{g}(E(i),E(j))
is constant on the set of relevant edges
\mathcal{E}_{g}^{-}
.
In this limit, the positional constraint exerts no selective pressure.

2.

The Structural Outcome (Selective Survivability):

If
g
is not degenerate under
E
, then the post-event network
\mathcal{N}^{+}

necessarily
exhibits fragmentation, while selectively admitting cohesion.
Specifically:

•

Fragmentation:
Relations bridging incompatible positions are structurally forced to collapse (
f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0
).

•

Cohesion:
Relations within compatible configurations are permitted to survive (
f_{g}(E(u),E(v))=1
).

This selective survivability yields the coexistence of fragmentation and potential cohesion whenever compatible relevant relations exist.

Proof.

Assume that
g
is not degenerate under
E

in the sense of Definition
10.1
.
Then the image of the map
\phi:(i,j)\mapsto f_{g}(E(i),E(j))
restricted to
\mathcal{E}_{g}^{-}
is exactly
\{0,1\}
.
Thus, there exist pairs
(i,j),(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{g}^{-}
such that

f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0\quad\text{and}\quad f_{g}(E(u),E(v))=1.

Fragmentation.

Consider a pair
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{g}^{-}
such that
f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0
.
We establish the necessity of fragmentation by proving the
non-existence
of any post-event configuration in which this relation is maintained.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists a valid post-event state in which the relation
R_{ij}
is maintained.
By Definition
9.2
(Post-Event Maintenance), this implies that the fixed positions must satisfy the positional requirements:

E(i)\in\Lambda_{i}(R_{ij})\quad\text{and}\quad E(j)\in\Lambda_{j}(R_{ij}).

However, Definition
7.2
dictates that
\ell\in\Lambda_{i}(R_{ij})
only if the gain axis
g
is satisfied at
\ell
.
Since
f_{g}(E(i),E(j))=0
, the condition is structurally violated at the positions fixed by
E
.
Because Axiom
5.1
(Uniqueness of Position) precludes agents from occupying alternative positions to resolve this conflict, the assumption of maintenance contradicts the structural data.
Thus, such a configuration cannot exist, rendering the collapse of
R_{ij}
structurally unavoidable.

Cohesion.

For the pair
(u,v)
with
f_{g}(E(u),E(v))=1
, the positional constraint imposed by
g
is satisfied.
Hence, any
g
-shared minimum relation between
u
and
v
, if present in

\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
, is structurally admissible after
E

by Lemma
8.4
(b).

Conclusion.

Since non-degeneracy implies the existence of both types of pairs, the post-event structure necessarily displays non-uniform survivability. The only escape from this outcome is Degeneracy (Case 1).
∎

Remark 10.3

(Equivalence-Class Interpretation)
.

If the compatibility function
f_{g}
defines an equivalence relation on the position space
\mathcal{L}
, then structural incompatibility occurs exclusively across distinct equivalence classes, while compatibility is confined within each class.
Specifically, if
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
is the partition induced by
f_{g}
, then any relevant relation bridging distinct classes
L_{a}\neq L_{b}
is severed, whereas relations within
L_{a}
are structurally admissible.

11
Corollaries and Implications

This section derives further structural consequences of Theorem
10.2
, highlighting the limits of relational maintenance.

Corollary 11.1

(Uniqueness of Existence-Dependence)
.

The only structural condition that permits universal cohesion without inducing fragmentation is the class of existence-dependent gain axes (where
f_{g}\equiv 1
globally). All other dependency types impose selective relational mortality.

Corollary 11.2

(Structural Inescapability of Out-Groups)
.

Under a position-dependent gain axis, any cohesive subset
C\subseteq\mathcal{P}
formed by
f_{g}
is necessarily incompatible with at least one player outside
C
.
The formation of an ”in-group” structurally necessitates the definition of an ”out-group” whenever constraints are non-trivial.

12
Conclusion

This paper has developed a static, structural theory
of division and cohesion
based on minimal conditions for relation maintenance.
Rather than modeling interaction dynamics or strategic adjustment,
the analysis isolates the structural consequences
of enforcing positional commitments
through bifurcation events.

The core contribution lies in identifying

position-dependent gain axes

as a fundamental source of selective relational survival.
When relational viability depends on positional compatibility,
the fixing of positions necessarily induces
the rupture of incompatible relations alongside
the maintenance of compatible ones.

Theorem
10.2
formalizes this not merely as a classification,
but as a result of
structural impossibility
:
universal cohesion is unattainable under non-degenerate positional constraints.
Division and cohesion do not describe opposing processes unfolding over time,
but coexist as inevitable outcomes of the same structural intervention.

This perspective clarifies why empirical observations
often report intensified in-group cohesion
alongside increasing inter-group separation,
without requiring additional explanatory mechanisms.
The corollaries further delineate the limits of this phenomenon.
Only
existence-dependent
gain axes (or degenerate mappings)
permit cohesion without inducing fragmentation.
Conversely, any gain axis that is genuinely position-dependent
imposes unavoidable boundaries on relational compatibility.

These boundaries are structural in nature
and persist regardless of agents’ intentions,
beliefs, or preferences.
By reducing division and cohesion
to properties of relation-maintenance constraints,
the framework offers a common language
for the analysis of polarized networks,
institutional sorting,
and identity-based alignment,
while remaining agnostic about dynamics and incentives.
Future work may embed this structural core
into dynamic or strategic settings,
but the present results establish
the minimal conditions under which
division and cohesion must be understood
as intrinsically relative and structurally necessitated phenomena.

Appendix A
Independence of Axioms and Core Assumptions

This appendix establishes the logical independence of the core axioms
and assumptions used in Theorem
10.2
.
For each item, we construct a concrete countermodel that satisfies
all other assumptions but violates the target item.
We then demonstrate that the
structural necessity
established in Theorem
10.2
fails;
specifically, we show that it becomes possible to achieve
Universal Cohesion
(maintenance of all relations) even in non-trivial structural settings, thereby breaking the inevitability of fragmentation.

Throughout, let the player set be
\mathcal{P}=\{1,2,3\}
,
let the position space satisfy
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 2
,
and let
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
be nonempty.

Methodological Note on Independence.

A claim that an axiom or assumption is
independent
is established by constructing a countermodel
that satisfies all remaining axioms and definitions of the framework,
while violating the target item.
In each case, notions of relation, maintenance, and structural outcome are interpreted according to the original definitions,
except where the violated axiom explicitly licenses a modification.

A.1
Independence of the Minimum Condition Axiom

We violate Axiom
3.5
while keeping all other assumptions.

Countermodel.

Let
G=\{g\}
and let
g
be position-dependent with a compatibility
function
f_{g}:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}
that is not constant.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{initial}}
contain a relation

R_{12}
for which
a(1,g)=1
, and let
E
be a bifurcation event such that
f_{g}(E(1),E(2))=0
.
Thus, the setting is
non-degenerate
in the sense of Theorem
10.2
.

Now
replace
the withdrawal rule by the following weakened principle:

Even if a gain axis required by some player is unsatisfied, no player withdraws.
(Equivalently, the structural constraint does not enforce edge removal.)

Failure of the theorem’s conclusion.

Under this countermodel,
R_{12}
is maintained despite positional incompatibility.
Consequently, the post-event network exhibits
Universal Cohesion without Fragmentation
.
This contradicts the ”Structural Necessity” clause of Theorem
10.2
, proving that the impossibility result relies essentially on the binding nature of minimum conditions.

A.2
Independence of the Uniqueness of Position

We violate Axiom
5.1
while keeping all other assumptions.

Countermodel.

We modify the position assignment so that each player may occupy a
set
of positions.
Let
\tilde{E}:\mathcal{P}\to 2^{\mathcal{L}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
assign multiple positions to each agent.
We redefine maintenance such that a relation is compatible if
there exists
a pair of positions in the assigned sets that satisfies
f_{g}
.

Let
g
be position-dependent. Construct
\tilde{E}
such that for every pair
(i,j)
, there exists
\ell_{i}\in\tilde{E}(i)
and
\ell_{j}\in\tilde{E}(j)
with
f_{g}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})=1
.

Failure of the theorem’s conclusion.

In this setting, even if specific positions are incompatible (non-degeneracy at the point level), players can ”navigate” the constraint by utilizing alternative positions.
This allows for the simultaneous maintenance of all relations.
Thus,
Universal Cohesion
is achieved, and the necessity of fragmentation (Theorem
10.2
, Case 2) collapses once the uniqueness constraint is lifted.

A.3
Independence of the Non-Degeneracy Condition

We violate the assumption that the gain axis is non-degenerate (position-dependent).

Countermodel.

Assume
g
is existence-dependent, i.e.,
f_{g}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})=1
for all
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
.
This strictly corresponds to
Case 1 (Degeneracy)
of Theorem
10.2
.

structural consequence.

In this trivial limit, no separation effect is possible (
f_{g}
never takes the value 0).
Consequently, Universal Cohesion is the default outcome.
This confirms that the ”Necessity of Fragmentation” is strictly conditional on the non-degeneracy of the gain axis; without it, the impossibility result does not apply.

A.4
Independence of the Nontriviality of the Position Space

We violate the assumption
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 2
.

Countermodel.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{\ell^{\ast}\}
.
Any bifurcation event
E
maps all players to
\ell^{\ast}
.
The value
f_{g}(E(i),E(j))
is constant for all pairs.

structural consequence.

This forces the system into
Case 1 (Degeneracy)
.
Positional incompatibility is structurally impossible in a singleton space.
Thus, the mechanism generating fragmentation cannot operate, confirming that a non-trivial position space is a prerequisite for the theorem’s main implication.
