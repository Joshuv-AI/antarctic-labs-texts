Cesni‐cel (ARI0002h) in ultra‐high‐risk multiple myeloma with plasma cell leukaemia or central nervous system involvement
Authors: Carlos Jimenez‐Mira, Natalia Tovar, Núria Martínez‐Cibrián, Verónica González‐Calle, Nil Albiol, Nieves López‐Muñoz, Aina Oliver‐Caldés, Azucena González, José Miguel Mateos, Marta Español‐Rego, Laura Rosiñol, Manel Juan, Paola Charry, José María Sanchez‐Pina, Sergio Navarro, Daniel Munárriz, Lucía López‐Corral, Valentín Ortiz‐Maldonado, Sara Varea, Eulalia Olesti, Julio Delgado, Álvaro Urbano‐Ispizua, Joaquín Martínez‐López, María Victoria Mateos, Luis Gerardo Rodríguez‐Lobato, Carlos Fernández de Larrea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUltra‐high‐risk multiple myeloma (uHRMM), defined by extra‐medullary disease (EMD, including central nervous system [CNS] or plasma cell leukaemia [PCL]), remains as a predictor of early relapse after chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR‐T) therapy. We analysed the characteristics, toxicities, response and survival of patients with PCL/CNS‐multiple myeloma (MM) treated with cesnicabtagene autoleucel (ARI0002h) at three Spanish centres (2020–2025). PCL was defined as per International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) 2021 criteria; CNS‐MM by leptomeningeal and/or intraparenchymal involvement. Nineteen patients (68% female; median age 61) were included: 12 patients had PCL, 5 had CNS‐MM, and 2 had dual involvement. Fifty per cent had primary PCL. In CNS‐MM, all had leptomeningeal disease and one had also intraparenchymal lesions. Fifty‐seven per cent received CNS‐directed therapy. Seventy four per cent harboured high‐risk cytogenetics. Median prior lines: 3; 74% had haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT); two had prior B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA) exposure. One patient died pre‐apheresis and one during manufacturing; 17 (89%) received ARI0002h. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurred in 76% (none ≥G3), G3 immune effector cell (IEC)‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) occurred in one case (not CNS‐MM) and IEC‐associated haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis‐like syndrome (IEC‐HS) occurred in 18% (one fatal). Overall response rate (ORR) was 88% at day +100, of which 87% achieved ≥ very good partial response (VGPR) and 80% complete CNS response. Median progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were 10.5 and 15.9 months respectively. ARI0002h induces deep responses in relapsed uHRMM, supporting inclusion of PCL and CNS‐MM in clinical trials.

INTRODUCTION

Ultra‐high‐risk multiple myeloma (uHRMM) is characterized by aggressive clinical–biological features: refractoriness or <1‐year relapse to front‐line therapy, ≥2 high‐risk cytogenetic abnormalities (HRCA), independence from bone marrow microenvironment (extra‐medullary disease [EMD] or plasma cell leukaemia [PCL]) and/or high‐risk gene expression profile signature; 1 factors associated with shortened median progression‐free/overall survival (mPFS/mOS).

Primary PCL (PPCL) is concomitantly diagnosed at the time of MM (60% of cases) while secondary PCL (SPCL) arises during relapse/refractoriness (RRMM). 2 PCL accounts for <5% of multiple myeloma (MM) cases and its aggressive course has historically resulted in dismal outcomes with a mOS of <1 year. 3 , 4 , 5

EMD is detected in up to 15% of patients during RRMM. 6 , 7 , 8 Common sites involve muscle/skin, pleura, liver and central nervous system (CNS); 7 , 8 , 9 the latter being found in <1% of patients with a historical mOS <7 months. 10

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells targeting B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA) have revolutionized the therapeutical landscape of RRMM since the approval of ciltacabtagene autoleucel and idecabtagene vicleucel. 11 , 12 In addition, academic products like cesnicabtagene autoleucel (cesni‐cel; ARI0002h) have also shown relevant clinical activity and represent a valid alternative to overcome accessibility issues and unmet medical needs (PCL, CNS‐MM and other rare complications) not previously explored in trials with commercial products. 13

The aim of our study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of ARI0002h in patients with PCL and/or CNS‐MM.

METHODS

Participants and data collection

This is a retrospective, observational and multicentric analysis from three academic Spanish centres including 19 patients with PCL and/or CNS‐MM referred for treatment with ARI0002h between November 2020 and 2025. Four patients were treated in the CARTBCMA‐HCB‐01 study 13 while 15 were accepted on a compassionate use/hospital exemption basis. Inclusion criteria required PCL/CNS‐MM detection during disease evolution (from MM diagnosis until ARI0002h infusion).

The study was conducted after receiving institutional board approval by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Clínic de Barcelona and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects.

Objectives

Primary efficacy end‐points were overall response rate (ORR) at day +100 from infusion, CNS response during follow‐up and PFS/OS defined as the time between ARI0002h infusion and disease progression or death (PFS) or death due to any cause (OS). The primary safety end‐point was the incidence of immune effector cell (IEC)‐associated inflammatory states (cytokine release syndrome [CRS], IEC‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome [ICANS] and IEC‐associated haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis‐like syndrome [IEC‐HS]).

Secondary end‐points include best response achieved, bone marrow measurable residual disease (BM‐MRD) negativity, infectious complications in the first month after infusion, IEC‐associated haematotoxicity (ICAHT) and neurotoxicity besides ICANS.

Evaluation

PCL was detected by morphological examination of peripheral blood films and was defined by ≥5% peripheral blood plasma cells (PBPCs). 2 Patients who met criteria at the time of MM diagnosis were classified as PPCL while the remaining were defined as SPCL.

CNS disease was detected using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain/spine, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation and/or by histological evaluation of CNS lesions. Leptomeningeal disease was defined by MRI enhancement and/or evidence of plasma cells (PC) in CSF by morphology or flow cytometry. 10 Intraparenchymal involvement was defined by parenchymal lesions with PC infiltration by histology or, in patients with inaccessible lesions, compatible images without alternative diagnosis. 10

HRCA were defined as chromosome 1 abnormalities (del1p32, +1q/amp1q), t(4;14), t(14;16) or alterations involving TP53 (del17p, TP53m). Two or more concomitant HRCA were defined as multi‐hit.

CRS, ICANS and IEC‐HS were graded based on the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) criteria. 14 , 15 Neutrophil‐ICAHT (N‐ICAHT) was graded based on the European Hematology Association/European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EHA/EBMT) criteria and platelet‐ICAHT (T‐ICAHT) was graded based on T‐ICAHT system. 16 , 17 Infections were graded based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. 18

Response and progression were determined based on the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) 2016 criteria. 19 BM‐MRD negativity was assessed by next‐generation flow (NGF) cytometry with a minimum sensitivity of 10−5.

Treatment

CNS‐directed treatment and bridging therapy (BT) indication and regimen were made based on physician decision. Standard lymphodepleting (LD) chemotherapy and ARI0002h initial infusion were administered based on its approved dosing regimen (Table S1) while booster infusion was administered to patients with some degree of response at day +100 and no dose‐limiting toxicities during the first infusion. 13 Bendamustine 90 mg/m2/day (days −5 to −4) was allowed as alternative LD in renal impairment based on protocol.

Statistical analysis

Data were reported using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were employed for qualitative variables and medians and ranges for quantitative variables. Median follow‐up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. The estimation of time to event (OS/PFS) was made using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log‐rank test. Statistical analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0.0.

Treatment

Fifteen patients (15/18, 83%) received BT: 10 (67%) received alkylator‐based schemes, 4 (27%) with alkylator‐free (mainly carfilzomib‐based) and 1 (6%) received radiotherapy (RT) alone. The most used combination was cyclophosphamide/corticosteroids (27%).

In patients with CNS‐MM (n = 7), 4 (57%) received CNS‐directed therapy and only 2 (29%) were CNS‐free at ARI0002h infusion (Table 2).

IT included methotrexate, cytarabine and hydrocortisone.

Median production time for cesni‐cel was 9 days (7–12) with a median turnaround time of 28.5 days (20–65) and a median vein‐to‐vein (V2V) time of 32 days (22–69). One patient died from PD during manufacturing.

Of 19 patients, 17 (89%) received cesni‐cel: 11 patients with PCL, 5 patients with CNS‐MM and 1 patient with dual involvement. Median time from PCL/CNS‐MM diagnosis to ARI0002h infusion was 5.62 months (0.07–64.8); in patients with SPCL or CNS‐MM (n = 12), median time from MM diagnosis to ARI0002h was 67.9 months (10.4–130).

Of 17 patients, 16 (94%) received standard LD chemotherapy while 1 patient (6%) received bendamustine due to acute kidney injury secondary to cast nephropathy.

Of 17 patients, 14 (82%) received full cesni‐cel dose, 2 cases (12%) required discontinuation due to ARI0002h‐related toxicity (both received two aliquots with a total of 1.2 × 106 CAR‐cells/kg) and 1 patient (6%) received a reduced dose due to manufacturing inability to reach the targeted concentration (1.45 × 106 CAR cells/kg). Overall, 11 patients (11/17, 65%) received a booster dose with a median time from A1 of 4 months (3.5–5.5).

Safety

CRS was seen in 13 cases (13/17, 76%; none ≥G3). No differences were seen in CRS incidence and severity between PCL and CNS‐MM, as 10 (10/12, 83%) and four (4/6, 67%) patients developed any grade CRS respectively. Nine cases (9/13, 69%) required treatment, being tocilizumab (+/−dexamethasone) the most employed therapy (78%) (Figure 2).

No cases of ICANS were recorded in the CNS‐MM subset while one G3 case was detected in the PCL group. In addition, no tumour inflammation‐associated neurotoxicity (TIAN) nor delayed neurotoxicity was observed.

Three cases (3/17, 18%) of IEC‐HS were detected (3/3 in PCL subset), two non‐severe (≤G2) and one G5 IEC‐HS with a fatal outcome on day +19 from infusion (a patient with SPCL).

All patients developed any grade early ICAHT, with seven cases (7/17, 41%) developing ≥G3 N‐ICAHT and six (6/17, 35%) ≥G3 T‐ICAHT. In patients reaching day +100 (n = 15), seven (7/15, 47%) developed late N‐ICAHT (3/7 ≥G3) and five (5/15, 33%) developed late T‐ICAHT (2/5 ≥G3).

Infectious complications were seen in 11 patients (11/17, 65%) at day +30, with nine cases (9/11, 82%) developing viral infections and two patients (2/11, 18%) having bacterial aetiologies.

Efficacy

Fifteen patients (15/17, 88%) had evaluable response at day +100: 10 with PCL, 4 with CNS‐MM and 1 with PCL/CNS‐MM. Two cases (2/17, 12%) were not available for response assessment: one due to G5 IEC‐HS and one due to data cut‐off before day +100.

Under intent‐to‐treat approach, ORR at day +100 was 88%. Among response‐evaluable patients in the per‐protocol population (n = 15), eight cases (8/15, 53%) achieved a very good partial response (VGPR) or better. Thirteen patients (13/15, 87%) achieved ≥VGPR as best response during follow‐up, as five patients (5/15, 33%) improved responses after day +100. BM‐MRD negativity rate was 100% in all patients with available information at day +100 (14/15; one case not assessed).

Among patients who received a booster dose (n = 11), four cases (4/11, 36%) were already in complete response (CR) at the time of the second infusion. In the remaining seven cases, five patients (5/7, 71%) deepened the response following the additional dose while two cases (2/7, 29%) maintained it (one was already in VGPR).

In patients with PCL and evaluable response (n = 11), ORR was maintained between PPCL and SPCL as 5/6 (83%) and 5/5 (100%) achieved ≥VGPR respectively. In addition, all three patients with EMD achieved a complete metabolic response at day +100.

In patients with CNS‐MM and evaluable response (n = 5), four patients (4/5, 80%) achieved a complete CNS response during follow‐up. In addition, all three patients with CSF involvement had CSF negativity with ARI0002h detection in the CSF by digital polymerase chain reaction (PCR). No difference in response was observed based on previous CNS‐directed therapy administration. Furthermore, no differences were observed in the previously BCMA‐exposed patient (CR). Survival

Median follow‐up time for all patients receiving ARI0002h (n = 17) was 20.7 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 2.5–38.8). Patients with PCL and CNS‐MM had median follow‐up time of 21 (95% CI, 0–61.8) and 10.3 months (95% CI, 7.9–12.7) respectively.

Overall, mPFS in patients who received cesni‐cel was 10.5 months (95% CI, 5.8–15.1) (Figure 4A). At the end of follow‐up, 12 patients (12/17, 70%) had progressed while one case (1/17, 6%) died due to non‐relapse mortality. In patients with PD (n = 12), two patients (2/12, 17%) had serological progression, four (4/12, 33%) had a clinical relapse and six (6/12, 50%) presented with both. Median time from PD to death or end of follow‐up was 5.5 months (95% CI, 2.8–8.1).

In patients with CNS‐MM (n = 6; Figure 4B), only two cases (2/6, 33%) had PD at 4.6 and 23.8 months (one with CNS recurrence) while the remaining four were event‐free at last follow‐up, with an estimated 12‐month PFS rate of 80%.

Among patients with PCL (n = 12; Figure 4C,D), mPFS was 9.3 months (95% CI, 6.4–12.2) and no significant differences were seen between PPCL and SPCL, with mPFS of 8.2 (95% CI, 4.3–12.1) and 10.5 months (95% CI, 6.7–14.3) respectively (log‐rank test, p = 0.97).

Patients who received a booster dose displayed better results in terms of mPFS (12.5 vs. 4.9 months; log‐rank test, p = 0.008).

At the end of follow‐up, mOS in patients receiving ARI0002h was 15.9 months (95% CI, 12.6–19.2) (Figure 5A) with an estimated 12‐month OS rate of 73%. A total of seven deaths (7/17, 41%) were recorded after infusion: six died from relapse‐related mortality and one died from IEC‐HS.

In the CNS‐MM subset (n = 6; Figure 5B), only one death was recorded at 5.3 months; the estimated 12‐month OS rate was 80%.

In patients with PCL (n = 12; Figure 5C,D), mOS was 15.2 months (95% CI, 12.1–18.3) with an estimated 12‐month OS rate of 73%. No differences were seen between PPCL and SPCL groups, with mOS of 15.2 (95% CI, 12.6–17.7) and 19.9 months (95% CI, 6.3–25.5) respectively (log‐rank test, p = 0.72).

Patients who received a booster dose displayed better results in terms of mOS (not reached vs. 9.5 months; log‐rank test, p ≤ 0.001).

DISCUSSION

EMD/PCL shares a biological background characterized by PC independence from marrow microenvironment, a feature associated with a higher prevalence of HRCA. 3 , 9 Seventy‐four per cent of our population harboured one HRCA and 37% presented with multi‐hit disease, with TP53 and chromosome 1 abnormalities being the most frequent. These results align with recently published data by the IMS/IMWG, 20 and worse outcomes are expected in patients with such features.

Survival in CNS‐MM is poor with an mOS <7 months from diagnosis. 10 , 21 These results can be attributed to high‐risk biological features and poor blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration by conventional agents, as pomalidomide and selinexor are the only systemic drugs with consistent CNS effect. 10 , 21 This has often prompted the use of CNS‐directed therapy through IT drug administration or RT, yielding inconclusive results in terms of OS. 10 In our study, 57% of patients with CNS‐MM received CNS‐directed therapy, and only half of those were CNS‐free at ARI0002h infusion: the variable and limited activity of conventional agents highlights the challenging management of CNS‐MM prior to advanced cellular therapies.

OS has steadily improved in newly diagnosed PPCL with the addition of new agents, with a mOS of 28.4 months in patients treated with carfilzomib/lenalidomide‐based triplets plus HSCT and a 3‐year OS of 70% with daratumumab‐based quadruplets. 22 , 23 However, RR‐PPCL has a dismal prognosis resembling SPCL with mOS of 4.2 months and a 1‐year OS rate of only 19%. 5

In this setting, a rapid manufacturing capacity to salvage patients is imperative and might be solved by point‐of‐care, academic products with fast availability (median V2V of ARI0002h was 32 days as compared with real‐world data (RWD) from cilta‐cel, often exceeding the 2‐month mark). 24 , 25

CAR T cells targeting BCMA have revolutionized the treatment of RRMM; however, scarce information was available in PCL/EMD‐CNS with cilta‐cel or ide‐cel since patients were excluded from trials. 26

RWD have recently emerged showing EMD/PCL was independently associated with early progression in the MyCARe model (92% higher hazard of 5‐month relapse). 27 In addition, standard of care (SOC) cilta‐cel/ide‐cel has been recently evaluated in PCL in the study led by Galarza‐Fortuna et al. showing an ORR of 86% (70% ≥VGPR) and mPFS/mOS of 9 and 13 months, respectively, with a 79% incidence of CRS (6% ≥G3) and 15% ICANS (one G5 case). 28 SOC cilta‐cel/ide‐cel has also been evaluated in CNS‐MM in the study led by Gaballa et al.: ORR was 80% (70% ≥VGPR) and mPFS/mOS were 6.3 and 13.3 months, respectively, with 80% developing CRS and 30% ICANS (10% G3). 29

Our results align with previously published data in non‐uHRMM, 11 , 12 , 13 as 87% achieved ≥VGPR; in addition, a similar response to SOC CAR‐T in PCL/CNS‐MM was observed (of note, the former had an over‐representation of patients treated with ide‐cel and their cilta‐cel group displayed a better result in terms of PFS/OS). 28 , 29 Besides, all patients with CSF involvement had detectable ARI0002h in the CSF by digital PCR, showing BBB penetration by CAR T cells and thus becoming a promising option given the poor results with previous standard non‐cellular approaches.

Overall, mPFS and mOS were 10.5 and 15.9 months, respectively; remaining worse than results in pivotal trials 11 , 12 , 13 ; however, our results are in accordance with RWD in PCL/CNS‐MM: a better outcome in patients with CNS‐MM was shown in addition to an excellent safety profile 29 and a better outcome was also observed in patients with SPCL when compared with commercial CAR‐T, 28 highlighting the role of EMD as a risk factor for early progression.

Finally, safety profile remained in accordance with previous data. Any grade CRS was seen in 76% of patients with no cases of ≥G3 CRS and a low incidence of IEC‐HS (18%, albeit one fatal case was reported). Interestingly, no cases of ICANS were seen in the CNS‐MM group as compared with the 30% incidence reported in the previous studies, 29 with no detected cases of delayed neurotoxicity during follow‐up. Despite patients with PCL/CNS‐MM being excluded from trial enrolment given the presumed increased toxicity, the incidence of IEC‐associated toxicities remains equivalent to patients without such features. Hence, future trial design should allow PCL/CNS‐MM inclusion given the dismal prognosis with conventional approaches.

Overall, patients with PCL/CNS‐MM have an equivalent response rate to non‐uHRMM patients but fail to maintain it over time. Future perspective should focus in trial inclusion given the initial response and dismal prognosis with conventional therapy, accelerated exposure and sequencing of T‐cell‐based therapies such as bispecific antibodies 30 , 31 , 32 or CAR T cells (like GEM‐PLASMACAR (NCT06830733), an ongoing phase II trial evaluating the efficacy of ARI0002h in newly diagnosed PPCL), rapid manufacturing to avoid patient loss and post‐CAR T‐cell maintenance therapy given the high rates of early relapse.

Our study has several limitations, including the retrospective design and small sample size, which require careful interpretation of the results and thus limit comparison between groups and across studies. Further prospective trials are needed to better characterize these populations and factors associated with improved outcomes; however, we believe that our results align with previous reports showing that BCMA‐directed CAR T cells are safe and effective in CNS‐MM and PCL and should be pursued in patients with such features.

In conclusion, ARI0002h is active in patients with PCL and CNS‐MM, achieving high rates of response with a manageable safety profile. These results justify evaluation in clinical trials and open the door for the role of maintenance therapy after CAR‐T in ultra‐high‐risk patients.
