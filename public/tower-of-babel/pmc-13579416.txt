Protocol for assessing wildlife exposure and risk from pesticides in tropical food chains with limited biomonitoring data
Authors: Shaorong Chen, Zijian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPesticides threaten wildlife through terrestrial food chains, but data on wildlife exposure are scarce. Here, we present a protocol to assess pesticide health risks in wildlife using an integrated physiologically-based kinetic approach. We describe steps for data compilation, regional contamination scoring, and three-tiered exposure estimation using tissue, plant, and soil. We then detail procedures for ecological risk calculation, spatial mapping, and uncertainty analysis. This protocol enables traceable, quantitative, spatially resolved risk assessments for herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Chen and Li.1Subject areas: Bioinformatics, Health Sciences, Environmental sciences

Before you begin

Background

Pesticides are widely used in agricultural, forestry, public health, and urban settings, but they can enter the environment through spray drift, runoff, leaching, volatilization, atmospheric deposition, and transboundary or long-distance transport.2,3,4 Consequently, pesticide residues may reach wildlife habitats and accumulate in environmental media such as soil, water, vegetation, and prey organisms.5,6,7,8 Through food-web transfer, herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores may be exposed via diet, drinking water, inhalation, dermal contact, or ingestion of contaminated plants and prey.9,10,11 Wildlife are essential for maintaining ecosystem structure, function, biodiversity, and stability.12,13 Therefore, assessing pesticide-related health risks in wildlife is important for protecting ecological health.13 However, biomonitoring data for free-ranging wildlife are often limited and may only include pesticide concentrations measured in whole bodies or internal organs such as liver, kidney, muscle, or fat.14,15 These data gaps make conventional risk assessment difficult. To address this limitation, this protocol develops a physiologically based kinetic (PBK) modeling framework that integrates limited internal biomonitoring data with modeled external exposure estimates.16 This internal–external exposure approach supports wildlife health risk assessment under data-limited conditions and provides a transparent method for evaluating pesticide-related risks across wildlife species and trophic groups.

Innovation

The innovation of this protocol is the development of an integrated modeling approach to track pesticide transfer through the terrestrial trophic food chain, from grass and other environmental media to herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores. The model can estimate pesticide distribution at each trophic level and use contamination in lower-level organisms to infer dietary exposure in higher-level wildlife. In addition, the framework can apply limited biomonitoring data, such as pesticide concentrations in tissues or organs, to back-calculate external exposure from internal body burdens. This internal–external modeling approach supports wildlife risk assessment under data-limited conditions. Model assumptions

This protocol relies on a set of core assumptions that underpin the exposure and risk calculations:

Steady-state condition

Pesticide concentrations in wildlife tissues and environmental media are assumed to be at steady state (i.e., input equals elimination over the exposure period). This assumption enables the use of biotransfer factors (BTFs) and the back-calculation of daily intake from tissue residues. It is appropriate for chronic, continuous exposure scenarios but may overestimate or underestimate doses under episodic or pulsed exposure.

Linear dose-response relationship

The calculation of hazard quotients (HQ) and ecological carcinogenic risks (ECR) assumes linearity between the external dose (ADD) and the toxicological endpoint, consistent with the USEPA’s low-dose linear extrapolation for carcinogens and threshold-based approach for non-carcinogens.

Species extrapolation

When species-specific BTFs or physiological parameters are unavailable, values from closely related species or allometric scaling are used. This introduces uncertainty that is quantified in Step 22 but may still bias estimates if the target species differs substantially in metabolic capacity or tissue composition.

Tissue concentration represents dietary exposure. For Tier 1, the measured liver concentration is assumed to reflect the dietary intake of the pesticide, with minimal contribution from non-dietary routes (inhalation, dermal absorption, drinking water). This assumption is reasonable for lipophilic pesticides with high oral bioavailability but may underestimate exposure for highly volatile or water-soluble compounds.

Plant uptake is transpiration-driven. The soil-plant transfer model (Step 10) assumes that pesticide movement from soil to leaves is dominated by transpiration-driven mass flow, with diffusion and sorption described by the model parameters. This assumption is most valid for moderately hydrophobic compounds (log K∼ow∼ between 1 and 4) and under non-water-stressed conditions.

Global mean concentration is representative. The contamination score (S) is calculated relative to a global mean concentration for each pesticide, weighted by the number of sampling sites. This global mean is assumed to represent a reasonable central tendency, although it is influenced by the uneven geographic distribution of data.

A stratified strategy for estimating wildlife exposure levels

Timing: 10–15 days

A three-tier approach is applied. Adopt a hierarchical evidence-based approach wherein empirical tissue measurements take precedence over dietary reconstructions, and both supersede soil-to-plant modeling. Anticipate expanding uncertainty bounds as one moves from Tier 1 through Tier 3.

Expected outcomes

This protocol generates three primary outputs. First, it produces region-specific contamination scores (Ssoil, Splant, Sanimal) that rank pesticide contamination levels across countries or spatial units relative to the global mean. Second, it yields tier-specific average daily dose (ADD) estimates for herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores, derived from either measured tissue concentrations (Tier 1), measured plant concentrations (Tier 2), or soil-to-plant modeled concentrations (Tier 3). Third, it calculates non-carcinogenic hazard quotients (HQ) and carcinogenic ecological risks (ECR) for each species-pesticide combination, with associated 95% confidence intervals from Monte Carlo simulations. All results are compiled into structured tables and visualized as spatial maps, enabling comparative risk assessment across regions and trophic levels. Users can expect to identify high-risk regions and data gaps, and to prioritize future monitoring efforts.

Limitations

First, the toxicity benchmarks are human-health oriented. The RfD and OSF values employed in this protocol were primarily obtained from the USEPA IRIS database, which does not account for the physiological characteristics, metabolic capacities, or species-specific sensitivities of different wildlife taxa. Also, the external intake is back-calculated from limited biomonitoring data using a steady-state PBK model. In many cases, the measured pesticide concentration represents only a single point in time and may not reflect the full internal exposure history of the animal. Because pesticide residues in wildlife tissues can decrease over time through elimination, metabolism, growth dilution, or other dissipation processes, the steady-state assumption may lead to uncertainty or bias in the estimated intake. Future studies should consider incorporating toxicokinetic dissipation parameters, such as elimination rates or biological half-lives, or collecting repeated biomonitoring measurements over time to better characterize temporal variation in internal exposure. Consequently, the resulting hazard quotients and ecological carcinogenic risks should be interpreted as screening-level indicators rather than definitive risk determinations. Second, extrapolating PBK model parameters introduces uncertainty. The BTF and other species-specific input parameters were largely derived from laboratory or domesticated animals. Applying these values to free-ranging wildlife inevitably adds uncertainty. Although allometric scaling and species-specific data were used wherever possible, unmeasured interspecific differences in metabolic pathways, tissue partitioning, and elimination rates may bias the predictions. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that body weight and intake rates are major drivers of uncertainty, underscoring the need for improved species-specific inputs and independent validation. Third, limitations exist in plant exposure modeling. In the absence of measured pesticide concentrations in wild plants, this protocol applies a plant PBK transfer model originally designed for fruit trees, supplemented by the Trapp model. This dual-model approach helps fill data gaps but has not been fully validated for wild herbaceous species, and the associated uncertainty requires further evaluation. Fourth, geographic data coverage is uneven. The 100 studies compiled in the original application of this protocol covered only 30 countries, with sampling heavily concentrated in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia, while Africa, Oceania, and Central Asia remain severely under-represented. Therefore, the identification of high-risk regions largely reflects the distribution of available literature rather than the true global picture of pesticide contamination. The elevated risk estimates observed in ecologically protected areas also reveal critical gaps in current monitoring systems, particularly regarding wildlife tissues and remote ecosystems. Finally, the contamination score is relative by nature. Contamination score is a comparative index derived from global mean concentrations and does not correspond to any toxicological safety threshold. A high contamination score value for a given region indicates that contamination levels are above the global average but does not directly imply actual ecological risk. The level of risk must be assessed jointly with the ADD and toxicity benchmarks.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Extreme concentration values bias the contamination score (S). [See Step 6].

Extremely high or low pesticide concentrations at a few sampling sites may strongly influence the log-transformed deviation from the global mean, leading to overestimation or underestimation of the regional contamination score.

Potential solution

Examine pesticide-level distributions and identify outliers using boxplot or Grubbs’ test. Perform sensitivity analysis by recalculating contamination scores after removing values above the 99th percentile or below the 1st percentile. Report both the original and trimmed scores to show the influence of extreme values.

Problem 2

Missing BTF for a target wildlife species. [See Step 8].

The required BTF for a specific species and tissue (e.g., fox liver) is not available in the supplementary tables or published literature, preventing direct back-calculation of ADD.

Potential solution

Use allometric scaling based on body weight and metabolic rate from a closely related species with known BTF. Alternatively, adopt a default BTF from a surrogate species (e.g., use rat BTF with a safety factor of 2) and explicitly document the extrapolation method. Perform a sensitivity analysis by varying the BTF by ±50% to assess its impact on the final HQ/ECR.

Problem 3

Inconsistent results between Tier 2 and Tier 3 exposure estimates for the same species and region. [See Step 9 and Step 10].

When both measured plant concentrations (Tier 2) and measured soil concentrations (Tier 3) are available for the same country, the calculated ADD values for herbivores or carnivores may differ substantially, making it difficult to decide which estimate to report.

Potential solution

Always prioritise Tier 2 over Tier 3 because Tier 2 uses measured plant data, which is one step closer to the actual exposure pathway. If the discrepancy is large (e.g., more than one order of magnitude), investigate possible causes: differences in sampling sites, sampling years, or unit conversions. As a sensitivity measure, report both estimates in supplemental information and discuss the range in the main text, using the more conservative (higher) estimate for risk screening.

Problem 4

Unavailability of environmental or physiological parameters required for the Trapp soil-plant transfer model (Tier 3). [See Step 10].

The transpiration rate model requires air temperature, relative humidity, leaf surface area, stomatal resistance, and other parameters. These data may not be available for many sampling sites, especially in remote or under-studied regions.

Potential solution

Use default values provided in Table S1 for parameters such as leaf mass per area, conductance, and water content. For meteorological parameters (temperature, humidity), use long-term averages from the nearest WorldClim or ERA5 grid cell. If multiple parameters are missing, consider a simpler empirical BAF (e.g., from literature) and treat the resulting plant concentration as a high-uncertainty estimate, flagged in the results.

Problem 5

Monte Carlo simulation does not converge or requires excessive computation time when many input parameters are sampled. [See Step 21].

The standard 10,000 iterations may be insufficient if the output distribution has a long tail, or conversely, may be too slow when the model is complex (e.g., Tier 3 with many parameters).

Potential solution

Increase the number of iterations to 50,000 or 100,000 and check convergence by tracking the running mean of the median and 95th percentile. For speed, use parallel processing (e.g., foreach in R) or reduce the number of parameters by fixing those with very small variance (CV < 10%). Alternatively, replace full Monte Carlo with a Latin Hypercube sample of 2,000–5,000 runs, which often achieves similar accuracy with fewer iterations.
