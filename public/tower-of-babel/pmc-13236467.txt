Risk factors for early and mid-to-late pregnancy loss and their effects on reproductive performance in dairy cows
Authors: Hyun-Gu Kang, Jae-Kwan Jeong, Ill-Hwa Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceThe risk factors and reproductive consequences of pregnancy loss may differ by gestational stage, but stage-specific patterns before and after placentation is firmly established have not been adequately characterized.ObjectiveTo identify the risk factors for pregnancy loss at different gestational stages, before and after 60 days of gestation when the placentation is firmly established and quantify the subsequent reproductive performance.MethodsThis study analyzed the records from 6,444 pregnancies confirmed by transrectal ultrasonography 31 days after artificial insemination. Pregnancy loss between 31–61 days was categorized as late embryonic or early fetal loss (LEL–EFL), and losses between 62–260 days were categorized as mid-to-late fetal loss (M–LFL). Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the risk factors. Survival analysis was performed to assess the probability of becoming pregnant again by 400 days postpartum.ResultsThe cumulative incidence of pregnancy loss was 5.8% for LEL–EFL and 7.7% for M–LFL; LEL–EFL accounted for 43.2% of all losses. A higher body condition score at calving tended to reduce the risk of early loss (p < 0.1), whereas the synchronization protocols (p < 0.0001), first postpartum service (p < 0.0001), and summer insemination (p < 0.1) increased it. Peripartum disorders increased the risk at both stages (odds ratio: 1.30–1.72, p < 0.05–0.0001), and twin pregnancies increased M–LFL (p < 0.0001). Cows with pregnancy loss had a lower probability of becoming pregnant again by 400 days postpartum and longer calving-to-pregnancy intervals than unaffected cows (227.1–376.3 days vs. 107.3 days).Conclusions and RelevanceThe patterns of pregnancy loss were stage-specific, and early and later losses had distinct and progressively more adverse effects on reproductive performance. Optimizing the body condition at calving, refining breeding management, and improving peripartum health may reduce pregnancy loss and improve reproductive outcomes.Keywords: Embryonic mortality, fetal mortality, body condition score, periparturient disease, heat stress

INTRODUCTION

Maintaining adequate reproductive efficiency on dairy farms is essential for ensuring farmers’ profitability. Pregnancy loss, categorized into embryonic and fetal loss, can cause substantial economic damage through reduced production and impaired reproductive performance [1]. Cows experiencing pregnancy loss must be rebred and reconceived to maintain productivity, incurring additional costs for reproductive treatments and inseminations [2,3]. Furthermore, prolonged calving intervals from pregnancy loss can increase the risk of culling [4]. The economic burden of pregnancy loss has been estimated at approximately USD 555 per case in the USA [5] and USD 1,415 per abortion in Argentina [6].

The reported incidences of identifiable pregnancy loss vary widely, ranging from 10.0% to 23.8% across studies [7,8,9]. Differences in the definition of pregnancy loss, farm management practices, season of artificial insemination (AI), cow-specific characteristics, and other variables may influence this variation [10,11,12]. Importantly, previous studies have applied heterogeneous, often narrow gestational windows, most commonly restricting pregnancy loss to the first 90 days of gestation, without a consistent rationale for the chosen period [10,11]. A recent meta-analysis on the timing of pregnancy loss [13] summarized these heterogeneous definitions, reporting average incidences of 27% for early embryonic loss (EEL; 19–32 days of gestation), 13% for late embryonic loss (LEL; 30–45 days), 7% for early fetal loss (EFL; 45–60 days), and 2% for late fetal loss (LFL; 60–90 days of gestation). These inconsistencies in definitions motivated this stage-specific classification, in which late embryonic or early fetal loss (LEL–EFL) are distinguished from mid-to-late fetal loss (M–LFL), defined as occurring between 62 and 260 days of gestation. This extended definition of M–LFL, enabled by the present long-term dataset, allows an evaluation of pregnancy loss across mid and late gestation and provides a broader understanding of fetal attrition than has been available in previous reports.

Minimizing the impact of pregnancy loss requires identifying the risk factors and implementing effective countermeasures. Several factors have been associated with embryonic or fetal loss, including heat stress, cow breed, sire identity, postpartum complications, the number of inseminations after calving, and milk yield [14,15,16]. In addition, twin pregnancies, postpartum decreases in the body condition score (BCS), and mastitis are recognized risk factors for early fetal loss [17,18,19]. Despite the considerable research into the risk factors, most studies have focused on specific gestational periods, especially early pregnancy loss [11,20], with few examining how the timing of pregnancy loss impacts subsequent reproductive performance.

The risk factors and reproductive outcomes may differ according to the timing of pregnancy loss, reflecting the physiological and histological changes before and after approximately 60 days of gestation, when implantation is firmly established. Early pregnancy, before complete placentation, is particularly vulnerable to metabolic, endocrine, and environmental stresses that disrupt the maternal recognition of pregnancy. In contrast, later pregnancy, after stable placentation, is influenced more by uterine and placental pathology, placental perfusion, and nutritional constraints associated with rapid fetal growth [21]. Based on this rationale, this study hypothesized that categorizing pregnancy loss into LEL–EFL (31–61 days) and M–LFL (62–260 days), according to the time of implantation, consolidation of placentation, and the onset of chorioallantoic nutrition, would show distinct risk profiles and reproductive impacts. Such information could inform the development of targeted preventive measures to reduce pregnancy loss and improve the reproductive efficiency on dairy farms. Therefore, this study examined the incidence and risk factors of LEL–EFL and M–LFL, and their respective effects on the reproductive performance and culling in dairy cows.

METHODS

Participating farms

This study evaluated 28 dairy farms in Korea, each maintaining 25–260 lactating cows, from 2011 to 2024. The distribution of farm size was as follows: 36% for farms with 30 cows or fewer, 32% for farms with 31 to 50 cows, and 32% for farms with 51 or more cows. Cows were kept in loose housing systems and were fed total mixed rations containing brewers’ grain, alfalfa hay, cottonseed, beet pulp, corn silage, tall fescue, timothy hay, minerals, and vitamins, formulated to provide 17%–20% crude protein, 4.8%–5.0% crude fat, and 25%–30% neutral detergent fiber. All cows that calved were milked twice daily, and the mean annual milk yields ranged from 9,525 to 11,730 kg across farms. All barns were equipped with fans and shade to reduce heat during the hot season from May to October.

Health and reproductive management

Herd health and reproductive checks were performed by the authors twice a month. These checks included monitoring for health issues and ultrasonographic examination of the reproductive organs (ovaries and uterus). BCS was recorded using a five-point scale with quarter-point divisions [22]. Peripartum disorders were defined according to previous reports [23,24,25]. Dystocia was defined as difficult calving requiring manual/mechanical assistance or surgery. A retained placenta was defined as the retention of the fetal membranes for more than 24-h postpartum. Septicemic metritis was diagnosed based on the presence of fever with a watery, fetid, red-brown uterine discharge. Ketosis was diagnosed by the presence of anorexia, depression, and acetone-like breath odor. Milk fever was diagnosed based on the signs of nervousness, weakness, and inability to rise after calving. Abomasal displacement was diagnosed by abdominal auscultation, revealing a characteristic ping sound. Clinical endometritis was diagnosed by the presence of mucopurulent uterine discharge and confirmed by ultrasonographic examination. The same veterinarians in the research team diagnosed and treated all health disorders until resolution, as confirmed during follow-up visits. The cows underwent a milk ring test every three months for brucellosis and the tuberculin test annually for tuberculosis. Vaccination against foot-and-mouth disease was performed twice yearly.

The voluntary waiting period from calving to the first AI was 40 days. Estrus detection combined an automatic estrus monitoring device with visual observations. The synchronization protocols included Ovsynch, Presynch–Ovsynch, Double–Ovsynch, and modified Presynch–Ovsynch protocols [26,27,28]. The reproductive data were collected for at least 400 days postpartum or until culling.

Pregnancy diagnosis, twin identification, and classification of pregnancy loss

Pregnancy diagnosis was performed via transrectal ultrasonography using a 6.5 MHz array transducer (SR-1C-Wireless Rectal Scanner; SongKang GLC Co., Ltd., Korea) at 31 ± 1.9 days and 61 ± 1.7 days post-AI, and by rectal palpation at 182 ± 2.1 days. An ultrasonography system equipped with color Doppler was used to visualize and hear the fetal atrial beat. A cow was considered pregnant at 31 days after AI if a corpus luteum (CL) was present and a viable embryo, with a heartbeat, clearly defined anechoic amniotic fluid, and appropriate size for gestational age, was visible in the uterus. Pregnancy at 61 days was confirmed by the presence of a living fetus with normal fetal movements and normal allantoic and amniotic fluid in the uterus. A twin pregnancy was defined as the visualization of two embryos or fetuses. The number and laterality of CL were recorded when identifiable. Pregnancy diagnosis at 182 days was determined by rectal palpation of an enlarged middle uterine artery with a strong fremitus.

LEL or EFL was defined as a pregnancy confirmed at 31 days post-AI but no longer present at 61 days. These cases were collectively categorized as LEL–EFL. M–LFL was defined as a pregnancy confirmed at 61 days post-AI but subsequently lost, as indicated by the observed estrus, detection of an aborted or mummified fetus by 260 days post-AI, a diagnosis of not pregnant at the 182-day check, or ultrasonographic evidence on at least two consecutive examinations showing that a previously viable fetus could no longer be visualized or showed no heartbeat. Thus, pregnancy loss was classified as LEL–EFL (31–61 days of gestation) or M–LFL (62–260 days of gestation).

Data collection and study design

This observational study was designed, analyzed, and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology for Veterinary Science (STROBE-Vet) guidelines for veterinary epidemiological research [29]. A total of 6,444 pregnancies in 3,327 cows, diagnosed as pregnant via ultrasonography at 31 days after AI, were collected from 28 dairy farms in Korea between 2011 and 2024. The monthly mean ambient temperature ranged from –1.48°C in January to 26.0°C in August, while the relative humidity (RH) ranged from 69.7% in April to 87.1% in August in the study area. The monthly and seasonal THI values were then summarized. The seasonal distribution of maximum THI was 55–69 for March to May (spring), 74–81 for June to August (summer), 56–74 for September to November (autumn), and 43–47 for December to February (winter). The dataset included information on the parity, dates of reproductive events (calving, AI, pregnancy diagnosis, and pregnancy loss), BCS at calving and AI, peripartum disorders, and twin pregnancy. Peripartum disorders included dystocia, retained placenta, septicemic metritis, ketosis, milk fever, abomasal displacement, and clinical endometritis.

Based on these data, this study examined: 1) the incidence of LEL–EFL and M–LFL, 2) the risk factors associated with each loss category, and 3) the impacts of LEL–EFL, M–LFL, and their combination on reproductive outcomes (likelihood of re-establishing pregnancy by 400 days postpartum, the interval from calving to pregnancy, and the number of inseminations per conception) as well as the culling risk, compared with unaffected cows. Prolonged calving-to-conception intervals are a common herd-level reason for involuntary culling. Moreover, in this production system, cows whose open period approaches or exceeds approximately 365 days after calving are usually classified as infertile and become candidates for involuntary culling. Therefore, the reproductive outcomes up to 400 days postpartum were collected to capture the conceptions occurring around this management threshold and reflect how the reproductive performance is evaluated in these herds.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SE of the means, with a few exceptions. For the statistical analyses, cows were categorized by parity (primiparous or multiparous); by BCS at calving and AI (≤ 2.75, 3, 3.25, or ≥ 3.5); by the occurrence of peripartum disorders (no or yes); by AI type (AI following natural estrus or a synchronization protocol), and by the number of AIs after calving (first, second, or ≥ third). The AI season was categorized as spring (March to May), summer (June to August), autumn (September to November), or winter (December to February). All analyses were performed using SAS statistical software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, USA).

The risk factors for LEL–EFL, M–LFL, and culling were evaluated using logistic regression with the GLIMMIX procedure and the odds ratio (OR) option. The logistic regression model for LEL–EFL and M–LFL included BCS at calving and AI, the number of AIs after calving, AI type (natural estrus or synchronization protocol), parity, history of peripartum disorders, season of AI, and twin pregnancy status (single or twin embryos). The logistic regression model for culling included parity, history of peripartum disorders, BCS at calving, and pregnancy loss category (controls, LEL–EFL, M–LFL, or both). Cow, year, and farm were included as random effects in both models to account for the repeated records and clustering. Candidate explanatory variables were first examined in univariable models, and variables with p values < 0.20, along with those considered biologically important, were entered into the multivariable model. Model selection used backward elimination based on likelihood-ratio tests, retaining biologically important covariates regardless of statistical significance. Biologically plausible interactions were evaluated only in the multivariable logistic model for LEL–EFL and M–LFL by adding two-way product terms between selected variables, particularly BCS at calving or at AI with parity and AI season with AI type. Each interaction term was added one at a time and retained if the p value < 0.05 and the pattern was biologically plausible. The model fit and assumptions were assessed by inspecting residuals and influence statistics, checking for multicollinearity using variance inflation factors, and evaluating the global goodness-of-fit by comparing the observed and predicted outcome frequencies across risk groups using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The ORs and 95% confidence intervals were calculated.

The probability of re-establishing pregnancy by 400 days postpartum was analyzed using a Cox proportional hazards model with the PHREG procedure. The time variable was the number of days from calving to conception. The cows that died, were sold, or were not pregnant by 400 days postpartum were censored. The model included BCS at calving, history of peripartum disorders, parity, pregnancy loss category (controls, LEL–EFL, M–LFL, or both), and the interactions among these variables. The cow, year, and farm were included as random effects. A shared frailty term for farms and a subject-specific frailty term for cow were included to account for unobserved heterogeneity and dependence among the observations from the same farm and for repeated pregnancies within cows. In the PHREG model, the proportional hazards assumption was assessed by testing the interactions between each covariate and the logarithm of time, and by inspecting log–log survival plots and plots of scaled Schoenfeld residuals as a function of time; it was considered adequately met. The mean interval from calving to conception was estimated using survival analysis according to the Kaplan–Meier method in the LIFETEST procedure. The survival plots were generated using the Survival option in MedCalc (version 11.1; MedCalc Software, Belgium).

The number of inseminations per conception was analyzed using a general linear model that included parity, history of peripartum disorders, BCS at calving, and pregnancy loss category (controls, LEL–EFL, M–LFL, or both) as fixed effects. The model assumptions were evaluated by visually inspecting the residual histograms and Q–Q plots to assess the approximate normality, and by plotting the residuals as a function of the fitted values to assess homoscedasticity; these assumptions were considered adequately met. The differences were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05, and 0.05 < p < 0.1 was considered to indicate a trend.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics

The mean prevalence of dystocia, retained placenta, septicemic metritis, metabolic disorders including ketosis, milk fever, and abomasal displacement, and clinical endometritis was 5.3%, 8.9%, 0.6%, 3.0%, and 9.4%, respectively. Among the 6,444 pregnancies in 3,327 cows diagnosed as pregnant 31 days after AI, 375 (5.8%) experienced LEL–EFL and 494 (7.7%) experienced M–LFL, for a total of 869 pregnancies (13.5%) with pregnancy loss. Regarding repeated pregnancy losses, 18 of the 375 LEL–EFL pregnancies had a second loss, and two had a third loss. Among the 494 M–LFL pregnancies, nine had a second loss, and one had a third loss. In addition, 36 pregnancies experienced LEL–EFL and M–LFL within the same cow, of which three had a third LEL–EFL and one had a fourth loss (LEL–EFL).

The occurrence rates of LEL–EFL, M–LFL, and total pregnancy loss varied markedly across 28 dairy farms, ranging from 2.3% to 10.4%, 1.7% to 12.6%, and 6.9% to 18.8%, respectively. Fig. 1 presents the frequency of embryonic or fetal age-specific loss. LEL–EFL occurring between 31 and 61 days of gestation during the peri-implantation and early placentation period accounted for 43.2% of all losses, whereas M–LFL, occurring between 62 and 260 days of gestation after the placenta was firmly established, ranged monthly from 5.9% to 13.9%.

Impacts of LEL–EFL and M–LFL on reproductive performance and culling

The PHREG analysis was used to evaluate the effects of LEL–EFL, M–LFL, and their combination on the reproductive performance (Table 2). The probability of re-establishing pregnancy by 400 days postpartum was significantly lower (p < 0.0001) in cows with LEL–EFL, M–LFL, or both, than in the controls. In practical terms, cows that experienced pregnancy loss at any stage took substantially longer to conceive again, as reflected by their longer calving-to-pregnancy intervals in Fig. 2. Fig. 2 shows a clear separation of the survival curves. The control cows became pregnant much earlier than those with pregnancy loss, and the curve for cows with M–LFL was furthest to the right, indicating the longest delay in re-establishing pregnancy. Cows with a BCS of 3, 3.25, or ≥ 3.5 at calving were more likely to conceive by 400 days postpartum (p < 0.0001) than cows with a BCS ≤ 2.75. Multiparous cows (p < 0.0001) were less likely to conceive than primiparous cows. In addition, cows with a history of peripartum disorders had a reduced likelihood of re-establishing pregnancy by 400 days postpartum (p < 0.0001) than healthy cows.

The odds of culling were significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in cows with LEL–EFL (OR, 6.10), M–LFL (OR, 22.02), or both (OR, 57.03) than in the controls. Multiparous cows also had a higher likelihood of being culled (OR, 2.33; p < 0.0001) than primiparous cows.

DISCUSSION

In this study, 5.8% of pregnancies were lost between 31–61 days of gestation (LEL–EFL) and 7.7% between 62–260 days (M–LFL), yielding an overall pregnancy loss of 13.5% and substantial variation in loss rates between farms. This wide variability in pregnancy loss between farms likely reflects the differences in reproductive management practices, including estrus detection and the timing of insemination, as well as the variation in nutritional status, heat abatement, and general health management among farms. The overall pregnancy loss in the present study aligns closely with the 13.7% reported by Barański et al. [8] and falls within the 10.8%–17.2% range reported previously [16,30]. Nevertheless, differences in pregnancy loss rates across studies are likely due in part to variations in the timing of pregnancy diagnosis, differences in reproductive and nutritional management, and contrasting climatic conditions that influence heat stress and overall health status. This finding of a higher proportion of LEL–EFL, which occurs before placentation is fully established and accounted for 43.2% of all cases, is consistent with earlier findings [12,31] and was higher than the M–LFL of 5.9%–13.9% per month. This early-stage vulnerability likely reflects incomplete placental attachment and heightened sensitivity to nutritional, physiological, and environmental stressors before the onset of chorioallantoic nutrition at around 60 days of gestation [21,32].

The risk profiles differed markedly between stages. LEL–EFL was associated with a low BCS at calving, use of synchronization protocols, insemination at the first postpartum service, and AI during summer, whereas M–LFL was linked primarily to twin pregnancies. Peripartum disorders increased the risk at both stages. A low BCS at calving increased the likelihood of LEL–EFL, but BCS at AI was not predictive. Hence, a poor body condition at calving, reflecting severe negative energy balance during the transition period, is associated with an altered metabolic status, including elevated non-esterified fatty acids and beta-hydroxybutyrate and reduced insulin, which can impair oocyte and embryo competence [33,34,35]. By contrast, a higher BCS at calving likely provides an energy reserve that attenuates the energy deficit during early lactation, and good nutritional status may also support adequate luteal function and progesterone secretion, improving early pregnancy maintenance. Previous studies reported that BCS > 3 at calving improves pregnancy survival between 43 and 260 days of gestation [9], and that BCS 2–3 during early gestation is associated with greater loss than BCS 3.25–4 [11]. The loss of BCS during early gestation has also been linked to higher embryonic loss [17]. These associations between BCS and pregnancy loss are also believed to reflect the effects of negative energy balance on the emergence of follicular waves and on luteal progesterone secretion after ovulation, which may in turn compromise the oocyte quality and subsequent embryonic or fetal development, as suggested by Carvalho et al. [33] and Ruebel et al. [34]. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of maintaining adequate energy reserves around calving and early lactation for pregnancy maintenance.

Cows inseminated after synchronization had a higher LEL–EFL risk than those bred on natural estrus, with no apparent effect on M–LFL. Although the mechanism underlying this association was not clarified in the present study, it may involve the effects of synchronization protocols on the follicular dynamics and luteal function. The induced follicular waves and repeated luteolysis can shorten the successive luteal phases, leading to suboptimal progesterone concentrations before and after insemination, thereby compromising oocyte maturation, early embryo development, and uterine receptivity [36], which predisposes the cows to pregnancy loss during the LEL–EFL period. Nevertheless, reports on the association between synchronization protocols and pregnancy loss are inconsistent: one study observed increased early loss under heat stress [37], whereas others found no direct association [14,38]. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify the physiological and endocrine differences between synchronized and naturally cycling cows. Insemination at the first postpartum service also increased the LEL–EFL risk, which may be related to incomplete postpartum uterine involution and suboptimal luteal development at the time of first AI [9,31]. This finding is consistent with Abdalla et al. [16] and likely reflects the improvements in nutrition and health status, as well as a more fully developed luteal function, at longer postpartum intervals for second and later inseminations [31,39].

Seasonal heat stress emerged as a key factor for LEL–EFL. In the authors’ region, the summer THI values (74–81) exceed those in the cooler months (43–69), and the summer AI was associated with higher early loss. A similar seasonal pattern is reported elsewhere [15]. At the cellular level, heat stress promotes oxidative stress and activates apoptosis pathways in the reproductive tissues and developing embryos, and also alters endometrial gene expression [40,41]. Heat stress can further impair ovarian steroidogenesis, producing an unfavorable endocrine and uterine environment that compromises gamete and embryo quality, uterine receptivity, and maternal endocrine function [40,41,42], increasing the risk of pregnancy loss. In particular, García-Ispierto et al. [15] reported that elevated maximum THI during 21–30 days of gestation, coinciding with implantation, increased early fetal loss, which is consistent with the finding that heat stress had a greater impact on LEL–EFL than on M–LFL. In the present study, inseminations performed during summer, when maximum THI values were highest, were associated with a higher incidence of LEL–EFL, whereas the losses during cooler seasons were lower. This seasonal pattern aligns with the THI thresholds identified by García-Ispierto et al. [15], suggesting that periods with a high THI around the time of insemination and implantation are critical for maintaining pregnancy.

Peripartum disorders, including retained placenta, uterine infections, and other conditions, increased the risk of both stages in the present study, as observed in previous studies [35,43,44]. Although detailed data are not shown, pregnancy loss was also examined according to the type of peripartum disorder. For LEL–EFL, metabolic disorders, including ketosis, milk fever, and abomasal displacement, as well as retained placenta and dystocia, appeared to have the greatest impact. For M–LFL, dystocia, retained placenta, and clinical endometritis showed the strongest associations with pregnancy loss. The potential mechanisms include chronic uterine inflammation, impaired placental function, and disruption of ovarian–uterine endocrine signaling [35,45]. Twin pregnancies were strongly associated with M–LFL, which is consistent with reports of increased late losses in twin-bearing cows [15,46]. According to the uterine capacity theory, the uterus has a limited capacity to support multiple fetuses because of the finite uterine space and blood flow [47]. In twin pregnancies, the developing placentomes and the expanding allantochorion of each fetus compete for attachment sites and vascular supply, particularly because fetal growth accelerates during mid- and late-gestation [47,48]. This placental vascular competition and reduced effective uterine capacity may compromise fetal growth and survival, predisposing twin-bearing cows to pregnancy loss during the M–LFL period. Interestingly, López-Gatius et al. [18] reported that three-quarters of twin-associated losses between 43 and 120 days occurred from 68 and 90 days, aligning with the finding that twins predominantly influenced M–LFL. Nevertheless, this study did not distinguish twins as unilateral or bilateral, and pregnancy outcomes have been reported to differ according to this orientation [49]. Therefore, this lack of information on twin laterality is a limitation of this dataset. Similarly, the factors contributing to mid to late fetal loss in twin pregnancies may include increased metabolic demands [18] and uterine space limitations, particularly in unilateral twins [49]. Given previous reports, a notable feature of this study is that synchronization protocols were primarily associated with LEL–EFL, whereas twin pregnancies were predominantly associated with M–LFL.

LEL–EFL and M–LFL impaired the reproductive performance and herd retention. Cows with losses had longer calving-to-conception intervals, required more inseminations per conception, and were more likely to be culled. Pregnancy loss may reflect or induce persistent inflammatory activity in the uterus, delayed uterine involution and endometrial repair, and impaired luteal support, all of which can reduce the likelihood of establishing and maintaining a subsequent pregnancy [44]. The mean delay from calving to pregnancy in LEL–EFL cows (119.8 days) closely matched the 108-day delay reported by Quintero Rodríguez et al. [20], whereas M–LFL and combined losses showed even greater delays, as noted by Suarez et al. [4]. In addition to pregnancy loss, this study showed that BCS at calving, peripartum disorders, and parity are associated with reproductive performance [35,44]. These variables can be viewed as indicators of the overall energy balance, metabolic and health status, and robustness during the transition period, all of which have a major impact on fertility. The increased AI per conception in cows with pregnancy loss is consistent with Keshavarzi et al. [3] and suggests that such losses may reflect impaired uterine recovery or underlying compromised fertility [3]. Prolonged calving-to-conception intervals are a common herd-level reason for involuntary culling. In this production system, when the days open period approaches or exceeds approximately 365 days, cows are usually classified as infertile and become candidates for involuntary culling. In line with this, the culling odds increased progressively from LEL–EFL (OR, 6.10) to M–LFL (OR, 22.02) to combined losses (OR, 57.03), paralleling Suarez et al. [4], who reported a 35% culling rate in cows with fetal loss compared to 4.6% in unaffected cows. Although M–LFL cases impose greater per-case economic losses [5,50], the higher frequency of LEL–EFL indicates that both stages contribute substantially to overall losses.

In conclusion, early (LEL–EFL) and later (M–LFL) pregnancy losses in dairy cows differ in prevalence, associated risk factors, and performance consequences, underscoring the importance of stage-specific prevention strategies. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is one of the few studies to quantify pregnancy loss up to 260 days of gestation using a large, multi-farm dataset. LEL–EFL, affecting 5.8% of pregnancies, was associated with modifiable management factors including low BCS at calving, peripartum disorders, use of synchronization protocols, early postpartum insemination, and summer heat stress. M–LFL, affecting 7.7% of pregnancies, was linked primarily to twin gestations and residual effects of peripartum disorders. Although individual M–LFL cases had greater economic impact, the higher incidence of LEL–EFL suggests that both stages contribute substantially to herd-level losses. Prevention should focus on maintaining optimal BCS at calving, aligning breeding with postpartum recovery and favorable thermal conditions, and strengthening peripartum health management to reduce pregnancy loss and improve the reproductive efficiency and longevity.
