The Wasserstein space of stochastic processes in continuous time
D. Bartl, M. Beiglböck, G. Pammer, S. Schrott, X. Zhang

Abstract
. Researchers from different areas have independently defined extensions of the usual weak convergence of laws of stochastic processes with the goal of adequately accounting for the flow of information. Natural approaches are convergence of the Aldous–Knight prediction process, Hellwig’s information topology, convergence in adapted distribution in the sense of Hoover–Keisler and the weak topology induced by optimal stopping problems. The first main contribution of this article is that on continuous processes with natural filtrations there exists a canonical adapted weak topology which can be defined by all of these approaches; moreover, the adapted weak topology is metrized by a suitable adapted Wasserstein distance 𝒜 ​ 𝒲 \mathcal{AW} . While the set of processes with natural filtrations is not complete, we establish that its completion consists precisely of the space FP {\rm FP} of stochastic processes with general filtrations. We also show that ( FP , 𝒜 ​ 𝒲 ) ({\rm FP},\mathcal{AW}) exhibits several desirable properties. Specifically, it is Polish, martingales form a closed subset and approximation results such as Donsker’s theorem extend to 𝒜 ​ 𝒲 \mathcal{AW} . keywords: causal transport, adapted topologies, continuity of optimal stopping, Donsker’s theorem

Introduction

When attempting to represent a natural phenomenon using a stochastic process model, it is likely that the model will not provide a perfect representation of reality.
In order to analyze how such a model error affects the specific issue being addressed, it is convenient to endow the collection of (laws of) stochastic processes with an appropriate concept of distance or topology.

To that end, denote by
D([0,1];\mathbb{R})
the space of
càdlàg
paths equipped with the usual
J_{1}
-topology and write
\mathcal{P}(D([0,1];\mathbb{R}))
for the set of all Borel probability measures on
D([0,1];\mathbb{R})
/ the set of laws of càdlàg processes.
Naturally,
\mathcal{P}(D([0,1];\mathbb{R}))
can be equipped with the standard weak topology, or variants of it like the Wasserstein distance.
However, the weak topology does not adequately address many of the continuity-related questions that often arise in the study of stochastic processes.
For instance, classical optimization problems such as optimal stopping or utility maximization do not exhibit continuous dependence on the underlying process, and every process can be approximated by processes that become deterministic immediately after time 0.

As a result, numerous researchers have introduced various modifications of the weak topology tailored to address the specific goals of their research problems.
That includes Aldous’ extended weak convergence
[
9
]
based on Knight’s prediction process
[
53
]
, Hoover–Keisler’s convergence in adapted distribution
[
43
]
, Hellwig’s topology
[
42
]
, and the topology induced by causal / adapted Wasserstein distances
[
62
,
63
,
29
,
56
,
11
]
.
The first main result of our article presented in Theorem
1.1

shows that the seemingly different approaches can be used to define the same
adapted weak topology
on the laws of continuous processes.
Moreover, this topology is the coarsest topology for which optimal stopping problems become continuous.

1.1.
Different approaches to adapted topologies

The weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(D([0,1];\mathbb{R}))
falls short in the context of stochastic processes because it establishes similarity based solely on path resemblance, overlooking the crucial aspect of available information at any given time.
To illustrate this issue briefly, let us examine the following processes.

Aldous’ topology
[

.

One approach to formalizing the notion of ‘information’ lies in examining the conditional distribution / conditional law of a given process.
Denote by
X
the canonical process on
\mathcal{X}:=D([0,1];\mathbb{R})

X_{t}(x):=x(t)

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{P}}:=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\sigma(X_{s}:s\leq t+\varepsilon)\vee\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{P})

This suggests that a natural approach to strengthen the weak topology within the framework of laws of stochastic process could involve exploring appropriate topological structures for the conditional laws of these processes.
Aldous’ extended weak topology is obtained using that idea.

To be more precise, if
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is endowed with the usual weak topology, then it is standard to verify that the
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})

In particular, the so-called (first order)
prediction process

{\rm pp}(\mathbb{P})=\big({\rm pp}_{t}(\mathbb{P})\big)_{t\in[0,1]}:=\big(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}(X|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{P}})\big)_{t\in[0,1]}

is a càdlàg stochastic process taking its values in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
.
Aldous endows
D([0,1];\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}))
with the
J_{1}

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{P}\big(D([0,1];\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}))\big),\qquad\mathbb{P}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}\big((X_{t},{\rm pp}_{t}(\mathbb{P}))_{t\in[0,1]}\big)

is continuous.

While Aldous used Skorohod’s
J_{1}
-topology on
D([0,1];\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}))

Hoover–Keisler’s topology

42

Hoover–Keisler endow the càdlàg paths with the Meyer–Zheng topology. Moreover, they consider

{\rm pp}^{2}(\mathbb{P})
is the prediction process of the càdlàg process

Their topology is the initial topology of
\mathbb{P}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}(({\rm pp}^{r}(\mathbb{P}))_{r\in\mathbb{N}})

Hellwig’s topology
[
41

originates in a finite discrete-time context.
For any fixed time point
t

His topology is given as the initial topology of the corresponding mappings.
In Section
6
we detail the continuous-time extension of Hellwig’s topology.

The optimal stopping topology.

We recall that one of the motivations behind considering refined variations of the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
stems from the fact that fundamental stochastic optimization problems do not exhibit continuity w.r.t. the weak topology.
Arguably, one fundamental optimization problem in that context is that of optimal stopping.
Let
\varphi\colon\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, continuous and non-anticipative function (that is,
\varphi(x,t)
depends solely on
(x_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
), and set

{\rm OS}(\mathbb{P},\varphi):=\inf\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\varphi(X,\tau)]:\tau\text{ is an $(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{P}})_{t\in[0,1]}$-stopping time}\big\}.

The optimal stopping topology is defined as the coarsest topology for which
\mathbb{P}{\mapsto}{\rm OS}(\mathbb{P},\varphi)
is continuous for every function
\varphi
as above.

The adapted Wasserstein distance.

In the context of the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, the influential role of the optimal transport perspective using Wasserstein distances has left a profound mark on various fields from PDE, calculus of variation and probability to machine learning. Interestingly, although the classical Wasserstein distance is not well-suited for analyzing differences between stochastic processes (because it metrizes the weak topology), there exists a suitable variant thereof designed precisely for this purpose.
Fix a metric
d_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
that is complete and compatible with the
J_{1}
-topology (see Remark
3.4
), denote by
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
the set of all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
for which
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[d^{p}_{\mathcal{X}}(0,X)]<\infty
, and define the
strict adapted Wasserstein distance

\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{\rm(s)}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\inf_{\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\rm bc}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d^{p}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,Y)]^{1/p}.

The difference to the usual Wasserstein distance is that one restricts to so-called
bi-causal
couplings, i.e. couplings that are non-anticipative and can be viewed as a Kantorovich analogue of non-anticipative transport maps.
It was shown in a discrete-time framework that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}
constitutes the analogue of the classical Wasserstein distance in the realm of stochastic processes: it induces the same topology as the (discrete-time variants of) Aldous’ topology, Hoover–Keisler’s topology, the optimal stopping topology and has many further desirable properties, see
[
12
,
18
]
.

Perhaps surprisingly, in the present continuous-time setting, the previous observations no longer apply.
Indeed, the distance
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}
seems too strong to analyse
general
stochastic processes.
For instance, it is not separable and Donsker’s theorem does not hold true with
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}
, as the
only
bi-causal coupling between a random walk and the Brownian motion is the product measure, see Appendix
C
.

With this in mind, when our objective is to modify
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}
tailored for comparing general stochastic processes, it becomes necessary to
relax the bi-causality constraint
.
In fact, a natural approach towards that goal draws a parallel to the reasoning behind equipping the càdlàg paths
D([0,1];\mathbb{R})
with the
J_{1}
distance rather than the sup-norm, and it consists in setting

\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}):=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\left(\varepsilon+\inf_{\pi\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\text{\rm bc}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d^{p}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,Y)]^{1/p}\right).

Roughly put, the idea is that whether a certain information is arriving
\varepsilon
earlier or later in time should not have a substantial impact but be penalized only on the level of
\varepsilon
, see Definition
3.3
.

Causal distance
[
56
,
1
]
.

Another distance based on optimal transport principles stems from Lassalle’s causal transport problem.
Set

\mathcal{CW}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}):=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\inf_{\pi\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{{\rm c}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\Big(\varepsilon+\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d^{p}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,Y)]^{1/p}\Big)

where couplings are causal if they are non-anticipative from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{Q}
(but not necessarily vice versa) and
\varepsilon
-causality represents the same relaxation as previously employed.
While
\mathcal{CW}_{p}
cannot be a distance (as it lacks symmetry), a natural remedy is to consider the
symmetrized causal Wasserstein distance

\mathcal{SCW}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\max\{\mathcal{CW}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}),\mathcal{CW}_{p}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\}
.

1.2.
The adapted weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
.

Recall that
X
is the canonical process on
\mathcal{X}=D([0,1];\mathbb{R})
.
We say that the
p
-th moments of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
converge to
\mathbb{P}
if
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(0,X)]\to\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(0,X)]
.
With this notation set in place, we are ready to state our first main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}
in the Aldous
{}_{\text{MZ}}
topology and the
p
-th moments converge.

(ii)

\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}
in the Hoover–Keisler topology and the
p
-th moments converge.

(iii)

\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}
in Hellwig’s topology and the
p
-th moments converge.

(iv)

\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{P})\to 0
.

(v)

\mathcal{SCW}_{p}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{P})\to 0
.

(vi)

\mathcal{CW}_{p}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{n})\to 0
.

If, in addition,
X
has
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. continuous paths,
(i)-(v) are further equivalent to

(vii)

\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}
in the optimal stopping topology and the
p
-th moments converge.

Theorem
1.1
is reassuring as it demonstrates that the previously discussed approaches give rise to the same concept.
Moreover, we will see in Section
6
that convergence in (i)-(vi) is equivalent to the convergence of all probabilistic operations built from iterating
d^{p}
-bounded continuous functions and conditional expectations. We refer to this topology as the adapted weak topology.

In addition, Theorem
1.1
turns out to be very useful in applications of the adapted weak topology.
For example, it is often more straightforward to show that a sequence converges in the adapted weak topology when employing Hellwig’s definition.
Conversely, when the goal is to establish a continuity property for a specific problem defined on
\mathbb{P}
, a stronger formulation of the topology proves advantageous.
For instance, the characterization via
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
is useful since causal couplings enable the transfer of features between processes, specifically the proof that optimal stopping problems continuously depend on the adapted weak topology is based on transferring stopping times from one process to another via causal couplings.

Notably, the continuity assumptions for the equivalence of (vii) and the other items stated in Theorem
1.1
are found to be crucial.
For instance, when
X
does not have
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. continuous paths, then (i)-(vi) do not imply (vii). Also, Theorem
1.1
fails if we replace adapted Wasserstein distance by its strict counterpart, not even (iv) and (v) are equivalent in this case, see Remark
C.3
. Note also that the order of
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{n}
in (vi) is important:
\mathcal{CW}_{p}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{P})\to 0
is precisely equivalent to convergence in the ordinary
p
-Wasserstein distance.

If the metric
d_{\mathcal{X}}
is bounded
in the context of Theorem
1.1
the requirement that the
p
-th moments converge is satisfied automatically.
In particular, in this case the topology induced by
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
does not depend on
p
and is equal to the Hoover–Keisler topology.

Remark 1.2
.

While in the discrete-time setting there is only
one
adapted topology on the set of adapted processes
[
12
,
60
,
33
]
, the situation is more nuanced in continuous time.
Theorem
1.1
asserts that when interpreting existing approaches to adapted topologies in the weak way, we arrive at the same concept. One the other hand, there are also stricter ways to interpret these approaches, which lead to different topologies.
Examples are the Aldous
{}_{J_{1}}
topology which is stronger than the Aldous
{}_{\text{MZ}}
topology as well as the strict version of the adapted Wasserstein distance.
Specifically the
strict adapted Wasserstein distance is useful for instance in connection with Talagrand type inequalities
[
56
,
38
]
and to provide stability and sensitivity results in mathematical finance, see e.g.
[
11
,
45
]
.
Our view is that the adapted weak topology is a natural ‘weakest’ topology which accounts for the role of information and that depending on the intended purposes one may want to consider different strengthenings.

Our next result shows that
Donsker’s theorem
extends to the realm of the adapted weak topology.
Moreover, this also holds true for Euler approximations of Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs).
This result seems interesting when combined with the previously mentioned continuous dependence observed for different optimization problems.
Indeed, it implies that they can be approximated effectively through discrete-time stochastic processes. We give here an informal version and refer to Section
7
for the full details.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
.

(i)

Let
\mathbb{P}
be the standard Wiener measure and let
\mathbb{P}^{n}
be the law of the standard scaled random walk with step size
\frac{1}{n}
.
Then
\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{P})\to 0
.

(ii)

Let
\mathbb{P}
be the law of the solution to the SDE
dY_{t}=\mu_{t}(Y_{t})dt+\sigma_{t}(Y_{t})dB_{t}
where
B
is the standard Brownian motion and
\mu
and
\sigma
satisfy mild regularity conditions, and let
\mathbb{P}^{n}
be the law of the Euler approximation of
Y
with step size
\frac{1}{n}
.
Then
\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathbb{P})\to 0
.

1.3.
General stochastic processes

The findings in the previous section provide a fairly comprehensive depiction of the adapted weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, i.e. for stochastic processes with natural filtrations.
Nevertheless, this framework presents two notable limitations.
Firstly, the metric space
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{AW}_{p})
is not complete, and this persists also when using other natural distances instead of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
.
Secondly, following the usual theory of stochastic processes, one would like to consider processes together with a
general filtration
and not just the natural filtration.

In fact, these limitations present their mutual resolution: an interpretation of the incompleteness of
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{AW}_{p})
is that the space
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
is too ‘small’ to adequately represent all processes one would like to consider.
We show that the extra information that can be stored in an ambient filtration is precisely what is needed to arrive at the completion of
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{AW}_{p})
. Put differently, there exist sequences of stochastic processes with natural filtration that converge to processes whose filtration stores strictly more information.
To make this precise we need the following definition.

Definition 1.4
.

A
filtered process

{\mathbb{X}}
is a five tuple

{\mathbb{X}}=\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},X\big)

where
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a filtration satisfying the usual conditions, and
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is an adapted càdlàg process.
We write
\mathcal{FP}
for the collection of all filtered processes and
\mathcal{FP}_{p}
for those satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(0,X)]<\infty
.

Clearly
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
can be viewed as a subset of
\mathcal{FP}
: it corresponds to all processes
{\mathbb{X}}
with
natural
filtration, i.e. for which
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bigcap_{s>t}\sigma(X_{u}:u\leq s)\vee\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{P})
.
Moreover, it is straightforward to extend the concept of bi-causality and the definition of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
from the previous setting of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
to the present setting of
\mathcal{FP}
, see Section
3
.

It is worthwhile to emphasize that the space
\mathcal{FP}
contains a multitude of processes one would perceive as being ‘equivalent’.
For example, if
{\mathbb{X}}
is a filtered process and
{\mathbb{Y}}
is derived from
{\mathbb{X}}
by adding independent information into the filtration that does not influence the behavior of
X
, one would typically not treat
{\mathbb{X}}
and
{\mathbb{Y}}
as distinct processes.
In analogy to the definition of the
\mathcal{L}_{p}/L_{p}
spaces in analysis, we consider the quotient space

\textup{FP}_{p}:=\mathcal{FP}_{p}/_{\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}}\quad\text{where ${\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}{\mathbb{Y}}$ iff $\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=0$ }.

The following is our second main result.

Theorem 1.5
.

\mathcal{AW}_{p}
is a metric on
\textup{FP}_{p}
and
(\textup{FP}_{p},\mathcal{AW}_{p})
is the completion of
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{AW}_{p})
.

It turns out that the equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}
captures essential properties of a stochastic process.
For instance, if two processes are equivalent, then the values of the respective optimal stopping problems are equal.
Indeed, let
\varphi\colon\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, non-anticipative function, and for
{\mathbb{X}}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},X)\in\mathcal{FP}
set

{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi):=\inf\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\varphi(X,\tau)]:\tau\text{ is an }(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\text{-stopping time}\big\}.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\varphi
be as above.

(1)

Let
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}_{p}
.
If
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}{\mathbb{Y}}
, then
{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)={\rm OS}({\mathbb{Y}},\varphi)
.

(2)

Let
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\in\mathcal{FP}_{p}
such that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
.
If
X
has almost surely continuous paths and
\varphi
is continuous, then
{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},\varphi)\to{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)
.

To put it differently, Theorem
1.6
(i) states that
\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}
selects all information of processes relevant to optimal stopping problems; in particular,
{\rm OS}(\cdot,\varphi)
may be defined on
\textup{FP}_{p}
.
The following section explores this phenomenon in a broader context, and we show that
\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}
selects ‘all’ relevant information of a given process.

The Hoover–Keisler equivalence and topology.

Based on formalizing what assertions belong to the ‘language of probability’ Hoover–Keisler
[
43
,
42
]
have made precise what it should mean that two processes
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
have the same probabilistic properties; we write
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\rm HK}{\mathbb{Y}}
.
They show that
\sim_{\rm HK}
can be characterized via the
iterated
variant of the prediction process considered in the previous section.
For
{\mathbb{X}}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},X)\in\mathcal{FP}
, denote its prediction process by

{\rm pp}({\mathbb{X}}):=\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}(X|\mathcal{F}_{t})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}.

Since
{\rm pp}({\mathbb{X}})
is a càdlàg process taking its values in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, one may define
its
prediction process

{\rm pp}^{2}({\mathbb{X}}):=\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}({\rm pp}({\mathbb{X}})|\mathcal{F}_{t})\right)_{t\in[0,1]},

which again is a càdlàg process, this time taking its values in
\mathcal{P}(D([0,1];\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})))
.
This procedure is iterated infinitely often resulting in the càdlàg process
{\rm pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}):=({\rm pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}}))_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
, see Section
2.2
for more details, and

{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\rm HK}{\mathbb{Y}}\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}\left({\rm pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})\right)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}\left({\rm pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Y}})\right).

It turns out that
\sim_{\rm HK}
and
\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}
are equal:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}_{p}
.
Then
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\rm HK}{\mathbb{Y}}
if and only if
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}{\mathbb{Y}}
.

Remark 1.8
.

It is natural to ask whether it is necessary to iterate the prediction process (infinitely many times). Indeed, it follows from
[
18
, Section 7]
that for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
there exist filtered processes
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}
with
\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}}))=\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{Y}}))
, which lead to different optimal stopping values. Thus,
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}
are distinct in terms of
\sim_{\textup{HK}}
but equivalent from the perspective of
\textup{pp}^{r}
. Hence, the weak topology induced by
\textup{pp}^{r}
(as well as the Aldous
J1
topology) are not even Hausdorff on
FP
. In particular, they cannot coincide with the topology generated by
\mathcal{AW}
.

In analogy to the Hoover–Keisler topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
introduced in Section
1.1
, the characterisation of
\sim_{\rm HK}
via
{\rm pp}^{\infty}
naturally induces a topology on
FP
, the
Hoover–Keisler topology
(on
FP
), defined as the initial topology of
{\mathbb{X}}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}(\rm pp^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}))
.

Theorem 1.9
.

For
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\in\textup{FP}_{p}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{X}}
in the Hoover–Keisler topology and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X^{n},0)]\to\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,0)]
.

(ii)

\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
.

(iii)

\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
.

An especially valuable feature of the usual weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is the ubiquity of compact sets ensured by Prohorov’s theorem.
Remarkably, the latter extends to the present context.

Theorem 1.10
.

For a set
\Pi\subseteq\textup{FP}_{p}
, the following are equivalent.

(i)

\Pi
is precompact w.r.t.
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
.

(ii)

\{\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{X}}):{\mathbb{X}}\in\Pi\}
is precompact w.r.t.
\mathcal{W}_{p}
.

In point (ii),
\mathcal{W}_{p}
denotes the usual Wasserstein distance, in particular, (ii) is equivalent to the following: for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a compact set
K\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
\sup_{{\mathbb{X}}\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[(1+d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,0))1_{K^{c}}(X)]\leq\varepsilon
.
Let us note that Proposition
1.10
was first established by Hoover
[
42
]
(in a somewhat different framework), see also
[
27
]
.

Canonical representatives.

It is possible to construct for each filtered process
{\mathbb{X}}
a canonical filtered process
\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}
satisfying
\mathcal{AW}({\mathbb{X}},\overline{{\mathbb{X}}})=0
. These canonical filtered process have a specific standard Borel probability space as their basis, see Section

-equivalence class contains a representative that is based on a standard Borel probability space.

2.1.
Notations and conventions

For a filtered process
{\mathbb{X}}
, we usually refer to the elements of the tuple
{\mathbb{X}}
as
\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}},\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}},\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}},(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{X}}})_{t\in[0,1]},X
, that is,
\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}
denotes the base space of the filtered process
{\mathbb{X}}
etc.
For notational simplicity, for a stochastic process
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
and
t>1
, we set
Y_{t}:=Y_{1}
and employ similar conventions for filtrations etc.

Given a set
\Omega
, a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
on
\Omega
, and a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, we write
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{P}}
for the completion of
\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}
-nullsets.
Equalities between random variables on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
are always to be understood
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
On a product space
\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\times\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
we will always be explicit, as a.s.-equality depends on the choice of the coupling
\pi
.
If
\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
is a
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}
, we can consider it as a
\sigma
-algebra on the product space
\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\times\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
, i.e. we identify
\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
with
\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}\otimes\{\emptyset,\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}\}
to shorten the notation.
For
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}
we write
\mathcal{F}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{G}
if
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are independent given
\mathcal{H}
.
For a topological space
A
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(A)
the set of all Borel probability measures on
A
.
If
A
is a metric space,
\mathcal{P}_{p}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(A)
is the subset of all those probability measures with finite
p
-th moment.
On
\mathcal{P}(A)
we consider the topology of weak convergence of probability measures (induced by testing with integrals w.r.t. continuous bounded functions) and on
\mathcal{P}_{p}(A)
the topology induced by the
p
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}
.
If
B
is another metric space and
f\colon A\to B
a Borel map, for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A)
we denote by
f_{\#}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(B)
the push-forward of
\mu
under
f
.

We write
\mathcal{X}
for the path space, i.e.
\mathcal{X}=D([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
or
\mathcal{X}=C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
if we restrict to continuous paths. The set of continuous functions from
A
to
B
is denoted by
C(A;B)
, the subset of bounded functions by
C_{b}(A;B)
.
If
B=\mathbb{R}
or
B=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we often simply write
C(A)
and
C_{b}(A)
if clear from context.
For a function
f\in\mathcal{X}
(i.e. a path) and
t\in[0,1]
, we define the stopped path
f^{t}
by
f^{t}(s)=f(t\wedge s)
for all
s\in[0,1]
.

Finally, finite and countable product spaces are always endowed with the product topology.

2.2.
An introduction to filtered processes

In this section we summarize the necessary background from continuous-time filtered processes.
Most of the material is taken from
[
27
]
.

First recall from the introduction that a
filtered process

{\mathbb{X}}
is a five tuple

{\mathbb{X}}=\big(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},X\big),

where
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a filtration satisfying the usual conditions, and
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is an adapted càdlàg process. We write
\mathcal{FP}
for the class of all filtered processes.

The prediction process of
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
, denoted by
\textup{pp}({\mathbb{X}})
, is the up to indistinguishability unique càdlàg version of the measure-valued-martingale

(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}}(X|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{X}}}))_{t\in[0,1]}.

As
\textup{pp}({\mathbb{X}})
can be seen as a random variable on the space
\mathsf{M}_{1}:=D([0,1];\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{0}))
, where
\mathsf{M}_{0}:=\mathcal{X}
. It is possible to consider
its
prediction process and further iterate this procedure, cf.
[
43
,
42
]
:

•

The prediction process of rank 1 is
\textup{pp}^{1}({\mathbb{X}})=\textup{pp}({\mathbb{X}})\in\mathsf{M}_{1}
.

•

The prediction process of rank
r+1
is the (unique up to indistinguishability) càdlàg-version of

(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}(\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}})|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{X}}}))_{t\in[0,1]}\in D([0,1];\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{r}))=:\mathsf{M}_{r+1}.

•

The prediction process of rank
\infty
is defined as

\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}):=(t\mapsto(\textup{pp}^{1}_{t}({\mathbb{X}}),\textup{pp}^{2}_{t}({\mathbb{X}}),\dots))\in D\bigg([0,1];\prod_{r=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{r})\bigg)=:\mathsf{M}_{\infty}.

For
r\geq 1
, we use the Meyer–Zheng topology
[
57
]
, i.e. the spaces
\mathsf{M}_{r}
are endowed with topologies according to the convention
1
1

1

In the case
\mathsf{M}_{0}=\mathcal{X}=D([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
this space is still equipped with Skorohod’s
J_{1}
-topology as outlined in the introduction.

that the set of càdlàg paths
D(S)=D([0,1];S)
with values in a topological space
S
is equipped with the topology of convergence in measure w.r.t. the measure
\lambda+\delta_{1}
, where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

It is important to consider the Meyer–Zheng topology on the space of càdlàg processes, as it ensures relative compactness in law for a sequence of martingales
(X^{n})_{n}
if and only if
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|X^{n}_{1}|]<\infty
, see
[
57
]
.
This leads to a Prokhorov-type compactness result for filtered processes (see Proposition
2.4
), which is crucial for the analysis in this paper.

Remark 2.1
.

The chosen topology on
D([0,1];S)
has the drawback of not being Polish.
However, it remains Lusin, i.e., homeomorphic to a Borel subset of a Polish space
[
57
, Appendix]
.
Lusin spaces are stable under operations such as
S\mapsto\mathcal{P}(S)
,
S\mapsto D(S)
and countable products; in particular
\mathsf{M}_{r}
is Lusin for all
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Moreover, they provide sufficient regularity for our purposes, behaving similarly as Polish spaces.
For instance, conditional laws of
S
-valued random variables are well-defined, measure-valued martingales in
\mathcal{P}(S)
have càdlàg versions, and all expressions in the definition of iterated prediction processes are well-posed. For further details, see
[
27
, Section 2]
.

Note that the prediction processes of finite rank are measure-valued martingales, that is for every Borel and bounded
f:\mathsf{M}_{r}\to\mathbb{R}
, the real-valued process
Y_{t}(\omega):=\int fd\textup{pp}^{r}_{t}({\mathbb{X}})(\omega)
is a real-valued
(\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
-martingale. As the random variable
\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}})
is
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
-measurable, we have for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
,

\textup{pp}^{r+1}_{1}({\mathbb{X}})=\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}})|\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{X}}})=\delta_{\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}})}.

In this sense,
\textup{pp}^{r+1}({\mathbb{X}})
contains more information about
{\mathbb{X}}
than
\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}})
does. In a specific sense, which was made precise by Hoover–Keisler
[
43
]
,
\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})
contains all relevant probabilistic information about
{\mathbb{X}}
. Following their approach, we identify two filtered processes with each other if the prediction processes of rank
\infty
have the same law:

Definition 2.2
.

We say that
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
are Hoover–Keisler equivalent, denoted as
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\textup{HK}}{\mathbb{Y}}
if
\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}))=\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Y}}))
.
The space of filtered process is defined as
\textup{FP}=\mathcal{FP}/_{\sim_{\textup{HK}}}
.

Note that in the introduction we defined the space of filtered processes as the factor space
\textup{FP}=\mathcal{FP}/_{\sim_{\mathcal{AW}}}
. We will show in Section
3
that these definitions agree.
For now, we stick to
\mathcal{FP}/_{\sim_{\textup{HK}}}
to be consistent with the papers
[
27
,
42
,
43
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

The Hoover–Keisler topology on
FP
, denote by
HK
, is the initial topology w.r.t.

\textup{FP}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{\infty}):\quad{\mathbb{X}}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})).

It is immediate from this definition that
(\textup{FP},\textup{HK})
is homeomorphic to a subspace of the metrizable space
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
and hence metrizable. Therefore, it is sufficient to work with sequences when proving properties of the topology
HK
.
Moreover, it is shown in
[
27
, Theorem 1.5]
that
(\textup{FP},\textup{HK})
is a Polish space, but in this paper no explicit metric for this topology is constructed.
In Section
3
of the present paper, we will show that the adapted Wasserstein distance is a complete metric for
(\textup{FP},\textup{HK})
.

We proceed and state the announced Prohorov-type theorem for
(\textup{FP},\textup{HK})
, see
[
27
, Theorem 1.6]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

A set of filtered processes
A\subseteq\textup{FP}
is relatively compact in the Hoover–Keisler topology if and only if the respective set of laws
\{\mathcal{L}(X):{\mathbb{X}}\in A\}
is tight.

Definition 2.5
.

We denote with
\mathcal{FP}_{p}
the collection of filtered processes
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
with finite
p
-th moment, that is
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[d^{p}(X,0)]<\infty
and set
\textup{FP}_{p}=\mathcal{FP}_{p}/_{\sim_{\textup{HK}}}
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}\in\textup{FP}_{p}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{X}}
in
\textup{HK}_{p}
if
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{X}}
in
HK
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}}[d^{p}(X^{n},0)]\to\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[d^{p}(X,0)]
.

Since relative compactness in the space of probability measure with finite
p
-th moment, equipped with the
p
-Wasserstein distance
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{W}_{p})
is equivalent to tightness and uniform integrability of the
p
-th moments (see e.g.
[
10
, Proposition 7.1.5]
), the following consequence of Proposition
2.4
is immediate.

Corollary 2.6
.

A set of filtered processes
A\subseteq\textup{FP}_{p}
is relatively compact w.r.t.
\textup{HK}_{p}
if and only if the respective set of laws
\{\mathcal{L}(X):{\mathbb{X}}\in A\}
is relatively compact in
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{W}_{p})
.

Next we want to assign to each filtered process a canonical filtered process. To that end, we need some notation:
Z=(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
denotes the canonical process on
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
, so for every
t\in[0,1]
,
Z_{t}
is a (infinite) vector of measures
(Z^{r}_{t})_{r\geq 1}
where
Z^{r}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{r-1})
.

Moreover, we set
\overline{X}:=Z^{0}:=\delta^{-1}(Z_{1}^{1})
, where
\delta^{-1}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{X}
is a Borel function that satisfies
\delta^{-1}(\delta_{x})=x
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Note that
\delta^{-1}
restricted to the set of Dirac measures is continuous.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
. The
canonical filtered process
associated to
{\mathbb{X}}
is given by

\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}=(\mathsf{M}_{\infty},\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{M}_{\infty}},\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})),(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},\overline{X}),

where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{M}_{\infty}}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is the right-continuous completion of the canonical filtration on
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
, and
\overline{X}
is the map defined above.

We denote the collection of all canonical filtered process as
\mathcal{CFP}
.

The canonical filtered process associated to
{\mathbb{X}}
has the property that its prediction process of rank
\infty
satisfies
\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}(\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}))=\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}))
. Thus, every
HK
-class contains exactly one element from
\mathcal{CFP}
. This fact was established in
[
27
, Theorem 1.3]
and implies the following

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
.
Then
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\textup{HK}}\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}
.
Moreover,
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\textup{HK}}{\mathbb{Y}}
if and only if
\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}=\overline{{\mathbb{Y}}}
.

Remark 2.9
.

Note that for every
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
the function
\overline{X}:\mathsf{M}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{X}
is continuous on the support of
\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}))
.
This will be used in the sequel.

We end this section with useful observations that relate continuity of generated filtrations with continuity properties of the prediction process.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
.
We call
t\in[0,1]
a continuity point of
{\mathbb{X}}
if
s\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}_{s}({\mathbb{X}}))
is continuous at
t
.

The next result, which was shown in
[
27
, Corollary 4.16]
, is a consequence of the prediction processes being measure-valued martingales with càdlàg paths.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
. Then
[0,1]\setminus\textup{cont}({\mathbb{X}})
is countable. Hence,
\textup{cont}({\mathbb{X}})
is dense in
[0,1]
.

Next, we recall the characterization of continuity points given in
[
27
, Proposition 4.19]
, that will be useful throughout this article.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
and
\mathcal{G}_{s}:=\sigma^{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}_{r}({\mathbb{X}}):r\leq s)
.
The filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
is right-continuous and for
t\in[0,1]
the following are equivalent:

(i)

t\in\textup{cont}({\mathbb{X}})
, that is,
s\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}_{s}({\mathbb{X}}))
is continuous at
t
.

(ii)

The filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
is continuous at
t
, that is
\mathcal{G}_{t}=\mathcal{G}_{t-}
.

(iii)

The paths of
\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})
are a.s. continuous at
t
.

Finally, we introduce the concept of adapted functions. An adapted function is an operation that takes a filtered process
{\mathbb{X}}
and returns a random variable on the underlying probability space of this process. More precisely,
f
is an adapted function (we write
f\in\textup{AF}
) if it can be built using the following operations:

(AF1)

Every
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X})
is an adapted function, and we set
f[{\mathbb{X}}]:=f(X)
.

(AF2)

If
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\textup{AF}
and
g\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
g(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\in\textup{AF}
and we set
g(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})[{{\mathbb{X}}}]:=g(f_{1}[{{\mathbb{X}}}],\dots,f_{n}[{{\mathbb{X}}}])
.

(AF3)

If
f\in\textup{AF}
and
t\in[0,1]
, then
(f|t)\in\textup{AF}
and
(f|t)[{{\mathbb{X}}}]:=\mathbb{E}[f[{{\mathbb{X}}}]|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{X}}}]
.

It is possible to characterize Hoover–Keisler-equivalence via adapted functions.

Proposition 2.13
.

For
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
we have
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\textup{HK}}{\mathbb{Y}}
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[f[{\mathbb{X}}]]=\mathbb{E}[f[{\mathbb{Y}}]]
for every
f\in\textup{AF}
.

For
f\in\textup{AF}
, we write
T(f)\subseteq[0,1]
for the collection of those
t\in[0,1]
such that
\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
appears in

. Specifically, we set
T(f)=\emptyset
for
f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X})
, set
T(g(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}))=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}T(f_{i})
, and further set
T((f|t))=T(f)\cup\{t\}
.

Using this notions, we can state the following definition.

Definition 2.14
.

We say that
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{X}}
in the
adapted weak topology
if
\mathbb{E}[f[{\mathbb{X}}^{n}]]\to\mathbb{E}[f[{\mathbb{X}}]]
for every
f\in\textup{AF}
satisfying
T(f)\subseteq\textup{cont}({\mathbb{X}})\cup\{1\}
.

Theorem 2.15
.

The Hoover–Keisler topology coincides with the adapted weak topology.

3.
The adapted Wasserstein distance

The aim of this section is to give a precise definition of the adapted Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
on the space of filtered processes
\textup{FP}_{p}
. The main result of this section is Theorem
3.13
, which states that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
metrizes the Hoover–Keisler topology plus convergence of
p
-th moments.

3.1.
\varepsilon
-bicausal couplings and the definition of
\mathcal{AW}

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
and let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. A coupling between
{\mathbb{X}}
and
{\mathbb{Y}}
is a probability measure on
(\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\times\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}},\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{Y}}})
with marginals
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
. We say that a coupling
\pi
between
{\mathbb{X}}
and
{\mathbb{Y}}
is

(1)

\varepsilon
-causal
from
{\mathbb{X}}
to
{\mathbb{Y}}
if
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
under
\pi
for all
t\in[0,1]
;

(2)

\varepsilon
-bicausal
if
\pi
is both
\varepsilon
-causal from
{\mathbb{X}}
to
{\mathbb{Y}}
and
\varepsilon
-causal from
{\mathbb{Y}}
to
{\mathbb{X}}
.

We write
\textup{Cpl}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
,
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm{c}}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
and
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm{bc}}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
for the set of couplings,
\varepsilon
-causal couplings and
\varepsilon
-bicausal couplings between
{\mathbb{X}}
and
{\mathbb{Y}}
, respectively.
For
\varepsilon=0
we shall drop the prefix
\varepsilon
to the causality constraints, e.g. causal means
0
-causal.

In what follows, we set
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}_{t,s}:=\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}_{s}
for
t,s\in[0,1]
.
The following characterization of causality will be used frequently in what follows.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
,
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
,
t\in[0,1]
, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
under
\pi
.

(2)

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1}]=E_{\pi}[V|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon}]
for all bounded
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}
-measurable
V
.

(3)

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}_{t+\varepsilon,t}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon}]
for all bounded
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}
-measurable
U
.

(4)

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V(U-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}])]=0
for all bounded
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}
-measurable
U
, bounded
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}
-measurable
V
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (i), (ii) and (iii) is a consequence of
[
47
, Proposition 5.8]
. We show that (ii) is equivalent to (iv). Clearly (ii) is equivalent to

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{1}]]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}]]

for all bounded
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}
-measurable
U
and bounded
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}
-measurable
V
. Since the conditional expectation is a self-adjoint operator
2
2

2

This means that
\mathbb{E}[F\mathbb{E}[G|\mathcal{H}]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[F|\mathcal{H}]G]
for all bounded random variables
F,G
and
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{H}
.
and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{1}]=U
, (
3.1
) is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[UV]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}]V],

which is another way of writing (iv).
∎

Definition 3.3
.

On
\mathcal{FP}_{p}
we define the
adapted Wasserstein distance
by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}):=\inf_{\varepsilon\geq 0}\inf\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]^{1/p}+\varepsilon:\pi\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bc}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})\right\}.

If
\mathcal{X}=C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we set
d_{\mathcal{X}}
to be the supremum norm; if
\mathcal{X}=D([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
we fix
a complete metric
d_{\mathcal{X}}
that induces the
J_{1}
-topology and satisfies
d_{\mathcal{X}}(f,0)\leq C\|f\|_{\infty}
.

Remark 3.4
.

Note that while the usual
J_{1}
-distance is not complete, there are complete distances that induce the
J_{1}
-topology and satisfy
d_{\mathcal{X}}(f,0)\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
, see e.g.
[
28
, Section 12]
.
Moreover, the definition of the adapted Wasserstein distance and the results in this chapter are largely independent of the specific path space
\mathcal{X}
and its metric, except for separability and completeness.
There is also flexibility in penalizing couplings that are
\varepsilon
-bicausal rather than strictly bicausal. For simplicity, we penalize with
\varepsilon
, but alternative penalties may be more natural for specific classes of processes (e.g.,
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for continuous martingales).

An observation that turns out to be useful is that for canonical filtered processes the infimum in the definition of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
is attained.
This follows, similar as in the case of the usual Wasserstein distance, from a suitable compactness of
\varepsilon
-bicausal couplings:

Proposition 3.5
.

For
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}\in\mathcal{CFP}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the sets
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm{c}}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
and
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm{bc}}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
are compact in
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{\infty}\times\mathsf{M}_{\infty})
.

Proof.

We first claim that
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is compact.
To that end, write

\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})=\bigcap_{t\in[0,1]}C_{t},\quad\text{where }C_{t}:=\left\{\pi\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon}^{\mathbb{X}}}{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}}\text{ under }\pi\right\}.

The claim follows if
C_{t}
is compact for every fixed
t\in[0,1]
.
Fix such
t
.

By Lemma
3.2
, a coupling
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is an element of
C_{t}
if and only if

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}_{t}]\left(U-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}]\right)\right]=0\text{ for all }U\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}})\text{ and }V\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{Y}}).

We reformulate the latter condition in a manner that makes compactness more readily apparent.

To that end recall that
\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}=\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}=\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
and the latter is a Lusin space.
Denote by
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}_{t}\colon\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\to\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}})
a regular disintegration of
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
, and similarly for
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}
and
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{1}
.
These disintegrations exist since
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
is Lusin, see
[
32
, Corollary 10.4.13]
.
Moreover, for measurable bounded
f:\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\to\mathbb{R}
, define
\Phi_{f,g}\colon\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\times\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Phi_{f,g}(\omega_{x},\omega_{y}):=\int g(\eta_{y})\,\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}_{t}(\omega_{y},d\eta_{y})\left(\int f(\eta_{x})\,\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{1}(\omega_{x},d\eta_{x})-\int f(\eta_{x})\,\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}(\omega_{x},d\eta_{x})\right).

Then, by definition, (
3.3
) is equivalent to
\int\Phi_{f,g}\,d\pi=0
for all
f
and
g
as above.

In the next step, we change the topology on
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
:
By
[
50
, Theorem 13.2 and Theorem 13.11]
there is a finer Polish topology
\mathcal{T}
on
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
that generates the original Borel
\sigma
-field and for which the disintegrations

\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}_{t}
,
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{t+\varepsilon}
, and
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}_{1}

are continuous.

Thus, by a monotone class argument,

C_{t}=\bigcap_{f,g\in C_{b}(\mathsf{M}_{\infty},\mathcal{T})}\left\{\pi\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}):\int\Phi_{f,g}\,d\pi=0\right\}.

Denote by
w_{\mathcal{T}}
the coarsest topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{M}_{\infty}\times\mathsf{M}_{\infty})
for which
\rho\mapsto\int\Psi\,d\rho
is continuous for every bounded
\Psi\colon\mathsf{M}_{\infty}\times\mathsf{M}_{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
\mathcal{T}\otimes\mathcal{T}
-continuous.
Since
\mathcal{T}
is a Polish topology,
\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is
w_{\mathcal{T}}
-compact, see e.g.
[
69
, Lemma 4.4]
.
Moreover, since
\Phi_{f,g}
is
\mathcal{T}\otimes\mathcal{T}
-continuous and bounded,
C_{t}
is a
w_{\mathcal{T}}
-closed subset, hence
w_{\mathcal{T}}
compact.
Finally, note that
w_{\mathcal{T}}
is finer than the weak topology (because
\mathcal{T}
is finer topology than the original topology on
\mathsf{M}_{\infty}
). Hence,
C_{t}
is weakly compact, as claimed.

The same argument with the roles of
{\mathbb{X}}
and
{\mathbb{Y}}
reversed shows that
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{X})
is compact, which completes the proof. ∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}\in\mathcal{FP}
,
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
Then
\pi
is
\varepsilon
-bicausal if and only if
\pi
is
\delta
-bicausal for all
\delta>\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We only prove the non-trivial direction, i.e. that if
\pi
is
\delta
-bicausal for all
\delta>\varepsilon
, then
\pi
is
\varepsilon
-bicausal.

To that end, let
t\in[0,1]
.
By Lemma
3.2
, we have for all bounded random variables
U
and
V
that are measurable w.r.t. to
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Y}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}
, respectively,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[UV|\mathcal{F}_{t+\delta}^{\mathbb{X}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[U|\mathcal{F}_{t+\delta}^{\mathbb{X}}]\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V|\mathcal{F}_{t+\delta}^{\mathbb{X}}].

By right-continuity of
(\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t})_{t}
and backward martingale convergence, as
\delta\downarrow\varepsilon
, all terms in (
3.4
) converge to the respective terms with
\varepsilon
in place of
\delta
.
Thus,
\pi
is
\varepsilon
-causal by Lemma
3.2
.
Reversing the roles of
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

The infimum in the definition of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
is attained for
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}\in\mathcal{CFP}_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
and
(\pi_{n})_{n}
be minimizing for
\mathcal{AW}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
, that is,

\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=\lim_{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]^{1/p}+\varepsilon_{n}\right)

and
\pi_{n}\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{\rm bc}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
.
After passing to a (not-relabelled) subsequence, there is
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
such that
\varepsilon_{n}\to\varepsilon
.
Clearly, it is enough to show that there is
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bc}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]\leq\liminf_{n}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)].

It is a property of canonical filtered processes that the maps
X:\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\to\mathcal{X}
and
Y:\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\to\mathcal{X}
are continuous on the support of
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
respectively, see Remark
2.9
. Hence, there is a closed set
A\subseteq\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\times\Omega^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
such that
d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y):A\to[0,\infty)
is continuous and
\textup{supp}(\pi)\subseteq A
for all
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
. Therefore,
\textup{Cpl}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})\to\mathbb{R}:\rho\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]
is lower-semicontinuous.

To prove (
3.5
), possibly after passing to another subsequence, we may assume without loss of generality that
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
is either increasing or decreasing.

In case that
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
is increasing, the claim follows from the compactness of
\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bc}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
that was established in Proposition
3.5
, and from lower-semicontinuity of
\rho\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]
.

In case that
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
is decreasing, by Proposition
3.5
, possibly after passing to a subsequence, there is a coupling
\pi
such
\pi_{n}\to\pi
weakly.
Moreover, for every
\delta>\varepsilon
and all
n
large enough,
\pi_{n}
is
\delta
-bicausal.
By Proposition
3.5
,
\pi
is
\delta
-bicausal and as
\delta>\varepsilon
was arbitrary, Lemma
3.6
implies that
\pi
is
\varepsilon
-bicausal.
The claim again follows from lower semicontinuity of
\rho\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]
.
∎

A basic yet important concept in the classical theory of Wasserstein distances is that of ‘gluing’. In the present setting it takes the following form.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{Z}\in\mathcal{CFP}
, let
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
and
\rho\in\textup{Cpl}(\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{Z})
.
Then there is
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}})
such that

\pi=({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}}})_{\#}\Pi,\qquad\rho=({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}}}})_{\#}\Pi,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ under $\Pi$}.

If, in addition,
\pi
is
\varepsilon_{1}
-causal from
{\mathbb{X}}
to
{\mathbb{Y}}
and
\rho
is
\varepsilon_{2}
-causal from
{\mathbb{Y}}
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
\sigma:=({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}}}})_{\#}\Pi
is
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
-causal from
{\mathbb{X}}
to
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
and the latter is countably generated (because
{\mathbb{Y}}
is a canonical filtered process and hence based on a standard Borel probability space, see Section
2
), there are disintegrations
\pi^{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}
and
\rho^{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}
of
\pi
and
\rho
w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}
:

\displaystyle\pi(d\omega_{x},d\omega_{y})

\displaystyle=\pi^{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}_{\omega_{y}}(d\omega_{x})\,\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Y}}(d\omega_{y}),

\displaystyle\rho(d\omega_{y},d\omega_{z})

\displaystyle=\rho^{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}_{\omega_{y}}(d\omega_{z})\,\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Y}}(d\omega_{y}),

see e.g.
[
32
, Corollary 10.4.13]
.
Now define

\displaystyle\Pi(d\omega_{x},d\omega_{y},d\omega_{z}):=\rho^{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}_{\omega_{y}}(d\omega_{z})\,\pi^{\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}_{\omega_{y}}(d\omega_{x})\,\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Y}}(d\omega_{y}).

Clearly, we have
({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}}})_{\#}\Pi=\pi
and
({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}}}})_{\#}\Pi=\rho
. For functions
U,V,W
that are bounded and measurable w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}},\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, respectively, it follows from the definition of
\Pi
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[UVW]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\left[V\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[U|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}]\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}]\right],

i.e. we have
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}
under
\Pi
.

It remains to prove that
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}
for every
t\in[0,1]
under
\Pi
and hence under
\sigma
. To that end, note that we have

(1)

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1}
under
\Pi
for all
t\in[0,1]
,

(2)

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t+\varepsilon_{2}}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}
under
\Pi
for all
t\in[0,1]
,

(3)

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}
under
\Pi
.

Fix
t\in[0,1]
. For any bounded
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}
-measurable random variable
W
, by first using the conditional independence
3
and then the causality condition
2
, we have that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}_{1,1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t+\varepsilon_{2}}].

It follows that for every bounded
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Z}}_{t}
-measurable random variable
W
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}_{1,1}]|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t+\varepsilon_{2}}]|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{Y}}_{t+\varepsilon_{2}}]|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}_{1,1}]|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}],

where the second and fourth equality are due to (
3.6
) and the third equality is due to the causality condition
1
at time
t+\varepsilon_{2}
. Therefore, we have shown that
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[W|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[W|\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}]
. By Lemma
3.2
the latter is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{Z}}\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\models$}_{\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}
under
\sigma
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

For all
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathcal{CFP}_{p}
we have
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Z}})\leq\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})+\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.7
there are
\varepsilon_{1}\geq 0
and
\pi_{1}\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{\rm bc}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
which attain the infimum in the definition of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
, and
\varepsilon_{2}
and
\pi_{2}\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{\rm bc}(\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{Z})
which attain the infimum in the definition of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{Z}})
.

By Lemma
3.8
there exists
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}})
such that
({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}}})_{\#}\Pi=\pi_{1}
,
({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{Y}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}}}})_{\#}\Pi=\pi_{2}
and

\sigma:=({\textup{pr}_{\Omega^{\mathbb{X}}\times\Omega^{\mathbb{Z}}}})_{\#}\Pi\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}_{\rm bc}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Z}).

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Z)]^{1/p}+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(X,Y)]^{1/p}+\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}[d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(Y,Z)]^{1/p}+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})+\mathcal{AW}_{p}(\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{Z}).\qed

3.3.
Symmetrized causal distance

When showing that certain processes converge w.r.t.
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
, it can be challenging to construct couplings that are
\varepsilon
-causal in both directions at the same time.
In contrast, it is often simpler to find couplings that are just
\varepsilon
-causal in one direction.
Hence, computing the
causal distance

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{CW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}):=\inf_{\varepsilon\geq 0}\inf\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d^{p}(X,Y)]^{1/p}+\varepsilon:\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\rm c}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})\right\}

turns out to be simpler than computing
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{CW}_{p}
is not a distance as it lacks symmetry, and it is natural to consider the
symmetrized causal Wasserstein distance
, defined via

\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}):=\max\{\mathcal{CW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}),\mathcal{CW}_{p}({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{X}})\}.

We first show that

can be defined on

, and in Proportion

in fact induces the same topology as
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
. To this end, we start with the following observation.

Remark 3.17
.

For every
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{CFP}_{p}
, the infima in the definitions of
\mathcal{CW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
and
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
are attained. This follows from Proposition
3.5
using the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
3.7
.

Lemma 3.18
.

\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
is a pseudo-metric on
\mathcal{FP}_{p}
.
Moreover, for every
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}_{p}
, we have that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=0
if and only if
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\textup{HK}}{\mathbb{Y}}
.

Proof.

As in the case of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
, Proposition
A.1
yields that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
does not depend on the representative of the
HK
-class, so we can assume w.l.o.g. that
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{CFP}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
takes values in
[0,\infty)
and that it is symmetric. Moreover, by exactly the same arguments as presented in the proof for Lemma
3.9
, one can show that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
satisfies the triangle inequality.

Regarding the second claim, we need to show that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=0
if and only if
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=0
.
Since
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}\leq\mathcal{AW}_{p}
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=0
whenever
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})=0
.

By Remark
3.17
, the infimum in the definition of
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
is attained, i.e. there are
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
and
\sigma\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{X}})
with
X=Y

\pi
-a.s. and
\sigma
-a.s.
Following line by line the arguments given in
[
60
, Lemma 2.6]
, one can show by induction on the rank
r
that
\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{X}})=\textup{pp}^{r}({\mathbb{Y}})

\pi
-a.s. Hence,
\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}})=\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Y}})

\pi
-a.s. and in particular,
\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{X}}))=\mathcal{L}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}({\mathbb{Y}}))
.
∎

Proposition 3.19
.

\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
is a complete metric on
\textup{FP}_{p}
and induces the same topology as
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.18
,
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
is a metric on
\textup{FP}_{p}
.
It remains to show that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
induces the same topology as
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
.
To that end, note that the topology induced by
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
is clearly weaker than the one induced by
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
, and at the same time finer than the weak-convergence topology.
In particular, if
{\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}
are such that
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
, then, by Corollary
2.6
, the set
\{\mathcal{L}(X^{n}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is relatively compact w.r.t.
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
.
Since any limit point
{\mathbb{Y}}
of
({\mathbb{X}}^{n})_{n}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
also satisfies
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{X}})=0
, Lemma
3.18
asserts that
{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{Y}}
.
In particular,
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
. Completeness of
\mathcal{SCW}_{p}
can be shown exactly the same way as completeness of
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
in Theorem
3.13
.
∎

4.
Continuity of optimal stopping problems

The main result of this section is that the value of an optimal stopping problem

{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi):=\inf_{\tau\in\mathrm{ST}(\mathbb{X})}\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X,\tau)]

continuously
depends on the process
\mathbb{X}
with respect to the adapted weak topology, for all cost functions
\varphi\colon\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
that are regular in a suitable sense. Remarkably, such continuous dependence on the input process does not hold for the usual weak topology, as it was already pointed out by Aldous.

Here and in the following, for
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
, the set of all
[0,1]
-valued stopping times w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{X}})_{t\in[0,1]}
is denoted by
\rm{ST}(\mathbb{X})
.

Definition 4.1
.

A measurable function
\varphi\colon\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\to[-\infty,\infty]
is said to be
non-anticipative
if for every
t\in[0,1]
and
f,g\in\mathcal{X}
which coincide on
[0,t]
,
\varphi(f,t)=\varphi(g,t)
.

We will see in Corollary
4.5
below that
{\rm OS}(\cdot,\varphi)
is well-defined on
FP
in the sense that its value does not depend on the particular choice of the
\sim_{\textup{HK}}
-representative.

Assumption 4.2
.

The cost function
\varphi
is non-anticipative and satisfies the following continuity property: for all sequences
(s_{n})_{n}\subseteq[0,1]
and
(f_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that
s_{n}\to s
and
\lVert f_{n}-f\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
with
f\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have that
\varphi(f_{n},s_{n})\to\varphi(f,s)
.

Note that Assumption
4.2
is satisfied whenever the map
(t,x)\mapsto\varphi(x,t)
is jointly continuous. In particular, it is satisfied for Markovian cost functions, i.e.
\psi(x,t)=\psi(t,x(t))
for
\psi
continuous.

Proposition 4.3

(Qualitative continuity of OS)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{X}
in the adapted weak topology and that
{\mathbb{X}}
has continuous paths. Then, for every bounded cost function
\varphi
satisfying Assumption
4.2
,

{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},\varphi)\to{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi).

Note that if we have
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
, we can replace the boundedness assumption on
\varphi
by the growth condition
\varphi(t,f)\leq C(1+d_{\mathcal{X}}^{p}(f,0))
. Further note that the continuity assumptions on the paths of
{\mathbb{X}}
is necessary, see Example
D.1
below.

The proof of the continuity result for optimal stopping is based on the following observation:
A causal coupling
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
allows to pull back a stopping time
\tau\in\rm{ST}({\mathbb{Y}})
to a randomized stopping time for
{\mathbb{X}}
. More generally,
\varepsilon
-causal couplings allow to do this up to a time shift of
\varepsilon
, as the following proposition details.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})
, and
\tau\in\rm{ST}({\mathbb{Y}})
be given. Then there is a family
(\sigma_{u})_{u\in[0,1]}\subseteq\rm{ST}({\mathbb{X}})
such that for every
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
bounded from below

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[\varphi(X,\sigma_{u})]\,du=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\tau+\varepsilon)].

In particular, there exists a stopping time
\sigma\in\rm{ST}({\mathbb{Y}})
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[\varphi(X,\sigma)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\tau+\varepsilon)].

Proof.

For every
u\in[0,1]
, define

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\,\pi(\tau\leq t-\varepsilon\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1})\geq u\right\}\wedge 1.

By the definition of
\varepsilon
-causality, the conditional probability
\pi(\tau^{*}\leq t-\varepsilon\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{1})
is
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}
-measurable.
As the hitting time of a right-continuous adapted process,
\sigma_{u}
is a stopping time of
\mathbb{X}
and the infimum over
t
in (
4.2
) is attained.

By conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{1}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
and Fubini’s theorem, we find

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\sigma_{u})]\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\varphi(X,\sigma_{u})\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}\right]\right]\,du=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\int_{[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\varphi(X,\sigma_{u})\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}\right]\,du\right]

As
u\mapsto\sigma_{u}(\omega^{{\mathbb{X}}})
is the quantile function of
\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(\tau+\varepsilon\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}})(\omega^{{\mathbb{X}}})
for a.e.
\omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}\in\Omega^{{\mathbb{X}}}
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\sigma_{u})\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}]\,du=\int_{[0,1]}\varphi(X,\sigma_{u})\,du=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\tau+\varepsilon)\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\mathbb{X}}].

Therefore, we conclude (
4.1
) by taking expectations.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\varphi
be non-anticipative and bounded from below. If
{\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
satisfy
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\rm HK}{\mathbb{Y}}
, then

{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)={\rm OS}({\mathbb{Y}},\varphi)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
, it suffices to show that
\rm{OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)=\rm{OS}(\overline{{\mathbb{X}}},\varphi)
, where
\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}\in\mathcal{CFP}
is the canonical representative associated to
{\mathbb{X}}
. By Lemma
A.3
(i)
there is a coupling
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}({\mathbb{X}},\overline{{\mathbb{X}}})
such that
X=\overline{X}

\pi
-a.s. Therefore, by Proposition
4.4
, for any
\tau\in\rm{ST}(\overline{{\mathbb{X}}})
there is
\sigma\in\rm{ST}({\mathbb{X}})
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[\varphi(X,\sigma)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\tau)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}}}[\varphi(\overline{X},\tau)].

By reversing the roles of
{\mathbb{X}}
and
\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}
in the above argument, for any
\tau\in\rm{ST}({\mathbb{X}})
there is
\sigma\in\rm{ST}(\overline{{\mathbb{X}}})
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\overline{{\mathbb{X}}}}}[\varphi(\overline{X},\sigma)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}}[\varphi(X,\tau)].

Hence,
\rm{OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)=\rm{OS}(\overline{{\mathbb{X}}},\varphi)
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\varphi
be a bounded cost function that satisfies Assumption
4.2
and let
(\pi_{n})_{n}
with
\pi_{n}\in\textup{Cpl}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}[d_{\mathcal{X}}(X^{n},X)\wedge 1]\to 0
. We have, for every null sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}\bigg[\sup_{|s-t|\leq\varepsilon_{n}}|\varphi(X^{n},s)-\varphi(X,t)|\bigg]=0.

Proof.

By conditional independent gluing of the couplings
\pi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, at their common marginal
\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{X}}}
, we find random variables
\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{X}
on a common probability space such that
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{X})=\mathcal{L}(X^{n},X)
and hence
\tilde{X}^{n}\to\tilde{X}
in probability. After possibly passing to a subsequence, we have
\tilde{X}^{n}\to\tilde{X}
almost surely.

If
d_{J_{1}}(f_{n},f)\to 0
and
f\in C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
\|f_{n}-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
as well. Using this, Assumption
4.2
and the compactness of
[0,1]
, we find that a.s.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{|s-t|\leq\varepsilon_{n}}|\varphi(\tilde{X}^{n},s)-\varphi(\tilde{X},t)|=0.

Hence, (
4.3
) follows using dominated convergence.
∎

We are now ready to prove the qualitative continuity result.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{X}}
in the adapted weak topology and that
X
has continuous paths. By Theorem
3.13
, there is a sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n}
of
\varepsilon_{n}
-bicausal couplings such that
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d_{\mathcal{X}}(X^{n},X)\wedge 1]\to 0
.

We start by showing that
{\rm OS}(\mathbb{X},\varphi)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\rm OS}(\mathbb{X}^{n},\varphi)
.
To that end let
\tau_{n}
be an optimal stopping time for
{\rm{OS}}({{\mathbb{X}}}^{n},\varphi)
(or, almost optimal if it does not exists).
By Proposition
4.4
, we have
{\rm{OS}}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}[\varphi(X,\tau_{n}+\varepsilon)]
, hence

\displaystyle{\rm{OS}}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)-{\rm{OS}}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},\varphi)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}[\varphi(X,\tau_{n})-\varphi(X^{n},\tau_{n}+\varepsilon_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}\!\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\!|\varphi(X,t)-\varphi(X^{n},t+\varepsilon_{n})|\bigg]\to 0,

where the last step follows from Lemma
4.6
.

The same arguments show that

{\rm{OS}}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},\varphi)-{\rm{OS}}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n}}\!\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\!|\varphi(X^{n},t)-\varphi(X,t+\varepsilon_{n})|\bigg]\to 0

and hence
{\rm OS}(\mathbb{X},\varphi)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\rm OS}(\mathbb{X}^{n},\varphi)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

For Lipschitz cost functions, more refined quantitative estimates are possible.
To that end, we require the following concept.

Definition 4.7
.

For a filtered process
\mathbb{X}\in\textup{FP}
, we call

\delta_{\mathbb{X}}(\varepsilon):=\sup_{\tau\in\rm{ST}(\mathbb{X})}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{s\in[0,\varepsilon]}|X_{\tau+s}-X_{\tau}|\bigg]\qquad\text{for }\varepsilon\in[0,1]

the
modulus of continuity
of
\mathbb{X}
.

Using the same arguments as in the proof of Corollary
4.5
, one can see that the modulus of continuity is independent of the
\sim_{\textup{HK}}
-representative. It is worthwhile to note that the modulus of continuity can be easily estimated for certain standard processes (their standard definitions can be found in Section
7
).

Example 4.8
.

The following hold.

(1)

If
{\mathbb{B}}
is the standard Brownian motion, then
\delta_{\mathbb{B}}(\varepsilon)\leq 2\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

(2)

If
{\mathbb{S}}
is the solution of the SDE
dS_{t}=\mu_{t}(S_{t})\,dt+\sigma_{t}(S_{t})\,dB_{t}
, then
\delta_{\mathbb{S}}(\varepsilon)\leq\|\mu\|_{\infty}\varepsilon+2\|\sigma\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

(3)

If
{\mathbb{B}}^{n}
is the scaled random walk with step size
1/n>0
, then
\delta_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(\varepsilon)\leq 4\sqrt{1/n\vee\varepsilon}
.

(4)

If
{\mathbb{S}}^{n}
is the Euler approximation for
{\mathbb{S}}
, then
\delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(\varepsilon)\leq 2\lVert\mu\rVert_{\infty}(1/n\vee\varepsilon)+4\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{1/n\vee\varepsilon}
where
1/n>0
is the step size.

The proof of Example
4.8
is standard and given in Appendix
B.1
.
Here is our main quantitative continuity result for the value of optimal stopping problems.

Proposition 4.9

(Quantitative continuity of OS)
.

Assume that the cost
\varphi
is non-anticipative and
L
-Lipschitz, that is,
for every
f,g\in D([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
,

|\varphi(f,t)-\varphi(g,s)|\leq L\left(\sup_{u\in[0,s]}|f(s)-g(s)|+\sup_{u\in[s,t]}|f(u)-g(s)|\right).

Moreover, let
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}\in\textup{FP}
and let
\pi\in\textup{Cpl}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm{bc}}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
.
Then we have

|{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)-{\rm OS}({\mathbb{Y}},\varphi)|\leq L\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|X-Y\|_{\infty}]+\delta_{\mathbb{X}}(\varepsilon)+\delta_{\mathbb{Y}}(\varepsilon)\right).

A particular simple and yet relevant example of a class of functions
\varphi
that satisfy the assumption in Proposition
4.9
are those depending only on the current state,
i.e.,
\varphi(f,t)=\psi(f(t))
where
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz. A further example are cost functions depending on the running maximum, e.g.
\varphi(f,t)=\psi(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}f(s))
where
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz.

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

For notational simplicity, assume that there is an optimal stopping time
\tau\in\rm{ST}({\mathbb{Y}})
, i.e.
\rm{OS}({\mathbb{Y}},\varphi)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y,\tau)]
(else take an almost optimal one).
By Proposition
4.4
and the assumptions on
\varphi
we find

\displaystyle\rm{OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)-\rm{OS}({\mathbb{Y}},\varphi)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varphi(X,\tau+\varepsilon)-\varphi(Y,\tau)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\varphi(X,\tau+\varepsilon)-\varphi(Y,\tau+\varepsilon)|]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|\varphi(Y,\tau+\varepsilon)-\varphi(Y,\tau))|]

\displaystyle\leq L\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|X_{\tau+\varepsilon}-Y_{\tau+\varepsilon}|\right]+L\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\bigg[\sup_{u\in[0,\varepsilon]}|Y_{\tau+u}-Y_{\tau}|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq L\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|X-Y\|_{\infty}]+L\delta_{\mathbb{Y}}(\varepsilon).

Reversing the roles of
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
completes the proof.
∎

Note that Example
D.1
shows that the assumption that the limit process has continuous paths is necessary in Proposition
4.3
. This example naturally raises the question of which continuity assumptions on
\varphi
and
({\mathbb{X}}^{n})_{n},{\mathbb{X}}
are necessary to ensure that
{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},\varphi)\to{\rm OS}({\mathbb{X}},\varphi)
. Addressing this question lies beyond the scope of this paper and is deferred to future research.

We end this section with a ‘continuity’ result of a different flavor:

Proposition 4.10
.

For every
p\geq 1
, the set of martingales is closed in
(\textup{FP}_{p},\mathcal{AW}_{p})
.

Proof.

Assume first that
\mathcal{X}=C([0,1])
.
Let
({\mathbb{X}}^{n})_{n}
be a sequence of martingales that converges to
{\mathbb{X}}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
.
We have to show that
{\mathbb{X}}
is a martingale, i.e., that
\mathbb{E}[|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}]|]=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
To this end, fix
t\in[0,1]
.
For each
n
, let
\varepsilon_{n}\geq 0
and
\pi^{n}\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}^{\varepsilon_{n}}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})
be optimal for
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
n
be sufficiently large such that
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{n}
.
Invoking (the conditional version of) Jensen’s inequality, one may show that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}]\big|\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}[X_{1}-X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon,t}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}}]\big|\Big].

Thus, by the triangle inequality

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}-X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathbb{X}}]\big|\Big]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}[X_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon,t}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}}]-X_{t+\varepsilon}^{n}\big|\Big]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}\Big[\big|X_{t+\varepsilon}^{n}-X_{t+\varepsilon}\big|\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{t+\varepsilon}-X_{t}\big|\Big]\\
&=:I_{1}^{n}+I_{2}^{n}+I_{3}.\end{split}

We estimate each term separately.
Clearly
I_{2}^{n}\leq\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}})\to 0
.
Moreover
I_{3}\to 0
when
\varepsilon\searrow 0
by (right) continuity of the paths of
X
and the dominated convergence theorem.
In order to control
I_{1}^{n}
, observe that

(4.5)

\displaystyle X_{t+\varepsilon}^{n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}\big[X_{1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon,t}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}}\big],

where the first equality is due to
{\mathbb{X}}^{n}
being a martingale and the second follows from
\varepsilon_{n}
-bicausality of
\pi^{n}
.
Plugging (
4.5
) into
I_{1}^{n}
, we obtain

I_{1}^{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}[X_{1}-X_{1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{t+\varepsilon,t}^{{\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}}]\big|\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{n}}\Big[\big|X_{1}-X_{1}^{n}\big|\Big]\leq\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{n},{\mathbb{X}}),

where the first inequality is again due to the conditional Jensen’s inequality.
Hence, also
I_{1}^{n}\to 0
for
n\to\infty
, which completes the proof as
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary.

The proof when
\mathcal{X}=D([0,1])
follows from the same arguments as presented above, with the additional step of replacing
X_{t}
by approximations
\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{t}^{t+\delta}X_{s}\,ds
to ensure continuity (since point evaluations
f\mapsto f(t)
are not continuous on
D([0,1])
.
∎

5.
Processes with natural filtration: basic properties

As explained in the introduction, the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
corresponds to processes with natural filtration. We start with a precise definition of the class of processes which are Hoover–Keisler-equivalent to processes with natural (right-continuous, completed) filtrations.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, let
X
be the identity on
\mathcal{X}
and set
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mu}:=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\sigma^{\mu}(X_{s}:s\leq t+\varepsilon)
for
t\in[0,1]
.
We call

{\mathbb{S}}^{\mu}:=\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}^{\mu}_{\mathcal{X}},\mu,(\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},X\right)

the
standard naturally filtered process
with law
\mu
.
A process
{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{FP}
is called
naturally filtered
if
{\mathbb{X}}\sim_{\rm HK}{\mathbb{S}}^{\mu}
, where
\mu=\mathcal{L}(X)
.
The collection of all naturally filtered processes is denoted by
\mathcal{NFP}
.

By definition, being naturally filtered is a property that is independent of the representative of the Hoover–Keisler-equivalence class, hence the set
\textup{NFP}:=\mathcal{NFP}/_{\sim\textup{HK}}
is a well-defined subset of
FP
.

The aim of this section is to prove fundamental properties of this type of processes, e.g. that they are dense among all processes. To this end, we start with the following useful characterization of naturally filtered processes.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{FP}
and set
\mu:=\mathcal{L}(Y)
. Then
(\textup{id},Y)_{\#}\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{c}}({\mathbb{Y}},{\mathbb{S}}^{\mu})
. Further set

\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\sigma^{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}(Y_{s}:s\leq t+\varepsilon),\,\mathcal{G}^{1}_{t}:=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\sigma^{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}(\textup{pp}^{1}_{s}({\mathbb{Y}}):s\leq t+\varepsilon),\,\mathcal{G}^{\infty}_{t}:=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\sigma^{\mathbb{P}^{{\mathbb{Y}}}}(\textup{pp}^{\infty}_{s}({\mathbb{Y}}):s\leq t+\varepsilon).

Then
the following are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathbb{Y}}
is naturally filtered.

(ii)

\mathcal{G}^{1}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{t}

7.1.

Formally a filtered process

In the present setting, the following characterization of Brownian motion turns out to be technically convenient.
To state it, denote by

to be the standard naturally filtered process (see Definition

.

\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{B}})=0

Since the set of martingales is closed in

On the other hand, the implication

(i)

In particular, it follows from Lemma

A fundamental property of the Brownian motion is that it emerges as the continuous-time limit of scaled random walks, which is formalized in Donsker’s theorem.
It is worthwhile to emphasize that with all the preparatory work, it is straightforward to show that Donsker’s theorem extends from the setting of weak convergence to the present setting (of weak adapted convergence).

The set of laws

which are a Brownian motion and a scaled random walk, respectievly, with the desired property.

The ‘in particular’ claim follows from tail integration (i.e. for every random variable
U
,
\mathbb{E}[U^{2}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|U|\geq\sqrt{u})\,du
) and the previous estimate.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Proposition
7.3
.
The idea is to use Lemma
7.5
to couple
B
and
B^{n}
over small time-intervals, and paste them together in a bicausal way.

Proof of Proposition
7.3
.

Let us assume without loss of generality that
1/\varepsilon
and
K:=\varepsilon n
are integers.
We start by proving the first claim in the proposition.
For each
i=1,\dotso,1/\varepsilon
, take an independent copy of the probability space in Lemma
7.5
with the given
\varepsilon
and
K
. We denote it by
(\Omega^{i},\mathcal{F}^{i},\mathbb{P}^{i})
, on
which there is a scaled random walk
(X_{t}^{i})_{t\in[0,\varepsilon]}
with step size
1/n
and a Brownian motion
(Y^{i}_{t})_{t\in[0,\varepsilon]}
as in Lemma
7.5
.
Consider the product space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
of
(\Omega^{i},\mathcal{F}^{i},\mathbb{P}^{i})_{i=1,\dotso,1/\varepsilon}
, and define the processes
(B^{n}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
and
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
by

\displaystyle B^{n}_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X^{i}_{\varepsilon}+X^{\ell+1}_{t-\ell\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Y^{i}_{\varepsilon}+Y^{\ell+1}_{t-\ell\varepsilon}

for all
t\in\left[\ell\varepsilon,(\ell+1)\varepsilon\right)
where
\ell=0,\dotso,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-1
.

According to the construction, it is clear that
(B^{n}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a standard scaled random walk with step size
1/n
and
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a Brownian motion in their respective natural filtrations. Then since
B^{n}
is piecewise constant,

(7.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|B^{n}_{t}-B_{t}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq 1/\varepsilon}|B^{n}_{i\varepsilon}-B_{i\varepsilon}|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq 1/\varepsilon}\sup_{t\in[0,\varepsilon]}|X^{i}_{t}-Y^{i}_{t}|\right]=:\mathbb{E}[I]+\mathbb{E}[II].

In the remainder of the argument, let
C_{1},\dotso,C_{6}
be suitable positive constants.
In order to estimate the first term appearing on the right-hand side in (
7.6
), note that the process
(B^{n}_{i\varepsilon}-B_{i\varepsilon})_{i=1,\dotso,1/\varepsilon}
is a discrete-time martingale since its increments are independent with mean
0
. Hence an application of BDG inequality
[
49
, Chapter 3, Theorem 3.28]
shows that

\mathbb{E}[I]\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[|B^{n}_{1}-B_{1}|^{2}\right]}=C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{1}_{\varepsilon}-Y^{1}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right]}\leq\frac{C_{2}\log(K)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}},

where we use Lemma
7.5
in the last inequality.

Let us then estimate the second term on right hand side in (
7.6
). For every
x\geq 0
, using Lemma
7.5
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(II\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(C_{3}\log(K)+x\right)\right)\leq\frac{C_{4}\exp(-C_{5}x)}{\varepsilon}.

For
x\geq\frac{2}{C_{5}}\log(1/\varepsilon)
, the term on the right-hand side is bounded from above by
C_{4}\exp(-\frac{C_{5}x}{2})
.
Therefore, by tail integration,
\mathbb{E}[II]\leq C_{6}\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(K)}{\sqrt{n}}
.

In conjunction with (
7.6
), we thus obtain

(7.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|B^{n}_{t}-B_{t}|\right]\leq C_{2}\frac{\log(\varepsilon n)}{\sqrt{n}}+C_{6}\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(K)}{\sqrt{n}}\leq(C_{2}+2C_{6})\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}}.

As for the second claim in the proposition, note that by the definition of
\mathcal{AW}_{1}
and by (
7.7
),

\mathcal{AW}_{1}({\mathbb{B}},{\mathbb{B}}^{n})\leq\inf_{\varepsilon\in[0,1]}\left((C_{2}+2C_{6})\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon n}}+\varepsilon\right)\leq 2(C_{2}+2C_{6})\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt[3]{n}}.\qed

We finish this section with the proof of Proposition
7.4
:

Proof of Proposition
7.4
.

Let us start by observing that
(X,X^{n})_{*}\mathbb{P}
is a
\frac{1}{n}
-bicausal coupling between
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{X}^{n}
.
Indeed, since the volatility
\sigma
of SDE is strictly positive, we have that for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}}_{t}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\sigma^{\mathbb{P}}(B_{s}:s\leq t+\varepsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{X}^{n}}_{t}=\sigma^{\mathbb{P}}(B_{k/n}:k/n\leq t),

from which it clearly follows that
(X,X^{n})_{*}\mathbb{P}
is
\frac{1}{n}
-bicausal.

It remains to estimate
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}-X_{t}^{n}|]
.
To that end, first note that since
X^{n}
is piecewise constant,

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|X_{t}-X_{t}^{n}|\leq\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|X_{k/n}-X^{n}_{k/n}|+\max_{0\leq k<n}\sup_{t\in[k/n,(k+1)/n)}|X_{t}-X_{k/n}|=:I+II.

The term
\mathbb{E}[I]
can estimated by
[
52
]
: By Theorem 10.2.2 therein,

\mathbb{E}[I]\leq C_{1}/\sqrt{n}
where
C_{1}
is a constant depending only on the Lipschitz constants, Hölder constants and supremum norms of
\mu
and
\sigma
.
Thus, it remains to estimate
\mathbb{E}[II]
.
By the definition of
X
,

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\leq\max_{0\leq k<n}\sup_{t\in[k/n,(k+1)/n)}\left|\int_{k/n}^{t}\mu_{u}(X_{u})\,du\right|+\max_{1\leq k<n}\sup_{t\in[k/n,(k+1)/n)}\left|\int_{k/n}^{t}\sigma_{u}(X_{u})\,dB_{u}\right|

\displaystyle=:II^{\mu}+II^{\sigma}.

Clearly
|\int_{k/n}^{t}\mu_{u}(X_{u})\,du|\leq\|\mu\|_{\infty}/n
almost surely for every
k
and
t\in[k/n,(k+1)/n)
and hence
\mathbb{E}[II^{\mu}]\leq\|\mu\|_{\infty}/n
.
The estimate on
\mathbb{E}[II^{\sigma}]
follows from similar arguments as presented for the proof of Lemma
7.5
: first, in analogy to (
7.4
), we have that for every
k
and every
y\geq 0
,

(7.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[k/n,(k+1)/n)}\left|\int_{k/n}^{t}\sigma_{u}(X_{u})\,dB_{u}\right|\geq y\frac{\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq 4\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2}\right).

Indeed, if
\sigma
were constant, this would immediately follow from (
7.4
).
For non-constant
\sigma
, it can be deduced e.g. from the Dubins-Schwarz theorem
[
49
, Chapter 3, Theorem 4.6]
.
Therefore, it follows from (
7.8
), the union bound over
0\leq k<n
, and tail-integration that
\mathbb{E}[II^{\sigma}]\leq C_{2}\frac{\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{\log(n)}}{\sqrt{n}}
for an absolute constant
C_{2}
.

The combination of all the established estimates on
I
and
II
completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Auxiliary statements

B.1.
Auxiliary statements for quantitative optimal stopping

Proof of Example
4.8
.

We first note the following standard fact:
For a square integrable martingale
\mathbb{Y}
, by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the BDG-inequality (conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
) we have that

(B.1)

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbb{Y}}(\varepsilon)\leq 2\sup_{\tau\in\rm{ST}(\mathbb{Y})}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{\tau+\varepsilon}-Y_{\tau}|^{2}\right]}.

Further note that
\mathbb{E}[|Y_{\tau+\varepsilon}-Y_{\tau}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{\tau+\varepsilon}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{\tau}^{2}]
by the martingale property.

From (
B.1
) the claim for the Brownian motion is straightforward:
\mathbb{E}[B_{\tau}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\tau]
and
\mathbb{E}[B_{\tau+\varepsilon}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\tau]+\varepsilon
.

The proof for the SDE case follows from similar arguments, using (
B.1
) for the martingale part.
Indeed, using the triangle inequality and the Itô isometry.

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbb{S}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\tau\in\mathrm{ST}(\mathbb{S})}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,\varepsilon]}\left|\int_{\tau}^{\tau+s}\mu_{u}(S_{u})\,du\right|\right]+\sup_{\tau\in\mathrm{ST}(\mathbb{S})}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{\tau}^{\tau+s}\sigma_{u}^{2}(S_{u})\,du\right|\right]^{1/2}.

The claim for the random walk follows from similar arguments as in the case of Brownian motion. Suppose
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is a scaled random walk with step size
1/n>0
.
Then it is clear that
\mathbb{E}[(B^{n}_{\tau})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\lfloor n\tau\rfloor/n]
, and hence
\mathbb{E}[(B^{n}_{\tau+\varepsilon})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[(B^{n}_{\tau})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon+1/n
.
Using (
B.1
), the proof follows.

Denote by
M^{n}
the martingale part of the Euler scheme
\mathbb{S}^{n}
in (
7.2
).
Using the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(\varepsilon)\leq 2\lVert\mu\rVert_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\vee\varepsilon\right)+\sup_{\tau\in\mathrm{ST}(\mathbb{S}^{n})}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,\varepsilon]}|M^{n}_{\tau+s}-M^{n}_{\tau}|\right],

and denoting by
\langle M^{n}\rangle
the quadratic variation of
M^{n}
,

\mathbb{E}[(M^{n}_{\tau+\varepsilon})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[(M^{n}_{\tau})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\langle M^{n}\rangle_{\tau+\varepsilon}]-\mathbb{E}[\langle M^{n}\rangle_{\tau}].

Moreover, using (
B.1
) and the fact that
\langle M^{n}\rangle_{\tau+\varepsilon}-\langle M^{n}\rangle_{\tau}
is bounded from above by
2\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\infty}^{2}(1/n\vee\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(\varepsilon)\leq 2\lVert\mu\rVert_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\vee\varepsilon\right)+4\lVert\sigma\rVert_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\vee\varepsilon}.

This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma

We may assume w.l.o.g. that there is a uniformly distributed random variable

-measurable and independent of

The advantage of this assumption is that then

corresponds to the set of so-called ‘randomized stopping times’ and by

Lemma B.1
.

There exists a countable set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq C_{b}(S)
such that every
f\in C_{b}(S)
is the pointwise increasing limit of a sequence in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a countable base of the topology on
S
.
For
U\in\mathcal{B}
and
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}
with
p\geq q
, set

f_{U,p,q}:=p1_{U}+q1_{U^{c}}.

We first show that every
f\in C_{b}(S)
is the pointwise supremum of such functions.
To that end, let
\varepsilon>0
.
By continuity of
f
, for every
x\in S
, there is
B_{x}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
f(y)\geq f(x)-\varepsilon
for all
y\in B_{x}
.
Let
q\in\mathbb{Q}
with
q\leq-\|f\|_{\infty}-2\varepsilon
, and
p_{x}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
f(x)-2\varepsilon\leq p_{x}\leq f(x)-\varepsilon
.
Then

f_{B_{x},p_{x},q}\leq f\quad\text{and}\quad f_{B_{x},p_{x},q}(x)\geq f(x)-2\varepsilon.

Next we approximate
f_{U,p,q}
from below by continuous functions.
By Urysohn’s lemma, for all
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}
with
p\geq q
and all

satisfying

(where
{\rm cl}(V)
denotes the closure of

), there is a continuous function

such that

g_{U,V,p,q}=p\text{ in }V,\qquad g_{U,V,p,q}(x)=q\text{ in }U^{c}\quad\text{and }q\leq g_{U,V,p,q}\leq p.

Using the separation axiom

, it follows that

f_{U,p,q}=\sup_{V\in\mathcal{B}\text{ such that }{\rm cl}(V)\subseteq U}g_{U,V,p,q}.

Hence, we can choose
\mathcal{C}
as the collection of all finite maxima of functions of the form

, where

with

and

.
∎

Appendix C
On the strict adapted Wasserstein distance

In this section, we elaborate on the assertion made in the introduction that topology induced by the strict adapted Wasserstein metric

\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(\rm s)}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}}):=\inf_{\pi\in\textup{Cpl}_{\mathrm{bc}}({\mathbb{X}},{\mathbb{Y}})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[d^{p}_{\mathcal{X}}(X,Y)]^{1/p}

has certain shortcomings for the analysis of general stochastic processes.

First, let us show that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(\rm s)}
does not generate a ‘weak topology’.
To that end, denote by
TC
the set of all strictly increasing, absolutely continuous functions
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
that satisfy
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi(1)=1
. Consequently, each
\varphi
represents the cumulative distribution function of a probability measure on
[0,1]
, and we identify
TC
with a subset of the set of all probability measures on
[0,1]
. Now, let
{\mathbb{B}}
be the standard Brownian motion. For every
\varphi\in\textup{TC}
, we denote by
{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi}
the naturally filtered process defined by
X_{t}^{\varphi}=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\dot{\varphi}(t)}\,dB_{t}
, where
\dot{\varphi}
denotes the (weak) derivative of
\varphi
.
Hence,
X^{\varphi}
has the same law as the time-changed Brownian motion
(B_{\varphi(t)})_{t\in[0,1]}
. Taking
\mathcal{X}=C([0,1])
with
d_{\mathcal{X}}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
, the topologies generated by
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(\rm{s})}
and
\mathcal{AW}_{p}
on
\{{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi}:\,\varphi\in\textup{TC}\}
are analogous to the topologies of total variation and weak convergence on
TC
respectively:

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\varphi,\varphi^{n}\in\textup{TC}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the following holds.

(i)

\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi})\to 0
if and only if
\varphi^{n}\to\varphi
in total variation, i.e.
\dot{\varphi}^{n}\to\dot{\varphi}
in
L^{1}(dt)
.

(ii)

\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi})\to 0
if and only if
\varphi^{n}\to\varphi
weakly, i.e.
\varphi^{n}(t)\to\varphi(t)
for every
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

We start with (i).
It follows from an application of
[
11
, Proposition 3.3]
and the BDG inequality that there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending only on
p
such that

c_{1}\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi})\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sqrt{\dot{\varphi}^{n}(t)}-\sqrt{\dot{\varphi}(t)}\right)^{2}\,dt\right)^{1/2}\leq c_{2}\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi}).

Hence,
\mathcal{AW}_{p}^{(s)}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi})\to 0
if and only if
\varphi^{n}
converges to
\varphi
in the Hellinger distance and this is equivalent to
\varphi^{n}\to\varphi
in total variation.

As for (ii), suppose first that
\varphi^{n}\to\varphi
weakly.
Since
\varphi
is continuous, it follows that
\lVert\varphi^{n}-\varphi\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
.
Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
n
is large enough,

\pi=\mathcal{L}\left((B_{\varphi^{n}(t)})_{t\in[0,1]},(B_{\varphi(t)})_{t\in[0,1]}\right)

is an
\varepsilon
-bicausal coupling between
{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}}
and
{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi}
.
It readily follows that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi})\to 0
.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{AW}_{p}({\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi^{n}},{\mathbb{X}}^{\varphi})\to 0
.

Then clearly
\mathcal{L}((X^{\varphi^{n}}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]})\to\mathcal{L}((X^{\varphi}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]})
weakly which implies that
\mathcal{L}(X^{\varphi^{n}}_{t})\to\mathcal{L}(X^{\varphi}_{t})
for every fixed
t\in[0,1]
.
Since
X^{\varphi}_{t}
and
X^{\varphi^{n}}_{t}
are Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\varphi(t)
and
\varphi^{n}(t)
respectively, the claim follows.
∎

bicausal coupling between a random walk and the Brownian motion is the product coupling. Therefore, the convergence of scaled random walks to Brownian motion does not hold w.r.t. the strict adapted Wasserstein metric.
As it happens, this is the outcome of a more general phenomenon.

Let
