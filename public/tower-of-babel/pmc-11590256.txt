Finding the optimal treatment model: a systematic review and meta- analysis of manipulative interventions following failed initial treatment of radial head subluxation in children
Authors: RuiLing Xu, ShengHu Zhou, QingSong Tang, XinLing Miao, YingEr He, Xiang Ren, Kang Zhao, Jie Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe hyperpronation (HP) maneuver, known for its high success rate in reducing radial head subluxation after initial treatment failure, has gained significant favor among surgeons over the traditional supination-flexion (SF) maneuver. Despite its perceived advantages, the optimal treatment approach remains a topic of debate in the medical community due to uncertainties surrounding repeat and crossover reduction outcomes. Further research and clinical assessments are needed to establish a definitive treatment protocol ensuring optimal patient outcomes. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to compare the efficacy of HP and SF maneuvers following unsuccessful initial treatments for radial head subluxation.MethodsA thorough search of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases up to May 13, 2024 was conducted. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool evaluated study quality, and RevMan 5.3 facilitated systematic review calculations. Subgroup analyses explored reasons for heterogeneity by considering second reduction attempts with crossover and repeat maneuvers. Sensitivity analyses using fixed and random effects models were performed for ambiguous decisions.ResultsNine studies with 170 patients were analyzed. The success rate for the second reduction was significantly higher with HP (OR = 2.48, 95% CI 1.18 to 5.20, P = 0.02) compared to SF. Repeat maneuver success rate for the second reduction also favored HP (OR = 3.79, 95% CI 1.57 to 9.16, P<0.01). However, no significant difference was found in the success rate of the crossover maneuver for the second reduction (OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.16 to 3.47, P = 0.71).ConclusionThe initial reduction method was unclear, possibly favoring HP over SF following initial treatment failure for radial head subluxation in children. When the initial reduction technique is clear, the choice between HP and SF for second reduction can be adjusted based on the physician’s proficiency in the method and the patient’s cooperation.

Introduction

Radial head subluxation (RHS) is the most common upper extremity injury seen in pediatric emergency departments among children [1, 2], with annual incidence rates estimated at 5.18 to 7.69 per 10,000 children aged six years and younger [3]. The typical mechanism of injury involves sudden axial traction on the arm with the elbow extended [4], often occurring when a child is lifted abruptly by the arm [2, 5]. However, it is important to note that up to half of these injuries may result from other causes [3, 6].

Two established techniques for reducing radial head subluxation are the supination-flexion (SF) maneuver and hyperpronation (HP) maneuver [7]. While SF is commonly recommended due to its alignment with the dominant hand of right-handed clinicians addressing left arm injuries, its success is not guaranteed [8]. Studies evaluating the efficacy of SF have shown varying initial success rates, ranging from 53.3% [9] to 98.7% [10]. HP, introduced by Hutchinson in 1886 [11], has gained popularity recently, especially as the preferred initial reduction attempt. Although evidence supporting the benefits of HP is limited, studies have reported success rates for the first attempt ranging from 79% [12] to 95.6% [13]. A systematic review by Krul M et al. [14] found that HP was more effective than SF for second attempts following initial failures, however, this study did not consider crossover maneuver.

New randomized trials comparing HP and SF for radial head subluxation have been published [9, 10]. While most studies support the efficacy of HP for second reduction, Green et al. [15] found higher success rates with crossover maneuvers in the SF group after initial failures compared to the HP group (100% vs. 67.7%). To address uncertainties about the optimal approach for second reduction, recent evidence needs thorough examination. This systematic review aims to compare HP and SF regarding second reduction success rates and the impact on repeated and crossover maneuvers during second reduction.

Methods

Study protocol and registration

Adhering to the PRISMA guidelines, this study followed a pre-established protocol for the systematic review and meta-analysis process. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (Registration CRD42024528550) before data extraction initiation, ensuring transparency and rigor to in our methodology.

Inclusion criteria

We included studies that met the following criteria: (1) original research articles employing quantitative research designs such as RCTs, cohort studies, and case-control studies; (2) included studies focused on patients experiencing failed initial treatment of radial head subluxation necessitating second reduction; (3) studies comparing SF maneuver versus HP maneuver following failed initial treatments were considered; (4) repeat maneuver, where the second reduction mirrored the first, and crossover maneuver, where the second reduction differed from the first, were defined in our inclusion criteria (Fig. 1); (5) only articles published in English were included.

Exclusion criteria

Reviews, commentaries, case reports, and animal studies were excluded from our analysis, alongside letters to the editor, conference papers, books, editorials, and notes.

Databases and search strategy

On May 13, 2024, a thorough literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, and The Cochrane Library using a strategic combination of MeSH terms and keywords, specifically targeting the following phrases: “Radial Head Subluxation” or “pulled elbow” or “nursemaid’s elbow” or “annular ligament displacement” or “RHS” or “slipped elbow” or “toddler elbow”and “child” or “infant” or “newborn” or “pediatric”and “Supination-flexion” or “supination flexion” or “hyper-pronation” or “hyperpronation” or “hyper pronation” or “forced pronation” or “manipulation” or “manipulative”. Additionally, relevant studies of patients experiencing failed initial treatment of radial head subluxation necessitating second reduction were identified through scrutiny of the references cited in selected articles to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Study selection

Initial screening of search results involved two authors independently assessing eligibility, with a third author intervening in cases of disagreement. Through collaborative discussions, conflicts were resolved by carefully considering presented arguments until a consensus was achieved. Abstracts were reviewed, duplicates were eliminated, and potentially relevant studies underwent thorough full-text evaluations for inclusion.

Data extraction

Two authors independently extracted essential data details from selected studies, including author(s) name, sample size, publication year, repeat maneuver, crossover maneuver, and number of positive cases.

Assessment of risk of bias

Bias assessment was conducted by two authors using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool [16]. Discrepancies were addressed through dialogue with a third reviewer. This tool evaluated randomized controlled trials for biases in seven aspects: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other bias.

Statistical analysis

Precise statistical analyses were carried out using Review Manager software (Revman version 5.3), calculating odds ratios with a 95% confidence interval to determine effect sizes.

Heterogeneity assessments

Heterogeneity among studies was examined using Chi-squared and I-squared tests to comprehend variability. Significant heterogeneity was indicated by a low p-value (P<0.1) from the Chi-squared test or an I2 value exceeding 50%.

Data synthesis

Meta-analysis was conducted on various data groups using fixed-effect or random-effect models depending on the heterogeneity results. In cases of significant heterogeneity, random-effect models were utilized to analyze the data. The results were illustrated through forest plots.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses explored reasons for heterogeneity based on the second reduction attempt techniques, encompassing crossover and repeat maneuvers.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis were conducted using fixed and random effects models, with the exclusion of trials with the highest and lowest weights to ensure the robustness of the findings [17].

Outcomes

Identifcation of relevant studies

An initial search yielded a total of 221 studies. Following deduplication, 83 duplicates were removed. Subsequently, during title and abstract screening, 121 records were excluded based on title (79) and abstract (42) relevance. 17 publications proceeded to full-text review, with 9 meeting inclusion criteria for our systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 2).

Success rate

The success rate of second reduction was examined, focusing on treatment outcomes for patients with unsuccessful initial radial head subluxation management. This analysis included 9 studies with a total of 170 patients. Of these, 50 patients (29.4%) underwent a second reduction with HP treatment, while 120 patients (70.6%) received SF treatment. HP showed a notably higher success rate for second reduction compared to SF (OR = 2.48, 95% CI 1.18–5.20, P = 0.02) as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Subgroup analysis

Regarding crossover maneuver, a separate assessment of 29 patients across three studies revealed that 17 patients (58.6%) in the HP group, while 12 patients (41.4%) in the SF group. Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference between the two groups in the utilization of crossover maneuver (OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.16–3.47, P = 0.71). In the repeat maneuver analysis involving 141 patients from six studies, 33 patients (23.4%) in the HP group underwent second reduction compared to 108 patients (76.6%) in the SF group. The success rate for second reduction was significantly higher with HP treatment (OR = 3.79, 95% CI 1.57–9.16, P<0.01) as depicted in Fig. 6.

The sensitivity analysis, which included using a fixed-effect model and excluding trials with extreme weights, showed no significant deviations compared to previous findings, confirming the robustness of our study.

Discussion

Key findings

The study demonstrated that in cases where initial treatment for radial head subluxation fails, the likelihood of success reduction is higher when second attempt employs HP maneuver as opposed to SF maneuver. Specifically, the success rate of second reduction using repeat maneuver was notably higher in the HP group compared to the SF group. However, when evaluating the return to success rate of second reduction using a crossover maneuver, no significant differences were observed between the two groups.

Comparisons with the existing evidence

The results showed that the success rate of second reduction was significantly higher in the HP group compared to the SF group, with rates of 64.3% versus 39.2%. These findings are consistent with a previous study by Krul M et al. [14] study. Interestingly, Krul M et al. [14]reported a success rate of 90.8% (365/402) for HP and 73.6% (301/409) for SF in the initial reduction. However, in our study, the success rate for HP and SF in the second reduction was 57.7% (30/52) and 33.3% (40/120) respectively. This suggests that regardless of the reduction method, the success rate of second reduction was lower than that of the initial reduction. The lower success rate in the second reduction may be due to children’s lack of cooperation and fear during the process, leading to difficulties in performing second reduction maneuver. This highlights the importance of communicating with family members and providing emotional support to children during second reduction, to create a warm and comforting environment.

This study uniquely analyzed success rates between HP and SF during crossover maneuver, a comparison that had not been previously pooled or reported in the meta-analysis by Krul M et al. [14]. patients who did not achieve success with the initial reduction method but subsequently underwent a crossover maneuver in the SF group exhibited higher success rates of reduction compared to those in the HP group (75.0% vs. 70.6%). One potential reason is the higher occurrence of radial head subluxation in the left arm, coupled with the fact that the majority of clinicians performing the maneuver are right-handed [2, 5]. Performing SF aligns more naturally with the clinicians’ dominant hand when dealing with injuries to the patient’s left arm, which could lead to a smoother reduction process. Furthermore, Garcia-Mata et al. [20] illustrated that if initial maneuver fails to provide pain relief and restore active movement in the elbow, a second reduction employing a crossover maneuver should be considered to enhance the likelihood of success. In clinical practice, we have observed that HP is more commonly utilized and aligns with clinical norms. However, a noteworthy finding in this systematic review is that one article reported the impact of the affected side on the reduction maneuver. Specifically, for individuals sustaining injuries to the left arm, 71% (29 out of 41) were successfully reduced using SF, compared to 89% (33 out of 37) successfully reduced using HP (p = 0.044) [12]. Additional biomechanical research is required to determine conclusively why HP is more effective than SF for left arm injuries.

In the clinical setting, if the emergency department physician is unsuccessful in the initial reduction and refers the patient to a specialist pediatric orthopedic surgeon, selecting the appropriate reduction technique becomes crucial. This study demonstrated that the success rate of second reduction was significantly higher in the HP group compared to the SF group, with success rates of 62.7% versus 36.7% in the repeat maneuver. It is possible that variations in doctors’ experience levels may have influenced the results. These findings are consistent with the study by Gündel, P. A et al. [22]. The study suggests that HP may be an effective approach to improve the success rate of reduction, especially in cases where the initial reduction maneuver is unclear and during repeated reduction. This discovery carries significant implications for decision-making in choosing a reduction maneuver, as changing the reduction technique could significantly influence the trust of the family in the physician.

Limitations

The study’s main limitations are varied. Despite an increase in the available randomized controlled trials for inclusion compared to prior meta-analyses, the number remains relatively low for second reduction crossover maneuver. This scarcity hinders the strength of our conclusions. Second, we could not stratify outcomes based on potentially influential factors such as doctors’ experience level, affected limb side, and injury timing, all of which could greatly affect the success rate of second reduction. Finally, the diversity in ages and levels of fear across all studies in both repeat and crossover groups might have introduced bias into the result.

The implications of the findings

This study carries important implications for research and clinical practice. Future studies should aim to include larger sample sizes of children undergoing second reduction using crossover maneuver to enhance our understanding of success rate. Additionally, investigating the impact of interval reduction times (e.g. 5 min, 10 min) on enhancing success rate and alleviating children’s fear is crucial. Moreover, exploring early diagnostic and treatment approaches, like ultrasound, for atypical mechanisms of radial head subluxation can improve initial reduction success rate and minimize additional pain.

Conclusions

The initial reduction method was unclear, possibly favoring HP over SF following initial treatment failure for radial head subluxation in children. When the initial reduction technique is clear, the choice between HP and SF for second reduction can be adjusted based on the physician’s proficiency in the method and the patient’s cooperation.
