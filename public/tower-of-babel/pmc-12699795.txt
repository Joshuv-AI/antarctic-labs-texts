The risk of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) in pediatric patients under dental and oral surgery
Authors: Tsutomu Sasazaki, Masanori Tsukamoto, Yusuke Oku, Kazuya Matsuo, Takeshi Yokoyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is a relatively common complication of general anesthesia. We encountered PONV in pediatric patients after dental and oral maxillofacial surgeries under general anesthesia. In particular, there is a higher incidence of PONV due to bleeding of the oral cavity and aspiration of gastric contents. In this study, we retrospectively investigated the risk factors of PONV on the first postoperative day in pediatric patients.MethodsThis study was approved by the Kyushu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee and was conducted retrospectively, so informed consent for participation was not obtained. The anesthesia records of patients (aged 0–12 years) were retrospectively reviewed. The patient underwent dental and oral maxillofacial surgeries. The patients’ background, anesthesia time, use of anesthetic drugs, nitrous oxide (N2O), history of PONV, motion sickness, disability, and time to oral intake were investigated. In the univariate analysis, Fisher’s exact and χ2 tests were used, and a multivariable analysis was performed using stepwise logistic regression to determine the risk factors of PONV.ResultsA total of 589 patients were enrolled, with a mean age of 5.6 ± 2.8 years, and were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of PONV. Eighty-six patients (14.6%) complained of PONV. The occurrence of PONV was correlated with age, anesthesia time, fentanyl, N2O, history of PONV, and motion sickness. Fentanyl was the highest risk factor (odds ratio = 1.76; 95% confidence interval = 1.33–2.32).ConclusionOur findings suggest that the administration of fentanyl may be associated with an increased risk of PONV in pediatric patients undergoing dental and oral and maxillofacial surgery under general anesthesia.Keywords: PONV, Pediatric patients, Oral surgery, General anesthesia

Introduction

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is one of the frequent side effects following general anesthesia [1–3]. Nausea is a sensation of impending emesis that is frequently accompanied by autonomic changes, such as increased heart rate and salivation. Vomiting is a complex mechanism that usually consists of three related behaviors: nausea, vomiting, and the expulsion of stomach contents [4–6]. It can cause discomfort, affect patient satisfaction, and lead to other complications, such as dehydration and delayed recovery [2, 4, 5, 7]. The incidence of PONV in pediatric patients is alarmingly high, as it is estimated to be between 33% and 82% depending on patient risk factors [3–9]. In the literature, the risk factors for PONV vary by surgery type and the use of volatile anesthetics [10–12]. Furthermore, patients who have undergone oral surgery are predisposed to PONV. The incidence of PONV is particularly high due to bleeding, oral swelling, and aspiration of gastric contents due to flooding at the surgical site [13–15]. It may be difficult for pediatric patients to communicate the intensity and characteristics of their symptoms because their coping mechanisms are less developed than those of adults [16, 17]. Few studies have focused on dental and oral maxillofacial surgeries [13–15], and there is a need for further investigation.

Therefore, this retrospective survey aimed to determine the cause of PONV in pediatric patients undergoing dental and oral maxillofacial surgeries. An increase in PONV is expected during oral procedures in pediatric patients under general anesthesia. The primary outcome was the overall incidence of PONV after general anesthesia. Secondary outcomes included the risk factors for PONV.

Methods

We retrospectively investigated the medical and anesthetic records of pediatric patients (aged 0–12 years) who underwent dental and oral maxillary facial surgery between August 2021 and July 2023. The study was approved by Kyushu University Institutional Review Board for Clinical Research (Approval No.23168-00 on August 28, 2023), and the need for consent to participate was waived, then informed consent to participate was not obtained from all of the participants (parents or guardians with child assent), which complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Pediatric patients who had undergone dental treatment or oral maxillofacial surgery and remained in the hospital for 1 day after surgery were included in the analysis. The exclusion criteria included pediatric patients with dysphagia, and the postoperative sedation for more than 1 h. Patients were also excluded if they had planned postoperative admissions to the pediatric intensive care unit.

General anesthesia

The patients fasted for 2 h before surgery from clear fluids and abstained for at least 6 h from milk and solids. No premedication was administered to patients in the morning. The parents accompanied the patient to the operating room for induction of general anesthesia. Anesthesia was induced by inhalation of sevoflurane in oxygen and/or nitrous oxide (N2O) after noninvasive monitoring of oxygen saturation (SpO2). During anesthesia induction, the patient and parents were not separated until the patient was awake. After loss of consciousness, electrocardiography (ECG), blood pressure (BP), and heart rate (HR) were monitored. Fentanyl, remifentanil, atropine, and rocuronium were administered via the peripheral intravenous route. After intubation, anesthesia was maintained using inhalational anesthetics and/or propofol in oxygen and air. Rocuronium was administered if required during the procedure. In all cases, a gastric tube was nasally inserted for continuous drainage, and gravity emptied the stomach contents. Furthermore, fentanyl, remifentanil, ondansetron or acetaminophen were administered for intraoperative analgesia and PONV. After the procedure, all anesthetics were discontinued after the removal of the gastric tube and suction of the trachea. After spontaneous and regular breathing, eye opening, and verbal instructions, the patient was extubated. The first oral intake was allowed within the first 2 h after surgery, and dinner was served as usual.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using MATLAB (version 2015a; MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). In the univariate analysis, Fisher’s exact and χ2 tests were used to compare the groups. Multivariate analysis was performed using stepwise logistic regression to separately determine the risk factors for PONV. Data are shown as the mean (SD) or median (range). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we found that PONV occurred in 86 cases (14.6%), which was lower than the incidence reported in a previous study examining PONV in the oral field [16]. We also found that the independent risk factors for PONV were fentanyl, motion sickness, age, anesthesia time, history of PONV, and N2O [3, 7, 11].

Opioids sensitize the optic nerve and vestibular areas during exercise, thereby causing exercise-induced PONV [3, 11]. The mechanism of action is direct stimulation of the CTZ, increased vestibular sensitivity, and decreased gastric, small and large intestinal motility [13, 18, 19]. Increased opioid use has been found to increase the risk of PONV in a dose-dependent manner [3]. In many cases of minor surgeries in the oral region, drugs such as acetaminophen can be substituted for opioids [20]. The ability of pediatric patients to communicate the intensity and characteristics of their symptoms is challenging because their coping mechanisms are less developed than those of adults [14, 17]. Previous studies have reported a low risk of PONV in children younger than 3 years of age, which increases with age, though the mechanism is poorly understood [3, 9, 21]. This may be related to the developmental aspect associated disorders or symptoms such as cyclic vomiting syndrome, motion sickness, and nausea peak in children at ages 5 years or older [14, 22] This study also showed that the risk of PONV was higher with older age [3]. A longer duration of anesthesia affects PONV. Particularly in pediatric patients, venous access may be difficult, and exposure to volatile anesthetics may last longer, even for very short surgeries [21].

A history of PONV or motion sickness is a strong predictor of PONV [7, 8, 11, 23]. Several genetic polymorphisms have been identified that influence PONV [8, 11, 19]. Furthermore, some degree of genetic contribution to the development of PONV may result in the observed familial association; however, its exact nature has not been explained [7, 14].

The epidemiology of PONV has been well described in the literature regarding abdominal, gynecological, plastic, ear, nose, throat, and other types of surgery [3, 11–13, 16, 18, 23]. However, until now, few studies have specifically analyzed the prevalence of PONV and the related risk factors among pediatric patients undergoing dental and oral maxillofacial surgery [13, 16]. Because water is injected during procedure in the dental field, PONV can occur due to water flowing into the stomach [15, 24]. The blood in the stomach has been postulated to be one of the strongest peripheral emetogenic stimuli [24]. In some cases, nasogastric tubes increase the incidence of PONV by stimulating the glossopharyngeal nerves [25]. Therefore, in our clinical practice, a nasogastric tube is routinely placed during the induction of general anesthesia to drain gastric contents, a pharyngeal pack is placed in the oropharynx at the beginning of all procedures, and the pack and tube are removed before the patient is extubated [6, 13, 16, 24]. The use of a nasogastric tube in this study may have resulted in a lower incidence of PONV compared to previous reports [23].

The effects of N2O on PONV are well documented, ranging from 49% to 67% [5, 13]. N2O appears to cause PONV through direct stimulation of the vomiting center and interaction with opioid receptors, stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system and peripheral pathways, and distention of the air-containing cavities of the middle ear, gallbladder, and digestive tract [5, 13]. Our study also found 158 cases in which N2O and oxygen were used in the induction of general anesthesia, and the higher amounts used had an impact on PONV [5].

Disabled patients with intellectual and/or physical disabilities were not at risk of developing PONV in this study. However, children with neurodevelopmental disorders, such as cerebral palsy, spina bifida, and congenital metabolic abnormalities, are often associated with gastrointestinal disorders [25–27]. Such children are more susceptible to drugs and PONV. Therefore, caution should be exercised in the use of narcotic analgesics when PONV is considered [18, 28].

Prophylactic antiemetics are essential to prevent postoperative PONV in patients. Subhypnotic doses of propofol are effective in reducing PONV associated with general anesthesia [9, 29, 30]. Total intravenous anesthesia with propofol is a clinically relevant anesthetic technique with low emetogenic potential [3, 10, 18, 25]. It was reported that there were many types of antiemetic agents, including antagonists of serotonin, and histamine [3, 7, 19] In addition, benzodiazepine and corticosteroids exhibit antiemetic properties [15]. In recent years, 5-hydroxytryptamine 3 (5-HT3) receptor antagonists, such as ondansetron, have been widely used for the prevention of PONV [3, 19]. Although the mechanism through which dexamethasone exerts its antiemetic effect remains unknown, the antiemetic effect is widely accepted and supported by many studies [17].

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, because this is a retrospective study, the assessment of PONV was based on medical records, which may have underestimated mild or transient symptoms. Furthermore, this was a single-center study, and data was limited to the first postoperative day, which might limit the results.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the administration of fentanyl may be associated with an increased risk of PONV in pediatric patients undergoing dental and oral and maxillofacial surgery under general anesthesia.
