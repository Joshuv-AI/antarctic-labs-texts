Prevalence, incidence and risk factors of syphilis among men who have sex with men in China from 2013 to 2025: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Junshi Wang, Maoqi Zhu, Robert D Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSyphilis has re-emerged in China in recent decades, particularly among men who have sex with men (MSM). We aimed to assess the prevalence, incidence, and associated factors of syphilis among MSM in China.MethodsWe systematically searched major English (MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library) and Chinese (CNKI, Wanfang, CBM, VIP, Airiti Library) databases for studies on syphilis prevalence or incidence among MSM in China published from January 1, 2013 to March 1, 2025. Study qualities were evaluated using the Hoy et al.’s risk-of-bias tool and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Random-effects meta-analysis models were used to estimate pooled syphilis prevalence (%) and incidence (per 100 person-years, PYs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Meta-regression analyses were performed to assess differences across subgroups.ResultsA total of 441 studies (429 prevalence and 33 incidence) were included. The pooled syphilis prevalence among general MSM was 8.8% (95% CI: 8.3–9.4). Study location (R²=0.13) and study year (R²=0.11) each contributed significantly to the high heterogeneity observed (I² = 98.5%) among the general MSM prevalence studies. MSM with high-risk sexual behaviors or related risk factors exhibited higher prevalence. The pooled incidence among all MSM was 7.8 per 100 PYs (95% CI: 6.0–9.8), with similarly high heterogeneity (I² = 96.4%). Both syphilis prevalence and incidence declined over time.ConclusionSyphilis prevalence and incidence remain high among high-risk MSM subgroups in China. More rigorous studies and targeted interventions are needed to obtain more accurate estimates and to further reduce syphilis infection rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12176-8.

Background

Syphilis is a common sexually transmitted disease (STD) caused by Treponema pallidum that could affect the reproductive, nervous and dermal systems. The 2021 Global Burden of Disease Study reported that approximately 70 million people were affected by syphilis worldwide and its global prevalence has increased over the past 20 years [1].

After the founding of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, the government launched health campaigns that effectively eradicated syphilis [2]. However, since the 1980s, syphilis has re-emerged in high-risk subgroups of the population in China, including as men who have sex with men (MSM). National surveillance data showed syphilis prevalence among MSM in mainland China is 9.1% in 2009, higher than that in other high-risk groups such as female sex workers (2.4%) and drug users (3.4%).

Two global meta-analyses and one Asia-specific meta-analysis indicated that the syphilis epidemic among MSM in China represents a significant disease burden. From 2000 to 2020, the pooled syphilis prevalence among MSM in China was 9.7%, compared to a global average of 7.5% [3]. Similarly, Zheng et al. found a prevalence of 12.0% among Chinese MSM (versus 10.4% globally) and an incidence of 8.6 per 100 PYs (versus 7.6 per 100 PYs globally) during 2012–2022. They also noted a prevalence of 5.0% for Hong Kong and much higher pooled estimates in Taiwan (18.4% for prevalence and 20.0 per 100 PYs for incidence) [4]. Mahmud et al. reported that syphilis prevalence among MSM in China is 10.0% from 2000 to 2020, higher than the Asian regional average of 9.9% [5]. However, all three studies focused on national-level and only included studies indexed in English databases records. In addition, the inclusion criteria for MSM varied between these analyses.

The only two meta-analyses published in the past decade on this topic found that the prevalence of syphilis among MSM in China ranged from 7.1% to 12.3% during 2001–2015, and the incidence was about 9.6 per 100 PYs for 2006–2012 [6, 7]. However, these studies have certain limitations. First, their data coverage ends in 2015, potentially misrepresenting the current epidemic given the significant societal and public health changes since then. Secondly, both studies performed only limited subgroup analyses by time period and geographic region. Thirdly, these studies did not investigate other critical sources of heterogeneity, such as variations in study design (including sampling methods and settings) or the impact of population demographics and risk behaviors on the estimated rates.

Methods

Study design

The systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (Supplementary Table 1) and the protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024614555) [8]. We performed a comprehensive search of major English-language databases (MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus and Cochrane Library) and Chinese-language databases (CNKI, Wanfang, CBM, VIP and Airiti Library) for studies on syphilis prevalence and/or incidence among MSM population published between 2013 and Mar 1, 2025 to provide an updated synthesis of the literature following prior meta-analyses [6, 7]. Our search strategy was designed to be highly sensitive in identifying eligible studies (Supplementary Table 2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria for this systematic review and meta-analysis were structured using the CoCoPop (Condition, Context, Population) framework: the condition is syphilis infection and related factors, the context is China, and the population is men who have sex with men (MSM). For this review, MSM is defined as individuals assigned male at birth who have engaged in penetrative or receptive anal or oral sex with another man at least once in their lifetime. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table 1, and we also screen the included studies of relevant meta-analyses to identify any additional eligible reports. All retrieved references were managed using Zotero software (Version 7.0.15) to remove duplicates and identify studies meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study selection and data extraction

Two authors (J.W. and M.Z.) independently screened the titles and abstracts and then assessed full texts for inclusion using the criteria (Table 1). All authors were blinded to each other’s decisions during the screening process. Any disagreements were discussed by the entire review team after unblinding. A third author (R.D.S.) acted as an arbiter to resolve any discrepancies during the discussion.

Two authors (J.W. and M.Z.) extracted and cross-checked the following information from each included study (recorded in Microsoft Excel): first author, publication year, study year, study population, study location (province), sampling method, sample size, number of infected cases and sampling setting (e.g.: online, STD clinics, or in community settings).

For cross-sectional studies, we recorded the reported syphilis prevalence. For randomized clinical trials (RCTs), we used the baseline syphilis prevalence. For cohort studies, we extracted any baseline prevalence (if applicable) and the incidence of syphilis during follow-up.

Guided by prior studies and by China CDC’s Guidelines for HIV Prevention Interventions among MSM [9], we extracted several potential moderator variables for the univariable meta-regressions: sampling method, study year, risk of bias, sample size, diagnostic criteria, sampling setting, and study location. For study year, we used the year the study was completed (or the publication year if the completion year was not reported). For diagnostic criteria, we noted that mainland China and Taiwan require both a non-treponemal and treponemal test to diagnose syphilis, whereas Hong Kong and Macau have no official criteria. We therefore considered studies employing both types of syphilis tests as meeting diagnostic criteria for our analysis.

We also extracted data on any potential risk factors reported by at least three studies for subgroup analyses. To maximize inclusivity of relevant articles while minimizing issues of generalisability of studies, we classified the included studies as either focusing on the general MSM population (no additional inclusion criteria other than being MSM) or on specific MSM subgroups. Based on data availability, we extracted syphilis prevalence and/or incidence of different age (reference group was: MSM ≤ 25 years old, “young MSM”), participants’ schools (reference: senior high school and college students), current alcohol use status (reference group: those who consume alcohol), recreational drug use (reference group: those who use drugs), engagement in commercial sex (reference group: those who have engaged in commercial sex), condom use in the past six months (reference group: those who consistently use condoms), education level (reference group: junior high school or below), primary partner-seeking method (reference group: online), sexual orientation (reference group: transgender women), gender of sexual partners (reference group: exclusively male partners), number of sexual partners (reference group: ≤1 partner), HSV-2 status (reference group: HSV-2 positive), and HIV status (reference group: HIV positive). Different studies used different age categorizations for MSM. We therefore analyzed age in two ways: (1) using standard age groups with cut-offs at 25, 35, and 45 years (“Age group” analysis), and (2) using 30 and 50 as cut-offs in studies, defining MSM under 30 years as “young MSM” and those older than 50 years as “older MSM” (“Specific age category” analysis).

Quality assessment

We assessed the quality of prevalence studies using the 10-item risk-of-bias tool by Hoy et al. [10] and assessed the quality of incidence studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11]. Two authors (J.W and M.Z) independently performed the assessments, and any discrepancies were resolved in consultation with a third author (R.D.S).

According to Hoy et al.’s criteria, scores with 7–10 indicate a low risk of bias, 4–6 indicate a moderate risk, and 0–3 indicate a high risk in prevalence studies. For NOS, scores of 7–9 are considered to indicate a low risk of bias, 4–6 indicate a moderate risk of bias, and 0–3 indicate a high risk of bias.

Syphilis prevalence and incidence calculation

Syphilis prevalence was defined as the ratio of the number of individuals with concordant positive treponemal and non-treponemal test results to the total number of individuals who underwent both tests. For studies not conforming to the diagnostic criteria, prevalence was defined as the ratio of the number of individuals with a positive result on any syphilis test to the total number of individuals tested. Syphilis incidence was defined as the ratio of the number of newly detected syphilis-positive individuals during the follow-up period to the total person-years of follow-up. These calculation methods are widely used in syphilis epidemiological studies [12, 13].

Statistical analysis

We used restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random effects meta-analyses models with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to calculate the pooled prevalence of syphilis among general MSM and the pooled incidence among all MSM, each with 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with I2 >50% indicating high heterogeneity. We assessed potential publication bias using Doi plot and LFK index [14]. These methods are considered more suitable for meta-analyses of proportions than funnel plots [15]. We performed meta-regressions to estimate syphilis prevalence and incidence across different MSM subgroups. We also conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, as well as sensitivity analyses excluding studies with a high risk of bias and those not meeting the diagnostic criteria, to examine the influence of individual studies, studies with high risk of bias, and studies not meeting the diagnostic criteria on the pooled results.

For syphilis prevalence subgroup analyses, we conducted two sets of subgroup analyses. First, we performed univariable meta-regressions on the general MSM studies to assess the impact of temporal, geographic, and methodological factors on prevalence heterogeneity, and we also ran a multivariable meta-regression to explore determinants of heterogeneity. Second, we combined data from general MSM studies (with detailed individual-level data) with data from specific subgroup studies and carried out univariable meta-regressions to compare prevalence across various demographic, social, and behavioral subgroups of MSM (e.g., those engage in commercial sex or not, living with or without HIV).

For syphilis incidence, since most incidence studies (24 of 33) focused on HIV-negative MSM populations, and none of the four general MSM incidence studies provided detailed subgroup data, we performed a univariable meta-regression using study population as the moderator to compare syphilis incidence across different subgroups.

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2) with the meta, metafor, metasens, sf and orchard package. We considered two-sided p values < 0.05 to be statistically significant.

Results

Literature review

We identified 1959 records from four English databases and 2681 records from Chinese databases, with 755 English records and 631 Chinese records remaining after merging the duplications. 486 English records and 253 Chinese records were excluded from the title/abstract screening, and 99 English records and 107 Chinese records were excluded from the full-text reassessment. Ultimately, 170 English-language studies and 271 Chinese-language studies (total N = 441, Fig. 1) met the eligibility criteria. Of these 441 studies, 398 were cross-sectional studies, 4 were RCTs, and 39 were cohort studies.

Characteristics of studies

Of all prevalence studies (429 studies, 675,701 total participants): 368 studies focused on general MSM. The remaining studies investigated specific subgroups of MSM, including: five on MSM under 25 years old; two on “young MSM” (under 30 years); four on “older MSM” (over 50 years); eight on student MSM; three on MSM engaged in commercial sex; three on MSM not engaged in commercial sex; 20 on HIV-positive MSM; 13 on HIV-negative MSM; two on transgender women; and one on MSM who use recreational drugs (Supplementary Table 3).

Of prevalence studies of general MSM populations including detailed individual data and studies of specific MSM populations: 23 studies related to age, eight studies related to student MSM, four studies related to MSM populations with or without alcohol consumption, 17 studies related to MSM populations with or without recreational drug use, 19 studies related to MSM populations engaging or not engaging in commercial sex, seven studies related to consistent or inconsistent condom use, 11 studies on methods for seeking sexual partners, five studies related to sexual and gender minorities, 12 studies related to the gender of sexual partners, three studies related to the number of sexual partners, three studies related to HSV-2 infection status, and 164 studies related to HIV infection status.

Incidence studies (33 studies, 29,826.3 PYs of follow-up): four studies focused on general MSM. The others were primarily in specific groups: one on MSM under 25 years old; one on student MSM; three on HIV-positive MSM; and 24 on HIV-negative MSM (Supplementary Table 4).

Risk of bias

Using Hoy et al.’s tool, the risk-of-bias scores for the 429 prevalence studies ranged from 2 to 7. In total, 31 studies were rated as low risk, 391 as moderate risk, and 7 as high risk (median score 6, IQR 5–6) (Supplementary Table 3). For the 33 incidence studies, NOS scores ranged from 4 to 8, with 15 rated low risk and 18 moderate risks (median score 6, IQR 6–7) (Supplementary Table 4).

Prevalence analysis

In our analysis of syphilis prevalence among general MSM population in China, the pooled prevalence from 2013 to 2025 was 8.8% (95% CI: 8.3–9.4). Heterogeneity across studies was very high (I2 = 98.5%). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled prevalence estimates ranging only from 8.8% to 8.9%, with no single study disproportionately influencing the overall result (Supplementary Fig. 1). Excluding studies assessed as having a high risk of bias resulted in a pooled prevalence of 8.9% (95% CI: 8.3–9.5) and excluding studies that did not meet our defined syphilis diagnostic criteria yielded a pooled prevalence of 8.7% (95% CI: 8.1–9.3). Meta-regression analysis showed that there was no statistically significant difference in syphilis prevalence between the original estimate and the estimates obtained after excluding studies with a high risk of bias or those not meeting the diagnostic criteria (Supplementary Table 5).

Figure 2 shows prevalence by province: Hong Kong and Macau lack relevant data, Nei Mongol and Taiwan had prevalence over 15%, five provinces (Beijing, Tianjin, Tibet, Guizhou and Guangdong) had 10–15%, three provinces (Jilin, Yunnan and Jiangxi) had about 2.5–5%, Hubei had below 2.5%, and all other provinces ranged between 5% and 10%.

Specific MSM population analysis

Figure 4 shows that syphilis prevalence was significantly higher in several specific MSM subgroups: MSM aged over 45 years (17.2%, 95% CI: 12.3–22.6; p < 0.001), older MSM (19.8%, 95% CI: 16.0-23.8; p < 0.001)), college student MSM (6.5%, 95% CI: 4.0-9.5; p = 0.032)), MSM who used recreational drugs (11.1%, 95% CI: 8.0-14.6; p < 0.001), MSM who engaged in commercial sex (14.2%, 95% CI: 11.2–17.6; p = 0.008), MSM who did not consistently use condoms in the past six months (12.2%, 95% CI: 8.4–16.5; p < 0.001), MSM who primarily find sexual partners through offline settings (11.1%, 95% CI: 9.2–13.1; p < 0.001), MSM with a homosexual sexual orientation (20.2%, 95% CI: 13.7–27.6; p = 0.008)), MSM with two or more sexual partners in the past six months (11.5%, 95% CI: 7.9–15.5; p < 0.001), HSV-2 positive MSM (35.1%, 95% CI: 25.0-45.9; p < 0.001) and HIV positive MSM (24.9%, 95% CI: 23.4–26.4; p < 0.001).

In contrast, MSM with a college education or above (11.5%, 95% CI: 8.1–15.5; p < 0.001) have significantly lower prevalence. No significant differences in syphilis prevalence were observed between MSM who consumed alcohol or not (10.6% (95% CI: 3.4–20.9) vs. 9.9% (95% CI: 3.1–19.9), p = 0.372), nor between MSM who have sex with females and those who did not (12.5% (95% CI: 9.0-16.5) vs. 12.9% (95% CI: 9.4–16.9), p = 0.127) (Table 4).

Incidence analysis

The pooled incidence of syphilis among all MSM in China was 7.8 per 100 PYs (95% CI: 6.0-9.8) from 2013 to 2025, with high heterogeneity (I2 = 96.4%). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that exclusion of any single study did not meaningfully affect the pooled incidence, with estimates consistently ranging between 7.5 and 8.1 per 100 PYs (Supplementary Fig. 3). Analysis of changes over time showed a downward trend in incidence over time, decreasing from 10.0 per 100 PYs (95% CI: 6.7–13.9) in 2008 to 4.7 per 100 PYs (95% CI: 1.8–8.7) in 2022 (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 8).

Figure 5 presents the syphilis incidence rates across different MSM subgroups. Using general MSM as the reference group (4.5 per 100 PYs, 95% CI: 1.8–8.2), we found no significant difference in incidence for student MSM (8.1 per 100 PYs, 95% CI: 1.2–20.6; p = 0.442), HIV-negative MSM (7.4 per 100 PYs, 95% CI: 5.9–9.2; p = 0.135), or MSM under 25 years old (3.4 per 100 PYs, 95% CI: 0.1–10.6; p = 0.736). However, HIV-positive MSM had a significantly higher incidence (20.5 per 100 PYs, 95% CI: 13.3–29.3; p < 0.001) compared to general MSM (Table 5).

In additional meta-regressions using HIV-positive MSM as the reference group, syphilis incidence among HIV-positive MSM was significantly higher than among HIV-negative MSM (p < 0.001), general MSM (p < 0.001), and MSM under 25 years old (p = 0.002). There was no significant difference between HIV-positive MSM and student MSM individuals (p = 0.084).

Publication bias

In our assessment, we found no significant publication bias among the general MSM prevalence studies (LFK index = 1.58, indicating minor asymmetry), whereas the incidence studies suggested some evidence of publication bias (LFK index = 2.43, indicating major asymmetry) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

This nationwide meta-analysis included 441 studies of syphilis among MSM in China from 2013 to 2025, comprising 429 prevalence studies and 33 incidence studies. To our knowledge, it is the first meta-analysis to assess the syphilis epidemiology and associated factors among both general and specific MSM populations in China.

Our study yields several important findings. First, we observed a decreasing trend over time in both syphilis prevalence and incidence among MSM in China, alongside significant regional disparities in prevalence. Second, our analysis clarifies the impact of sampling settings and diagnostic criteria on prevalence estimates. Prevalence derived from online recruitment was substantially lower than that from VCTs or community venues. Studies using only treponemal tests may overestimate prevalence by capturing cumulative rather than current infections. Although rapid testing kits use treponemal tests, potential improper procedures during self-administration could lead to an underestimation of prevalence. Finally, we identified several specific populations at higher risk; those who are older, have lower educational levels, use recreational drugs, engage in commercial sex, or are living with other STIs, as these individuals are more likely to engage in risky sexual behaviors. These findings provide crucial evidence for designing rigorous epidemiological studies and developing targeted interventions.

Prevalence among general MSM population

We found a pooled syphilis prevalence of 8.8% among general MSM in China (2013–2025). This is lower than the 9.7–12.0% pooled prevalence for China reported in recent global or regional meta-analyses [3–5]. The lower estimate in our study could be due to the inclusion of more recent data and the exclusion of studies focusing on high-risk MSM subgroups (e.g. those living with HIV). However, this prevalence remains higher than that reported among people who inject drugs (5.0%) and female sex workers (4.3%) in China [16, 17].

In the univariable analysis, prevalence was significantly higher in studies using convenience sampling or TLS compared to those using mixed sampling. However, these differences disappeared in the multivariable model. This suggests that the effect of sampling methods may have been confounded by other factors such as recruitment setting. For example, all four studies that employed TLS were conducted in community venues, as these are common gathering places for MSM.

We observed a clear downward trend in syphilis prevalence among General MSM over the study period. In 2010, Chinese government launched the National Syphilis Prevention and Control Program (2010–2020) to curb syphilis transmission. Zeng et al. (2025) reported that this program significantly reduced syphilis prevalence transmitted via same-sex contact among people living with HIV in China [18]. Our findings further demonstrate the program’s effectiveness. However, studies from the last three years still reported MSM syphilis prevalence as high as 7.4%. This highlights the need to continue strengthening syphilis prevention and education among MSM to meet the WHO’s goal of a 90% reduction in syphilis incidence by 2030.

Studies rated as moderate or low risk of bias reported significantly higher prevalence than those rated high risk. Most studies with a high risk of bias (5 of 7) used self-administered rapid testing kits as testing method, such non-standardized procedures might have led to under-detection of cases.

Our analysis found no significant difference in prevalence between studies with small sample sizes (≤ 500 participants) and those with larger samples (>500). This finding is consistent with a previous global meta-analysis [3].

While adherence to the diagnostic criteria showed no association with prevalence in univariable analysis, it emerged as a significant factor in the multivariable model. After adjusting for all contributing factors, studies adhering to the diagnostic criteria showed a lower prevalence than those employing treponemal tests only. This may be because a positive result in studies following the full diagnostic criteria represents a current infection, whereas a positive result in studies using only treponemal tests represents a cumulative infection (since treponemal antibodies typically persist for life) [19]. Furthermore, there was no significant difference in prevalence between studies adhering to the diagnostic criteria and those using only non-treponemal tests, suggesting that any false positives from non-treponemal tests did not materially impact the overall syphilis prevalence estimates. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the number of studies using only non-treponemal tests was considerably smaller than in the other two groups.

Previous studies have noted that syphilis prevalence among MSM can differ by recruitment venue; for example, MSM recruited from bathhouses/saunas had higher prevalence than those recruited online [20, 21]. We observed a similar pattern, with syphilis prevalence being higher in studies sampling from healthcare settings (STD clinics: 11.7% or VCTs: 17.7%) or community venues (e.g. bathhouses, saunas, bars, sex parties; 14.2%) compared to those sampling online (5.8%).

We observed significant spatial differences in prevalence across China. Among the six mainland regions, prevalence was highest in Northern China and lowest in Southwestern China, consistent with prior findings [6]. The reason for this regional disparity remains unclear, likely due to limited data on regional differences in risk factors. We also found that reported prevalence in Taiwan was significantly higher than in other regions. This high estimate may be biased, as the few available studies from Taiwan were all conducted in high-risk settings (e.g., healthcare settings or community venues), which probably overestimates the actual population prevalence.

Notably, Taiwan has established a notifiable diseases surveillance system integrated with its universal health insurance data, it does not collect personal identifiers of infected cases, which could limit detailed epidemiologic analysis [22]. Moreover, we were only able to include one eligible study from Hong Kong (focused on HIV-negative MSM) and none from Macau. Interestingly, a report published by the Hong Kong Department of Health in 2024 found a syphilis prevalence of 10.7% among local MSM who had been tested in the past year – much higher than that in our included Hong Kong study (4.9%) or the previous global meta-analysis estimate for Hong Kong (5.0%) [23]. Given these regional disparities and data gaps, future region-specific studies on syphilis (and other STDs) among MSM are crucial to clarify emerging trends, address regional differences, and inform tailored public health strategies.

Prevalence among different specific MSM subgroups

Our analysis of various demographic, social, and behavioral subgroups found higher syphilis prevalence in MSM subgroups who were older, used recreational drugs, engaged in commercial sex, did not consistently use condoms, had lower education levels, or had multiple sexual partners. By contrast, alcohol use and the gender of sexual partners were not significant factors affecting prevalence. These patterns generally align with a recent global meta-analysis [4].

Recreational drug use, engagement in commercial sex, and lower educational levels are all associated with high-risk sexual behaviors, such as unprotected sex and having multiple concurrent sexual partners. One explanation is that older MSM may lack syphilis prevention knowledge and experience sexual repression, leading to more unprotected anal intercourse [24]. Additionally, Ning et al. (2018) found that some older MSM may have been misclassified in studies, suggesting that actual prevalence in that group could be even higher [25]. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions for MSM at higher risk of engaging in risky sexual behaviors, as such strategies may reduce syphilis prevalence more effectively than generalized approaches.

With regard to coinfections, previous research has noted a positive association between HSV-2 infection and syphilis [26]. Consistent with this, we found significantly higher syphilis prevalence among HSV-2 positive MSM compared to HSV-2 negative MSM. All three studies examining HSV-2 prevalence among MSM utilized ELISA to detect HSV-2 IgG antibodies [27–29]. This indicates that the HSV-2 status is associated more with cumulative infection than with current infection [30]. Consequently, MSM living with HSV-2 may be at higher risk of syphilis infection as a result of engaging in more high-risk behaviors, rather than as a direct effect of the HSV-2 infection itself. Given the limitations of the detection method, the unclear biological mechanism linking HSV-2 and syphilis, the high heterogeneity, and the limited sample size, this result should be confirmed by further research.

Syphilis can increase the risk of acquiring other STDs (like HIV) by causing genital ulcers, and conversely HIV infection can increase the risk of syphilis by weakening the immune system [31]. In our study, syphilis among MSM living with HIV was significantly higher than that among HIV-negative MSM, in line with previous findings [4]. David et al. (2020) further showed that syphilis and HIV infections each increase the risk of the other in MSM, and that improving syphilis testing and treatment rates could theoretically eliminate syphilis transmission among MSM [32]. However, syphilis testing rates among MSM in China remain much lower than HIV testing rates [33]. For example, Zhang et al. (2017) reported that while 54% of MSM had been tested for HIV, only 30% had ever been tested for syphilis [34].

Incidence among all MSM and different specific MSM subgroups

We found a pooled incidence of 7.8 per 100 PYs among all MSM (2013–2025), which is lower than the 8.6 per 100 PYs reported by Zheng et al. (2024) in 2012–2022, possibly due to the inclusion of more recent data as well [4].

Owing to limited data, our comparisons of syphilis incidence were restricted to broad MSM groups. The higher incidence observed in HIV-negative MSM studies compared to general MSM likely reflects the fact that most incidence studies targeted HIV-negative MSM and often reported syphilis incidence only as a secondary outcome (with HIV incidence as the primary focus). Moreover, potential publication bias in the incidence data indicates that some lower-incidence results may be underrepresented in the literature.

In addition, our results suggested that HIV-positive MSM have higher syphilis incidence than HIV-negative MSM, consistent with findings from Australia [35]. Apart from the HIV-positive group, we did not observe significant differences in incidence between general MSM and other specific subgroups. These findings are based on relatively few studies, so further research is needed to better understand the incidence patterns in different MSM populations.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, all 441 included studies used non-probability sampling methods, with convenience sampling the most common. This likely reflects the fact that MSM populations are hard to reach, making probability-based sampling impractical. Despite this limitation, after adjusting for confounders, we found no significant prevalence differences among the various non-probability sampling methods, consistent with a previous global meta-analysis [3].

Secondly, although we employed random-effects models and subgroup meta-regressions to explore heterogeneity, a substantial amount of heterogeneity remained unexplained. This suggests that other influencing factors were not captured or reported in the included studies.

Thirdly, despite our comprehensive screening and data extraction, certain potential determinants of syphilis risk (e.g. participation in group sex, or the frequency of STIs testing in the past six months, and uptake of Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)) could not be analyzed due to lack of detailed data in the primary studies. For example, PrEP may lead to risk compensation, where individuals using it are more inclined to engage in condomless sex, thereby increasing their risk of acquiring bacterial STIs such as syphilis [36]. A cohort study in the United States demonstrated that PrEP use was associated with a subsequent rise in syphilis incidence [37]. However, among all 441 included studies, only one examined the relationship between PrEP and syphilis prevalence; that study found that MSM who had never used PrEP but were willing to do so had a significantly higher syphilis prevalence than both current or former PrEP users and those unwilling to do so [38]. This contrast with findings from other countries underscores the need for further studies on the link between PrEP and syphilis risk among MSM in China. Similarly, because of limited data and few studies, we were also unable to further explain the significant differences in syphilis prevalence between studies focusing solely on college students and those including both senior high school and college students, nor the differences between studies on transgender women versus MSM of any sexual orientations.

Fourthly, we were unable to classify the included studies based on their screening approach. China currently lacks specific syphilis screening guidelines for MSM. Instead, the National Guidelines for HIV Prevention Interventions among MSM (often used to guide syphilis screening) only stipulate annual syphilis screening as part of the national sentinel surveillance [9]. This sentinel surveillance typically involves a mix of active screening for asymptomatic MSM and passive screening for MSM who present for care, and it employs diverse sampling methods and settings [21]. Consequently, we were unable to categorize the studies as employing either an active or a passive screening approach. Despite this limitation, we systematically extracted the sampling methods and settings from each study and conducted subgroup analyses to explore variations in syphilis prevalence across different sampling contexts.

Finally, there were far fewer studies on syphilis incidence than on prevalence, and most incidence studies focused on HIV-negative MSM, limiting their representativeness for the broader MSM population. Compared to prevalence, which serves as a snapshot of disease burden and aids in resource allocation, incidence more effectively captures the characteristics of syphilis infection risks among MSM populations across different time periods and geographical regions. It also facilitates the identification of related risk factors and causal inference and can be utilized to evaluate the effectiveness of syphilis prevention measures, thereby providing greater clinical utility. Therefore, this relative scarcity highlights a significant gap in our understanding of syphilis infection risk among MSM populations. Moreover, in many of those incidence studies, HIV incidence was the primary outcome, which might lead to follow-up periods that were suboptimal for detecting syphilis infections, particularly given the risk of syphilis reinfection.

We recommend that future studies of syphilis among MSM employ sampling methods that more closely approximate probability sampling (e.g. RDS), utilize both non-treponemal and treponemal tests for diagnosis with detailed reporting of each test’s sensitivity and specificity, collect comprehensive information on participants’ sexual behaviors (e.g., engagement in group sex, chemsex, PrEP use), and report the screening approach clearly [39]. Furthermore, additional rigorous cohort studies targeting general MSM populations are needed to obtain more accurate estimates of syphilis incidence and to elucidate the demographic, social, and behavioral correlates of infection risk.

Conclusion

Our findings describe the temporal trends and geographic distribution of syphilis among MSM in China, and shed light on how various demographic, social, and behavioral factors affect infection rates in this population. Current prevalence and incidence rates suggest that prevention measures appear to have yielded some positive results, as evidenced by the overall declining trends. However, syphilis prevalence and incidence remain high among high-risk subgroups such as MSM living with HIV, middle-aged and older MSM, and those who use recreational drugs. Therefore, further efforts are needed – including strengthening integrated syphilis-HIV testing, increasing MSM-focused syphilis prevention and education, and combatting the stigma faced by MSM. Moreover, additional well-designed studies are required to refine prevalence and incidence estimates and to better identify risk factors for syphilis among MSM in China.
