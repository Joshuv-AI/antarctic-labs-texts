Whole-cell and cell-free biosensor-driven metabolic engineering
Authors: Jiho Seok, Mark P Styczynski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolic engineering employs microbial cell factories to produce high-value products from low-cost feedstocks. Designing, optimizing, and evaluating biosynthetic pathways in microbial cell factories is essential, yet these processes remain time- and labor-intensive. Biosensors help metabolic engineers address this challenge by converting target metabolite concentrations into observable outputs, enabling efficient assessment of microbial production. Whole-cell biosensors, which operate within living microorganisms, and cell-free biosensors, which function independently of cell growth using transcription–translation machinery, have contributed to microbial biosynthesis optimization through distinct approaches. This review summarizes recent advances in biosensor-driven metabolic engineering facilitated by whole-cell and cell-free biosensors.

Introduction

Metabolic engineering entails the redirection of metabolic pathways in microbial cell factories to synthesize valuable products from inexpensive substrates [1]. It has attracted attention due to its potential to enable environmentally friendly and sustainable chemical production. However, effective pathway design and optimization remain challenging due to the complexity of microbial systems. For instance, identifying the most suitable enzymes for reactions and the biological parts such as promoters or ribosome binding sites (RBSs) for optimal gene expression often requires extensive trial and error [2]. These efforts are further complicated by the need for time-consuming and costly chromatography to measure metabolite levels to characterize pathway intermediates and identify bottlenecks.

Whole-cell biosensors (WCB) are cells that are genetically engineered to convert the intracellular concentration of a target metabolite into useful reporter outputs such as fluorescence, luminescence, colorimetric change, or cell physiology traits (Figure 1a)[3]. Because these outputs can be interpreted in real-time without additional purification or chromatography, WCB can significantly reduce the time required for design-build-test-learn (DBTL) cycles in metabolic engineering.

Recently, cell-free systems that harness transcription and translation machinery for applications outside of a cell have emerged as a promising tool for screening (Figure 1a) [4]. By eliminating time-consuming steps such as cell growth and transformation, these systems facilitate rapid testing, providing advantages in prototyping bioparts and optimizing biosynthetic pathways. The use of linear DNA expression templates in cell-free systems even further reduces the length of DBTL cycles by minimizing cloning time, enabling the more rapid evaluation of some parts of metabolic engineering designs [5].

Whole-cell and cell-free biosensors, which have served as valuable tools for constructing efficient microbial cell factories, continue to expand in application and sophistication. Recent progress in CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) and high-throughput screening technologies has enabled increasingly advanced sensors for a broad swath of applications [6]. Moreover, the emergence of biofoundries has further suggested the possibility of developing self-driving laboratories capable of automatically designing and constructing desired microbial cell factories using feedback from such biosensors [7]. This review summarizes recent advances and potential future directions in whole-cell and cell-free biosensors, focusing on their distinct roles in supporting metabolic engineering.

Biosensor-assisted enzyme screening and selection

Identification of efficient enzymes from among multiple candidates to enhance biosynthetic pathway functionality and flux is often an essential step of metabolic engineering (Figure 1b). In vitro evolution methods enable the generation of many enzyme candidates and variants in a short time frame, making screening or selecting high-performing candidates from the library critical to success; biosensors often help enable these screening and selection efforts.

Whole-cell: screening and selection platform for mutant libraries

Directed evolution produces diverse mutants that may have desired functions or traits by inducing mutations or gene recombination, with WCBs enabling downstream screening or selection of clones (Figure 1b). The most common strategy for WCBs for these applications is to genetically engineer a strain to use a transcription factor (TF) to sense a target metabolite and convert its intracellular concentration into reporter gene expression, enabling screening or selection for high or low metabolite levels. Screening involves quantifying the expression of reporter genes associated with fluorescence, luminescence, or colorimetric changes. Selection relies on the expression of reporter genes linked to cell growth or fitness. Such WCBs have been widely used to identify efficient enzymes for producing biofuels, amino acids, and terpenes [8–10]. Moreover, dual readout systems that incorporate both fluorescent reporters and growth-related genes have been developed to reduce false positives [11,12].

In cases where suitable TFs for a target compound are unavailable, multiple alternative strategies exist. For example, one may instead detect a secondary molecule induced by the target or convert the compound into a detectable metabolite via enzymatic catalysis [13,14]. Another strategy involves applying directed evolution to the biosensor itself to modulate substrate specificity for an existing TF. One recent example involved engineering a promiscuous substrate-binding TF via directed evolution to recognize plant alkaloids for which no naturally occurring TF has been identified [15]. The resulting biosensor was then used to screen an enzyme library involved in plant alkaloid biosynthesis, ultimately identifying a novel enzyme that replaced four separate methylation steps. Furthermore, this biosensor was improved through additional directed evolution to achieve high specificity for a particular alkaloid target, resulting in increased productivity and reduced formation of undesired regioisomers [16].

While directed evolution introduces mutations artificially, adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) induces natural selection by imposing specific stress conditions for evolution that can be coupled to WCB strategies. To adapt to a given stress, microbial strains accumulate diverse mutations across the genome, and altering the stress conditions enables the selection of desired phenotypes. When the desired trait is to enhance the productivity of some compound, growth-based selection can be performed using a WCB that detects the compound and links its detection to the expression of an antibiotic resistance gene. For example, in a biosensor system designed to recognize 3-hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) and use tetracycline resistance as a reporter, gradually increasing the tetracycline concentration allowed only 3-HP high-producing mutants to survive [17]. Reporter systems can also take other forms, including the expression of biosynthetic genes that produce essential nutrients required by auxotrophs [18].

ALE coupled to WCBs can also be used to improve strain tolerance for toxic products. For instance, ALE-based WCBs have been used to evolve tolerance to organic acids such as 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid and protocatechuic acid [19,20]. The resulting strains exhibited improved growth rates and reduced cell death, enabling the selection of variants with enhanced production capabilities. Moreover, ALE can also expand substrate utilization capacity. Applying selective pressure by providing specific sole carbon sources such as allulose, xylose, or acetate enabled the survival of strains that can efficiently metabolize these compounds [21–23].

Cell-free: screening system with accelerated design-build-test-learn cycle

Cell-free biosensors use biological transcription and translation of machinery outside of cells and thus are independent of cell growth (Figure 1b). Thus, while in vivo selection used in WCBs is not feasible for cell-free biosensors, in vitro sensing and screening can be performed with the benefit of avoiding interference from cytotoxicity and the influence of intracellular microenvironments. In addition, cell-free systems enable shorter DBTL cycles for screening enzymes because they avoid time-consuming cell growth steps and minimize the cloning needed to test dosing impacts in metabolic pathways.

Despite these advantages, cell-free biosensors have faced limitations in screening mutant libraries. In whole-cell systems, plasmid mutant libraries can be transformed so that each cell carries only one variant of a gene. This one-cell/one-variant structure allows direct screening or selection of enzymes at the single-cell level, as each mutant can be linked to its corresponding enzyme activity. In contrast, cell-free systems require systematic strategies for the distribution and retrieval of mutant libraries.

To address this challenge, high-throughput screening methods based on water-in-oil droplet compartmentalization have been developed and explored. For example, liposomes have enabled the assignment of individual plasmids to separate liposomes containing cell-free machinery, followed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for screening [24]. Another approach used a microfluidic device to generate droplets loaded with plasmids for cell-free expression, allowing for the screening of toxic protease variants [25]. These methods can be further strengthened by the ability of cell-free systems to sometimes completely bypass cloning steps by using simple polymerase chain reaction (PCR) strategies to generate linear expression templates in place of plasmids, which are the simplest expression template that can be used in whole cells [26,27]. Moreover, a notable recent approach leveraged the ability of cell-free systems to assemble bacteriophage [28]. A phagemid library encoding enzyme variants and the M13 phage backbone was independently assembled into functional bacteriophages in a cell-free system. This virus-based compartmentalization enabled screening by fluorescence to identify high read-through activity RNA polymerase with a twofold increase in read-through activity.

Biosensor-assisted pathway design and optimization

Metabolic engineers also often seek to move beyond random mutations by developing rational approaches to designing microbial cell factories. Systems biology approaches have been used to identify which genes should be enhanced, suppressed, or deleted to optimize biosynthesis of a target molecule, as well as to identify how multiple gene expression changes interact with one another to impact product levels. Synthetic biology approaches have focused on fine-tuning gene expression through biopart combinations and evaluating the biosynthetic activity of various combinations of enzyme homologs.

Whole-cell: identification and modularization of genetic targets for pathway optimization

Identification of genes whose modulation can improve product titers is an often successful strategy for rational metabolic engineering in the complicated and sometimes poorly defined regulatory networks of metabolism [29]. For example, to identify downregulation targets for a microbial cell factory, a screening strategy was recently developed by using small regulatory RNAs (sRNAs) [30]. A genome-scale synthetic sRNA library was introduced into an Escherichia coli WCB that converts intracellular malonyl-CoA levels into a fluorescent signal [31]. This system enabled the analysis of how reduced expression of specific genes contributed to the accumulation of malonyl-CoA, a precursor to various valuable compounds.

Recent advances in CRISPR-based approaches have further deepened insights into metabolic engineering with WCBs (Figure 2). In particular, CRISPRi, which represses gene expression, has been applied for screening target genes via genome-wide RNA libraries. For example, knockout targets to boost porphyrin synthesis in Rhodobacter sphaeroides were identified with this approach, as were knockdown targets to enhance recombinant protein expression in Bacillus subtilis [32,33]. By tailoring the composition of RNA libraries to specific engineering goals, one can achieve selective identification of genes aligned with specific objectives. For example, focusing the library on transporter genes enabled the identification of transporters involved in nutrient uptake and product export [34]. Similarly, libraries designed around specific genomic loci have been used to identify optimal gene integration sites [35].

After gene targets are identified, WCBs can be used to facilitate modulation of expression levels, with CRISPR again being a valuable tool (Figure 2). A titratable sgRNA system with CRISPRi was used to repress two different genes at different levels, leading to a 40.6% increase in pcoumaric acid yield [36]. In another study, crRNAs with varying repression strengths were designed to inhibit three genes in a competing pathway branch of GlcN6P synthesis [37]. Also, gene activation using CRISPRa has been applied to the development of orthogonal promoters with different strengths [38]. These promoter combinations were then used for biosynthetic profiling toward target compound production. Additionally, CRISPR-mediated base editors and Cre/Lox recombination systems have been employed to introduce multiplexed mutations into bioparts with ongoing efforts to establish optimal expression configurations [39–41].

Cell-free: mining and characterization of regulatory elements

The frequent need in metabolic engineering for precise control of gene expression to maximize cofactor and flux balances highlights the importance of identifying bioparts that have predictable expression levels. Establishing a broad collection of bioparts with varying strengths has become a de facto requirement for many metabolic engineering efforts. The short DBTL cycles of cell-free biosensors make them well-suited for high-throughput screening of diverse biopart combinations.

Cell-free systems have been created from lysates of widely used metabolic engineering chassis organisms including E. coli, B. subtilis, and Vibrio natriegens to enable biopart mining across the tree of life (Figure 2) [42–44]. These platforms facilitated the screening of promoter and RBS libraries, resulting in the identification of bioparts enabling diverse expression levels. Similar systems have also been developed using Corynebacterium glutamicum and Pseudomonas putida, enabling rapid experimental validation of expression strength predicted by the RBS Calculator [45,46].

A defining characteristic of cell-free biosensors is their independence from cell growth, which is especially beneficial for mining bioparts from nonmodel organisms. For instance, although species such as Streptomyces, Clostridium, and Synechocystis have potential biosynthetic advantages for specific metabolic products, systematic development of bioparts in these organisms has been limited by slow growth, low transformation efficiency, and challenges in genetic manipulation of these organisms [47–49]. The development of cell-free systems and their integration with biosensors significantly reduced the time required for biopart prototyping in these organisms. This strategy has also been applied to non-canonical microbial hosts, broadening the scope of usable chassis organisms [50–53].

Prototyping of bioparts and their combinations via cell-free systems has also been implemented at a pathway level (Figure 2). A representative example is the in vitro prototyping and rapid optimization of biosynthetic enzymes (iPROBE), which assembles multiple enzyme homologs in a cell-free environment to identify the most efficient combinations [54–56]. These optimized enzyme sets are then transferred to in vivo systems such as Clostridium that are otherwise challenging to test systematically due to their slow growth. This strategy has yielded high efficiencies across multiple biosynthetic pathways while maintaining a strong correlation between cell-free and in vivo expression profiles. While iPROBE employed High Performance Liquid Chromatography to evaluate pathway efficiency, integrating cell-free biosensors for small molecules or even proteins into such workflows could further accelerate screening [57]. Recent studies demonstrated this potential by integrating cell-free biosensors into cell-free prototyping systems for the production of curcumin and nicotinamide mononucleotide [58, 59].

Advances in biofoundries and future directions

Advances in robotics that have broadly impacted science have also begun to influence biosensor-driven metabolic engineering [60]. Many recent efforts have focused on the establishment of biofoundries, which are integrated facilities that automate biological experiments [61]. Strain engineering has been fully automated in some of these biofoundries, significantly reducing labor requirements in molecular biology workflows [62,63]. Biofoundries have also enabled automated screening of combinatorial libraries without human intervention [64]. Coupling these platforms with cell-free biosensors, which allow easy parameter adjustments and the use of linear expression templates, has further accelerated high-throughput testing [65–67].

More recently, self-driving laboratories combining biofoundries and AI have emerged, enabling fully automated enzyme design and screening processes. For example, a tRNA synthetase variant with 2.4-fold improved activity was developed within 10 days in a fully automated whole-cell system [68]. Separately, an AI-powered biofoundry produced a 90-fold improvement in substrate specificity and a 16-fold increase in catalytic activity of a halide methyltransferase within four weeks [69]. The self-driving approach has also been extended to a cell-free system, where fully automated development of glycoside hydrolase thermostability was achieved [70].

As biofoundries become increasingly sophisticated, they are likely to address key challenges faced in biosensor-based metabolic engineering [71]. Discovery and development of TFs for metabolite detection remain two critical bottlenecks in biosensor construction. Combining machine learning-based design with de novo protein synthesis to construct TF libraries, followed by automated screening, is expected to enable rapid identification of useful biosensor components.

Expanding TF libraries could also facilitate the development of biosensors for byproduct detection. Constructing multiple biosensors for byproducts or intermediates would enable an efficient downstream process. Furthermore, advances in biosensors with broader dynamic ranges are expected from these efforts [72]. Sensors capable of linear detection over wide concentration ranges could replace chromatography-based analysis, providing real-time monitoring solutions. While some technologies for real-time monitoring of key cofactors and enzyme expression levels have been developed [73–76], broader application of such tools would allow dynamic optimization of production processes, adjusting operating conditions in real-time.

Despite their own respective limitations, whole-cell and cell-free biosensors have successfully supported metabolic engineering by enabling enzyme library screening, identification of regulatory enzyme targets, and prototyping of diverse bioparts. Leveraging these systems through advanced biofoundries is expected to drive transformative progress in metabolic engineering.
