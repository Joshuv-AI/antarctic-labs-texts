1
Introduction

The notion of divisible codes was first introduced by Ward
[
18
]
in 1981. Divisible codes are linear codes whose codeword weights share a common divisor larger than one. A linear code
C
is called
\Delta
-divisible
if the weights
\mathrm{wt}(c)
of all codewords
c\in C
are divisible by some positive integer
\Delta
. In such a case, we call
\Delta
a
\mathit{divisor}
of
C
. There is an extensive survey on divisible codes by Kurz
[
10
]
(We will use some results from
[
10
]
later). In
[
18
]
, Ward proved a fundamental theorem regarding divisible codes, which states that any
\Delta
-divisible code
C
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
\gcd(\Delta,q)=1
is equivalent to a
\Delta
-fold repetition of some code
C^{\prime}
, supplemented by sufficiently many 0 coordinates. Therefore,
p
-power divisible codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
will be of particular interest, where
p
is the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(we will write
q=p^{f}
throughout this paper). Many structural codes such as simplex codes, cyclic codes, and generalized Reed-Muller codes exhibit
good divisibility
, meaning that all weights are divisible by a “large”
p
-power; see
[
1
,
19
,
21
]
. Griesmer codes, as a special class of length-optimal codes including the aforementioned simplex codes, also seem to exhibit good divisibility.

We first recall the Griesmer bound. Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
code. In 1960, Griesmer
[
7
]
proved that for binary linear codes (i.e.,
q=2
), we have
n\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lceil\frac{d}{2^{i}}\right\rceil
. Later, in 1965, Solomon and Stiffler
[
13
]
generalized this result, leading to the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(The Griesmer Bound
[
7
,
13
]
)
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
code. Then

n\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{i}}\right\rceil.

The bound in Theorem
1.1
will be called
the Griesmer bound
and the quantity on the right-hand side of the above inequality will be denoted by
g_{q}(k,d)
; that is,
g_{q}(k,d):=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{i}}\right\rceil
. Any
[n,k,d]_{q}
code
C
with code length meeting the Griesmer bound will be called a
Griesmer code
.

Besides the simplex codes, there are many other examples of Griesmer codes, such as maximum distance separable (MDS) linear codes, the
1^{\text{st}}
order Reed-Muller codes and Belov codes (see
[
2
,
6
]
). We shall present two examples of Griesmer codes arising from finite geometry as follows.

Example 1.2
.

A
unital
in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q^{2})
is a set
\mathcal{U}
of
q^{3}+1
points such that every line meets
\mathcal{U}
in 1 or
q+1
points. Let
G
be a
3\times(q^{3}+1)
matrix with columns
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{q^{3}+1}
such that
\mathcal{U}:=\{\left\langle g_{1}\right\rangle,\left\langle g_{2}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle g_{q^{3}+1}\right\rangle\}
is a unital in
\mathrm{PG}(2,q^{2})
. Then the linear code
C
with
G
as a generator matrix is a
[q^{3}+1,3,q^{3}-q]_{q^{2}}
Griesmer code. Note that
C
is actually a two-weight code with nonzero weights
q^{3}-q
and
q^{3}
.

Example 1.3
.

An
ovoid
in
\mathrm{PG}(3,q)
is a set
\mathcal{O}
of
q^{2}+1
points such that no three of them are collinear and every hyperplane meets
\mathcal{O}
in 1 or
q+1
points. Let
G
be a
4\times(q^{2}+1)
matrix with columns
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{q^{2}+1}
such that
\mathcal{O}:=\{\left\langle g_{1}\right\rangle,\left\langle g_{2}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle g_{q^{2}+1}\right\rangle\}
is an ovoid in
\mathrm{PG}(3,q)
. Then the linear code
C
with
G
as a generator matrix is a
[q^{2}+1,4,q^{2}-q]_{q}
Griesmer code. Note that
C
is in fact a two-weight code with nonzero weights
q^{2}-q
and
q^{2}
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code. In 1984, Dodunekov and Manev
[
5
]
first showed that in the case when
q=2
, if
2^{e}|d
, then
2^{e}|\mathrm{wt}(c)
for all
c\in C
. In 1998, Ward proved the following divisibility result on Griesmer codes.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem 1 in
[
20
]
)
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{p}(k,d),k,d]_{p}
Griesmer code where
p
is a prime. If
p^{e}|d
, then
p^{e}|\mathrm{wt}(c)
for all
c\in C
.

The above theorem shows that Griesmer codes over the prime field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
have a good divisibility property. However, when the field size is
q=p^{f}

(f>1)
, in general, we cannot hope for a divisibility result as good as in the case when
q=p
; that is, if
C
is a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and
q^{e}|d
,
q^{e}
may not divide
\mathrm{wt}(c)
for all
c\in C
. For example, the
hexacode

H
(see
[
4
]
for definition) is a
[6,3,4]_{4}
code containing 45 codewords of weight 4, 18 codewords of weight 6 and the zero codeword. The divisor of
H
is 2 but not 4 although
4|d=4
. Despite this obstruction, Ward proved further divisibility results on Griesmer codes.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code.

1.

If
q|d
, then
C
is
p
-divisible.

2.

In the case when
q=4
, if
2^{e}|d
, then
2^{e-1}
is a divisor of
C
.

Ward then proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.6

(Conjecture 10 in
[
21
]
)
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. Suppose that
p^{e}|d
and
p^{e}\geq q
. Then
p^{e+1}/q
is a divisor of
C
.

In Section
5
, we will prove Theorem
1.7
using the
p
-adic algebraic method, extending Theorem 1 of
[
20
]
and Proposition 13 of
[
21
]
. In Section
6
, we will prove Theorem
1.8
from the geometric point of view.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. If
q^{e}|d
, then
p^{e}|\mathrm{wt}(c)
for all
c\in C
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. If
p^{e}|d
, then
\left\lceil p^{e-(f-1)(q-2)}\right\rceil
is a divisor of
C
.

Note that Conjecture
1.6
and Theorem
1.7
can be rephrased as follows.

Conjecture
1.6
′
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. If
p^{e}|d
, then
\left\lceil p^{e-(f-1)}\right\rceil
is a divisor of
C
.

Theorem
1.7
′
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. If
p^{e}|d
, then
p^{\lfloor e/f\rfloor}|\mathrm{wt}(c)
for all
c\in C
.

The above reformulation of Conjecture
1.6
makes it clear that Theorem
1.8
can be viewed as a similar, but weaker result compared with Conjecture
1.6
. These results provide new information about Griesmer codes, which will be helpful in searching for Griesmer codes or proving the nonexistence of Griesmer codes with certain parameters. For nonexistence proofs of certain Griesmer codes, see, e.g.
[
22
,
12
,
8
]
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
and Section
3
, we will give some preliminaries and present two proofs of the Griesmer bound, both of which will be useful later. In Section
4
, we will show that any
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code with
q^{e}|d
has a basis consisting of
k
codewords such that the first
\min\{e+1,k\}
of them span a Griesmer subcode with constant weight
d
and any
k-1
of them span a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode. In Section
5
, using the
p
-adic algebraic method together with this basis, we apply a divisibility criterion given by Ward
[
19
]
to prove Theorem
1.7
. Based on Theorem
1.7
, again using the basis mentioned above, we prove Theorem
1.8
from the geometric point of view.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Residual Codes, Projected Codes and Shortened Subcodes

First, we review some basic definitions.

The
support
of a word
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, denoted by
\mathrm{supp}(c)
, is the set of nonzero coordinates of
c
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(c):=\{i\mid c_{i}\neq 0\}
.

The
residual code
of
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with respect to a word
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
, is the code obtained by puncturing
C
at
\mathrm{supp}(a)
. The codeword
\mathrm{res}(c,a)\in\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
denotes the codeword obtained by puncturing
c\in C
at
\mathrm{supp}(a)
. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}(C,a)

\displaystyle=\{(c_{j})_{j\notin\mathrm{supp}(a)}\mid c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in C\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{res}(c,a)

\displaystyle=(c_{j})_{j\notin\mathrm{supp}(a)},\quad\forall c\in C.

The
projected code
of
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with respect to a word
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Proj}(C,a)
, is the code obtained by projecting
C
at
\mathrm{supp}(a)
. The codeword
\mathrm{proj}(c,a)\in\mathrm{Proj}(C,a)
denotes the codeword obtained by projecting
c\in C
at
\mathrm{supp}(a)
. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}(C,a)

\displaystyle=\{(c_{j})_{j\in\mathrm{supp}(a)}\mid c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in C\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{proj}(c,a)

\displaystyle=(c_{j})_{j\in\mathrm{supp}(a)},\quad\forall c\in C

For
1\leq i\leq n
, the
shortened subcode

C_{i}
is the subcode of
C
consisting of codewords in
C
such that the
i
-th coordinate is 0, i.e.,

\displaystyle C_{i}:=\{c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in C\mid c_{i}=0\}.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that residual codes, projected codes and shortened subcodes of linear codes are still linear codes.

2.2
The Geometric Setting

The geometric approach towards linear codes embodies geometric intuition and can allow us to prove some interesting results. It is worth noting that Landjev
[
11
]
used the polynomial method from the geometric point of view to reprove Theorem
1.4
. We will use the geometric approach to prove Theorem
1.8
. First, we recall the geometric setting in the classical projective space
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
below.

Let
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
. The classical projective space
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
is defined as the geometry in which the point set
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all 1-dimensional subspaces of
V
, the line set
\mathcal{L}
is the set of all 2-dimensional subspaces of
V
, …, the set
\mathcal{H}
of hyperplanes is the set of all
(k-1)
-dimensional subspaces of
V
, where the incidence relation is the natural inclusion of subspaces.

Definition 2.2
.

A
multiset

\mathcal{M}
(of points) in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
is a collection of points in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
with multiplicity.

We may regard vectors as points if there is no ambiguity. Sometimes it is advantageous to use the definition of a multiset
\mathcal{M}
of points in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

A
multiset

\mathcal{M}
(of points) in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
is a mapping
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

For each point
P
, the number
\mathcal{M}(P)
is the multiplicity of the point
P
in the multiset
\mathcal{M}
. If
\mathcal{M}(P)\in\{0,1\}
for all points
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then
\mathcal{M}
is simply a set. We can also add two multisets
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
(regard them as functions) to obtain a multiset
\mathcal{M}_{1}+\mathcal{M}_{2}
. For any subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}
, we denote
\mathcal{M}(S):=\sum_{P\in S}\mathcal{M}(P)
and the multiset
\mathcal{M}|_{S}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}|_{S}(P):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}(P),&\text{if }P\in S,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
C
be an
[n,k]_{q}
code and
G
be a generator matrix of
C
. The
effective length
of the linear code
C
, denoted by
n(C)
, is the number of nonzero coordinates of
C
(or the number of nonzero columns of its generator matrix
G
). If
G
contains no zero columns, then we will say that
C
has full length. In such a situation, we may identify
C
with the multiset
\mathcal{M}
corresponding to all the columns of a generator matrix
G
of
C
since different generator matrices of
C
will give rise to the “same” multiset up to equivalence. Formally speaking, two multisets in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
are said to be
projectively equivalent
if they are associated with linear codes that are semilinearly isomorphic. We will say that the multiset
\mathcal{M}
is
\Delta
-divisible when
C
is
\Delta
-divisible. We will write
\mathcal{M}_{C}
to indicate the multiset corresponding to a linear code
C
, or simply
\mathcal{M}
when
C
is clear from the context. Throughout, we always assume that an
[n,k,d]_{q}
code
C
has full length.

Under the assumptions stated above, we can utilize this geometric framework to express the weight
\mathrm{wt}(c)
of a codeword
c=aG
, where
a
is a row vector, in a linear code
C
with a generator matrix
G
in the following way.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k]_{q}
code with a
k\times n
generator matrix
G
. Then any codeword
c\in C
can be uniquely written as
c=aG
for an
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
. Moreover, for
a=0
,
c
is the zero codeword and so
\mathrm{wt}(c)=0
; for any nonzero
a
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{wt}(c)=n-\mathcal{M}(H_{a}),

where
\mathcal{M}
is the multiset corresponding to the columns of
G
and
H_{a}:=a^{\perp}
is the hyperplane consisting of vectors that are orthogonal to
a
.

Geometrically, the weight of
c=aG\in C
is equal to the number of points of
\mathcal{M}
not in
H_{a}
. This proposition establishes the connection between the weights of codewords in a linear code
C
, and the intersection sizes of hyperplanes with the multiset corresponding to a generator matrix
G
of
C
. As a corollary, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k]_{q}
code. Then
C
is
\Delta
-divisible if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{C}(H)\equiv n\pmod{\Delta}

for all hyperplanes
H\in\mathcal{H}
in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
.

3
Two Proofs of the Griesmer Bound

The Griesmer bound was proved by Griesmer in the case when
q=2
, by Solomon and Stiffler for general prime powers
q
. In order to keep the paper self-contained, we will give two proofs for the Griesmer bound in this section.

3.1
The First Proof of the Griesmer Bound

We first prove a lemma about the residual code
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
where
a
is a minimum weight codeword of
C
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
code with
k\geq 2
and
a
be a minimum weight codeword of
C
. Then
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
is an
[n-d,k-1,d^{\prime}]_{q}
code with
d^{\prime}\geq\left\lceil d/q\right\rceil
.

Proof.

First we claim that
\dim\mathrm{Res}(C,a)=k-1
. Clearly
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
is a linear code; so it makes sense to talk about its dimension. Let
P_{a}
be the puncturing map

\displaystyle P_{a}:C

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathrm{Res}(C,a)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\longmapsto P_{a}(c):=\mathrm{res}(c,a)

It is easy to see that
P_{a}
is a linear map. Since
a
is a minimum weight codeword, we have
\mathrm{ker}(P_{a})=\left\langle a\right\rangle
, the 1-dimensional subspace spanned by
a
. Thus,
\dim\mathrm{Res}(C,a)=k-1
.

Next, we prove
d^{\prime}\geq\left\lceil d/q\right\rceil
. Let
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in C
be a codeword which is not a multiple of
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. Then
\mathrm{res}(b,a)\neq 0
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}:=\mathbb{F}_{q}\backslash\{0\}
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we define

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}:=\left|\left\{i:a_{i}=\alpha b_{i}\neq 0\right\}\right|,\text{ where }\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}.

Then we have

\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}{A_{\alpha}}=\mathrm{wt}(b).

(1)

Note that
a-\alpha b\in C
, we have

\mathrm{wt}(a-\alpha b)=d-A_{\alpha}+\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))\geq d,

from which it follows that

A_{\alpha}\leq\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a)).

(2)

Combining (
1
) with (
2
), we obtain

\mathrm{wt}(b)\leq q\cdot\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a)).

(3)

Since
\mathrm{wt}(b)\geq d
, we have
q\cdot\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))\geq d
and so
\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))\geq\left\lceil d/q\right\rceil
.
∎

The First Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We proceed by induction on the dimension
k
. Clearly the bound holds for
k=1
. Assume that the conclusion holds for linear codes of dimension less than
k
. Applying the induction hypothesis and Lemma
3.1
on
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
, we have

\displaystyle n-d\geq\sum_{i=0}^{(k-1)-1}\left\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{q^{i}}\right\rceil\geq\sum_{i=0}^{(k-1)-1}\left\lceil\frac{\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil}{q^{i}}\right\rceil=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{i}}\right\rceil.

The proof is now complete.
∎

3.2
The Second Proof of the Griesmer Bound

By
\gamma(\mathcal{M})
we denote the maximum point multiplicity of a multiset of points
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
. A linear code
C
is called
projective
if
\gamma(\mathcal{M}_{C})=1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
code and
\mathcal{M}
be the corresponding multiset of
C
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathcal{M})\geq\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{k-1}}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
corresponds to a generator matrix
G=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
of
C
where
g_{i}=(g_{1,i},\ldots,g_{k,i})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\backslash\{0\}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\left\langle g_{1}\right\rangle\in\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
has the maximum multiplicity. Suppose
g_{\ell,1}\neq 0
for some
\ell
since one of
g_{i,1}

(i=1,\ldots,k)
must be nonzero. Note that the
\ell
-th row of
G
has weight
\mathrm{wt}(g_{\ell,1},\ldots,g_{\ell,n})\geq d
. Therefore, among the columns indexed by
\mathrm{supp}(g_{\ell,1},\ldots,g_{\ell,n})
, there are at least
\left\lceil\mathrm{wt}(g_{\ell,1},\ldots,g_{\ell,n})/q^{k-1}\right\rceil\geq\left\lceil d/q^{k-1}\right\rceil
columns of
G
which are the same in
\mathrm{PG}(k-1,q)
by the pigeonhole principle because there are
q^{k-1}
different points whose
\ell
-th coordinate is nonzero. The proof is now complete.
∎

The Second Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We again use induction on the dimension
k
. Clearly, the bound holds for
k=1
. Assume that the conclusion holds for linear codes of dimension less than
k
. Suppose that
g_{i_{1}},\ldots,g_{i_{\gamma(\mathcal{M})}}
are the same up to a scalar factor, i.e.,
\left\langle g_{i_{1}}\right\rangle=\cdots=\langle g_{i_{\gamma(\mathcal{M})}}\rangle=:\left\langle g\right\rangle
. Consider the shortened subcode
C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}
of
C
as follows:

\displaystyle C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}:=\left\{c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in C:c_{i}=0,\forall i\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\gamma(\mathcal{M})}\}\right\}.

Since
C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}\subsetneqq C
,
\dim C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}\leq k-1
. Moreover, since
\left\langle g_{i_{1}}\right\rangle=\cdots=\langle g_{i_{\gamma(\mathcal{M})}}\rangle
, the dimension decreases by 1 and so
\dim C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}=k-1
. Note that
C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}
is an
\left[n,k-1,d^{\prime}\right]_{q}
code with
n(C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle})=n-\gamma(\mathcal{M})
and
d^{\prime}\geq d
. By induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle n-\gamma(\mathcal{M})\geq\sum_{i=0}^{(k-1)-1}\left\lceil d^{\prime}/q^{i}\right\rceil\geq\sum_{i=0}^{(k-1)-1}\left\lceil d/q^{i}\right\rceil

and by Lemma
3.2
,
\gamma(\mathcal{M})\geq\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{k-1}}\right\rceil
, then we see that

\displaystyle n\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\left\lceil d/q^{i}\right\rceil+\gamma(\mathcal{M})\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\left\lceil d/q^{i}\right\rceil+\left\lceil d/q^{k-1}\right\rceil=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lceil d/q^{i}\right\rceil,

which proves the case when the dimension is
k
. Now the proof is complete.
∎

From these two proofs of the Griesmer bound, we derive the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
k\geq 2
. Then

1.

For any codeword
a
with minimum weight
\mathrm{wt}(a)=d
, the residual code of
C
with respect to
a
,
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
is a
\left[g_{q}\left(k-1,\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil\right),k-1,\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil\right]_{q}
Griesmer code.

2.

The shortened subcode
C_{\left\langle g\right\rangle}
is a
\left[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d\right]_{q}
Griesmer code, where
\left\langle g\right\rangle\in\mathcal{M}_{C}
is any point attaining the maximum point multiplicity, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{C}(\left\langle g\right\rangle)=\gamma(\mathcal{M}_{C})
.

As a corollary, we obtain the maximum point multiplicity of Griesmer codes.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code. Then
\gamma(\mathcal{M}_{C})=\left\lceil d/q^{k-1}\right\rceil
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code. Then
C
is projective if and only if
d\leq q^{k-1}
.

4
A Basis for Griesmer Codes

Before proceeding to the proofs of Theorem
1.7
and Theorem
1.8
, we shall show that any
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code with
q^{e}|d
has a basis (which we implicitly consider as ordered) consisting of
k
codewords such that the first
\min\{e+1,k\}
of them span a Griesmer subcode with constant weight
d
and any
(k-1)
of them span a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode. We will gather here some related results first.

Proposition 4.1

(Corollary 5 in
[
20
]
)
.

Suppose that
C
is a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and that
q^{k-1}|d
. Then all nonzero codeword of
C
have weight
d
.

We remark here that constant weight linear codes are projectively equivalent to a replication of Hamming duals, as shown in
[
3
]
, with a new proof provided in
[
17
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
be the residual code of
C
with respect to a minimum weight codeword
a\in C
. Let
P_{a}
be the puncturing map as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
and
c^{\prime}
be a minimum weight codeword in
\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
.

1.

If
q|d
, then any preimage of
c^{\prime}
under
P_{a}
must be a minimum weight codeword in
C
. In other words, the number of the preimages of
c^{\prime}
with minimum weight
d
is
q
.

2.

If
q\nmid d
, then there exists a preimage of
c^{\prime}
having minimum weight
d
in
C
, moreover, the number of these preimages is at least
d+q-\left\lceil d/q\right\rceil q
.

In summary, there always exists a preimage of
c^{\prime}
having minimum weight
d
in
C
.

Proof.

There are a total of
q
preimages of
c^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
as seen in the proof of Lemma
3.1
. Now we take an arbitrary preimage
b\in C
of
c^{\prime}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{res}(b,a)=c^{\prime}
.

1.

When
q|d
: According to (
3
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{wt}(b)\leq q\cdot\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))=q\cdot\mathrm{wt}(c^{\prime})=q\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil=q\cdot\frac{d}{q}=d.

Thus,
\mathrm{wt}(b)=d
. This proves the first part.

2.

When
q\nmid d
: Write
d=d_{1}q+d_{0}
where
1\leq d_{0}\leq q-1
. Then
\mathrm{wt}(c^{\prime})=\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil=d_{1}+1
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.1
,
A_{\alpha}\leq d_{1}+1
and if there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
A_{\alpha}=d_{1}+1
, then
\mathrm{wt}(b-\alpha a)=d
. Now suppose that
A_{\alpha}\leq d_{1}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. According to (
1
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{wt}(b)=\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}{A_{\alpha}}\leq d_{1}+1+(q-1)d_{1}=d_{1}q+1.

On the other hand,
\mathrm{wt}(b)\geq d=d_{1}q+d_{0}\geq d_{1}q+1
, so
\mathrm{wt}(b)=d
and
d=d_{1}q+1
in this case. Now we know that there exists a preimage
b\in C
of
c^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{wt}(b)=d
. We consider (
1
) again,

\displaystyle\mathrm{wt}(b)=\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a))+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}{A_{\alpha}}=d_{1}+1+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}{A_{\alpha}}=d_{1}q+d_{0}

by
A_{\alpha}\leq d_{1}+1
we see that there are at least
d_{0}-1
elements
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
such that
A_{\alpha}=d_{1}+1
. Hence, there are at least
d_{0}
preimages of
c^{\prime}
with minimum weight
d
.

The proof is now complete.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The first part of Proposition
4.2
can also be proved by using Proposition
4.1
with
C=\left\langle a,b\right\rangle
and
k=2
.

Proposition 4.4

(c.f. Proposition 4 in
[
20
]
)
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code. Then for any codeword
a\in C
with minimum weight
\mathrm{wt}(a)=d
, there is a supplementary
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode
C^{\prime}
, i.e.,
\left\langle a\right\rangle\cap C^{\prime}=0
and
\left\langle a\right\rangle+C^{\prime}=C
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and
C^{\prime}
be a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode of
C
which is supplementary to a minimum weight codeword

. Then the puncturing map
P_{a}:C\to\mathrm{Res}(C,a)
restricted to
C^{\prime}
is a bijection.

Proof.

Since
\dim C^{\prime}=\dim\mathrm{Res}(C,a)=k-1
, it suffices to show that
\ker(P_{a}|_{C^{\prime}})=\{0\}
. This can be seen by noticing that
\ker(P_{a}|_{C^{\prime}})=C^{\prime}\cap\left\langle a\right\rangle=\{0\}
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and
q^{e}|d
. Then there exists a generator matrix
G
of
C
such that the first
\min\{e+1,k\}
rows of
G
span a Griesmer subcode with constant weight
d
. Moreover, any
k-1
rows of
G
generate a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.4
, we can choose a generator matrix
G
of
C
such that the first row
a_{1}
has minimum weight
d
, moreover, the last
k-1
rows of
G
span a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode
C^{\prime}
that is supplementary to the first row
a_{1}
of
G
. Note that at this moment we just fix the first row
a_{1}
for
G
so the last
k-1
rows still need to be determined and will be determined later. Since the effective lengths of
C
and
C^{\prime}
are respectively
n=g_{q}(k,d)
and
n^{\prime}=g_{q}(k-1,d)
, then there are exactly

\displaystyle g_{q}(k,d)-g_{q}(k-1,d)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{i}}\right\rceil-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-2}\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{i}}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{k-1}}\right\rceil

columns of
G
having the form
(\alpha,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
where
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

Now we consider the residual code of
C
with respect to the first row
a_{1}
, namely
\mathrm{Res}(C,a_{1})
. By Theorem
3.3
, we know that
\mathrm{Res}(C,a_{1})
is a
\left[g_{q}\left(k-1,\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil\right),k-1,\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil\right]_{q}
Griesmer code. For this residual code
\mathrm{Res}(C,a_{1})
, by Proposition
4.2
, there exists a codeword
b\in C
such that
\mathrm{wt}(\mathrm{res}(b,a_{1}))=\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil
and then we apply the above operation again so that there are exactly

\displaystyle g_{q}\left(k-1,\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil\right)-g_{q}\left(k-2,\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1-1}\left\lceil\frac{\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil}{q^{i}}\right\rceil-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-2-1}\left\lceil\frac{\left\lceil\frac{d}{q}\right\rceil}{q^{i}}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{k-1}}\right\rceil

columns of
G
having the form
(0,\beta,\ldots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
where
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Moreover, Lemma
4.5
guarantees the existence of some
a_{2}\in C^{\prime}
that satisfies the same key properties as
b
does in the above construction. Iterating this process, we can produce a basis
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
of
C
to obtain a generator matrix
G
with exactly
\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{k-1}}\right\rceil
columns which are multiple of
e_{j}=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
with
1
in the
j
-th place and 0 in other places for all
j=1,\ldots,k
. Furthermore, if
e\geq k-1
, by Proposition
4.1
,
C
is a constant weight code; if
e<k
, by construction, we see that the effective length of the subcode
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{e+1}\}
is
g_{q}(e+1,d)
. Therefore,
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{e+1}\}
is a
[g_{q}(e+1,d),e+1,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and so by Proposition
4.1
, it is a constant weight subcode of
C
.

Finally, we show that any
k-1
rows of
G
span a Griesmer subcode. Let
S
be a set of some
k-1
rows of
G
and
D
be the subcode spanned by
S
. If
S
contains the first row of
G
, then the minimum weight of
D
is
d
. Moreover,
D
has effective length
n-\left\lceil\frac{d}{q^{k-1}}\right\rceil=g_{q}(k-1,d)
by the above construction, and hence this code
D
must be a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode of
C
. If
D
is spanned by the last
k-1
rows of
G
, then
D
is exactly the supplementary Griesmer subcode
C^{\prime}
. We are done.
∎

For a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code

, we may call the corresponding multiset
\mathcal{M}_{C}
a
Griesmer multiset
and
\gamma(\mathcal{M}_{C})=\left\lceil d/q^{k-1}\right\rceil
. In
[
21
]
, a point
g
of a Griesmer multiset with the maximum point multiplicity is called an

. From the proof of Theorem
4.6
, we see that there are at least
k
endpoints of an
[n,k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code. The following proposition gives a better lower bound for the number of endpoints of a Griesmer code.

Proposition 4.7

(Lemma 11 in
[
21
]
)
.

Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code. Then the number of endpoints of

is at least
g_{q}(k,t)
, where
t=d-(\left\lceil d/q^{k-1}\right\rceil-1)q^{k-1}
.

5
The Algebraic Proof of
Theorem
1.7

5.1
Ward’s Divisibility Criterion

In 1990, Ward developed the
weight polarization
method
[
19
]
, and, as an application, gave a new proof of Ax’s theorem
[
1
]
. In 1998, Ward empolyed this technique to prove Theorem
1.4
(
q=p
). However, it seems difficult to apply this method when
q=p^{f}
with
f\geq 2
. One of the difficulties lies in analyzing the
p
-adic valuation of a sum involving certain binomial coefficients. To overcome this difficulty, we compute some specific
p
-adic valuations. More precisely, the sum mentioned above has the following form:

\displaystyle c(r,s;1):=\binom{rq}{s}+\binom{rq}{(q-1)+s}+\cdots+\binom{rq}{a(q-1)+s}+\cdots=\sum_{i\equiv s\pmod{q-1}}\binom{rq}{i},

where
1\leq r,s\leq q-1
and the above summation is in fact a finite sum because the binomial coefficient
\binom{rq}{a(q-1)+s}
will be 0 if
a\geq q
. The summation of a similar form has been extensively studied, see
[
14
,
16
,
15
]
.

Below, we will give a brief overview of weight polarization in the context of
p
-adic numbers.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be the ring of
p
-adic integers and
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
be the field of
p
-adic numbers. For
q=p^{f}
,
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
has a unique unramified extension
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi_{q-1})
whose residue class field is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
\xi_{q-1}
is a primitive
(q-1)
-st root of unity. Let
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]
be the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi_{q-1})
. Note that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]
is a local ring and the maximal ideal of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]
is
(p)
.
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(\xi_{q-1})
is the splitting field of
X^{q}-X
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]
contains the full group
U_{q-1}
of
(q-1)
-st roots of unity. Let
U=U_{q-1}\cup\{0\}
.

We have a canonical map

\varphi:\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]/(p)\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}.

Note that
\varphi
is a bijection when restricted to
U
. For each
x
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]
, there is a unique
T(x)\in U
satisfying

\displaystyle x\equiv T(x)\pmod{p}.

The element
T(x)
is called the
Teichmüller representative
of
x
, and the set
U
consists of Teichmüller representatives. When there is no ambiguity, if
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we also use
T(x)
to denote the
Teichmüller lift
of
x
, namely the element in
U
such that
\varphi(T(x))=x
. For a vector
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, let
T(c)
be the Teichmüller lift of
c
:

\displaystyle T(c)=(T(c_{1}),\ldots,T(c_{n})).

If
R
is any commutative ring, and
a_{i}\in R^{n}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
, the component-wise product (sometimes called Schur product or Hadamard product) of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}
will be denoted by
a_{1}\circ\cdots\circ a_{r}
. For
a\in R^{n}
, we will write
\overbrace{a\circ\cdots\circ a}^{r}
as
a^{\circ(r)}
, and use
\sigma(a)
to denote the sum of the components of
a
. By
S_{p}(x)
we denote the digit sum of
p
-adic expansion of
x\in\mathbb{N}
. By
\nu_{p}(x)
we denote the
p
-adic valuation of
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\xi_{q-1}]
. The divisibility criterion we will employ is Theorem 5.3 of
[
19
]
:

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem 5.3 in
[
19
]
)
.

Let
C
be a linear code in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with additive spanning set
B
. Then
p^{e}
is a divisor of

if and only if

\displaystyle e\leq\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}S_{p}(r_{i})-f+\nu_{p}\left(\sigma\left(T(b_{1})^{\circ(r_{1})}\circ\cdots\circ T(b_{m})^{\circ(r_{m})}\right)\right)

for all
M=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
and all choices of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
in
B
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code and
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
be a basis as constructed in Theorem
4.6
, i.e.,
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{e+1}\}
is a constant weight subcode and any
k-1
of them span a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode. Note that
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
is a
[g_{q}(2,d),2,d]_{q}
subcode of
C
. If
q|d
, then by Proposition
4.1
,
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
is a constant weight code. Let

\displaystyle B=\{\lambda a\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\}\cup\{\lambda a_{2}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\}\cup\cdots\cup\{\lambda a_{k}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\}

where
a=a_{1}+\alpha a_{2}\in C\backslash\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then

is an additive spanning set of
C
. Moreover, if
q|d
, we have
\mathrm{wt}(a)=d
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let
A_{i}=T(a_{i})
, and
\alpha^{\prime}=T(\alpha)
for short. If we consider
B
as the additive spanning set of
C
, then
p^{e}
is a divisor of
C
if and only if

p^{e-\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}S_{p}(r_{i})+f}\;\big|\;\sigma\left(T(A_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}A_{2})^{\circ(r_{1})}\circ A_{2}^{\circ(r_{2})}\circ\cdots\circ A_{k}^{\circ(r_{k})}\right)

(4)

for all

with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, where all scalar multiples
\lambda
’s are omitted since they do not change the

-adic valuation.

First of all, we demonstrate how to expand the Schur product in (
4
), which can be found in
[
19
]
. Let
1\leq r\leq q-1
and
X,Y\in U=U_{q-1}\cup\{0\}
. After expanding
(X+Y)^{rq^{n}}
with
X^{q}=X
and

, we obtain the following equality:

(X+Y)^{rq^{n}}-X^{r}-Y^{r}=\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}c(r,k;n)X^{q-1+r-k}Y^{k},

where

is a sum of some binomial coefficients. Let

, we will see that

\displaystyle T(X+Y)^{r}=X^{r}+Y^{r}+\sum_{k=1}^{q-1}c(r,k)X^{q-1+r-k}Y^{k},

where

, with the limit taken in the field of

Proposition 5.2

(c.f. Proposition 8 in
[

]
)
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and let

,

. Then for

,

\displaystyle T(x+y)^{\circ(r)}=X^{\circ(r)}+Y^{\circ(r)}+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}c(r,i)X^{\circ(r-i)}\circ Y^{\circ(i)}+\sum_{i=r}^{q-1}c(r,i)X^{\circ(q-1+r-i)}\circ Y^{\circ(i)}.

5.2
The Algebraic Method

In this subsection, we will prove Theorem
1.7
by applying Theorem
5.1
with the basis
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
and the corresponding additive spanning set
B
as mentioned above. Before that, we need some results below on the
p
-adic valuation of binomial coefficients.

Theorem 5.3

(Kummer’s theorem,
[
9
]
)
.

Given integers
n\geq m\geq 0
and a prime number
p
, the
p
-adic valuation of the binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{m}
is given by

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\binom{n}{m}=\frac{S_{p}(m)+S_{p}(n-m)-S_{p}(n)}{p-1},

which is equal to the number of carries when
m
is added to
n-m
in base
p
.

Corollary 5.4
.

The
p
-adic valuation of
\binom{np^{s}}{mp^{s}}
is equal to the
p
-adic valuation of
\binom{n}{m}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.3
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\binom{np^{s}}{mp^{s}}=\frac{S_{p}(mp^{s})+S_{p}(np^{s}-mp^{s})-S_{p}(np^{s})}{p-1}=\frac{S_{p}(m)+S_{p}(n-m)-S_{p}(n)}{p-1}=\nu_{p}\binom{n}{m}

since
S_{p}(\ell\cdot p^{t})=S_{p}(\ell)
for any positive integers
\ell,t
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
x,y,z
be nonnegative integers such that
z=x+y
. If
\nu_{p}(x)=m
and
\nu_{p}(z)=n
for some
n\geq m
, then
\nu_{p}\binom{z}{x}\geq n-m
; in other words, there are at least
n-m
carries in the base-
p
addition of
z=x+y
.

Proof.

Consider the
p
-adic valuation of the binomial coefficient
\binom{z}{x}
, we have

\nu_{p}\binom{z}{x}=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{z}{x}\binom{z-1}{x-1}\right)\geq\nu_{p}\left(\frac{z}{x}\right)=\nu_{p}(z)-\nu_{p}(x)=n-m.

By Theorem
5.3
, the number of carries in the base-
p

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
x,y,z
be nonnegative integers such that
z=x+y
. Write
x=x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime},z=z^{\prime}+z^{\prime\prime}
, where
\nu_{p}(x^{\prime}),\nu_{p}(z^{\prime})\geq n
and
0\leq x^{\prime\prime},z^{\prime\prime}\leq p^{n}-1
. Assume that the
p
-adic expansions of
x^{\prime\prime}
and
z^{\prime\prime}
are
x^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{i}p^{i},z^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}z_{i}p^{i}
. If
x_{i}\geq z_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
and
x_{0}>z_{0}
, then there are at least
n
carries in the base-
p
addition of
z=x+y
.

Proof.

We claim that the number of carries in the base-
p
addition of
z=x+y
is equal to the number of carries in the base-
p
addition of
z^{\prime}=w+y
, where
w=x-z^{\prime\prime}=x^{\prime}+(x^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime\prime})
.
Note that if there are no carries in the base-
p
addition of

, then
S_{p}(c)=S_{p}(a)+S_{p}(b)
by Theorem
5.3
. By repeatedly applying this observation, we see that

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\binom{z}{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{p}(x)+S_{p}(y)-S_{p}(z)}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{p}(x^{\prime})+S_{p}(x^{\prime\prime})+S_{p}(y)-S_{p}(z^{\prime})-S_{p}(z^{\prime\prime})}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{p}(x^{\prime})+S_{p}(x^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime\prime})+S_{p}(y)-S_{p}(z^{\prime})}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{p}(x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime\prime})+S_{p}(y)-S_{p}(z^{\prime})}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{p}(w)+S_{p}(y)-S_{p}(z^{\prime})}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}\binom{z^{\prime}}{w}.

Hence the claim holds. Note that
w=x^{\prime}+(x^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime\prime})
and the lowest digit of

is
x_{0}-z_{0}>0
, thus
\nu_{p}(w)=0
. Since
\nu_{p}(z^{\prime})\geq n
, by Lemma
5.5
, we know that the number of carries in the base-
p
addition of
z^{\prime}=w+y
is

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\binom{z^{\prime}}{w}\geq\nu_{p}(z^{\prime})\geq n.

The proof is now complete.

Lemma 5.7
.

For

, the

-adic valuation of

Lemma 5.8

Proposition 5.9

Now we use the backwards induction on

\displaystyle p^{s_{3}}\;\big|\;\sigma\left(T(A_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}A_{2}+\beta^{\prime}A_{2})^{\circ(r+p^{j})}\circ A_{2}^{\circ(r_{2}-p^{j})}\circ A_{3}^{\circ(r_{3})}\circ\cdots\circ A_{k}^{\circ(r_{k})}\right),

\displaystyle p^{s_{3}}\;\big|\;c(r+p^{j},p^{j})\sigma\left(T(A_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}A_{2})^{\circ(r)}\circ A_{2}^{\circ(r_{2})}\circ A_{3}^{\circ(r_{3})}\circ\cdots\circ A_{k}^{\circ(r_{k})}\right).

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(c(r+p^{j},p^{j}))=\nu_{p}\binom{r+p^{j}}{p^{j}}=\frac{S_{p}(p^{j})+S_{p}(r)-S_{p}(r+p^{j})}{p-1}

\displaystyle p^{e-\frac{1}{p-1}(S_{p}(r)+\sum_{i=2}^{k}S_{p}(r_{i}))+f}\;\big|\;\sigma\left(T(A_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}A_{2})^{\circ(r)}\circ A_{2}^{\circ(r_{2})}\circ A_{3}^{\circ(r_{3})}\circ\cdots\circ A_{k}^{\circ(r_{k})}\right)

7
Summary and Discussions

7.1
Summary

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. Suppose that
q^{e}|d
. In this paper, we have found a basis
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
of
C
such that
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\min\{e+1,k\}}\}
span a constant weight code. Moreover, any
k-1
of them span a
[g_{q}(k-1,d),k-1,d]_{q}
Griesmer subcode. In 1998, Ward applied Theorem
5.1
to prove Theorem
1.4
. However, Ward only considered Griesmer codes over the prime field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
(rather than over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q
is a proper prime power). In the case where
q
is a proper prime power, the
p
-adic valuation of the coefficients
c(r,k)
becomes more difficult to analyze. We are able to overcome these difficulties and prove Theorem
1.7
. Secondly, using this basis together with Theorem
1.7
, we are able to prove Theorem
1.8
from the geometric point of view.

7.2
Discussions

Recall that Ward’s conjecture
[
21
]
can be restated as follows:

Conjecture
1.6
′
.

Let
C
be a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code where
q=p^{f}
. If
p^{e}|d
, then
\left\lceil p^{e-(f-1)}\right\rceil
is a divisor of

.

By Theorem
6.3
, we reduce Ward’s conjecture to the case when
k\leq q-1
; so the problem is now simplified as follows.

Problem 7.1
.

Prove or disprove Ward’s conjecture for
k\leq q-1
.

Previously, the best divisibility results were Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
. Currently, the state of the art is given by Theorem
1.7
, Theorem
1.8
and Theorem 14 of
[
21
]
.

We believe that the truth is closer to Theorem
1.7
and Theorem
1.8
than to Ward’s conjecture; in other words, Ward’s conjecture might be false. The evidence to support our guess is that in the proof of Theorem
1.7
, the inequalities can hold as equalities for specific cases.

If we are trying to prove Ward’s conjecture, the method in the proof of Theorem
1.7
seems difficult to improve.

On the other hand, in order to disprove Ward’s conjecture, we need to find a counterexample. The authors suggest searching for a
[g_{q}(k,d),k,d]_{q}
Griesmer code with parameters
k,d,q=p^{f}
subject to

\displaystyle q:

\displaystyle\quad\text{non-prime }q\geq 8,

\displaystyle k:

\displaystyle\quad 4\leq k\leq q-1,

\displaystyle d:

\displaystyle\quad f+1\leq\nu_{p}(d)<\min\{f(q-2),f(k-1))\},

which may provide a counterexample to Ward’s conjecture. It turns out that Griesmer codes satisfying the above conditions may not exist when the code parameters are relatively small. This fact presents a challenge to finding a counterexample.
