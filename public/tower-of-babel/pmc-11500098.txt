Development of a quantitative prediction algorithm for human cord blood-derived CD34+ hematopoietic stem-progenitor cells using parametric and non-parametric machine learning models
Authors: Chi-Kwan Leung, Pengcheng Zhu, Ian Loke, Kin Fai Tang, Ho-Chuen Leung, Chin-Fung Yeung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transplantation of CD34+ hematopoietic stem-progenitor cells (HSPCs) derived from cord blood serves as the standard treatment for selected hematological, oncological, metabolic, and immunodeficiency disorders, of which the dose is pivotal to the clinical outcome. Based on numerous maternal and neonatal parameters, we evaluated the predictive power of mathematical pipelines to the proportion of CD34+ cells in the final cryopreserved cord blood product adopting both parametric and non-parametric algorithms. Twenty-four predictor variables associated with the cord blood processing of 802 processed cord blood units randomly sampled in 2020–2022 were retrieved and analyzed. Prediction models were developed by adopting the parametric (multivariate linear regression) and non-parametric (random forest and back propagation neural network) statistical models to investigate the data patterns for determining the single outcome (i.e., the proportion of CD34+ cells). The multivariate linear regression model produced the lowest root-mean-square deviation (0.0982). However, the model created by the back propagation neural network produced the highest median absolute deviation (0.0689) and predictive power (56.99%) in comparison to the random forest and multivariate linear regression. The predictive model depending on a combination of continuous and discrete maternal with neonatal parameters associated with cord blood processing can predict the CD34+ dose in the final product for clinical utilization. The back propagation neural network algorithm produces a model with the highest predictive power which can be widely applied to assisting cell banks for optimal cord blood unit selection to ensure the highest chance of transplantation success.Keywords: Hematopoietic stem cells, AABB, FACT, CAP, Cord blood bank, Unrelated hematopoietic stem cell transplantSubject terms: Health care, Stem cells, Haematopoietic stem cells, Mesenchymal stem cells, Bioinformatics

Introduction

The first hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) derived from umbilical cord blood (UCB) was performed in a 5-year-old boy with Fanconi anemia in France in 19891. Since then, UCB, in addition to bone marrow and peripheral blood, has served as a viable source of stem cells for transplant, with the UCB-HSCT developing into a standard treatment for over a hundred disorders in hematologic, oncological, immunologic, neurologic, dermatologic, rheumatologic, and metabolic for paediatric and adult patients receiving autologous or allogeneic (genetically distinct related or unrelated) transplantation for decades worldwide2,3. Currently, HSCT is a critical therapeutic intervention for a variety of diseases. It plays a significant role in the management of conditions such as Glanzmann’s thrombasthenia4, and sickle cell disease5. In the case of relapsed B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, HSCT is one of several treatment options6, which include therapies like Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, among others. Over 1.5 million life-saving HSCTs have been delivered in more than 1500 transplantation facilities globally7.

The storage eligibility of the final cord blood product in most public or private (family) cord blood banks (CBB) is determined according to the viability and enumeration of CD34+ cells, total nucleated cells (TNC), mononuclear cells (MNC), as well as product volume or weight. Two prestigious international accrediting bodies, the Association for the Advancement of Blood and Biotherapies, previously known as the American Association of Blood Banks (AABB), the Foundation for the Accreditation of Cellular Therapy (FACT), and NetCord (NetCord-FACT), have developed corresponding standards to ensure compliance in operation from cord blood collection to clinical administration for auditing public and private (family) CBBs. According to both AABB’s Standards for Cellular Therapy Services (10th Edition) and the NetCord-FACT’s Cord Blood Accreditation Manual (7th Edition), explicit tests of the viability and cell count of CD34+ cells and TNC are both required for post-processed cord blood units (CBU) prior to cryopreservation. NetCord-FACT states that the cell dose of TNC and CD34+ cells in a cryopreserved CBU for unrelated administration are required to reach at least 5.0 × 108 and 1.25 × 106, respectively; the post-thawed viability of CD34+ cells is required at least 70% in a thawed contiguous segment or representative sample before the engraftment.

The CD34+ hematopoietic stem-progenitor cells (CD34+ cells), typically representing < 1% of mononuclear cells in cord blood, play crucial roles in multi-lineage hematopoietic reconstitution and immune recovery8. Prior clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that the dose of infused CD34+ cells in the HSCT is critical in predicting the success of engraftment and survival for the related and unrelated transplantation in comparison to TNC content8–14. Recent studies suggested a dose of 1–1.2 × 105 CD34+ cells per kilogram of a patient’s body weight to achieve hematopoietic recovery15,16, and a dose of 2 × 105 is considered the threshold for partially mismatched human leukocyte antigens (HLA) transplants with 10–20% cell death due to the freeze-thaw procedure17,18. The suggested dose of CD34+ cells for autologous infusion, though remaining unknown, is presumably lower than that of an unrelated transplant considering the absence of histocompatibility disparity. Although the ISHAGE method for standardising CD34+ enumeration has been implemented, not all CBBs adopted it for CD34+ enumeration or may have performed the enumeration incorrectly17. This is further complicated that not all CBBs count CD34+ cells in processed CBUs, making it challenging to compare or infer the CD34+ dose among CBBs17.

In previous studies the univariate and multivariate analyses have been employed to evaluate the correlation of a single or multiple predictors, involving obstetric factors, maternal and neonatal parameters, as well as fetal biometry and the outcome (i.e., CD34+ cells)19–25. These statistical analytics are sensitive to imbalanced data without producing a probabilistic value. In this study, we developed three prediction algorithms adopting parametric (multivariate linear regression) and non-parametric (random forest and back propagation neural network) machine learning-based statistical modelling with both maternal and neonatal factors involved, which were utilized in identifying the optimal mathematical predictor of the CD34+ cell dose in the final cord blood product for cryopreservation. These developed mathematical tools are envisaged to be widely applicable in the cord blood industry to identify optimal CBU for engraftment.

Materials and methods

CBU collection and selection

A retrospective analysis involving 802 processed CBUs collected in 2020–2022 was performed. The CBUs for analysis were randomly selected by the Advanced Random Integer Generator (Random.org) depending on the sample’s associated unique International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT)128 barcode26, and processed with the semi-automated or automated cord blood processing method powered by the AutoXpress® (AXP®) II System (ThermoGenesis Holdings, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. All data are retrieved in the database managed by the Hong Kong-based CBB HealthBaby, a subsidiary of SGX-listed company (CordLife Group) accredited by NetCord-FACT, AABB, and the College of American Pathologists (CAP). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s).

Analysis of cord blood parameters

The statistical models were constructed by a total of 24 selected predictor variables (discrete: 4; continuous: 20), namely the discrete variables: [baby gender (male or female), delivery type (Natural birth or Caesarean), process type (manual or AXP®), and ABO blood group typing], and the continuous variables: [clot/fibrin weight (g), cord blood net weight (g), cord blood net volume (ml), the volume of hydroxyethyl starch added (ml), pre- and post-processing CBU volume (ml), pre- and post-processing leukocyte count (109/L), pre- and post-processing lymphocyte count (109/L), pre- and post-processing monocyte count (109/L), post-processing TNC count (108/L), TNC recovery rate (%), MNC recovery rate (%), final CBU volume with cryoprotectant (ml), the final concentration of cryoprotectant (%), the time interval between addition of cryoprotectant to cryopreservation (min), the time interval between CBU collection and storage (min), post-processing TNC viability (%)]. The proportion of CD34+ cells among TNC in the final cryopreserved cord blood product was used as the single outcome variable φ, as defined in the International Society for Hematotherapy and Graft Engineering (ISHAGE) protocol using BD FACSCalibur Flow Cytometer27.

In our study, the R package (version 4.2.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was employed for data wrangling and analysis. Key packages like tidyverse (1.3.2), rio (0.5.29), rstatix (0.7.0), Hmisc (4.7-0), corrplot (0.92), bruceR (0.88), car (3.1-0), MASS (7.3–58), and leaps (3.1) were utilized for tasks such as descriptive statistics, analysis of variance, correlation, and linear regression analyses. For our supervised machine learning tasks, the randomForest package (4.7–1.1) was chosen because of its robust performance in various contexts, including its ability to handle high-dimensional data and its robustness to overfitting28. The Metrics package (0.1.4) supported performance evaluation. The neuralnet package (1.44.2) facilitated the implementation of the BP neural network learning algorithm, which is known for its efficacy in pattern recognition and learning complex models from large datasets29, while ggplot2 (3.3.6) was used for data visualization. These methods were selected based on their proven track record in the field of predictive modeling and their suitability for the complex nature of medical data analysis, as detailed in relevant literature30,31.

Comparative analysis of parametric and non-parametric predictive models for φ

A total of 15 predictor variables (X2, X3, X5, X6, X7, X9, X10, X11, X12, X13, X14, X16, X20, λ1, and λ3) associated with the 802 CBU samples were selected to establish a mathematical model to predict an outcome φ depending on parametric (multivariate linear regression) and non-parametric algorithms (random forest and BP neural network).

In this study, the 802 datasets were randomly collected in a 3:1 ratio and separated into a training cohort (579) and validation cohort (193), which were taken to develop and validate the supervised machine learning models for classifying data and predicting the outcome φ. The mathematical pipeline enables the feeding of labelled datasets (input) to train algorithms, with its weights adjusted by the model until the model has been fitted appropriately as a part of the cross-validation process. We compared the performance or the predictive power of the three prediction algorithms, using the average absolute deviation (MAD) to evaluate the variability indicating the average gap between observation and their mean, root means squared error (RMSE) to evaluate the error of a model in predicting quantitative data, and forecast accuracy (the difference between the actual and predicted value does not exceed 0.05 in 150 random samplings) for the deviation of the actual φ from the forecasted φ.

First, multivariate linear regression was employed to construct a mathematical model to forecast φ which was performed to generate the coefficients of a formula to predict a logit transformation of the presence probability of the characteristics of interest. Table 4 displays the unstandardized (B) and standardized (β) regression coefficients with the respective standard errors (SE) as the reference for forecast and comparison of the effects to the output φ, respectively. A linear regression equation was obtained from the training-validation model as follows: φ = 0.168 + X2(8.260) + X3(− 8.281) + X5(0.021) + X6 (− 0.049) + X7(0.185) + X9(− 0.002) + X10(0.012) + X11(0.155) + X12(0.098) + X13(− 0.167) + X14(− 0.138) + X16(0.054) + X20(0.048) + λ1(− 0.032) + λ3(− 0.011); while X7 (p < 0.01), X12 (p < 0.05), X14 (p < 0.01) and λ1 (p < 0.001) were validated to be statistically significant. As shown in Fig. 1A, the forecasted values fail to overlap well with the actual (original) values, with the MAD, RMSE, and forecast accuracy of 0.0774, 0.0982, and 36.27, respectively (Table 5).

Second, the random forest (RF) modelling method was adopted to predict φ, where two parameters, Ntree and Mtry, were generally required to optimize the algorithm, which represented the number of tree nodes and variables randomly sampled as candidates at each split in the RF model, respectively, and are likely to play the most significant roles in the forecast accuracy. We first set the Mtry to the default value (square of the total number of all predictor variables) and searched for the optimal Ntree value. A random forest was established with different Ntree values (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500) to identify the number of trees corresponding to a stable classifier, with ten RF classifiers built for each Ntree value, in which the out-of-bag rate error (OOB Error) was recorded, and the number of trees where the OOB Error stabilized and reached the minimum was determined. Similarly, we applied a procedure such that the RF ran 10 times. The optimal number of predictor variables for the split was selected for which the OOB Error stabilized and reached the minimum.

Figure 2 indicates that the OOB error tended to stabilize when Ntree was > 200 (Fig. 2A), and be reduced while increasing Mtry (Fig. 2B), reaching a minimum when Mtry was at 5. Accordingly, the optimal values of Ntree and Mtry were set to 500 and 5, respectively. As depicted in Fig. 2C, both measures of the mean decrease accuracy (% IncMSE) and mean decrease Gini (IncNodePurity) described the prediction capability of mean square error with randomly permuted predictor variables, as well as the variable significance based on the Gini impurity index for calculating the splits in tress respectively. These results consistently indicated that several predictor variables such as X6, X7, X11, and X13, with the highest % IncMSE and IncNodePurity values, were crucial for these regressors in the predictive power of the random forest model. As shown in Fig. 1B, predicted values significantly deviated from the actual (original) values, with the MAD, RMSE, and forecast accuracy of 0.0797, 0.0999, and 36.79, respectively (Table 5).

Last, we chose an artificial BP neural network model to predict φ, which mainly functions with input, hidden, and output layers. The number of nodes (i.e., neuron or Perceptron) in the input and output layers is fixed regardless of whether it is a regression or classification task. The number of layers and nodes in each hidden layer and the two primary hyperparameters controlling the architecture or topology of the network, will primarily determine the performance of neural network.

First, affected by the number of hidden layers, a neural network without hidden layers can only represent linearly differentiable functions or decisions; In contrast, with 1 hidden layer, it can fit any function that “contains a continuous mapping from one finite space to another,” and the equipment of 2 hidden layers with an appropriate activation function can enable to represent any decision boundary of arbitrary precision by which any smooth mapping of any precision can be fitted; finally, when the number of hidden layers > 2, the extra hidden layers are capable of learning complex descriptions. Given that the objective to predict φ here and the datasets chosen for modelling comprise of various processing and neonatal attributes, a neural network modelling with 3 hidden layers is finally determined for prediction.

According to the formula , Ni represents the number of neurons in the input layer; No for the number of nodes in the output layer, Ns for the number of samples in the training set, and a as an arbitrary value. Usually, the range can be taken from 2 to 10. All the input variables were used for training to output the single variable φ, so the output layer node was set to 1, i.e., No was 1, and the final number of neurons selected was 15, i.e., Ni is 15. The number of hidden neurons should range from the size of the input layer and the size of the output layer; the number of hidden neurons should be 2/3 of the size of the input layer plus 2/3 of the size of the output layer; the number of hidden neurons should be less than twice the size of the input layer, accordingly, the number of hidden neurons set in this study was in the range of [5.7,7.5], and finally, we selected (2, 6, 6) as the combination of hidden layer nodes.

As shown in Fig. 1C, despite the failure of the substantial match of predicted values with the actual (original) values; with the MAD, RMSE, and forecast accuracy of 0.0689, 0.1025, and 56.99, respectively (Table 5); all performance evaluation parameters are significantly optimized in comparison to linear regression and random forest modelling as depicted in the superimposition of all three line plots (Fig. 1D).

Discussion

It was previously reported that TNC count lies the most robust variable to identify CBUs with a threshold of 2 × 106 or above CD34+ cells17. The diversity in baby gender, gestational age lower than 39 weeks, delivery type, and TNC counts exhibited a statistically significant correlation with CD34+ cells in the multiple regression analysis32. While the CBU volume failed to serve as the best predictor of CD34+ cells32. A recent study revealed that maternal age, birth weight, placental weight, and volume of CBUs but not gravida status, gestational age, or delivery type played crucial roles in predicting the TNC and CD34+ cell counts. However, gestational age (i.e., term vs. preterm) did not intervene the content of CD34+ cells in the same study25. In contrast, the delivery type and maternal age did not affect CD34+ cell count33. The discrepancy can be possibly explained with different statistical evaluation methods, sample size, and feature selection.

In this study, we carried out a supervised machine learning-based modelling comprising parametric multivariate linear regression, non-parametric random forest, and BP neural network methods in predicting the proportion of CD34+ cells (φ), with the prediction algorithms generated by training 20 continuous and 4 discrete predictor variables related to the processing of 802 randomly selected CBU. The algorithms are anticipated to facilitate the determination of CBUs with the optimal dose of CD34+ cell for engraftment to optimize clinical outcomes and can serve as the internal control measure to estimate φ upon receiving the incoming CBU for processing in the processing facility.

Random forest and BP neural networks are favored for predictive modeling in medical applications, such as estimating the CD34+ cell dose in cord blood units, due to their proficiency in handling complex, nonlinear relationships inherent in biological data28,34. Random Forest, an ensemble method, leverages multiple decision trees to reduce overfitting and improve prediction accuracy28, offering robustness against noise and the capability to process heterogeneous data with implicit feature selection35. Its parallel computing efficiency and interpretable output, by ranking feature importance, make it particularly suitable for medical research where understanding variable significance is crucial36. On the other hand, BPNNs excel in capturing intricate patterns through their layered structure and backpropagation algorithm, adjusting weights to minimize prediction errors34. This flexibility allows BPNNs to model complex interactions and adapt to the data’s underlying structure, improving as more data becomes available37,38. Their ability to learn feature representations reduces the need for manual feature engineering, which is a significant advantage in analyzing diverse medical datasets39. Together, these methods provide a powerful toolkit for developing accurate and generalizable models, critical for making informed clinical decisions in cord blood transplantation scenarios.

A prior study reported that the dose of TNC and CD34+ cells in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) were drastically reduced by 58.7% and 62.7%, respectively. Additionally, the differentiation potency of CD34+ cells into multiple cell types involving granulocyte, erythrocyte, monocyte, megakaryocyte granulocyte, and monocyte was all attenuated. The results suggest that GDM alters in utero environments during pregnancy, so as to negatively impact the content and quality of CD34+ cells in cord blood40. Therefore, the inclusion of other maternal variables related to the general wellness of expectant mothers at different gestational ages in the training datasets could help modify the predictive power of CD34+ cells.

Previous studies have indicated that the CBU’s quality would be significantly impacted by the interval from the CBU collection to processing in a CBB41, reporting that this duration over 24 h significantly decreased the mean and median of TNC and CD34+ cells in comparison to 48 h and 72 h. Our findings are in agreement, showing a negative correlation between the predictor variable X19, which represents the time interval from CBU collection to storage (in minutes), and the CD34 + cell count. Enhancing the predictive capacity of our model is feasible by incorporating a larger array of predictor variables, as well as by employing sophisticated transformations and interactions42. This variable is particularly valuable as it can direct CBUs to minimize the time from collection to freezing. Such data is instrumental in establishing a maximum allowable processing time, which can serve as a benchmark for the processing facility to improve process control and maintain CBU quality.

In this study, we identified several predictor variables that significantly contribute to the predictive power of the non-parametric algorithms employed. These include pre-processing leukocyte count and lymphocyte count, as well as post-processing lymphocyte count. Additionally, aligning with findings from other above-mentioned studies in the field, the post-processing TNC count has been demonstrated to be a pivotal factor. Most notably, the post-processing TNC count serves as an essential indicator of graft potency43.

The robustness of non-parametric algorithms like random forests and neural networks in our study hinges on their ability to model complex and non-linear relationships between these biologically significant predictors and the outcomes. These algorithms effectively capture the intricate interactions between variables, which might be missed by traditional parametric methods. This capacity for nuanced modeling is particularly advantageous when dealing with heterogeneous medical data, where subtle variations can have significant implications for patient outcomes.

By leveraging the strengths of these algorithms and the predictive relevance of the chosen variables, our models have shown considerable promise in accurately predicting post-transplant recovery metrics, thereby providing a valuable tool for clinicians in the decision-making process.

Multiple techniques can serve and have been applied to boosting the performance of a linear regression model, involving the creation of simple new features, target variable transformation, clustering of common data points, addition of boosting algorithms, and hyperparameter tuning (tree depth, estimators and learning rate). The predictive power can be modified by adjusting several predictor variables (input) with the highest % IncMSE and IncNodePurity value (Fig. 2) and optimizing the algorithm such as hyperparameter tuning, cross-validation, and averaging multiple classifiers in the random forest model. More datasets may be fed for training and fine-tuning the hyperparameter (e.g., data invention, rescaling, transformation, and feature selection) and hierarchy in the BP neural network (i.e., number of hidden layers) (Fig. 3), so as to elevate the model accuracy. The present study lies a solid foundation for developing a mathematical pipeline to predict φ using machine learning-based modelling.

Cord blood has been demonstrated as a viable source of stem cells, of which the collection is non-invasive, a relaxed HLA matching requirement to expedite the course of transplantation, especially for ethnic and racial minorities. The CD34+ cell count (i.e., cell dose) is limited per CBU; the patients’ weight and HLA profiles should be considered for engraftment; CBU is primarily adopted in paediatric patients at present44,45. The safety and clinical outcome of double partially-HLA-mismatched CBUs transplantation have been demonstrated by increasing clinical studies, overcoming the bottleneck of inadequately dosed single-unit CBU46. The advanced development in cord blood stem cell proliferation in vitro facilitates clinical CBT47,48. Due to the insufficient availability of public clinical data on the CBT and HLA genotype and haplotype frequencies, it is intractable to clearly determine the HLA-match likelihoods for CBT. Various studies across nations over the two decades consistently demonstrated the guaranteed probability of identifying a (4/6) HLA-match CBU for engraftment when the inventory size reaches 100,000 CBUs49,50, which can even reach over 98% of identifying a 5/6 HLA-matched unit for CBT in India, Japan, and South Korea with 100,000 CBUs50.

According to the World Marrow Donor Association (WMDA), despite the reservation of over 8 million cord blood units for transplant, 10,000–15,000 patients fail to identify a matched donor per year, especially for racial and ethnic minorities51,52. Public and family CBBs across nations are desperate for cord blood banking for life-saving therapy and concerted efforts are essential to promote CBU inventory growth worldwide and facilitate CBT clinically53,54.

The performance of the three tested prediction models significantly varied from each other, with the highest forecasting accuracy delivered by the BP neural network modelling (Fig. 3; Table 5). The models can be suitably applied to predicting or verifying other critical parameters associated with the CBT’s clinical efficacy, such as the enumeration and viability of TNCs. The predictive power of the models heavily depends on the sample size, the number (i.e., breath), and the dimension (i.e., depth) of the predictor variables selected for training. The sample size (802) and the number of predictor variables (25) applied in the study are insufficient to generate a model with a clinically significant predictive power.

We recognize several pivotal parameters that significantly influence the predictive power of our models, which may also serve as potential preliminary predictive biomarkers.

The multivariate linear regression model highlighted cord blood net weight and volume, along with post-processing leukocyte count, as key predictors of CD34+ cell yields. Similarly, the random forest model identified pre-processing and post-processing lymphocyte counts as substantial variables. The BP neural network, which exhibited the highest predictive power, was particularly responsive to the interval between CBU collection and post-processing TNC viability.

These parameters are crucial not only for improving the accuracy of our predictive models but also for establishing a basis for identifying preliminary predictive biomarkers. Our findings indicate that factors related to neonatal characteristics and processing attributes are intimately linked with the yield of CD34+ cells in cord blood.

Acknowledging the current limitations of our predictive models, we are dedicated to further research to refine and substantiate these biomarkers. Our objective is to authenticate these initial biomarkers and discover additional ones through more comprehensive analysis, potentially incorporating detailed facets of maternal health, neonatal outcomes, and intricacies of cord blood processing.

While the identified parameters are statistically significant within our models, their biological relevance demands additional investigation. We intend to work in tandem with clinical specialists to unravel the mechanistic implications of these parameters, which will offer a deeper understanding of their impact on HSPC abundance.

In summary, our established predictive models represent an initial step toward a more sophisticated and clinically relevant tool for cord blood banks and transplantation services. Advancing these models and pinpointing accurate predictive biomarkers is promising for enhancing the selection process of cord blood units, ultimately bettering transplantation success rates for patients in need of HSCT.In this study, we reported the proportion of CD34+ cells as a percentage of the TNC within the CBU. While this proportion serves as an initial metric, we recognize the clinical imperative of determining the absolute CD34+ cell count for HSCT. The absolute count is a critical factor, as it is intricately linked to the likelihood of successful engraftment and positive patient outcomes.

Recognizing this, the machine learning algorithms developed in this present study has the potential to estimate not only the proportion of CD34+ cells but also their absolute count within CBUs. By integrating various predictive variables and refining our models, the user can further extend the current capabilities to provide a reliable estimation of absolute CD34+ cell numbers—a parameter of considerable clinical relevance.

The preliminary work presented here lays the foundation for such advancements. The algorithms we propose have the potential to be calibrated and validated for predicting absolute CD34+ cell counts, thereby enhancing the utility of our predictive model in clinical settings. With further development and validation, these algorithms could significantly aid in the selection process of CBUs, ensuring that transplants are conducted with units that meet the cellular dose requirements for optimal patient outcomes.

In the current study, we have crafted an algorithm designed to be highly configurable, catering to the specific requirements of the users. This flexibility enables users to aptly select variables to be treated as inputs or outputs, tailoring the algorithm’s application to their particular research or operational needs. Such customization facilitates the exploration of new and intricate relationships between various parameters, enhancing our understanding of the underlying biological and process-related factors.

Furthermore, the algorithm’s utility is not confined to theoretical analysis; it has practical implications in the realm of internal processes and quality control mechanisms. A common application in cord blood processing is the use of flow cytometry to evaluate the viability and quantify CD45+ and CD34+ cell populations. Routine quality control practices typically involve precise gating strategies to accurately identify and measure these cell populations, ensuring the reliability of the results.

Our algorithm enriches this process by providing a predictive benchmark for the expected values of these crucial parameters. It acts as a preemptive checkpoint, offering foresight into the anticipated viability and counts of CD45+ and CD34+ cells, against which actual flow cytometry outcomes can be evaluated. This predictive capability is invaluable; it alerts laboratory personnel to any anomalies that may indicate a deviation from established quality thresholds, thereby facilitating early intervention and corrective actions if needed.

By leveraging the predictive power of this algorithm, cord blood banks can bolster the precision and reliability of their cell viability assessments. It introduces an advanced, data-driven dimension to quality control protocols, enabling labs to maintain excellent quality standards and process integrity. In essence, the integration of our algorithm into routine quality assessments empowers cord blood banks to maintain a proactive stance in quality assurance, ensuring that every product meets the highest benchmarks of quality and efficacy.

Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a mathematical pipeline incorporating both parametric and non-parametric approaches to create three predictive models for estimating the CD34+ cell content in transplant-ready CBUs. By refining these models and effectively implementing the associated algorithms, we have improved our capacity to predict CD34+ cell content. Among these models, the BP neural network has demonstrated a notable level of accuracy in its predictions, which may assist in the more effective selection of CBUs for engraftment.

Additionally, the algorithm designed in this research provides a practical tool for internal process control and quality assurance purposes. By comparing expected and actual outcomes, the algorithm serves as a supportive measure to existing quality control protocols, helping to maintain the consistency and quality of CBUs.

The contribution of our algorithm to the field of cord blood banking is a modest yet meaningful step towards enhancing the selection process of CBUs and supporting the maintenance of quality standards in stem cell transplantation practices.
