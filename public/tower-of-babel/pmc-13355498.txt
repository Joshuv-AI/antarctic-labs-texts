Clinical features and outcomes of MPN with concurrent MPL and JAK2/CALR Mutations
Authors: Yu-Han Chen, Shruti Gajjar, Victor You, Rami Komrokji, Valentina Nardi, Harrison Tsai, Zhuoer Xie, Andrew Kuykendall, Gabriela S. Hobbs
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are a group of hematologic malignancies driven by mutations in JAK2, CALR, or MPL and considered mutually exclusive.1, 2, 3 Among the 3 main driver mutations, JAK2 is the most common, occurring in ∼98% of patients with polycythemia vera (PV), 50% to 60% with essential thrombocythemia (ET), and 55% to 65% with myelofibrosis (MF). CALR is found in ∼20 % of the patients with MF and ET.4 In contrast, MPL mutations are relatively uncommon, absent in polycythemia vera, and detected in 3% to 4% of ET and 6% to 7% of MF cases.5 Case reports and small case series describe instances of concurrent mutations.6 Data on the characteristics of concurrent driver mutations remain limited, and their prognostic significance is not well established. Our study aims to characterize the clinical presentation of patients with MPNs who have MPL mutations with concurrent JAK2 or CALR mutations.2

This retrospective case series was conducted at Massachusetts General Brigham (MGB) and Moffitt Cancer Center (MCC) and includes patients treated between 2010 and 2022. The study was approved by the institutional review board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and a data use agreement between both hospitals. We included patients diagnosed with MPNs meeting the World Health Organization 2022 criteria with concurrent MPL and JAK2 or CALR mutations.7 Eligible participants were those with a confirmed diagnosis of MPN by the World Health Organization 2022 Classification.7 Exclusion criteria included patients with diagnoses other than MPN, follow-up of <6 months, or incomplete data. At MGB, data were available for all MPNs, whereas at MCC, data were limited to patients with MF.

Data collected included (1) demographics, (2) disease characteristics, (3) treatment history, and (4) prognostic assessment (Revised International Prognostic Score of Thrombosis for ET [R-IPSET]8 and Mutation-Enhanced International Prognostic Score System [MIPPS]70+ version 2.0).9 Clinical outcomes evaluated included disease progression to post-ET MF or acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Thromboembolic and bleeding events were also documented for MGB patients.

In the statistical analysis, continuous variables were represented using medians, ranges, and interquartile ranges because of the nonnormal distribution of the data. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Descriptive statistical methods were used to summarize patient characteristics and clinical outcomes.

At MGB, 57 patients with MPL mutations and a diagnosis of MPN were identified, and 5 (15.2%) had concurrent MPL and either JAK2 or CALR mutations. At MCC, 58 eligible patients with MF and MPL mutations were identified, of whom 6 (10.3%) had concurrent MPL and either JAK2 or CALR mutations. Thus, the final study cohort included 11 patients. Of the 11 patients, 4 were diagnosed with ET, including 2 who progressed to post-ET MF, 6 with primary MF (PMF), and 1 with unclassified MPN. The median age at diagnosis was 74 years (range, 46-86), and the median follow-up duration was 33 months (range, 14-182). The distribution of ethnicity included White (n = 8), Black (n = 2), and Hispanic (n = 1).

Among patients with MPL and JAK2 comutations, MPL variants were W515L, W515R, and Y519D. The corresponding JAK2 mutations were predominantly V617F, with 1 case of T875N. Notably, patients with MPL and CALR comutations carried less common MPL variants of R592Q and P440L. The median variant allele frequency (VAF) for MPL was 12.35% (available in 10 patients) with a range of <5 to 39.8. For JAK2, there were 9 patients with available VAF, with a median of 35% and a range of 0.2% to 89.9%. Table 2 summarizes the molecular information of the patients. High-molecular-risk (HMR) mutations were present in 5 of the 11 patients, most frequently ASXL1, followed by EZH2 and SRSF2. Other comutations were detected in 9 patients. Notably, 1 patient with MPL Y519D and JAK2 V617F harbored multiple additional gene alterations.

None of the patients underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and 1 progressed to AML. The patient with progression to AML was treated supportively and died 1 month after diagnosis. In addition to this patient, another 4 patients had died at the time of last follow-up, including 3 with PMF and 1 with post-ET MF. Thromboembolic and bleeding events occurred in 2 of the 5 MGB patients, both after progression to post-ET MF.

Concurrent MPL mutations with either JAK2 or CALR were found in ∼12% of the MPL-mutated cases in our cohort. These patients demonstrated heterogeneous clinical presentations across the MPN disease spectrum; moreover, 45.4% of the cohort had additional HMR mutations. Although the 3 driver mutations in MPNs are generally mutually exclusive, several studies have provided evidence of co-occurrence.10,11 Our finding suggests that although MPL mutations are uncommon and co-occurring driver mutations have historically been considered rare, their frequency has previously been underestimated.

Among the 8 patients with available VAF data, MPL exhibited higher VAF in 3 patients, whereas JAK2 demonstrated higher VAF in 5 patients. The patient with JAK2 and CALR mutations showed comparable VAF between the 2 mutations. Our result implied that there was no fixed acquisition hierarchy between MPL and JAK2 or CALR. Notably, when JAK2 represented the dominant clone, VAF reached as high as 90%, whereas MPL-dominant cases demonstrated lower maximal VAF to ∼40%. However, previous studies reveal that MPL can present with high VAF in patients with single-driver mutations.12 Nevertheless, this interpretation is limited by incomplete VAF data and the lack of longitudinal sequencing.

The existing case reports have largely focused on the 3 main driver mutations and have not provided detailed information regarding co-occurring HMR or other mutations. One previous study reported that among 19 patients with dual driver mutations, 53% of the patients harbored 13 additional mutations across 8 genes.13 In our cohort, nearly half of the patients carried additional HMR mutations. In our study, 5 of the 11 patients had died at the last follow-up, including 4 with PMF and 1 with post-ET MF. Although this raises the possibility that dual mutations are associated with worse outcomes, the small cohort size precludes any causal inference.

Despite our efforts, this study has several limitations. The rarity of concurrent MPL with JAK2 or CALR mutations resulted in a small sample size, limiting statistical power. The inclusion of ET, PMF, post-ET MF, and unclassified MPN introduces biological heterogeneity, limiting cross-group comparability. The retrospective design introduces potential bias because of variability in diagnostic and treatment practices across institutions. In addition, thrombotic and bleeding events were not available across institutions. Heterogeneous follow-up duration further limits assessment of long-term outcomes, including progression to MF or AML. We excluded patients with <6 months of follow-up because of insufficient longitudinal data; however, this may introduce immortal time bias. Most excluded patients had only a single consultation, incidental fixed acquisition findings, or incomplete diagnostic evaluation. Finally, the predominance of White patients limits generalizability. Larger, more diverse cohorts with longer follow-up and comparison with single-driver–mutated MPN are needed to better define the prognostic and therapeutic implications of these comutations.
