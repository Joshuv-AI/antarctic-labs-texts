Patient-reported outcomes following anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion versus laminectomy for degenerative cervical myelopathy: analyses of a national multicenter dataset
Authors: Sachiko Kawasaki, Paul Gerdhem, Kazuya Kitamura, Anna MacDowall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeEvidence comparing anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) and laminectomy for degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM), particularly regarding patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), remains limited. This study compared postoperative PROMs, complication rates, and reoperation rates between ACCF and laminectomy in patients with DCM.MethodsThis retrospective study utilized prospectively collected registry data from the Swedish Spine Registry. All patients with DCM who underwent ACCF or laminectomy between 2006 and 2022 with available baseline and two-year European Myelopathy Score (EMS) data were identified. Complications and reoperations recorded in the registry were also assessed. EMS improvement at 2 years, postoperative complications, and reoperation rates were compared between the ACCF and laminectomy groups. Mixed-effects regression models were used to analyze factors associated with EMS improvement, complications, and reoperations.ResultsThe ACCF and laminectomy groups included 248 and 498 patients, respectively. Both groups achieved comparable EMS improvements at 2 years (median +1 point). The laminectomy group had significantly fewer patient-reported complications than the ACCF group (odds ratio [OR]: 0.13, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.05–0.30, p < 0.01). Worse preoperative EMS was significantly associated with higher odds of patient-reported complications (OR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.68–0.92, p < 0.01) and after-discharge reoperations (OR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.65–0.95, p = 0.01).ConclusionLaminectomy achieved comparable patient-reported myelopathy improvement with fewer complications than ACCF, supporting it as an effective option for appropriately selected patients with DCM.

Introduction

Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is caused by compression of the cervical spinal cord by pathological factors within the spinal canal. Both dynamic and static repetitive damage lead to spinal cord disturbances (Shedid et al., 2007; Baptiste and Fehlings, 2006). The symptoms of DCM include limb sensory and motor dysfunctions, as well as bladder dysfunction. The primary surgical goals for patients with DCM are decompression of the spinal cord and improvement of myelopathy. Surgical approaches can be divided into two categories: anterior and posterior. Previous studies comparing anterior and posterior approaches have reported controversial results (Ghogawala et al., 2021; El-Ghandour et al., 2020; Hirai et al., 2018; Seng et al., 2013). Compared with anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) provides sufficient decompression on the ventral side of the vertebral body and intervertebral segments. Several studies comparing ACCF and laminectomy for DCM have reported controversial results in the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score, postoperative cervical alignment, complications, and reoperations (Wada et al., 2001; Shibuya et al., 2010; Hirai et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2014). Laminectomy, which requires no instruments, is more cost-effective for posterior decompression than laminoplasty using implants. Among laminectomy techniques, muscle-preserving selective laminectomy (Shiraishi, 2002; Shiraishi et al., 2003) has been highlighted as a less invasive alternative to laminoplasty and reported to be associated with lower complication rates and better cost-effectiveness than ACDF (Kitamura et al., 2022). However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has directly compared ACCF and laminectomy for DCM with respect to patient-reported myelopathy outcomes.

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly utilized to validate the efficacy and value of surgery for DCM (Squires et al., 2023). Several measures are available for evaluating myelopathy, such as the JOA score, modified JOA (mJOA) score, Nurick scale (Nurick, 1972), and European Myelopathy Score (EMS) (Herdmann et al., 1994). Among these, the EMS and patient-derived mJOA scores (Rhee et al., 2018) are PROMs.

This study aimed to compare the postoperative 1) improvement in EMS and 2) rates of complications and reoperations after ACCF, as well as laminectomy without fusion for the treatment of DCM registered in the national Swedish Spine Registry (Swespine).

Materials and methods

Study population

Individuals aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with DCM and treated with ACCF or laminectomy without fusion were identified in Swespine. Patients were excluded if they had undergone previous spine surgery, had missing baseline or 2-year follow-up EMS data, or were treated with surgical techniques other than ACCF or laminectomy. Surgeries were performed between 2006 and 2022 across 20 hospitals. Swespine data and patient-reported outcome measures

Swespine captures approximately 80% of all spine surgeries performed in Sweden and is recognized for its high coverage, completeness, and diagnostic accuracy (Endler et al., 2017). In Swespine, patients independently completed baseline questionnaires, postal follow-up questionnaires, and PROMs without assistance from a surgeon (Strömqvist et al., 2001). Preoperative patient data included demographics, smoking status (yes/no), and PROMs, such as the EMS, Neck Disability Index (NDI), EQ-5D, and Numeric Rating Scales (NRS) for neck and arm pain. Surgeons recorded information on the diagnosis, surgical treatment, surgical levels, perioperative complications during the inpatient stay, and reoperation data. Follow-up questionnaires and PROMs were sent to the patients at 1, 2, and 5 years postoperatively (Strömqvist et al., 2009). Post-discharge complications (thrombosis, infection, hoarseness, and dysphagia) were patient-reported and answered in a binary yes/no format. Hoarseness and dysphagia were defined as present when they remained for at least 1 month after surgery. Reoperations were divided into two categories: those performed during hospital stay and those performed after discharge.

The EMS was developed to reflect Western myelopathy (Herdmann et al., 1994). It consists of five components: gait function, bladder and bowel function, hand function, proprioception and coordination, and paresthesia/pain. The scores range from 5 to 18 points, with lower scores indicating worse myelopathy. Although the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) has not been defined, a previous study reported that the MCID was 0.5 points, but the area under the curve was low (0.68–0.69) (Mjåset et al., 2022).

The NDI consists of 10 questions that assess the impact of neck disability. It is scored from 0 to 50 points, and the total score is converted to a percentage scale from 0% to 100% (with higher scores indicating worse disability). The MCID is 15 points (Kögl et al., 2025).

The EQ-5D is composed of five components. The total score is converted to a scale ranging from −0.5 to 1, with higher scores reflecting better quality of life (Strömqvist et al., 2009; Burström et al., 2001). The MCID is 0.24 (Ambati et al., 2025; Parker et al., 2013).

The NRS for neck or arm pain ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10 points (worst imaginable pain) (MacDowall et al., 2018). The MCID is 2.5 points (Kögl et al., 2025; Carreon et al., 2010).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.5.1). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The normality of continuous data was assessed through visual inspection of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. As none of the variables were normally distributed, continuous data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages (%). The number of surgeries performed per year and per institution were compared between the groups using the chi-square test. Changes in PROMs at the two-year follow-up and the length of hospital stay were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Chi-square tests were used to compare the percentages of patients who reached the MCID for PROMs, as well as complications and reoperations rates between the groups. A linear mixed-effects model was conducted to compare the delta change in the EMS between the groups from baseline to 2-year follow-up, adjusted for baseline age (continuous), sex (female versus[vs.] male), baseline body mass index (BMI; continuous), baseline smoking habits (smoker vs. non-smoker), baseline PROMs (EMS, NDI, EQ-5D, and NRS for neck and arm pain; continuous), number of surgical levels (continuous), and year of operation (continuous). The hospital was included as a random intercept to account for interfacility variability. Standardized regression coefficients, standard errors, 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values were reported.

In the secondary analyses, the incidence of each complication and reoperation was compared between the groups using Fisher's exact test. Additionally, mixed-effects logistic regression models were used to identify preoperative factors associated with complications and reoperations. The fixed effects included groups (ACCF vs. laminectomy), baseline age, sex, baseline BMI, baseline smoking habits, baseline EMS, number of surgical levels, and year of operation. The hospital was modeled as a random intercept to account for interfacility variability. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated.

Results

In this cohort, 248 participants underwent ACCF, and 498 underwent laminectomy. Baseline characteristics, number of operated levels, and baseline PROMs of both groups are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the ACCF group were younger, had fewer operated levels, and showed a higher baseline EMS than those in the laminectomy group. Fig. 2 presents the number of surgeries performed in each year (Fig. 2-A) and at each hospital (Fig. 2-B), indicating significant differences between the groups in operated year and hospital (both p < 0.001). There were statistically significant differences between the groups in NDI improvement, NRS neck pain improvement, and patient-reported complication rates (Table 2). Although the medians of postoperative NRS neck changes were identical between groups (−1 vs. −1), the distribution in the ACCF group was shifted toward lower values, with significantly higher ranks compared with those of the laminectomy group (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, W = 41746, p = 0.019). Reoperations were performed in 15 patients (6%) in the ACCF group (19 reoperations) and 16 patients (3%) in the laminectomy group (17 reoperations). The rate of in-hospital reoperations was significantly higher in the ACCF group than in the laminectomy group (p = 0.038) (Table 4). Discussion

This is the first observational study based on a national spine registry using PROMs to validate surgical outcomes after ACCF or laminectomy for DCM. In this cohort, a trend can be observed toward a shift from the ACCF method to laminectomy within the country over the past decade. Our findings demonstrate that laminectomy resulted in myelopathy improvement comparable to that achieved in the ACCF group. Moreover, laminectomy may be associated with fewer patient-reported complications than ACCF.

Although several studies have compared the anterior and posterior approaches for DCM, no national multicenter investigation has assessed patient-reported myelopathy outcomes. Among the myelopathy assessments, EMS has minimal barriers and a recommendation B (moderate) for neuromuscular functions (Yanez et al., 2022), although few studies have used EMS as outcome measure (Kitamura et al., 2022). Compared with ACDF, ACCF can provide direct ventral decompression, even at the vertebral body levels, as well as intervertebral segments, and may achieve larger decompression area and better myelopathy improvement. A previous study comparing ACCF with laminectomy for traumatic spinal cord injury reported comparable JOA scores and quality-of-life improvements (Yin et al., 2022). In this study, we specifically compared EMS improvement after ACCF with that after posterior laminectomy in patients with DCM using large-scale registry data.

In our analysis, EMS improvement and the proportion of patients achieving MCID were comparable between the groups, although the baseline EMS was higher in the ACCF group than in the laminectomy group. These findings suggest that laminectomy has similar beneficial effects on myelopathy. Patients undergoing ACCF demonstrated better improvements in NDI and NRS for neck, which may be explained by their younger age (approximately 10 years younger) and significantly better baseline EMS values, compared with the laminectomy group rather than the surgical method. While most studies have shown comparable NDI improvements between anterior and posterior approaches (Ghogawala et al., 2021; Kitamura et al., 2022; Kato et al., 2017; Fehlings et al., 2017), several studies have reported better improvements in anterior methods (El-Ghandour et al., 2020; Bao et al., 2025). A previous study comparing neck pain after posterior decompression with or without fusion in patients with DCM did not demonstrate superior neck pain outcomes following fusion (de Dios et al., 2022). However, recent evidence suggests that neck pain is closely associated with myelopathic symptoms themselves (Burn et al., 2026).

A linear mixed-effects model revealed that lower baseline EMS was associated with greater postoperative improvement, in contrast to earlier studies reporting poorer outcomes in patients with more severe preoperative symptoms (Soda et al., 2022; Tetreault et al., 2013, 2015, 2018). However, in these studies, myelopathy symptoms were assessed using mJOA scores, which mainly focus on neurological symptoms judged by surgeons and not by patients themselves, thereby introducing assessment or reporting bias. EMS was reported to be able to reveal functional deficits better than the JOA score by additionally assessing proprioception and coordination (Vitzthum and Dalitz, 2007). Consistent with other studies (Karpova et al., 2013; Tetreault et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2024), young age, non-smoking status, and low BMI were linked to better EMS improvements.

The rate of complications remains controversial owing to the numerous complications (Ghogawala et al., 2021; Yoshii et al., 2021; Morishita et al., 2020). In this study, the laminectomy group had fewer patient-reported complications, particularly lower incidences of hoarseness and dysphagia. These are the major complications associated with the anterior approach. While several studies have reported a high incidence of infection with the posterior approach (Kato et al., 2017; Morishita et al., 2020), in this study, no significant differences were found between the groups both in surgeon-reported and patient-reported infection rates. One might expect a higher complication rate in the laminectomy group, given their older age and presumed increased susceptibility to complications. However, contrary to this expectation, the ACCF group exhibited a substantially higher complication rate. These findings suggest that ACCF may be associated with a higher rate of reoperations compared with laminectomy. The relatively low surgeon-reported complication rates observed in the present study should be interpreted with caution. Although Swespine prospectively records complications in routine clinical practice, complications may still be underreported, partly because the relatively short hospital stay may reduce the likelihood of documenting complications that become clinically apparent after discharge. Therefore, the complication rates observed in this registry may not be directly comparable with those reported in prospective clinical studies with dedicated complication surveillance.

The overall reoperation rates were low (6% for ACCF and 3% for laminectomy). Because the groups were not comparable at baseline, we specifically examined the variables that differed significantly between them. Patients undergoing ACCF had more favorable baseline characteristics (younger age, fewer compressed levels, and higher baseline EMS). Notably, surgical method, age, sex, BMI, number of operated levels, and smoking status were not associated with reoperation rate. Only baseline EMS and year of operation were independently associated with fewer reoperations. Among after-discharge reoperations, the rate of decompressions at a new index level was higher in the ACCF group than in the laminectomy group (2% vs. 0%, p < 0.01). This may be explained by the higher risk of adjacent segment pathology after fusion surgery, which is supported by other studies (Hilibrand and Robbins, 2004). Furthermore, younger age was identified as a potential risk factor for this pathology after anterior cervical fusion (Wu et al., 2019). In our cohort, patients in the ACCF group were younger, which might have contributed to the observed outcomes.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design with suspected selection bias; reliance on patient-reported outcomes for complications that might be subjective and prone to bias; variability in surgical procedures determined by individual surgeons and institutions; and unequal group sizes that may introduce an imbalance in baseline characteristics despite statistical adjustment. To minimize the potential impact of surgical variability, the institution was included as a random effect in a mixed-effects model, allowing us to adjust for clustering and differences in surgical practices across centers. Propensity score matching can address this problem, but has drawbacks, including loss of power and information. Furthermore, because radiographic data were unavailable, we could not evaluate important factors influencing surgical approach selection, such as cervical alignment and K-line status (Lee et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2018; Sakai et al., 2016). Laminectomy has been associated with postoperative kyphosis (Hirai et al., 2018; Bao et al., 2025; Montano et al., 2019), whereas muscle-preserving laminectomy may better preserve cervical alignment (Nagoshi et al., 2025; Nori et al., 2018). This cohort included both conventional and muscle-preserving laminectomies, and we were unable to analyze these techniques separately. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of established surgical indications and should not be generalized to all patients with DCM.

The strengths include the large cohort size, independent patient reporting of PROMs, and multicenter registry-based design, which provides broad coverage and high external validity.

Conclusion

Laminectomy for DCM achieves patient-reported myelopathy improvement comparable to that of ACCF and is associated with fewer patient-reported complications in this nationwide registry. These findings support laminectomy as an effective posterior surgical option for appropriately selected patients, while the choice of surgical approach should remain individualized according to patient characteristics and radiographic findings.
