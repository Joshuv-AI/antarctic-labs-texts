Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Confocal Laser Endomicroscopy for Predicting Invasion Depth of Superficial Esophageal Mucosal Lesions: A Cohort Study
Authors: Yue Xu, Renquan Luo, Jiali Tang, Lu Liu, Haideng Yang, Jinbang Peng, Zhenzhen Wang, Xiuxiu Jin, Lingyan Shen, Lingling Yan, Jiacheng Li, Binbin Gu, Jun Wang, Xiang Jin, Xianbin Zhou, Ying Zhou, Jiaxiu Ying, Congni Zhu, Siyan Yan, Shaowei Li, Xinli Mao, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Accurate assessment of invasion depth in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma is essential for optimal treatment. Probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy (pCLE) enables real-time in vivo imaging, but its interpretation depends heavily on endoscopist expertise. This study aimed to develop and validate an artificial intelligence-assisted pCLE (AI-pCLE) system for the differential diagnosis of low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN) from high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia with submucosal invasion (HGIN-SM1).METHODS:In this retrospective single-center study, 1,234 pCLE images were used to develop and validate the AI model. Histopathology from resected specimens served as the reference standard. Six deep learning algorithms were evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). The best-performing model was selected and compared with 10 endoscopists.RESULTS:For the differentiation between LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions, the AI-pCLE system exhibited a sensitivity of 97.4%, specificity of 92.6%, accuracy of 95.3%, PPV of 94.17%, and NPV of 96.70%, respectively. In comparison, the mean sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV of the endoscopists were 83.79%, 85.26%, 84.46%, 88.14%, and 82.61%, respectively. With AI-pCLE assistance, the accuracy, sensitivity, and NPV of the endoscopists increased to 94.41% (P = 0.001), 96.98% (P = 0.006), and 96.24% (P = 0.001), respectively. The diagnostic performance of the AI-assisted system was comparable with that of expert endoscopists.DISCUSSION:The AI-pCLE system demonstrated robust diagnostic performance in differentiating LGIN from HGIN-SM1 lesions, indicating its potential as a reliable tool for evaluating the invasion depth of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors of the digestive tract worldwide. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) represents the predominant histological subtype, accounting for 85% of all cases (1). The Japanese Esophageal Society's guidelines recommend endoscopic resection for high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia(HGIN), lesions with invasion confined to the lamina propria mucosa and muscularis mucosa layers, and submucosal cancers with invasion ≤200 μm (submucosal layer 1 [SM1]), whereas low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN) could be managed with endoscopic surveillance (2). Accordingly, accurate differentiation between LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions is critical for selecting the optimal therapeutic strategy for these patients.

Currently, accurate differentiation between LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions still remains a substantial clinical challenge. Although narrow-band imaging (NBI) represents the standard endoscopic modality for evaluating superficial ESCC (3), its limited magnification (typically 80×) precludes precise cellular-level assessment and shows low sensitivity for detecting LGIN (4,5). By contrast, probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy (pCLE) is an emerging imaging technique that enables real-time visualization of cellular morphology during endoscopy examination (6). Several studies have shown that pCLE has significant diagnostic value for NBI-occult lesions and demonstrates high accuracy in identifying early ESCC and HGIN (7,8). However, its clinical implementation requires endoscopists to possess robust pathological knowledge and undergo dedicated specialized training (9).

Considerable advances have been made in artificial intelligence (AI) technology for medical imaging diagnosis. Deep learning algorithms based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been increasingly applied to the diagnosis of esophageal diseases (10–12). By standardizing the image analysis process, these algorithms can mitigate interobserver variability and offer objective decision support for junior endoscopists, thereby improving diagnostic consistency and accuracy (13). A novel deep convolutional neural network system previously developed by our team has demonstrated excellent performance in detecting ESCC under NBI endoscopy, with 100% sensitivity, and successfully identified additional LGIN and HGIN lesions (14). Therefore, based on these results, we further developed and evaluated an AI-assisted probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy system (AI-pCLE) for predicting invasion depth, with the aim of accurately differentiating LGIN from HGIN-SM1 lesions.

METHODS

Patients and cohorts

We retrospectively enrolled pCLE images of LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions obtained from the Department of Gastroenterology at Taizhou Hospital between March 2023 and March 2025. Duplicate and low-quality images that could interfere with image interpretation were excluded, including those with artifacts, blur, defocus, bleeding, and surfaces with mucus (Figure 1). All pCLE images were acquired using an endoscope (Olympus GIF-H260Z or GIF-H290Z) with GastroFlex ultra-high definition probes (Cellvizio, Manoaqua, Paris, France) and stored in portable network graphics format.

Flow chart. AI-pCLE, artificial intelligence-assisted confocal laser endomicroscopy; HGIN-SM1 lesions, high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia-submucosal invasion <200 μm; LGIN, low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia.

A total of 1,234 pCLE images from 128 patients were enrolled in this study. Data set partitioning was performed at the patient level, ensuring that images from the same patient were assigned exclusively to either the training set or the independent test set. Approximately 80% of patients were allocated to the training set, with the remaining 20% assigned to the test set. Histological diagnosis served as the gold standard for image classification. Based on histopathological findings, pCLE images were manually annotated as either LGIN or HGIN-SM1 by 2 endoscopists, each with ≥5 years of experience in confocal endomicroscopy. Discrepancies in annotation were resolved by a senior endoscopist. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou Hospital (No. K20240937) and conducted in strict compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before study enrollment.

Development and evaluation of the AI system

To improve the model's robustness and generalization capability, we implemented multistage preprocessing for the input images as follows: First, all input images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels, and geometric transformations (including rotation and flipping) were applied to augment data diversity. Second, edge enhancement operations (including deboxing and stroking) was performed to highlight critical image features. Finally, pixel-level normalization was conducted to standardize the pixel value distribution.

To achieve high-precision classification of the confocal images of esophageal lesions, representative deep learning models were adopted, including model 1 (MobileNetV3), model 2 (EfficientNetV2), model 3 (ShuffleNetV2), model 4 (residual network 34 [ResNet-34], model 5 [DenseNet-121], and model 6 [ConvNeXT]) (Figure 2). All networks were initialized with ImageNet pretraining weights, with a uniform training configuration being maintained, including a batch size of 32, an initial learning rate of 0.001, and 30 training epochs in total. The loss function adopted was cross-entropy, whereas optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer. Under these settings, the performance of various models was assessed using the training set, and the effective parameters for the prediction accuracy model were obtained. The model with the best performance was subjected to test evaluation in the validation set.

Framework of AI-pCLE. AI-pCLE, artificial intelligence-assisted confocal laser endomicroscopy; HGIN, high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia-submucosal invasion <200 μm; LGIN, low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia.

To improve model interpretability, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied to generate heatmaps that highlight the image regions contributing to the model's predictions.

Comparison between AI-pCLE and endoscopists

The diagnostic efficacy of the AI-assisted pCLE system was evaluated using an independent test Data set. To comprehensively evaluate the model's classification performance, we quantified multiple core metrics including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy; in addition, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to calculate the area under the curve and generate confusion matrices. Furthermore, we compared the diagnostic performance of the AI model with that of endoscopists, who were stratified by clinical experience levels: expert group (≥10 years of experience), midlevel group (≥5 years of experience), and junior group (<5 years of experience). The diagnostic accuracy of these endoscopists was assessed under 2 parallel conditions: with AI assistance and without AI assistance. Critically, only the labeling physicians, pathology reviewers, and algorithm development team had access to the imaging and histopathological diagnostic data, whereas the 10 endoscopists tasked with interpreting the test images remained fully blinded to all clinical metadata throughout the entire evaluation process.

Statistical analysis

Performance metrics were primarily calculated at the individual image level. All quantitative metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, were reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). For image-level analyses, 95% CI were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact binomial method.

Because multiple pCLE images could originate from the same lesion or patient, potentially introducing within-patient correlation, 95% CIs were additionally estimated using cluster-robust variance estimators, with individual patients designated as the clustering unit. Comparative analyses were performed using the McNemar test, including pairwise comparisons between AI model predictions and endoscopists' interpretations on the same test Data set, as well as within-endoscopist comparisons of diagnostic performance with versus without AI assistance; 2-sided P values were reported for all such analyses.

Agreement between the AI model and individual endoscopists was evaluated using the Cohen kappa coefficient, with values calculated separately for each experience group (expert, midlevel, and junior) and summarized descriptively. Overall interobserver agreement cross all participating endoscopists was assessed using the Fleiss kappa statistic. Using postoperative histopathology as the reference standard, paired binary diagnostic results for the same lesions were compared between the AI-pCLE system and preoperative endoscopic biopsy using the McNemar test. Agreement between the AI-pCLE system and biopsy was assessed using the Cohen kappa coefficient. A 2-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the patients

Between March 2023 and March 2025, a total of 1,234 pCLE images acquired from 128 patients with esophageal lesions were retrospectively retrieved from the clinical database of Taizhou Hospital. Eligible images were enrolled for analysis following rigorous screening criteria, including pathological diagnosis, image quality evaluation, and clinical data integrity. Stratified random sampling method was used to divide the data into a training set (1,023 images corresponding to 127 lesions: 43 LGIN and 84 HGIN-SM1 lesions) and an independent test set (211 images corresponding to 30 lesions: 9 LGIN and 21 HGIN-SM1 lesions), which ensured a representative distribution of lesions in both sets. Comparison between AI-pCLE and endoscopists

The diagnostic performance of the optimized AI-pCLE model, developed through previous validation, was compared with the results of 10 endoscopists. The endoscopists achieved an overall accuracy of 84.46%, sensitivity of 83.79%, specificity of 85.26%, PPV of 88.14%, and NPV of 82.61%, with experts outperforming mid-level and junior colleagues. The AI-pCLE system showed numerically higher accuracy (95.3% vs 91.5%), sensitivity (97.4% vs 90.8%), and specificity (92.6% vs 92.3%) than expert endoscopists, with no statistically significant differences(P > 0.05). Notably, compared with 3 midlevel endoscopists (85.9%) and 4 junior endoscopists (78.08%), the AI-pCLE system was shown to achieve significantly greater accuracy (95.3%; P < 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively). Beyond improving the diagnostic performance of endoscopists, AI assistance also improved their interobserver agreement. Among expert, midlevel, and junior endoscopists, the interobserver agreement was substantial (κ = 0.778), substantial (κ = 0.647), and moderate (κ = 0.418), respectively. The AI system yielded the strongest interobserver consistency with expert endoscopists (κ = 0.764), followed by midlevel endoscopists (κ = 0.665) and junior endoscopists (κ = 0.448). Moreover, with the assistance of AI-pCLE, the interobserver agreement among midlevel and junior endoscopists improved to nearly perfect agreement, whereas the interobserver consistency of expert endoscopists showed no significant change (see Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B531 summarizing interobserver agreement results).

Comparison of AI-pCLE with biopsy

Using histopathological diagnosis from endoscopic resection specimens as the reference standard, we compared the histopathologic discrepancy rates of AI-pCLE and biopsy against the reference standard, and further evaluated the diagnostic agreement between AI-generated predictions and biopsy results. For overall lesions, the discrepancy rates were 4.74% for AI-pCLE and 29.17% for biopsy, with no statistically significant difference between the 2 methods (P = 0.655). Consistent results were noted in subgroup analyses for LGIN (7.37% vs 14.29%; P = 1.000) and HGIN-SM1 lesions (2.59% vs 35.29%; P = 0.564). Consistent analysis revealed high consistency between AI-pCLE predictions and postoperative histopathology (κ = 0.904, P < 0.05), as well as between biopsy pathology and postoperative histopathology (κ = 0.841, P < 0.05). In addition, substantial diagnostic agreement was observed between AI-pCLE and biopsy results (κ = 0.735, P < 0.05), indicating good diagnostic concordance between the 2 methods (See Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B532 comparison of AI-pCLE with biopsy).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we successfully established an AI-assisted diagnostic system based on pCLE. The AI system achieved accurate identification of LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions through multimodal validation. The AI-pCLE system achieved diagnostic performance comparable with expert endoscopists, while significantly improving the diagnostic accuracy of midlevel and junior endoscopists. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and validate an AI-assisted pCLE system for distinguishing LGIN from HGIN-SM1 lesions, suggesting its potential clinical value in confocal endoscopic diagnosis.

AI-assisted NBI exhibits high sensitivity in the early diagnosis of esophageal cancer, yet its accuracy in lesion subtyping remains limited, especially in differentiating LGIN from HGIN-SM1 lesions (15,16). Previous studies have attempted to combine AI with pCLE to improve diagnostic performance. Ma et al (17) established an AI-assisted pCLE system for distinguishing esophageal cancer from noncancer, achieving an accuracy of 90.9% and sensitivity of 92.0%, respectively; however, this model was not designed to evaluate the invasion depth of esophageal lesions. It should be noted that NBI, as a conventional endoscopic technique, can identify esophageal cancer and noncancer, and is more convenient operationally than pCLE, which requires a dedicated confocal probe (18). However, the unique cellular horizontal resolution of pCLE can provide a solid technical basis for precise histopathologic subtyping (6).In this study, the AI-pCLE system achieved an accuracy of 95.3% and a sensitivity of 97.4%, which were numerically higher than the corresponding values for expert endoscopists, although the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Given that the diagnostic performance of pCLE is comparable with that of traditional biopsy (19), this AI system might serve as a potential optical biopsy tool, offering an objective foundation for the surveillance of LGIN and the selection of therapeutic strategies for HGIN-SM1 lesions.

Previous studies have used AI-assisted white-light imaging or NBI to distinguish esophageal cancer from noncancer or identify different invasive depths (e.g., EP-SM1 and SM2-3) (20–22). For example, Wang et al developed an AI model based on a single-shot multibox detector to distinguish LGIN, HGIN, and ESCC, with a histological grading accuracy of 92%. However, the specificity was 70.4%, which may lead to unnecessary treatment (23). By contrast, the pCLE used in this study can obtain real-time images of tumor subcellular structures and accurately evaluate cell morphology and vascular characteristics. This technical advantage may contribute to the high specificity of our AI-pCLE system (92%), potentially reducing unnecessary treatments such as endoscopic submucosal dissection caused by the over grading of LGIN lesions. These findings highlight the unique value of pCLE in providing cellular-level imaging information and support its role as a promising tool for the precise diagnosis of esophageal cancer.

In clinical practice, AI-pCLE may be positioned as a problem-solving modality after NBI examination. NBI serves as the first-line tool for screening and lesion delineation, whereas pCLE may be applied selectively to NBI-positive or equivocal regions when distinguishing LGIN from HGIN-SM1 remains challenging, when precise grading is required to guide treatment selection, or when biopsy sampling error is a concern. In this context, AI-pCLE outputs, including diagnostic classifications and probability scores, may assist endoscopists in identifying diagnostically relevant confocal fields, improving interpretative consistency regardless of operator experience, and supporting real-time decisions regarding surveillance, endoscopic resection, or targeted biopsy. Importantly, this approach is designed to complement rather than replace histopathology and may optimize the use of pCLE by reserving it for cases in which NBI alone is inconclusive.

To assess the clinical value of the AI-pCLE system, this study compared the diagnostic performance of endoscopists with varying levels of experience before and after AI-pCLE assistance. In the absence of AI-pCLE system assistance, midlevel and junior endoscopists show lower accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity compared with expert endoscopists, confirming that there is an empirical dependence on confocal diagnosis. Previous studies have reported that AI systems can improve the diagnostic performance of nonexpert operators; however, their postassistance performance remains inferior to that of expert endoscopists (20,24). In our study, AI-pCLE significantly improved the diagnostic performance of both midlevel and junior endoscopists (P < 0.01). Notably, the diagnostic accuracy of junior endoscopists reached 94.67%, which was comparable with the independent diagnosis of expert endoscopists (91.47%). Furthermore, interobserver agreement among endoscopists improved from moderate to nearly perfect levels, indicating that the AI-pCLE system could mitigate subjective interpretive variability by deep learning algorithms. Accordingly, this system holds great promise as a clinical decision-making tool to assist nonexpert endoscopists in accurately identifying and stratifying esophageal lesions.

The AI-pCLE system developed in this study exhibited distinct advantages in the diagnosis of early esophageal cancer. Li et al (25) established a computer-aided diagnostic system to distinguish noncancerous lesions from early ESCC, improving endoscopists' diagnostic sensitivity from 73.9% to 92.4%. However, this system required dual-modal imaging in combination with NBI and white-light imaging, which entailed relatively complicated operation. Ma et al (17) used an integrated confocal laser endomicroscopy system to differentiate noncancerous lesions from early ESCC, improving endoscopists' diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity from 88.2% and 80.4% to 88.6% and 89.8%, respectively. By contrast, AI-pCLE achieved a more substantial improvement in diagnostic performance for LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions under a single confocal imaging mode, with sensitivity and accuracy increasing from 83.79% and 84.46% to 96.98% and 94.41%, respectively (P < 0.01). Such a remarkable enhancement may be ascribed to the accurate identification of microscopic features by deep learning algorithms in combination with confocal imaging.

In the validation of AI-pCLE, AI assistance was found to improve endoscopists' specificity and PPV, albeit without reaching statistical significance (P > 0.05). This finding may be attributed to the experts' inherently high diagnostic proficiency, which led to more conservative interpretation of lesions with AI support, thus increasing the incidence of false positive results. Accordingly, there were significant improvements to sensitivity and NPV without commensurate improvements in specificity or PPV, which could be addressed by optimizing AI system performance or reconfirming lesions.

Similarly to the work by Zhang et al, (26) who systematically evaluated deep CNNs in the classification of gastroesophageal reflux disease, this study further demonstrated that the DenseNet architecture delivered favorable performance well in the classification of superficial esophageal lesions. The core merit of DenseNet lies in its dense connection mechanism, which could enable the cross-layer features reuse, integrate multilevel pathological information derived from confocal images, and enhance the ability of the model to identify subtle morphological changes in target tissues. DenseNet not only alleviated the gradient-vanishing problem in deep networks but also improved the training efficiency and classification accuracy. Our study further supported the applicability of DenseNet in high-resolution medical image analysis and provides a technical basis for AI-assisted diagnosis of esophageal lesions. In addition, Grad-CAM visualization showed that the model focused predominantly on diagnostically relevant regions, including abnormal cellular and vascular patterns. This observation provided qualitative evidence supporting the plausibility and interpretability of the model's predictions.

The AI-pCLE system developed in this study demonstrated significant advantages in multiple aspects. First, in diagnosing esophageal lesions, the system showed excellent accuracy (95.3%), sensitivity (97.4%), and specificity (92.6%). This superior efficacy was likely attributable to the robust feature-extraction capability of the deep neural networks to extract features from pCLE images when analyzing pCLE images. Second, we implemented a stratified evaluation strategy, comparing deep CNNs with different computational complexities, including the lightweight MobileNetV3 and ShuffleNet, balanced models such as EfficientNetV2 and ResNet-34, and complex architectures such as DenseNet-121 and ConvNeXt, and ultimately, identified the optimal model by comprehensive multimodel comparative analysis. Through this hierarchical evaluation strategy, we comprehensively characterized the performance of networks of differently scaled deep networks for ESCC images, thereby laying a robust evidence-based foundation for model selection in practical clinical applications. Most importantly, the system could significantly improve the diagnostic accuracy of endoscopists (from 84.46% to 94.41%; P < 0.05), especially junior endoscopists with limited clinical experience. Owing to these advantages, this system may serve as a valuable auxiliary tool in the clinical diagnosis of esophageal lesions.

Beyond diagnostic performance, several factors should be considered for translating AI-pCLE into routine clinical practice. From a translational perspective, the clinical implementation of AI-pCLE would require regulatory approval as software-as-a-medical-device (SaMD), external validation across diverse patient populations, and standardized quality control protocols for image acquisition (27). Furthermore, targeted training and familiarization would be necessary to ensure that endoscopists could effectively integrate AI outputs into real-time decision-making. Although AI assistance has the potential to shorten the learning curve for pCLE interpretation, it remains essential that endoscopists master the fundamental principles of confocal imaging and understand the inherent limitations of the system (28). Future studies are warranted to evaluate the usability, workflow impact, and cost-effectiveness of AI-pCLE in routine clinical settings.

This study involved certain methodological limitations that should be acknowledged. First, given the single-center, retrospective design used, the external validity of our findings required further validation in prospective multicenter studies. Second, the exclusion of low-quality images may introduce selection bias and spectrum bias, which could partially overestimate diagnostic performance, particularly specificity, and reduce the applicability of the model to more heterogeneous real-world clinical images. In addition, the relatively small independent test cohort may have limited the statistical power of some comparisons, particularly subgroup analyses and comparisons with expert endoscopists, which may increase the risk of type II error and lead to potential underestimation of true differences. Moreover, because multiple pCLE images could originate from the same lesion or patient, image-level analyses may introduce within-patient correlation; therefore, cluster-robust variance estimators were applied, with patients treated as the clustering unit. Third, the anatomical distribution in the test cohort was not adequately balanced, and lesions from certain regions (e.g., the cervical or abdominal esophagus) were not represented, which might limit generalizability of our findings to these specific sites. Further prospective multicenter studies with broader anatomical representation are warranted. Fourth, the model training Data set did not incorporate imaging features of benign esophageal lesions and normal esophageal images, which might compromise the diagnostic specificity in routine clinical practice. However, it should be noted that these benign lesions could be clearly distinguished using NBI, which partially mitigated this limitation (18,29,30). Finally, our system was developed based on static image analysis, whereas routine clinical diagnosis relied on dynamic video-based endoscopic evaluation. In the future, we will improve the deep learning architecture and establish a multimodal diagnostic system to comprehensively evaluate the added value of this AI-assisted strategy.

In conclusion, to the best of our knowledge, this study developed a novel AI-pCLE diagnostic system that enabled accurate differentiation between esophageal LGIN and HGIN-SM1 lesions. The system exhibited diagnostic performance comparable with that of expert endoscopists, and significantly enhanced the diagnostic capability of mid-level and junior endoscopists. These findings demonstrate that AI-pCLE holds great promise as a reliable clinical decision-support tool for the early and precise stratification of esophageal lesions.
