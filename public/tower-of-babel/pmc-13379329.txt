From Kimchi to Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis: Leuconostoc citreum LB-P8 as a Microbiome-Based Therapeutic Approach
Authors: Sarah Peisl, Espen Melum
Published: 2026

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic, progressive cholestatic liver disease with a lack of United States Food and Drug Administration/European Medicines Agency–approved medical therapies, and so far, liver transplantation remains the only definitive option. Although a phase III trial with long-term positive benefit has been reported and several promising trials are ongoing, there is a need for improved treatment strategies.1 In recent years, there has been an increased focus on the gut-liver axis in PSC, consistently linking the intestinal microbiota to disease pathogenesis and progression.2 The strong clinical association between PSC and inflammatory bowel disease further supports this concept, suggesting that intestinal dysbiosis and mucosal immune dysfunction may contribute to hepatobiliary injury.1 As such, modulation of the gut microbiome represents an attractive treatment approach for PSC. Against this backdrop, the study by Park and colleagues provides a comprehensive and thorough evaluation of a microbiome-based therapeutic approach using Leuconostoc citreum LB-P8, a bacterial strain originally isolated from Kimchi, as a novel intervention in cholestatic liver disease.3 In this study, reported in the current issue of Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology, the authors employ a multilayered approach by combining in vitro systems with multiple in vivo models, alongside metabolomic analyses, 16S microbiome profiling, and spatial transcriptomics, to examine the effects of LB-P8.

Gut-Liver Crosstalk and Antifibrotic Signaling

First, by using an in vitro coculture system with stellate cells and intestinal epithelial cells as a model for the gut-liver axis, the authors provide mechanistic evidence that LB-P8 can modulate fibrogenic signaling. Exposure of intestinal epithelial cells to LB-P8, compared with a microbial control, attenuated transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)–driven activation of hepatic stellate cells, accompanied by reduced expression of profibrotic and inflammatory genes. By performing transcriptomic analyses, the study further points to interference with TGF-β–mediated signaling, supporting the notion that microbiota-derived factors may directly influence core profibrotic pathways that could be relevant to PSC via the gut-liver axis.4,5

The observations using the coculture system were followed up by animal experiments using a range of different models of cholestatic liver injury, including bile duct ligation, 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidin (DDC) diet, and genetic Mdr2-deficient mice (Mdr2-/-). Across these models, LB-P8 treatment was associated with consistent improvements in fibrosis, inflammation, and biochemical markers, as well as reduced expression of key inflammatory and profibrotic mediators. However, effects were less pronounced in the genetic model, pointing towards the heterogeneity of the models used. Park et al further explored regulated pathways for microbiota-driven modulation of fibrogenic signaling by spatial profiling using Nanostring GeoMX analyses of the DDC model. Effects from LB-P8 treatment could be demonstrated across cholangiocytes, myofibroblasts, and immune cells, including attenuation of TGF-β signaling in epithelial compartments and modulation of immune-associated transcriptional programs within the peribiliary niche.

Dual Mechanism of Action: Microbiome Remodeling and Metabolite-Mediated Effects of LB-P8

The in vivo effects of fresh LB-P8 administration in bile duct–ligated mice were accompanied by remodeling of the gut microbiota, characterized by suppression of potentially pathogenic taxa such as Enterobacteriaceae and restoration of beneficial commensals, including Alistipes, Marvinbryantia, and members of the Lachnospiraceae family. These findings strongly suggest that there is an interaction between LB-P8 and the gut microbiome that partly mediates the therapeutic effect.

By performing metabolomic analyses, Park et al provide data on potential driving molecules as the basis for their observations. Conditioned media from LB-P8–treated intestinal epithelial cells in the in vitro system exhibited a distinct metabolic signature that suppressed profibrotic and inflammatory gene expression in both stellate cells and macrophages, supporting a metabolite-driven mechanism of gut-liver crosstalk in addition to the microbiome remodeling. The authors further corroborate this using a subset of candidate molecules, including sinensetin, plumbagin, allopurinol, 3-hydroxyvaleric acid, and indolelactic acid, that recapitulated the beneficial effects of live LB-P8 when orally administered to DDC-fed mice.

Implications for Intestinal Inflammation

In addition to the liver centric observations, Park et al also provide evidence that LB-P8 is of relevance for gut inflammation, an observation of particular interest for PSC given the close clinical association with inflammatory bowel disease. In DSS-treated mice, a murine colitis model, LB-P8 ameliorated inflammation and fibrosis. Treatment was also associated with improvements in disease activity, preservation of colon length, and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, alongside decreased expression of profibrotic and inflammatory mediators. These findings suggest that targeting the gut-liver axis may have implications across both compartments rather than treating them as separate disease entities.

Toward Microbiome-Based Therapeutic Strategies in Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis

Through comprehensive and well-executed experiments, Park et al demonstrate clear efficacy of LB-P8 that is preserved with lyophilized preparations, although some effects appear more pronounced with live bacteria. Importantly, it seems like the beneficial properties of LB-P8 are not strictly dependent on bacterial viability, with repercussions for development of potential therapeutics. The identification of bioactive metabolites capable of recapitulating key effects in vivo further supports the feasibility of metabolite-based approaches as an alternative to live microbial therapies. Still, key questions remain regarding the optimal mode of administration, the role of live bacteria, and whether stabilized formulations or defined metabolite combinations offer the most effective and practical strategy for treatment.

This current study aligns well with growing interest in microbiome-targeted therapies, including probiotics, antibiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation, which have yielded promising results, although evidence from large prospective studies and randomized controlled trials is still limited.6

What remains is to determine whether LP-B8 or Kimchi is ready for treatment for patients with PSC. With a phase II clinical trial already underway (ClinicalTrials.gov, Number: NCT06699121), forthcoming data will be critical to determine whether these promising preclinical effects of LB-P8 translate into meaningful therapeutic benefit in patients with PSC.
