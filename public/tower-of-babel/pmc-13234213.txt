Anterior cervical perforation by levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device threads with preserved intrauterine position: A case report
Authors: Taina Ferreira Sanches, Carolina Paschoal Cucci, Mariane Nunes de Nadai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 42-year-old woman presented for routine gynecological evaluation without symptoms. During speculum examination, the threads of a 19.5 mg levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device (LNG-IUD) were observed emerging through the anterior cervical lip outside the endocervical canal. Three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound confirmed correct positioning of the LNG-IUD within the uterine cavity despite the anomalous thread trajectory. After intracervical local anesthesia, the exteriorized thread segment was sectioned to release the transfixing tract, and the device was successfully removed under ultrasound guidance using Hartmann forceps without complications. The patient remained asymptomatic during follow-up. This case highlights that visible LNG-IUD threads do not always indicate normal endocervical positioning and emphasizes the importance of careful gynecological examination and three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound evaluation for diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Intrauterine devices are widely used contraceptive methods and are considered safe, effective, and long-acting. Among the devices available in Brazil, copper intrauterine devices and levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine devices (LNG-IUDs) are the most commonly used. Despite their widespread use and favorable safety profile, complications may occur. The most frequent adverse events include changes in bleeding patterns, dyspareunia, and absence of visible strings during gynecological examination [1], [2].

Among levonorgestrel-releasing systems, previous studies have not demonstrated significant differences in efficacy or expulsion rates between the 52 mg LNG-IUD and lower-dose systems such as the 19.5 mg device [3], [4].

Uterine perforation is one of the least frequent but potentially most serious complications associated with intrauterine contraception. It usually involves the uterine body and may occur during insertion or later due to migration of the device. However, perforations specifically localized to the cervix are infrequently described in the literature.

The literature includes only a limited number of reports describing cervical perforation associated with LNG-IUD threads. Boog and Kumar described perforation of the anterior cervical lip by the threads of a copper intrauterine device, highlighting the importance of clinical vigilance even when the gynecological examination appears otherwise normal [5]. Such situations may be associated with pelvic pain, bleeding, and difficulty during device removal, in addition to the potential risk of injury to adjacent structures [5], [6], [7].

Missing strings may raise suspicion of expulsion, retraction into the uterine cavity, or uterine perforation. However, infrequently described cases demonstrate that the strings may remain visible while perforating cervical tissue outside the endocervical canal, usually through the anterior cervical lip [5], [6], [7]. These situations may represent a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, particularly when imaging confirms that the LNG-IUD remains correctly positioned within the uterine cavity.

The present report describes the case of a woman undergoing routine gynecological evaluation in whom LNG-IUD threads were identified emerging through the anterior cervical lip despite preserved intrauterine positioning of the device.

Case Presentation

A 42-year-old woman, gravida 2 para 2, attended a gynecological consultation for routine evaluation. She denied gynecological, urinary, or systemic symptoms. The patient reported use of a 19.5 mg levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device inserted approximately three years earlier at another healthcare facility, without known complications after insertion. No regular gynecological follow-up had been performed since placement.

During speculum examination, the LNG-IUD threads were observed emerging through an anomalous tract transfixing the anterior cervical lip (Fig. 1). The external cervical os appeared preserved, without evidence of inflammation, bleeding, or abnormal discharge. On bimanual examination, the uterus was anteverted, mobile, and of normal size, without cervical motion tenderness. After intracervical local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine, the exteriorized portion of the LNG-IUD thread traversing the anterior cervical lip was sectioned to release the transfixing tract. Gentle traction was subsequently applied using Hartmann forceps under ultrasound guidance, allowing complete removal of the device without complications. Following the procedure, counseling regarding alternative contraceptive methods was provided, and the patient opted for a progestin-only oral contraceptive. Follow-up evaluation 30 days later demonstrated no symptoms or evidence of complications.

Discussion

The primary functions of intrauterine device threads are to allow confirmation of device presence and facilitate removal. Alterations in thread visibility are relatively common in clinical practice and are generally attributed to retraction into the cervical canal or uterine cavity, unnoticed expulsion, or uterine perforation [8], [9], [10]. However, the presence of visible threads in an abnormal location, particularly outside the endocervical canal, represents an unusual finding requiring careful evaluation.

In infrequently described cases, LNG-IUD threads may remain visible while perforating cervical tissue. Gbolade reported a case in which the threads of a levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system protruded through the anterior cervical lip approximately 1 cm from the external cervical os, suggesting formation of a fistulous tract following trauma associated with tenaculum use during insertion [11]. Similar cases were previously described by Silverman and Rubinstein, who attributed the perforation to the “curved memory” of threads in older intrauterine device models [12].

Compared with previously published reports, the present case is notable because the LNG-IUD remained correctly positioned within the uterine cavity despite the presence of a transfixing cervical tract. This finding supports the hypothesis that thread-related cervical perforation may occur independently of device displacement, possibly secondary to prior cervical trauma or localized tissue remodeling.

One of the most accepted hypotheses is that traumatic cervical lesions, particularly those related to tenaculum use during insertion, may lead to fistulous tract formation through which the threads migrate or become encapsulated within cervical tissue during healing. These alterations are frequently asymptomatic or may present only with nonspecific symptoms such as mild pelvic discomfort or irregular bleeding [7].

The present case emphasizes that visibility of LNG-IUD threads does not necessarily indicate normal positioning within the endocervical canal. Careful speculum examination is essential, especially when the threads appear to emerge from atypical locations. In these situations, three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound plays an important role in confirming correct positioning of the device and assisting in management planning. Failure to recognize this condition may result in inappropriate removal attempts, increasing the risk of tissue injury or incomplete extraction.

The case also demonstrates that outpatient removal under ultrasound guidance may be safely and effectively performed even in the presence of cervical perforation by LNG-IUD threads. After intracervical local anesthesia, the exteriorized thread segment was sectioned to release the transfixing tract, followed by gentle traction using Hartmann forceps under ultrasound guidance, allowing complete removal without complications. This minimally invasive approach is consistent with previously described reports involving clinically stable patients.

Although most reported cases are asymptomatic or associated with nonspecific symptoms, awareness of this condition is important because early diagnosis allows adequate procedural planning and minimizes the risk of additional cervical injury.

Three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound is a valuable tool for confirming correct device positioning even in cases of atypical thread localization. In previously described reports as well as the present case, LNG-IUD removal was successfully accomplished without major complications, supporting outpatient management in selected patients.

Conclusion

Cervical perforation caused by LNG-IUD threads is an infrequently described complication, with few cases reported in the literature. Although generally asymptomatic, these atypical presentations require careful attention during gynecological examination and imaging evaluation, particularly when the threads are visible in an abnormal location.

The present case highlights that visible LNG-IUD threads do not always indicate normal endocervical positioning. Cervical perforation should be considered when threads are identified emerging from atypical sites, even in asymptomatic patients. Careful speculum examination combined with three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound allows accurate diagnosis, and minimally invasive outpatient removal under ultrasound guidance appears to be a safe and effective management strategy.
