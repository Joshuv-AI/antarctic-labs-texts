Perspectives on Stochastic Localization
Bobby Shi, Kevin Tian, Matthew S. Zhang

Abstract
We survey different perspectives on the stochastic localization process of [ Eld13 ] , a powerful construction that has had many exciting recent applications in high-dimensional probability and algorithm design. Unlike prior surveys on this topic, our focus is on giving a self-contained presentation of all known alternative constructions of Eldan’s stochastic localization, with an emphasis on connections between different constructions. Our hope is that by collecting these perspectives, some of which had primarily arisen within a particular community (e.g., probability theory, theoretical computer science, information theory, or machine learning), we can broaden the accessibility of stochastic localization, and ease its future use.

Preliminaries

We repeatedly use the following two standard facts in stochastic calculus.

Lemma 1

(Itô’s lemma)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be twice-differentiable, and suppose
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
follows the SDE
\textup{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{m}_{t}\,\textup{d}t+\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{t}
where
\{\mathbf{m}_{t},\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are adapted to the filtration generated by a Wiener process
\{\bm{W}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Then
\{f(\mathbf{x}_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
is also a drift-diffusion process, following the SDE

\displaystyle\textup{d}f(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\textup{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{\top}\right\rangle\,\textup{d}t

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{m}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{\top}\right\rangle\Bigr)\,\textup{d}t+\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{t}\right\rangle.

Lemma 2

(Fokker-Planck equation)
.

Suppose
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
follows the SDE
\textup{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{m}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\,\textup{d}t+\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{t}
where
\{\mathbf{m}_{t},\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are adapted to the filtration generated by a Wiener process
\{\bm{W}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Then letting

denote the law of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we have for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\partial_{t}\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{m}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in[d]\times[d]}\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{ij}.

4
Diffusion models

In this section, we relate stochastic localization to a framework known as
denoising diffusion probablistic models
(DDPMs), which was popularized by
[
HJA20
,
SSKKEP21
]
and is widely used in the practice of generative modeling. The goal of diffusion models in the context of generative modeling is to sample from a distribution
\pi
, given a dataset of i.i.d. draws from it.

The DDPM framework in particular is based on a time-changed backwards Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process. Recall that the OU process is the Langevin dynamics with a standard Gaussian as its stationary measure, i.e., it follows the following SDE from
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\textup{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=-\mathbf{x}_{t}\,\textup{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\textup{d}\bm{B}_{t},\text{ where }\{\bm{B}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\text{ is a Wiener process.}

(5)

We make the following simple observation.

Lemma 6
.

The distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0}
in (
5
) is
\mathcal{N}(\exp(-t)\mathbf{x}_{0},(1-\exp(-2t))\mathbf{I}_{d})
.

Proof.

Solving the SDE in (
5
) as an Itô integral, we have

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\exp(-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-(t-s))\,\textup{d}\bm{B}_{s}.

The mean of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is thus
\exp(-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}
as claimed, and the covariance scales proportionally to the quadratic variation, i.e.,

2\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-2(t-s))\,\textup{d}s=1-\exp(-2t)
.
∎

Let us define
\pi_{t}
to be the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
according to (
5
), where we draw
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\pi
, the target of our sampling algorithm. We apply a one-to-one backwards time change
\tau:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
, i.e., a
\tau
that satisfies
\tau^{\prime}(t)<0
for all times
t\geq 0
, and sets
\tau(0)=\infty
and
\tau(\infty)=0
. This induces an equivalent backwards process, indexed by a backwards time
u\geq 0
:

\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}:=\mathbf{x}_{\tau^{-1}(u)},\quad\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}:=\pi_{\tau^{-1}(u)}.

(6)

To see how (
6
) relates to generative models, if we let
\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{\infty}
, which we can simulate with a draw from the stationary distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\mathbf{I}_{d})
, then running the “backwards process” and producing a sample
\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{\infty}
is equivalent to drawing from the target distribution
\pi^{\leftarrow}_{\infty}=\pi_{0}=\pi
.

We now give a helpful characterization of the backwards process
\{\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}\}_{u\geq 0}
. The following result is standard, and an early derivation of it can be found, e.g., in
[
And82
]
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\textup{d}\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}\,\textup{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\textup{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, where
\{\bm{B}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Wiener process. Let
\tau:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
satisfy
\tau^{\prime}(t)<0
for all
t\geq 0
,
\tau(0)=\infty
,
\tau(\infty)=0
. Letting
\pi_{t}
be the law of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, and following the notation (
6
), we have for a Wiener process
\{\bm{W}_{u}\}_{u\geq 0}
that

\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}=|(\tau^{-1})^{\prime}(u)|\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}+2\nabla\log\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(x^{\leftarrow}_{u})\bigr)\,\textup{d}u+\sqrt{2|(\tau^{-1})^{\prime}(u)|}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{u}\,.

Proof.

By the Fokker-Planck equation (Lemma
2
), the measures
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
follow the PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\left(-\mathbf{x}\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)+\Delta\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\bigl(\left(\mathbf{x}+\nabla\log\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\bigl(\left(\mathbf{x}+2\nabla\log\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right)\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)-\Delta\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,.

Performing a change of variables, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow})

\displaystyle=-|(\tau^{-1})^{\prime}(u)|\nabla\cdot\left((\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}+2\nabla\log\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}))\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow})\right)+|(\tau^{-1})^{\prime}(u)|\Delta\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow})\,.

The conclusion follows by inverting the Fokker-Planck equation with respect to the above display.
∎

We can now introduce our main object of study in this section.

Perspective 4
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}[\mathbf{x}]
exists. Define a stochastic process
\{\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}\}_{u\geq 0}
as follows,
where
\{\bm{W}_{u}\}_{u\geq 0}
is a Wiener process:

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\mathbf{I}_{d}),\\
\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}=\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}}{2u(u+1)}+\frac{1}{u(u+1)}\nabla\log\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u})\right)\,\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{u}.\end{gathered}

(
\mathsf{rev}
)

Note that (

rev

) is simply the SDE driving the backwards dynamics (
6
), according to our derivation in Lemma
7
, under the time change

\tau(t)=\frac{1}{\exp(2t)-1},\quad\tau^{-1}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{u+1}{u}\right),\quad(\tau^{-1})^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{1}{2u(u+1)}.

(7)

We remark that (

) is not the original backwards SDE from
[
SSKKEP21
]
, Eq. (6), which reads

\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}=\left(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}+2\nabla\log\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}(\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u})\right)\,\textup{d}u+\sqrt{2}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{u}\,.

(8)

The above SDE is the result of applying Lemma
7
with a time change satisfying
(\tau^{-1})^{\prime}(u)=-u
. However, because there is no well-defined
\tau:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
with this property, applications in finite time require cutting off the forwards SDE at a time
T>0
such that
\pi_{T}\approx\pi_{\infty}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\mathbf{I}_{d})
, and only defining the backwards SDE up to time
T
(i.e.,
\tau(t)=T-t
). To avoid these boundary issues, Perspective
4
uses (
7
), which just gives a reparameterization of (
8
) if we restrict to
[0,T]
.

As a final technical tool, we require Tweedie’s formula (cf.
[
Rob56
]
) to rewrite the
\nabla\log\pi^{\leftarrow}
term, which is often called the
score
of the backwards process.

Lemma 8

(Tweedie’s formula)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
be the output of
\mathbf{x}\sim\pi
passed through a noisy Gaussian channel, let
\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})
be the posterior distribution, and let
\nu(\mathbf{y})
be the marginal of
\mathbf{y}
:

\pi(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})\propto\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr)\pi(\mathbf{x})\,,\quad\nu(\mathbf{y})\propto\int\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr)\pi(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}.

Then,

\nabla\log\nu(\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}[\mathbf{x}]-\mathbf{y})
.

Proof.

Note that the proportionality constant in
\nu
does not affect
\nabla\log\nu(\mathbf{y})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\nabla\log\nu(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})\pi(\mathbf{x})\textup{d}\mathbf{x}}\int\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}}{\sigma^{2}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr)\pi(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}[\mathbf{x}]-\mathbf{y}\right)\,.

∎

Lemma
8
is remarkably useful in practical applications. Recall from Lemma
6
that intermediate distributions in the forwards and backwards processes are outputs of noisy Gaussian channels, initialized from
\pi
. Further, the backwards SDEs (

rev

), (
8
) are entirely explicit, except for the score term
\nabla\log\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}
.
Lemma
8
says that we can approximate this score term by predicting appropriate posterior means
\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}
, where
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}
is an intermediate iterate. In practice, this predictor can be learned by minimizing an empirical risk over samples from
\pi
, and modern machine learning models (e.g., deep neural networks) achieve good prediction error. Moreover, fairly strong bounds are available that convert errors arising from score approximation and finite-time discretization to errors in the sampling process, see e.g., a line of work initiated by
[
CCLLSZ23
,
LLT23
]
.

We conclude by relating Perspective

, as observed by

.

The processes

Proof.

First, consider the SDE (

\displaystyle\textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}u}\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\right)\mathbf{c}_{u}\,\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\,\textup{d}\mathbf{c}_{u}

(9)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2u+1}{2(u(u+1))^{\frac{3}{2}}}\mathbf{c}_{u}\,\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\mathbf{m}_{u}\,\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2u+1}{2u(u+1)}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}\,\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\mathbf{m}_{u}\,\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{u},

where

is the mean of the measure
\propto\exp(\mathbf{c}_{u}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\frac{u}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})\pi(\mathbf{x})

On the other hand, observe that

in (

in (

\displaystyle=\sqrt{u(u+1)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{0}\right]-(u+1)\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}.

Combining (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textup{d}\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}&=\Bigl(\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}}{2u(u+1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\leftarrow}_{u}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{0}\right]-\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\leftarrow}_{u}}{u}\Bigr)\textup{d}u+\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(u+1)}}\,\textup{d}\bm{W}_{u}\\

Renormalization

We present another perspective called renormalization
[
WK74
,
Pol84
]
. The idea is to decompose a measure
\pi
via infinitesimal convolutions, and describe how so-called “renormalized potentials” evolve as a Markov process. We follow the recent presentation of
[
BBD24
]
.

In this section, we use
\sigma,\tau
to denote times in
[0,1]
, and
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the set of smooth compactly-supported measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
3
3

3

An extended discussion on Markov semigroup theory is beyond our scope. The results here extend beyond the class
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined here via approximation; we refer the reader to
[
BGL13
]
for additional background.
We now introduce our renormalized potentials and measures
\{V_{\tau},\nu_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}
, and the Polchinski semigroup

\{\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}\}_{0\leq\sigma\leq\tau\leq 1}
that they induce.

Perspective 5
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\pi}[\mathbf{x}]
exists. Define a family of
renormalized potentials and measures
as follows: let
V_{1}(\mathbf{x}):=-\log\pi(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, and

\displaystyle V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=-\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(1-\tau)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{1}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right)\right]\right),

(
\mathsf{renorm}
)

\displaystyle\nu_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right),\text{ for all }\tau\in[0,1].

Also, define the induced
Polchinski semigroup
: for all
0\leq\sigma\leq\tau\leq 1
, let

\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f(\mathbf{x}):=\exp\left(V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(\tau-\sigma)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right)f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right].

(
\mathsf{PSG}
)

It is clear that
\nu_{1}=\pi
in (

renorm

), and we let
\nu_{0}:=\delta_{\mathbf{0}_{d}}
be a Dirac measure by weak continuity.

We next clarify the relationship between (

renorm

) and (

PSG

). Intuitively,
\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}
advances time from
\sigma
to
\tau
(in the sense described in Section
1.2
), and the corresponding densities are
\nu_{\sigma}
,
\nu_{\tau}
.

Lemma 9
.

Following notation in Perspective
5
, we have that
\mathsf{P}_{\rho,\sigma}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}=\mathsf{P}_{\rho,\tau}
for all
0\leq\rho\leq\sigma\leq\tau\leq 1
. Moreover,
\mathsf{P}^{*}_{\sigma,\tau}\nu_{\sigma}=\nu_{\tau}
for all
0\leq\sigma\leq\tau\leq 1
, in the sense that

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[f\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f\right]\text{ for all }f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(12)

Proof.

By applying (

PSG

) twice,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\rho,\sigma}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(\sigma-\rho)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\right)\exp\left(V_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(\tau-\sigma)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})\right)f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(\sigma-\rho)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(\tau-\sigma)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})\right)f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(\tau-\rho)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right)f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right]=\mathsf{P}_{\rho,\tau}f(\mathbf{x}),

where we use the fact that if
\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\bm{\Sigma}_{1}),\mathbf{x}_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
then
\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{x}_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\bm{\Sigma}_{1}+\bm{\Sigma}_{2})
.

Next, note that for all
\tau\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle(2\pi\tau)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\tau\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\tau\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},(1-\tau)\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{1}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp\left(-V_{1}(\mathbf{z})\right)\right]=\exp\left(-V_{0}(\mathbf{0}_{d})\right),

so the normalizing constant of
\nu_{\tau}
is
(2\pi\tau)^{\frac{d}{2}}\exp\left(V_{0}(\mathbf{0}_{d})\right)
. Thus, for all
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{0,\tau}f(\mathbf{0}_{d})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(V_{0}(\mathbf{0}_{d})\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{d},\tau\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left[\exp(-V_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}))f(\mathbf{z})\right]

In this section, we place stochastic localization in the context of the Schrödinger bridge

We begin by describing a general formulation of the Schrödinger bridge optimization problem.

Perspective 6
.

Let
\mu,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let

on

\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathrm{KL}(P\;\|\;R)\textrm{ such that }P_{0}=\mu,\;P_{1}=\pi.

\mathsf{SSB}

In other words, we seek the path measure on
\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with prescribed starting measure

and ending measure
P_{1}=\pi

being the Wiener path measure in mind, as we specialize calculations at the end of the section to this case.

We now introduce some helpful notation. When

is a path measure,
P_{01}
denotes the marginal density of its endpoints
(P_{0},P_{1})

\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

\mu
and
\pi

\gamma

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose left and right marginals, denoted

and

\pi

The first observation regarding (

SSB

Let

The chain rule for the KL divergence gives

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})a(\mathbf{p}_{[0,\tau]})b(\mathbf{p}_{[\tau,1]})g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})a(\mathbf{p}_{[0,\tau]})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[b(\mathbf{p}_{[\tau,1]})g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim P^{*}}[a(\mathbf{p}_{[0,\tau]})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim P^{*}}[b(\mathbf{p}_{[\tau,1]})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}],

where the first and last equalities are general results about conditioning, and the second equality uses the Markov property of
R
. We note that we do not divide by
0
,
P^{\star}
-almost surely.

The second claim in (
29
) applies the Markov property of
R
:

\displaystyle\frac{P^{\star}_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})}{R_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[\frac{P^{\star}(\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]})}{R(\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]})}\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]=f_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})g_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}).

Similarly, for any
0\leq\sigma\leq\tau\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{P^{\star}_{\sigma,\tau}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{R_{\sigma,\tau}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[\frac{P^{*}(\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]})}{R(\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]})}\mid\mathbf{p}_{\sigma,\tau}=(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\sigma,\tau}=(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\sigma}=\mathbf{s}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{t}\right]=f_{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})g_{\tau}(\mathbf{t}),

where the third equality applies that

is Markov. The last two parts of (

\displaystyle P^{\star}_{\sigma\mid\tau}(\mathbf{s}\mid\mathbf{t})=\frac{P^{\star}_{\sigma,\tau}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{P^{\star}_{\tau}(\mathbf{t})}=\frac{f_{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})}{f_{\tau}(\mathbf{t})}R_{\sigma\mid\tau}(\mathbf{s}\mid\mathbf{t}),

\displaystyle P^{\star}_{\tau\mid\sigma}(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{s})=\frac{P^{\star}_{\sigma,\tau}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})}{P^{\star}_{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})}=\frac{g_{\tau}(\mathbf{t})}{g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{s})}R_{\tau\mid\sigma}(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{s}).

∎

Our next step is to derive the infinitesimal generator of

Lemma 15

Following notation in Lemma

First, by using (

\displaystyle=\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}\frac{1}{\eta}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})(u(\mathbf{p}_{\tau+\eta})-u(\mathbf{p}_{\tau}))g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[f(\mathbf{p}_{0})g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\eta\searrow 0}\frac{f_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}\left[(u(\mathbf{p}_{\tau+\eta})-u(\mathbf{p}_{\tau}))g(\mathbf{p}_{1})\mid\mathbf{p}_{\tau}=\mathbf{z}\right]}{\eta f_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})g_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{p}_{[0,1]}\sim R}

7
Dynamic Schrödinger bridge

We present the first of two alternative perspectives on the static Schrödinger bridge problem: a dynamic reformulation framed as computing an optimal drift with a target end measure.

In this section, let
L^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\mathbf{u}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\tau}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\textup{d}\tau<\infty\}
, let
\{\bm{W}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}
be a Wiener process, and let
\{\mathscr{F}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}
be the filtration generated by
\{\bm{W}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}
.

Perspective 7
.

Let
\mu,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and define

\mathcal{U}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\mathbf{u}\text{ is adapted to }\{\mathscr{F}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}\}.

The induced
dynamic Schrödinger bridge
problem is:

\begin{gathered}\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\mathbf{u_{\tau}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\Bigr],\\
\text{such that }\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\mathbf{u}_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{\tau},\quad\operatorname{law}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mu,\quad\operatorname{law}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\pi.\end{gathered}

(
\mathsf{DSB}
)

We make use of Girsanov’s theorem, which is a classic change-of-measure principle on the space
L^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We note that the specialization of Girsanov’s theorem to determinsitic drifts was already introduced as the Cameron-Martin theorem (Lemma
5
).

Lemma 16

[

.

Suppose

adapted to
\{\mathscr{F}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\mathbf{u}_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{\tau},\qquad\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{\tau},

with initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{a}
. Let
P_{\mathbf{a}}
be the probability measure under which
\{\bm{W}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}
is a standard Brownian motion, and similarly let

be the probability measure under which
\{\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}

Then,
6

Entropic optimal transport

We conclude with a second alternative perspective on the static Schrödinger bridge problem: a connection to entropic optimal transport, following
[
PC19
,
CNR25
]
.

Perspective 8
.

Let
\mu,\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. The induced
entropic optimal transport
problem is:

\displaystyle\underset{{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\pi)}}{\min}\biggl\{\int\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\textup{d}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\gamma\;\|\;\mu\otimes\pi)\biggr\}\,.

(
\mathsf{EOT}
)

Note that the solution to (

EOT

) exists and is unique, by strict convexity of
KL
in its first argument, and compactness of the set
\Gamma(\mu,\pi)
(defined in Section
6
), the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\pi
. Here,
\mu\otimes\pi
refers to the trivial coupling (product measure) between
\mu
and
\pi
.

When
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, (

EOT

) recovers the classical optimal transport problem
[
CNR25
]
. One motivation for studying (

EOT

) is that while solving classical optimal transport can often be computationally intractable, (

EOT

) can be efficiently solved via methods such as Sinkhorn’s algorithm
[
Sin67
]
.

We now relate (

EOT

) to (

SSB

) in the case of generalized Wiener reference measures.

Theorem 7
.

The optimization problems in (

EOT

) (with
\varepsilon=1
) and (

SSB

) (with
R=W_{\mu}
) are the same, up to an additive constant shift (depending only on
\mu,\nu
).

Proof.

We start from (

SSB

). As the initial condition
R_{0}=\mu
is fixed,

\displaystyle\underset{{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\pi)}}{\min}\;\mathrm{KL}(\gamma\;\|\;R_{0,1})=

\displaystyle\underset{{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\pi)}}{\min}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu}[\mathrm{KL}(\gamma(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\;\|\;R_{1\mid 0}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}))]+\mathrm{KL}(\mu(\mathbf{x})\;\|\;R_{0}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\pi)}}{\min}\;\int-\log R_{0,1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}+\int\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\log\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

The first line used Lemma
12
.
Now, as

is a generalized Wiener measure, using the explicit form of
\log R_{0,1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+C
for a constant

, and

\int\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\log\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\mathrm{KL}(\gamma\;\|\;\mu\otimes\pi)=\int\mu(\mathbf{x})\log\mu(\mathbf{x})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{x}+\int\nu(\mathbf{y})\log\nu(\mathbf{y})\,\textup{d}\mathbf{y},

where the right-hand side is independent of

).
∎

\displaystyle C_{\operatorname{LS}}(\mathsf{P})\coloneqq 1-\sup_{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f]}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}f}.

Lower bounding the LSI constant is useful because it implies rapid mixing.
Specifically, a Markov chain with transition kernel
\mathsf{P}
, initialized at a distribution
\mu
with
\frac{\mu}{\pi}\leq\beta
pointwise, mixes to within
\varepsilon
in total variation of
\pi
in
O(\frac{1}{C_{\text{LS}}(\mathsf{P})}\log(\frac{\log\beta}{\varepsilon}))
steps; see e.g., Lemma 2.4 in
[
BCPSV21
]
. This is because
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\;\|\;\pi)
improves by a
1-C_{\text{LS}}(\mathsf{P})
factor in each step.

We next reproduce a key observation of
[
CE22
]
: entropy conservation of a localization process, as in Lemma
21
, lower bounds the LSI constant of the associated localization dynamics.

Lemma 22

(
[
CE22
, Proposition 19]
)
.

Assume that
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a localization process associated to
\pi
, and that
\mathsf{P}_{T}^{\pi}
is the transition kernel (
34
) associated to its localization dynamics. Then,

\displaystyle C_{\operatorname{LS}}(\mathsf{P}_{T}^{\pi})\geq\inf_{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{T}}[f]]}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]}\,.

Proof.

Throughout this proof,
\mathbb{E}
with no subscript implies that the expectation is over the randomness used in defining
\pi_{T}
(i.e., the localization process), and we let
\mathsf{P}:=\mathsf{P}_{T}^{\pi}
for short.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary. By Jensen’s inequality applied to the convex function
c\to c\log c
, with random variable
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]
and measure
\mathbb{E}[\frac{\pi_{T}(\mathbf{x})}{\pi(\mathbf{x})}\cdot]
(as
\mathbb{E}[\frac{\pi_{T}(\mathbf{x})}{\pi(\mathbf{x})}]=1
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}(\mathbf{x})}{\pi(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr]\log\left(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}(\mathbf{x})}{\pi(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr]\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}(\mathbf{x})}{\pi(\mathbf{x})}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr)\Bigr].

Integrating over
\mathbf{x}\sim\pi
, this implies via Fubini’s theorem that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f\log\mathsf{P}f]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\biggl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}}{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr]\log\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}}{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr]\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}}{\pi}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{T}}{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr]\biggr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f]\Bigr].

Finally,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{T}}[f]]}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f\log f]]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}f\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}f]}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{T}}[f\log f]]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f\log\mathsf{P}f]}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f\log f]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]\right)-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f\log\mathsf{P}f]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f]\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f]\right)}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[\mathsf{P}f]}{\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]},

where we used that
\pi
is stationary for
\mathsf{P}
, as well as
\mathbb{E}\pi_{T}=\pi
, in the third line.
This concludes the proof upon infimizing over
f
on both sides.
∎

Our final result applies the development thus far to the localization dynamics (

RGD

).

(

)
.

If

-strongly log-concave (i.e.,

Throughout this proof, let

From the definition of the localization process (

Lemma 23

(
[

, Lemma 3.10]
)
.

Following notation in Perspective
5
, assume that there exist

We next derive functional inequalities for the renormalized measures in Perspective

. Recall that we say a measure

(37)

and similarly,

\textup{Ent}_{\pi}\left[f\right]\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right].

(38)

We remark that a standard linearization argument shows that a log-Sobolev inequality implies a Poincaré inequality with the same constant (Exercise 1.7,
[

Proposition 1
.

Following notation in Perspective
5
, assume that there exist
\{\lambda_{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}
such that for all
\tau\in[0,1]
,

\nabla^{2}V_{\tau}\succeq\lambda_{\tau}\mathbf{I}_{d}
. Define for all
\tau\in[0,1]
,

\Lambda_{\tau}:=\int_{\tau}^{1}\lambda_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\sigma,\quad\frac{1}{\gamma_{\tau}}:=\int_{0}^{\tau}\exp\left(-2(\Lambda_{\sigma}-\Lambda_{\tau})\right)\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Then for all
\tau\in[0,1]
and

,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_{\tau}}[f]\leq\frac{2}{\gamma_{\tau}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],

(39)

and

\left(1-\exp\left(-\int_{\tau}^{1}\gamma_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)\right)\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[f]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{\tau}^{\mathbf{v}}}[f]\right].

(40)

Proof of (
39
).

Here we prove the log-Sobolev inequality (
39
), postponing (
40
) until later. We will develop a more general claim that subsumes (
40
) as well as an analog for variance stability.

Write
\Phi(x)=x\log x
. By compactness of
f
’s support, we have

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}[\Phi(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)]=\Phi\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}[f]\right),

recalling that
\mathsf{P}_{0,\tau}f=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}[f]
(Lemma
9
).
Then,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_{\tau}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}[\Phi(f)]-\Phi\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}[f]\right)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}[\Phi(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)]\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Then by first applying the chain rule, and then recalling the action of
\mathsf{L}_{\sigma}
given in Lemma
10
,

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}[\Phi(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\mathsf{L}_{\sigma}(\Phi(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f))+\Phi^{\prime}(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)\left\lVert\nabla\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)\mathsf{L}_{\sigma}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f+\Phi^{\prime}(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f)\left\lVert\nabla\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],

so that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ent}_{\nu_{\tau}}[f]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{\tau}\exp\left(-2\left(\Lambda_{\sigma}-\Lambda_{\tau}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\sigma}}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\sigma,\tau}\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\frac{2}{\gamma_{\tau}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla\sqrt{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right].

The only inequality was Lemma
23
, and then we used the definition of
\gamma_{\tau}
and Lemma
9
.
∎

We have thus far shown the bound (
39
), i.e., a log-Sobolev inequality for
\nu_{\tau}
. As discussed previously, this also implies a Poincaré inequality holds with the same constant.

We next describe how to obtain the stability estimate (
40

\Phi
be a convex function, and let

\Psi_{\nu}[f]:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\Phi(f)]-\Phi(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]).

For example, when

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}\left[\Phi(f)\right]-\Phi\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}[f]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\tau,1}\Phi(f)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\Phi\left(\mathsf{P}_{\tau,1}f\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\Phi\left(\mathsf{P}_{\tau,1}f\right)\right]-\Phi\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\mathsf{P}_{\tau,1}f\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\tau}}\left[\Psi_{\pi_{\tau}^{\mathbf{v}}}[f]\right]+\Psi_{\nu_{\tau}}[\mathsf{P}_{\tau,1}f]

for any

, where we used Lemma

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Phi
be a convex function, and define
\Psi
as in (

Following notation in Perspective
5
,
assume that for all
\tau\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\Psi_{\nu_{\tau}}[\mathsf{P}_{\tau,1}f]

Lemma 26
.

Let
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be
\alpha
-strongly log-concave and let
\nu:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be
\beta
-strongly log-concave, for some
\alpha,\beta>0
. Then their convolution
\mu\ast\nu
is
\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}
-strongly log-concave.

Proof.

By definition,
\gamma
-strong log-concavity of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is equivalent to
f\exp(\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2})
being log-concave. Now, define the function

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\mu(\mathbf{y})\nu(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\exp\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

We claim that
f
is log-concave if
\gamma\leq\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}
. This holds because

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\mu(\mathbf{y})-\nabla^{2}\log\nu(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})-\nabla^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\succeq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}&\alpha\mathbf{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\beta\mathbf{I}_{d}&-\beta\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
-\beta\mathbf{I}_{d}&\beta\mathbf{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}-\gamma\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}&\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(\beta-\gamma)\mathbf{I}_{d}&-\beta\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
-\beta\mathbf{I}_{d}&(\alpha+\beta)\mathbf{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix},

and we can verify that the last matrix is positive semidefinite by checking its determinant. Finally, the conclusion holds because the
\mathbf{x}
-marginal of
f
is log-concave by the Prékopa-Leindler inequality, which simplifies to the convolution of
\mu
and
\nu
being
\gamma
-strongly log-concave.
∎
