Family‐Wise Error Rate Control in Clinical Trials With Overlapping Populations
Authors: Remi Luschei, Werner Brannath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe consider clinical trials with multiple, overlapping patient populations that test multiple treatment policies specifically tailored to these populations. Such designs may lead to multiplicity issues, as false statements will affect several populations. For type I error control, often the family‐wise error rate (FWER) is controlled, which is the probability to reject at least one true null hypothesis. If the joint distribution of the test statistics is known, the FWER level can be exhausted by determining critical values or adjusted α‐levels. The adjustment is typically done under the common ANOVA assumptions. However, the performed tests are then only valid under the rather strong assumption of homogeneous null effects, that is, when the null hypothesis applies to all subpopulations and their intersections. We show that under cancelling null effects, when heterogeneous effects cancel out in some or all subpopulations, this procedure does not provide FWER control. We also suggest different alternatives and compare them in terms of FWER control and their power.

Introduction

Clinical trials with multiple target populations have become increasingly important in recent years in the context of personalized medicine, which aims to find tailored treatments for individual patients. This introduces greater complexity in trial designs, as treatment effects may vary among patient subgroups. Subgroup effect heterogeneity can be either quantitative, when the subgroup effects are in the same direction but differ in magnitude, as in Figure 1b, or qualitative, when the subgroup effects have opposite directions, being beneficial for some groups and harmful for others, see Figure 1c [1, 2]. In practice, the target populations of a trial may include multiple subgroups and be overlapping, meaning that individual patients can belong to multiple populations simultaneously. Examples include enrichment trials, umbrella and basket trials, as well as platform trials (see e.g., Baldi Antognini et al. [3]). This leads to a multiplicity issue, as patients may be subjected to several treatment decisions, potentially resulting in an exposure to inefficient therapies.

Different kinds of subgroup effect heterogeneity (SEH) between two subgroups S1 and S2, when comparing an investigational treatment E to a control C (figure adapted from Wang et al. [1]). (a) No SEH. (b) Quantitative SEH. (c) Qualitative SEH.

To address this, Sun et al. [4] recommend controlling the family‐wise error rate (FWER), ensuring that the probability of making one or more false discoveries across all tested hypotheses remains bounded. Alternatively, one could also control the population‐wise error rate (PWER) introduced by Brannath et al. [5], which is an average of FWERs that are restricted to the subpopulations, and thereby becomes more liberal than the FWER. In the following section, we briefly describe how to control the FWER in a setting with overlapping populations. A similar method is applicable for the PWER.

FWER Control in a Single Stage Design

Let us consider a trial with a patient population 𝒫 that can be partitioned into disjoint subpopulations 𝒮1,…,𝒮n. We are interested in testing treatments within certain combinations of these subpopulations, represented by a finite collection of index sets ℐ⊆2{1,…,n}. In each 𝒫I:=⋃i∈I𝒮i, I∈ℐ, we want to test an experimental treatment EI in comparison to a control treatment C. Let μ𝒮i,T∈ℝ denote the expected response to treatment T∈𝒯i within subpopulation 𝒮i, where 𝒯i={EI:i∈I}∪{C} is the set of treatments administered in 𝒮i. We assume throughout that higher response values correspond to better outcomes. The treatment effect of EI in 𝒫I is defined as (1) θI=μ𝒫I,EI−μ𝒫I,C=∑i∈Iπ𝒮iπ𝒫Iθ𝒮i,EI, where θ𝒮i,EI=μ𝒮i,EI−μ𝒮i,C is the mean treatment effect of EI in 𝒮i, and π𝒮i denotes the prevalence of 𝒮i in 𝒫 (so that π𝒫I=∑i∈Iπ𝒮i). Our null hypotheses of interest are: HI:θI≤0,for allI∈ℐ. The FWER is defined as the probability to reject at least one true HI for I∈ℐ. It is said to be strongly controlled at level α∈(0,1) if FWERθ=ℙ⋃I∈ℐ0(θ){rejectHI}≤αfor allθ=(θI)I∈ℐ, where ℐ0(θ):={I∈ℐ:θI≤0} is the set of true null hypotheses under the configuration θ. As observed by Ondra et al. [6], overlapping populations 𝒫I generally induce correlation among the test statistics, such that nonparametric procedures like the Bonferroni correction may be overly conservative. If the joint distribution of the test statistics is known, more powerful procedures can be constructed. Suppose that each hypothesis HI is tested with a test statistic ZI and a common rejection threshold c. Then the FWER under a specific configuration θ equals FWERθ(c)=ℙ⋃I∈ℐ0(θ){ZI>c}. The maximal FWER typically occurs under the global null hypothesis θ=0, that is, when θI=0 for all I∈ℐ, as is the case for many common test statistics such as contrast z‐ or t‐statistics under normality assumptions (see, e.g., Theorem 1 in Luschei and Brannath [7]). In this case, strong and exhaustive control of the FWER at level α∈(0,1) can be achieved by selecting the threshold c that satisfies FWER0(c)=α. Alternatively, one can compute FWER‐adjusted p‐values by pI=FWER0(zIobs), where zIobs denotes the observed value of ZI.

Problem Formulation

As noted by Ondra et al. [6], many authors assume normally distributed patient outcomes and apply the procedure described above to the contrasts defined in (1) under the ANOVA assumptions. We will demonstrate that under a qualitative effect heterogeneity between the subgroups 𝒮i, this approach may fail to control the FWER (and similar error rates such as the PWER). This is due to the fact that a qualitative effect heterogeneity may remain under the global null hypothesis due to cancelling subgroup effects (see the illustration in Figure 2c). In such a situation, the overall treatment effect can be zero in a population 𝒫I, but the subgroup‐specific effects are nonzero and heterogeneous in sign.

Subgroup effect heterogeneity under the global null hypothesis. The arrows show the adjustment of the effects from Figure 1 so that the treatment effect in the overall population 𝒮1∪𝒮2 equals 0. The prevalences of 𝒮1 and 𝒮2 are assumed to be equal. (a) No SEH + global null ⇒No SEH. (b) Quantitative SEH + global null ⇒No SEH. (c) Qualitative SEH + global null ⇒Qualitative SEH.

As a result, the contrasts θI cannot be reliably estimated from the data under the null hypothesis in the ANOVA framework. The reason is that they depend on the unknown subpopulation prevalences, while the ANOVA model estimates the contrasts conditional on the observed subgroup sample sizes. This leads the ANOVA to implicitly reweight the subgroup effects in a way that does not reflect the true population mixture. While that is negligible under homogeneous effects (which are uniformly zero under the global null; see Figure 2b), under heterogeneity it distorts the expected value of the contrasts from zero and thereby creates a bias which questions error control, as will be detailed in Section 2.

The ANOVA Subpopulation Model

For each treatment T∈𝒯i, let n𝒮i,T denote the number of patients from subgroup 𝒮i assigned to treatment T. Similarly, let n𝒫I,T represent the number of patients in population 𝒫I receiving treatment T∈{EI,C}. Let N denote the total sample size. In the ANOVA model, the observations in every 𝒮i are assumed to be of the form Y𝒮i,T(k)=μ𝒮i,T+ε(k),k=1,…,n𝒮i,T,T∈𝒯i, where ε(k)∼N(0,σ2) is the residual of patient k, and the residuals are assumed to be stochastically independent across patients. The test statistic for HI is (2) ZI=Y‾𝒫I,EI−Y‾𝒫I,CσVIwithVI=n𝒫I,EI−1+n𝒫I,C−1, where Y‾𝒫I,EI and Y‾𝒫I,C are the average responses under treatments EI and C in the population 𝒫I. For simplicity we assume that the variance σ2 is known. Conditional on the subgroup sample sizes, the test statistics follow a multivariate normal distribution with the location parameter ν=(νI)I∈ℐ given by (3) νI=1σVI∑i∈In𝒮i,EIn𝒫I,EIμ𝒮i,EI−n𝒮i,Cn𝒫I,Cμ𝒮i,C, and the correlation matrix ∑=(∑IJ) given by (4) ∑IJ=1VIVJ∑i∈I∩Jn𝒮i,EIn𝒫I,EIn𝒫J,EI1(EI=EJ)+n𝒮i,Cn𝒫I,Cn𝒫J,C,I≠J where 1(EI=EJ) is the indicator function that equals 1 if EI=EJ and 0 otherwise. If σ2 is unknown and estimated as a pooled variance, the test statistics follow a multivariate t‐distribution with parameters ν,∑ and N−s degrees of freedom, where s is the total number of combinations of subpopulations and treatments.

As we have seen in Section 1.1, strong and exhaustive control of the FWER requires knowledge of the parameters ν and ∑ under the global null hypothesis θ=0. The covariance matrix ∑ is known by design, as it depends only on the (known) subgroup sample sizes. In contrast, ν cannot be exactly determined because it depends on the unknown constants μ𝒮i,EI and μ𝒮i,C. However, when excluding the possibility that the subpopulation effects θ𝒮i=μ𝒮i,EI−μ𝒮i,C have opposite signs, ν converges to 0 under the null hypotheses as the total sample size N approaches infinity. This follows from the fact that the sample proportions n𝒮i,T/n𝒫I,T converge to their population counterparts π𝒮i/π𝒫I. Without this assumption, it is possible for subpopulation effects θ𝒮i to be opposite and to cancel each other out when weighted by their prevalences. In such cases, the overall null hypothesis θ=0 may still hold when ν does not necessarily converge to zero. Consequently, we would be unable to approximate ν, even for the purposes of an asymptotic test. We demonstrate this in the following example.

Marginal Tests

To account for a potential quantitative subgroup effect heterogeneity in the test statistics, in every population 𝒫I we consider observations of the form (6) Y𝒫I,T(k)=μ𝒫I,T+(μ𝒮i∗,T−μ𝒫I,T)+ε(k),k=1,…,n𝒫I,T,T∈{EI,C}=μ𝒫I,T+γ𝒮i∗,T+ε(k), where the index i∗ is now seen as a random variable which takes the values i∈I with probabilities π𝒮i/π𝒫I. Thus, a discrete random variable γ𝒮i∗,T with a positive variance is added to the expected response in population 𝒫I. For the residuals, we still assume ε(k)∼N(0,σ2), and independence from the γ𝒮i∗,T. The test statistics are then ZI=Y‾𝒫I,EI−Y‾𝒫I,Cσ𝒫I,EI2/n𝒫I,EI+σ𝒫I,C2/n𝒫I,C,I∈ℐ where σ𝒫I,T2=Var(γ𝒮i∗,T)+σ2 is the variance in population 𝒫I under treatment T∈{EI,C}. The expected value of ZI is now νI=θI, and the correlations ∑IJ are the expectation of formula (4) over the subgroup sample sizes. We therefore estimate the correlations as in formula (4).

We approximate the distribution of ZI by a t‐distribution with degrees of freedom according to Satterthwaite [9], dfI=(σ𝒫I,EI2/n𝒫I,EI+σ𝒫I,C2/n𝒫I,C)2(σ𝒫I,EI2/n𝒫I,EI)2/n𝒫I,EI−1+(σ𝒫I,C2/n𝒫I,C)2/n𝒫I,C−1. As shown by Hasler and Hothorn [10], FWER control can then be reached by computing individual critical values cI for each ZI from the n‐variate t‐distribution with dfI degrees of freedom: (7) 1−Φθ,∑,dfI(cI,…,cI)=α, where ∑ is as in (4). This especially means that the maximal FWER obtained under θ=0 will be controlled at least approximately. Alternatively, the distribution of the marginal test statistics could be approximated via the bootstrap procedure presented in Section 3.1. Asymptotic FWER‐control would then also apply to the marginal tests for the same reasons.

Shrinkage Method

The marginal tests from Section 3.2 may possibly become too conservative when the subgroup effects are highly heterogeneous, due to overestimation of the population variances. A solution to this problem could be to apply a shrinkage method that reduces the estimated population variances. We can achieve this by shrinking the subgroup means μ𝒮i,T, T∈{EI,C}, towards their arithmetic mean μT. To this end, we employ an empirical Bayes estimator based on the hierarchical model Y‾𝒮i,T|μ𝒮i,T∼N(μ𝒮i,T,σ2/n𝒮i,T),μ𝒮i,T∼N(μT,τT2). The resulting estimator is μ^𝒮i,T=τT2τT2+σ2/nSi,T(Y‾𝒮i,T−μT)+μT (see e.g., Morris [11]). We estimate μT by Y‾¯T, the arithmetic mean of the subgroup sample means Y‾𝒮i,T, i∈I. To estimate the between‐subgroup variance τT2, we use the method of DerSimonian and Laird [12], because of its computational efficiency. If the number of subpopulations #I is at least four, a James‐Stein estimator may alternatively be used: μ^𝒮i,T=1−(#I−3)σ2∑j∈I(Y‾𝒮j,T−Y‾¯T)2n𝒮j,T(Y‾𝒮i,T−Y‾¯T)+Y‾¯T (see e.g., Taketomi et al. [13], Efron and Morris [14]). In both cases, we then obtain the shrinked variance from σ^𝒫I,T2=Var^(γ𝒮i∗,T)+σ2=∑i∈In𝒮i,Tn𝒫Iμ^𝒮i,T2−∑i∈In𝒮i,Tn𝒫Iμ^𝒮i,T2+σ2 and plug it into the test statistics.

Stratified Effect Estimate

It may be more efficient to calculate the mean differences within the strata first, and then weight them according to the respective strata sizes. That is, to use (8) θ^I=∑i∈InSin𝒫Iθ^𝒮i,EI=∑i∈InSin𝒫IY‾𝒮i,EI−Y‾𝒮i,C, as effect estimates. One can quickly see that θ^I converges almost surely to the true θI for N→∞, due to the almost sure convergence of the fractions n𝒮i/N to π𝒮i. Moreover, θ^I corresponds to a double robust effect estimator in the causal inference framework, specifically the augmented inverse‐propensity weighting estimator (IPWE), where the response model includes subpopulation membership as a covariate (see e.g., Hernan and Robins [15]). By standardizing, we obtain as test statistics ZI=θ^In𝒫I−1σ2∑i∈I(δ𝒮i,EI−1+δ𝒮i,C−1)n𝒮i+θ^𝒮,I∑^θ^𝒮,IT, where δ𝒮i,T denotes the observed allocation rate to treatment T∈{EI,C} in subpopulation 𝒮i, and where θ^𝒮,I=(θ^𝒮i,EI)i∈I and ∑^I is the covariance matrix of the multinomial distribution M(n𝒫I,πI), for πI=(π𝒮i)i∈I, which is estimated from the observed sample sizes. The calculation of the variance of θ^I can be found in Appendix B.

We will apply the bootstrap procedure presented in Section 3.1 to determine the joint null distribution of the ZI for FWER control. We note that the estimate given in (8) can be written as a smooth function of the strata‐wise sample sizes and observations, such that it also fits the smooth function model and asymptotical error control will be reached.

Random Effects Model

Another approach to account for heterogeneous subgroup effects is a random effects model, where the observations in every population 𝒫I are modeled as Y𝒫I(k)=μ𝒫I,C+Aθ𝒫I+γ𝒮i,C(k)+Aγ𝒮i,EI(k)+ε𝒫I(k),ε𝒫I(k)∼N(0,σ2),k=1,…,n𝒫I. Here γ𝒮i,C and γ𝒮i,EI are two random effects associated with the subpopulation 𝒮i, and A=1(T=EI) is the indicator for the treatment assignment (1 for EI, 0 for control). The null hypotheses HI are tested using Wald tests on the fixed effects. As we do not know the joint distribution of the test statistics, we apply a Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiplicity.

Simulation Study

We conduct a systematic comparison of the FWER and the multiple power (i.e., the expected proportion of correct rejections) of the different tests presented in Section 3 using simulation studies. All programs are written in R. The corresponding R script files are available at the following link: https://github.com/rluschei/fwer‐seh.

Simulation Design

As in Example 1, we consider two distinct target populations, 𝒫1 and 𝒫2, constructed from a common set of three disjoint subpopulations. The target populations may be nested, for example, 𝒫1=𝒮1∪𝒮2∪𝒮3 and 𝒫2=𝒮2∪𝒮3, or partially overlapping, such as 𝒫1=𝒮1∪𝒮2 and 𝒫2=𝒮2∪𝒮3. We assume that the same investigational treatment is tested in 𝒫1 and 𝒫2.

The simulation consists of two levels. In an outer simulation loop, we generate 100 study setups which are characterized by a prevalence vector, subgroup‐specific treatment effects and control group means. Conditional on each setup, an inner Monte‐Carlo loop with 1000 iterations is used to repeatedly generate trial data and apply each of the test procedures. The rejection decisions obtained are then used to estimate the FWER or power of each test procedure for that setup.

In the outer simulation loop, we first generate the prevalences of the three subpopulations. To do this, we draw three independent random numbers uniformly from the interval [0,1] and normalize them so that they sum to one, giving the prevalence vector π=(π𝒮1,π𝒮2,π𝒮3) which will later be used to generate subgroup sample sizes. Next, we assign treatment effects to the subgroups. For FWER investigation, we start by generating the treatment effect for a subgroup that lies in the overlap of 𝒫1 and 𝒫2. This effect is drawn uniformly from the interval [−1,1] and scaled by a pre‐specified effect heterogeneity factor (EHF), such as 0, 1, or 10, to reflect varying degrees of treatment effect heterogeneity. The treatment effects for the remaining subgroups are then computed by solving the linear equation system: θ𝒫I=π𝒫I−1∑i∈Iπ𝒮iθ𝒮i=0,I∈ℐ, so that the global null hypothesis holds. It is uniquely solvable as the effect in the overlap of 𝒫1 and 𝒫2 has already been specified. To investigate the power, the treatment effects for all subgroups are independently drawn from the interval [−EHF,EHF] under the constraint that they fall under the alternative hypotheses. To introduce some variability in the absolute response levels, we also define a control group heterogeneity factor (CHF), with values such as 0, 1, or 10. This factor determines the expected control group responses: one subgroup has an expected response of 0, another receives a response equal to the CHF, and a third subgroup receives twice that amount.

With these quantities fixed, the simulation proceeds to the inner Monte Carlo loop, where trial data are repeatedly generated. First, the subgroup sample sizes are drawn from a multinomial distribution with N∈{250,500,1000} patients and probabilities given by the prevalence vector π. In particular, this ensures that the sample sizes are integer. Within each subgroup, normally distributed outcomes are then generated under equal allocation to the treatment groups (which can be achieved approximately with a stratified randomization). As in Example 1, the residual variance is fixed at σ2=0.25. We then apply the different tests from Sections 2 and 3 to both 𝒫1 and 𝒫2. For all tests that rely on the bootstrap procedure, 1000 bootstrap samples are drawn within each inner Monte Carlo iteration.

Finally, based on the rejections obtained in the Monte Carlo loop, we estimate the FWER of each test procedure as the proportion of iterations in which at least one null hypothesis is falsely rejected, and the power as the average proportion of correct rejections.

As we have already seen in Example 1, the FWER of the ANOVA tests with the t‐approximation from Section 2 turns out to be highly inflated, to an extent depending on the inhomogeneity of the subgroup effects. For instance, with EHF = 10, the mean FWER rises to 0.3145, far exceeding the nominal α‐level. In contrast, the bootstrap method applied to the ANOVA tests yields a considerably smaller FWER, but under high effect heterogeneities, the error is no longer controlled in the mean (0.0337 for EHF = 10). The marginal tests with the t‐approximation (using the Satterthwaite‐formula) consistently control the FWER under the target level, regardless of effect or control heterogeneity, but they become very conservative under high heterogeneities. The marginal tests with the bootstrap‐approximation perform similarly to the ANOVA bootstrap tests, but perform somewhat worse under high effect heterogeneity. Shrinkage by the empirical Bayes method applied to the marginal tests (with both the t‐ and bootstrap approximation) gives almost no difference compared to the marginal tests. The mean FWER values of the stratified estimator are comparable to those of the marginal tests. Finally, the random effect models are extremely conservative throughout, primarily due to convergence issues. Overall, the bootstrap approximation for the ANOVA tests shows the best performance in these settings.

The complete summary statistics for the distribution of the FWER over the 100 study setups can be found in Table C1 of Appendix C. They show that the variance of the FWER is generally very small for all tests (except the uncorrected ANOVA) and increases only slightly with increasing heterogeneity. In addition, the median FWER values are often smaller than the respective means and noticeably closer to α for all bootstrap methods (e.g., 0.027 for the ANOVA tests with bootstrap when EHF = 10). Larger outliers are rare and occur only in study settings where certain subpopulations have extremely low prevalences (<0.01), which makes sense as only very small sample sizes are possible in such cases.

We have also conducted the same simulations with smaller (N=250) and larger (N=1000) total sample sizes. For N=250 patients, correspondingly higher FWER values are observed, but the comparison of the methods is similar to the statements above. The detailed results can be found in Table C2 in Appendix C. For N=1000, all bootstrap‐based tests achieve values noticeably closer to the target α=0.025. In particular, the bootstraped ANOVA tests perform relatively well even under high effect heterogeneity, with a mean FWER of 0.0284 and a median FWER of 0.0260 for EHF = 10, CHF = 0. On the other hand, this still corresponds to a deviation from the target of 14% resp. 4%.

We did the same simulations also in the case with overlapping target populations (instead of nested populations) and found no particular differences in the results.

In further simulations, we applied a 2:1 randomization to the treatments in all three strata. Furthermore, we also applied a 2:1 randomization only in the intersection of the target populations (and a 1:1 allocation otherwise) to model the situation where two distinct treatments are tested. The corresponding results for N=500 patients are shown in Table C2 of Appendix C (allocation patterns “B” and “C”). While they are very similar to the equal allocation cases under the 2:1 assignment in all strata, when restricting on the intersection only the stratified tests stay robust to increasing control group heterogeneity. The other bootstrap methods then become more conservative, while the marginal tests with the t‐approximation become excessively liberal.

Another modification that we did was to assume only equal allocation probabilities to the treatments, instead of doing an exactly equal allocation. This is achieved by drawing the allocation numbers from a corresponding multinomial distribution. In that case, all tests are becoming slightly more liberal. See the detailed results in Appendix C, Table C2 (allocation pattern “D”).

Finally, we generalized the simulations to settings with larger numbers of subpopulations and observed similar results. In particular, for studies with more than three subpopulations, we compared the two shrinkage methods from Section 3.3, but found no major differences between them, and hardly any improvement compared to the marginal tests.

Simultaneous Confidence Intervals

We can use the critical values and standard errors of the different tests from Section 3 to derive corresponding simultaneous confidence intervals. For each I∈ℐ, let cI denote the critical value associated with the test. Depending on the approximation method, cI is either obtained from the Satterthwaite approximation in Equation (7) or, in the case of bootstrap methods, as the (1−α)‐quantile of the empirical distribution of the bootstrapped maxI∈ℐZI∗. Real Data Example

We compare the different tests introduced in Section 3 to the standard ANOVA tests and unadjusted testing in a real data example using a data set created by Kesselmeier et al. [16] from the MAXSEP study [17], which evaluated the effects of meropenem compared to a combination therapy of moxifloxacin and meropenem in patients with severe sepsis. The data consist of 1000 resamples of N=500 patients who were tested for two binary biomarkers B1 (baseline lactate value >2mmol/L) and B2 (baseline C‐reactive protein value >128mg/L), and who were randomly assigned to two arms of an umbrella trial. Assuming a positive effect of one unit in patients with a positive B1 and B2, and 0.25 points negative effects in the other strata, we computed the test statistics and critical values of the different tests in the overall population. The results are shown in Figure 3. One can see that the null hypothesis would often be rejected in unadjusted testing and in the standard ANOVA tests, whereas the bootstrap‐method, the marginal tests, the shrinkage method, and the stratified tests are noticeably more conservative. This aligns with the already observed degrees of FWER exhaustion in Section 4.2.

Discussion

Treatment effects usually vary between patients, sometimes substantially. We have seen that a qualitative effect heterogeneity, where the effects differ in direction across subgroups, can compromise type I error control and necessitates a more conservative approach than doing the standard ANOVA contrast tests. We were able to reach this through various methods presented in Section 3. The best performance—with the smallest exceedance of the significance level in different settings—is achieved by the bootstrap approximation of the ANOVA test statistics. However, it should be noted that noticeable exceedance of the significance level was still observed for the sample sizes examined, which decreases as the sample sizes increase. The other bootstrapped tests (with the marginal, shrinked, and the stratified test statistics) perform very similarly, and sometimes even better. The marginal tests with the t‐approximation may become overly conservative under high subgroup heterogeneities. The mixed effect models perform very poorly in general, probably due to misspecification of the random strata‐wise effects, which are assumed to be redrawn from the normal distribution in every repetition of a study. It seems more natural instead to define them via a discrete random variable as we did for the marginal tests.

If the variance in a population is the same under the investigational and the control treatment, as is the case, for example, with non‐predictive biomarkers (which do not provide any indication of how likely patients are to respond to the treatments), the marginal tests reduce to the t‐test and the standard approach remains valid. The standard ANOVA tests also remain valid when testing the stronger intersection hypotheses HI=∩i∈IH𝒮i, where H𝒮i:θ𝒮i≤0 is the null hypothesis restricted to subpopulation 𝒮i. However, this reduces the validity of our testing in another sense, as a significant test result only indicates a treatment effect being present in at least one stratum, without identifying which one. Doing separate tests across all subpopulations may also be very ineffective, as this may greatly increase the number of hypotheses to be tested, and since the sample sizes within each stratum are typically much smaller than in the target populations. This can lead to a significant loss of power.

Low power is generally a problem in multipopulation studies, especially when some of the subpopulations have small prevalences or are highly stratified. Brannath et al. [5] proposed an alternative type I error rate for such situations, the population‐wise error rate (PWER). It is more liberal than the FWER while still controlling an average of the FWER restricted to the subpopulations. Therefore, the issue discussed here also applies to PWER‐control and can be handled analogously. The same holds for closed testing procedures and step‐up or step‐down methods.

We further note that the lack of type I error control in the ANOVA model also occurs in the special case of a single target population composed of heterogeneous subpopulations. This shows that the problem addressed here is not due to the multiplicity correction via FWER or PWER control, but rather to the bias in estimating the treatment effect in a target population when conditioning on its subgroup sample sizes.

All methods presented in Section 3 were defined, for simplicity, under the assumption of equal variances across subpopulations and treatments. However, they can equally be applied under the more realistic assumption of heterogeneous variances. This only requires minor modifications to the test statistics and their correlations, which then depend on these variances and must also be estimated.

Finally, we want to address an apparent paradox which comes when defining treatment effects via a discrete random variable (instead of a constant) as we did in Section 3.2. If first a subpopulation realizes with a certain probability and then the patient outcomes are generated in this population, our observations would no longer be independent. This means that we would probably perform worse at FWER control than if we had known nothing about the heterogeneity of the strata. In fact, one should think differently: with each patient, the pair consisting of the response value and the strata membership is realized (in a single step), and therefore, we can still assume that the observations are independent.

Our considerations may also be relevant in adaptive and group sequential study designs. We will address this in our future research.
