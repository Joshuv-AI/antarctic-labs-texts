Research on multi-objective optimization method of Z-shaped pipeline structure based on Kriging model
Authors: Chunya Sun, Zhengdong Xiao, Yanqiu Xiao, Zhifang Xu, Wanbin Cui, Pengpeng Wang, Zhanpeng Fang, Guangzhen Cui, Lianhui Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSlurry pipeline transportation is widely used in dredging and serves as an essential method for conveying solid materials. However, accurately describing the interaction between slurry and particles through numerical simulations, while optimizing the pipeline structure to improve the performance of slurry pipelines, poses a significant engineering challenge. In this study, a Z-shaped continuous pipeline, designed using B-spline curves, is implemented in the slurry circulation system. The validity of the CFD–DEM method is confirmed through slurry circulation experiments, and the effects of various structural parameters on transportation efficiency and flow characteristics are investigated. By integrating the Kriging surrogate model with the NSGA-II algorithm, a multi-objective optimization method is proposed to reduce computational complexity and improve the sediment-carrying capacity of the Z-shaped pipeline. Using a slurry shield tunneling machine with a diameter of 6.24 m as an example, this study optimizes a Z-shaped pipeline with an inner diameter of 0.3 m and an axial height of 1.5 m. The optimized structural parameters increase the average particle velocity by 8.9% and reduce particle accumulation by 21.3%. Additionally, the interaction between the pipeline’s inclination angle , the B-spline control parameter , and the sediment-carrying capacity is analyzed.Keywords: B-spline, Kriging model, Multi-objective optimization, Pipeline transportationSubject terms: Civil engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

In the field of underground tunnel construction, particularly in projects like subways, tunnels, and water conservancy works, the slurry shield tunneling machine is one of the most widely used excavation tools. Its advantages are especially pronounced in projects that cross-rivers and lakes1,2. The circulation system is the most critical subsystem within the slurry shield machine, as it stabilizes the excavation face and facilitates the transport of slurry and debris. The efficiency of slurry transport directly influences the tunneling progress and overall performance of the machine. Insufficient sediment-carrying capacity can lead to serious engineering accidents, such as the blockage of the excavation chamber and clogging of the discharge pipeline.In the slurry pipeline system, the layout of the pipes is complex, particularly with the use of Z-shaped pipelines at the slurry inlet and discharge sections of the slurry tank. These Z-shaped pipes are used to change the direction of slurry flow and avoid interfering with other components. Moreover, the Z-shaped pipeline allows for the diversion and convergence of slurry and water within the system. When a multi-phase fluid flows through the Z-shaped bend, the combined effects of gravity, centrifugal force, and buoyancy create complex flow characteristics, impacting the overall conveying efficiency3. Therefore, studying the flow characteristics within Z-shaped pipelines is essential, and improving their sediment-carrying capacity is particularly important.

Currently, numerical simulation methods are one of the effective approaches for simulating particle-fluid coupling systems. Compared to experimental methods, simulation techniques provide greater controllability over experimental variables and lower time costs. Among these, the Computational Fluid Dynamics-Discrete Phase Model (CFD-DPM) is a commonly utilized method for simulating fluid-particle interactions. Mohebi et al.4 utilized CFD-DPM to determine the erosion-prone areas in the hopper, and found that the erosion rate of 90-degree elbows was the highest, followed by 60-degree elbows, and the erosion rate of 45-degree elbows was lower, thus providing guidance and preventive measures. Elaswad et al.5 utilized CFD-DPM to investigate the behavior of solid particles of varying sizes in an accelerating annular pipe filled with fluid. Their findings revealed that smaller particles tend to mimic the flow of the fluid, whereas larger particles exhibit a lag effect induced by their inertia.It is noteworthy that the CFD-DPM model lacks consideration of inter-particle interactions, thereby precluding an accurate depiction of phenomena such as particle collisions, aggregation, and blockages. In contrast, the introduction of the Computational Fluid Dynamics-Discrete Element Method (CFD–DEM) has effectively addressed this issue by incorporating mechanisms for inter-particle interactions, facilitating a more precise simulation of collision, aggregation, and blockage phenomena. Guo et al.6 constructed a three-dimensional transient grout pebble numerical model based on the CFD–DEM coupling approach, and investigated the influences of pebble shape, pebble size, pebble grading, and slurry velocity on the critical velocity of pebbles, pebble trajectory, and pipeline pressure drop. For pebbles of the same shape and pipe inclination angle, the critical velocity of pebbles rises with the increase of the equal volume particle size initially, and then declines. For horizontal straight pipes, the pressure drop increases exponentially with the rising of slurry velocity. Li et al.7 employed CFD–DEM to simulate the movement behavior of non-spherical particles in vertical-bending-horizontal pipelines. Under the identical transportation environment, as the sphericity of the particles in the pipeline reduces, the transportation speed increases, the fluid flow rate decreases, the kinetic energy conversion rate of the liquid and solid phases rises, and the pressure drop of the particles in the pipeline decreases along with the decrease of the sphericity. Yang et al.8 utilized CFD–DEM coupled simulation to explore the effects of slurry inlet velocity, stone volume concentration, and pipeline inclination on the pressure drop and conveying capacity of the slurry shield pipe system. With the growth of slurry inlet velocity and stone volume concentration, the pressure drop and the conveying capacity increase exponentially. Chen et al.9 adopted the coupled CFD–DEM to study the internal flow structure of spherical, square platen, and linear coarse particles, and deliberated the working conditions of non-spherical particle plugging, and depicted the particle flow characteristics under the plugging conditions based on the flow state transformation and concentration distribution. Schnorr Filho et al.10 carried out analytical CFD–DEM computations to study the effects of water flow and particle injection rate on transport rate and settlement. It was discovered that for lower velocities or particle insertion rates, more particles settle in the elbow region and form a crystal-like lattice that persists, and that as the particle rate decreases, the particle velocity in the horizontal portion drops faster than that in the vertical portion.Based on the research findings, the CFD–DEM simulation process is adept at taking into account multiple intricate forces and interactions between particles and the flow field. However, its high computational demand often necessitates the use of the single-factor variable method when examining the effects of design variables in order to mitigate computational expenses.When examining the intricate relationships and interactions among multiple factors, a substantial number of simulation experiments are necessary to validate the analysis results, thereby consuming a greater amount of research resources. It is worth mentioning that employing surrogate modeling methods to establish the relationships between design variables and performance outputs can serve as a more efficient alternative to the time-consuming and resource-intensive original model. By conducting sensitivity analysis on each design variable through the construction of surrogate models, one can comprehensively consider the impact of each design variable. Furthermore, utilizing optimization methods based on surrogate models, where the surrogate model is used to compute the objective during the optimization process, represents a more efficient alternative to high-fidelity multi-physics computational models11. Meanwhile, optimization methods have been widely applied in the engineering field to address various challenges, demonstrating exceptional performance in the design and improvement of pipeline systems12–15.

This paper proposes the application of B-splines to a Z-shaped conveying pipeline in order to establish a parametric model for the pipeline. Utilizing CFD–DEM, a fluid-particle coupling numerical calculation model for the Z-shaped pipeline within the circulation system is constructed. The accuracy of the CFD–DEM approach is validated through a slurry circulation test. By integrating the Kriging surrogate model with the NSGA-II algorithm, a multi-objective optimization method for the continuous Z-shaped pipeline structure is developed. As an example, the method is applied to a slurry shield machine with an excavation diameter of 6.24 m used in a specific engineering project. In this case, the Z-shaped pipeline, with an inner diameter of 0.3 m and an axial height of 1.5 m, is optimized. The interaction between the pipeline inclination angle, the B-spline control parameter , and the sediment-carrying capacity of the pipeline is analyzed.

Geometric model

Currently, B-spline curves are widely used in fields such as computer graphics, computer-aided design (CAD), and numerical analysis. In the context of pipe bend design, B-spline curves offer a higher degree of smoothness compared to traditional arc curves. Additionally, by adjusting the position and weight of the control points, B-spline curves can precisely control both the shape of the curve and surface, providing a high level of flexibility.

B-spline construction of Z-shaped pipeline

To construct a Z-shaped continuous pipeline using B-spline curves, it is common practice to establish a local coordinate system to constrain the control points of the B-spline curve. However, this paper proposes an alternative approach by controlling the shape of the B-spline curve through the manipulation of the distances between control points. This method reduces the number of design variables while still satisfying the design requirements. Considering the shape and computational complexity of the Z-shaped continuous pipeline, the Clamped B-Spline method is employed to construct the B-spline curve17. This method ensures that for a B-spline of degree p, the redundancy of the initial and final parameters is , causing the start point of the B-spline curve to coincide with the first control point , and the end point to align with the last control point .In this study, the number of control points used to construct the B-spline curve is n = 3, with a basis function degree of p = 2, resulting in a total of m = 6 nodes. The constraints ensure that the first two control points of the B-spline curve are positioned along the same horizontal line as the straight section of the pipeline, while the last two control points are aligned with the diagonal section, ensuring a smooth transition by making the B-spline curve tangential to both lines. CFD–DEM coupling model

During the slurry shield tunneling process, the sediment cut by the cutter head is broken down by the crusher and transported into the pipeline with the slurry. The mixture is then discharged through a multistage slurry pump. However, the interaction mechanism between the slurry and particles during transport within the slurry circulation system remains unclear. The flow process is complex, and the flow state is difficult to observe. In this paper, the rheological characteristic model of the grout is obtained through experimental analysis. Using CFD–DEM, a coupled simulation of the movement of particles and slurry in the Z-shaped continuous pipeline is conducted.

CFD–DEM coupling method

CFD–DEM coupling method is a simulation technique which combines CFD and DEM. CFD is used to simulate the velocity and pressure fields of fluids, while DEM is used to simulate the movement, interaction and collision of particles. The CFD–DEM coupling method can be divided into resolved and un-resolved approaches19,20. In this study, the unresolved CFD–DEM model is employed, and the coupling process involves the following three steps: (1) retrieving particle radius, velocity, and position data from the DEM side and transmitting it to the CFD side; (2) the CFD side then calculates the void fraction and interaction forces (e.g. pressure gradient, drag force, etc.) exerted by the fluid phase on the solid particles; (3) finally, the reactive forces applied by the solid particles on the fluid phase are calculated based on Newton’s third law of motion. Method of optimization

Establishing a fluid-solid coupling numerical model for multiphase flow, while ensuring computational accuracy, incurs significant time and cost. It is worth noting that adopting a surrogate model to capture the relationship between inputs and outputs can replace the original model, which is time-consuming and resource-intensive. Furthermore, using optimization methods based on surrogate models where the surrogate model is used to compute the objective function during the optimization process can serve as a more efficient alternative for high-fidelity multi-physics numerical models. In this paper, the NSGA-II algorithm is combined with the Kriging model to optimize the B-spline bend pipe, aiming to enhance the sediment-carrying performance of slurry circulation pipelines.

Experimental design

Design of Experiments (DOE) is a systematic methodology for planning, conducting, analyzing, and interpreting controlled scientific experiments. Experimental design involves the careful selection of factors to be investigated and their levels, as well as determining the experimental setup that provides the maximum amount of information with the minimum number of experiments. Commonly used experimental design approaches include Parameter Study (PS), Full Factorial Design (FFD), Fractional Factorial Design (Fractional Factorial), Orthogonal Array (OA), Central Composite Design (CCD), Box-Behnken Design, Latin Hypercube Design (LHD), and Optimal Latin Hypercube Design (Opt LHD). Before constructing the surrogate model, sample points must be selected, and the response values of those points should be obtained through experiments. The quality of the sample point selection directly affects the accuracy of the surrogate model. The more uniformly the sample points are distributed across the entire design space, the higher the accuracy of the surrogate model. Opt LHD ensures sufficient sample points in all dimensions by evenly distributing points within the hypercube space, addressing the drawback of poor distribution uniformity that can arise from random LHD sampling. As a result, the accuracy of experimental outcomes can be improved. The design approach seeks to minimize estimation error for a given sample size. Therefore, the Opt LHD sampling method is employed in this paper.

NSGA-II algorithm

Intelligent optimization algorithms have been widely applied to multi-objective problems, with common algorithms including NSGA-II, SPEA-2 and MOPSO, etc. These algorithms are highly representative in engineering applications and have gained widespread recognition24. Previous studies have compared the performance of NSGA-II, SPEA-2 and MOPSO25,26, demonstrating that NSGA-II exhibits significant advantages in computational efficiency, particularly when addressing bi-objective and tri-objective optimization problems. Compared to multi-objective optimization methods based on the weighted sum approach, NSGA-II does not require predefined weights for objectives, effectively avoiding biases caused by inappropriate weight settings27. Therefore, this study employs the NSGA-II algorithm to solve the bi-objective optimization problem.

The NSGA-II algorithm is one of the most widely used multi-objective evolutionary optimization algorithms, demonstrating significant robustness28. Variation and crossover are the two primary genetic operations in the NSGA-II algorithm, with each target parameter calculated for fitness and selected independently. During the selection process, NSGA-II employs a strategy based on non-dominated sorting and crowding distance, incorporating an elitist approach. Compared to earlier NSGA algorithms, NSGA-II is a fast non-dominated sorting algorithm with lower computational complexity. Additionally, when addressing complex multi-objective optimization problems, it typically generates a superior approximate Pareto optimal solution set while maintaining the diversity of this set.

The implementation process of the NSGA-II algorithm is illustrated in Fig. 10 (Section 3). Initially, a random initial population is generated. Next, the population undergoes non-dominated sorting based on individual fitness values, assigning each individual to a specific non-domination rank and calculating its crowding distance to measure solution diversity. During the selection phase, suitable individuals are chosen as parents from the current population based on their non-domination rank and crowding distance. These parent individuals undergo crossover and mutation operations to produce new offspring. The offspring are then merged with the current population (parents) to form a temporary population. Non-dominated sorting is performed again on this temporary population, and crowding distances are updated. Subsequently, a fixed-size new population is selected based on the priority of non-domination rank and crowding distance. This new population serves as the parent population for the next generation. Through iterative execution of these steps, the population progressively converges toward the Pareto front, ultimately yielding an approximate Pareto-optimal solution set for the multi-objective optimization problem.

The velocity cloud map of the slurry flow in the pipeline at different inclination angles (), as shown in Fig. 19, reveals distinct flow characteristics. It is evident that the slurry flow velocity is significantly lower on the side where sediment accumulates within the inclined pipeline, compared to the higher velocities observed on the sediment-free side. This discrepancy arises from the reduction in the cross-sectional flow area due to sediment deposition, leading to decreased flow velocities in areas with substantial sediment accumulation, which are more prone to blockages. As the inclination angle increases, a general trend of decreasing slurry flow velocity is observed. Beyond an inclination angle of , the regions of higher slurry velocity are predominantly localized on the inner side of the bends. According to Bernoulli’s principle, when the flow direction changes at the first bend, the fluid on the inside decelerates, while the fluid on the outside accelerates. At the same time, most of the fluid being pushed toward the outer diameter of the bend results in a further reduction in speed. The volume fraction of fluid on the inner side decreases, allowing a small amount of fluid to pass through the bend at higher speeds. As the fluid continues along the inclined pipe, the velocity stabilizes until it reaches the second bend, where a similar velocity profile is observed.

Conclusions

This paper applies B-spline curves to the design of slurry circulation system pipelines and explores the impact of different inclination angles on the sediment-carrying capacity of the pipeline. A multi-objective optimization approach is employed, with the inclination angle and B-spline control parameters , , , and as design variables, and the number of particles and average particle velocity within the pipeline as objective values. The specific implementation methods and optimization results are as follows:

A simulation model is established based on CFD–DEM theory for solid-fluid coupled numerical simulation calculations. The accuracy of the simulation model was validated by comparing the simulation results with experimental data.

This study employs the CFD–DEM method to perform a numerical simulation of the slurry transportation process in pipelines, with a particular focus on the sediment-carrying capacity. However, the investigation does not address the wear characteristics of the pipeline. Future research could integrate theoretical analysis with numerical simulations to explore the mechanisms and characteristics of pipeline wear in greater depth, providing valuable insights for optimizing pipeline design and operation.
