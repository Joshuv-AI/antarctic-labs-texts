Developing the new diagnostic model by integrating bioinformatics and machine learning for osteoarthritis
Authors: Jian Du, Tian Zhou, Wei Zhang, Wei Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a common cause of disability among the elderly, profoundly affecting quality of life. This study aims to leverage bioinformatics and machine learning to develop an artificial neural network (ANN) model for diagnosing OA, providing new avenues for early diagnosis and treatment.MethodsFrom the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, we first obtained OA synovial tissue microarray datasets. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with OA were identified through utilization of the Limma package and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Subsequently, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis and machine learning were employed to identify the most relevant potential feature genes of OA, and ANN diagnostic model and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were constructed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the model. In addition, the expression levels of the feature genes were verified using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Finally, immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using CIBERSORT algorithm to explore the correlation between feature genes and immune cells.ResultsThe Limma package and WGCNA identified a total of 72 DEGs related to OA, of which 12 were up-regulated and 60 were down-regulated. Then, the PPI network analysis identified 21 hub genes, and three machine learning algorithms finally screened four feature genes (BTG2, CALML4, DUSP5, and GADD45B). The ANN diagnostic model was constructed based on these four feature genes. The AUC of the training set was 0.942, and the AUC of the validation set was 0.850. In addition, the qRT-PCR validation results demonstrated a significant downregulation of BTG2, DUSP5, and GADD45 mRNA expression levels in OA samples compared to normal samples, while CALML4 mRNA expression level exhibited an upregulation. Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed B cells memory, T cells gamma delta, B cells naive, Plasma cells, T cells CD4 memory resting, and NK cells The abnormal infiltration of activated cells may be related to the progression of OA.ConclusionsBTG2, CALML4, DUSP5, and GADD45B were identified as potential feature genes for OA, and an ANN diagnostic model with good diagnostic performance was developed, providing a new perspective for the early diagnosis and personalized treatment of OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-024-05340-4.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent chronic degenerative joint disease characterized by the deterioration of cartilage, inflammation of synovial tissue, and joint dysfunction [1, 2]. In recent years, the prevalence of OA has gradually increased due to the aging population and rising obesity rates [3, 4]. Research findings indicate that among men aged 65 and above, the prevalence of OA in the hand, knee, and hip is 60%, 33%, and 5% respectively [5]. Furthermore, OA stands as a primary cause of disability in older adults, significantly compromising their quality of life while imposing substantial economic burdens on individuals and society at large [6, 7]. Although drugs and physical therapy can effectively alleviate symptoms and slow down the progression of OA, a complete cure for the disease remains elusive [8].

Currently, the diagnosis of OA primarily relies on clinical manifestations and imaging examinations [9]. The former is characterized by joint pain, morning stiffness, and dysfunction [10, 11]. The latter encompasses X-rays, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [12]. However, the initial clinical manifestations of OA are mild and the imaging findings are inconspicuous. Consequently, there is a limited array of diagnostic modalities and efficacious treatment strategies available during the early stages of OA. With the rapid advancement and widespread application of microarray sequencing technology, bioinformatics can extract disease-related biomarkers from complex genomic data, offering novel theoretical guidance for early diagnosis and therapeutic targets in diseases [13]. Therefore, it is crucial to discover new efficacious biomarkers for the early diagnosis and treatment of OA.

In this study, we aimed to develop a novel diagnostic model for the early detection and treatment of OA. Firstly, we utilized the Limma package and WGCNA to screen for genes that are differentially expressed. Subsequently, we employed PPI analysis along with three machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM, and RF) to identify key feature genes. Following this, an ANN diagnostic model was constructed and its accuracy was validated using an independent dataset. Additionally, we investigated the correlation between feature genes and immune cells, offering a novel perspective for the early diagnosis and treatment of osteoarthritis.

Materials and methods

Data download and processing

Five datasets were downloaded from the GEO database(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), respectively GSE55235, GSE55457, GSE12021, GSE206848, and GSE41038.Among them, GSE55235, GSE55457, GSE206848, and GSE41038 were used as the training set, containing a total of 31 normal and 30 OA synovial tissue samples, and GSE12021 was used as the validation set containing 9 normal and 10 OA synovial tissues samples. In this study, we used the “GEOquery” and “tidyverse” packages to annotate platform information for all datasets and converted probe IDs to corresponding gene symbols [14].In addition, to eliminate batch effects caused by different platforms, we merged GSE55235, GSE55457, GSE206848, and GSE41038 for normalization and batch effect removal using the “sva” and “limma” packages [15].For more information on the above datasets, please refer to Table 1.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis between Normal and OA samples in the training set was performed using the R software “limma” package. The threshold was set as |log 2FC|>1 and adjusted P < 0.05. In addition, the R software “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” packages were used to draw volcano maps and heat maps of DEGs.

WGCNA analysis

The WGCNA software package was used to analyze the gene expression data in the training set to calculate gene correlations [16].Firstly, the abnormal data were filtered to improve the stability of the model, and the topological overlap matrix (TOM) was established by selecting a soft threshold β. Then, hierarchical clustering and dynamic cut tree algorithm were used to identify the most significant modules of genes to analyze the association between modules and disease characteristics. Finally, DEGs were intercrossed with genes in the significant module by using Venn diagram.

Function enrichment analysis

To analyze the biological significance of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the R software “clusterProfiler “package [17]. In addition, P < 0.05 was considered as significant enrichment and was visualized using the R software “enrichplot”, “ggplot2” and “GOplot” packages.

Protein–protein interaction network construction

The 72 genes were imported into the STRING database (http://string-db.org). Then, the biospecies selected “Homo sapiens” and the interaction score was set to 0.4 to construct the PPI network。In addition, the data obtained from the PPI network were imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) for network structure analysis. To further identify hub genes within the network, the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape software was used. In the CytoHubba plug-in, three algorithms, Degree, Closeness, and Betweenness, were selected, and the top 30 hub genes screened by each algorithm were designated as node genes. Finally, the overlapping genes screened by the three algorithms are shown by Venn diagram to facilitate further analysis.

Feature genes were screened by machine learning

To further screen the feature genes, three machine learning algorithms, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM) and random forest (RF) were used. Firstly, the “randomForest” package [18] in the RF algorithm was used, and genes with importance score > 1 were included in the screening criteria. Second, candidate genes were identified using the “glmnet” package [19] of the LASSO algorithm and the “e1071” package [20] of the SVM algorithm. Finally, Venn diagram was used to show the feature genes screened by the three machine learning algorithms.

Construction and validation of artificial neural networks

According to the expression level of the characteristic Gene, the gene was scored to obtain the Gene Score table. If the expression level of the gene was higher than the median value of gene expression, the gene score was recorded as 1. Similarly, a gene score was scored as 0 if its expression level was lower than the median gene expression value. Based on the Gene Score table, an ANN model was constructed using the “neuralnet” and “NeuralNetTools” packages [21], and the model parameters were set to 5 hidden layers.。In addition, the performance of the ANN model was evaluated by plotting the ROC curve using the “pROC” package [22] and calculating the area under the curve (AUC) representation. To further verify the accuracy of the model, we used the GSE12021 dataset to construct the ROC curve.

Clinical data collection

In this study, normal synovial tissue was collected from three patients with meniscus injuries, and synovial tissue was collected from three patients with OA who were undergoing knee replacement surgery, following the acquisition of written informed consent from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eighth Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Ethical approval number: 309202406063212) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from synovial tissue of patients using Trizol (Servicebio) reagent, followed by the conversion of the extracted total RNA into cDNA utilizing AG Prime Script® RT Master Mix reagent. Subsequently, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was conducted employing 2xSYBR Green qPCR Hub Mix (Service). The primer information for the four featured genes is presented in Table 2, with the GAPDH gene serving as the reference gene. Relative gene expression was calculated utilizing the 2−△△Ct method.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and GraphPad Prism 9. A comparison of gene expression differences between the two groups was conducted using an independent sample t-test. Data visualization was carried out with GraphPad Prism 9, while a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

According to the CIBERSORT algorithm, the parameter perm = 1000 and P < 0.05 were employed as the filtering conditions, enabling calculation of the relative content of 22 immune infiltrating cells in both normal control samples and OA samples. Then, the R software “corrplot” package was used to create a heat map of immune cell correlations in the samples. In addition, the R software “vioplot” package was utilized to generate box plots, illustrating the disparities between immune cells in normal control samples and OA samples [23].

Correlation analysis of signature genes and immune cell infiltration

The R software package ‘reshape2’ was utilized to process the gene expression data and obtain the expression levels of core genes. The filtering condition for circulating immune cells was set at a significance level of p < 0.05. The R software packages “ggpubr” and “ggExtra’” were utilized to generate lollipop maps illustrating the correlation between the featured genes and immune cells.

Results

Identification of DEGs

A total of 141 DEGs were identified between the Normal and OA samples in the training set, comprising 55 up-regulated genes and 86 down-regulated genes. To provide a more visually informative representation of the DEGs, volcano plots and heat maps were generated (Fig. 2A, B).

WGCNA

To identify the gene modules most associated with OA, WGCNA analysis was performed using the expression levels of 9262 genes. The optimal soft threshold of 8 was chosen(R2 = 0.9) to establish the scale-free network (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, cluster analysis with a minimum module size set at 60 was employed to identify highly similar modules (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, dynamic cutting yielded five gene modules represented by different colors, among which the blue module consisting of 304 genes exhibited the highest correlation with OA (cor = 0.72, P = 9e-11) (Fig. 3C). Ultimately, a total of 72 overlapping genes were identified as potential candidates, including 12 exhibiting up-regulation and 60 showing down-regulation (Fig. 3D).

Functional enrichment analysis

The GO enrichment analysis of 72 DEGs showed that in biological function (BP), they were mainly enriched in fat cell differentiation, response to oxygen levels and response to lipopolysaccharide; In cellular function (CC), It was mainly enriched in RNA polymerase II transcription regulator complex, Bcl-2 family protein complex and RNA polymerase II transcription repressor complex and other processes; In molecular function (MF), It is mainly enriched in DNA − binding transcription activator activity, RNA polymerase II − specific, DNA − binding transcription activator activity, and cytokine receptor binding (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the KEGG enrichment analysis of 72 DEGs revealed their predominant enrichment in the MAPK signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 4B).

PPI network construction

The establishment of a PPI network, consisting of 57 nodes and 247 edges, was accomplished by utilizing 72 DEGs (Fig. 5A). In addition, the top 30 PPI genes were screened using three algorithms, namely Degree, Closeness, and Betweenness in the CytoHubba plugin (Fig. 5B, C and D), followed by taking their intersection. Ultimately, a total of 21 potential hub genes were identified (Fig. 5E). Among them, the 20 hub genes were up-regulated genes (JUN, MYC, IL6, ATF3, EGR1, FOSB, NR4A1, CDKN1A, GADD45B, KLF4, TNFAIP3, NR4A2, JUND, ZFP36, BTG2, DUSP5, FOSL1, CXCL2, CYR61 and RGS1) and one hub gene (CALML4) was down-regulated.

Machine learning to screen feature genes

The LASSO algorithm was employed to select 5 diagnostic genes (Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, SVM models were constructed to identify 18 candidate genes with the lowest error and highest accuracy (Fig. 6C, D). In the meantime, RF screened 13 candidate genes (Fig. 6E, F). Finally, the candidate genes obtained by the three machine learning algorithms were intercrossed to obtain 4 representative feature genes, including BTG2, CALML4, DUSP5 and GADD45B (Fig. 6G).

Diagnostic efficacy of feature genes in predicting OA

The diagnostic relevance of the 4 signature genes was assessed through training and validation sets. Firstly, the results from the training set demonstrated significant differences in expression levels between Normal and OA samples for all four signature genes (Fig. 7A). Secondly, an ANN model was constructed using these 4 feature genes, comprising of 4 input layers, 5 hidden layers, and 2 output layers (Fig. 7B). We further assessed the accuracy of the model by evaluating its performance on both the training and validation sets. The AUC of the training set was 0.942 (95%CI: 0.878–0.989) (Fig. 7C), while the AUC value of the validation set was 0.850 (95%CI: 0.633-1.000) (Fig. 7D). In addition, ROC curves were also constructed for these 4 signature genes, yielding AUC values of 0.892 (BTG2), 0.860 (CALML4), 0.865 (DUSP5), and 0.881 (GADD45B) in the training set, respectively (Fig. 7E). The validation set exhibited AUCs of 0.944 (BTG2), 0.822 (CALML4), 0.878 (DUSP5), and 0.933 (GADD45B) correspondingly (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, external datasets were utilized to validate the expression levels of these four signature genes, which consistently supported the aforementioned results obtained from our study’s dataset analysis (Fig. 8A-D). Overall, the ANN model demonstrates good diagnostic performance and holds potential as an early diagnostic tool for OA.

Discussion

OA is a prevalent global degenerative bone disease that primarily affects the knee joint and often leads to irreversible structural changes, ultimately resulting in joint deformity and disability [24, 25]. While clinical manifestations and imaging examinations are the primary diagnostic methods for OA, early diagnosis and treatment remain significant challenges in clinical practice [26]. Increasing evidence suggests a close association between synovial inflammation and the onset and progression of OA [27, 28]. Therefore, identifying diagnostic markers in OA synovial tissue holds great importance for early diagnosis and treatment.

In this study, we employed the Limma package and WGCNA to screen 72 DEGs from a cohort of 31 normal samples and 30 OA samples. GO enrichment analysis revealed their potential involvement in adipocyte differentiation as well as transcription factor and cytokine receptor binding. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated their potential association with crucial signaling pathways such as MAPK, IL-17, and TNF signaling pathways. To unravel the intricate interactions among these DEGs, we identified 21 hub genes through protein-protein interaction network analysis. Additionally, employing three machine learning algorithms (SVM-RFE, LASSO, and RF), we successfully identified four diagnostic signature genes (BTG2, CALML4, DUSP5, and GADD45B). Moreover, based on these four signature genes, an artificial neural network-based diagnostic model was constructed which exhibited promising accuracy when evaluated using external datasets; the AUC for both training set and validation set were found to be 0.942 and 0.850 respectively. These findings indicated the excellent diagnostic performance of the four signature genes in OA.

Of the four signature genes identified, BTG2 is a gene that inhibits cell proliferation, and its expression is closely related to many cellular activities such as cell growth, apoptosis and proliferation [29, 30]. In addition, the BTG2 also exerts a crucial influence on the regulation of immune infiltration by T cells and B cells [31].Studies have shown that the expression level of BTG2 is decreased in OA synovial tissue and cartilage tissue, which may be involved in a variety of inflammatory reactions in OA tissue [32].Another study showed that BTG2 expression levels were reduced in OA cartilage tissue and chondrocytes compared to OA normal cartilage tissue and chondrocytes and may play a key role in the pathogenesis of OA [33]. Although, the aforementioned content suggests a potential association between BTG2 and the occurrence and progression of OA, the pathogenesis of this relationship remains unclear and requires further investigation.

CALML4 is a member of the Ca2+/ calmodulin family (calcium-binding proteins) and regulates the Ca2+/ calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II pathway [34]. Additionally, calcium channels play a crucial role in the metabolism and maintenance of the extracellular matrix, while up-regulation of CALML4 can enhance cartilage degradation by facilitating calcium-dependent signaling pathways [35]. Although the role of CALML4 in OA has not been reported to date, our study reveals that CALML4 is up-regulated in OA and may serve as a potential biomarker for OA.

DUSP5 is a dual-specificity phosphatase 5 and plays an important regulatory role in cell death, immunity, and inflammation [36]. Studies have shown that DUSP5 has anti-inflammatory functions and is involved in the pathogenesis of various diseases, such as autoimmune arthritis [37]. A recent study showed that the expression level of DUSP5 is found to be decreased in OA cartilage tissue compared with normal cartilage tissue, thereby indirectly modulating IL-1β-induced inflammation through the inhibition of NF-κB and ERK signaling pathways, thus contributing to the improvement of OA [38].

GADD45B is a small molecule protein that is mainly involved in many cellular activities such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and migration. Additionally, GADD45B has been proposed to have a significant role in the regulation of chondrocyte homeostasis [39]. Studies have shown that GADD45B can lead to the activation of JNK, arthritis and joint destruction, which provides a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of arthritis [40].It has also been shown that the expression level of GADD45B is decreased in rat articular cartilage, which may be closely related to the inflammation of OA cartilage tissue [41].

The abnormality of immune cells is a fundamental factor contributing to immune dysfunction. Previous studies have demonstrated the crucial role of immune cells in the pathogenesis and progression of OA [42]. In this study, we utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to assess disparities in immune cell infiltration between Normal and OA samples. Our findings revealed significant elevations in B cell memory and T cell gamma delta populations in OA samples compared to normal samples, which aligns with previous research suggesting their importance in the occurrence and progression of OA [13]. In contrast, B cells naive, Plasma cells, T cells CD4 memory resting, and NK cells activated were significantly reduced in OA samples. Next, we found that BTG2 correlated with T cells CD4 memory resting, Monocytes, Eosinophils, Plasma cells, Macrophages M2, and T cells gamma delta. Meanwhile, CALML4 was associated with Macrophages M0, B cells naive, and T cells CD4 memory resting. Meanwhile, DUSP5 was associated with B cells naive, T cells CD4 memory resting, Mast cells activated, Mast cells resting, and B cells memory. In addition, GADD45B was associated with Eosinophils, T cells CD4 memory resting, Dendritic cells activated, Monocytes, B cells naive, and Mast cells activated, Macrophages M2, T cells gamma delta and B cells memory correlated. Therefore, these genes may be related to the occurrence and development of OA by regulating these several immune cells.

Although the diagnostic model we have constructed demonstrates good diagnostic performance, there are still some limitations. Firstly, to expand the sample size, we integrated multiple OA synovial tissue datasets. Although batch correction methods were applied to reduce batch effects from different platform datasets, variability between samples may still affect the accuracy and consistency of gene expression. Additionally, this method may also suppress some important signals. Secondary, our study primarily relies on publicly available datasets, which have relatively small sample sizes, and this may impact the statistical significance and reliability of the study results. Therefore, further expanding the sample size is critical to enhancing the reliability of the results. Lastly, although we have validated the differential expression of key genes using qRT-PCR on clinical samples, further validation with more clinical data and experiments is needed to confirm the reliability and stability of the model.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified four feature genes associated with OA through bioinformatics and machine learning, and developed an artificial neural network diagnostic model based on these genes, which demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic performance for OA. Therefore, these genes have the potential to serve as model for the early diagnosis of OA and provide potential genetic targets for personalized treatment of OA.
