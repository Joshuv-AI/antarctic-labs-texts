Nd: YAG laser rescue of sulcus-placed glaucoma drainage device tube occlusion: a two-case series

Background

Glaucoma drainage devices (GDDs) are widely used in refractory glaucoma and have become increasingly important in eyes at high risk of filtering surgery failure or with complex anterior segment anatomy [1, 2]. The Aurolab Aqueous Drainage Implant (AADI) is a low-cost, nonvalved silicone device modeled after the Baerveldt implant and has shown comparable efficacy to more established GDDs, including the Ahmed glaucoma valve, in both adult and pediatric populations [2–7].

Despite their effectiveness, GDDs may fail because of postoperative tube-related complications. Tube occlusion is a recognized complication following GDD implantation, with reported incidence ranging from 5% to 11% [8, 9]. The internal tube ostium may become blocked by iris tissue, vitreous, fibrin, neovascular membrane, lens capsule, or inflammatory debris [8–14].

Recently, sulcus tube placement has been increasingly used in selected pseudophakic or aphakic eyes and in other complex situations to reduce corneal endothelial risk compared with anterior chamber placement while maintaining effective IOP control. However, sulcus placement does not eliminate the risk of tube blockage and may introduce distinct technical and anatomical considerations [15, 16].

Management of tube blockage ranges from observation to laser intervention to surgical revision or vitrectomy, depending on the obstructing substrate and the overall condition of the eye [2, 8, 12, 17]. Surgical revision or vitrectomy may be effective, but these procedures can carry substantial morbidity, especially in monocular, pediatric, aphakic, post-keratoplasty, and high-risk posterior segment eyes. Laser-based approaches, including Nd: YAG membranectomy, vitreolysis, and capsulotomy, have been described as minimally invasive alternatives to surgical revision, but the existing literature consists predominantly of small retrospective case series and isolated case reports of tube occlusion in Ahmed, Baerveldt, Molteno, Schocket, or PreserFlo devices [7–14, 16, 17]. To our knowledge, no prior cases of sulcus-placed AADI tube occlusion have been reported to be managed with laser, despite its growing use in low- and middle-income countries and in eyes in which surgery carries a disproportionate risk.

We report 2 cases of successful Nd: YAG laser rescue of sulcus-placed AADI tube obstruction, one by iris and one by vitreous, in high-risk vision-critical eyes at a tertiary eye hospital in Bangladesh. We also provide a structured review of the literature on laser management of GDD tube occlusion and propose a tissue-specific clinical decision framework.

Case presentation

Case 1: iris occlusion of AADI tube

An 11-year-old female with primary congenital glaucoma (PCG) presented with uncontrolled intraocular pressure (IOP) in the left eye, the only seeing eye. She had undergone combined trabeculotomy-trabeculectomy in her left eye at the age of 2 years, which subsequently failed. The right eye was nonfunctional and had a prosthesis (Fig. 1A), and the left eye had a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 6/36 (-1.50 DCyl @ 120°) and an IOP of 28 mmHg with maximum tolerated anti-glaucoma medications. Given the refractory nature of the glaucoma and the high stakes of managing the only eye, AADI (350 mm2) implantation was performed in the left eye with tube placement in the ciliary sulcus of the phakic eye.

Key clinical features of Case 1 – (A) Prosthetic right eye; (B) Visible sulcus-placed AADI tube in mid-dilated pupil (white arrow); (C) Confirmation of patent sulcus-placed AADI tube with UBM (red star); (D) Late tube-blockage with iris (yellow arrow); (E) Immediate post-Nd: YAG laser iridotomy showing visible patent tube with hyphaema (red arrow); (F) Patent sulcus-placed tube in subsequent follow-up visit (red arrow). (AADI – Aurolab Aqueous Drainage Implant, UBM – Ultrasound Biomicroscopy)

On slit-lamp examination with a mid-dilated pupil, the tube tip was initially visible and patent in the immediate postoperative period (Fig. 1B). Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) later confirmed the location of the sulcus tube (Fig. 1C). During follow-up, BCVA remained 6/36, and IOP remained controlled at approximately 12–18 mmHg with brinzolamide alone. However, 10 months after surgery, the patient presented with an elevated IOP of 36 mmHg with reduced vision to 6/60. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy revealed mild corneal edema with iris tissue occluding the tube lumen (Fig. 1D). The diagnosis of iris-related tube occlusion was confirmed.

Nd: YAG laser iridotomy was performed at the slit lamp without a contact lens. The laser was delivered in Q-switched mode, with energy ranging from 2.3 to 5.0 mJ, using 43 shots directed at the iris tissue overlying and occluding the tube lumen, with an estimated cumulative energy of approximately 150–170 mJ. Following laser treatment, the tube lumen was confirmed to be patent on slit-lamp examination (Fig. 1E).

Two hours after laser treatment, IOP transiently rose to 40 mmHg. Mild hyphema and a moderate anterior chamber reaction were present, with grade 3 cells and grade 2 flare. These findings resolved spontaneously over 3 days under prednisolone acetate (four times daily for 15 days) and brinzolamide (three times daily for 30 days). Thereafter, IOP stabilized and remained around 12 mmHg without antiglaucoma medication. At the most recent follow-up, 7 months after the laser treatment, BCVA remained 6/36, the tube remained open, IOP was under control without anti-glaucoma medications, and there was no recurrence of iris occlusion (Fig. 1F).

Case 2: vitreous occlusion of AADI tube

A 38-year-old man with a history of extensive flame burn injury involving the whole body and severe ocular surface compromise presented with severely compromised vision and uncontrolled glaucoma in his only functional right eye (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1). He had previously undergone bilateral penetrating keratoplasty elsewhere with open-sky vitrectomy and remained aphakic. The left graft later failed, and only the right eye retained 5/60 vision. Despite maximal tolerated anti-glaucoma medication, IOP in the right eye remained uncontrolled at around 40 mmHg. AADI (350 mm2) implantation was performed in the right eye with tube placement in the ciliary sulcus.

Key clinical features of Case 2 – (A) Bilateral post-PK eyes where left graft failed; (B) Sulcus-placed AADI tube blockage with vitreous strands (white arrow); (C) Post-Nd: YAG laser vitreolysis (black arrow); (D) Exposed AADI plate on subsequent follow-up (yellow arrows); (E) Decompensated cornea with bullous keratopathy, and anterior migration of the sulcus-placed AADI tube, touching the corneal endothelium (red star). (PK – Penetrating Keratoplasty, AADI – Aurolab Aqueous Drainage Implant)

The tube was ligated with an absorbable suture (6 − 0 Polyglactin) during surgery, following the standard technique for non-valved devices without any intraluminal polypropylene suture. At the early postoperative follow-up, one week after surgery, he maintained hand motion (HM) vision with an IOP of 22 mmHg while using a combination of timolol maleate and dorzolamide. Approximately 4 weeks after surgery, the patient experienced a rise in IOP to 32 mmHg due to spontaneous ligature release and the onset of aqueous flow. Slit-lamp examination showed vitreous occluding the tube tip (Fig. 2B).

Nd: YAG vitreolysis was performed without a contact lens. Energy ranged from 2.3 to 5.0 mJ, and 10 shots were delivered to lyse the vitreous strand obstructing the tube, with an estimated cumulative energy of approximately 30–40 mJ. No contact lens was used, as the clear corneal graft provided adequate visualization. Care was taken to direct the laser at the vitreous adhesion rather than the silicone tube itself. The vitreous strand was disrupted, and aqueous flow through the tube was visually confirmed (Fig. 2C). Maximum anti-glaucoma medications were continued initially, along with prednisolone acetate four times daily for 15 days. Two hours after laser treatment, IOP decreased from 32 mmHg to 28 mmHg, and the tube appeared functionally reopened (Fig. 2C).

However, the subsequent course was unfavorable because of the severely compromised ocular surface. At follow-up on the 16th day post-laser, the patient reported a deterioration of vision from 5/60 to perception of light with accurate projection of rays (PL, PR). Slit-lamp biomicroscopy showed an exposed AADI plate (Fig. 2D), a decompensated corneal graft with bullous keratopathy, tube migration into the anterior chamber, and the tube lying almost against the corneal endothelium (Fig. 2E).

The patient had severe generalized burn-related ocular surface disease with limbal stem cell deficiency, predisposing to conjunctival instability and erosion. Progressive conjunctival erosion led to device exposure. Because of the risk of infection and ongoing ocular surface compromise, the AADI was explanted immediately and was kept under maximum IOP-lowering medications and antibiotic coverage to reduce the risk of endophthalmitis. Unfortunately, the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

This report describes two clinically important, mechanistically distinct cases in which Nd: YAG laser was used to rescue obstruction of sulcus-placed AADI tubes in high-risk eyes.

AADI has become an important option for refractory glaucoma, particularly in resource-constrained settings, due to its affordability, with published studies demonstrating meaningful IOP lowering and acceptable safety profiles [2, 3]. Its nonvalved design shares many postoperative behavior patterns with other large-plate nonvalved devices, including tube-related complications after ligature release [1, 2].

Insertion of GDD tubes into the sulcus is gaining popularity because it may reduce the risk of corneal endothelial cell loss compared with anterior chamber insertion, with similar intraocular pressure control [15, 16]. However, sulcus tubes are prone to blockage by neighboring intraocular structures, e.g., iris, vitreous, inflammatory membranes, fibrin, blood, and lens capsule [8, 10–14]. In our first case, UBM confirmed the intended sulcus location in a phakic eye. In the second case, the tube later migrated into the anterior chamber and came into near contact with the corneal endothelium due to plate exposure and profound surface instability, rather than as part of the initial laser event.

A retrospective series by Singh et al. reviewed 13 eyes undergoing Nd: YAG membranectomy for blocked tube shunts and found that 11 of 13 tubes could be reopened initially, although re-blockage was frequent [8]. Subsequent case-based reports have described successful Nd: YAG laser management of different obstructive substrates, including Ahmed tube occlusion by the posterior capsule [9], by the anterior capsule after combined cataract and glaucoma surgery [10], and by capsule, vitreous, and iris in the eye with Boston keratoprosthesis [11]. These reports support the general principle that, when the obstructing tissue is visible and accessible, a laser may restore tube function without immediate surgery, but they also make clear that long-term durability depends on the nature of the obstruction and the eye itself.

In the first case, a delayed iris-related blockage in a monocular pediatric eye was successfully relieved, resulting in sustained medication-free IOP control. The case report by Islam and Blake described laser iridoplasty as an alternative approach in iris incarceration of a drainage device, particularly when Nd: YAG iridotomy was thought likely to provoke hyphema in a neovascular eye [12]. Their report reinforces that iris obstruction is a recognized laser-manageable substrate, though the optimal laser modality depends on iris vascularity and behavior.

In the second case, early vitreous obstruction following ligature release in an aphakic flame burn survivor with prior keratoplasty, prior open-sky vitrectomy, and a failed contralateral graft was initially relieved by Nd: YAG vitreolysis, but later implant exposure necessitated explantation due to ocular surface failure. Prior literature has generally leaned toward surgical treatment, especially when vitreous incarceration is extensive or recurrent. Desatnik et al. reported management of glaucoma implants occluded by vitreous incarceration and found that vitrectomy played the main role in definitive treatment [14]. Ang et al. also described recurrent vitreous occlusion of glaucoma drainage device tubes in aphakia, emphasizing the possibility of repeated obstruction and the frequent need for surgical management [13]. Vinod et al. later described the surgical management of vitreous occlusion of a glaucoma drainage implant [17]. Against that background, the initial success of Nd: YAG vitreolysis in our second case is clinically meaningful, even though the implant was later lost for a different reason.

The timing of obstruction also offers a practical teaching point. In Case 1, obstruction occurred approximately 10 months after surgery, suggesting late mechanical iris occlusion rather than early device failure. In Case 2, obstruction occurred shortly after ligature release in a nonvalved implant. This early post-ligature phase is a plausible window for vitreous migration or incarceration in susceptible eyes, especially aphakic eyes with prior vitrectomy [13, 14, 17]. This distinction may help clinicians anticipate likely mechanisms and select an initial rescue strategy.

The complications in Case 1, the post-laser mild hyphema and IOP spike, are recognized complications of Nd: YAG laser iridotomy, reported in up to 15% and 7.7% of cases, respectively, in the Singh et al. series [8]. Both resolved spontaneously within 3 days, and the tube has remained patent without antiglaucoma medications at 7 months. This finding is consistent with Singh et al.’s observation that iris-occluded tubes respond more favorably to laser than fibrous membranes and supports the practical view that laser rescue, particularly for iris obstruction, may carry short-term inflammatory consequences and should be followed by close observation [8]. In Case 2, the mechanism of vitreous occlusion is clinically instructive. In non-valved GDDs such as the AADI, the tube is ligated at surgery to prevent early hypotony. When the ligature dissolves at approximately 4–6 weeks, the sudden establishment of aqueous flow can draw vitreous toward the tube tip, particularly in aphakic or vitrectomized eyes. This scenario is a recognized risk factor for vitreous incarceration [11, 13]. In our case, the vitreous was amenable to Nd: YAG vitreolysis because it formed a discrete adhesion rather than a dense plug, where previous literature has generally favored vitrectomy when vitreous incarceration is persistent or recurrent [14, 17]. Although the Nd: YAG laser initially restored tube function, the long-term outcome was dominated by conjunctival erosion, implant exposure, graft decompensation, and tube migration in an eye with extreme ocular surface fragility. Device exposure is a recognized risk in compromised conjunctiva and ocular surface disease [7, 11]. In this patient, severe burn-related limbal stem cell deficiency was likely the key determinant of ultimate implant failure.

The strength of this report is that it presents two mechanistically distinct obstructions in high-risk eyes and distinguishes initial laser success from later device-related failure caused by unrelated surface disease. In Case 1, a one-eyed child with prior failed glaucoma surgery had successful outpatient laser rescue with an excellent result. In Case 2, laser vitreolysis was a reasonable first rescue step for an aphakic eye where every further operation carried major risk, even though the implant was later lost to surface failure.

This report describes only two cases from a single center, limiting generalizability. The 7-month follow-up in Case 1 is consistent with late failure, despite exceeding the 11-week critical re-blockage window, and the development of cataracts, whether or not the tube touches the lens capsule. No contact lenses were used during the laser procedure in either case, which may have affected precision, and the results may not reflect all surgeons’ preferred techniques. Post-laser objective imaging (UBM or AS-OCT) to confirm tube patency was not performed; tube patency was assessed clinically by slit-lamp visualization and IOP response. Future cases would benefit from objective post-laser imaging documentation. Prospective collection of a larger case series with longer follow-up is warranted. Even so, the report adds clinically useful evidence in an area where the literature remains sparse and heterogeneous.

Based on our cases and the existing literature, we propose a tissue-specific clinical decision framework for managing GDD tube occlusion: (1) Identify the occluding tissue by slit-lamp biomicroscopy with or without gonioscopy; (2) For iris tissue - Nd: YAG iridotomy, or argon/diode iridoplasty if neovascularization is present; (3) For thin vitreous sheet or strand - Nd: YAG vitreolysis; (4) For posterior or anterior capsule - Nd: YAG capsulotomy; (5) For thick fibrous membrane, neovascular tissue, or dense vitreous plug - surgical revision. Post-laser monitoring should include IOP assessment at 1 h, 1 day, 1 week, and monthly for at least 3 months, given the reported re-blockage window of 11 weeks.

Nd: YAG laser may be a useful, minimally invasive, first-line rescue option for selected cases of iris- or vitreous-related obstruction of sulcus-placed GDD tubes, particularly when the obstructing tissue is visible and accessible and when immediate surgery carries substantial risk. Laser rescue is most appropriate when the obstructing tissue is thin, visually identifiable, and accessible at the slit lamp. Dense fibrotic membranes, neovascular tissue, or recurrent vitreous incarceration may still require surgical intervention. Even when laser successfully restores tube flow, long-term implant survival depends on the structural and ocular surface condition of the eye.
