Adding tibial nerve neurodynamic techniques to a rehabilitative pain management strategy improved neuropathy severity and quality of life in patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy: a randomized sham-controlled trial
Authors: Mahdi Ashoori, Seyed Ebrahim Hashemi, Mohammadreza Pourahmadi, Mehdi Dadgoo, Mahboobeh Sadat Hosseini, Hasti Lotfi, Maryam Ahmadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic Peripheral Neuropathy (DPN), affects 30% of type 2 diabetics, leading to severe foot problems. Existing treatments offer limited efficacy. This randomized controlled trial explores Neurodynamic Techniques (NDTs) for their impact on neuropathy severity, quality of life (QoL), nerve function, range of motion (ROM), and nerve mechanosensitivity in DPN patients.MethodsForty DPN patients, confirmed by electrodiagnostic and laboratory tests, were randomized into real and sham tibial NDT groups. Both groups received a basic complementary treatment plus their respective NDTs. Outcome measurements included Michigan Diabetic Neuropathy Score (MDNS), QoL, tibial nerve conduction velocity, distal latency, amplitude of compound muscle action potential, F-wave latency, plantarflexion straight leg raise (PFSLR) ROM and dorsiflexion SLR (DFSLR) ROM tested to the first onset of symptoms (P1) and maximally tolerated symptoms (P2), and nerve mechanosensitivity.ResultsMDNS demonstrated a significant improvement, with a mean difference (MD) of -4.60, indicating a large effect size (Cohen’s d: -0.93) and surpassing the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 2.48, with a P-value of 0.001. QoL improved significantly (MD: -13.25, Cohen’s d: -0.73, MCID: 9.00, P-value: 0.006). Nerve conduction parameters remained unchanged. Small to medium effects were observed in PFSLRP1 ROM for both limbs (right limb MD: 3.35, Cohen’s d: 0.38, MCID: 4.42, P-value: 0.05; left limb MD: 4.40, Cohen’s d: 0.56, MCID: 3.88, P-value: 0.06), and both limbs’ PFSLRP2 ROM (right limb MD: 2.10, Cohen’s d: 0.21, MCID: 4.80, P-value: 0.07; left limb MD: 4.80, Cohen’s d: 0.57, MCID: 4.17, P-value: 0.05). Left DFSLRP1 ROM also improved (MD: 2.35, Cohen’s d: 0.29, MCID: 4.07, P-value: 0.04). Left tibial mechanosensitivity to P1 improved significantly (MD: 2.05, Cohen’s d: 0.64, MCID: 1.57, P-value: 0.04). Other DFSLR ROM outcomes and other mechanosensitivity outcomes remained unchanged throughout this trial.ConclusionsWhile NDTs did not enhance nerve conduction parameters, they significantly reduced neuropathy severity and improved QoL and PFSLR ROM in individuals with DPN.Trial registrationThis study has been pre-registered at https://www.irct.ir/, (registration number: IRCT20220401054379N1) on October 13, 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05168-3.Keywords: Diabetic neuropathy, Peripheral nerves, Neurodynamic techniques, Nerve mobilization, Nerve conduction studies, Electromyography, Range of motion

Introduction

Background

Diabetes is the most prevalent metabolic disease [1]. In 2021, over 520 million people had diabetes, and global diabetes prevalence was reported at 6.1% [2]. It is also known as the eighth leading cause of death and disability [3]. Approximately 50% of adults with diabetes will be affected by diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) [4]. DPN is the primary risk factor for the development of diabetic foot ulcers, which can lead to infection and amputation [5, 6]. Although it is anticipated that health expenditures of diabetes and its complications will reach more than 1 trillion US$ by 2045 [7], current management of DPN is confined to diabetes and pain control, which does not directly target the affected nerves and remains ineffective for most patients [8].

People with DPN demonstrated reduced nerve excursion and altered nerve biomechanics, which are related to DPN severity [9]. Neurodynamic techniques (NDTs) as a method of neural mobilization are defined as a direct nerve-related intervention in which forces are applied to nerve structures through postures and multi-joint movements [10]. Therefore, it is posited that NDT may exert beneficial effects on DPN symptoms by enhancing nerve mobility. Animal studies have also shown the potential impact of NDT in experimentally induced neuropathy. Neural mobilization techniques improved mechanical allodynia by reducing proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin 1β (IL-1β) concentration in the nerves with DPN [11]. NDTs induce biological changes in sensory and motor neurons, improving cell differentiation, neurite outgrowth, and neuron survival [12]. Neural mobilization reduced hyperalgesia in rats with induced DPN, potentially through alleviating nerve degeneration [13]. Therefore, NDT could be a viable method to manage DPN in humans based on the positive effects in animal studies.

Azharrudin and colleagues conducted a scoping review of the existing human studies to investigate the effectiveness of neural mobilization in DPN [14]. Their review indicated a limited number of studies addressing this topic [14]. In 2017, Boyd et al. demonstrated the safety of slider neurodynamic exercises in patients diagnosed with DPN [15]. However, there has been no reported evidence regarding the safety of tensioner techniques in this population. Doshi et al. designed a pre-post study that revealed tibial NDT to be effective in improving sensory nerve conduction velocity (NCV) [16]. Furthermore, a case study by Manu et al. showed that tibial NDT positively influenced pain levels, range of motion (ROM), quality of life (QoL), and magnetic resonance diffusion tensor imaging parameters, which are being used in diagnosing focal and small microstructural polyneuropathy in individuals with DPN [17]. A case series by Ali et al. also reported that NDT alleviated neuropathic pain in patients with upper extremity DPN [18]. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), Alshimy et al. found that NDT enhanced median sensory and tibial motor NCV, improving pain and functional activity in patients with DPN [19]. Lastly, another RCT by Gehlot and colleagues indicated that sciatic nerve mobilization increased the ROM in the ankle, knee, and hip and improved weight-bearing asymmetry among patients with DPN [20].

Despite limited human studies [15–20], there is a notable scarcity of RCTs exploring the effectiveness of NDTs in individuals with DPN. Therefore, this RCT is designed to investigate the efficacy of NDT on neuropathy severity, nerve conduction parameters, QoL, ROM, and nerve mechanosensitivity. We hypothesized that DPN patients receiving NDT would enhance neuropathy severity, nerve conduction parameters, QoL, straight leg raising (SLR) ROM, and nerve mechanosensitivity compared to those receiving sham NDT.

Methods

Trial design and registration

This study was a pre-post two-arm parallel-group, randomized, sham-controlled superiority trial. The allocation ratio was 1:1. It was pre-registered at (registration number: IRCT20220401054379N1) on October 13, 2022, and published [21]. The present study is reported according to the CONSORT statement [22] and TIDieR checklist [23].

Participants

People with DPN were recruited from the outpatient Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation clinic at Baqiyatallah Hospital, Tehran, Iran. To participate in this study, participants needed to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) Be adults aged between 40 and 70 years who have been diagnosed with T2DPN based on their most recent laboratory tests (HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or fasting blood glucose ≥ 125 mg/dl or two hour oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 200 mg/dl) conducted within the last three months [24], as well as an electrodiagnostic examination [25] performed by expert endocrinologist (MSH) and physiatrist (SEH). (2) Having a minimum of 90° hip flexion, full knee extension, and an ankle ROM between 0° dorsiflexion and 30° plantarflexion [9]. The standard normal reference values for adult nerve conduction studies used in this study are shown in Table 1 [26]. The exclusion criteria for the study were established as follows: (1) People who experienced persistent low back pain, neck pain, radiculopathy, or sciatica lasting a minimum of three days within the previous six months, as determined by a four-limb electrodiagnostic study. (2) Individuals with a history of chemical or alcohol dependency. (3) Individuals diagnosed with syphilis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, complex regional pain syndrome, or tarsal tunnel syndrome. (4) People with open wounds or infections in the lower extremities. (5) Amputation above the metatarsophalangeal joint. (6) A history of chemotherapy or spinal surgery conducted within the past year. (7) Previous peripheral nerve injury or surgery. (8) Pregnancy. (9) Individuals exhibiting severe DPN, indicated by a Michigan Diabetic Neuropathy Score (MDNS) exceeding 30, along with having nerve conduction abnormalities in the sural, peroneal motor, median sensory and motor, and ulnar sensory nerves [15, 27]. (10) Participants’ reluctance to participate in the study. Participants were not involved in our research’s design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans.

Interventions

Rehabilitative pain management strategy

Participants in both groups received a rehabilitative pain management strategy as a complementary treatment, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and tibial nerve massage [28–30]. Both groups received TENS at a frequency of 80 Hz, a duration of 0.2 milliseconds, and an intensity of 2 to 3 times the sensory threshold for 20 min [28, 31, 32]. One electrode was positioned at the popliteal fossa, while the second was at the medial tarsal tunnel. Additionally, a tibial nerve massage, consisting of longitudinal and transverse techniques, was applied for four minutes to both the medial tarsal tunnel and the popliteal fossa [30, 33].

Experimental intervention

In addition to this complementary treatment, participants in the experimental group received real tibial nerve distal and proximal slider NDTs [30]. Participants were arranged in a side-lying position on the intended side, with pillows utilized to ensure proper spinal alignment and maintain a neutral spine posture [30].

Real tibial nerve’s distal slider technique [30]. In this initial position, the therapist positioned the patient’s hip at 80° of flexion while maintaining full knee extension (Fig. 1a). The therapist then rhythmically mobilized the ankle toward dorsiflexion, eversion, and toe extension and instructed the patient to extend their head and neck simultaneously (Fig. 1b). This technique was performed for 10 repetitions in five sets [12, 34].

Real and sham tibial NDT. a starting position for real tibial NDT: lower leg at hip 80° flexion, 0° abduction and the knee extended. b real tibial distal slider technique: the therapist moves the ankle into dorsiflexion, eversion combined with toes extension, and the patients are asked to extend their head and neck simultaneously. c real tibial proximal slider technique: in the same starting position, the therapist moves the ankle toward plantarflexion, inversion combined with toes flexion and asks the patients to flex the head and neck simultaneously. d starting position for sham tibial NDT: lower leg at hip 15°−20° flexion, 20°abduction and knee extended. e sham distal slider technique: in the initial position, patients are asked to extend their head and neck while the therapist is moving the ankle into dorsiflexion, eversion combined with toes extension. f sham proximal slider technique: participants move their head and neck into flexion as the therapist guides their ankle into plantarflexion, inversion combined with toes flexion

Real tibial nerve’s proximal slider technique [30]. In the same starting position (Fig. 1a), the patient performed head and neck flexion while the therapist was simultaneously mobilizing the ankle into plantarflexion, inversion and toe flexion (Fig. 1c). This technique was also performed in five sets of 10 repetitions [12, 34].

Sham intervention

The cadaveric experiment conducted by Martin et al. showed that hip abduction decreases the nerve structure’s strain [35]. Accordingly, Gonzalez et al. introduced a believable sham NDT by implementing these techniques in hip abduction while reducing the amount of hip flexion [36]. To perform sham NDT in the side-lying position, the therapist placed the patient’s hip in 20° degrees of flexion and abduction while maintaining the knee in full extension (Fig. 1d) [36]. Participants in the sham intervention group received sham tibial nerve distal and proximal slider NDTs [36].

Sham tibial nerve’s distal slider technique [36]. In the starting position (Fig. 1d), the patient was asked to perform head and neck extension while the therapist performed NDT by moving the ankle into dorsiflexion, eversion, and toe extension (Fig. 1e) for 10 repetitions in five sets [12, 23, 30, 34].

Sham tibial nerve’s proximal slider technique [36]. In the same initial position (Fig. 1d), the therapist rhythmically mobilized the ankle into plantarflexion, inversion, and toe flexion while instructing the patient to flex their head and neck (Fig. 1f). This technique was executed for 10 repetitions, organized into five sets [30].

The first author (MA) is an expert physiotherapist and the therapist of the present study. The interventions were conducted over eight sessions, scheduled twice a week over a one-month period [12, 34]. The researchers did not alter participants’ usual care, including the continuation of prescribed medications. Participants were instructed to refrain from engaging in any other rehabilitative co-interventions throughout the duration of the trial.

Outcomes

The second author (SEH), an expert physiatrist, was the study’s assessor, who was not aware of the participants’ allocated intervention. Outcomes were assessed at the baseline and after the end of the interventions.

Primary outcome measurement

The primary outcome measure for this RCT was the Michigan Diabetic Neuropathy Score (MDNS) [27]. MDNS consists of 46 points, with a higher number indicating a higher severity level [27]. Feldman and colleagues discovered that integrating MDNS with nerve conduction studies offers a method to confirm the diagnosis of DPN [27]. The sensory assessment includes evaluating vibration perception threshold (VPT), light touch, and pinprick sensation using a 128-Hz tuning fork, a 10-gram monofilament, and a standard pin, respectively [27]. The strength component assesses the strength of ankle dorsiflexion, great toe extension, and finger abduction [27]. Finally, the DTR of the Achilles, quadriceps femoris, biceps brachii, and triceps brachii were measured using a standard hammer [27].

Secondary outcome measurements

Tibial nerve conduction parameters, QoL, SLR ROM, and mechanosensitivity formed the secondary outcomes of the study.

At present, the clinical electrophysiological examination is recognized as the most appropriate method for quantitatively assessing DPN [37]. The sensitivity and specificity of nerve conduction studies were reported at 0.80 and 0.82, respectively [38]. It is shown that nerve conduction studies have excellent intra-rater reliability; however, a lack of reference values often leads to poor inter-observer agreement between expert assessors [39, 40]. Tibial nerve conduction parameters, including NCV (m/s), distal latency (mS), the amplitude of compound muscle action potential (CMAP, mV), and F-wave latency (mS), were measured by Neuropack® S3 EDX System (Nihon Kohden, Japan) [26]. An orthodromic supramaximal stimulation was applied once to the popliteal fossa for proximal stimulation, followed by application over the posterior aspect of the medial malleolus for distal stimulation [26]. The recording site was the abductor hallucis brevis [26]. An antidromic impulse is required [26] to elicit the F-wave. Therefore, the cathode was placed more proximal than the anode to avoid an anodal block [26].

The Persian version of the neuropathy-specific quality of life (NeuroQoL) questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87) was used to quantify the QoL [41]. This questionnaire comprises 27 questions designed to assess the frequency of various symptoms. Each question offers five response options, with a scale ranging from 1, indicating “never,” to 5, indicating “all the time” [41]. It consists of six distinct subscales, which encompass: (1) pain and paresthesia; (2) inability to perceive temperature or objects through the feet; (3) imbalance while standing and walking; (4) limitations in daily activities; (5) interpersonal barriers; and (6) emotional distress (reliability of subscales = 0.86–0.95) [41].

SLR ROM was measured using a manual goniometer [42]. Test-retest reliability of the manual goniometry for hip flexion ROM is appropriate, with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.91 [43]. The participant was asked to raise their leg with an extended knee, once with ankle plantarflexion (PF) and then with ankle dorsiflexion (DF), until the onset of symptoms (P1) and maximally tolerated symptoms (P2) [42]. A decrease in SLR ROM upon ankle dorsiflexion addition serves as a recognized indicator of peripheral nerve mechanosensitivity [42]. Boyd and colleagues reported that the ICC values between trials were as follows: 0.87 (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.69, 0.95) for PFSLRP1, 0.96 (95% CI: 0.91, 0.99) for PFSLRP2, 0.78 (95% CI: 0.50, 0.91) for DFSLRP1, and 0.88 (95% CI: 0.73, 0.95) for DFSLRP2 [44].

Sample size

A priori sample size calculation was performed using G Power 3.1 software based on the primary outcome (MDNS). Informed by a primary pilot study with 10 participants measured MDNS, the required sample size was determined to be 40 participants (20 in each group), assuming a significance level α of 0.05, power of 80%, effect size F = 0.53, and a potential 30% drop-out rate. This RCT employed a fixed sample size design with no interim analyses.

Randomization and blinding

DPN patients (n = 40) were randomly allocated to the experimental and sham intervention groups with a 1:1 allocation ratio. A block-balanced randomization method was used with four-character blocks, including A and B. A computer random number generator was used to select the blocks. The clinic secretary, an independent entity from the research team, generated the random allocation sequence before the start of the procedure. Then, allocation instructions were placed in numbered, opaque, and sealed envelopes to prevent the predictability of the subsequent instructions. The participants and outcome assessor remained blinded regarding the treatment allocation throughout the trial.

Statistical methods

STATA software version 14.2 (StataCorp LLC) and SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corp) were utilized for statistical analysis. Normality of continuous variables distribution was checked by analyzing P-P plot, histogram, mean and median similarity, Shapiro-Wilk statistical test, skewness and kurtosis analysis. The distribution of all data was normal. The mean differences (MD) of baseline demographic quantitative variables were compared with 0.2 SD of all participants. In addition to comparing the absolute difference in proportions, the chi-square statistical test was used to compare the gender between groups. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) test with an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach would be used if Levene’s test validated the data. Participants were analyzed in the groups in which they were randomized, regardless of treatment changes. We used multiple imputations to perform ITT and manage the missing data [45]. The baseline data values before the intervention were included as a covariate. The estimated effects were presented as MD with a [95% Confidence Interval (CI)], and as Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) with a [95% CI], calculated using Cohen’s d. The effect size Cohen’s d was interpreted as follows: < 0.2 = no/trivial effect; 0.2–0.5 = small effect; 0.5–0.8 = medium effect; 0.8–1.2 = large effect; and 1.2–2.2 = very large effect [46]. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was calculated using (0.5 × pooled standard deviation (SD) [47]), and MD was compared with MCID to show the clinical importance of the intervention. This distribution-based MCID was used because no anchor-based MCID was available for the outcomes of this study. The number needed to treat (NNT) was also calculated by an online calculator for all outcome measurements to represent the number of patients that needed to be treated with tibial nerve NDTs over four weeks to achieve a positive outcome compared to sham NDTs [48]. Alpha ≤ 0.05 was considered a statistically significant level.

Results

Participants

From January 2023 to March 2024, 103 patients with DPN symptoms were evaluated to check the predetermined eligibility criteria, of whom 63 did not meet the eligibility criteria. Therefore, 40 patients (n = 20 per group) met the eligibility criteria and consented to participate in the study. Two participants from the experimental group dropped out, and three from the sham intervention group were lost to follow-up (Fig. 2).

Primary outcome

The mean MDNS (MD: −4.60 [−7.78, −1.42], SMD: −0.93 [−1.58, −0.27], MCID: 2.48) was statistically and clinically significant between groups (P-value: 0.001; Fig. 3). The mean of sensory (MD: −1.85 [−3.53, −0.17], SMD: −0.71 [−1.35, −0.07], MCID: 1.30, P-value: 0.006) and motor subscales (MD: −2.45 [−3.90, −1.00], SMD: −1.08 [−1.74, −0.42], MCID: 1.13, P-value: 0.001) were statistically and clinically significant. Unlike the other two subscales, there was no statistically or clinically significant difference for the mean DTR subscale (MD = −0.20 [−1.99, 1.59], SMD: −0.07 [−0.69,0.55], MCID: 1.39, P-value: 0.84).

Secondary outcomes

Nerve conduction parameters

We observed that the mean of the right tibial NCV (MD: −0.46 [−3.23, 2.31], SMD: −0.11 [−0.73, 0.51], MCID: 2.15, P-value: 0.06) was not statistically and clinically significant. Likely, the mean difference of the left tibial NCV (MD: 0.62 [−1.76, 3.02], SMD: 0.17 [−0.45, 0.79], MCID: 1.86, P-value: 0.17) was not statistically and clinically significant. The MD in the right tibial CMAP was 0.41 [−0.52, 1.34], which did not reach statistical significance (P-value: 0.24). The SMD was 0.28 [−0.34, 0.90], indicating a small effect. Considering the MCID of 0.72, there was no significant clinical difference. Similarly, for the left tibial CMAP, the MD was − 0.07 [−0.95, 0.81], also not statistically significant (P-value: 0.34). The SMD of −0.05 [−0.67, 0.57] fell within the trivial zone. With an MCID of 0.68, there were no meaningful clinical differences for this outcome. No statistical or clinical significance was found in the MD of the right tibial nerve’s distal latency (−0.39 [−1.23, 0.47]; MCID: 0.66; P-value: 0.33). The SMD was − 0.29 [−0.91, 0.33], indicating a small effect. The MD for the left tibial distal latency was − 0.08 [−0.81, 0.65], which did not demonstrate statistical significance (P-value: 0.66), and had a trivial SMD (−0.07 [−0.69, 0.55]). We found no statistically significant differences in the right F-wave latency (MD: −4.84 [−16.20, 6.52], P-value: 0.77). The SMD was maintained in the small effect zone (−0.28, [−0.90, 0.34]). According to the reported MCID of 8.61, there were no clinically significant differences. The mean of left F-wave latency (MD: 2.48 [−6.52, 11.46], SMD: 0.18 [−0.44, 0.80], MCID: 6.82) was not statistically and clinically significant between groups (P-value: 0.83).

QoL

A statistically significant between-group difference was observed in NeuroQoL (MD: −13.25 [−24.89, −1.61], P-value: 0.006). The SMD indicated a medium effect (−0.73 [−1.37, −0.09]). Importantly, when comparing the MD with the reported MCID of 9.00, it became evident that the observed difference holds clinical significance (Fig. 3).

Discussion

NDTs were statistically and clinically effective with respect to the overall MDNS, as well as its sensory and motor sub-scores. According to the results of the reported SMD, NDT decreased MDNS with a large SMD (NNT ≈ 2). Therefore, one patient with DPN benefits from NDT if it is applied to two patients. However, the between-group difference in the DTR sub-score of MDNS was not statistically or clinically significant. The sensory sub-score consisted of vibration perception threshold, light touch, and pain sensation. Singh and colleagues found that neural mobilization techniques significantly decreased vibration perception threshold in DPN patients [49]. Ali and colleagues, in a case series study, showed that the value of the DN4 pain questionnaire decreased significantly owing to radial, median, and ulnar NDTs [18]. The mean values of painful cold, burning, hypoesthesia to touch, itching, tingling, hypoesthesia to pinprick, and numbness improved significantly after the intervention [18]. Our study’s results are consistent with those of these studies in the sensory sub-score of MDNS. Contrary to the results of the present study, which showed improvement in the motor sub-scale of MDNS, NDTs did not enhance grip strength in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome [50]. This controversy among our findings with studies in carpal tunnel syndrome may be due to the inclusion of different populations. The motor sub-score of MDNS consists of great toe extension, ankle dorsiflexion, and toe abduction strength assessment. Among these three groups of muscles, the intrinsic foot muscles, responsible for toe abduction, are innervated by the medial and lateral plantar nerves, which are the final branches of the tibial nerve. However, the deep peroneal nerve innervates ankle dorsiflexors and great toe extensors. While we primarily targeted the tibial nerve, it is essential to note that repetitive mobilization of the ankle into plantarflexion and inversion, as well as dorsiflexion and eversion during the tibial slider NDTs, may also inadvertently exert strain on the deep peroneal nerve [30]. This represents a non-specific effect that should be taken into consideration. DTR sub-scale of MDNS also includes the DTRs of the upper limbs. Since our intervention was applied only to the lower limb and specifically to the tibial nerve, this statistically and clinically non-significant difference can be justified.

NDTs improved patients’ overall QoL with a medium SMD (NNT ≈ 3). This indicates that if these techniques are applied to at least three patients, one can benefit from NDTs regarding QoL. The observed improvement in the MD of QoL surpassed the reported distribution-based MCID of 9.00, indicating clinical significance. However, it is important to interpret this clinically relevant difference cautiously, considering the inherent limitations of distribution-based MCIDs as outlined in the limitations section below. Manu et al. showed that tibial NDTs could improve the QoL in a patient with DPN [17]. Our findings are consistent with this case report. NeuroQoL evaluates DPN-related physical and emotional problems with six sub-scales, which quantify pain and paresthesia, the ability to detect temperature and objects through the feet, standing and walking balance, restrictions in daily activities, emotional distress, and interpersonal problems [41]. It is also shown that functional activity measured by Katz index of independence and pain improved significantly after NDTs in patients with DPN [19]. In this study, we only focused on reporting the overall QoL and did not present the individual subscales separately. Therefore, our discussion is just confined to the overall QoL. It is demonstrated that NDTs modulate pain and hyperalgesia by altering the expression of the TACAN gene [51]. This gene acts as a receptor in the dorsal root ganglion, responsible for hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia [51]. Although it is not investigated directly throughout this study, it can be hypothesized that NDTs can reduce neuropathy severity through the mechanisms described below, leading to a better QoL.

It is shown that impaired nerve conduction parameters are strongly associated with microvascular complications in type 1 diabetes mellitus [52]. Alshimy and colleagues showed that median sensory and tibial motor NCV improved following 12 sessions of NDTs [19]. It was also found that tibial sensory NCV improved significantly following 15 sessions of NDTs [16]. However, the findings of our study are contrary to the results of these two studies. We found that NDTs did not improve the tibial NCV of both legs (right NNT ≈ 16, NNT ≈ 11), clinically and statistically. This lack of consistency may be due to more treatment sessions in the other studies. As the optimal dosage of NDTs remains unknown, additional treatment sessions may improve NCV. It was revealed that motor latency reduced significantly following median NDTs in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome [50]. Our findings regarding motor distal latency are inconsistent with those of Zaheer and colleagues [50]. Our findings indicated no clinical or statistical improvement in the distal latency of the tibial nerve (right NNT ≈ 6, left NNT ≈ 25). This may result from applying tensioner techniques used by Alshimi et al. and Zaheer et al., which can lead to neurophysiological effects [19, 50]. The amplitude of CMAP indicates the number of muscle fibers undergoing depolarization and are usually impaired during axonal neuropathies [26]. The findings of the current study indicate that neither a significant clinical difference nor a statistical difference was observed regarding CMAP amplitude, with NNT values of approximately 6 for the right tibial nerve and approximately 35 for the left tibial nerve. F-wave provides an indirect measure of motor conduction time, reflecting the time required for the action potential to travel along the entire length of the motor nerve axon to and from the spinal cord [53, 54]. Therefore, it can assess the most proximal segments of the nerve [53, 54]. We did not find any significant statistical and clinical (NNT ≈ 6 for the right tibial nerve and NNT ≈ 10 for the left tibial nerve) difference for F-wave latency.

The lack of significant results in the nerve conduction parameters might be attributed to the absence of the tibial tensioner NDTs in this study, which could lead to neurophysiological impacts [55]. Although we found clinically and statistically significant differences in MDNS and QoL, we did not find any significant differences in the electrophysiological outcomes. It is indicated that improvements in nerve conduction parameters occur gradually over time [56, 57]. In cases of compression neuropathies, such as carpal tunnel syndrome, most of the improvement in nerve conduction parameters is typically observed within the first three months following release surgery [56, 57]. However, it is important to note that the electrophysiological outcomes may not completely return to baseline, even years after the resolution of clinical symptoms [56, 57]. Therefore, long-term follow-up studies are crucial for assessing the efficacy of treatments on nerve conduction outcomes. The findings of these studies indicate that enhancements in electrophysiological outcomes are not essential for alleviating clinical symptoms [56, 57]. Consequently, this issue can explain the inconsistency between improvements in electrophysiological outcomes and the enhancement of MDNS and QoL. Furthermore, it is essential to consider that demyelination pathologies typically exhibit a better prognosis than those characterized by axonal loss and degeneration. The current study did not perform a subgroup analysis based on the underlying pathology, which may explain the observed non-significant differences in nerve conduction parameters.

NDTs can improve homeostasis and blood flow in and around the nerve by mobilizing the peripheral nervous system and diminishing scar tissue in relation to itself and the surrounding mechanical interface [58]. NDTs can improve neuropathy symptoms owing to mechanical and neurophysiological effects. These techniques can also contribute to the diminution of intraneural edema, intraneural fluid dispersion, and reduction of intraneural pressure or swelling, which will ease hypoxia by mobilizing the peripheral nervous system and improving the function of the nerve [59–61]. These techniques can lead to the regeneration of peripheral nerves by stimulating the activity of Schwann cells through the stimulation of movement-dependent receptors on cell membranes [16]. NDT enhances nerve regeneration, accelerates sensorimotor function recovery, and regulates mechanical pain [51]. NDTs can modulate endogenous opioid expression, including the delta-opioid receptor and the Kappa-opioid receptor in the periaqueductal gray, which are related to pain relief [62]. Neural mobilization techniques can also reduce hyperalgesia by mitigating the degeneration of peripheral nerves [13]. It is also demonstrated that by lowering proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β concentration in the nerves with DPN, neural mobilization techniques improved mechanical allodynia [11]. In addition to their neurophysiological effects, NDTs can also exert various mechanical effects. It is indicated that slider NDTs promote significant excursion of the peripheral nerve within its mechanical interface [58]. This movement effectively reduces mechanical tensions on the nerve, potentially alleviating associated symptoms [58].

Boyd and colleagues showed that neurodynamic exercises immediately improve PF and DF SLR ROM [15]. The results of our study are consistent with those of Boyd et al. in PFSLR ROM. Right PFSLRP1 (NNT ≈ 5) and PFSLRP2 (NNT ≈ 8) were improved with a small SMD. This small effect was not clinically and statistically significant. However, left PFSLRP1 and PFSLRP2 were improved clinically with a medium effect (NNT ≈ 3). Non-neural structures, such as the hamstring muscles’ accommodation to elongation following NDTs, are a reason for PFSLR ROM improvement [15, 63]. Thus, the effects of NDTs extend beyond neural tissues. Along with their neural benefits, these techniques also enhance the flexibility of the hamstring muscles. However, our findings do not align with those reported by Boyd and colleagues in the context of DFSLR ROM, and NDTs could not improve DFSLR ROM statistically and clinically (right DFSLRP1 (NNT ≈ 10), left DFSLRP1 (NNT ≈ 6), right DFSLRP2 (NNT ≈ 59), and left DFSLRP2 (NNT ≈ 7)).

As dorsiflexion can increase strain in lower extremity posterior neural structures [64], it is hypothesized that DFSLR ROM likely indicates the peripheral nervous system’s role in ROM [42]. Therefore, DFSLR ROM may serve as a method to monitor ROM alterations possibly due to the nervous system’s mechanosensitivity [15]. Given that the amount of SLR ROM reduction in response to ankle dorsiflexion addition is considered a measure of peripheral nerves’ mechanosensitivity [42], our study suggests a statistically and clinically relevant effect regarding the left SLRP1 (NNT ≈ 3) with a moderate effect. However, right SLRP1, right SLRP2, and left SLRP2 improvements were not clinically and statistically important (NNT ≈ 4). Boyd and colleagues demonstrated that the minimal detectable change was 0.4° [44]. Among the various outcomes of the SLR ROM and mechanosensitivity assessments, the MD observed for the right DFSLRP2 was lower than the established minimal detectable change. This observation suggests that the change falls within the limits of measurement error for the right DFSLRP1. Considering the small and medium SMDs observed, the study may have been underpowered to detect statistically significant changes in mechanosensitivity, suggesting a need for larger confirmatory studies. In the healthy controls, the ratio of mechano-responsive to mechano-insensitive C-fiber nociceptors was 2:1 [65]. This ratio flipped to 1:2 in those with DM and early DPN [65]. Interestingly, this altered ratio coincided with a general decrease in the overall number of these C-fibers [65]. While the present study did not assess the aforementioned ratio of mechano-responsive to mechano-insensitive nociceptors, it is plausible that NDTs may enhance nerve mechanosensitivity by potentially restoring a more balanced ratio within patients with DPN.

Since sham NDTs are a type of manual therapy technique, these techniques should not be regarded as an inactive or inert treatment [66]. While it is demonstrated that applying NDTs in an abducted and less flexed hip position reduces nerve excursion [35], these manual techniques may still produce other non-specific effects than neurodynamic-specific effects. In addition to the physiological effects of manual touch during these sham manual therapy techniques [67, 68], repetitive passive movements of the ankle, applied through sham NDTs, can assist in preserving the mobility of joints and soft tissues, reduce the risk of restricted joint mobility, and improve blood circulation [69]. Consequently, the sham NDT in this study is an active treatment and its potential effects in the sham intervention group are considered as non-specific effects. This issue can affect the comparison in this study, suggesting that a more conservative interpretation is advisable [66].

The current study is not without limitations. DPN is a kind of symmetric polyneuropathy which affects all peripheral nerves. While we applied NDTs only on the tibial nerve, conducting other trials that will apply NDTs for all lower extremity peripheral nerves, including tibial, sural, and peroneal nerves is suggested. Due to insufficient safety and feasibility studies, we did not apply tensioner NDTs, as mechanosensitivity is decreased in DPN patients [42]. However, it has been shown that tensioner NDTs can cause neurophysiological effects and are essential for nerves to adapt during mechanical loads [55]. So, further studies are required to investigate the safety and effectiveness of tensioner NDTs in the intended population. Furthermore, although using the distribution-based MCIDs is acceptable, especially when anchor-based MCIDs are unavailable, using half an SD distribution-based MCID is not without limitations. The calculated MCID in this study is just a statistically based threshold and does not include patients’ or clinicians’ attitudes. As a result, this method cannot capture what is genuinely meaningful to patients and clinicians. This method also fails to account for the differences in baseline disease severity. Consequently, the current study’s findings on the clinical aspects should be interpreted with caution until the anchor-based MCIDs are available. Moreover, the best dosage for NDTs is not known yet. Therefore, it is recommended to conduct future RCTs with repeated measures designed to determine the best dosage for NDTs in patients with DPN. Furthermore, regarding pathology, DPN covers a spectrum from demyelination to axonal degeneration [26]. This study did not perform subgroup analysis according to the diagnosed pathology. Considering different prognoses of NDTs on axonal or demyelinating neuropathies, subgroup analysis according to the pattern of nerve involvement is suggested in future studies. Underpowered studies are vulnerable to type II errors. Due to insufficient power in some outcomes, especially in nerve conduction parameters, further studies with larger sample sizes should be designed to draw a more definitive conclusion. Ultimately, while several potential mechanisms are mentioned in the discussion, we did not measure those mechanisms directly. So, future studies are recommended to direct these mechanisms explicitly.

Conclusion

To conclude, this RCT indicates that NDTs enhance neuropathy severity and improve the quality of life in patients with DPN. Therefore, as a conservative management strategy, NDT mitigates neuropathy severity and enhances the overall quality of life. To prevent any potential misinterpretation of the findings from the current study, it is important to convey that the clinically significant differences observed do not imply that NDTs can treat DPN. Moreover, this study indicates that NDTs have a small to medium effects on increasing PFSLR ROM. This improvement observed in the left limb is clinically significant; however, the differences noted in the right limb do not reach the threshold for clinical significance. Given the small to moderate SMD observed for mechanosensitivity, along with the absence of statistically significant differences, it is evident that this study is underpowered. To draw conclusive results regarding mechanosensitivity, it is essential to conduct larger studies. It is important to note that NDTs do not significantly influence nerve conduction parameters and DFSLR ROM. Further investigations with increased statistical power are necessary to establish conclusive evidence regarding these outcomes.
