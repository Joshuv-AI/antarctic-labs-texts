The contribution of trabeculae and the failure initiation in the proximal femur: a finite element analysis
Authors: Peng Jia, Yi Yang, Zheng Fang, Xin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study employed finite element analysis to simulate the process of hip fractures, aiming to: (1) quantify the contribution of trabeculae to proximal femoral strength, (2) compare fracture patterns before and after the removal of trabecular bone, and (3) identify the fracture initiation site.MethodsBilateral hip CT scans from 10 healthy volunteers were included in this study. Two types of geometric models of the ipsilateral proximal femur were constructed using Mimics: an intact model (cortical-cancellous composite model) and a cancellous-excised model. In total, 20 geometric models were generated (two per volunteer). Material properties were assigned based on Hounsfield Unit (HU) values. A lateral fall condition was simulated, and the element deletion technique in Abaqus was employed to simulate crack initiation and propagation, with the complete formation of a fracture line defined as the endpoint of the finite element simulation.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 10 volunteers (6 males and 4 females), with a mean age of 71.7 years. Among the intact models, 6 exhibited femoral neck fractures, 3 showed intertrochanteric fractures, and 1 presented with a femoral head fracture. The mean failure load was approximately 4255 N for the intact models and 1387 N for the cancellous-excised models, representing an average reduction in bone strength of 67.2%. Regions of relatively high von Mises stress in the intact models were predominantly localized to the medial and lateral cortex of the femoral neck, regardless of fracture types. Tissue failure consistently initiated at the endosteal-trabecular junction of the proximal femur and progressively propagated until complete structural fracture occurred. Although three different fracture types were observed in the intact models, all cancellous-excised models resulted in femoral neck fractures.ConclusionsTrabecular bone in the proximal femur plays a critical role in load transfer and contributes significantly to its mechanical strength. Hip fractures initiate at the junction between the endosteum and trabecular bone. Effective prevention of trabecular bone loss or targeted reinforcement of the bone tissue at the interface between trabecular and cortical bone may potentially reduce the incidence of hip fractures in the elderly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06447-y.Keywords: Proximal femoral fractures, Trabecular bone, Biomechanics, Finite element analysis

Introduction

Hip fractures represent a significant public health challenge [1–6]. Recent surveys estimate that the total number of hip fractures among people aged over 65 years will reach 1.3 million by 2050 [7]. The 1-year mortality rate for patients with a hip fracture ranges from 8 to 36%, with males exhibiting higher rates than females. Approximately 20% of patients require long-term care, and only 40% are able to recover their pre-injury functional levels [8–10]. Bone mineral density (BMD) remains the most widely used quantitative marker for fracture risk assessment. However, a substantial proportion of fragility fractures occur in individuals whose BMD falls within the osteopenic or even normal range [11]. This limitation arises because BMD does not capture critical factors closely linked to the mechanical strength of the proximal femur, such as bone geometry and the spatial distribution of bone mass. In contrast, finite element analysis (FEA) offers a more comprehensive biomechanical assessment. Ridzwan et al. demonstrated that FEA explain 57% of the variance in proximal femur failure load, compared to only 39% explained by BMD measured via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [12].

The contribution of trabecular bone to proximal femoral strength has long been a topic among researchers. Martens et al. [13] reported that scraping away the trabecular bone in the proximal femur reduced its overall strength by approximately 50%. Similarly, Jang et al. [14] highlighted that trabecular bone bears 54% of the total load in the proximal femur, with the remaining 46% carried by cortical bone. In contrast, Koivumaki et al. [15] argued that the contribution of trabecular bone to the mechanical strength of the proximal femur is negligible. Furthermore, the initiation and propagation of hip fractures have not been fully elucidated. Some scholars argued that hip fractures initiate in the cortical bone, specifically in the relatively thin superolateral cortex of the femoral neck [16, 17], while others contended that tissue damage begins in the trabecular bone [18]. Resolving these controversies are essential for a complete understanding of proximal femoral biomechanics and fracture pathogenesis.

Falls are a leading cause of hip fractures in the elderly, with over 90% of fragility fractures attributed to falls [19]. The element deletion method is a finite element analysis technique used to simulate crack initiation and propagation. In the past few years, numerous studies have successfully applied this technique to simulate crack initiation and propagation in human proximal femur [20, 21]. Specifically, when the damage variable within an element reaches its critical value, the element is deleted. This deletion causes stress redistribution around the damaged area (where the crack initiates) and simulates the direction of crack propagation by continuously deleting fully damaged elements in the mesh. In the present study, we employed Hounsfield Units (HU) derived from CT scans to assign heterogeneous, patient-specific material properties to bone tissue [22–24], thereby creating high-fidelity finite element models of the proximal femur. The element deletion method was then used to simulate the crack initiation and propagation under a sideway fall configuration.

To our knowledge, no study has employed this advanced finite element framework to systematically examine the influence of complete cancellous bone removal on the mechanical strength and fracture patterns of the proximal femur. Therefore, the present study has three objectives: (1) to quantify the contribution of trabecular bone to the biomechanical strength of the proximal femur; (2) to compare fracture patterns before and after the removal of trabecular bone; and (3) to identify the initiation site of hip fractures.

Materials and methods

Subject recruitment and CT protocols

Given the complex morphology and inter-individual variability of the femur [25, 26], ten healthy elderly volunteers (aged over 60 years) were recruited from the Radiology Department of our institution. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our Hospital and informed consent was obtained from each participant. The subjects were scanned by using a 64-row CT scanner (Drive, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a standard protocol from the iliac crest to 3–5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter. The scanning parameters were as follows: 120 kVp, 280 mA, 1 mm slice thickness, a 512 × 512 matrix, and a 380 mm field of view in spiral reconstruction mode.

Establishment of geometric models

Bilateral hip CT images were processed using Mimics 21.0 (Materialise Inc., Leuven, Belgium). Priority was given to the right proximal femur for modeling; the left side was used unless image quality was inadequate. Denoising preprocessing was first performed to enhance image quality, followed by three-dimensional extraction of the bone structures. Cortical and cancellous bones were accurately distinguished based on grayscale value differences. For each selected proximal femur, two types of geometric models were constructed: an intact model (cortical-cancellous composite model) and a cancellous-excised model. A total of 20 geometric models were generated from the 10 volunteers. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) were assembled on the surfaces of the greater trochanter and femoral head using 3-matic 13.0 (Materialise Inc., Leuven, Belgium) to prevent local stress concentration and simulate the protective effect of soft tissues [12, 27, 28]. After assembly, meshing was performed using tetrahedral four-node elements with a characteristic element length of 1.5 mm. The cancellous-excised models contained an average of 654,368 elements, while the intact models contained an average of 1,014,630 elements. Bone tissue exhibits anisotropy and inhomogeneity due to its multiscale hierarchical structure. However, previous studies have demonstrated that the results of finite element simulations using isotropic or anisotropic material property assignments are comparable [29, 30]. Accordingly, the present study adopted an isotropic material property definition for both cortical and cancellous bone. To represent a sideways fall configuration, each femoral shaft was positioned 10° from horizontal and the femoral neck axis internally rotated by 15˚ relative to a vertical axis. During the loading process, the bottom of the PMMA attached to the greater trochanter was fixed in the X-axis direction [12]. The top of the PMMA connected to the femoral head served as the loading surface, where a uniformly distributed pressure was applied with a maximum value of 8 MPa. Based on the initial area of the loading surface, the corresponding maximum force was calculated to be 8 kN. To prevent unintended fractures from torsional moments, the distal end of the femur was allowed to rotate freely but was constrained in displacement along the X-axis (Fig. 2) [12, 27, 28].

Regarding the damage parameters of bone, values were set based on previous literature as follows: the yield strain was 0.33% in tension and 0.81% in compression [35]; the critical strain was 1.57% in tension and 2.5% in compression [36]; and the critical damage values corresponding to tensile and compressive states were 0.5 and 0.95, respectively [37].

In accordance with the damage definition in Abaqus software, this study considers damage initiation in bone to occur when the equivalent strain surpasses the yield strain [33]. The value of the damage variable (D) was calculated using Eq. 1.4, corresponding to damage initiation in Abaqus. As deformation progresses, a further increase in equivalent strain leads to a continuous rise in the damage variable, representing the damage propagation as defined by Abaqus and characterized by a reduction in the stiffness of the affected element. When the equivalent strain at a material point reaches the critical value, the element deletion criterion in Abaqus is activated. If all integration points within an element meet the deletion criterion, the element is removed and serves as a crack source. Upon subsequent loading, the crack propagates progressively and eventually forms a continuous fracture path. At this stage, the reaction force of the bone model changes abruptly, indicating the occurrence of complete structural fracture [38].

Finite element analysis was performed on all 20 models. During the computation, the stress and strain states at each integration point were updated at the conclusion of every increment. The equivalent strain and equivalent principal strain were simultaneously calculated and stored in the STATEV() variable, serving as the basis for determining whether the damage variable (D) required updating and for identifying whether the integration point was under tension or compression. If an update of the damage variable was necessary, the revised value was applied in subsequent increments, and the elasticity matrix was modified according to Eq. 1.3. All finite element computations were conducted on a 64-core CPU, with an average computational time of 10.5 h per model.

Mesh convergence analysis

A mesh convergence analysis was conducted on Sample “E” to validate the mesh size used in this study. For the intact model of Sample “E”, the characteristic element length was uniformly reduced to 1 mm (without changing the element type), increasing the total number of elements to 2,600,309. The finite element analysis of this refined model showed a reaction force of approximately 3831 N at the point of complete fracture path formation. Compared to the model with a 1.5 mm element size, the difference in reaction force was only 4.63%, and the fracture path characteristics-including location, morphology, and propagation direction-were consistent between the two models. Given the minor deviation in force and the consistency in fracture behavior, it is concluded that a characteristic element length of 1.5 mm provides sufficient computational accuracy for this study.

Statistical analysis

Femoral strength data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m. (n = 10 paired models). A paired, two-tailed Student’s t-test (GraphPad Prism) was used to compare the fracture strength between the intact and cancellous-excised models. Exact p values are provided in the Results, with significance levels defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

These values are comparable to the bone strength parameters reported in the cadaveric biomechanical study by de Bakker et al. (Mean: 4032 N, ranged from 2162 to 7036 N) [16]. In the ten cancellous-excised models, the mean failure load was approximately 1387 N, with minimum and maximum values of 686 N and 2831 N, respectively. Discussion

Hip fractures are common among the elderly [39, 40]. Hip fractures result from the combined influence of multiple factors, among which reduced bone strength plays a major role. Although BMD exhibits a well-established correlation with fracture risk, it accounts for no more than 70% of bone strength. Finite element analysis integrates computational biomechanics principles with individualized clinical CT data to simulate the bone failure process in a mechanism-driven manner. Its accuracy has been validated in numerous cadaveric biomechanical studies [27, 41, 42]. The trabecular architecture in the proximal femur represents a “self-optimized” configuration that achieves maximal mechanical load-bearing capacity with minimal bone mass [43, 44]. However, its biomechanical contribution to proximal femoral strength has long been a focus of debate among scholars. This study found that after the removal of trabecular bone, the mechanical strength of the proximal femur decreased by an average of approximately 67.2%. Under lateral fall boundary conditions, elevated von Mises stress values were predominantly observed in the medial and lateral cortices of the femoral neck. When the external force exceeded the tissue’s load-bearing capacity, failure initiated at the endosteal-trabecular bone junction and propagated progressively, ultimately resulting in complete structural fracture. Additionally, following the removal of trabecular bone, all models consistently exhibited femoral neck fractures. This indicates that the unique trabecular architecture of the proximal femur plays a crucial role in stress transmission.

Although cortical bone has traditionally been emphasized as the primary load-bearing component, growing evidence highlights the significant contribution of trabecular bone to the strength of the proximal femur. This study showed that the removal of trabecular bone leads to an approximate 67.2% decrease in proximal femur strength, underscoring its crucial role in maintaining bone integrity. The proximal femur consists of a dense outer cortical shell surrounding a porous inner trabecular network. Cortical bone is the primary contributor to rigid structural support, whereas trabecular bone is responsible for load transfer and energy absorption [45]. Previous studies have indicated that trabecular bone decreases earlier and more significantly than cortical bone with advancing age [46]. Brance [47] et al. assessed volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD) at the proximal femur in 1354 healthy individuals (aged 20–90) utilizing three-dimensional DXA. They reported a significant decline in vBMD, with reductions of 16.6% in men and 23.1% in women, primarily driven by trabecular bone loss. Similarly, Nicks et al. [48] found significant age-related declines in both cortical and trabecular vBMD. Although the absolute decreases were comparable between compartments, the proportional reduction in trabecular vBMD was 2 to 5 times greater than that in cortical bone. Wolff’s law posits that mechanical loads are transmitted along the trajectories of the trabeculae. Consequently, a significant reduction or absence of trabecular bone compromises these internal load transfer pathways. With the degradation of this intrinsic toughening system, the elderly lose the ability to absorb and dissipate energy as effectively as the young, thereby predisposing them to fractures even from low-energy events such as falls.

Zhang et al. [49] identified two distinct trabecular systems in the proximal femur: one located between the femoral head and neck, which facilitate stress transmission between the femoral head and neck; the other situated between the femoral neck and shaft, responsible for transmitting stress between the femoral neck and diaphysis. In this study, while the intact models exhibited three fracture types, the complete removal of trabecular bone consistently converted all observed fractures (one femoral head and three intertrochanteric) into femoral neck fractures. As shown in Fig. 3, the regions of highest von Mises stress were consistently localized to the medial and lateral cortices of the femoral neck, irrespective of the fracture type. These findings not only underscore the susceptibility of the femoral neck to fracture but also support the concept that the trabecular network acts as a “stress redistributor”, thereby complementing its mechanical roles in cushioning and energy absorption. Consequently, its removal disrupts the natural transfer of stress through the trabecular system. This leads to a concentration of stress within the already highly-loaded femoral neck region, ultimately producing a consistent fracture pattern.

Previous studies predominantly utilized cadaveric specimens to investigate vulnerable regions and fracture initiation sites in the proximal femur [16, 17]. However, this methodology is inherently limited in its ability to capture subtle internal changes within the trabecular bone. This study utilized, finite element analysis to simulate the complete structural failure of the proximal femur under a lateral impact. Results demonstrated that while stress concentrations were highest in the medial and lateral femoral neck cortices, fracture consistently initiated at the endosteal-trabecular junction. Propagation of this initial failure culminated in complete structural collapse. This leads us to hypothesize that this failure pathway is a specific response to loading beyond physiological limits. The requirement of compatible deformation dictates that a body must maintain continuity in its displacement field (without cracks or overlaps) after loading, with the displacement of all points satisfying the Saint-Venant compatibility equations—a fundamental constraint in continuum mechanics. The cortical and trabecular compartments exhibit a substantial mismatch in elastic modulus [50]. This abrupt transition in material properties induces intense local strain to achieve globally compatible deformation. Consequently, the trabecular bone undergoes substantial strain at the interface to comply with the constrained deformation of the stiffer cortex, giving rise to a predisposition for crack initiation and propagation. The terms “bone bruise” and “bone contusion” describe trabecular microfractures accompanied by marrow haemorrhage and oedema. These lesions are best visualized on MRI as a hyperintense signal on T2-weighted sequences, as they are often undetectable by conventional radiography or CT. This clinical evidence corroborates the view that failure initiates not in the cortex, but within the trabecular bone. We therefore conclude that under lateral fall loading, initial damage occurs primarily within the trabecular compartment, with cortical fracture ensuing rapidly thereafter. As the crack gradually propagates medially, it ultimately leads to complete structural failure. This conclusion also provides a novel perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of hip fractures. Fung et al. [51] evaluated three cement configurations in the proximal femur for their ability to resist fracture. While hollow and solid configuration demonstrated comparable performance, reducing the cement volume to avoid cortical contact markedly diminished its reinforcing effect. In alignment with these findings, our results confirm the effective mechanical support at the endosteal-trabecular junction is critical for preventing proximal femoral fractures. When analyzing bone fracture, Dempster et al. [52] compared its structure to a large tree: a lumberjack need only cut a V-shaped notch on one or both sides of the trunk to easily fell it. Although the volume removed by the V-shaped notch is negligible compared to the entire tree, it significantly reduces the overall biomechanical strength, resulting in an “amplification effect”. Compared to cortical bone, cancellous bone has a relatively larger surface area and more remodeling sites, making it more sensitive during the bone remodeling process [53]. Therefore, the authors suggest that bone remodeling at the endosteal-trabecular interface may represent a potential critical point influencing the biomechanical strength of the proximal femur. Reducing bone loss at this junction or reinforcing the structural integration between the two compartments could be an effective strategy for reducing the incidence of hip fragility fractures.

Pulkkinen et al. [54] suggested that individuals with lower bone strength are more susceptible to femoral neck fractures, whereas those with higher bone strength tend to experience intertrochanteric fractures. Ridzwan et al. [12] observed a trend of relatively lower bone strength in individuals with intertrochanteric fractures, though no significant statistical difference was identified. In the present study, 6 cases resulted in femoral neck fractures and 3 in intertrochanteric fractures; however, owing to the small sample size, no significant trend between bone strength and fracture type was identified. Therefore, future large-scale finite element analysis simulations will be of great value in elucidating the relationship between bone strength and fracture type, thereby contributing substantially to epidemiological research on hip fractures.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small and only individuals aged over 60 years were included, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings related to the failure mode of the proximal femur. However, given hip fragility fractures are uncommon in younger adults, the simulations were conducted under relatively homogeneous conditions to preliminarily investigate the failure mechanisms underlying hip fractures. Future studies with larger sample sizes stratified by age and sex are necessary to validate these findings and will represent a direction for our subsequent research. Second, the material properties of cortical and trabecular bone were assumed to be homogeneous in this study. Nevertheless, material properties for both bone components were assigned based on HU values, which partially mitigated the limitations of this experimental method. Third, the models did not accurately construct the structural connectivity between the trabecular and cortical bone. Future studies could employ high-resolution CT data to reconstruct three-dimensional microarchitecture of trabecular bone and incorporate micro-finite element analysis to simulate the failure process of the proximal femur, thereby providing more robust evidence for understanding the mechanism of hip fractures. While this study has delineated the biomechanical effects of complete cancellous bone removal, the fundamental question of how the loss of specific trabecular structures influences fracture risk remains. Investigating the individual roles of the primary and secondary trabecular systems is, therefore, a necessary and logical next step.

Conclusion

This study further confirmed the significant contribution of trabecular bone to the mechanical strength of the proximal femur and its crucial role in resisting hip fractures. Trabecular bone in the proximal femur plays a critical role in load transfer, with hip fractures initiating at the junction between the endosteum and trabecular bone. Effective prevention of trabecular bone loss or targeted reinforcement of the bone tissue at the interface between trabecular and cortical bone may potentially reduce the incidence of hip fractures in the elderly.
