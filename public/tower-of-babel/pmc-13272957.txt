High-strength liquid metal composite–hydrogel interfaces enable robust stretchable electronics
Authors: Bingqian Jiao, Wei Wang, Yang Feng, Dingze Zhou, Xinghua Wang, Yanmin Zhou, Xia Wang, Yinghui Shang, Qigang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStretchable conductors are essential building blocks for next-generation wearable electronics and soft robotics. Among them, liquid metal-based conductors offer exceptional deformability but suffer from poor interfacial adhesion to substrates, often resulting in leakage under mechanical stress that compromises electromechanical stability and device durability. Here we report a universal interface-fusion printing strategy for fabricating metal-particle semi-embedded hydrogels, in which interconnected liquid metal and silver particles are firmly anchored at the hydrogel surface. The resulting liquid metal-based composite layer achieves a high interfacial adhesion strength of 234.4 kPa to the hydrogel substrate and a conductivity of 1.18 × 106 S m−1. This robust interface prevents liquid metal leakage and ensures stable electrical connection under extreme conditions, including prolonged ultrasonication, 300 MPa impacts, and thousands of stretching cycles. The strategy forms an interpenetrating cross‑linked polymer network through cross-interfacial assembly, fusing the circuit and substrate into an integrated structure. This simple and scalable method enables the fabrication of high‑resolution circuits for a wide range of electronic devices. We demonstrate its performance in applications including stretchable circuits, on-skin biosensors, and underwater soft robots.Subject terms: Gels and hydrogels, Electronic devices, Polymer synthesis

Introduction

Soft and stretchable electronics are poised to revolutionise wearable devices1–3, soft robotics4,5, medical devices6–8, and human–machine interfaces9,10. Realising this vision requires stretchable conductors that serve as deformable interconnects, accommodating strain in concert with compliant substrates11. Structural engineering strategies, such as serpentine patterns and wrinkled architectures, have been used to impart macroscale stretchability to otherwise rigid electronics12–14. By contrast, devices fabricated from intrinsically stretchable conductors exhibit enhanced mechanical stability, mitigating deformation-induced failure and enabling more robust soft electronics15–17.

Liquid metals (LMs), particularly gallium-based alloys, have received considerable attention as elastic conductors because of their metallic conductivity and extreme deformability18–20. Building on these advantages, various fabrication techniques for LM-based circuits21, including direct-write printing22, injection into microchannels23, stencil printing24, transfer printing25,26 and stamping27, are now well established. However, the poor compatibility and weak adhesion of LM-based circuits on flexible substrates pose a major obstacle to the formation of stable interfaces22,28. The lack of robust interfacial bonding renders LMs vulnerable to extrusion and leakage under mechanical stress, compromising the reliability, uniformity, and long-term stability of LM-based circuits in practical applications29,30.

To address this challenge, several strategies have been developed. Notable examples include the use of plasma or ozone treatment to modify the substrate and enhance the adhesion of LMs31. Additionally, methods involving the hydroxylation of the substrate32 and the introduction of Ag or Cu at the interface to promote alloying33 have also been explored. More recently, a mechanically interlocked interface was achieved by semi-embedding Cu–EGaIn into porous microfibre mats, yielding an interfacial adhesion strength of 22.4 kPa34. Despite these advances, robust conductor–substrate bonding remains difficult to sustain under complex mechanical deformation and harsh environmental exposure, which can trigger interfacial delamination and compromise electromechanical durability.

In this article, we report a universal interface-fusion printing strategy for fabricating a metal-particle semi-embedded hydrogel (MPEH), in which interconnected liquid metal and silver (LM/Ag) particles are firmly anchored at the hydrogel surface. This strategy employs polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a macromolecular bridge by incorporating it into both the hydrogel substrate and the printed LM/Ag/PVA composite ink (Fig. 1a). Cooperative dehydration between the printed ink and the hydrogel substrate simultaneously induces self-crosslinking of the PVA in the ink, which immobilizes the interconnected LM/Ag particles, and drives cross-interfacial cross-linking with the PVA in the hydrogel, thereby forming an interpenetrating crystalline network. This network secures the LM/Ag/PVA composite layer to the hydrogel, yielding a high interfacial adhesion strength of 234.4 kPa. The LM/Ag/PVA circuit is partially embedded at the hydrogel surface, providing good surface conductivity (1.18 × 106 S m−1) and enabling direct electrical interfacing with external components (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Fig. 1). This strategy can securely anchor LM/Ag/PVA circuits to various hydrogels, including PVA, polyacrylamide, poly(acrylic acid) and poly(N,N-dimethylacrylamide), underscoring its broad applicability. The robust interface prevents LM leakage and ensures stable electrical connection under extreme conditions, including prolonged ultrasonication, repeated 300 MPa impacts, and thousands of stretching cycles. Consequently, the MPEH exhibits high stretchability (>400%), strain-insensitive resistance (only a 0.15-fold increase at 400% strain), and exceptional underwater electromechanical stability (>7 days).

This simple and effective method seamlessly integrates soft, hydrated hydrogels with flexible circuits, uniting the distinct advantages of both materials. It enables the fabrication of high-resolution circuits (Fig. 1e) and supports stretchable electronic devices across a wide range of applications. As demonstrations, we fabricated a highly stretchable “GEL” light-emitting diode array that remains fully functional under 200% tensile strain (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Movie 1), along with various epidermal wearable sensors for real-time physiological monitoring (Fig. 1f). By combining the tissue-like properties of hydrogels with embedded circuitry, the MPEH enables the creation of a hydrogel jellyfish robot that delivers continuous visual feedback during underwater locomotion and demonstrates superior electromechanical durability, further advancing the integration of bioinspired systems with electronic technologies (Fig. 1g).

Results

Fabrication of MPEH via the interface-fusion printing strategy

The fabrication of MPEH involves stencil printing, dehydration, and rehydration, with the detailed process and mechanism illustrated in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2. The PVA hydrogel substrate was first prepared by a freeze–thaw method. The LM/Ag/PVA composite ink was then formulated by high-shear mixing of eutectic gallium–indium alloy (EGaIn; 75.5 wt% Ga, 24.5 wt% In), silver microparticles, and an aqueous PVA solution (Supplementary Fig. 3). The prepared composite ink was patterned onto the surface of the hydrogel substrate using a mask. The ink–hydrogel assembly was first dried and then rehydrated in deionised water to obtain the MPEH.

PVA is a key component of MPEH and was selected for three advantageous properties: (1) good biocompatibility35,36, which permits direct skin contact; (2) the ability to act as a dispersant and binder for nanoparticles37,38, allowing composite inks with tunable viscosity and high dispersion stability; and (3) dehydration-driven crystallization39, which generates high-density cross-linking that favours the formation of robust interfacial bonding. Silver microparticles were chosen for their high conductivity and compatibility with LM20. A range of rheological tests on the LM/Ag/PVA composite ink revealed clear shear-thinning behaviour. The ink exhibited suitable viscosity and flow characteristics, making it well suited for stencil printing (Supplementary Fig. 4). These properties result from the abundant hydroxyl groups along the PVA chains, which form extensive hydrogen bonds with the gallium oxide shell on the LM particles, preventing particle sedimentation and ensuring a uniform dispersion32,40. The interactions between LM particles and PVA were analyzed using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 5). Compared to pure PVA hydrogels, the characteristic peak at 3310 cm−1, attributed to the hydroxyl groups, shifted to 3291 cm−1 in the LM/Ag/PVA composite, confirming the hydrogen bonding interaction between the LM particles and PVA. The composite ink was prepared by adding EGaIn before the Ag particles (Methods), with the oxide layer on the LM particles preventing reactions between Ag and In. After drying, while Ag and LM particles physically contacted to form conductive pathways, the elemental distribution map showed only slight, not pronounced, alloying between Ag and In (Supplementary Fig. 1).

During dehydration, the ink–hydrogel assembly underwent two key transitions: the interconnection of LM/Ag particles to form conductive pathways and the cross-linking of PVA chains to establish robust interfacial bonding. We first investigated the structural evolution of the LM/Ag/PVA composite ink during drying (Supplementary Fig. 6). Initially, the presence of water prevented effective contact between the metal particles, resulting in a non-conductive state. As water evaporated, the concentrations of LM and Ag particles gradually increased, reducing the spacing between the inclusions. Evaporation induced the contact, compression, and coalescence of LM particles, while the Ag particles acted as additional electronic bridges. These processes enabled current to pass through the composite, thereby forming a continuous conductive network (Supplementary Fig. 7)41–43. Consistent with this mechanism, Supplementary Fig. 8 showed that the resistance of the LM/Ag/PVA composite decreased from 4.43 × 107 Ω to 0.15 Ω after 30 min of drying.

In addition, the drying process caused the printed composite ink to solidify into a conductive film, formed through a dual crosslinking mechanism involving both PVA self-crosslinking and LM particle crosslinking. Initially, dehydration induced the PVA chains in the ink to gradually aggregate and fold into crystalline domains, thereby forming a crosslinked network39. This was confirmed by the characteristic diffraction peak at 2θ = 19.7° of the PVA crystals in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the film (Supplementary Fig. 9). The PVA chains in the amorphous regions adopted randomly entangled conformations throughout the composite and, together with the crystalline domains, contributed to a continuous PVA network. Subsequently, the LM particles, through their oxide layer, formed abundant hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups on the PVA chains, acting as additional crosslinking points44. The crosslinking effect of the LM particles was further confirmed through immersion experiments in a 1 M NaOH solution. The alkaline solution removed the oxide layer from the LM particles, thereby eliminating the hydrogen bonding between the LM and PVA. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, the pure PVA hydrogel exhibited no significant change before and after immersion, indicating that the crystalline crosslinking of PVA remained unaffected by the alkaline solution. In contrast, the mechanical strength of the LM/Ag/PVA film significantly decreased after immersion, although it still maintained its self-supporting properties and did not dissolve completely. This confirmed that the LM particles served as additional crosslinking points through hydrogen bonding with PVA. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images, combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of the film, further demonstrated that the PVA crosslinked network firmly anchored the particles within the film (Supplementary Fig. 11). The LM particles had an approximate diameter of 50–100 µm, and the silver particles measured around 50–100 µm.

Next, we investigated the interfacial cross-linking of PVA chains from both the composite ink and the substrate during dehydration (Fig. 2b). Dehydration simultaneously induced self-crosslinking of the PVA chains in the ink, along with their interdiffusion, entanglement, and cross-linking with the PVA chains in the hydrogel substrate, forming a dense cross-linked network. The resulting interpenetrating PVA network seamlessly fused the composite ink with the hydrogel substrate, yielding a stable LM/Ag/PVA conductive layer at the hydrogel surface. The migration of PVA chains was visualised by grafting distinct fluorescent groups onto the PVA chains in the ink and the hydrogel. Fluorescence imaging confirmed the interdiffusion of PVA chains from the ink and the hydrogel during dehydration, with the interfacial entanglement region measuring approximately 60 µm in thickness.

The cross-linking points formed during dehydration are crystalline domains composed of tightly folded PVA chains bound by hydrogen bonds39,45. Accordingly, the crystallinity of the PVA hydrogel substrate before and after drying was evaluated using wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 12). The initial hydrogel exhibited a low crystallinity (~1.7%), and its loosely arranged PVA chains facilitated entanglement with the PVA chains in the ink. Following the dehydration, the crystallinity of the hydrogel increased markedly to 16.1%, indicating the formation of additional cross-linking sites. The hydrogels before and after drying consistently showed a characteristic diffraction peak at 2θ = 19.7°, corresponding to the (101¯) crystalline plane of PVA39,46. The cryo-SEM images reveal that the hydrogel after drying had a denser three-dimensional porous structure (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results confirm that the drying process drives the migration and crystallization of PVA chains between the composite ink and the hydrogel substrate, leading to their seamless fusion and the formation of an interpenetrating cross-linked PVA network throughout the material.

The dried MPEH was fully rehydrated in deionised water to restore its flexibility. The MPEH exhibited an overall swelling ratio of 1.87, which was attributed to water absorption by the hydrogel substrate. Owing to the dense packing and intrinsic hydrophobicity of the LM/Ag particles in the conductive layer, this layer absorbed virtually no water and therefore maintained a swelling ratio of 1.00, as indicated by the swelling curve in Supplementary Fig. 14. Swelling equilibrium was achieved within two hours and remained stable thereafter. To maintain the precision of the printed patterns during the interface-fusion printing process, the hydrogel substrate was fixed to a glass plate so that dehydration-induced shrinkage occurred only along the longitudinal axis, while the transverse dimension remained stable, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 15.

Structure and interfacial robustness of MPEH

During dehydration, PVA chains interdiffused and fused at the ink–hydrogel interface, leading to a partial overlap between the conductive layer and the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 16). This interfacial fusion caused the LM/Ag particles to become partially embedded within the hydrogel substrate, forming a semi-embedded configuration. The microstructure of the MPEH was subsequently examined (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 17–18). Cross-sectional and surface images showed that the LM/Ag particles were immobilized within the PVA network and remained in close contact, forming continuous conductive pathways. Notably, the surface of the conductive layer was covered with a PVA coating, and the LM/Ag particles were not exposed to air. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, the carbon (C) elemental map revealed a C signal on the surfaces of the LM particles, confirming the presence of a PVA coating. However, the Ga–In signals from the LM particles partially overlapped with the C signal, compromising its visualization. In higher-magnification images, the PVA coating on the composite surface was clearly visible. The polymer encapsulated the particles and bound them together into a continuous macroscopic film.

To further characterize the surface composition of the LM/Ag/PVA conductive layer, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on both the pure LM and LM/Ag/PVA films. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20, two distinct Ga 2p peaks were observed for pure LM, corresponding to Ga3+ in Ga2O3 (binding energy 1118.9 eV) and Ga0 state (binding energy 1116.9 eV)47. The O 1s spectrum of pure LM exhibited two peaks assignable to Ga2O3 (binding energy 530.6 eV) and oxygen vacancies (binding energy 531.8 eV), respectively. In the LM/Ag/PVA film, the Ga0 peak decreased markedly, indicating oxidation of the LM within the composite. The presence of a PVA coating on the composite surface was further confirmed by an additional C–O peak (binding energy 532.0 eV) in the O 1s spectrum48,49. These results confirm that the surface of the LM/Ag/PVA composite is coated with PVA. Micro-CT imaging further confirmed the continuity and structural integrity of the MPEH over a macroscopic area of 4 mm × 4 mm (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 21).

Owing to the bridging function of the PVA network, the conductive layer forms strong interfacial adhesion with the hydrogel substrate. As shown in Fig. 2d, the MPEH exhibits a uniform and continuous architecture, with no gap between the conductive layer and the substrate. To evaluate the interfacial adhesion strength, we applied the LM/Ag/PVA conductive layer onto various flexible substrates, including polyacrylamide (PAM) hydrogels, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyurethane (PU), and polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene-ran-butylene)-block-polystyrene (SEBS). Lap-shear tests were then conducted to quantify the interfacial shear strength. As shown in Fig. 2f, two identical substrates sandwiched the conductive layer and were each bonded to a glass plate to prevent undesired deformation during measurement. On the PVA hydrogel, the conductive layer exhibited a high shear strength of 234.4 kPa, whereas its adhesion to the other substrates was substantially weaker, with shear strengths of 27.4 kPa on PAM hydrogel, 10.5 kPa on SEBS, and 8.7 kPa on PU (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 22). Adhesion to PDMS was too weak to measure. This pronounced difference results from strong cross-linking between the conductive layer and the PVA hydrogel substrate. In contrast, on other substrates, the conductive layer adheres through weak interactions that cannot provide firm interfacial adhesion. A comparison of the electrical conductivity of the conductive materials and its adhesion strength to the flexible substrate in related studies is provided in Fig. 2g (further details are available in Supplementary Table 1).

Mechanical and electrical properties of MPEH

The robust cross-interfacial cross-linking enhances the mechanical stability of the interface. As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 23, the conductive layer on the MPEH remained intact at 400% tensile strain, whereas the conductive layer fractured at 200% strain on the PAM hydrogel substrate and at 100% strain on PDMS, PU, and SEBS. Finite element analysis was performed to examine the stress distribution within the conductive layer under tensile deformation (Fig. 3b). A weak interface led to pronounced stress concentration, causing fracture and delamination upon stretching. Increasing the interfacial strength significantly mitigated this stress concentration, enabling the conductive layer to maintain structural integrity under large deformations50.

The stable interface enables the MPEH to maintain constant resistance even under extreme impacts. To evaluate electrical robustness, we subjected both the MPEH and a control liquid metal-coated hydrogel (LCH) to high-power underwater ultrasonication at 60 W and repeated ball-drop impacts reaching 300 MPa. The LCH was prepared by directly spreading LM onto a PVA hydrogel surface. Under ultrasonication, all LM on the LCH detached within 10 s, eliminating electrical connectivity (Fig. 3c, d). By contrast, the conductive layer of the MPEH remained intact after 10 min of ultrasonication, with no LM leakage and virtually no change in surface resistance. SEM images acquired before and after ultrasonication showed no discernible alteration in surface morphology, further confirming the absence of LM leakage (Supplementary Fig. 24). Impact tests were conducted by repeatedly dropping a 100 g ball from a height of 1 m onto the samples (Supplementary Fig. 25). The MPEH maintained constant resistance throughout the impacts, whereas the LCH failed electrically upon the first drop (Fig. 3e). These results show that simply coating LM on a substrate cannot guarantee electrical stability because weak interfacial adhesion leads to LM leakage and circuit failure, whereas the interface-fusion printing strategy effectively overcomes these issues.

The inherent stretchability of both the LM and the PVA hydrogel, combined with the high-strength interfacial bonding, endows the MPEH with good tensile properties and strain-insensitive electrical resistance. We first investigated the effect of LM/Ag particle content on the tensile properties and electrical conductivity of the MPEH (Methods). As shown in Fig. 3f, the elongation at break of the MPEH gradually increased from 406.1% to 506.0% with increasing LM/Ag particle loading, then slightly declined to 475.3% at 80.4 wt%. The elastic modulus exhibited a similar trend, ranging from 0.9 to 1.0 MPa (Supplementary Fig. 26). As shown in Fig. 3g, the electrical conductivity of the MPEH increased with higher particle content, reaching 1.18 × 106 S m−1 at 80.4 wt%, a value comparable to that of bulk LM. Figure 3h shows that the MPEH exhibits exceptional strain insensitivity, maintaining nearly constant resistance under strains up to 400% (R/R0 = 1.15 at 80.4 wt%, 1.27 at 75.6 wt%, 1.53 at 67.7 wt%, and 3.65 at 52.4 wt%). These values are substantially lower than those predicted by Pouillet’s law for a conventional incompressible bulk liquid metal conductor of constant conductivity (Supplementary Note 1). Considering both electrical and mechanical performance, the MPEH with 80.4 wt% loading was chosen for further investigation; as shown in Fig. 3i, its resistance remained stable (R/R0 = 1.20) after 1000 cycles at 100% strain.

The Ag particles in the LM/Ag/PVA conductive layer enhance both conductivity and electromechanical stability. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 27, the LM/Ag/PVA film exhibited higher electrical conductivity than the LM/PVA film (1.18 × 106 vs 1.84 × 105 S m−1). Consistently, the resistance change during stretching showed that the LM/Ag/PVA film maintained greater electromechanical stability than the LM/PVA film. This improvement arises because the Ag particles act as rigid bridges during deformation, filling the gaps that develop between LM particles and thereby sustaining continuous conductive pathways in the MPEH.

Owing to the semi-embedded structure of LM/Ag particles in the MPEH, stable electrical connections can be achieved simply by placing rigid electronics onto the conductive traces of the MPEH. The transmission line method51 was employed to measure the contact resistance between the conductive traces and equivalently zero-ohm-resistance, tinned copper contacts (Supplementary Note 2). By varying the trace length (i.e., the spacing between adjacent copper contacts), recording the total resistance, and fitting the data to a linear model (Fig. 3j; coefficient of determination, R2 = 0.995), the contact resistance—determined as half of the y-intercept—was calculated to be 0.03 Ω, lower than previously reported LM-based contacts52. In summary, the semi-embedded structure provides not only stable electrical interconnections but also robust electromechanical stability, both of which are essential for high-performance flexible electronic devices.

In addition, the environmental stability of the MPEH was investigated. The water retention ratio was measured at 25 °C and 75% relative humidity. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, the MPEH exhibited a water retention ratio of 85.56% after 7 days, and its tensile performance remained consistent with that in the initial state, demonstrating good environmental stability. Applying a petrolatum coating to the surface further enhanced its environmental durability, with the coated MPEH showing a water retention ratio of 91.21% after 7 days. Notably, the coating did not affect the mechanical performance of the MPEH.

MPEH-based functional circuits

The interface-fusion printing approach is simple and convenient, requiring no complex procedures or specialised equipment. This scalable and efficient method allows for the creation of diverse patterned circuits by adjusting the mask design. The printed traces achieve a minimum line width of 100 µm (Supplementary Fig. 29). The electromechanical stability of the MPEH with 100-μm-wide conductive traces was evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 30). At a tensile strain of 122%, the MPEH exhibited an R/R0 of 2.21, which is lower than the value predicted by Pouillet’s law. Resistance measurements over 1000 loading–unloading cycles at 100% strain further confirmed the electromechanical stability of the MPEH, with an R/R0 of 4.05 after 1000 cycles. Benefiting from the semi-embedded structure of LM/Ag particles within the MPEH, discrete electronic components can be directly mounted onto the patterned surface to assemble a broad range of flexible electronic devices for various applications (Fig. 4a). As a demonstration, we fabricated a high-density, complex circuit featuring four components per square centimetre and a minimum line spacing of 100 µm (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 31). The device conformed seamlessly to the wrist and maintains stable performance even during dynamic activities such as running (Supplementary Movie 2).

MPEH-based flexible biosensors

Leveraging the versatile fabrication of LM/Ag circuits and the hydrogel substrate’s good biocompatibility, MPEH enables the development of epidermal biosensors capable of real-time monitoring of human motion and vital signs for health assessment and clinical diagnosis (Fig. 5a). As a proof of concept, we printed a strain gauge composed of silver microflakes onto the MPEH and attached the device to a finger to continuously track joint motion (Fig. 5b). Minute tensile strains created gaps between adjacent microflakes, sharply increasing the gauge’s resistance (Supplementary Fig. 34). This configuration discriminated the flexion of individual finger joints while remaining insensitive to motion elsewhere on the hand: when the gauge was positioned on region 2 (R2), bending in that region induced a large resistance change, whereas bending region 1 (R1) left the signal unchanged (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Movie 5). This is attributed to the good strain-insensitivity of the MPEH circuits. The device also maintained a consistent response over 50 bending cycles (Supplementary Fig. 35).

The second device is an epidermal photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor for pulse monitoring (Fig. 5d). Its circuit layout and fabrication are described in the Methods section and Supplementary Fig. 36. Thanks to its ultrathin profile (~300 µm), the sensor conformed intimately to the skin; simultaneously, its transparent substrate permitted infrared light to enter the tissue with little attenuation, enabling high-fidelity, real-time pulse monitoring (Fig. 5e). We next exploited MPEH as a soft electrophysiological electrode (Fig. 5f). In the final fabrication step, deionised water in the hydrogel was replaced with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) to boost ionic conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 37). The resulting electrode, benefitting from seamless ion–electron coupling within MPEH, exhibited a lower skin impedance than commercial hydrogel electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 38). Electromyographic (EMG) tests further verified its performance: the electrode sensitively captured muscle contractions of varying intensities, matching the signal quality of commercial gel electrodes (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 39). In addition, the biocompatibility of the MPEH was evaluated. Two groups of MPEH samples were immersed in culture medium for 24 h to prepare conditioned medium, with one group subjected to ultrasonication during immersion to simulate extreme conditions. Cell viability and live/dead staining showed no evident cell death in either group, confirming the biosafety of the MPEH (Supplementary Fig. 40). Overall, by leveraging the hydrogel substrate’s good skin affinity and biocompatibility, MPEH shows great potential for flexible wearable applications.

Underwater jellyfish robot based on MPEH

The MPEH integrates the softness and tissue-like properties of hydrogels with flexible circuits, further advancing the integration of bioinspired systems and electronic technologies. Inspired by marine soft-bodied organisms, we developed a hydrogel jellyfish robot. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, the onboard electronics comprise five modules: wireless communication, motor drive, power supply, control unit, and a flexible LED circuit. The structural design of the robot is detailed in Fig. 6b. The control circuit board was embedded within the jellyfish’s head, and a motor actuated six tentacles through a transmission mechanism, enabling precise and programmable swimming motions. The jellyfish’s body, composed of PVA hydrogel, replicated the morphology and mechanical compliance of biological tissues, with an MPEH-based flexible LED circuit integrated onto its surface and connected to the control system. The jellyfish robot’s swimming speed was controlled by tuning the motor’s rotation rate and visually signalled by a colour-coded LED circuit: blue denoted high speed, whereas green denoted low speed.

Figure 6c presents two propulsion modes—ascending and descending—executed by the jellyfish robot. Each stroke cyclically stretched the MPEH-based LED circuit as the tentacles opened and closed, while the LEDs maintained steady emission, highlighting the circuit’s exceptional electromechanical durability under prolonged underwater deformation (Supplementary Movie 6). By combining high optical transparency with underwater electromechanical robustness, the MPEH-based circuits enable instantaneous, on-board visual feedback during locomotion, establishing a self-reporting soft robotic system. Collectively, these results highlight the good electromechanical stability of MPEH under dynamic underwater conditions and support its potential for integrating bioinspired systems with electronic technologies.

Universality of the interface-fusion printing strategy

In addition to PVA hydrogels, this strategy is applicable to other hydrogel substrates, including PAM, poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) and poly(N,N-dimethylacrylamide) (PDMAA). A thin layer of PVA solution was applied to the hydrogel surface to allow permeation into the matrix, after which MPEH was prepared using the same procedure. Lap-shear tests (Supplementary Fig. 41) showed that untreated hydrogels exhibited interfacial adhesion strengths of 27.39 kPa (PAM), 7.89 kPa (PDMAA) and 12.92 kPa (PAA). With PVA pre-treatment, these values increased to 81.24 kPa, 78.37 kPa and 63.04 kPa, respectively, indicating that PVA acted as a macromolecular bridge that markedly enhanced interfacial adhesion. Electromechanical stability was further assessed (Supplementary Figs. 42–43). All three hydrogels exhibited strain-insensitive resistance, with R/R0 values of 1.45 at 400% strain (PAM), 1.26 at 250% strain (PDMAA) and 1.24 at 156% strain (PAA). Under cyclic loading, each sample maintained stable electromechanical performance over 1000 loading–unloading cycles at 100% strain. Together, these results demonstrate that interface-fusion printing is compatible with diverse hydrogel substrates and offers broad generalizability.

Discussion

We have reported a universal interface-fusion printing strategy for fabricating MPEH with high interfacial stability and exceptional conductivity, enabling robust and stretchable electronics. This strategy employs PVA as a macromolecular bridge by incorporating it into both the hydrogel substrate and the printed LM/Ag/PVA composite ink. Cooperative dehydration promotes interconnection among the LM/Ag particles in the ink, forming conductive pathways, while simultaneously triggering self-crosslinking of the PVA chains in the ink as well as their cross-interfacial cross-linking with the PVA chains in the hydrogel substrate. The interpenetrating PVA network seamlessly integrates the LM/Ag/PVA composite with the hydrogel substrate, anchoring the particles in a semi-embedded configuration at the hydrogel surface. It achieves a high interfacial adhesion strength of 234.4 kPa at the LM/Ag/PVA composite–hydrogel interface and surface conductivity of 1.18 × 106 S m−1. The robust interface prevents LM leakage and ensures stable electrical connection under extreme conditions, including prolonged ultrasonication, repeated 300 MPa impacts, and thousands of stretching cycles. The MPEH exhibits high stretchability (>400%), strain-insensitive resistance (only a 0.15-fold increase at 400% strain), and exceptional underwater electromechanical stability (>7 days).

The interface-fusion printing method is simple and compatible with various hydrogel substrates. It enables the preparation of high-resolution circuits with minimum line widths of 100 µm. The MPEH, integrated with diverse electronic components, supports a broad range of functions—including square-wave signal output, light emission, human-motion detection, pulse monitoring, and EMG signal acquisition—while maintaining stable performance under uniaxial and biaxial stretching, as well as folding. By leveraging the soft, tissue-like properties of the hydrogel together with integrated electronic functionality, the MPEH enables the creation of a hydrogel jellyfish robot that provides continuous and stable visual feedback during underwater locomotion, advancing the convergence of bioinspired systems and electronic technologies. The MPEH allows seamless integration of LM circuits with diverse hydrogels, establishing a versatile and robust platform that opens directions in hydrogel bioelectronics, soft robotics, and implantable devices.

Methods

Materials

Poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA, 1750 ± 50, Mw 74,800–79,200) was purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Liquid metal (EGaIn, 75.5% Ga, 24.5% In, wt%) was purchased from Dingguan Metal Technology Co. Silver microparticles (5 µm) were purchased from Manli Nano Technology Co. Acrylamide (AAm), acrylic acid (AA), N, N-dimethylacrylamide (DMAA), N, N’-Methylenebis(acrylamide) (MBAA), and α-Ketoglutaric acid were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene-ran-butylene)-block-polystyrene (SEBS, G1645) was purchased from Kraton Co. Polyurethane (PU, PUD-2307) was purchase from SiwoChem Co. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Silicone SYLGARD 184 Elastomer Kit) was purchased from Dow Chemical Co. All materials were used as received without further purification.

Fabrication

Preparation of the PVA hydrogel substrate

The PVA hydrogel substrate was prepared using a freeze–thaw method. First, a 10 wt% PVA solution was prepared by completely dissolving PVA in deionised water at 95 °C under vigorous stirring. The solution was then cast between two glass plates to a thickness of 1 mm, frozen at −20 °C for 12 h, and subsequently thawed at room temperature to form the PVA hydrogel.

Preparation of the LM/Ag/PVA composite ink

A series of composite inks with varying LM/Ag particle loadings were prepared. Specifically, 5 g of 7.5 wt% PVA solution and 5, 10, 15, or 20 g of LM were mixed and dispersed using a high-speed homogenizer (FJ200-S) at 10,000 rpm. Next, 0.5 g of silver microparticles was added and stirred thoroughly until well mixed. The resulting inks contained LM/Ag particle concentrations of 52.4 wt%, 67.7 wt%, 75.6 wt%, and 80.4 wt%, respectively. The optimal composite ink formulation consists of 5 g of 7.5 wt% PVA solution, 20 g of LM particles, and 0.5 g of Ag particles. After drying, the resulting conductive composite contains 95.8 wt% LM, 2.4 wt% Ag, and 1.8 wt% PVA. Unless otherwise stated, this formulation was used for all experiments in this study.

Preparation of MPEH

First, the PVA hydrogel substrate was fixed onto a glass plate, and a laser-engraved stencil was placed on its surface. The composite ink was then printed onto the exposed regions. After printing, the stencil was removed, and the printed ink–hydrogel assembly was dried in an oven at 50 °C for 6 h, until a constant weight was reached (see the setup in Supplementary Fig. 15). Finally, the dried material was fully rehydrated in deionised water to obtain the MPEH. For use as electrophysiological electrodes, the deionised water was replaced with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4).

Preparation of other hydrogel substrates

For the PAM hydrogel, 2 g of AAm, 50 µL of 2 wt% MBAA solution, and 50 mg of α-ketoglutaric acid were dissolved in 10 mL of deionised water. The resulting solution was cast between two glass plates with a thickness of 1 mm and exposed to ultraviolet light (365 nm, 90 mW/cm2) for 30 minutes. PAM had a molecular weight (Mw) of 418,354.

For the PAA hydrogel, 2 g of AA, 50 µL of 2 wt% MBAA solution, and 50 mg of α-ketoglutaric acid were dissolved in 10 mL of deionised water. The resulting solution was cast between two glass plates with a thickness of 1 mm and exposed to ultraviolet light (365 nm, 90 mW/cm2) for 30 minutes. PAA had a molecular weight (Mw) of 336,820.

For the PDMAA hydrogel, 2 g of DMAA, 50 µL of 2 wt% MBAA solution, and 50 mg of α-ketoglutaric acid were dissolved in 10 mL of deionised water. The resulting solution was cast between two glass plates with a thickness of 1 mm and exposed to ultraviolet light (365 nm, 90 mW/cm2) for 30 minutes. PDMAA had a molecular weight (Mw) of 361,995.

The prepared hydrogel substrates were coated with a 5 wt% PVA solution and left for 1 h, allowing the PVA chains to permeate the matrix. The LM/Ag circuits were then integrated onto these hydrogels using the same fabrication procedure.

The molecular weights of these polymers were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC).

Characterization

Microstructure characterization

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of MPEH were characterized by SEM (ZEISS Sigma 360). The elemental distribution (including C, Ga, In and Ag) of the samples was characterized by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, OXFORD Xplore 30). The cross-sectional morphology of the PVA hydrogel was characterized by Cryo-SEM (HITACHI 8100). Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) was conducted with an Xradia 620 Versa microscope (Zeiss) at an acceleration voltage of 140 kV. Samples were placed on a holder and rotated horizontally through 360° to obtain two-dimensional images. The three-dimensional structure of the samples was reconstructed using Avizo software. Confocal images were carried out using an Olympus FV3000 microscope. PVA-FITC emits green fluorescence under 488 nm excitation, while PVA-RBITC emits red fluorescence under 561 nm excitation.

Characterization of PVA hydrogel crystallinity

The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses were conducted with a DSC250 instrument (TA Instruments, USA) on the hydrogels in their dry state. X-ray scattering (XRD) data were collected using a D8 Advance diffractometer. We used excess chemical cross-links to fix the amorphous polymer chains before air-drying hydrogels in an incubator at 37 °C39.

Interfacial adhesion tests

The composite ink was coated onto the surfaces of various flexible substrates, each firmly adhered to a glass plate, covering an area of 20 mm × 10 mm. Once partially dried, the composite ink surfaces of two samples were carefully brought into contact and allowed to dry fully. Hydrogel samples were immersed in water and allowed to swell prior to testing. Lap-shear tests were then performed using a universal mechanical testing machine (UTM 2502) at a speed of 30 mm min−1. The interfacial shear strength was calculated by dividing the maximum measured force by the overlap area.

Mechanical property characterization

The mechanical properties of MPEH were characterized using a universal testing machine (UTM 2502). Rectangular strips (50 mm × 10 mm) were cut from the samples for tensile testing. Changes in sample resistance during stretching were monitored using a Keithley DMM6500 connected to a computer. A four-terminal measurement method was employed to ensure accurate resistance readings and minimise interference.

Electrical property characterization

Electrical conductivity of MPEH was measured using a standard four-point probe tester (RTS-8). For skin-contact impedance measurements, two strips (10 mm × 30 mm) were placed on the inner side of the left arm, spaced 5 cm apart centre-to-centre. Prior to application, the skin was cleaned with ethanol to ensure adequate contact. Commercial gel electrodes were used as the control group.

Finite element analysis

Finite element simulations were performed to compare the stress states of the conductive layer under different interface bonding strengths. The model comprised an upper conductive layer, a zero-thickness interfacial layer in the middle, and a lower polymer layer. The conductive layer was defined by an elastoplastic damage constitutive law (Young’s modulus of 7 MPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, isotropic hardening for plastic deformation, and a Ductile damage criterion), while the polymer layer was defined by an elastic constitutive law (Young’s modulus of 0.9 MPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.39). The interface was governed by a Cohesive constitutive model. Finally, the material’s left edge was fully constrained using an Encastre boundary condition, and a lateral displacement of 8 mm was applied along the length direction on the right edge to simulate external stretching.

Circuit design and signal processing of epidermal PPG sensor

The PPG sensor circuit is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 24. The intensity of red light emitted by the LED (IR26-21C/L110/TR8, EVERLIGHT; 940 nm) was controlled by a 200-ohm resistor (R20; 1206W4F2000T5E), and the emitted light was detected by a photodiode (PT26-21B/TR8, EVERLIGHT; 940 nm). The current generated by the photodiode was converted into a voltage across a 20 k-ohm resistor (R21; 1206W4F2002T5E, UNI-ROYAL). Next, a 100 nF capacitor network (C36; CL31B104KBCNNNC, SAMSUNG) filtered out high-frequency noise, followed by AC coupling through C29 (CL31B225KBHNNNE, SAMSUNG). The resulting AC PPG signal was then amplified 680-fold by a non-inverting amplifier comprising a 1 k-ohm resistor (R23; 1206W4F1001T5E, UNI-ROYAL), a 680 k-ohm resistor (R24; 1206W4F6803T5E, UNI-ROYAL), a 51 k-ohm resistor (R22; 1206W4F5102T5E, UNI-ROYAL), and an operational amplifier (U4; LM358DR, TI). Notably, R22 provided a DC return path for the bias current. Finally, the processed PPG signal was recorded using an oscilloscope (DS100, Guangzhou Xingyi Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.).

Collection of EMG signals

To acquire EMG signals, a commercial EMG monitor (Wuxi Borunyin Technology Co., LTD) was connected to the samples. Three strip-shaped MPEH patches measuring 10 mm × 30 mm each were prepared. Human skin testing was approved (tjdxsr2025170), and informed consent was obtained from the participant.

Jellyfish robot fabrication

All mechanical components, including the head and transmission assembly, were designed in SolidWorks and fabricated by stereolithography (SLA) using transparent photosensitive resin (Shenzhen Future Factory Co., Ltd.). Components were manually assembled, and a 2.5 × 1.5 × 5 mm bearing was linked to six tentacles via a custom transmission mechanism. The body was cast in PVA hydrogel to reproduce the morphology of a natural jellyfish, and a MPEH-based LED circuit was mounted on its exterior. Two LEDs of distinct colours were incorporated to signal swimming speed. The circuit surface was sealed using a commercially available waterproof adhesive film (Tegaderm, 3 M).

The electronic control system comprised a microcontroller (STM32F1), a wireless transceiver (E62‑433T20D, 433 MHz, 20 dBm), a motor driver (TB6612FNG) and a 5 V constant‑voltage lithium‑ion battery. The compact circuit board was housed within the robot’s head and wired to a 6 V, 60 rpm DC motor and the LED module. Following assembly, the circuit board was encapsulated in silicone rubber to ensure watertight integrity. Operational commands were transmitted wirelessly from a computer. In descending mode, a steady blue LED indicates high‑speed swimming and a steady green LED denotes low speed. In ascending mode, the blue LED flashes at high speed while the green LED remains continuously illuminated at low speed.
