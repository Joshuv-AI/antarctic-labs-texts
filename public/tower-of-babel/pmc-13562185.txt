Zebrafish larvae swim bladder serves as a model to study Mycobacterium abscessus infection kinetics, host–pathogen interactions and drug efficacy
Authors: Ram M Basnet, Shashank Gupta, Kenneth N Olivier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycobacterium abscessus, a nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM), is a cause of chronic lung infection primarily affecting people with underlying conditions such as cystic fibrosis and other causes of bronchiectasis. Mostly caudal vein infection of zebrafish embryos has been used for modelling NTM infection which differs from the pathogenesis of human respiratory infection. Given the histological and physiological similarity of the zebrafish swim bladder to human lung, we developed a localized swim bladder infection model of M. abscessus, representing the three-way interactions between bacteria, epithelial cells, and immune cells seen in the human lung.MethodsWild-type zebrafish larvae were injected with M. abscessus:mCherry into the posterior area of the swim bladder at 5 days post-fertilization and growth of M. abscessus in the swim bladder region was verified by imaging and enumerating the CFU counts. Similar injections were performed in swim bladders of transgenic Tg(mpx:GFP) and Tg(mpeg:GFP) zebrafish larvae to observe host neutrophil- and macrophage-pathogen interactions respectively. The expression of the proinflammatory cytokine, TNF-α was also measured to quantify host immune response. To study drug efficacy in this model, zebrafish larvae were infected into the swim bladder with M. abscessus:eGFP, treated with clarithromycin or omadacycline by immersion and CFU counts were determined at 2- and 4-day post-treatment.ResultsM. abscessus formed a biofilm-like, localized temporal mycobacterial growth in the swim bladder, with minimal spread into the systemic circulation. This localized growth of M. abscessus induced recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages, forming granuloma-like structures in the swim bladder along with a significant increase in TNF-α. Both clarithromycin and omadacycline treatment resulted in a significant reduction of bacterial biofilm-like growth in the swim bladder.ConclusionOverall, this study established the first zebrafish: M. abscessus swim bladder infection model to study localized NTM infection, host–pathogen interactions, and drug efficacy studies.Keywords: Mycobacterium abscessus, Zebrafish, Swim Bladder, Host–pathogen interaction, Drug efficacy

Background

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are ubiquitous, free-living, environmental saprophytic organisms known to occupy water systems, soil, and vegetation. There are around 220,000 cases of NTM infection in the US, and the cases are rising at 8.2% each year each year [1]. Common NTM infections in people include Mycobacterium abscessus and Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC). Mycobacterium abscessus (Mab or M. abscessus) commonly affects patients with underlying pulmonary conditions such as cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, other causes of bronchiectasis, and immune deficiency [2]. It is most frequently associated with pulmonary disease, which generally remains localized to the lung, and skin and soft tissue infections in humans [3]. The clinical presentation of M. abscessus infection is highly variable. Some patients remain clinically stable in the absence of treatment, while others progress rapidly to respiratory disease [4]. The treatment of M. abscessus infection utilizes multidrug regimens consisting of oral macrolides (when susceptible in vitro) such as clarithromycin along with parenteral antibiotics such as amikacin, cefoxitin, imipenem, and tigecycline [5]. Guidelines based treatment regimens for M. abscessus have been associated with limited efficacy and poor tolerability emphasizing the importance of new drug development.

The commonly used animal models of M. abscessus infection include murine and embryonic-larval zebrafish model. As wild type mice rapidly clear the M. abscessus infection, immunocompromised mice such as GM-CSF deficient mice and dexamethasone injected mice are commonly used to model M. abscessus infection [6]. Lesser-used animal models to model M. abscessus infection include amoebae and moths, which model various aspects of M. abscessus infection [7, 8].

Zebrafish embryos-larvae have been used as a model for testing NTM virulence, host–pathogen interactions and drug efficacy [9, 10]. However, in most studies, the methodology for creating the zebrafish/M. abscessus infection model involves injecting bacteria into the caudal vein and enumerating bacterial burden and documenting survival [11–13]. To study host–pathogen interactions, most of the zebrafish studies involve injecting bacteria into the hindbrain ventricle and documenting the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages [14, 15]. This disseminated infection model lacks the three-way interaction between epithelial cells, bacteria and host immune cells relevant to human lung infections. The swim bladder provides an optimum microenvironment for creating a localized infection with an ability to observe the interactions between bacteria, host epithelial cells and immune cells [16, 17]. Also, the air-filled swim bladder provides the air–liquid interface platform for bacterial growth resembling the conditions seen in human airways.

The swim bladder in zebrafish is an organ ontogenetically similar to the human lung. They both are an extension of the gut tube and form anterior to the liver and pancreas. Adult zebrafish have two swim bladders, anterior and posterior connected to each other via the pneumatic duct. Unlike the exchange of air in the alveoli of human lungs, the swim bladder in zebrafish functions primarily to maintain buoyancy in water [18–20]. The zebrafish swim bladder is an air-filled sac with three-layered tissue: inner epithelial, middle mesenchyme and outer mesothelium [19]. At a physiological level, the epithelial lining of the human airways and zebrafish swim bladder are similar in that they both produce mucus, surfactants and antimicrobial peptides [17, 20–23]. Indeed, surfactant is required for the inflation of the swim bladder [21, 24]. Zebrafish larvae have a fully inflated posterior swim bladder at around 4–5 days post fertilization (dpf) [19]. It provides a space where bacteria can be injected without causing structural deformity. Infecting the posterior swim bladder during the 1st week of development provides the opportunity to observe the infection course while the larvae are still transparent. Additionally, this embryo/larva infection model is manipulable in a small space (petri dish or multiwell plate) thereby providing a full system infection model that is rapid and feasible within the typical lab environment.

In this study, we infected the swim bladder of zebrafish larvae at 5 dpf with M. abscessus and developed an infection model to study virulence, host–pathogen interactions and drug screening (Fig. 1). The zebrafish larvae swim bladder M. abscessus infection model described here broadens the available toolkit for mycobacterial, immunological, and therapeutic research in NTM field.

Methods

Zebrafish embryo/larvae maintenance

The adult wild type (TAB and AB) and transgenic Tg(mpx:GFP) [25] and Tg(mpeg:GFP) [26] zebrafish lines housed in the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) zebrafish core or UNC Zebrafish Aquaculture Core were naturally bred overnight to obtain freshly spawned eggs the next morning. For the UNC Zebrafish Aquaculture core, all experimental procedures performed in this study were reviewed and approved by the University of North Carolina Chapel Hill Animal Care and Use Committee. Animals were housed in an AAALAC-accredited facility in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The embryos were then transferred and maintained in the NIH IACUC approved satellite zebrafish facility (protocol# H-0310) or in the UNC IACUC approved Animal Procedural Space Exception room (IACUC ID# 23–262.0-B) in an incubator at 28.5 degrees C with a 14 h light/:10 h dark cycle. The embryos were raised in fish water (60 mg/L Instant Ocean Sea Salts) with daily water change. The staging of zebrafish embryos was done as described in Kimmel et al. [27]. Embryos 24 h post-fertilization were treated with 0.003% 1-phenyl-2-thiourea (PTU) to prevent pigmentation. From 5 dpf, the zebrafish larvae were fed once a day with GEMMA micro 75 (Skretting) or rotifers.

Preparation of M. abscessus

The fluorescence-labelled bacterial strains used in this study were type strain M. abscessus ATCC19977:mCherry and M. abscessus ATCC19977:GFP and clinical strain MAB_062600_1635:mCherry. The type strain was used for the caudal vein infection kinetics and drug efficacy experiments, as it is a well-characterized, genetically stable reference strain suited to standardized, reproducible measurement; the genome-sequenced clinical strain was used for the swim bladder localized infection and host–pathogen interaction experiments, as it represents a naturally disease-associated isolate whose genomic features could be related to observed phenotypes. M. abscessus isolates were grown in a shaker to an optical density (OD) of 0.8 at 250 rpm and 37 °C in 125-mL flasks containing 25 ml Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 1oleic acid/albumin/dextrose/catalase (OADC) 10% (vol/vol)) (Benton, Dickinson and Company), 0.2% glycerol (Fisher Scientific), and 0.1% tween-80 (Sigma Aldrich). The mCherry and GFP strains were grown under the selection of 250ug/mL of hygromycin and kanamycin respectively. The bacteria were transferred to a 50 mL Falcon tube and centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 15 min. The pellet was then decanted and suspended in 10 mL of freezing medium (20% glycerol in 7H11 medium). Then, 5 mL of glass beads (3 mm) (Sigma Aldrich) was added to the falcon tube and vortexed for 2 min. The bacteria were then sonicated 3 times in a cycle of 10 s in, and 30 s out. After sonicating, the bacteria were allowed to settle for 10 min. They were then passed back and forth through a 27G needle (Benton, Dickinson and Company) 10 times to obtain a single cell suspension. The OD of the resulting single cell suspension was measured with a spectrophotometer (Genesys-30, Theromoscientific), and the bacteria were aliquoted and stored at −80 ⁰C until further use. The aliquots were thawed and used to prepare the bacteria on the day of injection.

Microinjection of bacteria in the swim bladder

The swim bladder is fully inflated with air in the zebrafish larvae at 4–5 dpf [19]. We performed swim bladder infections in the zebrafish larvae (wild type and transgenic) at 5 dpf with 250–300 CFU of M. abscessus (Fig. 2). Briefly, zebrafish larvae were anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) 0.16 mg/mL (Syndel) and transferred with a transfer pipette to a 90 mm petri dish coated with 1.5% agarose (Genesse Scientific). Once inside the petri dish, under the visualization of a stereomicroscope (Leica, Germany), the larvae were oriented in the lateral position with an embryo poker. A borosilicate needle was loaded with 2–2.5 uL of M. abscessus and mounted in a micromanipulator (World Precision Equipment, USA) connected to pneumatic microinjector (Sutter, USA). Under microscopy, the borosilicate needle was oriented facing towards the zebrafish head angled at 45–50 degree. The needle was gradually moved towards the swim bladder with the micromanipulator. As the needle approached the swim bladder, it was gently pricked with the needle and advanced to the posterior area of swim bladder where the bacteria were injected. After injection of bacteria, the needle was slowly withdrawn from the swim bladder. The infected embryos were then transferred to fresh fish water for recovery.

Procedural illustration of swim bladder infection of M. abscessus in zebrafish larvae at 5 dpf

Caudal vein infection of zebrafish embryos

Caudal vein infection was performed for the comparison of infection kinetics between the swim bladder and intravenous infection methods. At 30 h post fertilization (hpf), the embryos were manually dechorionated with tweezers and anesthetized in MS-222 0.16 mg/mL. The embryos were then placed in 1.5% agarose plates in left lateral position with the tail of the embryos facing the injection needle. Under the visualization of a stereomicroscope, ≈250–300 CFU (3 nL of injection volume) of M. abscessus was microinjected into the caudal vein with borosilicate glass needles aided by a pneumatic microinjector.

Drug treatment

The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of omadacycline and clarithromycin against M. abscessus ATCC19977 is 0.12ug/mL and 2ug/mL respectively [28]. The infected zebrafish larvae were initiated on immersion drug treatment with omadacycline 7.6 ug/mL (Paratek Pharmaceuticals) and clarithromycin 150 ug/mL (Sigma-Aldrich) at 24 hpf. Omadacycline was prepared in sterile water, and clarithromycin was prepared in sterile water with 0.05% DMSO. Around 20 infected zebrafish larvae per group were placed in omadacycline or clarithromycin solution in a 90 mm petri dish containing 30 mL of drug solution. The drug solution was changed daily. Ten whole-body zebrafish larvae per group were collected at two and 4 days post treatment (dpt) to measure the bacterial burden in the zebrafish larvae.

Measurement of bacterial burden

To measure the post infection bacterial burden, homogenate was obtained from each whole-body zebrafish larva. At each timepoint. 5–10 larvae were individually homogenized with glass homogenizers in 500 uL 1X PBS (Corning). The homogenate was plated either directly or after dilution onto a 7H11 agar plate supplemented with OADC 10% (vol/vol) (Becton, Dickinson and Company) and antibiotics cocktail. The CFU were counted after 3–5 days, and a dot plot graph was used to show the CFU counts at various timepoints.

Visualization of host–pathogen interactions

To investigate whether the localized bacterial growth in the swim bladder could be utilized for studying host–pathogen interactions, we performed swim bladder infection of Tg(mpx: GFP) and Tg(mpeg:GFP) zebrafish larvae. Tg(mpx: GFP) carries GFP under the promotion of the mpx gene primarily expressed in the neutrophils. Tg(mpeg:GFP) carries GFP under the promotion of mpeg gene primarily expressed in macrophages. After infection, the host immune responses were visualized in vivo with widefield fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR for TNF-α expression

RNA extraction was done on 15 zebrafish larvae (whole-body) per sample infected in the swim bladder with M. abscessus ATCC 19977 and collected at 4 dpi. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Concentration of RNA was measured using the Qubit assay (Thermofisher Scientific) and 1 µg of RNA was used to retrotranscribe cDNA using a high-capacity cDNA retrotranscription kit (Applied Biosystems). RT-qPCR was performed using a Quantstudio 3 (Applied Biosystem) machine. Reactions were performed in a 10 µL volume, with 2 µM of each primer, 6 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystem), and 25 ng of cDNA. The amplification cycle consisted of an initial 5 min denaturation step at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of two-step amplification (95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s) and a melting cycle. Each reaction was performed in triplicate, and the relative expression of each gene was calculated with the ΔΔCt method, using β-actin as a reference gene. The primer sequences for β-actin and TNF-α were obtained from Zheng et al. [29] and listed in Table 1.

Imaging

Zebrafish images were obtained with a Zeiss Axiozoom V16 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with PlanNeoFluar Z 1.5X/0.37 FWD 30 mm lens, Zeiss Axiocam 506 mono camera, Zeiss Illuminator fluorescence microscope light source:HXP 200 C and connected to a workstation with Zen blue software. For imaging, the embryos/larvae were briefly anaesthetized with MS-222 0.16 mg/mL, then placed in the lateral position in a petri dish coated with 1.5% agarose. The processing of the images was performed with Zen blue and Image J FIJI software [30].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism software version 10.6.1 for Windows (GrapPad Software, Boston, MA USA, www.graphpad.com). Welch's t-test, One-way ANOVA/Kruskal–Wallis test with post hoc Dunn’s multiple comparison were done to test for significance (p < 0.05).

Results

Localized growth of M. abscessus in the swim bladder with minimal spread in the systemic circulation:

After swim bladder infection, the bacterial kinetics of zebrafish were tracked at various timepoints in the infected embryos. Our results showed localized growth of M. abscessus in the infection site (swim bladder) in a time dependent manner with minimal spread of bacteria into the systemic circulation. Bacterial burden in CFU count showed an initial two days of rapid and significant growth of 2 Log10 followed by slower growth of 0.3 Log10 by 4 dpi (Fig. 3A). The bacteria formed a biofilm-like growth along the epithelial lining of the swim bladder (Fig. 3B). There was a minimal spread of bacteria outside the infection site in the tail region (Fig. 3B, 4 dpi, lower panel). For comparison, bacterial kinetics in the caudal vein injection showed a predilection for the head region, with additional colony growth observed along the post-vent region (Fig. 3C). Thus, the swim bladder infection model can be utilized for evaluating localized biofilm-like growth of M. abscessus spatiotemporally. However, the infected embryos did not show any morphological defect or mortality at the end of experiment i.e. 5 dpi.

Migration of innate immune cells to the swim bladder infection site

Innate immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages have already developed in the zebrafish larvae by 5 dpf [31, 32]. In response to the localized M. abscessus growth, there was a recruitment of neutrophils (Fig. 4A) and macrophages (Fig. 4B) to the site of infection/bacterial growth with formation of granuloma-like structures in the swim bladder (Fig. 4A). Thus, localized swim bladder infection of zebrafish larvae can be utilized to study host–pathogen interactions.

Analysis of proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α

Studies performed in zebrafish have shown that systemic infection via caudal vein injection results in an increase in TNF-α in zebrafish embryos [33]. As infection with M. abscessus in the swim bladder resulted in the induction of an innate immune cell migration response, we also investigated whether there was a corresponding increase in TNF-α, one of the major proinflammatory cytokines. Our results showed a significant increase in TNF-α in infected larvae compared to uninfected controls (Fig. 5) demonstrating this localized M. abscessus swim bladder infection model can induce a host proinflammatory immune response.

Testing the efficacy of drugs

We assessed use of this model to test the effectiveness of antimycobacterial drugs. We selected two different classes of antibiotics differing in mechanism of action. Clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic, is a protein synthesis inhibitor known to be effective against M. abscessus. Omadacycline, a glycopeptide antibiotic that inhibits M. abscessus cell wall synthesis has recently been shown effective at treating pulmonary M. abscessus infection in a Phase 2 clinical trial [34]. Our results in Fig. 6 showed that clarithromycin decreased the mycobacterial burden by 0.62 Log10 (p < 0.05) while omadacycline reduced the bacterial load by 0.54 Log10 (p < 0.05) compared to sham treated controls after 4 days of treatment (Fig. 6B and C). These results show the potential utility of the swim bladder model in testing the efficacy of the antimycobacterial drug.

Discussion

In this study we showed that the early-stage zebrafish larvae swim bladder infection forms a localized biofilm like bacterial growth in the swim bladder epithelium which could be utilized for diverse purposes of investigating the virulence, host–pathogen interactions and in vivo drug screening of M. abscessus infection.

Previous studies demonstrated that Klebsiella pneumoniae-induced injury of the zebrafish swim bladder replicates a lung injury model, with neutrophil migration to the infection site and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [35]. Further, Zhang et al. reported that delivery of exogenous lipopolysaccharide, polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid, or silica nanoparticles into the swim bladder activated time- and dose-dependent neutrophil recruitment to the microinjection site and increased inflammatory cytokine mRNA levels. The bronchoalveolar lavage collected from pneumonia patients induced significant neutrophil recruitment compared to lavage from the control group [36]. Consistent with these studies, we also observed neutrophil recruitment and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α expression to the site of M. abscessus infection in addition to macrophage recruitment. Thus, our zebrafish swim bladder infection model closely resembles the zebrafish injury models, validating the zebrafish swim bladder as a tool to study pathogen-induced inflammatory reactions.

Previous work on M. abscessus aggregation showed the biofilm-like aggregates resulting in M. abscessus persistence of both rough (R) and smooth (S) morphotypes [37]. The zebrafish swim bladder infection model could be used for the comparative study of host immune responses of different strains and morphotypes of bacteria. The immune responses could be quantified either by measuring the fluorescence intensity or counting the number of immune cells in the swim bladder region.

Our study employed the immersion method of exposure for the drug efficacy studies of omadacycline and clarithromycin. In zebrafish, drug exposure could also be performed by the microinjection method in which the drug is injected directly into the zebrafish posterior cardinal vein [38]. The swim bladder sac also provides a localized site for direct microinjection of drugs as an alternative way for assessing drug efficacy of antimycobacterial compounds with varying water solubility. It also provides cost effectiveness for expensive compounds that would be economically not feasible to evaluate using traditional immersion-based exposure methods. The localized swim bladder infection model provides the added advantage of looking at the effectiveness of drug against biofilm-like bacterial growth in a localized space filled with air.

When compared to other in vivo M. abscessus infection models, the zebrafish/M. abscessus swim bladder infection model presents advantages and limitations that should be considered. As the M. abscessus primarily infects the human lung, this model simulates the complex interaction pattern between bacteria, immune cells and epithelial cells seen in the human respiratory tract. The zebrafish/M. abscessus swim bladder infection model allows the delivery of a very limited number of bacteria (around 200–300 CFUs per embryo), thus mirroring the initial stages of bacterial infection and biofilm formation seen with the human infection. The spatio-temporal kinetics of bacterial infection and its spread to various organ systems from the swim bladder can be studied using this model. However, like other embryonic zebrafish/M. abscessus infection models, the swim bladder model lasts for 10–12 days and cannot be employed for long-term, chronic infection studies mimicking the chronic M. abscessus pulmonary infection in humans. The short course treatment of 4 days in the zebrafish swim bladder infection model is inadequate to clear the infection, and it could be ineffective for evaluating the development of drug resistance. We have also used type strain (ATCC 19977) and clinical strain (MAB_062600_1635) in our study for different purposes. Our previous study has shown that both the type strain and clinical strain showed similar bacterial kinetics and virulence [39]. The zebrafish swim bladder model could potentially provide a rapid, inexpensive screen for novel treatment efficacy before taking testing into more time consuming and expensive mouse model experiments.

In summary, this zebrafish swim bladder M. abscessus infection model shares similarities to the human lung NTM infections and adds the advantages of highly reproducible infection burden, while facilitating the study of M. abscessus and innate immune system interaction dynamics at the mucosal level. Further, this model can be efficiently exploited to compare the antimycobacterial activity of different drugs and can be widely utilized to screen compounds improving outcomes of pulmonary NTM infections. In addition, this model is quick, inexpensive, easily scalable and can be done in a typical laboratory environment with minimal space requirement.
