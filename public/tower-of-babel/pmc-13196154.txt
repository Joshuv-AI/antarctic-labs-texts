Patient- and provider-level determinants of self-assessed health and well-being among adults with chronic conditions in Slovenian primary care
Authors: Nena Kopčavar Guček, Aleksander Stepanović, Nina Ružić Gorenjec, Zalika Klemenc-Ketiš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic diseases significantly impact patients’ quality of life and healthcare systems. In Slovenia, understanding the factors influencing self-assessed health and well-being can support more effective primary care policies. The purpose of this study was to identify patient- and provider-level factors associated with general health and well-being among Slovenian adults with chronic conditions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted as part of the OECD-led PaRIS initiative. Data were collected from 2133 patients aged ≥ 45 years with at least one chronic condition and 70 primary care providers. Well-being was measured via the WHO-5 Well-Being Index; general health was self-rated. Mixed-effects models were used to assess associations between well-being (linear mixed model for a continuous outcome) and general health (logistic mixed-effects model for a binary outcome) and sociodemographic, behavioural, and provider-level variables.ResultsGeneral health was rated as excellent, very good, or good by 62.5% of the patients. The mean well-being score was 60.9 (SD = 20.9). The key patient-level predictors of better health and well-being were higher income (medium vs. low OR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.26, 2.15]; high vs. low OR = 2.28, 95% CI [1.62, 3.21]), regular physical activity (OR = 1.98, 95% CI [1.57, 2.50]), age and BMI. The last two were nonlinearly associated with optimal well-being near a BMI of 25 and peaking at approximately 65–69 years of age. Male sex was associated only with greater well-being, whereas employment (OR = 2.41, 95% CI [1.67, 3.50]) and higher education (OR = 1.52, 95% CI [1.17, 1.97]) were only associated with better health. Among the provider-level factors, only the public health care setting was positively associated with well-being. Surprisingly, receiving written self-management instructions more often was linked to slightly lower well-being. Observed differences in wellbeing reached clinical relevance (10 points) for age, BMI, and regular physical activity (b = 10.46, 95% CI [8.48, 12.43]).ConclusionsPatient-level factors were strongly associated with self-assessed health and well-being among patients with chronic conditions, whereas provider-level organisational features showed limited associations. Care in public health care settings was associated with higher well-being. These findings support the need for age- and BMI-sensitive interventions, behaviour-focused care, and socially equitable policies in chronic disease management.Keywords: Patient-reported outcomes, WHO-5 Well-Being Index, Chronic disease, Primary care

Introduction

Chronic diseases represent a major and growing challenge for primary care systems [1], as they require long-term, coordinated management rather than episodic treatment. Beyond clinical control, people living with chronic conditions face persistent physical, psychological, and social demands that substantially shape their everyday functioning and quality of life. As a result, contemporary chronic care increasingly emphasises outcomes reported directly by patients, particularly self-rated health and well-being, as central indicators of care effectiveness [2].

In recent years, the well-being of patients with chronic illnesses has become a central concern within primary care settings, reflecting a broader shift toward integrated, patient-centred models of health delivery [3]. Chronic conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and autoimmune disorders often require long-term, multifaceted care that extends beyond symptom management [4]. As a result, primary care is increasingly seen not only as a gatekeeper for clinical services but also as a foundational platform for supporting patients’ overall well-being—physically, emotionally, and socially [5]. A growing body of evidence suggests that integrated, multidisciplinary care models in primary care settings improve both clinical outcomes and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for individuals with chronic illnesses. These models typically involve collaboration among general practitioners, nurses, care coordinators, and allied health professionals, along with digital support tools and patient education [6].

Chronic patients report significantly lower general health than does the broader population [7]. General health scores reflect not only physical status but also mental health, functional ability, and quality of life [8]. These scores can predict adverse outcomes and improve with targeted, patient-centred care. The routine use of these measures in primary care is valuable for monitoring, planning, and improving chronic disease management [9].

In chronic disease management, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly used to capture health outcomes beyond clinical indicators. Among these, self-rated general health and subjective well-being represent related but conceptually distinct constructs. Self-rated general health reflects a global appraisal of physical health and functional status and is a strong predictor of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilisation. In contrast, well-being primarily captures emotional and psychological functioning and how individuals experience their daily lives while living with chronic disease [10].

Although closely linked, these outcomes are not interchangeable. Patients may report acceptable general health while experiencing low well-being due to psychological distress or social burden or conversely maintain good well-being despite physical limitations through effective coping and self-management. Assessing both outcomes, therefore, provides a more comprehensive picture of patients’ lived experiences and allows identification of factors that may differentially influence physical and psychosocial dimensions of health [11].

This dual-outcome approach aligns with the principles of people-centred care and the OECD PaRIS conceptual framework [12], which positions patient-reported health outcomes and well-being as core indicators for evaluating the performance of primary care systems in chronic disease management.

In addition to individual characteristics, features of the primary care organisation may also influence patients’ well-being. Organisational factors such as continuity of care, accessibility, coordination, and support for self-management can shape patients’ sense of security, autonomy, and trust in the healthcare system [13]. These organisational features may be associated with patient-reported outcomes through mechanisms such as enhanced perceived support, improved continuity of care, reduced treatment burden, and more person-centred communication, all of which can shape patients’ experiences beyond clinical outcomes {Nkhoma, 2022 #1548}. Examining provider-level organisational characteristics alongside patient-level factors, therefore, allows a more comprehensive understanding of how primary care environments contribute to both health and well-being among people with chronic conditions [14].

The aim of this study was to identify patient-level and provider-level factors associated with self-rated general health and well-being among adults with chronic conditions in Slovenia. By explicitly examining both individual characteristics and primary care practice features, the study adopts a multilevel perspective aligned with contemporary people-centred chronic care models and the OECD PaRIS framework.

Methods

Type of study and settings

This cross-sectional observational study was part of the OECD-led international PaRIS initiative, which aims to standardize measures of health outcomes and patient experiences in primary care, particularly for those with chronic conditions. The study design and methods were developed collaboratively with partners, including NIVEL, Ipsos MORI, the University of Exeter, the Avedis Donabedian Institute, and OptiMedis AG. A nested sampling approach was used, selecting patients from within individual family medicine practices. The full details of the PaRIS protocol are published elsewhere [12].

This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies [15].

Participants

Providers

A total of 978 family medicine practices were identified from the publicly available registry of the Slovenian Health Insurance Institute and invited to participate; 81 responded. In the present paper, we limited ourselves to the 70 practices with at least one patient with chronic conditions. Practices were eligible if they held a valid licence to provide outpatient care to adult patients in a primary care setting. Those serving primarily institutional populations (e.g., nursing homes) or who did not regularly treat patients with chronic conditions were excluded [16].

Patients

Eligible patients were community-dwelling adults aged 45 years or older who had at least one recorded visit with their family physician or other practice staff in the past six months. Patients with severe cognitive or physical impairments that prevented participation (e.g., terminal illness or advanced dementia) were excluded [16]. Each participating practice prepared a list of eligible patients according to the inclusion criteria. From each list, researchers randomly sampled 300 patients.

Our sample consisted of 2133 respondents with at least one chronic condition, with the number of chronic patients per practice ranging from 2 to 66, a median of 26, and an interquartile range (IQR) of 17-42.25.

Tools

Data were gathered via two main instruments: the Primary Care Practice Questionnaire (PCPQ) [17] and the Primary Care Service User Questionnaire (PCUQ) [18]. Both were based on the PaRIS conceptual framework, which outlines key elements of primary and chronic care. In this paper, we investigate two outcome measures, self-assessed well-being and general health, described in detail below.

Self-assessed well-being was measured using the WHO-5 Well-Being Index, a validated instrument developed by the World Health Organization [19]. The WHO-5 Well-Being Index consists of five positively worded items assessing emotional well-being over the previous 2 weeks, rated on a 6-point Likert scale from 0 (“at no time”) to 5 (“all of the time”). Raw scores range from 0 to 25 and were multiplied by four to yield a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better well-being. A score below 50 is commonly used to indicate poor well-being, while lower thresholds (e.g., < 28) have been applied for screening for depression. The threshold for a clinically relevant change was 10 points on the WHO-5 Well-Being Index [20]. The WHO-5 Well-Being Index’s well-being score was analysed as a continuous outcome.

General health was measured by a single self-rated question: “In general, how would you rate your health: excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” This is the most widely used global health measure [21]. For the final outcome, this item was coded binary: excellent/very good/good vs. fair/poor. This categorisation is commonly used in epidemiological and primary care research to enhance interpretability and clinical relevance, particularly when modelling associations rather than estimating fine-grained health gradients. Previous studies have shown that this dichotomisation retains strong predictive validity for morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilisation, while reducing model complexity and facilitating cross-study comparisons [22].

We examined the following set of patient-level and provider-level explanatory variables, selected in advance based on background knowledge. The same set of explanatory variables was considered for both outcome measures in the multivariable models.

Patient-level explanatory variables

The following patient characteristics were included: age (the recorded five-year age groups were treated as numerical), sex (male/female), body mass index (BMI, numerical variable), regular physical activity (yes/no), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), level of education (higher/lower), employment status (yes/no), place of residence (rural/urban), living alone (yes/no), and gross income level (low/medium/high). Additionally, we assessed whether patients had discussed any of the following four health behaviour topics with a healthcare professional in the past 12 months: physical activity, healthy eating, smoking, or alcohol use. For the purposes of this analysis, these items were combined into a single binary variable (“talked”: yes/no), indicating whether any lifestyle-related counselling had occurred. This aggregation was chosen to capture overall engagement in behaviour-related counselling, to enable a large sample size in the multivariable models (questions on counselling about smoking and alcohol had many missing values because they were posed only to smokers and alcohol users), and to keep the number of variables in the models lower.

The provider-level variables included the practice location (rural/urban), practice ownership (public/private with a contract with the national health insurance system), and five indicators of care organization and coordination:

A review of patient data was considered “Yes” if the practice routinely reviewed at least one of the following indicators: clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction or care experience, hospital admissions or emergency department visits, preventive care coverage, or medication adherence.

Data collection

Family medicine practices were invited to participate via postal mail, which included comprehensive study information, a consent form, and detailed patient sampling instructions. A member of the research team subsequently contacted each practice by telephone to confirm their willingness to participate. Practices that provided written consent were granted access to an electronic questionnaire and assigned unique identification codes to ensure anonymization and facilitate data linkage.

Patients were approached through their preferred communication channel: survey links were distributed via email or SMS, and paper questionnaires were mailed when requested. Non-respondents received up to two follow-up reminders through the same communication method used for the initial contact. Each questionnaire was uniquely coded to maintain participant anonymity and to enable linkage with corresponding practice data. Participants returned signed consent forms and, where applicable, completed paper questionnaires in prepaid return envelopes. Reminders were dispatched approximately two to three weeks following the initial invitation.

Data analysis

Categorical variables were summarized by frequencies and percentages, and numerical variables were summarized by means and standard deviations, or by medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for asymmetrical distributions. The number of missing values was reported. In the multivariable models, complete case analysis was used.

Sampling weights provided by the PaRIS initiative were not applied in the analyses. As the objective of the study was to assess associations between patient-level and provider-level characteristics and self-rated health and well-being, rather than to generate population-representative estimates, unweighted multilevel models were considered appropriate. In addition, PaRIS weights are primarily intended for descriptive cross-national analyses and are not routinely applied in multilevel regression settings.

The linear mixed-effects model was fitted for well-being, and the logistic mixed-effects model for general health. In both models, we used the selected explanatory variables as fixed effects and random provider-level intercepts, accounting for patients’ provider grouping (allowing different intercepts for different providers).

The explanatory variables for the fixed effects were chosen in advance based on background knowledge, whereas the functional form was based on goodness-of-fit analysis. Specifically, we checked if numerical variables needed nonlinear effects using the available goodness-of-fit analysis [23] for the linear mixed-effects model, and checked whether the non-linear part of the effect is statistically significant for the logistic mixed-effects model. The non-linear associations observed for age and BMI coincided with prior evidence. They were modelled using restricted cubic splines (RCSs). In advance, we choose RCSs with four knots because they usually provide sufficient flexibility, and we also check RCSs with three and five knots. The final choice for the number of knots was based on goodness-of-fit analysis and graphical inspection of the nonlinear effects. In the final models, all RCSs had four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles (the default choice for knot positions in the used package rms [24]). Additionally, using goodness-of-fit [23], we checked that the part of the model with categorical fixed effects is correctly specified. Moreover, we checked the normality assumptions of the residuals for the final model reported. The goodness-of-fit analysis [23] for the final well-being model is reported in the results. To assess the model’s convergence for general health, the final model was refitted with all five available optimizers using the allFit function from the lme4 package [25] (reported in the results). Although the intraclass-correlation coefficient (ICC) was < 0.01 for both models (suggesting that random effects may not be necessary), mixed-effects models were retained to appropriately account for the hierarchical structure of the data (patients nested within providers).

A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were carried out with R statistical software, version 4.4.1 [26].

Self-assessed general health

General health was assessed as excellent/very good/good by 1332 (62.5%, n = 2131) participants. The following patient-level factors were significantly associated with good general health: age (non-linear association), higher education (OR = 1.52, 95% CI [1.17, 1.97]), employment (OR = 2.41, 95% CI [1.67, 3.50]), higher income (medium vs. low OR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.26, 2.15]; high vs. low OR = 2.28, 95% CI [1.62, 3.21]), exercise (OR = 1.98, 95% CI [1.57, 2.50]), alcohol use (OR = 1.33, 95% CI [1.03, 1.71]), and lower BMI (OR = 0.98, 95% CI [0.95, 1.00]). No provider-level variables reached statistical significance (Table 3). Among significant factors, exercise and employment had the strongest association with general health, with ORs around 2 and whole 95% CIs for OR above 1.5. Next, income and education had ORs above 1.5. Alcohol use was weakly associated with general health, with OR 1.33 and lower bound of 95% CI for OR at 1.03. The association between BMI and general health was very weak as a patient with 3 points lower BMI had 6% increase in odds for good general health (OR = 1.06).

Self-assessed well-being

The mean well-being index score was 60.9 with a standard deviation of 20.9 points. The following patient-level factors were significantly associated with better well-being: BMI (non-linear association), age (non-linear association), male sex (b = 2.69, 95% CI [0.58, 4.80]), exercise (b = 10.46, 95% CI [8.48, 12.43]), and higher income (medium vs. low b = 3.95, 95% CI [1.60, 6.30]; high vs. low b = 4.68, 95% CI [1.83, 7.53]) (Table 4). Exercises reached clinical relevance in the sample, but could not be generalized to the population. Differences with respect to sex and income were not clinically relevant. Well-being improved when BMI increased from underweight to approximately 25. Then, it steeply decreased from approximately 25 to approximately 30. After 30, it continued to decrease, but less steeply (Fig. 2, left). Well-being was similar in the first two age categories (i.e., age span 45–54). Then, it increased until the age category of 65–69 years, and it decreased from 70 to 74 years onwards (Fig. 2, right). Observed differences in BMI and age were clinically meaningful.

At the provider level, most structural and procedural characteristics were not significantly associated with patients’ well-being. However, receiving care in a public health care setting was linked to greater well-being (b = 3.48, 95% CI [0.53, 6.43]), whereas the more frequent provision of written self-management instructions was associated with slightly lower well-being (b=-1.74, 95% CI [-3.30, -0.17]) (Table 4), but neither of the two associations was clinically relevant.

Discussion

Main findings

Our study revealed that chronic patients in Slovenia reported moderate levels of well-being and general health. Self-rated health and well-being are shaped more by personal and lifestyle factors than by provider-level characteristics. Importantly, not all statistically significant associations were clinically relevant. Only differences in physical activity, age, and BMI exceeded the 10-point threshold of the WHO-5 Well-Being Index, suggesting that these factors are likely to have meaningful implications for patients’ well-being in clinical practice.

The Slovene population reports slightly poorer self-rated health but comparable well-being relative to OECD averages [27]. The mean well-being score was notably above the commonly used clinical threshold of 50, which signals possible poor well-being or risk of depression [19]. However, the relatively wide standard deviation reflects considerable heterogeneity within the population, suggesting that while many patients report acceptable well-being, a substantial proportion are likely to experience significant distress.

Age and exercise emerged as clinically relevant predictors of both health and well-being. On the other hand, BMI emerged as clinically relevant only for well-being, whereas income was not. Healthcare system characteristics had a limited association, although public health care settings were linked to better well-being, potentially through greater accessibility or continuity of care.

Comparison with other studies

The multivariable models revealed several patient-level factors significantly associated with well-being and general health. BMI demonstrated a nonlinear association, consistent with prior findings that both low and high BMI values can be associated with lower well-being, possibly due to health complications or psychosocial factors [28, 29]. Similarly, age was also found to have a nonlinear effect, with well-being increasing into midlife and then declining with advancing age. This pattern has been reported in population-level studies, suggesting that midlife may represent a peak in psychological resources or coping capacity, whereas older age is often associated with multimorbidity, functional decline, and social losses [30, 31].

No statistically significant sex differences in well-being were observed in our sample, which may reflect sample characteristics or limited statistical power. This finding contrasts with broader epidemiological data suggesting that women are more likely to report lower well-being and higher rates of affective disorders, particularly in populations with chronic illness [32]. Among behavioural factors, physical exercise is one of the strongest positive predictors of well-being and general health, underscoring its known benefits for mental health, especially among those managing chronic conditions [33].

Income showed a clear and significant association with general health, with both medium and high incomes associated with better outcomes. This reflects well-established socioeconomic patterns in health, where financial security contributes to access to care, lower stress, and better health behaviours—all of which are associated with improved general health [34, 35].

At the provider level, most structural characteristics—including care planning and data sharing—were not significantly associated with well-being, suggesting that these processes alone may not be directly associated with patients’ emotional states. The unexpected negative association between the frequency of written self-management instructions and well-being should be interpreted with caution. One possible interpretation is reverse causality [36], whereby patients with poorer well-being or more complex disease trajectories are more likely to receive written instructions as part of intensified care. In this context, written materials may serve as markers of greater disease burden rather than as independent determinants of well-being. Additionally, the effectiveness of written self-management instructions may depend on patients’ health literacy, cognitive capacity, and readiness to engage in self-care [37]. For some individuals, particularly those with lower health literacy or multiple comorbidities, written instructions may increase perceived treatment burden or anxiety rather than provide reassurance. Importantly, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes conclusions about directionality or causality, and future longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these mechanisms.

Most provider-level organisational characteristics were not significantly associated with patients’ self-rated health or well-being. These findings may reflect several non-mutually exclusive explanations. First, organisational features such as care plans, data review, or information exchange may primarily be associated with clinical processes and long-term outcomes, with less immediate associations with subjective well-being [38]. Second, the relatively homogeneous organisation of Slovenian primary care [39] —characterised by strong public provision, universal coverage, and standardised practice structures—may limit variability and reduce the ability to detect associations at the provider level. Finally, the available indicators may not fully capture relational or experiential aspects of care, such as continuity, trust, or communication quality, which are more directly linked to patient-reported outcomes [40].

The findings of this study are consistent with early results from the OECD PaRIS initiative, which indicated that patient-level characteristics and social determinants tended to explain a larger share of variation in patient-reported outcomes than organisational features of primary care practices. While cross-country comparisons were beyond the scope of this analysis, the Slovenian results align with the broader PaRIS narrative, emphasising the central role of socioeconomic factors, physical activity, and subjective health perceptions in shaping well-being among people living with chronic conditions [41].

Practice- and policy-level implications

Patient-level factors—particularly physical activity, income, age, and BMI—were consistently associated with both self-rated health and well-being. These findings support the importance of strengthening behaviour-focused and socially informed approaches within primary care.

In particular, the magnitude of the association between physical activity and well-being exceeded the threshold for clinical relevance, suggesting that even modest increases in activity may be meaningfully associated with improvements in patients’ perceived well-being. This supports the integration of structured physical activity counselling, brief interventions, and referral to community-based programmes into routine primary care for people living with chronic conditions.

The non-linear associations observed for age and BMI further indicate that interventions should be tailored rather than uniform. Older patients—particularly beyond the age of 70—may benefit from targeted strategies addressing functional decline, multimorbidity-related burden, and social vulnerability. Similarly, the BMI pattern suggests that both underweight and obesity are associated with lower well-being, supporting a balanced, patient-centred approach to weight management that considers both physical and psychosocial dimensions.

At the public health level, these findings reinforce the potential importance of population-wide strategies that promote physical activity and healthy weight, particularly among individuals with chronic conditions. They also highlight the importance of addressing socioeconomic inequalities, as income was consistently associated with outcomes, even if it did not reach clinical relevance for well-being.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Participation of primary care practices was voluntary, which may have introduced selection bias if participating practices differed systematically from non-participating ones in terms of organisation, resources, or engagement with quality improvement. Similarly, patient participation relied on self-selection, potentially favouring individuals who were more engaged with their care or more motivated to respond to health surveys. In addition, several behavioural variables, including physical activity, smoking, and alcohol use, were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Although the number of chronic conditions was available, it was not included in the multivariable models because it did not capture key dimensions of disease burden, such as severity, duration, or functional impact. As a result, it may not adequately reflect the complexity of multimorbidity, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Also, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference.

A complete-case analysis was used in the multivariable models. This approach may introduce bias if missing data are not completely at random, potentially affecting the representativeness of the analysed sample.

The findings should be interpreted with caution, given potential measurement limitations, including reliance on self-reported data and the use of simplified indicators for complex constructs such as organisational characteristics and multimorbidity. However, the consistency of key associations across outcomes suggests that these biases are unlikely to fully explain the observed results.

Conclusion

This study adds to the literature on patient-reported outcomes in chronic care by demonstrating that self-rated health and well-being, while related, capture distinct dimensions of patients’ experiences in primary care. A key contribution lies in identifying nonlinear associations among age, body mass index, and both outcomes, highlighting that well-being and perceived health do not simply decline with higher age or greater BMI among people with chronic conditions. These findings refine existing knowledge by showing that midlife and moderate body weight represent periods of relative advantage, with implications for more targeted and nuanced interventions.

By adopting a multilevel perspective, the study also shows that patient-level and socioeconomic factors explain substantially more variation in patient-reported outcomes than provider-level organisational characteristics in the Slovenian primary care context. Together, these insights support a shift toward people-centred chronic care strategies that prioritise behavioural, social, and contextual determinants of health, and underscore the value of integrating patient-reported outcomes into policy and practice evaluation frameworks such as the OECD PaRIS initiative.
