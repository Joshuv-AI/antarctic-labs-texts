Formula-One Prompting: Adaptive Reasoning Through Equations For Applied Mathematics
Natapong Nitarach, Pittawat Taveekitworachai, Kunat Pipatanakul

Abstract
Prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Program-of-Thought (PoT) improve LLM mathematical reasoning by structuring intermediate steps in natural language or code. However, applied mathematics problems in domains like finance, physics, and cryptography often require recalling or deriving governing equations, a step that current approaches do not explicitly leverage. We propose Formula-One Prompting (F-1), a two-phase approach that uses mathematical equations as an intermediate representation before adaptive solving. F-1 first formulates governing equations from problem descriptions, then selects a solving strategy among CoT, PoT, or direct computation based on the generated equations, all within a single LLM call. Results across five models and four benchmarks show F-1 outperforms CoT by +5.76% and PoT by +8.42% on average. Crucially, gains are largest in applied domains: +13.30% on FinanceMath over CoT, and within OlympiadBench, larger gains on physics (+2.55%) than pure math (+0.44%). This demonstrates that F-1 is more effective than CoT in applied mathematics problems.

3
Formula-One Prompting

Figure 1:
Comparison of prompting methods for mathematical reasoning. Traditional methods (Zero-shot, CoT, PoT) proceed directly to solving. F-1 adds an equation formalization phase before adaptively selecting the solving strategy, all within a single call.

Given a mathematical problem
P
, F-1 produces a solution through a single LLM generation:

P\;\xrightarrow[\text{Phase 1}]{\textit{formalize}}\;E\;\xrightarrow[\text{Phase 2}]{\textit{solve}}\;A

where
P
is the problem,
E
represents the formalized equations, and
A
is the final answer. The key insight is that equation structure (closed-form vs. iterative, symbolic vs. numerical) guides strategy selection: Direct calculation for simple substitutions, CoT for multi-step derivations, or PoT for numerical computation. Crucially, both phases occur within a
single call
, not as a multi-stage pipeline.

The equation-first design provides two benefits: (1) forcing identification of mathematical structure before committing to a solving strategy, and (2) leveraging equation structure as a natural signal for strategy selection. Ablation analysis (Section
6
) confirms formalization combined with adaptive selection contributes more than selection alone.

3.1
Phase 1: Problem Formalization

The formalization phase extracts governing equations from the problem by performing the following steps, in any order:

Identify Givens

Extract all explicitly stated values, conditions, and constraints. For example, in “A bank offers 5% annual interest compounded monthly,” the givens include (1) the rate (5%), (2) compounding frequency (monthly), and (3) any principal or time mentioned.

Identify Targets

Determine what the problem asks to find or prove, clarifying the goal before attempting any solution strategy.

Formulate Equations

Express the mathematical relationships connecting givens to targets, forcing the model to understand the underlying structure before committing to a solution approach.

3.2
Phase 2: Adaptive Solving

Based on the formalized equations, F-1 instructs the model to select among three solving strategies: Direct calculation, CoT, or PoT. These represent three fundamental modes of mathematical problem-solving: algebraic substitution, symbolic reasoning, and numerical computation. The prompt does not encode explicit rules; instead, F-1 relies on internal knowledge of the model to match equation characteristics to strategies.

3.3
Prompt Design

F-1 uses a
unified prompt
that instructs the model to perform both phases in a single generation:

System Prompt (Unified Core)

You are an AI assistant that solves problems mainly through equations.

User Prompt Template

{problem}

Phase 1 (Formalization):

Identify givens and target. Write the key equations.

Phase 2 (Adaptive Solving):

Solve via CoT (think step-by-step), PoT (compute with code), direct calculation, or elimination.

Verify your solution, then put your final answer in \boxed{}.

The model receives this prompt once and produces formalization, strategy selection, and solution in a single response. The verification step serves as a self-check, encouraging the model to validate its answer against the formalized equations before committing to a final response. 7
Discussion

Training Data Composition

We speculate that F-1’s stronger performance on applied domains may reflect training data composition. High-quality applied mathematics content (textbooks, scientific papers) naturally presents problems through explicit equations
(
12
;
7
)
, while synthetic CoT datasets for competition mathematics are more prevalent
(
15
)
. F-1’s formalization-first approach may thus better align with how expert-authored applied content structures mathematical reasoning. Testing this hypothesis through controlled data ablations and broader evaluations is left to future work and is out of scope for this study.

Computational Efficiency

F-1 maintains single-call efficiency while achieving the highest efficiency ratio (1.51 on average), outperforming Zero-Shot (+25.8%), CoT (+19.8%), and PoT (+21.8%). The overhead of F-1 is minimal, requiring only +68 additional input tokens and -212 total tokens (
Appendix

B
) compared to Zero-Shot, while F-1 achieves comparable or better accuracy than other single-call methods. This single-call design makes F-1 well suited for production systems where efficiency, reliability, and scalability are essential.

8
Conclusion

We presented F-1, an equation-first prompting technique that uses mathematical equations as intermediate representation for adaptive solving. Experiments across four benchmarks and five models show F-1 outperforms CoT by +5.76% and PoT by +8.42% on average, with largest gains on applied domains (+13.30% on FinanceMath). Our ablation confirms equation formalization is the primary driver, and strategy selection analysis shows F-1 achieves 73% accuracy on applied domains, reaching 81-84% of the upper bound. Future work includes domain-specific templates and combining F-1 with multi-call approaches.

Limitations

Our evaluation spans models from 30B parameters to frontier scale. While models such as Qwen3-30B consistently benefit from F-1, we do not include smaller models (e.g., 7B or 13B). The equation formalization phase requires reliable symbolic abstraction and structured reasoning, which may not be well supported by smaller models. Studying how F-1 performs with model scale, or how it could be simplified to accommodate smaller models, is a natural direction for future work. In addition, we evaluate a limited set of model families; extending F-1 to a broader range of architectures and training paradigms is left for future investigation.

F-1 relies on the model’s ability to extract governing equations and reason about their structure. Its effectiveness therefore likely depends on specific model capabilities, such as symbolic manipulation and mathematical abstraction. A detailed analysis of the relationship between these abilities and F-1 performance is beyond the scope of this work, but would provide valuable insight into when and why F-1 succeeds or fails.

Some benchmarks in our evaluation are necessarily small due to data scarcity. For instance, AICrypto contains only 18 problems, reflecting the limited availability of formalized cryptographic proof benchmarks
(
28
;
19
)
, and OlympiadBench TP_physics includes 25 problems. While our main conclusions are supported by larger subsets–OlympiadBench OE (n=910), FinanceMath (n=200), and IMO-Bench (n=460)–constructing larger and more diverse benchmarks for equation-driven reasoning remains an important direction for future work.

Our study focuses on mathematical reasoning in physics, finance, and cryptography, where governing equations provide a natural intermediate representation. Generalizing F-1 beyond equation-centric settings–for example to broader scientific reasoning or legal analysis–would likely require different forms of formalization, and we leave this direction to future work. We also do not evaluate F-1 on multiple-choice or simple arithmetic benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K), where equation formalization may be unnecessary or yield limited gains, and thus consider it out of scope for this paper.

F-1 is designed as a single-call, two-phase prompting method. Unlike more complex approaches such as ToT or XoT
(
26
;
13
)
, it does not explicitly backtrack or explore alternative strategies when the initial choice is suboptimal. This is a deliberate design choice that prioritizes simplicity and computational efficiency. Extending F-1 with multi-call mechanisms–such as backtracking, repeated sampling, or other test-time scaling techniques–represents a natural direction for future work and may further improve robustness.

Ethics Statement

This work introduces a new prompting technique aimed at improving mathematical reasoning in LLMs. We do not foresee direct negative societal impacts arising from the method itself. However, enhancing applied mathematical reasoning capabilities may increase the risk of misuse, such as automated cheating or unfair assistance in educational contexts. We therefore encourage responsible deployment, including the use of appropriate safeguards and policies when applying such systems in learning and assessment settings.

More broadly, novel prompting techniques can elicit new and sometimes unexpected behaviors from LLMs. Due to the stochastic nature of these models, such behaviors may be difficult to fully predict or control. While we observe no explicit harmful behavior in our experiments, users and practitioners should remain aware of this inherent uncertainty when deploying advanced prompting methods in real-world applications.

A.5
F-1 Design Principles

The F-1 prompting strategy is built on three core principles:

1.

Equation-First Formalization
: Problems are first translated into mathematical equations before any solving attempts. This mirrors expert problem-solving behavior and reduces ambiguity.

2.

Adaptive Solving
: After formalization, the model selects the most appropriate solving method (CoT, PoT, or direct calculation) based on problem characteristics. This flexibility allows F-1 to leverage the strengths of each approach.

3.

Verification Phase
: Solutions are checked against the original equations, providing a built-in error-detection mechanism that catches computational and logical errors.

D.1
Example 1: Lorentz Transformation (OlympiadBench Physics)

Problem

Show that
\frac{\partial X}{\partial x}
is independent of
x
and
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
is independent of
t
, given the homogeneity of space and time (translation invariance).

Gold Answer:
Independence (partial derivatives depend only on
v
, not on
x
or
t
).

Zero-Shot Response
(Judgment: Partial | Score: 7/10)

By homogeneity (translation invariance):

Spatial homogeneity:
For any shift
a
,
X(x+a,t,v)
must relate frames the same way as
X(x,t,v)
, up to the corresponding shift of the primed origin. This implies
X(x+a,t,v)-X(a,0,v)=X(x,t,v)
. Differentiate with respect to
a
at
a=0
to get
\partial X/\partial x(x,t,v)=\partial X/\partial x(0,0,v)
, i.e.,
\partial X/\partial x
is independent of
x
. Equivalently,
\partial/\partial x(\partial X/\partial x)=0
.

Temporal homogeneity:
For any shift
b
,
T(x,t+b,v)-T(0,b,v)=T(x,t,v)
. Differentiate with respect to
b
at
b=0
to get
\partial T/\partial t(x,t,v)=\partial T/\partial t(0,0,v)
, i.e.,
\partial T/\partial t
is independent of
t
. Equivalently,
\partial/\partial t(\partial T/\partial t)=0
.

Thus, the numerical value for the derivative of these partial derivatives with respect to their respective variables is zero.

So the final answer is \boxed{0}.

Zero-Shot Judge Feedback

Feedback:
Your use of homogeneity as translation invariance and differentiating with respect to the translation parameter is exactly the right idea and correctly shows that
\partial X/\partial x
and
\partial T/\partial t
are independent of
x
and
t
. The only issue is the final boxed answer: what is constant is these partial derivatives (they can depend on
v
but not on
x
or
t
), not that they themselves must equal zero. It would be better to conclude explicitly that
\partial X/\partial x(x,t,v)=\text{constant}(v)
and
\partial T/\partial t(x,t,v)=\text{constant}(v)
, rather than writing the answer as 0.

Scores:
Correctness: 7/10 | Reasoning: 8/10 | Completeness: 7/10

and
\partial T/\partial t
is independent of

.

2) Given and to find:

and
