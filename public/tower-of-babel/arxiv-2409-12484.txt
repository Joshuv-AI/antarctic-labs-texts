Abstract
. A basic fact taught in undergraduate algebra courses is that every finite nilpotent group is a direct product of p p -groups. Already Bruck [ 5 ] observed that this does not generalize to loops. In particular, there exist nilpotent loops of size 6 6 which are not direct products of loops of size 2 2 and 3 3 . Still we show that every finite nilpotent loop ( A , ⋅ ) (A,\cdot) has a binary term operation ∗ * such that ( A , ∗ ) (A,*) is a direct product of nilpotent loops of prime power order, i.e., ( A , ∗ ) (A,*) is supernilpotent. As an application we obtain that every nilpotent loop of order p ​ q pq for primes p , q p,q has a finite basis for its equational theory.

Background and results

We start by reviewing some concepts and notation of general algebra that we will need.
An
algebra

{\mathbf{A}}:=(A,F)
is a structure with universe
A
and a list of basic operations
F
on
A
.
For
k\geq 1
, an operation

is a
term operation
of
{\mathbf{A}}
if
g
is a composition of
basic operations and projections of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
More generally,
g\colon A^{k}\to A
is a
polynomial operation
of
{\mathbf{A}}
if
g
is a composition of
basic operations, projections and constants of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
An algebra
(A,G)
is a
reduct
of
{\mathbf{A}}
if every operation in
G
is a term operation of
{\mathbf{A}}
;
it is a
polynomial reduct
of
{\mathbf{A}}
if every operation in
G
is a polynomial operation of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Two algebras on the same universe are

if they have the same term functions.

A

is an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot,\backslash,/,1)
with binary operations
\cdot,\backslash,/
and a constant

1
satisfying

x\backslash(xy)=y\quad x(x\backslash y)=y\quad(yx)/x=y\quad(y/x)x=y\quad x\cdot 1=1\cdot x=x.

The left division
\backslash
and right division
/
are uniquely determined by the multiplication

\cdot
by these identities.
Hence equivalently a loop can be considered as a groupoid
(A,\cdot)
with identity
1
such that for all

a,b\in A
the equations
ax=b
and
ya=b
have unique solutions
x,y\in A
.
So for
a\in A
, the left and right translations

L_{a}\colon A\to A,x\mapsto ax,\quad R_{a}\colon A\to A,x\mapsto xa,

are bijections. They generate the
multiplication group

\mathrm{Mlt}({\mathbf{A}})
of
{\mathbf{A}}
, that is,

\mathrm{Mlt}({\mathbf{A}}):=\langle L_{a},R_{a}\ :\ a\in A\rangle.

In fact finite loops
(A,\cdot,\backslash,/,1)
are always term equivalent to their reduct
(A,\cdot)
,
which is why we will not distinguish between the two representations in the following.
Groups can be viewed as loops with associative multiplication.

An algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is a

if it has a ternary

operation

,
that is,

m(x,y,y)=x=m(y,y,x)
for all

.
In particular, a group
(A,\cdot,^{-1},1)
has a Mal’cev term
m(x,y,z):=xy^{-1}z
.
A loop
(A,\cdot,\backslash,/,1)

has Mal’cev terms
(x/y)z
and
x(y\backslash z)
.

We refer to
[
10
]
for the definition and basic properties of the (term condition) commutator
[\alpha,\beta]

of congruences
\alpha,\beta
for an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
. This generalizes the classical commutator of normal subgroups in
groups and allows us, for instance, to talk about abelian, nilpotent and solvable algebras.
Let
0_{A},1_{A}
be the trivial congruence (equality) and the total congruence on
{\mathbf{A}}
, respectively.
Then
{\mathbf{A}}
is

nilpotent
if there exist a series of congruences

1_{A}=\alpha_{0}\geq\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{k}=0_{A}

on
{\mathbf{A}}
such that

[\alpha_{i},1_{A}]\leq\alpha_{i+1}\text{ for all }i<k.

As for groups we call such a series of congruences

.
We call
{\mathbf{A}}

nilpotent
if it is
k
-nilpotent for some
k\geq 1
;
we call
{\mathbf{A}}

abelian
if it is
1
-nilpotent.

This notion specializes to classical nilpotence for groups (see the discussion in
[
10
]
)
and to central nilpotence for loops in the sense of Bruck
[
5
]

(see Stanovský and Vojtěchovský
[

]
for a proof).

Generalizing the binary commutator mentioned above, Bulatov has proposed

in
[

]
,
which Aichinger and Mudrinski have used to define another notion of nilpotence in
[

]
.
We refer to their paper and to Moorhead’s

17
]
for the definition and basic properties of higher commutators.
An algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is

supernilpotent
if the
k+1
-ary higher commutator of the total congruence

on
{\mathbf{A}}

is the trivial congruence

, i.e.,

[\underbrace{1_{A},\dots,1_{A}}_{k+1\text{ times}}]=0_{A}.

We call
{\mathbf{A}}

supernilpotent
if it is
k
-supernilpotent for some
k\geq 1
.
By definition
1
-supernilpotent,
1
-nilpotent and abelian are equivalent for every algebra.

For groups nilpotence and supernilpotence turn out to be the same but in general they are different.
A finite Mal’cev algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
of finite type is supernilpotent iff it is a direct product of nilpotent algebras
of prime power cardinality
[
1
]
. In particular a finite loop is supernilpotent iff it is a direct
product of nilpotent loops of prime power order.
Furthermore for all Mal’cev algebras
{\mathbf{A}}
and all

the following are equivalent:

(1)

{\mathbf{A}}
is
k
-supernilpotent;

(2)

{\mathbf{A}}
is
k
-nilpotent and all
commutator terms
on
{\mathbf{A}}
,
i.e., term functions satisfying

.

Aichinger and Mudrinski prove this equivalence for commutator polynomials
in

on a nilpotent algebra all commutator polynomials have essential arity at most

iff all
commutator terms have essential arity at most

. The facts needed are:

•

Every polynomial

on

is of the form

and constants

•

Every term function

(note that although this result states the assumption that

Long before the notion of (higher) commutators of congruences existed, Bruck

observed that

He also showed that for any prime

-power order

Wright

generalized this to the result that a ﬁnite loop

is nilpotent.
Recently Seminanišiniová and Stanovský showed that if a loop is

-nilpotent

For infinite loops the converse is still open.

Unfortunately the relation between nilpotence and supernilpotence for algebras that are not Mal’cev

but Moore and Moorhead gave an example of an infinite supernilpotent algebra that is not nilpotent,

We can now state our main result in a concise way.

Theorem 1.1
.

Every finite nilpotent loop has a supernilpotent loop reduct.

Theorem
1.1
is proved in Section
2
.
It gives a positive answer to
[
14
, Question 1.5]
in the case of loops.
We discuss some applications to equational theories of nilpotent loops in Section
3
.
In Section
4
we show that Theorem
1.1
can neither be extended to arbitrary
loops nor sharpened to always obtain an abelian reduct.

Every nilpotent loop of order a prime or

is in fact an abelian group
(however for any odd prime
p

that are not groups
[
8
]
).
Hence Theorem
1.1
yields in particular that every nilpotent loop
of order

Conversely every finite nilpotent loop can be obtained by expanding a supernilpotent loop by some binary
operation.

A subloop

of a loop

is

if for all

(ab)N=a(bN)=a(Nb).

If

, then the set of left cosets

forms a quotient loop
{\mathbf{A}}/N
with multiplication

(aN)\cdot(bN):=(ab)N

.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot)
be a finite nilpotent loop with normal subloop

such that

is supernilpotent.
Then
{\mathbf{A}}
is term equivalent to an algebra

such that

is a supernilpotent loop with
(A,*)/N={\mathbf{A}}/N

and

is a binary operation

.

Corollary

is also proved in Section

.

Freese and McKenzie observed that every nilpotent Mal’cev algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
has polynomial functions

such that

is a nilpotent loop (see the discussion after Corollary 7.7 in
[

]
).
Hence Theorem

immediately yields the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Every finite nilpotent Mal’cev algebra has a supernilpotent Mal’cev algebra as polynomial reduct.

The following questions remain open:

(1)

Is finiteness necessary for Theorem

(2)

Can Corollary

2.
Proofs of the main results

Let
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot,\backslash,/,1)
be a loop, let
\alpha
be a congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Then
\alpha
is uniquely determined by the normal subloop
N:=1/\alpha
(the class of
1
) of
{\mathbf{A}}
,
more precisely

\alpha=\{(x,y)\in A^{2}\ :\ x/y\in N\}=:\ \equiv_{N}.

Conversely, every normal subloop
N
of
{\mathbf{A}}
induces a congruence
\equiv_{N}
as above whose classes are just
the left (or equivalently right) cosets of
N
in
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Because of this correspondence, it is customary in loop theory to denote quotients
{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha
by
{\mathbf{A}}/N

for the corresponding normal subloop
N
. It also allows us to translate commutators of congruences
\alpha,\beta
of

{\mathbf{A}}
into commutators of normal subloops via

[1/\alpha,1/\beta]:=1/[\alpha,\beta].

So
{\mathbf{A}}
is nilpotent iff it has a central series of normal subloops

A=C_{0}\geq C_{1}\geq\dots\geq C_{k}=1

such that
[C_{i},A]\leq C_{i+1}
for
i<k
.
It is not hard to see that whenever such a central series exists for some finite
{\mathbf{A}}
,
it can be refined to one where all consecutive quotients
C_{i}/C_{i+1}
have prime power order.

Our main result will follow readily from the next lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite nilpotent loop with a central series of normal subloops

A=C_{0}>C_{1}>\dots>C_{k}=1

and factors
C_{i}/C_{i+1}
of prime power order for
i<k
.

Then for every
i\leq k
there exists a binary term operation
*_{i}
on
{\mathbf{A}}
such that
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:=(A,*_{i})
is a loop and

{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{i}
is supernilpotent.

Proof.

Let us start with a comment on notation.
Recall that for any
j\leq k
, the normal subloop
C_{j}
induces a congruence
\alpha_{j}
on the loop
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Clearly
\alpha_{j}
is still a congruence for any reduct
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
of
{\mathbf{A}}
that we will construct in this proof.
Further
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/\alpha_{j}
is a reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha_{j}={\mathbf{A}}/C_{j}
and has the same universe
A/\alpha_{j}=A/C_{j}
.
Hence we will denote
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/\alpha_{j}
by
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{j}
for simplicity throughout this proof.
Since all our reducts
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
will turn out to be loops with normal subloops
C_{j}
,
this is actually consistent with the usual notation in loops.

We will prove the lemma by induction on
i
. For
i=1
, let
*_{1}
be the multiplication of
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot)
.
Then
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}={\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}/C_{1}
is abelian of prime power order by assumption, in particular,
1
-supernilpotent.

Next let
i\geq 1
and assume
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=(A,*_{i})
is a loop reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}
such that
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{i}
is supernilpotent for

i\geq 1
.
If not denoted otherwise, all loop operations are those from
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
in the following.

Let
|C_{i}/C_{i+1}|
be a power of a prime
p
. Since
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{i}
is supernilpotent by induction assumption,
it is a direct product of loops of prime power order. In particular
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{i}\cong\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{V}
where
|P|

is a power of
p
and
|V|
is coprime to
p
.
By the Homomorphism Theorem
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{i+1}
has a unique maximal
p
-power order subloop
{\mathbf{E}}
,
which is the extension of
C_{i}/C_{i+1}
by
\mathbf{P}
.
Our goal is to define a new loop reduct
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}
such that

(2.1)

{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{i+1}\cong{\mathbf{E}}\times\mathbf{V}.

Since the latter is supernilpotent as a direct product of nilpotent loops of prime power order,
this will establish the induction step and hence the lemma.

First note that there exists
n\geq 1
such that

x^{n}=x\text{ for all }x\in E\text{ and }x^{n}=1\text{ for all }x\in V.

Here
x^{n}:=(..((\underbrace{xx)x)\dots)x}_{n}
denotes the left associated
n
-th power of
x
with respect to
*_{i}
.
The natural number
n
as above exists since for any nilpotent loop of order a power of a prime
q
,
the group of right translations is a
q
-group
[
6
, Lemma 2.2 of Section VI.2]
.
So we can choose
n
to be congruent to
1
modulo the order of the multiplication group of
{\mathbf{E}}
(which is a
p
-group)
and
n\equiv 0
modulo the order of the multiplication group of
\mathbf{V}
(which is a direct product of
q
-groups, for primes
q\neq p
).

We write
\bar{x}:=xC_{i+1}
for
x\in A
,
\bar{A}:=A/C_{i+1}
and
C:=C_{i}/C_{i+1}
. Let

h\colon\bar{A}\to\bar{A},\ \bar{x}\mapsto\bar{x}^{n}.

Our choice of
n
yields that
h
modulo
C
just induces the projection homomorphism of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}/C\cong\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{V}
onto
\mathbf{P}
.
In particular
h(\bar{A})\subseteq E
and also
h|_{E}=\mathrm{id}_{E}
. Hence
h
behaves like a projection of
\bar{A}
onto
E

but it need not be a homomorphism of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i}=(A/C_{i+1},*_{i})
.
For
x,y\in A
define

r(x,y):=(xy)^{n}/(x^{n}y^{n}).

Modulo
C_{i+1}
this term is equal to the quotient of
h(\overline{xy})
and
h(\bar{x})h(\bar{y})
, i.e.

h(\overline{xy})=\overline{r(x,y)}\,\bigl(h(\bar{x})h(\bar{y})\bigr)\text{ for all }x,y\in A.

Since
h
modulo
C
is a homomorphism,

\overline{r(x,y)}\in C\text{ for all }x,y\in A.

Now define
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}:=(A,*_{i+1})
with

x*_{i+1}y:=(xy)/r(x,y).

We write
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i+1}
for the reduct of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
with universe
A/C_{i}
and operation induced by
*_{i+1}
.
Since
r
clearly vanishes on
E
and on
V
as well, the new
*_{i+1}
and the old
*_{i}
induce the same operations
on
E
and on
V
.
In particular
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i}/E\cong\mathbf{V}\cong\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i+1}/E
.
To see that
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i+1}
also has a quotient isomorphic to
{\mathbf{E}}
,
we check that
h\colon\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i+1}\to{\mathbf{E}}
is a homomorphism.
For that let
x,y\in A
and consider

\displaystyle h(\bar{x}*_{i+1}\bar{y})

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{xy}/\overline{r(x,y)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\overline{xy}^{n}/\overline{r(x,y)}^{n}

\displaystyle\text{by the centrality of }\overline{r(x,y)}\text{ in }\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\overline{xy}^{n}/\overline{r(x,y)}

\displaystyle\text{by the definition of }n\text{ and }\overline{r(x,y)}\in E

\displaystyle=h(\bar{x})h(\bar{y})

\displaystyle\text{by definition of }r(x,y)

\displaystyle=h(\bar{x})*_{i+1}h(\bar{y})

\displaystyle\text{since }*_{i}=*_{i+1}\text{ on }h(\bar{A})=E.

Since
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i+1}/\ker h\cong{\mathbf{E}}
, we see that
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i+1}
has coprime quotients
{\mathbf{E}}
and
\mathbf{V}
.
Hence (
2.1
) follows once we show that
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}
is a loop.

For this we use a second induction on
j=i,\ldots,k
to prove that

(2.2)

{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{j}:=(A/C_{j},*_{i+1})\text{ is a loop}.

The base case
j=i
follows from our previous observation that
*_{i+1}
and
*_{i}
coincide on
E
and on
V
.
In particular
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{i}={\mathbf{A}}_{i}/C_{i}
is a loop.

Next let
j\geq i
and assume
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{j}
is a loop. We claim that the right multiplication by any fixed
b\in A
,

(2.3)

A/C_{j+1}\to A/C_{j+1},\ xC_{j+1}\mapsto(x*_{i+1}b)C_{j+1},\text{ is a bijection}.

For the proof consider
a,a^{\prime}\in A
with
a*_{i+1}b\equiv_{C_{j+1}}a^{\prime}*_{i+1}b
.
Since
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{j}
is a loop by induction assumption, this yields
a\equiv_{C_{j}}a^{\prime}
.
Hence we have
c\in C_{j}
such that
a^{\prime}=a\cdot c
(using the original multiplication
\cdot
of
{\mathbf{A}}
).

Define the term
p(x,y,z):=(x\cdot y)*_{i+1}z
on
{\mathbf{A}}
. By the assumption above we have

p(a,1,b)\equiv_{C_{j+1}}p(a,c,b).

Since
C_{j}/C_{j+1}
is central in
{\mathbf{A}}/C_{j+1}
, the term condition characterization
of centrality
[
10
, Definition 3.2.]
yields that

1=p(1,1,1)\equiv_{C_{j+1}}p(1,c,1)=c.

In other words
c\in C_{j+1}
and hence
a\equiv_{C_{j+1}}a^{\prime}
.
Thus the map in (
2.3
) is injective. Bijectivity follows from the finiteness of
A
.

Similarly the left multiplication by any fixed element modulo
C_{j+1}
is bijective.
Thus (
2.2
) is proved.
For
j=k
we obtain that
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}
is a loop.

Summing up,
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{i+1}
is a loop of order
|E|\cdot|V|
with coprime quotients
{\mathbf{E}}
and
\mathbf{V}
.
Thus
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{i+1}\cong{\mathbf{E}}\times\mathbf{V}
and (
2.1
) is proved.
In particular
{\mathbf{A}}_{i+1}/C_{i+1}
is supernilpotent.
∎

.

For

in Lemma

.

We may choose a central series of normal subloops of
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot)
,

such that factors

have prime power order for all

.
Since

.
This then yields

for all

. Let

Then the range of

.

3.
An application to term functions and equational theories

Vaughan-Lee
[
22
]
showed that every finite supernilpotent loop has a finite basis of equations for its
equational theory (see
[
10
, Chapter 14]
for a generalization to Mal’cev algebras).
It remains open whether any finite nilpotent loop is finitely based.
We hope that Theorem
1.1
can serve as a first step towards a possible positive answer.

To illustrate this, we give an explicit description of term functions on finite central-by-supernilpotent
(in particular
2
-nilpotent) loops.
In
[
14
]
this approach was already used to show that a particular nilpotent (but not supernilpotent)
loop of size 12 that was proposed by Vaughan-Lee in
[
22
]
as potentially non-finitely based
is in fact finitely based.
In Theorem

we show that all nilpotent loops of size

for primes

are finitely based.

For an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
, let
\mathrm{Clo}({\mathbf{A}})
denote the set of term functions of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
For algebras
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
and a function

, let
\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}}
be the
clonoid

from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
generated by
r
, i.e., the smallest set of finitary functions from

to

that contains

and is closed under compositions with term functions of
{\mathbf{A}}
on the domain and with term functions of
{\mathbf{B}}

on the codomain side (see
[

]
for the definition and background on clonoids).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot)
be a finite loop with identity
1
and central normal subloop
N
such that

{\mathbf{A}}/N
is supernilpotent.

(1)

Then
{\mathbf{A}}
is term-equivalent to
(A,*,r)
for some supernilpotent loop
(A,*)

with
{\mathbf{A}}/N=(A,*)/N
,
(N,\cdot)=(N,*)

and some
r\colon A^{2}\to N
that is constant on cosets modulo
N
and
r(1,1)=1
.

(2)

\mathrm{Clo}({\mathbf{A}})=\{f*w\ :\ f\in\mathrm{Clo}(A,*),w\in\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}\}.

Here we abuse notation by identifying functions
r,w
from
A
to
N
that are constant on cosets of

N
with their induced functions from
A/N
to
N
.

Proof.

(
1
)
As in the proof of Corollary
1.2
we obtain a supernilpotent loop reduct
(A,*)

with right division
/
of
{\mathbf{A}}=(A,\cdot)
such that
{\mathbf{A}}/N=(A,*)/N
and

r\colon A^{2}\to N,\ (x,y)\mapsto(xy)/(x*y).

Clearly
(A,*,r)
and
{\mathbf{A}}
are term equivalent and
r(1,1)=1
.
It remains to show that
\cdot
and
*
are equal on
N
and that
r
is constant on the cosets modulo
N
.

Note that
N
is central in
(A,\cdot)
and in
(A,*)
. Further it induces the same congruence
\alpha
on both loops
with classes of the form
xN=x*N
for
x\in A
.

Let
m(x,y,z):=(x/y)*z
be a Mal’cev term for
(A,*)
(and hence for
(A,\cdot)
). Let

. Then

[
10
, Proposition 5.7]
yields that

c*d=m(c\cdot 1,1\cdot 1,1\cdot d)=m(c,1,1)\cdot m(1,1,d)=c\cdot d.

Hence
(N,\cdot)=(N,*)
. Further for

, we get

r(a*c,b*d)=r\bigl(m(a,1,c),m(b,1,d)\bigr)=m\bigl(r(a,b),\underbrace{r(1,1)}_{=1},\underbrace{r(c,d)}_{=1}\bigr)=r(a,b).

Hence
r
is constant on the cosets modulo
N
and (
1
) is proved.

(
2
)
Let
D:=\{f*w\ :\ f\in\mathrm{Clo}(A,*),w\in\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}\}
.

The inclusion
\mathrm{Clo}({\mathbf{A}})\supseteq D
follows from
*,r\in\mathrm{Clo}({\mathbf{A}})
.
For the converse note that

contains all projections on

. It remains to show that

is closed under
*
and
r
. For this let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathrm{Clo}(A,*)
and

.
Then

(f_{1}*w_{1})*(f_{2}*w_{2})=(f_{1}*f_{2})*(w_{1}*w_{2})\in D_{k}

since the image of

is contained in

, which is central in
(A,*)
.
Next

r(f_{1}*w_{1},f_{2}*w_{2})=r(f_{1},f_{2})\in\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}

since

is constant on cosets modulo

. Thus
\mathrm{Clo}({\mathbf{A}})\subseteq D
.
∎

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a loop as in Lemma
3.1
. Since
{\mathbf{A}}
and
(A,*,r)
are term equivalent,
{\mathbf{A}}
has a finite equational basis if and only if
(A,*,r)
has one. We claim now that this is already the case if the clonoid
\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}
is finitely based (or, more precisely, the multi-sorted algebra with sorts
A/N
and
N
and operations
\cdot
on
A/N
and on
N
as well as
r\colon(A/N)^{2}\to N
).

Here, an equational basis of
(A,*,r)
can be obtained as the union of a finite equational basis of the supernilpotent loop
(A,*)
(which exists by
[
10
, Theorem 14.16.]
) and a list of identities corresponding to the equational basis of
\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}
. We do not include a formal proof of this statement, to avoid the technicalities that arise from discussing equational theories of multi-sorted algebras (see e.g.
[
21
]
). But, to illustrate the power of this approach, we are going to show that all nilpotent loops of size
pq
for primes
p,q
are finitely based. Besides Lemma
3.1
, our proof relies on the analysis of clonoids in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Every nilpotent loop of order
pq
for primes
p,q
has a finite equational basis.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a nilpotent loop of order
pq
for primes
p,q
. If
p=q
or
{\mathbf{A}}
is abelian, then
{\mathbf{A}}
has a finite
equational basis by
[
10
, Theorem 14.16.]
). So assume that
p\neq q
and that the center
N
of
{\mathbf{A}}
has
size
p
.

By Lemma
3.1
we may assume that

{\mathbf{A}}
is term equivalent to
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+,r)
with
r\colon A^{2}\to N
constant on cosets of
N
and
r(0,0)=0
.
In the following, we will abuse notation and use the same symbol for

r\colon A^{2}\to N
and the function
r\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{2}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
induced by it.
Then
C:=\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}
is a clonoid of
0
-preserving functions from
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q},+)
to
({\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+)
.
By
[
9
, Theorem 1.2]
every such clonoid is already generated by a single unary function.
Hence
{\mathbf{A}}
is term equivalent to
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+,f)
for some unary
f\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
satisfying
f(0)=0
.
Our first goal is to show that
C
(or, more precisely, the multi-sorted algebra with domains
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
operations
+^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}}
,
+^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}}
and
f\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
) is finitely based.

We denote the
n
-ary functions of
C
by
C^{[n]}
. Then
C^{[n]}
is a subspace of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}}
for every
n\geq 1
. We claim that

(1)

there exists
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
of multiplicative order
k|q-1
such that

B:=\{f(a^{i}x)\ :\ 0\leq i\leq k-1\}
is a basis of
C^{[1]}
and

(2)

\dim(C^{[n]})=k\cdot\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
.

(1) was essentially observed in
[
9
, Lemma 5.3]
. To verify (2), let
\mathcal{L}
be the set of all

1
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}
. It follows from
[
9
, Lemma 4.1]
that for every
h\in C^{[1]}

and every
L\in\mathcal{L}
generated by
\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}
, there exists a function
h_{L}\in C^{[n]}

such that
h_{L}(\lambda\mathbf{y})=h(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
and

h_{L}(\mathbf{x})=0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\setminus L
.
Moreover, as in
[
9
, Proof of Proposition 4.3.]
, one can see that
g\in C^{[n]}
iff
g
is the sum of
functions of the form
h_{L}
for
h\in C^{[1]}
,
L\in\mathcal{L}
. Combining these two observations,
it follows that
\{h_{L}\ :\ h\in B,L\in\mathcal{L}\}
forms a basis of
C^{[n]}
.
Hence
\dim(C^{[n]})=|B|\cdot|\mathcal{L}|=k\cdot\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
and (2) is proved.

By (1), for every
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
, there are coefficients
d_{0},\ldots,d_{k-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle f(cx)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}d_{i}f(a^{i}x).

We claim that all identities of the form (
3.1
) together with the identities holding in

{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
form an equational basis of the clonoid
C
.

To see this, recall that
C^{[n]}
is the linear closure of all functions
f(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i})
for

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
. In fact, we only need
coefficients from the set
A_{n}:=\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{k}\ :\ \alpha_{1}=\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}=0,\alpha_{i}=a^{j}
for some
i\leq n,0\leq j<k\}
by the identities (
3.1
).
Since
|A_{n}|=k\cdot\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
, we get from (2) that the set

B_{n}:=\{f(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i})\ :\ \alpha\in A_{n}\}
is a basis of
C^{[n]}
.

Thus any
n
-ary operation
t\in C
is equal to a unique linear combination of elements from
B_{n}
;
on a syntactic level, we can think about this as a normal form of
t
as a term operation of the multi-sorted algebra given by
f
, and the addition on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
. Moreover, any term can be rewritten into such a normal form
by the identities (
3.1
) and finitely many identities axiomatizing
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{q}
. So, in this sense,
C
has a finite equational basis.

In order to obtain an equational basis for
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+,f)
note that every term operation
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+,f)
can be uniquely written as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}+t^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
where
0\leq d_{i}<pq
for all
i\leq n
and
t^{\prime}
is the normal form of an element of
C
.
Thus, any set of identities that allows us to rewrite terms over
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+,f)
into such normal forms is an equational basis. It is easy to see that the finite set
\Sigma
given by

(1)

a finite equational basis of
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+)
,

(2)

f(x+f(y))=f(x)
,

(3)

f(x+qy)=f(x)
,

(4)

pf(x)=0
,

(5)

all identities of the form (
3.1
)

forms such an equational basis. As
{\mathbf{A}}
and
({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{p},+,f)
are term equivalent,
this finishes the proof.
∎

Note that, in the construction of the equational basis
\Sigma
in the proof of Theorem
3.2
,
the identities in (1) axiomatize
(A,*)
, while the identities (2),(3) and (4) correspond to the identities satisfied by the domain
{\mathbf{A}}/N
and codomain
(N,\cdot)
of the clonoid
C=\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}
respectively, and (5) concerns all actual non-trivial multi-sorted identities from the equational basis of the clonoid
C
.

In a very similar way, we can obtain an equational basis of
(A,*,r)
in the general situation of Lemma
3.1
from an equational basis of
\langle r\rangle_{{\mathbf{A}}/N,(N,\cdot)}
, together with finite equational bases of
(A,*)
,
{\mathbf{A}}/N
and
N
.

4.
Reducts of groups

In view of Theorem
1.1
it is natural to ask

(1)

whether also non-nilpotent loops may have supernilpotent reducts and

(2)

when nilpotent loops have abelian reducts?

We give answers in the setting of groups starting with the first question.

Lemma 4.1
.

No finite non-nilpotent group has a supernilpotent loop (or nilpotent group) as reduct.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a finite group with supernilpotent loop reduct
(G,*)
.
Then every subgroup of
{\mathbf{G}}
is preserved by
*
and hence also a subloop of
(G,*)
.
Since
(G,*)
is supernilpotent, it has a unique subloop
P
of
p
-power order with
|G|/|P|
coprime to
p

for any prime
p
.
Hence
P
is the unique Sylow
p
-subgroup of
{\mathbf{A}}
for any prime
p
. Thus
{\mathbf{A}}
itself is nilpotent.
∎

Any
2
-nilpotent group
(G,\cdot)
of odd order has an abelian reduct by the following construction
due to Baer
[
2
, Theorem B.1]
and called the “Baer trick” in
[
12
, Lemma 4.37]
.
For such a group, the squaring map
s\colon G\to G,\ x\mapsto x^{2}
, is a bijection with inverse
x\mapsto x^{k}

for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
It is then not hard to see that
(G,*)
with

x*y:=xy\,[y,x]^{k}

is an abelian group reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
.
The construction clearly fails if
[y,x]
has no pre-image under
s
, in particular,
if the derived subgroup of
{\mathbf{G}}
has even exponent.
In that case we show that
{\mathbf{G}}
does not have any (polynomial) abelian group reduct at all.
This implies that even in the group case we cannot strengthen Theorem
1.1
to always
obtain abelian reducts.

We start with a general characterization of Mal’cev reducts of
2
-nilpotent groups.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}=(G,\cdot)
be a
2
-nilpotent group.

(1)

Then
m
is a Mal’cev polynomial of
{\mathbf{G}}
iff there exists some
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

m(x,y,z)=xy^{-1}z\,\left([x,y]\,[x,z]^{-1}\,[y,z]\right)^{c}\text{ for all }x,y,z\in G.

In particular, every Mal’cev polynomial is a Mal’cev term.

(2)

Every polynomial loop reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
is isomorphic to some group reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
.
Further
(G,*)
is a group reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
iff there exists some
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

x*y=xy[x,y]^{c}\text{ for all }x,y\in G.

Proof.

(
1
) Assume
m(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is a Mal’cev polynomial of
{\mathbf{G}}
using constants
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in G
.
Since
{\mathbf{G}}
is
2
-nilpotent, commutators are central and we can write
m
in the form

(4.1)

m(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=dx_{1}^{a_{1}}x_{2}^{a_{2}}x_{3}^{a_{3}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{3}\prod_{j=1}^{n}[x_{i},g_{j}]^{b_{ij}}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}[x_{i},x_{j}]^{c_{ij}}

for some integers
a_{i},b_{ij},c_{ij}
and a constant
d\in G
.

From
m(1,1,1)=1
we obtain
d=1
.
Using the Mal’cev identities we see

\displaystyle x_{1}=m(x_{1},1,1)=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}[x_{1},g_{j}]^{b_{1j}},

\displaystyle x_{3}=m(1,1,x_{3})=x_{3}^{a_{3}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}[x_{3},g_{j}]^{b_{3j}}.

Replacing the corresponding factors in (
4.1
), we obtain the simplification

(4.2)

m(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}x_{2}^{a_{2}}x_{3}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}[x_{2},g_{j}]^{b_{2j}}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}[x_{i},x_{j}]^{c_{ij}}.

By the Mal’cev identity again we have

1=m(x_{2},x_{2},1)=x_{2}\underbrace{x_{2}^{a_{2}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}[x_{2},g_{j}]^{b_{2j}}}_{=x_{2}^{-1}}.

Hence we can reduce (
4.1
) further to

(4.3)

m(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}x_{3}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}[x_{i},x_{j}]^{c_{ij}}.

Finally we see from

x=m(x,y,y)=x[x,y]^{c_{12}+c_{13}}\quad\text{ and }\quad x=m(y,y,x)=x[y,x]^{c_{13}+c_{23}}

that we may choose
c_{12}=-c_{13}=c_{23}
.
Writing
c
for
c_{13}
yields the form of
m
claimed in (
1
).

(
2
) Let
*_{e},/,\backslash,e
be polynomial functions on
{\mathbf{G}}
such that
{\mathbf{A}}:=(G,*_{e},/,\backslash)

is a loop with identity
e
. Then
(x/y)*_{e}z
is a Mal’cev polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}
and hence of
{\mathbf{G}}
. So by (
1
)
we have
(x/y)*_{e}z=xy^{-1}z\,\left([x,y]\,[x,z]^{-1}\,[y,z]\right)^{c}

for some
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Setting
y=e
we see that

x*_{e}z=(x/e)*_{e}z=xe^{-1}z\,\left([x,e][x,z]^{-1}[e,z]\right)^{c}\text{ for all }x,z\in G.

For
e=1
, the identity of
{\mathbf{G}}
, this simplifies further to

x*_{1}z=xz\,[x,z]^{-c}.

Using that
{\mathbf{G}}
is
2
-nilpotent and commutators are bilinear,
it is now straightforward to check that
(G,*_{1})
is associative and hence a group.
Further
\varphi\colon(G,*_{1})\to(G,*_{e}),\ x\mapsto xe
, is easily seen to be an isomorphism.
Since
(G,*_{1})
is a group, so is
(G,*_{e})
.
Thus every polynomial loop reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
is a group.
Since
1
is the only constant term function of
{\mathbf{G}}
, every (term) loop reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
is a group with
multiplication
*_{1}
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

A
2
-nilpotent group
{\mathbf{G}}
has an abelian group as (polynomial) reduct iff the derived subgroup of
{\mathbf{G}}

has odd exponent.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
every polynomial group reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}=(G,\cdot)
is isomorphic to
(G,*)
with

x*y=xy\,[x,y]^{c}

for some
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Now
(G,*)
is abelian iff

xy\,[x,y]^{c}=yx\,[y,x]^{c}.

The latter is equivalent to

[x,y]^{2c+1}=1\text{ for all }x,y\in G.

Clearly such an integer
c
exists iff the derived subgroup of
{\mathbf{G}}
has odd exponent (possibly
1
).
∎

Example 4.4
.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}=(G,\cdot)
be a group with derived subgroup of exponent
2
(e.g., any non-abelian group of order
8
).
By Lemma
4.2

(1)

every loop reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
is equal to
{\mathbf{G}}
or
{\mathbf{G}}^{op}=(G,*)
with
x*y=yx
,

(2)

every polynomial loop reduct of
{\mathbf{G}}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{G}}
,

(3)

every Mal’cev polynomial of
{\mathbf{G}}
is equal to
xy^{-1}z
or
zy^{-1}x
.

We further remark that
(G,xy^{-1}z)
is a
minimal
Mal’cev algebra, meaning that it has a Mal’cev term,
but does not have any proper reducts with a Mal’cev term. This directly implies that it is also a minimal Taylor
algebra in the sense of
[
3
]
since for finite solvable algebras having a Taylor term is equivalent to
having a Mal’cev term by results from tame congruence theory
[
11
]
.

We give two more examples showing that in general it is not possible to decrease the nilpotence class
of a
2
-group or of a group of class greater than
2
by taking a group reduct:

For
n\geq 2
, let
{\mathbf{D}}_{2^{n+1}}
be the dihedral group of order
2^{n+1}
.
Then
{\mathbf{D}}_{2^{n+1}}
has a
2
-element center and is
n
-nilpotent but not
n-1
-nilpotent.
By Lemma
4.2
and induction on
n
it follows that every group reduct of
{\mathbf{D}}_{2^{n+1}}
has the same properties,
in particular, is not
k
-nilpotent for
k<n
.

The wreath product
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
is nilpotent of class
3
.
We claim that
{\mathbf{G}}
has no abelian group reduct
(G,*)
. Suppose otherwise. Then every
g\in G
generates the
same subgroup and hence has the same order in
{\mathbf{G}}
and in
(G,*)
. Since
{\mathbf{G}}
has exponent
9
and a normal
subgroup
N
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}^{3}
, this implies that
(G,*)
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{9}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{3}^{2}
.
Hence every
g\in G\setminus N
has order
9
, which is clearly not true in
{\mathbf{G}}
. So
{\mathbf{G}}
has no abelian reduct.
By Corollary
4.3
it does not have a
2
-nilpotent group reduct either.
