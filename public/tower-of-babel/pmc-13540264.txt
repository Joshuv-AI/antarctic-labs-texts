Facial emotional expression in response to alcohol‐promoting commercials in individuals with alcohol use disorder: A pilot study
Authors: Takefumi Ueno, Naho Nakayama, Shou Fukushima, Kazunori Matsuguchi, Kazuhiro Tojiki, Chenyu Qian, Shisei Tei, Hidehiko Takahashi, Junya Fujino
Published: 2026

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a chronic relapsing disorder in which environmental alcohol cues can trigger craving and relapse. 1 , 2 , 3 Alcohol‐promoting digital commercials are a pervasive form of alcohol‐related exposure in contemporary society, yet the psychological and emotional processes they elicit in individuals with AUD remain poorly characterized. 3 , 4 Facial expressions convey emotions, intentions, and evaluative reactions, 5 , 6 and automated facial expression analysis may provide a scalable complementary method for assessing rapid and implicit cue reactivity.

Fifty participants were initially recruited (AUD [n = 25] and healthy control [HC] [n = 25]). After withdrawals and technical exclusions, 16 individuals with AUD (47.6 ± 7.8 years; 3 females) and 23 HCs (42.3 ± 10.2; 3 females) were included. Current psychiatric comorbidity was assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the Hizen Psychiatric Medical Center and the Institute of Science Tokyo Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent. Please see Supplementary Methods for details.

Participants viewed four 15‐s video clips from digital alcoholic‐beverage commercials and four 15‐s clips from soft‐drink commercials as controls. Consistent with prior studies, 3 , 6 soft‐drink stimuli were carefully matched to the alcohol‐related commercials in design, complexity, color composition, motion, and the presence of human faces. Each clip was presented twice in pseudorandom order, with a 10–15‐s fixation interval between clips (Figure 1a). Participants also rated their desire to drink alcohol after each clip; drinking desire was calculated as the difference between the mean ratings for alcohol‐related and soft‐drink commercials. Emotional intensity (EI) of happy, sad, angry, surprised, scared, disgusted, and neutral during video viewing was quantified using FaceReader (Figure 1b), which classifies facial expressions based on the Facial Action Coding System. 5 , 6 EI values ranged from 0 to 1 were log‐transformed (log[1 + EI]) before analysis. For each emotional category, mixed‐design analyses of variance assessed group effects (HC vs. AUD), condition effects (alcohol vs. soft‐drink), and their interaction. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two‐tailed). Please see Supplementary Methods for details.

Age and sex did not differ significantly between groups, although estimated full‐scale intelligence quotient (IQ) was slightly lower in the AUD group (Table S1). Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) scores were significantly higher in AUD than HCs. Drinking desire scores did not differ significantly between groups (HC 2.7 ± 1.8; AUD 1.8 ± 1.9, p = 0.14). Figures 1c and S1 depict mean facial EI values across conditions. For happiness EI, the condition effect was not significant (F = 1.48, p = 0.23), whereas significant group (F = 5.97, p = 0.02) and group × condition effects (F = 4.24, p = 0.046) were observed. Post hoc analysis showed higher happiness EI in AUD than HCs during alcohol‐related commercials (p = 0.04), although this did not survive Bonferroni correction. For sadness EI, significant group (F = 11.70, p < 0.01), condition (F = 5.04, p = 0.03), and group × condition effects (F = 5.84, p = 0.02) were found. Sadness EI increased during alcohol‐related versus soft‐drink commercials only in AUD (p < 0.01) and was higher in AUD than HCs during alcohol‐related commercials (p < 0.01). For neutral EI, only the group effect was significant (F = 4.70, p = 0.04), with no condition effect (F = 3.39, p = 0.07) or group × condition interaction (F = 1.98, p = 0.17). No significant main effects or interactions were detected for the remaining facial expressions (Table S2). As a sensitivity analysis, mixed‐design analyses were performed with estimated full‐scale IQ included as a covariate. All effects for sadness remained significant after adjustment for IQ (group: F = 8.79, p < 0.01; condition: F = 5.18, p = 0.03; and interaction: F = 6.19, p = 0.02), whereas the group and interaction effects for happiness (F = 3.04, p = 0.09; F = 3.67, p = 0.06, respectively) and the group effect for neutral EI (F = 2.93, p = 0.095) were no longer significant.

These preliminary findings suggest that alcohol‐promoting commercials elicit altered facial emotional responses in AUD. The concurrent modulation of happiness‐ and sadness‐related expressions may reflect emotional ambivalence toward alcohol cues: alcohol‐related advertising may activate appetitive reward expectancies while also evoking negative self‐referential affects. 7 , 8 The robust, condition‐specific sadness response suggests that negative emotion may be a particularly important component of alcohol‐cue reactivity in AUD. Conversely, the effects observed for happiness and neutral EI were attenuated after adjustment for IQ, requiring a cautious interpretation.

Alcohol marketing may increase immediate alcohol consumption. 9 However, the generalizability of these findings requires further investigation. Animated characters and computer‐generated imagery are also commonly used in digital advertising. Given evidence that cartoon and real faces may be processed differently at the neural level, 10 future studies should examine whether alcohol‐related commercials featuring animated content elicit emotional responses comparable to those evoked by real‐world commercials. Further investigation of emotional reactivity to digital alcohol advertising in individuals with AUD may contribute to public health discussions regarding strategies to reduce potentially harmful advertising exposure among vulnerable populations.

This study has several limitations, including its modest sample size, psychiatric comorbidities, heterogeneous psychotropic medication use, and cross‐sectional design. Although soft‐drink stimuli were carefully matched to alcohol‐related stimuli, complete equivalence across naturalistic commercial features, such as foreground–background ratio and zooming speed, was difficult to achieve. Nevertheless, the present findings suggest the potential utility of automated facial expression analysis as a complementary tool for assessing emotional cue reactivity in AUD, while underscoring the need for further validation in larger longitudinal samples.
