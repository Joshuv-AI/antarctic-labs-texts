A star is born: Explosive Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes
Bas Lodewijks

Abstract
. We study a family of Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes in random environment that explode, i.e. that grow infinitely large in finite time with positive probability. Building on recent work of the author and Iyer (“On the structure of genealogical trees associated with explosive Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes”, arXiv:2311.14664, 2023), we weaken certain assumptions required to prove that the branching process, at the time of explosion, contains a (unique) individual with infinite offspring. We then apply these results to super-linear preferential attachment models . In particular, we fill gaps in some of the cases analysed in Appendix A of the work of the author and Iyer and study a large range of previously unattainable cases.



Keywords:
Explosive Crump–Mode–Jagers branching processes, super-linear preferential attachment, preferential attachment with fitness, random recursive trees, condensation.

60J80, 90B15, 05C80.

1.
Introduction

In a Crump–Mode–Jagers (CMJ) branching process (named after
[
4
,
11
]
) an ancestral
root
individual produces offspring according to a collection of points on the non-negative real line. Each individual ‘born’ produces offspring according to an identically distributed collection of points, translated by their birth time. One is generally interested in properties of the population as a function of time. Classical work from the 1970s and ’80s related to this model generally deals with the
Malthusian case
, which, informally, refers to the fact that the population grows exponentially in time; see e.g.
[
3
,
12
]
and the references therein for an overview of classical CMJ theory.

Far fewer results exist for CMJ branching processes when a Malthusian parameter does not exist. One family of CMJ branching processes for which this is the case, is the family of
explosive
CMJ branching processes. Here, the total progeny of the branching process grows infinitely large in
finite
time with positive probability. Early work on such CMJ branching processes by Sevast’yanov
[
18
,
19
]
, Grey
[
5
]
, Grishechkin
[
6
]
, and Vatutin
[
20
]
studies necessary and sufficient conditions for (non-)explosion of Bellman-Harris processes (a special type CMJ branching processes). More recent work on this topic distinguishes between processes where individuals produce finitely many offspring in finite time almost surely, and processes where individuals can produce infinite offspring themselves in finite time. In the former case, Komjáthy provides general criteria for explosion of CMJ branching processes in terms of solutions of a functional fixed point equation
[
13
]
, and also extends the necessary and sufficient criteria for explosion in branching random walks in
[
1
]
. In the latter case, Oliveira and Spencer, and very recently Iyer
[
9
]
, study a particular family of explosive CMJ branching processes with exponential inter-birth times
[
15
]
, and Sagitov considers Bellman-Harris processes where individuals, upon death, may produce infinite offspring
[
17
,
16
]
.

Recent work of the author and Iyer investigates a family of explosive CMJ branching processes in
random environment

[
10
]
. Here, each individual born in the branching process is assigned an i.i.d. random weight, and the distribution of the point process that governs the offspring of the individual depends on their weight. Sufficient conditions for the emergence of
infinite stars
(individuals that produce infinite offspring) and
infinite paths
(an infinite lineage of descendants) in the branching process, stopped at the (finite) time of explosion are formulated in a general set-up. Other sufficient criteria for explosion for CMJ processes in random environment are also studied in
[
8
]
.

Overview of our contribution.
In this paper we study the emergence of
infinite stars
in a explosive CMJ processes in random environment. In particular, we weaken certain assumptions provided in previous work of the author and Iyer
[
10
]
, under which we prove that an infinite star appears almost surely.

We use these results in an application to
super-linear preferential attachment
trees with fitness. This model consists of a sequence of discrete-time trees in which vertices are assigned vertex-weights and arrive one by one. A new vertex connects to a random vertex in the tree, selected with a preference for vertices with high degree and/or vertex-weight. We largely extend the class of models for which such results can be proved. This significantly improves on the results in
[
10
]
, and also adds to the recent work of Iyer
[
9
]
on persistence in (super-linear) preferential attachment trees (without fitness).

Model definition.
We consider

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{n}

\mathbb{N}^{0}:=\{\varnothing\}
contains a single element which we call the

We denote elements

as tuples, so that, if

u_{1}\cdots u_{k-1}
; for example,

\varnothing

u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
and
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})

denote their concatenation. Suppose that
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
is a complete probability space and
(S,\mathcal{S})
is a measure space. We also equip
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
with the sigma algebra generated by singleton sets. Then, we fix random mappings
X:\Omega\times\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
,
W:\Omega\times\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\rightarrow S
, and define
(X,W):\Omega\times\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\rightarrow[0,\infty]\times S
, so that
(\omega,u)\mapsto((X(u))(\omega),W_{u}(\omega))
.
In general, for

and

, one interprets
W_{u}
as a ‘weight’ associated with
u
, and

the waiting time or

times between the birth of the
(j-1)
th and
j
th
child
of

We use the values of

to associate
birth times

\mathcal{B}(u)
to individuals

. In particular, we define
\mathcal{B}:\Omega\times\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
recursively as follows:

\mathcal{B}(\varnothing):=0\quad\text{and, for $u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty},i\in\mathbb{N}$,}\quad\mathcal{B}(ui):=\mathcal{B}(u)+\sum_{j=1}^{i}X(uj).

We note that a value of

indicates that the individual

has stopped producing offspring, and produces at most

children.

We introduce some notation related to elements

. We use
|\cdot|
to measure the

of a tuple

, so that

|u|=k

. If, for some

is a

of
x
. Further, given

, we write

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
with the lexicographic total order

. Given elements

if either

is an ancestor of
v
, or
u_{\ell}<v_{\ell}
where
\ell=\min\left\{i\in\mathbb{N}:u_{i}\neq v_{i}\right\}
. For

, we let

denote the time, after the

of
u

to produce
i

(1.1)

\mathcal{P}_{i}(u):=\sum_{j=1}^{i}X(uj)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{P}(u):=\mathcal{P}_{\infty}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X(uj).

We use the notation

For

\mathscr{T}_{t}

of individuals with birth time at most

(X,W)
-
Crump–Mode–Jagers

(X,W)
-
Crump–Mode–Jagers

With regards to the process
(\mathscr{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, we define the stopping times
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that

(1.2)

\tau_{k}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|\mathscr{T}_{t}|\geq k\},

where we adopt the convention that the infimum of the empty set is
\infty
.
One readily verifies that
(|\mathscr{T}_{t}|)_{t\geq 0}
is right-continuous, and thus
|\mathscr{T}_{\tau_{k}}|\geq k
(when
\tau_{k}<\infty
). For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define the tree
\mathcal{T}_{k}
as the tree consisting of the first
k

\mathscr{T}_{\tau_{k}}

(1.3)

\tau_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}

of the process, at which the branching process has grown infinitely large (given the process survives). We also define the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{T}_{k}
.

Remark 1.1
.

With the more commonly used notation for CMJ branching processes, one assigns a point process (denoted
\xi^{(u)}
) to each

[
14

). This
\sigma
-finiteness is implied by the classical Malthusian condition, but, in this general setting, we believe it is easier to have a framework where one can directly refer to the points
\mathcal{B}(u1),\mathcal{B}(u2),\ldots

2.
Results

Throughout this paper, we make the following general assumptions, that are common when studying (explosive)
(X,W)
-CMJ processes. We assume that
the pairs
((X(uj))_{j\in\mathbb{N}},W_{u})
with
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
are i.i.d. For a given
w\in S
, we let
(X_{w}(uj))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a sequence
(X(uj))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, conditionally on the weight
W_{u}=w
. We also assume throughout that, for any
w\in S
and any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, the random variables

(X_{w}(ui))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are mutually independent.

2.1.
Sufficient criteria for an infinite star in CMJ branching processes in random environment

Our main interest and aim is to understand
how
an explosive CMJ branching process explodes. We determine sufficient conditions under which the branching process explodes due to an individual giving birth to an infinite number of children in finite time (an
infinite star
). The assumptions are as follows.

Assumption 2.1
.

(a)

There exist positive random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with finite mean such that, for any
w\in S
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(2.1)

\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}X_{w}(i)\preceq Y_{n}.

(b)

There exists an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=\infty
, and

(2.2)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{n}}(Y_{n})\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{n}}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varnothing))<\infty.

(c)

We have
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup\{k:X(1)=\cdots=X(k)=0\}\right]<1
. Additionally, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, almost surely

(2.3)

\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}X(i)>0.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that under Condition
(a)
of Assumption
2.1
, we have
\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_{\infty}<\infty\right)=1
, since, for example, the root
\varnothing
produces an infinite number of children in finite time, almost surely.

\blacktriangleleft

Remark 2.3
.

In Assumption
2.1
we can consider Condition
(a)
as a
uniform explosivity
condition: regardless of its weight, the distribution of the time until an individual produces infinite offspring, after having already produced
n
children, is dominated by
Y_{n}
. This assumption is essential to work around dependencies that arise due to the random environment (i.e. the weights of individuals).

Condition
(b)
is used in the proof of Proposition
3.2
, and provides an upper bound for the expected number of children that produce infinite offspring before their parent does.

Condition
(c)
is a technical assumption, necessary to rule out certain trivial cases. Indeed, if, for example,
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup\{k:X(k)=0\}\right]>1
, the tree consisting of all the individuals born
instantaneously
at time
0
is a supercritical Bienaymé-Galton-Watson branching process. Hence, with positive probability this tree is infinitely large, whilst it may not contain an individual with infinite offspring.
\blacktriangleleft

Remark 2.4
.

Condition
(b)

weakens and combines
two conditions in Assumption
2.2
in
[
10
]
. There, it was assumed, with
\lambda_{n}:=c\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}\right]^{-1}
and
c<1
, that
\lambda_{n}
increases and diverges, and that

(2.4)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{n}}(Y_{n})<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{n}}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varnothing))<\infty.

It is clear that these assumptions imply Condition
(b)
.

\blacktriangleleft

We then have our main result.

Theorem 2.5

(Infinite star)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the infinite tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
almost surely contains a node of infinite degree
(
an infinite star
)
.

2.2.
Application

We apply the result of Theorem
2.5
when the inter-birth times are
exponentially
distributed. In particular, this allows us to relate the results for
(X,W)
-CMJ branching processes to a family of recursively grown discrete trees, known as
super-linear
preferential attachment trees with
fitness
, introduced in
[
10
]
. This is a sequence of trees where vertices are introduced one by one and are assigned vertex-weights (fitness values). When a new vertex is introduced, it connects to one of the vertices already in the tree, where vertices with high degree or high fitness are more likely to make connections with new vertices.

We generally consider trees as being rooted with edges directed away from the root, and hence the number of ‘children’ of a node corresponds to its
out-degree
. More precisely, given a vertex labelled
v
in a directed tree
T
we let
\Deg^{+}(v,T)
denote its out-degree in
T
. We now define the preferential attachment with fitness (PAF) model.

Definition 2.6
.

Suppose that
(W_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. copies of a random variable
W
that takes values in
S
, and let
f\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\times S\rightarrow(0,\infty)
denote the
fitness function
. A preferential attachment tree with fitness is the sequence of random trees
(T_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
T_{1}
consists of a single node
1
with weight
W_{1}
and for
n\geq 2
,
T_{n}
is constructed, conditionally on
T_{n-1}
, as follows:

(a)

Sample a vertex
j\in T_{n-1}
with probability

(2.5)

\frac{f(\Deg^{+}(j,T_{n-1}),W_{j})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(\Deg^{+}(i,T_{n-1}),W_{i})}.

(b)

Connect
j
with an edge directed outwards to a new vertex
n
with weight
W_{n}
.

The correspondence between preferential attachment trees with fitness and
(X,W)
-CMJ process with exponential inter-birth times is as follows. For each
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, set
X(ui)\sim\exponentialrv\left(f(i-1,W_{u})\right)
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
. The trees
(\mathcal{T}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
associated with the
(X,W)
-CMJ process then satisfy

(2.6)

\{T_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}\overset{d}{=}\{\mathcal{T}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Additionally, with
T_{\infty}:=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}T_{n}
, we also have
T_{\infty}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. As a result, the structural properties of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
can be translated to
T_{\infty}
. The equality in distribution is a consequence of the memory-less property and the fact that the minimum of exponential random variables is also exponentially distributed, with a rate given by the sum of the rates of the corresponding variables; see for example
[
7
, Section 2.1]
. The use of continuous-time embeddings of combinatorial processes is pioneered by Athreya and Karlin
[
2
]
.

We require the following assumptions on the fitness function
f
:

(
w^{*}
)

\exists w^{*}\in S:\forall w\in S,j\in\mathbb{N}:f(j,w)\geq f(j,w^{*})\quad\text{and }\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(j,w^{*})}<\infty.

That is, there exists a minimiser
w^{*}\in S
that, uniformly in
j\in\mathbb{N}
, minimises
f(j,\cdot)
, and the reciprocals of
f(j,w^{*})
are summable. Whilst the latter condition is necessary to have an
(X,W)
-CMJ branching process where individuals can produce an infinite number of children in finite time (i.e. where an infinite star can appear), the former assumption is to avoid certain technicalities only. We also define the quantities

(2.7)

\mu_{n}^{w}:=\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(i,w)},\qquad w\in\mathbb{R}_{+},n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\text{and set }\mu_{n}:=\mu_{n}^{w^{*}}.

Define
\mu_{x}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}
by linear interpolation. We then have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.7

(Infinite star in super-linear preferential attachment trees)
.

Let
(T_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a preferential attachment tree with fitness function
f
that satisfies Assumption (

w
∗

). Let
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\infty)
be increasing and tend to infinity with
n
, such that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
\lambda_{n}<f(i,w^{*})
for all
i\geq n\geq N
. If

(2.8)

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\bigg(\prod_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{f(i,w^{*})}{f(i,w^{*})-\lambda_{n}}\bigg)\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{f(i,W)}{f(i,W)+\lambda_{n}}\right]<\infty,

then the tree
T_{\infty}
contains a unique node of infinite degree, and no infinite path, almost surely.

Remark 2.8
.

Though we present a corollary for the specific choice of exponentially distributed inter-birth times, similar results can be proved to hold when considering other distributions for the inter-birth times (e.g. beta, gamma, Rayleigh distributions).
\blacktriangleleft

The corollary follows by showing the conditions in Assumption
2.1
are met. It is readily verified that Condition
(c)
is satisfied, as the exponential distribution does not have an atom at zero. Setting

(2.9)

Y_{n}:=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\widetilde{X}_{w^{*}}(i),

where
\widetilde{X}_{w^{*}}(i)
is an independent copy of
X_{w^{*}}(i)
, and applying Assumption (

w
∗

), Condition
(a)
follows. Finally, Condition
(b)
is equivalent to (
2.8
) when the inter-birth times are exponentially distributed and
Y_{n}
is as in (
2.9
). The uniqueness of the node of infinite degree and absence of an infinite path follows from
[
10
, Theorem
2.12
]
, and is true in general for inter-birth time distributions that have no atoms.

We juxtapose Corollary
2.7
with the following result from
[
10
]
:

Theorem 2.9

(Theorem
3.4
in
[
10
]
)
.

Recall
\mu_{n}^{w}
from (
2.7
). If, for some
c>1
and all
w\geq 0
, we have

(2.10)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{f(i,W)}{f(i,W)+c(\mu_{n}^{w})^{-1}\log n}\right]=\infty,

then
T_{\infty}
contains a unique infinite path and no node of infinite degree, almost surely.

In the upcoming sub-section we provide a range of examples for which the conditions in Corollary
2.7
can be satisfied, for which we use
\lambda_{n}=\delta\mu_{n}^{-1}\log n
, where
\delta>0
is a sufficiently small constant and
\mu_{n}
is as in (
2.7
). For this choice of
\lambda_{n}
, we see that Corollary
2.7
and Theorem
2.9
are close to being converse results. Indeed, we confirm this in a range of examples in the upcoming sub-section.

2.2.1.
Super-linear preferential attachment with fitness

We proceed by studying a family of preferential attachment trees (with fitness) for which we can verify the conditions provided in Corollary
2.7
. In doing so, we extend the number of models for which it is known that
T_{\infty}
contains a unique node of infinite degree from a handful of examples to larger, but more particular, class. This class consists of a family of
multiplicative
fitness functions. That is, vertex-weights take values in
S=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
f(i,w)=g(w)s(i)
for some functions
g\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to(0,\infty)
and
s\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
. To satisfy Assumption (

w
∗

), we assume that

(2.11)

\exists w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\ \forall w\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:g(w)\geq g(w^{*})>0,\qquad\text{and,}\qquad\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(i)}<\infty.

Our main interest is functions
s
that grow
barely faster than linear
, for which the summability condition in (
2.11
) is only just satisfied. For example,
s(n)=n\log(n+2)\log\log(n+3)^{\sigma}
, with
\sigma>1
. Earlier work by the author and Iyer in
[
10
]
is only able to deal with cases for which
s(n)
grows fast enough, that is, faster than
n(\log n)^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>2
. Here, we extend this to a much wider range of functions that grow slower.

We introduce the following assumptions on the function
s
.

Assumption 2.10
.

Suppose
s
satisfies (
2.11
). Moreover, suppose there exist
\beta\in(0,1)
and
p\in(1,1+\beta)
such that

(2.12)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s(n)}{n^{\beta}}=\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s(n)}{n^{p}}=0,

and there exist
C>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
,

(2.13)

s(n)\leq C\frac{n^{1+\beta-p}}{\log n}\inf_{i\geq n}s(i).

Remark 2.11
.

Though the first limit in Assumption
2.10
may seem unnecessary when
s
satisfies (
2.11
), one can construct examples of
s
with a sufficiently sparse summable subsequence that grows slowly. For example, with
\varepsilon>0
small,

(2.14)

(n+2)(\log(n+2))^{2}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Though this choice of
s
satisfies (
2.11
), the subsequence
s(n_{k})
with
n_{k}=k^{2}
grows relatively slowly compared to the values
s(n)
when
n
is not a perfect square, so that the first limit in Assumption
2.10
is satisfied only for
\beta<\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}
. The inequality in Assumption
2.10
quantifies how much slower such sparse slowly-growing sub-sequences are allowed to grow. Over-all, Assumption
2.10
thus allows us to deal with some choices of
s
with sparse slowly growing subsequences.
\blacktriangleleft

We then have the following result.

Theorem 2.12

(Barely super-linear preferential attachment with fitness)
.

Suppose that

, where
s
satisfies Assumption
2.10
and

and
s
satisfy (
2.11
). Suppose there exists a sequence
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and constants

(2.15)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(g(W)>k_{n}\right)\leq n^{-(1+\varepsilon)},\qquad\text{for all }n\geq n_{0},

and

are satisfied, where

. Then, the limiting infinite tree

contains a unique infinite star and no infinite path, almost surely.

Remark 2.13
.

The conditions in Assumption

allow us to construct a sequence

such that

for all

and all

large, whilst (

) is met.
\blacktriangleleft

2.3.1.
Super-linear preferential attachment without fitness

To conclude, we consider the case

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(i)}<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\exists\kappa>0\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\max_{i\leq n}\frac{s(i)}{i+1}\leq\kappa\frac{s(n)}{n+1}.

(Barely super-linear preferential attachment)
.

Let

) and Assumption

.

The condition in (

to

\blacktriangleleft

Though not entirely general, the conditions in Corollary

3.
Proofs of main results

This section is dedicated to proving the results presented in Section
2
. In Section
3.1
we focus on the main result; Theorem
2.5
. Section
3.2
is dedicated to proving Theorem
2.12
.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.5

We introduce the following terminology, used in the remainder of the section. Recall
\mathcal{P}_{k}
and
\mathcal{P}
from (
1.1
). For
a,b\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
we say that

\text{``$a$ has at least $k$ children before $b$ explodes''}\quad\text{if}\quad\ \mathcal{B}(a)+\mathcal{P}_{k}(a)<\mathcal{B}(b)+\mathcal{P}(b).

We also say that

\text{``$a$ explodes before all of its ancestors''}\quad\text{if}\quad\ \forall\ell<|a|:\mathcal{B}(a)+\mathcal{P}(a)<\mathcal{B}(a_{|_{\ell}})+\mathcal{P}(a_{|_{\ell}})

Finally, for
a\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
with
|a|\geq 1
, we say that
a=a_{1}\cdots a_{m}
is
a_{1}
-
conservative
if, for each
j\in\left\{2,\ldots,m\right\}
, we have
a_{j}\leq a_{1}
. We then have the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exist
\zeta<1
and
K=K(\zeta)>0
such that for all
a_{1}>K(\zeta)
and all integers
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a:|a|=m\\
a\text{ is $a_{1}$-conservative}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{P}\!\left(a\text{ has at least $a_{1}$ children before }\varnothing\text{ explodes}\right)\leq\zeta^{m-1}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(Y_{a_{1}})\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{1}}).

Proof.

Suppose that
a=a_{1}\cdots a_{m}\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
. For
a
to have at least
a_{1}
children before the explosion of
\varnothing
, all the ancestors of
a
(including
a
) need to be born, and then
a
needs to produce
a_{1}
many children. All this needs to happen before
\varnothing
produces infinitely many children, starting to count from its
(a_{1}+1)^{\mathrm{st}}
child. That is,

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(a\text{ has at least $a_{1}$ children before }\varnothing\text{ explodes}\right)=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\!\mathcal{P}_{a_{1}}(a)+\!\sum_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{P}_{a_{j}}(a_{|_{j-1}})\leq\!\!\!\!\sum_{k=a_{1}+1}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!X(k)\!\bigg).

Using Assumption
2.1

(a)
and a Chernoff bound, with
\lambda_{a_{1}}>0
, we arrive at the upper bound

(3.3)

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\!\mathcal{P}_{a_{1}}(a)+\!\sum_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{P}_{a_{j}}(a_{|_{j-1}})\leq Y_{a_{1}}\!\bigg)\leq\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{a_{1}}(Y_{a_{1}}))\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{1}})\prod_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{j}}),

where the last line follows from the fact that the sequence
(\mathcal{P}_{j}(u))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is independent and distributed like
(\mathcal{P}_{j}(\varnothing))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
. When we sum over the possible conservative sequences
a
that are
a_{1}
-conservative, each
a_{j}
takes values between
1
and
a_{1}
, for
j=2,\ldots,m
. Thus,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a:|a|=m\\
a\text{ $a_{1}$-conservative}\end{subarray}}{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(a\text{ has at least $a_{1}$ children before }\varnothing\text{ explodes}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{2}=1}^{a_{1}}\sum_{a_{3}=1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\sum_{a_{m}=1}^{a_{1}}\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{a_{1}}(Y_{a_{1}}))\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{1}})\prod_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{j}})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{a_{1}}(Y_{a_{1}}))\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{1}})\left(\sum_{n=1}^{a_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})\right)^{m-1}.

It thus remains to show that, for
a_{1}
sufficiently large,

\sum_{n=1}^{a_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})<\zeta.

As
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{n}}(Y_{n})\geq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, by Assumption
2.1

(b)
,

(3.5)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{n}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})<\infty

also holds.
As a result, for any
\eta>0
there exists
N=N(\eta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
a_{1}>N
,

(3.6)

\sum_{n=N}^{a_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})<\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{n}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})<\frac{\eta}{2},

where the inequality uses the fact that
\lambda_{n}
is increasing in
n
. On the other hand, since
\lambda_{n}
diverges with
n
, bounded convergence (bounding the integrand by
1
) yields

(3.7)

\lim_{a_{1}\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{P}_{n}=0\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(1)=\cdots=X(n)=0\right).

We note that, by Assumption
2.1
(c)
, there exists
\xi>0
such that

(3.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup\{k:X(1)=\cdots=X(k)=0\}\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(X(1)=\cdots=X(n)=0\right)<1-\xi.

Hence, the right-hand side of (
3.7
) is at most
1-\xi
for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
. So, we first take
\eta<\xi
and
N
large enough so that we have the upper bound in (
3.6
). Then, we take
K\geq N
sufficiently large, so that for all
a_{1}\geq K
,

(3.9)

\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{\ell})<1-\xi/2.

Combined, we thus arrive at

(3.10)

\sum_{n=1}^{a_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{1}}}(\mathcal{P}_{\ell})<1-\xi/2=:\zeta.

Using this in (
3.4
), we conclude the proof.
∎

The above lemma provides an upper bound for the probability of the event that a vertex
a
explodes before the root of
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, in the case that
a
is
a_{1}
-
conservative
. When
a
does not satisfy this condition, we can view
a
as a concatenation of a number of conservative sequences. That is, we write
a=\overline{b}_{1}\cdots\overline{b}_{\ell}
, where
\overline{b}_{i}=b_{i,1}\ldots b_{i,m_{i}}
for each
i\in[\ell]
and for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N},(m_{i})_{i\in[\ell]}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, and
(b_{i,j})_{i\in[\ell],j\in[m_{i}]}
, such that
\overline{b}_{i}
is
b_{i,1}
-conservative for each
i\in[\ell]
. By the independence of birth processes of distinct individuals (or in fact, the independence of disjoint sub-trees), we are able to apply Lemma
3.1
to each conservative sequence in the concatenation to arrive at a bound for the expected number of individuals that explode before all their ancestors.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exists
K^{\prime}>0
sufficiently large, such that

(3.11)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{a\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:a_{1}>K^{\prime},a\text{ explodes before all its ancestors}\right\}\right|\right]<\infty.

Proof.

As explained before the proposition statement, we think of sequences
a\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
as a concatenation of conservative sequences. Let
a=a_{1}\ldots a_{m}
be a sequence of length
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that there exist
k\in[m]
and indices
1:=I_{1}<I_{2}<\ldots<I_{k}
such that
a_{1}=:a_{I_{1}}<a_{I_{2}}<\ldots<a_{I_{k}}
and
a_{i}\leq a_{I_{j}}
for all
i\in\{I_{j}+1,\ldots,I_{j+1}-1\}
and
j\in[k]
(with
I_{k+1}:=m+1
). That is, the
I_{j}
are the indices of the running maxima of
a
. We also set
I_{k+1}:=m+1
and
a_{0}=\varnothing
. We think of
a
as a concatenation of the conservative sequences
a_{I_{j}}\cdots a_{I_{j+1}-1}
, with
j\in[k]
. These sub-sequences can be seen as corresponding to an
a_{I_{j}}
-conservative individual, rooted at
a_{I_{j}-1}
, for each
j\in[k]
. We can thus apply Lemma
3.1
to these sub-sequences.

Since, by definition we have
a_{I_{\ell+1}}>a_{I_{\ell}}
, applying a similar logic to (
3.2
), we have the inclusion

(3.12)

\displaystyle E_{a}:={}

\displaystyle\{a\text{ explodes}\text{ before any of its ancestors explodes}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq{}

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{k}\{a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell+1}-1}\text{ gives birth to at least }a_{I_{\ell}}\text{ children before }a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell}-1}\text{ explodes}\}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{k}\bigg\{\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}(a_{|_{I_{\ell+1}-1}})+\sum_{j=I_{\ell}}^{I_{\ell+1}-2}\mathcal{P}_{a_{j+1}}(a_{|_{j}})\leq\sum_{i=a_{I_{\ell}}+1}^{\infty}X(a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell}-1}i)\bigg\}=:\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{k}E_{a,\ell}.

Now, note that the events
(E_{a,\ell},\ell\in[k])
are not independent, since, for a given
\ell
, the term
\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}(a_{|_{I_{\ell+1}-1}})
in
E_{a,\ell}
may be correlated with the summands
X(a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell+1}-1}i)
appearing in
E_{a,\ell+1}
(via the weight
W_{a_{I_{\ell+1}-1}}
). However, as we assume that, for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, the random variables
(X(uj))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, conditionally on
W_{u}
, are independent, it follows that these events are conditionally independent, given the weights of
a
and all its ancestors,
W_{\varnothing},W_{a_{1}},W_{a_{1}a_{2}},\ldots,W_{a}
. Thus,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{}

\displaystyle(E_{a}\,|\,W_{\varnothing},W_{a_{1}},W_{a_{1}a_{2}},\ldots,W_{a})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}(a_{|_{I_{\ell+1}-1}})+\sum_{j=I_{\ell}}^{I_{\ell+1}-2}\mathcal{P}_{a_{j+1}}(a_{|_{j}})\leq\!\!\!\!\sum_{i=a_{I_{\ell}}+1}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!X(a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell}-1}i)\,\bigg|\,W_{\varnothing},W_{a_{1}},W_{a_{1}a_{2}},\ldots,W_{a}\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:stochastic-bound}}}{{\leq}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}(a_{|_{I_{\ell+1}-1}})+\sum_{j=I_{\ell}}^{I_{\ell+1}-2}\mathcal{P}_{a_{j+1}}(a_{|_{j}})\leq Y^{(a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell}-1})}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}\,\bigg|\,W_{\varnothing},W_{a_{1}},W_{a_{1}a_{2}},\ldots,W_{a}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}\,\Big|\,W_{\varnothing},W_{a_{1}},W_{a_{1}a_{2}},\ldots,W_{a}\right),

where each
Y^{(a_{1}\cdots a_{I_{\ell}-1})}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}
is independent and distributed like
Y_{a_{I_{\ell}}}
and does depend on the vertex-weights. Now, each of the events
\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}
are independent, as they depend on different weights. Hence, so are each of the terms appearing in the product, so that taking expectations on both sides yields

(3.14)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{a}\right)\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}\right).

We now let
d_{j}:=I_{j+1}-I_{j}-1
for
j\in[k-1]
and
d_{k}:=m-I_{k}
denote the number of entries between the running maxima of
a
. We can then define, for
(d_{j})_{j\in[k]}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
(with
[0]:=\varnothing
),

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{k}({}

\displaystyle a_{I_{1}},a_{I_{2}},\ldots,a_{I_{k}},d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})

\displaystyle:=\{a\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:\text{ For all }j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\text{ and all }i\in[d_{j}],\ a_{I_{j}+i}\in[a_{I_{j}}]\}

as the set of all sequences
a
with running maxima
a_{1}=a_{I_{1}},\ldots,a_{I_{k}}
, and
d_{j}
many entries between the
j^{\text{th}}
and
(j+1)^{\text{th}}
maximum. For brevity, we omit the arguments of
\mathscr{P}_{k}
. We then write the expected value in the proposition statement as

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\\
a_{1}>K^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{a}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a:|a|=m\\
a_{1}>K^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{a}\right)\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{I_{k}}>\ldots>a_{I_{1}}>K^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(d_{\ell})_{\ell\in[k]}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}d_{\ell}=m-k\end{subarray}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}\right).

In the first step, we introduce a sum over all sequence lengths
m
. In the second step, we furthermore sum over the number of running maxima
k
, the values of the running maxima
a_{I_{1}},\ldots a_{I_{k}}
, the number of entries
d_{\ell}
between each maxima
I_{\ell}
and
I_{\ell+1}
(or between
I_{k}
and
m
if
\ell=m
), and all sequences
a\in\mathscr{P}_{k}
that admit such running maxima and inter-maxima lengths. Moreover, we use (

) to bound
\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{a}\right)
from above, now that we know the number of running maxima in

.

We can now take the sum over

into the product, due to the fact that we can decompose each sequence

into a concatenation of sequences

, with

. This yields,

(3.17)

\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}_{k}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}\right)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{(\ell)}:|a^{(\ell)}|=d_{\ell}+1\\
\text{$a^{(\ell)}$ $a_{I_{\ell}}$-conservative}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}\right).

We can then directly apply Lemma
3.1
to each of the sums in the product. Indeed, as

, we can take

large enough to obtain, for some

, the upper bound

(3.18)

\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{(\ell)}:|a^{(\ell)}|=d_{\ell}+1\\
\text{$a^{(\ell)}$ $a_{I_{\ell}}$-conservative}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{P}\!\left(\widetilde{E}_{a,\ell}\right)\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\zeta^{d_{\ell}}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{a_{I_{\ell}}}}(Y_{a_{I_{\ell}}})\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{I_{\ell}}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}}).

We can take out the factors

and use that the

sum to

. Using this in (

) yields

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}{}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{a_{I_{k}}>\ldots>a_{I_{1}}>K^{\prime}}\zeta^{m-k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(d_{j})_{j\in[k]}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}d_{\ell}=m-k\end{subarray}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{a_{I_{\ell}}}}(Y_{a_{I_{\ell}}})\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{I_{\ell}}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m-1}{k-1}\zeta^{m-k}\sum_{a_{I_{1}}>K^{\prime}}\cdots\sum_{a_{I_{k}}>K^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{a_{I_{\ell}}}}(Y_{a_{I_{\ell}}})\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{a_{I_{\ell}}}}(\mathcal{P}_{a_{I_{\ell}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m-1}{k-1}\zeta^{m-k}\left(\sum_{n>K^{\prime}}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda_{n}}(Y_{n})\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{n}}(\mathcal{P}_{n})\right)^{k}.

Proof of Theorem

The desired result follows by verifying the conditions on

as in the statement of Theorem

It is clear that

and tends to zero by (

), so that
\lambda_{n}
is increasing and diverges. Then, by Assumption
2.10
, we fix

small and take

large so that for all

(3.24)

\mu_{n}^{-1}=\bigg(\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(w^{*})s(j)}\bigg)^{-1}\leq g(w^{*})\bigg(\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\eta j^{p}}\bigg)^{-1}=(\eta g(w^{*})(p-1)+o(1))n^{p-1}.

At the same time, again for
N
large and all

, we have

, so that

(3.25)

\lambda_{n}=\delta\log(n)\mu_{n}^{-1}\leq(\eta\delta g(w^{*})(p-1)+o(1))\log(n)n^{p-1}\leq(\eta^{2}\delta g(w^{*})(p-1)+o(1))\frac{\log(n)}{n^{(1+\beta)-p}}s(n).

The final condition of Assumption
2.10
then implies that

(3.26)

\lambda_{n}\leq(\eta^{2}\delta Cg(w^{*})(p-1)+o(1))\inf_{i\geq n}s(i),

for all

and some large

. As we can choose

(3.27)

\lambda_{n}=o(\inf_{i\geq n}s(i)),

as desired.

We then prove (
2.8
).
By the choice of

and assuming (without loss of generality) that

, we have that (
2.8
) is equivalent to

(3.28)

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\prod_{i=n}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{s(i)}{s(i)-\lambda_{n}}\bigg)\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{g(W)s(i)}{g(W)s(i)+\lambda_{n}}\right]<\infty.

By using that

for all

, we can bound each term in the sum from above by

(3.29)

\displaystyle\exp{}

\displaystyle\bigg(\lambda_{n}\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(i)-\lambda_{n}}-\lambda_{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k_{n}s(i)+\lambda_{n}}\bigg)+\exp\bigg(\lambda_{n}\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(i)-\lambda_{n}}\bigg)\mathbb{P}\!\left(g(W)>k_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(\lambda_{n}\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(i)-\lambda_{n}}-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{s(i)+\lambda_{n}/k_{n}}\bigg)+\exp\bigg(\lambda_{n}\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s(i)-\lambda_{n}}\bigg)\frac{1}{n^{1+\varepsilon}},

where we take

and use that

Then, by the choice of

\exp\bigg((\delta+o(1))\log(n)-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{s(i)+\lambda_{n}/k_{n}}\bigg)+n^{-(1+\varepsilon-\delta+o(1))}.
