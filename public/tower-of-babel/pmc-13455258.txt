Resilience of adolescents and young adults towards climate-related stressors - a systematic review
Authors: Elisabeth Schiechtl, Veronika Deisenrieder, Markus Bader, Thomas Marke, Lars Keller, Katharina Hüfner, Lisa Wintner, Marzieh Abdoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change represents an increasing source of stress for adolescents and young adults, contributing to higher risks of anxiety and impaired mental health.MethodsThis systematic review synthesizes current evidence on resilience to climate-related stressors among individuals aged 10–25 years. A systematic search of PubMed, PsycINFO, and from the Education Resources Information Center (ERIC) (PROSPERO: CRD42025644668) identified 6,975 individual references.ResultsTen studies met the inclusion criteria. The studies, conducted across high-income countries (e.g. USA, Germany, Australia), Asian LMICs (Thailand, Nepal), and sub-Saharan African countries with high climate and health vulnerability (e.g., South Africa, Uganda), comprised 9,439 participants with approximately equal gender distribution. Methodological designs included qualitative (n = 2), quantitative (n = 4), and mixed-method (n = 4). Quantitative studies applied standardized resilience scales (e.g., BRS, CYRM-R) or related constructs such as coping, self-efficacy, and disaster preparedness. Using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, 70% of studies were rated as high quality. Five interconnected protective domains were identified: connection to culture, community, and nature; supportive social networks; youth agency and engagement; education and preparedness; and psychological skills. Gender and prior disaster experience appeared as vulnerability factors. None of the included studies provided a clear definition of resilience, highlighting a conceptual gap in the current literature. Overall, resilience among young people appears as a dynamic, socially embedded process shaped by psychosocial and ecological systems.ConclusionsEducational and participatory interventions integrating climate literacy, emotional regulation, and collective action show promise for strengthening climate resilience and mental well-being among youth. Introduction

Climate change represents a significant global challenge affecting human health physically, psychologically, and socially [1] It intensifies extreme heat and heavy precipitation events [2] causing environmental damage and considerable psychological distress, particularly among adolescents and young adults [3].

Adolescence and early adulthood are critical developmental periods marked by ongoing brain maturation, increased academic and social demands, and the transition toward adult roles and responsibilities. These factors contribute to an elevated risk for psychological and behavioural problems compared to adults [4], a vulnerability that is further intensified by climate-related stressors [5]. As climate change presents an ongoing threat with long-term consequences throughout their lives, adolescents and young adults face heightened concern about the future, amplifying feelings of uncertainty [6].

Climate-related stress refers to a climate-altered socio-environmental system in which anthropogenic climate change shapes everyday life. This includes direct exposure to climate-related hazards, like floods, debris flows and indirect impacts like on social, economic, and environmental conditions, and awareness of the climate crisis alongside perceived insufficient societal action. Together, these factors can function as potential stressors for mental health and overall well-being, especially for vulnerable or marginalised populations, like people who are suffering from a mental illness or young people [7]. A social-ecological framework [8] conceptualises youth mental health risks as arising from interacting climate-related threats over time, including acute events, gradual environmental change, socio-economic disruption, and anticipatory awareness. Within this framework, young people are understood to live within a broader ecological system shaped by climate change, where physical, social, and symbolic dimensions co-occur and accumulate. Thus, being affected by climate change encompasses not only awareness or knowledge of the climate crisis but also direct exposure to climate-related stressors, whether fast- or slow-onset.

Climate change adversely affects mental health both directly, by causing anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress disorder through exposure to climate-related disasters [9, 10], and indirectly, through chronic worry and eco-anxiety (i.e. sadness and fear arising from awareness of climate threats) [11, 12]. Many young individuals report fear, helplessness, and disappointment, particularly when they perceive political and societal responses to climate change as insufficient [13]. Such responses can be highly prevalent and functionally relevant. In an international study [6], 60% of adolescents and young adults aged 16–25 reported being very worried or extremely worried about climate change, 84% reported moderate anxiety, more than 50% described emotions such as sadness, anger, helplessness, and guilt, and more than 45% indicated that these feelings and worries impaired their daily routines and functioning. It is important to clarify that emotional responses to climate change, which constitutes a significant threat to human health, should be interpreted as non-pathological unless the symptoms meet established diagnostic criteria for a mental disorder [14].

Given these mental health impacts, resilience, the process of managing stress and adapting to adversity or change [15], plays a crucial role in supporting young individuals. It is widely recognized as dynamic and evolving over time and is shaped by multiple factors [16], including the individual, family, community, and societal domains [17]. Resilience becomes apparent in response to stressors, emerging through efforts to avoid or withstand disruption [18].

In the context of the climate crisis, resilience involves not only recovery following a crisis but also the ability to learn, grow, and prepare for future challenges [19]. Since adolescents and young adults spend most of their daily lives in educational settings, schools represent an essential environment for delivering interventions and programs aimed at fostering resilience to climate-related adversity. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) underscores the importance of strengthening resilience to protect mental health and calls for research that can inform education, health, and social policy [20]. However, systematic evidence on psychological resilience to climate-related stressors in adolescents and young adults remains scarce, highlighting an urgent need for further investigation.

This review aims to synthesise current evidence on resilience factors in youth exposed to climate- related stressors, identify opportunities for intervention and provide input for future research and policy development.

Methods

This systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO international database (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42025644668, registration number CRD42025644668). The reporting adheres to the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews [21].

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on December 9, 2024, across three electronic databases: Pubmed, PsychINFO and ERIC. The search strategy aimed to identify peer-reviewed studies examining psychological resilience in towards climate related stress in adolescents and young adults. In the screening process, the term resilience served as the primary inclusion criterion, and no a priori working definition of resilience was applied. We included studies that evaluated interventions explicitly aiming to strengthen resilience towards climate related stress and/or examined resilience in relation to climate change–related stressors. To accommodate the syntax and indexing differences of each database, search terms were minimally adapted while preserving the core concepts of interest.

Eligibility criteria

Studies published in English or German between 2014 and 2025, focusing on adolescents and young adults (aged 10–25), were included. Eligible studies investigated resilience-enhancing or mental health interventions aimed at improving psychological resilience, as well as observational studies assessing resilience in the context of climate change. Intervention studies required a control group (waitlist, active, or alternative treatments), whereas observational studies did not. Reviews, book chapters, conference abstracts, and opinion papers were excluded. A detailed list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is presented in Table 1.

Screening and data extraction

Two independent reviewers (MA, ES) conducted the study selection process. Initial screening of titles and abstracts (N = 6975) was performed using the software DistillerSR [22]. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (KH) when consensus could not be reached. Following screening, 43 full-text articles were retrieved and managed in Excel [23] for full-text review. The reviewers independently assessed the full texts and subsequently discussed discrepancies, resulting in a final inclusion of 10 articles for data extraction. Quality assessment

Study quality was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [24], enabling evaluation of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method designs. Two reviewers (MA, ES) independently conducted the quality appraisal, with any unresolved disagreements adjudicated by a third reviewer (LMW). The MMAT assesses methodological quality without providing an overall score however, a cut-off of meeting at least 4 out of 5 criteria was used to classify studies as high quality, while those meeting three or fewer criteria were considered low quality.

Results

Study selection

A total of 7035 records were identified through database searches (PubMed: 4140; ERIC: 264; PsycINFO: 2631). After removing 60 duplicates, 6975 records were screened by title and abstract, resulting in 6932 exclusions. Full texts of 43 reports were assessed for eligibility, with 33 excluded due to study population, publication type, intervention/outcome, or context criteria. Ultimately, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review, indicating that empirical evidence on this topic remains limited. Due to methodological heterogeneity, quantitative pooling was not feasible. This heterogeneity was driven primarily by variation in outcome assessment (validated resilience scales versus resilience-related proxies), differences in primary versus secondary endpoints, intervention characteristics, and the timing of outcome measurement. In addition, the small number of studies included in the final synthesis would further limit the interpretability and robustness of any pooled quantitative estimates. Qualitative findings were synthesized narratively.

Study characteristics

The studies included samples of individuals aged 10 to 25 years and were conducted across diverse geographic regions, with many originating from areas particularly vulnerable to climate change. A total of 9,439 participants were included across the studies, with most investigations comprising both female and male participants. One study [25] focused exclusively on a female sample. Most studies did not report sex or gender differences in resilience or related traits and behaviours. However, one study [26] examined the influence of gender and prior disaster experience on attitudes toward disaster preparedness. Two of the included studies employed a qualitative research design [27, 28], while four studies utilized a quantitative approach [29–32]. Four studies adopted a mixed-methods design [25, 26, 33, 34].

All included studies referenced resilience at some point, however, only two studies [29] explicitly focused on resilience in terms of the primary research objective. In the remaining studies, resilience was addressed more peripherally, typically appearing in discussion or conclusion sections rather than as a central analytical theme. Notably, none of the studies provided a clear or operational definition of resilience in the context of climate change. (Table 2).

Framing and operationalizing of resilience in the context of climate change

The included studies were related to adolescents’ resilience to climate-change-related stress. Most of these studies addressed adolescents’ resilience to climate-change-related stress indirectly, by assessing both protective factors (e.g. family functioning) and adaptive outcomes (e.g. change of cultural practices), rather than defining resilience as an explicit primary endpoint. Among the quantitative studies, two used standardized resilience scales, Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) [35] and Child and Youth Resilience Measure-Revised (CYRM-R) [36] to directly measure resilience [29]. Other quantitative studies assessed resilience-related factors such as transformative experience [34], self-efficacy and emotion regulation [30], stress and family functioning/social support [32], knowledge, attitudes, beliefs [26], as well as agency, sense of community, and confidence [25].

Qualitative studies expanded this perspective by emphasising cross-cultural and gendered dimensions of resilience [25], the dual framing of climate change as both a global and deeply personal stressor [34], the importance of collective, democratic youth action and solidarity [27], and the role of connection to nature and cultural identity [28]. Socioecological and cultural embeddedness

Across several studies, resilience was closely linked to strong connections to culture, community, and the natural environment. Petrasek MacDonald et al. [28] highlighted the importance of being on the land, including traditional knowledge, cultural values, and practices such as eating country foods, being in nature, and engaging in subsistence activities. These were closely tied to connection to Inuit culture, strong community ties, supportive relationships, and staying busy. Engagement with local environments also emerged as an important factor, with Gallay et al. [27] showing that place-based civic science fosters meaningful interaction with local contexts, interpreted as supporting eco-resilience.

Similarly, Inpin et al. [26] found that resilience was associated with inherited local knowledge from ethnic minority backgrounds in communities frequently exposed to environmental hazards.

Supportive social networks and collective dimensions

Supportive social environments were identified as another important dimension of resilience. Heinemeyer et al. [25] highlighted the role of cross-cultural connection, appreciation of diverse perspectives, and increased empathy, as well as the importance of connection with like-minded peers and confidence in discussing climate-related emotions. These findings point to the importance of relational and collective processes in fostering resilience.

Agency and engagement

A second key theme relates to the role of active engagement and participation in fostering resilience. Several studies highlighted that opportunities for adolescents to actively engage with climate-related issues enhanced motivation, agency, and perceived relevance.

For example, Leckey et al. [34] demonstrated that participation in a student filmmaking program promoted personal relevance, motivation to act, curiosity about climate science, and recognition of both local and global climate impacts, with more than 50% of participants rating the programme as transformative. Similarly, Hoernke et al. [33] identified new opportunities for learning and engagement through participation in citizen science initiatives, alongside challenges related to technology use.

In addition, Heinemeyer et al. [25] emphasized that young people can actively contribute to community resilience by engaging with climate mitigation alongside broader social and economic inequalities.

Literacy and preparedness

Several studies indicated that climate-related knowledge and preparedness are important components of resilience. Inpin et al. [26] emphasized the role of higher education as a key actor in supporting disaster recovery and public education through research-based knowledge.

Similarly, Leckey et al. [34] found that participants developed increased awareness of both local and global climate impacts, as well as the environmental and social consequences of climate change. Hoernke et al. [33] further highlighted the role of participation in citizen science as a means of developing knowledge and experience related to climate change.

Psychological resources

At the individual level, resilience was associated with a range of psychological resources and adaptive capacities. Bandeira et al. [29] identified feeling safe as a significant predictor of both overall and personal resilience. Similarly, Lass-Hannemann et al. [30] found that expressive flexibility and self-efficacy were significant predictors of mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, and health-related quality of life.

Qualitative findings further emphasized adaptability as a key protective factor, with Petrasek MacDonald et al. [28] describing how youth adjusted traditions to new environmental contexts and expressed acceptance of change (e.g., “We just learn to deal with it”).

Risk factors and vulnerabilities

In addition to protective factors, several studies highlighted risk factors and vulnerabilities associated with climate-related stress. Meltzer et al. [32] showed that family-level factors played a significant role, with adolescents in families with low functioning having 1.81 times greater odds of distress, those whose caregivers coped poorly with parenting demands having 2.85 times greater odds, and those with caregivers reporting low mental health having 3.14 times greater odds of psychological distress. Furthermore, Inpin et al. [26] found that gender and prior disaster experience significantly influenced students’ attitudes toward disaster preparedness (p < 0.05), highlighting the importance of tailoring resilience-building interventions to individual backgrounds and experiences.

Lykins et al. [31] indicate that exposure to severe bushfire events is associated with elevated levels of depression, anxiety, stress, and climate-related distress, alongside reduced psychological resilience among young people compared to non-exposed peers.

Quality appraisal

Studies Leckey et al. [34], Inpin et al. [26], Petrasek MacDonald et al. [28], Gallay et al. [27], Lykins et al. [31], Bandeira et al. [29], Hoernke et al. [33] received higher quality ratings according to the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, whereas studies Heinemeyer et al. [25], Lass-Hannemann et al. [30], Meltzer et al. [32] were assessed as having comparatively lower methodological quality.

Discussion

The small number of articles (n = 10) included in the final analysis shows a significant research gap and suggests that adolescent resilience to climate change related stressors is an underexplored, yet important field. The included studies were conducted across 18 countries spanning high-, middle-, and low-income contexts in Africa, Europe, North America, Asia, and Oceania. This geographic diversity reflects markedly different climate exposure profiles, socio-economic conditions, and cultural understandings of resilience. These contextual differences likely shape how resilience is expressed and sustained, potentially shifting its emphasis from individual psychological adaptation to collective, relational, and community-based processes. At the same time, this heterogeneity may have contributed to variability in resilience outcomes and limits direct comparability across studies.

As most of the included studies did not provide a definition of resilience when referring to the climate-related stress, this presents a conceptual challenge. A similar issue is reported in a literature review on eco-anxiety [37]. To ensure the conceptual clarity, validity and comparability of findings, it is essential to clarify how resilience is defined, operationalised, and actively cultivated to support mental health when dealing with climate-related stress [38]. Two studies employed validated resilience scales [29, 31] whereas the remaining studies relied on resilience-related proxies or qualitative assessments.

Resilience in this setting should be conceptualized more broadly, extending beyond immediate adaptive responses to encompass psychological, social, and educational dimensions. It is also important to consider varying cultural conceptualisations and lived experiences of resilience, and the need for definitional clarity to be grounded in diverse knowledge systems, cultures, disciplines and worldviews. Accordingly, studies should adopt culturally grounded, trauma- and equity-informed designs developed with BIPOC youth and communities to avoid pathologizing adaptive emotional responses and to ensure that research questions, measures, and supports are contextually appropriate [27].

Resilience efforts should be youth-centred, incorporating youth-identified priorities through creative and participatory science approaches and cross-sectoral collaboration among schools, local authorities, researchers, health professionals, and community members to mobilise complementary expertise and resources (e.g., infrastructure and funding) [25, 28, 34]. Co-creating programmes is suggested [25].

Evidence suggests that delivering climate change literacy in a way that supports adolescents’ sense of agency and self-efficacy may foster more meaningful engagement with climate challenges across personal, societal, environmental, and political domains [39]. Moreover, addressing individual and collective resilience within Climate Change Education provides an opportunity to articulate a more comprehensive understanding of resilience, grounded in a moderate constructivist perspective on learning [40]. Within this framework, the personal conceptions held by adolescents and young people both on topics of climate change and personal resilience can be systematically explored, forming a foundation upon which targeted educational interventions may be developed. Simultaneously, it is essential to provide emotional support in addressing the climate crisis. To date, this component has only been partially integrated into climate change literacy programmes. However, its inclusion is crucial to enable young people to develop emotional literacy and to grow into actively engaged and responsible citizens [41].

This requires moving beyond disciplinary instruction toward “science-in-context,” linking climate science to socially significant issues [42]. Addressing real-world problems and locally tangible climate-impacts connects learning to students’ lifeworlds, fostering critical reflexivity, participation, and action [43]. Such approaches build the knowledge, skills, and attitudes central to education for sustainable development (ESD) competencies [44], enabling students to “understand, value, and relate to the world differently,” while deepening their engagement with science, community, and culture [45]. Furthermore, climate change education that moves beyond individual behaviour change and enables collective action, civic participation, and ethical reflection have been shown to buffer against eco-anxiety and support constructive engagement [6].

Much of the existing evidence on resilience-building interventions for young people has focused on adversities in school settings [46], whilst little is known about resilience building interventions targeting climate-related stress. To address both the psychosocial and ecological dimensions of resilience [47], the existing frameworks could be used as a basis for creating targeted programmes for climate-related distress.

Some limitations of this review should be noted. These include potential publication bias, a geographic concentration of studies in certain regions (particularly high-income contexts), and heterogeneity that limits generalisability.

Future directions.

Future research should adopt a multi-level approach that moves beyond an exclusive focus on individual-level resilience to also incorporate collective and community resilience [48]. Strengthening common resilience factors that have been shown to support recovery from adversity across diverse stress contexts [46] remains crucial. Concurrently, climate-specific components need to be integrated, including educational approaches that foster a scientifically grounded understanding of the climate crisis as a global and systemic issue. This entails knowledge of its anthropogenic causes, projected impacts (e.g., increased frequency of heatwaves and extreme weather events, and capacities to engage in mitigative and adaptive solution approaches), and the exacerbation of social inequalities and physical and psychological health outcomes.

A methodologically important step is to measure resilience using specific, validated scales and to examine correlations between these scales and commonly used proxy measures to clarify the extent to which they converge or capture related aspects of resilience. Evidence would benefit from identifying the most effective approaches to support young people’s resilience capacities and to inform educational and policy initiatives [49]. However, there is currently no robust evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of the approaches listed in these climate-specific studies, when compared to resilience enhancing interventions in other contexts [15]. Competencies such as coping strategies, climate change literacy, and opportunities for engagement and action, though promising, require rigorous evaluation for their impact on resilience in the context of climate-related challenges.

As resilience trajectories remain poorly understood, future studies should examine how climate– related stressors modulate resilience over time. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how repeated or multiple exposures shape young people’s experiences, coping processes, and mental health over time [32]. Quantifying climate change exposure [30] and integrating temporally and spatially high-resolution climate models could enable more consistent and valid measurement of climate-related impacts, improve comparability across studies, and better capture the chronic and acute environmental changes to which populations are exposed.

Research on gender differences in climate-related distress and resilience remains scarce. Existing evidence indicates that girls and young women experience higher levels of climate-related worry and impairment than boys and young men [50], likely reflecting broader gendered vulnerabilities rooted in social and cultural inequalities [51]. This research gap highlights the need for gender-sensitive approaches in building psychological resilience to ensure interventions are effective and equitable. Climate change events disproportionately affect the mental health of vulnerable populations. Individuals with low socioeconomic status, minoritised ethnic or racial backgrounds, Indigenous communities, and those experiencing housing insecurity tend to experience poorer mental health outcomes following climate-related events compared to less vulnerable groups [52]. Incorporating intersectional analyses of sex/gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and culture and examining broader systemic and justice-related factors that influence resilience of youth in the context of climate change is essential.

Psychology can play a vital role in fostering climate change resilience in youth, not only by providing targeted psychological health support but also by developing climate-informed assessment tools and therapeutic approaches at both the individual and community level. At the systemic level, psychology contributes to addressing climate change by conducting research, guiding policy development, and shaping organizational practices [53]. While large-scale public health interventions are essential for fostering resilience, it is important to acknowledge that responsibility cannot be placed solely on youth, who often perceive their agency as limited [54]. Accordingly, climate change resilience in youth must be understood within the broader context of public health, which is shaped by complex systems with multilevel determinants and interdependent factors, structural interventions at the system level and individual behaviours contribute, underscoring the need for integrative, ecological approaches across all levels [55].

Conclusion

The reviewed studies collectively reveal a complex, multidimensional portrait of resilience among adolescents navigating the realities of climate change. Rather than residing in a single trait or intervention, resilience emerges as a dynamic process shaped by psychosocial, cultural, and contextual factors. Notably, the studies come from countries that are affected by climate change to varying degrees and in different ways, adding further nuance to how resilience manifests across contexts. These insights not only highlight promising protective factors but also point toward key opportunities—and challenges—for supporting young people as the climate crisis intensifies.

Across many of the included papers, strong and supportive relationships—be they with family, peers, or wider communities—repeatedly surface as central pillars of adolescent resilience. These findings suggest that resilience is not merely about “bouncing back,” but also about “leaning in”—drawing strength from cultural identity, collective action, and a sense of purpose in community and environmental stewardship. Place-based approaches and active participation in environmental education and civic science, such as filmmaking or citizen science programs, appear particularly suitable for cultivating these qualities.

Taken together, these studies suggest that adolescent resilience in times of climate change is best understood as a collective, context-dependent, and action-oriented process. Effective resilience-building strategies should focus on adaptive as well as transformative and action-oriented aspects while safeguarding social support and relationships.

To understand what is required to effectively foster psychological resilience toward climate change we need to adopt an interdisciplinary approach to integrate knowledge of climate risks, psychological processes and learning environment.
