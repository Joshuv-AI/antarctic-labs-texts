ACAD8-mediated valine catabolism inhibits mTOR signaling to suppress lipid accumulation and progression of clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Runkun Fan, Zirui Dong, Weisong Wu, Yujie Jia, Hourui Tan, Qi Wang, Xiaoping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•WGCNA identified a lipid and branched-chain amino acid metabolism–related module closely associated with ccRCC clinical features.•ACAD8 was identified as a key gene downregulated in ccRCC, with low expression predicting poor prognosis.•ACAD8 suppressed ccRCC proliferation, migration, invasion, and lipid accumulation both in vitro and in vivo.•Mechanistically, ACAD8 promoted valine degradation, thereby inhibiting mTOR/SREBP1 signaling and lipogenesis.•Valine supplementation reversed the tumor-suppressive effects of ACAD8, confirming a valine-dependent regulatory mechanism.Keywords: ccRCC, ACAD8, Lipid accumulation, Valine

Background

Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC) is a malignant genitourinary tumor and one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide. In the United States, an estimated 80,980 new cases of kidney and renal pelvis cancer and 14,510 related deaths were projected for 2025 [1]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the main pathologic type of RCC and accounts for about 75%−80% of RCC [2]. Approximately one-third of patients with RCC present with distant metastases at diagnosis [3]. Despite advancements in treatment, advanced or metastatic RCC continues to have a poor prognosis, with a median overall survival ranging from 46 to 56 months [4].

Lipid droplets are evolutionarily conserved organelles, encapsulated by a phospholipid monolayer, that safely store hydrophobic neutral lipids, such as cholesterol esters and triglycerides, within the aqueous cytosol [5]. ccRCC has a hallmark histological feature of lipid droplet formation and metabolic reprogramming profile [6]. Moreover, lipid accumulation also plays a pivotal role in the progression of ccRCC [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Understanding these metabolic alterations offers valuable insights into the tumorigenesis and progression of ccRCC, providing new opportunities for the development of targeted therapies that exploit the metabolic vulnerabilities of these cancer cells [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

ACAD8 (Acyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Family Member 8) is a mitochondrial enzyme crucial for the β-oxidation of branched short-chain fatty acids, particularly in the catabolism of valine. It catalyzes the initial dehydrogenation step of isobutyryl-CoA to (2E)-2-methylpropenoyl-CoA, which is a key reaction in the valine degradation pathway [15,16]. Despite its important metabolic function, there are currently few studies that focus on the role of ACAD8 in cancer. Meanwhile, valine, as one of the branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), plays a role in tumor progression through various mechanisms [[17], [18], [19]]. And targeting valine metabolism has been a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer [[20], [21], [22]]. However, the metabolism of valine in ccRCC and its role in tumor progression remain poorly understood.

In this study, we identified ACAD8 as a prognostic factor for ccRCC using Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). Our study indicated that ACAD8 is expressed at lower levels in renal cancer cell lines and tissues compared to normal cell lines and tissues and loss of ACAD8 promoted lipid accumulation and progression. Mechanistically, loss of ACAD8 impairs valine catabolism, resulting in valine accumulation and subsequent activation of mTOR signaling. This activation promotes SREBP1 and its downstream lipogenic targets, including FASN and SCD, thereby enhancing lipogenesis.

Methods

Dataset

Data related to the TCGA-KIRC project were retrieved from the UCSC Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net/). Additional data from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were also utilized (GSE53757).

Bioinformatics analysis

Detailed methods and principles have been described in previous studies [23,24]. Transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information for ccRCC patients were obtained from the TCGA-KIRC dataset via the UCSC Xena platform [25]. After data preprocessing and quality control, a total of 522 tumor samples with complete clinical information and 72 adjacent normal tissue samples were included for analysis. Differential genes were identified using the “limma” package [26] with criteria of P value < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1. For each differentially expressed gene, patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups using the median expression level as the cutoff, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the survival package. Genes showing significant differences in overall survival (P < 0.05) were selected. These differential genes were then used for weighted co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) with a soft threshold of 3 [27]. The genes were categorized into 12 modules based on their expression patterns. Functional enrichment analysis of genes in the brown module was performed using DAVID. To assess the diagnostic potential of ACAD8 expression in ccRCC tissue, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. Based on ACAD8 expression, ccRCC patients were divided into two groups: high-expression and low-expression, using the median expression level as the cutoff. Overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS) and Progression-free interval(PFI) for these two groups were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed by dividing samples into high- and low-expression groups according to the median expression of ACAD8 with GSEA software (version 4.1.0, USA) [28,29].

Some of the Gene expression correlation analysis was performed using the GEPIA platform (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), which integrates TCGA and GTEx datasets. Correlations were calculated using the Spearman method with default parameters. Gene expression correlations were calculated using Spearman correlation analysis. Correlation strength was defined as weak (|r| < 0.3), moderate (0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5), and strong (|r| ≥ 0.5).

ccRCC tissues and cell lines

20 pairs of ccRCC tissues and corresponding normal tissues were obtained from patients at the Department of Urology, Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. None of the patients received adjuvant therapy prior to surgery. The study was conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Human Research Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology and adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration.

Common RCC cell lines, including ACHN, 786-O, A498, Caki-1, and OSRC2, as well as the normal renal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2. All cell lines were authenticated via Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling before experimentation. The culture medium consisted of 10% fetal bovine serum added to DMEM medium with 1% 100 × penicillin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 under controlled conditions.

Western blots

Similar methods have been documented in previous studies [30]. For protein extraction, cells or ground tissue were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer, supplemented with proteasome and phosphatase inhibitors, and incubated for 30 min. During this time, the samples were sonicated three times for 10 s each. Afterward, bicinchoninic acid (BCA) reagent (P0010, Beyotime, China) was added to the lysate, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to measure protein concentration. The lysates were then mixed with 5 × loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. The protein mixtures were resolved by SDS-PAGE for 70 min, and then transferred to a 0.22 µm PVDF membrane (03,010,040,001, Roche, Swiss Confederation). To block non-specific binding, the membrane was incubated in 5% skimmed milk for 90 min. The membrane was then incubated sequentially with primary and secondary antibodies. After three washes with TBST, the proteins were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (BL520B, Biosharp, China). A detailed list of primary and secondary antibodies used in this study can be found in supplementary Table 1. Depending on the molecular weight of the target protein, prestained protein molecular-weight markers providing reference bands at 10, 15, 25, 35, 45, 60, 75, 100, 140, 180, 245, and approximately 310 kDa were used, and the relevant marker positions are indicated in the immunoblot figures.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Experimental procedures were conducted according to the methods outlined in a previous study [31]. RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent and the RNApure Tissue & Cell Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. For reverse transcription, 1 µg of RNA was utilized, and the resulting cDNA was used in qPCR reactions with 2 × Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (G3328, Servicebio, China). ACTB was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. The sequences of primers used in this study are provided in supplementary table 2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

Fresh tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a series of ethanol washes, followed by antigen retrieval using hydrogen peroxide and blocking with bovine serum albumin. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and color development. Finally, structurally intact areas were selected for microscopic imaging. Detailed information on the primary antibodies used in this study is provided in supplementary table 1.

Lentiviral construction, and transfection

shRNA was used to knock down ACAD8. The construction of lentivirals for the overexpression or knockdown of ACAD8 was obtained from Genechem Co. Ltd (China). ccRCC cells were transfected following the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

CCK-8 assays

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. ccRCC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. After treatment under the indicated conditions, 10% of total volume of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1–2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

3D colony formation assays

For 3D cell culture, cells were cultured in standard 2D conditions until 70–80% confluence, then detached using trypsin/EDTA solution and resuspended in appropriate culture media (DMEM or RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, depending on the cell line). After counting, an appropriate number of cells (approximately 2–5 × 10³ cells per 50 μL Matrigel mixture per well) were mixed with cold Matrigel at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v) on ice. The Matrigel-cell mixture (50 μL per well) was then pipetted into each well of a 24-well plate chamber slide, ensuring an even layer, and incubated at 37 °C for 10–30 min to allow solidification. Once Matrigel® solidified, 500–1000 μL of complete medium was added, and the cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The medium was changed every 2–3 days. After the formation of 3D structure, pictures were taken for further analysis [32].

EdU assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay. ccRCC cells were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates and cultured under the indicated conditions. EdU (10 µM) was added to the medium and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with a Click-iT reaction mixture for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Transwell assays

Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chambers. For the migration assay, ccRCC cells (5 × 10⁴ per well) were suspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber. For the invasion assay, the upper chamber was precoated with Matrigel (Corning, USA), and 1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, cells remaining in the upper chamber were removed using a cotton swab, while migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were captured under a microscope, and cells were counted in at least five randomly selected fields per well using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assays

ccRCC cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured until they reached nearly 100% confluence. A sterile 200-μL pipette tip was used to create a uniform scratch in the cell monolayer. The detached cells were gently washed away with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fresh serum-free medium was added to each well. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 h, 36 h and 48 h using an inverted microscope. The wound closure rate was quantified by measuring the gap area at each time point using ImageJ software.

2D colony formation assays

ccRCC cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 500–1000 cells per well and cultured in complete medium for approximately 10–14 days until visible colonies formed. The medium was replaced every three days. After incubation, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. The colonies were then washed, air-dried, and photographed. Colonies consisting of >50 cells were counted using ImageJ software.

BODIPY staining

To assess intracellular lipid accumulation, BODIPY staining was performed. ccRCC cells were seeded in six-well plates and cultured under the indicated conditions. After treatment, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature.

Following fixation, cells were incubated with BODIPY 493/503 at a final concentration of 2 µM for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings across all experimental groups.

For quantitative analysis, images were processed using ImageJ software. A consistent threshold was applied to all images to identify lipid-positive regions, and the lipid droplet–positive area was quantified and normalized to the total cell area.

BCAA(Valine, leucine, isoleucine) measurements

Intracellular branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) levels were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (valine: CB12470-Hu; leucine: CB12471-Hu; isoleucine: CB12472-Hu; COIBO BIO, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with modifications for cellular metabolite extraction. Briefly, cells were cultured under indicated conditions and harvested at approximately 70–80% confluence. Culture medium was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove extracellular metabolites. Cells were then collected using a cell scraper and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. For intracellular metabolite extraction, cell pellets were resuspended in pre-cooled 80% methanol (−20 °C), followed by vigorous vortexing and incubation on ice for 10 min to precipitate proteins and extract soluble metabolites. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were transferred to new tubes. The collected supernatants were dried using a vacuum concentrator and subsequently reconstituted in the sample buffer provided with the ELISA kits. After thorough mixing, samples were subjected to ELISA analysis.

Valine concentrations were determined using a competitive ELISA format, while leucine and isoleucine were measured using corresponding ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, standards and samples were added to 96-well plates, followed by incubation with enzyme conjugates. After washing, substrate solution was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated from known concentrations.

All samples were analyzed in duplicate. Intracellular BCAA levels were normalized to cell number (nmol per 10⁶ cells). Tissue samples (50 mg per sample) were normalized to tissue weight.

TG (triglyceride) and T-CHO (total cholesterol) measurements

Following the manufacturer's instructions, cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes and then lysed using 100 μl of 2% Triton X-100. Separate groups were set up for blank, calibration, and sample, with four replicate wells in each group. The groups were then incubated with a total cholesterol assay kit or a triglyceride assay kit in a 37 °C cell culture incubator for 10 min. The signal values of each group were measured at a wavelength of 500 nm. Finally, the total cholesterol and triglyceride content for each group were calculated according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

Tumor formation assay and valine supplementation in mice

Animal experiments were conducted following approval from the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Stable ACAD8-overexpressing 786-O cell lines were generated using lentiviral transfection. The cells were harvested, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS before being subcutaneously injected into the axillae of 4-week-old Balb/c nude mice. The mice were housed in pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Biosafety Level 3 Laboratory at Huazhong University of Science and Technology. A total of 5 × 10⁶ cells were injected per mouse. The mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment for approximately 30 days. Tumor growth was monitored every 5 days starting from day 10 post-injection. After the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, photographed, and subsequently used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Oil Red O staining.

Valine supplementation was administered by dissolving l-valine in the drinking water at a final concentration of 3% (w/v). The selected concentration was based on previous studies investigating dietary valine supplementation in murine models [33]. Supplementation was initiated after tumor establishment, when tumors reached a measurable size, and was maintained continuously throughout the experimental period.

Oil red O staining

Frozen tissue sections (5–10 μm) were prepared using a cryostat and air-dried at room temperature. The sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10–15 min and then rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with 60% isopropanol for 1–2 min to facilitate staining. Oil Red O working solution (freshly prepared by diluting a stock solution in isopropanol with distilled water at a ratio of 3:2 and filtered before use) was applied to the sections and incubated for 10–20 min at room temperature. After staining, sections were washed with distilled water to remove excess dye and counterstained with hematoxylin for 30–60 s if required. Finally, the sections were mounted with aqueous mounting medium and imaged under a light microscope. Oil Red O staining was quantified using ImageJ software. Briefly, the positively stained area and staining intensity were measured, and lipid accumulation was calculated as the integrated optical density (IOD), defined as staining intensity multiplied by positive area, normalized to the total tumor tissue area. This value was used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were performed using Student's t-test or paired Student's t-test, as appropriate. The association between ACAD8 expression and clinical characteristics of ccRCC patients was assessed. Univariate Cox regression analyses were carried out with SPSS 25.0. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The schematic diagram depicting the proposed mechanism was created using Biorender. ns: nonsignificant, P ≥ 0.05; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. Error bars represent the mean ± SD.

Results

WGCNA identified the hub module for ccRCC

To investigate potential prognostic factors in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), we downloaded transcriptomic and clinical data from the TCGA-KIRC dataset via UCSC Xena. After data preprocessing and quality control, the analyzed dataset included 522 tumor tissue samples with complete clinical information, along with 72 adjacent normal kidney tissue samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing normal versus tumor tissues, using thresholds of |logFC| > 1 and P value < 0.05 (Fig. 1A). Among these DEGs, 2,817 were identified as prognostically significant based on overall survival (OS). Briefly, for each gene, patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups using the median expression level, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed. Genes showing significant differences in OS (P < 0.05) were considered prognostically significant. To further analyze the expression patterns of these 2,817 DEGs, we conducted weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). The gene dendrogram and module colors were visualized, and a scale-free network was established with a soft threshold of β = 3 (Fig. 1B). Modules with similarity ≥0.75 were merged, resulting in 11 co-expression modules plus the grey unassigned module (Fig. 1C–D and S1A-S1B). These modules were then correlated with clinical traits, with the brown module showing the most significant association with factors such as T-stage (r = −0.26, P = 6e-09), TNM-stage (r = −0.27, P = 5e-10), and G grade (r = −0.31, P = 8e-13) (Fig. 1E and S1C). As a result, the brown module, containing 396 genes, was identified as the hub module. Module membership was positively correlated with gene significance for M stage (Fig. 1F). Gene Ontology (GO) and REACTOME pathway analyses revealed that the genes in the brown module were mainly involved in metabolic pathways, such as branched-chain amino acid catabolism and lipid metabolism (Fig. 1G). These findings implicated branched-chain amino acid catabolism and lipid metabolism in the ccRCC-associated brown module.

Low ACAD8 expression in ccRCC predicted poor prognosis

By intersecting lipid metabolism-related gene sets and branched-chain amino acid metabolism-related gene sets obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, GSEA website), together with genes from the brown module identified by WGCNA, we identified ACAD8 and ACADSB as the key genes shared between all gene sets (Fig. 2A). As the role of ACADSB in RCC has already been explored [34], we focused our attention on ACAD8. Analysis of data from the TCGA-KIRC project and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database revealed that ACAD8 expression was significantly lower in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2B–D). Further analysis revealed that ACAD8 expression decreased with the progression of clinical staging, including T stage, N stage, TNM stage, and G grade (Fig. 2E–H). The Kaplan-Meier curves indicated that low ACAD8 expression was associated with poorer overall survival (OS), disease specific survival (DSS), and Progression-free interval(PFI) (Fig. 2I–K). ROC analysis showed that ACAD8 expression distinguished ccRCC tissues from adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2L). Univariate regression analysis further confirmed that ACAD8 expression serves as an prognostic factor (Table 1). To validate the above findings, we confirmed the low expression of ACAD8 in ccRCC at the mRNA level in 20 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues and at the protein level in 12 paired samples (Fig. 2M–N and S5A). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) results further corroborated these findings (Fig. 2O). Subsequently, compared to HK-2, ACAD8 expression was significantly lower in RCC cell lines (Fig. 2P–Q).

To explore the underlying mechanisms contributing to the reduced expression of ACAD8 in RCC, we analyzed its copy number variation (CNV) and promoter methylation status in the TCGA-KIRC cohort using the GSCA database [35]. As shown in Figure S2A, CNV alterations of ACAD8 were observed at relatively low frequencies, including both amplifications (5.68%, predominantly heterozygous amplification at 5.49%) and deletions (5.30%, predominantly heterozygous deletion at 5.11%).

Correlation analysis revealed a modest positive association between ACAD8 expression and CNV levels, and a negative correlation with promoter methylation (Figures S2B and S2D). However, given the low frequency and limited magnitude of CNV alterations, these data do not support a dominant role for CNV in regulating ACAD8 expression.

In contrast, ACAD8 promoter hypermethylation was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal controls (Figure S2C), and was associated with poorer patient outcomes (Figures S2E–S2G).

Collectively, these findings suggest that promoter hypermethylation, rather than CNV alterations, is more likely to contribute to the downregulation of ACAD8 in ccRCC.

ACAD8 inhibited ccRCC proliferation and migration in vitro

Given the promising role of ACAD8 as a prognostic biomarker in ccRCC, we further investigated its involvement in ccRCC progression. Given the lower expression of ACAD8 in 786-O and OSRC-2, we selected these two cell lines for stable ACAD8 overexpression using lentivirus (Fig. 3A–B). CCK-8 assays revealed that ACAD8 overexpression significantly inhibited the proliferation of ccRCC (Fig. 3C). Both 2D and 3D colony formation assays showed that ACAD8 overexpression inhibited the colony-forming ability of ccRCC cells (Fig. 3D–G). 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays further demonstrated that restoring ACAD8 expression suppressed cell division in ccRCC (Fig. 3H). In addition, transwell assays demonstrated that ACAD8 overexpression inhibited the migration and invasion of ccRCC cells (Fig. 3I–J). Wound healing assays yielded similar results(Fig. 3K–L).

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of ACAD8's function in ccRCC, we stably knocked down ACAD8 in A498 and Caki-1 cell lines, which have relatively high ACAD8 expression, using lentivirus (Fig. 4A–B). It was found that ACAD8 knockdown promoted cell proliferation, colony formation, and division in ccRCC cells (Fig. 4C–F). Additionally, transwell and wound healing assays demonstrated that knocking down ACAD8 enhanced the migration and invasion capabilities of ccRCC cells (Fig. 4G–I). These gain- and loss-of-function experiments indicate that ACAD8 suppresses ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro.

ACAD8 inhibited SREBP1 activation and lipogenesis in ccRCC

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) results suggested that ACAD8 potentially regulates lipid biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, using the GEPIA database [36], we discovered that ACAD8 expression was significantly negatively correlated with the expression of FASN and SCD, which were involved in lipogenesis (Fig. 5B). Therefore, we hypothesized that ACAD8 may be associated with abnormal lipogenesis in ccRCC. By using BODIPY to label lipid droplets, we found that ACAD8 overexpression significantly inhibited lipid droplet accumulation in ccRCC (Fig. 5C and S3A). The levels of triglycerides and total cholesterol in the cells were measured, and it was found that ACAD8 overexpression significantly reduced the triglyceride content in ccRCC cells, while the reduction in total cholesterol content was not significant (Fig. 5D and S3B). Among the key regulators of lipogenesis, the SREBP family, including SREBP1 and SREBP2, plays a crucial role [37]. While SREBP2 regulates cholesterol resynthesis and uptake, SREBP1 is primarily involved in fatty acid synthesis. The activation of SREBP1 depends on its own cleavage, which releases the active N-terminal fragment of SREBP1. This fragment then translocates to the nucleus, initiating the transcription of downstream genes such as FASN and SCD, thereby promoting lipogenesis [[38], [39], [40]]. Subsequently, results of western blots and qPCR showed that ACAD8 overexpression did not alter the precursor level of SREBP1, but significantly inhibited its cleavage and activation, leading to a decrease in the expression of FASN and SCD (Fig. 5E–G and S5B). Additionally, in stable ACAD8 knockdown ccRCC cells, we observed a significant increase in neutral lipid droplets and triglyceride content (Fig. 5H–I and S3C). SREBP1 activation was also detected using western blots, along with elevated expression of FASN and SCD using qPCR (Fig. 5J–K, S3D, and S5C). These results suggested that the loss of ACAD8 activated SREBP1 in ccRCC, leading to increased expression of FASN and SCD and enhanced lipogenesis.

ACAD8 promoted valine catabolism and inhibited mTOR signaling activation

ACAD8 was recognized as a key enzyme in valine catabolism, and its overexpression promoted valine degradation [15,41]. Similarly, Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed by stratifying TCGA-KIRC samples into ACAD8-high and ACAD8-low groups based on the median expression level. The results showed that branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) degradation-related pathways were significantly enriched in the ACAD8-high group (Fig. 6A), suggesting a potential association between ACAD8 expression and BCAA catabolism. To validate this, we measured the levels of leucine, isoleucine, and valine in ccRCC cells following stable ACAD8 overexpression using corresponding assay kits. The results showed that ACAD8 significantly reduced valine levels, whereas its effect on the other two branched-chain amino acids was not significant (Figs. 6B and S4A-S4B). Furthermore, in ccRCC cells with stable ACAD8 knockdown, we confirmed that the loss of ACAD8 led to an increase in valine concentration (Fig. 6C). Consequently, we focused on valine metabolism in ccRCC and found that valine levels were significantly elevated in ccRCC tissues compared to normal kidney tissues (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, ACAD8 expression was negatively correlated with valine content in ccRCC tissues (Fig. 6E). These findings indicated that ACAD8 promoted valine degradation, and its low expression in ccRCC led to valine accumulation in tumor cells.

Previous studies have shown that branched-chain amino acids contribute to the activation of mTOR signaling, while mTOR activation significantly stimulates SREBP1, promoting de novo lipogenesis [42,43]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on the TCGA-KIRC cohort, in which samples were stratified into ACAD8-high and ACAD8-low groups according to the median expression level, indicated that ACAD8 expression was associated with mTOR signaling (Figure S4C). In addition, DepMap analysis revealed that ACAD8 expression correlated with sensitivity to the dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor apitolisib (Figure S4D), suggesting a functional link between ACAD8 and mTOR signaling. Consistently, mTOR signaling activity was significantly suppressed in ccRCC cells with stable ACAD8 overexpression, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of mTOR and its downstream target S6K (Fig. 6F and S5D). In contrast, ACAD8 knockdown enhanced mTOR signaling activation (Fig. 6G and S5E). Together, these results indicate that ACAD8 negatively regulates mTOR signaling in ccRCC.

Given that ACAD8 is involved in valine catabolism as previously discussed, we next investigated whether valine mediates the effect of ACAD8 on mTOR signaling. Analysis using the STITCH database suggested a potential association between valine and the mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 6H) [44]. To explore the underlying mechanism, we predicted potential valine-interacting proteins using Swiss Target Prediction and constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using STRING (Fig. 6I) [45]. Notably, no direct association between predicted ACAD8 and core components of the mTOR pathway was identified, suggesting that ACAD8 is unlikely to regulate mTOR signaling through direct interaction.

Previous studies have reported that valine can activate mTOR signaling via TAS1R1 in certain contexts [46]. However, TAS1R1 knockdown did not significantly affect ACAD8-mediated regulation of mTOR signaling in ccRCC cells (Fig. 6J–L and S5F), indicating that this pathway is not involved in our model.

Given the known role of amino acids such as leucine in activating mTOR through Rag GTPases [47], we further examined whether valine functions via a similar mechanism. Our results demonstrated that valine promotes the interaction between Rag and Raptor, thereby activating mTOR signaling in RCC (Fig. 6M and S5G).

ACAD8 inhibited lipogenesis and tumor progression by promoting valine degradation

The above results showed that ACAD8 promoted valine catabolism and inhibited mTOR signaling. To determine whether ACAD8 suppresses lipogenesis and tumor progression by regulating valine levels, some rescue experiments were conducted. Valine was supplemented in ACAD8-overexpressing ccRCC cells, and western blot results revealed that the inhibitory effects of ACAD8 on mTOR and SREBP1 were effectively reversed upon valine addition (Fig. 7A and S5H). This indicated that ACAD8 regulates mTOR and SREBP1 activation in a valine-dependent manner. CCK-8 and 3D colony formation assays demonstrated that valine supplementation significantly restored the proliferative and colony-forming abilities of ccRCC cells inhibited by ACAD8 overexpression (Fig. 7B–D). The EdU assays demonstrated that ACAD8 inhibited tumor cell division in a valine-dependent manner (Fig. 7E–F). Furthermore, transwell assays showed that valine supplementation significantly restored the migratory and invasive abilities weakened by ACAD8 overexpression (Fig. 7G–H). Subsequently, we assessed intracellular lipid droplets and found that the addition of valine significantly restored the reduction in lipid droplets induced by ACAD8 overexpression (Fig. 7I–J). Taken together, ACAD8 inhibited lipogenesis and tumor progression by promoting valine degradation.

ACAD8 inhibited lipogenesis and ccRCC progression in vivo

Building on the in vitro experiments, we further investigated the effects of ACAD8 on tumor growth and lipid accumulation in vivo. 786-O cells with stable ACAD8 overexpression were constructed using lentivirus and injected subcutaneously into nude mice. Subsequently, valine levels in mouse tumors were supplemented by adding valine to their diet. Our results demonstrated that ACAD8 overexpression led to a reduction in tumor size compared to the control group, whereas regular administration of a valine-enriched diet in mice counteracted this tumor-suppressive effect (Fig. 8A–C). Subsequently, the subcutaneous tumors were analyzed through immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Oil Red O staining. The IHC results revealed a marked decrease in Ki67 staining in the ACAD8-overexpression group, indicating that ACAD8 suppressed tumor cell proliferation in vivo (Fig. 8D–E). IHC analysis of ACAD8, mTOR, and SREBP1 also demonstrated that ACAD8 inhibited mTOR signaling and reduced nuclear SREBP1 (N-SREBP1) expression in vivo (Fig. 8D–E). Using a valine assay kit, we found that a high-valine diet restored the ACAD8-induced decrease in valine levels (Fig. 8F). Moreover, Oil Red O staining showed that ACAD8 overexpression significantly decreased lipid accumulation in ccRCC, while the addition of valine markedly promoted the restoration of lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 8G).

Based on above findings, we concluded that the loss of ACAD8 led to an increase in valine levels, which in turn activated mTOR signaling, subsequently triggering SREBP1 activation. This promoted the expression of FASN and SCD, ultimately resulting in lipid accumulation and progression of ccRCC (Fig. 8H).

Discussion

As one of the most lethal urological cancers, ccRCC poses a significant global health burden due to its high incidence, aggressive nature, and frequent resistance to conventional therapies. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy, metastatic ccRCC remains a major clinical challenge with limited treatment options and poor prognosis [2,4,48]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms driving RCC progression, including key signaling pathways and metabolic alterations, is essential for identifying novel therapeutic targets and improving patient outcomes. In this study, we performed WGCNA analysis using the TCGA-KIRC database and identified ACAD8 as a key regulatory gene in ccRCC. ACAD8 was significantly downregulated in ccRCC, and its low expression correlated with poor patient prognosis. Mechanistically, ACAD8 deficiency disrupts valine catabolism, leading to valine accumulation and subsequent activation of the mTOR/SREBP1 axis. This cascade promotes lipogenic reprogramming and lipid accumulation, ultimately facilitating tumor progression. Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), primarily including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, have garnered significant attention in tumor metabolism research in recent years. Beyond serving as fundamental building blocks for protein synthesis, BCAAs play critical roles in energy metabolism, signal transduction, and tumor microenvironment remodeling [17,49,50]. Our study identifies ACAD8 as a key catabolic enzyme in ccRCC, whose downregulation leads to valine accumulation, resulting in mTOR activation and subsequent lipid accumulation and progression of ccRCC. These findings provide novel insights into the role of BCAA metabolism in lipid accumulation in ccRCC.

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway is a central regulator of cell growth, proliferation, and metabolism. Aberrant activation of mTOR has been observed in various cancers, and dysregulation of mTOR signaling contributes to tumor development and progression by promoting uncontrolled cell division and survival [51]. In particular, mTORC1 (mTOR complex 1) can phosphorylate and activate SREBP1 (sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1), thereby upregulating fatty acid synthesis-related genes such as FASN (fatty acid synthase) and SCD (stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1), promoting de novo lipogenesis [52,53]. Consequently, targeting the mTOR pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in oncology, especially ccRCC [54]. Our study demonstrates that accumulated valine acts as an agonist of mTOR signaling in ccRCC. Interestingly, previous studies have reported that valine can activate mTOR signaling through the heterodimeric amino acid receptor TAS1R1/TAS1R3 and intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling in non-cancerous systems [46]. However, depletion of TAS1R1 did not significantly affect ACAD8-mediated regulation of mTOR signaling in our study, suggesting that this receptor-dependent mechanism may not be predominant in ccRCC. One possible explanation is that intracellular valine accumulation resulting from impaired ACAD8-mediated catabolism engages intracellular amino acid sensing pathways distinct from extracellular receptor-mediated signaling. In line with this, our data support a model in which valine activates mTOR signaling through a Rag GTPase-dependent mechanism [47], highlighting a context-dependent mode of amino acid sensing in cancer cells.

From a translational perspective, our findings may have several potential clinical implications. First, ACAD8 expression could serve as a prognostic biomarker, as its downregulation is associated with poor patient outcomes. Second, given the role of valine accumulation in driving mTOR activation, dietary modulation of branched-chain amino acid intake or metabolic interventions targeting BCAA catabolism may represent a complementary therapeutic approach. However, these possibilities require further investigation in preclinical and clinical settings.

In conclusion, our study identifies ACAD8 as a critical regulator of valine metabolism and mTOR signaling in ccRCC, providing new insights into the interplay between amino acid metabolism and lipid metabolic reprogramming. These findings may offer potential avenues for biomarker development and therapeutic intervention in ccRCC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights ACAD8 as a promising prognostic biomarker and a metabolic vulnerability in ccRCC. Mechanistically, ACAD8 suppresses tumor progression by regulating mTOR signaling and lipogenesis through valine catabolism. These findings link BCAA metabolism with lipid accumulation and may inform future therapeutic strategies for ccRCC.
