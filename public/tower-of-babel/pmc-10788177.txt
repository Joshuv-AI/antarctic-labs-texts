Changes in population structure and genetic diversity of Misaki horses between 2015 and 2020
Authors: Ikuo KOBAYASHI, Kotono NAKAMURA, Ibuki SAITO, Masaru AKITA, Teruaki TOZAKI, Masamichi MIYAZAKI, Kazuki HANO, Masaki TAKASU
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
For the preservation of Misaki horses, changes in the population structure and genetic diversity of the horses for 5 years were analyzed using population and genotype data from 2015–2020. The microsatellite genotyping was performed, and the average number of alleles (Na), expected heterozygosity (He), and observed value (Ho) were calculated. Moreover, the average generation length (GL) was estimated from the population management record. Then, no significant differences in Na, He, and Ho were found between 2015 and 2020, suggesting their genetic diversity had been maintained for 5 years. Moreover, the average GL was estimated as 4.6 years. Compared to other native horses, a short average GL suggesting a rapid generation renewing is a characteristic of the Misaki population.

Misaki horses, one of the eight native Japanese horses, are maintained in a semi-feral state at Cape Toi of the Miyazaki prefecture, southern Kyushu Island of Japan [2, 5]. The horses have been raised freely on pasture, which established in 1697 as a Takanabe clan ranch for approximately 300 years at Cape Toi. Since the ranch was founded, the horses have been kept with very little care, but a routine patrolling for the management of the environment and an annual event called Uma-Oi. At the time of the domain, “Koma-oi” was held once a year to capture young horses for training as war horses, and this event continues until today and is known as “Uma-oi” for health checkups and deworming of the Misaki horses (Fig. 1Fig. 1.At an annual event called Uma-Oi, horses are gathered at one place for health checkups and deworming. Such semi-feral horses and its surrounding environment are quite rare not only in Japan, but also worldwide; therefore, “the semi-feral horses of Cape Toi” has been designated as a natural monument of Japan [2, 3].

However, because the Misaki horses are semi-feral, there are many unknowns regarding their conservation biology [3]. Under such circumstance, we analyzed the genetic diversity of the horses using microsatellites in 2015, and acquired basic information on their genetic diversity and pedigree [3]. Since then, annual microsatellite genotyping of the horses has been performed using collected blood samples at Uma-oi.

Herein, using population and genotype data from 2015–2020, changes in the population structure and genetic diversity of Misaki horses for 5 years were analyzed to determine a critical point of view for the preservation of the horses.

This study was designed following the Regulations for Animal Experiments at Gifu University and was approved by the institution’s Committee for Animal Research and Welfare (approval number #15137). The published data in 2015 were used for the re-analysis of changes in the population structure and genetic diversity of Misaki horses [3]. The data in 2015 were obtained from 77 horses born between 1996 and 2015 (39 females and 38 males), and the data in 2020 were from 72 horses (39 females, 31 males, and 2 geldings born between 2003 and 2020).

An aliquot portion of blood samples collected at Uma-oi were analyzed in this study. From the blood samples with added EDTA as anticoagulant, DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN K.K., Tokyo, Japan). A total of 31 microsatellite markers (AHT4, AHT5, ASB2, ASB17, ASB23, CA425, and HMS2, HMS3, HMS6, HMS7, HTG4, HTG10, LEX33, TKY19, TKY28, TKY321, VHL20, TKY279, TKY287, TKY294, TKY297, TKY301, TKY312, TKY325, TKY333, TKY337 341, TKY343, TKY344, TKY374, TKY394) were used [9, 12] and were genotyped as previously reported [3, 6,7,8, 10, 11]. Allele discrimination was based on the complementation panel adopted at the International Society of Animal Genetics workshop to determine equine parentage [9]. From these results, the average number of microsatellite alleles (Na), expected heterozygosity (He), observed value (Ho), and fixation index (Fis) were calculated using GENEPOP [4]. The average Na, He, and Ho were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Data were statistically evaluated using paired t-test, and statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Next, the number of horses born and died during the 5-year period was extracted from the population management record of the Misaki horses. Then, the average generation length (GL), reflecting speed of generation change of the horses, was confirmed from the management data on their reproductive life span and age at first reproduction: the average GL was defined as the horse age that gave birth to the first offspring [1].

The Na, He, and Ho values for 2015 were calculated as 3.4 ± 1.2, 0.497 ± 0.194, and 0.508 ± 0.207, respectively; those for 2020 were 3.2 ± 1.1, 0.487 ± 0.208, and 0.503 ± 0.224, respectively; (Fig. 2Fig. 2.Comparison of Na (average number of allele), He (expected heterozygosity), and Ho (observed heterozygosity) of Misaki horses between 2015 and 2020. The Na, He, and Ho values in 2020 were 3.161 ± 1.081, 0.487 ± 0.208, and 0.503 ± 0.224, respectively, and were not different from those in 2015, which were 3.419 ± 1.185, 0.497 ± 0.194, and 0.508 ± 0.207, respectively. Data in 2015 were from our previous study (Kobayashi et al., J Vet Med Sci. 81: 707–711, 2019). Bar indicates SD.). Moreover, the Fis in 2015 and 2020 were −0.021 and −0.022. For all data, no significant differences were found between 2015 and 2020, suggesting no significant change in the genetic diversity of the Misaki horses for five years.

According to the population management records [3], we found population replacement for 5 years of Misaki horses. In 2015, there were 95 individuals, and in 2020, there were 111 individuals, indicating an increase of 16 individuals. Break down the numbers as follows: 112 births and 85 deaths. Of the 111 horses in 2020, 85 died in 5 years; thus, 76.6% of the horses had been replaced within 5 years. Moreover, Misaki horses gave birth to their first offspring at three to six years old. Of the 19 Misaki horses that had their first offspring between 2015 and 2020, two were three years old, eight were four years old, five were five years old, and four were six years old at the time of their first birth. Thus, the average GL was estimated as 4.6 years.

The Na, He, and Ho in 2020 were 3.2 ± 1.1, 0.487 ± 0.208, and 0.503 ± 0.224, respectively. Compared with other Japanese native horses (Na, He, and Ho in Kiso horses were 6.3, 0.674, and 0.662; those in Yonaguni horses were 4.4, 0.591, and 0.601; those in Miyako horses were 4.2, 0.701, 0.649; those in Tokara horses were 3.0, 0.424, 0.481) [6,7,8, 11], the Misaki horses have smaller value in the heterozygosity of microsatellites, suggested to be a vulnerable population. However, comparing the heterozygosity between 2015 and 2020, the genetic diversity of the Misaki horses has been maintained. In addition, compared with the 9.9 years of the average GL of Kiso horse, a Japanese native horse, the average GL of Misaki horse is short [10]. A short average GL means a rapid generation renewing, which is a characteristic of the Misaki population.

In this study, we revealed changes in the population structure and genetic diversity of the Misaki horse between 2015 and 2020. Information about Misaki horses as we obtained in this study is important for their preservation, and to increase tools for the proper preservation of Misaki horses, it is necessary to conduct further research on the conservation of the Misaki horse.
