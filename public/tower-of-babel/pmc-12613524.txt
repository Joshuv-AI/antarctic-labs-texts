Double inlet left ventricle in a 12-year-old European bison: a case report with anatomical assessment and postmortem computed tomography evaluation
Authors: A Godlewska, K Barszcz, H Przespolewska, W Bielecki, K Zabiega, M Buczyński, W Mądry, K Szymczak, M Kwiatkowska, M Grzegorczyk, O Szaluś-Jordanow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDouble inlet left ventricle (DILV) is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly characterized by a functionally univentricular atrioventricular connection, in which most or all atrial inflow is directed to the dominant, morphologic left ventricle via either two separate atrioventricular valves or a common atrioventricular valve. The second ventricle is typically a rudimentary (incomplete) morphologic right ventricle. While well-documented in humans, the occurrence of DILV in veterinary medicine remains poorly studied. This report describes a male European bison (Bison bonasus) that survived for 12 years with DILV and additional cardiac malformations. Surprisingly, despite the severity and complexity of these defects, the animal was culled for reasons unrelated to cardiac disease. A complete field necropsy was performed, but only the heart was subjected to further investigation. Due to specimen disruption, the heart was reconstructed with the assistance of pediatric cardiac surgeons and analyzed using post-mortem computed tomography imaging, which provided valuable insights into its anatomical structure and spatial relationships. This case highlights the importance of integrated pathology and advanced imaging in congenital heart disease, while acknowledging the practical limitations of ante-mortem investigations in free-ranging animals.Keywords: Congenital heart defect (CHD), Functionally univentricular heart (FUVH), Double inlet left ventricle (DILV), Ventricular septal defect (VSD), Atrial septal defect (ASD), Post-mortem computed tomography (PMCT), European bison, Wisent

Introduction

Functionally univentricular heart (FUVH), historically and somewhat imprecisely, referred to as a single ventricle, is broadly used to describe a group of rare congenital heart defects (CHDs) in humans, in which one ventricle is morphologically and/or hemodynamically dominant. At the same time, the contralateral one is rudimentary and incomplete [1–4]. Among the various FUVH phenotypes, double inlet left ventricle (DILV) is one of the most well-documented in human cardiology and accounts for approximately 2% of CHDs in humans [3, 5–8]. The exact etiology remains unclear. Sporadic genetic factors are thought to play a role, and familial occurrences have also been reported. Advances in prenatal echocardiography have significantly improved early detection, facilitating better management of this condition [5, 7, 9].

DILV is defined by a double inlet atrioventricular (AV) connection to the morphologic left ventricle (mLV), with inflow through either two separate AV valves or a common AV valve. The contralateral ventricle is typically a rudimentary morphologic right ventricle (mRV). In line with the sequential, morphology-based nomenclature, the description of a DILV heart should specify the AV connection, including the presence and extent of any overriding/straddling, as well as ventricular topology as right- or left-handed, the ventriculoarterial (VA) connections, great artery relationships, and the nature of the interventricular communication or ventricular septal defect (VSD), specifying its site and (non-) restrictiveness [2, 6, 7, 10–12]. VA connections (concordant/discordant/double outlet) should be described separately from arterial relationships (right–left/dorsal-ventral), avoiding conflation of these independent features [10]. As DILV seldom occurs in isolation, associated anomalies typically involve abnormalities of the AV junction/valves (beyond those required to achieve a double inlet connection), altered VA connections and great-artery relationships, such as transposition, and outflow tract obstructions, such as pulmonary or aortic stenosis. A secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) may occasionally be present, though in most cases, the atrial septum remains intact [5–7, 13–16].

DILV produces an admixture physiology, with the mLV supporting both systemic and pulmonary circulations. Depending on the VA connections and any outflow obstruction, this may result in volume and/or pressure loading, leading to chamber dilatation, hypertrophy, and progressive dysfunction over time [6, 10, 11, 17, 18]. The direction and magnitude of the interventricular shunt depend on systemic and pulmonary vascular resistances, as well as the presence of outflow lesions. Both increased and reduced pulmonary blood flow have been reported, e.g., discordant VA connections with a non-restrictive VSD versus pulmonary atresia/stenosis or a restrictive VSD. Therefore, pulmonary hypertension (PH) secondary to volume overload is not universal [1, 11].

In both human and veterinary medicine, echocardiography, particularly two-dimensional and Doppler imaging, serves as the primary diagnostic tool, allowing for visualization of the dominant mLV, AV valve attachments, and any associated defects, such as VSD or outflow tract obstructions [12, 15, 16, 19–21]. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) are valuable for more detailed structural and spatial assessment. Additionally, cardiac catheterization and angiography may be performed to evaluate hemodynamic parameters, measure pulmonary pressures, and assess vascular anatomy [12, 15].

Given the severity and complexity of DILV, surgical intervention is necessary in most cases early in life to ensure long-term survival in human patients. Treatment typically involves neonatal palliation tailored to pulmonary blood flow, with pulmonary artery banding for overcirculation or a systemic-to-pulmonary shunt for undercirculation, followed by staged cavopulmonary connections and ultimately Fontan completion to establish a functionally univentricular circulation. The goal is to route systemic venous return directly to the pulmonary arteries, unloading the single systemic ventricle and balancing pulmonary blood flow [3, 4, 6, 7, 10, 17, 21]. While the Fontan procedure effectively simplifies the circulatory system and reduces ventricular volume load, it carries specific risks, and patients must meet rigorous criteria to qualify for the surgery [5, 12]. The mortality rate between stages is notably high, ranging from 5% to 15%, although it has decreased significantly with procedural modifications [3]. The Fontan procedure is not feasible in neonates due to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance [7]. Although long-term survival after univentricular repair appears favorable, these patients require lifelong, specialized follow-up [5, 7, 12]. Recently, alternative approaches, including staged ventricular septation, have been considered, with promising early outcomes, as reported by Prasanna et al. [22].

Despite extensive documentation in human medicine, studies on DILV in veterinary species remain scarce. This limits our understanding of clinical presentation, natural history, and the compensatory arrangements that may permit survival without intervention, particularly in free-ranging animals where ante-mortem imaging is seldom feasible.

Case description

The specimen was a male European bison (Bison bonasus), also known as a wisent, approximately 12 years old, weighing 680 kg. Trail-camera footage showed that the animal remained with the herd, only slightly lagging. A full-body necropsy revealed scattered degenerative changes on the articular surfaces of the thoracic and pelvic limb joints, chronic catarrhal, post-inflammatory lesions consistent with previous parasitic infestation, and marked periorbital swelling, accompanied by corneal opacity with superficial erosions/ulcerations, conjunctival hyperemia, oedema, and anterior uveitis. The ocular lesions were characteristic of thelaziosis, supporting the decision to humanely cull the affected bison. Anatomopathological examination also identified significant cardiac anomalies, prompting the submission of the heart for further diagnostic evaluation.

Initial necropsy incisions had partially disrupted the specimen, including the absence of the central venous inflows, transection of the arterial roots with loss of the coronary ostia and proximal segments of the coronary arteries, and suboptimal opening of the cardiac chambers, thereby complicating the assessment of the heart’s geometry and spatial orientation. On external inspection, both atrial appendages were of similar size, triangular in outline, with a broad base and wide junction to the atrial cavity. They constituted the predominant portion of the externally visible atrial wall, features consistent with right appendage morphology on both sides (Figs. 1 and 2). The right-sided AV valve exhibited overriding and straddling, with predominant chordal attachments to the mLV. It had three distinct leaflets, while the left-sided AV valve had two. Notably, one leaflet of the left AV valve showed fibrous continuity with the aortic valve (Fig. 3). Taken together, these features strongly support the identification of the right AV valve as tricuspid and the left AV valve as mitral. The pulmonary trunk was positioned on the right and ventrally. At the same time, the aorta was located centrally and dorsally, wedged between the atria, consistent with concordant VA connections and normally related great arteries (Fig. 4). The pulmonary valve had three thin, fully mobile leaflets. No subpulmonary or subaortic obstruction was noted. Ventricular relationships were compatible with a dominant mLV and right-handed topology. The mLV exhibited a markedly thicker wall compared to the mRV. A large VSD was identified, involving the entire inlet portion of the interventricular septum and most of the trabecular (muscular) part. Additionally, a defect in the atrial septum was visualized. Collectively, these findings established the diagnosis of DILV with associated lesions (Figs. 5 and 6).

We collaborated with pediatric cardiac surgeons with 20 years of experience to restore the heart’s structural configuration using a stepwise suturing approach. Following reconstruction, the heart was filled with a contrast medium as described by Szaluś-Jordanow et al. [23, 24]. Subsequently, contrast-enhanced PMCT was performed on a 16-slice helical scanner (Toshiba TSX-034 A) using 1-mm collimation at 120 kV with automatic exposure control. This imaging modality allowed for the assessment of heart geometry, chamber size, and their anatomical relationships in three-dimensional spatial reconstructions. CT scans confirmed DILV with a large, non-restrictive VSD and ASD. It further delineated the VSD extent (inlet and muscular components) and excluded discrete subpulmonary or subaortic outflow tract obstruction. PMCT findings were concordant with the necropsy impression of overriding and straddling of the right-sided AV valve (Figs. 7 and 8). The right ventricle was hypoplastic, although it retained inflow and outflow. Owing to the sectioning of the specimen, the pectinate muscles, the coronary sinus, and the systemic and pulmonary venous connections could not be fully assessed.

Discussion

Patients with unoperated FUVH anatomy are generally believed to have a poor long-term prognosis. Survival into adulthood with preserved ventricular function and good functional status is uncommon. Within this spectrum, most live-born infants with DILV proceed to univentricular palliation [7]. Without surgery, death typically occurs in the first years of life from heart failure, hypoxaemia, or complications of chronic cyanosis [7, 18, 25]. Only a minority avoid surgery because their systemic and pulmonary circulations remain relatively balanced, or unfavorable hemodynamics make them ineligible for such an intervention [4, 12, 25]. Rare adult survivors without palliation have been reported, e.g., a 28-year-old patient with patent ductus arteriosus and elevated pulmonary resistance present from birth [18]. In a cohort of FUVH (34% with DILV), managed without Fontan, predominantly due to pulmonary vascular disease and PH, the median survival was 46 years, albeit with increased risks of mortality, heart failure, and arrhythmias [4, 17]. Individual reports also include a 46-year-old woman with DILV, transposition of the great arteries (TGA), pulmonary stenosis (PS), and moderate AV-valve regurgitation who maintained good single ventricle systolic function and completed three caesarean deliveries without complications, and a woman in her 60 s with DILV who remained active on medical therapy alone, with a normal pulmonary valve and normally related great arteries (NRGA) [5, 26]. Exceptional long-term survival has also been documented in some cases, even into the seventh or eighth decade, solely with appropriate medical management, challenging conventional expectations regarding the natural history of DILV in the absence of corrective surgery [5, 12, 18, 25, 27].

Overall, the data suggest that survival without palliation can occur, albeit infrequently. Patients having DILV and concurrent PS may represent an ideal phenotype for long-term survival due to the “balanced” circulation this combination creates. The preferential streaming of oxygenated blood to the systemic circulation helps maintain adequate oxygen delivery to tissues. At the same time, PS serves a protective role by shielding the pulmonary vasculature from volume overload [4, 12, 25, 26]. By contrast, longevity in DILV without PS into later decades is even more uncommon [5, 26].

The wisent’s pulmonary valve was morphologically normal, with three fully mobile leaflets and an annulus slightly larger than the aortic annulus. These findings argue against valvular PS and do not suggest proximal pulmonary arterial hypoplasia. Such an arrangement would typically favor elevated pulmonary blood flow after the postnatal decline in pulmonary vascular resistance, predisposing the heart initially to volume overload heart failure and, subsequently, to PH if left unaddressed [4, 12, 25, 26]. Since only the heart was submitted for further examination, it is impossible to rule out the possibility that the pulmonary arteries were narrowed in their distal segments. As previously reported, the heart and lungs should be examined together whenever feasible to provide a comprehensive view of the cardiovascular pathology [23].

In selected human cases, prolonged survival has been attributed to effective pharmacological therapy combined with lifestyle modification and activity restriction, enabling a relatively good quality of life [5, 25]. Particularly, pulmonary vasodilators may be crucial in managing unoperated FUVH patients with unprotected pulmonary circulation, potentially improving their prognosis [25]. By necessity, free-ranging wisents expend substantial energy on movement and foraging, which limits the scope for activity restriction, let alone sustained pharmacological therapy, in the face of cardiopulmonary disease or any other condition. Finally, Poterucha et al. emphasize the need for further research to refine treatment strategies and identify factors contributing to the long-term survival of patients with FUVH [25].

There are only a few documented cases of domestic animals with DILV in the veterinary medicine literature. One report describes a case of a 9-day-old calf with a cardiac murmur and no clinical symptoms [28]. The calf was eventually euthanized, and it is unknown how the defect would have affected its development or what its future prognosis would have been. Another article discusses the case of a 4-year-old horse. The animal was clinically stable for over one year. Therefore, no therapy was instituted [8]. Besides DILV, both of these animals were diagnosed with a hypoplastic RV and a VSD (there were two VSDs in the foal).

Additionally, the foal had mitral valve stenosis [8, 28]. DILV has also been reported in three bovids and one equid on post-mortem examination [8]. One of them was a three-year-old horse with a history of poor growth and exercise intolerance. The animal was euthanized [29].

In the case of small mammals, this CHD has also been documented, albeit exceedingly rarely. One such report describes a four-month-old dog that presented with poor body condition, growth retardation, coughing, and reduced exercise tolerance. The animal suffered cardiac arrest during angiography and did not survive [30]. More recently, Peterson et al. reported a case of a one-year-old cat that was referred for further evaluation due to the presence of a grade V/VI heart murmur. The owners reported decreased energy levels and intermittent episodes of abdominal breathing following physical exertion. The diagnosis of DILV was established through comprehensive echocardiography, fluoroscopy with non-selective angiography, and computed tomography angiography (CTA). Despite the severity of its cardiac abnormalities, the cat remained clinically stable and did not require pharmacological intervention. Follow-up assessments conducted two and a half years after the initial diagnosis revealed no significant progression of the disease [15].

These cases pertain exclusively to young animals. In contrast, we reported an incidentally discovered DILV with straddling and overriding of the right AV valve in a male European bison originating from the Bieszczady Mountains. To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of this nature in a free-ranging wildlife species. Wisents generally have a lifespan of 14 to 24 years [31]. Remarkably, this individual survived until the age of 12 before being culled for reasons unrelated to its cardiac condition. The animal remained in good condition, weighing 680 kg, which is typical for its age and species. For comparison, in the wild-living population of the Białowieża forest, the average weight of adult males is estimated to be around 634 kg [31]. Based on observations from camera trap recordings, the wisent moved slightly more slowly than the rest of the herd. It should be emphasized that all blind wisents, including this individual, whose blindness was attributed to thelaziosis, move less confidently. However, it is difficult to determine whether its slightly reduced pace compared to the herd was due to ocular pathology, arthropathy, cardiac disease, or a combination of these conditions. No ante-mortem investigations were performed. Diagnostics such as echocardiography are not realistically feasible in large, free-ranging animals.

In our specimen, the right-sided AV valve overrode the septum and showed predominant straddling with chordal attachments within the mLV, whereas the contralateral ventricle was a rudimentary mRV. In contemporary morphology, DILV sits on a continuum of AV connections, rather than representing a discrete, all-or-none category [2, 10, 11]. At one end are hearts with a biventricular AV connection and mild overriding of the right-sided AV valve across the septum. With increasing straddling, the effective commitment of inflow to the mLV becomes predominant [10]. Beyond a certain threshold of annular commitment and chordal insertion, the arrangement is best described as a functionally univentricular AV connection, classically termed double inlet to the mLV [2, 10]. Quantitative cut-offs are not uniform across series. Therefore, the description should specify the direction of annular overriding, the extent of straddling, and the ventricle receiving the predominant inflow [10]. Overriding denotes that the AV junction/annulus bridges the septum so that portions of the valvar orifice open into both ventricles. Straddling refers to the chordae/leaflets inserting into both ventricles [10]. These phenomena usually coexist and are frequently accompanied by septal malalignment, which is relevant for the size and location of the interventricular communication [10, 11].

In our specimen, the constellation of a large, non-restrictive interventricular communication, right-handed ventricular topology, and concordant VA connections with NRGA is consistent with the historical Holmes heart within the straddling/overriding DILV spectrum [2, 11].

Assigning atrial appendage isomerism requires demonstrating bilateral appendage morphology based on the extent of pectinate muscles relative to the AV junction, together with corroboration from the coronary sinus anatomy and systemic/pulmonary venous arrangements [2, 10]. In our specimen, the left-atrial wall was incompletely preserved, the coronary sinus orifice could not be confidently identified, and lungs with great veins were unavailable, precluding a complete venous assessment. While some macroscopic features were suggestive of right-sided morphology bilaterally, a definitive assignment of atrial appendage isomerism was not feasible [2, 10].

Despite suboptimal acquisition parameters and a partially disrupted specimen, iterative post-processing and surgical reapproximation yielded a diagnostically valuable two and three-dimensional PMCT dataset. This challenging reconstruction clarified spatial relationships and anatomical connections that were not fully appreciated during the initial necropsy. It also allowed detailed delineation of the right-sided AV valve, including its multiple attachments within the left ventricle. Together, these results underscore the importance of combining traditional anatomopathological examination with advanced imaging modalities, such as CT, to comprehensively understand complex cardiac anomalies.

Conclusions

The main contribution of this report is the sequential, morphology-based documentation of a Holmes-heart variant within the DILV spectrum, emphasizing how gross reconstruction and PMCT together resolve the AV connection (overriding/straddling), ventricular topology, VA relationships, and the nature of the interventricular communication in a European bison. This incidentally identified complex CHD in a 12-year-old free-ranging male underscores the spectrum of AV connections in DILV and illustrates that long-term survival without intervention can occur in selected hemodynamic constellations.

Features suggestive of right atrial appendage isomerism were present, but we intentionally refrained from a definitive diagnosis because venous connections and the coronary sinus could not be assessed.

A deeper understanding of such anomalies could enhance veterinary cardiology and provide new insights for further research on cardiovascular health, potentially offering valuable perspectives relevant to human medicine as well.

Limitations of this study

Detailed information on the animal’s overall health and medical history is unavailable. Critically, specimen acquisition was suboptimal, as only the heart was submitted, and prior sectioning had partially disrupted and incompletely preserved key structures. These constraints did not alter the final anatomical diagnosis but limited the scope of hemodynamic interpretation.

We acknowledge that our study did not map the specialized conduction system. Prior work illustrates the typical course of the AV node/bundle relative to the VSD in DILV. It underscores the value of advanced techniques (e.g., micro-CT or histologic serial sectioning) when available. Such analysis could further refine morphologic interpretation in future veterinary cases.
