Emergent cavity-QED dynamics along the edge of a photonic lattice
Enrico Di Benedetto, Xuejian Sun, Marcel A. Pinto, Luca Leonforte, Chih-Ying Chang, Vincent Jouanny, Léo Peyruchat, Pasquale Scarlino, Francesco Ciccarello

Abstract
We investigate qubits coupled to the boundary of a two-dimensional photonic lattice that supports dispersionless edge modes – unlike conventional edge modes that sustain propagating photons. As a case study, we consider a honeycomb lattice ("photonic graphene") of coupled resonators with a zigzag edge, where the edge modes form a flat band defined only over a restricted region of momentum space. We show that light–matter interactions are effectively captured by a dissipative cavity-QED model, wherein the emitter coherently couples to a fictitious cavity mode emerging as a superposition of edge modes. This mode has support on only one sublattice and, most notably, displays an unconventional power-law localization around the qubit – yet remaining normalizable in the thermodynamic limit – with a spatial range that can be tuned by introducing lattice anisotropy We predict occurrence of vacuum Rabi oscillations and efficient state transfer between distant emitters. An experimental demonstration using superconducting circuits is proposed.



Two-dimensional (2D) photonic lattices with non-trivial topology
support chiral edge modes that spectrally arise within bandgaps of bulk modes

;
Ozawa et al. 2019
;

;

In such dynamics, an excited emitter typically undergoes an

Here, we predict that light-matter interactions on the edge of a 2D photonic lattice can exhibit a different character, resembling instead

As a case study, we consider a set of qubits (two-level quantum emitters) coupled to the edge of photonic

For suitable edge geometry, e.g., the

Since these modes are frequency-degenerate, one might expect an emitter to effectively couple to a specific superposition of the edge modes that is spatially-localized around its position
De Bernardis et al. 2021
;
Di Benedetto et al. 2025
. This picture, reducing the dynamics to an effective Jaynes-Cummings model
Jaynes and Cummings 1963
;
Haroche and Raimond 2006
with the localized mode playing the role of the cavity, indeed typically applies to conventional photonic flat bands spanning the whole BZ and spectrally gapped from other bands
De Bernardis et al. 2021
;
Di Benedetto et al. 2025
. In those cases, the fictitious cavity mode is usually exponentially localized around the emitter with the mode’s localization length representing an effective cavity volume.
In the present lattice, however, edge modes are not gapped from bands of bulk modes and, moreover, their support does not span the full BZ [see
1
(b)], which plays against existence an exponentially-localized superposition.
This raises the question of whether a cavity-QED-like model can be defined to capture the physics of the present system.

We will find that an effective localized-mode picture is still possible, but in a broader – and, in some respects, unconventional – sense. In particular, the emergent localized mode shows an atypical power-law decay in real space away from the qubit. Building on this, we present an effective cavity-QED effective model for many qubits, accounting for losses into bulk lattice modes, which is used to predict occurrence of vacuum Rabi oscillations and state transfer between emitters.

Effective cavity-QED model for
\delta{=}0
: one qubit.—
We first set
\delta{=}0
[cf.

We expand

Dispersive dipole-dipole interactions for

Anisotropic lattice.—
Much of the above physics stems from the property that modes

have support only on the

[cf. Eq. (

the hopping rate between resonators

and

Two bands of bulk modes and a set of zero-frequency edge modes

For

still arise outside the two Dirac points, which however now lie at

decreases below 1, the support of the partial flat band progressively shrinks, whereas it broadens as

increases above 1 until matching the full BZ for

.
As long as
\beta{<}2
, one can still define a cavity-QED model as in Eq. (

,

Conclusions.—
We studied a system of qubits emitting into the boundary of a 2D lattice sustaining dispersionless edge modes defined on a limited region of the BZ (partial flat band). The essential physics is captured by an effective cavity-QED model, where the emitter leaks into bulk modes and at the same couples to an emergent mode showing an unusual power-law localization. The range of this mode, which is connected to the length of the flat band support in the BZ, affects substantially photon-mediated qubit-qubit interactions, benefiting in particular long-range state transfer.

We note that, rigorously speaking, for
\delta{=}0
and
\beta{<}2
the Markovian model (
5
) is valid for
\Delta{\not=}0
since the bath resolvent is singular at the Dirac points. Such singularity is very similar to the one occurring for qubits coupled to the
bulk
of a honeycomb lattice, which was shown to lead to non-Markovian decay
González-Tudela and Cirac 2018
;
Redondo-Yuste et al. 2021
. In our system, as long as the coupling strength is weak as in all previous instances, such non-Markovian effects are negligible for all practical purposes or manifest at extremely long times, which is supported by the excellent agreement between the cavity-QED model and exact numerical simulations.

We point out that coupling qubits to
bulk
resonators is experimentally demanding with current circuit-QED technology, which provides a further major motivation/advantage for the present study.

From the perspective of topological photonics
Ozawa et al. 2019
, our work introduces a new paradigm of light-matter interactions when emitters are coupled to edge modes of a 2D lattice.
On the other hand, it unveils a novel system yielding power-law photon-mediated interactions, which are known to lead to a number of phenomena such as exotic many-body phases
Gong et al. 2016a
;
Gong et al. 2016b
;
Žunkovič et al. 2018
;
Islam et al. 2013
;
Sánchez-Burillo et al. 2020
.

II
Properties of the flat band of edge modes

The frequency-degenerate edge modes

A major property of standard FBs is that the corresponding subspace can be spanned through a basis of compact localized modes i.e., modes strictly localized in a finite region of space

This property relies crucially on two assumptions, namely the fact that the FB has support on the full BZ and it is topologically trivial

Our edge modes

21

than the BZ for

We show next that, unlike standard full FBs, this is not possible in the system under study.

Assuming

(

(24)

III
Derivation of the effective cavity-QED model

III.1
Markovian master equation: brief review

Let an open system
S
be in contact with a bath
B
with an interaction Hamiltonian of the form
H_{\rm SB}(t)=\sum_{i}X_{i}(t)\otimes E_{i}(t)
(in the interaction picture), where
X_{i}
(
E_{i}
) are operators on
S
(
B
). Introducing operators
\Pi(\varepsilon)
each of which projects
S
onto the eigenspace of energy
\varepsilon
, one can expand
X_{i}
as
X_{i}(\omega)=\sum_{\,\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon=\omega}\Pi(\varepsilon)X_{i}\Pi(\varepsilon^{\prime})
. Hence, the interaction Hamiltonian can be expressed as

H_{\rm SB}(t)=\sum_{i,\omega}e^{-i\omega t}X_{i}(\omega)\otimes E_{i}(t)=\sum_{i,\omega}e^{i\omega t}X_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\otimes E_{i}^{\dagger}(t)\,.

(28)

Next, starting from the Redfield master equation and performing the Markov and weak-coupling approximations, one obtains the integro-differential equation for the reduced density matrix of
S

Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\derivative{t}\rho_{S}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential s\Tr_{B}{\commutator{H_{\rm SB}(t)}{\comm{H_{\rm SB}(t-s)}{\rho_S(t)\otimes\rho_B}}}\,,

(29)

where
{\rm Tr}_{B}
stands for the partial trace over
B
.
Substituting (
28
) and keeping only slowly-rotating terms corresponding to nearly-resonant bath modes, one ends up with the master equation

\derivative{t}\rho_{S}(t)=\sum_{ij}\sum_{\omega}\Gamma_{ij}(\omega)\quantity[X_j(\omega)\rho_S(t) X_i^\dagger(\omega) - X_i^\dagger(\omega) X_j(\omega) \rho_S(t)]+\hc\,,

(30)

where
\omega
is the characteristic frequency of
S
while matrix
\Gamma_{ij}(\omega)
is given by

\Gamma_{ij}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential s\,e^{i\omega s}\Tr_{B}{E_{i}^{\dagger}(t)E_{j}(t-s)\rho_{B}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}(\omega)+i\,S_{ij}(\omega)\,.

(31)

The real part of this matrix describe the irreversible decay rate into the bath, while the imaginary part renormalizes the free Hamiltonian of
S
.

III.2
Single-qubit master equation

The Hamiltonian of the total system can be rearranged as

H=H_{\rm JC}+\Delta\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma+\sum_{k,q,\mu}\omega_{\mu}(k,q)\,\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(k,q)\mathcal{B}_{\mu}(k,q)+g\sum_{k,q}\sum_{\mu=\pm}\quantity( f_\mu(k,q) \mathcal{B}^\dagger_\mu(k,q)\,\sigma+\hc)\,,

(32)

where

H_{\rm JC}=g\sum_{\absolutevalue{k}>t}\quantity( \frac{\mathcal{N}_k}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \mathcal{E}_k \sigma^\dagger+ \hc)

(33)

is the coupling Hamiltonian describing the interaction between the qubit and all edge modes
\{{\cal E}_{k}\}
. Here,
t=2\arccos{\beta}
and
\varepsilon_{k}(0,0)=\mathcal{N}_{k}/\sqrt{2\pi}
is the amplitude of the edge mode
\mathcal{E}_{k}
at site
a_{00}
to which the atom is coupled.
Also,

is the amplitude of the bulk mode
\mathcal{B}_{\pm}(k,q)
at the resonator coupled to the emitter, reading

f_{\pm}(k,q)=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(k,q)\quantity[ 2\cos{\frac{k}{2}}\sin{q}]=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{\cos{\frac{k}{2}}\sin{q}}{\quantity[\omega(k,q)]^{2}}

(34)

in the
\delta{=}0

The Hamiltonian
H_{\rm JC}
can be arranged as [cf. Eq. (
4

H_{\rm JC}=g\quantity[ \sigma^\dagger\qty(\sum_{\abs{k}>t} \frac{\mathcal{N}_k}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \mathcal{E}_k + \hc)]\equiv\Omega\quantity(\sigma^\dagger\mathcal{C} + \hc)\,,

(35)

where
\Omega=g/\sqrt{A}
and we have introduced a
superposition mode

\mathcal{C}
[cf. Eq. (
6

\mathcal{C}=\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\absolutevalue{k}>t}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{k}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathcal{E}_{k}=\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{nm}c(n,m)a_{nm}\,.

(36)

In this expression,
c(n,m)
is the amplitude of the mode in real space (which will be computed in a particular case in
section VII
) and
\mathcal{A}
plays the role of a normalization constant, which can be computed e.g. by requiring that
\commutator{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}^\dagger}=1
i.e., that the mode obeys bosonic commutation rules. From this, one sees that

\commutator{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{C}^\dagger}=\mathcal{A}\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\absolutevalue{k}>t}\absolutevalue{\mathcal{N}_k}^{2}\to\mathcal{A}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{t}^{\pi}\differential k\sqrt{-\quantity[2/\beta^2-1+(2/\beta^2)\cos k]}\,,

(37)

which always converges (regardless of
\beta
) in the thermodynamic limit (the precise value is discussed in
section VII
).
We stress that, due to the normalization of the emergent mode
{\cal C}
, the coupling strength to this mode remains finite in the thermodynamic limit. This takes care of the interaction with the edge modes.

Regarding the bulk modes, these can be treated as an effective bosonic reservoir to which the qubit is coupled to within the Born-Markov approximation. This can be justified by looking at the local density-of-states of such modes on the resonator
a_{00}
. Indeed, recall that we are interested in frequencies nearly-resonant with the FB at
\Delta=0
, which on the other hand is resonant with the Dirac point emerging from the bulk modes dispersion law [see Eq. (
17
)]. Around this point, it is well known
Girvin and Yang 2019
that the density-of-states of graphene bulk modes scale linearly with their energy, being exactly zero on resonance with the Dirac point i.e., at
\Delta=0
. This means that, since light-matter coupling is indeed proportional to said density, the interaction with bulk modes will be much weaker than the one with the FB of edge modes.

Having said that, replacing in Eq. (
31
)
\omega=\Delta
,
X_{1}=X_{2}^{\dagger}=\sigma
and
E_{1}=E_{2}^{\dagger}=g\sum_{k,q,\mu}f(k,q)

yields

\Gamma_{ij}(\Delta)=\delta_{ij}\delta_{i1}\,g^{2}\sum_{k,q}\sum_{\mu=\pm}\absolutevalue{f(k,q)}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential s\,e^{i(\Delta-\omega_{\mu}(k,q))s}\,,

(38)

hence

\gamma(\Delta)=2\pi g^{2}\sum_{k,q}\sum_{\mu=\pm}\absolutevalue{f(k,q)}^{2}\,\delta(\Delta-\omega_{\mu}(k,q))

(39)

while (due to the symmetries of the integrand)
S_{ij}(\Delta)=0
. Going back to the Schrödinger picture, this leads to master equation (
5
) in the main text.

III.3
Many-qubit master equation

The derivation described in the last section can be generalized to the case of
N_{q}

The interaction Hamiltonian of the qubits with the FB of edge modes can be arranged as

H_{\rm JC}=g\sum_{j}\quantity(\sigma_j \Tilde{\mathcal{C}}^\dagger_j + \hc)\,,

(40)

where each ladder operator
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j}
(one for each qubit

{\cal C}
(apart from the normalization factor) in the single-qubit case with
m\rightarrow m-m_{j}

These

\commutator{\Tilde{\mathcal{C}}_i}{\Tilde{\mathcal{C}}^\dagger_j}\neq\delta_{ij}
. To end up with a well-defined set of
N_{q}
modes fulfilling bosonic commutation rules, following Ref.
De Bernardis et al. 2021
we perform a transformation defined by the
N_{q}\times N_{q}
invertible matrix
\mathbf{M}
such that
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{P}
, where
\mathbf{P}
is the same as in Section
section VII
. Modes
\{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\}
are transformed into

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\sum_{j}(M^{-1})_{ij}\,\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\,,

(41)

which now fulfill
\commutator{{\cal C}_i}{{\cal C}^\dagger_j}=\delta_{ij}
. The
interaction Hamiltonian (
40
) in terms of the new commuting modes reads

H_{\rm JC}=g\sum_{ij}\quantity(\sigma_i M_{ij} \mathcal{C}^\dagger_i + \hc)\,.

(42)

On the other hand, the bulk modes can be traced out using the same procedure as before. Notice that in this case, though, each
f(k,q)
acquires a

.
Proceeding as before, the master equation in Schrödinger picture reads

\derivative{t}\rho_{S}=-i\commutator{\Delta\sum_{i} \sigma_i^\dagger\sigma_i + g \sum_{ij} \qty(\mathcal{C}_i M_{ij} \sigma_j^\dagger+ \Hc)}{\rho_S}+\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}(\Delta)\quantity[\sigma_j\rho_S \sigma_i^\dagger-\frac{1}{2}\acomm{\sigma_i^\dagger\sigma_j}{\rho_S}]\,,

(43)

where the matrix of decay rates
\gamma_{ij}
reads

\gamma_{ij}(\omega)=2\pi g^{2}\sum_{k,q,\mu}\absolutevalue{f(k,q)}^{2}\cos{(k\abs{m_i-m_j})}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mu}(k,q))\,.

(44)

As expected, the diagonal elements of this matrix coincides with the single-qubit decay rate while the off-diagonal terms will feature an addition
\cos{k\abs{m_i-m_j}}
term. Notice that this master equation is a valid description of the system as long as the qubits are
close
to each other, so as to neglect effects due to the time-delay in the light propagation.

IV
Matrix elements of the decay rate matrix

In this section, we focus on the computation of the matrix elements of matrix
\gamma
. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case
\beta\leq 2
i.e., when the FB is not gapped from the rest of the spectrum.

IV.1
Calculation of
\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)

In the thermodynamic limit, one can express the single qubit decay rate or, equivalently, the element
\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)
in the dissipation matrix arising from the two qubit master equation as

\frac{\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}=\frac{8}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential k\frac{\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\sin^{2}{(q)}}{\beta^{2}+4\beta\cos{(k/2)}\cos{q}+4\cos^{2}{(k/2)}}\sum_{\mu}\delta(\Delta-\omega_{\mu}(k,q))\,.

(45)

Now, since

\sum_{\mu}\delta(\Delta-\omega_{\mu}(k,q))=2\absolutevalue{\Delta}\,\delta(\Delta^{2}-J^{2}\quantity[\omega(k,q)]^{2})\,,

(46)

one can simplify the
\mu
dependency and also to conclude that the denominator is proportional to
(\Delta/J)^{2}
. Thus

\frac{\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}=\frac{32J^{2}}{\pi\absolutevalue{\Delta}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\sin^{2}{(q)}\,\delta(\Delta^{2}-J^{2}\quantity[\omega(k,q)]^{2})\,.

(47)

Changing
x=\cos{q}
and
y=\cos{k/2}
, we get that

\begin{split}\frac{\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}&=\frac{64J^{2}}{\pi\absolutevalue{\Delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential x\int_{0}^{1}\differential y\frac{y^{2}}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\delta\quantity(4\beta J^2\,y \qty(x-\qty[\frac{\Delta^2/J^2-\beta^2-4y^2}{4\beta y}]))=\\
&=\frac{16}{\beta\pi\absolutevalue{\Delta}}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential x\int_{0}^{1}\differential y\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\delta\quantity(x-\qty[\frac{\Delta^2/J^2-\beta^2-4y^2}{4\beta y}])=\\
&=\frac{4}{\beta^{2}\pi\absolutevalue{\Delta}}\int_{c_{0}}^{c_{1}}\differential y\sqrt{\frac{16\beta^{2}y^{2}-\quantity[\Delta^2/J^2-\beta^2-4y^2]^{2}}{1-y^{2}}}\,,\end{split}

(48)

where
c_{0},c_{1}
depends on the values of
\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J
and
\beta
. In particular, one finds that

\begin{split}0<\frac{\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{J}\leq 1&:\begin{cases}\beta<\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J&c_{0}=(\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J-\beta)/2,\quad c_{1}=(\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J+\beta)/2\\
\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J\leq\beta\leq 2-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J&c_{0}=(\beta-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J)/2,\quad c_{1}=(\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J+\beta)/2\\
\beta>2-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J&c_{0}=(\beta-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J)/2,\quad c_{1}=1\\
\end{cases}\\
\frac{\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{J}>1&:\begin{cases}\beta<2-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J&c_{0}=(\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J-\beta)/2,\quad c_{1}=(\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J+\beta)/2\\
2-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J\leq\beta\leq\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J&c_{0}=(\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J-\beta)/2,\quad c_{1}=1\\
\beta>\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J&c_{0}=(\beta-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J)/2,\quad c_{1}=1\\
\end{cases}\end{split}\,.

(49)

Since we are interested in the dynamics of qubits quasi-resonant to the FB, we will focus in the following in the case
0<\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J\leq 1
. In this limit, provided that
\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J\leq\beta\leq 2-\absolutevalue{\Delta}/J
, w can simplify the integral bounds by making the substitution
y=\frac{\beta+\absolutevalue{\Delta}t/J}{2}
from which

\frac{\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}=\frac{4\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{\pi\beta^{2}J^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential t\sqrt{\frac{\quantity[1-\qty(J\frac{1-\beta}{\Delta} + t)^2]\quantity[(1+\beta+\Delta t/J)^2 - (\Delta/J)^2]}{\quantity(2+\beta+\Delta t/J)\quantity(2-\beta-\Delta t/J)}}\,.

(50)

For
\Delta\simeq 0
, we can approximate the integral, yielding

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{8\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{\beta\pi J^{2}\sqrt{4-\beta^{2}}}\int_{-1}^{1}\differential t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}=\frac{4\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{\beta J^{2}\sqrt{4-\beta^{2}}}\,.

(51)

Setting
\beta=1
yields the expression of the single qubit decay rate discussed in the main text.

Notice that this expression would yield a divergent decay rate for
\beta\to 0^{+}
or
\beta\to 2^{-}
. These two limits, though, are out of the validity region of such approximation for any non-zero value of
\Delta
.
As a matter of fact, both limits have clear physical interpretation. For instance, if
\beta=0
then the Hamiltonian reduces to a collection of 1D homogeneous chains (stretching along the
\mathbf{e}_{2}
direction), parametrized by
n
. In this case, we expect
\gamma_{ii}
to reduce to the standard decay rate into a cosine band. This is easily checked as in this case
\omega(k,q)=2J\cos{(k/2)}
and so

\begin{split}\frac{\gamma_{ii}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}&=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential k\quantity[\delta(\Delta-2J\cos{(k/2))} + \delta(\Delta+2J\cos{(k/2))}]\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\sin^{2}{(q)}=\\
&=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential k\quantity[\delta(\Delta-2J\cos{(k/2))} + \delta(\Delta+2J\cos{(k/2))}]\equiv\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential k\,\delta(\Delta-2J\cos{(k)})\,,\end{split}

(52)

yielding the expected result according to Fermi Golden Rule. On the other hand, in the
\beta=2
limit, the Dirac cone spectral shape is modified becoming a so–called
semi-Dirac
cone
Redondo-Yuste et al. 2021
, whose dispersion law is linear in one quasi-momentum component and quadratic in the other. For this reason, we expect
\gamma_{ii}
to be smaller in this case than its value for
\beta<2
. This intuition is verified by numerically computing the integral and it is shown in
3
(e).

IV.2
Calculation of
\gamma_{i\not=j}(\Delta)

In the same way as before, one would like to compute

\frac{\gamma_{ij}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}=\frac{8}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential k\frac{\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\sin^{2}{(q)}}{\beta^{2}+4\beta\cos{(k/2)}\cos{q}+4\cos^{2}{(k/2)}}\cos{k(m_i-m_j)}\sum_{\mu}\delta(\Delta-\omega_{\mu}(k,q))\,.

(53)

Doing the same simplifications as before, this integral is written as

\frac{\gamma_{ij}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}=\frac{32J^{2}}{\pi\absolutevalue{\Delta}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\sin^{2}{(q)}\cos{(k\abs{m_i-m_j})}\,\delta(\Delta^{2}-J^{2}\quantity[\omega(k,q)]^{2})\,.

(54)

The previous calculation suggests to use the substitution
x=\cos q
and
y=\cos{k/2}
. In order to do so, one recalls that

\cos{n\theta}=\sum_{r=0}^{2r\leq n}(-1)^{r}\binom{n}{2r}\cos^{n-2r}{(\theta)}\sin^{2r}{(\theta)}

(55)

and using this, it is possible to write

\begin{split}\frac{\gamma_{ij}(\Delta))}{g^{2}}&=\frac{4}{\beta^{2}\pi\absolutevalue{\Delta}}\sum_{r=0}^{r\leq\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j}}(-1)^{r}\binom{2\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j}}{2r}\int_{c_{0}}^{c_{1}}\differential y\sqrt{16\beta^{2}y^{2}-\quantity[\Delta^2/J^2-\beta^2-4y^2]^{2}}\quantity(1-y^2)^{r-1/2}y^{2\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j}-2r}\end{split}

(56)

with the same prescription about the integral limits as before. For
\Delta\simeq 0
, the integral can be approximated as

\begin{split}\frac{\gamma_{ij}(\Delta)}{g^{2}}&\simeq\frac{8\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{\beta\pi J^{2}\sqrt{4-\beta^{2}}}\sum_{r=0}^{r\leq\absolutevalue{m_i - m_j}}(-1)^{r}\binom{2\absolutevalue{m_i - m_j}}{2r}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{2\absolutevalue{m_i - m_j}-2r}\left(1-\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{r}\times\int_{-1}^{1}\differential t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}=\\
&=\frac{4\absolutevalue{\Delta}}{\beta J^{2}\sqrt{4-\beta^{2}}}\cos{\qty(2 \abs{m_i - m_j} \arccos{\frac{\beta}{2}})}\,.\end{split}

(57)

Even in this case we see that around the Dirac point, the scaling of the decay rate is linear in
\absolutevalue{\Delta}
. Notice that in general, the matrix elements of the
\gamma
matrix depends on
\beta
in a non-monotonic way. The decay rates reach a minimum in correspondence of
\beta=\sqrt{2}
, in which case dissipation will be minimized. The same comments regarding the
\beta\to 0^{+}
and
\beta\to 2^{-}
limits present at the end of
subsection IV.1
apply here.

V
Exact solution of the dissipative Jaynes-Cummings model

The master equation (
5
) reported in the main text describes a dissipative Jaynes-Cummings model in which –unlike standard cases– a
lossy
qubit couples to a
lossless
bosonic cavity. In a different context, the same model was already considered in Ref.
Lorenzo et al. 2017
, where a complete analytical solution in the single-excitation sector is discussed.
Taking inspiration from this, here we discuss the general solution of our model, depending on the values of
\Delta
and
\beta<2
.

Suppose that the system is initially in the pure state
\rho=\outerproduct{e}{e}
, corresponding to an initially excited qubit. At each later time, the probability of finding the qubit in its excited state can be found by solving the master equation. To do so, one notices that (
5
) can be rewritten as

\dot{\rho}=-i\quantity(\mathcal{H}_{\rm NH}\, \rho- \rho\mathcal{H}^\dagger_{\rm NH})+\gamma(\Delta)\sigma\rho\sigma^{\dagger}\,,

(58)

where the last term is the usual recycling term and the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian reads

\mathcal{H}_{\rm NH}=\quantity(\Delta-i\frac{\gamma(\Delta)}{2})\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma+\Omega\quantity(\sigma\mathcal{C}^\dagger+\hc)\,.

(59)

Within the single-excitation sector, spanned by states
\ket{e, {\rm vac}}
and
\ket{g, \mathcal{C}}
, the matrix elements of the recycling term yields no contribution. This allows to cast the master equation into a Von-Neumann-like equation, where the dynamics is generated by the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\rm NH}
. Solving this is equivalent to solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

i\derivative{t}\ket{\Psi(t)}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm NH}\ket{\Psi(t)}\,,

(60)

where
\rho(t)=\outerproduct{\Psi(t)}{\Psi(t)}
. Writing the state of the system at a generic time
t
as
\ket{\Psi(t)}=c_{e}(t)\ket{e,\rm vac}+C_{\mathrm{g}}(t)\ket{g, \mathcal{C}}
and solving for
c_{e}(t)
, we end up with the second order homogeneous linear differential equation

\derivative[2]{t}c_{e}(t)+\quantity(i\Delta+\frac{\gamma(\Delta)}{2})\derivative{t}c_{e}(t)+\Omega^{2}c_{e}(t)=0\,,

(61)

with the boundary conditions
c_{e}(0)=1
and
\dot{c}_{e}(0)=-i\Delta-\gamma(\Delta)/2
. Taking
c_{e}(t)=e^{\lambda t}
as an ansatz for the solution, the characteristic polynomial associated to the differential equation reads
p(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}+\quantity(i\Delta+\gamma(\Delta)/2)\lambda+\Omega^{2}
, whose roots are

\lambda_{\pm}=-\frac{1}{2}\quantity(i\Delta+ \frac{\gamma(\Delta)}{2})\pm\frac{i}{2}\sqrt{4\Omega^{2}-\quantity(i\Delta+\frac{\gamma(\Delta)}{2})^{2}}\equiv-\frac{\mu}{2}\pm\frac{i}{2}\sqrt{4\Omega^{2}-\mu^{2}}\,

(62)

with
\mu=i\Delta+\gamma(\Delta)/2
. This allows us to write the general solution as

c_{e}(t)=e^{-\mu t/2}\quantity[\cos{\qty(\frac{\sqrt{4\Omega^2-\mu^2}}{2}t)} - \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{4\Omega^2-\mu^2}}\sin{\qty(\frac{\sqrt{4\Omega^2-\mu^2}}{2}t)}]\,.

(63)

We now perform some approximations. Given that we are in the weak coupling regime i.e.,
g\ll J
, and since we are interested in the emerging dynamics when the qubit is (nearly-)resonant with the effective cavity mode, we can approximate

\displaystyle\sqrt{4\Omega^{2}-\mu^{2}}\simeq\Omega_{R}\quantity[1 - \frac{i}{2} \frac{\Delta\gamma(\Delta)}{\Omega_R^2}]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\Omega^{2}-\mu^{2}}}\simeq\frac{1}{\Omega_{R}}\quantity[1 + \frac{i}{2} \frac{\Delta\gamma(\Delta)}{\Omega_R^2}]\,,

(64)

where
\Omega_{R}=\sqrt{4\Omega^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
is the vacuum Rabi frequency.
Accordingly, the general solution can be thus approximated as

c_{e}(t)\simeq e^{-\mu t/2}\quantity[\cos{\qty(\frac{\Omega_R}{2}t)} - \frac{\mu}{\Omega_R}\sin{\qty(\frac{\Omega_R}{2}t)} + \frac{i}{2}\frac{\mu\Delta\gamma(\Delta)}{\Omega_R^3} \sin{\qty(\frac{\Omega_R}{2}t)}]\,.

(65)

To leading order in
g
, the probability of finding the qubit in the excited state can be approximated as

p_{e}(t)=\absolutevalue{c_e(t)}^{2}\simeq e^{-\gamma(\Delta)t/2}\quantity[1 - \frac{4\Omega^2}{\Omega_R^2}\sin^2{\qty(\frac{\Omega_R}{2}t)}]\,.

(66)

Thus, in the weak-coupling regime, the the qubit undergoes damped vacuum Rabi oscillations with oscillation frequency
\Omega_{R}
and damping rate
\gamma(\Delta)
.

VI
Resonant State-transfer dynamics in a two qubit system

In this section, we briefly discuss the exact solution to the dynamics of two qubits coupled to edge of
zigzag
graphene on resonance with the FB (
\Delta=0
). In this case, the effective model in (
8
) would predict a purely-coherent dynamics generated by the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{\rm JC}=g\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{2}\quantity(\sigma_j^\dagger M_{jj'} \mathcal{C}_{j'} + \hc)\,,

(67)

where
M_{jj^{\prime}}
is the matrix element of the
2\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{M}
defined as
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{P}
, being
\mathbf{P}
the FB projector computed at the qubits’ positions. For the purpose of this section, we are not interested in the exact values of those coefficients, but it is worth noting that, as
P_{jj^{\prime}}
scales as a power-law with the distance
\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j}
between the emitters [see Sec.
section VII
], also the elements of
\mathbf{M}
will inherit the same scaling.

We now assume that the system is initially in state
\ket{\Psi(0)}=\ket{e_1 g_2,{\rm vac}}
i.e., first (second) qubit in the excited (ground) state and the field in the vacuum state. Thus, within the single-excitation sector, the state of the system at a generic time
t
reads

\ket{\Psi(t)}=c_{e1}(t)\ket{e_1 g_2,{\rm vac}}+c_{e2}(t)\ket{g_1 e_2,{\rm vac}}+c_{g1}(t)\ket{g_1 g_2,{\cal C}_1}+c_{g2}(t)\ket{g_1 g_2,{\cal C}_2}\,.

(68)

This allows to write down the Schrödinger equation as

\begin{cases}i\derivative{t}c_{e1}(t)=gM_{11}c_{g1}(t)+gM_{12}c_{g2}(t)\\
\\
i\derivative{t}c_{e2}(t)=gM_{21}c_{g1}(t)+gM_{22}c_{g2}(t)\\
\\
i\derivative{t}c_{g1}(t)=gM_{11}c_{e1}(t)+gM_{12}c_{e2}(t)\\
\\
i\derivative{t}c_{g2}(t)=gM_{21}c_{e1}(t)+gM_{22}c_{e2}(t)\\
\end{cases}\,,

(69)

with
c_{e1}(0)=1
and
c_{e2}(0)=c_{g1}(0)=c_{g2}(0)=0
as initial conditions. Assuming as an ansatz that
c_{ei}=A_{i}e^{-i\omega t}
and
c_{gi}=\tilde{A}_{i}e^{-i\omega t}
, we are able to recast the problem into an eigenvalue problem, reading

\mathcal{M}\vec{A}=\frac{\omega}{g}\vec{A}\,,

(70)

where
\vec{A}=\quantity(A_1\;\; A_2\;\;\Tilde{A}_1\;\;\Tilde{A}_2)^{T}
and dynamical matrix is given by

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{2\times 2}&M\\
M^{\dagger}&\mathbf{0}_{2\times 2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(71)

where
\mathbf{0}
is the zero matrix. Due to the symmetries of the problem, we have that
M_{11}=M_{22}
and
M_{12}=M_{21}
, meaning that matrix
\mathbf{M}
is real and Hermitian.
Now, it is clear that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}
are

\displaystyle\omega_{1,\pm}=-g_{0}\pm g_{1}\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{2,\pm}=g_{0}\pm g_{1}\,,

(72)

where, for the sake of notation, we posed
g\,M_{11}=g_{0}
and
g\,M_{12}=g_{1}
. Correspondingly, the eigenstates reads

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{1,\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\pm 1\;\;-1\;\;\mp 1\;\;1)^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{2,\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\pm 1\;\;1\;\;\pm 1\;\;1)^{T}\,.

(73)

In this way, we can express the solution to the Schrödinger equation as

\begin{cases}c_{e1}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\quantity(e^{-\omega_{1-} t } + e^{-\omega_{1+} t } + e^{-\omega_{2-} t }+ e^{-\omega_{2+} t })\\
c_{e2}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\quantity(e^{-\omega_{1-} t } - e^{-\omega_{1+} t } - e^{-\omega_{2-} t }+ e^{-\omega_{2+} t })\\
c_{g1}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\quantity(-e^{-\omega_{1-} t } - e^{-\omega_{1+} t } + e^{-\omega_{2-} t }+ e^{-\omega_{2+} t })\\
c_{g2}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\quantity(-e^{-\omega_{1-} t } + e^{-\omega_{1+} t } - e^{-\omega_{2-} t }+ e^{-\omega_{2+} t })\end{cases}\,.

(74)

Upon taking the squared modulus of
c_{ei}(t)
, we are able to compute the population of the two qubits as

\displaystyle P_{1}(t)=\absolutevalue{c_{e1}(t)}^{2}=\cos^{2}{(g_{0}t)}\,\cos^{2}{(g_{1}t)}\,,

\displaystyle P_{2}(t)=\absolutevalue{c_{e2}(t)}^{2}=\sin^{2}{(g_{0}t)}\,\sin^{2}{(g_{1}t)}\,.

(75)

This shows indeed the the two qubits coherently exchange an excitation and explains also the origin of the beatings observed in
2
(d-e) of the main text.

Notice that, in order to have perfect state transfer at a time
t=\tau
i.e.,
P_{1}(\tau)=0
and
P_{2}(\tau)=1
, we would need
\sin^{2}{\quantity(g_0\tau)}=1
and
\sin^{2}{\quantity(g_1\tau)}=1
. The former condition is reached at times
\tau_{0}=\frac{\pi+2p\pi}{2g_{0}}
while the latter corresponds to
\tau_{1}=\frac{\pi+2q\pi}{2g_{0}}
, being
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, perfect state transfer is reached if, and only if,

p=\frac{g_{0}}{g_{1}}q+\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\frac{g_0}{g_1}-1)\,,

(76)

obtained from
\tau_{0}=\tau_{1}
. We see that this equation has a solution of the integer field only if the ratio between
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
is commensurate i.e.,
g_{0}/g_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This is not the case here, explaining why, apart from unaccounted losses in the bulk, it is not possible in this case to reach perfect state transfer. Nonetheless, still
94\%
fidelity can be achieved for qubits coupled to
a_{00}
and
a_{02}
, as shown in
2
(d-e) if the main text.

VII
Flat band projection operator: relevant matrix elements

The projector onto the FB subspace is defined as the operator
\mathbf{P}{=}\int\differential k\outerproduct{\mathcal{E}_k}{\mathcal{E}_k}
, where
k
runs over the region in Eq. (
21
) and with
\ket{\mathcal{E}_k}=\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\dagger}\ket{\rm vac}
is the single-photon edge states corresponding to edge modes.
Setting and
t=2\arccos{(\beta/2)}
, the relevant matrix elements of the flat band projector between the
a
-resonators on the zigzag edge at positions
m_{i}
and
m_{j}
(i.e., those relevant to our study) are

P_{ij}(\beta)=\matrixelement{a_{0\,m_i}}{\bf P}{a_{0\,m_j}}=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\pi}\int_{t}^{\pi}\differential k\quantity(\beta^2-2-2\cos{k})\,\cos{\qty[k(m_i-m_j)]}\,.

(77)

Setting
m{=}\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j}
, the integral can be calculated exactly and for
\beta<2
we end up with

P_{ij}(\beta<2)=\frac{t}{\beta^{2}\pi}\times\begin{cases}2\sin{(t)}+(\beta^{2}-2)\quantity(\pi-t)&{\rm for}\,\,m=0\\
(1-\beta^{2}/2)\sin{(t)}-2\arcsin{(\beta/2)}&{\rm for}\,\,m=1\\
(2-\beta^{2})s(m)+s(m+1)+s(m-1)&{\rm for}\,\,|m|\geq 2\end{cases}\,,

(78)

where
s(x)=\sin{(tx)}/(tx)
is a cardinal sine function with a rescaled argument, depending on
t
, hence on
\beta
.

For
\beta\geq 2
, the lower bound of integral (
77
) vanishes and we obtain

P_{ij}(\beta\geq 2)=\begin{cases}1-2/\beta^{2}&{\rm for}\,\,m=0\\
-1/\beta^{2}&{\rm for}\,\,m=1\\
0&{\rm for}\,\,|m|\geq 2\end{cases}\,.

(79)

Thus, interestingly,
P_{ij}
scales as the inverse of the distance
m
between emitters for
\beta<2
, while it becomes strictly compact for
\beta\geq 2
.

Notice that
P_{ii}(\beta)
corresponds to the integral in Eq. (
37
), for which reason the normalization constant in the effective cavity modes introduced before can be in general expressed as
\mathcal{A}=1/\quantity[P_{ii}(\beta)]^{2}
.
Furthermore, as it appears evident from the form of the cavity mode, we have that
c(0,m_{i}-m_{j})=P_{ij}(\beta)
. Thus,
P_{ij}(\beta)
also represents the shape of the effective cavity mode computed on the zigzag edge.

In the following, we leverage this knowledge in order to discuss the scaling at long distances of the cavity modes, both on the edge and toward the bulk.

VIII
Shape of the effective cavity modes

As discussed in the main text, each qubit coupled to the edge of graphene seeds its
own
effective cavity. Although the analytic expression of such cavity is cumbersome, still one can study the shape of such cavity at long distances in order to highlight its scaling.

We start by looking at the shape of the cavity mode along the zigzag edge, namely the quantity
c(0,m)
in the
m\to\infty
limit. Recalling that this quantity is equivalent to the FB projector, for
m\geq 2
this quantity can be written as

c(0,m)\propto(2-\beta^{2})\frac{\sin{tm}}{m}+\frac{\sin{t(m+1)}}{m+1}+\frac{\sin{t (\abs{m}-1)}}{m-1}\,,

(80)

where
t=2\arccos{(\beta/2)}
.
In the
m\to\infty
imit, one can expand

\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{m}\pm 1}=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{m}}\quantity( \frac{1}{1\pm\abs{m}^{-1}})\simeq\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{m}}\quantity[1 \mp\frac{1}{\abs{m}} + \frac{1}{\abs{m}^2}]

(81)

and so, recalling that
\sin{t(m\pm 1)}=\sin{tm}\cos{t}\pm\cos{tm}\sin{t}
, where
a
is a real constant, we get that the FB projector can be expanded as

c(0,m\gg 1)\propto-\beta\sqrt{4-\beta^{2}}\,\frac{\cos{(tm)}}{m^{2}}+(\beta^{2}-2)\frac{\sin{(tm)}}{m^{3}}\,.

(82)

This entails that the wavefunction of the cavity mode on the edge scales as
m^{-2}
. Accordingly, also
P_{ij}\propto\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j}^{-2}
.

The computation of the scaling along the bulk direction is a bit more involved. For the sake of simplicity, we start by focusing on the cavity seeded by a single qubit in the
\beta=1
case. The amplitude of the effective cavity [cf. (
6
)] on cavity
a_{nm}
is given by

c(n,m)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{3}{<}\absolutevalue{k}{<}\pi}\!\differential k\,{\cal N}_{k}\,\varepsilon_{k}(n,m)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{3}{<}\absolutevalue{k}{<}\pi}\!\differential k\,[-(1+2\cos{k})]e^{ik(n/2+m)}\exp{-\frac{n}{\lambda_k}}

(83)

where
\mathcal{N}_{k}=\sqrt{-(1+2\cos{k})}
and
\varepsilon_{k}(n,m)
is the amplitude of edge mode
\mathcal{E}_{k}
on the corresponding cavity. From this expression, one can see the cavity mode as a superposition of edge modes, each with its own localization length
\lambda_{k}
ranging from
0
to infinity. Now, if we are interested on studying
c(n\gg 1,m)
, we could argue that only edge modes which are extremely extended i.e., those for which
\lambda_{k}\gg 1
, will contribute to the integral in this limit. For this reason, one can expand the integrand around
k=2\pi/3
and, recalling that
\exp{-1/\lambda_k}=2\cos{(k/2)}
, we end up with

c(n,m)\propto\int_{k_{0}}^{k_{1}}\differential k\quantity[\sqrt{3}\qty(k-\frac{2\pi}{3}) - \frac{3n+1}{2}\qty(k-\frac{2\pi}{3})^2]\cos{\qty(k\qty(\frac{n}{2}+m))}\,,

(84)

where
k_{0}=2\pi/3
and
k_{1}-k_{0}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3n+1}
are chosen in order to guarantee that the integrand is still positive over the whole integration path (as the original integrand was). Evaluating this integral gives a number of terms depending on powers of
n
. The term at leading order for
n\gg 1
scales as
n^{-2}
. Luckily, this argument still can be made in the general
\beta<2
case noticing that the integrand we get as a result of the expansion has the same form as the one discussed here.

In the end, we get that, both on th edge and along the open direction, the cavity mode wavefunction
c(n,m)
scales as a power-law with exponent
-2
.

IX
Qubit-qubit interaction potential in the dispersive regime

The dispersive regime is reached when the detuning of the qubits’ frequency and the FB is much bigger than the interaction strength i.e. ,
\absolutevalue{\Delta}\gg\Omega
. In principle, one could go to this regime in the gapless system, but this would be detrimental as, since the decay rates into bulk scales linearly with
\absolutevalue{\Delta}
, it would entail a strong dissipation into bulk modes. To circumvent this issue, one can open a gap in the system by setting
\delta\not=0
, which would shift the FB frequency to
\delta
. In this way, one can tune the qubits’ frequency into the bandgap i.e.,
\absolutevalue{\Delta}<\delta
, allowing for the dispersive regime to be reached while also avoiding any leak of the qubits’ population in the bulk.

Thus, assuming both
\absolutevalue{\Delta}<\delta
and
\absolutevalue{\Delta-\delta}\gg\Omega
(dispersive condition) and considering only the case in which the qubits are coupled to the edge, we can expand the interaction Hamiltonian of the full system up the second order in perturbation theory
Bravyi et al. 2011
, yielding the effective Hamiltonian (valid provided that the bath is initially in its vacuum state)
\mathcal{H}_{\rm eff}=\sum_{ij}\mathcal{K}_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{\dagger}\sigma_{j}
, where the interaction potential
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
reads

\frac{\mathcal{K}_{ij}}{g^{2}}=\frac{1}{\Delta-\delta}c(0,\absolutevalue{m_i-m_j})+G_{\rm bulk}(\Delta)\,,

(85)

where
c(0,m)
is the cavity mode wavefunction (equivalent to the matrix element of the FB projector) [see
section VII
] and
G_{\rm bulk}(\Delta)
is the matrix element of the bulk modes’ Green’s operator, computed at the qubit frequency
\Delta
, computed between the two resonators to which qubit
i
and
j
are coupled to.

Although being a 2D system, for which the general theory developed in Ref.
Di Benedetto et al. 2025
would predict the bulk contribution
G_{\rm bulk}(\Delta)
to be negligible with respect to the one coming from the FB, in this case we have to include this term in order to reproduce the correct shape of the interaction potential. This stems from the fact that, under the considered choice of BCs, the system is mapped into a collection of 1D systems, entailing in particular the divergence of the density of states of bulk modes at the edge of each bands in the
\delta{\not=}0
case.
This addition of this term might prejudice the scaling of the interaction potential but, hopefully, this is not the case, as we show next.

The bulk modes’ Green’s function entering in the interaction potential reads

G_{\rm bulk}(\Delta)=\sum_{\mu=\pm}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\differential k\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\frac{\innerproduct{a_{0m_i}}{\mathcal{B}_\mu(k,q)}\!\innerproduct{\mathcal{B}_\mu(k,q)}{a_{0m_j}}}{\Delta-\omega_{\mu}(k,q)}\,,

(86)

which in the
\beta=1
(pristine/isotropic graphene) case can be simplified as

G_{ij}(\Delta)=\frac{2(\Delta+\delta)}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\frac{4\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\,\sin^{2}{(q)}\,\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}}{\quantity[\omega^2(k,q)-(\delta/J)^2]\quantity[\Delta^2-J^2\omega^2(k,q)]}\,.

(87)

We now expand
[\Delta^{2}-J^{2}\omega^{2}(k,q)]^{-1}
around its minimum as

\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}-J^{2}\omega^{2}(k,q)}\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}-\delta^{2}}\quantity[1+\frac{3(k-2\pi/3)^2/2 + (q-\pi)^2/2}{\Delta^2-\delta^2}]

(88)

which, upon substitution yields

\begin{split}G_{\rm bulk}(\Delta)&\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta-\delta}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\,8\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\,\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}\quantity(1+\frac{3(k-2\pi/3)^2}{2(\Delta^2-\delta^2)})\quantity(\int_0^\pi\dd q \frac{\sin^2{q}}{\omega^2(k,q)-(\delta/J)^2})\\
&+\frac{1}{2(\Delta-\delta)(\Delta^{2}-\delta^{2})}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\,8\cos^{2}{(k/2)}\,\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}\quantity(\int_0^\pi\dd q \frac{(q-\pi)^2\sin^2{q}}{\omega^2(k,q)-(\delta/J)^2})\,.\end{split}

(89)

The first integral in square brackets (first row) can be evaluated exactly, yielding

\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential q\frac{\sin^{2}{q}}{\omega^{2}(k,q)-(\delta/J)^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{8\cos^{2}{(k/2)}}\quantity[4\cos^2{(k/2)}-1]=\frac{\pi}{8\cos^{2}{(k/2)}}\quantity[1+2\cos{k}]\,,

(90)

while the second one is computed numerically yielding an almost constant value of
{\simeq}\,4
for every value of
k
, meaning that

\begin{split}G_{ij}(\Delta)&\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta-\delta}\quantity(1+\frac{2}{\Delta^2-\delta^2})\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\quantity[1+2\cos{k}]\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}\\
&+\frac{3}{(\Delta-\delta)(\Delta^{2}-\delta^{2})}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\,4\cos^{2}{(k/2)}(k-2\pi/3)^{2}\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}\,.\end{split}

(91)

Now the first integral gives a contribution that is exactly zero for
\absolutevalue{m}>2
and so we can drop it if we are interested in the long–distance scaling of this term. The second integral can be rewritten as

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\,4\cos^{2}{(k/2)}(k-2\pi/3)^{2}\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\,\quantity[1+\cos{k}](k-2\pi/3)^{2}\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}\\
&=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k(k-2\pi/3)^{2}\cos{\qty(k\abs{m})}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential k\,\quantity[\cos{k(\abs{m}+1)}+\cos{k(\abs{m}-1)}](k-2\pi/3)^{2}\end{split}

(92)

which can be evaluated analytically, giving a rather involved expression. Luckily, one can show that in the
\absolutevalue{m}\to\infty
limit, this expression yields a contribution scaling as
\absolutevalue{m}^{-2}
.
Knowing that, it is clear that both contributions to the the interaction potential
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
will have the same scaling for long distances, the same as the cavity mode. As a result, one gets power-law mediated interactions with an exponent equal to
-2
.

X
Experimental Implementation

The system presented in the main text can be directly implemented using superconducting lumped-element LC resonators
Underwood et al. 2016
;
Morvan et al. 2021
;
Jouanny et al. 2025
arranged in a honeycomb lattice [see
S1
(a)].
Each lattice site consists of a parallel combination of inductor
L_{g}
and capacitor
C_{\mathrm{g}}
connected to ground.
Neighboring sites are coupled via capacitors
C_{\mathrm{c}}
, creating the nearest-neighbor hopping required for the tight-binding model. The resonator frequency is determined by
\omega_{r}=1/\sqrt{L_{g}C_{\Sigma}}
, where
C_{\Sigma}=C_{\mathrm{g}}+zC_{\mathrm{c}}
represents the total capacitance at each site and
z
is the coordination number.
For sites with different coordination numbers (such as edge sites), the ground capacitance is adjusted to maintain a fixed
C_{\Sigma}
, thus ensuring uniform on-site frequencies across the lattice.
In the tight-binding limit where
C_{\mathrm{c}}\ll C_{\Sigma}
, the hopping amplitude between neighboring sites takes the simple form
J=(C_{\mathrm{c}}/C_{\Sigma})\omega_{r}/2
.
This capacitive coupling scheme works particularly well with compact, high-impedance resonators that have been implemented in circuit QED setups
Scigliuzzo et al. 2022
;
Jouanny et al. 2025
.
While we focus on capacitive coupling, inductive coupling provides an alternative implementation route with similar physics
Youssefi et al. 2022
;
Youssefi et al. 2025
.

Figure S1:

(a) Electrical circuit schematic of hexagonal lattice.
Each site contains an LC resonator (
L_{g},C_{g}
) with neighboring sites coupled via capacitors
C_{c}
.
(b) Scaling of nearest-neighbor hopping
J
, second-nearest-neighbor hopping
J^{\prime}
, third-nearest-neighbor hopping
J^{\prime\prime}
, and frequency disorder
\sigma_{\omega}
with coupling strength
C_{c}/C_{\Sigma}
.
Stronger coupling enhances both the desired nearest-neighbor hopping
J
and parasitic longer-range hoppings (
J^{\prime}
,
J^{\prime\prime}
) while reducing relative disorder effects.

The edge termination is naturally implemented in finite-size devices.
Interestingly, since edge modes appear only for specific edge shapes—such as zigzag or bearded edges—and are absent in others, like the so-called armchair configuration
Tan et al. 2021
, we expect the coherent dynamics described in the main text to be observable or not depending on the specific choice of edge shape.
One practical approach to explore these two different scenarios employs a rectangular geometry with two zigzag edges that host the flat band edge modes, while the other boundaries form armchair edges that do not support edge states. This represents just one possible implementation; other geometries that incorporate zigzag edges would equally support the desired edge mode physics.
As an additional experimental verification, qubits placed along the trivial (armchair) edges can serve as control measurements, demonstrating that the predicted qubit-edge mode coupling and resulting dynamics occur only in the presence of the topological flat band edge modes.

To accurately model the system beyond the tight-binding approximation, we employ a full circuit analysis using Lagrangian formalism
Vool and Devoret 2017
;
Jouanny et al. 2025
. The system is described by constructing the complete capacitance matrix
\mathbf{C}
, which includes both the self-capacitance
C_{\Sigma}
of each resonator and the coupling capacitance
C_{\mathrm{c}}
between nearest neighbors.
Combined with the inverse inductance matrix
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
(diagonal with elements
1/L_{g}
), this yields the system Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}=\sqrt{\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{-1}}
, whose eigenvalues correspond to the collective mode frequencies.

Two primary non-idealities affect the experimental realization:
long-range
hopping terms and
disorder
in the system’s parameters.
Mathematically speaking, long-range hopping terms are a byproduct of the inversion of the capacitance matrix
\mathbf{C}
.
This means that, even when we physically implement only nearest-neighbor capacitive couplings
C_{\mathrm{c}}
, the circuit equations generate second-nearest-neighbor hoppings
J^{\prime}
, third-nearest-neighbor hoppings
J^{\prime\prime}
, and higher-order terms. These arise from the collective nature of the electromagnetic modes and represent an important deviation from the ideal tight-binding model.
These long-range hopping terms are further enhanced by spurious capacitances between non-adjacent sites, which arise from electric field coupling in any physical implementation.
At the same time, fabrication variations in both inductance and capacitance values lead to disorder in circuit parameters.
Importantly, while disorder in both on-site frequencies and hopping amplitudes is present, only the on-site frequency disorder (diagonal disorder) breaks the chiral symmetry of the system.
Disorder in hopping amplitudes preserves chiral symmetry and thus does not affect the frequency of the edge modes discussed in the main text
Ramachandran et al. 2017
.
Therefore, we focus our analysis on the on-site frequency disorder with standard deviation
\sigma_{\omega}
arising from variations in
L_{g}
and
C_{\mathrm{g}}
.

In both cases, chiral-symmetry breaking gives the zero-energy flat band a finite spectral width and causes the edge modes, which ideally reside only on the
a
-sublattice, to acquire non-zero amplitude on both sublattices. Preliminary analysis show that, in order to observe the presented phenomenology, we need to work in a parameter region where this spectral width is small compared to the qubit coupling
g/2\pi
.
Moreover, for sufficiently large disorder compared to the hopping strength, the system enters an Anderson localization regime
Anderson 1958
where all modes become spatially localized at random positions.

In light of the above, the choice of circuit parameters involves a fundamental trade-off [see
S1
(b)].
For fixed resonator frequency, increasing the coupling strength by raising the ratio
C_{\mathrm{c}}/C_{\Sigma}
improves robustness against disorder by increasing all hopping strengths (
J
,
J^{\prime}
,
J^{\prime\prime}
) but maintaining fixed frequency disorder.
However, stronger coupling simultaneously enhances the unwanted higher-order hopping terms, making the nearest-neighbor approximation in the main text less accurate.

Through numerical simulations of the full circuit Hamiltonian, we find that the parameters mentioned in the main text – i.e.,
\omega_{r}/2\pi=6
GHz,
J/2\pi=100
-200 MHz and
g/2\pi=20
MHz – represent a reasonable compromise (see
S1
). For these values, the next-nearest-neighbor hopping
J^{\prime}/J
and frequency disorder
\sigma_{\omega}/J
are in the range
5-10\,\%
with the spectral width of the flat band being reasonably smaller than
g/2\pi
.
Although those non-idealities are significant, preliminary analysis suggests that the predicted effects should remain observable.
