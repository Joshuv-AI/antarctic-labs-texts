Semiconductor laser etching of chicken eggshells: Process–structure relationships and microstructural damage
Authors: Mingyan Zhao, Wei Gao, Teng Zhou, Duo Fu, Yanhong Wei, Zhonglian Wang, Mingxuan Liang, Chuan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To reduce uncontrolled eggshell cracking during puncture of embryonated chicken eggs, this study investigated how etching with a 450 nm continuous-wave blue semiconductor diode laser affected processed-region geometry, microstructure, puncture-induced cracking, and hatchability. The laser-processed eggshells were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy coupled with SEM (SEM–EDS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and Raman spectroscopy, as well as qualitative high‑speed imaging. Increasing the nominal laser power or decreasing the scanning speed increased the heat-affected zone (HAZ) width, material removal zone (MRZ) width, and etching depth. Changes in nominal laser power were accompanied by more pronounced changes in the local bottom-surface morphology, whereas changes in scanning speed were mainly accompanied by changes in the spatial dimensions and penetration depth of the processed region. XRD and Raman analyses showed that calcite CaCO₃ remained the predominant crystalline phase after laser processing, while weak CaO-related XRD reflections observed under more severe processing conditions indicated limited phase transformation. In the functional validation experiments, puncture-induced crack incidence decreased with increasing nominal laser power and increased with increasing scanning speed, whereas hatchability exhibited the opposite parameter-dependent trends. Puncture-induced crack incidence was strongly and negatively correlated with hatchability (Spearman’s ρ = −0.943, P < 0.001). These findings demonstrate parameter-dependent relationships among laser-processing conditions, puncture-induced cracking, and hatchability within the investigated experimental conditions.

Introduction

Embryonated chicken eggs are widely used for virus propagation in vaccine production, including human vaccines against influenza, rabies, and other viral diseases, as well as avian influenza vaccines for poultry such as chickens, ducks, and geese (Thian et al., 2025; Vashishtha et al., 2025; Wang F. et al., 2025; Wang L. et al., 2025). In the conventional mechanical puncture method, a flat-head threading needle is first used to perforate the eggshell at a site adjacent to, but outside, the natural air cell, as shown in Fig. 1. Negative pressure is then manually applied to reposition the air cell to an appropriate location inside the eggshell. Subsequently, the virus is inoculated onto the chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) of a viable embryonated egg on embryonic day 10 through the injection head. Viral replication then proceeds during embryonic development. Finally, the chorioallantoic fluid is collected from the embryonated eggs and further processed to produce the final vaccine product (Abdul-Cader et al., 2018).Fig. 1Schematic of the conventional mechanical puncture and subsequent injection procedure for an embryonated chicken egg.

Schematic of the conventional mechanical puncture and subsequent injection procedure for an embryonated chicken egg.

This method partially mitigates the challenges associated with eggshell removal. However, it remains subject to several notable limitations. First, it cannot guarantee successful repositioning of the air cell. Second, the precise distance between the eggshell and the chorioallantoic membrane following air cell repositioning remains uncertain. Consequently, experimenters risk inadvertently damaging the membrane during peeling, thereby compromising experimental success. Third, in eggs exhibiting abnormal development—where the natural air cell is not situated at the blunt end—the artificial air cell technique becomes inapplicable, rendering the entire experiment invalid. Moreover, the eggshell is a thin-walled, biomineralized composite material. When punctured with a flat-head needle, the thin-walled eggshell is susceptible to cracking, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Eggshell cracks have been reported to increase contamination and embryonic mortality and reduce hatchability (Moosanezhad Khabisi et al., 2012). With the continuing use of embryonated chicken eggs for virus propagation, reducing uncontrolled eggshell cracking and improving the reproducibility of puncture procedures remain practically relevant. These considerations motivated the development of a controllable laser-assisted method for preparing the eggshell before virus inoculation.Fig. 2Representative crack pattern produced by mechanical puncture of a chicken eggshell.

Representative crack pattern produced by mechanical puncture of a chicken eggshell.

Laser etching, as a mature and advanced manufacturing technology, has been widely applied in various industrial production sectors (Hartman et al., 2023; Sadeghi et al., 2025; Brand et al., 2025). Simultaneously, extensive research has explored the application of laser technologies to biomaterials. An et al. performed an in‑depth investigation into the relationship between nanosecond laser etching characteristics and processing parameters for carbon fiber‑reinforced polymer (CFRP), elucidating the complex thermal transport phenomena and material‑removal mechanisms inherent to laser processing of CFRP (An et al., 2025).

Swaminathan et al. provided a comprehensive review of advances in semiconductor laser diode technology for sustainable energy applications in leather processing, highlighting its capability for precise cutting control via pulse-width modulation, which significantly reduces both energy consumption and thermal degradation during leather cutting (Swaminathan et al., 2025). Zhang et al. comparatively evaluated multiple hatchability-related metrics—including hatching rate, healthy chick rate, and initial chick weight—before and after laser processing of fertilized eggs, confirming that, under optimized processing parameters, laser scorching does not adversely affect embryonic development or hatchling viability (Zhang N. et al., 2019). Huang et al. developed a needle-free injection device for avian embryos, employing a push–pull solenoid valve to deliver precise micro-doses of liquid, specifically designed for in ovo administration in chicken embryos (Huang K. et al., 2022). Recent studies have shown that diode-laser interactions with biological and biomedical materials are strongly influenced by laser parameters and thermal-management conditions. In particular, laser wavelength, power, irradiation duration, and cooling conditions can affect energy deposition, temperature evolution, and thermal damage in biological tissues (Cheng et al., 2024; Radhika et al., 2024). For biomineralized dental tissues, Cîrdei et al. (2024) compared diode lasers operating at 450, 808, and 980 nm and observed wavelength-dependent changes in the surface morphology and mineral composition of primary enamel. More recently, microscopic and crystallographic analyses showed that 445 nm diode-laser irradiation could induce localized melting and structural reorganization of dental enamel (Nammour et al., 2025). In addition, Ahrens et al. (2024) demonstrated that repeated 445 nm diode-laser scanning of titanium implant surfaces produced cumulative temperature increases, highlighting the importance of selecting appropriate laser parameters and controlling heat accumulation. Chen et al. employed CO₂ laser processing to mark eggshells, replacing conventional ink-based labeling and effectively eliminating the risk of chemical contamination Chen et al. (2009a). The same group subsequently conducted a finite element analysis of CO₂ laser marking (Chen et al., 2009b), demonstrating that the resulting heat-affected zone was confined to the shell surface and does not compromise the internal integrity of the eggshell. Previous studies by Chen et al. focused mainly on CO₂-laser coding of eggshells and finite-element prediction of the associated temperature field and superficial heat-affected region. In contrast, the present study used a 450 nm continuous-wave blue semiconductor diode laser to produce controlled structural modification of chicken eggshells before virus inoculation. The main contributions were the systematic analysis of power- and speed-dependent changes in the HAZ, MRZ, etching depth, and penetration behavior; combined surface and cross-sectional characterization using SEM, EDS, XRD, Raman spectroscopy, and high-speed imaging; and a cautious analysis of crack-like discontinuities and microstructural damage in the anisotropic porous shell.

The present study aims to develop a more controllable method for creating an access site in the eggshell. A controlled weakened region was first pre-formed on the eggshell surface using a semiconductor laser, followed by mechanical puncture with a flat-tipped hypodermic needle to reduce uncontrolled cracking during puncture (Chen et al., 2020). Because the eggshell is a thin-walled biomineralized composite material, laser energy deposition must be carefully controlled, and the associated energy-transfer processes and material responses are governed by multiple interrelated factors, including microstructural architecture, crystalline phase composition, and organic matrix content. Therefore, this study systematically investigated the effects of different semiconductor-laser etching parameters on the morphology, dimensions, elemental and phase characteristics, and microstructural damage of chicken eggshells, and evaluated the subsequent puncture performance of the processed regions. SEM, EDS, XRD, Raman spectroscopy, and qualitative high-speed imaging were employed to characterize the processed-region geometry, microstructure, elemental and phase changes, and visually detectable dynamic processing phenomena under different laser-processing conditions. The parameter-dependent relationships among laser-processing conditions, processed-region geometry, microstructural damage, and puncture performance were further analyzed. The results of this study provided an experimental basis for subsequent functional evaluation, biological safety assessment, and process optimization of laser-assisted eggshell processing.

Materials and methods

Materials

The experimental materials consisted of eggs sourced from the Wuyin Egg Industry Poultry Farm located in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province. These eggs measured approximately 50-60 mm along the long axis and 40-50 mm along the short axis, with a shell thickness of 0.3–0.5 mm. All eggs were produced by a uniform batch of hens. The fertilized eggs were incubated for approximately 10 days at 37.5–38.5°C and 50%–60% relative humidity under adequately ventilated conditions. At embryonic day 10, embryo viability was confirmed by candling based on visible embryonic movement and a developed chorioallantoic vascular network. Only viable embryonated eggs were then removed from the incubator and laser processed at room temperature. If virus inoculation is performed, the embryonated eggs are incubated at 33–35°C for 48–72 h post-inoculation, followed by immediate cooling at 4°C for 6 h prior to allantoic fluid collection. The remaining eggs are continuously incubated at 33–35°C with a relative humidity of 50%–60% until hatching.

The eggshell is a natural biomineral material characterized by numerous internal voids, giving rise to substantial anisotropy. From a microscopic perspective, it exhibits a vertically arranged columnar architecture (Zhang W. et al., 2025). Structurally, from the exterior to the interior, it comprises three distinct layers: the vertical crystal layers, the palisade layer, and the mammillary layer. The ultrastructure of the eggshell is illustrated in Fig. 3. Calcium carbonate (Nakano et al., 2003) constitutes approximately 96% of the overall composition of the eggshell and plays a crucial role in determining its fracture strength (Gu L. et al., 2023). Because calcite is the dominant crystalline phase of calcium carbonate in eggshells, the thermal properties of calcite were used to approximate the basic thermal properties of the eggshell. Accordingly, the thermal conductivity k was estimated to be 3.85 W/(m·K), the reflectivity R was estimated to be 0.05, and the thermal diffusivity α was estimated to be 1.67 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s.Fig. 3Cross-sectional architecture of the chicken eggshell.

Cross-sectional architecture of the chicken eggshell.

Laser processing equipment

The experiment was conducted using a three-axis laser processing system. The X and Y-axes were driven by synchronous belts, whereas the Z-axis was adjusted using a stainless-steel lead screw with a positioning accuracy of ±20 μm. Laser processing was performed using a 450 nm blue semiconductor diode laser operated in continuous-wave mode. The nominal output powers used in the laser-power series were 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 W. The manufacturer-specified focused spot area was approximately 0.06 mm² at a nominal working distance of 28 mm and a nominal defocus of 0 mm. Accordingly, the maximum nominal area-averaged irradiance at 20 W was P/A = 3.33 × 10⁸ W/m². Fig. 4(a) presents a schematic diagram of the laser processing system, which comprises a control system, a diode laser, condenser, and F-theta lens. Given that the eggshell surface is characterized by irregular curvature, we designed a positioning clamping mechanism specifically for this experiment. The traditional three-point positioning method (Rieck et al., 2021) was implemented; four concave cylindrical surfaces were symmetrically arranged on either side of the egg to facilitate clamping through screw rotation. The clamping method is illustrated in Fig. 4(c). Before each experiment, the Z axis was adjusted so that the local eggshell surface at the center of the 3 mm circular trajectory coincided with the nominal focal plane. The nominal defocus was fixed at Δz = 0 mm. During X–Y scanning, the Z axis remained fixed throughout the circular trajectory.Fig. 4Laser-processing system and eggshell-positioning fixture.

Laser-processing system and eggshell-positioning fixture.

Selection of laser etching parameters

When analyzing the laser etching process of eggshells, three fundamental heat-transfer mechanisms should be considered: heat conduction, heat convection, and thermal radiation. Heat conduction refers to energy transfer within the material through lattice vibrations (Liu D. et al., 2025); heat convection describes heat exchange between the laser-affected region and the surrounding environment (Levintal et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024; Sternberg et al., 2025); and thermal radiation is associated with the absorption, emission, and reflection of laser energy at the material surface (Skvortsov et al., 2003). During laser irradiation, the eggshell absorbs part of the incident laser energy, resulting in a localized temperature rise and enhanced lattice vibrations. When the local temperature exceeds the critical threshold required for phase transformation or material removal, phase transition and ablation may occur.

For the theoretical description of laser energy input, the continuous-wave laser was represented as a moving Gaussian surface heat source. The transient heat-conduction equation and surface energy-balance equation were used to compare the relative effects of laser power and scanning speed (Forslund et al., 2019; Orekhov et al., 2022):q″(x,y,t)=2ηPπw02exp[−2((x−vt)2+y2)w02]where q′′ is the absorbed surface heat-flux density of the eggshell, in W/m²; η is the effective absorptivity, dimensionless; P is the nominal laser power, in W; w0 is the effective beam radius, in m; v is the scanning speed, in m/s; t is time, in s; and x and y are the surface coordinates, in m. When laser transmission and scattering are neglected, the effective absorptivity can be approximated asη=1−Rwhere R is the surface reflectivity of the eggshell. In this order-of-magnitude analysis, the manufacturer-specified spot area of 0.06 mm² was treated as an equivalent circular spot area.

The transient temperature field was described by the heat-conduction equation (Forslund et al., 2019):ρcp∂T∂t=∇·(k∇T)where ρ is the material density, in kg/m³; cp is the specific heat capacity, in J/(kg·K); k is the thermal conductivity, in W/(m·K); ∇ denotes the spatial gradient operator; and T is the temperature, in K. The thermal diffusivity is defined asα=kρcPwith units of m²/s. The surface energy-balance boundary condition can be expressed as (van de Laar and Naus, 2022):−k∂T∂z|z=0=q′−h(Ts−T∞)−εσ(Ts4−T∞4)where h is the convective heat-transfer coefficient, in W/(m²·K); Ts is the eggshell surface temperature, in K; T∞ is the ambient temperature, in K; ε is the surface emissivity, dimensionless; and σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, with a value of 5.67 × 10−8 W/(m²·K⁴). z is the inward surface-normal coordinate, in m. To evaluate the relative contributions of convective and radiative surface heat losses, an order-of-magnitude comparison was performed. A representative eggshell surface-temperature range of 573–1023 K was selected to span moderate thermal exposure and conditions approaching the onset of significant calcite decomposition (Kristl et al., 2019; Minakshi et al., 2019). A convective heat-transfer coefficient of h = 10 W/(m2⋅K) was adopted as an order-of-magnitude estimate for natural convection from an egg-sized curved surface in quiescent air, consistent with classical free-convection correlations for isothermal spheres (Churchill, 1983). A surface emissivity of ε=0.95 was adopted based on a reported thermal emissivity of approximately 0.96 for chicken eggshells or closely related avian eggs (Björn et al., 2016). For an ambient temperature T∞ of 298 K, the estimated convective heat flux was approximately 2.8 × 103–7.3 × 103 W/m2, whereas the radiative heat flux was approximately 5.4 × 103–5.9 × 104 W/m2.

In comparison, using a thermal conductivity of k = 3.85 W/(m·K) and an eggshell thickness of 0.3–0.5 mm, the characteristic conductive heat flux within the eggshell was estimated to be approximately 2.1 × 106–9.3 × 106 W/m². Therefore, the combined convective and radiative heat losses accounted for approximately 0.2%–1.1% of the conductive heat flux. Over the laser-power range investigated in this study, the combined convective and radiative losses were also less than approximately 0.1% of the nominal absorbed laser heat flux.

Accordingly, convection and radiation were retained in the general surface energy-balance equation. However, for the first-order analysis of the short-duration laser–eggshell interaction, the process was approximated as being dominated by heat conduction, and the effects of convection and radiation were neglected. Based on the above theoretical analysis, nominal laser power and scanning speed were identified as the two primary process parameters governing the laser energy input and interaction time. Therefore, these two parameters were selected as the principal variables for the subsequent laser-etching experiments. Measurement and characterization methods

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the laser-etched eggshell samples were examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8010, Hitachi). Elemental composition and distribution were evaluated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy coupled with SEM (SEM–EDS; SU8010, Hitachi). Phase composition and molecular-structure information were obtained by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D2 PHASER, Bruker) and Raman spectroscopy (i-Raman, B&W Tek), respectively. For each technique, the sample-preparation procedure, acquisition parameters, analyzed region, and criteria used for phase or spectral identification are reported separately in the corresponding methods section. The dynamic laser-etching process was recorded using a high-speed camera (NEO 25M/C, Photron). The high-speed imaging analysis was restricted to qualitative visualization of recurring optical emission, plume-like features, and visible ejecta.

In this study, two directions for laser‑etching characterization are defined (Fig. 5). The normal direction refers to the surface‑normal (diametrical) direction relative to the laser‑beam circular cross‑section, and the tangential direction refers to any direction tangential to this beam cross‑section. To maintain a consistent measurement scale, ensure appropriate dimensions and spatial resolution for XRD and Raman spectroscopic characterization, and reduce the risk of internal stress induced by laser etching, a complete circular laser-etching trajectory with a diameter of 3 mm was adopted, and each eggshell was etched once along this trajectory. In this study, the heat-affected zone (HAZ) (Li T. et al., 2025) was defined as the region in which the surface morphology differed from the original eggshell morphology after laser etching, and the width of this region was taken as the HAZ width. The material removal zone (MRZ) (Kong et al., 2024) was defined as the region exhibiting a height difference greater than 10% of the eggshell thickness relative to the surrounding surface (Zhao et al., 2026).Fig. 5The normal direction denotes the surface‑normal direction perpendicular to the local eggshell surface; the tangential direction lies tangential to the local eggshell surface.

The normal direction denotes the surface‑normal direction perpendicular to the local eggshell surface; the tangential direction lies tangential to the local eggshell surface.

For qualitative SEM morphological characterization, three independently processed eggshells were examined for each experimental condition, and five spatially separated regions were observed on each eggshell (Hugenschmidt et al., 2023). Representative SEM micrographs were selected from these observations to illustrate the recurring morphological features. For semi-quantitative SEM–EDS analysis, three independently processed eggshells were analyzed per condition. EDS point spectra were acquired at five spatially separated measurement points on each eggshell, and the five point measurements were averaged to obtain one eggshell-level value. For XRD and Raman analyses, three independently processed eggshells were analyzed per condition (n = 3), with one specimen prepared from each eggshell. The three independent specimens were analyzed separately to assess the consistency of the major phase and spectral features. Because the XRD and Raman analyses were intended primarily for qualitative identification of phase and spectral characteristics, the presented results are representative, showing the common and reproducible features observed across the three independent specimens. For qualitative high-speed imaging, three independently processed intact embryonated eggs were recorded per condition (n = 3), with one continuous processing sequence recorded from each egg. For quantitative HAZ and MRZ width measurements, 10 independently processed eggshells were analyzed per condition. Five spatially separated measurements were obtained from each eggshell and averaged to obtain one eggshell-level value.

Functional validation and statistical analysis

For puncture and hatchability tests, 50 viable embryonated eggs were used for each laser-processing condition and the untreated control. Embryo viability was confirmed by candling on embryonic day 10. The eggs were punctured at the center of the processed or corresponding untreated region using a flat-tipped needle at 10 mm/s. A visible fracture line around or extending from the puncture site was recorded as a puncture-induced crack. After puncture, wax was applied only to the laser-etched heat-affected zone (HAZ) surrounding the puncture site to seal the opening. The eggs were then incubated at 33–35°C and 50%–60% relative humidity until hatching. Hatchability was calculated as the percentage of successfully hatched eggs. Cochran–Armitage trend tests were used to evaluate trends in crack incidence and hatchability, and Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess their association. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

For the preliminary hemagglutination assay, six embryonated eggs were included in each selected laser-power, laser-scanning-speed, and manual-perforation control group. The eggs were inoculated with influenza virus using 50 μL of virus suspension and incubated at 33–35°C for 48–72 h, followed by cooling at 4°C for 6 h before allantoic-fluid collection. The reciprocal of the highest dilution showing “++” agglutination with the H9 antigen was recorded as the HA titer.

Results and discussion

Etching morphology and HAZ characteristics at different powers

This study consisted of two independent parameter series, including the laser-power series and scanning-speed series. All laser-processing conditions and fixed parameters are summarized in Table 2. Fig. 6 illustrates the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) morphologies of the etched regions at varying power levels. The laser scanning speed was set to 15 mm/s, and the distance between the laser head and the eggshell surface at the etching position was maintained at a nominal working distance of 28 mm. In laser-processing studies, the region surrounding the laser-etched area that exhibits thermally induced changes is commonly defined as the heat-affected zone (HAZ) (Li T. et al., 2025), as shown in Fig. 6(b). The material removal zone (MRZ) was defined as the depressed region within the HAZ where the surface height was reduced by more than 10% of the local eggshell thickness relative to the surrounding untreated surface (Zhao et al., 2026), as shown in Fig. 6(c). The surface of the eggshell prior to etching, as illustrated in Fig. 6(a), is predominantly composed of crystalline calcium carbonate salts, which exhibit the typical physical structure associated with common crystalline salts. At an normal laser power of 4 W (a relatively low level), as depicted in Fig. 6(b), the processed region was dominated by the HAZ, while the MRZ remained minimal. At 4 W, some CaCO₃-rich particles within the morphologically altered region exhibited rounded edges (Hauner et al., 2017). When the normal laser power was increased from 8 to 16 W, a distinct MRZ appeared and gradually expanded. At 20 W, the processed region exhibited more pronounced wrinkled edges and layered stacking structures (LAS) (Ding et al., 2023), as shown in Fig. 6(f). Overall, increasing nominal laser power produced progressively stronger surface modification and a larger material-removal region. This phenomenon may be associated with possible local softening and redistribution of the eggshell material during laser etching, followed by differential contraction during cooling. Owing to the pronounced anisotropy of the eggshell (Tanaka et al., 2020), the extent of local thermal modification, material redistribution, and contraction during cooling may vary among different positions along the etched edge, and these effects may collectively contribute to the observed layered stacking structure (LAS) (Ding et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). Therefore, higher laser energy input promotes more substantial local thermal modification, material redistribution and cooling‑driven contraction, which give rise to more prominent layered‑stacking features.Fig. 6Surface SEM micrographs of chicken eggshells processed at different nominal laser powers. The bottom surface of the untreated eggshell is depicted in Fig. 7(a), where calcium carbonate crystalline salts are arranged in a sheet-like structure on the surface (Xing X. et al., 2019). At 8 W, isolated continuous regions appeared among the lamellar structures. At 12 W, the continuous regions occupied a larger proportion of the processed bottom surface. When the power was increased to 16 W, deeper and wider crack-like discontinuities appeared. These crack-like discontinuities were associated with the greater local laser energy input at higher nominal powers and the progressive modification, collapse, and interconnection of the pre-existing porous structure (Li et al., 2004; Triantafyllidis et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2011; Tanaka et al., 2020). To further investigate the laser-induced material removal process, SEM was used to characterize the surface morphologies of the normal direction etching regions produced at different normal laser power levels, as shown in Fig. 8. When the normal laser power was at 4 W, as depicted in Fig. 8(b), the etching area exhibited only a slight depression, which is referred to as the HAZ. At 4 W, the processed region exhibited a shallow depression accompanied by small rounded deposits at the bottom surface. When transitioning from 8 W to 12 W, as shown in Figs. 8(c) and (d), an inverted cone shape emerged on the etched surface. This phenomenon occurred because laser beam energy diminished progressively with increasing depth, leading to a continuous reduction in material removal and thus resulting in an inverted cone morphology. As shown in Fig. 8(e), increasing the normal laser power to 16 W resulted in deeper energy penetration during etching. Part of the laser energy was transferred to the shell membrane surface, and because the shell membrane hindered further energy transmission, the remaining energy continued to act on the mammillary layer, leading to lateral material removal. As a result, the etched surface developed an hourglass-shaped profile. At a power level reaching 20 W—as demonstrated in Fig. 8(f)—a portion of the eggshell structure at specific positions became compromised and ultimately perforated. The findings indicate that while increasing power enhances etching depth initially, excessive laser intensity can cause lateral removal of the mammillary layer—resulting in inefficient energy use.Fig. 8SEM morphologies of normal direction etching areas of laser beams at different powers, V = 15 mm/s, nominal working distance: 28 mm, (a) Not processed; (b) P = 4 W; (c) P = 8 W; (d) P = 12 W; (e) P = 16 W; (f) P = 20 W.

Etching morphology and HAZ characteristics at different speeds

Fig. 10 shows SEM morphologies of the laser-processed regions at different scanning speeds. The laser scanning power and nominal working distance were consistently set at 20 W and 28 mm, respectively. At an etching speed of 120 mm/s, as depicted in Fig. 10(b), the etched region exhibited both HAZ and MRZ. The excessively rapid movement of the laser resulted in a pronounced circular interpolation effect (Gassara et al., 2012), leading to an irregular etching shape. Characteristics of the HAZ and the MRZ

The variations in the widths of the affected regions, namely the heat-affected zone (HAZ) and material removal zone (MRZ), as a function of normal laser power are shown in Fig. 14(a). For each eggshell, the HAZ and MRZ widths were measured at five spatially separated positions, and the mean of these five measurements was used as the eggshell-level value. As the normal laser power increased, the HAZ width continuously expanded. This phenomenon can be attributed to the increased energy input at higher power levels, which promotes greater local energy input and broadens the thermally affected region (Leone et al., 2018). The MRZ width also exhibited an increasing trend with increasing normal laser power. These results indicated that enhanced etching capacity increases the volume of thermally affected eggshell material, ultimately leading to a greater amount of material removal. The variations in HAZ and MRZ widths as a function of scanning speed are shown in Fig. 14(b). As the scanning speed increased, the MRZ width showed a clear decreasing trend. This behavior can be explained by the reduced laser–material interaction time at higher scanning speeds, which limits local energy input and decreases the extent of material removal. Conversely, lower scanning speeds prolong the interaction time, resulting in greater local energy input, a wider HAZ, and an increased MRZ width. Phase and elemental characteristics of the laser-processed regions

To evaluate the phase composition of the eggshell after laser processing, X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained from the bottom surface of the material-removal zone (MRZ). Fig. 15(a) shows the XRD patterns obtained under different nominal laser powers. The untreated eggshell was dominated by reflections assigned to calcite CaCO₃, including the (104), (108), (116), and (214) planes. No reflections attributable to CaO were detected in the untreated sample. After laser processing at 4 W, CaCO₃ remained the predominant crystalline phase, and no distinct CaO-related reflections were detected. An additional calcite reflection indexed to the (300) plane became visible. However, the visibility and relative intensity of individual diffraction peaks can be affected by preferred crystallographic orientation, local changes in crystallinity, specimen geometry, and the region selected for analysis. At nominal laser powers of 8 W and above, weak reflections assigned to the (200) and (311) planes of cubic CaO were observed. These CaO reflections have also been reported for CaO synthesized from eggshell-derived calcium precursors (Habte et al., 2019). The CaO reflections increased slightly in intensity with increasing normal laser power but remained substantially weaker than the predominant calcite reflections. These results indicate that CaCO₃ remained the predominant crystalline phase, whereas limited formation of a Ca-rich secondary phase may have occurred under the more severe processing conditions (Qi et al., 2002; Zeng et al., 2010; Iwama and Maekawa, 2022). Fig. 15(b) shows the XRD patterns obtained at different laser scanning speeds. No distinct CaO-related reflections were detected at the highest scanning speed of 120 mm/s. As the scanning speed decreased, weak reflections assigned to CaO became detectable and showed slightly greater relative intensities. Nevertheless, CaCO₃ remained the predominant crystalline phase under all investigated scanning-speed conditions. However, the weak intensity of these reflections indicated that the transformed fraction was not the dominant phase. Overall, the XRD patterns were dominated by calcite CaCO₃, while weak CaO-related reflections were observed under higher-power and lower-speed processing conditions, indicating a limited laser-induced phase transformation from CaCO₃ to CaO.Fig. 15XRD experimental results of the bottom surface of the etched removal area: (a) XRD experimental results under different nominal laser powers, (b) XRD experimental results under different laser speeds.

Functional biological validation

As shown in Table 3, Table 4, the biological validation yielded consistent results across all parameter groups. In the laser-power series, the laser scanning speed was fixed at 15 mm/s. The crack incidences at 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 W were 86%, 52%, 10%, 0%, and 0%, respectively. Cochran–Armitage trend analysis showed that crack incidence decreased significantly with increasing nominal laser power (Z = −10.97, P < 0.001). The corresponding hatchability values were 50%, 60%, 66%, 74%, and 70%, respectively, indicating an overall increasing trend with nominal laser power within the investigated range, although hatchability decreased slightly at 20 W due to excessive laser penetration and associated embryo damage. This reduction was associated with excessive laser penetration through the eggshell, which caused direct thermal damage to the embryos. During hatching, some embryos exhibited burned feathers, and some embryos died inside the eggshell before successful hatching. In the laser-scanning-speed series, the nominal laser power was fixed at 20 W. The crack incidences at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 mm/s were 0%, 4%, 22%, 58%, and 82%, respectively. Crack incidence increased significantly with increasing laser scanning speed (Z = 10.35, P < 0.001). The corresponding hatchability values were 76%, 72%, 64%, 58%, and 50%, respectively, showing a significant decreasing trend with increasing laser scanning speed (Z = −3.07, P = 0.002). The untreated control group exhibited a crack incidence of 88% and a hatchability of 52%. Across all 10 laser-treated groups and the untreated control group, group-level crack incidence was strongly and negatively correlated with group-level hatchability (Spearman’s ρ = −0.943, P < 0.001). Heat transfer and ablation mechanism

Fig. 20 summarizes the parameter-dependent changes observed during continuous-wave semiconductor-laser processing of chicken eggshells. Increasing the nominal laser power or decreasing the scanning speed increased the nominal energy input to the processed trajectory and was associated with wider HAZ and MRZ dimensions and greater etching depth (Forslund et al., 2019; Orekhov et al., 2022; Leone et al., 2018).Fig. 20Diagram of parameter-dependent structural evolution during laser etching; (a) varying with nominal laser power; (b) varying with laser speed.

Diagram of parameter-dependent structural evolution during laser etching; (a) varying with nominal laser power; (b) varying with laser speed.

Fig. 20(a) summarizes the morphological changes observed with increasing nominal laser power at a fixed scanning speed of 15 mm/s. At 4 W, the processed region was dominated by the HAZ, and only a very narrow MRZ was detected. At 8–12 W, a clear MRZ developed, the etching depth increased, and the bottom-surface morphology became progressively more continuous. At 16 W, deeper and wider crack-like discontinuities appeared within the processed region. The excessive penetration depth at 20 W also affected hatchability, as thermal exposure through the penetrated eggshell region caused embryo damage observed during the hatching process. The cross-sectional profile changed from a shallow depression to an inverted-cone-shaped region and subsequently to an hourglass-like profile as the nominal laser power increased. Similar parameter-dependent changes in affected-region dimensions and material-removal depth have been reported in laser processing of other composite and brittle materials (Leone et al., 2018; An et al., 2025).

The phase and elemental analyses showed that calcite CaCO₃ remained the predominant crystalline phase after laser processing. Weak XRD reflections corresponding to the (200) and (311) planes of cubic CaO were observed under higher-power processing conditions. Similar CaO reflections have been reported for CaO materials synthesized from eggshell-derived calcium precursors (Habte et al., 2019). Meanwhile, the SEM–EDS results showed an increase in the Ca/O mass ratio with increasing nominal laser power. The SEM–EDS data were used as semi-quantitative compositional evidence because elemental quantification can be affected by the specimen geometry, surface morphology, matrix correction, and electron interaction volume (Newbury and Ritchie, 2015). Raman spectra retained a prominent band at approximately 1083 cm⁻¹ under all processing conditions, corresponding to the symmetric stretching vibration of the carbonate group in calcite CaCO₃ (De La Pierre et al., 2014). Taken together, these results indicate that the observed response was dominated by morphological and microstructural modification of the CaCO₃-rich eggshell.

Fig. 20(b) summarizes the changes associated with laser scanning speed at a constant nominal power of 20 W. As the scanning speed decreased from 120 to 48 mm/s, the HAZ width, MRZ width, and etching depth increased, while the bottom-surface morphology remained comparatively similar. Layered features, enlarged pore-like structures, and crack-like discontinuities became more evident at lower scanning speeds. At 24 mm/s, the processed region penetrated through the eggshell and exhibited an hourglass-like cross-sectional profile accompanied by extensive structural damage. The dependence of the processed-region dimensions on the laser scanning conditions is consistent with previous studies showing that scanning speed and laser residence time affect the extent of laser-induced material modification (Ahrens et al., 2024; An et al., 2025).

The puncture experiments showed that the laser-processed region altered the subsequent mechanical response of the eggshell. Under the standardized puncture speed of 10 mm/s, crack incidence decreased with increasing nominal laser power and increased with increasing scanning speed. Hatchability showed the opposite parameter-dependent trends. Across all laser-treated and control groups, crack incidence was negatively correlated with hatchability. This relationship is consistent with previous findings that eggshell cracking increases contamination and embryonic mortality and reduces hatchability (Moosanezhad Khabisi et al., 2012). Processing conditions that resulted in complete eggshell penetration were considered to pose a higher risk of internal embryo injury, thereby defining the practical processing range for laser-assisted eggshell puncture.

Conclusion

This study investigated the process–structure relationships, microstructural damage, puncture-induced cracking, hatchability, and hemagglutination outcomes of chicken eggs processed using a 450 nm continuous-wave semiconductor laser.1.Both nominal laser power and scanning speed affected the HAZ width, MRZ width, and etching depth. Increasing the nominal laser power increased the heat-source intensity, whereas decreasing the scanning speed prolonged the local laser–material interaction time and increased the effective energy input per unit length. Both changes increased the extent of material removal. Nominal laser power produced more pronounced changes in the local microstructure of the processed bottom surface, whereas scanning speed primarily regulated the spatial dimensions and penetration depth by controlling the laser–material interaction time and effective energy input per unit length. Excessive energy input, including processing at 20 W or at the lowest investigated scanning speed of 24 mm/s, resulted in full-thickness penetration and severe structural damage.2.Laser processing produced localized thermal modification and material redistribution within the eggshell. With increasing laser power or decreasing scanning speed, the processed regions exhibited progressively more pronounced pore collapse, crack-like discontinuities, and structural damage. XRD and Raman analyses showed that calcite CaCO₃ remained the predominant phase under all investigated conditions. Weak XRD reflections corresponding to CaO were observed under some higher-power or lower-speed conditions, accompanied by an increase in the SEM–EDS Ca/O mass ratio, indicating limited phase transformation and a relative decrease in oxygen content. Overall, the response of the eggshell to laser processing was dominated by localized material removal and microstructural modification.3.Under the standardized mechanical puncture speed of 10 mm/s, puncture-induced crack incidence decreased significantly with increasing nominal laser power and increased significantly with increasing scanning speed. In the laser-power series, crack incidence decreased from 86% at 4 W to 0% at 16 and 20 W, while hatchability increased overall from 50% at 4 W to 70% at 20 W, with the highest hatchability of 74% observed at 16 W. In the scanning-speed series, crack incidence increased from 0% at 24 mm/s to 82% at 120 mm/s, while hatchability decreased from 76% to 50%. Across the 10 laser-treated groups and the untreated control group, crack incidence was strongly and negatively correlated with hatchability (Spearman’s ρ = −0.943, P < 0.001). These results demonstrated significant parameter-dependent associations among laser-processing conditions, puncture-induced cracking, and hatchability.
