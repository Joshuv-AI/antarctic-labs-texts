Federated PCA and Estimation for Spiked Covariance Matrices: Optimal Rates and Efficient Algorithm
Jingyang Li, T. Tony Cai, Dong Xia, Anru R. Zhang

Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) has gained significant recent attention in machine learning for its enhanced privacy and data security, making it indispensable in fields such as healthcare, finance, and personalized services. This paper investigates federated PCA and estimation for spiked covariance matrices under distributed differential privacy constraints. We establish minimax rates of convergence, with a key finding that the central server’s optimal rate is the harmonic mean of the local clients’ minimax rates. This guarantees consistent estimation at the central server as long as at least one local client provides consistent results. Notably, consistency is maintained even if some local estimators are inconsistent, provided there are enough clients. These findings highlight the robustness and scalability of FL for reliable statistical inference under privacy constraints. To establish minimax lower bounds, we derive a matrix version of van Trees’ inequality, which is of independent interest. Furthermore, we propose an efficient algorithm that preserves differential privacy while achieving near-optimal rates at the central server, up to a logarithmic factor. We address significant technical challenges in analyzing this algorithm, which involves a three-layer spectral decomposition. Numerical performance of the proposed algorithm is investigated using both simulated and real data.



1
Introduction

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and its variants are fundamental tools in statistics and machine learning, particularly valuable for dimension reduction and data visualization when high-dimensional data lie in a low-dimensional space. PCA has been widely applied in data denoising and compression, feature extraction, clustering analysis, factor models, correlation analysis, and regression analysis. Population principal components are typically defined using the leading eigenvectors of population covariance matrices. The estimation and inference of these components from sample data have been extensively studied across various fields, including probability, statistics, and machine learning (see, for example,
Vershynin (2018)
;
Jolliffe and Cadima (2016)
;
Silverstein and Bai (1995)
;
Bickel and Levina (2008)
;
Koltchinskii and Lounici (2016)
;
Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi (2011)
;
Cai et al. (2013)
;
Cai et al. (2015)
;
Zhang et al. (2022)
;
Cai and Zhang (2016)
). See
Cai et al. (2016)
for a survey on optimal estimation of high-dimensional covariance structures.

With the digital shift in human activities, such as social networking, online shopping, and healthcare, vast amounts of personal information are collected and analyzed by large information technology firms and governmental organizations. The centralization of data storage raises critical concerns about the misuse of sensitive personal information, whether through intentional abuse or unintentional leaks. Traditional privacy-preserving methods like anonymization have proven insufficient, particularly in the context of classical PCA. As shown by
Dwork et al. (2006)
and
Chaudhuri et al. (2013)
, classical PCA is vulnerable to alterations in individual data points and poses a significant risk of personal information leakage.

Differential privacy (DP) provides a robust framework to ensure that published statistics do not reveal whether any individual’s data was included in the dataset. Initially introduced by
Dwork et al. (2006)
, DP has become a widely accepted standard in both industrial and governmental applications
(
Erlingsson et al., 2014
;
Ding et al., 2017
;
Apple Differential Privacy Team, 2017
;
Abowd, 2016
;
Abowd et al., 2020
)
. DP is typically achieved by adding random noise to statistical outputs, using mechanisms such as the Gaussian or Laplace mechanisms. However, this randomization can compromise the accuracy of statistical methods. Consequently, a growing body of literature explores the trade-offs between privacy and accuracy in fundamental statistical and machine learning problems. The minimax optimal rates for differentially private PCA and covariance matrix estimation under the spiked model are established in
Cai et al. (2024b)
.

Federated Learning (FL) is a decentralized machine learning framework where local clients train their models and communicate with a central server without sharing raw data
(
McMahan et al., 2017
)
. Instead, clients privatize their learned models and share them with the central server or other clients, enabling collaborative machine learning while maintaining data privacy. Federated learning has applications in healthcare, finance, Internet of Things (IoT), and more. However, the heterogeneity of datasets, privacy constraints, and the increasing number of local clients pose significant challenges to understanding the theoretical performance of federated learning. Under the DP constraint, the special case where each client holds only one datum is referred to as the
local
differential privacy setting
(
Duchi et al., 2013
)
.

This paper investigates the minimax optimal rates in federated PCA under the spiked covariance model, considering diverse privacy constraints and sample sizes at local clients. A surprising and significant finding is that the minimax optimal rates achieved by the central server are the (scaled) harmonic mean of the minimax optimal rates achieved by local clients. This indicates that federated learning is multiply robust, meaning the central server attains a consistent estimator as long as at least one local client provides a consistent estimator. We believe this phenomenon is general and applies to many other federated learning problems under DP constraints.

The lower bound is established by leveraging a matrix version of the van Trees’ inequality, inspired by a similar strategy in
Cai et al. (2024a)
. This matrix version of van Trees’ inequality is of independent interest. Additionally, we develop a computationally efficient algorithm that preserves DP at local clients and achieves the minimax optimal rate at the central server (up to logarithmic factors). The final estimator is obtained by applying three layers of spectral decomposition, posing significant technical challenges in deriving the sharp upper bound.

When there is only one local client, federated PCA simplifies to DP-PCA, and the upper bound derived in this study aligns with the results presented in
Cai et al. (2024b)
. However, we emphasize that the technical contributions of these two works are fundamentally distinct. The primary contribution of
Cai et al. (2024b)
lies in the precise characterization of the sensitivity of empirical spectral projectors and eigenvalues under the spiked covariance model, which serves as the foundation for our methodology and theoretical framework for Federated PCA presented in this paper. Specifically, their results are directly leveraged to determine the appropriate level of artificial noise to be added at each local client. In contrast, the technical challenges in Federated PCA stem from the need to perform a sharp analysis of aggregated PCA across multiple clients. Our proposed method involves not one, but
three
layers of spectral decomposition, and the precise perturbation analysis of the final estimator relies on an explicit characterization of both the stochastic error and the artificial noise introduced in the first and second layers, respectively. Each layer’s spectral decomposition is represented by a Neumann series expansion, leading to a composition of three Neumann series. Consequently, we had to develop a unified strategy to derive concentration bounds for numerous higher-order perturbation terms, which required new techniques beyond those used in single-client DP-PCA. See the proof sketch of Theorem
1
for more details on our approach.

1.3
Related work

Differentially private PCA was studied by
Blum et al. (2005)
;
Chaudhuri et al. (2011)
;
Dwork et al. (2014b)
in a deterministic setting without assuming data are independently sampled from a common distribution.
Liu et al. (2022)
investigated online methods and established the minimax optimal rate for rank-one PCA under the spiked model.
Cai et al. (2024b)
leveraged spectral tools and established the minimax optimal rates for general rank-
r
PCA and covariance matrix estimation. Federated PCA with homogeneous sample sizes and privacy constraints was studied by
Grammenos et al. (2020)
, assuming data arrive sequentially and all data points are uniformly bounded. Their estimator is sub-optimal without exploiting the statistical properties of sample data under the spiked covariance model.
Wang and Xu (2020)
studied non-interactive local differentially private PCA assuming that the observations are sampled independently from a common distribution but are uniformly bounded.

1.4
Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce a federated algorithm for differentially private PCA and covariance estimation. The algorithm incorporates three layers of spectral decomposition and employs the Gaussian mechanism to ensure privacy guarantees. We demonstrate that the algorithm produces valid DP estimators of the population covariance matrix and its spectral projectors, achieving minimax optimal error rates up to logarithmic factors. Additionally, we provide a proof sketch of the main theorem, outlining the technical challenges and our proof strategy.
Section
3
establishes the minimax lower bounds for differentially private federated PCA and covariance estimation. The proof leverages a matrix version of Van Tree’s inequality, which we believe is of independent interest. In Section
4
, we comprehensively evaluate the performance of our algorithm through numerical experiments and real data analysis, comparing it with existing methods. All technical proofs are included in the supplementary material.

2
Optimal Federated PCA by Gaussian Mechanism

In this section, we present the federated PCA and covariance matrix estimators under distributed differential privacy constraints. Due to the different levels of sensitivity of eigenvectors and eigenvalues, our approach estimates the eigenvectors and eigenvalues separately. Based on the given privacy budget, each local client produces its own differentially private estimator of the eigenvectors and send them to the central server. The central server aggregate these estimators with specially designed weights. Since the aggregation may break the geometric constraints of eigenvectors, an additional step of eigen-decomposition is applied, from which the spectral projector serves as the final estimator of eigenvectors. The algorithm essentially consists of
three
layers of spectral decomposition: two performed by the local clients and one by the central server. These multiple spectral decompositions are crucial for ensuring differential privacy and achieving minimax optimality. They pose significant technical challenges to the theoretical analysis, where we leverage sophisticated spectral representation tools
(
Xia, 2021
;
Cai et al., 2024b
)
to carefully examine the behavior of three-layer eigen-decompositions.

After the differentially private estimator of eigenvectors is determined, the central server broadcasts them back to the local clients. These are then used to produce differentially private estimators of eigenvalues at each local client according to the given privacy budget. The central server receives these estimators, aggregates them by a weighted sum, and outputs the final estimator of the covariance matrix. The details of our approach are summarized in Algorithm

Minimax Lower Bound

In this section, we establish the minimax lower bounds for PCA and covariance matrix estimation under the federated
(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta})
-DP constraints. These lower bounds match, up to logarithmic factors and

Under the spiked model with a covariance matrix

and
\mathcal{M}_{{\boldsymbol{n}},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}}

and

\varepsilon_{j}=O(1)

Theorem 3
.

Suppose

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{U}\in\mathcal{U}({\boldsymbol{n}},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta})}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-UU^{\top}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\geq\frac{c_{0}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Psi_{0}^{-2}(n_{j},\varepsilon_{j},\delta_{j})}\bigwedge(2r),

(16)

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{M}({\boldsymbol{n}},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta})}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\geq c_{0}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Psi_{0}^{-2}(n_{j},\varepsilon_{j},\delta_{j})}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Psi_{1}^{-2}(n_{j},\varepsilon_{j},\delta_{j})}\right)\bigwedge(2r\lambda^{2}).

5
Discussions

This paper establishes minimax optimal rates and demonstrates the multiple robustness and scalability of federated PCA. The central server’s estimator remains consistent as long as at least one local estimator is consistent. Moreover, even if all local estimators are inconsistent, the central estimator can still be consistent given a sufficient number of local clients. These findings highlight federated learning’s potential for reliable and robust statistical inference in a privacy-preserving manner, paving the way for further research and application in fields requiring stringent privacy and data security measures.

For simplicity, we assume in this paper that the mean vector of the data distribution is either zero or known. However, the approach can be readily adapted to handle cases where the mean is unknown. In such instances, we calculate the client-specific sample covariance matrix as
\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}=(n_{j}-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\big(X_{i}^{(j)}-\bar{X}^{(j)}\big)\big(X_{i}^{(j)}-\bar{X}^{(j)}\big)^{\top}
, where
\bar{X}^{(j)}
denotes the sample mean vector for the
j
-th local client. Under the Gaussian assumption, the distribution of
(n_{j}-1)\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}
remains Wishart, which preserves the validity of all technical proofs presented in this work, except that the sensitivity of empirical spectral projectors and eigenvalues need to be carefully re-examined. For analytical convenience, we assume a Gaussian data distribution throughout our study. Extending these results to sub-Gaussian or more general distributions is an intriguing avenue for future research. Nevertheless, as highlighted earlier, the main technical challenges lie in developing a unified framework to bound higher-order perturbation terms that arise from the three layers of spectral decomposition.

An interesting future research direction is the study of federated SVD under the low-rank matrix denoising model. While SVD and PCA are closely related in traditional settings, they differ significantly in the context of federated learning under DP constraints due to differences in measurement units. Specifically, the covariance matrix is symmetric, whereas the low-rank signal in the matrix denoising model can have dimensions that differ drastically
(
Cai and Zhang, 2018
)
. This introduces additional challenges and unique features when investigating minimax optimal rates for estimating the left and right singular subspaces under distributed differential privacy constraints. Nonetheless, we believe the multiple robustness phenomenon observed in federated PCA also applies to federated SVD, with the minimax optimal rate at the central server being the harmonic mean of the minimax optimal rates achievable at each local client.

Additionally, it is worthwhile to explore the minimax optimal rates in federated sparse PCA
(
Cai et al., 2013
)
and tensor PCA
(
Zhang and Xia, 2018
)
under privacy constraints. These problems often rely on iterative algorithms, making the development of sharp upper bounds technically challenging. Moreover, these settings are known to exhibit a statistical-to-computational gap even without privacy constraints. Understanding the interplay between privacy constraints and computational feasibility in these problems remains an open and important research problem.

\displaystyle\leq\int\int\bigg(e^{\varepsilon_{j}}{\mathbb{P}}\big((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg|X^{(j)}=x^{(j)},\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}=\widetilde{x}_{i}^{(j)}\big)+\delta_{j}\bigg)p_{X^{(j)}}(x^{(j)})p_{\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}}(\widetilde{x}_{i}^{(j)})dx^{(j)}d\widetilde{x}_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=e^{\varepsilon_{j}}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)+\delta_{j}.

Therefore, for an arbitrary
\tau>0
to be determined later, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon_{j}}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt+\tau\delta_{j}+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{1}\varepsilon_{j})\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt+\tau\delta_{j}+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt,

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
\max_{j\in[m]}\varepsilon_{j}=O(1)
. And similarly we can show

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{-}\geq t\bigg)dt-C_{1}\varepsilon_{j}\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{-}\geq t\bigg)dt

\displaystyle\quad-\tau\delta_{j}-\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt.

Combine these two inequalities and we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}G_{i}^{(j)}\leq{\mathbb{E}}\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}+2C_{1}\varepsilon_{j}{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|+2\tau\delta_{j}+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{-}\geq t\bigg)dt.

(44)

The first term in above right hand side vanishes. We now bound
{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|
. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big({\mathbb{E}}\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p(\widetilde{X}^{(j)}_{i};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\rangle\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(\nabla\log p(\widetilde{X}^{(j)}_{i};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\big)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]}.

Using the data processing inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(\nabla\log p(\widetilde{X}^{(j)}_{i};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\big)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(\nabla\log p(\widetilde{X}^{(j)}_{i};\Theta)\big)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}.

From (
37
) and (
40
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(\nabla\log p(\widetilde{X}^{(j)}_{i};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\big)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\|W^{-2}\|.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\|W^{-2}\|}\cdot\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]}.

(45)

It remains to bound the tail probabilities
{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)
and
{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{-}\geq t\bigg)
. Without loss of generality, we take
i=1
.
We shall first consider
{\mathbb{E}}|G_{1}^{(j)}|^{k}
for some large and absolute integer
k>0
.
Recall
G_{1}^{(j)}={\mathbb{E}}\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p(X^{(j)}_{1};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\rangle
. By definition, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|G_{1}^{(j)}|^{k}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big|{\mathbb{E}}\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p(X^{(j)}_{1};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\rangle\big|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big|\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j}),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle\big|^{k}.

where the inequality is due to Jenson’s inequality and recall

\displaystyle S_{j}:=\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})),

\displaystyle S_{j,1}:=\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p(X_{1}^{(j)};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))

Observe that

\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j}\big),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j,i}\big),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle.

Therefore

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}|\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j}),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle|^{k}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigg|\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}+\Big<\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j,i}\big),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\Big>\bigg|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\|_{\rm{F}}^{2k}+2^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg|\Big<\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j,i}\big),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\Big>\bigg|^{k}.

Denote

Y^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}:=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{2}^{(j)},\cdots,Y_{n_{j}}^{(j)}\\
\end{bmatrix}\quad{\rm and}\quad Z^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}:=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{2}^{(j)},\cdots,Z_{n_{j}}^{(j)}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Then we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j,i}\big)

\displaystyle=\big((\lambda+\sigma^{2})^{-1}-\sigma^{-2}\big)\bigg(\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}Z_{i}^{(j)}Y_{i}^{(j)\top}W^{-1}\bar{\Theta}^{\top}+\bar{\Theta}W^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}Y_{i}^{(j)}Z_{i}^{(j)\top}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\big((\lambda+\sigma^{2})^{-1}-\sigma^{-2}\big)\Big(\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}Z^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}Y^{(j)\top}_{2:n_{j}}W^{-1}\bar{\Theta}^{\top}+\bar{\Theta}W^{-1}Y_{2:n_{j}}^{(j)}Z_{2:n_{j}}^{(j)\top}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}\Big).

By the definitions in eq. (
36
), we know that all entries of
Y^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}
are i.i.d. obeying distribution
N(0,\lambda+\sigma^{2})
. Similarly, all entries of
Z^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}
are i.i.d. obeying
N(0,\sigma^{2})
. Based on these facts, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j,i}\big),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle=2\big((\lambda+\sigma^{2})^{-1}-\sigma^{-2}\big)^{2}\Big<Z^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}Y^{(j)\top}_{2:n_{j}}W^{-1},Z_{1}^{(j)}Y_{1}^{(j)\top}W^{-1}\big>.

By denoting
\mu:=\big((\lambda+\sigma^{2})^{-1}-\sigma^{-2}\big)^{2}(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n_{j}}\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big(S_{j,i}\big),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle=2\mu\langle\bar{Z}^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}\bar{Y}^{(j)\top}_{2:n_{j}}W^{-1},\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j)}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)\top}W^{-1}\rangle,

where
\bar{\cdot}
are the normalized version, i.e., the entries of
\bar{Z}^{(j)}
and
\bar{Y}^{(j)}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables.

Using the tower rule, we get

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}|\langle\bar{Z}^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}\bar{Y}^{(j)\top}_{2:n_{j}}W^{-1},\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j)}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)\top}W^{-1}\rangle|^{k}={\mathbb{E}}|\langle\bar{Z}^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}},\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j)}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)\top}W^{-2}\bar{Y}^{(j)}_{2:n_{j}}\rangle|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq k^{k/2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j)}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{k}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\bar{Y}^{(j)\top}_{2:n_{j}}W^{-2}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{k}

\displaystyle=k^{k/2}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{(k/2)-1}(p-r+2i)\cdot\prod_{l=0}^{(k/2)-1}(n_{j}-1+2l)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq C^{k}k^{k}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{(k/2)-1}(p-r+2i)\cdot\prod_{l=0}^{(k/2)-1}(n_{j}-1+2l)\cdot\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}^{k},

where, in the first and last inequalities, we used Lemma
4
to show that
{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{k}\leq(Ck^{1/2}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}})^{k}
. Here
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Similarly, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\|_{\rm{F}}^{2k}

\displaystyle=(2\mu)^{k}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j)}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)\top}W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2k}

\displaystyle=(2\mu)^{k}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j)}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2k}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-1}\bar{Y}_{1}^{(j)}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq C^{k}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(p-r+2i)\cdot k^{k}\|W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2k}.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|G_{1}^{(j)}|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}|\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j}),\nabla\psi(\Theta)(S_{j,1})\rangle|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq C^{k}k^{k}\mu^{k}\bigg((p-r)^{k}\|W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2k}+(p-r)^{k/2}n_{j}^{k/2}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}^{k}\bigg).

We can similarly show the upper bound for
{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}
as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}\leq C^{k}k^{k}\mu^{k}\bigg((d-r)^{k}\|W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2k}+(d-r)^{k/2}n_{j}^{k/2}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}^{k}\bigg).

By Markov’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(|G_{i}^{(j)}|\geq t\bigg)={\mathbb{P}}\bigg(|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}\geq t^{k}\bigg)\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}}{t^{k}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt\leq\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}}{t^{k}}dt=\frac{1}{k-1}\tau^{-k+1}{\mathbb{E}}|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}.

Observe that, by setting
\tau=(\delta_{j}^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k})^{1/k}
, we get

\displaystyle\quad\tau\delta_{j}+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((G_{i}^{(j)})^{+}\geq t\bigg)dt+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\bigg((\widetilde{G}_{i}^{(j)})^{-}\geq t\bigg)dt

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{k-1}\delta_{j}\tau+\cdots+\frac{1}{k-1}\delta_{j}\tau}_{k-1\text{terms}}+\frac{2}{k-1}\tau^{-k+1}{\mathbb{E}}|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2({\mathbb{E}}|G_{i}^{(j)}|^{k})^{1/k}\delta_{j}^{\frac{k-1}{k}}

\displaystyle\leq Ck\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\big((d-r)\|W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}+(d-r)^{1/2}n_{j}^{1/2}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}\big)\delta_{j}^{\frac{k-1}{k}}.

By plugging the above bound into (
44
), we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}G_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}\varepsilon_{j}\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\|W^{-2}\|}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\quad+Ck\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\big((d-r)\|W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}+(d-r)^{1/2}n_{j}^{1/2}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}\big)\delta_{j}^{\frac{k-1}{k}}.

Together with (
43
), we get

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}n_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\|W^{-2}\|}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\quad+Ckn_{j}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\big((p-r)\|W^{-1}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}+(p-r)^{1/2}n_{j}^{1/2}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}\big)\delta_{j}^{\frac{k-1}{k}}.

Therefore, as long as

\displaystyle Ck\cdot n_{j}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\big((p-r)r+(p-r)^{1/2}r^{1/2}n_{j}^{1/2}\big)\delta_{j}^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\leq C_{1}^{2}n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}},

we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\|\nabla\psi(\Theta)(\nabla\log p({\mathcal{D}}_{j};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}))\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\big]\leq C_{1}^{2}n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\|W^{-2}\|.

As a result, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big)\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta\leq C_{1}^{2}n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|,

(46)

where recall that
W\sim W_{r}(I_{r},p)
follows the Wishart distribution.

Using the data processing inequality, we have another upper bound for
{\mathbb{E}}\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big)
as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big)\leq\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big),

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}
is defined in (
39
).
From (
38
) and
{\mathcal{C}}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}{\mathcal{C}}_{j,i}
, we see

\displaystyle\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ{\mathcal{C}}_{j}\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big)=\frac{2n_{j}\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}(p-r)\textsf{tr}(W^{-2}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big)\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta\leq\frac{4n_{j}\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}\frac{r}{p-r}.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big)\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta\leq\min\bigg\{C_{1}^{2}n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|,\frac{4n_{j}r}{p-r}\bigg\}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}.

Finally, we plug these bounds into the right hand side of the inequality in Lemma
3
, we obtain (recall that we focus on the regime
\max_{j\in[m]}\varepsilon_{j}=O(1)
)

\displaystyle\int\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-\psi(\Theta)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta\gtrsim\frac{r^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min\big\{n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|,\frac{n_{j}r}{p-r}\big\}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\sigma^{2}}+r}

Finally we bound
{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|
.
Denote the event
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}:=\{\|W-pI_{r}\|\leq p/2\}
.
From the basic concentration inequality of sample covariance matrix
(
Koltchinskii and Lounici, 2017
)
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{F}}_{0})\geq 1-e^{-c_{1}p}
.
Under
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
, we have
\lambda_{\min}(W)\geq p/2
.
So we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|\cdot\mathds{1}({\mathcal{F}}_{0})+{\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|\cdot\mathds{1}({\mathcal{F}}_{0}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq 4p^{-2}+({\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|^{2})^{1/2}\cdot e^{-c_{1}p/2}

\displaystyle\leq 4p^{-2}+({\mathbb{E}}\|W^{-2}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2})^{1/2}\cdot e^{-c_{1}p/2}

\displaystyle=4p^{-2}+({\mathbb{E}}\textsf{tr}(W^{-4}))^{1/2}\cdot e^{-c_{1}p/2}.

The term
{\mathbb{E}}\textsf{tr}(W^{-4})
can be computed using the Theorem 4.1 of
Von Rosen (1988)
, which implies
{\mathbb{E}}\textsf{tr}(W^{-4})\cdot e^{-c_{1}p/2}\leq p^{-2}
.

Lower bound for covariance matrix estimation.

We consider a subset
\Theta_{1}
of
\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})
:

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}=\bigg\{\Sigma=\lambda UU^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I:U\in{\mathbb{O}}_{p,r}\bigg\}

In this set, both
\lambda
and
\sigma^{2}
are known to us, and it boils down to estimating
U
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta_{1}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\widehat{U}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta})}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})}\lambda^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-UU^{\top}\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{0}pr}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot p^{-1}r^{-1})\Big)}(\lambda\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{4})\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2}).

(47)

Now if
\lambda/\sigma^{2}\geq 1
, then in addition to (
47
), we consider another set

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}=\bigg\{\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}(\lambda+\sigma^{2})VV^{\top}+(\lambda+\sigma^{2})I_{r}&0\\
0&\sigma^{2}I_{p-r}\\
\end{bmatrix}:V\in{\mathbb{O}}_{r,\frac{r}{2}}\bigg\}.

For any
\Sigma\in\Theta_{2}
, it admits the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V&V_{\perp}\\
\end{bmatrix}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}\textsf{diag}(\underbrace{2\lambda+\sigma^{2},\cdots,2\lambda+\sigma^{2}}_{\frac{r}{2}\text{~times}},\lambda,\cdots,\lambda)\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V&V_{\perp}\\
\end{bmatrix}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I,

where
V_{\perp}\in{\mathbb{O}}_{r,\frac{r}{2}}
is the orthogonal complement of
V
. Since
\lambda/\sigma^{2}\geq 1
, we can conclude
\Theta_{2}\subset\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})
.
Now the original problem reduces to a smaller one. Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}(\lambda,\sigma^{2})=\bigg\{\Sigma=

\displaystyle V\Lambda V^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I:

\displaystyle V\in{\mathbb{O}}_{r,\frac{r}{2}},\Lambda=\textsf{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}),c_{0}\lambda\leq\lambda_{r}\leq\cdots\leq C_{0}\lambda\bigg\}.

Then from (
47
), we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\widetilde{\Theta}(\lambda+\sigma^{2},\lambda+\sigma^{2})}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{c_{0}r^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot r^{-2})\Big)}(\lambda+\sigma^{2})^{2}\right)\bigwedge\big(r(\lambda+\sigma)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{c_{0}r^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot r^{-2})\Big)}\right)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2})

Note that the estimation of
V
in
\widetilde{\Theta}_{2}
is a sub-problem of estimating
\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V&V_{\perp}\\
\end{bmatrix}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}
in
\Theta_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\widetilde{\Theta}(\lambda+\sigma^{2},\lambda+\sigma^{2})}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{c_{0}r^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot r^{-2})\Big)}\right)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2}).

Together with the bound in (
47
), we conclude when
\lambda/\sigma^{2}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\quad\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\geq\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta_{1}\cup\Theta_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\bigg(\bigg(\frac{c_{0}pr}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot d^{-1}r^{-1})\Big)}(\lambda\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{4})\bigg)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2})\bigg)\bigvee\bigg(\bigg(\frac{c_{0}r^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot r^{-2})\Big)}\bigg)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2})\bigg).

Notice when
\lambda/\sigma^{2}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c_{0}dr}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot p^{-1}r^{-1})\Big)}(\lambda\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{4})\right)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2})\geq\left(\frac{c_{0}r^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot r^{-2})\Big)}\right)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2}).

Therefore we conclude for any
\lambda,\sigma^{2}
satisfy the condition in the theorem,

\displaystyle\quad\inf_{\widehat{\Sigma}}\sup_{\Sigma\in\Theta(\lambda,\sigma^{2})}{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\bigg(\bigg(\frac{c_{0}pr}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot p^{-1}r^{-1})\Big)}(\lambda\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{4})\bigg)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2})\bigg)\bigvee\bigg(\bigg(\frac{c_{0}r^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(n_{j}\wedge(n_{j}^{2}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\cdot r^{-2})\Big)}\bigg)\bigwedge(r\lambda^{2})\bigg).

This finishes the proof of Theorem
3
.

A.5
Proof of Lemma
3

We use
x=\{X^{(j)}_{i},i=1,\cdots,n_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
to represent the collection of all data,
X^{(j)}=\{X^{(j)}_{i},i=1,\cdots,n_{j}\}
, and
\Theta
to be the parameter.
Condition on
\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, we define the random matrices

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-\psi(\Theta),

\displaystyle B_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k,l)\in[p]\times[r]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Theta_{kl}}\big([\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta)]_{kl}\cdot p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m})\cdot p(\Theta)\big)\frac{1}{p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m})p(\Theta)},

where
p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})
is the conditional density with parameter
\Theta
and we have

p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j}).

Now we define the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[\langle A,B\rangle|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]=\int\int\langle A,B\rangle p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})p(\Theta)d\Theta dx
. Then using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we see

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|A\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]\geq\frac{({\mathbb{E}}[\langle A,B\rangle|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}])^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]}.

(48)

Simple calculation shows

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle A,B\rangle|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]

\displaystyle=\int\int\sum_{ij}[\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-\psi(\Theta)]_{ij}\cdot\sum_{k,l}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Theta_{kl}}\big([\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta)]_{kl}p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})p(\Theta)\big)d\Theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\int\sum_{ij,kl}[\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta)]_{kl}^{2}\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big)p(\Theta)d\Theta,

where the second equality holds from integration by parts,
\int p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})dx=1
and
\sum_{ij,kl}[\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta)]_{kl}^{2}=\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big)
. Meanwhile,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|A\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]=\int\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-\psi(\Theta)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta.

Notice the right hand side is still a function of
\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}
.
Next we consider the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\bigg(\Delta\psi_{ij}(\Theta)+\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})+\nabla\log p(\Theta)\rangle\bigg)^{2}.

Since
\psi(\Theta)
is independent of
\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\Delta\psi_{ij}^{2}(\Theta)=\int\sum_{ij}\Delta\psi_{ij}^{2}(\Theta)p(\Theta)d\Theta.

Notice

\displaystyle\int\nabla\log p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\cdot p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})dx=\nabla\big[\int p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})dx\big]=0,

(49)

where the last equality is due to
\int p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})dx=1
.
Thus,
{\mathbb{E}}\nabla\log p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})=0
.
Also notice
\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(\Theta)\rangle=0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})+\nabla\log p(\Theta)\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\sum_{k=1}^{m}\nabla\log p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{k})\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{k})\rangle^{2},

where in the last line the cross terms vanish due to (
49
). Recall

{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})={\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(\nabla\log p(X^{(j)};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\big)^{*}\circ\nabla\log p(X^{(j)};\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\big].

Therefore

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(X^{(j)},\Theta|\widehat{U}_{k})\rangle^{2}=\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{k})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)\big)\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta

Using (
49
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{ij}\Delta\psi_{ij}(\Theta)\cdot\langle\nabla\psi_{ij}(\Theta),\nabla\log p(x,\Theta|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\})\rangle=0.

So we conclude

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]=\int\sum_{ij}\Delta\psi_{ij}^{2}(\Theta)p(\Theta)d\Theta+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ{\mathcal{I}}(\Theta|\widehat{U}_{j})\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big)\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta.

Now taking expectation w.r.t.
\widehat{U}_{j}
in (
48
), and using Jenson’s inequality yield the desired result

\displaystyle\int{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{U}\widehat{U}^{\top}-\psi(\Theta)\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}\cdot p(\Theta)d\Theta

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}\frac{({\mathbb{E}}[\langle A,B\rangle|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}])^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\frac{\bigg(\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big)p(\Theta)d\Theta\bigg)^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\bigg(\int\textsf{tr}\big(\nabla\psi(\Theta)\circ\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}\big)p(\Theta)d\Theta\bigg)^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}[\|B\|_{\rm{F}}^{2}|\{\widehat{U}_{j}\}]\big]}.

Technical Lemma

Lemma 4
.

Let
g_{1},\cdots,g_{m}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
, and
c_{1},\cdots,c_{m}\geq 0
. Then for any integer
l\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g_{i}^{2})^{l}\leq(Cl\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i})^{l}

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Proof.

We show this by expanding
(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g_{i}^{2})^{l}
. In fact, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g_{i}^{2})^{l}={\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{l}=1}^{m}c_{i_{1}}\cdots c_{i_{l}}g_{i_{1}}^{2}\cdots g_{i_{l}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{i}c_{i})^{l}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}g^{2l}\leq(Cl)^{l}\cdot(\sum_{i}c_{i})^{l},

where
g\sim N(0,1)
and we use the moment bound for Gaussian in the last inequality.
∎

Lemma 5

(
Koltchinskii and Lounici (2017)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. samples from
N(0,\Sigma)
, and
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}
. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|\asymp\|\Sigma\|\bigg(\frac{\widetilde{r}}{n}\vee\sqrt{\frac{\widetilde{r}}{n}}\bigg),

where
\widetilde{r}=\frac{\textsf{tr}(\Sigma)}{\|\Sigma\|}
is the effective rank of
\Sigma
.
Moreover, there exists an absolute constant
C_{1}>0
, such that for all
t\geq 1
, with probability exceeding
1-e^{-t}
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|-{\mathbb{E}}\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|\bigg|\leq C_{1}\|\Sigma\|\bigg(\frac{t}{n}\vee\sqrt{\frac{t}{n}}\bigg).

Lemma 6
.

Let
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a sub-Gaussian random vector with
{\mathbb{E}}X=0
, and denote
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}
its
\psi_{2}
norm. Then we have for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}\geq t\bigg)\leq 4^{d}\exp(-\frac{Ct^{2}}{\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}})

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be an
1/2
cover of the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
, then
N\leq 4^{d}
. Notice
\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}=\langle X,\frac{X}{\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}}\rangle
. Then there exists some
x_{0}\in\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, such that
\|x_{0}-X/\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq 1/2
. Now

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}=\langle X,\frac{X}{\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}}\rangle=\langle X,\frac{X}{\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle X,x_{0}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}+\langle X,x_{0}\rangle.

This implies
\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq 2\langle X,x_{0}\rangle
. We conclude

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\|X\|_{\ell_{2}}\geq t)\leq 4^{d}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\langle X,x_{0}\rangle\geq t/2)\leq 4^{d}\exp(-\frac{Ct^{2}}{\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}).

∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
X
be random variable such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq\max\{a+bt,\sqrt{a+bt}\})\leq e^{-t}

for some
1>a>0,b>0
and all
t>0
, then we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a+b+2ab+2b^{2},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq a^{2}+2ab+2b^{2}+16a^{3}b+96b^{4}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X|^{2}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|^{2}\geq s)ds=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)2sds.

We then decompose the integral into three parts:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{a}}+\int_{\sqrt{a}}^{1}+\int_{1}^{\infty}.

(50)

For the first part, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)2sds\leq a.

For the second part, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{a}}^{1}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)2sds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1-a}{b}}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq\sqrt{a+bt})2(a+bt)^{1/2}\frac{1}{2}(a+bt)^{-1/2}bdt

\displaystyle\leq b\int_{0}^{\frac{1-a}{b}}e^{-t}dt\leq b.

For the third part, we have

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)2sds

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1-a}{b}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq a+bt)2(a+bt)bdt

\displaystyle\leq 2b\int_{\frac{1-a}{b}}^{\infty}e^{-t}(a+bt)dt

\displaystyle\leq 2ab+2b^{2}

For the fourth order moment, we have similarly

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X|^{4}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|^{4}\geq s)ds=\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)4s^{3}ds.

Using the decomposition as in (
50
), we have for the first part,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{a}}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)4s^{3}ds\leq a^{2}.

For the second part, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{a}}^{1}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)4s^{3}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1-a}{b}}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq\sqrt{a+bt})4(a+bt)^{3/2}\frac{1}{2}(a+bt)^{-1/2}bdt

\displaystyle\leq 2b\int_{0}^{\frac{1-a}{b}}e^{-t}(a+bt)dt

\displaystyle\leq 2ab+2b^{2}.

For the third part, we have

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq s)4s^{3}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1-a}{b}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq a+bt)4(a+bt)^{3}bdt

\displaystyle\leq 4b\int_{\frac{1-a}{b}}^{\infty}e^{-t}(a+bt)^{3}dt

\displaystyle\leq 16a^{3}b+96b^{4}.

∎

Appendix C
Some Linear Algebras

C.1
Derivation for
\nabla\psi(\Theta)

Let the map
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
be defined as
\psi(\Theta)=\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}
. Then the gradient of
\psi
evaluated at
\Theta
is a linear map:
\nabla\psi(\Theta):{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
. We set

\displaystyle\psi_{1}:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\text{~as~}\psi_{1}(\Theta)=\Theta,

\displaystyle\psi_{2}:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}\text{~as~}\psi_{2}(\Theta)=(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1},

\displaystyle\psi_{3}:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times p}\text{~as~}\psi_{1}(\Theta)=\Theta^{\top}.

Then using product rule, we have for any
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}
,

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(Y)

\displaystyle=\nabla\psi_{1}(\Theta)(Y)\cdot\psi_{2}(\Theta)\cdot\psi_{3}(\Theta)+\psi_{1}(\Theta)\cdot\nabla\psi_{2}(\Theta)(Y)\cdot\psi_{3}(\Theta)

\displaystyle\quad+\psi_{1}(\Theta)\cdot\psi_{2}(\Theta)\cdot\nabla\psi_{3}(\Theta)(Y).

Notice here
\nabla\psi_{1}(\Theta):{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}
is defined as
\nabla\psi_{1}(\Theta)(Y)=Y
and
\nabla\psi_{3}(\Theta):{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times p}
is defined as
\nabla\psi_{3}(\Theta)(Y)=Y^{\top}
.
Now we compute
\nabla\psi_{2}(\Theta)
. Following definition of gradient,
\nabla\psi_{2}(\Theta):{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
. We set
\psi_{2,1}(\Theta)=\Theta^{\top}\Theta
, and
\psi_{2,2}(M)=M^{-1}
.Then using product rule,

\displaystyle\nabla\psi_{2,1}(\Theta)(Y)=Y^{\top}\Theta+\Theta^{\top}Y.

We also define
\psi_{2,3}(M)=M
.
\nabla\psi_{2,2}(M):{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
can be calculated using product rule. Notice for any
N\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
,

\displaystyle 0=\nabla(\psi_{2,2}\cdot\psi_{2,3})(M)(N)=\nabla\psi_{2,2}(M)(N)\cdot M+M^{-1}\cdot N,

which implies
\nabla\psi_{2,2}(M)(N)=-M^{-1}NM^{-1}
. Notice
\psi_{2}(\Theta)=\psi_{2,2}\circ\psi_{2,1}(\Theta)
. Using chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\psi_{2}(\Theta)(Y)

\displaystyle=\nabla(\psi_{2,2}\circ\psi_{2,1})(\Theta)(Y)=\nabla\psi_{2,2}(\psi_{2,1}(\Theta))\big(\nabla\psi_{2,1}(\Theta)(Y)\big)

\displaystyle=\nabla\psi_{2,2}(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)\big(Y^{\top}\Theta+\Theta^{\top}Y\big)

\displaystyle=-(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}(Y^{\top}\Theta+\Theta^{\top}Y)(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}.

We have

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(\Theta)\cdot\nabla\psi_{2}(\Theta)(Y)\cdot\psi_{3}(\Theta)=-\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}(Y^{\top}\Theta+\Theta^{\top}Y)(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(\Theta)(Y)

\displaystyle=Y(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}-\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}(Y^{\top}\Theta+\Theta^{\top}Y)(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}+\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}Y^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}Y(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}+\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}Y^{\top}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top},

where
\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}=I_{p}-\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}
.

C.2
Derivation for
\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}

Once we obtain the closed-form for
\nabla\psi(\Theta)
, we can compute its adjoint
\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}
. For any
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times r}
,
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
,
we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}(M),Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle M,\nabla\psi(\Theta)(Y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle M,\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}Y(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}\Theta^{\top}+\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}Y^{\top}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}(M+M^{\top})\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1},Y\rangle.

So we conclude
\nabla\psi(\Theta)^{*}(M)=\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}\bar{\Theta}_{\perp}^{\top}(M+M^{\top})\Theta(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)^{-1}
.
