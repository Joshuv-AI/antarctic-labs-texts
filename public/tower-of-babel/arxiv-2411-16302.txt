Krylov Complexity in the Schrödinger Field Theory
Peng-Zhang He, Hai-Qing Zhang

Abstract
We investigate the Krylov complexity of Schrödinger field theories, focusing on both bosonic and fermionic systems within the grand canonical ensemble that includes a chemical potential. Krylov complexity measures operator growth in quantum systems by analyzing how operators spread within the Krylov space, a subspace of the Hilbert space spanned by successive applications of the superoperator [ H , ⋅ ] [H,\cdot] on an initial operator. Using the Lanczos algorithm, we construct an orthonormal Krylov basis and derive the Lanczos coefficients, which govern the operator connectivity and thus characterize the complexity. Our study reveals that the Lanczos coefficients { b n } \{b_{n}\} are independent of the chemical potential, while { a n } \{a_{n}\} exhibits a dependence on it. Both { a n } \{a_{n}\} and { b n } \{b_{n}\} show linear relationships with respect to n n . For both bosonic and fermionic systems, the Krylov complexities behave similarly over time, especially at late times, due to the analogous profiles of the squared absolute values of their autocorrelation functions | φ 0 ​ ( t ) | 2 \absolutevalue{\varphi_{0}(t)}^{2} . The Krylov complexity grows exponentially with time, but its asymptotic scaling factor λ K \lambda_{K} is significantly smaller than the twice of the slope of the { b n } \{b_{n}\} coefficients, contrasting to the relativistic field theories where the scaling aligns more closely with the twice of the slope of { b n } \{b_{n}\} .

2
Preliminaries

To ensure the reader’s understanding and make the paper self-contained, we will provide a detailed introduction to the basics of Krylov complexity in this section. This section outlines the theoretical framework for computing Krylov complexity. We first introduce the construction of Krylov basis and Lanczos coefficients through the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process. Next, we use the moment method to establish the relationship between the Wightman power spectrum, moments, and the Lanczos coefficients. Finally, we connect the spectral function to the Wightman two-point function, which is essential for our field theory computations in the subsequent sections.

2.1
Krylov complexity

In the quantum theory, a Heisenberg operator
O(t)
satisfies the Heisenberg equation
[
60
]

\partial_{t}O(t)=i[H,O(t)],

(1)

where
H
is the time-independent Hamiltonian. The operator
O(t)
may be either Hermitian or non-Hermitian. We can define a superoperator
\mathcal{L}\equiv[H,\cdot]
such that

\mathcal{L}O(t)\equiv[H,O(t)].

(2)

Then we have

\partial_{t}O(t)=i\mathcal{L}O(t).

(3)

This expression resembles the Schrödinger equation for a state, which has a formal solution:

O(t)=e^{i\mathcal{L}t}\mathcal{O}(0)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{L}^{n}O,

(4)

where
O\equiv O(0)
and

\mathcal{L}^{0}O=O,\qquad\mathcal{L}O=[H,O],\qquad\mathcal{L}^{2}O=[H,[H,O]],\qquad\cdots.

(5)

The operator
O(t)
belongs to a subspace of operators known as the Krylov space, which is spanned by
\{\mathcal{L}^{n}O\},n=0,1,2,\cdots
. The Krylov space is also a Hilbert space, and one can define an inner product for it. One can define the Hilbert space vector as
\left|A\right)
corresponding to operator
A
, then the inner product can be formulated as
[
1
,
61
]

(A|B)^{g}_{\beta}=\int_{0}^{\beta}g(\lambda)\expectationvalue{e^{\lambda H}A^{\dagger}e^{-\lambda H}B}_{\beta}d\lambda.

(6)

In this formula,
\beta
represents the inverse temperature, while
\expectationvalue{\cdots}_{\beta}
is the thermal expectation value

\expectationvalue{A}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{Z}\tr(e^{-\beta H}A),\quad Z=\tr(e^{-\beta H}).

(7)

For (
6
) to be a suitable inner product, the function
g(\lambda)
should satisfy

g(\lambda)\geq 0,\qquad g(\beta-\lambda)=g(\lambda),\qquad\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\lambda g(\lambda)=1.

(8)

It can be observed that

\expectationvalue{e^{\lambda H}A^{\dagger}e^{-\lambda H}\mathcal{L}B}_{\beta}=\expectationvalue{e^{-\lambda H}(\mathcal{L}A)^{\dagger}e^{-\lambda H}B}_{\beta},

(9)

indicating that
\mathcal{L}
is a Hermitian operator in Krylov space. Assuming that
O
is initially normalized, then

\expectationvalue{e^{\lambda H}O^{\dagger}(t)e^{-\lambda H}O(t)}_{\beta}=\expectationvalue{e^{\lambda H}O^{\dagger}(0)e^{-\lambda H}O(0)}_{\beta}.

(10)

This means that regardless of whether the operator
O
is Hermitian, an initial normalized operator will always remain normalized as it evolves.

In the Krylov space,
\{\mathcal{L}^{n}O\}
is the basis, as we mentioned above, but such a basis is typically neither orthogonal nor normalized. We aim to construct an orthonormal basis, known as the Krylov basis
\{\left|O_{n}\right)\}
, which can be constructed from the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process. The Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process is a method that involves the following procedures
[
62
]
. Let
\xi,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\cdots,\eta_{n}
be elements of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then

\xi=u_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}\eta_{n}+\tau,\qquad(u_{i}\in\mathbb{C},{\rm and}~(\eta_{i},\tau)=0),

(11)

where
(\eta,\tau)
denotes the inner product of
\eta
and
\tau
in the Hilbert space, and
\tau
is also a vector in Hilbert space. That is to say, any vector in
\mathcal{H}
can be written as a linear combination of
\eta_{1},\cdots,\eta_{n}
, plus a vector
\tau
perpendicular to the
\eta
’s. In the Krylov space, it is clear that there is

\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}{\alpha^{n}}_{m}\left|O_{m}\right)+b_{n+1}\left|O_{n+1}\right),

(12)

where
{\alpha^{n}}_{m}=\left(O_{m}\right|\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right)
,
b_{n+1}=\left(O_{n+1}\right|\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right)
. If
\{{\alpha^{n}}_{m}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
are real numbers, then

b_{n+1}={\alpha^{n}}_{n+1}={\alpha^{n+1}}_{n}.

(13)

It can be observed that

\left(O_{m}\right|\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right)=\left(O_{n}\right|\mathcal{L}\left|O_{m}\right)={\alpha^{n}}_{m}={\alpha^{m}}_{n}=0,\qquad\text{for}\ m-n\geq 2~\text{and}~n\geq 0.

(14)

Specifically, Let
\left|O_{0}\right)=\left|O\right)
be a normalized operator, then

\mathcal{L}\left|O_{0}\right)=a_{0}\left|O_{0}\right)+b_{1}\left|O_{1}\right).

(15)

In accordance with the notation used in (
12
), we define
a_{0}={\alpha^{0}}_{0}=\left(O_{0}\right|\mathcal{L}\left|O_{0}\right)
. Subsequently, we can set

a_{n}={\alpha^{n}}_{n}=\left(O_{n}\right|\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right).

(16)

Following the previous operations, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left|O_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{1}\left|O_{0}\right)+a_{1}\left|O_{1}\right)+b_{2}\left|O_{2}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left|O_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{2}\left|O_{1}\right)+a_{2}\left|O_{2}\right)+b_{3}\left|O_{3}\right),

(18)

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{n}\left|O_{n-1}\right)+a_{n}\left|O_{n}\right)+b_{n+1}\left|O_{n+1}\right),

(19)

\displaystyle\cdots

These two sequences
\{b_{n}\}
and
\{a_{n}\}
are called the Lanczos coefficients. In the Krylov space, the operator
O(t)
can be expressed as
[
1
]

\left|O(t)\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i^{n}\varphi_{n}(t)\left|O_{n}\right),

(20)

where
\varphi_{n}(t)=(O_{n}|O(t))
. It is not difficult to see that
\varphi_{n}(0)=\delta_{n0}
and
\varphi_{n}(t)=0
for
n<0
. The vector
\left|O(t)\right)
is always normalized, which is equivalent to

\sum_{n}\absolutevalue{\varphi_{n}(t)}^{2}=1.

(21)

By substituting equation (
20
) into the Heisenberg equation (
1
), we can express the left side of equation (
1
) as follows:

\partial_{t}\left|O(t)\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i^{n}\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)\left|O_{n}\right),

(22)

and the right side becomes:

\displaystyle i\mathcal{L}\left|O(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i^{n+1}\varphi_{n}(t)\mathcal{L}\left|O_{n}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i^{n+1}\varphi_{n}(t)\left[b_{n}\left|O_{n-1}\right)+a_{n}\left|O_{n}\right)+b_{n+1}\left|O_{n+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i^{n}[i^{2}\varphi_{n+1}b_{n+1}+ia_{n}\varphi_{n}(t)+b_{n}\varphi_{n-1}(t)]\left|O_{n}\right),

where we have assumed that
b_{0}=0
. By matching the coefficients of
\left|O_{n}\right)
, we derive the discrete Schrödinger equation

\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=ia_{n}\varphi_{n}(t)+b_{n}\varphi_{n-1}(t)-b_{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}(t).

(24)

The evolution of operators over time, or their growth, can now be understood as a hopping problem on a one-dimensional chain. In this paper, we define the Krylov complexity as follows
[
18
]
:

K(t)=1+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\absolutevalue{\varphi_{n}(t)}^{2},

(25)

in order to be consistent with previous literature in quantum field theory
[
10
]
. It is important to emphasize that operators’s growth is directly related to an increase in the number of contributing
n
. However, this does not imply that increased operators will consistently increase the Krylov complexity. Detailed discussions on this topic can be found in
[
10
]
.

To obtain the Krylov complexity, one must first derive the Lanczos coefficients and solve the discrete Schrödinger equation (
24
). The Lanczos coefficients can be calculated using the moment method
[
63
]
, where the moments are defined as follows:

\mu_{n}\equiv\left(O\right|\mathcal{L}^{n}\left|O\right)=\left(O\right|\left.(-i)^{n}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\left|O(t)\right)\right|_{t=0}=(-i)^{n}\left.\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\varphi_{0}(t)\right|_{t=0}.

(26)

In the second equality, we used the Heisenberg equation. To compute the moments, one can introduce an important quantity known as the auto-correlation function

\displaystyle C(t):

\displaystyle=(O(t)|O)=\varphi_{0}^{\ast}(t),

(27)

and the Wightman two-point function,

\displaystyle\Pi^{W}(t)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(t)=(O|O(t))=\expectationvalue{e^{\beta H/2}O^{\dagger}(0)e^{-\beta H/2}O(t)}_{\beta}

(28)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}(0)e^{iH(t+i\beta/2)}O(0)e^{-iH(t+i\beta/2)}}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{O^{\dagger}(0)O(t+i\beta/2)}_{\beta}.

Here, we have defined the inner product using the Wightman inner product

(A|B)=\expectationvalue{e^{H\beta/2}A^{\dagger}e^{-H\beta/2}B}_{\beta},

(29)

which means taking
g(\lambda)=\delta(\lambda-\beta/2)
in Eq.(
6
). The autocorrelation function (
27
) can also be written as:

C(t)=\expectationvalue{O^\dagger(t-i\beta/2)O(0)}_{\beta}.

(30)

The Fourier transform of the auto-correlation function is referred to as the Wightman power spectrum

f^{W}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}C(t).

(31)

The moments (
26
) then can be rewritten in terms of
f^{W}(\omega)
as,

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{n}[f^{W}(\omega)]^{\ast}.

(32)

Since
\mu_{0}=1
,
f^{W}(\omega)
should satisfy the normalization condition

1=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega[f^{W}(\omega)]^{\ast}.

(33)

Once we have obtained the moments, we can calculate the Lanczos coefficients through the following recurrence relations
[
63
]

\displaystyle M^{(n)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{(n-1)}_{k}-L^{(n-1)}_{n-1}\frac{M^{(n-1)}_{k}}{M^{(n-1)}_{n-1}},

(34)

\displaystyle L^{(n)}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{M^{(n)}_{k+1}}{M^{(n)}_{n}}-\frac{M^{(n-1)}_{k}}{M^{(n-1)}_{n-1}},

(35)

where
n=1,\cdots,2K
,
k=n,\cdots,2K-n+1
and

M^{(0)}_{k}=(-1)^{k}\mu_{k},\qquad L^{(0)}_{k}=(-1)^{k+1}\mu_{k+1},\qquad k=0,\cdots,2K.

(36)

The resulting Lanczos coefficients are

b_{n}^{2}=M^{(n)}_{n},\qquad a_{n}=-L^{(n)}_{n},\qquad n=0,\cdots,K.

(37)

These equations may seem complicated, but they are not difficult to operate in practice. We just need to regard
M^{(n)}_{k}
and
L^{(n)}_{k}
as two matrices,
k
as the row index, and
n
as the column index. Then, once we have calculated the
n
-th column of
M
, we can calculate the
n
-th column of
L
. After obtaining these coefficients, we can use the Runge-Kutta method in the time direction to solve the discrete Schrödinger equation to obtain
\varphi_{n}(t)
(see Appendix
A
). Consequently, we can compute
K(t)
based on the definition of Krylov complexity.

2.2
Wightman function and spectral function

In quantum field theory, spectral functions can represent various correlation functions. We will demonstrate that the auto-correlation function
C(t)
or Wightman power spectrum
f^{W}(\omega)
can also be derived from the spectral function. Similar operations can also be referred to in
[
9
]
.

Consider two bosonic or fermionic operators,
\phi_{a}
and
\psi_{b}
, with subscripts used to denote their internal degrees of freedom. We declare that the bosonic operator
\psi_{b}
is defined as
\phi_{b}^{\dagger}
, while the fermionic operator
\psi_{b}
is expressed as
\bar{\phi}_{b}=\phi^{\dagger}_{b}\gamma^{0}
, where
\gamma^{0}
represents the Dirac matrix
[
66
,
67
]
. Define the Wightman function as

\displaystyle D^{>}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\equiv\expectationvalue{\phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{x})\psi_{b}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}_{\beta},

(40)

\displaystyle D^{<}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\equiv\eta\expectationvalue{\psi_{b}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})\phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{x})}_{\beta}.

(41)

For the bosonic field,
\eta=1
, while for the fermionic field,
\eta=-1
. Using
O(t)=e^{iHt}Oe^{-iHt}
, we have

\phi_{a}(t+i\beta,\mathbf{x})=e^{-\beta H}\phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{x})e^{\beta H}.

(42)

From this equation, one can demonstrate the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) relation in the configuration space as

D^{<}_{ab}(t+i\beta,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\eta D_{ab}^{>}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(43)

Assuming that the system has translational symmetries in both time and space directions, we can derive

\displaystyle D^{>}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=D^{>}_{ab}(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(44)

\displaystyle D^{<}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=D^{<}_{ab}(t-t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(45)

Without loss of generality, we can set
(t^{\prime}=0,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0})
and perform a Fourier transform on the Wightman function to obtain

\displaystyle D^{>}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}e^{i(\omega t-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})}D^{>}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};0,\mathbf{0}),

(46)

\displaystyle D^{<}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}e^{i(\omega t-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})}D^{<}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};0,{\mathbf{0}}).

(47)

Applying the KMS relation in configuration space enables the derivation of the KMS relation in momentum space:

D^{>}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\eta e^{\beta\omega}D^{<}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k}).

(48)

The one can introduce a new correlation function

\rho_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\expectationvalue{[\phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{x}),\psi_{b}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]_{\mp}}_{\beta},

(49)

in which
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mp}
takes the commutator for the bosonic field or the anticommutator for the fermionic field. Its Fourier transform is referred to as the spectral density or spectral function

\rho_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=D^{>}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})-D^{<}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k}).

(50)

From (
48
) and (
50
), one can get

\displaystyle D^{>}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\frac{\eta e^{\beta\omega}}{\eta e^{\beta\omega}-1}\rho_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k}),

(51)

\displaystyle D^{<}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\eta e^{\beta\omega}-1}\rho_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k}).

(52)

To calculate the Krylov complexity of bosonic or fermionic operators, we can define

\Pi^{W}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x})=\expectationvalue{\psi_{b}(0,\mathbf{0})\phi_{a}(t+i\beta/2,\mathbf{x})}_{\beta}=\eta D_{ab}^{<}(t+i\beta/2,\mathbf{x};0,\mathbf{0}).

(53)

Then we have

\Pi^{W}(t)=\Pi^{W}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{0}).

(54)

Performing a Fourier transform on (
53
) yields

\displaystyle\Pi^{W}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\eta\int dt\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}e^{i(\omega t-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})}D^{<}_{ab}(t+i\beta/2,\mathbf{x};0,\mathbf{0})

(55)

\displaystyle=\eta\int dt\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}e^{\beta\omega/2}e^{i(\omega t-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})}D^{<}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};0,\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\eta e^{\beta\omega/2}D^{<}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta e^{\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}}{\eta e^{\beta\omega}-1}\rho_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k}).

Then, we can derive the following expression:

\displaystyle f^{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}[\Pi^{W}(t)]^{\ast}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}e^{i\omega t}[\Pi^{W}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x})]^{\ast}\delta^{(d-1)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{0})

(56)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}\int\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}e^{i\omega t}[e^{-i(\omega^{\prime}t-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})}\Pi^{W}_{ab}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})]^{\ast}\delta^{(d-1)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=\int d\omega^{\prime}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}[\Pi^{W}_{ab}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})]^{\ast}\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})=\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}[\Pi^{W}_{ab}(-\omega,\mathbf{k})]^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta e^{-\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}}{\eta e^{-\beta\omega}-1}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}[\rho_{ab}(-\omega,\mathbf{k})]^{\ast}.

3
Wightman power spectrum of Schrödinger field theory

In the Schrödinger field theory, the Lagrangian for non-relativistic free bosons and fermions with mass
m
is given by

\mathscr{L}=\psi^{\dagger}\left(i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}\right)\psi.

(57)

Verifying that the Euler-Lagrange equations derived from this Lagrangian are indeed the Schrödinger equation is straightforward. Suppose
\psi
is an operator with canonical commutation relations. In that case, it describes a collection of identical non-relativistic bosons, and while
\psi
is an operator with canonical anti-commutation relations, the field describes identical fermions
[
59
]
. This Lagrangian is symmetric under the following global
U(1)
transformation

\psi\rightarrow e^{i\alpha}\psi,\qquad\psi^{\dagger}\rightarrow\psi^{\dagger}e^{-i\alpha}.

(58)

The corresponding conserved charge is

N=\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}\mathscr{N}=\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}\psi^{\dagger}\psi,

(59)

where
\mathscr{N}
is the charge density. The conjugate field of
\psi
is

\Pi=\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\dot{\psi}}=i\psi^{\dagger}.

(60)

Then we can write down the Hamiltonian density as

\mathscr{H}=\Pi\dot{\psi}-\mathscr{L}=\psi^{\dagger}\left(-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}\right)\psi.

(61)

In the grand canonical ensemble, the partition function is

\mathcal{Z}=\tr(e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}).

(62)

in which
H
is the Hamiltonian with
H=\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}\mathscr{H}
and
\mu
is the chemical potential
3
3

3

Please do not confuse with the chemical potential
\mu
with the moments
\mu_{i}
in the Eq.(
26
)
. Consequently, the partition function can be rewritten in the path integral form as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\Pi\mathcal{D}\psi\exp(\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}(\Pi i\partial_{\tau}\psi-\mathscr{H}+\mu\mathscr{N}))

(63)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\psi^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}\psi\exp{\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int d^{d-1}\mathbf{x}(\mathscr{L}+\mu\mathscr{N})},

where
\tau=it
. The inverse of the thermal propagator of the Schrödinger field is

\mathcal{D}^{-1}(K)\equiv\mathcal{D}^{-1}(\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=i\omega_{n}+\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m}-\mu,

(64)

where
K=(k^{0},\mathbf{k})=(-i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})
. From the thermal propagator, we can obtain the spectral function as
4
4

4

Since the situation we are considering does not contain internal degrees of freedom, we can safely omit the subscript “
ab
” in the spectral function
\rho_{ab}
in Eq.(

). The derivation of Eq.(

) can be found in the Appendix

.

\rho(\omega,\mathbf{k})=2\pi\delta(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\omega),\qquad\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m}-\mu.

(65)

It should be noted that we cannot directly substitute this spectral function into the equation (
56
) to calculate the Wightman power spectrum because the field
\psi
here, in the sense of (
29
), is not necessarily normalized. Therefore, we can redefine

\rho(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{N}\delta(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\omega)

(66)

as the spectral function, where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant that needs to be determined from the equation (
33
).

To calculate
f^{W}(\omega)
, we first compute the integral

I\equiv\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}[\rho(-\omega,\mathbf{k})]^{\ast}=\mathcal{N}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\delta(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\omega).

(67)

Using the identity

\int\frac{d^{d}\mathbf{l}}{(2\pi)^{d}}=\int\frac{d\Omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dll^{d-1},\qquad\Omega_{d}\equiv\int d\Omega_{d}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)},

(68)

we have

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\Omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\frac{k^{d-2}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\delta\left(\frac{k^{2}}{2m}-\mu+\omega\right)

(69)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\Omega_{d-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\frac{k^{d-2}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\sqrt{\frac{m}{2(\mu-\omega)}}\delta(k-\sqrt{2m(\mu-\omega)})\Theta(\mu-\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\Omega_{d-1}\frac{m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}[2(\mu-\omega)]^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\Theta(\mu-\omega).

in which
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function with
\Theta(x<0)=0
and
\Theta(x\geq 0)=1
. Then, absorbing all constants into
\mathcal{N}
, we get

f^{W}(\omega)=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\beta,d,\eta)(\mu-\omega)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\frac{\eta e^{-\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}}{\eta e^{-\beta\omega}-1}\Theta(\mu-\omega).

(70)

Here,
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\beta,d,\eta)
is the normalization factor determined by the normalization condition of the Wightman power spectrum (
33
). For simplicity, in this paper, we will only consider the case of five dimensions
5
5

5

Our selection of
5
dimensions
(d=5)
stems from the fundamental constraints in handling the Wightman power spectrum integral of the form (
70
). Specifically:

•

Even-dimensional spacetime limitations:

When spacetime dimension is even
(d=2k,k\in Z)
, the term
(\mu-\omega)^{(d-3)/2}
in the Wightman spectrum produces integrals of the type
(\mu-\omega)^{k-3/2}/\sinh\left(\beta\omega/2\right)
. But in this case, the moments will be hard to be evaluated. The same difficulty is also encountered in
[
9
]
.

•

Odd-dimensional spacetime advantage:

When the spacetime is of odd dimension, the Wightman power spectrum is integrable. But for general spacetime with
d=2k+1
, we still cannot obtain an analytical expression. Therefore, we choose
a simple
case,
d=5
.

.
Thus, for bosonic fields and fermionic fields, the Wightman power spectrum can be written in the specific form as

f^{W}(\omega)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{N}(\mu,\beta,5,1)\frac{(\omega-\mu)}{2}\frac{1}{\sinh\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}\Theta(\mu-\omega),\qquad\text{bosonic field}\\
&\mathcal{N}(\mu,\beta,5,-1)\frac{(\mu-\omega)}{2}\frac{1}{\cosh\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}\Theta(\mu-\omega).\qquad\text{fermionic field}\end{aligned}\right.

(71)

4
Lanczos coefficients and Krylov complexity of Schrödinger field theory

Since the Wightman power spectrum has distinct expressions for bosonic and fermionic fields, as shown in Eq.(
71
), we will examine the bosonic and fermionic cases separately. In this section, our calculations primarily follow the method in our previous paper
[
10
]
, which involves expanding the Wightman power spectrum into a series form and keeping only the terms that play a major role in the results. However, as
\mu>0

This section presents our main computational results. We separately analyze bosonic and fermionic Schrödinger fields in the grand canonical ensemble. For each case, we:

•

derive the Wightman power spectrum from the spectral function;

•

compute moments using the power spectrum;

•

determine Lanczos coefficients
\{a_{n}\},\{b_{n}\}

•

solve the discrete Schrödinger equation to obtain the Krylov complexity.

Key findings include the linear growth of the Lanczos coefficients and the universal exponential scaling of complexity at late times.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have applied the Krylov subspace method in the grand canonical ensemble to investigate the Krylov complexity of the non-relativistic Schrödinger field with various chemical potentials, especially as the chemical potential is zero or negative.
We found that regardless of whether the chemical potential is zero or negative, or whether it is for bosonic or fermionic case, the Lanczos coefficients
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
are approximately linear and very close to

\displaystyle\beta a_{n}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-4(n+1)-\mu,

\displaystyle\beta b_{n}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2n+1.

This is somewhat unexpected, as the Wightman power spectrum and the auto-correlation functions differ in various situations. However, they possess similar Lanczos coefficients and consequently exhibit similar behaviors of Krylov complexity. We suspect that there must be some key quantities that are similar between them, and we find that this quantity is the square of the absolute values of the auto-correlation function
\lvert\varphi_{0}(t)\rvert^{2}
. That is to say, similar behavior of the
\lvert\varphi_{0}(t)\rvert^{2}
for bosonic and fermionic cases will yield similar behaviors of the Krylov complexity. It is worth noting that our Lanczos coefficients always absent staggering, which is different from some previous results in field theory. Therefore, by examining examples in Appendix
C
, we found that the conditions for the staggering presented in
[
9
]
can be further generalized. Our conclusion is

•

if the Wightman power spectrum is symmetric and the zero point is located at the symmetry axis, the Lanczos coefficients
b_{n}
will exhibit staggering.

•

if the Wightman power spectrum is asymmetric, the Lanczos coefficients
b_{n}
will not be staggering.

However, in detail, there are still some discrepancies between the bosonic and fermionic cases. We found that the Krylov complexities for both bosonic and fermionic cases will overlap at early times. However, as time passes, the Krylov complexity for the bosonic case with a larger chemical potential grows slightly faster than that with a smaller chemical potential. On the contrary, for the fermionic case, the Krylov complexity with a smaller chemical potential grows slightly faster than that with a larger chemical potential as time increases.
By fitting the slopes of
K(t)
at a later time, we found that for the bosonic case, as the chemical potential decreases, the asymptotic slope increases. However, for the fermionic case, as the chemical potential decreases, the asymptotic slope decreases. It is difficult to explain the discrepancies of the Krylov complexity for the bosonic and fermionic cases precisely, but we can speculate that these discrepancies must be related to the different statistical properties of the bosonic and fermionic fields, or equivalently the algebraic structure for them. The Bose-Einstein distribution for the bosonic field is
n_{B}(\omega)=1/(e^{-\beta\omega}-1)
7
7

7

For free bosons, the Bose distribution usually given in textbooks is
1/(e^{(E_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu)\beta}-1)
. From the first line of Eq. (3.13) in the manuscript, we know
E_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu=-\omega
, so the Bose distribution can be written as
n_{B}(\omega)=1/(e^{-\beta\omega}-1)
. For free fermions, we get
n_{F}(\omega)=1/(e^{-\beta\omega}+1)
.
and the Fermi-Dirac distribution for the fermionic field is
n_{F}(\omega)=1/(e^{-\beta\omega}+1)
. Fortunately, we found that the Wightman power spectrum (
71
) can be rewritten as

f^{W}(\omega)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{N}(\mu,\beta,5,1)(\mu-\omega)e^{\frac{-\beta\omega}{2}}n_{B}(\omega)\Theta(\mu-\omega),\qquad\text{bosonic field}\\
&\mathcal{N}(\mu,\beta,5,-1)(\mu-\omega)e^{-\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}n_{F}(\omega)\Theta(\mu-\omega).\qquad\text{fermionic field}\end{aligned}\right.

(110)

From the above formula we can see that the different statistical distributions for the bosonic and fermionic fields have direct impact to the Wightman power spectrum
f^{W}(\omega)
, therefore, it then further has impacts to the Krylov complexity. However, it is hard to see the relation between the asymptotic scaling of Krylov complexity and the chemical potential, since as we compute the Krylov complexity we need to Fourier transform
f^{W}(\omega)
to get the autocorrelation function
\varphi_{0}(t)
. Then we use the Lanczos coefficients and
\varphi_{0}(t)
to get the values of
\varphi_{n}(t)
. Finally, the Krylov complexity is obtained by summing up the
n\absolutevalue{\varphi_n(t)}^{2}
. These tedious processes make us hard to tell the exact relations between the chemical potential and the Krylov complexity, but rather we can only see the relations from numerical computations. Moreover, both in the bosonic and fermionic cases, the asymptotic slope will approach
2.746
for small chemical potentials. This is because when the chemical potential is small enough, the power spectra of the Boson field and Fermi field gradually converge. Interestingly, this slope is smaller than twice the slope of
b_{n}
. This is because we are considering the Krylov complexity of non-Hermitian operators; in this case, the Lanczos coefficients
a_{n}
will contribute to the Krylov complexity. We expect that our work will shed light on the study of Krylov complexity of the non-relativistic field theory with non-Hermitian operators.

Appendix A
Solve the discrete Schrödinger equation

To solve the discrete Schrödinger equation, we need first to discretize time, letting
t_{i}\approx(i-1)\Delta t,
where
i=1,2,\ldots
,
\Delta t
is a small time interval. Then we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}(t_{i}+\Delta t)

(111)

\displaystyle\approx\varphi_{n}(t_{i})+\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t_{i})\Delta t

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}(t_{i})+[b_{n}\varphi_{n-1}(t_{i})-b_{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}(t_{i})+ia_{n}\varphi_{n}(t_{i})]\Delta t.

Write
\varphi_{n}(t_{i})
as
\varphi^{i}_{n}
and introduce the vector
\vec{\varphi}^{i}=(\varphi^{i}_{0}\ \varphi^{i}_{1}\cdots)^{\text{T}}
, then (
111
) can be written in a more compact form

\vec{\varphi}^{i+1}=\vec{\varphi}^{i}+A\vec{\varphi}^{i}\Delta t,

(112)

where
A
is a matrix

A=\left(\begin{matrix}ia_{0}&-b_{1}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
b_{1}&ia_{1}&-b_{2}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&b_{2}&ia_{2}&-b_{3}&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{matrix}\right).

(113)

To utilize the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, (
112
) can be rewritten as

\vec{\varphi}^{i+1}=\vec{\varphi}^{i}+\frac{\Delta t}{6}(K_{1}+2K_{2}+2K_{3}+K_{4}),

(114)

where

\displaystyle K_{1}=A\vec{\varphi}^{i},\qquad K_{2}=A(\vec{\varphi}^{i}+K_{1}\Delta t/2),

(115)

\displaystyle K_{3}=A(\vec{\varphi}^{i}+K_{2}\Delta t/2),\qquad K_{4}=A(\vec{\varphi}^{i}+K_{3}\Delta t).

(116)

Once the Lanczos coefficients
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
are obtained, it is equivalent to knowing the matrix
A
. Knowing
\varphi_{n}(0)
also means knowing
\vec{\varphi}^{1}
, and from (
114
), one can calculate
\vec{\varphi}^{i}
.

Appendix B
Derive equation (
65
)

Here, only the bosonic case is considered, and the results for the fermionic case are the same as those for the bosonic case. Define the retarded correlator

D^{R}_{ab}(t,\mathbf{x};t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\equiv i\expectationvalue{\Theta(t-t^{\prime})[\phi_{a}(t,\mathbf{x}),\psi_{b}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]}.

(117)

Note that

\Theta(t)=i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega+i\epsilon},\qquad\epsilon=0^{+}.

(118)

Therefore, in momentum space, we have

D^{R}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{\rho_{ab}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega-i\epsilon}.

(119)

Make use of the identity

\frac{1}{x\pm i\epsilon}=\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{x}\mp i\pi\delta(x),

(120)

we obtain

\rho_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=2\imaginary D^{R}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k}),

(121)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the principal value. On the other hand, the relationship between the imaginary-time correlation function, which is also known as the thermal propagator, and the spectral density is

\mathcal{D}_{ab}(\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{\rho_{ab}(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k})}{\omega^{\prime}+i\omega_{n}}.

(122)

That is to say, by simply replacing
\omega_{n}
with
i\omega-\epsilon
, one can obtain the retarded correlation function from the thermal propagator

D^{R}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\mathcal{D}_{ab}(\omega_{n}\rightarrow i\omega-\epsilon,\mathbf{k}).

(123)

The thermal propagator of Schrödinger field theory is given by (
64
), then we have

\displaystyle\rho_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\imaginary D^{R}_{ab}(\omega,\mathbf{k})

(124)

\displaystyle=2\imaginary\mathcal{D}(\omega_{n}\rightarrow i\omega-\epsilon,\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\Im{\frac{1}{i(i\omega-\epsilon)+\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m}-\mu}}

\displaystyle=2\Im{\frac{1}{\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m}-\mu-\omega-i\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=2\Im{\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m}-\mu-\omega\right)+i\pi\delta\left(\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2m}-\mu-\omega\right)}

\displaystyle=2\pi\delta(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\omega).

This is the equation (
65
) in the main text.
