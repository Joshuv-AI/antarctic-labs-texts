An extended substrate spectrum of the proton organic cation antiporter and relation to other cation transporters
Authors: Cauzar Ali Khan, Nicolai Kirsch, Jürgen Brockmöller, Kyra‐Elisa Maria Redeker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost central nervous system (CNS) active drugs are organic cations, which need carrier proteins for efficient transfer through the blood–brain barrier (BBB). A genetically still unidentified proton organic cation (H+/OC) antiporter is found in several tissues, including endothelial cells of the BBB. We characterized the substrate spectrum of the H+/OC antiporter and the overlap in substrate spectrum with OCTN1, OCTN2 or OCT3 by screening 87 potential substrates for transport activity. Based on high antiport rates, 45 of the tested substances were substrates of the H+/OC antiporter. They included antidepressants (like tranylcypromine or nortriptyline), antipsychotics (like levomepromazine) and local anaesthetics. Concentration‐dependent transport was confirmed for 38 of the substrates. Transport uptake depending on a pH gradient across the cell membrane confirmed that 43 drugs were indeed substrates of the H+/OC antiporter. However, the patterns of pH dependence differed between the substrates, possibly indicating different modes of transport or the existence of multiple antiporter proteins. The substrate overlap between the H+/OC antiporter and OCTN1, OCTN2 or OCT3 was minimal, indicating that the latter three are not the proteins underlying the H+/OC antiporter activity. Overall, about 50% of positively charged drugs may be substrates of the antiporter, which may be the most important membrane transport protein for many drugs.

INTRODUCTION

Most neuropsychiatric drugs are organic cations that are positively charged in most human body tissues. For these drugs and other organic cations with beneficial or adverse effects on the brain, we need to understand how they enter the brain. Although most organic cations, except for quaternary amines, may pass cell membranes by so‐called non‐ionic diffusion, this process is rather slow, particularly with substances having a pKa above 8.5. Thus, for those substances, membrane transporters are required for effective transport. Earlier studies have characterized the membrane transport of about 100 organic cations via a genetically not yet identified proton organic cation (H+/OC) antiporter. Its transport activity has been found in several tissues but particularly at the blood–brain barrier (BBB). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

Based on functional criteria, the H+/OC antiporter has been proposed to be responsible for the uptake of organic cations by exchanging protons or cations with other cations without the requirement of sodium or potassium. 2 , 5 , 6 Transport is saturable and most efficient at 37°C, but that does not distinguish the H+/OC antiporter from other transporters in the human body. 7 A relatively specific criterion for the involvement of the H+/OC antiporter is its high antiport activity, also known as trans‐stimulation. Trans‐stimulation here describes the enhanced transport of one organic cation from intracellular to extracellular when exchanged with another organic cation from extracellular to intracellular. 4 , 7 , 8

Additionally, the transport via the H+/OC antiporter is highly dependent on pH: Under intracellular acidic or extracellular alkaline conditions, the transport of substrates is accelerated, whereas no or low transport is observed at low intracellular proton concentrations. 7 , 9 The activity of the H+/OC antiporter was found in various cell lines, of which the hCMEC/D3 cell line is the most frequently applied model cell line reflecting the endothelial cells at the BBB. 8 , 10

Substrates of the H+/OC antiporter known so far include opioids (oxycodone and tramadol), psychostimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamine derivates and cocaine), central nervous system (CNS) active antihistaminergic drugs (diphenhydramine [DPH]), CNS active alpha2 agonists (clonidine), and nicotine and varenicline. 6 , 8 , 9 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 The selection of substrates for the present study was based on the aim of getting a more comprehensive overview of the BBB transport of medically relevant drugs (Table S1). Most substances included in our screening were selected because they act in the brain and, therefore, have to pass the BBB. These drugs included antidepressants, alpha2 agonists, antipsychotics, first generation antihistaminics, monamine oxidase inhibitors and opioids. However, we also included drugs exhibiting adverse effects in the brain like beta blockers and local anaesthetics, because the extent of their adverse effects might correlate with their passage through the BBB via the H+/OC antiporter. Some anti‐infective drugs were included mostly because their BBB penetration is relevant in treatment of CNS infections. In addition, a few other substances were tested to explore the substrate spectrum of the H+/OC antiporter, which is not exclusively localized at the BBB but also in several other tissues. Since SLC transporters in some instances do also have substrates with chemical properties unexpected from their typical substrate spectrum, 17 we also included a few uncharged and negatively charged substrates to explore if they were substrates of the H+/OC antiporter.

The gene or genes coding for the H+/OC antiporter have yet to be identified. Numerous organic cation transporters (such as OCT1) have already been tested to determine if they fulfil the functional criteria of the H+/OC antiporter; however, none of the already comprehensively characterized transporters has the characteristic substrate spectrum and transport features of the H+/OC antiporter. 18 A transporter composed of two proteins, TM7SF3 and LHFPL6, had several characteristics and substrates in common with the H+/OC antiporter. 19 However, transport rates were only moderate, and more studies are needed on that transporter. Most likely, candidates in this respect are the organic cation transporter 3 (OCT3), and the organic cation/carnitine transporter 1 and 2 (OCTN1 and ‐2). All three are also expressed at the BBB.31,32 Therefore, we tested good substrates of the H+/OC antiporter as possible substrates of these transporters to explore a potential overlapping substrate spectrum with the H+/OC antiporter. The multidrug and toxin extrusion carriers MATE1 and MATE2K are also H+/OC antiporters, but earlier studies particularly with cell models of the blood brain barrier have shown that these two carriers are not the proteins underlying the respective proton organic cation antiport activities at the BBB. 7 , 8

In summary, we wanted to extend the known substrate spectrum of the H+/OC antiporter systemically based on different drug groups and compare substrates to several known organic cation transporters. Substrates tested here are listed in Table S1 according to their respective therapeutic class and their chemical properties. The identification of more and very good substrates of the H+/OC antiporter might help in future studies to identify the carrier proteins underlying this transport activity. In addition, it might help to understand by which routes drugs or other substances are transported via the BBB. That knowledge may also contribute to a better understanding for drug–drug interactions at the BBB.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Substances

The substances studied here (Table S1) were selected mostly because for their therapeutic activity for which they have to pass the blood brain barrier or because they are known to exhibit adverse effects in the brain. These drugs and internal standards needed for mass spectrometric analysis were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (Taufkirchen, Germany), Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Canada), Santa‐Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, USA), Biomol GmbH (Hamburg, Germany), Tocris Bioscience (Bristol, UK) and Biotrend Chemikalien GmbH (Köln, Germany) with purities of at least 95%.

Cell lines

The in vitro uptake experiments were performed with the clone D3 of human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) kindly provided by Weksler and colleagues. 22 , 23 This cell line is a lentivirus‐immortalized human endothelial brain cell line. 24

The HEK293 T‐REx cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Darmstadt, Germany) overexpressing hOCT3, or the organic cation/carnitine transporter hOCTN1 and hOCTN2, and the empty‐vector pcDNA5/FRT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) transfected control cell line were generated as described before via stable transfection using the recombinant site‐specific FlpIn system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 23 , 25 , 26 The FlpIn system allows the integration of the gene of interest to a pre‐defined locus in the host cell genome using the Flp recombinase. The correct integration of the vector was validated by PCR reactions confirming single genomic integration and the correct genomic position and by reverse transcription (RT)‐PCR conforming the gene expression. The functional transport properties were validated with typical substrates of the respective transporter as published earlier. 25 , 27 , 28

Cell culture

The hCMEC/D3 cells were cultured under sterile conditions in RPMI 1640 (Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium, Sigma‐Aldrich) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and the antibiotics penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 μg/ml; all three items purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific) as well as 1 ng/ml basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). 16 Cell culture flasks and 12‐well plates for uptake experiments were pre‐coated with rat collagen I (R&D System, Minneapolis, USA).

The HEK293 cells overexpressing specific transporters were grown under sterile conditions in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Sigma‐Aldrich) with D‐glucose (4.5 g/l), L‐glutamine (584 mg/l) and pyruvate (110 mg/l), 10% (v/v) FBS, and the antibiotics penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 μg/ml).

All cell lines, HEK293 and hCMEC/D3, were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 and cultured up to maximally 30 passages.

In vitro uptake experiments

The hCMEC/D3 cells were plated on 12‐well plates with 600 000 cells per well after being pre‐coated with rat collagen. The HEK293 cell lines overexpressing specific transporters were plated on 24‐well plates with 300 000 cells per well after being pre‐coated with poly‐D‐lysine (Sigma‐Aldrich). The uptake experiments were performed after 48 h of incubation, when the cells reached confluency.

For standard uptake experiments, transporter‐overexpressing HEK293 cells were washed with 37°C HBSS+ (the + standing for 10 mM HEPES (4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid) added to the Hank's balanced salt solution and adjustment to pH 7.4; Thermo Fisher Scientific) while being placed on a heating plate (37°C). Then, each well was incubated with 2.5 μM of a substance of interest diluted in 37°C HBSS+ for exactly 2 min. The uptake was stopped by the addition of 4°C HBSS+ and removal from the heating plate, followed by two washing steps with cold HBSS+. Finally, the cells were lysed with 250 μl (24‐well plate) of 80% (v/v) acetonitrile/water (LGC Standards, Wesel, Germany) for 10–15 min. In regard to the analysis via mass spectrometry, the lysis buffer contained an internal standard suitable for the respective substance (Table S2). The transporter‐mediated uptake into the HEK293 cells overexpressing OCT3, OCTN1 or OCTN2 was expressed as the substrate uptake ratio by transporter‐overexpressing cells over empty‐vector transfected control cells. To adjust variations between cell numbers in different cell lines, total protein quantification was determined with several wells for each cell line lysed in RIPA buffer and using a bicinchoninic acid assay with bovine serum albumin as calibration. 29

For trans‐stimulation experiments, hCMEC/D3 cells were washed once with 37°C HBSS+ and then pre‐incubated with 1 μM of DPH or 5,6‐methylenedioxy‐2‐aminoindane (MDAI) diluted in HBSS+ for 30 min at 37°C. During this incubation period, DPH or MDAI is taken up by the cells. Then, the buffer containing DPH or MDAI was removed, and a substance of interest (concentration 250 μM) was added for 2 min. During this time, the intracellular DPH or MDAI is exchanged with the extracellular substrate, meaning that DPH or MDAI is transported out of the cell while the test substance, if it is indeed a substrate, is transported into the cell. The exchange was stopped by addition of 4°C HBSS+ and the removal from the heating plate, followed by two washing steps with cold HBSS+. Finally, the cells were lysed by incubation with 500 μl (12‐well plate) of 80% (v/v) acetonitrile/water (LGC Standards, Wesel, Germany) for 10–15 min containing a suitable internal standard for DPH or MDAI (Table S2).

The exchange of DPH/MDAI with another substance was normalized to a control well that was incubated with pure HBSS+ for 2 min not including any substance. The readout of these antiport assays is the ratio of DPH or MDAI intracellular amount with the 2 min substrate incubation over the DPH or MDAI intracellular amount without addition of a substrate (pure HBSS+). A ratio of 1.0 means that there is no net antiport, and a ratio of, for instance, 0.1 means that 90% of the preloaded DPH or MDAI was transported out of the cell within the 2 min. A ratio of 0.7 exchange (meaning 30% of DPH or MDAI was washed out within 2 min after the addition of the other substance) was defined as relevant antiport to classify a good substrate. However, any statistically significant ratio lower than 1.0 indicates antiport. Therefore, substrates with ratios between 0.7 and 0.9 were defined as poor substrates, and ratios above 0.9 were classified as no substrates. Ratios statistically significant above 1.0 in this antiport assay indicate no net antiport.

The acidic or alkaline intracellular conditions for pH‐dependent cellular uptake was achieved with either pre‐incubation or simultaneous incubation of HBSS supplemented with 30 mM ammonium chloride (NH4Cl). NH4Cl was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich, and the pH 7.4 of the HBSS+ buffer was verified again. First, the cells were washed once with 37°C HBSS+. For simultaneous incubation (intracellular alkalization), substrates (2.5 μM final concentration) were diluted in HBSS+ containing 30 mM NH4Cl and incubated with hCMEC/D3 cells for exactly 2 min. In pre‐incubation experiments (intracellular acidification), hCMEC/D3 cells were incubated for 30 min with HBSS+ containing 30 mM NH4Cl. Then, the NH4Cl containing HBSS+ was removed, and the transport experiments were performed for exactly 2 min with 2.5 μM substrate diluted in pre‐warmed HBSS+ without NH4Cl. The uptake was stopped with 4°C HBSS+ and the removal of the heating plate, followed by two washing steps with cold HBSS+. Afterwards, the cells were lysed with 500 μl (12‐well plate) of 80% (v/v) acetonitrile/water (LGC Standards, Wesel, Germany) for 10–15 min containing a suitable internal standard (Table S2).

High‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS/MS) concentration analyses

In preparation for analysis, the cell lysates containing 80% (v/v) acetonitrile/water were evaporated with nitrogen gas and then dissolved in 0.1% formic acid.

The analysis of the intracellular substance concentrations was performed with HPLC‐MS/MS. The HPLC separation was performed with a Shimadzu Nexera 2 UHPLC system connected with an LC‐30AD pump, a SIL‐30AC autosampler, a CBM‐20A communication module and a CT0‐20AC column oven, and an online degassing module (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The stationary phase for separation was a Brownlee SPP RP‐Amide column with an inner dimension of 4.6 × 100 mm and a particle size of 2.7 μm preceded by a Phenomenex C18 pre‐column. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and a substrate dependent proportion of the organic additive mixture acetonitrile/methanol 6/1 (v/v), which is displayed in Table S2 (HPLC‐MS chemicals from LGC Standards). The chromatography was conducted with a flow between 300 and 500 μl/min at a temperature of 40°C.

For detection, an API 4000 tandem mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Darmstadt, Germany) was used with the detection parameters stated in Table S2. Quantification of detection results was accomplished with the Analyst software (Version 1.6.2, AB SCIEX, Darmstadt, Germany).

The uptake of several chiral substrates by the H+/OC antiporter was tested with the hCMEC/D3 cell line. The enantiomers of bisoprolol, desvenlafaxine, metoprolol and pindolol were separated on a Chiralpak CBH column (100 × 3 mm inner dimension, 5 μm particle size; Sigma‐Aldrich) with a mobile phase consisting of 10 mM ammonium acetate at pH 5.8 and 10% (v/v) isopropanol. For the remaining substances, an Astec Chirobiotic V2 (250 × 2.1 mm inner dimension, 5 μm particle size; Sigma‐Aldrich) was used with different mobile phases: Fluoxetin and N‐desmethyltramadol were separated with a mobile phase consisting of 20 mM ammonium acetate (pH 5.8) and 85% (v/v) methanol, clenbuterol and methylphenidate and tranylcypromine with 20 mM of ammonium acetate (pH 5.8) and 92% (v/v) methanol, and biperiden, bupivacaine and trimipramine with 20 mM of ammonium acetate (pH 4.5) and 98% (v/v) methanol. All racemic substances were analysed at a flow rate of 300 or 400 μl/min at 25°C and detected via mass spectrometry (Table S2).

Calculations, statistics and software

For analysis of the HPLC‐MS/MS data, the ratios of analyte peak areas over internal standard peak areas were calculated and normalized to the protein concentrations measured for the respective assay. The transporter‐mediated uptake in transporter‐overexpressing HEK293 cells was expressed as the ratio of uptake into transporter‐overexpressing cells over uptake into empty‐vector transfected control cells. The trans‐stimulation experiments were normalized to control wells that were treated for the same time of 2 minutes with pure buffer instead of the substance of interest. A trans‐stimulation ratio of <0.7 (>30% efflux of preloaded organic cation) was defined as a cut‐off for antiport: good H+/OC antiporter substrates with trans‐stimulation of DPH and MDAI <0.7, poor H+/OC antiporter substrates between 0.7 and 0.9, and no substrates >0.9. Uptake ratios are displayed in bar graphs as mean and standard error of the mean of independent experiments performed on different days as stated in the figure legends or tables.

For the concentration‐dependent uptake experiments, the concentrations of the analytes were determined from the respective calibration curves. The calibration curve was based on four known concentrations of each substrate dissolved in lysis buffer and processed like cell samples.

In any studies on transporter‐mediated uptake of substances, we have to differentiate between (passive) diffusion‐mediated uptake and transporter‐mediated uptake. In all transport experiments with the hCMEC/D3 cell line, we cannot experimentally distinguish between the passive diffusion and the H+/OC antiporter‐mediated transport. To approximately distinguish that, the diffusion‐mediated fraction in cell uptake was derived from the line defined by the slope of the uptake at the three highest concentration data points and subtracted from the total uptake to get a “net” uptake mediated by H+/OC antiporter in the cell line as illustrated earlier in more detail in fig. 1 of the manuscript by Sachkova et al. 16 In the HEK293 cell lines overexpressing OCTN1, OCTN2 or OCT3, the total cell uptake in the transporter‐overexpressing cells is defined by the sum of the uptake mediated by the overexpressed transporter plus the uptake by passive diffusion plus the uptake via endogenous transporters expressed in the HEK293 cells. The sum of the latter two is measured as the uptake in empty‐vector transfected cells. That uptake into empty‐vector transfected cells was subtracted from the uptake into the overexpressing cells before estimating the transport kinetic parameters.

The transport kinetic parameters Vmax and KM were determined with non‐linear regression by using the Michaelis–Menten equation (v = vmax × [S] / (Km + [S])) after subtraction of the linear term from the total uptake using GraphPad Prism 5.01 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA, USA). The ratio of Vmax over KM results in the intrinsic clearance (Clint).

Basic physical and chemical properties such as logD at pH 7.4, the most basic pKa values and topological polar surface area (TPSA) were calculated with the Instant JChem package and MarvinSketch of Chemaxon (version 21.2.0, Budapest, Hungary).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies. 30

RESULTS

Firstly, we screened 87 potential H+/OC antiporter substrates (Table S1) concerning their transport into hCMEC/D3 cells. These cells represent the human BBB endothelium. The screening was performed using the trans‐stimulation assay. Then, those substances that exhibited significant antiport were further characterized for pH dependence of their influx transport, and transport kinetic constants were estimated. Then, we tested whether a representative sample of these organic cations might be substrates of the well‐characterized transporters OCTN1, OCTN2 and OCT3. Finally, we compiled all currently known H+/OC antiporter substrates and characterized the molecular features of typical antiporter substrates.

As illustrated in Figure 1, the two antiport assays mostly showed comparable results. For 35 substrates, there was consistent >30% efflux of both preloaded cations. Based on the somehow arbitrarily chosen cut‐off of 0.7, MDAI exchange transport identified 12 more substrates than DPH exchange transport (Table 1). Interestingly, 20 substances even increased the intracellular concentration of MDAI above the control, and 15 substances increased the intracellular concentration of DPH, indicating that in these instances, the presumed substrate might in fact act as an inhibitor of the efflux transport but is not a substrate of the H+/OC antiporter.

H+/OC antiport was particularly frequent in some of the therapeutic drug groups. For instance, local anaesthetics (such as articaine and tetracaine) showed an efficient exchange of the preloaded substrate between 30% and 70%. Several antipsychotics (such as haloperidol and pipamperone) and a few first generation antihistaminic drugs (such as clemastine) reached antiport rates between 50% and 70%. The two tested 5HT3‐antagonistic antiemetics (granisetron and palonosetron), as well as all tested anticholinergics (scopolamine, biperiden, solifenacin, fesoterodine and propiverine), demonstrated a highly increased antiport. Even higher trans‐stimulation ratios up to 90% efflux of the preloaded organic cation were observed for many antidepressants (such as trimipramine and nortriptyline). Also, the norepinephrine serotonin reuptake inhibitor atomoxetine used in the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder showed with both antiport‐substrates a very strong exchange ratio.

PH‐dependent uptake

Another criterion for the activity of the H+/OC antiporter is uptake dependent on the intracellular pH and the pH gradient at the outer cell membrane. Because of the proton organic cation antiport, an enhanced cellular uptake is expected under intracellular acidic conditions and the opposite under alkaline intracellular conditions. Therefore, the uptake of 43 substances that could be considered as substrates of the H+/OC antiporter based on the two trans‐stimulation experiments (Table 1) was tested on the pH dependence of their transport (Figure 2). By pre‐incubating the cells with a buffer containing 30 mM NH4Cl for 30 min, the intracellular milieu turns acidic during the prolonged incubation. Simultaneous incubation with NH4Cl elevates the intracellular pH to the alkaline range due to diffusion of ammonia (NH3), which then intracellularly becomes ionized again to NH4 + and binds intracellular protons, thus reducing the driving force for the transport via the H+/OC antiporter.

pH‐dependent uptake of presumed H+ for 30 min and substrate incubation in HBSS+ buffer for 2 min (left) and 2 min co‐incubation of substance in HBSS buffer with additional 30 mM NH4Cl (right). H+OC antiporter substrates are expected to show reduced uptake after co‐incubation and enhanced uptake after pre‐incubation with NH4Cl. Uptake was normalized to the intracellular concentration without any NH4Cl pre‐ or co‐incubation. The data points are displayed as means ± SEM. The experiments were conducted several times independently from each other for every substance on different days (exact number is given in Table 1) and statistically analysed using the Student's t‐test comparing the uptake with NH4Cl mediated modification of intracellular pH with the uptake without pH modification (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

In summary, almost all substrates pre‐selected based on their antiport activity showed a reduction in cell uptake under intracellular alkaline conditions (co‐incubation) and enhanced cell uptake under acidic conditions (pre‐incubation) (Table 1, Figure 2). In total, 23 of the tested 43 presumed H+/OC antiporter substrates showed statistically significant stimulation of transport by intracellular acidification, and 35 showed a statistically significant reduction of uptake by intracellular alkalization. In particular, ropinirole, galantamine and naltrexone showed the strongest reduction after alkalization and at the same time very increased stimulation after acidification, which was also observed for a number of further substances, such as doxylamine, scopolamine and lidocaine.

Interestingly, with 11 substances, the intracellular acidification (by pre‐incubation with NH4Cl) did not show a significant effect, while a strong and statistically significant effect of intracellular alkalization could be observed. This was the case with fesoterodine or cabergoline, which also showed a strong trans‐stimulation effect (Figure 2, Table 1). This data might be interesting to differentiate between different transporters currently in summary termed H+/OC antiporter. However, further experiments are needed to verify this hypothesis.

As control experiments, we also included in the assay on pH dependence some substances known to pass the BBB but could not be defined as antiporter substrates based on trans‐stimulation experiments, such as bromocriptine or nimodipine. These substances also did not show significant effects depending on the intracellular pH.

A surprising observation was made for maraviroc that was only a possible substrate of the H+/OC antiporter based on trans‐stimulation with MDAI, but not with DPH (Figure 1). However, it showed a significantly decreased uptake under alkaline conditions.

Could the transport activity measured in the hCMEC cells be due to OCTN1, OCTN2 or OCT3?

So far, the “genetic identity” of the H+/OC antiporter has not been revealed yet. However, the coding gene(s) might be found in the family of solute carriers, possibly even in the well‐characterized transporter subfamily SLC22. Therefore, we tested if the antiporter substrates were also substrates of OCT3 as well as OCTN1 and OCTN2 (Figure 4). All three transporters were earlier shown to be expressed at the BBB and in the hCMEC cell line. 20 , 21 , 33

A few antiporter substrates, such as atomoxetine or mepivacaine, had a slightly increased uptake by the tested transporters. However, in contrast to well‐known substrates of OCTNs, such as ergothionine and carnitine, which showed uptake ratios up to 30, uptake ratios of antiporter substrates did not exceed 2.5. This data (Figure 4) leads to the conclusion that the transport activity found in the hCMEC cells is not due to the known expression of OCT3, OCTN1 or OCTN2 in these cells, at least not for the vast majority of substrates tested here.

How enantio‐specific is the H+/OC antiporter as reflected by cell uptake into the hCMEC/D3 cell line?

In many cases, enantiomers have different medical effects, as it is known for citalopram, where the S‐enantiomer escitalopram is responsible for the inhibition of serotonin reuptake, whereas the R‐enantiomer seems to inhibit the serotoninergic and behavioural effects of escitalopram. 34 Also, pharmacokinetics may significantly differ between the enantiomers, as shown by the more than tenfold differences between the two enantiomers of tranylcypromine. 35

With none of the 12 racemates tested, we observed major enantio‐specific uptake by the hCMEC/D3 cells. However, there was some enantioselectivity in the uptake of the enantiomers of fluoxetine and trimipramine (Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

We tested potential substrates of the H+/OC antiporter on the hCMEC/D3 cell line to determine antiport activity as well as pH‐dependent and concentration‐dependent uptake. Out of 87 mostly cationic drugs selected for their therapeutic or adverse effects on the human brain, we identified 45 good substrates, 13 possible but poor substrates and 29 non‐substrates (Figure 1). Thus, the H+/OC antiporter may be relevant for the BBB passage of about 50% of cationic CNS active drugs.

The drugs studied here were pre‐selected primarily because of their therapeutic or adverse effects on the brain. For instance, local anaesthetics should not act in the brain but may exhibit adverse effects in the brain at higher doses. All local anaesthetics studied here were substrates of the H+/OC antiporter. It might be interesting to ask whether the H+/OC antiporter may also be present in the myelin sheets of peripheral nerves, which have to be passed in therapeutic use of the local anaesthetics. Also, most anticholinergic drugs studied here were substrates of the H+/OC antiporter, and the antiporter may contribute to their effects and adverse effects in the brain. However, this does not apply to the quarternary amines since, thus far, not any quarternary ammonium compound was identified as an H+/OC antiporter substrate. Because no transport was expected, we did not systematically include quarternary amines, but for instance, for methylscopolamine, it was verified that it is indeed not a substrate. 18

Here, we compiled all current data on H+/OC antiporter substrates (Table S3) and summarized substrates and non‐substrates in Figure 6. As illustrated, most H+/OC antiporter substrates can be found in the group of antidepressants, psychostimulants, antipsychotic drugs and opioids, which are all drugs that need to pass the BBB to act on their targets within the brain. On the other hand, most of the tested antibiotics, virostatics and cystostatics were no substrates.

Categorization of substances as (good) H+/OC antiporter substrates and as poor or no substrates within their drug groups based on the mean value of trans‐stimulation with 5,6‐methylenedioxy‐2‐aminoindane (MDAI) and diphenhydramine (DPH) as seen in Figure 1, Tables 1 and S3 (abbreviation: MAO, monoaminoxidase).

Trans‐stimulation (i.e., antiport) measurement is an efficient means to identify H+/OC antiporter substrates because one only has to establish the quantification methods for one substance released from the cell as a consequence of the antiport. Here, we used two such exchange substrates, and 33 substrates were consistently identified as substrates when using a cut‐off of 0.7 or less. When using the ratio of 0.9 as a cut‐off, 45 substrates were consistently identified as substrates (Figure 1, Table 1).

In the absence of an intracellular organic cation serving as an antiport substrate, lack of pH dependence would exclude the involvement of the H+/OC antiporter. Based on the nominal effect of the alteration of the intracellular pH (acidification or alkalization), none of the substrates were rejected as possible substrates of the H+/OC antiporter. However, based on statistical significance, the transport was consistently decreased upon alkalization and increased upon acidification only in 24 of the 43 substrates tested (Figures 2, 7).

Comparison of all performed assays. A substrate is indicated with “+” and a dark blue colour, that is, trans‐stimulation (TS) <0.7, statistically significant effect after alkalization and acidification, and concentration‐dependent uptake. A negative result (TS < 0.7, no significant pH‐dependent effect) is indicated with “‐.”

While the trans‐stimulation alone illustrates that not with all substances an unequivocal identification can be achieved, by taking the pH‐dependent assay in account, overall, 18 substrates can be identified that fill the criteria of trans‐stimulation with DPH and MDAI as well as showing a significant effect after acidification and alkalization (Figure 7). Based on the trans‐stimulation assay, all 38 substrates that were tested for concentration‐dependent (saturated) transport showed saturable transport kinetics but with a wide range of affinities and maximum transport rates (Table 2).

The chemical properties such as the molecular weight, logD at pH 7.4, the most basic pKa value and the TPSA are well‐established criteria for substances to compare substrates and no substrates. As illustrated in Figure 8, many substrates of the antiporter were relatively lipophilic with a higher logD at pH 7.4 compared to the non‐substrates. Also, the TPSA strongly differed between substrates and non‐substrates (Figure 8). In comparison, poor substrates show similar chemical properties as non‐substrates in all four criteria. Therefore, it may be suggested that a trans‐stimulation ratio between 1.0 and 0.7 (Figure 1) is not suited to confirm antiport. We only separately showed this group with poor antiport results because any such cut‐off remains an arbitrary definition. The overlap in the TPSA and logD values between substrates and non‐substrates of the H+/OC antiporter is relatively small (Figure 8). Nevertheless, it is currently not possible to identify a substrate based on a single physicochemical parameter or a parameter combination. A limitation of all these results on chemical predictors is that we did not test substrates covering the full range of chemical properties. For instance, we did not test organic anions although the example of imperatorin illustrates that also uncharged molecules may be substrates of the H+/OC antiporter. 37 We also did not analyse a wider range of quaternary amines. However, as summarized in Table S3, none of the six quaternary amines analyzed here and in earlier publications were substrates of the H+/OC antiporter.

Further, we compared the substrate concentrations at half‐maximal transport activity (KM) with typical lowest and highest therapeutic blood concentrations. As illustrated in Table 3, the KM was typically 10 to 100‐fold higher than the highest blood concentrations. This comparison might suggest that the H+/OC antiporter is not relevant for transporting these substances. However, the KM determined in vitro also for other transporters like OCT1 is often significantly above the typical plasma concentrations, but nevertheless, the plasma concentrations are significantly dependent on the genetically determined expression of the transporter OCT1. That was shown, for instance, for the drugs sumatriptan and fenoterol. 61 , 62 Several points may explain that. First, of course, there may also be a relevant transport below KM. Second, local drug concentrations might be higher. There might even be access to the transporter by the accumulation of the substrates in the lipid bilayer membrane as suggested by the hydrophobic vacuum cleaner model. 63 There were, however, also highly potent drugs like the relatively hydrophilic cabergoline (Table S1), whose blood concentrations were about four orders of magnitude lower than the KM (Table 3). For those substances, penetration through the BBB may be poor. Alternatively, one may speculate that a very high‐affinity, but low‐capacity transport exists that was not detected due to the limited sensitivity of our analytical methods.

A special issue in understanding the relationship between (the here still unknown) transporter structure and the transport activities is the challenge for enantioselectivity. Since all the typical physicochemical parameters of enantiomers are identical (or almost identical), any differences in the transport of enantiomers are due to specific steric interactions between the substrate and the transporter. In this respect, the low extent of enantioselectivity illustrated in Figure 5 may indicate only a few highly specific interactions. However, the hCMEC/D3 model cell line used here may not only indicate the activity of one SLC transporter, and even counteracting activities of efflux transporters expressed in the cells may blur possible enantioselectivity of the H+/OC antiporter in some instances.

Several groups have tried to identify the “genetic identity” of the H+/OC antiporter, mainly using knock‐down approaches in cell models exhibiting H+/OC antiporter activity. For instance, small interfering‐RNA (si‐RNA)‐medicated knock‐down of PMAT, OCT2 or OCTN1/2 did not change the transport of one H+/OC antiporter substrate (imperatorin). 37 However, imperatorin is an atypical substrate of the H+/OC antiporter because it lacks a positively charged nitrogen. An alternative approach is utilizing prototypical substrates of cation transporters like MPP+, carnitine, ergothioneine or choline. 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 Several groups have utilized inhibitors in cells with H+/OC antiporter activity, and potent inhibitors include verapamil, nortriptyline and quinidine (e.g., Higuchi et al. 68 and Chapy et al. 69 ). However, these inhibitors are not specific and do inhibit a broad range of transporters and biomolecules beyond transporters like drug‐metabolizing enzymes. The probably most important recent advancement was the identification of a dimer of the proteins TM7SF3 and LHFPL6 possibly contributing to proton‐coupled organic cation antiport at the BBB. 19 However, transport activities were only moderate, and no further studies on that transporter have been published. In an earlier screening for substrates of the H+/OC antiporter, 18 we found that a few were also moderate substrates of OCTN1 or OCTN2. However, as illustrated in Figure 4, only very few substrates of the H+/OC antiporter were also (moderate) substrates of the OCTNs, and the figure may even indicate a mutual exclusion of OCT and H+/OC antiporter substrates as illustrated, for instance, in the example of carnitine, methylnaltrexone, fluoxetine or tyramine (Figure 4).

To our best knowledge, the organic cation transporter OCT3 was never characterized as an antiporter. However, OCT3 was of interest as an alternative transporter at the BBB relevant for those drugs not transported by the H+/OC antiporter. Indeed, for instance, phenylephrine, frovatriptan and ipratropium were transported very efficiently by OCT3 but did not show a high trans‐stimulation ratio in hCMEC/D3 cells (Figures 2 and 4).

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the extended substrate screening presented in combination with the data published earlier indicates that about 50% of CNS active drugs may be transported at the blood–brain barrier by the genetically not yet identified H+/OC antiporter. Substrates of this transporter tend to be more lipophilic and have a smaller polar surface area than non‐substrates. The data of this study underline the importance to identify the protein(s) mediating that transport activity. As shown by the present and earlier studies, the H+/OC antiport is not explained by the OCT1, OCT2, OCT3 or OCTN1 or OCNT2 activities. Inhibition or stimulation of the H+/OC antiporter may play a relevant role in causing drug–drug interactions in polypharmacy with cationic CNS active substances, which is one of the reasons to further study this apparently highly active transporter at the blood–brain barrier.
