Functional immune changes are conserved in COVID-19 and trauma patients receiving intensive care
Authors: Alexander J T Wood, Gesa J Albers, Willem Cornelissen, James D Maiden, Deirdre E Cuff, Mitra Ashayeripanah, Patrick Schriek, Samantha J Emery-Corbin, Nichollas E Scott, Shihan Li, Jan Schröder, Adam M Deane, Jose A Villadangos, Laura Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritical illness, including COVID-19 and trauma, induce immune dysregulation associated with morbidity and mortality. Direct comparison is crucial to identify shared pathobiological mechanisms to guide precision therapies. This prospective cohort study undertook a comprehensive, longitudinal comparison of innate immune cell function and plasma protein expression in critically ill patients at days 1, 3, and 5 of intensive care unit admission. We enrolled 26 COVID-19 patients, 20 trauma patients, and 18 healthy controls and analysed plasma proteins and immune cell phenotypes and responses to S. aureus bioparticles. An unsupervised multi-omics factor analysis (MOFA) identified shared sources of biological variation. Here we show shared, profound immune alterations, including neutrophilia, decreased non-classical monocytes and dendritic cells (DCs), and a hyper-phagocytic and reactive oxygen species producing neutrophil state in both patient cohorts compared to healthy controls. Classical monocytes from patients were unable to upregulate CD11c, CD86, and SIRPα upon stimulation, and a phagocytosis-impaired SIRPα–CD11b– DC population was enriched from day 1. Innate immune activation profiles could segregate patients and were associated with subsequent impaired innate/adaptive crosstalk. This direct comparison of severe COVID-19 and trauma patients demonstrates many shared immune alterations between these distinct critical illnesses, which may benefit from similar targeted immunomodulation.Subject terms: Translational immunology, Sepsis, SARS-CoV-2

Introduction

Critical illness syndromes such as sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pancreatitis, and shock are frequently characterised by a complex interplay of systemic inflammation, organ dysfunction and vulnerability to secondary infection1–3. The increased availability of deep phenotyping techniques to delineate mechanisms driving these syndromes has led to precision trials in critical care medicine including the PANTHER4 and TRAITS5 platforms. A key feature of this paradigm is a syndrome-agnostic approach among critical illness states, which may see common biological features predictive of treatment response (treatable traits)6 targeted with similar therapies, regardless of initial insult.

Whilst the immunopathology of both COVID-19 pneumonia7,8 and severe traumatic injury9 have been the subject of sustained research efforts, there is a paucity of studies comparing these illnesses using the same assays in the same experimental framework. Such work is crucial to compare the direction, magnitude and temporal changes of any observed immunological perturbations, and guide application of precision therapies according to treatable traits. To address this gap, we designed the Functional Immunophenotyping in Critical Illness (FICI) study to provide a comprehensive analysis of innate immune phenotypes in patients with multi-trauma and severe COVID-19 pneumonia requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission. By linking detailed, rapid cellular functional assessments with cytokine and plasma protein expression data, FICI aimed to elucidate the similarities and differences in host immune responses between these two distinct critical illness syndromes.

Results

Clinical and demographic characteristics

To enable our investigation into immune functional alterations in critical illness we enrolled a total of 64 participants into an observational cohort study between August 2020 and August 2022. This comprised 18 healthy adult controls, 26 patients with COVID-19 (COVID-19) and 20 patients with traumatic injury (Trauma) admitted to the Royal Melbourne Hospital ICU, however, one trauma patient withdrew prior to sampling leaving 19 patients with biological data in this cohort. A summary of baseline clinical and demographic details for included participants is available in Table 1 and the sample collection at each time point is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A. The median age of the control group was 37 years, compared to 56 and 52 years for the COVID-19 and trauma groups, respectively. There was a slight bias of males in the COVID-19 (69%) and trauma (60%) groups. Only three COVID-19 patients were vaccinated, noting recruitment occurred largely prior to vaccine rollout in Australia and during the Delta variant wave10. Median predicted ICU mortality was low in both groups and the median APACHE III score (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III; reflecting illness severity) was similar amongst COVID-19 (median: 54, IQR: 39–71) and trauma patients (median: 51, IQR: 32–69). The prevalence of type 2 diabetes amongst COVID-19 patients was 23% (6/26) vs. 5% (1/20) for trauma patients. A high proportion of patients required vasoactive support (COVID-19: 88% trauma: 90%) and invasive mechanical ventilation during their ICU admission (COVID-19: 85% trauma: 95%). The median length of stay in ICU was 7.2 (3, 11.4) days and 9.4 (3.9, 15.5) days for the COVID-19 and trauma cohorts, respectively. One patient in the COVID-19 cohort (3.8%) and 3 patients in the trauma cohort (15 %) were tracheostomised and all patients except one in the COVID-19 cohort survived their ICU admission.

Baseline cohort characteristics

Comprehensive datasets of plasma protein and immune cell proportions and functions in critical illness

We set out to undertake a comprehensive functional, cellular and plasma protein immunophenotype analysis of two discrete critical illness states. Severe COVID-19 is a form of viral sepsis and inflammation is infection-induced. Meanwhile, most trauma patients had an initially sterile insult. We collected peripheral blood from COVID-19 and trauma patients admitted to the ICU on days 1, 3 and 5 of their admission as described in the ‘Methods’. While trauma patients’ ICU admission and day 1 sample collection occurred within hours of injury, COVID-19 patients had symptoms for a median of 7 days prior to day 1 of ICU admission sample collection. Blood was collected from healthy controls on a single occasion. Figure 1A outlines the experimental workflow with each blood sample to generate 5 datasets: (1) ex vivo phenotyping by spectral cytometry, (2) untargeted plasma protein analysis via liquid chromatography mass spectrometry-based proteomics (‘mass spectrometry’) and (3) targeted analysis via cytometric bead array (‘CBA’), (4) cell stimulated marker ('CSM') assay by spectral cytometry, (5) phagocytosis assay by flow cytometry. The final dataset was the patient clinical data (6). Full details available in ‘Methods’. Briefly, ex vivo phenotyping involved staining unstimulated blood with a 24-colour spectral cytometry panel (plus viability stain, Supplementary Table 1) within 4 h of collection at room temperature for analysis of neutrophil, monocyte and dendritic cell subsets. For cell stimulated marker (CSM) assays, blood was either left unstimulated or stimulated with S. aureus-coated bioparticles (unconjugated) for 1 h then stained with the same 24-colour panel and fold change in stimulated marker expression calculated. Gating for both these assays is in Supplementary Fig. 1B and example data from the CSM assay is in Supplementary Fig. 2A. Phagocytosis assays also stimulated blood for 1 h but with S. aureus bioparticles conjugated to pHrodoRed (fluorescent marker of phagocytosis) and with addition of dihydrorhodamine-123 (DHR), which is a fluorescent indicator of phagocytosis-associated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production11. Blood was stained with a 13-colour panel (plus viability stain, Supplementary Table 2) and pHrodoRed (phagocytosis) and DHR (ROS production) mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) calculated within target cell populations. Gating for phagocytosis assays is in Supplementary Fig. 2B, and example data are in Supplementary Fig. 2C, clearly showing the neutrophils are the most phagocytic cell type and produce the most ROS. As lymphocytes had negligible amounts of either functional readout, they were used as an internal control to normalise for experimental and instrument variation, with lymphocyte MFI subtracted from target cell populations. Sample numbers included for analysis of each assay by cohort and time point are in Supplementary Table 3.

COVID-19 and severe trauma are associated with profound, yet similar, alterations in proportions of circulating immune cells

Our ex vivo phenotype analysis revealed that trauma and COVID-19 patients had significantly increased proportions of neutrophils (as a percent of all CD45+ haematopoietic cells) across all observed days (Fig. 1B). Classical CD14+CD16– monocyte fractions were not different in either patient cohort compared to controls but were slightly reduced in COVID-19 patients relative to trauma patients. Intermediate CD14+CD16+ monocytes, the smallest monocyte subset, were consistently reduced in COVID-19 patients relative to controls. Both patient cohorts exhibited markedly reduced non-classical CD14–CD16+ monocytes, plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) and conventional dendritic cell (cDC) percentages compared to healthy controls at all timepoints. When we interrogated the dendritic cell subsets further, we found trauma patients had an enriched cDC1 subset, as a proportion of total cDCs, relative to COVID-19 patients and healthy controls. The cDC2 subset within total cDCs was initially depleted in the COVID-19 cohort relative to control and trauma, after which it converged back toward control levels. Overall, by day 5 all ICU patients had similarly increased circulating neutrophils and decreased non-classical monocytes, pDCs and cDCs compared to healthy controls, indicating similar kinetics of the circulating immune cells between these two different insults.

Plasma protein levels in COVID-19 and trauma patients demonstrate more similarities than differences

To understand the inflammatory milieu generated by either viral or traumatic insult that circulating immune cells are being exposed to in vivo, we assessed cell-free plasma protein abundance levels. We used two complementary approaches, untargeted mass spectrometry-based proteomics (relative abundance with both raw protein group (PG) quantities and iBAQ values calculated, Fig. 2A and Supplementary Data file 1) and cytometric bead array using three BioLegend LegendPlex kits (quantitated levels, Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data file 2).

We detected and included in analysis 727 proteins by mass spectrometry and 33 proteins by cytometric bead array kits, with an overlap of 11 proteins detected by both. COVID-19 and trauma induced similar alterations in terms of magnitude and direction relative to controls for many plasma proteins. Notable examples include albumin, complement receptor of immunoglobulin (CRIG), serum amyloid A proteins (SAA), myeloperoxidase (MPO), interleukin 1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), neutrophil gelatinase associated lipocalin (NGAL) and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) (Fig. 2A, showing raw PG quantities).

There were exceptions to this pattern, particularly C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)-10, interleukin (IL)-7 and IL-18, which were elevated in COVID-19 patients, but either minimally elevated or not at all in trauma patients compared to healthy controls, consistent with post-viral immune activation. IL-6, a key target for immune therapeutics in COVID-19, was elevated in both cohorts but more markedly in the trauma cohort (Fig. 2B, control IL-6 median ± IQR = 2.5 ± 4.8 pg/mL). Calprotectin (MRP8/14) levels had a large dynamic range, with median ± IQR for healthy controls 1.48 × 106 ± 7.71 × 105 pg/mL compared to day 1 COVID-19 patients 6.01 × 107 ± 3.27 × 108 pg/mL and day 1 trauma patients 9.03 × 106 ± 2.00 × 107 pg/mL. Cystatin C (a marker of renal injury) had continually increased abundance over 5 days in COVID-19 patients relative to controls and the trauma cohort. Markers of muscle damage including myoglobin and creatine kinase M-type were highest in the trauma cohort at day 1, though myoglobin concentrations amongst patients converged during the study period. We also found rapidly elevated levels of Chitinase 3 like 1 protein (CHI3L1, also known as YKL-40) in trauma patients that normalised by day 5, this has been previously proposed as a marker of traumatic brain injury12.

Whilst important differences exist between plasma protein expression in COVID-19 and trauma patients, it is noteworthy that a shared inflammatory response is evident which may be amenable to therapeutic manipulation. Correlation between the two assays on mutually detected proteins was strong (Fig. 2C, comparing iBAQ and pg/mL values), thus the mass spectrometry proteomics dataset with its far greater proteomic depth was used for subsequent machine learning techniques.

Machine learning techniques for unbiased detection of factors driving broad immunophenotypes independent of clinical data

To identify patterns of shared biological variation across our multi-modal dataset, we performed an unsupervised multi-omics factor analysis (MOFA). MOFA is a machine learning method designed to integrate multiple data types by identifying a set of latent factors, so named because they are not readily interpretable by humans due to the large number of datapoints involved13,14. Each latent factor is a grouping of readouts or ‘features’ from the different assays (i.e., a particular plasma protein expression level, a cellular subset’s phagocytic function or an individual cell surface marker’s response to bacterial stimulus) which co-vary across individuals, thereby capturing multi-modal biological processes which differentiate patients. These latent factors can then be paired with metadata (such as mortality or COVID-19/trauma status) to understand how the biological changes vary according to diagnosis or outcome.

The model was trained on three datasets comprising all cohorts and time points without cohort or clinical information (unsupervised): plasma mass spectrometry (assay 2; 727 plasma protein features), CSM assay (assay 4; 180 cell surface marker features), and phagocytosis assay (assay 5; 70 features). The CSM and phagocytosis assays measured the response of circulating immune cells to S. aureus bioparticle stimulus by change in cell surface marker expression, ROS production or phagocytosis as described in ‘Methods’ and Fig. 1A. MOFA then decomposed the total variance across patients and attributed it to each of the generated factors. The contribution of each assay to the variance captured by the latent factors was proportional to its number of measured features; plasma mass spectrometry contributed the most (~56%), followed by the CSM assay (~39%) then the phagocytosis assay (~30%) (Fig. 3A).

The analysis generated 15 latent factors. As is common in MOFA, one factor (factor 2) was dominated by the assay with the highest number of features (proteomics) and was therefore excluded from further analysis to focus on factors representing shared biological signals across the 3 assays. Figure 3A demonstrates the number of features in each included assay (left panel), the variance explained by each assay (middle panel) and the variance explained by each factor, with factors 1 and 3 responsible for shared variation across the 3 assays. The top 20% flow cytometric or plasma proteomic features contributing to each factor are in Supplementary Data file 3. We next visualized the data based on these factors using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). This unbiased analysis revealed that the biological data alone could cluster samples according to their clinical cohort, though with significant inter-individual heterogeneity and overlap (Fig. 3B). Upon examining the distribution of the top two factors (factors 1 and 3), we found that factor 1 was enriched in trauma patients and, to a lesser extent, in COVID-19 patients, but was largely absent in healthy controls. Conversely, factor 3 was most prominent in healthy controls, followed by trauma patients, and was least pronounced in COVID-19 patients (Fig. 3B). We conducted further analyses within the COVID-19 cohort to confirm that comorbidities of diabetes or renal dysfunction (measured by Cystatin C levels) did not affect latent factor loading (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To understand the biology of each latent factor, we performed GO pathway analysis of the top 10 proteins from the mass spectrometry dataset and found the patient loaded factor 1 was dominated by innate immune signalling, neutrophil degranulation, cytokine, complement and toll-like receptor pathways—all associated with an innate inflammatory response (Supplementary Data file 3). The opposite of this is seen in the healthy cohort loaded factor 3, where the enriched protein pathways were more homeostatic, relating to lipid processing, protein processing and metabolism of vitamins, lipids and proteins (Fig. 3C). Both factors exhibited enriched platelet activation pathways which were removed from Fig. 3C to allow visualisation of more distinct pathways across the factors. No proteins were excluded from analysis. The complete lists of enriched pathways for factors 1 and 3 are available in Supplementary Data file 4.

While plasma proteins revealed distinct innate inflammatory and lipid metabolism signatures across our cohorts, we used functional assays to specifically examine immune cell behaviour in COVID-19, trauma patients and healthy controls. Our unsupervised MOFA analysis identified several key features defining factors 1 and 3 across both the CSM assay (Fig. 3D) and phagocytosis assay (Fig. 3E). These included the co-stimulatory markers CD80 and CD86, the antigen presenting molecule HLA-DR, common γ chain receptors CD16 and CD64 and markers involved in phagocytosis (C5aR [CD88] and SIRPα). The unbiased identification of these features across both functional assays indicated an important biological driver of inter-individual differences and prompted a deeper analysis of their alterations by cell, time, individual and cohort.

Neutrophils in COVID-19 and trauma patients are hyper-phagocytic with preserved responses to subsequent bacterial challenge

We first analysed in vivo inflammation induced alterations in circulating neutrophils (gating in Supplementary Fig. 1B). Neutrophils from both COVID-19 and trauma patients exhibited significantly higher phagocytosis of pHrodoRed-conjugated S. aureus beads compared to healthy controls, and this capacity increased significantly over time in both groups (Fig. 4A). ROS production, assessed via DHR assay, was also increased in COVID-19 and trauma patient neutrophils compared to healthy controls, and this was consistent over the 5 days in ICU (Fig. 4A).

Significant alterations in neutrophil surface marker expression were observed between the groups directly ex vivo and following in vitro stimulation with unconjugated S. aureus beads. We focused here on the markers from the CSM assay that were in the top 20% of MOFA factors 1 and 3 (Fig. 3D). We found, compared to healthy controls, unstimulated neutrophils from COVID-19 and trauma patients had significantly lower expression of the common γ-chain receptor CD16 (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Interestingly, at day 1 patients could upregulate this marker to a greater extent (relative to unstimulated blood), yet their stimulated levels remained lower than healthy controls on all days (Fig. 4B). CD11c is an adhesion molecule (integrin alpha X) that, with CD18 (integrin beta 2), forms the heterodimeric complement receptor 4 (CR4; αXβ2) and is thought to be a marker of neutrophil maturation15. Conversely to CD16, unstimulated expression of CD11c was slightly increased on patient cells over time (Supplementary Fig. 5A), with all cohorts able to upregulate to a similar extent following stimulation, with COVID-19 patients having most stimulated upregulation at day 1 (Fig. 4C). Neutrophil expression of the common γ-chain receptor CD64 has been proposed as a marker of infection16, and while unstimulated levels were increased in COVID-19 patients (Supplementary Fig. 5A), this was also seen in trauma patients, which indicated expression was driven by conserved inflammatory mechanisms. Intriguingly, while COVID-19 and trauma patient neutrophils upregulated CD64 upon in vitro stimulation, healthy control cells did not, suggesting different protein regulatory processes in severe inflammation (Fig. 4D). Signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα, CD172a) is involved in the inhibition of phagocytic function as it interacts with CD47 expressed by self as a ‘don’t eat me’ signal. SIRPα can also aid in neutrophil migration into tissues17. While unstimulated levels of SIRPα were equivalent between healthy controls and patient cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 5A), at days 1 and 3 trauma patients had significantly greater magnitude of stimulated upregulation relative to healthy controls (Fig. 4E). Therefore, neutrophil capacity to upregulate SIRPα upon phagocytic stimulation was conserved regardless of inflammation trigger.

Collectively, these findings demonstrated a distinct and conserved neutrophil phenotype and functional profile in COVID-19, and trauma patients compared to healthy controls, characterised by enhanced phagocytosis and ROS production, reduced unstimulated CD16 expression, and heightened expression of maturation and functional markers CD11c, CD64, and SIRPα following in vitro stimulation.

Classical monocytes in COVID-19 and trauma patients converge to similar functional phenotypes over 5 days in ICU

CD14+CD16– classical monocytes are the more-studied innate immune cell in critical illness. In vivo they can differentiate into tissue-resident macrophages, replacing those eliminated by severe inflammation. In keeping with a previous study that demonstrated upregulation of complement receptor 3 (CR3; heterodimer of CD11b and CD18) on monocytes in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia18, we found that circulating classical monocytes had increased phagocytosis in COVID-19 patients at day 5. COVID-19 patients had significantly elevated monocyte ROS production at all days compared to healthy controls and compared to trauma patients at day 1. By day 5 trauma patient monocytes also had increased ROS production compared to healthy controls, suggesting these functional changes of increased phagocytic capacity and ROS production require several days post insult to appear (Fig. 5A).

The top monocyte markers in MOFA factors 1 and 3 overlapped with those on neutrophils, yet with some divergent expression trends. Here we saw that CD11c, a marker of monocyte activation associated with inflammatory monocytes in mild, but not severe, COVID-1919, was expressed at similar levels on unstimulated cells between cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 5B). However, by day 5 both patient cohorts had impaired upregulation following stimulation (Fig. 5B). This trend was also evident for CD11b (Supplementary Fig. 5C), although with higher unstimulated levels in COVID-19 patients (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The class II antigen presenting molecule human lymphocyte antigen (HLA)-DR isoform has been previously found to be downregulated on circulating monocytes in sepsis and COVID-1920,21, which we also observed in our trauma and COVID-19 patients (Supplementary Fig. 5B). However, the capacity to upregulate HLA-DR was preserved as, following 1 h of in vitro stimulation, similar upregulation (relative to baseline unstimulated cells) was seen in all cohorts at all time points compared to healthy controls, although patient upregulated levels were still lower than healthy controls (Fig. 5C, right panel). Similarly, the capacity of monocytes to upregulate co-stimulatory marker CD86 was preserved in patient cohorts, despite some variation in baseline unstimulated levels (Fig. 5D). Loss of SIRPα on monocytes has been shown in septic patients and is a feature of immune paralysis following systemic inflammatory response syndrome22. Although the unstimulated levels of SIRPα were similar on both patient cohorts within 5 days of ICU admission to healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 5B), COVID-19 patients and to a lesser extent trauma patients, had impaired ability to upregulate SIRPα following in vitro stimulation (Fig. 5E).

Taken together, we see a similar trend that COVID-19 and trauma patient monocytes have similar functional alterations from healthy controls by day 5, notably enhanced phagocytosis, reduced HLA-DR on unstimulated cells and an increasingly impaired ability to upregulate CD11c, CD11b and SIRPα upon stimulation.

A phenotype of impaired dendritic cells in critical illness that occurs rapidly post-injury

Conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) are critical for initiating protective cellular immunity and are known to become paralysed (impaired) following systemic inflammatory response syndrome23. This paralysis is characterised by loss of phagocytic capacity, reduced ability to present antigens, and downregulation of antigen presenting (HLA-DR) and co-stimulatory (CD86, CD83, CD80) molecules22,24–26. cDCs are a rare subset in blood, and we did not have sufficient events in phagocytosis assay wells to split into cDC1 and cDC2 subsets, so data are shown for the total cDC population. Compared to healthy controls, cDCs from trauma patients had preserved phagocytic function, whilst cDCs from COVID-19 patients at day 5 in ICU showed impaired function (Fig. 6A). Dendritic cells produce very low levels of ROS, and these levels were not different between cohorts at any timepoint (Fig. 6A). We also found that HLA-DR was significantly downregulated on unstimulated cDCs in both cohorts at day 1 and this remained low for all patients until day 5 in ICU (Supplementary Fig. 5D). However, in a similar trend to monocytes, the ability of trauma cDCs to upregulate HLA-DR was preserved and significantly greater than COVID-19 patients at days 1 and 3 in ICU, although stimulated levels remained lower than healthy control cDCs (Fig. 6B). Therefore, although surface levels of HLA-DR may be a useful biomarker, this pathway did not seem intrinsically impaired following ex vivo bacterial stimulation.

We next used unsupervised dimensionality reduction (tSNE) and clustering analysis (FlowSOM) to explore cDC surface markers associated with phagocytic function. We detected two unique populations, largely within COVID-19 patients, that could be best defined by SIRPα and the adhesion molecule CD11b (integrin alpha M). Intriguingly, it was the SIRPα–CD11b– cDCs that had significantly lower phagocytic capacity compared to SIRPα+CD11b+ cDCs (Fig. 6C). Of note, we conducted the same tSNE/FlowSOM analysis of neutrophils and classical monocytes and did not identify any additional phagocytosis-associated phenotypes that varied significantly between the cohorts.

Previously, enhanced SIRPα expression on monocytes and alveolar macrophages has been associated with impaired phagocytosis22. When analysing total cDCs, the surface levels of SIRPα alone were unchanged across cohorts regardless of stimulation (Fig. 6D), suggesting that, similar to neutrophils, alternative mechanisms beyond the SIRPα/CD47 axis govern phagocytic capacity in blood cDCs. When we re-analysed our ex vivo phenotyping data with this hierarchical cDC gating, we discovered this phagocytosis impaired SIRPα–CD11b– subset of cDCs was significantly enriched in both COVID-19 and trauma patients from day 1 until day 5 in ICU (Fig. 6E). Therefore, this phenotype is rapidly induced and persists with ongoing inflammation.

Together, total conventional dendritic cells show preserved ability to upregulate HLA-DR, but a unique subset is functionally impaired early following either sterile or infectious inflammatory triggers.

Using multi-omics factor analysis to discover immune biomarkers of patient outcomes

Given similar markers were driving cohort variance, largely borne out as differences in healthy compared to ICU patients, we analysed our MOFA top factors (1 and 3) exclusively within the ICU patient cohorts. K-means clustering analysis of COVID-19 and trauma samples by these factors revealed three clusters (Fig. 7A). COVID-19 samples fell mostly into cluster 2 which had lowest loading of these factors (Fig. 3B), while trauma patients were mostly split between cluster 1 and 3. Analysis of cluster assignment over time revealed COVID-19 patients fluctuate between clusters 2 and 3, characterised by lower MOFA factor 1 (innate activation) loading. In contrast, the trauma patients were split between clusters 1 and 3 with a shift toward cluster 1 (high MOFA factor 1) on day 3. Therefore, using unbiased analysis of a suite of plasma and immune functional data we identified a temporal transition of trauma patients into an inflammatory immunological signature by day 3 in ICU. Defining these clusters revealed that patients in the ‘innate activated’ cluster had worse sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) scores, which did not differ between COVID-19 and trauma cohorts (scores matched to sample by day, Fig. 7B, C). This could not be predicted by Australian and New Zealand Risk of Death (ANZROD27 calculated upon ICU admission), which did not differ by cluster or between COVID-19 and trauma cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B).

To assess which immune markers linked to these clusters had significant differences in clinical outcomes we split all patients between those who transitioned into ‘innate activated’ cluster 1 at day 5; and those who did not. We then re-analysed our day 1 ex vivo phenotyping data, which was not part of the MOFA analysis, which was designed to understand functional changes upon cell stimulation. This revealed that while all patients had reduced expression of HLA-DR on classical monocytes compared to healthy controls, patients within ‘innate activated’ cluster 1 had significantly reduced CD86 on classical monocytes, non-classical monocytes and cDCs compared to other patient clusters and to healthy controls (Fig. 7D). The patients in cluster 1 also had increased CD88 (C5aR) on neutrophils, classical monocytes and intermediate monocytes compared to other patients (Fig. 7D). Although guided by biological data analysed without cohort assignment, the comparison ended up comparing mostly trauma to COVID-19 patients. The “cluster 1 at day 5” group comprised n = 7 trauma and the “cluster 2 and 3 at day 5” group comprised n = 5 trauma and n = 14 COVID-19 patients. Therefore, these differences may reflect some altered immune phenotypes unique to infectious versus sterile induced inflammation.

As our trauma cohort showed a rapid divergence between clusters, we thought conserved predictive markers could be identified by a similar analysis within this cohort. Therefore, we performed analysis by dividing the trauma patients into those that fell into inflamed cluster 1 at day 3 (n = 12, red) and those that did not (n = 5, green). This cluster assignment may have clinical relevance, given an observed trend of trauma patients in cluster 1 for worse SOFA scores (Fig. 7E) that again was not predicted by day 1 ANZROD (Supplementary Fig. 6C). We found at day 3 trauma patients in cluster 1 had increased CD32 expression on non-classical monocytes compared to healthy controls and significantly decreased CD16 on intermediate monocytes compared to cluster 3 (Fig. 7F). Both clusters 1 and 3 had similarly reduced expression of HLA-DR on classical monocytes and cDCs compared to healthy controls. The ‘innate activated’ cluster 1 had significantly higher PD-L1 (CD274) expression on cDCs compared to cluster 3 and enriched proportions of the phagocytosis impaired SIRPα–CD11b– subset compared to healthy controls at day 1 (Fig. 7F). Analysis of our cytometric bead array data (also not included in the MOFA model) revealed all trauma patients had higher plasma levels of IL-6 compared to healthy controls at day 1, which remained higher at day 3 only in cluster 1 (Fig. 7G). Between the clusters, MMP-9 was significantly higher in cluster 1 than cluster 3 and healthy controls at day 1, with IL-10 higher in cluster 1 than cluster 3 at day 3. Therefore, early changes in CD16 on intermediate monocytes, PD-L1 on cDCs and levels of MMP-9 should be explored for predictive potential of outcome.

We also capitalised on the opportunity to analyse how our data were affected by immunomodulatory drug treatments in the COVID-19 cohort. Within this cohort 25/26 patients received the steroid dexamethasone, and we excluded the one patient who did not from further analyses. Of the 25 patients treated with dexamethasone, 21/25 received the antiviral Remdesivir, 3 received Tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor), 13 received Baricitinib (JAK/STAT inhibitor) and 10 received neither drug. Most patients received this treatment prior to, or during the sampling window (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Baricitinib through acting on JAK/STAT pathways inhibits IL-6, and this treatment group had the lowest average levels of plasma IL-6 (measured by CBA), although this was not significantly different to the other groups (Fig. 7H). Interestingly, Baricitinib treatment was associated with significantly lower levels of IL-8 compared to no treatment, while the 3 Tocilizumab treated patients had highest IL-10 levels (Supplementary Fig. 6E). There was no difference in neutrophil or classical monocyte phagocytic function between immunomodulatory treatment groups (Fig. 7I). Levels of CD86 expression on monocyte subsets and cDCs revealed that Baricitinib treatment group did not differ to no treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6F), with the only difference being reduced CD86 on intermediate monocytes in Tocilizumab group at day 1. Interestingly, by day 5 in ICU the 3 Tocilizumab treated patients had significantly reduced levels of the impaired SIRPα–CD11b– cDCs compared to Baricitinib treatment (Fig. 7J). Therefore, despite evidence of clinical efficacy28,29, these drugs did not have profound effects on immune markers and functions measured in our assays. As we only assayed patients out to day 5 post ICU admission, we cannot rule out more significant impacts on immune function associated with these drugs beyond this time point.

Finally, as we had the self-reported day of symptom onset for our COVID-19 cohort, we were able to analyse our observed convergences between cohorts by adjusting our data to time post symptom onset, rather than day post ICU admission. While this did not alter the trauma cohort, the COVID-19 cohort sampling range was from days 1 to 18 post symptom onset (Supplementary Fig. 7A). By standardising the cohorts in this manner, we could collectively assess the trends by cohort post symptom onset. The impaired SIRPα–CD11b– cDCs, as a proportion of all unstimulated ex vivo cDCs, peaked soon after onset and slowly resolved over time (Supplementary Fig. 7B). The reduced HLA-DR on unstimulated ex vivo cDCs and monocytes occurred rapidly and persisted for duration of sampling window. The altered expression of CD86 on these populations was more a feature of MOFA analysis than cohort analysis and appeared to resolve over time. The increased CD88 on neutrophils occurred rapidly and appeared to resolve within 5 days (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Amongst plasma cytokines quantified by CBA, IL-10 increased early in COVID-19 and then returned to healthy levels by approximately day 15. MMP9 increased rapidly in both patient cohorts and only started to resolve beyond day 15, while rapidly increased CXCL10 was only a feature of COVID-19 and this slowly resolved over 18 days post symptom onset (Supplementary Fig. 7D). From the plasma proteins quantified by mass spectrometry reduced Albumin (ALB), and increased serum amyloid A1 (SAA1) and myeloperoxidase (MPO), occurred rapidly in all patients and was sustained for sampling period. Myoglobin (MB) increased in all patients at onset, highest in trauma, and slowly resolved over 18 days. These data highlight the convergence in the datasets by time post insult, rather than by type of insult or time of admission to ICU. Discussion

The FICI study sought to define the similarities and differences between two disparate critical illness syndromes within the same experimental framework, so that the magnitude and direction of identified differences would be directly comparable. Understanding immune dysfunction common to different critical illness syndromes may facilitate the use of targeted therapies based on common pathobiology across disease states. There were several key findings from our study, with the major findings summarised in Fig. 8. Firstly, both cohorts were characterised by remarkably similar changes in many aspects of immunology. Increased neutrophil along with decreased intermediate and non-classical monocyte and total dendritic cell fractions (Fig. 1B) were observed relative to healthy controls. Our data are consistent with previous studies21,30–32 but go further to demonstrate the magnitude of changes in these cell populations is similar between these cohorts across many innate immune cell lineages. Key differences between the critically ill groups were observed in the classical monocyte and cDC1 subsets (both increased in trauma relative to COVID-19) which may reflect more significant changes to antigen recognition and presentation pathways unique to severe trauma26.

There are several strengths to our study: we studied live cells within 4 h of sampling and measured functional cellular responses to a common bacterial pathogen (S. aureus – using bacterial coated bioparticles) as well as plasma protein and cytokine expression. Our study covered several time points out to day 5 post ICU admission and analysed severe traumatic injury and COVID-19 viral infection within the same experimental framework, facilitating direct functional comparisons between these cohorts. Our cohorts were similar to other critically ill COVID-1933–37 and trauma38,39 cohorts with respect to age, sex, presence of comorbidities such as diabetes and incidence of traumatic brain injury (trauma cohort only). However, we did not study details of the COVID-19 strain (noting the study was undertaken during the Delta variant wave in Victoria, Australia) or specific antibody responses. Following from earlier studies implicating immune impairment in monocytes and dendritic cells, we focused our analyses on cells of the phagocytic myeloid compartment and did not assess adaptive immune function. Whilst the FICI sample size is similar to other mechanistic studies in critically ill patients with COVID-1933,36,40–42 or trauma39, the modest number of patients enrolled in each cohort means caution should be applied when generalising our findings to groups with different comorbidity, injury or infection burdens. Our data demonstrate a profoundly altered plasma proteome in response to critical illness. We demonstrated a uniformity in response for many plasma proteins between COVID-19 and trauma patients (Fig. 2), with many differences becoming less apparent over time, suggesting an element of homogeneity driven by the host response to severe insult. Whilst well-described changes such as increased IL-6 and IL-1RA20,31 were observed in both cohorts, CXCL10, IL-7 and IL-18 levels were higher in COVID-19, despite immunosuppressive therapy in almost all cases. Our demonstration that IL-6 was higher in severe trauma at days 1 and 3 of ICU admission support previous meta-analyses which similarly demonstrated lower IL-6 expression in COVID-19 patients compared to hyper-inflammatory ARDS and sepsis cohorts, though notably those studies did not include a trauma population43,44. The concentrations of IL-1RA and IL-6 in our trauma cohort are consistent with other groups over similar time periods31.

Our proteomics data are consistent with those from other groups investigating severe COVID-19 and demonstrate a similar profile of increased inflammatory plasma proteins including SAA1, SAA2, LBP, CRP, LRG1, SERPINA10, ITIH3, C1R in severe COVID patients (WHO ordinal outcome scale of clinical improvement levels 5–7) relative to controls or less severe cases (levels 3–4). Furthermore, the observed downregulation of lipid metabolic pathways in our study is consistent with the work of these groups, which also displayed a reduction in expression of multiple apolipoproteins and GPLD1 associated with increasing COVID-19 severity. Comparisons with trauma proteomic datasets revealed additional similarities, though not as marked as the COVID-19 comparisons: of a total of 44 proteins differentially expressed in the plasma of patients with severe torso (6 proteins) or severe brain (38 proteins) trauma compared to minor trauma39, only one protein (PPBP) was evident in the top 20% of features in factor 1 or 3 of our MOFA. Li and colleagues38 performed proteomic analyses of trauma patient samples drawn from two clinical trials examining the efficacy of prehospital plasma (Prehospital Air Medical Plasma; PAMPer) and tranexamic acid (Study of Tranexamic Acid During Air and Ground Medical Prehospital Transport; STAAMP) in trauma resuscitation. They demonstrated a proteomic signature associated with non-resolving organ dysfunction which included elevated levels of myoglobin (MB), brain acid soluble protein 1 (BASP1), pulmonary surfactant–associated protein D2 (SFTPD)38 reflecting musculoskeletal, brain and lung injury. All of these were significant contributors to our MOFA factor 1 or 3 and were similarly upregulated in our data (Supplementary Data file 3). Despite differences in cohorts, sample preparation and analytical techniques, the concordance of key proteins between our proteomic data and related datasets validates the comparisons we have made between COVID-19, multi-trauma patients and healthy controls.

The finding of similarly increased innate immune signalling (factor 1) evident in both COVID-19 and trauma patients with downregulated lipid signalling pathways (factor 3) being unique to COVID-19 bears further consideration. Neutrophil degranulation products, such as myeloperoxidase and neutrophil elastase, degrade apolipoproteins and inhibit the anti-atherogenic functions of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol45,46 which may be responsible for the observed reduction in lipoprotein-related signalling. However, we would expect to see such results mirrored across both cohorts given broadly similar levels of neutrophil activation. The timing of measurement relative to initial insult may explain the difference, with trauma patients having less time since insult for the consumption and impairment of apolipoproteins to occur. Another hypothesis proposed by Messner and colleagues36 is that lower apolipoprotein levels in COVID-19 predisposes patients to severe disease (which comprised our entire cohort), with this being less relevant in the trauma cohort. A potential mechanism for this observation is reduced neutrophil regulation amongst patients with low APOA1 levels47, leading to collateral organ damage and severe COVID-19.

To understand the influence of common comorbidities on immune function, we assessed MOFA factor 1 and 3 associations with type 2 diabetes and cystatin C, a sensitive indicator of glomerular filtration, which has been associated with worse outcomes in COVID-1948. We confined these analyses to COVID-19 patients because there was only one patient with type 2 diabetes in the trauma cohort. We did not show any statistically significant association between diabetes, cystatin C and MOFA Factor 1 or 3 loading (Supplementary Fig. 4). There was a trend at all three timepoints toward higher Factor 1 loading amongst diabetic patients, consistent with a more severe inflammatory response and worse clinical outcomes demonstrated by other authors49,50. Our subgroup analyses are limited by small sample size and require validation in studies designed to answer these specific questions on the interaction between immune cell function, comorbid disease and acute illness.

Despite these differences in cytokine signalling, cell surface marker expression (relevant markers selected by unbiased MOFA; Fig. 3) and functional responses to in vitro challenge with S. aureus, for neutrophils, monocytes and dendritic cells were broadly similar across the COVID-19 and trauma cohorts (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). We observed hyper-phagocytic neutrophils with enhanced unprimed ROS production in both patient cohorts relative to control individuals, consistent with previous reports in the COVID-19 literature using a S. aureus stimulus35,42. Studies of neutrophil phagocytosis and oxidative burst in trauma patients are conflicting; Hazeldine and colleagues31 demonstrated reduced phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) stimulated ROS production in trauma patients relative to controls within hours of injury, whereas Janicowa et al.32 showed a trend toward higher phagocytosis and ROS production in their E. coli and dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) based assays. In a study of trauma patients, Seshadri et al.51 demonstrated monocyte phagocytosis was unchanged, but ROS production was increased. Using parallel recruitment from the same ICU, and analysis with the same assays, we found minimal functional differences between trauma and COVID-19 patients when stimulated with S. aureus. Both patient groups demonstrated higher levels of phagocytosis and ROS production in neutrophils and classical monocytes relative to control samples. We demonstrated the capacity of neutrophils to upregulate phagocytosis-related cell surface markers is also preserved in critically ill patients (Fig. 4), despite reductions in baseline expression of key markers such as CD16. Unlike neutrophils, classical monocytes exhibited an impaired ability to upregulate CD11c, CD11b, CD86 and SIRPα on stimulation (Fig. 5). The combined loss of HLA-DR (antigen presentation) and CD86 (co-stimulation) was a conserved feature of classical monocytes and conventional dendritic cells for patients in cluster 1, being those with highest loading of MOFA factor 1 and worse SOFA scores. This indicates a potential loss of capacity to stimulate T cell responses, a key feature of immune paralysis post-SIRS22,52. Future studies should explore functional validation via assessment of T cell responses.

We identified a phagocytically impaired SIRPα–CD11b– cDC population which was enriched in both patient cohorts from early within their illness which may play a role in subsequent impairment of adaptive immunity. The immunological and clinical significance of this subset requires further investigation, ideally with functional and multi-omics studies to delineate the characteristics of these cells. Taken together, our data suggest a common hyperactivated neutrophil signature which has been implicated in organ damage in different disease states35,53–55, combined with a failure of subsequent innate-adaptive cell crosstalk which may predispose patients to subsequent immunosuppression22,24–26. These findings are consistent with the current paradigm of parallel, rapidly developing, mixed hyper-inflammatory and immunosuppressive responses induced by critical illness56–58. The similarity of these functional changes between our COVID-19 and trauma cohorts suggests therapies targeted at ameliorating them may be applied irrespective of initial insult but tailored to the individual response. Future work may focus on validating these uniform signatures in larger cohorts with differing insults.

Interestingly, while increased SIRPα is clearly associated with reduced phagocytosis in monocytes22, our analysis in neutrophils (and dendritic cells) revealed the opposite trend. This suggests that SIRPα expression does not dominantly control phagocytic function in these cells. Focusing on the highly phagocytic neutrophils, in both ICU cohorts they were highly activated, and the positive association between SIRPα expression and phagocytosis likely reflects activation-dependent co-upregulation rather than a causal effect, with inhibitory signalling being overridden under inflammatory conditions, with SIRPα perhaps limiting excessive tissue damage. Additionally, SIRPα is known to be important in neutrophil extravasation and transmigration into tissues17, such as the lung, where, in addition to CD47, SIRPα can interact with surfactant proteins A and D59. Cell-specific differential glycosylation patterns of SIRPα may regulate ligand binding and thus downstream effects of signalling60. Together, these findings indicate that post-translational modifications and ligand specificity likely drive cell type-specific SIRPα function, influencing both neutrophil migration and function.

We next explored how differences in the expression of the two most important MOFA factors changed over time between the cohorts (Fig. 7). This demonstrated the trauma group shifted between clusters 1 (innate immune/neutrophil dominated) and 3 (enriched lipid signalling), whereas the COVID-19 cohort oscillated between clusters 2 and 3 (lower innate immune and lipid signalling pathway expression). Amongst the trauma cohort, cluster 1 membership at day 3 was associated with higher (worse) SOFA scores and altered CD16 and PD-L1 expression on monocytes and dendritic cells as well as higher levels of MMP-9, indicating more severe and persistent immunological injury in this group. These phenotypic changes within the trauma group may reflect a period of rapid change immediately post insult compared with the COVID-19 group, who had established viral infection and systemic inflammation at the time of ICU admission. Amongst COVID-19 patients, we were unable to demonstrate many functional differences associated with immunomodulator treatment, though there was a non-significant trend toward reduction in IL-6 concentration associated with Baricitinib treatment as in previous studies61,62. Using the day of reported symptom onset for the COVID-19 cohort to standardise with the trauma cohort, we assessed overall longitudinal trends in our assays. This analysis supports that the convergence noted in the two distinct patient groups is predominantly driven by time post inflammation onset, rather than inflammation trigger type or timing of critical care interventions.

The apparent discrepancy between a lack of functional immune effects of immunomodulators in the setting of clinical efficacy demonstrated in large randomised controlled trials29, is most likely explained by tissue compartment-dependent effects, significant inter-individual heterogeneity and sample size. An important mechanism of Baricitinib is downregulation of proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine release in the lung compartment by transmigrated monocytes, resulting in less neutrophilic infiltration and reduced ARDS severity, as demonstrated in rhesus macaques and human airway models63,64. We sampled circulating blood cytokines and immune cell subsets, which may not capture functional changes unique to the lung or other tissue compartments. Previous studies of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from critically ill patients with COVID-19 using single-cell RNA sequencing indicated increased neutrophil activation and reduced neutrophil CD16 expression as well as reduced dendritic cell HLA-DR and CD86 in severe cases and in keeping with our data40,41. Reduced neutrophil phagocytosis in the pulmonary compartment was suggested but not directly assayed40. Our observation of a trend towards lower circulating IL-6 levels associated with Baricitinib may reflect markedly reduced concentrations in the lung, which has been described as a significant source of circulating cytokines in COVID-1965. To address these mechanistic questions, the ideal study would sample the lung via bronchoalveolar lavage and incorporate pre-treatment sampling to control for the significant inter-individual heterogeneity encountered in critical illness syndromes. Functional assays such as cytokine release, phagocytosis and NETosis of live cells at standardised time points post infection and treatment would be critical to capture the functional implications of disease and direct effects of the drugs in the clinical environment. Nesting of such intensive immunological studies within randomised trials for the next pandemic is challenging but may facilitate a more structured approach, leverage shared resources and utilise the unconfounded treatment assignment of randomisation to establish causality.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates remarkable similarities in the numbers and function of innate immune cells as well as plasma protein expression between very different clinical syndromes and suggests modulation of these responses is better targeted to individual phenotypes at a given time irrespective of clinical presentation.

Methods

Study ethics approval

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Melbourne Health Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/63982/MH-2020). The protocol allowed small volumes of blood (30 mL) to be collected from existing arterial or venous cannulas during routine care. Patients or their medical treatment decision maker could subsequently elect to destroy samples and delete all data. This approach is consistent with Victorian law66. Healthy volunteers provided written, informed consent prior to enrolment.

Sex as a biological variable

Both male and female patients were included in this study and the proportions of each sex in each cohort is reported in the ‘Results’.

Study design

The Functional Immunophenotyping in Critical Illness (FICI) study was a single-centre prospective observational cohort study. From August 2020 through to August 2022, patients with severe COVID-19 and major trauma were recruited from the Royal Melbourne Hospital (RMH) ICU. There are only two trauma referral centres in the state of Victoria (population approximately 6.3million), which allowed us to include a representative sample of patients following major trauma. Healthy controls were recruited from the staff and student bodies of RMH and the University of Melbourne.

Eligibility

Patients were eligible if they were 18 years or older, expected to remain in the ICU for more than 24 h and had an arterial or central venous cannula in situ or planned to be inserted for clinical reasons. The ‘healthy control’ group was comprised of age and sex appropriate healthy volunteers not previously known to be infected with COVID-19, that did not have chronic health conditions other than hypertension and were not required to take regular medication, except for antihypertensives.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for the COVID-19 group was admission to the ICU for monitoring or treatment of suspected COVID-19 infection. PCR test result was not required at time of enrolment but was used for subsequent confirmation. Inclusion criteria for trauma patients were being admitted to the ICU with a requirement for exogenous organ support. Trauma patients were screened negative for COVID-19 by clinical criteria or RAT/PCR test according to hospital protocols.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they were pregnant, had an active haematological malignancy, were HIV positive, had a known immunological abnormality or a history of use of immunosuppressive medication.

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical record (EPIC) after all patients had been enrolled. All data from the medical record was collected during routine clinical monitoring and care by bedside nurses and pathology team. Demographic data included date of birth, sex, smoking status, body mass index, number of COVID-19 vaccinations received and the date of the most recent vaccination and for COVID-19 patients, the self-reported date of symptom onset. Clinical data captured included sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score67 and drug dosages.

Blood collection and processing

Peripheral blood samples of 30 mL were collected in 9 mL heparinised vacutainers (BD, Franklin Lakes, USA) from existing arterial or central venous catheters on day 1 (< 24 h post ICU admission), day 3 (between 48 and 72 h post ICU admission) and day 5 (between 96 and 120 h post ICU admission). If blood could not be collected for day 3 and/or day 5 samples (if, for example, the patient was discharged from ICU, or the cannula was removed) the already collected samples were still utilised in the study, but no further samples were collected. Healthy control blood was collected at a single time point.

Blood was kept at room temperature (RT) and used within 4 h of collection for ex vivo phenotyping, activated phenotyping and phagocytosis assay. The remaining blood was centrifuged at 400 × g at RT for 15 min and plasma was transferred into 2 mL cryovials and stored in a −80 °C freezer. The buffy coat was diluted 1:1 with RPMI-1640 and centrifuged over LymphoprepTM (STEMCELLTM Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) for 20 min at RT at 400 × g. PBMCs were cryopreserved in 10% Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma Life Science, St. Louis, USA, Lot# RHBH9424) and 90% Foetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Bovogen Biologicals Pty Ltd., Keilor East, Australia).

Ex vivo phenotyping analysis

Within 4 h of collection, 400 µL of undiluted peripheral blood was stained with the flow cytometry panel in Supplementary Table 2. The anti-CD16, CD32 and CD64 mAbs were first added for 10 min (to both stain and block FcR), then the remainder of the antibody panel was added for 20 min at RT. RBC were lysed by addition of 1 mL of OptiLyse C (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, USA) for 10 min. To achieve complete RCB lysis we then added 2 mL of an NH4Cl solution for a further 10 min (8.34 g/L NH4Cl, 1 g/L NaHCO3, 0.037 g/L EDTA Tetra Sodium Salt dihydrate in distilled water, pH 7.3 ± 0.2; Doherty Institute Media Preparation Unit). Samples were washed twice in PBS by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min then resuspended in 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, USA) in PBS and stored at 4 °C. Samples were acquired on a 5-laser Aurora spectral cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) between 20 and 28 h after staining.

Cell stimulated marker assay

Within 4 h of collection, blood was diluted 1:1 with IMDM and 200 µL of blood/IMDM mixture transferred into 2 × wells in a 96-well flat-bottomed plate (Corning Inc., Corning, USA). One well was left unstimulated, and the second well was stimulated with S. aureus BioParticlesTM that were not conjugated to a fluorescent dye (blank BioParticles; InvitrogenTM, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). After 1 h incubation at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, assay wells were stained with the same flow cytometry panel as for ex vivo phenotyping (Supplementary Table 2). As per ex vivo analysis, the CD16, CD32 and CD64 mAbs were added first for 10 min before remaining panel added and the same RBC lysis, wash and resuspension protocol was followed. Samples were acquired on a 5-laser Aurora spectral cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) between 20 and 28 h after staining.

Phagocytosis assay

Blood processing and plate set up was identical to the CSM assay above. To induce and measure phagocytosis we used pHrodoTM Red S. aureus BioParticlesTM (pHrodo BioParticles; InvitrogenTM, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) which are beads coated with Staphylococcus aureus. The pHrodoRed dye emits a detectable fluorescence in the low pH environment of the phagolysosome, providing a readout for phagocytosis. To measure ROS production, we used dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). This reagent, when administered in conjunction with S. aureus BioParticlesTM, is also engulfed by phagocytic cells and is oxidised by ROS into a fluorescent compound. We added 15 µg/mL of pHrodoRed BioParticles and 30 µM of DHR, mixed by pipetting and incubated the sample for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5%. The staining and RBC lysis procedure was the same as detailed above for activated phenotyping assay. Data were recorded on a 5-laser BD Fortessa cytometer (355 nm UV, 405 nm Violet, 488 nm Blue, 561 nm Yellow-Green and 633 nm Red).

Flow cytometry

Both antibody panels (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) were combined into master mixes immediately before staining, all staining was done at RT. The CD16, CD32 and CD64 mAbs were combined into an anti-FcR mix and stained first for 10 min. The remaining mAb master mix included Monocyte TruStain buffer (BioLegend, 5 μL per test) and Brilliant Stain Buffer Plus (BD; 10 μL per test) and was added for another 20 min. Single colour controls used a combination of beads (for markers with low expression in blood) and blood. Daily QC was run on both Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) and Fortessa (BD Biosciences) cytometers by running SpectroFlo® or CS&T beads, respectively. For unmixing/compensating data, a full set of single colour controls was run monthly, or whenever a new vial of antibody was opened. Unstained, DHR and pHrodoTM Red controls were run on each sample. Spectral data were unmixed using SpectroFlo® software v3 (Cytek) and Fortessa data were compensated and analysed using FlowJo™ v10 (BD). Unmixed spectral data were analysed in FlowJo™ v10 using a pre-defined gating strategy (Supplementary Fig. 1). Data were excluded from analysis if RBC contamination could not be gated out from leukocyte populations. Cutoffs for cell population data being included in analysis was >50 events.

Cytometric bead array

Levels of cytokines in patient plasma were quantified using three BioLegend LEGENDplex™ (San Diego, USA) cytometric bead array (CBA) kits: Human Vascular Inflammation Panel 1 (13-plex #740552); Human Inflammation Panel 1 (13-plex #740809) and COVID-19 Cytokine Storm Panel 1 (14-plex #741089). Collectively these kits allowed for the detection of 34 unique soluble proteins (IL-1β, IFNα2, RANTES, IL-8, IFNγ, IL-2, IL-12p70, TNFα, IL-7, IL-17A, MCP-1, IL-1RA, IL-18, IL-6, CXCL8, IL-23, IL-10, CXCL10, IL-33, G-CSF, CCL3, Myoglobin, OPN, ICAM-1, MRP8/14, MPO, VCAM-1, NGAL, SAA, MMP-9, CRP, IGFBP-4, Cystatin C and MMP-2). CRP results were not included in analysis as they were above the dynamic quantifiable range of the assay. Some analytes overlapped between Human Inflammation Panel 1 and COVID-19 Cytokine Storm Panel 1 kits and data were only included from Human Inflammation Panel 1 kit. Plasma samples that had been stored at −80 °C were thawed and split across 4 × 96-well plates for each CBA kit. Plates contained a mix of plasma samples with both early and more recent collection time points from each cohort. This was done to reduce any potential batch effects of cohort or confounding effects of duration of storage in −80 °C freezer. CBA followed manufacturers’ V-bottom plate protocol, with the exception that volumes of 7 μL, instead of 25 μL, were used. Data were recorded on a 4-laser CytoFLEX S cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Sample preparation for proteomic analysis

Neat plasma was prepared using an on-bead digestion protocol68. Briefly, 2 μL of neat plasma biofluid underwent protein lysis in 2% SDS lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris (2-Carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) and 40 mM 2-chloracetamide (2-CAA). After, protein reduction/alkylation was performed for 10 min at 95 °C, on-bead precipitation using acetonitrile (ACN, 70% v/v) was performed in a protein Lo-bind deep-well plate (Eppendorf) for 20 minutes at RT on the ThermoMixer C (Eppendorf) shaking at 450 rpm. Samples were then placed on a magnetic rack, supernatants removed, and three 200 μL washes performed: twice with 70% ethanol, and one 100% ACN. After washing, trace solvent was evaporated using a CentriVap (Labconco), before addition of digestion buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) which contained Lys-C (Wako, 129-02541) and SOLu-Trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, EMS0004), both at a 1:25 enzyme-to-protein ratio. Digestion was performed for 1 h at 37 °C with shaking at 450 rpm. Digestion was halted by addition of 10% formic acid to a final concentration of 1%. Acidified digests were desalted using commercial C18 stage tips (AffiniSep), vacuum centrifuged to dryness, and peptides reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, 2% ACN in preparation for LC-MS/MS. Samples were randomised across plates, but all longitudinal samples from a patient were kept together on the same plate. Plate layout controls for batch and digest effects included four pooled samples (pooled patient plasma digested independently as four technical replicates), and 19 patient samples duplicated across the two plates used. All controls were processed independently. We did not detect any plate-based (batch) or digestion-related technical artefacts. A total of 151 unique samples were analysed including 4 pooled plasma samples and 147 patient samples (19 with n = 2 technical replicates and 128 with n = 1 replicates).

DIA-based proteomic analysis

Prepared purified peptides from each sample were re-suspended in Buffer A* (2% acetonitrile, 0.01% trifluoroacetic acid) and separated using a two-column chromatography setup composed of a PepMap100 C18 20-mm by 75 μm trap and a PepMap C18 500-mm by 75 μm analytical column (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were concentrated onto the trap column at 5 μL/min for 6 min with Buffer A (0.1% formic acid, 2% DMSO) and then infused into an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 300 nL/min via the analytical column using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were separated using a 66-min analytical gradient undertaken by altering the buffer composition from 3% Buffer B (0.1% formic acid, 77.9% acetonitrile, 2% DMSO) to 4.5% B over 0.5 min then from 4.5% B to 23% B over 51.5 min, then from 23% B to 40% B over 4 min, then from 40% B to 80% B over 1 min. The composition was held at 80% B for 2 min and then dropped to 3% B over 0.1 min before being held at 2% B for another 6.9 min. The Lumos mass spectrometer was operated in a data-independent manner automatically switching between the acquisition of a single Orbitrap MS scan (350–1100 m/z and a resolution of 60k) and 50 MS2 scans (NCE 33%, 30k resolution, an Automatic Gain Control of 800%, a mass range of 200–2000 m/z and a maximal injection time of 54 ms) of an isolation width of 13.7 m/z collected over the mass range of 361 to 1033 m/z. The proteomic dataset was searched using a DIA-library free approach using Spectronaut (version 18.5.231110) with Cross Run Normalisation enabled using median-based normalisation. Data files were searched against the human proteome (UP000005640) using a protease specificity of Trypsin allowing oxidation of Methionine as a variable modification and Carbamidomethyl set as a fixed modification of Cysteine. A minimum of two peptides were required with proteins identified from a single unique peptide excluded. Data were log2 transformed and missing values imputed based on the total observed protein intensities with a range of 0.3 σ and a downshift of 2.5 σ using Perseus (v1.4.0.6)69. The Spectronaut search was undertaken allowing a 1% protein level FDR. Both raw protein group (PG) quantities and iBAQ values were calculated and are provided in Supplementary Data file 1.

MOFA and machine learning models

We z-scaled data prior to MOFA integration. Outlier proteomics samples, with scaled abundances globally more than one standard deviation above the mean were removed. Flow cytometry median fluorescence intensities (MFI) from the phagocytosis assay were normalised by log fold change against lymphocyte MFI. For the CSM assay, MFI values were scaled from stimulated minus unstimulated condition MFI values. The three datasets were integrated using MOFA2 with default data, model, and training options13,14. The weight of the three assays on the model factors was visualised using the plot_variance_explained function. We determined contribution of variables to variance, using Pearson correlation of factor 1 vs numerical representations of cohort, patient, and time. Protein-factor plots were generated using the plot_data_scatter function.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as median ± IQR as specified in figure legends. Mann–Whitney multiple comparisons were conducted using the Holm–Šídák method when conducting between group analysis. Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparison tests were used to identify differences between clinical cohorts and healthy volunteers. Wilcoxon matched pairs ranked tests were used when measuring changes upon activation. All correlations were non-parametric Spearman rank correlations. Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.0) or GraphPad Prism 9.3.1 using a threshold p value of 0.05. All p-values are two-tailed. Exact p-values for all analyses are provided in the Source data files.
