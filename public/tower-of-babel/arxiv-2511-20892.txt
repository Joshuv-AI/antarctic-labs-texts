Representation Interventions Enable Lifelong Unstructured Knowledge Control
Xuyuan Liu, Zhengzhang Chen, Xinshuai Dong, Yanchi Liu, Xujiang Zhao, Shengyu Chen, Haoyu Wang, Yujun Yan, Haifeng Chen

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) often produce incorrect or outdated content. Updating their knowledge efficiently and accurately without costly retraining is a major challenge. This problem is especially hard for complex, unstructured knowledge in a lifelong setting, where many edits must coexist without interference. We introduce RILKE ( R epresentation I ntervention for L ifelong K nowledg E Control), a robust and scalable method that treats knowledge control as interventions within the model’s representation space. Leveraging representation-space expressiveness, we identify two properties enabling RILKE to deliver fine - grained control over complex, unstructured knowledge while maintaining general utility with frozen base weights. During training, RILKE learns paraphrase-robust and edit - localized modules that limit each update to a low-dimensional subspace to minimize cross-edit interference. At inference, a query-adaptive router selects the appropriate module to guide the model’s generation. In evaluation on knowledge editing benchmarks with LLaMA and Qwen models, RILKE are scalable to large-scale datasets, demonstrating high edit success, strong paraphrase generalization, and preserving general utility with modest memory overhead. These results show RILKE is an effective and scalable solution for lifelong knowledge control in LLMs.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) excel at knowledge-intensive NLP tasks
(
33
;
3
)
, yet their knowledge is static. Once deployed, they cannot adapt to evolving real-world information, often producing outdated or inaccurate content. While methods like full retraining or continual pretraining can update a model, they are computationally prohibitive and prone to catastrophic forgetting
(
20
;
2
)
. An alternative, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), injects new facts at inference time
(
5
)
but can conflict with the model’s parametric memory
(
21
;
14
)
and is sensitive to retrieval quality
(
32
)
. These challenges underscore the need for
lifelong model editing
: methods to precisely and efficiently update LLM knowledge with minimal side effects
(
34
)
.

However, existing editing techniques fall short in lifelong settings, especially when dealing with
unstructured
,
free-form
text that cannot be reduced to simple factual triplets (
i.e.
, Subject–Relation–Object)
(
9
)
. Parametric approaches, which directly modify model weights
(
25
;
11
)
, suffer from
edit collapse
: as edits accumulate, performance degrades until the model becomes unusable
(
41
;
29
)
. This degradation is particularly severe for unstructured knowledge. Similarly, external-memory methods, while helpful for accumulating knowledge, often lack the expressiveness to capture the nuances of complex, unstructured information with a single weight-space module
(
37
;
42
)
.

In contrast to modifying model weights, interventions in the representation space—the hidden states where information is processed—offer a powerful substrate for knowledge control due to their rich semantic structure, yet this space remains largely underexplored
(
10
;
40
)
. To our knowledge, the only related work attempting representation-level editing is limited to structured knowledge and supports only single, instantaneous edits
(
22
)
. It fails to leverage the representation space’s potential for complex, unstructured knowledge and cannot handle lifelong settings where edits must accumulate and coexist without interference.

In this work, we unlock the potential of representation-level editing for precise, paraphrase-robust, and lifelong knowledge control. We introduce
RILKE
(
R
epresentation
I
ntervention for
L
ifelong
K
nowledg
E
Control), a framework that controls LLM behavior by intervening in the model’s hidden representations. We first identify and validate two key geometric properties of these representations that enable localized and scalable editing. Building on this, we develop a robust training strategy to ensure edits generalize across paraphrases. For lifelong learning, we design a dynamic routing mechanism that activates the correct intervention at inference time, mitigating interference between edits. Finally, we introduce a shared-subspace intervention that clusters similar edits into a single module, enabling grouped control and significantly improving memory efficiency. Across diverse models and benchmarks,
RILKE
provides a stable, lightweight, and interpretable solution for the continual knowledge control in LLMs.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

We propose a robust training scheme that achieves precise, generalizable control through LLM representation-space interventions.

•

We propose a dynamic router that selectively activates relevant interventions while preserving unrelated knowledge and overall utility.

•

We develop a shared-subspace intervention strategy for memory-efficient, scalable control over large, growing sets of knowledge.

Preliminaries

This section introduces the notation used throughout the paper, along with the core concepts of LLMs and the Representation Fine-Tuning (ReFT).

Consider a token sequence
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, where each
x_{i}
is an element of a vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
. An LLM parameterized by
\theta
defines a joint probability over
\boldsymbol{x}
through an auto-regressive factorization:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{<i}\right)

where
p_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{<i}\right)
denotes the model’s predictive distribution over
\mathcal{V}
for the token at position
i
, conditioned on the prefix
\boldsymbol{x}_{<i}
. For an
L
-layer model, let
\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}
be the hidden representation at position
i
in layer
l
. The next token probability distribution is obtained by applying a softmax function to the linear projection of the hidden state of the final layer
\boldsymbol{h}^{L,i}
, using a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
:

\vskip-3.99994ptp_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{<i}\right)=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{h}^{L,i}\right).

To generate sentence
\boldsymbol{x}
, the LLM iteratively computes
p_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{<i}\right)
, samples a token
x_{i}
at position
i
, and appends it to the context for the next step. This process terminates upon generating a designated end-of-sequence token or when a predefined maximum length is reached.

Representation Fine-tuning(ReFT)

(
40
)
is a fine-tuning approach for LLMs that, instead of updating model weights, learns an
intervention module
to steer intermediate hidden states so they produce the target outputs, leaving the original weights unchanged. Building on the
linear representation hypothesis

(
27
;
30
)
that concepts are encoded in linear subspaces of the hidden space, ReFT specifies a low-dimensional intervention subspace at layer
l
via
\mathbf{R}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}(r\ll d)
, whose rows are constrained to be orthonormal. Given the original layer-
l
hidden state

\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i
-th token, ReFT first computes a subspace-local shift
(\mathbf{A}^{l}\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}+\mathbf{b}^{l}-\mathbf{R}^{l}\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i})
, maps it back to the original space with

\mathbf{R}^{l\top}
, and adds the result to initial representation
\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}
, yielding

\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i};\phi^{l})=\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}+\mathbf{R}^{l\top}(\mathbf{A}^{l}\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}+\mathbf{b}^{l}-\mathbf{R}^{l}\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}),

(1)

where
\phi^{l}=(\mathbf{R}^{l},\mathbf{A}^{l},\mathbf{b}^{l})
denotes the learnable parameters of this intervention module.

Computation in layers beyond
l
are identical to the base model: let
\mathbf{F}^{>l}
be the
mapping from layer
l+1

to the last layer before the output head, the next-token distribution is
p_{\theta}\left(x_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{<i}\right)=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{F}^{>l}(\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i};\phi^{l})\right))
.
For brevity, we will omit the token and layer indices
(i,l)
when the context is clear.

3
Motivation: Geometric Properties of LLM Representations

Lifelong knowledge control in LLMs requires incorporating extensive new
unstructured
knowledge while preserving existing capabilities. This poses three core challenges:
(i) unstructured complexity
, where edits must generalize across diverse paraphrases;
(ii) catastrophic forgetting
, where new updates must not interfere with existing knowledge; and
(iii) scalability
, where the system must manage a growing number of edits with low memory overhead. We posit that the key to overcoming these challenges lies not in the
weight space
, but in the model’s
representation space
, whose expressivity enables fine-grained control over long-form, unstructured knowledge, while its
geometric properties
guide effective control. We identify and validate two such properties that form the foundation of our method:

Property 1
.

For a query
\boldsymbol{x}
with layer-
l
representation
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
its paraphrase
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
maps, in the layer-
l
representation space, to a representation closer to
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
than those of semantically unrelated queries
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\|\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}<\|\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}

Property 2
.

Given two semantically related
query pairs
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
,
even when ReFT(Eq.(
1
)) is trained individually for each,
, their learned layer-
l
intervention subspaces remain aligned: i.e., the projection matrices

\mathbf{R}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}
and
\mathbf{R}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}
are highly similar.

Here, we use the hidden state of the last token (
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{h}^{l,i}
for a sentence
\boldsymbol{x}
of length
i
) to represent the entire query, which is believed to encode the semantic information of the entire query
45
;
23
. Prop.
1
offers a direct solution to
unstructured complexity
via paraphrase-robust training and addresses
catastrophic forgetting
by routing queries to disentangled knowledge modules. For each edit, we train an item-specific representation intervention and leverage representation geometry to route incoming queries to the appropriate module. To enhance robustness, we supervise over a semantics-stable neighborhood around the hidden state during training, ensuring that lexically varied but semantically equivalent queries activate the same updated knowledge. Prop.
2
sheds light on
scalability
by linking semantics to the learned intervention weights: related knowledge concentrates in a shared low-dimensional subspace. A single module can thus identify this subspace and efficiently store and apply multiple edits.

We validate these properties empirically. As shown in Fig.
1a
, we observe a clear separation in the L2-distance distributions between hidden states of query pairs and randomly paired queries at a fixed layer(layer 15 of
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
), confirming Prop.
1
. For Prop.
2
, we investigate how representation similarity relates to the alignment of their learned intervention subspaces by comparing layer-
l
representation similarity with the cosine similarity of their projection matrices
\mathbf{R}^{l}
(see Eq.(
1
)). As seen in Fig.
1b
, more similar representations yield more strongly aligned intervention subspaces, suggesting that semantically related knowledge can be controlled via grouped interventions.

Figure 2:
Overview of the
RILKE
framework. During training, the intervention module
\Phi
maps
\boldsymbol{h}_{\text{ori}}
to the target
\boldsymbol{h}_{\text{edit}}
, while
\boldsymbol{h}_{\text{ori}}
is stored as the knowledge index. At inference, the router selects the intervention module
\Phi
whose associated index is closest to the input query’s representation, enabling the model to generate the desired output.

4
Method: Towards Generalizable, Scalable, Lifelong Knowledge Control

To address the challenges of
generalizability
,
lifelong learning
, and
scalability
in knowledge editing, we introduce
RILKE
(
R
epresentation
I
ntervention for
L
ifelong
K
nowl
E
dge Control).
RILKE
is a framework designed to achieve robust and life-long control over an LLM’s knowledge. During training, it isolates updates by assigning a dedicated intervention module to each knowledge item, which prevents catastrophic forgetting. At inference, a query-adaptive router activates the appropriate module, ensuring the intervention is applied only when relevant. To scale this approach, we further introduce a batched training scheme that clusters semantically related edits to share a single intervention module, improving memory efficiency.

4.1
Consistency-Robust Training for Generalizable Knowledge Control

Knowledge-editing methods often suffer from poor
generalizability
: an edit works for a specific query but fails on its paraphrases. Consider an unstructured knowledge item
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}
is the input query and
\boldsymbol{y}
the target response. Vanilla ReFT
(
40
)
, for instance, exhibits this limitation because its training process learns an intervention
\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
to be activated only by the exact representation
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
of the original query
\boldsymbol{x}
. Consequently, a paraphrased query
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, whose representation
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is nearby but not identical, may fail to trigger the edit.

Recalling Prop.
1
and Eq.(
1
), the layer-
l
representation of any paraphrased query
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
lies in an
\varepsilon
-ball around
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
i.e.
,

\|\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

We therefore impose an equivalent intervention target throughout this neighborhood. Incorporating the deviation into the ReFT map, a first-order expansion yields

\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\varepsilon;\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}};\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})+{\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l\top}(\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l}-\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l})\big)}\varepsilon

so the paraphrase-induced variation is governed by the term
\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l\top}(\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l}-\mathbf{R}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l})\big)\varepsilon
.
To mitigate this effect, we enforce consistency of the final vocabulary distribution within the ball. We can see the edited predictive distribution for initial representation
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is:

p_{\theta,\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x})=\operatorname{softmax}\!\big(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{F}^{>l}(\,\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}};\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}))\big).

Then, we draw
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{Q}
(
e.g.
,
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
) on
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and form a perturbed branch:

p_{\theta,\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x})=\operatorname{softmax}\!\big(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{F}^{>l}(\,\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\varepsilon;\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}))\big).

We penalize the discrepancy between the two distributions using the KL divergence:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{robu}}(\boldsymbol{x};\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta,\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x})\big\|p_{\theta,\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x})\right)

We incorporate this robustness term into the standard language modeling objective, which minimizes cross-entropy loss in a teacher-forcing manner, as in vanilla ReFT
(
40
)
. The general objective for updating the knowledge item
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
with a single-knowledge intervention module is, therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{|\boldsymbol{y}|}\log p_{\theta,\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\!\left(y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{<i}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{robu}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{Q}}\!\big[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{robu}}(\boldsymbol{x};\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\big],

where regularization promotes the generalization of edited knowledge to paraphrased queries.

4.2
Query-Adaptive Routing for Lifelong Knowledge Control

To mitigate
catastrophic forgetting
, we keep the base model frozen and, for each unstructured knowledge instance
\boldsymbol{x}
, train a dedicated intervention module
\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
using the aforementioned method. At inference, however, the model must select and apply the appropriate intervention to ensure that each prompt elicits the relevant updated knowledge, which introduces the additional challenge of accurately routing each query to its correct module.

To achieve precise control during inference, leveraging Prop.
1
, we introduce a router that selects the appropriate intervention for incoming queries.
We construct an index set
\{\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{m}

for the edit dataset with
m
samples, using the layer-
l
representations of each training example on the original model. This is because these representations remain unaffected by the training process of intervention modules, which operate only beyond layer-
l
. Each index is then linked to its trained intervention
\{\phi^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
. At inference, given a query
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
with key
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, the router
\pi(\cdot)
assigns the incoming query to the indexed representation with minimal
l_{2}
distance and returns its associated intervention:

\vskip-1.99997pt\pi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},1\leq j\leq m}\;\left\|\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

RILKE
then applies
\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}};\phi^{l}_{\pi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})})
to enact the targeted edit during generation, while all other interventions remain inactive.

Importantly, with this strategy, each training query is deterministically assigned to its corresponding intervention module, as its key exactly matches the targeted index. For unseen queries (
e.g.
, paraphrased queries), we observe that most are still routed to the correct module even after large-scale editing, further supporting Prop.
1
.

4.3
Shared Subspace Intervention for Memory-efficient Management

Lifelong settings require scalable editing of massive knowledge bases. While assigning a dedicated intervention module to each knowledge instance affords fine-grained control, this approach scales poorly, incurring a memory cost that grows linearly with the number of edits and creating a significant bottleneck during training. To address this scalability challenge, we build on the insight from Prop.
2
, which establishes that semantically related edits can share a common intervention subspace. We therefore propose a memory-efficient alternative: cluster similar knowledge instances and train a single shared intervention module for each cluster.

We partition knowledge instances into semantically homogeneous, size-bounded groups to ensure that a single intervention subspace can serve each group effectively. Concretely, we impose two constraints on every cluster: (i) a within-cluster similarity low-bound
\tau_{\mathrm{sim}}\in(0,1)
(or equivalently, a merge threshold
d_{\mathrm{thr}}=1-\tau_{\mathrm{sim}}
), and (ii) a maximum cluster size
s_{\max}
. Let
m
denote the number of knowledge items and collect their layer-
l
representations into
\mathbf{H}=\big[\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}\,\,\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}\,\,\cdots\,\,\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{m}}\big]
. We run hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) over the columns of
\mathbf{H}
using an initial similarity low-bound
\tau_{\min}
to obtain provisional clusters. Any cluster exceeding
s_{\max}
is then recursively refined by increasing the similarity floor and re-running HAC within that cluster. The procedure terminates when all clusters satisfy
|\mathcal{C}_{c}|\leq s_{\max}
, yielding
k
clusters
\{\mathcal{C}_{c}\}_{c=1}^{k}
that are both semantically coherent and size
-
controlled (see details in Algo.
A.2
).

Given the clustered knowledge, we train, for each cluster
\mathcal{C}_{c}
, a cluster-shared intervention module using all
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
under the objective in Sec.
4.1
, yielding

\phi^{l}_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}=\big(\mathbf{R}^{l}_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},\,\mathbf{A}^{l}_{\mathcal{C}_{i}},\,\mathbf{b}^{l}_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}\big).

Let
\kappa(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
be the mapping from

\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
to its corresponding cluster
\mathcal{C}_{i}
(
i.e.
,
\kappa:\{\boldsymbol{x}\}\to\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k}\},\;\kappa(\boldsymbol{x_{j}})=\mathcal{C}_{i}
such that
\boldsymbol{x_{j}}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
).
Then, at inference, for query
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
with key
\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, router
\hat{\pi}(\cdot)
map it to the corresponding intervention by identifying the cluster to which the closest knowledge item belongs:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})\;=\;\kappa(\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},1\leq j\leq m}\left\|\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}-\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\right\|_{2}^{2}).

Finally, the corresponding cluster intervention

\Phi(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}};\phi^{l}_{{\hat{\pi}(\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})}})
is applied
for the target edit.

Taken together, these strategies yield
RILKE
, which unifies: (i) robust representation interventions for precise, paraphrase-generalizable knowledge control; (ii) an adaptive inference-time router that activates the appropriate module; and (iii) cluster-level interventions that manage semantically similar knowledge for memory-efficient scalability —collectively enabling lifelong control of unstructured knowledge in LLMs.

6
Analysis: Can Shared Subspace really control Similar Knowledge

Building on Prop.
2
, which posits that semantically similar edits inhabit a shared low-dimensional intervention subspace, we examine how joint training couples individual edits. We randomly sample knowledge item and train
RILKE
under three settings:
(i) Individual
: each sampled item has its own adapter;
(ii) Dissimilar Batched
: items are randomly assigned to groups and trained jointly with a single adapter per group, simulating training with unrelated knowledge; and
(iii) Similar Batched
: each sampled item is batched with its co-clustered items—clusters formed from the full dataset—and trained using a cluster
-
shared adapter to simulate training with similar knowledge. Importantly, in setting (iii), co-cluster batches may include items from the same cluster that were not part of the original training sample; these items are used solely to construct cluster-level training batches. Evaluation is restricted to the originally sampled items, ensuring a consistent comparison set. This design enables a clean comparison of the effects of joint training with dissimilar items (ii) versus highly similar items (iii).

We compare, for each item, the distances of the
edit vector

\mathbf{V}_{\text{edit}}
in settings (ii)/(iii) relative to (i). Concretely, recalling Eq.(
1
), we refer to the intervention applied in the target subspace as
edited vector
, say
\mathbf{V}_{\text{edit},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}=\mathbf{R}^{l\top}(\mathbf{A}^{l}\boldsymbol{h}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}+\mathbf{b}^{l}-\mathbf{R}^{l}\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{l})
for
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. Since
\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{l}
is fixed across regimes for
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, differences in the resulting edit vector reflect only the learned intervention in (ii)/(iii) relative to (i), isolating the effect of the training strategy.

In this setup, we find training with similar items (setting (iii)) yields edit vectors
\mathbf{V}_{\text{edit},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\text{(iii)}}
that are consistently closer to their individually trained counterparts
\mathbf{V}_{\text{edit},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\text{(i)}}
than those obtained by training with dissimilar items (setting (ii)) in 91 of 100 sampled cases. A PCA visualization of the edited vectors
\{\mathbf{V}_{\text{edit},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\}
in Fig.
4
also illustrates this trend: vectors from (iii) remain close to those from (i), whereas (ii) shifts away.

Figure 4:
Visualization of
{\mathbf{V}_{\text{edit}}}
under different training settings. Training with similar data keeps the edited vectors close to the individual training, whereas dissimilar batching drives them away, underscoring the necessity of clustering similar knowledge for effective shared-subspace control.

These results highlight the effect of
RILKE
in mitigating interference and preserving edit specificity by clustering semantically similar knowledge before applying shared-subspace intervention.

Related Work

LLM Representations Space Analysis.

Prior studies has demonstrated that LLMs encode rich semantics within their activation space
(
45
;
24
;
35
)
. Core behaviors, including trustworthiness
(
24
)
, refusal
(
1
)
, and reasoning
(
23
;
7
;
4
)
, have been linked to specific components of the representation space. Several methods have been proposed to leverage these components:
15
manipulates generation style via style vectors;
6
discovers persona vectors for customized control; and
40
introduces fine-tuning directly within the representation space to enable style modulation.

Knowledge Edit.

Existing model editing methods either modify a small subset of parameters or incorporate external memory while keeping the original weights intact. Parametric approaches directly update model weights to encode new knowledge. Meta-learning methods
(
28
;
43
)
employ hypernetworks to predict parameter updates, while locate-then-edit techniques
(
25
;
26
)
identify and modify neurons responsible for the target knowledge.
11
projects updates onto the null space of preserved knowledge to mitigate interference. In contrast, external memory approaches preserve the original weights by introducing additional components:
16
;
36
;
8
retrieve discrete codebook entries to overwrite intermediate activations for localized edits, while
42
enables simultaneous editing and unlearning via shared memory modules.

Unstructured Knowledge Edit.

Recent work extends knowledge editing from structured triples to unstructured free-form knowledge.
39
observes limitations in prior evaluation protocols and proposes a new benchmark.
9
enhances locate-then-edit paradigms to update parameters across layers, improving their efficacy on unstructured text.
19
introduce chunk-based autoregressive editing to enable edit long knowledge. While these methods address the challenges of unstructured knowledge, they face scalability issues: performance often degrades after repeated edits, and general usability diminishes as more knowledge is updated.

Conclusion

In this work, we advance knowledge editing in LLMs from a representation-centric perspective.
We begin by identifying two key properties—
generalizability
and
locality
—that underpin robust and targeted interventions.
Building on these principles, we introduce
RILKE
, a framework that enables precise, interpretable, and lifelong knowledge control through representation-space interventions.
To improve scalability, we further propose a shared-subspace strategy that clusters semantically related knowledge, enabling batched updates via a single adapter.
Experimental results demonstrate the reliability, scalability, and memory efficiency of
RILKE
for lifelong knowledge control in LLMs.
