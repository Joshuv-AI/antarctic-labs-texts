STRUCTSENSE: A TASK-AGNOSTIC AGENTIC FRAMEWORK for STRUCTURED INFORMATION EXTRACTION with HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP EVALUATION and BENCHMARKING
Tek Raj Chhetri, Yibei Chen, Puja Trivedi, Dorota Jarecka, Saif Haobsh, Patrick Ray, Lydia Ng, Satrajit S. Ghosh

Abstract
The ability to extract structured information from unstructured sources—such as free-text documents and scientific literature—is critical for accelerating scientific discovery and knowledge synthesis. Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in various natural language processing tasks, including structured information extraction. However, their effectiveness often diminishes in specialized, domain-specific contexts that require nuanced understanding and expert-level domain knowledge. In addition, existing LLM-based approaches frequently exhibit poor transferability across tasks and domains, limiting their scalability and adaptability. To address these challenges, we introduce StructSense , a modular, task-agnostic, open-source framework for structured information extraction built on LLMs. StructSense is guided by domain-specific symbolic knowledge encoded in ontologies, enabling it to navigate complex domain content more effectively. It further incorporates agentic capabilities through self-evaluative judges that form a feedback loop for iterative refinement, and includes human-in-the-loop mechanisms to ensure quality and validation. We demonstrate that StructSense can overcome both the limitations of domain sensitivity and the lack of cross-task generalizability, as shown through its application to diverse neuroscience information extraction tasks.

Related Works

This section provides an overview of related work, which is categorized into three main groups: ontology and rule-based approaches, machine learning (ML)-based approaches, and large language model (LLM)-based approaches. This categorization is intended to highlight the range of methodologies employed in the literature. The ML-based category, as used here, encompasses both traditional ML and deep learning (DL) approaches, excluding LLMs. LLM-based studies are considered separately from the conventional ML and DL approaches due to the paradigm shift they have introduced in language processing and their versatile general-purpose capabilities.

2.1
Ontology & rule-based based information extraction

Zaman et al.
[
26
]
proposed an ontology-based IE framework for automated content and metadata extraction from scientific documents. Their approach combines standard regular expressions with fuzzy rule-based regular expressions for SIE and employs Word2Vec for word sense disambiguation. To enhance extraction accuracy and ease of extraction, the system first converts input PDF (Portable Document Format) documents into XML (Extensible Markup Language), plain text, and document (DOC/DOCX) formats, allowing each to contribute differently to the extraction process. The extracted information is then transformed into RDF
9
9

9

https://www.w3.org/RDF
triples using a custom ontology that models key metadata elements such as authorship and titles. However, two main limitations are evident in their approach. First, the scope of the ontology is narrow and lacks integration with existing broader ontologies or metadata schemas. Second, while the rule-based extraction method can be effective for well-structured formats, it lacks the robustness and flexibility of modern data-driven approaches—such as DL and transformer-based models (e.g., LLMs)—which are better equipped to handle unstructured formats, diverse linguistic expressions, and inconsistencies commonly found in real-world documents.

Similarly, Schröder et al.
[
10
]
proposed a structured, rule-based approach to automatically extract and generate semantic provenance documentation from Electronic Lab Notebooks (ELN) in the biomedical domain, aligned with the FAIR principles. ELN records typically document wet-lab protocols. The study focuses on capturing comprehensive retrospective provenance information–including parameter settings and experimental assumptions–to answer the provenance questions W7:
What, When, Where, How, Who, Which,
and
Why
. Their approach exploits the inherent structure of ELN documents (e.g., headings) and applies regular expressions to extract key information, such as chemical and biological entities, by identifying common textual patterns. The extracted data are then represented as knowledge graphs using semantic models such as PROV-O
10
10

10

https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o
. Although the authors successfully processed seven real-world ELN protocols and enabled complex SPARQL
11
11

11

https://www.w3.org/TR/sparql11-query
queries to retrieve detailed provenance information, such as who conducted the experiment and in what sequence, their rule-based approach lacks flexibility in handling linguistic variability and adapting to new or unseen protocol structures—limitations that ML-based methods, particularly those leveraging LLMs, are better equipped to address.

Gutierrez et al.
[
27
]
extended ontology-based components for the IE (OBCIE) architecture by presenting two orthogonal extensions, which are (i) a hybrid approach that incorporates extraction rules (e.g., regular expressions) and ML (e.g., CRF)to improve extraction accuracy and (ii) ontology-based error detection to identify and correct inconsistencies. Unlike previous approaches, their framework supports the flexible integration of various ontologies, including those from third-party sources such as BioPortal
12
12

12

https://bioportal.bioontology.org
, thus addressing a key limitation of earlier ontology-based systems. Although the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their proposed extensions, they also acknowledge areas for improvement, particularly the need to refine information extractors to minimize extraction errors. Furthermore, a notable limitation of their study is that the evaluation was performed on a synthetic dataset derived from student responses, rather than on unstructured, real-world text. As such, the generalizability and performance of the system in open-domain settings remain to be validated. Similarly, Liu and El-Gohary
[
28
]
proposed an ontology-based IE approach combined with a semi-supervised CRF method to address the limitations of supervised learning, particularly the need for large amounts of labeled data. Their approach leverages the BridgeOnto ontology, which enriches the IE process with domain-specific semantics related to maintenance activities and deficiencies described in bridge inspection reports. Although their method effectively improves entity extraction, the authors acknowledge a key limitation in reliably extracting relations between entities, which LLMs are good at and that this study utilizes.

2.2
ML-based information extraction

Nayaka & Rajeev
[
29
]
proposes an ensemble approach combining CNN (convolutional neural network) and BiLSTM (Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory) models to extract scholarly metadata, demonstrating superior performance compared to traditional algorithms such as decision trees and support vector machines (SVM). While their method addresses several limitations of rule-based approaches, it still has notable constraints. Specifically, it relies heavily on layout and formatting cues (e.g., font-based features), which may limit its robustness across differently structured documents. Additionally, unlike general-purpose LLMs, their approach lacks inherent generalizability and requires domain-specific training data, reducing its adaptability to new or unseen document types.

Andruszkiewicz and Rybinski
[
30
]
proposed a hybrid approach for extracting information about scientific publications—including journals and conferences—and linking them to researchers. Their method combines rule-based techniques (e.g., regular expressions) with classical machine learning models such as Conditional Random Fields (CRFs) and SVM to build and enrich a scientific knowledge base. To enhance data quality, the system also incorporates a crowdsourcing component for user-driven verification and correction. The authors acknowledge key limitations in their work, particularly the narrow scope of entity types considered and improving the models used for extraction.

Zhang et al.
[
31
]
proposed an end-to-end framework for information extraction in document understanding, eliminating the need for traditional staged pipelines. Their model jointly performs OCR and entity extraction using a multistage neural architecture that integrates LSTM, BiLSTM, transformer-style attention mechanisms, and multimodal fusion. The system extracts entity information directly from document images by leveraging both visual and textual context.

2.3
LLM-based information extraction

Agrawal et al.
[
11
]
presented a study demonstrating the effectiveness of a few-shot learning approach for a wide range of clinical IE tasks, including abbreviation disambiguation, coreference resolution, medication extraction, and biomedical evidence extraction, using guided instructions. They also introduced three newly annotated datasets to support benchmarking efforts. While their method demonstrated improved performance, Agrawal et al. also noted a key limitation: the challenge of guiding LLMs to adhere to an exact schema. This highlights the potential benefit of integrating external tools, a capability that agent-based systems possess, which this study follows.

Li et al.
[
4
]
conducted a study on scientific discourse tagging, where they annotated sentences or clauses in biomedical papers with rhetorical roles such as goal, fact, hypothesis, method, and implication. Their approach combined SciBERT embeddings for contextualized word representations, an LSTM-based attention mechanism for encoding word-to-sentence relationships, and a BiLSTM-CRF sequence tagger for classification. They demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in two benchmark datasets—SciDT
[
32
]
and PubMed-20k-RCT
[
33
]
—and demonstrated the utility of their model in downstream tasks such as claim extraction and fragment detection of evidence. Although their work represents a significant advance in scientific information extraction and understanding, its effectiveness in domain-specific contexts, particularly in capturing the depth of knowledge and ensuring alignment with curated domain ontologies, remains an open area for further research.

Wei et al.
[
34
]
conducted work on scientific claim extraction, framing the task as a binary classification problem—classifying sentences as either a claim or a non-claim. Unlike Li et al.’s work, where claim extraction is treated as a downstream application of scientific discourse tagging, Wei et al. focus directly on claim extraction as the primary objective of their study. To address the challenges of limited annotated data and the limitations of self-supervised contrastive learning (which often ignores feature correlations within the same class), they introduced supervised contrastive learning as their core approach. Their models—CNN-1D, USE-Dense, and WC-BiLSTM—were trained using datasets such as PubMed-RCT (for transfer learning pretraining), SciARK
[
35
]
, and their in-house SciCE dataset. While their work demonstrated the superiority of supervised contrastive learning, their focus remains on binary claim detection.
Similarly, studies such as

2.4
Summary

In summary, the review of related work demonstrates the evolution of IE techniques, from ontology-based and ML/DL approaches to recent advancements using LLMs. While ontology-based methods offer strong domain semantics and align with FAIR principles, they lack the adaptability of ML/DL; conventional ML/DL models provide learning capabilities but often require large annotated datasets. Hybrid approaches have emerged to combine these strengths but still lack the robustness of LLMs. We observe an increasing use of LLMs in IE because their capability has facilitated end-to-end extraction. However, current LLM-based IE systems face key limitations: they often lack integration with domain knowledge, which hampers accuracy in specialized fields; they exhibit limited task generality, making them difficult to adapt across diverse IE scenarios
[
38
]
without fine-tuning; and they typically do not leverage agentic capabilities, reducing their potential for iterative reasoning, goal-directed behavior, and human-in-the-loop (HIL) collaboration. This study addresses these gaps by introducing a task-agnostic agentic framework that draws on the strengths of LLMs while extending their functionality through the integration of specialized tools and ontological knowledge. The proposed approach aims to enable more accurate, adaptable, and semantically enriched information extraction.

StructSense

Figure
1
shows the architecture of
StructSense
, a task-agnostic multi-agent framework for structured information extraction that orchestrates the extraction, evaluation, and refinement of structured data from unstructured sources.
StructSense
is designed with a modular architecture, decomposing the system into specialized agents based on distinct tasks. It consists of four different specialized agents, namely
(i) extractor agent; (ii) alignment agent; (iii) judge agent; and (iv) feedback agent
. This modularity improves reusability, simplifies system maintenance, and facilitates the management of complex information extraction workflows.

Figure 1:
Architecture of the
StructSense

The extractor agent is responsible for converting unstructured raw text into structured information. It processes raw textual input and outputs structured data in JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) format. The content and structure of the generated JSON output vary depending on the specific task configuration. In addition to the raw text, the extractor, like other agents, also receives agent and task configurations as input. The agent configuration defines parameters such as the choice of LLMs and associated API keys. The task configuration specifies the task’s objectives, input/output formats, and any applicable constraints or instructions to guide the agent’s behavior.

The alignment agent, the second component of the system, takes as input both the structured output of the extractor agent and the knowledge curated from an external source - specifically, the ontology database illustrated in Figure
1
. The alignment agent performs the task of mapping (i.e., aligning) the extracted entities to their canonical counterparts in the ontology or KGs (or concept alignment). This agent is critical because it provides the curated and structured domain knowledge that LLM lacks
[
39
,
40
,
41
]
. Most importantly, the alignment agent provides knowledge grounding, which helps mitigate the ambiguities. For example,
the word cortex is polysemous (see Table
1
): in neuroscience, it denotes a brain region, while in plant biology it describes an outer cell layer
. Without ontological knowledge grounding, an LLM can hallucinate and select the wrong sense. The alignment agent with access to the structured curated knowledge links each occurrence to the appropriate ontology term, ensuring accurate, context-aware outputs (see Table
1
). Additionally, with access to new domain knowledge, it minimizes the need to pretrain or fine-tune LLMs for every new domain-specific knowledge update. In addition, it mitigates the inefficiencies that may be caused by models having to relearn existing knowledge
[
40
]
.

The third component of
StructSense
is the judge agent, which serves as a critic or LLM-as-a-judge. It evaluates the output produced by the alignment agent, specifically, the structured information extracted from text and the corresponding aligned ontological concepts. The design of the judge agent is informed by prior state-of-the-art
[
42
,
43
,
44
]

approaches, which demonstrate that incorporating self-evaluation mechanisms into agent workflows or LLM outputs can enhance overall performance. The judge agent assigns a confidence score ranging from 0 to 1, where 1 denotes a perfect match (100%). This approach of prompting LLMs to provide a score or confidence estimate is based on the prior studies and established benchmarks that adopt similar methodologies
[
45
]
.

The fourth component of
StructSense
is the feedback agent, which integrates the HIL component to address the limitations of LLMs (or LLM-based applications), which, despite their impressive capabilities, are prone to hallucinations, producing content that appears plausible but lacks factual background
[
46
]
. This component builds on recent state-of-the-art findings demonstrating that human oversight enhances the reliability and trustworthiness of LLM-generated outputs by mitigating such hallucinations within LLM or LLM-based agentic systems
[
47
,
48
]
.

The final two critical components are the ontology database and the memory. The ontology database stores curated domain knowledge in the form of neuroscience-specific ontologies, serving as the foundation for structured and semantically relevant alignments. Memory, on the other hand, retains the execution context and is passed across agents, enabling each agent to remain aware of operations of other agents and maintain contextual continuity throughout the processing pipeline.

5.1
Case Study: Neuroscience Domain

As a case study to evaluate the
StructSense
, we have selected the neuroscience domain due to its inherent complexity and interdisciplinary nature. Neuroscience involves diverse data modalities, terminologies, and methodological approaches, making it an ideal testbed for structured information extraction. Our choice is further motivated by the requirements of the ongoing projects—specifically, the Brain Behavior Quantification and Synchronization (BBQS)
19
19

19

https://brain-bbqs.org
project, ReproNim
20
20

20

https://www.repronim.org/index.html
project and the BICAN
21
21

21

https://www.portal.brain-bican.org
—which require structured information extraction and aligning domain-specific knowledge.

We selected three representative tasks: (i)Schema-based extraction for clinical and psychological assessments from the ReproNim project;(ii) metadata and resource extraction from the BBQS project; and (iii) named entity recognition (NER) term extraction from the BICAN project. These tasks span a range of structured information extraction challenges, allowing us to evaluate the generalizability and adaptability of
StructSense
across diverse use cases.

Task 1: Schema-based extraction from assessment instruments

This task evaluates
StructSense
’s ability to convert semi-structured assessment instruments—often stored in PDF or proprietary formats—into machine-readable metadata aligned with ReproSchema
[
49
]
, a community standard for describing cognitive, behavioral, and clinical assessments. The task addresses three core needs: (i) advancing FAIR principles in assessment data, (ii) managing the semantic complexity of items, scales, and branching logic, and (iii) reducing the manual burden of digitizing validated instruments.
StructSense
must extract multi-level metadata, including instrument-level descriptors, item properties (e.g., prompts, response options, scoring rules), and behavioral logic (e.g., conditional flows). Success in this task enables scalable integration of standardized instruments into reproducible research workflows.

Task 2: Metadata and resource extraction

A fundamental step when starting new research involves identifying relevant resources such as models, datasets, and computational tools. This process is often labor-intensive and time-consuming. Automating the extraction and organization of such information can not only streamline the research workflow but also facilitate a deeper understanding of resource usage patterns, modeling paradigms, and the scientific evolution of the field–reason for selecting this task. This task is concerned with extracting structured metadata and resource-related information, including the type of resource (e.g., model, dataset, tool), the category or application domain (e.g., pose estimation), the target entity (e.g., human, animal), a descriptive summary, the resource name, and the corresponding access URL (e.g., GitHub, Hugging Face).

Task 3: NER term extraction

This task evaluates
StructSense
’s ability to extract NER terms from neuroscience, such as brain regions, cognitive constructs, and experimental methods, from full-text neuroscience articles. This task was selected for the case study based on two key considerations: (i) the inherent complexity of extracting domain-specific terminology in a multidisciplinary field such as neuroscience, which requires nuanced semantic understanding; and (ii) the limited availability of curated NER datasets within the neuroscience domain, particularly in contrast to the more extensively resourced biomedical domain. As such, this task serves as a foundational step for assessing the agent’s capabilities and guiding the development of future benchmark datasets.

5.3
Evaluation criteria

All results across the three tasks were evaluated under two settings: (i) with Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) and (ii) without HIL. In HIL mode, human oversight is incorporated, allowing users to provide feedback, such as correcting the agent’s output or offering guidance in natural language, prior to generating the final result. In contrast, the non-HIL mode operates autonomously without human intervention. Below we provide the detailed evaluation criteria applied to each task selected from our case study.

Due to the varying nature of the tasks, we adopt different evaluation strategies. Tasks 1 and 2 involve relatively well-defined outputs and allow objective evaluation using precision, recall, and F1 score. Task 3, in contrast, involves NER from complex scientific texts, where ground truth is less clearly defined and entities are highly context-dependent. As such, we combine quantitative and qualitative assessments to compare model performance for this task.

Task 2: Metadata and resource extraction

As in the previous tasks, we evaluated the metadata and resource extraction output using AIProofBuddy. Specifically, we evaluated the precision of key resource attributes: (i) name; (ii) type; (iii) category; (iv) description; (v) target; (vi) specific targets; (vii) URL (or resource URL); (viii) mentions of models; (ix) mentions of datasets; (x) mentions of benchmarks; (xi) mentions of tools; and (xii) the source paper. We distinguish between target and specific target, where target refers to a high-level category (e.g., animal) and specific target denotes narrower concepts within that category (e.g., mice, fish). Our evaluation approach excludes stop words and prioritizes semantic diversity, in accordance with established practices in benchmark creation, such as those used in SQuAD
[
50

. This consideration is critical given the inherent variability in human annotations. For example, extracted phrases such as “fish schooling dataset”, “fish dataset”, and “fish schooling behavior dataset” are all treated as correct, as they convey semantically equivalent information. Additionally, we evaluate the
ontology alignment
for both target and specific target fields to ensure consistent semantic mapping. Specifically, we assess whether the extracted information aligns exactly with the expected ontology identifier (prefixed or full IRI), ontology label, and ontology name—each representing key semantic attributes of interest. We employ quantitative evaluation metrics, including
precision
,
recall
, and
F1 score
, as well as the
ontology concept alignment rate (or concept alignment rate)

Evaluation metrics summary

To compare the performance of different language models used within
StructSense
, we employ task-specific metrics that reflect the nature of each task. For Tasks 1 and 2, we compute standard metrics—
precision
,
recall
, and
F1 score
—based on structured annotations made in the AIProofBuddy interface. Annotators label each extracted item as
correct
,
incorrect
, or
missing
, which we use to derive:

•

True Positives (TP):
Correctly extracted items.

•

False Positives (FP):
Incorrect or spurious extractions.

•

False Negatives (FN):
Items that should have been extracted but were missed.

From these, we compute:

Precision

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}

(2)

Recall

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

(3)

F1 Score

\displaystyle=2\times\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(4)

We report micro-averaged metrics, where counts are aggregated across all documents within a task. This ensures proportional contribution of each extracted item to the final score.

By tailoring our evaluation criteria to each task, we preserve fairness, task-relevance, and comparability across models, consistent with the aims of
StructSense
as a flexible and generalizable evaluation framework for structured document understanding.

7
Discussion

This study evaluated the structured extraction capabilities of LLMs across three tasks of increasing semantic openness, using both autonomous and Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) workflows. We organize the discussion along three dimensions: task type, model behavior, and HIL influence. We further consider trade-offs in cost and speed, confidence calibration, and semantic fidelity. Together, these analyses reveal how model performance varies with task structure, how HIL affects output quality, and where current models fall short in structured scientific extraction.

7.1
Task Type and Structural Constraints

Task design plays a central role in model performance. Task 1—schema-based extraction—is highly deterministic, with a fixed and fully specified target structure. In contrast, Task 2 (metadata and resource extraction) introduces moderate variability due to ontology alignment, and Task 3 (NER term extraction) requires open-ended interpretation with context-dependent labeling.

Model performance followed this gradient. All models performed best on Task 1, with Claude-Sonnet 3.7 and GPT-4o-mini achieving near-perfect recall and precision under both HIL and non-HIL conditions. Task 2 results were more variable; however, HIL consistently improved recall and F1 scores across all models, particularly DeepSeek. Task 3 exhibited the most pronounced gaps: entity coverage, semantic diversity, and ontology mapping dropped sharply for GPT-4o-mini and DeepSeek in the absence of HIL. Claude-Sonnet 3.7 maintained the highest entity detection and semantic coherence across all settings.

These findings confirm that model reliability declines as task openness increases. In structured settings, models operate with high precision and completeness; in semantically ambiguous tasks, model limitations—especially in recall and semantic grounding—become evident. HIL mitigates these deficiencies most effectively in the latter category, where model outputs benefit from corrective or guiding signals.

7.2
Model Behavior Across Tasks

Across all tasks, Claude-Sonnet 3.7 consistently achieved the highest performance. It demonstrated strong structural awareness, high recall, and robust ontology alignment, particularly in semantically open settings such as Task 3. These results suggest Claude’s internal representations generalize well across schema and semantic domains. Notably, its performance gains under HIL were modest, indicating a high level of baseline capability.

GPT-4o-mini exhibited moderate task performance but reported maximal self-confidence across conditions. Its recall and semantic accuracy lagged behind Claude, particularly in Tasks 2 and 3. Interestingly, its judge-assigned quality scores in Task 3 were slightly higher in the non-HIL setting, suggesting that HIL may introduce variance when the base model has limited semantic stability. This raises questions about model calibration and the interaction between user feedback and internal decision heuristics.

DeepSeek V3 0324 showed the lowest performance across tasks, especially in autonomous settings. However, it exhibited the largest relative improvements when HIL was introduced, particularly in Tasks 2 and 3. Its outputs under guidance were more semantically coherent, with improved ontology alignment and entity accuracy. These gains underscore the value of structured feedback for mid-tier models, and suggest that HIL can elevate underperforming systems to functional utility.

Together, these comparisons indicate that while Claude-Sonnet 3.7 benefits from HIL only marginally, weaker models rely on it for substantial performance gains. Additionally, the divergence between confidence reporting and actual performance, especially in GPT-4o-mini, highlights the need for more transparent and calibrated confidence estimation in LLM outputs.

7.3
Impact of Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) Feedback

Incorporating human feedback generally improved model performance across tasks, with the magnitude of improvement varying by both task complexity and model capability. HIL contributed most significantly in settings where the extraction task was underspecified, semantically rich, or context-sensitive—conditions under which autonomous generation often failed to meet reference standards.

In Task 1, the deterministic schema constrained the output space, minimizing the utility of human feedback. Claude-Sonnet 3.7 and GPT-4o-mini both achieved near-perfect recall in both HIL and non-HIL settings, while DeepSeek showed only marginal improvement. In contrast, Tasks 2 and 3 exhibited more substantial gains. HIL enhanced recall and F1 scores across all models in Task 2, and improved entity coverage, semantic diversity, and ontology alignment in Task 3.

The relative benefit of HIL was greatest for DeepSeek. In Task 3, its ontology alignment accuracy rose from 40.0% (non-HIL) to 86.7% (HIL), and its entity quality improved markedly. Claude-Sonnet 3.7 also showed gains, particularly in semantic diversity and coverage, though its baseline performance was already high. GPT-4o-mini’s results were mixed: while HIL improved entity diversity, it slightly reduced its judge score in Task 3, suggesting that human input may not uniformly benefit smaller models with less stable generation dynamics.

These patterns highlight the interaction between feedback utility and model capability. Strong models with high baseline performance may receive diminishing returns from HIL, while weaker models derive substantial benefit. Moreover, the effectiveness of HIL depends on task structure: in open-ended or semantically ambiguous tasks, human input provides critical disambiguation and grounding.

In terms of speed, HIL does not consistently impact speed, and its effects vary depending on the model and task. Note that these speeds may be influenced by external factors such as server location or back-end infrastructure. Since we utilize OpenRouter for accessing LLMs, fluctuations in response time may occur depending on which server handles the API request. We attribute some of the observed speed inconsistencies to this variability in infrastructure. In terms of token utilization, we observe a trend similar to that of cost, with the HIL setting consistently resulting in higher token consumption.

7.4
Operational and Semantic Considerations

Beyond core performance metrics, several additional factors inform the practical utility of large language models for structured extraction. These include operational cost, processing speed, confidence calibration, and semantic fidelity.

HIL consistently increased operational cost and token usage across all tasks and models, with the most pronounced effect observed in Claude-Sonnet 3.7. For instance, in the NER task, Claude’s cost increased by over 70% with HIL. Despite higher costs, speed did not exhibit a consistent trend: in some cases, HIL marginally reduced throughput, while in others, performance remained stable. These findings suggest that while HIL enhances quality, it introduces non-negligible computational overhead, necessitating trade-offs in resource-constrained applications.

A persistent mismatch between model confidence and output quality was observed, particularly in GPT-4o-mini. All models reported maximal self-confidence in Task 1, even when recall was incomplete (as in GPT-4o-mini and DeepSeek). In Task 3, GPT-4o-mini exhibited higher confidence despite lower entity accuracy. This underscores the need for calibrated confidence, especially when outputs guide human decisions.

Finally, semantic fidelity—defined as ontological or conceptual proximity to ground truth—often diverged from surface-level accuracy. This was especially evident in Task 3. For example, Claude-Sonnet 3.7 and DeepSeek frequently produced semantically appropriate but non-exact ontology matches (e.g., subfamily mappings for biological taxa), which would be penalized under strict string-match evaluation. This behavior suggests that evaluation frameworks should account for conceptual alignment, particularly in knowledge-rich domains where taxonomies are hierarchical or flexible.

These operational and semantic dimensions are central to determining the deployability and reliability of extraction systems in real-world scientific workflows.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that model performance, feedback sensitivity, and extraction quality are jointly shaped by task structure, model architecture, and the presence of human guidance. While top-performing models such as Claude-Sonnet 3.7 show strong autonomous performance, HIL remains a valuable mechanism for improving accuracy and semantic robustness in mid- and low-tier systems. Future work should explore scalable, adaptive HIL frameworks and design evaluation metrics that better capture semantic plausibility in structured scientific contexts.
