Lactobacillus salivarius potentiates gastrointestinal cancer immunotherapy through its metabolite chenodeoxycholic acid
Authors: Bohan Zhang, Yifan Zhang, Yi Cai, Siyuan Cheng, Zihan Han, Xiaochen Yin, Tong Xie, Yan Tan, Yang Feng, Yuxin Wang, Xiaohuan Guo, Lin Shen, Zhi Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune checkpoint inhibitor therapy has improved gastrointestinal (GI) cancer management; however, many patients exhibit resistance. Although gut microbiota influences immunotherapeutic responses, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Metagenomic analysis of 278 GI cancer patients reveals that Lactobacillus salivarius (L. salivarius) is enriched in responders and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy in syngeneic tumor models by increasing the infiltration of antitumor M1-like macrophages and CD8+ T cells and enhancing CD8+ T cell effector function. L. salivarius-associated chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) is identified as a functional metabolite. CDCA recapitulates the antitumor effects of L. salivarius and significantly improves anti-PD-1 efficacy in vivo, an effect attenuated by depleting macrophages or CD8+ T cells. Mechanistically, CDCA-induced reactive oxygen species triggers immunogenic cell death in tumor cells and polarizes macrophages toward antitumor M1 phenotype, activating CD8+ T cell antitumor immunity. These findings identify L. salivarius and its associated metabolite CDCA as a promising adjuvant for potentiating immunotherapy in GI cancers.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers accounted for 23.6% of new cancer cases and 34.2% of cancer-related deaths globally.1 The development of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapies, particularly the development of antibodies targeting PD-1 and its ligand, PD-L1, has revolutionized contemporary cancer treatment.2 However, the objective response rate of patients with GI cancers to ICIs was only 10%–20%,3 primarily because of the distinct biological characteristics of the tumor microenvironment (TME) that influence immunotherapeutic responses.4 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel combination strategies to improve the efficacy of ICIs.

The gut microbiome has emerged as a pivotal factor that influences ICI efficacy. Several studies have shown that the gut microbiome plays a crucial role in modulating the clinical response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies in various cancers, including gastric cancer (GC) and colorectal cancer (CRC).5,6,7 Oral administration of specific bacterial strains, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, Bifidobacterium breve, and Fusobacterium nucleatum, enhances ICI efficacy by modulating innate and adaptive immunities.8,9,10 Specific bacterial commensals and their metabolites modulate host antitumor immunity.11 For instance, certain microbial bile acid metabolites such as ursodeoxycholic acid enhance ICI responses by suppressing regulatory T cell activity.12 Preclinical and clinical studies have revealed distinct gut microbiota compositions in responders and non-responders to ICIs for GI cancers.5,13 However, the precise mechanistic pathways linking microbial metabolites to clinical outcomes have not yet been fully defined.

The influence of microbes and their metabolites on the efficacy of ICIs highlights the significant connection between bacteria and the TME. The TME comprises tumor cells, effector immune cells (e.g., CD8+ T cells), and immunosuppressive cells (e.g., tumor-associated macrophages [TAMs]).14 Although the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapies traditionally relies primarily on cytotoxic CD8+ T cells subsets, emerging evidence has highlighted the crucial role of TAMs in driving immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance.15,16 TAMs are highly plastic and can be polarized into antitumor M1-like and pro-tumor M2-like subtypes.17 Previous studies have shown that M2 macrophages contribute to resistance to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy by suppressing T cell cytotoxicity.18,19,20 Furthermore, most TAMs in tumor tissues are M2 subtypes.21 Therefore, remodeling M2 TAMs into M1 TAMs is considered an effective strategy for enhancing the effector function of CD8+ T cells and potentiating anti-PD-1 therapy. Emerging evidence suggests that bile acids primarily influence cancer development and treatment by modulating macrophage polarization and tumor cell proliferation.22 However, the specific mechanisms underlying these processes require further investigation.

Concurrently, inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a complementary strategy for initiating potent antitumor immunity.23 This process is characterized by the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from stressed or dying tumor cells, which, in turn, activate the innate immune system and promote the recruitment and activation of cytotoxic T cells to suppress tumor growth.24 Consequently, the development of effective strategies to induce ICD is a promising approach for cancer immunotherapy.25

In the present study, based on metagenomic sequencing data from a clinical cohort of GI cancer patients, we identified L. salivarius as a beneficial commensal bacterium closely associated with improved clinical responses to immunotherapy and demonstrated that it sensitizes GI tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy through the secretion of the bioactive metabolite chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA). Mechanistically, CDCA induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation to trigger ICD in tumor cells and reprograms TAMs toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype, thereby enhancing CD8+ T cell effector function. Collectively, our findings establish L. salivarius and its metabolite CDCA as a promising strategy to improve immunotherapy efficacy in GI cancer.

Results

L. salivarius improves anti-PD-1 efficacy

Among these, L. salivarius exhibited the most prominent enrichment, as indicated by having the highest LDA score. To investigate whether L. salivarius can modulate GI tumorigenesis and improve anti-PD-1 efficacy, we first established a syngeneic mouse model by subcutaneously inoculating a murine CRC cell line (MC38) into mice. Subsequently, the mice were treated via daily gavage with either E. coli (bacterial negative control) or L. salivarius. Once the tumors were established, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with anti-PD-1 antibodies or an IgG isotype control (Figure 1C). Interestingly, L. salivarius significantly inhibited tumor growth in immunocompetent mice. Moreover, compared with the control group in combination with anti-PD-1, L. salivarius significantly improved the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy, as evidenced by the reduced tumor volume and tumor weight (Figure 1D).

The efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy is closely associated with immune cell infiltration. Therefore, we performed flow cytometry analysis to examine the changes in immune cells in the TME induced by L. salivarius. Notably, compared with the E. coli group, the L. salivarius monotherapy resulted in decreased infiltration of macrophages, an increased CD86+/CD206+ TAMs ratio, and enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration. The combination of L. salivarius and anti-PD-1 treatment also resulted in an increased CD86+/CD206+ TAMs ratio, CD8+ T cell infiltration, and the proportion of IFN-γ+ GZMB+ CD8+ T cells, indicating enhanced antitumor immunity (Figures 1E–1G; Figure S1A). Further depletion of CD8+ T cells following CD8α-neutralizing antibody diminished the antitumor effect of L. salivarius, indicating that CD8+ T cells are required for its efficacy (Figures S1B and S1C). Moreover, L. salivarius treatment significantly reduced the proliferative potential and induced the apoptosis of tumor cells in mice (Figures 1H and 1I).

We further validated our findings using a YTN16 syngeneic mouse model. In comparison, L. salivarius significantly reduced the tumor size and markedly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy as well (Figures S1D–S1F). Consistently, the L. salivarius treatment group showed increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, enhanced IFN-γ+ GZMB+ CD8+ T cells, and an increased CD86+/CD206+ TAMs ratio, both in monotherapy and combination therapy (Figures S1G and S1H). Consistently, significantly lower cell proliferation and increased cell apoptosis, as indicated by Ki-67 staining and TUNEL staining, were observed in L. salivarius-gavaged mice compared with the control group (Figures S1I and S1J). In addition, we examined changes in other immune cell populations within the TME, including CD4+ T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and dendritic cells. In both syngeneic mouse models, these immune cell components did not show significant changes following L. salivarius treatment (Figures S2A–S2D). Therefore, we propose that L. salivarius enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy by promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration and effector function, together with increased infiltration of CD86+ macrophages.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the direct tumor-suppressive effect of LSCS, RNA sequencing was performed on MC38 cells treated with 10% LSCS or Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS, control culture medium). Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis revealed that mitochondrial-related signaling pathways and unfolded protein binding were significantly upregulated following LSCS treatment (Figure 2D). Moreover, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that LSCS significantly upregulated the antigen processing and presentation pathways, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) pathways, and the chemical carcinogenesis-ROS pathway (Figure 2E). Given that ER stress and ROS production are key components in regulating the pathways associated with ICD,26 we speculated that LSCS induces ICD. Collectively, these data indicate that L. salivarius-associated metabolites directly inhibit tumor cell proliferation by inducing ICD.

L. salivarius enhances the response to immunotherapy through its metabolite CDCA

To identify functional metabolites produced by L. salivarius that could modulate anti-PD-1 efficacy, we performed untargeted metabolomics analysis of plasma from mice with or without L. salivarius treatment, as well as on L. salivarius-conditioned media. Principal-component analysis (PCA) score plots show a separation following L. salivarius treatment (Figure 3A). Profiling highlighted the significant enrichment of 2-methylbutyl beta-D-glucopyranoside, CDCA, and 3-oxo-7alpha,12alpha-hydroxy-5beta-cholanoic acid in L. salivarius + anti-PD-1-treated mice (Figures 3B and 3C). Subsequently, we examined in vitro metabolomic profile and identified 12 microbiota-related metabolites, including L-glutamate, 2-hydroxycapronic acid, 8-aminooctanoic acid, CDCA, beta-muricholic acid, and 2-deoxyuridine-5-monophosphate. Specifically, to investigate the potential of microbial metabolites in predicting ICI responsiveness, we conducted a metabolomic analysis to gain an unbiased view of the metabolic landscape associated with ICI response in GI malignancies. Baseline plasma samples from a dedicated cohort of ICI-treated patients were analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to determine whether responders and non-responders harbored distinct microbial metabolite profiles. In total, 71 metabolites were differentially expressed between the responders and non-responders (Figures 3G and 3H; Figure S3A). Pathway enrichment analysis between the responder and non-responder groups also showed significant differences in the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathway and arginine metabolism (Figure 3I). The overlap of the three datasets represented in the Venn diagram shows that CDCA was the only metabolites consistently enriched in the LSCS, plasma samples of L. salivarius-gavaged mice, and immune benefit population (Figure 3J). We further examined the enrichment characteristics of CDCA within the three metabolomic profiles and confirmed low CDCA metabolic activity at baseline, whereas L. salivarius administration increased CDCA levels (Figure 3K). Importantly, among patients who benefited from immunotherapy, those with higher plasma CDCA levels had longer progression-free survival (PFS) than those with lower plasma CDCA levels (Figure 3L). Analysis of 63 paired metagenomic and metabolomic samples revealed a positive association between the abundance of L. salivarius and the concentration of CDCA (Figure S3B). Previous studies have reported that Lactobacillus strains generally possess bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity, which deconjugates conjugated bile acids (e.g., glycochenodeoxycholic acid [GCDCA] and taurochenodeoxycholic acid [TCDCA]) to release free primary bile acids CDCA. We, therefore, hypothesized that BSH is the key enzyme mediating this process. In vitro enzymatic assays showed that after incubation with LSCS, conjugated bile acids (GCDCA/TCDCA) were efficiently deconjugated, as evidenced by increased release of free glycine and taurine. Moreover, co-incubation with the BSH inhibitor GR7 diminished this effect (Figure S3C). Collectively, these results suggest that CDCA may be a functional metabolite associated with L. salivarius that contributes to its antitumor activity.

CDCA significantly inhibited tumor cell viability, as determined by the CCK-8 assay, but had no significant effect on normal GI epithelial cells (Figures 4A and 4B). The colony formation assay further confirmed that CDCA inhibited the clonogenic potential of GI cancer cells (Figure 4C). To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we performed RNA sequencing on CDCA-treated MC38 cells, which identified 2,396 upregulated and 2,862 downregulated genes (Figure 4E). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that CDCA, like LSCS, significantly enriched the antigen processing and presentation pathway and protein processing in the ER pathways (Figure 4F). GSEA further revealed that CDCA treatment enriched GO terms associated with cellular redox homeostasis, unfolded protein binding, proteasome complex, and immune response (Figure S3D), indicating the induction of ICD. Supporting this, the expression of genes associated with ICD process was significantly upregulated in CDCA-treated cells (Figure 4G).

Given that both ROS accumulation and ER stress are hallmarks of ICD, we hypothesized that CDCA drives ICD through an ROS-ER stress axis. Flow cytometry confirmed that CDCA markedly elevated intracellular ROS (Figure 4H). In parallel, western blotting showed that CDCA upregulated GRP78 and increased phosphorylation of PERK and eIF2α (Figure 4I), indicating activation of the GRP78-PERK-eIF2α branch of the ER stress response. To confirm ICD functionally, we assessed two characteristic DAMPs, extracellular ATP release and cell-surface calreticulin (CRT) exposure. CDCA significantly enhanced both ATP release and CRT exposure (Figures 4J and 4K). Together, these results indicate that CDCA induces ROS accumulation and activates GRP78-PERK-eIF2α-mediated ER stress, thereby triggering ICD in tumor cells.

CDCA reprograms TAMs toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype and activates CD8+ T cell

To further confirm the antitumor effect of CDCA in vivo, we established a subcutaneous MC38 tumor model. CDCA gavage significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with vehicle control (Figures 5A–5C). Within the tumor, CDCA increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+ GZMB+ CD8+ T cells, while reducing macrophage infiltration (Figure 5D). Immunofluorescence staining further showed an elevated CD86+/CD206+ TAM ratio in CDCA-treated tumors (Figure S4A). To determine whether macrophages and CD8+ T cells are mechanistically required for these effects, we performed antibody-mediated depletion in vivo (Figure S4B). Depletion of either CD8+ T cells (Figures S4C and S4D) or macrophages (Figures S4E and S4F) significantly attenuated the antitumor efficacy of CDCA, as reflected by increased tumor weight and volume, demonstrating that both cell populations are indispensable for CDCA-enhanced tumor control. To further explore the direct effects of CDCA on these two immune cell types, we turned to in vitro models. CDCA alone neither enhanced the tumor-killing capacity of CD8+ T cells nor promoted their IFN-γ production (Figures S5A and S5B), arguing against a direct action on CD8+ T cells. Since bile acids are well-recognized regulators of macrophage polarization, we instead hypothesized that CDCA re-educates macrophages and thereby modulates CD8+ T cell function indirectly. To recapitulate the TME, murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were differentiated into M0 macrophages and then exposed to MC38 tumor cell-conditioned medium with or without CDCA, after which their polarization state was profiled using established macrophage gene sets (Figure 5E). Relative to untreated TAMs, CDCA-treated TAMs showed marked downregulation of the M2 signature genes Mrc1, Arg-1, and Chi3l3 (Figure 5F) and upregulation of the M1 signature genes Il1b, Nos2, and Il6 (Figure 5G). Consistently, secretion of the immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β was significantly reduced in the supernatant of CDCA-treated TAMs (Figure 5H).

To gain further insight into the effects of macrophage polarization, we profiled BMDMs by bulk RNA sequencing, which confirmed that CDCA reprogrammed TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype, with upregulation of M1 and downregulation of M2 marker genes (Figure S5C). Notably, the cellular response to oxidative stress pathway was significantly enriched following CDCA treatment, and KEGG analysis further highlighted enrichment of the IL-17 and NF-κB signaling pathways (Figures 5I and 5J). ROS are key drivers of M1 macrophage activation and function,27 acting primarily through downstream inflammatory cascades such as NF-κB, MAPK, and Nrf2.28 Given the central roles of ROS-NF-κB axis in M1 macrophage differentiation,28 together with our prior finding that CDCA induces oxidative stress in tumor cells, we hypothesized that a similar ROS-NF-κB axis may mediate CDCA-induced macrophage reprogramming. Consistent with this, CDCA significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels in BMDMs (Figure 5K) and western blot analysis showed markedly increased phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα (Figure 5L), confirming activation of the ROS-NF-κB axis. We further investigated the mechanism of CDCA-induced ROS production. Neither FXR nor TGR5 antagonists inhibited CDCA-mediated ROS generation (Figure S5D), indicating a receptor-independent pathway. As an amphipathic molecule, CDCA can directly disrupt mitochondria, the primary intracellular ROS source. As measured by JC-10 staining, CDCA depolarized the mitochondrial membrane (Figure S5E) and decreased cellular NAD+/NADH ratios (Figure S5F). Moreover, treatment with the mitochondrial ROS scavenger MitoTEMPO abolished CDCA-induced ROS production, suggesting that mitochondria are a potential source of CDCA-mediated ROS overproduction (Figure S5G). However, further investigation is required to elucidate the precise mechanism.

We next determined whether this phenotypic shift had functional consequences for CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Using a co-culture model (Figure 5M), we found that compared with M0 macrophages (set as the 100% reference baseline for T cell-mediated cytotoxicity), TAMs significantly impaired the CD8+ T cell-mediated killing of MC38-OVA tumor cells, whereas CDCA treatment reversed this suppressive effect and restored tumor cell killing capacity (Figure 5N). Accordingly, CD8+ T cell secretion of IFN-γ and GZMB was significantly increased upon CDCA treatment (Figure 5O). Together, these results demonstrate that CDCA reprograms TAMs toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype via the ROS-NF-κB axis, thereby restoring CD8+ T cell activity.

CDCA enhances the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy

To confirm the effect of CDCA on modulating antitumor immunity, we established an MC38 syngeneic mouse model by daily gavage of CDCA, with or without anti-PD-1 (Figure 6A). Supplementation with CDCA significantly promoted anti-PD-1 efficacy and phenocopied the effect of L. salivarius, as evidenced by reduced tumor weight and tumor volume compared with the vehicle control (Figures 6B and 6C). TME analysis revealed that CDCA treatment increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells and CD86+/CD206+ TAM ratio (Figure 6D). Consistently, significantly lower cell proliferation and increased cell apoptosis, as indicated by Ki-67 staining and TUNEL staining, were observed in CDCA-treated mice (Figures 6E and 6F). To explore the translational potential of CDCA in improving immunotherapy in CRC, we next established an AOM/DSS-induced CRC mouse model, which mimics physiological CRC development in humans. Consistent with the syngeneic model, CDCA plus anti-PD1 therapy significantly reduced tumor number and tumor load relative to anti-PD-1 with vehicle control (Figures S6A–S6C), suggesting that CDCA boosts the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy against CRC. Flow-cytometric analysis of colonic tumors showed that the combination significantly increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and CD8+ effector T cell function and raised the proportion of CD86+MHCII+ TAMs (Figure S6D). Elevated CD8+ T cell infiltration and the upregulation of effector and immune checkpoint molecules are well-established biomarkers of responses to immunotherapy.29 Therefore, we first focused on the CD8+ T cell cluster, which was further subdivided into the CD8T_activated, CD8T_cycling, CD8Tcm, and CD8Tex subsets. CDCA treatment increased the proportion of CD8T_activated cells from 31.3% to 43.6% but decreased the proportion of exhausted T cells in tumors (Figure 7C). To reveal the underlying mechanism at the gene level, we compared the expression levels of the CD8+ T cell transcriptome between the anti-PD-1 and CDCA plus anti-PD-1 groups (Figure 7D). KEGG analysis showed that the chemokine signaling pathways, leukocyte transendothelial migration, T cell receptor signaling pathway, and natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity were significantly upregulated in mice treated with CDCA plus anti-PD-1, suggesting an immune activation pattern for CDCA (Figure 7E). Subsequent single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) revealed significantly higher enrichment scores for type II interferon response gene sets and cytotoxicity (e.g., GZMB and IFN-γ) in the CDCA-treated group than in the control group (Figures 7F and 7G). However, we observed no difference in the expression of exhaustion-related genes such as PD-1 and LAG3 in intratumoral CD8+ T cells between the control and CDCA groups (Figure S7B). Shifts in the subset distribution and the upregulation of effector signature genes underscore the importance of CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity in CDCA-mediated immune activation.

Our previous experiments have revealed that CDCA significantly reduces overall macrophage infiltration while reprogramming the M2-like TAMs into M1-like TAMs. Therefore, we performed further analysis of the monocyte-macrophage lineage at single-cell resolution. Examination of M1 and M2 marker expression in macrophages from both groups revealed the downregulation of M2 signature genes (Cx3cr1, Igf1, Maf, and Ms4a4a) (Figure 7H) and upregulation of M1 signature genes (Nos2, Ly6c2, Ccr2, and Psme2) in the CDCA plus anti-PD-1 group, suggesting that CDCA potentially inhibits M2-like polarization while promoting an M1-like phenotype (Figure 7I). Patients with elevated CXCL9 expression and M1 TAMs showed longer PFS and improved response to ICIs.30 Interestingly, CXCL9 was upregulated in CDCA-treated macrophages (Figure 7J). Given the established critical role of cellular crosstalk in shaping antitumor immunity, cell-cell communication analysis was performed to elucidate the crosstalk between CD8+ T cells and TAMs during treatment. CellChat analysis identified altered T-NK to macrophage communication pathways, specifically an activated IFN-II signaling axis in the CDCA plus anti-PD-1 group compared with that in the anti-PD-1 monotherapy group (Figure S7C). Because IFN-γ is a key driver of M1 macrophage polarization, we further probed whether the interferon produced by CD8+ T cells contributes to TAM evolution. The results revealed that CD8+ T cells can interact with TAMs via the IFNG-IFNGR1/2 axis (Figures 7K and 7L), leading us to speculate that CD8+ T cells may also promote M1 macrophage polarization through this mechanism.

We further examined malignant cells by analyzing their transcriptomic landscape and functional states. GO analysis revealed that the antigen processing and presentation pathway, immune response, negative regulation of the DNA biosynthetic process, and positive regulation of the apoptosis pathway were upregulated in the CDCA plus anti-PD-1 samples (Figure 7M). We also performed ssGSEA and found that CDCA significantly promoted the enrichment of gene sets associated with apoptosis, oxidative phosphorylation, ROS, and the interferon-gamma response in tumor epithelial cells (Figure 7N). Moreover, the enrichment of ICD markers (Anxa1, Eif2ak3, Hsp90aa1, Atf6, Atf4, Ccl5, Cxcl10, and Cxcl9) and apoptosis genes (Casp3, Casp7, Casp1, Casp9, Casp8ap2, and Bax) corroborated the induction of ICD (Figure S7D). Our results indicate that CDCA combination therapy induces ICD in tumor cells and promotes an M1-like shift in TAMs, a shift that is potentially amplified by close interaction with CD8+ T cells via their production of IFN-γ.

Discussion

In this study, we identified that the gut commensal L. salivarius can enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. Particularly, we identified that CDCA, a bile acid metabolite associated with L. salivarius, is the key effector that remodels the TME by reprogramming macrophages and, in parallel, triggers ICD in tumor cells. Our findings offer valuable insight into the molecular mechanism of L. salivarius-mediated antitumor immunity and highlight the potential of oral supplementation with L. salivarius or CDCA as a strategy to potentiate ICI efficacy in GI cancers.

We first demonstrated that L. salivarius significantly enhanced anti-PD-1 efficacy and induced a remarkable tumor regression. This agrees with growing evidence that Lactobacillus family confers benefits to tumorigenesis and immunotherapy in various cancers, and microbiome modulation is viewed as a promising approach for improving responses to ICIs.31,32 TME analysis revealed that L. salivarius increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and enhanced their effector function, and concurrently increased M1-like TAMs, together indicating a more immunostimulatory TME. Reprogramming of TAMs toward an M1-like state is one of the key mechanisms for overcoming ICI resistance, which contributed to reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment and effectively restored CD8+ T cell effector function. In supporting our identification, earlier reports have linked L. salivarius to improved immunotherapy outcomes in GC clinical cohort and inhibition of colitis-related tumorigenesis.13,33Taken together, our results suggested that the probiotic L. salivarius holds significant translational promise for enhancing ICI efficacy in GI cancers by reshaping the immunosuppressive TME.

Gut microbes regulate the ICI efficacy via metabolic pathways. Lactobacillus species are known to produce butyrate and other tryptophan-derived molecules that participate in immune system regulation.11,34,35 Increasing evidence suggests that bacteria-derived bile acid metabolites exert a wide range of biological effects.36 Jin et al. demonstrated that the microbiota-derived metabolite 3-oxo-Δ4,6-LCA suppresses tumor progression and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy by promoting stem-like CD8+ T cells in the TME.37 Bile acid signaling has been studied mainly in hepatocellular carcinoma, yet how individual bile acids shape immunotherapy response has remained undefined. Here, our plasma metabolomics tied the primary bile acid synthesis pathway to ICI response in GI cancer. Through metabolomic profiling and biological validation, we identified CDCA, a primary bile acid metabolite originating from L. salivarius, as a functional immunomodulatory component that improves anti-PD-1 therapy. Bile acid pathway in host and microbe is highly dynamic, with rapid biotransformation of intermediates in vivo. We reasoned that L. salivarius deconjugates conjugated-CDCA into free CDCA via its intrinsic BSH activity. BSH-mediated deconjugation is an enzymatically simple reaction whose host and microbial catalysts are well documented, so other BSH-bearing commensal bacteria may also contribute to CDCA generation. While this makes the biotransformation of CDCA physiologically plausible, its specific attribution to L. salivarius in vivo warrants further investigation.

CDCA has been implicated in the development and progression of several cancers, such as endometrial and esophageal cancers,38,39 and prior work has characterized it either as a microbiota-derived chemopreventive agent or as a directly cytotoxic molecule.40,41 However, its potential to enhance checkpoint blockade in GI cancers has not yet been addressed. Remarkably, we found that CDCA administration recapitulated the antitumor phenotype of L. salivarius, significantly inhibiting tumor growth and sensitizing the tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy. Mechanistically, L. salivarius and its metabolite CDCA reprogrammed macrophages toward an M1-like antitumor phenotype through an ROS-NF-κB axis, leading to increased infiltration and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cell, contributing to immune activation and improved anti-PD-1 efficacy. Increased M2-like TAM infiltration strongly correlates with poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy across multiple solid tumor types.42 CDCA has been reported to suppress acute myeloid leukemia by inhibiting M2-like polarization via the ROS/p38 MAPK/DGAT1 pathway, but its function in solid tumors has remained unclear.39 ROS serve as critical mediators of M1 macrophage polarization, primarily through activation of the NF-κB pathway.43 Upon stimulation, NF-κB drives the transcription of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and iNOS,44 which not only define the M1 phenotype but also facilitate T cell recruitment and activation. Consistent with this mechanism, our study demonstrated that CDCA potently enhances intracellular ROS levels, associated with the activation of NF-κB pathway and subsequent upregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOS. Moreover, our data further suggested that mitochondria serve as a likely target of CDCA for ROS generation, although the precise mechanism underlying this effect requires further investigation. Collectively, our findings underscored that the reprogramming of TAMs, along with subsequent changes in T cell infiltration and function, constitute a pivotal mechanism driving the immune-modulatory effects of CDCA.

Beyond immunomodulation, CDCA also exerts direct antitumor effects on cancer cells. A subset of anticancer therapies stimulates cancer cell-autonomous pathways, leading to a form of immunostimulatory apoptosis called ICD, which is characterized by the coordinated induction of ROS and ER stress.45 In line with this, nanocarriers delivering ICD inducers such as doxorubicin enhance ICD and alleviate immunosuppression, effectively sensitizing ICI efficacy in preclinical tumor models,46 which highlights the value of pairing ICD induction with anti-PD-1 therapy. However, the contribution of gut microbiota and their metabolites to the regulation of ICD remains poorly understood. In the present study, our data suggested that CDCA induces ROS production, which may subsequently trigger ER stress via the putative ROS-GRP78-PERK-eIF2α axis, thereby contributing to ICD induction.

Our identification of CDCA as a dual-acting agent that both triggers ICD and repolarizes TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype, therefore, reveals a mechanism that may overcome the inherent limitation of ICD attenuation within the immunosuppressive cold TME.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that L. salivarius and its associated CDCA enrichment could improve anti-PD-1 efficacy in GI cancers. Mechanistically, CDCA exerts a dual effect through ROS induction. On the one hand, it triggers ER stress and subsequent ICD in tumor cells. On the other, it activates the NF-κB pathway to drive TAM polarization toward the M1 antitumor phenotype, thereby enhancing CD8+ T cell effector function. Collectively, our results demonstrated that fecal L. salivarius and plasma CDCA may be noninvasive biomarkers for predicting the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with GI cancer. Moreover, supplementation with either L. salivarius or CDCA represents a promising adjuvant strategy for improving clinical outcomes in individuals receiving immune checkpoint blockades.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations to our study. First, the precise molecular mechanism underlying CDCA-induced ROS production remains to be fully defined. Second, genetic manipulation of the candidate bacterial biosynthetic pathway would be needed to formally establish L. salivarius as a direct cellular source of CDCA production. Third, whether CDCA can be used as a regulator of macrophages and CD8+ T cells for adjuvant immunotherapy needs to be validated in larger clinical cohorts.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

This study enrolled patients with advanced gastric cancer or colorectal cancer at Beijing Cancer Hospital. A total of 278 patients were included in the metagenomic analysis cohort, with baseline stool samples collected from all participants. A subset of 106 patients with matched baseline plasma samples was further subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiling via LC-MS/MS. The patient population consists of both females and males, aged 18–90 years old. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants have been established to ensure patient safety and minimize the risk of confounding factors that could interfere with the study results. The Inclusion Criteria included: 1) Patients aged 18 years or older with gastrointestinal tumors. 2) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2 at baseline. 3) Clinically eligible for ICI therapy with no prior ICI exposure. 4) Patients who provide written informed consent to participate in the trial. The Exclusion Criteria included: 1) Patients who have had a fever or serious infection within one month prior to starting immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. 2) Patients who have received broad-spectrum antibiotics orally or intravenously within one month prior to or during the immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. 3) Patients who have undergone gastrointestinal resection or diversion surgery within the past six months. 4) Patients with active autoimmune diseases. Detailed clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients are provided in Table S2.

All participants received standardized immunotherapy-based treatment. Therapeutic responses were evaluated strictly in accordance with the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST v1.1), including complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). Patients with sustained objective response (PR/SD) for at least 6 months after treatment initiation were defined as responders, whereas patients with progressive disease (PD) within 6 months of treatment were defined as non-responders. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Cancer Hospital (Approval No.: 2018KT66) in 2018, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to participation. All human samples and clinical data used in this study were fully de-identified to protect patient privacy.

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at a temperature of 22 ± 2°C, relative humidity of 50% ± 1%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Peking University health science center (BCAA0316) and followed institutional guidelines. MC38 cells (5×105 cells/mouse in 100 μL PBS) and YTN16 cells (5×106 cells/mouse in 100 μL PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of mice. L. salivarius or E. coli (1 × 108 CFU in 100 μL PBS) was administered daily by oral gavage. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were administered 100 μg of PD-1 blocking antibody (RMP1-14, BioXcell) via intraperitoneal injection every three days. Tumor volume was monitored by measuring the length (a) and width (b) with a vernier caliper and calculated using the formula V = a × b2/2. To investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms, mice were treated with CDCA (HY-76847, MedChemExpress) at 10 mg/kg/day via oral gavage.

For AOM/DSS-induced colorectal cancer tumor model, 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with azoxymethane (AOM, 10 mg/kg body weight). The second day, mice were provided water with 2.5% DSS for a week, and then instead of water for one week. Mice were given intragastric administration with CDCA at 10 mg/kg/day via oral gavage every other day when provided water. Anti-PD1 or isotype control (100 μg) were given to mice by intraperitoneal injection every 3 days after the fourth DSS cycle, For in vivo depletion of CD8+ T cells and macrophages, anti-mouse CD8α (A2102, Selleck) and anti-mouse CSF1R (A2159, Selleck) antibodies were administered intraperitoneally every three days.

Cell lines and cell culture

HT-29 (RRID: CVCL_0320), HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), AGS (RRID: CVCL_0139), HGC27 (RRID: CVCL_1278), NCM460 (RRID: CVCL_0460), GES1 (RRID: CVCL_EQ22), and MC38 (RRID: CVCL_B288) were grown in RPMI-1640 media supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin synergistic combination at 37 C with 5% CO2. The mouse gastric cancer cell line YTN-16 was kindly provided by Professor Sachiyo Nomura. All cell lines were regularly authenticated and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The YTN-16 line was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.1% MITO+ Serum Extender (Corning). For experiments, cell lines were treated with either 10% LSCS or 100 μM CDCA as indicated. To minimize the risk of bacterial contamination, the LSCS and ECCS were first centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter before being added to tumor cells. When establishing tumor model, MC38 and YTN-16 cells were digested using trypsin with EDTA from petri dishes.

Preparation of L. salivarius and E. coli

L. salivarius was isolated from fecal samples of healthy Chinese donors by Xbiome and cultured in de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) medium. E. coli was cultivated in LB medium. Bacterial cells from both cultures were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and adjusted to a final concentration of 1×108 colony-forming units (CFU) per 100 μL for subsequent in vivo administration. LSCS and ECCS were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm sterile filter to minimize bacterial contamination. The filtered conditioned media were then applied to tumor cells for subsequent experiments.

Method details

Metagenomic sequencing analysis

Raw paired-end 150 bp sequencing reads generated by the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Novogene) were processed by a standardized in-house pipeline consisting of the following tools: fastp v0.23.1 was used to trim sequencing and PCR adapters and filter short sequences (<50 bp), followed by KneadData v0.10.0 to trim low-quality reads (a sliding window of 4 and an average quality score of 20 was used) and decontaminate against the hg19 human genome; MetaPhlAn4 (mpa_vJun23_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202307 database) with default parameters was used to generate taxonomy profiles.Differential abundance analysis of taxonomic features was performed by the LEfSe algorithm (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) (R implementation https://github.com/waldronlab/lefser/blob/master/R/lefser.R). Features were first screened for significance (p < 0.05) by Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, followed by an LDA score greater than 2 to identify robust features. Student’s t test was employed for direct comparisons between response groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistics and visualization were conducted in R (version 3.6.3).

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Samples were separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C and stored at −80°C until analysis. The resulting supernatant was transferred to sterile 1.5 mL microfuge tubes, and 400 μL of methanol was added to every 100 μL of supernatant. This 80% (v/v) methanol solution was vortexed, incubated at −80°C for 2 h, and then centrifuged again. The final supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials for immediate LC-MS analysis.

To minimize the impact of missing values, the 80% rule was applied, retaining only metabolites detected in ≥80% of participants within at least one study group. Quality control (QC) samples, prepared from pooled plasma aliquots, were analyzed intermittently to monitor instrument stability. Metabolites with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples were excluded and imputed at half the minimum value for that variable. Data were then subjected to total area normalization and log-transformation to stabilize variance. Finally, mean-normalized data from both positive and negative ion modes were merged. The R package XCMS (version 1.6.2) was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and 7-cycle interactive validation was used to evaluate the stability of the model. The raw peak intensity data were pre-processed in metaX. An in-house MS2 database, the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, www.hmdb.ca), and the METLIN metabolite database (metlin.scripps.edu) were used for more reliable identification of metabolites. The selection of significantly different metabolites was determined based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) obtained from the OPLS-DA model and the p value of Student’s t test.

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were dissected, washed with PBS, and processed into single-cell suspensions using a high-activity tumor tissue enzymatic digestion kit (DHTE-5001, RWD, China) in conjunction with a single-cell suspension preparation instrument (DSC-400, RWD, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting cell suspension was filtered and resuspended in FACS buffer. After blocking Fc-γ receptors with TrueStain FcX (BioLegend), cells were stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies. Viability staining was performed using a Zombie dye to exclude dead cells. For IFN-γ and GZMB examination, cells were ex vivo stimulated with PMA/ionomycin for 4 h. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized according to the manufacturer’s instruction (BioLegend). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1). Cells were gated in live cells and CD4+ T cells (CD45+ CD3+ CD4+ cells), CD8+ T cells (CD45+ CD3+ CD8+), activated CD8+ T cells (CD69+ CD8+), activated CD4+ T cells (CD69+ CD4+), exhausted CD4+ T cells (PD1+ TIM3+ CD4+), exhausted CD8+ T cells (PD1+ TIM3+ CD8+), cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (CD45+ CD3+ IFN-γ+ GZMB+ CD8+), Tregs (CD25+ FoxP3+ CD4+), macrophages (CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+), “M1-like” macrophages (CD86+ CD11b+ F4/80+), “M2-like” macrophages (CD206+ CD11b+ F4/80+), DCs (CD45+ CD11c+ MHCII+), and MDSC (CD45+ CD11b+ Gr-1+).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (15596018, Ambion) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. mRNA expression levels were quantified by qRT-PCR with SYBR Green (TIANGEN). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial pre-denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. All gene expression values were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2ˆ−ΔΔCt method.

Bulk RNA sequencing

To minimize interference from dead cells, RNA was exclusively isolated from adherent viable cell populations after discarding the supernatant containing dead cells. Cell viability was measured during sample collection using trypan blue staining, confirming >80% viability across all experimental groups. Total RNA was extracted from MC38 cells using TRIzol reagent. Library construction and sequencing were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw fastq reads were processed with fastp to remove low-quality sequences, yielding clean reads for subsequent analysis. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM. Read counts per gene were obtained with HTSeq-count. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in R (v3.2.0) to assess sample reproducibility. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with a p value <0.05. DEG expression patterns across groups and samples were visualized via hierarchical clustering in R. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs were conducted in R based on the hypergeometric distribution, with significantly enriched terms visualized in bar, chord, and bubble plots. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software. A predefined gene set was tested for enrichment at the extremes of a ranked list of genes ordered by differential expression between sample groups.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

To minimize potential confounding factors, samples for single-cell sequencing were collected at a unified experimental time point. From each tumor, a standardized weight of viable tissue was harvested, consistently targeting the tumor periphery to exclude necrotic areas. The scRNA-seq libraries were generated using the 10× Genomics Chromium Controller Instrument and Chromium Single Cell 3′ v4 Reagent Kits (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using a 150 bp paired-end run.

The Seurat package (version 4.1.1, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) was used for cell normalization and regression based on the expression table according to the UMI counts of each sample and percentage of mitochondrial reads to obtain the scaled data. PCA was constructed based on the scaled data with the top 2,000 highly variable genes, and the top 10 principal components were used for tSNE and UMAP construction. Using a graph-based clustering method, we obtained the unsupervised cell cluster results based on the top 10 principal components, and we calculated marker genes by the FindAllMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test algorithm under the following criteria: (1) Log2FC > 0.25; (2) p value <0.05; (3) min.pct >0.1. To identify cell types in detail, the clusters of the same cell type were selected for re-tSNE analysis, graph-based clustering, and marker analysis.

To identify differentially expressed genes among samples, the FindMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test algorithm was used under the following criteria: (1) Log2FC > 0.25; (2) p value <0.05; (3) min.pct >0.1, which was more conducive to obtaining different functional genes among samples. To elucidate the biological implications of the DEGs and marker genes, GO analysis was performed. GO annotations were downloaded from NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), the Gene Ontology database (http://www.geneontology.org/), and UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org/). Fisher’s exact test was applied to identify significant GO categories, and FDR was used to correct the p values. Pathway analysis was used to explore the significant pathways of the DEGs and marker genes based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Fisher’s exact test was used to identify significant pathways, and the threshold of significance was defined by the p value and FDR.

For gene set enrichment analysis, Fisher’s exact test was applied to calculate the p value for each gene set. Raw p values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Such enrichment analysis was applied to annotations including GO (v2.5.13), KEGG (201900613), Hallmark (h.all.7.0, https://www.gseamsigdb.org/gsea-/msigdb/index.jsp), and customized gene sets. A customized 41-signature gene set including immune- and cytokine-related terms was collected from the CellPhoneDB database. To characterize the relative activation of a given gene set, we performed QuSAGE (v2.42.0) analysis. We further applied single-cell gene set enrichment analysis based on the curated gene sets and the normalized gene expression matrix using the ssGSEA function in the GSVA package (v1.32.0). Violin diagrams were drawn in R, and differences in the apoptosis score between groups were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum algorithm.

To enable a systematic analysis of cell–cell communication molecules, cell communication analysis was performed based on CellPhoneDB (v1.1.0), a public repository of ligands, receptors, and their interactions. Membrane, secreted, and peripheral proteins of each cluster were annotated. Significant means and cell communication significance (p value <0.05) were calculated based on the interactions and the normalized cell matrix obtained by Seurat normalization. Cell communication analysis was additionally performed using the R package CellChat (v1.1.3) with default parameters; the CellChatDB mouse database was used. All two groups of samples were normalized together, and then each group was extracted and analyzed and compared in parallel, under the assumption that they shared cell types.

KI67 and TUNEL staining

Paraffin-embedded tumor sections were used to evaluate KI67 and TUNEL expression. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval with sodium citrate, the slides were incubated in 1% hydrogen peroxide to abolish endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by 5% BSA blocking. Slides were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. DAB was used to develop signals. The proliferation index and apoptosis index were calculated as the percentage of positively stained cells relative to the total number of tumor cells counted. For each sample, cells with typical tumor morphology were manually quantified in at least five randomly selected fields, thereby excluding cells from the tumor microenvironment.

Western blot

Paraffin-embedded tumor sections were used to evaluate KI67 and TUNEL expression. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval with sodium citrate, the slides were incubated in 1% hydrogen peroxide to abolish endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by 5% BSA blocking. Slides were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. DAB was used to develop signals. The proliferation index and apoptosis index were calculated by dividing the number of positively stained cells by the total number of cells.

ELISA

The supernatants of cells stimulated with different interventions were collected (1000 g, 20 min, 4°C) respectively, and then concentrations of mouse TGF-β, IL-10 and IFN-γ were detected by related ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability and colony formation assays

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Selleck, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results were quantified by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450). For the colony formation assay, cells were seeded in biological triplicates in a 6 well plate at 600 cells per well in 2 mL of media. After 48 h, cells were treated with LSCS or CDCA as mentioned in the legends. Assays were concluded at 14 days by fixing in methanol for 10 min and staining with 0.5% crystal violet.

ATP and ROS quantification

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well culture plates and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with CDCA (50 μM). After a further 24 h incubation, ATP and ROS levels were quantified according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Calreticulin (CRT) quantification

Cells were seeded into 24-well culture plates at a density of 1 × 105/well and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with CDCA. After a 24 h incubation, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min, rinsed three times with PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody targeting CRT (Abcam, ab92516), rinsed three times with PBS, and treated with the corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody. The fluorescence of CRT was quantified by flow cytometry.

Isolation and culture of murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femur and tibia of 6–7 weeks male C57BL/6 WT mice and differentiated to mature macrophages for 7 days. Briefly, cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS, containing 10ng/mL M-CSF (Perprotech). On day 7, a CM collected from tumor cells was added for TAM differentiation for 24 h. Subsequently, TAMs were randomly assigned to control group or CDCA intervention group after differentiation. After 24 h of further incubation, cells and culture supernatants were collected for subsequent experiments including CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity assay.

T cell isolation and co-culture assays

CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleens by negative magnetic selection using an EasySep Mouse T cell Isolation Kit (19853, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). The isolated CD8+ T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Cat#21985023, Thermo Fisher, USA), and 10 ng/mL mouse recombinant interleukin-2 (70801, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada), and were activated with mouse anti-CD3/CD28 beads (Cat#100–1572, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) for 48 h.

Macrophages were differentiated from the bone marrow of these mice and were pretreated with CDCA before co-culturing with T cells. CDCA was washed out from BMDMs culture medium with PBS. CD8+ T cells were then co-cultured with macrophages under various experimental conditions for 24 h, after which the CD8+ T cells were harvested for analysis. For tumor cell lysis assays, 1 × 104 MC38-OVA cells were plated in 96-well plates, and 5 × 104 T cells were added to each well. Supernatants were collected 20 h later for cytokine analysis. Tumor cell lysis was assessed by flow cytometry.

Immunostaining

Tissues were fixed with formalin for 24 h, and then placed in ethanol for 24 h. Paraffin embedded tissues were sectioned (6 mm). Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) using a pressure cooker for 3 min. After blocking with goat serum for 40 min, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 40 min at room temperature in the dark. TSA signal amplification was then applied for 10 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9. Significant differences between groups were determined by an unpaired Student’s t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. Results are presented as mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Specific details for each experiment are provided in the corresponding method details sections.
