Stochastic Modeling of Fish Schooling Dynamics: Decomposition into Collective and Individual Movements
Elena G. de Lamo, M. Carmen Miguel, Romualdo Pastor-Satorras

Abstract
The movement of fish in schools can be effectively modeled using stochastic frameworks, particularly through descriptions based on random walk dynamics. In this study, we analyze fish motion by decomposing it into two components: the movement of the school’s center of mass and the relative motion of individual fish with respect to this center. We demonstrate that the center of mass follows an active random walk subjected to hard wall interactions and driven by white noise, while individual fish move independently within an effective field generated by the group. Their trajectories can be described as active random walks within a potential shaped by the spatial distribution of the school, influenced by multiplicative noise. Our findings enhance the understanding of collective animal behavior and offer valuable insights into the stochastic modeling of fish movement.



Understanding the collective motion of fish schools offers a powerful lens through which to examine how social interactions among individuals can give rise to complex, coordinated group behaviors and consensus decision-making processes
Couzin et al. 2005
;
Danchin et al. 2004
;
Miguel et al. 2018
;
Ojer and Pastor-Satorras 2022
.
These systems show a remarkable ability to maintain spatial cohesion, avoid collisions, and adaptively respond to environmental cues, all without the need for centralized control, fixed hierarchies, or permanent leadership structures
Vicsek and Zafeiris 2012
;
Puy et al. 2024a
.
Such emergent behaviors are not only ecologically important, especially in contexts such as predator avoidance, migration, and foraging, but also serve as inspiration for the development of bioinspired algorithms in swarm robotics and distributed decision-making systems
Brambilla et al. 2013
.

Stochastic modeling
Gardiner 1985
;
Øksendal 2014
offers a compelling framework to capture the inherently probabilistic nature of animal motion
Méndez et al. 2014
;
Codling et al. 2008
.
In particular, random walk dynamics have proven to be effective in describing individual- and group-level animal behaviors under uncertainty, including foraging strategies
Viswanathan et al. 2011
, spatial exploration
Bartumeus et al. 2003
, and orientation
Codling et al. 2008
.
When applied to fish schools, this framework allows us to dissect the relationship between global group motion and the local deviations of individuals around that motion using statistical physics approaches
Murakami et al. 2015
.
Here, we extend this approach by introducing a method to estimate effective mean-field potentials and generalized noise terms from empirical data that can effectively describe the collective movement patterns observed in experimental fish school.

Our modeling approach involves a decomposition of the system into two essential components: (i) the stochastic dynamics of the school’s center of mass (CM), and (ii) the relative motion of individual fish with respect to this CM reference frame.
We show that the former can be captured by an active Brownian particle influenced by environmental constraints, such as spatial boundaries, while the latter can be modeled through stochastic differential equations incorporating an effective mean-field
potential and multiplicative noise, a widespread feature in both natural and engineered systems
Horsthemke and Lefever 2006
.
This dual-level formulation provides a coherent means of quantifying the contributions of spatial confinement, social interactions, and intrinsic variability in shaping group trajectories.
Unlike additive noise, multiplicative noise depends on the system’s current state and can significantly influence its dynamics.
It plays a crucial role in driving transitions between states and intermittent patterns of activity, such as those observed in synchronization, in neuronal systems, or in population dynamics in ecology
Bauermann and Lindner 2019
.
State transitions and intermittent turning cascades have also been reported in the dynamics of schooling fish
Puy et al. 2024b
;
Múgica et al. 2022
.
Ultimately, our findings contribute to a deeper theoretical understanding of self-organization in biological collectives and offer a robust foundation for generalizing these principles to other systems of interacting agents.

To analyze the movement of schooling fish within an experimental tank, we decompose the laboratory-frame trajectory of each individual, denoted as
\vec{r}_{i}^{\mathrm{lab}}(t)
, into two components:

\vec{r}_{i}^{\mathrm{lab}}(t)=\vec{R}(t)+\vec{r}_{i}(t).

(1)

The first term represent the CM of the group, defined as
\vec{R}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{r}_{i}^{\mathrm{lab}}(t)/N
, which describes the collective position of the school at any time
t
.
The second component,

\vec{r}_{i}(t)=\vec{r}_{i}^{\mathrm{lab}}(t)-\vec{R}(t)
, captures the motion of each individual relative to the CM reference frame.
This decomposition allows us to separate the collective (CM) dynamics from the internal fluctuations of individuals around the group’s motion.
The characteristics of relative motion in the CM reference frame have been previously explored in the literature, particularly in studies of diffusive behavior and collective fluctuations in flocks and schools
Cavagna et al. 2013
;
Murakami et al. 2015
.
To empirically investigate both the behavior of the CM trajectory and the internal dynamics relative to it, we analyzed the motion of individuals in schools of black neon tetra (
Hyphessobrycon herbertaxelrodi
), a species known for its strong schooling behavior and responsiveness to environmental cues. The fish were freely swimming in a quasi-two-dimensional experimental tank. For full experimental details, see Supplemental Material (SM) Sec. SM-I.

We begin by analyzing the dynamics of the school’s CM trajectory. In the top panels of Fig.
1
(top panels), we present color density plots of the probability density functions (PDFs) of the CM’s position and velocity.

. Results for other system sizes are presented in the Supplementary Figure SM11.

The figure reveals that the CM trajectory exhibits persistent motion, constrained by the boundaries of the experimental tank.
Notably, the trajectory of the CM tends to slide along the walls, a behavior consistent with previous observations in real fish schools
Calovi et al. 2018
;
Lecheval and Mann 2023
.
This wall-following tendency reflects the interplay between the self-propelled dynamics of the group and the confining geometry of the environment. Note that this does not imply that the center of mass continuously moves in a loop along the tank walls; rather, it may follow a more complex trajectory. This is illustrated in Fig. SF12, where a portion of the trajectory is overlaid on the position PDF. A slight asymmetry in the distribution, which also appears as a tilt in the CM velocity PDF, arises from anticipatory turning near corners. This occurs as the group adjusts its trajectory upon simultaneously perceiving two adjacent walls—an effect that becomes apparent in larger groups but is absent in smaller ones, see Fig. SF11. Meanwhile, the PDF of the CM velocity exhibits a characteristic crater-shaped profile, typical of self-propelled active particles

Romanczuk et al. 2012
.
This indicates that the movement of the school’s CM can be understood within the framework of active matter physics, influenced by confinement forces arising from the boundary effects of the tank walls.

The velocity PDF lacks rotational symmetry and is further influenced by the square shape of the tank. This leads to pronounced peaks along the positive and negative axes, stemming from abrupt turns at the tank’s corners. These sharp turns cause transient velocity boosts, manifesting as localized maxima in the distribution. Interestingly, the velocity components along the principal axes can be accurately modeled using a Gaussian mixture, with observations in other systems of self-propelled particles
Erdmann et al. 2000
, see SM Fig. SF1. To replicate this behavior observed experimentally, we model the CM dynamics using a set of stochastic differential equations that describe an active Brownian pseudo-particle with a characteristic size corresponding to the average radius of gyration of the school,
R_{g}
.
The assumption of a finite size naturally arises from the fact that the CM of the school cannot approach the walls as closely as any individual fish. This pseudo-particle is subjected to a confining force—originating from the boundaries of the tank—as well as an active friction term and a noise term characteristic of self-propelled systems
Romanczuk et al. 2012
:

\frac{d\vec{R}}{dt}=\vec{V},\qquad\frac{d\vec{V}}{dt}=\vec{F}(\vec{R})-\gamma(V)\vec{V}+\sqrt{2D}\xi(t).

(2)

For the active friction, we choose a Schienbein-Gruler-type form
-\gamma(V)\vec{V}=-\gamma_{0}(V-V_{0})\frac{\vec{V}}{V}
, with
V
the CM’s velocity modulus, proposed for the biological movement of non-polar particles with characteristic speed
V_{0}

Schienbein and Gruler 1993
.
For the confining force, we adopt the non-potential hard-wall model proposed in
Calovi et al. 2018
to account for the tendency of fish to move parallel to walls:

\vec{F}(\vec{R})=U_{0}\sum_{w\in\mathrm{Walls}}\exp\left\{\frac{R_{g}-d_{w}}{\ell}\right\}g(\theta_{w})\hat{n}_{w},

(3)

where the sum extends over the four tank walls. Here,
U_{0}
modulates the force strength,
\hat{n}_{w}
is the vector normal to the wall
w
,
R_{g}
is the pseudo-particle size,
d_{w}
is the distance of the CM to the wall
w
,
\ell
is a characteristic length scale, and
\theta_{w}
is the angle between the velocity
\vec{V}
and the normal vector
\hat{n}_{w}
. The function
g(\theta_{w})=1+a\cos(2\theta_{w})
modulates the force, making it weaker when the pseudo-particle moves parallel to the wall and stronger if it moves toward or away from it. Given the Gaussian mixture nature of the velocity profiles (see SM Fig. SF1), we also incorporate an additive Gaussian uncorrelated noise with intensity
D
. The evaluation of the model parameters is described in SM Sec. SM-II. It is noteworthy that the friction coefficient
\gamma
increases approximately linearly with the pseudo-particle size (see SM Fig. SF2)
, which can be interpreted in analogy with Stoke’s law for the drag on a spherical particle at low Reynolds number
Batchelor 1967
.
We integrate numerically the set of stochastic equations, Eq. (
2
), using the Euler-Maruyama scheme
Toral and Colet 2014
with a timestep equal to the empirical frame interval
\Delta t=0.02
s.
This approach allows the simulations to accurately reproduce the empirical PDFs of the CM position and velocity, as shown in Fig.
1
(bottom).

We now focus on the behavior of the fish relative to the CM. In Figs.
2
(a) and (c), we present color density plots of the PDFs of fish position and velocity in this reference frame. Both distributions exhibit a clear rotational symmetry. For the position distribution, we compute the radial density
\rho(r)
, shown in Fig.
2
(b).
This quantity displays a universal scaling behavior across different school sizes, expressed as
\rho(r)=\rho_{0}F(r/R_{g})
, where
\rho_{0}=N/(\pi R_{g}^{2})
is the average density for each data set. Regarding the velocity PDFs, the distributions along the axes, shown in Fig.
2
(d), reveal a distinct exponential, non-Gaussian shape, suggesting the possible presence of multiplicative noise. Exponentially tailed speed distributions have also been observed in large midge swarms
Kelley and Ouellette 2013
. In this context, the impact of multiplicative noise has been analyzed
Reynolds and Ouellette 2016
. Similarly, in the migration of social amoebae, velocity components have been found to follow non-Gaussian distributions
Bödeker et al. 2010
, which has led to the adoption of Langevin dynamics models with multiplicative noise as a natural framework to capture such behavior.

To model fish behavior in the center of mass (CM) frame, we employ a system of stochastic equations in polar coordinates. This system describes the velocity modulus or speed
v
, the orientation of the velocity vector
\theta
, and the turning rate
\omega
, which is related to the angular velocity of the velocity vector
Zienkiewicz et al. 2015
;
Gautrais et al. 2012
. Supplementary Fig. SF14 illustrates the PDF of the turning rate
\omega
, which exhibits a non-Gaussian form. In Supplementary Fig. SF15, we examine the correlations and cross-correlations between
v
and
\omega
. This figure reveals that the empirically measured speed and turning rate show weak cross-correlations between different fish, compared to the strong auto-correlation observed for the same fish.
This observation suggests that the behavior of a fish interacting with other members of the school can be effectively represented as that of an
isolated
individual subject to a potential that, in a mean-field approximation, captures the influence of the group. While this approximation is a priory quite stringent, we will demonstrate that it allows us to reproduce many features observed in empirical schools.
Accordingly, we propose to model the movement of individual fish as independent in the CM reference frame, with interactions between fish being replaced by a confining effective potential
U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)
that prevents them from drifting away from the CM. To estimate the form of this effective confining potential, we draw inspiration from the example of equilibrium Brownian motion in a potential
U
with constant friction
Romanczuk et al. 2012
.
In this case, the radial particle distribution is proportional to
e^{-U(r)/A}
, where the constant
A
depends on temperature.
Assuming this relation holds in our non-equilibrium context, we define the confining effective potential as
\exp\left\{-U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)/A\right\}=\rho(r)
, where
\rho(r)
is the empirically observed radial particle density illustrated in Fig.
2
(b).
The empirical
\rho(r)
is nearly flat for
r<R_{g}
and decays exponentially for
r>R_{g}
.
Therefore, we choose to fit it with the continuous function

\exp\left\{-U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)/A\right\}=\rho(r)\equiv\frac{a}{b+e^{\alpha r}},

(4)

where the parameters
a
,
b
, and
\alpha
are obtained from a non-linear curve fitting, see SM Sec. SM-III. From this expression, the related effective force is

\vec{f}_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)=-\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)}{\partial r}\hat{r}\equiv-Af_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)\;\hat{r},

(5)

where
\hat{r}=\vec{r}/r
and the force
f_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)=\alpha/(be^{-\alpha r}+1)
is approximately zero for
r<R_{g}
, since
\alpha\propto 1/R_{g}
(see Fig. SF3), and constant for
r\gg R_{g}
. Insect swarms also exhibit large-scale cohesion that maintains individuals’ connectivity, as noted in Ref.
Kelley and Ouellette 2013
. That study suggested the emergence of an effective potential well that binds individuals to the
swarm, while allowing them to move freely within it. This interpretation closely aligns with our findings on school cohesion, where we observe a smoothly increasing potential well that confines individuals within the group, as described by Eq. (
4
). However, for
r<R_{g}
, they behave like particles in a free gas system.

Under these assumptions, the motion of a fish in the CM reference frame is described by the following set of stochastic equations, expressed in polar coordinates and in the Itô representation
Gardiner 1985
:

\begin{split}\dot{x}&=v\cos\theta,\qquad\dot{y}=v\sin\theta,\\
\dot{v}&=F_{\mathrm{eff}}^{v}+f_{v}(v)+g_{v}(v)\;\xi_{v}(t),\\
\dot{\theta}&=\omega+F_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\theta},\\
\dot{\omega}&=f_{\omega}(\omega)+g_{\omega}(\omega)\;\xi_{\omega}(t).\end{split}

(6)

In the previous set of equations, the terms
F_{\mathrm{eff}}^{v}
and
F_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\theta}
represent the forces induced by the confining potential when expressed in polar coordinates (see SM Sec.
SM-IV
). In this coarse-grained description, the terms
f_{\alpha}
and
g_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha=v,\omega
, represent drift and diffusion components resulting from additional fish interactions
Romanczuk and Schimansky-Geier 2011
. To estimate these coefficients, we reason that, since they do not originate from the confining potential, they can be empirically determined from data on freely moving fish—specifically, from trajectories confined to
r<R_{g}
, where the trapping potential is essentially flat and the corresponding force is negligible.

We apply a kernel-based regression method
Lamouroux and Lehnertz 2009
to extract the first two moments of the Kramers-Moyal series. This method has been successfully used to analyze the fluctuating dynamics of various species, including
C. elegans

Costa et al. 2024
, zebra fish
Jhawar and Guttal 2020
, and migration of social amoeba
Bödeker et al. 2010
.
The resulting drift and diffusion coefficients, in the freely swimming regime, can be fitted to the functional forms:
f_{v}(v)=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{v}-\gamma_{2}v
;
g_{v}(v)=\delta_{1}v+\delta_{2}
;
f_{\omega}(\omega)=-\kappa\omega
and
g_{\omega}(\omega)=n_{1}|\omega|+n_{2}
. These drift and diffusion terms will, in a coarse-grained manner, encapsulate the complex interactions between fish that may arise from selective social behaviors, hydrodynamic effects, and higher-order interactions. Details of the fitting procedure are provided in SM Sec. SM-V. The specific parameter values for different experimental realizations are listed in SM, Supplemental Table ST3.
The only free parameter in this model,
A
, is adjusted by minimizing the squared error of the observable most sensitive to it, the radial distribution
\rho(r)
. See SM Sec. SM-V for additional details.

As we have seen, Eqs. (
2
) and (
6
) describe the movement of the school CM and the relative movement of fish in the CM frame, respectively.
By combining these equations, we can simulate
synthetic
schools of any size
N
: generating a single trajectory for the CM using Eqs. (
2
), and
N
independent trajectories in the CM frame using Eqs. (
6
), recovering the trajectories of
N
synthetic fish in the laboratory reference frame by using Eq. (
1
).
We can gain an analytical insight of such large-scale synthetic schools in the limit
N\to\infty
, which corresponds to a very large radius of gyration and, consequently, a very large tank.
In this limit, the confining force
F(\vec{R})
in Eq. (
2
) and the induced forces
F_{\mathrm{eff}}^{v}
and
F_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\theta}
in Eq. (
6
) can be neglected. Under these conditions, the distributions of speed and turning angle can be derived analytically and show good agreement with empirical data from trajectories within the region
r<R_{g}
, where size effects should be minimal (see SM Sec. SM-VII). For finite size
N
, numerical integration of the stochastic differential equations allows the generation of synthetic schools in which individuals exhibit many properties—both at the individual and collective levels—that are indistinguishable from those of real schools (see SM Sec. SM-VIII). In particular, at the individual level, our formalism reproduces the burst-and-coast swimming strategy characteristic of species like the black neon tetra
Videler and Weihs 1982
, which involves rapid acceleration bursts alternating with passive gliding (Supplementary Figure SF8). Additionally, it captures the mean square displacement of individual fish (Supplementary Figure Fig. SF9). At the collective level, our modeling framework accurately predicts several properties, such as the distribution of polarization (see Fig.
3
(d)) and the duration that individual fish spend at the boundary of the school (Supplementary Figure SF10). For further details, see SM Sec. SM-VIII.

In conclusion, this study presents a stochastic framework for modeling the swimming behavior of fish within an experimental tank. The approach separately considers the movement of the school’s center of mass and the individual dynamics of fish relative to this center. The central assumption is that, in the CM frame, fish can be approximated as moving independently under the influence of an effective mean-field potential that confines their motion. This potential encapsulates the attractive and alignment interactions between pairs of fish, while additional drift and multiplicative noise terms—derived from our experimental data—would capture more complex behavioral aspects, including non-reciprocal social interactions
(
Puy et al. 2024a
)
, hydrodynamic effects, and higher-order interactions. Altogether, this parsimonious yet powerful framework provides a comprehensive description of the emergent dynamics observed in real fish schools.

Our formalism successfully reproduces many key properties observed in real fish schools, including the velocity distributions and emergent collective behaviors. However, since interactions are treated at a mean-field level, the model inherently has limitations. Notably, it does not explicitly incorporate pairwise attractive and alignment forces, which means it cannot capture finer-scale social structures, such as the local density of neighbors around a focal fish
Katz et al. 2011
.
Future extensions could explore applications to other schooling species, such as zebrafish, which tend to form swarms with minimal global polarization, or to different animal groups exhibiting varied social and collective behaviors. Such developments would enhance the model’s versatility and deepen our understanding of the underlying principles governing collective animal movement.
