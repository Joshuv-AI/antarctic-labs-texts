Association between hypertension status and severity and tinnitus: a cross-sectional analysis of the Fasa adult cohort study
Authors: Amirhossein Najibi, Sina Bazmi, Mehrnaz Moradi, Zahra Mohammadi, Abdulhakim Al Kamel, Mohammad Hosein Yazdanpanah, Motjaba Farjam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies and meta-analyses suggest an association between hypertension and tinnitus; however, the influence of hypertension severity and control status remains unclear.ObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the association between hypertension and tinnitus in detail using a large, population-based dataset from a rural setting.DesignObservational cross-sectional study.SettingSheshdeh, Fasa, Iran.ParticipantsWe analysed data from 9775 individuals in the general population, aged 35–70 years, excluding those with a history of cancer, pregnancy or medical conditions known to cause tinnitus, such as stroke, seizures or multiple sclerosis. Additionally, although the study design aimed to exclude participants using aminoglycosides because of their significant ototoxic effects, no such users were identified during the study period.Primary and secondary outcomesHypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥140 mm Hg or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥90 mm Hg on at least two separate measurements or as current use of antihypertensive medications following a prior diagnosis. These medications included ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, diuretics, aldosterone antagonists and atenolol. Stage I hypertension was classified as an SBP of 140–159 mm Hg or a DBP of 90–99 mm Hg, while stage II was defined as an SBP of ≥160 mm Hg or a DBP of ≥100 mm Hg. Controlled blood pressure was defined as values below these thresholds. Tinnitus, assessed by a self-reported questionnaire, was defined as a continuous wheezing sound in the ear persisting for more than 1 week.ResultsAmong participants (4446 males, 5309 females; mean age 48.55 (SD 9.53) years), the prevalence of tinnitus and hypertension was 7.4% and 19.3%, respectively. Hypertension was significantly associated with higher odds of tinnitus (adjusted OR=1.34; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.62). Notably, even participants with controlled hypertension had a 27% increased odds (OR=1.27; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.59) compared with normotensive individuals. The odds were highest in those with uncontrolled grade II hypertension (OR=2.08; 95% CI 1.25 to 3.47), demonstrating a dose-response relationship.ConclusionsOur findings suggest a positive association between hypertension and tinnitus, with odds increasing alongside the severity and poor control of hypertension. Importantly, even controlled hypertension was associated with elevated odds, indicating that tinnitus screening may be warranted in all hypertensive patients, regardless of control status. These results underscore the need for heightened clinical awareness and further research into the pathophysiological mechanisms linking vascular health and auditory symptoms.Keywords: cardiovascular diseases, essential hypertension, hearing disorders, hypertension management, tinnitus

Introduction

Tinnitus is characterised by the perception of an unpleasant auditory sensation, often without an external sound source. It has been classified into two categories by Itard in the nineteenth century: subjective and objective.1 Globally, subjective tinnitus has a prevalence of 14% in the adult population, with 2% of this population experiencing a severe form of the condition.2 The prevalence of subjective tinnitus in an Iranian population was 6% according to a cohort study by Panahi et al.3 In severe cases, tinnitus can become disabling and impair an individual’s quality of life.4 5

Given the high prevalence of tinnitus and its disabling nature, epidemiological studies are needed to identify risk factors. Tinnitus has been linked to various psychological, neurological, infectious and neoplastic pathologies.6,9 Among cardiovascular risk factors, hypertension has emerged as a potential contributor to the development of tinnitus.10

Hypertension is defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) equal to or above 140 mm Hg and a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) equal to or above 90 mm Hg.11 A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Yang et al established a significant association between tinnitus and hypertension.12 Although controversial to some extent, this association has been linked to the direct pathological impact of hypertension by some authors13 14; while others have attributed it to ototoxic effects of anti-hypertensive medications. For instance, a study by Figueiredo et al identified a relationship between certain antihypertensive medications (diuretics, ACE inhibitors and calcium channel blockers) and tinnitus.15 By contrast, Ramatsoma and Patrick showed that tinnitus was more prevalent among hypertensive individuals, particularly those with grade three hypertension, but they failed to find a significant difference in tinnitus prevalence between those taking antihypertensive medications and those who did not.16 On the other hand, Samelli et al reported no significant association between hypertension and tinnitus after adjusting the analysis for age, introducing some controversy into the existing literature.17 In a cross-sectional study by Patel et al, the association between tinnitus and hypertension was significant in the population younger than 40 but not in the older population, suggesting a counterintuitive effect of age on tinnitus among hypertensive individuals.18 Among population-based studies, Schubert et al found an association between tinnitus and hypertension in the lifeline cohort study.19 Conversely, Huang et al found no correlation between a history of hypertension and tinnitus in their cohort.20 Finally, the role of hypertension control (ie, whether controlled hypertension is still associated with tinnitus) has been less extensively studied. Although Ramatsoma and Patrick showed that grade three hypertension significantly increases the odds of tinnitus compared with controlled hypertension, they did not compare the prevalence of tinnitus in those with controlled hypertension compared with normotensive individuals.

In the present study, we used a large, contemporary cohort from southern Iran to investigate the association between hypertension and tinnitus. We addressed the prevalence of tinnitus among individuals with hypertension and the associated impact of antihypertensive drugs, hypertension grading and hypertension control. This analysis aims to elucidate potential interrelations between these two conditions in Fasa Persian Cohort.

The Fasa Adult Cohort Study (FACS) provides a unique opportunity to examine this research question in a large, rural, community-based Middle Eastern population with detailed cardiovascular, behavioural and clinical data collected using standardised protocols. Unlike many prior studies that were clinic-based or limited to treated hypertensive populations, this cohort includes individuals across the full spectrum of blood pressure (BP) status, including untreated, treated, controlled and uncontrolled hypertension. Leveraging this dataset allows simultaneous evaluation of hypertension grading, BP control status and antihypertensive medication use within a single analytic framework. In particular, hypertension control status, reflecting real-world BP management rather than diagnosis alone, has been largely overlooked in prior tinnitus research and may provide clinically relevant insights beyond binary hypertension classification. We hypothesised that higher hypertension grades and uncontrolled BP would be associated with an increased likelihood of tinnitus, independent of antihypertensive medication use.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted on adults aged 35–70 years from FACS (n=10 138), a subset of the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) cohort focused on non-communicable diseases.21 22 The present study used baseline data from the FACS, a population-based cohort conducted in Fasa County, southern Iran. In the enrolment phase (2015–2016), all households within the predefined catchment area were identified using local census data and were systematically approached by trained field staff through door-to-door visits. All permanent residents aged 35–70 years were invited to participate. Participants who agreed to enrol were referred to a designated cohort centre, where data collection was performed by trained healthcare personnel using standardised protocols. Structured questionnaires were administered through face-to-face interviews to collect sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history and medication use. Physical examinations, including BP measurements, anthropometric assessments and other clinical evaluations, were conducted on-site at the cohort centre. The cohort achieved a high participation rate, comparable to other large population-based cohort studies conducted in Iran.21 For the current analysis, data were accessed in October 2024. A summary of participant recruitment is illustrated in online supplemental figure S1. Individuals with cancer, pregnancy or conditions known to cause tinnitus, such as stroke, seizures or multiple sclerosis,23 were excluded. Furthermore, although participants using aminoglycosides were intended to be excluded due to their considerable ototoxic side effects, none were identified as users at the time of the study.24 A total of 9755 participants were included in the final analysis.

Covariates

Baseline variables considered in the analysis included age, sex, marital status (single, married, widowed or divorced), socioeconomic status (measured using the Asset Index),25 education level (university degree or not), physical activity (calculated in metabolic equivalent of tasks over 24 hours), smoking (current regular smoker or not), alcohol use (defined as consuming approximately 200 mL of beer or 45 mL of liquor weekly for at least 6 months), illicit drug use (defined as weekly use of illicit drugs for 6 months or more), employment status (defined as working at least 8 hours per week including seasonal work), and daily caloric intake (calculated in kcal).

Additional variables relevant to tinnitus included excessive caffeine consumption (more than 400 mg/day),26 short sleep duration (nighttime sleep of fewer than 6 hours),23 hyperlipidaemia (high-density lipoprotein ≤40 mg/dL in men or ≤50 mg/dL in women, total cholesterol ≥200 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein ≥130 mg/dL or use of lipid-lowering medications),27 and ototoxic medication use (excluding aminoglycosides but including macrolides (eg, azithromycin, clarithromycin and erythromycin),24 nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, aspirin,28 loop diuretics,29 chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine,30 enalapril, lisinopril, amitriptyline and nortriptyline28). Other covariates included factors potentially influencing tinnitus outcomes, such as depression, other psychiatric disorders (primarily anxiety), rheumatoid arthritis, chronic lung diseases (including asthma), thyroid disorders, illicit drug use and concurrent dizziness.23 Sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, educational level and marital status), lifestyle factors (smoking status, physical activity and sleep duration), medical history (physician-diagnosed diabetes and dyslipidaemia) and medication use were collected through structured face-to-face interviews using standardised questionnaires.

Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were measured by trained healthcare personnel using calibrated instruments, and body mass index was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). BP measurements were obtained using standardised protocols as described below. Laboratory-based variables were derived from fasting blood samples collected at baseline.

The mean imputation method was applied to address the missing sleep duration data for 369 individuals (<5%). Given the low proportion of missing data and the role of sleep duration as a covariate rather than a primary exposure or outcome, mean imputation was considered unlikely to materially influence the estimated associations. This pragmatic approach has been commonly applied in large epidemiological studies under similar conditions. Missingness in the dataset covariates was negligible. Except for sleep duration, all variables used in the analyses had complete data.

Main independent and outcome variables

The participants’ BP was measured with a conventional mercury sphygmomanometer on the left arm while seated, after participants rested for 15 min.31 Two BP readings were taken 5 min apart, and the average SBP and DBPs (in mm Hg) were recorded. Hypertension was defined as having a SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mm Hg on at least two occasions, or current use of antihypertensive medications following a prior diagnosis.32 No hypertension was defined as the absence of a prior physician diagnosis of hypertension, no use of antihypertensive medications, and measured SBP <140 mm Hg and DBP <90 mm Hg. Controlled hypertension was defined as having a prior physician diagnosis of hypertension and/or current use of antihypertensive medication, with measured SBP <140 mm Hg and DBP <90 mm Hg at the study visit. A subset of these participants had a prior diagnosis of hypertension but were not using antihypertensive medications at the time of assessment, indicating BP control achieved through non-pharmacological measures such as lifestyle modification. Uncontrolled hypertension was defined as SBP ≥140 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥90 mm Hg, regardless of antihypertensive medication use.

BP-lowering medications included ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), diuretics, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), and beta-blockers. Stage I hypertension was defined as an SBP of 140–159 mm Hg or a DBP of 90–99 mm Hg, while stage II was defined as an SBP ≥160 mm Hg or a DBP ≥100 mm Hg.33

Tinnitus was characterised by the perception of sound (eg, ringing, buzzing or wheezing) without an external source, persisting for over 5 min and occurring at least once weekly over the preceding 12 months.34

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were presented as means and SDs for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Univariable analyses were performed using the independent t-test for continuous variables and the χ² test for categorical variables to assess their relationship with tinnitus.

Multivariable analyses were conducted using logistic regression to calculate ORs and 95% CIs. The models were adjusted for key confounding variables identified through literature review and univariable analysis (p<0.2). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors, with no evidence of problematic collinearity observed. Potential interaction effects between key covariates were explored, and no statistically significant interactions were identified. Results were reported separately for subgroups of patients using and not using BP-lowering medications to explore whether the association between hypertension and tinnitus persisted when BP was controlled or if tinnitus could be attributed to the ototoxic effects of the medications. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.23 (IBM), and a two-sided significance level of 0.05 was applied. For subgroup analyses with sparse data or evidence of separation, Firth’s penalised likelihood logistic regression was applied using Stata to reduce small-sample bias and obtain stable parameter estimates.35

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Population characteristics

The study included 9755 participants, comprising 4446 (45.6%) males and 5309 (54.4%) females, with a mean (SD) age of 48.55 (9.53) years. Tinnitus was reported by 728 individuals (7.46%), and 1887 participants (19.3%) had hypertension. The mean SBP and DBP were 111.32 (18.35) and 74.66 (11.93) mm Hg, respectively. Among individuals with hypertension, 1106 (11.3%) had controlled hypertension, 667 (6.8%) had grade I hypertension and 114 (1.2%) had grade II hypertension. A total of 1399 participants (14.3% of the population) were taking BP-lowering medications. Table 1 provides detailed characteristics of the study population.

Univariable analyses

As shown in table 1, tinnitus prevalence was significantly higher in females (p<0.001), older adults (p<0.001), individuals with lower physical activity levels (p<0.001), those with lower socioeconomic status (p<0.001), non-academic degree holders (p=0.01), unemployed participants (p<0.001) and those with lower daily calorie intake (p=0.02). Tinnitus also showed significant associations with rheumatoid arthritis (p<0.001), chronic lung disease (p<0.001), thyroid disorders (p=0.003), depression (p<0.001), other psychiatric conditions (p<0.001), hyperlipidaemia (p=0.03), short sleep duration (p<0.001), ototoxic medication use (p<0.001), dizziness (p<0.001), smoking (p=0.003), illicit drug use (p<0.001), regular alcohol consumption (p=0.006) and hypertension (p<0.001).

Multivariable analyses

Adjusted logistic regression analysis demonstrated that individuals with hypertension had 34% higher odds of tinnitus (OR=1.34, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.62). However, the association between hypertension and tinnitus was not statistically significant in either individuals using BP-lowering medications or those not using them, although the effect size was larger in the former group (OR=1.26 vs OR=1.13) (table 2, Table S1 and figure 1). The interaction between hypertension and the use of BP-lowering medication was also not significant (p=0.36).

The study’s key findings are summarised in a graphical abstract (online supplemental figure S2).

Among hypertensive individuals, compared with those with controlled hypertension, those taking BP-lowering medications had a 15% increased likelihood of tinnitus, but this finding did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, individuals with grade I hypertension had a 1% higher likelihood of tinnitus, and those with grade II hypertension had a 60% higher likelihood compared with those with controlled hypertension, but neither association was statistically significant (online supplemental table S2).

In sex-stratified analyses, hypertension was associated with higher odds of tinnitus among females but not males (online supplemental table S3). In model 1, hypertension was significantly associated with tinnitus in females (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.63; p=0.02), whereas the association in males did not reach statistical significance (OR 1.46, 95% CI 0.99 to 2.14; p=0.06).

When hypertension severity was considered with normotension as the reference (model 2), grade II hypertension was significantly associated with tinnitus in females (OR 2.55, 95% CI 1.45 to 4.45; p=0.001). No significant associations were observed among males for controlled, grade I, or grade II hypertension. In females, controlled and grade I hypertension were not significantly associated with tinnitus.

Using controlled hypertension as the reference (model 3), females with grade II hypertension had significantly higher odds of tinnitus (OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.15 to 3.73; p=0.02), whereas no significant associations were observed among males for either grade I or grade II hypertension.

There was no significant interaction between sex and hypertension status or severity in any model (all Ps for interaction >0.05), indicating that although the association appeared stronger and statistically significant in females, particularly for grade II hypertension, the sex differences were not statistically significant on an interaction scale.

Among participants receiving antihypertensive treatment, beta-blockers were the most frequently used medication class (60.8%), followed by ARBs (31.1%), ACE inhibitors (16.0%), CCBs (3.8%) and diuretics (13.6%) (online supplemental table S4). Because combination therapy was common, the total percentage exceeded 100%.

Most treated participants were on monotherapy (64.1%), while 24.9% received dual therapy, 9.6% triple therapy and 1.2% used four or more antihypertensive medication classes (online supplemental table S5), indicating that approximately one-third required combination regimens for BP control.

In multivariable logistic regression models, hypertension was not significantly associated with tinnitus across any antihypertensive medication class subgroup (online supplemental table S6). Compared with individuals without hypertension, no statistically significant associations with tinnitus were observed for ACE inhibitor monotherapy, ARB monotherapy, beta-blocker monotherapy, diuretic monotherapy, CCB monotherapy or combination therapy. No comparisons reached statistical significance (all p>0.05). Because sparse data and evidence of separation were observed in some medication-class subgroup analyses, particularly for CCBs, Firth’s penalised likelihood logistic regression was applied in these models to improve estimate stability. Nevertheless, CIs remained wide because of the limited sample size within several antihypertensive medication subgroups.

Discussion

There is limited evidence on how hypertension control and its grading contribute to the association between hypertension and tinnitus. Studies exploring such relationships have yielded mixed results and the causality as well as directionality of such associations has remained elusive. Our comprehensive analysis of over 9000 individuals from a rural cohort of the general population revealed a significantly higher prevalence of self-reported tinnitus in hypertensive patients, compared with normotensive individuals after adjusting for age, sex and a large set of sociodemographic and clinical variables. Those with grade II hypertension have the highest odds for experiencing tinnitus, but the association seems to fall within a spectrum; with tinnitus being almost 30% more prevalent even in those with controlled hypertension, compared with normotensive controls.

Tinnitus poses a substantial burden on health-related quality of life, especially among females, older adults and patients of Asian ethnicity.36 A meta-analysis of the global prevalence and incidence of tinnitus in 2022 revealed that it affects more than 740 million patients globally and that a large number of them perceive tinnitus as a major health problem.2 Nevertheless, evidence regarding its risk factors and comorbidities is scarce and lacks well-designed longitudinal studies required for causal inference.37 A number of previous studies have established a positive correlation between tinnitus and hypertension.15 16 18 38 39 Consistent with our findings, the majority of these studies have noted an age-related increase in tinnitus prevalence among adults with hypertension. However, a study by Patel et al found that tinnitus is associated with hypertension in adults aged 20–39 years and not in those above 40 years old.18 A number of animal studies have depicted the age-related deterioration in endochochlear protection against high BP effects as well as disruptions in the blood-perilymph barrier.40 41 This is in line with the clinical observation of tinnitus being more prevalent in the elderly, even in the absence of hypertension.2 36 Nevertheless, whether age plays an independent role in the pathogenesis of tinnitus is not clear.

Several plausible biological mechanisms may link hypertension to tinnitus. Chronic systemic hypertension is associated with altered vascular function, including arterial stiffness and endothelial dysfunction, which can compromise microvascular perfusion in end-organs such as the inner ear. Increased arterial stiffness has been directly associated with tinnitus in clinical studies, suggesting that impaired vascular compliance and altered haemodynamics may alter cochlear blood flow and sound perception (eg, increased pulse-wave velocity and central pulse pressure in tinnitus patients).42 The cochlea relies on finely regulated microcirculation to maintain the endocochlear potential and tissue oxygenation; aberrations in this microvascular supply could lead to local hypoxia, oxidative stress and metabolic disturbance of sensory hair cells and stria vascularis, which have been implicated in tinnitus and sensorineural hearing loss pathophysiology.13 43 Experimental evidence suggests that chronic hypertension can reduce cochlear endocochlear potential and disrupt ion homeostasis, further compromising auditory transduction.15 Additionally, systemic endothelial dysfunction, characterised by reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic states, may further impair microvascular regulation and the blood–labyrinth barrier, potentiating cochlear hypoperfusion and sensory dysfunction seen in tinnitus.44 45 Although these mechanisms remain to be confirmed in targeted physiological studies specifically linking BP variation to tinnitus percepts, they provide biologically plausible pathways by which hypertension could influence auditory outcomes.

In line with previous analyses,2 36 sex disparities in the prevalence of tinnitus extend beyond simple dichotomous classifications of hypertension. The observation that tinnitus was associated with hypertension only in females and not in males may be explained by the higher prevalence of tinnitus in this group rather than differential effects of hypertension on microvascular and macrovascular circulation. Although hypertension was statistically associated with tinnitus only among females in sex-stratified analyses, formal interaction testing did not support a statistically significant difference between sexes. This pattern may reflect a higher baseline prevalence of tinnitus among females, leading to greater statistical power to detect associations, rather than true sex-specific pathophysiological differences. This notion is supported by the observation that in a Japanese population in which tinnitus was slightly more prevalent in males, it was associated with hypertension in both sexes.46 Racial and cultural differences in the pathophysiology and management of hypertension and/or tinnitus may also explain part of this heterogeneity.38 47 Further animal and epidemiological studies are needed to confirm or reject these hypotheses.

In the present study, we did not observe a statistically significant association between antihypertensive medication use and tinnitus, either overall or across specific antihypertensive drug classes. In class-specific analyses, no significant associations with tinnitus were identified for ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta-blockers, diuretics, CCBs or combination therapy, compared with individuals without hypertension. However, several of these subgroup analyses were limited by small sample sizes, resulting in wide CIs and reduced statistical power. By contrast, a study by Figueiredo et al found tinnitus to be associated with the use of ACE inhibitors, thiazide diuretics and CCBs, suggesting a possible ototoxic effect of these agents.15 Another prospective study by Borghi et al has also reported tinnitus to be associated with the use of diuretics in patients with hypertension.48 However, it is worth mentioning that the study by Borghi et al did not include a normotensive control group, which limits conclusions regarding the causal role of antihypertensive medications in tinnitus and hearing loss. Moreover, Figueiredo et al reported thiazide and potassium-sparing diuretics but not loop diuretics to be associated with tinnitus,15 a finding that contradicts the traditional body of literature on the ototoxicity of loop diuretics, especially furosemide.29 49 50 Whether local trends in the prescription of diuretics for hypertension affect its association with tinnitus remains to be explored. However, several methodological factors may contribute to discrepancies between our study and others. First, confounding by disease severity and duration of hypertension may influence both the likelihood of being treated with certain drug regimens and the risk of auditory symptoms. For example, longer hypertension duration and higher BP stages have been associated with worse auditory outcomes (eg, hearing thresholds and sensory deficits), independent of medication class, in population analyses.51 These patterns suggest that individuals with more severe or long-standing hypertension, a group more likely to require combination or intensive pharmacotherapy, may inherently have a higher burden of vascular and microcirculatory changes that predispose to tinnitus, independent of drug effects. Second, studies that find associations with specific drug classes often enrol clinical hypertensive populations without normative control groups, limiting causal inference. Additionally, inconsistent findings regarding which classes show associations (eg, thiazide vs loop diuretics) underscore heterogeneity in prescription patterns, hypertension phenotypes and concomitant risk factors across settings, which may confound drug-specific signal detection substantially.15 In our dataset, detailed subgroup analysis across antihypertensive classes did not reveal significant associations with tinnitus, but wide CIs reflect sparse data within each category, limiting power. Thus, residual confounding by underlying hypertension severity and duration, and the corresponding need for specific therapeutic regimens, may be more influential than specific pharmacological ototoxic effects per se. Clearly, prospective studies with repeated measures of BP, objective auditory phenotyping and careful control for hypertension history are needed to disentangle these interrelated determinants.

The degree of hypertension control and its impact on the relationship between tinnitus and hypertension is another less-extensively studied aspect. Not only was tinnitus more prevalent in those with uncontrolled hypertension compared with controlled hypertension, it was also consistently more prevalent in those with controlled hypertension compared with normotensive individuals, according to our analysis. This is partly in line with previous literature reporting a higher prevalence of tinnitus in uncontrolled hypertension, compared with controlled.15 16 48 Additionally, tinnitus was significantly more prevalent in those whose hypertension was controlled by lifestyle modifications and/or other alternative methods (non-pharmacologic measures). This observation is a relatively novel finding and argues against the attribution of tinnitus in hypertensive patients to ototoxic effects of drugs.

Consistent with previous literature,23 52 we found a number of other chronic conditions such as chronic lung disease and rheumatoid arthritis to be associated with tinnitus. Moreover, depression showed a significant correlation with tinnitus prevalence in our cohort, which perfectly aligns with the results of systematic reviews and meta-analyses.53 54

Despite rigorous methodology, large sample size and an inclusive set of covariates, which support generalisability, our study has multiple limitations. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design of our study does not allow any conclusions about a cause-and-effect relationship between tinnitus and hypertension. Therefore, whether tinnitus is a byproduct or a risk factor for hypertension and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, as suggested by two large, nation-wide studies,55 56 is still unknown. Second, we failed to address characteristics of tinnitus such as intensity and laterality as well as detailed audiologic measurements, due to limited available data. Third, tinnitus was assessed through self-report without audiometric confirmation or otologic examination. Although the questionnaire was based on established guideline definitions and commonly used in large-scale epidemiological studies, misclassification cannot be excluded. This limitation may have led to non-differential misclassification, potentially biasing the observed associations toward the null. Although this study adjusted for a wide range of demographic, lifestyle and clinical covariates, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded due to the observational design. In particular, several established causes of tinnitus were not directly measured or included in the analysis, such as lifetime occupational or recreational noise exposure, detailed history of otologic infections or inflammatory conditions, vestibular disorders, and exposure to a comprehensive range of potentially ototoxic medications beyond antihypertensive agents. The absence of these variables may have led to unmeasured confounding and could have attenuated or obscured true associations. However, such non-differential residual confounding would be expected to bias the results toward the null, suggesting that the observed associations are unlikely to be spuriously exaggerated. An additional consideration is the timing of data collection. The present analysis is based on baseline data from the FACS collected between 2015 and 2016. Although follow-up phases of the cohort are ongoing, information on tinnitus was only systematically collected at baseline; therefore, no more recent data with comparable tinnitus assessment were available for this analysis. Importantly, both hypertension and tinnitus are chronic conditions with relatively stable diagnostic definitions over time, suggesting that the observed associations remain clinically relevant. Nevertheless, changes in hypertension management strategies, medication patterns, and population-level cardiovascular risk profiles over the past decade may influence the current magnitude of this association. Future studies incorporating repeated tinnitus assessments are needed to evaluate temporal trends and causal relationships.

Conclusions

This population-based study demonstrates that hypertension, particularly higher BP grades and poor BP control, is associated with increased odds of tinnitus, with more pronounced associations observed among females. These findings highlight the potential value of increased clinical awareness of tinnitus symptoms in individuals with hypertension. Routine enquiry about tinnitus during hypertension assessment may facilitate earlier identification of auditory complaints, improve patient counselling, and prompt appropriate referral when indicated. Longitudinal studies incorporating objective auditory assessments are warranted to clarify causality and inform targeted prevention strategies.
