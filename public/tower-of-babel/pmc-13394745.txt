Plasma Exosome Metabolomics Reveal Stage‐Specific Alterations in Elderly Women With Premetabolic and Metabolic Syndrome
Authors: Mengtao Qian, Dan Nie, Qianyi Gao, Xingbo Mo, Huan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMetabolic syndrome (MetS) is a chronic disorder that poses a major threat to global health. Exosomes have emerged as promising biomarkers for diagnosing and monitoring chronic diseases. However, stage‐specific alterations in the exosomal metabolome during MetS development remain poorly understood. This study aimed to characterize the plasma exosomal metabolome and explore candidate exosomal biomarkers in individuals with MetS.MethodsThis study included 20 patients with MetS, 23 individuals with pre‐MetS, and 45 healthy controls. Plasma exosomes were isolated and analyzed using untargeted liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry‐based metabolomics. Differential metabolites were defined by a dual‐threshold, that is, p < 0.05 from t‐test and variable importance in projection > 1 from partial least squares discriminant analysis, with fold change indicating their expression changes. Further, we employed machine learning algorithms to predict MetS status.ResultsWe identified 27 differential metabolites between the pre‐MetS and control groups, mainly enriched in histidine metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Of these, 12 metabolites were upregulated, and 15 were downregulated, with 1‐methylhistidine and isocitrate playing central regulatory roles. Comparison between the MetS and control groups revealed 45 differentially expressed metabolites, mainly enriched in thiamine metabolism, including 13 upregulated and 32 downregulated. In the pre‐MetS group, cladribine showed the highest area under the curve (AUC) (0.743, p < 0.05), whereas 3‐methylxanthine yielded the largest AUC (0.714, p < 0.05) in the MetS group.ConclusionOur study characterized stage‐dependent alterations in the plasma exosome‐derived metabolome in MetS and suggests that exosomal metabolomics may provide complementary molecular information on early MetS metabolic perturbations.Keywords: exosomes, exosomal features, metabolic syndrome, untargeted metabolomics, women

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) represents the clustering of multiple cardiovascular‐metabolic risk factors, primarily encompassing elevated blood pressure, abnormal blood glucose levels, dyslipidemia, and central obesity [1]. These factors collectively heighten the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and diabetes. With a global prevalence of approximately 20%–25%, MetS is regarded as a major challenge during the industrialization process in developing countries, and it is projected to affect over half of the population in the next two decades [2]. The increase in its prevalence is associated with sedentary lifestyles, environmental factors, and dietary habits, and it has emerged as a significant public health concern [3]. Currently, the prevalence of MetS in China continues to rise. A nationwide survey across 31 provinces revealed an increase from 24.2% in 2010–2012 to 31.1% in 2015–2017 [4, 5]. It is more prevalent in women, and approximately 44% of patients are over 50 years old [6].

MetS pathogenesis involves the interplay of inflammation, insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress [7, 8, 9]. Given this complex pathophysiology, biomarkers are critical for accurate diagnosis, patient stratification, and treatment monitoring. While traditional diagnostics rely on conventional blood measurements such as lipids and insulin, metabolomic and lipidomic approaches offer a powerful alternative, enabling the discovery of novel biomarkers and providing deeper insights into underlying disease mechanisms [10, 11]. Recent studies have implicated specific polar metabolites and lipids, including branched‐chain amino acids, trimethylamine N‐oxide, and betaine, as well as acylcarnitines, phospholipids, sphingomyelins, and triacylglycerols, as promising biomarkers [11]. Recent large‐scale metabolomics studies have shown that circulating metabolic signatures are associated with MetS and pre‐MetS. Plasma metabolic fingerprints have been reported to support MetS risk stratification, and a serum nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics‐based molecular metric has further highlighted the value of metabolomic and lipidomic profiling in MetS risk assessment [12, 13]. However, these studies mainly focused on whole‐plasma or serum profiles, whereas plasma exosome‐derived MetS metabolomic signatures remain poorly understood. Exosomes, 30–150 nm extracellular vesicles that mediate intercellular communication, have emerged as promising biomarkers due to their role in transporting bioactive molecules, including metabolites, between cells [14, 15, 16]. By delivering their metabolic “cargo” to recipient cells, exosomes can alter cellular metabolic pathways and influence the pathogenesis of various diseases. The metabolite profile of exosomes dynamically mirrors the pathophysiological state of their parent cells [17]. These attributes, coupled with their stability and accessibility in bodily fluids such as blood and urine, position exosomal metabolites as ideal candidates for noninvasive diagnostic and prognostic tools [18, 19]. Exosomal metabolites as biomarkers have made significant progress in fields such as oncology and neurodegenerative disorders [17, 20, 21]. However, in the complex pathological state of MetS, its specific exosomal metabolite profiles and their potential as circulating biomarkers for early diagnosis and risk stratification remain largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, previous studies on MetS in this area are mainly based on a single metabolic component; in particular, systematic studies based on plasma exosome metabolomics are relatively scarce.

This study aimed to identify plasma exosomal metabolites associated with MetS and evaluate their predictive ability.

Methods

Study Subjects

According to the criteria proposed by the American Endocrine Society and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists, the diagnosis of MetS requires the presence of at least three abnormalities among the following five indicators: (1) increased waist circumference: ≥ 88 cm for women and ≥ 102 cm for men; (2) elevated triglycerides (TG): ≥ 150 mg/dL); (3) reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C): < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women); (4) elevated blood pressure: systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg, or self‐reported use of antihypertensive medications); and (5) elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG): ≥ 100 mg/dL or self‐reported use of hypoglycemic medications). In addition, the presence of one to two of these metabolic abnormalities is defined as pre‐MetS.

In this study, 100 women were randomly selected from various communities in Suzhou City based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Individuals with failed exosome extraction and substandard quality control of exosomes or those with missing metabolomic detection data were excluded. Ultimately, 88 study subjects were included in the analysis. Subjects were divided into three groups according to the diagnostic criteria for MetS: the MetS group (≥ 3 metabolic abnormal components), comprising 20 individuals; the pre‐MetS group (1–2 metabolic abnormal components), comprising 23 individuals; and the healthy control group (no metabolic abnormal components), comprising 45 individuals.

Baseline data, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and medication use, were collected using a standardized interviewer‐administered questionnaire. Medication information covered antihypertensive, lipid‐lowering, and antidiabetic treatment, including recent use of selected medications. All participants fasted overnight for at least 8 h before blood collection. Fasting venous blood was collected the next morning into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes and centrifuged, and the separated plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis.

Plasma Exosome Isolation and Untargeted Metabolite Detection

Isolation and Purification of Plasma Exosomes

Exosomes were extracted from the plasma using a multistep centrifugation method. Initially, the thawed plasma was sequentially centrifuged at 4°C under conditions of 2000 × g for 30 min and 10,000 × g for 45 min to eliminate cell debris and large vesicles. The resulting supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane, followed by ultracentrifugation at 110,000 × g for 70 min to obtain the exosome pellet. Subsequently, the sample was applied dropwise onto a copper grid, negatively stained with phosphotungstic acid, and examined using transmission electron microscopy to observe its morphology and diameter.

Metabolite Extraction and Liquid Chromatograph‐Mass Spectrometer Analysis

Exosome samples were taken, and a mixed solution of acetonitrile‐methanol‐water (2:2:1) was added. After undergoing freeze‐thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen and tissue homogenization, the samples were centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was then concentrated and dried, followed by redissolution in acetonitrile and an internal standard containing 0.1% formic acid and 2‐chloro‐L‐phenylalanine. After filtration, the samples were subjected to liquid chromatograph‐mass spectrometer (LC‐MS) analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column, with gradient elution conducted separately in positive and negative ion modes. Mass spectrometry was performed using a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 instrument, with independent data acquisition in positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. The full‐scan resolution was set at 60,000, with a scan range of 100–1000 m/z. Secondary fragmentation was achieved using higher‐energy collisional dissociation with a collision energy of 30%, and to minimize interference, dynamic exclusion was employed. Samples from different groups were processed and analyzed in a randomized order to reduce systematic batch effects. Quality control samples were interspersed throughout the analytical sequence at regular intervals to monitor instrument stability and correct for potential batch effects.

Data Preprocessing

The raw MS data are first converted into a common file format compliant with the mzXML standard using the ProteoWizard software package's MSConvert tool. The chromatographic peak identification and quantification were performed using the R XCMS software package. The parameters are set as follows: bandwidth threshold of 2, mass deviation tolerance of 15 ppm, peak width detection range of 5–30 s, mass window width of 0.015, and mass difference threshold of 0.01, and the centWave algorithm is used for peak alignment. To eliminate systematic errors, signal correction is performed through support vector regression using quality control samples, and substances with a relative standard deviation > 30% are excluded for subsequent data analysis.

Metabolite identification was performed using a multi‐database joint retrieval strategy, including HMDB, MassBank, LIPID MAPS, and KEGG. The specific process is divided into two stages: primary identification determines the theoretical molecular weight of metabolites through the exact mass‐to‐charge ratio (m/z) of the parent ions, combined with information such as molecular mass (mass tolerance < 30 ppm) and adduct ion types to deduce the molecular formula, and finally achieves primary annotation of metabolites through multi‐database matching; secondary identification uses the obtained tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) fragmentation spectra to match with the standard spectra in the databases for feature ion matching, and these MS/MS‐supported annotations were regarded mainly as putatively identified metabolites, predominantly metabolomics standards initiative level 2, achieving dual verification. During the data preprocessing stage, systematic quality control is implemented, including missing value filling, data filtering, and normalization using a combination of logarithmic transformation and Pareto scaling to eliminate dimensional differences and optimize data normality. After preprocessing and QC‐based filtering, 5248 metabolite features were retained for first‐level differential analysis, among which 350 metabolites were annotated at the MS/MS‐supported secondary annotation level.

Statistical Analysis of the Data

Intergroup Comparison of Study Subjects

Descriptive analysis of baseline characteristics and intergroup comparisons was conducted using R version 4.3.2. For measurement data conforming to a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation was used for representation, and comparisons among the three groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance. The median (interquartile range) was used for nonnormally distributed measurement data, and comparisons among the three groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For pairwise comparisons, the Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U‐test was used as appropriate according to the data distribution. Categorical data were expressed as the number of cases (percentage), and the chi‐square test was used for comparison. All tests were two‐tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Screening of Differential Plasma Exosome Metabolites

The screening of differential plasma exosomal metabolites was performed using a combination of univariate and multivariate statistical analyses. Initially, univariate screening was performed using t‐tests (p < 0.05) and fold change (FC) analysis, with FC = 1 set as the threshold to define metabolite expression upregulation and downregulation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed for unsupervised pattern recognition and data dimensionality reduction. Additionally, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA), a supervised learning method, was utilized to enhance intergroup differentiation, with model performance evaluated using R 2 Y and Q 2. Ultimately, the criteria for screening differential metabolites were defined as simultaneously satisfying a variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and a t‐test p < 0.05, ensuring their significant contribution to intergroup differentiation and statistical significance for subsequent analysis.

Enrichment Analysis of Differential Metabolites

The list of screened differential metabolite names is input into the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 online analysis platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), and pathway analysis based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database is performed in the pathway analysis section. A differential metabolic pathway is defined as having a p < 0.05.

Screening and Diagnosis of Candidate Features

Based on the differential metabolites, key metabolites were sequentially screened using random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM). Diagnostic efficacy was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, with the area under the curve (AUC) as the evaluation metric. In addition, ROC analyses were performed for body mass index (BMI), TG/HDL ratio, and FPG as benchmark single clinical indicators to provide a clinical context for the candidate exosomal features. The closer the AUC is to 1, the higher the diagnostic value.

Sensitivity Analysis

Simplified sensitivity analyses were conducted for the top candidate exosomal feature with the highest AUC in each comparison. The associations between standardized metabolite intensities and disease status in the pre‐MetS versus control and MetS versus control comparisons were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models, with adjustment for age and BMI. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p‐values were calculated.

Candidate Differential Metabolites

Based on t‐test results (p < 0.05) and PLS‐DA analysis (VIP value > 1), a total of 55 differential metabolite peaks were identified between the pre‐MetS and control groups. Through comparison with the KEGG and HMDB databases, 27 differential candidate metabolites were ultimately identified (Table 2). Lipids and lipid‐like molecules, as well as benzene compounds, each accounted for 18.5%. Based on the FC values, 12 metabolites were upregulated, while 15 were downregulated. Reserpine acid methyl ester exhibited the highest FC value of 2.185, whereas quinoline had the lowest FC value of 0.606. Similarly, 99 differential metabolite peaks were identified between the MetS and control groups, with 45 candidate differential metabolites identified (Supporting Information: Table S2). Among these candidate metabolites, lipids and lipid‐like molecules (28.8%), organic acids and their derivatives (22.2%), and organic heterocyclic compounds (20.0%) accounted for relatively large proportions. Based on the FC values, 13 metabolites were upregulated, while 32 were downregulated. Picolinic acid had the lowest FC value of 0.640, and triclosan had the highest FC value of 2.020.

Expression Patterns of Differential Candidate Metabolites

The correlation between the metabolites is displayed in the heatmap matrix (Supporting Information: Figure S2). In the comparison between the pre‐MetS and control groups, the strongest positive correlation was observed between methoxsalen and isocitrate (r = 0.545), whereas the strongest negative correlation was observed between telaprevir and triclosan (r = −0.546). Regarding the candidate differential metabolites between the MetS and control groups, the highest negative correlation strength was between omeprazole sulfone and propoxur (r = −0.591), and the strongest positive correlation was between pantothenol and homo‐l‐arginine (r = 0.998).

Based on the clustered heatmap of normalized metabolite intensities, several candidate differential metabolites showed group‐specific abundance patterns (Supporting Information: Figure S3A). Triclosan showed relatively higher normalized abundance in the pre‐MetS group than in the control group, whereas 1,7‐diphenyl‐4‐hepten‐3‐one showed relatively higher normalized abundance in the control group. Several candidate metabolites also exhibited group‐specific variation in normalized abundance in the MetS versus control comparison (Supporting Information: Figure S3B). For example, 2‐phosphoglyceric acid and cyclohexane‐1‐carboxylate showed relatively lower normalized abundance.

Metabolic Pathway Analysis of Candidate Differential Metabolites

Using a threshold of p < 0.05 for pathway screening, the results revealed that the candidate differential metabolites between the pre‐MetS and control groups were significantly enriched in histidine metabolism and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Supporting Information: Figure S4). Notably, the TCA cycle pathway exhibited an impact value greater than zero. Further analysis indicated that candidate differential metabolites were also distributed in potential metabolic pathways such as glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism (Supporting Information: Table S3). Furthermore, the candidate differential metabolites between the MetS and control groups were primarily enriched in the thiamine metabolism pathway (p < 0.05), with the matched metabolite being thiamine (Supporting Information: Table S4).

Exploratory ROC Analysis of Candidate Exosomal Features

Among the models constructed using differential metabolites between the pre‐MetS and control groups, nine metabolites overlapped between the RF and SVM models: quinoline, d‐arabitol, 1‐methylhistidine, O‐phosphothreonine, 1,7‐diphenyl‐4‐hepten‐3‐one, muramic acid, sphingosine, cladribine, and triclosan (Figure 3). Five metabolites were common to both the RF and SVM models: 3‐methylxanthine, L‐iditol, N‐nitrosodibutylamine, picolinic acid, and terephthalate. The AUC values of these candidate exosomal features ranged from 0.623 to 0.745, indicating modest discriminatory ability in this exploratory dataset (Supporting Information: Table S5). Cladribine yielded the highest AUC value of 0.745 (95% CI: 0.622–0.864, p < 0.05) in the pre‐MetS comparison (Figure 4A), while 3‐methylxanthine demonstrated the highest AUC value of 0.714 (95% CI: 0.572–0.834, p < 0.05) in the MetS comparison (Figure 4B).

Comparison With the Conventional Clinical Indicators

BMI, TG/HDL, and FPG yielded AUCs of 0.692, 0.799, and 0.827, respectively, in the MetS versus control comparison, whereas the best‐performing exosomal candidate metabolite (3‐methylxanthine) had an AUC of 0.714.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted for the top candidate exosomal features using multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age and BMI. In the pre‐MetS versus control comparison, each one‐standard‐deviation increase in cladribine intensity was associated with lower odds of pre‐MetS (OR = 0.324, 95% CI: 0.132–0.653, p < 0.05). In the MetS versus control comparison, each one‐standard‐deviation increase in 3‐methylxanthine intensity was associated with lower MetS odds (OR = 0.397, 95% CI: 0.173–0.801, p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the associations between the two candidate exosomal features remained evident after adjustment for age and BMI, although they should be interpreted as exploratory.

Discussion

This study investigated the evolving metabolomic landscape across different stages of MetS. We identified 72 differential metabolites, predominantly enriched in histidine metabolism, the TCA cycle, and thiamine metabolism. Machine learning further nominated metabolites, such as cladribine and 3‐methylxanthine, as stage‐specific signals.

Recently, the rapid advancement of high‐throughput metabolite measurement technologies has enabled the widespread application of metabolomics in epidemiological studies by analyzing metabolite concentrations in large‐scale human blood samples. Previous plasma and serum metabolomics studies have shown that circulating metabolic signatures are closely associated with pre‐MetS and MetS. For example, plasma metabolic fingerprints have been used for large‐scale screening and personalized MetS risk stratification, whereas serum NMR‐based MetSCORE has been developed to evaluate MetS risk [12, 13]. Unlike conventional plasma or serum metabolomics, plasma exosome‐derived metabolomics focuses on metabolites enclosed within extracellular vesicles, which may provide complementary information related to parental cell status and vesicle‐mediated intercellular communication [19]. Our study collected fasting venous plasma samples from patients and employed LC‐MS technology to perform untargeted metabolomic analysis of plasma exosomal metabolites [22]. In total, 27 candidate differential metabolites were identified in the pre‐MetS group compared with those in the control group, whereas 45 candidate differential metabolites were identified in the MetS group. Multiple candidate differential metabolites, including 1‐methylhistidine, isocitrate, thiamine, and vitamin D, were enriched in critical metabolic pathways. Histidine metabolism, thiamine metabolism, and the TCA cycle were among the pathways of interest. Additionally, machine learning algorithms, which analyze large volumes of health data to identify potential disease patterns, are widely used to predict novel biomarkers and gain new insights into disease pathogenesis [23]. In this study, RF and SVM algorithms combined with ROC analysis were used to screen candidate exosomal features from the differential metabolites in both groups, including cladribine and 3‐methylxanthine. However, these values reflect limited classification performance in an untargeted discovery setting and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical diagnostic utility; these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than as validated diagnostic biomarkers. Notably, conventional clinical indicators showed stronger discriminatory performance in the MetS versus control comparison, which is expected because these variables are closely related to the diagnostic framework of MetS. By contrast, the comparatively better performance of the candidate exosomal feature in pre‐MetS suggests that exosomal metabolomic profiling may capture earlier molecular perturbations before overt abnormalities in routine clinical indicators become prominent.

In addition to the stage‐specific candidate features, the overlapping metabolites between the pre‐MetS and MetS comparisons may provide clues to early metabolic alterations that persist during MetS progression. Five metabolites, including d‐arabitol, triclosan, iloprost, tetracosanoic acid, and omeprazole sulfone, were shared between the two comparisons. Previous experimental evidence suggests that d‐arabitol may be linked to obesity‐related metabolic dysregulation and its relevance to body weight gain, fat accumulation, and insulin resistance [24]. Tetracosanoic acid may reflect very‐long‐chain fatty acid metabolism and peroxisomal β‐oxidation [25]. Iloprost is a prostacyclin analog that is related to vascular regulation and platelet activity, which are relevant to cardiometabolic dysfunction [26]. However, the use of triclosan and omeprazole sulfone may also reflect environmental exposure, medication use, or annotation uncertainty. Therefore, these overlapping metabolites should be interpreted as exploratory signals that require further validation.

Insulin resistance and inflammatory responses are considered the core mechanisms of MetS [27]. Thiamine plays a critical role in glucose metabolism, insulin synthesis, and insulin secretion [28]. Animal studies have shown that dietary thiamine intervention significantly alleviates metabolic abnormalities in Wistar‐kyoto rats [29]. Thiamine reduces the levels of advanced glycation end products by enhancing the activity of glyoxalase‐I, while simultaneously inhibiting the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway and its activators (such as IL‐1β and FFAs), thereby mitigating hepatic inflammation and oxidative damage. Furthermore, thiamine modulates the activity of lipid metabolism‐related enzymes (e.g., the hexosamine pathway), reduces cardiovascular risk indicators, enhances antioxidant enzyme activity and glutathione levels, and reverses oxidative stress [30]. Dietary thiamine intake exhibits a U‐shaped relationship for newly diagnosed hypertension [31]. This suggests that dietary thiamine supplementation can exert systemic therapeutic effects, ameliorating various metabolic abnormalities associated with MetS. Furthermore, from a genetic perspective, SLC35F3 is a key gene for thiamine transmembrane transport, and its dysfunction directly affects intracellular thiamine levels [32]. A Korean study found that the SLC35F3 rs10910387 T allele is a risk variant for thiamine deficiency, which can reduce blood thiamine concentration and synergize with high carbohydrate intake to significantly increase the risk of developing MetS [33]. Collectively, thiamine deficiency drives MetS progression through multiple pathological pathways. It promotes the onset and development of MetS by affecting energy metabolism, glycolysis, and the TCA cycle, as well as modulating the metabolism‐inflammation‐oxidative stress axis.

Lipid metabolites constituted a large proportion of the two sets of candidate differential metabolites identified in this study, which were significantly enriched in primary BA biosynthesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and steroid biosynthesis. Lipid metabolite changes can significantly impact cell membrane fluidity, thickness, accumulation, and membrane protein dynamics and function [34]. The biological functions of individual lipids are highly specific and play an important role in the pathogenesis of MetS. A plasma metabolomics study on type B aortic dissection indicated that as the severity of thoracic aortic dissection increased, the levels of glycocholic acid (r = −0.538, p = 0.008) and glycochenodeoxycholic acid decreased (r = −0.538, p = 0.008) [35], suggesting these two BAs may have a role in reducing cardiovascular disease risk. This aligns with our findings, where the expression level of glycochenodeoxycholic acid was quantitatively lower in patients with MetS than in the control group. Bile acids (BAs) play a key role in MetS regulation by activating the G protein‐coupled receptor (TGR5) [36]. Studies have shown that dietary supplementation with BAs, via TGR5 activation, significantly reduces weight gain induced by a high‐fat diet in mice [37]. Conversely, TGR5 knockout mice exhibit delayed glucose clearance [38]. BAs are also associated with increased insulin sensitivity in humans, and human genetic studies have revealed an association between genetic polymorphisms in TGR5 (such as SNP rs3731859) and type 2 diabetes risk [39].

Vitamin D plays a pivotal role in the regulation of blood pressure. The active form, 1,25(OH)2D, mediates vascular functions primarily by binding to the intracellular vitamin D receptor [40]. Vitamin A protects vascular endothelial function by inhibiting oxidative stress, reducing ROS production, and improving NO bioavailability [40]. Additionally, it helps regulate blood pressure by suppressing the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system overactivation [41]. Vitamin D deficiency promotes hypertension through multiple pathways. It activates the NF‐κB signaling pathway, fostering a pro‐inflammatory microenvironment in the epicardial adipose tissue and vascular wall that accelerates atherosclerosis [42]. Furthermore, deficiency induces proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial dysfunction, and arterial stiffness, all of which directly contribute to elevated blood pressure. Epidemiological evidence supports an association between vitamin D deficiency and an increased risk of hypertension [43].

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size, lack of an independent validation cohort, and use of nominal p‐values without multiple‐testing correction may limit the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the identified metabolites, RF/SVM‐based feature selection, and ROC analyses should be regarded as exploratory and may be susceptible to false‐positive findings or overfitting. Second, the study included only elderly women, which limits generalizability and cannot fully distinguish MetS‐related alterations from aging‐related metabolic changes. Third, although baseline information on diet, medication use, physical activity, and comorbidities was collected, these variables were not comprehensively incorporated into the metabolomics models, and sensitivity analysis, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. This is particularly relevant for xenobiotic‐ or drug‐related annotations, which may reflect medication exposure, environmental exposure, or annotation uncertainty rather than endogenous MetS‐specific metabolism. In addition, matched whole‐plasma metabolomics was not performed, so the exosomal metabolite profile could not be directly compared with the whole‐plasma metabolome. Finally, most metabolites were supported by database and MS/MS spectral matching and should therefore be interpreted as putatively annotated metabolites pending further validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, stage‐specific metabolomic signatures of MetS were identified in this study. Candidate metabolites such as cladribine and 3‐methylxanthine showed discriminatory potential and may provide complementary molecular information for the early identification of metabolic alterations.
