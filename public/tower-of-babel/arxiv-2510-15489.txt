Discretization of Variable-Coefficient Dynamical Systems via Rota Algebras
Miguel A. Rodríguez, Piergiulio Tempesta

Abstract
. We introduce a novel integrability-preserving discretization for a broad class of differential equations with variable coefficients, encompassing both linear and nonlinear cases. The construction is achieved via a categorical approach that enables a unified treatment of continuous and discrete dynamical systems. Within our theoretical framework, based on the finite operator calculus proposed by G. C. Rota, all difference operators act as derivations in an algebra of power series with respect to a suitable functional product. The core of our theory relies on the existence of covariant functors between the newly proposed Rota category of Galois differential algebras and suitable categories of abstract dynamical systems. In this setting, under certain regularity assumptions, a differential equation and its discrete analogues are naturally interpreted as objects of the same category. This perspective enables the construction of a vast class of integrable maps that share with their continuous analogues a wide set of exact solutions and, in the linear case, the Picard-Vessiot group.

1.1.
Statement of the problem

The discretization of dynamical systems in an
integrability preserving
way has been the subject of intensive investigation over the past decades, owing to its considerable impact across a wide range of fields, including numerical analysis, discrete mathematics, algorithm theory, quantum physics and statistical mechanics.

In particular, recent years have witnessed a growing interest in discrete differential geometry and related algebraic structures

,

,

,

,
[

]

[

,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
. Furthermore, lattice renormalization of field theories
[
27
]
, together with several modern approaches to quantum mechanics
[

]
and quantum gravity
[
3
]
,
[
18
]
,
[

]
,
require suitable discretizations of the underlying quantum field equations.

The purpose of this paper is to introduce a unified theoretical framework which allows us to address and solve, in a quite general sense and from a novel perspective, the longstanding problem of the integrability-preserving discretization of a large class of dynamical systems, both
linear and nonlinear
, even in the case of variable coefficients expressed in terms of real analytic functions.

Precisely, the main theorems of this work ensure the integrability-preserving discretization of two classes of differential equations with variable coefficients:

a) Linear dynamical systems of the form

(1.1)

a_{N}(x)\frac{d^{N}}{dx^{N}}y+a_{N-1}(x)\frac{d^{N-1}}{dx^{N-1}}y+\cdots+a_{1}(x)\frac{d}{dx}y+a_{0}(x)y+c_{0}(x)=0\ ;

b) Nonlinear dynamical systems of the form

(1.2)

\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}y=a_{N}(x)y^{N}+a_{N-1}(x)y^{N-1}+\cdots+a_{1}(x)y+a_{0}(x)\ .

In both cases
N\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,
c_{0}(x)
and
a_{0}(x),\ldots,a_{N}(x)
are real analytic functions.

The difference equations we obtain as discrete analogs of the families of equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are defined on a regular mesh of points. In the continuum limit they converge to the corresponding differential equations, thereby providing a
discrete approximation
of the original continuous models.

The proposed approach combines the theory of Galois differential algebras, the theory of finite difference operators in the formulation given by G.-C. Rota
[
40
]
, and S. Roman
[
38
]
, together with
category theory. In our framework, a given continuous dynamical system and its infinitely many discrete counterparts are regarded as distinct representations of an underlying abstract equation,
and are naturally interpreted as objects of a category associated with the system. The morphisms of this category correspond to the various discrete realizations of the abstract equation. Accordingly, these difference equations will be said to be
categorically equivalent
.

The central feature of our discretization scheme is that all operators involved–namely, the delta operators of the Roman-Rota formalism–act as
derivations
on suitable Galois function algebras.
Consequently, many structural properties of categorically equivalent equations are preserved. In particular, a large class of solutions is retained. We emphasize that, generally speaking, to the best of our knowledge, the classes of difference equations arising from our approach are novel, and not related to those obtained with other discretization schemes.

The theoretical framework developed in the present work allows us to substantially extend the results of
[
47
]
, where discretizations were restricted to nonlinear dynamical systems, and with
constant
coefficients only. In particular, our approach provides the first general treatment of discretizations for nonlinear systems with variable coefficients. Moreover, for the novel case of linear dynamical systems, we extend the Frobenius-type theorem presented in
[
36
]
–originally formulated for second-order equations–to encompass differential equations of arbitrary order with variable coefficients.

1.3.
A categorical discretization

Given a continuous dynamical system of the form (
1.1
) or (
1.2
), we first select a specific difference operator to serve as the discrete derivative, imposing the additional requirement that it be a delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
. The standard forward, backward and symmetric derivatives provide typical examples of discrete delta operators; however, one can construct infinitely many alternatives. Next, we define the Galois differential algebra in which the delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
acts as a derivation, by constructing the corresponding
*
-product. The original dynamical system is then defined in the Galois algebra, where it typically assumes the form of a recurrence equation. The resulting discretization procedure is inherently structure-preserving.

Thus, in general, we associate with the family of dynamical systems defined by eq. (
1.1
) a category of linear equations
\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}
;
similarly, the family (
1.2
) corresponds to a category of nonlinear equations, denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}
.
We also establish the existence of two natural functors
F:\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}
and

G:\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}
. Within this algebraic framework, different discretizations
of a given continuous dynamical system are represented as morphisms within the same category. In particular, differential equations can be mapped into difference equations

while preserving the underlying differential structure
. Consequently, the integrability properties of a given
continuous dynamical system are naturally inherited by its associated discrete counterparts and in particular, solutions of the continuous system are isomorphically mapped
to solutions of the corresponding discrete equations.

A crucial feature of the integrable maps associated with eqs. (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), according to the procedure outlined above, is that they can be interpreted as

discrete analogues of integro-differential equations
. Indeed, these maps are nonlocal recurrences possessing the general form

(1.3)

P(\Delta)u(n)=\mathcal{I}[u(n)].

Here
P(\Delta)
is a polynomial in a delta operator (typically representing a discrete derivative of a given order),
u(n)
is the dependent variable of the map,
n
is the independent discrete variable,
and
\mathcal{I}[u(n)]
is a functional depending on all values of
u
ranging from an initial point up to
n
.
This aspect reflects the inherent nonlocality of the equation (
1.3
). In turn, the origin of this nonlocality lies in the intrinsic nonlocality
of the
*
-product. This property can be interpreted in light of a fundamental no-go theorem proved in
[
20
]
, which asserts that it is impossible to define a field theory on an infinite mesh of points
endowed with a nontrivial product rule that simultaneously satisfies the Leibniz rule, translational invariance and locality. Therefore, the structure preserving discretization scheme
proposed in this work–based on preserving the Leibniz rule for difference operators–is intimately connected with the nonlocal character of the resulting integrable maps.

In this scenario, the simplest case arises when discretizing linear differential equations with constant coefficients. Here, the resulting discrete equations are standard, i.e.,
local
difference equations, depending on a fixed number of values of
n
determined solely by the order of the chosen discrete derivative
\Delta
. By contrast, when discretizing dynamical systems with variable coefficients or nonlinear systems, the nonlocal features of the theory become manifest.

1.4.
Main results

The general discretization theory we propose relies on the following new results.

I) We prove that the integrable maps representing discrete versions of the dynamical systems (
1.1
) or (
1.2
) satisfy the following property: if
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{k}
is an analytic solution of an equation of the form (
1.1
) or (
1.2
),
then
\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}p_{k}(n)
(where
p_{k}(n)
are the basic polynomials associated with the chosen delta operator) for each
n
is a solution of the corresponding discrete map. In other words,
under mild assumptions,
analytic solutions of the continuous systems (
1.1
) or (
1.2
)
are transformed into exact solutions of the associated integrable maps
(
1.3
).

II) A Galois theory for the integrable maps considered in this work is developed, starting from the novel notion of Rota category. Within this setting, we also establish the existence of an isomorphism between the
Picard–Vessiot group
of a linear differential equation with constant coefficients and that of the corresponding difference equation obtained through our categorical approach.

In the continuum limit, as the mesh spacing approaches zero, these new integrable maps reduce to the original differential equations.

1.5.
Discussion and future perspectives

We recall that the time scale calculus, initiated by Hilger in
[
16
]
represents a well-established and intriguing approach aimed at unifying discrete and continuous calculus. This unification is achieved by defining a general domain that can be continuous, discrete, or mixed (corresponding to time scales or, more generally, measure chains) and by introducing appropriate jump operators on this domain. Over the past two decades, this theory has been extensively investigated by many authors (see, e.g., the monograph
[
6
]
), resulting in a substantial body of results and applications.

However, the theoretical framework proposed in this article is independent of, and fundamentally distinct from, time scale calculus. Instead, our approach is based on the guiding principle of preserving the Leibniz rule in discrete calculus–through the notion of Rota’s Galois algebra–and on the use of an adapted point mesh for discretization, defined by the zeros of a set of basic polynomials.

We emphasize that our categorical approach, in principle, allows to reformulate the main theorems of this work for
arbitrary discrete derivatives
. This can be done by analogy with the case of the forward difference operator, which we have analyzed explicitly due to its prominence in applications. In particular, the extension of our theory to discretizations based on the backward difference operator is entirely straightforward.

An interesting direction for future research is the extension of the present theory to the case of
q
-difference equations.
A coherent Galois approach to this class of equations has been developed in
[
9
]
,
[
42
]
.
We hypothesize that a functorial correspondence can also be envisaged for this case, allowing a unified treatment of the Galois theory for differential equations and their categorically equivalent
q
-difference equations.

The discretization of partial differential equations, another fundamental problem, can in principle be addressed using the same categorical approach. Work on these research directions
is currently underway.

2.
Category theory and dynamical systems

2.1.
Algebraic preliminaries. Delta operators

The theory of delta operators, introduced in
[
40
]
as a foundational framework for combinatorics, has been extensively developed in the literature (see also
[
39
]
,
[
38
]
).
Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the space of polynomials in a variable
x\in\mathbb{K}
, where
\mathbb{K}
is a field of characteristic zero and let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of non–negative integers.

Let
T
denote the shift operator, whose action on a function
f
is given by
Tf\left(x\right)=f\left(x+1\right)
.

Definition 1
.

An operator
S
is said to be shift–invariant if it commutes with the shift operator
T
. A shift–invariant operator
\mathcal{Q}
is a delta operator if
\mathcal{Q}x=const\neq 0
.

We deduce immediately the following property.

Corollary 1
.

For every constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{Q}c=0
.

The most common examples of delta operators are provided by the derivative
D
and the forward discrete derivative
\Delta=T-1
.

Definition 2
.

Given a delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
, a polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}\left(x\right)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be the sequence of basic polynomials for
\mathcal{Q}
if the following conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle 1)\text{ \ }p_{0}\left(x\right)=1;

\displaystyle 2)\text{ \ }p_{n}\left(0\right)=0\ \text{for all }n>0;\text{ \ }

\displaystyle 3)\text{ \ }\mathcal{Q}p_{n}\left(x\right)=np_{n-1}\left(x\right).

Notice that, for a given delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
the sequence of associated basic polynomials is unique.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a delta operator acting on
\mathcal{P}
, let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of all delta operators, and
\{p_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the basic sequence of polynomials of order
n
uniquely associated with
\mathcal{Q}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the algebra of formal power series in
x
. Since the polynomials
\{p_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for any choice of
\mathcal{Q}
form a basis of
\mathcal{F}
, any
f\in\mathcal{F}
can be expanded as a formal series of the form

f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}p_{n}(x).

In this way, we extend the action of delta operators on functions. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a set of points on the real line, isomorphic to
\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
\mathcal{F_{L}}
the vector space of the formal power series defined on
\mathcal{L}
. The space
\mathcal{F}
(and, consequently,
\mathcal{F_{L}}
) can be endowed with an algebra structure by introducing the product, defined for each choice of
\mathcal{Q}
, via the relation

(2.1)

p_{n}(x)*_{\mathcal{Q}}p_{m}(x):=p_{n+m}(x).

This product for the forward difference operator
\Delta
, was proposed in
[
49
]
and in
[
19
]
. In what follows, we will use the symbol
*_{\mathcal{Q}}
whenever it is necessary to emphasize the dependence of the
*
-product on the choice of the delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
.
It can be shown that the space
(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q}})
, equipped with the usual operations of series addition, scalar multiplication, and the
*
-product (
2.1
), forms an associative algebra.

2.2.
Formal groups and delta operators

In this section, we will briefly demonstrate that the theory of delta operators admits a natural interpretation within the framework of formal group theory
[
34
]
.
Over the past decades, formal group theory has been extensively investigated due to its central role in algebraic topology
[
8
]
,
[
24
]
, cobordism theory
[
33
]
,
analytic number theory
[
17
]
,
[
46
]
,
[
48
]
, and related fields.

Following
[
15
]
,
[
8
]
, we remind that a commutative one–dimensional formal group law over a commutative, unital ring
A
is a
formal power series
\Phi(x,y)\in A\llbracket x,y\rrbracket
such that

(1)

\Phi(x,0)=\Phi(0,x)=x\,,

(2)

\Phi\left(\Phi(x,y),z\right)=\Phi\left(x,\Phi(y,z)\right)\,.

When
\Phi(x,y)=\Phi(y,x)
, the formal group law is said to be
commutative.

Let us consider the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Q}\left[c_{1},c_{2},...\right]
and the formal group logarithm

F\left(u\right)=u+c_{1}\frac{u^{2}}{2}+c_{2}\frac{u^{3}}{3}+\cdots

Let
G\left(v\right)
be its inverse series, i.e., the formal group exponential

(2.2)

G\left(v\right)=v-c_{1}\frac{v^{2}}{2}+\left(3c_{1}^{2}-2c_{2}\right)\frac{v^{3}}{6}+\cdots

so that
F\left(G\left(v\right)\right)=v
. The formal group law associated with these series, known as the
Lazard Universal Formal Group
, is given by

\Phi\left(u_{1},u_{2}\right)=G\left(F\left(u_{1}\right)+F\left(u_{2}\right)\right).

It is defined over the Lazard ring
L
, i.e. the subring of
\mathbb{Q}\left[c_{1},c_{2},...\right]
generated by the coefficients of
the power series
G\left(F\left(u_{1}\right)+F\left(u_{2}\right)\right)
. For any commutative one-dimensional formal group law over any ring
A
, there exists a unique homomorphism
L\to A
under which the Lazard group law
is mapped into the given group law (
universal property
of the
Lazard group).

In algebraic topology, delta operators are related to Thom classes and complex cobordism theory
[
35
]
.
In order to relate formal groups to difference delta operators, we first describe a simple technique for generating a family of difference delta operators. More precisely, we introduce the difference operators

(2.3)

\Delta_{p}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{k=l}^{m}\alpha_{k}T^{k}\text{,}\quad l\text{,
}m\in\mathbb{Z}\text{,}\mathbb{\quad}l<m\text{,\quad}m-l=p\text{,}\quad\sum_{k=l}^{m}\alpha_{k}=0\text{,}\quad\sum_{k=l}^{m}k\alpha_{k}=c

where
\sigma
can be interpreted as a mesh spacing and
\alpha_{k}\,
are constants with
\alpha_{m}\neq 0
,
\alpha_{l}\neq 0
. We choose
c=1
, to reproduce possibly the derivative
D
in the continuum limit. A difference operator of the
form (
2.3
) is said to be a delta operator of
order
p
, if it approximates the continuous derivative up to terms of order
\sigma^{p}

[
25
]
. Apart from the two constraints in relation (
2.3
), one can arbitrarily choose
m-l-1
more linear conditions to fix all constants
\alpha_{k}
.
Consequently, by means of the representation
T\sim e^{v}
(the “symbol” of the translation operator), each delta operator of the family (
2.3
) is associated with a group exponential (
2.2
) and then with a realization of the one-dimensional Lazard universal formal group law. Conversely, with the identification
v\sim D
, there corresponds to each formal group exponential a delta operator (see also
[
34
]
,
[
48
]
).

2.3.
The Rota category

The notion of Rota algebra has been introduced in
[
47
]
, as the natural Galois differential algebra in which the discretization procedure is performed. In this work, we develop a categorical approach to this concept.

Definition 3
.

A Rota differential algebra is a Galois differential algebra
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Q})
, where
(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q}})
is an associative algebra of formal power series, the product
*_{\mathcal{Q}}
is the composition law defined by (
2.1
), and
\mathcal{Q}
is a delta operator acting as a derivation on
\mathcal{F}
:

(2.4)

i)\quad\mathcal{Q}(a+b)=\mathcal{Q}(a)+\mathcal{Q}(b),\hskip 28.45274pt\mathcal{Q}(\lambda a)=\lambda\mathcal{Q}(a),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{K},

(2.5)

ii)\quad\mathcal{Q}(a*b)=\mathcal{Q}(a)*b+a*\mathcal{Q}(b).

As has been proved in
[
47
]
, there exists a unique Rota algebra
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Q})
associated with a delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
.

Definition 4
.

The Rota category, denoted by
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})
, is the collection of all Rota algebras
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Q})
, with morphisms defined by

(2.6)

\rho_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}:(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q}})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q^{\prime}}})

which are closed under composition.

The action of the morphism
\rho_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
on formal power series is defined by

(2.7)

\sum_{n}a_{n}p_{n}(x)\longrightarrow\sum_{m}a_{m}q_{m}(x),

where
\{p_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{q_{m}(x)\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
are the basic sequences associated with
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
respectively.
The property of closure under composition is trivial.

2.4.
New categories for dynamical systems

We introduce two new definitions of categories for differential equations of the form (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).
For
N\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, define
u^{*N}:=\underbrace{u*\ldots*u}_{N-times}
.

Definition 5
.

For any choice of the set of functions
\{a_{0}(x),\ldots,a_{N}(x),c_{0}(x)\}
, the category
\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}
of linear dynamical systems of order
N
is the collection of all equations of the form

(2.8)

lin(\mathcal{Q},u,*_{\mathcal{Q}}):=a_{N}(x)*\mathcal{Q}^{N}y+a_{N-1}(x)*\mathcal{Q}^{(N-1)}y+\ldots+a_{1}(x)*\mathcal{Q}y+a_{0}(x)*y+c_{0}(x)=0.

The set of correspondences

(2.9)

\lambda_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}:\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}},

(2.10)

lin(\mathcal{Q},y,*)\longrightarrow lin(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},y,*^{\prime}),

defines the class of morphisms of the category.

In other words, given a differential equation of the form (
1.1
), one may define a category whose objects are the equation (
1.1
)
together with all infinitely many of its discretizations, obtained by varying
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{D}
and constructing the corresponding morphisms (
2.10
).

Definition 6
.

For any choice of the set of functions
\{a_{0}(x),\ldots,a_{N}(x)\}
, the category
\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}

of nonlinear dynamical systems of order
m\in\mathbb{N}
consists of all equations of the form

(2.11)

eq(\mathcal{Q},y,*_{\mathcal{Q}}):=\mathcal{Q}^{m}y-a_{N}(x)*y^{*N}-a_{N-1}(x)*y^{*(N-1)}-\ldots-a_{1}(x)*y-a_{0}(x)=0.

The set of correspondences

(2.12)

\nu_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}:\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}},

(2.13)

eq(\mathcal{Q},y,*)\longrightarrow eq(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},y,*^{\prime}),

defines the class of morphisms of the category.

The closure of the morphisms
\lambda_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
and
\nu_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
under composition is easily verified.

Definition 7
.

Two objects in the category
\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}
(or in
\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}
) are said to represent two categorically equivalent equations. Alternatively, two equations are categorically equivalent if there exists a morphism of categories
\lambda_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
(or
\nu_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
) mapping one equation to the other.

A notable property of the categories introduced above is their hierarchical structure via subcategories, with inclusions determined by the natural identifications:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\}}:=\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{(N-k)-\mathrm{times}}\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\}}:=\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{k},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{(N-k)-\mathrm{times}}\}},

for
k<N
.

Lemma 1
.

For any fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and for any choice of the polynomials
\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}
, there exists a filtration of subcategories, given by the finite sequences

(2.14)

\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0}\}}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},a_{1}\}}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}}\}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}

and

(2.15)

\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0}\}}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},a_{1}\}}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}}\}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}

respectively.

Proof.

For each of the subsets
\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}}\}
, the restriction of the morphisms
\lambda_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
over these subsets still preserves the closure under composition and defines morphisms of subcategories. The same argument holds for the sequence related to
\mathcal{N}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}
.
∎

As a consequence of the previous construction, one can define functors between the category of Rota differential algebras and the categories of abstract dynamical systems defined above.

Theorem 1
.

For any choice of the functions
\{a_{0}(x),\ldots,a_{N}(x),c_{0}(x)\}
, the application

(2.16)

F:\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}},

(\mathcal{F}+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q}})\longrightarrow lin(\mathcal{Q},y,*),

\rho_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\longrightarrow\lambda_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}},

is a covariant functor.

Proof.

A direct verification shows that
F
preserves the composition of morphisms:

F(\rho_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\circ\rho_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}})=F(\rho_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}})\circ F(\rho_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}}).

If we denote by
id_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\rho_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}}
the identity morphism, we also have

F(id_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{A}))=id_{\mathcal{Q}}(F(\mathcal{A})),

where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})
.
∎

In the same manner one can prove that the application

(2.17)

G:\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F})\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}},

(\mathcal{F}+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q}})\longrightarrow eq(\mathcal{Q},y,*),

\rho_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\longrightarrow\nu_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}},

is a covariant functor.

The functors (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) capture the essential aspects of the proposed discretization, and provide
functorial Rota correspondences
between continuous and discrete dynamical systems. Moreover, these functors can be naturally extended to the subcategories introduced above.

Corollary 2
.

The restriction of the functors
F
and
G
to the subcategories defined by the filtrations (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) respectively remain covariant functors.

3.
Main theorems

In this section, we present the main results of the article. Using the categorical framework developed above, we construct a class of integrable maps associated with the dynamical systems
(
1.1
) and (
1.2
), respectively. These maps are categorically equivalent to the original continuous systems and represent them in the Galois algebra corresponding to the forward difference operator. The discretization is performed on an
equally spaced
mesh.

Theorem
2
establishes a solution to the problem of the integrability-preserving discretization of linear
n
-th order ODEs with analytic variable coefficients. Theorem
3
solves the analogous problem for the case of nonlinear first-order ODEs.

3.1.
Integrable maps from linear ODEs

Theorem 2
.

Consider the differential equation

(3.1)

lin(\partial,y):=a_{N}(x)\frac{d^{N}}{dx^{N}}y+a_{N-1}(x)\frac{d^{N-1}}{dx^{N-1}}y+\ldots+a_{1}(x)\frac{d}{dx}y+a_{0}(x)y+c_{0}(x)=0,

where
a_{i}(x)
,
i=0,\ldots,N
are analytic functions at
x=0
:

(3.2)

a_{i}(x)=\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{ik_{i}}}{k_{i}!}x^{k_{i}}.

Assume that

(3.3)

y(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}x^{k}

be a real solution of (
3.1
), analytic at
x=0
. Then the difference equation

(3.4)

\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{n}\frac{n!\alpha_{ik_{i}}}{(n-k_{i})!k_{i}!}\Delta^{i}u_{n-k_{i}}=0

or, in the equivalent form, the equation

(3.5)

\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-k}\binom{n}{k_{i}}\binom{i}{k}\alpha_{ik_{i}}u_{n-k_{i}+k}=0,

defined on a regular set of points
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}
indexed by the variable
n\in\mathbb{N}
, admits as a solution the series

(3.6)

u_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.

Proof.

We start by applying the morphism (
2.7
) to the series (
3.3
); we obtain the transformation

(3.7)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}x^{k}\longrightarrow\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}p_{k}(x).\hskip 28.45274pt

The discrete transform (
3.7
) is finite whenever
x\in\mathcal{L}
, provided that the points of the mesh
\mathcal{L}
coincide with the zeros of the basic sequence of polynomials
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. In the following, we choose our basic sequence to be the set of lower factorial polynomials. Consequently,
\mathcal{L}
denotes an equally spaced set of points on the real nonnegative axis, indexed by
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, we have

(3.8)

p_{k}(n)=\begin{cases}0,&\mathrm{if}\quad n<k,\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!},&\mathrm{if}\quad n\geq k.\end{cases}

Let us introduce the function
u:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(3.9)

u_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-l)!}b_{l}.

For its
inverse interpolating transform
, we have

(3.10)

b_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n-l}}{l!(n-l)!}u_{l}.

For the purpose of constructing the equation categorically equivalent to eq. (
3.1
) in
\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}
, we represent the product
x^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{s}}{\mathrm{d}x^{s}}y(x)
on the set
\mathcal{L}
by the action of the morphism
\rho_{\partial,\Delta}:(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot)\rightarrow(\mathcal{F},+,*_{\Delta})
between the standard (Rota) algebra of power series endowed with the pointwise product and that equipped with the forward difference operator. This morphism determines the general (umbral) correspondences

(3.11)

x^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{s}}{\mathrm{d}x^{s}}y(x)\longrightarrow\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\Delta^{s}u_{n-r},\qquad r,s\in\mathbb{N}

which can be specialized to the following cases:

(3.12)

\displaystyle y(x)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow u_{n},

(3.13)

\displaystyle x^{r}y(x)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}u_{n-r},

(3.14)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{s}}{\mathrm{d}x^{s}}y(x)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Delta^{s}u_{n}.

To prove these relations, observe that

(3.15)

x^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{s}}{\mathrm{d}x^{s}}y(x)\longrightarrow p_{r}(n)*\sum_{k=0}^{n+s}\frac{k!}{(k-s)!}\,b_{k}\,p_{k-s}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+s-r}\frac{k!}{(k-s)!}\,b_{k}\,p_{k-s+r}(n).

Then, we deduce that

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n+s-r}\frac{k!}{(k-s)!}\,b_{k}\,p_{k-s+r}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+s-r}\frac{n!\,k!}{(k-s)!(n-k+s-r)!}b_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n+s-r}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{n!\,k!}{(k-s)!(n-r-(k-s))!}\frac{(-1)^{k-j}}{j!(k-j)!}u_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\sum_{j=0}^{n+s-r}\left(\sum_{k=j}^{n+s-r}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{n-r}{k-s}\binom{k}{j}\right)u_{j}.

It can be shown that (see the Appendix
A
):

(3.17)

\sum_{k=j}^{n+s-r}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{n-r}{k-s}\binom{k}{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{s-i}\binom{s}{i}\delta_{n-r+i,j}.

Thus,

(3.18)

\displaystyle x^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{s}}{\mathrm{d}x^{s}}y(x)\longrightarrow\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\sum_{j=0}^{n+s-r}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{s-i}\binom{s}{i}\delta_{n-r+i,j}\right)u_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\sum_{i=0}^{s}(-1)^{s-i}\binom{s}{i}u_{n-r+i,j}u_{j}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\Delta^{s}u_{n-r}

To conclude the proof, observe that, by means of the action of the functor
F
, any formal power series
y
of eq. (
3.1
) is carried into a solution
u_{n}
of the equation

lin(\mathcal{Q},u,*_{\mathcal{Q}})=\lambda_{\partial,\Delta}(lin(\partial,y,\cdot)).

In addition, on the mesh
\mathcal{L}
, the sum
\sum_{k}b_{k}p_{k}(n)
truncates and converts into the finite sum (
3.6
). ∎

As a consequence of this proof, eqs. (
3.1
) and (
3.4
) are categorically equivalent.

Remark 1
.

Theorem
2
can be considerably generalized by choosing an arbitrary different delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
and the corresponding basic sequence
\{p_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In this case, we define the lattice
\mathcal{L}
to be the union set of all zeroes of the polynomials of the sequence
\{p_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The action of the morphism
\lambda_{\partial,\mathcal{Q}}
will provide a different, categorically equivalent representation of eq. (
3.1
) in
\mathcal{K}_{\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N},c_{0}\}}
.

3.2.
Integrable maps from nonlinear ODEs

Theorem 3
.

Consider a dynamical system of the form

(3.19)

eq(\partial,y):=\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}y-\sum_{r=0}^{N}a_{r}(x)y^{r}=0,

where
a_{j}(x)=\sum_{m_{j}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{jm_{j}}x^{m_{j}}
are analytic at
x=0
, with
\alpha_{jm_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=0,\ldots,N
.
Assume that

(3.20)

y(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}x^{k}

be a real solution of (
3.19
), analytic at
x=0
.
Then the difference equation

(3.21)

\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-i}\binom{m}{i}u_{n+i}-\sum_{r=0}^{N}\sum_{s_{r},j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}=0}^{n}\alpha_{rs_{r}}\frac{(1-r)^{n-s_{r}-i}n!}{(n-s_{r}-i)!}\prod_{l=1}^{r}\frac{u_{j_{l}}}{j_{l}!}=0

that represents eq. (
3.19
) on a regular set of points
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}
indexed by the variable
n\in\mathbb{N}
, admits as a solution the series

(3.22)

u_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.

Proof.

We consider the correspondence (
3.14
)

(3.23)

y^{(r)}(x)\to\Delta^{r}u_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{r}(-1)^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}u_{n+j},

where
u_{n}
is related to its inverse transform by the reciprocal eqs. (
3.9
) and (
3.10
). We will also determine how the morphism
\rho_{\partial,\Delta}:(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot)\rightarrow(\mathcal{F},+,*_{\Delta})
acts on the powers of
y(x)
.
Let
\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{j}
be the multiindices:
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
,
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{r})
,
k=\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}
,
j=\sum_{i=1}^{r}j_{i}
(see the Appendix
B
for a detailed proof of eqs. (
3.24
) and (
3.25
)):

(3.24)

\displaystyle y(x)^{r}=\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}x^{m}\right)^{r}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}x^{k}\to\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}p_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{j_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\frac{(-1)^{k_{i}-j_{i}}}{j_{i}!(k_{i}-j_{i})!}u_{j_{i}}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{j}=0,\,j\leq n}\frac{(1-r)^{n-j}n!}{(n-j)!}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{u_{j_{i}}}{j_{i}!}.

As usual, factorials of negative numbers in the denominators yield vanishing fractions. Then, the last series in eq. (
3.24
) have a finite number of terms only.

Also, we observe that the products of the form
x^{s}y^{r}
are mapped into the terms

(3.25)

\displaystyle x^{s}y^{r}=x^{s}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}x^{m}\right)^{r}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}x^{k+s}\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}p_{k+s}(x)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}\frac{n!}{(n-s-k)!}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{j_{i}=0}^{k_{i}}\frac{(-1)^{k_{i}-j_{i}}}{j_{i}!(k_{i}-j_{i})!}u_{j_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{j}=0,\,j\leq n-s}\frac{(1-r)^{n-s-j}n!}{(n-s-j)!}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{u_{j_{i}}}{j_{i}!}.

The product of a function
a(x)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}x^{s}
times a power of
y
is mapped into:

(3.26)

\displaystyle a(x)y^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}x^{s}\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}x^{m}\right)^{r}=\left(\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}x^{s}\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}x^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}x^{k+s}\to\sum_{s,\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}p_{k+s}(x)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\sum_{s,\mathbf{k}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}b_{k_{1}}\cdots b_{k_{r}}\frac{n!}{(n-k-s)!}=\sum_{s,\mathbf{j}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}\frac{(1-r)^{n-s-j}n!}{(n-s-j)!}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{u_{j_{i}}}{j_{i}!}.

By combining together all the previous results,
we obtain the proof that the difference equation (
3.21
) is categorically equivalent to the equation

(3.27)

\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}u_{n+j}-\sum_{q=0}^{N}\sum_{s_{q},\mathbf{j}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{qs_{q}}\frac{(1-r)^{n-s_{q}-j}n!}{(n-s_{q}-j)!}\prod_{i=1}^{q}\frac{u_{j_{i}}}{j_{i}!}=0

defined on
\mathcal{L}
, i.e. is the image of eq. (
3.19
) under the action of the morphism
\nu_{\partial,\Delta}
defined in
\mathcal{N}_{\{m;a_{0},\ldots,a_{N}\}}
. In turn, both are realizations of eq. (
2.11
).

To prove that the series (
3.22
) is solution of eq. (
3.21
), observe that the morphism
\rho_{\partial,\Delta}
provides the correspondence

(3.28)

\sum_{k}b_{k}x^{k}\longrightarrow\sum_{k}b_{k}p_{k}(n).

The action of the functor
G
will carry any analytic solution
y
of eq. (
3.19
), defined on the algebra
(\mathcal{F}+,\cdot)
, into a solution
u_{n}
of the corresponding equation

eq(\Delta,u,*_{\Delta})=\nu_{\partial,\Delta}(eq(\partial,y,\cdot)),

defined on the Rota algebra
(\mathcal{F},+,*_{\Delta})
. Once represented this equation on the lattice

truncates and converts into the finite sum (

∎

The same scheme of Remark

4.
Integrable dynamics in the Fourier space

The integrable maps arising from the preceding construction induce an auxiliary dynamics on the space of their Fourier coefficients, which is of independent interest. We consider here the case of nonlinear equations with constant coefficients, which provides a prototypical illustration of this alternative construction.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a dynamical system of the form

(4.1)

\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}y=a_{N}y^{N}+a_{N-1}y^{N-1}+\ldots+a_{1}y+b_{0},

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume that

(4.2)

y=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}x^{k}

be a real solution of (
4.1
), analytic at
x=0
.
Then the equation

(4.3)

\displaystyle\frac{(n+m)!}{n!}\zeta_{n+m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{N}\sum_{\overset{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N-1}=0}{l_{1}+\ldots+l_{N-1}\leq n}}\zeta_{l_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{l_{N-2}}\zeta_{l-l_{1}-\ldots-l_{N-1}}+\ldots

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle a_{2}\sum_{l_{1}=0}^{n}\zeta_{l_{1}}\zeta_{n-l_{1}}+a_{1}\zeta_{n}+b_{0}

possesses the solution

(4.4)

\zeta_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{l}\frac{(-1)^{n-l}b_{k}}{(n-l)!(l-k)!}.

Proof.

As a consequence of the definition of basic sequence for
\{p_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, one can easily prove that, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}

(4.5)

\Delta^{m}u_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-l)!}\frac{(l+m)!}{l!}\zeta_{l+m}.

Also,

(4.6)

u_{n}^{*p}=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{p}=0}^{\infty}\zeta_{l_{1}}\ldots\zeta_{l_{p}}p_{(l_{1}+\ldots+l_{p})}(n)=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-l)!}\cdot\sum_{\overset{l_{1},\ldots,l_{p-1}=0}{l_{1}+\ldots+l_{p-1}\leq l}}\zeta_{l_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{l_{p-2}}\zeta_{l-l_{1}-\ldots-l_{p-1}}

Combining the previous expressions yields the stated result.
∎

Both the linear case and the case of dynamical systems with nonconstant coefficients can be treated analogously. Their detailed analysis is left to the reader.

5.
Linear difference equations over Galois algebras and Picard-Vessiot theory

This section aims to extend certain fundamental results of Galois theory, established for homogeneous linear differential equations with constant coefficients, to their
alter ego
on
\mathcal{L}
as defined by Theorem
2
(See e.g.
[
21
]
and
[
32
]
for relevant definitions and results).

Let
\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{N-1}\}\in\mathbb{K}
. Let
\mathcal{B(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Q})}
be the space of linear operators acting on the Rota differential algebra
(\mathcal{F},+,\cdot,*_{\mathcal{Q}})
over
\mathbb{K}
. We introduce the linear operator
T[\mathcal{Q}]\in\mathcal{B(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{Q})}
defined by
T[\mathcal{Q}]:=\mathcal{Q}^{N}+a_{N-1}\mathcal{Q}^{N-1}+\ldots+a_{1}\mathcal{Q}+a_{0}
. We consider the linear differential equation

(5.1)

T[{\partial}](y):=y^{(N)}+a_{N-1}y^{(N-1)}+\ldots+a_{1}y^{\prime}+a_{0}y=0,

and its categorically equivalent difference equation

(5.2)

T[{\Delta}](u):=\Delta^{N}u+a_{N-1}\Delta^{N-1}u+\ldots+a_{1}\Delta u+a_{0}u=0.

It is trivial to show that the rings
C_{\partial}
and
C_{\Delta}
of constants for
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F},\partial)
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F},\Delta)
coincide.
The following result holds.

Lemma 2
.

The morphism
\rho_{\partial,\Delta}:(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{\partial})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{F},\Delta)
maps isomorphically a fundamental system of solutions
\mathcal{S}
of the linear differential equation (
5.1
) into a fundamental system
\rho_{\partial,\Delta}(\mathcal{S})
of the linear difference equation (
5.2
).

Proof.

As a consequence of Theorem
2
, power series solutions of eqs. (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are in one-to-one correspondence. The morphism
\rho_{\partial,\Delta}
maps basic sequences into basic sequences, so it preserves linear independence of solutions and the dimension of the associated vector space. Then
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\rho_{\partial,\Delta}(\mathcal{S})
is a fundamental set for eq. (
5.2
).
∎

We wish to define a Picard–Vessiot extension for eq. (
5.2
) by using the categorical approach developed before. To this aim, first we construct the
universal solution algebra

\mathcal{U}
of eq. (
5.2
). Let

(5.3)

\Delta Y=AY,\qquad A\in\mathfrak{gl}_{N}(\mathcal{F})

be its matrix form, where it is understood that
Y_{i+1,j}=\Delta Y_{i,j}
. We introduce an
N\times N
matrix of indeterminates
Y=(Y_{i,j})
and define

(5.4)

\mathcal{U}[\Delta]:=\mathcal{F}\left[Y_{ij},\frac{1}{\det(Y_{i,j})}\right],\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N.

Here we introduce the modified Wronskian

(5.5)

\det(Y_{i,j}):=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{11}&Y_{12}&\ldots&Y_{1N}\\
\Delta Y_{11}&\Delta Y_{12}&\ldots&\Delta Y_{1N}\\
\Delta^{2}Y_{11}&\ldots&&\Delta^{2}Y_{1N}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
\Delta^{N-1}Y_{11}&\ldots&&\Delta^{N-1}Y_{1N}\end{bmatrix}\ .

The columns of
Y
are formed by a fundamental system of solutions of the matrix equation
\Delta Y=AY
.

Definition 8
.

A Picard–Vessiot (PV) ring for the matrix equation (
5.3
) over the Rota algebra
(\mathcal{F},\Delta)
is a simple differential ring
\mathcal{O}
such that

i) There exists a fundamental matrix
Z\in\text{GL}_{n}(R)
for eq. (
5.3
).

ii) The ring
\mathcal{O}
is generated by
\mathcal{F}
, the entries of
Z
and
\frac{1}{\det(Z)}
.

To construct a PV-ring, we introduce the notion of
difference ideal
.

Definition 9
.

A difference ideal is an ideal generated by the elements of the form

(5.6)

\Delta^{N}X_{j}+a_{N-1}\Delta^{N-1}X_{j}+\ldots+a_{1}\Delta X_{j}+a_{0}X_{j},\qquad 1\leq j\leq N,

together with all elements obtained from them by successive applications of the operator
\Delta
.

The following result holds.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a maximal difference ideal of
\mathcal{U}[\Delta]
. Then the ring
\mathcal{V}[\Delta]:=\mathcal{U}[\Delta]/\mathcal{I}
is a Picard-Vessiot ring for the equation (
5.3
).

Proof.

It suffices to observe that in
\mathcal{U}[\Delta]/\mathcal{I}
the Wronskian (
5.5
) is invertible.
∎

With an abuse of notation, we shall denote by
\mathcal{U}[\partial]
the universal solution algebra of the matrix differential equation
Y^{\prime}=AY
associated to eq. (
5.1
), where
Y_{i+1,j}=Y^{\prime}_{i,j}
.
We introduce the notion of a differential Galois group for the previous equations.

Definition 10
.

Given the Rota algebra
(\mathcal{F},\Delta)
, let
\mathcal{V}[\Delta]
be a Picard-Vessiot ring over
\mathcal{R}
. The differential Galois group of
\mathcal{V}[\Delta]

over
(\mathcal{F},\Delta)
,
\text{DGal}\left(\mathcal{V}/(\mathcal{F},\Delta)\right)
is the group of the differential
(\mathcal{F},\Delta)
-isomorphisms.

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote a maximal differential ideal of eq. (
5.1
). The main result of this section is the following

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{W}[\partial]:=\mathcal{U}[\partial]/\mathcal{M}
a PV-ring for the linear homogeneous differential equation (
5.1
) and
\mathcal{V}[\Delta]=\mathcal{U}[\Delta]/\mathcal{I}
a PV-ring for the linear homogeneous difference equation (
5.2
). Then there exists an isomorphism of groups
\Phi
such that

(5.7)

\text{DGal}\left(\mathcal{W}/(\mathcal{F},\partial)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}\text{DGal}\left(\mathcal{U}[\Delta]/(\mathcal{F},\Delta)\right).

Proof.

According to Lemma
2
, given a fundamental set
\mathcal{S}
of solutions of eq. (
5.1
),
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\rho_{\partial,\Delta}(\mathcal{S})
is a fundamental set of solutions of eq. (
5.2
). This implies that the universal solution algebras
\mathcal{U}[\partial]
and
\mathcal{U}[\Delta]
are isomorphic. The morphism
\lambda_{\partial,\Delta}
maps a (maximal) differential ideal
\mathcal{M}
into a (maximal) difference ideal
\mathcal{I}
, so that the associated Picard-Vessiot rings are isomorphic. Then the differential isomorphisms of the Galois groups associated with eqs. (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are in one-to-one correspondence.
∎

Remark 2
.

An entirely analogous result holds for the difference equation
T[\Delta^{-}](u)=0
, where
\Delta^{-}:=1-T^{-1}
. However, the previous construction does not necessarily extend to an arbitrary delta operator
\mathcal{Q}
. In fact, the fundamental set of eq.
T[\mathcal{Q}](u)=0
generates a linear space whose dimension is, in general, greater than
N
.

6.
New integrable maps

As an illustration of the usefulness of the categorical approach developed above, we propose here some examples of integrable maps constructed according to the main theorems.
They are associated with ODEs that are particularly relevant in the applications.

6.1.
A Difference equation for the Gaussian function

We discretize the differential equation satisfied by the Gaussian function:

(6.1)

y^{\prime}(x)=-xy(x).

According to Theorem
2
, the associated integrable map reads

(6.2)

u_{n+1}-u_{n}+nu_{n-1}=0.

It admits the solution

(6.3)

u_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}g(k)\frac{n!}{(n-k)!},

where
g(k)
is the
k
-th coefficient of the Taylor expansion of the Gaussian function

(6.4)

e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g(k)\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

6.2.
Hypergeometric equation

The real hypergeometric differential equation

(6.5)

x(1-x)\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+\left[c-(a+b+1)x\right]\frac{dy}{dx}-aby=0,

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, possesses three regular singular points
\{0,1,\infty\}

[
2
]
. By way of example, we shall restrict to the singularity at
x=0
. Around this point, eq. (
6.5
) admits two algebraically independent solutions,

(6.6)

s_{1}:={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;x),

and a second solution
s_{2}
whose explicit form depends on the particular values of the parameters.
Theorem
2
provides the following discrete version of eq. (
6.5
)

(6.7)

\displaystyle(n+c)u_{n+1}-(n^{2}+(a+b+2)n+ab+c)u_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+n(a+b+2n)u_{n-1}-n(n-1)u_{n-2}=0.

If we introduce the finite Gauss sum

(6.8)

G(a,b;c;n):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!},

the solution of eq. (
6.2
) corresponding to
s_{1}
is provided by

(6.9)

u^{(1)}_{n}:=G(a,b;c;n).

Theorem
2
allows us to introduce integrable maps which discretize the differential equations characterizing families of orthogonal polynomials. Here we shall focus on the nontrivial case of the equation defining the classical family of Jacobi.

6.3.
A discrete Jacobi equation

Applying Theorem
2
, the equation

(6.10)

(1-x^{2})y^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left[\beta-\alpha-(\alpha+\beta+2)x\right]y^{\prime}(x)+m(m+\alpha+\beta+1)y(x)=0.

can be discretized in the form

(6.11)

\displaystyle u_{n+2}-(\alpha-\beta+2)u_{n+1}+\left(m^{2}-n^{2}+(m-n)(\alpha+\beta+1)+\alpha-\beta+1\right)u_{n}

\displaystyle+n(\alpha+\beta+2n)u_{n-1}-n(n-1)u_{n-2}=0

This equation admits as a solution the family of polynomials
\{J_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(n)\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

\displaystyle J_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+m+1)}{m!\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+m+1)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+m+k+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+m+1)}\frac{n!}{2^{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\frac{(-1)^{k-i}}{(n-k)!}.

Virtually all classes of orthogonal polynomials, including nonclassical and Sobolev-type ones, can be associated with integrable maps in an analogous way. The analysis of other similar cases is left to the reader.

6.4.
A nonlinear dynamical system

The differential equation

(6.12)

y^{\prime}(x)-x^{k}y(x)^{2}=0

possesses the categorical equivalent equation

(6.13)

u_{n+1}-u_{n}-\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}+n-k}}{(n-j_{1}-j_{2}-k)!}\frac{u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{2}}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!}=0.

A two parametric family of solutions of eq. (
6.13
) is provided by

(6.14)

u_{n}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}g(m)\frac{n!}{(n-m)!},\qquad\text{with}\quad g(m)=\frac{1}{m!}\frac{d^{m}}{dx^{m}}\frac{(-1)(k+1)}{x^{k+1}+c_{1}}\bigg|_{x=0}.

Many examples of nonlinear dynamical systems with
constant coefficients
are discussed in
[
47
]
.

6.5.
A new discrete Painlevé I equation.

Let us discuss the interesting case of the discretization of the classical Painlevé I equation. The differential equation is:

(6.15)

\frac{d^{2}y}{dy^{2}}-a_{2}(x)y^{2}-a_{1}(x)y-a_{0}(x)=0.

According to Theorem
3
, for
m=2
, and
N=2
, the associated difference equation is:

\displaystyle u_{n+2}-2u_{n+1}+u_{n}

\displaystyle-\sum_{s,j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{n}\alpha_{2s}\frac{(-1)^{n-s-j_{1}-j_{2}}n!}{(n-s-j_{1}-j_{2})!}\frac{u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{2}}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!}

(6.16)

\displaystyle-\sum_{s,j_{1}=0}^{n}\alpha_{1s}\frac{n!}{(n-s-j_{1})!}\frac{u_{j_{1}}}{j_{1}!}-\sum_{s=0}^{n}\alpha_{0s}\frac{n!}{(n-s)!}=0.

In particular, for the Painlevé I equation

(6.17)

y^{\prime\prime}=6y^{2}+x,

we have

(6.18)

a_{0}(x)=x,\quad a_{1}(x)=0,\quad a_{2}(x)=6.

Thus, we introduce the novel
discrete Painlevé I equation

(6.19)

u_{n+2}-2u_{n+1}+u_{n}-6\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n-j_{1}-j_{2}}n!}{(n-j_{1}-j_{2})!}\frac{u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{2}}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!}-n=0.

To check the continuum limit of eq. (
6.19
), we introduce in the semi-axis
x\geq 0
, a uniform mesh
\mathcal{L}_{h}
of points

(6.20)

x_{n}=nh,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

where
h>0
. The continuum limit is defined by
h\to 0
,
n\to\infty
and
nh
bounded. In this mesh the operator
\Delta_{h}
is defined as
\Delta_{h}u_{n}=\frac{u_{n+1}-u_{n}}{h}
, with basic polynomials

(6.21)

p_{k}(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(x-jh),

possessing zeros at the sites of the lattice. Thus:

(6.22)

p_{k}(nh)=\begin{cases}0,&\mathrm{if}\;n<k,\\
\dfrac{n!h^{k}}{(n-k)!},&\mathrm{if}\;n\geq k\end{cases}

and the inverse interpolating transform reads

(6.23)

u_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!h^{k}}{(n-k)!}b_{k},\quad b_{k}=\frac{1}{h^{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-j}}{j!(k-j)!}u_{j}.

Consequently, we rewrite eq. (
6.19
) in terms of the mesh amplitude
h
. We get

(6.24)

\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left(u_{n+2}-2u_{n+1}+u_{n}\right)-6\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{n}\frac{(1-r)^{n-j_{1}-j_{2}}n!}{(n-j_{1}-j_{2})!}\frac{u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{2}}}{j_{1}!j_{2}!}-nh=0.

A straightforward analysis shows that for
h\to 0
, equation (
6.24
) reduces to the Painlevé I equation (
6.17
).

7.
Nonlocal Lie symmetries and integrable maps: some conjectures

The categorical discretization introduced in Theorems
2
and
3
establish an isomorphism between smooth solutions of differential equations and solutions of the corresponding difference equations. Consequently, we hypothesize the existence of a correspondence among the Lie symmetry groups and algebras admitted by the continuous and discrete models respectively. For a comprehensive account of the modern theory of Lie symmetries, see
[
30
]
.

We first conjecture the existence of symmetry transformations.

Conjecture 1
.

The integrable map (
3.4
) (resp. (
3.21
)) admits a Lie group
\mathcal{G}
of nonlocal diffeomorphisms that leave the map invariant and transform solutions into solutions.

In general, objects categorically equivalent do not possess isomorphic fundamental solution sets, with the exception of linear equations with constant coefficients, as discussed in Section 6. Consequently, the full Lie algebras generated by the symmetries postulated in the previous conjecture, are, in general, not isomorphic. The subsequent conjecture establishes a weaker form of correspondence between these algebras.

Conjecture 2
.

The Lie algebra of the generators of the Lie group
\mathcal{G}
of the nonlocal symmetry diffeomorphisms associated with the map (
3.4
) (resp. (
3.21
)) contains a subalgebra which is isomorphic to the Lie algebra of the classical Lie point symmetries of the continuous dynamical system (
1.1
) (resp. (
1.2
)).
