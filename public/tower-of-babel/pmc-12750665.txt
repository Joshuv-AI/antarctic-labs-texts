Specific genetic and biological patterns underlying cortical morphological alterations in vascular cognitive impairment
Authors: Linna Ji, Sisi Peng, Hao Song, Ran Zhang, Dongwei Lu, Haoying He, Fan Fan, Yifan Fang, Juan Xu, Junjian Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pathogenic mechanisms of vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) are complicated, involving brain atrophy, parenchymal damage, and functional dysconnectivity associated with vascular risk factors, cerebrovascular diseases, and mixed pathologies. The alterations of the cortical structural similarity in VCI and their relationships with specific gene expression patterns and neurobiological characteristics have not been fully investigated.MethodsIndividual Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND) networks were constructed from structural MRI data from all participants. General linear model (GLM) and partial least squares (PLS) analysis were utilized to assess the alterations in MIND and the spatial associations of MIND differences with brain-wide transcriptional patterns. Finally, enrichment analysis of PLS weighted genes, along with cell-type-specific genes, and correlation analysis of MIND changes and neurotransmitter receptors, as well as mitochondrial metrics, were conducted to examine the neurobiological foundations of cortical morphometric similarity changes.ResultsA total of 245 individuals were enrolled, including 100 cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals and 145 VCI patients. Compared with the CU subjects, individuals with VCI showed reduced MIND in the frontal, parietal, and cingulate lobes. The PLS2− weighted genes correlated with MIND changes in VCI overlapped with genes related to oligodendrocytes and neurons. They were also substantially enriched in neuronal system activities and the RHO GTPase cycle associated with signal transduction and cytoskeleton regulation. The differences in MIND between the two groups were spatially associated with the levels of multiple neurobiological features.ConclusionsOur results improved the understanding of the transcriptional patterns and molecular features at the micro level that contribute to macroscale changes in morphological resemblance among individuals with VCI, offering potential clues for future diagnostic and therapeutic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-025-01934-0.Keywords: Vascular cognitive impairment, Morphometric inverse divergence, Transcriptional patterns, Neurobiological features, Structural similarity

Introduction

Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) is a syndrome characterized by cognitive dysfunction resulting from vascular risk factors (VRFs) and cerebrovascular diseases (CVDs) [1, 2]. Despite ongoing research, the underlying mechanisms of VCI remain unclear due to its pathological complexity and clinical heterogeneity. Using advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, some studies focused on the sizes and locations of cerebrovascular lesions and utilized the lesion-symptom mapping method to uncover the neural substrate of impaired cognitive functions [3–5]. Given that the relationships between neurological symptoms and focal stroke lesions are not completely one-to-one, a global network perspective has been suggested to complement the direct explanations of visible vascular lesions from a comprehensive view [6–8]. Previous findings indicated that disruptions of functional connectivity and diffusion fibers in certain brain networks, such as the dorsal attention, frontoparietal, and default mode networks, contributed to worse cognitive performances in VCI [9, 10].

However, gathering diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and functional MRI (fMRI) data often increases the economic cost and scan time, and image quality is susceptible to external factors such as head motion, possibly leading to biased research conclusions. In contrast, basic structural MRI (sMRI) can easily provide more accurate anatomical information and morphological features of the entire brain. Therefore, structural connectomes derived from sMRI can further supplement the existing evidence for VCI [11]. Recently, to better investigate morphological network connections, Sebenius et al. introduced a novel approach named Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND), which makes full use of multiple vertex-wise cortical measures (e.g., mean curvature [MC], surface area [SA], volume [Vol], sulcal depth [SD], and cortical thickness [CT]). The method can construct a brain similarity connectome at an individual level based on a single sMRI image. Therefore, it overcomes several technical constraints of earlier methods in this field, including group-level observation, aggregated statistics computation, and mixed metrics disturbance. For instance, compared with the morphometric similarity network (MSN), MIND networks exhibit enhanced robustness to the selection of parcellation atlases, improved reliability of network estimations across different subjects, and increased conformity with the key assumptions of cortical organization from the aspects of symmetry, cytoarchitectonics, and axonal connections [12]. A few studies have investigated MIND abnormalities and their potential molecular profiles in individuals with depression or schizophrenia [13–15], yet it is still unknown whether and how similarity networks constructed by MIND of VCI patients are changed.

Accumulating evidence suggests that gene expression profiles play a crucial role in shaping topological properties of macroscale brain network organization [16, 17], particularly affecting some metabolically costly hubs of the connectome [18]. Through driving protein interactions related to neuronal activity, these pervasive genetic effects can govern complex brain functions, such as cognition, emotion, and behavior [19, 20]. Therefore, integrating analysis of the transcriptomes and neuroimaging-derived connectomes offers an opportunity to explore the genetic basis and molecular mechanisms behind abnormal brain architectures in various neuropsychiatric disorders [21]. The Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) database, an openly accessible repository, contains microarray data from tissue samples of six donors. It encompasses over 20,000 gene expressions across 3,720 brain anatomical regions mapped in MRI-derived spaces [22]. With the superb spatial resolution, it has been considered a relatively appropriate tool for examining relationships between transcriptional expression and imaging measures [23, 24]. Moreover, advancements in data science have enabled researchers to delve into how macroscale structural connectome relates to microscale neurobiological properties in developmental and aging processes [25, 26]. Recent studies have explored the spatial distributions of multiple molecules and shared their whole-brain imaging maps, including neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial phenotypes [27, 28]. To date, few studies have assessed the correlations between cortical similarity alterations and genetic and biological profiles, as well as their underlying implications for the identification and treatment of VCI.

This study aimed to examine the features of the cortical morphological similarity network in VCI patients and to explore specific transcriptional patterns and neurobiological features underlying possible alterations of structural architecture. We hypothesized that, compared with the control group, the VCI participants would exhibit significant MIND changes across several cortical regions. The MIND difference would correlate with a genetic expression profile and multiple biological metrics. Fig. 1 illustrates the analytical framework of the study.

Methods

Participants information

The right-handed Chinese participants were recruited from the Neurology Department of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University and neighboring communities via advertisements. They were categorized into cognitively unimpaired (CU) control subjects and possible VCI patients. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for the participants can be found in the Supplemental Methods.

The data from clinical interviews and neuropsychological tests of all participants were documented by qualified neurologists and neuropsychologists. According to the available information, the possible VCI was defined as having cognitive dysfunction (i.e., a Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA] score ≤ 24) combined with common VRFs (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipemia, smoking, obesity) or mild to severe vascular brain injury on MRI (simple SVD score ≥ 1 [29]). In particular, patients with severe cortical vascular damage were excluded from the study to avoid an impact on the calculation of MIND. Most VRFs were defined as a self-reported clinical history or use of relevant medications. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) were measured, and mean arterial pressure was calculated as DBP+(SBP-DBP)/3. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2), and obesity was defined as a BMI of 28 or more. APOE genotype was tested and categorized as ε4 allele carrier and non-carrier. The CU group had normal cognition (MoCA score ≥ 25) and no severe vascular brain injury on MRI.

This study was approved by our institutional medical ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants or their guardians signed informed consent.

Neuroimaging data acquisition and processing

The MRI images were obtained at Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University. A total of 46 CU and 57 VCI images were acquired via a 3.0 T Siemens Trio Tim scanner, and 54 CU and 88 VCI MRI data were gathered via a 3.0 T GE Discovery MR750w scanner. The acquisition parameters of all 3D T1-weighted neuroimages were described in the Supplemental Methods.

All DICOM images were initially converted into NIFTI format. After visual inspections for artifacts and obvious anatomical abnormalities, participants with unqualified T1w images were excluded from the study. All included subjects’ MRI data were then processed by FreeSurfer 7.3.2 via the “recon-all” command (Detailed processes in the Supplemental Methods). Finally, cortical and subcortical measures, as well as the Euler index, were computed. The estimated total intracranial volumes (eTIV) of all participants were also extracted.

Construction of cortical similarity networks

Starting from 68 areas of the Desikan−Killiany (D-K) atlas [30], the surface of the cerebral cortex was further divided into 308 spatially adjacent areas using a backtracking algorithm [31]. The size of each area in the DK308 template is approximately 500 mm2, which can reduce the influence of parcel discrepancies [32]. The annotation corresponding to the atlas was then transformed from standard space to individual spaces of all participants by applying the “mri_surf2surf” command in FreeSurfer. Across 163,842 vertices per hemisphere, five structural features (CT, SA, SD, MC, and Vol) were standardized and aggregated within each cortical area to calculate inter-areal symmetric Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence through a k-nearest neighbor density algorithm. Based on the measure, MIND similarities among areas were estimated by 1/(1 + KL value), and a 308 × 308 MIND matrix was constructed for each participant (Fig. 1A) [12].

Case-control analysis of MIND

For the ith participant (i = 1, …, n), the global mean MIND similarity is the average of the ith 308 × 308 MIND network, whereas the regional mean MIND at the jth area (j = 1, …, 308) is the average of the jth row or column of the ith MIND matrix. In short, the regional MIND value represents the average weighted degree of each node in the MIND network. Therefore, the higher the MIND at a region is, the greater its similarity to all other regions, which means that the region may be a hub node in the whole brain MIND connectome.

The general linear models (GLM) were built, controlling for age, sex, and education, to investigate global and regional MIND differences between the CU controls and VCI patients [33]. In these models, MIND values were treated as the dependent variable, while the group served as the independent variable. The final case-control t statistics (contrast = VCI−CU) and Benjamini Hochberg−False Discovery Rate (BH-FDR) adjusted p values were computed and extracted for subsequent analysis. The significance was set at BH-FDR corrected p value < 0.05. In addition, to observe the spatial pattern of MIND alteration, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the regional MIND in the CU group and the case-control t values. Furthermore, to better interpret the implications of MIND changes, we employed two established cortical classifications: the functional Yeo 7 network based on resting-state fMRI connectivity [34, 35] and the cellular structure von Economo atlas according to cytoarchitectonic criteria [32]. We applied the same GLM to compare the case-control MIND differences across the networks and classes, controlling for age, sex, and education. The results were adjusted by the BH-FDR, with p values < 0.05.

Correlations of MIND with cognitive function

With age, sex, and education as covariates, we conducted the partial correlation analyses to examine the correlations between MIND similarities and the MoCA score in all participants. The mean global and regional MIND in cortical areas with significant case-control differences were calculated as the MIND measures. The results were corrected using BH-FDR, with p values < 0.05. Moreover, to assess whether the association of vascular burden with the global cognitive performance was mediated by the structural similarity alterations, we calculated the SVD total score based on Staals et al.’s method [36] and the mean MIND across the cortical regions where MIND changed significantly in the VCI patients. Mediation analyses were then conducted when adjusting for age, sex, and education.

Gene expression data processing and matrices estimation

The Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) [37] offers brain-wide gene expression data obtained from six postmortem brains (age = 42.50 ± 13.38 years, female/male = 1/5). We utilized the abagen toolbox to prepare and process the AHBA dataset [38]. Briefly, the standardized processing pipeline was as follows: (i) defining a parcellation; (ii) updating the probe-to-gene annotations according to Arnatkevičiūte et al. [39]; (iii) filtering low-intensity probes; (iv) selecting representative probes; (v) matching samples to brain areas; (vi) handling missing data; (vii) sample and gene normalization; and (viii) samples and regions combination (Detailed parameters in the Supplemental Methods). Ultimately, a 308 × 15,633 gene expression matrix was acquired, representing 308 brain regions and 15,633 retained genes. Since only two volunteers in the AHBA dataset have samples of the right hemisphere, the study exclusively focused on the left hemisphere. Finally, a 152 × 15,633 transcriptional expression matrix was considered for subsequent analysis.

Transcription-neuroimaging correlation analysis

A partial least squares (PLS) analysis was utilized to relate the MIND case-control t statistics computed exclusively within the left hemisphere (the response variable) to the 152 × 15,633 gene expression matrix (the predictor variable). Both variables were z-score normalized. The PLS components, which were the linear representations of the weighted transcriptional expression values, can be ranked according to their explained variances between the predictive and response variables. In general, the first few PLS components can be viewed as the low-dimensional indicators, which can better interpret the covariance of a data matrix with high-dimensional information [40]. To determine whether the variances explained by the PLS components considerably surpassed the expectation by chance, the spatial permutation test (permuting the response variable 10,000 times based on spherical rotation) was performed. In addition, a bootstrapping method (i.e., resampling 10,000 times with replacement of the 152 regions) was applied to test the significance of each gene contained in each PLS component. All genes were ordered based on their z scores, calculated by dividing each gene’s weight by its standard error. The p-value was adjusted by BH-FDR.

Aside from the genes with significance in PLS analysis, we also sorted all cortical risk genes relevant to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) from the AHBA dataset (https://help.brain-map.org/display/humanbrain/Documentation) and searched dementia-related genes from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to exploratorily investigate the impact of possible comorbid pathology on VCI patients. The links between dementia-related gene expression and between-group t values in the left hemisphere were tested using the Pearson method with BH-FDR p values < 0.05.

Enrichment analyses

To identify potential functional signatures and pathways of some genes in the significant PLS component, we followed the standardized workflow in Metascape software [41] to conduct the enrichment analysis of PLS+/- gene lists (FDR p < 0.05). With the genome as background and the Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome databases as reference, similar genes with counts ≥ 3, enrichment factors >1.5, and p values < 0.01 were selected and classified into various clusters. Moreover, a meta-analysis of multiple gene lists was executed to investigate whether the PLS- genes had common enriched pathways and biological processes with risk genes of all-cause dementia (ACD) and VaD from a recent GWAS [42].

Additionally, a tool of WebCSEA (Web-based Cell-type Specific Enrichment Analysis of Genes) [43] was utilized to preliminarily assess the tissue-cell-type (TC) specificity of the PLS+/- gene lists across 1,355 human TCs, which can provide insight into how these genes interact with multiple organ systems to impact VCI development. Particularly, to further validate the cell-type specific genes in the PLS+/− sets from the perspective of the neurological system, we overlapped the PLS+/- genes with the compiled genes across seven cell types, including astrocytes, microglia cells, endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursors (OPCs), and excitatory and inhibitory neurons, which were provided by Seidlitz et al. [44] based on five single-cell studies using adult cortical tissues. A permutation test was employed to generate the p values of overlapping results, which were then adjusted using the BH-FDR method. A similar meta-analysis for seven cell-type gene lists was also performed to identify common enrichment pathways.

Correlations between MIND and neurobiological features

The DK308-parcellated spatial maps of 26 neurobiological features, comprising nineteen receptors and transporters across nine different neurotransmitter systems, a microstructure of synapse density, and six mitochondrial features, were gathered from recent studies by Hansen et al. [27] and Mosharov et al. [28], which were also easily accessible by the neuromaps toolbox [45]. We explored whether specific features were co-localized with the MIND degree in CU participants via the correlation method. Besides, we examined the regional associations of these neurobiological features with MIND alterations between the CU group and VCI patients. All p values were calculated using the spatial permutation test and then corrected using BH-FDR.

Null model

A “spin”-based spatial permutation test was employed in this study to account for the spatial autocorrelations among various brain features [35, 46]. With the prerequisite of the preserved spatial covariance structure of the original maps, this method can generate many null Pearson’s correlation coefficients by conducting spherical rotations of brain maps. Here, we performed 10,000 spin permutation tests at parcel resolution to establish null distributions. All pspin values were defined as the ratio of the null correlation values that exceeded the actual coefficients.

Reproducibility analysis

To ensure the credibility of our results, several additional analyses were also performed: (i) examining the robustness of MIND differences between the two groups by regressing the effect of eTIV, Euler index, and scanner; (ii) evaluating how different parcel sizes affected MIND degrees in all participants by the correlation method; (iii) correcting possible scanner-related effects using ComBatLS [47] and further validating the between-group cortical similarity differences based on the corrected MIND degrees. (iv) ascertaining the reliability of functional enrichment findings in the PLS genes by the meta-analyses of multiple gene lists; (v) validating the reliability of AHBA gene expression patterns against the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) data across a wide age range (Supplemental Methods). The data were obtained from the GTEx Portal on 11/07/2025 [48, 49].

Case-control MIND differences

After regressing for age, sex, and education, the global MIND in VCI patients was significantly lower than that of CU subjects (t = −2.7331, p = 0.007). As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the regional MIND values of the two groups exhibited a roughly normal distribution across all 308 regions. A significant difference was found in the regional distributions between the CU controls and VCI patients (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p < 0.001). Specifically, Fig. 2B depicted similar cortical maps of the regional MIND distributions in the two groups, revealing that most frontal, temporal, and parietal regions had high MIND degrees, while the limbic and occipital lobes showed relatively low MIND degrees. Furthermore, the case-control t statistics in MIND degrees across 308 regions were mapped, in which positive values indicated increased MIND in VCI patients and negative values indicated decreased MIND (Fig. 2C). Compared to the CU participants, the regional MIND degrees of VCI patients significantly reduced in the left rostral middle frontal cortex (part 4), left supramarginal cortex (part 2), and right isthmus cingulate cortex (part 1) (Fig. 2C; Table S1). Moreover, the mean MIND across 308 regions in the CU subjects was strongly correlated with the MIND differences between the two groups (Fig. 2D; Pearson’s r = −0.40, pspin<0.001), implying that the MIND values of regions with greater cortical similarities in the CU subjects tended to decrease in the VCI patients.

At the subnetwork level, regarding the Yeo fMRI networks, MIND degrees of the VCI subjects showed significant reductions in the ventral attention, dorsal attention, limbic, default mode, somatomotor, and frontoparietal networks (Fig. 2E; Table S2), whereas in the von Economo classes, patients with VCI had notably decreased MIND values in the cytoarchitectural class 1 (primary motor cortex), class 2 (association cortex 1), and class 3 (association cortex 2) (Fig. 2F; Table S3).

In addition, we found that the average global and regional MIND in the left rostral middle frontal cortex (part 4), left supramarginal cortex (part 2), and right isthmus cingulate cortex (part 1) were significantly associated with the MoCA score, with age, sex, and education as covariates (BH-FDR p values < 0.05, Table S4; Fig. S1A). Mediation analyses showed that the mean regional MIND mediated the association between the SVD total score and the MoCA score (Fig. S1B).

Transcription-neuroimaging correlations

Limited by the available microarray data in the AHBA, we focused on the final generated transcriptional matrix of 152 regions in the left hemisphere × 15,633 genes selected by the abagen toolbox (Fig. 3A). Based on the matrix and the between-group t statistics, the PLS analysis was conducted to determine the transcriptional patterns related to the between-group MIND differences across 152 regions. According to the results of the permutation test, a total of 30.88% cumulative variance was significantly explained by the first and second PLS components (PLS1 and PLS2), but only PLS2 significantly explained 18.16% variance of the macrostructural similarity discrepancy (Fig. S2A and B; pspin<0.05). The distribution of the PLS2 weighted gene expression map exhibited an approximately gradient pattern, gradually increasing from the parietal and frontal lobes to the temporal and limbic regions (Fig. 3B). The PLS2 score was positively associated with the between-group t statistics in the left hemisphere (Fig. 3C; Pearson’s r = 0.43, pspin<0.001). All genes were sorted in descending order by their contributions to PLS2 measured by z scores (Supplement Data). As shown in Fig. 3D, some genes positively weighted on PLS2 had positive correlations with t statistics between the two groups (e.g., PANX2, r = 0.20, pspin=0.011). In contrast, genes with negative weights on PLS2 were negatively correlated with MIND differences between the two groups (e.g., GPX3, r = −0.38, pspin<0.001). This suggests that genes with positive weights were overexpressed in cortical areas with increased MIND degree in VCI subjects, and the opposite for negatively weighted genes. Finally, a total of 859 PLS2 + genes and 1034 PLS2− genes significantly contributing to the PLS2 score were identified and utilized for subsequent analyses (Supplement Data; BH-FDR p < 0.05).

To preliminarily explore the potential genetic impact of possible comorbid AD on MIND alterations in VCI patients, we collected all 28 AD-related genes from the AHBA database and found that all of them were contained in the 15,633 genes. Though all 28 genes had contributions to the PLS2 score, only 3 genes (APLP1, BACE2, and PLAU) were significantly correlated with t statistics between the left hemispheres of the two groups (Table S5; BH-FDR pspin<0.05). Moreover, a total of 638 dementia-related genes were identified from the NCBI. Of them, 525 genes were included in the 15,633 background genes, and 54 genes overlapped with PLS2+/- gene lists (16 genes in the PLS2 + and 38 genes in the PLS2-). A total of 23 genes were significantly correlated with the between-group MIND differences in the left hemisphere (Table S6; BH-FDR pspin<0.05).

Enrichment pathways of genes related to changes in MIND

Based on three ontology sources, we used Metascape software to annotate the functions of the PLS2 +/− genes and identify statistically significant biological processes. The top 5 enriched pathways of the PLS2 + genes were “Translation”, “Metabolism of RNA”, “plasma membrane bounded cell projection assembly”, “Neddylation”, and “SUMO is transferred from E1 to E2 (UBE2I, UBC9)”. (Fig. 4A; Fig. S3A; BH-FDR corrected p < 0.05). On the contrary, the PLS2− genes were predominantly enriched in cellular GO biological processes, “Neuronal System”, “RHO GTPase cycle”, and “Mitophagy-animal” pathways. (Fig. 4B; Fig. S3B; BH-FDR corrected p < 0.05). These mechanisms may play important roles in the changes of the regional MIND values in the VCI patients.

As depicted in Fig. 4C and B, the PLS2 + genes were significantly enriched in multiple cell types in the lymphatic and digestive systems, while the PLS2− genes were prominently involved in oligodendrocytes. Furthermore, we assigned the PLS2+/− genes to seven distinct brain cell types compiled from previous studies to examine their cellular specificity in the nervous system. The permutation test uncovered a significant overlap between the PLS2− genes and the genes found in oligodendrocytes (number = 160), excitatory neurons (number = 147), and inhibitory neurons (number = 98) (Fig. 4E; all BH-FDR adjusted p < 0.001), whereas no significant overlap was found within the PLS2 + gene list (Fig. S4). For these overlapping genes across different cell types, an enrichment analysis indicated that the changes in MIND of VCI patients may be linked to the biological processes of neuron morphogenesis, cell development, and synaptic signaling in glial cells and neurons. (Fig. 4F).

Relationships between MIND changes and neurobiological features

Following the aforementioned analysis results, we further investigated the relationships between 26 neurobiological features (Detailed information was listed in Table S7) and the MIND degrees in the two groups through correlation analyses. As shown in Fig. 5A and Table S8, the regional MIND degrees among the CU subjects were notably associated with the area-level densities of neurotransmitter receptors (5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, α4β2, M1, NMDA, mGluR5, CB1, MOR), the microstructural marker (synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2 A), and all mitochondria metrics (CI, CII, CIV, MitoD, TRC, MRC), which indicated that these features tended to be co-localized with more connected hub regions. Conversely, three transporters, serotonin (5-HTT), dopamine (DAT), and acetylcholine (VAChT) showed significant negative correlations with MIND values in the CU subjects.

Moreover, in the whole brain across 308 cortical regions, only the higher densities of α4β2 and MOR were significantly associated with reduced case-control t statistics (α4β2, r = −0.19, BH-FDR adjusted pspin=0.042; MOR, r = −0.22, BH-FDR adjusted pspin=0.042; Fig. 5B and C; Table S8), implying that cortical regions with these higher receptors may tend to have decreased MIND in VCI patients. No significant correlations were found among the other features. Furthermore, we examined the correlations separately for the left and right hemispheres. Figure 5D and Table S9 showed that neurotransmitters (5-HT4, α4β2, mGluR5, CB1, MOR, and 5-HTT) and mitochondria metrics (CI, CIV, MitoD, TRC) were notably associated with the between-group MIND differences in the left hemisphere, while no significant results were found in the right hemisphere.

Reproducibility analysis of MIND changes and gene expression

To determine the reliability of between-group MIND discrepancies in the two groups, we tested whether the MIND disparities were influenced by variations of eTIV, image quality (Euler index), and scanner. The mean global MIND degrees in all participants showed no relationship with eTIV but were associated with the Euler index. After taking eTIV, Euler index, or scanner as covariates, the case-control differences in MIND were still highly associated with those without correcting them (r = 0.99, pspin<0.001; Fig. S5A, B, and C). In addition, the MIND degrees of CU individuals assessed by the GE scanner were strongly associated with those scanned by the Siemens (r = 0.97, pspin<0.001; Fig. S5C), and the associations between the global MIND degrees based on the DK308 atlas and those computed by the DK68 atlas were also significant (r = 0.93, pspin<0.001; Fig. S5D). The results demonstrated that the MIND values were remarkably consistent across different scanners or parcel sizes. After correcting MIND degrees using ComBatLS, there were still significant differences in the global MIND (t = − 2.4374, p = 0.016) and regional MIND in the same three cortical areas (Table S10).

In our study, we incorporated the ACD- and VaD-related genes from a GWAS study in a multi-gene-list meta-analysis. The results unveiled several common biological processes between the genes linked to VaD and PLS2+/− genes. Notable pathways included “import into cell”, “regulation of secretion by cell”, “MAPK signaling pathway”, and “Nervous system development”, which aligned with the enriched processes of the PLS2 gene lists (Fig. S6). Moreover, we identified four genes from the VaD-related gene list that appeared in the PLS2 weighted gene lists, including DOK5, FOXA2, and PSMA3 in the PLS2 + genes, and WAC in the PLS2− genes. The expressions of FOXA2 and PSMA3 genes were significantly positively associated with the between-group MIND differences in the left hemisphere (FOXA2, r = 0.27, BH-FDR corrected pspin<0.001; PSMA3, r = 0.24, BH-FDR corrected pspin=0.002; Fig. S7A). Conversely, the WAC gene showed a negative association with the between-group MIND disparities (r = −0.26, BH-FDR corrected pspin=0.002; Fig. S7B). In addition, the validation results about the reliability of ABHA data were shown in Supplemental Results and Fig. S8.

Discussion

By constructing individual MIND networks of all participants, we first investigated alterations in cortical similarity patterns among VCI patients and linked MIND changes to transcriptional gene expression from the ABHA database and various neurobiological features from prior studies. The findings demonstrated that the MIND phenotypes and alterations in VCI patients correlated with distinctive gene expression patterns and neurobiological features, suggesting underlying pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic targets in VCI.

MIND networks have been validated to align with the foundational principles of cortical organizations, where similar brain regions are more likely to be connected by white matter fibers and belong to the same cytoarchitectonic class [12]. Our results indicated that the axonal connectivity throughout the brain of VCI patients was decreased. It is in accord with the “disconnection hypothesis” of VCI, which has been backed by many studies [6, 50]. Specifically, three regions within the frontal, parietal, and cingulate lobes had fewer anatomical connections and greater cytoarchitectural variations with other cortical areas. Additionally, the mean MIND of three regions mediated the relationship between the SVD total score and the MoCA score. Consistent with our results, prior studies have found that VRFs and CVDs tend to compromise the connectedness of the frontoparietal white matter fibers and the cingulum bundle, ultimately impairing executive and memory functions [51, 52]. From the subnetwork scales, we further found that the VCI-related changes in MIND appeared across multiple Yeo functional areas and von Economo cytoarchitectonic classes. These findings suggest that specific MIND alterations could serve as a promising imaging indicator of structural organization in the early stages of VCI. Given MIND’s easy accessibility and high reliability, future studies can verify its effectiveness as an auxiliary index in diagnosing and predicting cognitive decline in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Furthermore, we found that the PLS2 genes contributed to VCI-related MIND changes to varying degrees. Generally, the PLS2+/− genes tend to be overexpressed in cortical areas with increased/decreased MIND similarity in VCI patients. In short, compared with CU subjects, PLS2+/− genes were linked to increased/decreased cortical structural similarity in VCI patients. For example, PANX2 and GPX3 had the strongest positive and negative correlations with the case-control MIND differences, respectively. Prior studies have demonstrated that the large-pore ATP-permeable channel protein coded by PANX2 is mainly expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) and contributes to the development of ischemic brain injury [53]. GPX3, coding a plasma glutathione peroxidase widely expressed in the body, is important for preventing oxidative damage and regulating cellular metabolism, thereby influencing many CVDs and neurodegenerative disorders [54, 55]. In addition to previously identified genes related to VCI [56, 57], such as CNTNAP2, ZDHHC20, and VEGFA, the PLS analysis in our study also found some other possible candidate genes related to MIND changes among VCI patients, involving several genes regulated by those loci related to WMH-associated cortical atrophy or cortical thickness [58]. Our findings enhance understanding of the genetic factors linked to sporadic VCI and provide new insights for managing its progression through targeted therapeutic interventions.

The PLS2 + genes were notably enriched in functional pathways linked to nucleic acid metabolism and protein modification. These pathways have been explored in various neurovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, which are anticipated to serve as effective biomarkers or therapeutic targets for VCI [59–61]. The PLS2− genes showed significant enrichment in multiple pathways, involving the neuronal system, energy metabolism, cytoskeletal organization, protein homeostasis, synaptic transmission, and signal transduction. They may contain extra information for the development of VCI beyond pathways enriched for the VaD-related GWAS risk genes. Recently, Wilson et al. described eight hallmarks of most neurodegenerative diseases in detail. They further proposed an integrated framework highlighting the necessity for multi-dimensional classifications and multi-targeted interventions [62]. Converging evidence emphasizes the close interconnectedness of multiple mechanisms in regulating the onset and progression of VCI.

Moreover, the PLS2− genes were significantly overlapped with genes related to oligodendrocytes and neurons. As an integral part of neuronal circuits, oligodendrocytes are not only dynamic myelin providers to maintain the structural integrity and plasticity of axons, but also are metabolic and trophic suppliers to support multiple functions of synaptic regulation and signal transmission [63]. Dysfunction in oligodendrocytes can impair myelin sheaths, disrupt signaling pathways, and even trigger neuroinflammatory cascades [64]. This makes them hopeful therapeutic targets for VCI [65]. The roles of excitatory neurons and inhibitory neurons in neurodegenerative diseases and neurovascular coupling have also been investigated through animal experiments, as well as single-cell transcriptomic and neuroimaging approaches [66–69]. Furthermore, the multi-gene-list enrichment meta-analysis of seven brain cell types revealed that the “actin filament-based process” and “Neuronal System”, the two most enriched processes, were linked to microglia cells and astrocytes. Consistent with previous reports, microglia cells and astrocytes were vital elements in the CNS, working together with other neuronal elements to ensure brain homeostasis. Disturbances of their structures and functions can lead to excessive inflammation and damage to the neurovascular unit and synaptic circuits, culminating in cognitive decline [70, 71]. Although no significant enrichment was observed among the brain cell types, the PLS2 + genes were notably enriched in the lymphatic and digestive systems. This implies that systemic immune and intestinal homeostasis might play roles in the MIND changes of VCI. The effects of both mechanisms on VCI and their causal interactions have raised great concern over the past few years [72, 73].

Sebenius et al. reported that neuron-specific genes were highly expressed in hub regions with high MIND degrees [12]. Our results aligned with existing evidence, showing that neurotransmitter receptors, synaptic density markers, and mitochondrial metrics were co-located with the high-degree MIND areas. Intriguingly, three neurotransmitter transporters were highly presented in less connected regions of MIND networks. Moreover, in cortical regions where the densities of α4β2 and MOR were high in the healthy state, the MIND degrees tended to decrease in VCI patients. The α4β2 receptor, one of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), is mainly expressed in the CNS and plays a major role in regulating memory and learning functions [74]. Dysfunction of the cholinergic system can lead to cognitive impairment. Previous studies have shown that the α4β2 receptor was reduced in both the blood and brain of VCI patients and ischemic animal models [75]. Thus, corresponding therapies targeting cholinergic function have been developed and applied in clinical practice [76]. The MOR is a classical G-protein-coupled opioid receptor that participates in the regulation of the stress response, behavioral performance, and immune function [77]. The lowered MOR receptor availability could be relevant to the subclinical anxious and depressive symptoms in humans [78]. Across 152 regions in the left hemisphere, the densities of multiple neurotransmitters, including serotonin, glutamate, acetylcholine, cannabinoid, and opioid, as well as the tissue density and OXPHOS capacity of mitochondria, may be affected by conditions of ischemia and hypoxia. These changes could directly or indirectly lead to structural morphology changes in VCI [79, 80]. However, no significant results were observed in the right hemisphere. The implicit lateralization might be induced by hemispheric differences in molecular distributions, cortical abnormality profiles, and cerebrovascular damage loads, which warrant further investigation.

The findings in this study should be explained with the following considerations. First, as a heterogeneous disease, VCI has many etiologies and can be categorized into various forms. We primarily focused on the VRF-related VCI and subcortical ischemic VCI (SIVCI), which are mostly caused by CSVD. The relatively intact cortical structures make them suitable for similarity analysis, while their potential pathological mechanisms remain unclear. Owing to the restricted number of participants and cross-sectional design, additional longitudinal research is required to validate our findings and provide more evidence for VCI. Second, although the MIND degrees of CU subjects were highly correlated [81], the structural images were collected from two different MRI machines, and the results need to be verified in other unified datasets. Third, the AHBA database and mitochondrial neuroimage maps were derived from only six and one postmortem brain tissue samples, respectively. Given the scarcity of extensive postmortem data, these resources can serve as valuable normal references, enhancing the comparability of results across different studies. The demographic characteristics of the CU group in this study differ from those of the volunteers in the AHBA database and neuroimaging maps. As a result, the patterns of cortical gene expression and neurobiological features may vary to some degree. Fourth, we performed neuroimage-transcription correlation analysis only for the left hemisphere due to the restricted AHBA data, which may ignore potential lateralization discrepancies in gene expression. Last, limited by the sample size and study design, the underlying interactions between genes and the environment in sporadic VCI are unclear and still need to be explored in larger longitudinal cohort studies.

Conclusion

In summary, our research demonstrated a reduction in the cortical morphological similarity of VCI patients, involving the frontal, parietal, and cingulate lobes, as well as multiple functional and cytoarchitectural subnetworks, which highlights the variability patterns in the cortex and supports the existing hypothesis of disconnection in VCI. These alterations in the MIND network were not only spatially correlated with specific gene expression profiles, but also were associated with certain neurotransmitter receptors and mitochondrial features. The identified genes overlapped with several AD- and VCI-related risk genes reported in other studies and were notably expressed in different cell types. In terms of genetic functions, these genes were predominantly enriched in various neurobiological pathways, including cellular energy metabolism, cytoskeletal organization, protein homeostasis, and synaptic transmission, suggesting intricate mechanisms and comprehensive management of VCI. Overall, our results throw light on the potential genetic background and molecular mechanisms of macro-level cortical similarity alterations in VCI, providing new ideas for upcoming pathogenesis and therapeutic studies.
