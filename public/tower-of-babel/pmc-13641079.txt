Burden and Risk Factors for Severe Respiratory Syncytial Virus Infection among Pediatric Hematopoietic Cell Transplant Recipients
Authors: Diego R Hijano, Surabhi B Vora, Yilun Sun, Li Tang, Ronald H Dallas, Jose A Ferrolino, Madeline B Johnson, Kim J Allison, Monica Ardura, Emily Ansusinha, Juri Boguniewicz, Janine Dailey Garnes, Kathryn P Goggin, Alexis Gossett, Benjamin Hanisch, Nour Hasan, Sarah M Heston, Jennifer R Jackson, Madam Kumar, Courtney McDonald, Eric McGrath, William J Muller, Anne Rose, Frances Saccoccio, Beth K Thielen, Mary Suzanne Whitworth, Lara Danziger-Isakov, Janet A Englund, Gabriela Maron, Chikara Ogimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes substantial morbidity in pediatric hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) recipients; however, risk factors for severe disease remain incompletely characterized.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study across 16 U.S. transplant centers, evaluating children and adolescents (0–19 years) who underwent HCT and developed a laboratory-confirmed RSV infection between 2010 and 2019. Clinical characteristics, laboratory values, and immunosuppressive and transplant history were analyzed. Outcomes included new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement, RSV-related hospitalization, progression to lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI), invasive mechanical ventilation, and 90-day mortality. Univariate logistic regression was used to screen candidate predictors, followed by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator for variable selection, and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors associated with clinical outcomes.ResultsAmong 204 pediatric HCT recipients with RSV infection, 41.7% had RSV-related hospitalization, 21.2% required new or increased supplemental oxygen, and 4% required invasive mechanical ventilation within 28 days after the diagnosis. Mortality within 90 days was 5.4%, with one death attributable to RSV. In multivariable models, female sex, younger age (particularly <5 years), neutropenia (ANC <500/mm3), presence of co-pathogen in the respiratory tract and chronic lung disease were independently associated with this oxygen outcome, while non-peripheral graft sources and T-cell–depleting therapy were associated with RSV-related hospitalization.ConclusionsSevere outcomes are common in pediatric HCT recipients with RSV infection. Younger age, markers of impaired immune recovery, history of chronic lung disease, and T-cell–depleting therapy are major determinants of adverse outcomes. These findings support targeted risk stratification and may inform preventive and treatment strategies in this high-risk population.Keywords: respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), Pediatric hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT), immunocompromised children, RSV outcomes, risk factors

INTRODUCTION

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the leading cause of lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) in young children worldwide.1–4 In immunocompromised hosts, including pediatric hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) recipients, RSV can cause substantial morbidity with increased risks of pneumonia, respiratory failure, and death compared with otherwise healthy children.5–7

Prior studies in adult HCT recipients identified risk factors for poor outcomes and led to validated clinical scoring systems that guide prognostication and management in that population.8,9 In contrast, there are not comparable multicenter data to define risk factors for severe RSV disease in pediatric HCT recipients or to support development of pediatric-specific prediction tools. Existing pediatric literature is limited by small cohorts, single-center designs, and incomplete representation of transplant characteristics and immune status.10–12

The need for improved risk stratification in children is particularly pressing. Treatment options remain limited; ribavirin is the only licensed antiviral, yet its optimal dosing in children is uncertain, and inhaled formulations are costly and logistically challenging.13–20 RSV prophylaxis guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics do not specifically address HCT recipients.21,22 Although long-acting monoclonal antibodies such as nirsevimab and clesrovimab expand preventive options for young children, including immunocompromised hosts, their effectiveness, dosing, and tolerability for older children are unknown. Furthermore, three RSV vaccines are currently licensed in the United States, none of which is available for children.23,24 As new preventive and therapeutic strategies continue to advance, defining which immunocompromised children stand to benefit most becomes increasingly important, including identifying populations in whom the risk–benefit balance may favor participation in clinical trials of investigational antivirals.25–27

To address these gaps, we conducted a multicenter retrospective study across U.S. pediatric transplant centers to characterize the risk factors for morbidity and mortality associated with RSV infection in pediatric HCT recipients. We evaluated clinical, transplant-related, and immunologic determinants of disease severity, including RSV-related hospitalization, new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement, LRTI, invasive mechanical ventilation, and mortality.

METHODS

Study Design and Cohort

We conducted a multicenter retrospective observational cohort study coordinated by the Pediatric Infectious Diseases Transplant Network (PIDTRAN) at St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at St. Jude and by each participating institution, with a waiver of informed consent.

Children and adolescents were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) underwent HCT between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2019; (2) were younger than 20 years at the time of transplant; and (3) had a first RSV infection identified within 365 days post-transplant. Data from 16 U.S. pediatric transplant centers were included.

Study Definitions

RSV infection was defined as the presence of respiratory symptoms along with laboratory confirmation of RSV by a clinically validated diagnostic test. LRTI was defined as RSV detected from a lower respiratory tract specimen, such as tracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage, or as RSV detected from an upper respiratory tract specimen accompanied by new radiographic abnormalities consistent with LRTI and a clinician’s diagnosis of bronchiolitis or pneumonia. RSV-related hospitalization was recorded in the case report form as a predefined study outcome within 28 days after RSV diagnosis. No additional coding or adjudication was applied for patients already hospitalized at the time of diagnosis. Supplemental oxygen requirement was defined as new or increased oxygen support above baseline delivered by any modality and sustained for more than 4 hours per day. Lymphopenia, neutropenia, and monocytopenia were defined a priori as ALC <300/μL, ANC <500/mm3, and absolute monocyte count <100/μL, respectively, based on prior HCT respiratory virus literature and expert consensus within the study group. Organ failure at diagnosis was defined as documented dysfunction of one or more non-respiratory organ systems (eg, renal, hepatic, cardiovascular, or neurologic) present at the time of RSV diagnosis, as recorded in the medical record. Comorbidities were recorded as a binary variable indicating the presence of one or more clinician-documented conditions captured in the REDCap case report form, including heart, kidney, or liver failure; multiorgan failure; pulmonary disorders; or another specified condition. Chronic lung disease was also captured and analyzed as a separate prespecified variable. Respiratory co-pathogens included concomitant viral, bacterial, or fungal pathogens identified from respiratory specimens, while blood co-pathogens included viral DNAemia/viremia and bacterial or fungal bloodstream pathogens documented in the medical record. Formal adjudication of pathogen significance was not performed. Given the retrospective multicenter design, standardized physiologic thresholds were not applied, and all definitions were based on clinician documentation recorded in the medical record.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary objective of the study was to characterize the clinical outcomes and severity of RSV infection in pediatric HCT recipients using outcomes previously described in the literature and of established clinical relevance.27–30 These outcomes included new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement above baseline (primary outcome), RSV-related hospitalization assessed within 28 days after RSV diagnosis, development of LRTI and requirement for invasive mechanical ventilation, each assessed within 28 days after RSV diagnosis, and RSV-attributable death assessed within 90 days of diagnosis. The oxygen outcome and LRTI were evaluated as separate clinical outcomes according to the prespecified study definitions.

Data Collection

Clinical data were abstracted from the EMR into the REDCap system hosted at the PIDTRAN Coordinating Center at St. Jude. Cases were identified at each participating site by applying standardized study inclusion criteria to capture all eligible patients with laboratory-confirmed RSV infection during the study period. Collected variables included demographics, transplant characteristics, underlying diagnoses, comorbidities, graft-versus-host disease, receipt of immunosuppressive and antiviral therapies, palivizumab (the only RSV-specific monoclonal antibody available during the study period), immune globulin, and laboratory results, selected a priori based on prior HCT respiratory virus studies, including those conducted by PIDTRAN.31,32

Data Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Risk factors for each clinical outcome were evaluated using univariate and multivariable logistic regression with robust standard errors. Robust standard errors were used to reduce reliance on conventional model-based standard error estimates, particularly because some models included sparse covariate patterns that could result in high-leverage observations. Variables with a P-value < .10 in univariate analyses were included in a candidate pool and entered into least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) models for variable selection. This initial screening step was used to reduce the number of candidate predictors considered in LASSO, given the limited number of events for some outcomes and missing data among candidate predictors. The LASSO penalty parameter was selected using 10-fold cross-validation, with cross-validated ROC AUC used as the selection criterion. Variables retained by LASSO were included in the final multivariable logistic regression models, regardless of their individual p-values. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1, and a two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 204 pediatric HCT recipients with RSV infection were included. The median age at diagnosis was 6 years (range, 0–19), and 10.8% of patients were younger than 2 years. Males accounted for 54% of the cohort and 58% were White. Hematologic malignancies were the largest single underlying indication for transplantation (42.2%). Most children (84.8%) had undergone a single transplant, and 77% underwent allogeneic HCT. Myeloablative conditioning was used in 73% of recipients. T-cell–depleting agents (ATG or alemtuzumab) were included in conditioning regimens for 27% of patients (Table 1).

At the time of RSV diagnosis, 55.9% of children were receiving GVHD prophylaxis or treatment. Co-pathogens were identified within 48 hours of RSV detection in 30.9% of children from respiratory samples and 12.3% from blood samples; detailed distributions of respiratory and blood co-pathogens are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Steroid exposure within the preceding 2 weeks was found in 30.4% of the cohort. IVIG and palivizumab had been administered within 4 weeks prior to RSV diagnosis in 35.3% and 3.9% of patients, respectively. Organ failure at diagnosis was uncommon (5.4%) (Table 1). Ribavirin was administered to 49 (22.9%) patients. IVIG and palivizumab were administered within 28 days after RSV diagnosis in 90 (42.1%) and 34 (15.9%) patients, respectively. Because these therapies were administered after diagnosis and frequently in response to clinical severity, they were not included in the primary multivariable risk factor models to minimize confounding by indication.

Clinical Outcomes

Of the 204 children, 85 (41.7%) had RSV-related hospitalization, and 43 (21.2%) required new or increased supplemental oxygen during their illness. Of these 43 children, 18 (41.9%) had a baseline supplemental oxygen requirement before RSV diagnosis. Twenty-eight children (13.7%) presented with LRTI at diagnosis. Among the 176 patients who presented with URTI at diagnosis, progression status was available for 172, of whom 10 (5.8%) subsequently progressed to LRTI. Invasive mechanical ventilation was required in eight children (3.9%). Among these 43 children, the maximum level of respiratory support was low-flow oxygen in 26 (60.5%), high-flow nasal cannula in 6 (14.0%), CPAP/BiPAP in 1 (2.3%), and invasive mechanical ventilation in 8 (18.6%); support level could not be determined for 2 children (Supplementary Table 2). Eleven children (5.4%) died within 90 days of RSV diagnosis (median time from infection to death, 40 days; range, 11–68), with one death (0.5%) attributed directly to RSV (Figure 1).

Children requiring additional oxygen support above baseline were younger and had more profound cytopenias than those who did not (Table 1). Similar patterns were observed for invasive mechanical ventilation and death, although event numbers were limited (Supplementary Table 3). Deaths occurred predominantly among children with comorbidities and organ failure (Supplementary Table 4).

Risk Factors for Severe RSV Disease

New or Increased Supplemental Oxygen Requirement

In univariate analysis, younger age, shorter time from transplant to RSV diagnosis, exposure to ATG/Alemtuzumab, comorbidities, lower absolute lymphocyte counts (ALC), lower absolute neutrophil counts (ANC), and lower monocyte counts were all associated with new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement (Table 1). Table 2 shows two separate multivariable analyses using the lowest immune cell counts and the counts closest to RSV infection, in Multivariable Models 1 and 2, respectively. Neutropenia (ANC <500) was independently associated with this outcome (adjusted ORs 8.13 [P < .001] and 7.31 [P = .020], respectively). Although shorter time from transplant and lower ALC and monocyte counts were associated with this outcome in univariate analyses, none of these variables were retained in the final multivariable models following LASSO selection. Using children aged 2–4 years as the reference group, age ≥ 5 years was independently associated with lower odds of the oxygen outcome, whereas children <2 years had similar risk compared with those aged 2–4 years, indicating that the reduction in risk becomes apparent after 5 years of age. This protective association for children aged ≥5 years was consistent in a dichotomous analysis (<5 vs ≥5 years), in which age ≥ 5 years remained independently associated with lower odds of this outcome in both multivariable models (Supplementary Table 5). Female sex was independently associated with the oxygen outcome (adjusted ORs 3.82 and 3.67, P = .010 and .015), and chronic lung disease was also independently associated (adjusted ORs 4.23 [P = .033] and 4.33 [P = .024]). Co-pathogen detection in the respiratory tract was associated with this outcome in the lowest-cell-count model only (adjusted OR 2.38, P = .046). In the closest-cell-count model, low lymphocyte count (<300) was associated with this outcome in univariate analysis but was not retained after LASSO selection.

Hospitalization and LRTI

RSV-related hospitalization was recorded for 85 (41.7%) patients, although rates varied across institutions (Supplementary Table 6). In univariate analyses (Supplementary Table 7), this outcome was more frequent among children with comorbidities or chronic lung disease and among those who received ATG as part of transplant conditioning.

In both multivariable analyses using the lowest and closest cell counts (Supplementary Table 8), prior ATG/Alemtuzumab exposure was independently associated with higher odds of RSV-related hospitalization (adjusted OR 2.53, P = .008). In contrast, peripheral blood as the graft source was associated with lower odds of this outcome (adjusted OR 0.36, P = .006) in both models. Other factors that were significant in univariate analysis did not enter the final multivariable models.

Progression from URTI to LRTI was infrequent (n = 10), limiting us to univariate analyses (Table 3; Supplementary Table 9) and none of the factors were significant. In univariable analyses using a combined LRTI outcome (LRTI at diagnosis or progression from URTI to LRTI), children with LRTI were more likely to have nonmalignant underlying diagnoses, recent exposure to ATG or B cell depleting therapy, and lower lymphocyte counts before RSV diagnosis. However, in multivariable analyses (Supplementary Table 10), no factors remained statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This multicenter cohort study provides a pediatric-specific assessment of RSV severity after HCT, identifying risk factors for clinically meaningful respiratory compromise, particularly new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement and LRTI. This oxygen outcome occurred in one-fifth of patients and served as a robust marker of disease severity. Invasive mechanical ventilation and RSV-attributable mortality were uncommon, limiting further risk factor analysis.

A key contribution of this study is the identification of risk factors for new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement.33,34 This outcome captured clinically significant lower respiratory involvement and aligns with broader pediatric RSV experience in which respiratory support serves as a stable measure of disease severity.30,35,36 Female sex, age < 5 years, chronic lung disease, and neutropenia were independently associated with this outcome, indicating that severe disease was more closely linked to host factors than transplant-specific characteristics.

Markers of impaired immune recovery were consistently associated with severe outcomes. Using both nadir and values closest to RSV diagnosis, lower ALC, ANC, and monocyte counts showed similar directional associations with RSV-related hospitalization, the oxygen outcome, and mortality. Neutropenia demonstrated the strongest and most reproducible association with severe RSV outcomes and mirrored findings from predominantly adult HCT cohorts.8,9 While neutropenia may reflect delayed immune recovery in many pediatric HCT recipients, it may also result from subsequent therapies or intercurrent illness. Therefore, neutropenia should be interpreted as a clinically relevant marker of host immune status at the time of RSV infection rather than a direct measure of immune recovery. Shorter time from transplant was associated with the oxygen outcome in univariate analysis but was not retained in multivariable models after inclusion of laboratory markers of immune recovery. This finding suggests that these laboratory measures may more directly reflect susceptibility to severe RSV disease than time from transplantation alone. Chronic lung disease has been associated with respiratory support and mortality in pediatric HCT recipients with viral respiratory infections, including RSV.32 Its role in adult HCT cohorts, which comprise most RSV data, remains less clearly defined and warrants further study.

Age was also an important determinant of severe RSV disease. Although younger children in the general population are at highest risk for severe RSV-related lower respiratory tract disease, it has been unclear whether this vulnerability persists among pediatric HCT recipients. In our cohort, age ≥ 5 years was independently associated with a lower risk of the oxygen outcome, whereas children <2 and 2–4 years had comparable risk. During the study period, palivizumab prophylaxis was recommended for children younger than two years,21,22,37,38 and a protective trend was observed in univariable analysis. However, the small number of recipients and the strong correlation between age < 2 years and palivizumab receipt precluded disentangling the independent effects of age and prophylaxis. As preventive strategies expand, careful evaluation of age-related risk and RSV monoclonal antibody prophylaxis remains essential, as these factors distinguish pediatric from adult HCT populations and carry important practical implications.10,30,39 These findings underscore the need for pediatric-specific risk assessment tools rather than reliance on adult-derived scoring systems.21,22,37,38

Female sex was associated with higher odds of the oxygen outcome and should be interpreted cautiously. Although sex-related differences in respiratory virus severity have been described in children,40 their role in RSV severity after pediatric HCT is unclear. This association may reflect unmeasured confounding rather than a biological effect and warrants evaluation in larger prospective cohorts.

Risk factors for RSV-related hospitalization differed from those for the oxygen outcome and included recent exposure to T cell–depleting agents (ATG or alemtuzumab) and a graft source other than blood stem cells. However, RSV-related hospitalization in this population is influenced by institutional practice patterns, complicating its interpretation as a marker of severity. Recent exposure to T-cell–depleting therapies was associated with the oxygen outcome in univariable analyses but did not retain statistical significance in multivariable models. Although biologically plausible, its impact on RSV-related outcomes has not been well characterized in prior pediatric and adult studies. In contrast, steroid exposure within the two weeks preceding RSV diagnosis was not associated with RSV-related outcomes, despite steroid use being an established risk factor for poor outcomes in predominantly adult HCT recipients with respiratory viral infections.8,9,28,41

We attempted to identify factors associated with LRTI, but none were significant in multivariable models. Similar challenges have been described in other pediatric immunocompromised cohorts.39,42,43 Variability in radiographic interpretation and documentation likely contributed to these findings. Progression from URTI to LRTI was uncommon, limiting assessment of risk factors for progression.28,44

This study’s strengths include its multicenter design, diverse population, and harmonized data abstraction. Prior work in immunocompromised children has included small numbers of transplant recipients10 or focused primarily on adults.5,6,8 However, despite the large number of cases, the sample size did not permit multivariable analyses for some outcomes, including invasive mechanical ventilation and mortality. Although treatment variables were collected descriptively, post-diagnosis therapies were not included in the primary multivariable models because of the potential for confounding by indication. Given its retrospective design, unrecognized or unmeasured confounders cannot be excluded. In addition, RSV-related hospitalization should be interpreted cautiously because the predefined study variable did not distinguish new RSV-attributable admissions from continued or prolonged hospitalization among children who were already hospitalized when RSV was diagnosed. Consequently, we could not determine the incremental impact of RSV on hospitalization in these patients. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and refine pediatric-specific prediction models.

In conclusion, this multicenter study identifies risk factors for severe RSV outcomes in pediatric HCT recipients, particularly new or increased supplemental oxygen requirement, and highlights the roles of markers of impaired immune recovery, younger age, and preexisting pulmonary disease. These findings support more targeted prevention strategies informed by age and immune recovery. Notably, children aged 2–4 years had risk profiles comparable to those younger than 2 years, suggesting prevention strategies may warrant consideration beyond the youngest age group.
