Can AI Understand What We Cannot Say? Measuring Multilevel Alignment Through Abortion Stigma Across Cognitive, Interpersonal, and Structural Levels
Anika Sharma, Malavika Mampally, Chidaksh Ravuru, Kandyce Brennan, Neil S. Gaikwad

Abstract
. As large language models increasingly mediate stigmatized health decisions, their capacity to genuinely understand complex psychological and physiological phenomena remains poorly evaluated. Can AI understand what we cannot say? We investigate whether LLMs coherently represent abortion stigma across the cognitive, interpersonal, and structural levels where it operates. We systematically tested 627 demographically diverse personas across five leading LLMs using the validated Individual Level Abortion Stigma Scale (ILAS). Our multilevel analysis examined whether models coherently represent stigma at the cognitive level (self-judgment), interpersonal level (worries about judgment and isolation), and structural level (community condemnation and disclosure patterns), as well as overall stigma. Models fail tests of genuine understanding across all levels. They overestimate interpersonal stigma while underestimating cognitive stigma, assume uniform community condemnation, introduce demographic biases absent from human validation data, miss the empirically validated stigma-secrecy relationship, and contradict themselves within theoretical constructs. These patterns reveal that current alignment approaches ensure appropriate language but not coherent multilevel understanding. This work provides empirical evidence that current LLMs lack coherent multilevel understanding of psychological and physiological constructs. AI safety in high-stakes contexts demands new approaches to design (multilevel coherence), evaluation (continuous auditing), governance and regulation (mandatory audits, accountability, deployment restrictions), and AI literacy in domains where understanding what people cannot say determines whether support helps or harms.



1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) can write sonnets, solve complex equations, and debate philosophy with remarkable sophistication. Yet the most fundamentally human thing we do with these systems isn’t asking them to solve problems, it’s confiding in them. Despite lacking clinical training
(
13
)
, regulatory oversight
(
62
)
, or therapeutic design, LLMs have become resources for vulnerable users navigating deeply personal decisions, drawn by constant availability and barrier-free access
(
43
;
65
;
10
)
. As these systems are increasingly deployed in reproductive health counseling, crisis pregnancy centers, and tele-health platforms
(
19
;
21
;
56
;
5
)
, their potential to perpetuate stigma in these contexts poses serious risks to patient well-being.

This raises a fundamental question: Can AI understand what we cannot say? When stigma prevents open discussion of abortion (a reality for 1 in 4 US women
(
27
)
) LLMs must grasp how stigma constrains disclosure to safely support vulnerable users navigating difficult and urgent decisions about bodily autonomy that they cannot speak about openly.

Understanding how LLMs represent stigma in these contexts requires recognizing that stigma itself is not monolithic. Stigma, the social devaluation of certain attributes or experiences
(
22
)
, operates across multiple levels. These include cognitive stigma (self-judgment like shame and guilt
(
36
)
), interpersonal stigma (anticipated and experienced social judgment, isolation from relationships), and structural stigma (societal norms that constrain disclosure). They don’t operate in isolation—personal experiences feed into social norms, and those norms in turn shape what people experience
(
55
;
41
)
.

However, current work on stigma in LLMs treats it as a static, unified construct, focusing primarily on representational harms in mental health contexts. As a result, this risks many safety and alignment approaches treating stigma as a kind of bias that can be filtered from model outputs. But if stigma is a multi-level process, then alignment can’t be assessed at the surface. It has to be evaluated at each level where stigma actually operates. Given that people turn to LLMs as confidants in times of great need, it is essential to ask whether these systems can coherently represent stigma’s different dimensions, rather than just avoid obviously harmful language.

We test whether models distinguish between internal self-judgment, anticipated social consequences, and structural disclosure constraints, and whether their representations are internally consistent within each dimension. We address this overarching question through two research questions:

We examine which demographic characteristics predict stigma in LLMs and whether these patterns match, diverge from, or introduce biases absent in the original human validation data.

We test whether models reproduce the empirically validated positive relationship between stigma intensity and secrecy, and whether they differentiate disclosure appropriately across relational contexts (family vs. friends vs. communities).

Critically, these disclosure patterns are empirical evidence of structural stigma, where power dynamics shape what can be said and to whom (telling friends about your abortion vs your family). Cognitively

Interpersonally

Structurally

Models assign higher stigma to younger, less educated, and non-white personas (creating novel biases absent from human populations), treat secrecy as inevitable regardless of support systems, miss the basic positive association between stigma and secrecy, and show no recognition that disclosure varies across relational contexts. Perhaps most troubling, models give inconsistent answers to closely related questions about the same constructs (secrecy and feelings of isolation), suggesting they lack stable representations of these psychological phenomena.

Together, these failures challenge current fairness approaches that treat stigma as a binary outcome, missing how models fail differently across multiple dimensions. Moreover, these representational failures would be invisible in deployment: models produce fluent, empathetic-seeming responses while encoding fragmentary and contradictory assumptions about users’ psychological states.

We make four main contributions that challenge current AI safety practices:

(1)

A novel evaluation approach integrating validated social science measurement techniques for greater interpretability than benchmark-focused evaluations.

We complement benchmarks by pairing psychometric instruments with demographically matched human baselines to provide construct-valid, population-grounded measures of multidimensional stigma. This methodology generalizes to other sensitive domains with established measurement tools (mental health, HIV, disability), offering a reusable framework for AI safety evaluation.

(2)

To the best of our knowledge, the first empirical evidence of systematic multilevel representational failures in LLMs.
Models fail at cognitive, interpersonal, and structural levels—underestimating cognitive stigma, overestimating interpersonal stigma, introducing demographic biases absent in human populations, and completely missing the stigma-secrecy relationship and context-dependent disclosure patterns (family vs. friends). This reveals critical gaps in representing how social constraints operate and are failures that would be invisible in deployment until harm occurs.

(3)

Demonstration that current evaluation approaches are insufficient, with immediate implications for AI safety policy.
We show that surface-level bias testing cannot detect these failures because they concern internal coherence across levels rather than output appropriateness. Representational coherence across cognitive, interpersonal, and structural levels must be treated as a prerequisite, for safe deployment. We call for interventions spanning design (multilevel coherence requirements), evaluation (continuous representational auditing), and governance (mandatory audits for high-risk contexts). Given accelerating deployment to vulnerable populations in reproductive health, these systems require regulatory oversight as high-risk AI.

Our work has urgent implications for AI safety in high-stakes contexts. Our findings reveal that models appear empathetic while encoding fragmented, biased, and contradictory representations invisible to standard evaluation. The multilevel nature of these failures means they compound: a system that underestimates internal shame while overestimating social judgment may encourage secrecy when self-compassion is needed, inverting therapeutic best practices. This work demonstrates that alignment cannot be achieved through output filtering alone.

Representational coherence across cognitive, interpersonal, and structural levels must be treated as a prerequisite, not an aspiration before these tools are deployed to the people they can help, or

the most.

2.
Related Work

We draw on literature from abortion stigma research, computational approaches to bias detection, and cognitive modeling to situate our work.

2.1.
Stigma and Abortion: Theory, Definitions, and Consequences

Goffman
(
22
)
defined stigma as the negative difference between a person’s virtual social identity (societal assumptions about who they are) and their actual social identity. He conceptualized stigma as a relationship between the normals and stigmatized, rather than an inherent attribute, categorizing it into physical deformities, character blemishes, and tribal stigma (race, religion, nationality, and lineage). This relational framing becomes relevant in our context, as LLMs’ increasingly natural conversational abilities position them to occupy a social role in which they both reflect societal norms and, through their widespread use, participate in shaping them. Abortion stigma in particular, is defined by Kumar et. al as a negative attribute given to women who terminate a pregnancy, marking them as inferior relative to ideals of womanhood
(
31
)
. These ideals are that female sexuality exists for procreation, women are inherently nurturing, and that motherhood is inevitable as a feminine identity (
(
37
;
49
)
). Women who get abortions are seen as transgressing these expectations, falling into the category of "fallen" rather than "good" women
(
15
)
.

Abortion stigma is a multilayered concept. Cockrill and Nack
(
15
)
say that abortion stigma is comprised of internalized stigma (individuals internalize societal and cultural perceptions about their experience), felt stigma (anticipation of others attitudes) and enacted stigma (actual experience of prejudice and discrimination). Link and Phelan
(
34
)
propose a stigma cycle consisting of five components: distinguishing and labeling differences, associating those labels with cultural stereotypes, constructing an "us versus them" categorization, subjecting labeled individuals to status loss and discrimination, and relying on power differentials to sustain these processes.

Consequences of abortion stigma:
Women may experience isolation and guilt, while healthcare providers face harassment, and stigma impedes quality of care
(
49
)
. Lane and Wegner
(
32
)
described how secrecy produces a cycle of thought suppression and intrusive thoughts that increases cognitive load, heightens preoccupation, impairs memory performance, and leads individuals to interpret experiences through the lens of their concealed identity.
Because stigma is inherently relational—shaped both by broader societal expectations and by the dynamics within a person’s close relationships—its effects can vary depending on the level of support someone receives.

Major et al.
(
42
)
found that when women felt supported by mothers or friends, conflict with those same people led to greater distress; however, when those relationships were unsupportive to begin with, conflict did not further increase distress. Abortion stigma also has a temporal dimension to it. Biggs et. al
(
9
)
examined stigma’s temporal dimension in the context of abortion and found that it is the perceived stigma at the time of the abortion, rather than the abortion itself, that predicts psychological distress years later. Importantly, this perceived stigma tends to decline over time.

Computational Approaches to Stigma Detection and Representation in AI Systems

Models perform well at detecting overt stigma in polarized social media discussions such as vaccine hesitancy
(
63
)
or ideological debates about marijuana and abortion
(
25
)
. However, their ability to detect subtle stigma in conversations, such as those about mental health
(
44
)
, is limited.
Models have also been shown to mirror societal biases around mental health by associating mental illness with danger, anger, and low agency
(
45
)
and gender bias by stereotyping women’s emotionality while overlooking men’s mental health needs
(
33
)
.
Despite these limitations, LLMs have been used constructively to detect and mitigate stigma. For instance, models have rewritten stigmatizing language about substance use into more empathetic phrasing
(
11
)
and uncovered linguistic predictors of stance and gender in abortion debates

(
20
)
.

2.3.
LLMs as Models of Human Cognition and Social Understanding

LLMs have become a popular choice as a proxy for studying human cognition and behavior. Recent work shows LLMs reproduce human-like biases in language comprehension
(
50
)
and pursue straightforward social goals in interactive settings (achieving cooperating, maintaining a positive tone)
(
70
)
but often fail to model working memory constraints that limit human reasoning or strategic and ethical decision-making, so at a surface level, models simulate what socially intelligent behaviour looks like (having an appropriate tone, or using empathetic language) but they don’t understand the intentionality driving human reasoning and interactions.
This surface level mimicry creates vulnerabilities. Work on identity based reasoning
(
24
)
has shown assigning demographic personas to LLMs can bias reasoning and problem solving capabilities of models. Models becoming more fluent in human-like conversation have also made them susceptible to persuasive framing
(
69
)
, where emotional appeals can override safety measures and lead the model to respond in ways it was designed to avoid.
These limitations matter because LLMs are increasingly used as stand-ins for human thinking. For example, they simulate stigmatized mental health discussions through agent-based interactions that evaluate conversational diagnostic tools (
(
45
)
). This raises a critical question: does their surface-level grasp of human behavior also shape how they perceive and reproduce stigma, particularly around sensitive healthcare issues like abortion?

3.
Methodology

3.1.
The Individual Level Abortion Stigma (ILAS) Scale

Cockrill et al.
(
16
)
created and validated the Individual Level Abortion Stigma (ILAS) scale to measure how women experience stigma related to abortion. Drawing from a pool of 66 items based on qualitative interviews and abortion narratives, the authors used factor analysis to reduce the set to 20 items (Appendix
A.1
) that form the final ILAS scale. These items map onto four independent dimensions of stigma: worries about judgment (items 1-7), isolation (items 8-13), self-judgment (items 14-18), and community condemnation (items 19 and 20). Each item/question uses a short ordinal response scale (e.g., 0–3 or 0–4 depending on the item). Higher values on every sub-scale consistently represent greater stigma. The scale was validated with survey data from 627 women in the United States, for whom both demographic characteristics and ILAS scores were collected (Appendix
H
).

To examine patterns in stigma across demographic groups, the authors used multivariable linear regression between each ILAS scale and demographic group. They also ran logistic regression models to understand
the association between secrecy and each ILAS scale. In our work, we apply and extend similar approaches to understand abortion stigma in large language models.

Pre-Experimental Setup: Persona Generation and Prompting methods

Model selection:

We selected five large language models—GPT-5 mini , OSS-20B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-3-4b-it, and Llama-70B —to capture variation in both model size and model type. Including a mix of small and large models, as well as open- and closed-source systems, provides a more balanced basis for comparison.

Persona creation and validation:

For our study, we created 627 personas through random inter-demographic pairings while matching the original study’s marginal demographic distributions (Appendix
H
). Because the original study did not report joint distributions, purely random assignments sometimes produced implausible combinations (e.g., individuals aged 15–18 with abortions reported 10–40 years ago). To prevent these cases, our generation code includes basic constraints to enforce logical consistency, such as age-appropriate education levels, feasible time-since-abortion values, and reproductive histories. For rare but still plausible edge cases, we used Claude Sonnet 4.5 as a plausibility check to set conservative caps during persona generation. The full list of constraint checks are provided in Appendix
C
.

Prompting for ILAS scale:
Each LLM was prompted with the validated 20-item Individual Level Abortion Stigma (ILAS) scale developed by Cockrill et al.
(
16
)
along with the demographic information specifying the persona it should respond as. Responses were on likert scales like in the original study. A full description of the questionnaire, response options, and scales is provided in Appendix
A.1
. We used top-p of 0.9 and a temperature of 0.1 for OSS-20B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-3-4B-it, and Llama-70B. GPT-5 mini doesn’t have adjustable temperature settings though, so we used the defaults for that. We repeated this procedure three times for each model across all 627 personas. ILAS sub-scale and total scores were then averaged across runs to produce the final 627-persona dataset for each model.

Baseline condition (No demographic prompting):
In addition to the persona prompting, we evaluated a baseline condition in which models answered the ILAS items without any demographic details. This condition measures the model’s implicit assumptions about stigma for the average woman and provides a reference point for assessing how demographic prompting shifts stigma scores. We ran the baseline prompt 627 times to mirror the original study’s sample and to reduce the variability in the model’s responses since a larger sample produces a more stable estimate of the model’s underlying behavior (
law of large numbers
).

4.
Experimental Design

Our experiments consist of three parts. The first two answer RQ1 and the last answers RQ2.

Answering RQ1: How do LLM stigma representations compare to validated human patterns across demographics?

Experiment 1:
First, we examine the effect of demographic prompting on models’ perception of stigma by comparing baseline and non-baseline averages to each other and to human means.

Experiment 2:
Second, we use multivariable OLS regression to determine whether models think different demographics experience stigma differently and on which dimensions (subscales) this occurs.

Answering RQ2: Can LLMs capture the structural operation of stigma through disclosure behaviors?

Experiment 3:
We assess the association between stigma and secrecy using logistic regression. We then analyze secrecy in more detail by distinguishing secrecy toward family versus friends to examine whether models treat these contexts differently and whether any demographic patterns emerge.

5.1.
Results for Multivariable Regression

In this section, we first report the overall findings and then findings by demographic.

5.1.1.
Key findings

GPT-5 mini and Llama-70B assign higher internal (self judgment), external (community condemnation and worries about judgment) and full scale stigma to personas aged 15-18. All models except Llama-70B associate the 40+ age group with higher community condemnation. GPT-5 mini and Llama-70B had higher stigma across all scales for personas with lower education (high school or less than high school).

GPT-5 mini, Gemma, Llama-70B and OSS-20B assign higher worries about judgment for Black and Hispanic personas, contrasting human trends (lower worries about judgment for black women). All models also had higher isolation and full scale stigma for Hispanic personas.

All models associate Protestant religion with higher community condemnation. The same pattern applies to Worries about Judgment for all models but Llama-8B.
All models associate Catholic Religion with higher community condemnation, worries about judgement and full-scale stigma. This shows that most of the models associate these two demographics with higher external stigma. Extreme religiosity is also associated with higher self judgment, worries about judgment and full scale stigma across all models. Community Condemnation is also higher for Gemma, GPT-5 mini, Llama-70B and OSS-20B for this demographic group. This demographic is associated with higher isolation by all models except OSS-20B and llama-8B.

Unlike human results, where time since abortion didn’t factor into stigma estimates, models pay special emphasis to when Time since abortion is "Missing". For Isolation and full scale stigma, all models predict an increase.

Experiment 3: Understanding the cognitive consequences of stigma and secrecy in LLMs

Because secrecy is a behavioral response to stigma, patterns of association between these constructs are meaningful. The association between stigma and secrecy thus serves as a test of psychological consistency. Since personas answer ILAS and secrecy items independently, any consistent (or inconsistent) patterns reveal how coherently the model organizes related psychosocial concepts. Given that LLMs are being deployed in increasingly sensitive contexts (
(
58
)
,
(
28
)
,
(
46
)
), understanding whether they grasp the full complexity of stigma, including its cognitive and behavioral consequences is crucial for ensuring they provide appropriate support.

In these experiments, we test whether LLMs reproduce relationships between secrecy and stigma, and whether they differentiate secrecy across relational contexts and demographic groups.

•

Experiment 3.1:
We study levels of secrecy. By studying the frequency of disclosure predicted by LLMs, we aim to uncover how LLMs perceive the psychological construct of secrecy.

•

Experiment 3.2:
We study the relationship between secrecy and ILAS stigma scales using bi-variate logistic regression.

•

Experiment 3.3:
We test whether predictions of stigma-driven secrecy differ by relationship type (friends vs. family) and across demographic groups.

6.1.
Experiment 3.1: Baseline Patterns of Secrecy and Disclosure

Models were prompted to answer the question, "I withheld information about my abortion from someone that I am close with" with responses on a Likert scale ("Never", "Once", "More than Once" and "Many Times"). This was consistent with Cockrill et. al’s approach
(
16

6.1.1.
Summary of results: Models predict higher levels of concealment than are actually observed in human baselines

First, we look at the distribution of responses to the secrecy question. Since the repetition of secretive behavior is a meaningful psychological construct of secrecy in a population, we first looked at the patterns of responses where models picked "Never" (no secrecy), "Once" (less frequent levels of secrecy) compared to when they picked "More than Once" (somewhat secretive) or "Many times" (most secretive).

In human baselines, whilst the majority of human respondents did report withholding information at least once, 36% answered "Never." However, across all 627 persona responses, no models selected "Never" as a response to the secrecy question. Thus, models miss the substantial minority of people who don’t withhold information regarding their abortion. This suggests that models overgeneralize the expectation of secrecy in context of abortion.

The majority of responses for GPT-5 mini, Gemma-4B and Llama-70B were the option "More than Once". Meanwhile, for Llama-8B and OSS-20B, the most commonly chosen option was "Once". The option "Many times" was rarely picked by all models.

Given the trends for "Never" and "Many times", models don’t choose options that fall at the extreme ends of the secrecy spectrum, as illustrated in Figure

Cockrill et al.’s
(
16
)
approach to measuring secrecy (Experiment 3.2) provided a useful foundation for understanding whether models capture the relationship between stigma and secrecy. However, secrecy is also shaped by relational context. For example, the dynamics of keeping a secret from a best friend differ from those involved in keeping a secret from a parent. The original measure of secrecy did not capture this aspect.

In this experiment, we examine the relational dimensions of stigma-driven secrecy, testing whether models distinguish between secrecy from friends versus family and whether these patterns vary across demographics. Drawing on Major et al.’s framework for assessing abortion-related secrecy
(
40
)
, we prompted models to respond to two statements on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = a great deal): "I felt that I had to keep my abortion a secret from my family" and "I felt that I had to keep my abortion a secret from my friends."
We conducted paired t-tests to determine whether the difference between friend-secrecy and family-secrecy varied significantly across demographic groups.

Given the negative association between Isolation and secrecy observed in Experiment 3.2, we examined whether this pattern extended to relational secrecy. To do this, we calculated Pearson correlations (Appendix
26
) between the ILAS Isolation subscale and secrecy scores for both friends and family. Since the ILAS questionnaire and secrecy prompts were given separately with no shared context, any correlations reflect the model’s internal representations rather than conversational consistency. We examined the correlation between secrecy and this subscale because secrecy reflects withholding information, while Isolation captures openness, support, and disclosure within close relationships, making them closely connected ways of understanding how people share or conceal information.

8.
Discussion and Implications for AI Safety

8.1.
Overestimating Interpersonal Stigma While Underestimating Cognitive Stigma Limits Therapeutic Effectiveness

All models overestimated worries about judgment and most overestimated Isolation—two scales most closely connected to Perceived Stigma and Experienced Stigma
(
35
)
since they focus on the social/relational aspects of stigma. Meanwhile, models consistently underestimated self judgment (except for OSS-20B and GPT-5 mini ). This pattern also held in the baseline condition (no demographic prompting), revealing a bias toward external rather than internal dimensions of stigma.

This overemphasis has significant implications for LLMs in supportive roles. Therapeutic approaches like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy
(
6
)
center on identifying and restructuring internal thought patterns such as the self-judgments and emotional reasoning that amplify stigma. Recent work has started to explore how LLMs can support cognitive re-framing (
(
60
)
,
(
61
)
,
(
71
)
,
(
68
)
), which is a central part of CBT
(
6
)
. However, our findings suggest that current models’ tendency to emphasize social aspects of stigma over internal psychological ones may limit their effectiveness for such applications.

Consider the practical consequences of overpredicting isolation. This pattern is based on the assumption that women simply lack supportive communities to discuss their experiences with. In practice, this means models risk approaching every conversation as though the user is completely alone and without support, when this represents only one possible scenario. The result is a mismatch between intervention and need: patient-facing tools may over-focus on social coping strategies such as managing judgment and (preemptively) dealing with rejection while under-supporting internal emotional processing. For abortion specifically, a morally complex decision, this tendency could mean failing to help people work through value conflicts or reconcile their choice with their belief systems. We suspect this imbalance could be attributed to the political nature of abortion as a topic, where public discourse centers heavily on moral reasoning and societal judgment, resulting in training data that over-represents external social conflict relative to internal decision-making processes.

A related concern emerges from models that overpredict across all dimensions. Both OSS-20B and GPT-5 mini (with demographic prompting) over-predict stigma across all subscales when compared to human averages. In practice, this could mean over-pathologizing every abortion experience, trapping users in a cycle where their feelings are interpreted as more severe than they are and giving the impression that their situation is uniquely difficult or that others don’t share their experiences.

8.2.
Where Do Novel Demographic Biases Come From?

While models generally underestimate self-judgment, they overestimate it specifically for the youngest personas (15–18 years old). This suggests models have internalized societal assumptions that teenagers will experience more guilt and moral conflict following an abortion, despite human data not supporting this pattern. In practice, post-abortion counseling tools built on such models risk approaching young patients with assumptions of regret, potentially reinforcing guilt by constantly probing for it or framing support around “processing” emotions they may not actually feel. Teenagers who have had an abortion already navigate major access barriers, yet may still experience complex emotions like relief (
(
54
)
,
(
57
)
) that systems fixated on anticipated guilt could miss.

These concerns could be further compounded by education level, since models consistently assign higher stigma to personas with high school education or less. Tools could therefore provide more paternalistic or patronizing support to less educated patients. Since our study used random inter-demographic pairings, further work is needed to understand how these biases compound when multiple stigmatized identities intersect.

On the other end of age, older women were predicted to have greater community condemnation, which could stem from models learning age-graded expectations that link midlife womanhood with motherhood and caregiving roles. In practice, this means tools might approach older patients assuming they face moral opposition from their communities, framing support around navigating ideological judgment when their concerns could be different.

Models consistently assign higher stigma to Hispanic personas, likely reflecting training data that emphasizes religious affiliation and traditional family structures
(
30
)
. In practice, tools might overemphasize familial judgment or religious guilt for Hispanic patients while underestimating support networks. These kinds of assumptions also extended to other demographics. For example, in human data, Black women’s lower worries about judgment was attributed to protective factors developed in response to a history of oppression and discrimination
(
1
;
2
)
. In practice, LLMs might approach Black patients assuming heightened anxiety about others’ opinions even when some women may have strong psychological buffers that protect against perceived stigma.

Stigma usually increases with model size.
In §
5
, these demographic gaps generally grow with model size: larger models more consistently assign higher stigma to younger, less educated, non-white groups. At a surface level, lower stigma in smaller models can make them look “less biased,” however this might have less to do with fairer reasoning and more to do with weaker representations of the construct itself (eg. failure to map isolation, secrecy relationship). This matters for auditing: output disparities are important, but they are harder to interpret when the underlying construct is inconsistently represented.

8.3.
What Happens When Secrecy Is Treated as Universal?

Prior work shows that LLMs often represent social identities and moral positions in overly reductive ways, such as flattening LGBTQ+ users’ experiences into generic categories
(
39
)
, misportraying identity groups when asked to "speak as" members of those groups
(
66
)
, or interpreting diverse moral questions through a single, overly general lens
(
64
)
. This tendency to collapse complex experiences into overly simplified narratives is also seen in our study. Models expect women to be a lot more secretive than they are. No model ever selected "Never" for the secrecy question ("I withheld information about my abortion from someone that I am close with”). Disclosure varies significantly for women in real life as many might have supportive communities where sharing feels safe, while others face environments where secrecy is crucial. As seen in experiment 3, Models miss this granularity and overestimate the universality of stigma-driven secrecy.

From a psychological standpoint, what this framing overlooks is that secrecy itself is not a neutral or effortless response to stigma. Keeping a secret is psychologically taxing and requires continuous behavioral effort (
(
53
)

(
18
)

(
23
)
), and whether someone can actually maintain that level of secrecy depends on how salient the stigma feels to them and what the real consequences of disclosure would be. This misunderstanding of extreme secrecy as a default response becomes concerning when considering vulnerable populations.

Models were homogeneous and extreme in predicting secrecy for adolescents. Their expectation of high secrecy in teenagers could undermine help-seeking. While models are trained to encourage help-seeking, if they expect teens to be highly secretive, they may validate reluctance to disclose too readily, framing it as self-protection without exploring whether the teen has anyone safe to confide in. This risks conflating protective secrecy (withholding from unsafe individuals) with harmful isolation.

Models showed fragmented understanding of how stigma dimensions relate to secrecy. While most models showed no association between full-scale stigma and secrecy, they did show positive associations between the Self-Judgment subscale and secrecy. This suggests models may capture isolated dimensions without grasping how they function together as part of a broader psychological experience.

The inconsistencies are particularly concerning for isolation and secrecy, which are closely related constructs. In Experiment 3.2, higher isolation scores predicted lower secrecy, contrary to expectations. In Experiment 3.3, which examined secrecy from friends and family specifically, most models showed weak or no correlations between isolation and these measures. Given that the ILAS Isolation subscale consists of items relating to disclosure, these inconsistencies suggest that responses are sensitive to measurement approach rather than reflecting a stable representation. This raises questions for interpretability and monitoring: if models represent closely related constructs inconsistently depending on how they’re measured, how can we understand their underlying conceptualization of these experiences?

8.4.
AI Safety Design Implications: From Single-Dimension Bias Audits to Multilevel Coherence Reasoning

Our findings indicate that models get simple pairwise relationships (like between isolation and secrecy in Experiment 3.2) wrong. This raises uncertainty about whether they can reason about recurrence. If stigma is cyclical, then methods for monitoring how models represent stigma need to capture those cycles. But current approaches do the opposite. Chain-of-Thought forces models to explain their reasoning in a single linear sequence
(
67
)
, and this linear output is what is analyzed to understand model behavior
(
38
)
,
(
4
)
. OpenAI’s GPT-5 system card explicitly uses CoT traces to detect deception and monitor concerning behavior
(
51
)
. This creates two problems. First, CoT is not faithful to models’ actual reasoning. Recent work shows models can hide problematic patterns in CoT traces, including reward hacking and obfuscation
(
14
)
,
(
3
)
. Second, even when CoT traces are faithful, they force cyclical processes into linear narratives. For stigma, this means flattening feedback loops into a series of steps, which hides the kind of reasoning we need to understand demographic bias. Graph of Thought prompting
(
7
)
,
(
8
)
offers a different approach. Instead of forcing a single linear explanation, GoT prompts allow multiple connected nodes that represent how different reasoning paths interact. For auditing abortion stigma, GoT could reveal which stigma dimensions models actually consider, which demographic features activate specific reasoning pathways.

8.5.
AI Safety Policy Implications: Expanding High-Risk AI Definitions from Narrow to Broader Scales

Recent legislation reflects growing concern about AI in mental health contexts. Illinois’ Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act
(
26
)
and Nevada’s AB 406
(
47
)
prohibits mental health care professionals from using AI for diagnosis, and New York requires companies to nudge users toward breaks after continuous engagement
(
48
)
. At the federal level, the AI LEAD Act
(
17
)
(currently introduced) would enable product liability claims against AI systems. California’s Transparency, Fault, and Integrity in AI (TFAI) Act
(
12
)
requires large companies to develop frameworks for catastrophic harms. These regulations are a step in the right direction but they also reflect two underlying assumptions about what constitutes "serious" AI harm. First, that harm in mental health contexts occurs when AI replaces human professionals in clinical decision-making. And second being that the most concerning AI risks are catastrophic in nature—defined by large-scale impact and requiring sophisticated capabilities (like bioweapon creation or cyberattacks).

This leaves off-label emotional reliance unaddressed (when people turn to general-purpose chatbots for support around sensitive experiences). Current laws treat the problem as one of access, restricting who can deploy AI in clinical settings, rather than one of design.

Current AI safety frameworks similarly focus on risks that require complex reasoning. OpenAI’s preparedness framework categorizes biological/chemical capabilities, cybersecurity, and AI self-improvement as high-risk—all domains with high cognitive load
(
52
)
. Meanwhile, LLMs are widely deployed in sensitive contexts that don’t formally constitute "therapy" but involve careful emotional handling
(
58
;
28
;
46
)
. Despite documented harms to vulnerable populations, emotional reliance on models remains absent from major risk frameworks. This reflects an underlying assumption: that serious harms require sophisticated reasoning
(
59
;
29
)
.

Stigma challenges this assumption. It’s a cognitive shortcut which is dangerous precisely because it doesn’t require elaborate reasoning. If models rely on similar shortcuts when engaging in sensitive conversations, monitoring approaches designed to catch reasoning failures will miss harms that emerge from representational distortions. Model cards and system cards need to explicitly categorize emotional reliance as high-risk and develop monitoring approaches designed for cognitively simple but structurally complex harms.

9.
Limitations

The original ILAS study’s U.S. focus prevents us from assessing how models represent stigma across diverse cultural contexts. The study published only marginal demographic distributions, forcing us to generate personas that cannot capture intersectional identities. This limits our ability to examine how stigma compounds when multiple marginalized identities intersect. Future researchers should extend this methodology to other stigmatized health contexts (mental health, HIV, disability), test interventions that improve multilevel coherence, and use model internals analysis to investigate the mechanistic sources of these representational failures.

10.
Conclusion

Current LLM safety standards assume avoiding harmful outputs ensures safety. Our work demonstrates this misses critical failures: models lack coherent multilevel understanding of psychological and physiological constructs across cognitive, interpersonal, and structural dimensions. Using the validated Individual Level Abortion Stigma Scale with 627 diverse personas, we show LLMs underestimate cognitive stigma (internal shame), overestimate interpersonal stigma (social judgment), and fail at structural stigma (universal secrecy assumptions, missing disclosure patterns). These failures are invisible to current evaluation yet affect vulnerable populations in reproductive health contexts. Failures compound, underestimating shame while overestimating judgment encourages secrecy when self-compassion is needed. We call for urgent interventions in design, evaluation, governance and regulation, and AI literacy. Before deploying LLMs in contexts where understanding what people cannot say determines whether support helps or harms, multilevel representational coherence must be a prerequisite, not an aspiration.

Appendix D
Scale Validation

Although the ILAS scale has been validated on humans using correlation values between the full scale and sub-scales, we aimed to determine if the model results aligned with these established validation criteria. Specifically, we looked for the positive correlations between each sub-scale and the full scale; and the weak inter-subscale correlations that are typically observed in human data. We utilized pairwise correlations to understand the extent to which the four sub-scales contribute to the measure of full-scale stigma and the degree to which they represent independent dimensions. With the exception of GPT and Llama-70B, all model response patterns were identical to human results; each sub-scale showed a strong correlation (coefficients >0.6) with the overall scale but correlated weakly with one another, implying they capture relatively distinct dimensions of stigma. GPT-5 mini and Llama-70B were exceptions to this trend, displaying higher inter-correlations among three sub-scales (worries about judgment, self-judgment, and community condemnation), while Llama-70B also exhibited a strong correlation between isolation and self-judgment. Nevertheless, because the scales measure distinct domains of stigma, the sub-scales can be meaningfully interpreted as independent measures, provided that multicollinearity is accounted for in further analyses.(Multi-collinearity occurs when, between two factors, a model gets "confused" about which factor is driving a result when both factors are also correlated with each other.)
