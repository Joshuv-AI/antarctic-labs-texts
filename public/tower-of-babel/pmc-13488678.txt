Virtual Reality for Managing Procedural Pain in Children: A Concept Analysis with Walker and Avant Approach
Authors: Henny Suzana Mediani, Sri Hendrawati, Sukmawati Sukmawati, Ikeu Nurhidayah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProcedural pain and anxiety are common among children undergoing medical procedures. Traditional pharmacologic and behavioural interventions may not fully alleviate pain and distress in young children. Virtual Reality (VR) has emerged as a promising nonpharmacologic approach, yet conceptual ambiguity persists regarding its defining characteristics within paediatric nursing practice.PurposeTo clarify the concept of Virtual Reality for the management of procedural pain in children by delineating its defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents utilizing the Walker and Avant framework.MethodsWalker and Avant’s eight-step concept analysis framework was employed. Literature published between 2005 and 2025 was systematically identified from PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, and Web of Science. Twenty-eight eligible studies were synthesized through iterative comparison and conceptual coding to identify defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children.ResultsThree defining attributes were identified: (1) immersion and presence, referring to a sense of being within a three-dimensional simulated environment; (2) distraction and cognitive engagement, involving active redirection of attention away from pain stimuli; and (3) emotional regulation and anxiety reduction, promoting calmness and procedural cooperation. Antecedents include the presence of a painful procedure, availability of VR technology, trained providers, and child readiness. Consequences include reduced pain and anxiety, improved procedural cooperation, and increased satisfaction among children, parents, and healthcare providers. Empirical referents include pain intensity measure, fear and anxiety assessments, and presence related instruments.ConclusionVirtual reality represents an immersive, interactive, and emotion-focused nursing intervention for managing procedural pain and anxiety in children. Clarifying this concept supports its integration into evidence-based, child-centred, atraumatic nursing care.

Introduction

Pain is a complicated phenomenon, and managing it is becoming more widely acknowledged as essential to providing patients with high-quality care.1,2 The World Health Organization and prominent pediatric and pain associations have identified optimizing the management of childhood pain as a critical healthcare objective.1,3 Pain in pediatric patients continues to be a major and enduring worldwide health concern despite tremendous advancements in clinical treatment and pain management techniques.2–4 Procedural pain is one of the most frequent and distressing experiences for children receiving medical interventions.3–5 Routine medical procedures such as venipuncture, intravenous cannulation, wound dressing, immunization, lumbar puncture, or dental treatments often elicit intense pain, fear, and anxiety.4–6 Despite advances in paediatric pain science, procedural pain remains undertreated across many healthcare settings.6–8 Surveys of hospitalized children reveal that more than 70% experience moderate to severe pain during procedures, yet only a fraction receive adequate pain management.8–10 This gap persists even in developed healthcare systems with clinical guidelines for paediatric pain, highlighting the enduring challenge of translating knowledge into practice.

The impact of procedural pain extends beyond the immediate physical discomfort. Repeated exposure to painful medical procedures can have short-term behavioural, psychological, and physiological consequences. In the short term, children often exhibit distress responses such as crying, withdrawal, muscle tension, or refusal to cooperate.9–11 These responses can prolong procedures, increase procedural difficulty for healthcare providers, and heighten stress for both children and caregivers.10–12 Psychologically, poorly managed pain contributes to fear conditioning, anticipatory anxiety, and avoidance behaviours toward medical care.9,13 Children who repeatedly associate medical procedures with pain are more likely to develop needle phobia and procedural anxiety, which can persist into adolescence and adulthood.12,13

The long-term effects of inadequately controlled procedural pain are particularly concerning. Early pain experiences may influence the development of neural pathways involved in pain modulation, leading to altered pain sensitivity and heightened pain perception later in life.9,13 Furthermore, repeated painful experiences without sufficient relief can negatively shape a child’s cognitive appraisal of medical interventions, fostering mistrust toward healthcare providers.14,15 Evidence also indicates that parents’ anxiety and distress are closely linked to their child’s pain behaviours, creating a reciprocal cycle that amplifies pain perception and emotional distress.16,17

Affective, cognitive, behavioral, and sensory-discriminative aspects are all part of the multifaceted nature of pediatric pain. Developmental stage, prior medical experiences, parental reactions, and sociocultural influences all have an impact on children’s perceptions of pain.17–19 Therefore, a comprehensive, developmentally appropriate strategy that goes beyond pharmaceutical therapies is needed for effective pain management in pediatric populations.

Traditionally, pharmacologic interventions such as topical anaesthetics, sedatives, or analgesics have been used to reduce procedural pain. However, these methods are not always feasible, especially for brief or repetitive procedures.20 Additionally, pharmacologic options may have side effects, require physician supervision, or fail to address the emotional and cognitive aspects of pain adequately.21 As a result, nonpharmacologic interventions, including distraction, relaxation, and cognitive-behavioural strategies, have become essential components of multimodal paediatric pain management.22 Among these, Virtual Reality (VR) has emerged as an innovative, immersive technology that engages children’s attention and sensory systems to modulate pain perception.22,23

VR creates an interactive, computer-generated environment that immerses users through visual, auditory, and sometimes tactile stimuli.23,24 By shifting a child’s focus from the medical environment to a captivating virtual world, VR reduces the amount of attentional and emotional resources available for pain processing.23 Randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated VR’s effectiveness in decreasing pain intensity, anxiety, and physiological stress markers during medical procedures.23–25 However, despite growing empirical support, inconsistencies remain in how VR is defined, implemented, and evaluated across studies. Some describe it as a distraction technique, others as an immersive therapeutic environment, and still others as a cognitive modulation tool. This conceptual ambiguity impedes the synthesis of evidence, limits theoretical development, and hinders the standardization of VR-based interventions in clinical practice.

Therefore, this paper aims to clarify the concept of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children through Walker and Avant’s eight-step concept analysis framework.26 This analysis seeks to identify the defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of the concept. By doing so, it contributes to conceptual precision, strengthens the theoretical foundation of VR in paediatric pain management, and supports multidisciplinary healthcare professionals in implementing evidence-based, child-centred interventions.

Material and Methods

Design

This study employed a concept analysis design guided by Walker and Avant’s eight-step framework.26 Concept analysis is particularly appropriate when a construct, such as virtual reality appears in practice and policy discourse but lacks a clear definition, agreed attributes, and operational indicators. This approach was chosen to clarify and define the concept of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children by systematically identifying its defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. The goal was to strengthen theoretical understanding and provide a foundation for future research and nursing practice. Eight iterative steps make up this methodology: (1) choosing a concept; (2) identifying the goals and purpose of the analysis; (3) identifying all applications of the concept; (4) identifying the defining attributes; (5) creating a model case; (6) creating borderline and contrary cases; (7) examining antecedents and consequences; and (8) determining empirical referents.

Concept Selection and Aim

The concept selected was Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children. This concept was chosen because the use of virtual reality in pediatric healthcare has expanded rapidly over the past decade, particularly as a nonpharmacologic intervention for reducing pain and anxiety during invasive medical procedures. Despite growing empirical evidence supporting its use, the concept remains inconsistently defined across the literature. Virtual reality has been variously described as a distraction technique, an immersive digital intervention, a cognitive-behavioral strategy, or a therapeutic environment, resulting in conceptual ambiguity and inconsistent application in clinical practice and research.

Furthermore, substantial variation exists in the technologies used, including levels of immersion, hardware platforms, interactivity, and procedural contexts, making it difficult to establish clear conceptual boundaries. This lack of conceptual clarity may hinder evidence synthesis, intervention standardization, and the development of clinical guidelines for pediatric pain management. Therefore, a concept analysis was undertaken to clarify the meaning and essential characteristics of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children. The aim was to clarify its defining characteristics, preconditions, and outcomes to promote conceptual precision and guide clinical implementation.

Database Selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science databases.

Literature Search Strategy

The search included peer-reviewed literature published between January 2005 and June 2025. To guarantee transparency and rigor, a structured literature search and study selection approach was used, even though this study used Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework rather than a systematic or scoping review methodology. The identification, screening, eligibility evaluation, and inclusion of the literature that informed the idea analysis were all depicted in a PRISMA-style flow diagram.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms using Boolean operators. Key search terms included: “virtual reality,” OR “VR”; “paediatric pain, OR ‘child pain’,” “procedural pain,” “distraction therapy,” “children,” and “nursing care.” Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) were used to refine the search strategy. Additional manual searches were conducted through reference list screening to identify potentially relevant studies. Operational Definition of Virtual Reality

For the purposes of this concept analysis, Virtual Reality was operationally defined as an immersive computer-generated three-dimensional environment delivered through head-mounted displays or equivalent immersive technologies that provide users with a sense of presence and active engagement sufficient to divert attention from painful medical procedures. Only studies employing immersive or semi-immersive VR interventions designed to support procedural pain management in children were considered eligible.

Study Selection and Screening

All retrieved citations were exported into a reference management program, where duplicate records were identified and removed prior to screening. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers against the predefined eligibility criteria. Potentially eligible studies underwent full-text assessment. Disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion and consensus among the research team.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Articles were included if they (1) described, applied, or conceptually defined Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain or closely related peri-procedural experiences among children aged 3–18 years, and (2) provided conceptual, theoretical, or empirical insights relevant to nursing or paediatric pain management. Studies involving closely related peri-procedural contexts, including preprocedural anxiety immediately preceding invasive procedures and immediate postoperative pain following procedures, were retained when they contributed conceptually to identifying the defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, or empirical referents of Virtual Reality. This approach is consistent with Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework, which emphasizes examining conceptually relevant uses of a concept across related contexts to achieve conceptual clarity.

Excluded were studies focusing exclusively on chronic pain, long-term postoperative pain unrelated to procedural interventions, adult populations, or non-immersive digital distraction modalities such as conventional video games or two-dimensional animations. A total of 28 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the concept synthesis.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data extracted from the included studies were systematically reviewed to identify conceptual definitions, defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children. Data analysis followed Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework, whereby conceptual information was iteratively compared across studies to identify similarities and differences in the use of the concept.

The research team independently examined and coded the conceptual information extracted from each included study. Statements describing the characteristics, mechanisms, experiences, and outcomes of Virtual Reality interventions were extracted and organized into thematic categories. Through iterative comparison and discussion, recurring patterns were grouped into preliminary categories and subsequently refined into defining attributes. Conditions preceding the implementation of Virtual Reality were classified as antecedents, whereas resulting outcomes were classified as consequences. Any discrepancies in coding or conceptual interpretation were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. This iterative analytical process enabled the development of a conceptually coherent representation of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children.

Rigor and Trustworthiness

To enhance rigor, the search strategy and conceptual coding were reviewed by two nursing scholars experienced in paediatric pain management and concept analysis methodology. Conceptual consistency was maintained through iterative discussion and comparison of extracted data. The analytic process emphasized transparency and reflexivity to ensure that the findings accurately reflected the literature and the analysis’s theoretical intent.

Ethical Considerations

As this was a conceptual and literature-based analysis, institutional review board approval was not required. All included sources were publicly available and properly cited.

Characteristics of Included Studies

Uses of the Concept

The concept synthesis was mainly obtained from the 28 included papers, while extra theoretical references were employed to facilitate conceptual interpretation.

A literature study revealed that virtual reality has been employed in several healthcare settings. Initially, VR was described as a technological platform consisting of computer-generated three-dimensional environments delivered through immersive devices such as head-mounted displays.56,57 In healthcare context, virtual reality has developed into a therapeutic modality utilized for pain management, mental health therapy, rehabilitation, and medical education.

Throughout the literature, virtual reality has transitioned from a technological advancement to a multifaceted therapeutic intervention. Virtual reality for the management of procedural pain in children is an immersive, interactive, and emotion-regulating intervention that promotes a sense of presence, cognitive engagement, and anxiety reduction to reduce procedural pain, anxiety and distress during medical procedures.28,30,34,36,39,41,43,46,49,50

This advancement in virtual reality use indicates that the concept transcends mere technological application and has certain attributes that enhance its efficacy in alleviating procedural pain in youngsters. Consequently, delineating its distinguishing characteristics is crucial for elucidating the concept.

Defining Attributes

Defining attributes represent the essential characteristics of a concept that consistently appear in the literature. From the reviewed studies, three key attributes emerged that define Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children: 1)Immersion and Presence, the sense of “being there” in a three-dimensional virtual environment. Children experience visual, auditory, and sometimes tactile stimuli that engage multiple senses and divert attention away from painful stimuli.23,35,39,44,46,512)Distraction and Cognitive Engagement, VR provides an interactive distraction that competes for the child’s attention, reducing pain perception through attentional and emotional modulation mechanisms.23,28,30,38,39,42,44,51,523)Emotional Regulation and Anxiety Reduction, the immersive nature of VR supports emotional comfort by decreasing anticipatory anxiety, promoting relaxation, and increasing procedural cooperation.32,35,37,40–45,52,54

These attributes jointly distinguish VR from traditional distraction techniques, such as cartoons or guided imagery, because of its interactive, immersive, and multisensory engagement that produces measurable reductions in pain and distress.

Based on this defining attribute Virtual reality for the management of procedural pain in children is an immersive, interactive, and emotion-regulating intervention that fosters a sense of presence, cognitive engagement, and anxiety reduction to mitigate procedural pain and distress during medical interventions.

Model Case

A model case exemplifies all defining attributes of the concept. Consistent with the Walker and Avant concept analysis framework, the following case is a hypothetical and illustrative example, developed solely to demonstrate the defining attributes of Virtual Reality for managing procedural pain in children. This case does not represent a real patient and is not derived from clinical records.

Hypothetical Case Example

Ririn, a 7-year-old girl, is scheduled for a venipuncture procedure. She is provided with a VR headset featuring a virtual underwater adventure. As she explores the ocean, interacting with colourful fish, she becomes deeply immersed in the scene. During needle insertion, she remains calm and focused on the VR environment, exhibiting minimal distress. The nurse observes that Ririn’s attention is entirely diverted, her breathing remains regular, and she reports only mild discomfort afterward. This hypothetical model case demonstrates the essential defining attributes of the concept immersion, distraction, and emotional regulation which collectively contribute to reducing procedural pain perception and improved procedural cooperation.

Discussion

This concept analysis clarified Virtual Reality (VR) for managing procedural pain in children using the Walker and Avant framework as a multidimensional, immersive, and emotion-modulating intervention grounded in paediatric nursing principles. The three defining attributes, immersion and presence, distraction and cognitive engagement, and emotional regulation and anxiety reduction, collectively distinguish VR from traditional distraction techniques such as cartoons, music, or tablet applications. This distinction is fundamental because VR’s immersive quality engages multiple sensory channels simultaneously, thereby increasing attentional capture and reducing the cognitive resources available for pain processing.28,29,63

This concept analysis delineates the conceptual parameters of virtual reality in the context of procedural pain management for children, in contrast to earlier reviews that focused mainly on the clinical efficacy of virtual reality interventions. This concept analysis establishes a theoretical framework for standardizing interventions, measuring outcomes, and implementing evidence-based practices in pediatric nursing by identifying defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents.

Consistent with the eligibility criteria established for this concept analysis, several included studies examined closely related peri-procedural contexts, such as preprocedural anxiety and immediate postoperative pain. These studies were intentionally included because they contributed important conceptual insights into the defining attributes of Virtual Reality, including immersion, cognitive engagement, distraction, and emotional regulation. In a concept analysis, the objective is not to evaluate intervention effectiveness within a narrowly defined clinical condition but rather to clarify how a concept is represented across relevant contexts in the literature. Accordingly, their inclusion strengthened the conceptual comprehensiveness of the analysis while remaining fully consistent with Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework, which emphasizes examining conceptually relevant uses of a concept across related clinical contexts.

The results align with the Gate Control Theory proposed by Melzack and Wall, which suggests that pain perception is influenced by attention, emotional responses, and previous pain experiences.64 According to McCaul and Malott’s expansion of this idea, a person must focus on a painful stimuli in order for it to be seen as painful because humans have a finite amount of attention.56 The Gate Control Theory’s tenets are directly supported by virtual reality (VR), making it an effective non-pharmacological method for pain management. VR closes the gate to pain before the brain perceives it by upregulating non-painful neural impulses and rerouting cognitive attention through highly immersive, multisensory stimulation.56,64

VR’s ability to immerse children in a captivating virtual environment directly influences attentional gating pathways, thereby lowering the salience of nociceptive signals.25,28 Moreover, the emotional regulation attribute highlights VR’s potential to reduce anticipatory anxiety, an element often overlooked in procedural pain management. Anxiety is known to heighten pain perception; therefore, VR’s calming and engaging properties contribute meaningfully to procedural cooperation and overall distress reduction.23,32,35,39,41

Antecedents identified in this analysis underscore the need for appropriate technological infrastructure, trained personnel, and child readiness. These antecedents indicate that successful VR implementation requires organizational support, workflow integration, and clinical protocols that ensure safety and usability.35–42 The identified consequences reduced pain and anxiety, improved procedural cooperation, and enhanced satisfaction, mirroring the outcomes reported in the empirical literature and reinforcing VR’s potential as a child-centred, atraumatic care strategy consistent with paediatric nursing standards.35–42

The findings demonstrate that VR is best understood as an immersive, interactive, and emotion-regulating intervention that supports paediatric pain management by redirecting attention and modulating emotional responses during distressing procedures. By identifying defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents, this analysis contributes conceptual clarity to an increasingly used yet variably defined intervention in paediatric care.

The defining attributes, immersion and presence, distraction and cognitive engagement, and emotional regulation and anxiety reduction, distinguish VR from other nonpharmacologic distraction strategies commonly used in clinical practice. These attributes emphasize that the therapeutic value of VR lies not merely in distraction, but in the child’s subjective experience of being immersed in an alternative environment that meaningfully competes with nociceptive and emotional stimuli. This distinction is particularly relevant for nurses, who play a central role in selecting, implementing, and evaluating nonpharmacologic pain management interventions.

By articulating VR’s defining characteristics, antecedents, and outcomes, this concept analysis supports future standardization, protocol development, and research design. It also provides a clearer theoretical basis for explaining why and how VR produces analgesic and anxiolytic effects in children undergoing medical procedures.43,47,48

An important finding emerging from the review literature is that virtual reality should not be seen as a single, uniform intervention. Instead, virtual reality (VR) is a collection of technologies that vary in terms of user experience, hardware configuration, immersion, and interactivity. Head-mounted displays (HMDs), which totally block out the real world and give a strong sense of presence within a computer-generated world, are commonly used in fully immersive virtual reality systems. Semi-immersive systems, on the other hand, allow users to stay partially aware of their surroundings while presenting virtual environments through huge screens, projection systems, or dome-based displays. These variations in immersion may have an impact on how much attention is drawn away from unpleasant stimuli, which may procedure pain outcome. Studies included in this analysis primarily focused on immersive HMD-based VR systems, which consistently demonstrated reductions in pain and anxiety during procedures such as venipuncture, wound care, burn dressing changes, and intravenous insertion.

Variability also exists in the hardware and software used to offer VR interventions. While some systems use smartphone-based VR platforms that are more accessible and less expensive to implement, others use specialized HMDs with sophisticated tracking capabilities. Similarly, there are significant differences in the level of user interaction between programs. While some VR apps offer passive audiovisual experiences, others enable children to actively explore virtual worlds, manipulate items, or finish tasks. Greater interactivity enhances cognitive engagement, one of the defining attributes identified in this concept analysis, thereby strengthening attentional capture and emotional involvement during painful procedures.

VR deployment necessitates taking into account a number of practical and safety-related problems, notwithstanding its potential advantages. Cybersickness, which manifests as symptoms like nausea, dizziness, disorientation, or visual discomfort, can happen to certain children, especially when they are exposed for extended periods of time or when they are in fast-moving virtual surroundings.58–60 Additionally, when patients share VR equipment, infection prevention and control protocols are crucial. To reduce the risk of cross-contamination in healthcare settings, head-mounted displays and related attachments need to be cleaned and disinfected according to the proper procedures. These factors imply that technology design, usability, safety, and clinical context are just as important to the efficacy and viability of VR interventions as the conceptual qualities of immersion, distraction, and emotional regulation.

Implications for Paediatric Nursing Practice

The clarification of this concept has important implications for nursing practice. First, understanding the defining attributes of VR enables nurses to apply this intervention intentionally and appropriately within atraumatic, child-centred care models. Rather than viewing VR as a general entertainment tool, nurses can recognize it as a structured nursing intervention aimed at reducing procedural pain and anxiety.

Second, this analysis underscores the critical role of nurses as facilitators of VR-based interventions. Nurses are uniquely positioned to assess child readiness, introduce and manage VR equipment, monitor safety and comfort during procedures, and evaluate patient responses using validated pain and anxiety measures. Integrating VR into routine care may enhance procedural cooperation, reduce distress, and potentially decrease reliance on pharmacologic pain management strategies.

Third, the identified antecedents highlight the importance of organizational and educational support. The availability of age-appropriate VR technology, institutional protocols, and nurse training are essential for successful implementation. Nurse leaders and educators can use this clarified concept to inform clinical guidelines, competency frameworks, and continuing professional development programs focused on nonpharmacologic pain management.

Finally, the empirical referents identified such as pain intensity, anxiety, presence, and satisfaction provide nurses with concrete indicators to evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of VR interventions. These measures support the integration of VR into evidence-based practice, quality improvement initiatives, and outcome monitoring in paediatric settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this concept analysis. First, the analysis relied on published peer-reviewed literature, which may be subject to publication bias and may underrepresent experiences from low-resource or non-English-speaking contexts. Second, although a systematic search strategy was undertaken, the purpose of this review was concept clarification rather than evidence synthesis; therefore, no formal critical appraisal of methodological quality was conducted. Third, differences in paediatric age groups, procedural contexts, and Virtual Reality technologies across the included studies may limit the transferability of the conceptual findings. Finally, the model, borderline, and contrary cases presented are hypothetical examples developed solely for illustrative purposes within Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework and do not represent real patients or clinical prevalence.

Future Research Directions

The findings of this concept analysis suggest several avenues for future research. Empirical studies are needed to operationalize the defining attributes identified and to examine how immersion, distraction, and emotional regulation independently and collectively influence pain perception and anxiety in children. Further research should also explore the effectiveness of VR across different developmental stages and procedural contexts, as well as comparisons with other nonpharmacologic interventions.

Implementation-focused research is needed to assess feasibility, acceptability, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability of VR integration into routine nursing practice, particularly in high-volume or resource-limited settings. Additionally, future studies should aim to develop and validate standardized measurement tools aligned with the empirical referents identified in this analysis, including paediatric-specific measures of immersion and presence. Such research will strengthen the evidence base and support the broader adoption of VR as a nursing-sensitive intervention for managing procedural pain in children.

Conclusion

This concept analysis provides a clear and comprehensive definition of Virtual Reality for the management of procedural pain in children, based on Walker and Avant’s framework. VR is characterized by three defining attributes: immersion and presence, distraction and cognitive engagement, and emotional regulation, which collectively support pain and anxiety reduction during medical procedures. The identified antecedents and consequences demonstrate that VR is not merely a technological innovation but a holistic, child-centred intervention integral to atraumatic and evidence-based paediatric nursing practice.

Clarifying this concept enhances its theoretical and practical utility, enabling nurses, clinicians, and researchers to implement VR more effectively and consistently. As paediatric health systems increasingly shift toward patient-centred, minimally traumatic care, VR holds significant promise to transform how children experience medical procedures. Continued research, protocol development, and workforce training will further strengthen VR’s role in paediatric pain management and support its integration into routine clinical care. Understanding this concept enables nurses to integrate VR ethically and effectively into child-centred, atraumatic care models.
