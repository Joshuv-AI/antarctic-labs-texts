Self-consistent equations and Quantum Diffusion for the Anderson model
Adam Black, Reuben Drogin, Felipe Hernández

Abstract
. We consider the Anderson tight-binding model on ℤ d {\mathbb{Z}}^{d} , d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , with Gaussian noise and at low disorder λ > 0 \lambda>0 . We derive a diffusive scaling limit for the entries of the resolvent R ⁡ ( z ) R(z) at imaginary part Im z ∼ λ 2 + κ d \operatorname*{Im}z\sim\lambda^{2+\kappa_{d}} , κ d > 0 \kappa_{d}>0 , with high probability. As consequences, we establish quantum diffusion (in a time-averaged sense) for the Schrödinger propagator at the longest timescale known to date and improve the best available lower bounds on the localization length of eigenfunctions. Our results for d = 2 d=2 are the first quantum diffusion results for the Anderson model on ℤ 2 {\mathbb{Z}}^{2} . The proof avoids the use of diagrammatic expansions and instead proceeds by analyzing certain self-consistent equations for R ⁡ ( z ) R(z) . This is facilitated by new estimates for ‖ R ⁡ ( z ) ‖ ℓ p → ℓ q \|R(z)\|_{\ell^{p}\rightarrow\ell^{q}} that control the recollisions.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the Anderson tight-binding model

(1.1)

H=\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}+\lambda V,

where
\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
is the nearest-neighbor Laplacian on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
(normalized as in (
2.1
)), the potential
V:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
has independent standard Gaussian values, and
\lambda>0
is a coupling constant.

The Hamiltonian (
1.1
) was originally introduced by Anderson
[
And58
]
in order to study the propagation of electrons in a disordered environment, and more generally functions as a simple model for wave transport in random media. For
\lambda\gg 1
or
d=1
, there is a large body of literature showing that
H
exhibits
Anderson localization
, meaning that almost surely it has an orthonormal basis of exponentially decaying eigenfunctions, see
[
AW15
]
and the references therein.
In contrast, the small coupling regime
\lambda\ll 1
is the subject of several outstanding conjectures in mathematical physics (
[
Sim84
,
Sim00
]
).
Referred to as the
extended states
and
quantum diffusion
conjectures, they predict the existence of delocalized states whose Schrödinger evolution is described by a diffusion process.
We list a few forms of these conjectures informally,
which are expected to hold for
d\geq 3
and sufficiently small
\lambda
:

•

Diffusion of the propagator
: For fixed
\psi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
the probability density of the wavefunction
|(e^{-itH}\psi)(x)|^{2}
satisfies a heat equation under an appropriate scaling limit.
In particular, if
\psi
is localized, then

(1.2)

\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|x|^{2}|(e^{-itH}\psi)(x)|^{2}\sim\lambda^{-2}t,

implying that
e^{-itH}\psi
has most of its
\ell^{2}
mass at the
diffusive
scale
\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{t}.

•

Diffusion of the resolvent:

For
E
in the interior of the spectrum
\sigma(\Delta)
, the squared entries of the resolvent
R(z):=(H-E-i\eta)^{-1}
satisfy an elliptic equation under an appropriate scaling limit as
\eta\to 0
. In particular, they scale like the Green’s function of the Laplacian:

(1.3)

|R(z)_{xy}|^{2}\sim\lambda^{2}|x-y|^{2-d}.

•

Delocalization of eigenfunctions:
Solutions of
H\psi=E\psi
are
delocalized
for energies
E
in the interior of
\sigma(\Delta)
.
On a qualitative level, this means that the spectrum is continuous in the bulk.
Quantitatively, this should be interpreted to mean that most eigenfunctions of some truncation of
H
satisfy a lower bound on their “localization length,”
i.e., one of several measures of their spatial extent.

The factors of
\lambda^{-2}
and
\lambda^{2}
in the above are significant because they are, respectively, the critical time and energy scales past which diffusion may first be observed. Since the propagator and the resolvent are related by a Fourier transform in the time/energy variables, the first two items are thought of as equivalent, although in practice establishing the correspondence is non-trivial. In dimension
d=2
, it is instead conjectured that the spectrum of
H
is pure point with dynamically localized eigenfunctions.
Nevertheless, in this dimension, diffusion is still expected up to the timescale
e^{\lambda^{-2}}
(or dually down to the energy scale
\operatorname*{Im}z\sim e^{-\lambda^{-2}}
).

Progress on these conjectures, reviewed below, has been limited to a handful of results
and has mostly focused on establishing a scaling limit for the propagator
in expectation
and at timescales just beyond the diffusive threshold of
\lambda^{-2}
.
In this paper, we show that for
d\geq 2
the resolvent
R(z)
is diffusive down to energy scales
\operatorname*{Im}z\sim\lambda^{2+\kappa_{d}}
,
\kappa_{d}>0
,
with high probability
.
As consequences, we establish quantum diffusion (in a time-averaged sense) for the propagator at the longest timescale known to date and improve the best available lower bounds on the localization length of eigenfunctions.
Our results for
d=2
are the first quantum diffusion results for the Anderson model on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.

Mathematically rigorous analysis of delocalization of eigenfunctions began with

While progress on delocalization for the Anderson model itself has been very limited, there have been several breakthroughs in understanding delocalization in a variety of random band matrix ensembles

In these random matrix models the asymptotic parameter that replaces the coupling strength

Thus, they may be seen as interpolations between the Anderson Hamiltonian, which has randomness only on the diagonal, and better understood mean-field models, in which every entry is random.
At a very high level, the framework developed to analyze these ensembles proceeds by studying certain

To our knowledge, no works have appeared attempting to address the Anderson Hamiltonian (
1.1

The difficulty in doing so stems not only from the comparatively small amount of randomness present in the Anderson model, but also from its structure. Indeed, the Anderson model enjoys fewer symmetries than random band models, and the dispersive nature of the background Laplacian affects the small
\lambda

In this paper, we show that the self-consistent equation framework can nevertheless be used to derive diffusion of the Anderson Hamiltonian, provided that one has suitable dispersive estimates for the resolvent
R(z)

The analogous estimates for the free resolvent

A key novelty in this work is a perturbative argument based on

\operatorname*{Im}z\sim\lambda^{2}

In fact, the dispersive estimate we prove may be seen as a strengthened

Remarkably, this allows us to completely sidestep the combinatorial difficulties inherent to the diagrammatic approach pursued in

1.1.
Main ideas and discussion

Each of Theorem
1.1
-
1.3
follow from appropriate estimates on the quantity

(R(z)^{*}AR(z))_{00}=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}a(x)|R(z)_{0x}|^{2}

for some
a\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
, where
A
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal specified by
a
.
At a simplified level, there are three steps to understanding the quantity above.

The first step is to establish a concentration inequality for the entries of the resolvent via the aforementioned dispersive estimate, encoded as a bound on
\|R(z)\|_{\ell^{1}\to\ell^{4}}
.
In the second step, we derive a self-consistent equation for
{\mathbb{E}}R
whose error is controlled by the concentration inequality.
The third step is to show that
(RAR^{*})_{xx}
satisfies an elliptic equation and from this derive diffusion.

1.1.1.
Step 1: Concentration inequalities for the resolvent.

We think of
R(z)
as a function of the Gaussian entries
g_{x}
of
V
and apply the Gaussian Poincaré inequality to bound the variance.
To facilitate the calculation we write

V=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}g_{x}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|},

where
\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}
is the rank-one projection onto the site at
x
. Then we have

(1.7)

\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{x}}R=-\lambda R\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}R,

so the Gaussian Poincaré inequality implies

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Var}R_{xy}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}|\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{w}}R_{xy}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}|R_{xw}R_{wy}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{2}\Big({\mathbb{E}}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|R_{xw}|^{4}\Big)^{1/2}\Big({\mathbb{E}}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|R_{wy}|^{4}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\|R\|_{1\to 4}^{4}.

The main new technical contribution of this work is a bound for
\|R(z)\|_{1\to 4}
that is effective for
z=E+i\eta
with
\eta\ll\lambda^{2}
in the
diffusive regime. In particular, we will show in Proposition
3.1

(1.8)

\|R(z)\|_{1\to 4}\leq\lambda^{2-c_{d}}\eta^{-1}.

with high probability so that for some range of
\eta
much smaller than the diffusive scale
\lambda^{2}
, we still have
\operatorname*{Var}R_{xy}\ll 1
. A similar argument can be used to bound higher moments of
R_{xy}-{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}
.

1.1.2.
Step 2: The self-consistent equation for
{\mathbb{E}}R

In this section we compute a self-consistent equation for
{\mathbb{E}}R
by using the “resolvent method” explained in
[

]

The idea is to first renormalize by writing

\Delta+\lambda V-z=(\Delta-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta))+(\lambda V+\lambda^{2}\theta),

for

Setting
M_{\theta}:=(\Delta-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta))^{-1}
, we have by the resolvent identity

R=M_{\theta}-M_{\theta}(\lambda V+\lambda^{2}\theta)R.

We now take an expectation on both sides of (

), and use

{\mathbb{E}}VR
.
Indeed using
V=\sum_{x}g_{x}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}

) we compute

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}VR

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}{\mathbb{E}}[g_{x}R]

\displaystyle=-\lambda\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}\mathbb{E}[R\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}R].

Introducing the superoperator

\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}))\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}))

defined by

\mathcal{D}[A]:=\sum_{x}A_{xx}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|},

we have thus shown

\mathbb{E}R=M_{\theta}({\operatorname*{Id}}+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{D}[R-\theta{\operatorname*{Id}}]R)

Note we can choose any value of
\theta
, so now we set
\tilde{\theta}(z):={\mathbb{E}}R_{00}(z)
. Then writing

\tilde{M}=\tilde{M}(z)=M_{\tilde{\theta}(z)}(z)

Proposition 1.4
.

With
\tilde{\theta}(z):={\mathbb{E}}R_{00}(z)
and

as defined above, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}R=\tilde{M}({\operatorname*{Id}}+\mathfrak{E}_{\rm loc})\\
&\mathfrak{E}_{\rm loc}:=\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{D}[R-{\mathbb{E}}R]R.\end{split}

If

\theta=(\Delta-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta))^{-1}_{00}.

In Theorem

The most straightforward reason is that we adapt the framework of

1.2.1.
Formal perturbation theory and renormalization

First we briefly review the traditional diagrammatic approach to understanding the Anderson model, following a formal perturbative calculation of Spencer
[
Frö+12
, Chapter 1, Appendix B]
.
For simplicity we work with the expected resolvent
{\mathbb{E}}R(z)
.
First we write
R(z)
as a Born series expansion, perturbing around the free resolvent
R_{0}(z):=(\Delta-z)^{-1}
:

\mathbb{E}R(z)=R_{0}(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\lambda)^{k}\mathbb{E}R_{0}(z)(VR_{0}(z))^{k}.

When
\eta:=\operatorname*{Im}(z)\gg\lambda^{2}
, the series converges and is dominated by the first term which is of order
\lambda\eta^{-1/2}
.
To prove this convergence rigorously, one can establish that the bound

(1.15)

\|R_{0}^{1/2}(z)VR_{0}^{1/2}(z)\|_{2\to 2}{\,\lesssim\,}\eta^{-1/2}

holds with high probability.
A bound along these lines of (
1.15
) follows from the arguments in any of
[
Bou01
,
SSW02
,
BDH25
]
(though see also the computations in
[
Van54
,
Dav74
,
Spo77
,
EY00
]
for alternative explanations of this square-root cancellation phenomenon).

If
\eta{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{2}
the sum over
k
is no longer perturbative and one needs to incorporate cancellations in the sum. Omitting
z
from notation and writing
\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}=\delta_{x}
and
\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}
for the corresponding rank-one projection we can write the next non-vanishing term as follows

\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}R_{0}VR_{0}VR_{0}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}R_{0}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x|R_{0}|x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}R_{0}=\lambda^{2}(R_{0})_{00}R_{0}^{2}.

The right hand size has operator norm
\eta^{-1}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1})

which is much larger than
\eta^{-1}=||R_{0}||_{2\to 2}
if
\eta\ll\lambda^{2}
.
Similar calculations for larger
k
show that to sum the series one needs to take advantage of cancellations.
This is done by renormalizing, that is, by perturbing around
M_{\theta}=(\Delta-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta))^{-1}
rather than
R_{0}(z)
.
Performing a Born series expansion about
M_{\theta}
we obtain

(1.16)

\mathbb{E}R=M_{\theta}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}M_{\theta}((\lambda V+\lambda^{2}\theta)M_{\theta})^{k}

Now we choose
\theta
so that the second order terms in
\lambda^{2}
vanish:

\lambda^{2}\tilde{R}_{0}\theta\tilde{R}_{0}-\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\tilde{R}_{0}V\tilde{R}_{0}V\tilde{R}_{0}=0.

By the computation for
k=2
above this is equivalent to taking
\theta_{\rm sce}(z)
to solve the self-consistent equation

(1.17)

\theta_{\rm sce}(z)=(\Delta-z-\lambda^{2}\theta_{\rm sce}(z))^{-1}_{00},

which we observe is the same equation derived above (
1.12
).

With this renormalization, one expects that (setting
M_{\rm sce}=M_{\theta_{\rm sce}}
),

(1.18)

\mathbb{E}R\approx M_{\rm sce}.

To establish (
1.18
) one could in principle attempt to bound the remaining terms in (
1.16
)
and indeed, this is essentially the strategy of
[
ESY08
,
ESY07
]
.

1.2.2.
Resolvent estimates as crossing integrals

It is instructive to consider the first error term in (
1.16
) that does not cancel exactly as a consequence
of the choice of
\theta_{\rm sce}
. This term comes from the term in the Wick expansion of the fourth order term which involves a crossing, that is,

(1.19)

{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm cross}:=\lambda^{4}\sum_{x,y}M_{\rm sce}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}M_{\rm sce}\mathinner{|{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{y}|}M_{\rm sce}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{x}|}M_{\rm sce}\mathinner{|{y}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{y}|}M_{\rm sce}.

The operator
M_{\rm sce}
is a Fourier multiplier with symbol

m(p)=\frac{1}{\omega(p)-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta_{\rm sce})},

so writing out the entries of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm cross}
using the Fourier transform we obtain

\displaystyle\mathinner{\langle{w_{1}|{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm cross}|w_{2}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{4}(2\pi)^{-4d}\iint_{({\mathbb{T}}^{d})^{5}}\sum_{x,y}e^{i(w_{1}-x)\cdot q_{1}}e^{i(x-y)\cdot(q_{2}-q_{3}+q_{4})}e^{i(y-w_{2})\cdot q_{5}}\prod_{j=1}^{5}m(q_{j})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\vec{q}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{4}(2\pi)^{-4d}\iint_{({\mathbb{T}}^{d})^{4}}\delta(-q_{1}+q_{2}-q_{3}+q_{4})e^{i(w_{1}-w_{2})\cdot q_{1}}m(q_{1})\prod_{j=1}^{4}m(q_{j})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\vec{q}.

Thus one has

\max_{w_{1},w_{2}}|\mathinner{\langle{w_{1}|{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm cross}|w_{2}}\rangle}|{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{4}(\operatorname*{Im}(z+\lambda^{2}\theta_{\rm sce}))^{-1}I_{4}(z+\lambda^{2}\theta_{\rm sce}),

where
I_{4}(z)
is the

four denominator crossing integral
(see
[
ES07
,
Luk07
]
)

I_{4}(z):=\int_{({\mathbb{T}}^{d})^{4}}\delta(q_{1}-q_{2}+q_{3}-q_{4})\prod_{j=1}^{4}\frac{1}{|\omega(q_{j})-z|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\vec{q}.

We note that similar “crossing integrals”
appear in the derivation of kinetic limits of random wave equations
[
LS07
]

and also appear in the wave kinetic theory of nonlinear problems
[
DH21
,
ST21
]
. Note that
there is a trivial bound
I_{4}(z)\ll\eta^{-1}
which comes from using the
L^{\infty}
norm on
\frac{1}{|\omega(q_{4})-z|}
and using Fubini’s
theorem for the integrals on
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
, and
q_{3}
. This trivial estimate shows that the maximum entry of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm cross}

is
O(1)
. Thus any nontrivial estimate on
I_{4}
shows that the first error term is of subleading order. In
[
ESY07
]

an intricate diagrammatic argument is developed to show that indeed a nontrivial estimate on
I_{4}(z)
implies that the
sum over the remaining error terms is also of subleading order.

On the other hand one has by Plancherel’s theorem the identity

\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|\langle{x|(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-z)^{-1}|0}\rangle|^{4}=\int_{({\mathbb{T}}^{d})^{4}}\frac{\delta(p-q+r-v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v}{(e(p)-z)(e(q)-\bar{z})(e(r)-z)(e(v)-\bar{z})}.

That is, the four-denominator estimate controls the
\ell^{1}\to\ell^{4}
norm of the resolvent of the Laplacian,

\|(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-z)^{-1}\|_{\ell^{1}\to\ell^{4}}^{4}\leq I_{4}(z).

From this perspective, our estimate on
\|(H-z)^{-1}\|_{\ell^{1}\to\ell^{4}}
is a strict strengthening of the crossing estimate that
[
ESY07
]
uses.
One way to interpret our approach is that we choose not to perform a perturbative expansion all the way down to the free resolvent but instead
use bounds on the full resolvent
(H-z)^{-1}
itself. This is what allows us to stop the expansion and directly estimate the error term.

1.2.3.
As a dispersive estimate

Another way of understanding the quantity
\|R\|_{1\to 4}
is as a dispersive estimate. A calculation using Plancherel’s theorem and
the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality shows that

(1.20)

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\lambda^{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{\eta^{-1}}|e^{itH}\psi(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{2}&\leq e^{2}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\lambda^{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\eta t}|e^{itH}\psi(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{2}\\
&=e^{2}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\lambda^{2}\Big(\int|R(E+i\eta)\psi(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E\Big)^{2}\\
&{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{2}\Big(\int\|R(E+i\eta)\psi\|_{\ell^{4}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}E\Big)^{2}.\end{split}

To interpret the left hand side, note that
\int_{0}^{T}|e^{itH}\psi(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
measures the total probability of finding the particle at
location
x
for some time in
[0,T]
, and so
\lambda\int_{0}^{T}|e^{itH}\psi(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
measures the contribution to the probability amplitude
that the wavefunction
\psi
ever interacts with the potential at site
x
within time
[0,T]
. The square then heuristically measures the
probability that the particle revisits a given site
x

twice
within
[0,T]
. Thus, the left hand side measures the contribution from
all recollisions up to time
T
.

Now taking
\psi=\delta_{0}
, one observes that
\|R(E+i\eta)\delta_{0}\|_{\ell^{4}}\leq\|R(E+i\eta)\|_{\ell^{1}\to\ell^{4}}
. In conclusion,
\|R\|_{1\to 4}
can be used to estimate the likelihood that an initially localized wavefunction experiences any recollision up to time
T=\eta^{-1}
. That is, it encodes the sum over all recollision diagrams in the sense of
[
ESY07a
]
.

From another perspective, the inequality above is some kind of dispersive estimate, which in particular shows that
\psi_{t}
does not interact
too strongly with any particular potential site. Therefore, one expects a concentration phenomenon for
\psi_{t}(x)
. This suggests some heuristic
that concentration inequalities and crossing estimates should be related, as they both come from estimating the strength of interaction with single
sites.

1.3.
Comparison to classical models

The Anderson model is a discrete cousin of the random Schrodinger equation (not addressed in this paper),

i\partial_{t}\psi=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi+\lambda V\psi.

The semiclassical limit of this equation, corresponding to initial data
\psi_{0}
which is highly oscillatory, is the stochastic acceleration model

(1.21)

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}x=-\lambda\nabla V(x).

A diffusive limit for stochastic acceleration was first established in
d>2
by Kesten and Papanicolaou
[
KP80
]
(and see also
[
DGL87
,
KR06
]
for results in
d=2
). The idea in this proof is to establish a coupling between the motion described
by (
1.21
) and a diffusive Markov process. This coupling can be established easily up until the first time that the particle trajectory intersects itself. Similar analysis can be performed on other models of walks in random environments (see for example
[
BBS83
]
for an analysis on the wind-tree model).
The diffusive limit of stochastic acceleration has also been used directly to obtain a quantum diffusion result for highly oscillatory initial data
[
BKR03
]
.

In the Anderson model, the self interactions that break the Markov property correspond to crossing diagrams
in the expansion (
1.16
) (which is also the error term in the self-consistent equation). Our proof proceeds by simply bounding the total contribution of these terms (in the form of a bound on
\|R\|_{1\to 4}
, see (
1.20
) above), so in this sense is analogous to the “Kesten-Papanicolaou argument”. In particular, it is limited to the timescale
\lambda^{-4}
at which
point the crossing diagrams become nonnegligible without further renormalization. We note that previous approaches to quantum diffusion
[
ESY08
,
ESY07
,
Her24
]
required bounds on
all
crossing diagrams to reach the diffusive timescale. In the analogy with the classical problems, this would correspond to bounding not just the contribution of a single self-interaction but also the contributions from all possible
K
-fold self-interactions for
1\leq K\leq\lambda^{2}T
. Thus the innovation of this paper is that we manage to complete the analysis without considering these higher order self-interactions.

More recent work on classical models of diffusion improve upon the Kesten-Papanicolaou framework by generalizing the notion of the coupling to a Markov process and by “renormalizing” the coupled Markov process. In
[
LT20
]
a longer diffusive timescale is reached by allowing self-interactions but controlling their effect on the endpoint of the walk. To reach even longer timescales some renormalization is necessary, in the sense that one modifies the limiting Markov process as one progressively reaches longer timescales. An analysis to this effect was used in
[
ES22
]
on the interchange process to reach infinite timescales, resolving a long-standing conjecture of Toth. More recently, renormalization has been applied to a discrete model more analagous
to stochastic acceleration
[
EGH25
]
.

We stress that at this point it is not clear how to execute a renormalization argument analogous to that of
[
ES22
,
EGH25
]
(in the classical setting) or of
[
YYY21
,
YY21
,
YYY21
,
Dub+25
]
(in the random matrix setting) to reach superpolynomial time scales in the Anderson model. However, we are
hopeful that ideas in the present work might prove useful to do so.

Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we lay out notation and some simple facts that will be used throughout. In Section
3
we prove bounds on
\|R\|_{p\to q}
. Then in Section
4
we prove bounds on the entries of the resolvent using the self consistent equation for
R
. This is applied in Section
5
to perform an error analysis in the T-equation. Then in Section
6
we derive a comparison between the T-equation and an elliptic PDE on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Finally, in Section
7
we use these results to complete the proofs of the main theorems.

2.1.
The Laplacian and the Fourier transform

We normalize the Laplacian as

(2.1)

(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f)(x)=\sum_{|y-x|=1}f(y).

For a function

, we take its Fourier transform to be

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}e^{ix\xi}f(x),

and therefore

\displaystyle f(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}e^{-ix\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)\>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi,

where
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/(2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
.
The symbol of
\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
is thus

\displaystyle\omega(\xi):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}2\cos(\xi_{j}),

so that its spectrum is
[-2d,2d]
.
The critical points of
\omega
correspond
to values of
\xi
for which
\xi_{j}\in\{0,\pi\}
for each
j\in[d]
. Therefore
the critical
values
of
\omega
is given by the set
\Sigma_{\rm crit}=[-2d,2d]\cap(2d+4{\mathbb{Z}})
. We define
3
3

3

Technically we only need to include
0
in
d=3
, and the proof could be modified to take
\Sigma_{d}={\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-2d,2d]
in
d\geq 4
, but excluding
\Sigma_{\rm crit}
makes the analysis simpler.

\Sigma_{d}:=({\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-2d,2d])\cup\Sigma_{\rm crit}\cup\{0\},

with the value
0
being included because in
d=3
there is a flat umbilic point on the level set of
\{\omega=0\}
(as explained in
[
ES07
]
).

2.2.
Density of states and the self-consistent equation

The
density of states
for the Laplacian

\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
is the measure
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\sigma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(\omega(\xi))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

As shown in Lemma
A.3
,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma=\rho(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
has a density supported on
[-2d,2d]
which is strictly positive in
(-2d,2d)
. Aside from a logarithmic singularity at
0
in
d=2
, the density is bounded. The density
\rho
also has the expression

(2.2)

\rho(E):=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\frac{1}{|\nabla\omega(\xi)|}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

The significance of the function
\rho
is that

\rho(E)=\lim_{\eta\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname*{Im}((\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-z)^{-1}_{00}).

Since
(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-z)^{-1}
is analytic in
z
, its imaginary part is the harmonic extension of
\rho
to the upper half plane.
Similarly, its real part is the harmonic extension of
\rm{H}\rho
, where
\rm{H}
is the Hilbert transform.

Finally, we note that the self-consistent
equation

\theta=(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta))^{-1}_{00}

has a unique solution in the upper half-plane
{\mathbb{H}}
. This is proven in Proposition
A.6
.

2.3.
Truncation to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}

In Sections
3
-
5
we work with a truncation of the Hamiltonian
H
to the torus
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}:={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}/(L{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
, with
L=2\left\lceil\lambda^{-100}\right\rceil
.
Specifically, let
\Delta_{L}
be the nearest-neighbor Laplacian on this graph, normalized as in
2.1
, and let
H_{L}=\Delta_{L}+\lambda V
, where by a slight abuse of notation
V
denotes the potential restricted to the box
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap[-L/2,L/2]
and thought of as periodic. Here and throughout, we identify elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
with
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap[-L/2,L/2]^{d}
in the natural way.
Below, unlike in the introduction,
R(z)=(H_{L}-z)^{-1}
denotes the resolvent of this truncated operator whereas
R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(z)=(H-z)^{-1}
is the full resolvent.

The large value of
L
relative to the energy scale under consideration,
\operatorname*{Im}z>\lambda^{2+\kappa_{d}}
, allows us to pass freely between
R
and
R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
. By regarding elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
as elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with
\ell^{\infty}
norm at most
L
, we have the following:

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
with
|x|_{\infty},|y|_{\infty}\leq L/4
and
z=E+i\eta\in{\mathbb{H}}
with
\eta>\lambda^{4}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\mathinner{\langle{x|R(z)|y}\rangle}-\mathinner{\langle{x|R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(z)|y}\rangle}\right|\leq C\exp(-c\lambda^{-10}),

with constants that are uniform for all realizations of
V
.

Proof.

Recall the Combes-Thomas estimate
[
AW15
, Thm 10.5]
, valid for any self-adjoint Schrödinger operator on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
: there exists dimensional constants
c,C>0
such that if
|x-y|>C\eta^{-1}
then

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\mathinner{\langle{x|R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(z)|y}\rangle}|\leq C\exp(-c\eta|x-y|).

Observe that if either of
u,w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
has
\infty
-norm less than
L/2
then it is immediate from the definitions that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\langle{u|H_{L}|w}\rangle}=\mathinner{\langle{u|H|w}\rangle}.

So, we write

\displaystyle\mathinner{\langle{x|R(z)|y}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}\mathinner{\langle{x|R(z)|w}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{w|(H-z)R_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}}|y}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\mathinner{\langle{u|R_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}}|y}\rangle}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}\mathinner{\langle{x|R(z)|w}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{w|(H-z)|u}\rangle}.

If
|u|_{\infty}<L/2
, then we have that
\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}\mathinner{\langle{x|R(z)|w}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{w|(H-z)|u}\rangle}={\mathbf{1}}_{x=u}
. Therefore, by using (
2.3
)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\langle{x|R(z)|y}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\langle{x|R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(z)|y}\rangle}+\sum_{|u|_{\infty}=L/2}\mathinner{\langle{u|R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(z)|y}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\langle{x|R_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(z)|y}\rangle}+O(e^{-c\lambda^{-10}}),

as desired.
∎

2.4.
Constants and asymptotic notation

Throughout,
z=E+i\eta
always denotes a complex parameter with real part
E
and imaginary part

.

We regard
{\varepsilon}
and
\delta
as fixed small parameters throughout, and we will always take

.
We let
c
and

denote a constant that may change from instance to instance and depend only on
\delta
,
{\varepsilon}
, and the dimension

. In particular, it is understood that they are independent of
\lambda
,
\eta
and

. We write

to mean that

It is also convenient to use a variant of
stochastic domination
notation commonly used in random matrix theory, see, e.g.
[
EY17
]
. For random quantities
A
and
B
,
perhaps depending additionally on

, we write

(read as “

3.
A priori estimates for the resolvent

In this section we establish
\ell^{p}\to\ell^{q}
mapping properties of the resolvent on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
, denoted
R(z)=(H_{L}-z)^{-1}
.
To state the result we define the sequence of exponents
p_{d}
,

\displaystyle p_{d}=\begin{cases}6&d=2\\
\frac{14}{3}&d=3\\
\frac{2d}{d-3}&d>3,\end{cases}

and we write
p_{d}^{\prime}=\frac{p_{d}}{p_{d}-1}
. We can now state the main result of the section.

Proposition 3.1

(A priori resolvent bounds)
.

For
p\leq p_{d}^{\prime}<p_{d}\leq q
and
z=E+i\eta
with
\eta>\lambda^{10}
,
E\in[-2d,2d]
,
and
d(E,\Sigma_{d})>{\varepsilon}
, we have that

(3.1)

\|R(z)\|_{p\to q}\prec\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1.

Moreover, we have the bounds

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|R(z)\|_{2\to q}=\|R(z)\|_{p\to 2}\prec\eta^{-1/2}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1)^{1/2},

with implicit constants depending only on
{\varepsilon}
,
\delta
, and
d
.

There are two main ingredients involved in the proof of Proposition
3.1
. The first is an argument that allows us to transfer

\ell^{2}\to\ell^{q}
bounds (and, by duality,
\ell^{p}\to\ell^{2}
bounds) from spectral projections of the Laplacian
\Delta_{L}
to spectral
projections of the random Hamiltonian
H_{L}
. This is based on the main result of
[
BDH25
]
, which establishes a comparison in
\ell^{2}

operator norm for these spectral projections that is effective down to intervals of width
\lambda^{2}
(see Proposition
3.2
below). We can then recover bounds for the resolvent square-root
R^{1/2}

by decomposing into spectral projections, and we conclude by estimating (for
p\leq 2\leq q
):

\|R\|_{p\to q}\leq\|R^{1/2}\|_{p\to 2}\|R^{1/2}\|_{2\to q}.

The next ingredient we need is an
\ell^{2}\to\ell^{q}
bounds for the spectral projections of
\Delta
. This is equivalent to a Tomas-Stein type restriction estimate
[
Tom75
]
, and follow from appropriate decay bounds for the Fourier transform of the uniform measure on the level sets of the dispersion relation. In
d=2
this is a simple consequence of the curvature of the level sets (for levels away from
\Sigma_{2}
), but in
d\geq 3
this is made more complicated by the fact that these level sets have curves of vanishing Gaussian curvature.
The correct decay bound was established by Erdős and Salmhofer
[
ES07
]
for the
dispersion relation on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}
up to logarithmic losses. In
[
Tai21
]
, Taira established the correct pointwise decay without logarithmic losses, and a simpler proof was recently given by Cuenin and Schippa in
[
CS22
]
. In dimensions
d>3
we
are not aware of any work analyzing pointwise bounds for the decay of the Fourier transform of the level sets of the dispersion relation of the Laplacian. Instead we use a simple argument in which we write the spectral projection as an integral
of the propagator
e^{it\Delta}
and use a Strichartz estimate (as explained for example in
[
Ger23
, Section 5.4]
). This gives a suboptimal bound since we do not take into account cancellations in the time integral, but it suffices for our result.

A few simplifying comments are in order before we commence with the proof. First, note that the stochastic
domination notation hides powers of
\lambda^{-{\varepsilon}}
, so it suffices to consider
p<p_{d}^{\prime}
and
q>p_{d}
(that is, we do not need to treat the endpoint exponents). Second, we note that the free resolvent on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is functionally equivalent to the free resolvent on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
(in
particular, their
\ell^{p}\to\ell^{q}
mapping properties are the same)
by Corollary
A.2
.

3.1.
The transfer argument.

First, we record the following result comparing the spectral projections of
H
to those of
\Delta
.

Proposition 3.2

(Corollary 1.3 of
[

]
)
.

Let
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
be a fixed smooth bump function. Then for

\|\chi((H_{L}-E)/\alpha)-\chi((\Delta_{L}-E)/\alpha)\|_{2\to 2}\leq CK\lambda|\log\lambda|^{2}\alpha^{-1/2}

holds for all

and

with probability at least

This result follows directly from the proof of Corollary
1.3
in
[

]
. That result is stated for a Hamiltonian on

as in (

. It is easy to see that such a bound still holds for

s{\,\lesssim\,}L
because the Schrodinger evolution is now periodic with period
2\pi L
. Since we take
L\geq\lambda^{-100}\gg\lambda^{-2}
, this does not affect the proof.

Using Proposition
3.2
we may now transfer bounds from the square-root resolvent of the Laplacian to bounds
on the square-root resolvent of
H_{L}
. We always take
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
, so one can define the square root by taking a branch cut down the negative imaginary axis (really we can define
R^{1/2}(z)=\chi(z)
by applying the functional calculus to any root
\chi(z)
such that
\chi(z)^{2}=\frac{1}{z}
for
z\in{\mathbb{H}}
).
For the sake of simplicity we record the bound using the stochastic domination notation, but strictly speaking this gives a weaker tail bound than what follows from Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be any norm on the vector space of functions of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
.
Then for any
z=E+i\eta
with
\eta>\lambda^{100}
we have

\displaystyle\|R^{1/2}(z)\|_{2\to X}\prec(\lambda\eta^{-1/2}+1)\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}(z)\|_{2\to X}.

Proof.

By writing

\displaystyle R^{1/2}(z)=(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}R^{1/2}(z),

it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}R^{1/2}\|_{2\to 2}\prec\lambda\eta^{-1/2}+1.

To this end, choose a smooth partition of unity
1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\chi_{k}
where
\chi_{0}
and
\chi_{j}
are positive bump functions with supports satisfying

\displaystyle\operatorname*{supp}\chi_{k}\subset\begin{cases}\{t\mid|t-E|\leq\eta\}&k=0\\
\{t\mid 2^{k-2}\eta\leq|t-E|\leq 2^{k+1}\eta\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, we choose the partition so that the functions
\chi_{j}
with
j\geq 1
are translations and dilations of
\chi_{1}
so that Proposition
3.2
can be applied to all
\chi_{j}
uniformly.
Note that this implies

\displaystyle{\operatorname*{Id}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\chi_{k}(\Delta_{L})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\chi_{k}(H_{L}),

for
N:=\log_{2}(W/\eta)
where
W
is the diameter of the spectrum of
H_{L}
.
Since
W\prec\lambda^{-10}
and
\eta>\lambda^{100}
we have

N\prec 1
.

Using this decomposition we may write,

\displaystyle(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}R^{1/2}(z)=\sum_{k,\ell}(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}\chi_{k}(\Delta_{L})\chi_{\ell}(H_{L})R^{1/2},

where we have abbreviated
R=R(z)
.
Now observe that for each
k,\ell
we have

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}\chi_{k}(\Delta_{L})\chi_{\ell}(H_{L})R^{1/2}\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}\chi^{1/2}_{k}(\Delta_{L})\|_{2\to 2}\|\chi^{1/2}_{k}(\Delta_{L})\chi^{1/2}_{\ell}(H_{L})\|_{2\to 2}\|\chi_{\ell}^{1/2}(H_{L})R\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle\quad{\,\lesssim\,}2^{k/2}\eta^{1/2}\cdot 2^{-\ell/2}\eta^{-1/2}\cdot\|\chi^{1/2}_{k}(\Delta_{L})\chi^{1/2}_{\ell}(H_{L})\|_{2\to 2}.

If
|k-\ell|<3
then this is bounded by an absolute constant.

Otherwise if
|k-\ell|\geq 3
then
the support conditions on the
\chi_{k}
guarantee that

\displaystyle\chi_{k}^{1/2}(\Delta_{L})\chi_{\ell}^{1/2}(H_{L})=-\chi^{1/2}_{k}(\Delta_{L})\left(\chi^{1/2}_{\ell}(\Delta_{L})-\chi^{1/2}_{\ell}(H_{L})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\chi^{1/2}_{k}(\Delta_{L})-\chi^{1/2}_{k}(H_{L})\right)\chi^{1/2}_{\ell}(H_{L}).

By Proposition
3.2
, we have for
K\geq\sqrt{|\log\lambda|}
the bound

\displaystyle\|\chi^{1/2}_{k}(\Delta_{L})-\chi^{1/2}_{k}(H_{L})\|_{2\to 2}\leq CK|\log\lambda|^{2}2^{-k/2}\eta^{-1/2},

for all
k
with probability at least
1-e^{-cK^{2}}
. Therefore, for
|k-\ell|\geq 3
and
K
in the range given above we have

\displaystyle\|\chi_{k}^{1/2}(\Delta_{L})\chi_{\ell}^{1/2}(H_{L})\|_{2\to 2}\leq CK\lambda|\log\lambda|^{2}2^{-\max(k,\ell)/2}\eta^{-1/2}.

with probability at least
1-e^{-cK^{2}}
. Combining these estimates, we conclude that for each
K\geq\sqrt{|\log\lambda|}

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}R^{1/2}\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\in[N]\\
|k-\ell|<3\end{subarray}}C+CK\lambda|\log\lambda|^{2}\eta^{-1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\in[N]\\
|k-\ell|\geq 3\end{subarray}}2^{k/2-\ell/2}2^{-\max(k,\ell)/2}

\displaystyle\leq CK\lambda|\log\lambda|^{2}\eta^{-1/2}N+CN,

with probability at least
1-e^{-cK^{2}}
.
Using
N\prec 1
and the definition of stochastic domination this implies

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{1/2}R^{1/2}\|_{2\to 2}\prec\lambda\eta^{-1/2}+1.

∎

3.2.
Resolvent bounds for
\Delta_{L}

Next we need a slight generalization of the Tomas-Stein restriction estimate in
[
Tom75
]
(see also
[
MS13
, Section 11.1]
for a detailed exposition) to the lattice setting. This was done in
[
Kac13
]
, and we also include an abbreviated proof below for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\sigma
be the surface measure of a hyper-surface inside
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
satisfying

(3.3)

\displaystyle|\hat{\sigma}(x)|\leq C\left<x\right>^{-k},

for some
k>0
. Then

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\ell^{2}(\sigma)}\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}e^{ix\xi}f(\xi)\,\sigma(d\xi)\right\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\leq C\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\sigma)}

for
p>2(k+1)/k
.
The constant
C
in (
3.4
) depends only on the constant in (
3.3
) and the constant

(3.5)

\sup_{\xi\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},r>0}r^{1-d}\sigma(B(\xi,r)).

Proof.

By a standard duality argument, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\|f*\hat{\sigma}_{E}\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\leq C\|f\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}

for all
f\in\ell^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
, where
p^{\prime}
is the Hölder conjugate of
p
.

Let
1=\chi_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\chi_{j}
be a Littlewood-Payley partition of unity, i.e., for
j>0
,
\chi_{j}(x)=\chi(2^{-j}x)
for some
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
Restricting to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we may then write

\displaystyle f*\hat{\sigma}=f*\chi_{0}\hat{\sigma}+\sum_{j\geq 1}f*\chi_{j}\hat{\sigma}.

From Young’s inequality and (
3.3
), we have that

\displaystyle\|f*\chi_{j}\hat{\sigma}\|_{\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\leq C2^{-jk}\|f\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}.

On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle\|f*\chi_{j}\hat{\sigma}\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\cdot\|\hat{\chi}_{j}*\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}2^{j}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}.

To get the bound
\|\hat{\chi}_{j}\ast\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}{\,\lesssim\,}2^{j}
, note that
\hat{\chi}_{j}

is
L^{1}
-normalized and localized to a ball of radius
2^{-j}
. More precisely,

\hat{\chi_{j}}
satisfies

|\hat{\chi_{j}}(\xi)|{\,\lesssim\,}2^{jd}|2^{j}d(\xi,0)+1|^{-100d},

for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
so the bound
\|\hat{\chi_{j}}\ast\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}{\,\lesssim\,}2^{j}
follows from (
3.5
).

Interpolating yields

\displaystyle\|f*\chi_{j}\hat{\sigma}\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\leq C2^{j(-k\gamma+(1-\gamma))}\|f\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})},

when
p=\frac{2}{1-\gamma}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|f*\hat{\sigma}\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j\geq 0}2^{j(-k\gamma+(1-\gamma))}\|f\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})},

when
-k\gamma+(1-\gamma)<0
, or equivalently
p>2(k+1)/k
, as desired.
∎

Next we use Proposition
3.4
to obtain estimates for spectral projections by
integrating over level sets of the dispersion relation.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
d\geq 2
and define

\displaystyle\Pi_{E,\alpha}(\cdot):={\mathbf{1}}_{[E-\alpha,E+\alpha]}(\cdot),

for any
E\in\mathbb{R}
,
\delta>0
. Then for any
E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with
d(E_{0},\Sigma_{d})>\epsilon
and any
\alpha<\epsilon

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{E_{0},\delta}(\Delta_{L})\|_{2\to p}{\,\lesssim\,}\sqrt{\alpha}.

Proof.

We prove the analogous bound for
\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
, which then implies the statement by Lemma
A.1
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. First, for
d=3
, let
\sigma_{E}
be the surface measure on
\omega^{-1}(\{E\})
.
From Section 4.3 of
[
CS22
]
, there exists
a
C>0
such that for any
E
with
d(E,\Sigma_{3})>{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle|\hat{\sigma}_{E}(x)|\leq C\left<x\right>^{-3/4}.

Moreover, by continuity and compactness, the quantity

\displaystyle\sup_{r>0,\xi\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\frac{\sigma_{E}(B(\xi,r))}{r^{d-1}}

is bounded uniformly for
E
in this range as well so that by Proposition
3.4
we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\ell^{2}(\sigma)}\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}e^{ix\xi}f(\xi)\,\sigma(d\xi)\right\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\leq C\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\sigma)},

for any
p>14/3
. Using the coarea formula, we write

\displaystyle(\Pi_{E_{0},\delta}(\Delta)f)(x)=\int_{|\omega(\xi)-E_{0}|<\delta}e^{ix\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)\>d\xi=\int_{E_{0}-\delta}^{E_{0}+\delta}\int e^{ix\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)|\nabla\omega(\xi)|^{-1}\>\sigma_{E}(d\xi)dE,

where
|\nabla\omega|
is uniformly bounded below on the domain of integration.
Thus, from Minkowski’s inequality and (
3.6
), we have that

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{E_{0},\delta}(\Delta)f\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E_{0}-\delta}^{E_{0}+\delta}\left\|\int e^{ix\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)|\nabla\omega(\xi)|^{-1}\>\sigma_{E}(d\xi)\right\|_{\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{3})}\>dE

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{E_{0}-\delta}^{E_{0}+\delta}\|\hat{f}\|_{L^{2}(\sigma_{E})}\>dE

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\delta}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{3})},

which establishes the result for
d=3
.

For
d=2
, the proof is the same except that one instead has the estimate

\displaystyle|\hat{\sigma}_{E}(x)|\leq C\left<x\right>^{-1/2},

yielding the same result for
p>6
.
This follows from elementary stationary phase because the curvature of
\omega^{-1}(\{E\})
is uniformly bounded away from
0
for all
E
with
d(E,\Sigma_{2})>{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, this same bound holds in
any
d\geq 2
because there is always at least one direction of nonvanishing Gaussian curvature.

For
d>3
, we instead rewrite
\Pi_{E_{0},\delta}
in terms of the unitary propagator:

\displaystyle\Pi_{E_{0},\delta}(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{{\mathbf{1}}}_{[E_{0}-\delta,E_{0}+\delta]}(t)e^{it\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}}\>dt

so that by Cauchy-Schwarz

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{E_{0},\delta}(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}})\|_{2\to p}{\,\lesssim\,}\sqrt{\delta}\|e^{it\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\to\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}))}

Using the Strichartz estimate for
\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}

[
SK05
, Thm 1]
,

\displaystyle\|e^{it\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}}\|_{L^{2}_{t}\ell^{p}_{x}}{\,\lesssim\,}1,

valid for
d>3
and
p=\frac{2d}{d-3}
, completes the proof.
∎

Finally we decompose the square root of the resolvent
R^{1/2}
using spectral projections and complete the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
\epsilon>0
and
p>p_{d}
.
For any
z=E+i\eta
with
d(E,\Sigma_{d})>\epsilon

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}\|_{2\to p}{\,\lesssim\,}|\log\eta|+1

and

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}\|_{p^{\prime}\to 2}{\,\lesssim\,}|\log\eta|+1.

The implicit constant may depend on
p
.

Proof.

For
k>0
, let
\Pi_{k}
be the indicator function of

\displaystyle\{E^{\prime}\mid 2^{k-1}\eta<|E-E^{\prime}|\leq 2^{k}\eta\}\cap\{E^{\prime}\mid d(E^{\prime},\Sigma_{d})>{\varepsilon}\},

let
\Pi_{0}
be the indicator function of

\displaystyle\{E^{\prime}\mid\eta|E-E^{\prime}|\leq\eta\}\cap\{E^{\prime}\mid d(E^{\prime},\Sigma_{d})>{\varepsilon}\},

and let
\Pi^{c}
be the indicator function of

\displaystyle\{E^{\prime}\mid d(E^{\prime},\Sigma_{d})\leq{\varepsilon}\}.

We may therefore write

\displaystyle(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}=(\Delta_{L}-E-i\eta)^{-1/2}\left(\Pi^{c}(\Delta)+\sum_{k=0}\Pi_{k}(\Delta)\right),

where the sum over
k
contains at most
\log\eta
terms.

Since
p\geq 2
, we have that

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}\Pi^{c}(\Delta_{L})\|_{p\to 2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}\Pi^{c}(\Delta_{L})\|_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{d(E^{\prime},\Sigma_{d})\leq{\varepsilon}}\left(|E-E^{\prime}|^{2}+\eta^{2}\right)^{-1/4},

which is bounded in terms of
{\varepsilon}
, independently of
\eta
, by the assumption on
E
.

Otherwise, we use Lemma
3.5
to see that

\displaystyle\|(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1/2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\Pi_{k}(\Delta_{L})\|_{2\to p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\|(\Delta-z)^{-1/2}\Pi_{k}(\Delta)\|_{2\to 2}\|\Pi_{k}(\Delta)\|_{2\to p}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k\geq 0}(2^{-k/2}\eta^{-1/2})\cdot(2^{k/2}\eta^{1/2})

which establishes (
3.7
).
The estimate (
3.8
) now follows by duality since
R^{*}(z)=R(\overline{z})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We simply apply Lemma
3.3
and Proposition
3.6
successively:

\displaystyle\|R(z)\|_{p\to q}

\displaystyle\leq\|R^{1/2}(z)\|_{p\to 2}\|R^{1/2}(z)\|_{2\to q}

4.
Analysis of the self-consistent equation: proof of the local law

In this section, we follow the approach outlined in Section
1.1.2

and show
R(z)
is entrywise close to
M(z)
, defined as the solution to the self-consistent equation

(4.1)

\displaystyle M(z)=(\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}M_{00}(z)))^{-1}.

Note that for any
z\in\mathbb{H}
,
M(z)
exists and is unique by Proposition
A.6
, and that by Proposition
1.4

\mathbb{E}R
approximately solves a similar equation.

Theorem 4.1

(Local law)
.

Fix
\epsilon,\delta>0
and
d\geq 2
, and let
z=E+i\eta
with
d(E,\Sigma_{d})\geq\epsilon
.
Then

•

In any

\mathbf{d\geq 2}
,
and
\eta>\lambda^{2+2/9-\delta}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}|{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}-M_{xy}|

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}-\delta}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1)^{9/4}

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}|R_{xy}-{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}|

\displaystyle\prec\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1)^{7/4}.

•

In

\mathbf{d=3}
and
\eta>\lambda^{2+6/19-\delta}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}|{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}-M_{xy}|

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{\frac{3}{4}-\delta}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1)^{19/8}

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}|R_{xy}-{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}|

\displaystyle\prec\lambda^{\frac{3}{4}}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1)^{15/8}.

•

In

\mathbf{d\geq 7}
and
\eta>\lambda^{2+\frac{2d-12}{3d-12}-\delta}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}|{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}-M_{xy}|

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{1-\delta}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1}+1)^{1/2}.

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}|R_{xy}-{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}|

\displaystyle\prec\lambda.

All implicit constants above depend only on
\delta,\epsilon
, and
d
, and
M=M(z)

is defined by (
4.1
).

In the course of the proof it will be clear that an improvement to the Tomas-Stein exponent
p_{d}
from the previous section would lead to an improvement in the exponent of
\lambda
.

The fluctuations of
R_{xx}
are at least of order
\lambda
as can be seen by varying
V_{xx}
.
So the bound on
|R_{xx}-\mathbb{E}R_{xx}|
that we obtain in
d\geq 7
is sharp to leading order.

4.1.
The key ingredients

We use two principles in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(which are also applied
to understand
{\mathbb{E}}RAR^{*}
):

(1)

{\mathbb{E}}R
solves (
4.1
) up to error terms which are
controlled by the entrywise fluctuations of
R
.

(2)

The entrywise fluctuations of
R
are controlled by
\|R\|_{1\to 4}
.

The first principle combined with the
stability
of (
4.1
)
implies that to bound
\|\mathbb{E}R-M\|_{1\to\infty}
, one just needs to control the fluctuations of
R
.
This idea is by now standard in random matrix theory. The second principle is specific to random
Schrodinger operators and comes from the fact that the randomness only appears on the diagonal.

Below, we will frequently make use of the following special case of the resolvent identity, often known as the
Ward identity
:

(4.2)

\displaystyle R(z)R^{*}(z)=\frac{\operatorname*{Im}R(z)}{\operatorname*{Im}z}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Im}R=\frac{R-R^{*}}{2i},

valid for
R
the resolvent of any self-adjoint operator and
\operatorname*{Im}z>0
. In particular, taking a diagonal entry we have for any
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
and
z=E+i\eta

(4.3)

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}}|R(E+i\eta)_{xy}|^{2}=\eta^{-1}\operatorname*{Im}R(z)_{xx}.

This allows us to transfer of
\ell^{2}
-based quantities to entrywise ones.
For instance, taking a supremum over
x
in (
4.3
) and taking a square root we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\|R\|_{1\to 2}\leq(|\operatorname*{Im}z|)^{-1/2}\|R\|_{1\to\infty}^{1/2}.

Here and below we sometimes use the
1\to\infty
operator norm as a convenient shorthand for the entrywise supremum.

First we state a calculation showing
\mathbb{E}R_{xy}-M_{xy}
is bounded by the fluctuations of
R_{xy}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Fluctuations control expectations)
.

Let
\delta,\epsilon>0
,
d\geq 2
, and
z
be as in Theorem
4.1
.
If
\lambda\ll 1
and

(4.5)

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}R_{00}-M_{00}|\ll 1

then

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{E}}R-M\|_{1\to\infty}{\,\lesssim\,}(\lambda^{2}\eta^{-1})^{1/2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\|R-{\mathbb{E}}R\|_{1\to\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\|R\|_{1\to\infty}\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

First we use the approximate self-consistent equation to estimate
\mathbb{E}R_{xy}-\tilde{M}_{xy}
. Recall from Proposition
1.4
that

(SCE)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}R=\tilde{M}({\operatorname*{Id}}+{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm loc})\\
&\mathfrak{E}_{\rm loc}:=\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{D}[R-{\mathbb{E}}R]R.\end{split}

where the diagonalizing superoperator
{\mathcal{D}}
was defined in (
1.10
) and

\displaystyle\tilde{M}(z)=(\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}{\mathbb{E}}R_{00}(z)))^{-1}.

For any
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}
, we may write using
(SCE)

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}R_{xy}-\tilde{M}_{xy}|

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}|\mathinner{\langle{x|\tilde{M}{\mathbb{E}}({\mathcal{D}}[R-{\mathbb{E}}R])R|y}\rangle}|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{2}\|\tilde{M}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\|_{2}({\mathbb{E}}\|{\mathcal{D}}[R-{\mathbb{E}}R]\|_{2\to 2}^{2})^{1/2}({\mathbb{E}}\|R\|_{1\to 2}^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda\eta^{-1/2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\|R-{\mathbb{E}}R\|_{1\to\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\|R\|_{1\to\infty}\right)^{1/2}.

To pass to the last line we used the estimates
\mathbb{E}\|R\|_{1\to 2}^{2}{\,\lesssim\,}\eta^{-1}\mathbb{E}\|R\|_{1\to\infty}

and
\|\tilde{M}\|_{1\to 2}{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{-1}
. The first follows directly by
(
4.4
). For the second we first apply the Ward identity (
4.3
)
to
\tilde{M}
to estimate

\displaystyle\|\tilde{M}\mathinner{|{x}\rangle}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname*{Im}\tilde{M}_{xx}}{\eta+\lambda^{2}\operatorname*{Im}\mathbb{E}R_{00}(z)}.

Now note that, since
d(E,\Sigma_{d})>{\varepsilon}
, Lemma
A.4
and (
4.5
) imply
\operatorname*{Im}(\mathbb{E}R_{00})\gtrsim 1
for
\lambda

small enough. Moreover,
\text{Re}(\mathbb{E}R_{00}){\,\lesssim\,}1
, so that
taking
\lambda
sufficiently small we can apply Lemma
A.4
to
\tilde{M}
and obtain that
\operatorname*{Im}{\tilde{M}_{xx}}{\,\lesssim\,}1
.

Now we estimate
|\tilde{M}_{00}-M_{00}|
. The function
(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1}_{00}
is
C^{1}
in the
z
variable away from
\Sigma_{d}

(see Lemma
A.4
), so

\displaystyle|\tilde{M}_{00}-M_{00}|

\displaystyle=|(\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}{\mathbb{E}}R_{00}))^{-1}_{00}-(\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}M_{00}))^{-1}_{00}|

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{2}|{\mathbb{E}}R_{00}-M_{00}|.

Putting the above two bounds together gives us the claim for diagonal entries:

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}R_{00}-M_{00}|

\displaystyle\leq|{\mathbb{E}}R_{00}-\tilde{M}_{00}|+|\tilde{M}_{00}-M_{00}|

Proof.

By the coarea formula the density

|x|^{-1}
is barely not integrable in one dimension), so instead we replace (
A.1
) by

\int_{X_{\sigma}}\frac{dp}{|\nabla\omega(p)|}\leq C\log|\sigma|^{-1}.

For the lower bound, simply notice that
|\nabla\omega(p)|\leq 2d
, so
\rho(\sigma)\geq|X_{\sigma}|
.
The function
\sigma\mapsto|X_{\sigma}|
is positive on
(-2d,2d)
and continuous so the lower bound follows
from compactness.
∎

A.3.
Regularity of the resolvent entries

Lemma
A.3
implies some regularity for the entries of the resolvent which we use
to analyze the stability of the self-consistent equation. We have regularity on
the domains
D_{\varepsilon}
, given by

D_{{\varepsilon}}:=\{E+i\eta\ |\ d(E,\Sigma_{d})\geq{\varepsilon},\ \eta>0\}.

Lemma A.4
.

The function
\phi_{L}(z):=(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}_{L}}-z)^{-1}_{00}
satisfies

\|\phi_{L}\|_{C^{2}(D_{\varepsilon})}\leq C,

where
C
depends implicitly on
{\varepsilon}
but not on
\lambda
or
L
.
Moreover,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Im}\phi_{L}\gtrsim 1

Proof.

Because
L\gg\lambda^{-10}
it suffices to work with
\phi(z):=(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-z)^{-1}_{00}
, which can be written as

\phi(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\frac{dp}{\omega(p)-z}=\int\frac{1}{\sigma-z}\rho(\sigma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma.

This is the analytic continuation to the upper half plane of the function
H\rho+i\rho
, where

H\rho
is the Hilbert transform of
\rho
. Since
\rho
is smooth away from
\Sigma_{d}
,
the Hilbert transform
H\rho
is as well.
∎

A consequence of Lemma
A.4
and the Ward identity (
4.2
) is the following estimate for the resolvent.

Corollary A.5
.

For
z=E+i\eta
with
d(E,\Sigma_{d})>{\varepsilon}
and
\eta>0
,

\|(\Delta_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}-z)^{-1}\|_{1\to 2}{\,\lesssim\,}\eta^{-1/2}.

A.4.
Uniqueness of the self-consistent equation

Finally we record facts about the equation

\theta=((\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta))^{-1})_{00}.

Proposition A.6
.

For any
z\in D_{\epsilon}
, there is a unique
\theta(z)\in{\mathbb{H}}
solving

\displaystyle\theta(z)=(\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta(z))^{-1}_{00}

Moreover for
\lambda\ll 1
,
\theta(z)
satisfies

|\operatorname*{Im}(\theta(z))-\rho(z)|{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{2}.

Proof.

Fix
z\in D_{\epsilon}
. First we show existence and uniqueness.
Define the map
\Phi_{z}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
by

\Phi_{z}(w):=(\Delta_{L}-(z+\lambda^{2}w))^{-1}_{00}=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in(\frac{2\pi}{L}{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}2\cos(\xi_{i})-z-\lambda^{2}w}.

For any
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the mapping
w\mapsto\frac{1}{r-z-\lambda^{2}w},
maps
\mathbb{H}
to a compact subset of
\mathbb{H}
, and is a strict contraction in the standard hyperbolic distance on
\mathbb{H}
, since
\operatorname*{Im}z>0
.

\Phi_{z}
is an average of such maps, so
\Phi_{z}
has a unique fixed point in the upper half plane.

Now we show
|\operatorname*{Im}(\theta(z))-\rho(z)|{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{2}
. Note that it suffices to show

|\theta(z)|{\,\lesssim\,}1
, since then for
\lambda
small enough we could invoke Lemma
A.4
to conclude that

|\theta(z)-(\Delta_{L}-z)_{00}^{-1}|\leq|(\Delta-(z+\lambda^{2}\theta(z)))^{-1}_{00}-(\Delta_{L}-z)^{-1}_{00}|{\,\lesssim\,}\lambda^{2}.

To prove
|\theta(z)|{\,\lesssim\,}1
, we consider the action of
\Phi_{z}
on the set

B_{.5\epsilon\lambda^{-2}}(0)\cap\{w\ |\operatorname*{Im}w\geq\lambda\}
. Since
\lambda\ll 1
Lemma
A.4
implies that for any
w\in B_{.5\epsilon\lambda^{-2}}(0)\cap\{w\ |\operatorname*{Im}w\geq\lambda\}

|\Phi_{z}(w)|{\,\lesssim\,}1,

and

\operatorname*{Im}\Phi_{z}(w)\gtrsim 1.

The Brouwer fixed point theorem implies
\Phi_{z}
has a fixed point in the set
B_{.5\epsilon\lambda^{-2}}(0)\cap\{w\ |\operatorname*{Im}w\geq\lambda\}
and so by the uniqueness proven above this fixed point must be
\theta(z)
. Then the bounds above imply
|\theta(z)|{\,\lesssim\,}1.

∎
