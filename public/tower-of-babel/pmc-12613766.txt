Development and psychometric evaluation of virtual consultation scoring rubric for the standardized patient
Authors: Siaw Cheok Liew, Jessica Salt, Vinod Pallath, Karen Morgan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStandardized patients (SPs) are integral to training medical students in virtual consultation (VC) skills. A key aspect of their role is the ability to evaluate student performance and deliver meaningful feedback to enhance learning. This study aimed to design and validate a scoring rubric specifically for SPs to assess undergraduate medical students' performance during VC encounters.MethodsThis study adopted a seven-step approach to rubric development. The content and face validation of the rubric were conducted with the participation of relevant stakeholders. The reliability of the rubric was assessed by measuring the internal consistency and the inter-rater, intra-rater and test–retest correlation coefficient of the SPs evaluation scores of medical students’ performance in VC.ResultsA rubric comprising ten evaluation dimensions and a four-level scoring scale was developed. The item content validity index (I-CVI) exceeded 0.78, while the average content validity index (AVE-CVI) was 0.98. Face validity was assessed qualitatively which confirmed the rubric’s clarity and relevance. Five SPs participated in evaluating medical student's performance in VC encounters, each reviewing 35 videos. The rubric demonstrated strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82. Additionally, inter-rater, intra-rater, and test–retest correlation coefficients indicated a high level of reliability.ConclusionThe newly developed SP assessment rubric of medical students’ VC skills has reached the desired level of psychometric property making it suitable for use in training and assessing VC competency. Future research is required to test its applicability to be used in an alternative form, for example as a guide for SPs to provide formative verbal feedback to medical students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08139-x.Keywords: Virtual consultation, Simulated patients, Standardized patients, Rubric, Communication skills, Reliability

Introduction

Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) focuses on achieving clearly defined outcomes, with strategies like deliberate practice and continuous, meaningful feedback playing a vital role in supporting learners' progression toward competence [1]. Rubrics are vital tools that offer clear, structured criteria to define performance expectations and serve as a foundation for effective feedback [2]. Their development is informed by key educational theories which include constructivist learning, which emphasizes active knowledge construction through experience and reflection [3], adult learning theory, which values relevant, timely feedback for self-directed learners [4]; and social cognitive theory, which highlights the role of feedback in building self-efficacy [5].

An analytic rubric is often used as a guide in assessment to score and evaluate students' performance. It contains a set of criteria and scales that provide guidelines and expectations to ensure fairness and consistency in an assessment process [6]. In simulation-based education, the use of an assessment rubric enables standardized patients (SP) to assess and provide feedback to trainees in a more targeted and detailed fashion [7]. A standardized patient is an individual who has been carefully trained to present an illness or scenario in a systematic manner for the education and assessment of learners in the health care professions [8]. A rubric also helps to provide SPs with awareness and mutual understanding regarding the expectations of the level of standfard to evaluate the medical students [9]. Because an analytic rubric breaks down expected competencies into discrete elements, trainees are able to receive specific feedback, which allows them to reflect on their performance, understand their strengths and weaknesses, and subsequently plan their learning [6, 10].

The use of scoring rubrics in objective structure clinical examinations (OSCEs) enables SPs to act as examiners in the assessment process [11]. Through structured training, SPs can effectively adhere to standardised assessment criteria within a given assessment, for example the OSCE, thereby promoting consistency in evaluation and minimising grading discrepancies [12]. Although structured training enhances reliability in assessment, each OSCE context requires its own focused preparation to maintain performance quality. The use of scoring rubrics helps clarify the communication skills competencies under evaluation and provides clear expectations for the assessment of these skills [13]. Some high stake OSCE stations often require two clinician assessors. Replacing one of these roles by incorporating SPs as a second evaluator in OSCEs can reduce disruptions to clinical workflows and present a more cost-effective option than recruiting clinicians [14, 15]. This cost-effective approach optimises resource utilisation in medical education settings while providing SPs with valuable insights into educational practices [16].

In addition to their role in OSCEs, SPs are increasingly employed in the training of medical students for virtual consultation (VC) [17]. Because competency in VC is essential in modern healthcare delivery, the integration of SPs into VC training programmes ensures that medical students are well-prepared for clinical practice [18]. SPs can effectively simulate virtual patient encounters whereby students can practice communication skills, clinical reasoning, observational physical examination and patient-centred care within the context of VC [19]. SPs’ ability to provide constructive and insightful feedback on students' performance in VC enables targeted skill development [20]. SPs’ feedback serves as a valuable learning tool, empowering students to reflect on their practice, address deficiencies, and refine their VC skills, all of which contribute to their readiness for virtual healthcare delivery [21].

The need for the creation, development and psychometric analysis of a rubric for VC arises from the complexity and specialised nature of skills required in this field [22]. Unlike traditional face-to-face interactions, VC requires practitioners to have additional competencies such as effective use of technology, clear communication through virtual platforms, and observational physical examination, to name a few [18, 23]. A well-designed scoring rubric must go beyond offering SPs a structured framework for assessment and feedback. It should also function as a robust, reliable, and valid measurement tool. Therefore, establishing a psychometrically sound rubric is critical to ensure that VC skills are assessed with accuracy, fairness, and rigor [24].

By establishing the reliability of scoring and the validity of criteria, educators and SPs can confidently use the rubric to assess students' proficiency in VC and guide their learning effectively. Additionally, it serves as a valuable tool for faculty development, enabling educators to standardize assessment practices and support ongoing improvement in VC training programs. Thus, the creation of a validated rubric for VC contributes to the rigor and effectiveness of medical education, preparing students to deliver high-quality care in diverse clinical settings, including virtual environments.

Content validity

Experts’ opinions were sought to assess the content validity of the newly developed VC-SP rubric. The panel of experts comprised two VC practitioners, four medical doctors, one recent medical graduate and one clinical psychologist who was actively involved in communication skills teaching to medical students. The relevance or representativeness of the items that were included in the rubric was reviewed based on the degree of relevance of each criterion to the VC training. A 4-point Likert scale: 1 = the item is not relevant to the measured criterion; 2 = the item is somewhat relevant to the measured criterion; 3 = the item is quite relevant to the measured criterion; 4 = the item is highly relevant to the measured criterion was given to the panel of experts for evaluation. The accuracy and clarity of each criterion and its descriptions were assessed. To determine the content validity of the rubric, calculations for items that were scored 3 or 4 by the panel of experts were carried out to ascertain the item content validity index (I-CVI) and average-content validity index (AVE-CVI) values.

Face validity

The opinions of the stakeholders were sought to evaluate the appropriateness and usability of the SP-VC rubric. A group of medical practitioners (to provide a clinician–educator perspective), medical students (as the end-users being assessed), and SPs (as the assessors applying the rubric) were asked to assess the VC-SP rubric. Each group was invited to review the rubric and provide open-ended feedback on its clarity, ease of use, and completeness. Specific attention was given to whether the descriptors reflected authentic clinical communication behaviours, whether the scoring levels were sufficiently distinct, and whether the rubric could be applied consistently in practice. This process ensured that the rubric was perceived as clear, relevant, understandable, complete, easy to use and practical across all stakeholder groups, thereby strengthening its face validity.

Reliability

Inter-rater reliability, intra-rater reliability and test–retest reliability

The selection of SP raters for this study was randomly chosen from a group of SPs who had been trained in VC. All the raters had participated as a SP, at least twice, in a VC course. Five raters were chosen to independently score the VC interviews between a medical student and a SP. The raters were blinded to the scores of the other raters. The adequate sample size for the inter-reliability study involved the recruitment of at least 30 heterogeneous samples and a minimum of three raters [26]. This study aims to determine the level of inter-rater agreement for the SP-VC rubric for undergraduate medical students rated by five SPs. With each medical student’s interview is allowed five chances of rating, a minimum of 34 medical students’ interviews is needed to be assessed by the SP to achieve the statistical significance for an alpha value set at 0.05 and with a minimum power of 90.0% [27]. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) values denote < 0.5 for poor reliability, 0.5–0.75 for moderate reliability, 0.75–0.9 for good reliability and > 0.9 for excellent reliability [26].

Statistical analysis

The SPSS (version 20.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) software was used for data analysis. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to measure the reliability of the rubric based on the consistency, correlation and conformity of the data. ICC estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were based on mean rating (K = 5), absolute-agreement, and a 2-way mixed-effects model.

An analytical scoring rubric was determined by the research team as the chosen scoring strategy. Hence, a four-point scale with numerical scores between 1–4 was given to the performance levels with labels 1 = denoting unacceptable performance, 2 = needs improvement, 3 = meets expectations, and 4 = excellent. Descriptions of the different levels of performance for each of the criteria listed in the rubric were included. Appendix 1 shows the newly developed SP rubric.

Content validity

Two VC practitioners, four medical doctors, one recent medical graduate and one clinical psychologist who was actively involved in communication skills teaching to medical students participated to review the degree of relevance of each criterion in the rubric to the VC training. The content validity index (I-CVI) for the rubric was > 0.78, and the average-content validity index (AVE-CVI) value was 0.98.

Face validity

Five SPs participated to review and comment on the usability of the rubric. Verbal feedback indicated that the ten dimensions of VC skills rubric is comprehensive, sufficient and practical to be used for feedback and scoring of medical students’ performance. The SPs were excited that the rubric could be used to provide structure in their assessment of students’ performance. Five medical students who participated to review the rubric commented that the rubric is comprehensive, easy to comprehend, and provided them with clear guidelines for the expectations of a VC consultation.

Reliability of rubric

Inter-rater reliability

Five SPs evaluated 35 videos of medical students’ recorded virtual medical consultations, with the first evaluation conducted from December 2022 to February 2023 and the second from April to May 2023. Four SPs were male, one female. The mean age of the SPs was 60.80 (SD 13.7) years and four of them have more than 10 years of experience as an SP, mean 11.2 (SD 3.83) years. Each video was five minutes in duration. The mean and standard deviation (SD) scores for SP1 was 25.26 ± 2.13, SP2 was 25.74 ± 2.24, SP3 was 27.17 ± 2.70, SP4 was 25.77 ± 2.64 and SP5 was 25.80 ± 1.94. The inter-rater reliability of the rubric at a level of 95% confidence interval utilising two-way random absolute agreement inter-rater intraclass coefficient, average measures revealed an ICC (2,k) = 0.79, 95% CI [0.66, 0.88], F(34, 136) = 5.36, p < 0.001. This indicates good inter-rater reliability [26], supporting the consistent application of the rubric across the five raters.

Test–retest reliability

The same five SPs conducted a second evaluation of the same 35 videos from April to May 2023. The mean score for SP1 was 25.54 ± 2.66, SP2 was 26.03 ± 2.77, SP3 was 27.14 ± 2.71, SP4 was 24.94 ± 3.67 and SP5 was 26.34 ± 1.47. All five independent SP raters evaluated the same items using the same rubric. The resulting ICC values were SP1 0.89, SP2 0.81, SP3 0.98, SP4 0.76 and SP5 0.77, indicating good to excellent test–retest reliability [26].

These results suggest that the scores assigned by all the SPs were stable over time and that the rubric produced consistent results when applied repeatedly under similar conditions. The high level of agreement across the two time points supports the temporal reliability of the rubric and the assessment process.

Internal consistency

The correlations between all the ten rubric dimensions indicated that a correlation at the level of p < 0.05 with Cronbach alpha value of 0.82 was observed, showing the good existence of internal consistency between the dimensions.

Discussion

This study developed and validated an analytic scoring rubric that SPs can use to evaluate and score medical students’ performance in VC. This rubric was developed based on experts' consensus for VC training through a Delphi study [18, 28]. It provides a structured framework and offers SPs a reliable means to evaluate medical students' proficiency in conducting VC aiming to foster competency and preparedness for digitalised healthcare practice. The analytic approach of this rubric assessment allows for a detailed evaluation of various components of the VC process by breaking down expected skills into specific domains, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the VC competency. This analytic scoring rubric addresses a pressing need in this field and marks a significant contribution to medical education.

One of the challenges in an analytic rubric construction lies in the identification of appropriate evaluation criteria that captures the unique aspects of VC competency. It requires a balance between comprehensiveness and feasibility [29]. It was critical to develop unambiguous descriptors for each level of performance in the evaluation criteria for this new subject area [30]. Clear descriptors will help to minimise variability in interpretation among assessors and reduce the risk of subjective bias. This clarity enhances the validity of the rubric by ensuring that student performance is judged against consistent, observable standards rather than personal expectations. However, as with all rubrics, the application in practice can be challenging as assessors may interpret the descriptors differently or apply their subjective judgments which can reduce objectivity [31, 32].

Assessor training is essential to maximise the effectiveness of rubric utilisation in VC assessment. Training helps ensure that SPs interpret rubric descriptors consistently, minimizing subjective interpretations which can lead to variability in scoring and bias [29, 33, 34]. This alignment enhances the validity of the assessment by ensuring that it measures what it intends to measure. It is crucial that during SP training, the trainer should include a thorough review of the rubric, including its purpose, criteria, and descriptors [35]. The rationale behind each evaluation criterion listed and the expected performance levels of the medical students must be clearly set out. A large portion of time during training should focus on ensuring that the SPs accurately interpret the descriptors in the rubric [36].

In our training process, SPs received structured orientation on the purpose of the rubric and the skills being assessed. This is followed by calibration exercises using sample performances from students to align SP scoring and reduce subjectivity. Guidance emphasised focusing on observable behaviours, avoiding assumptions, and using descriptors rather than personal expectations when assigning scores. Training emphasized the importance of distinguishing between adjacent performance levels in the rubric. This process was significant in promoting consistency and reinforcing shared standards which in turn will enhance the validity and fairness of the assessment.

Having a consistent scoring across different assessors can increase the trustworthiness of the assessment outcomes. Effective training involves the use of examples and discussion of scenarios to explain the expected performance levels and establish a shared mental model among SPs [36]. Opportunities must be given to SPs to practice using the rubric to score the same simulated or real performances with immediate standardisation or calibration briefing whereby all scores are compared. This exercise helps identify discrepancies and promote consistency [37]. The other goal of training should be to overcome any possible resistance and emphasise the benefits of using analytical rubrics for assessment.

As some aspects of healthcare shift to the virtual space, the creation and psychometric evaluation of a VC grading rubric for SPs is a significant contribution to medical education. As with all studies, there are limitations. Although thorough, analytical rubrics may not encompass all aspects of performance, particularly those that are subjective or context-dependent. As a result, using analytical rubrics in assessments may limit the flexibility to account for unforeseen variances in student background and performance. Additionally, the limited sample size and lack of variation in SP characteristics may have an impact on the generalizability and application of the findings. This study showed evidence of content and face validity through expert review and pilot testing, but other areas of validity (such as concept and criterion validity) were not explored. This study has also provided evidence of inter-rater reliability through ICC and assessment of internal consistency but additional analysis such as generalisability study could further enhance understanding of the rubric's consistency in scoring. This study focused on the initial development and evaluation of the rubric, therefore, its performance over time was not assessed.

Future work includes a focus on conducting additional validation to explore the extent to which the rubric accurately measures outcomes. This could involve multi-site studies, larger sample sizes, and more diverse participant populations to enhance generalisability. Longitudinal studies could assess the rubric's stability and utility for tracking students' progress longitudinally. Future research could explore the alignment between simulated and real-world performance and the validity of the rubric in clinical practice. Further work includes refining and optimising the rubric components based on ongoing evaluation and feedback from various relevant stakeholders. Future research could explore the effectiveness of different feedback mechanisms and strategies in conjunction with the rubric. This detailed analytic rubric offers insights into areas for targeted improvement and curriculum refinement for VC practice.

Conclusion

In summary, developing a strong rubric in VC for SP feedback is critical for guaranteeing fair and accurate evaluation. Validity and reliability assessments using psychometric analysis established the rubric's credibility and applicability for successful performance evaluation. Regular training for SPs ensures their proficiency in using the rubric and contributes to the consistency and reliability. The development of a reliable, validated SP VC rubric contributes to a valid and reliable assessment of VC competency in medical students, leading to better development of VC skills.
