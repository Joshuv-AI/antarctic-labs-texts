Interleukin‐33‐Mediated Mechanical‐Loading Induced Degeneration of Knee Joint Chondrocytes
Authors: Jiahui Zhang, Yao Sun, Tongtong Chen, Hanqi Wang, Changwei Li, Yong Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOsteoarthritis (OA), a common degenerative joint disorder, continued to present significant challenges in clinical management due to an incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis. Although the full pathophysiology of OA remained unclear, emerging evidence implicated double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) released from damaged articular chondrocytes in promoting cartilage degeneration via the TLR3–IL‐33 signaling axis. Our investigation demonstrated that IL‐33 exerted dual pathological effects on chondrocytes: it induced cellular hypertrophy with upregulated osteogenic marker expression and mediated fluid shear stress (FSS)–induced matrix degradation. Notably, age‐related chondrocyte was associated with increased IL‐33 secretion. In vitro experiments revealed that IL‐33 administration significantly promoted chondrocyte hypertrophy and osteogenesis. Consistent with these findings, IL‐33‐knockout (KO) murine models showed marked resistance to FSS‐induced joint damage, with reduced cartilage erosion compared to wild‐type counterparts. These mechanistic insights not only advanced the understanding of OA progression but also highlighted IL‐33 inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy. This study provided a solid experimental foundation for the development of novel disease‐modifying interventions targeting this pathway.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), recognized as the leading cause of joint disability in aging populations, manifested as a multifactorial joint disorder involving complex pathophysiological interactions across articular components [1]. The disease was characterized by progressive cartilage erosion, dysregulated extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation, collectively disrupting the balance between joint tissue repair and catabolic degradation [2]. Articular cartilage (AC) degeneration was the hallmark of OA [3]. Although AC degradation could be triggered by various factors, mechanical loading was considered the primary initiator of OA pathogenesis, particularly in weight‐bearing joints [4]. Chondrocytes, the sole cell type in AC, underwent complex changes in OA, including alterations in both anabolic and catabolic metabolism. As central mediators of this regulatory cascade, chondrocytes sensed changes in their surrounding microenvironment through mechanosensitive channels [5]. Mechanical loading acted as a “double‐edged sword,” exerting divergent regulatory effects on chondrocyte activity and ECM metabolism. Fluid shear stress (FSS), a fluid‐derived mechanical force generated by pressure‐induced interstitial fluid flow across cell surfaces, represented a critical mechanical stimulus. This mechanotransduction process activated key biomechanical signaling cascades and induced transcriptional modifications. Current research identified several mechanosensitive signaling pathways involved in FSS sensing, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, RAS/MAPK cascade, NOTCH signaling, TGF‐β/BMP pathway, and NF‐κB activation network [6]. Abnormal mechanical loading of AC generated hydrostatic pressure, tensile strain, and FSS [7]. Unlike compressive loading, which served as a fundamental biomechanical parameter, FSS induced distinct shear strain patterns within cartilage tissue. This mechanical deformation was transmitted through the ECM to chondrocytes, with superficial zone chondrocytes exhibiting particular responsiveness. While compressive forces predominantly induced chondroprotective responses, surface shear stress tended to activate mechano‐inflammatory pathways, underscoring their distinct regulatory roles in cartilage homeostasis [8]. Although joint inflammation could be driven by inflammatory immune cells, blockade of IL‐1β or TNF‐α did not provide complete protection of joint structures, suggesting that additional signaling pathways contributing to joint catabolism remained to be elucidated. Interleukin‐33 (IL‐33), a member of the IL‐1 cytokine family, played a key role in various inflammatory processes and diseases [9]. Recognized as an “alarmin” cytokine, IL‐33 was rapidly stored and released in response to cell or tissue damage [10]. Notably, IL‐33 secretion could occur independently of cellular necrosis when viable cells encountered sub‐lethal stress or mechanical injury. This non‐lytic release involved coordinated events, including extracellular ATP efflux, amplification of cytosolic calcium flux, and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [11]. IL‐33 activated downstream signaling by binding to suppressor of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2)‐expressing cells [12], inducing interferon production, activation of immune cells, and recruitment and differentiation of macrophages, thereby influencing the surrounding cellular environment through paracrine signaling [13]. IL1RL1, the gene encoding IL‐33's receptor, produced four transcript isoforms through selective splicing in a cell type–independent manner, with soluble ST2 (sST2) and ST2 ligand (ST2L) being predominant [14]. ST2L, a transmembrane protein, contained a Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor (TIR) domain, while sST2, a secreted isoform lacking transmembrane and TIR domains, functioned as a decoy receptor that restricted IL‐33's access to ST2L [15]. The nuclear sequestration of IL‐33 underpinned its unique autocrine signaling mechanism. Constitutively retained within nuclear chromatin compartments, this dual‐function alarmin underwent rapid nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic translocation upon cellular compromise—particularly during necrotic membrane rupture. This spatial redistribution transformed IL‐33 into an extracellular danger signal, initiating pro‐inflammatory cascades via ST2 receptor engagement [16].

However, the regulatory relationship between FSS and IL‐33 in aged cartilage had not been fully characterized. Given the high prevalence of OA in elderly individuals, our investigation specifically focused on IL‐33–mediated pathways in this age‐associated pathology. Using a murine aging model, we systematically examined the functional interplay between FSS and IL‐33 in cartilage. Our data revealed elevated IL‐33 expression in senescent chondrocytes, which was further enhanced upon FSS exposure. Suppression of IL‐33 effectively mitigated FSS‐induced damage in senescent chondrocytes, with a concomitant reduction in cartilage hypertrophy and expression of osteogenic differentiation markers. These findings established IL‐33 as a key mediator of chondrocyte degeneration, with its downstream induction of endochondral ossification contributing directly to subchondral bone microarchitectural alterations. Collectively, our results identified a novel FSS‐driven mechanism in OA progression, in which IL‐33 release from senescent chondrocytes initiated hypertrophic transformation and endochondral ossification.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Twelve‐month‐old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. The animals that were randomly grouped were not performed in a blinded manner. The animals were housed with free access to water and chow in an air‐conditioned room with a 12‐h light–dark cycle, at 21°C to 23°C and 60% relative humidity in the animal facility at Charles River Laboratories, Shanghai, China.

Cell Culture

Isolated mouse chondrocytes were cultured in DMEM/F12 media. DMEM/F12 was supplemented, unless stated otherwise with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% Penicillin and Streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were maintained under sterile conditions in a humidified atmosphere of 37°C containing 95% oxygen and 5% (v/v) carbon dioxide unless stated otherwise.

Primary Chondrocyte Monolayer Culture and Stimulation

Mice full‐thickness cartilage slices were obtained from above the subchondral bone of the neonatal knee cartilage. For monolayer cultures, slices were minced and incubated sequentially with pronase and collagenase in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) until the fragments were digested. Released cells were seeded at 107/plate in 10‐cm culture plates in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Sydney, NSW, Australia) medium containing 10% FBS(Gibco), 50 U/mL penicillin and 50 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco) under standard culture conditions. After 7 days, confluent chondrocytes were split once and seeded at high density, and these first‐passage chondrocytes were authenticated by Alcian blue staining and mycoplasma contamination was tested before subsequent experiments. For all cell stimulation experiments, 105 cells were seeded in each well of 24‐well plates, or 106 cells were seeded in each well of 6‐well plates. When cells were grown to 80% confluence, the indicated doses of recombinant mouse IL‐33 (R&D, Abingdon, UK) were used to stimulate cells. After 24 h, cells were collected for RNA isolation or western blot.

Senescence‐Associated β‐Galactosidase Staining

P0, P5, and P10 chondrocytes were trypsinized and seeded into 6‐well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well. The next day, culture medium was removed and cells were washed with PBS three times and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS three times, β‐galactosidase staining was conducted with the cell senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase staining kit (Byotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) per the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were incubated at 37°C overnight and washed with PBS three times. Then, imaging was performed with the inverted microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Blue precipitation in the cytoplasm was observed, and three fields were randomly selected from each sample, and the percentage of positive cells was counted.

In Vitro FSS Experiment

FSS was applied by using a Flexcell STR‐4000 strain unit (Flexcell, North Carolina, USA). FSS was calculated based on the following formula: τ = 6μQ/wh2 (μ is the dynamic viscosity of perfusate, Q is the flow rate, w is the width, and h is the channel height). Briefly, chondrocytes were seeded onto the culture slips at a density of 2 × 105 cells and put into a parallel‐plate flow system. The system is a six‐chamber laminar flow device which includes a total of six 75 × 25 × 1 mm culture slips. After being exposed to 8, 16, and 24 dyne/cm2 FSS treatment for 4 h, the cells were collected for further experiments.

Real‐Time Quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA was prepared using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Low yield of RNA due to technical problems was predetermined. RNA was quantified by Thermo NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA). The cDNA synthesis was performed using ABScript III RT Master Mix for qPCR kit (ABclonal, WuHan, China). RT‐PCR was performed using an Applied Biosystems 7500 real‐time machine (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Quantification of gene expression was determined by the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method. The relative expression levels were determined by normalizing expression to actin. All the assays were performed in triplicate and repeated at least two times. The number of biological repetitions is three. The detailed sequences of primers used in this study are presented in Table 1.

Western Blot Analysis

The sixth generation of mouse articular chondrocytes was grown to confluence in culture slips and stimulated with FSS for 4 h before lysed in RIPA buffer (pH 7.4) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Pleasanton, CA, USA). For each sample, 40 μL of total cell lysate was fractionated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore Corp, Billerica, MA, USA). The membrane was blocked in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), 0.05% Triton X‐100, and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Samples were probed overnight with primary antibody: IL‐33 (Abcam Cat# 3874–1, RRID: AB_10896374), MMP13 (Abcam Cat# 1923–1, RRID: AB_765034), Runx2 (Abcam Cat# 5356–1, RRID: AB_10898061), and β‐actin (loading control) (Abcam Cat# 1844–1, RRID: AB_764433). The number of biological repetitions is three.

IL‐33 Intra‐Articular Injection

The mice required for the experiment were anesthetized and injected intraperitoneally at 1 mL/kg. After the mice were anesthetized, the hair of both lower limbs was shaved and sterilized with iodophor, followed by intra‐articular injection. A total of 20 μL of 0.1 μg/μL rIL‐33 was injected into the unilateral joint cavity of the mice, and 20 μL PBS was injected into the contralateral knee as a negative control. Mice were sacrificed 1 week later, and the knees were removed and stored in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological examination.

Micro‐CT

We dissected the left and right knee joints of mice free of soft tissue and fixed the samples in 4% buffered formalin for 48 h. The joint samples were then transferred into PBS and analyzed by high‐resolution micro‐CT (SkyScan 1076, Bruker‐microCT, Kontich, Belgium). The scanner was set at a voltage of 29 kV, a current of 169 μA, and a resolution of 12.6 μm per pixel to measure the subchondral bone and joint. Images were reconstructed using NRecon v1.7.1.0 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium), respectively. Sagittal images of the tibial subchondral bone were used to perform 3‐D histomorphometric analyses. The region of interest was defined to cover the whole subchondral bone. We used six consecutive images for 3‐D reconstruction and analysis using 3‐D model visualization software (DataViewer v1.5.0.4, Skyscan, US). 3‐D structural parameters were analyzed: BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, Tb.Sp, and BMD.

Histological Examination

For histological evaluation, mice were executed at the end of the experiment and the hind limbs were removed. The knee joints of mice were fixed in 4% neutral‐buffered formalin for 2 days, decalcified with 10% ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid at pH 7.4 for 4 weeks, and then dehydrated in a graded ethanol solution series, embedded in paraffin, sliced consecutively into 5 μm sections. Then, the sections were deparaffinized with xylene and hydrated, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), toluidine blue and safranin O‐fast green to examine the morphology and glycosaminoglycan content. The number of biological repetitions is three. Observation was performed under a light microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry

Heat‐mediated antigen retrieval was performed on paraffin‐embedded sections using citric acid buffer (Sigma Aldrich) warmed in a water bath at 100°C for 20 min. MMP‐13 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibody, and Runx2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibody and Actin antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were used at a 1:200 dilution. Quantification of positive cells/total cells was counted by two blinded operators.

Statistical Analysis

All data representative of three independent experiments are presented as mean ± SEM. We used two‐tailed t‐tests to determine significance between two groups. We performed analyses of multiple groups by one‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni post‐test of GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). For all statistical tests, we considered p‐value < 0.05 to be statistically significant.

Ethics Statement

All animal experiments were performed according to the protocol approved by the SJTU Animal Care and Use Committee and in direct accordance with the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China on Animal Care guidelines. All efforts were made to minimize suffering.

Results

IL‐33 Is Involved in the Degenerative Changes of Aging Cartilage

Given our focus on age‐related OA pathogenesis, we established a chondrocyte senescence model by serially passaging primary murine chondrocytes in vitro. To validate the progression of cellular senescence, primary (P0), fifth‐passage (P5), and tenth‐passage (P10) chondrocytes were subjected to β‐galactosidase histochemical staining. Quantitative analysis revealed a progressive increase in senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase activity with increasing passage number, indicating cumulative induction of senescence (Figure 1a). Based on the optimal balance between cell viability and senescence phenotype, sixth‐passage (P6) chondrocytes were selected for subsequent mechanobiological investigations. Notably, our experimental paradigm yielded an unexpected observation: aged cartilage specimens exhibited a marked increase in IL‐33 protein accumulation compared to young controls, as demonstrated by immunohistochemical and immunoblot analyses (Figure 1b,c). SOX9 is known to be essential for chondrogenesis and cell survival [17], while the ECM is primarily composed of aggrecan (ACAN) and type II collagen (COL‐II) [18]. RT‐PCR analysis showed significant decreases in the expression of ACAN, SOX9, and COL2A1 in P6 chondrocytes and ECM compared to P0 (Figure 1d). Based on these findings, we propose that IL‐33 may contribute to the degenerative processes associated with chondrocyte aging.

IL‐33 Upregulates the Expression of Chondrocytes Hypertrophy and Osteogenic Genes

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of IL‐33 elevation in chondrocyte–mediated matrix degradation, we administered recombinant IL‐33 (rIL‐33) to in vitro–cultured senescent chondrocytes. qRT‐PCR analysis demonstrated significant upregulation of MMP13, COL10A1, VEGF, and ALP mRNA levels in rIL‐33–treated versus untreated senescent chondrocytes (Figure 2a,d), indicating hypertrophic transformation. Given the established role of hypertrophic chondrocytes in osteophyte formation and OA pathogenesis, we further evaluated osteogenic differentiation markers following rIL‐33 stimulation. Immunoblotting revealed increased protein expression of bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4), runt‐related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), and osterix (OSX) in treated senescent chondrocytes (Figure 2b,c), supporting an IL‐33–mediated osteogenic shift. To validate these findings in vivo, intra‐articular injections of rIL‐33 were administered to 12‐month‐old wild‐type mice. Histological assessment showed exacerbated cartilage degeneration in rIL‐33–treated joints compared to PBS controls (Figure 2e), with concomitant elevation of MMP13 and RUNX2 expression confirmed by immunohistochemistry (Figure 2f). Importantly, parallel experiments in non‐senescent primary chondrocytes replicated these IL‐33–induced hypertrophic and osteogenic responses under identical conditions (Figure 2g), confirming the cytokine's conserved mechanistic role across different cellular aging states. While senescent chondrocytes alone did not initiate OA pathogenesis, they synergized with coexisting risk factors to amplify OA progression. This pathogenic model aligned with prior evidence demonstrating that mechanical overloading accelerated chondrocyte degeneration through cumulative cellular stress [19].

FSS Leads to Aging Cartilage Degeneration and Cell Matrix Degradation, and Induces the Upregulation of IL‐33 Expression in Senescent Chondrocytes

The mechanopathological significance of FSS in OA development warranted systematic investigation. Given the higher prevalence of OA in the aged population, we primarily utilized aged chondrocytes as our experimental model. To examine the effects of FSS on senescent cartilage, we applied physiologically relevant FSS magnitudes (8, 16, and 24 dyn/cm2) to P6 chondrocytes over a 4‐h stimulation period. Quantitative analysis showed that FSS dose‐dependently upregulated IL‐33 expression in senescent chondrocytes (Figure 3a). Concurrently, we observed a progressive increase in hypertrophic and osteogenic markers (ALP, MMP13, RUNX2, and OSX), along with marked suppression of cartilage‐specific genes (COL2A1, ACAN, and SOX9) (Figure 3b–e). These findings mechanistically established that FSS‐induced IL‐33 overexpression in senescent chondrocytes orchestrated dual catabolic effects: direct promotion of cellular degeneration and coordinated extracellular matrix degradation.

IL‐33 Knock out Reduces the Destruction of Articular Cartilage and Partially Rescues the Destructive Effect of FSS on Senescent Chondrocytes

Building upon prior evidence of IL‐33's role in promoting chondrocyte hypertrophy and the expression of osteogenic genes. we next investigated whether IL‐33 ablation could modulate these processes in vivo. Primary chondrocytes isolated from neonatal IL‐33 knockout (KO) mice (postnatal Days 4–5) were compared with wild‐type (WT) counterparts under hypertrophic induction. qRT‐PCR analysis showed significantly reduced expression of osteogenic regulators (OSX and BMP4) and hypertrophic markers (ALP, COL10A1, VEGF, and MMP13) in IL‐33 KO chondrocytes compared to WT controls (Figure 4a,b). When exposed to progressive FSS stimulation (8, 16, and 24 dyn/cm2 for 4 h), IL‐33‐deficient P6 chondrocytes exhibited dose‐dependent suppression of hypertrophic markers (ALP, COL10A1, and VEGF) and osteogenic transcription factors (RUNX2, OSX, and BMP4) relative to stressed WT cells (Figure 4c,d). To confirm these mechanobiological interactions in vivo, we implemented a 4‐week treadmill running protocol in 12‐month‐old WT and IL‐33 KO mice to amplify physiological articular shear stress. Post‐intervention analysis revealed elevated IL‐33 expression in exercised WT cartilage compared to sedentary controls, as quantified by immunofluorescence (Figure 4e). Histological assessment showed accelerated cartilage degeneration in exercised WT mice, characterized by proteoglycan loss and surface fibrillation (Figure 4f), accompanied by upregulated RUNX2 and MMP13 expression, consistent with in vitro findings. Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed increased RUNX2 and MMP13 in WT versus IL‐33 KO aged mice, with or without exercise (Figure 4g,h). Crucially, IL‐33 KO mice exhibited resistance to exercise‐induced cartilage deterioration, preserving matrix integrity under identical mechanical loading conditions. Complementary micro‐CT analysis provided high‐resolution quantification of subchondral bone architecture, revealing exercise‐induced alterations in trabecular bone mass and three‐dimensional microstructural organization (Figure 5a,b). Comparative analysis of sedentary WT and IL‐33 KO mice demonstrated impaired bone remodeling in the knockout group (Figure 5c), confirming IL‐33's regulatory role in physiological osteogenesis. Mechanical loading through exercise enhanced bone metabolic activity, consistent with the known anabolic effects of physical stimulation on skeletal tissue.

Our findings conclusively demonstrated that IL‐33 suppression achieved dual therapeutic effects: attenuation of senescence‐associated chondrocyte hypertrophy progression and partial mitigation of FSS‐induced degenerative changes in aged articular cartilage.

Discussion

As the global aging population increases, OA is expected to impose a substantial social and financial burden and contribute to the inefficient use of medical resources [20]. Despite its global significance, no approved treatment exists that can cure, reverse, slow, or prevent the progression of OA [21]. This is largely due to the complex and multifactorial nature of its pathogenesis, which involves the interplay of mechanical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors [22]. Research has shown that the onset and progression of OA are closely associated with chondrocyte senescence, cartilage degeneration, and ECM degradation [23]. Chondrocytes serve as both targets of external biomechanical and biochemical stimuli and key mediators of articular cartilage deterioration [24]. As one of the most mechanosensitive cell types in the human body, chondrocytes have been shown to respond to pressure, tension, and shear stress [25]. Daily activities such as walking, running, jumping, cycling, and stair climbing generate vertical hydrostatic pressure and FSS on hyaline cartilage [26]. The mechanical environment plays a critical role in regulating cellular biological activity and maintaining tissue homeostasis. Previous studies, including those by Li et al. [27] have demonstrated that prolonged unbalanced mechanical stimulation can induce cartilage damage [28]. Under such conditions, injured chondrocytes release alarmins, which trigger the recruitment of chondrogenic cells. These cells subsequently secrete large quantities of chemokines, cytokines, and degradative enzymes, promoting abnormal cartilage breakdown and joint inflammation. In this study, we identified IL‐33 as an inflammatory factor released as an alarmin during cell injury and elucidated its contribution to the pathological changes associated with OA.

Our experimental data demonstrated elevated IL‐33 expression in senescent murine chondrocytes, where its overexpression promoted senescence‐associated chondrocyte hypertrophy and ossification—both of which are critical drivers of OA progression. Building upon these findings and considering established risk factors for age‐related OA, we hypothesized that biomechanical loading through FSS may mechanistically link IL‐33 dysregulation to OA development. To investigate the effects of mechanical stimulation on senescent cartilage, we subjected aged chondrocytes to controlled FSS exposure. Notably, FSS stimulation: (1) Upregulated IL‐33 expression in senescent chondrocytes; (2) Downregulated cartilage matrix maintenance markers (COL2A1, ACAN); (3) Elevated hypertrophy‐associated markers (MMP13, COL10A1); (4) Induced IL‐33–dependent cartilage degradation, which was reversible through IL‐33 inhibition. These results led us to propose a model of nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic IL‐33 translocation in FSS‐stressed senescent chondrocytes. Experimental validation using IL‐33 KO mice showed significant suppression of FSS‐induced hypertrophic marker expression compared to wild‐type controls. Collectively, these findings established FSS as a critical biomechanical activator of IL‐33–mediated senescent chondrocyte hypertrophy, positioning IL‐33 as a central regulator of aged cartilage degeneration and a mechanistic link between abnormal mechanical loading and OA pathogenesis.

Our investigation revealed an unexpected association between IL‐33 signaling and two hallmark pathological features of OA: subchondral bone sclerosis and osteophyte formation. Microarchitectural deterioration of the subchondral bone, a recognized characteristic of OA, was found to correlate with IL‐33–mediated osteogenic programming. In FSS‐stimulated senescent chondrocytes, we observed marked upregulation of osteogenic transcription factors RUNX2 and OSX, accompanied by elevated BMP4 expression in rIL‐33–treated aged chondrocytes. These observations led us to propose a novel mechanobiological mechanism in which FSS promotes the expression of osteogenic genes in chondrocytes [29]. Emerging evidence suggests that hypertrophic chondrocytes possess inherent osteogenic potential and can transdifferentiate into bone‐forming osteoblasts. To investigate IL‐33's role in this process, we subjected IL‐33 KO and WT chondrocytes to hypertrophic induction over 21 days. Quantitative analysis showed significantly reduced expression of both osteogenic markers (OCN and BSP) and hypertrophic markers (COL10A1 and MMP13) in IL‐33–deficient cells compared to WT controls. Complementary in vivo experiments revealed accelerated articular cartilage degradation in aged mice following intra‐articular rIL‐33 administration. Notably, exercise‐challenged WT mice exhibited distinct subchondral bone microstructural changes compared to IL‐33 KO littermates, further supporting IL‐33's pivotal role in mechanically driven osteochondral remodeling.

Imaging analysis of murine knee joints revealed progressive subchondral bone remodeling during OA development, characterized by pathological bone sclerosis and osteophyte formation. Quantitative micro‐CT assessment demonstrated increased trabecular bone parameters in exercised aged WT mice compared to IL‐33 KO counterparts, including elevated trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone mineral density (BMD), and bone volume fraction (BV/TV), along with reduced trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp). These findings indicated enhanced subchondral bone anabolism in WT mice following mechanical loading, resulting in a biomechanically unfavorable environment due to aberrant bone formation. Critically, this mechanical mismatch between stiffened subchondral bone and the overlying cartilage predisposed articular surfaces to accelerated degeneration through abnormal stress distribution. Consistent with our findings, a study by Cox [30] reported that endochondral ossification under high mechanical loading led to finer structures with increased trabecular number.

A key limitation of our study is the lack of in‐depth investigation into the specific mechanisms by which IL‐33 contributes to cartilage degeneration. While the precise pathways remain to be fully elucidated, a proposed mechanism involves IL‐33 binding to its receptor ST2, inducing a conformational change and recruiting the IL‐1 receptor accessory protein (IL‐1RAcP) [31]. This receptor complex subsequently engages intracellular signaling components, including myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88), interleukin‐1 receptor–associated kinases 1 and 4 (IRAK1 and IRAK4), and TNF receptor–associated factor 6 (TRAF6), leading to downstream activation of MAP kinases and nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), thereby promoting chondrocyte proliferation and proinflammatory cytokine secretion [32]. Building on these findings, future research will aim to delineate the signaling pathways underlying IL‐33's dual roles in cartilage metabolism, with particular focus on ST2 receptor–mediated mechanisms and the spatial dynamics of IL‐33 activity (i.e., autocrine vs. paracrine signaling). Although our current study provides key insights into IL‐33–driven responses in senescent chondrocytes, the translational relevance to younger chondrocytes remains unclear. It is still unknown whether similar mechanobiological cascades involving IL‐33 contribute to the early pathogenesis of OA in juvenile cartilage. Further fundamental questions also persist: Is IL‐33's action primarily autocrine, or does it also involve paracrine signaling? What are the precise biomechanical cues that regulate its nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic translocation under shear stress? And what molecular machinery governs its release? The current understanding of IL‐33 release mechanisms—including passive necrotic leakage, active vesicular export, or regulated secretion—remains limited in the context of mechanically stressed chondrocytes. To address these gaps, future investigations will utilize advanced live‐cell imaging and optogenetic tension modulation to systematically dissect IL‐33 release dynamics. Several pathways have been proposed in other cell types: (1) In necrotic or injured cells, full‐length IL‐33 dissociates from chromatin and is released from the nucleus [33]. (2) Full‐length IL‐33 can translocate to the cytoplasm in a calcium‐dependent manner and be secreted via a gasdermin D–dependent mechanism; (3) Full‐length IL‐33 may also be cleaved into a truncated form lacking the nuclear domain, enabling nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic transfer and secretion through perforin‐2–mediated pores [34]. For example, Yamagishi et al. [35] reported that in senescent hepatic stellate cells, full‐length IL‐33 was cleaved into its active truncated form and released through gasdermin D N‐terminal pores, promoting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development. Similarly, Chen et al. [36] found that allergen protease exposure induced stress granule formation that carried IL‐33 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and facilitated its extracellular release through gasdermin D cleavage fragments. Whether IL‐33 follows similar release mechanisms in OA chondrocytes remains unknown. It is unclear if alternative pathways—such as ion channel–mediated export—may also be involved. Elucidating these mechanisms will bridge critical knowledge gaps in cartilage mechanobiology and inform the development of targeted IL‐33–based therapeutic strategies for aging‐associated joint disorders.

On the other hand, recognizing OA as a cartilage‐centered disease involving the entire joint, we also considered the effects of IL‐33 on other joint tissues, such as subchondral bone and synovial tissue, to better understand its crosstalk across joint compartments. Most studies suggest that IL‐33 exhibits anti‐osteoclastogenic and osteoanabolic properties [37]; however, others have reported IL‐33–induced reductions in osteoprotegerin expression by osteoblasts and increases in osteoclastogenic factor release, implying that IL‐33 may also promote bone resorption under inflammatory conditions [38]. This is supported by findings from Schulze et al. [39] who observed that IL‐33 expression increases during osteoblast differentiation and upregulates Tnfsf11, the gene encoding RANKL. Moreover, Il1rl1‐deficient mice displayed normal bone formation but increased bone resorption, resulting in reduced trabecular bone mass. Additionally, IL‐33 receptor expression has been detected in synovial tissue, macrophages, and activated neutrophils. Studies have shown that IL‐33 promotes neutrophil migration by activating synoviocytes and macrophages in animal models [40]. Talabot‐Ayer et al. [41] reported high IL‐33 mRNA expression in synovial tissue from OA patients, proposing that locally released IL‐33 contributes to joint inflammation by activating mast cells and neutrophils. These findings suggest that IL‐33 exerts deleterious effects across all joint compartments, with cartilage as the central target, mediating inter‐tissue crosstalk and amplifying the inflammatory milieu within the knee joint.

In summary, our study identified FSS as a critical biomechanical regulator of IL‐33 expression in senescent chondrocytes. We demonstrated that IL‐33 drives senescence‐associated chondrocyte hypertrophy and modulates osteogenic differentiation through transcriptional activation of bone‐related genes. Pharmacological inhibition of IL‐33 signaling significantly attenuated FSS‐induced cellular damage, accompanied by reduced expression of hypertrophic markers (COL10A1 and MMP13) and osteogenic regulators (RUNX2 and OSX). These findings establish IL‐33 as a central mediator of pathological cartilage remodeling, coordinating both chondrocyte hypertrophic transformation and endochondral ossification processes that contribute to subchondral bone microarchitectural deterioration. Importantly, targeted IL‐33 suppression emerges as a promising disease‐modifying strategy with therapeutic potential for mitigating age‐related OA progression. Collectively, our results define a novel mechano‐immunological axis in OA pathogenesis and provide a strong preclinical rationale for the development of IL‐33–targeted therapies aimed at preserving joint integrity in geriatric populations.
