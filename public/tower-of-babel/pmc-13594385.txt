Views of Advanced Nurse Practitioners on Their Roles and Responsibilities in Community Care for Home‐Dwelling Patients: A Qualitative Descriptive Study
Authors: Sanne Smulders, Geke Dijkstra, Anneke van Vught, Nienke Bleijenberg, Nynke Boonstra, Evelyn Finnema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore how Advanced Nurse Practitioners (ANPs) perceive and enact their role in community care and to provide insights into their role as described from their own views and experiences.DesignA qualitative, descriptive study.MethodsOnline focus group discussions. Quotations were coded inductively and categorized by group codes to account for meanings in answers, fragments and words. Introduction

Receiving the best standard of care in the most suitable setting is globally recognized as a fundamental right of every individual, reinforced by Goal 3 of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals: to ensure healthy lives and promote well‐being for all at all ages (United Nations 2017). Meanwhile, amid the global phenomenon of population aging (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division 2022), community nursing is increasingly facing considerable strain (WHO 2022).

In most countries, many older adults have expressed the desire to live independently at home for as long as possible (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division 2022). To preserve the quality, accessibility and affordability of healthcare services in the future, governments must address the challenges facing international healthcare policy in light of demographic developments (Nederlandse Zorg Autoriteit and Zorginstitiuut Nederland 2020). The substantial increase in the number of older adults and a concurrent sharp decline in the number of younger individuals in the workforce have led to labor market shortages in healthcare (International Counsil of Nurses 2022). This imbalance is further exacerbated by the substantial demand in the labor market for individuals seeking to enter the healthcare sector, known as the ‘inflow,’ alongside a high attrition rate (International Counsil of Nurses 2022). In the Netherlands, 40% of novice nurses resign from their positions within 2 years (RVS 2020). A similar phenomenon has been reported in most member states of the Organization for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD/European Commission 2024) for over a decade. Reasons vary per country; stress, low job satisfaction and low career opportunities are the most common (International Counsil of Nurses 2022). However, according to a recent Dutch report, two‐thirds of nurses who voluntarily resign from their positions remain in the healthcare sector, taking on different roles (Lang et al. 2024). Optimizing role development by stakeholders is essential when transitioning employees between roles and positions within the healthcare sector.

These realities underscore the urgent need to reconfigure the healthcare system in aging countries, as the existing framework, characterized by a shortage of healthcare professionals relative to an aging population and the mounting complexity of care, is unsustainable. The potential contributions of the Advanced Nurse Practitioner (ANP) to community nursing, which have not been thoroughly investigated, require clarification.

Background

The nurse practitioner was internationally introduced in 1965 by Loretta Ford and Henry Silver (Silver et al. 1967). The nurse practitioner (NP) role was established to enhance patient access by providing health care (Ford 1979). Presently, the role has evolved into an advanced practice with internationally recognized status at the master's degree level. A shared international understanding of the ANP role and its alignment with other healthcare roles is crucial in shaping the role within a broad international context (Thompson and McNamara 2021; Torrens et al. 2019). The increasing amount and complexity of integrated care in community health care has led to the creation of new roles for nurses, particularly ANPs (Maier et al. 2022). In the Netherlands, the Dutch Integral Care Agreement has driven change within the healthcare system toward an integral approach.

The professional identity of the ANP in relation to interprofessional collaboration and community health care is unknown. ANPs are trained according to the CanMEDS roles: medical expert, communicator, collaborator, leader, health advocate, scholar and professional (Kappert and Hoop 2019). These roles are designed to support nurses in their development as healthcare professionals, ensuring they master a wide range of skills and responsibilities required in the complex healthcare environment. Various competencies associated with these CanMEDS roles, such as leadership and organizational skills, have been investigated in relation to ANPs' roles and responsibilities, primarily in clinical settings (Kraaij et al. 2020). The work by Bryant‐Lukosius et al. (2004) has had significant implications for the ANP role in general, particularly regarding the integration of evidence‐based care and the improvement of patient care. It is well known that the role of the ANP varies internationally in its implementation. From a Dutch perspective, ANPs integrate both medical and nursing care. In the Netherlands, an ANP has prescribing authority and is qualified to independently establish and initiate both medical and nursing diagnoses and treatments. The ANP can play a key role in health care by performing both medical and nursing tasks. By gaining clarity in their professional identity and integrating evidence‐based practices into their work, ANPs can optimize their contributions to health care and support their professional development and the quality of care (Bryant‐Lukosius et al. 2004).

As demand for healthcare grows and challenges in providing home care persist, scholars have demonstrated that the role of the ANP holds promise in community care. Lovink et al. (2018) highlighted the underutilization of ANPs' potential in community care. Lovink et al. (2018) expressed concerns about the lack of a clear vision regarding the ANP's role in home‐dwelling‐based care, the structure of this role and care and the specific areas where the ANP should provide leadership. Furthermore, Lovink et al. (2018) emphasized the imperative of team performance, encompassing collaboration, trust and recognition of one another's expertise rather than competition. ANPs have been examining their role in caring for older adults living at home to optimize their scope of practice in relation to education levels and legislation. In summary, Lovink et al. (2018) highlighted the potential of the ANP role in caring for home‐dwelling patients near their homes, and this role is complemented in varied ways. The most effective approach to fulfilling it remains unclear. A scoping review (de Leede‐Brunsveld 2023) revealed that ANPs deliver highly complex care to home‐dwelling patients. This review highlighted the vital role of ANPs in community care across northwest Europe, noting strong appreciation from patients and informal caregivers. ANPs are crucial in meeting the increasing and complex care needs within home settings, positively impacting the work of other healthcare professionals (de Leede‐Brunsveld 2023). The review by de Leede‐Brunsveld (2023) served as the basis for this focus group study exploring how ANPs experience their role in community care. Recent developments have focused on strengthening primary and community care, emphasizing appropriate care at the right place. National policies highlight the crucial role of ANPs in achieving this, particularly in home care. However, the question remains: how can ANPs in community care optimally fulfil this ambition? The current literature on this topic provides insufficient insight.

The Study

Aim(s) and Objective

We began by exploring how ANPs experience their current roles within community healthcare organizations, specifically their role fulfilment. We expected to gain insights into their views and opinions regarding the exercise and expansion of this role in community care for home‐dwelling patients. This point was explored in relation to their professional identity and interprofessional collaboration. However, the role of the ANP in community care within a transforming healthcare landscape has not been clearly defined. This study aimed to explore how Advanced Nurse Practitioners (ANPs) perceive and enact their role in community care for home‐dwelling patients and to provide insights into their role as described from their own views and experiences.

Methods

Design

We employed a qualitative descriptive study design to gain a deeper understanding of ANPs' experiences in community healthcare, specifically their roles in community care, with a focus on home‐dwelling patients. We opted to conduct focus group discussions (FGDs) to foster mutual recognition and trust among participants, thereby facilitating interaction among them. Data were analysed using a thematic analysis approach informed by Colaizzi's seven‐step analytic method (Colaizzi 1978).

Sampling and Recruitment Procedure

According to a recent estimate from the Professional Association of ANPs in the Netherlands (V&VN VS), 55 ANPs are working in community care directly from a home care organization. Currently, the Netherlands has 6013 ANPs, 481 of whom are actively involved in providing primary healthcare, which encompasses general practitioner (GP) settings and healthcare centers. This number is expected to rise, and the Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport supports the potential of the ANP in community care. Therefore, our study required recruiting participants from across the country to identify areas that necessitated accurate role fulfilment.

We conducted purposive recruitment of participating ANPs by sending invitations to the networks for ANPs in primary and community healthcare, as well as through the professional association for ANPs in the Netherlands (V&VN VS). All ANPs hold a master's degree. The sole inclusion criterion was employment as an ANP in general practice (GP) settings, healthcare centers or community healthcare with a clear focus on the care of home‐dwelling patients.

Data Collection

We conducted three focus group discussions (FGDs) with ANPs in November and December 2023 in the Netherlands. Two of the FGDs were facilitated by A. van Vught and one by N. Bleijenberg as chairperson, being an experienced researcher and two observers, all of whom were research team members. We used a list of relevant topics distilled from previous research and a review of the literature (de Leede‐Brunsveld 2023; Lovink et al. 2018). They had been formulated previously by the research group. Starting with an introduction, the first topic addressed the following question: What is your current role as an ANP in community care? The chairperson led the discussion, and an observer took notes to ensure that all questions were addressed and monitored throughout the duration. Each FGD lasted strictly 120 min. The FGDs were conducted online, using a closed Microsoft (MS) Teams environment. The online MS Teams environment was chosen to minimize travel time for the participating ANPs. The number of focus groups was primarily determined by practical considerations. Across the three focus groups, participants' responses were rich and consistent, with the final group yielding no substantially new insights. Therefore, three focus groups were considered sufficient to address the study aim, resulting in a sufficiently rich and comprehensive dataset.

Data Analysis

To provide a comprehensive description of participants' experiences and perspectives while remaining closely grounded in the data, Colaizzi's seven‐step method was used as a systematic approach to data analysis. Although originally developed in phenomenological research, the seven‐step method was applied in this study as a structured analytical procedure to facilitate the systematic identification, organization and description of participants' experiences. The overarching methodological framework remained qualitative description throughout the study. During the coding process and throughout the data analysis phase, we conducted peer debriefings at each stage of Colaizzi's analysis method (Table 1). We systematically compared our findings with existing literature to bolster the credibility of our research. We thoroughly described each identified theme in the findings section, accompanied by relevant quotes to illustrate and substantiate our observations. Participants' quotations were coded inductively (open) and further categorized using group codes to capture significant meanings in answers, fragments and words. Considerable overlap in participant perspectives was observed across the groups during analysis, indicating that informational redundancy had been reached. We identified the group codes in four distinct role‐related themes: the Integrated Care Provider, the Innovator, the Professional and the Networker. The two principal researchers (S. Smulders & G. Dijkstra) ensured bracketing during the data analysis phase by maintaining a reflective log. To prepare a summary independently generated from a verbatim transcript, we formed two subgroups (S. Smulders, G. Dijkstra & N. Bleijenberg, A. van Vught) that analysed and coded the findings separately before integrating them, thereby ensuring the requisite degree of detachment from the results. Although some quotations reflected multiple themes, they were assigned to the theme that best captured their primary meaning. The emerging themes and their underlying significant meanings were reviewed and discussed by the research team, and thematic classification was established through consensus. The themes represent distinct but interconnected aspects of the ANP role.

Rigour

According to established evaluations of credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability in qualitative research (Guba and Lincoln 1989), we conducted peer debriefing with members of the research group throughout the study process. The two leading researchers S.Smulders & G. Dijkstra prepared a reflexive logbook and performed data analysis, coding and comparing two transcripts. Member check was provided by sending a summary of the data to the participants (by A. van Vught & S. Smulders). We maintained regular contact with the participants and their representatives whenever possible. Confirmability was achieved by illustrating the themes using quotes from the participants' responses. Comprehensive descriptions of the data collection process, participant characteristics and the research setting were provided to enable readers to evaluate the transferability of the findings to other contexts. We described the outcomes and implications of the data in accordance with the 32‐item checklist of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (Tong et al. 2007). The completed COREQ checklist is provided as Appendix S1.

Ethical Considerations

The study did not fall within the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) and was therefore considered as non‐WMO research by the Ethics Review Committee of HAN University of Applied Sciences, Nijmegen, the Netherlands (October 23, 2023) and by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the University Medical Center Groningen, University of Groningen, Groningen, the Netherlands (no. M24.331227, April 22, 2024). The FGDs were recorded in audio format. Verbatim transcripts were generated from these recordings to facilitate further analysis. Participants were informed both verbally and in writing about the purpose of the research, along with the pertinent elements of participation, including the use of recordings, confidentiality, voluntary participation, the possibility of withdrawing from participation at any time and their consent regarding the handling of recordings and the treatment of their data. Participants did not receive financial compensation for their participation in the study. Additionally, we obtained informed consent for participation in the FGD through an informed consent form. To ensure the anonymity of the participants, we did not include names, organizations or identifiable information in the generated transcripts. After transcribing, we deleted the recordings. The transcripts were retained in accordance with international data storage regulations, which stipulate a retention period of 15 years at the University Medical Center Groningen, University of Groningen, Groningen, the Netherlands. They are accessible only to the researchers.

Findings

Participants

Our study included 27 general health care ANPs as participants (25 females and 2 males). They worked for different healthcare organizations with a specific focus on community care (Table 2). The three FGDs consisted of 10, 6 and 11 participants, respectively. None of the participants dropped out prior to or during the focus group discussions (FGDs). Most participants showed a strong accent in their work on aging home‐dwelling patients (aged 65 years and older). We identified four role‐related themes: the Integrated Care Provider, the Innovator, the Professional and the Networker. These themes identified one main role: the Hybrid Professional (HP). The four themes were inductively developed through iterative analysis of participants' accounts and were not predefined. They were analytically distinguished based on their distinct meanings, while some conceptual overlap was acknowledged and their interrelationships became apparent through the integration process. This integration resulted in the Hybrid Professional (HP) framework, an analytical model grounded in participants' perceptions and experiences rather than one imposed a priori. All findings and the resulting framework should therefore be interpreted as participants' perceptions and experiences, not as objective evidence of the superiority or effectiveness of Advanced Nurse Practitioners (ANPs).

Integrated Care Provider

Most participants reported that the central aspect of their daily practice involved caring for older home‐dwelling patients and their families. The participants emphasized the importance of evaluating the care process and results in collaboration with patients and their families, as well as assessing the social network surrounding the patient. The participants highlighted the importance of the feeling of presence, close proximity and the provision of accessible healthcare for home‐dwelling patients as essential aspects of the ANP. Most participants reported having more time for the patient compared to other healthcare professionals, such as General Practitioners (GPs). However, they explicitly emphasized that having more time was not the primary goal of their role.

I have a little more time for the patient than the GP, geriatric specialist, or community nurse, and I think I see the patient as a whole. However, I also strive to understand the entire social environment of the patient, enabling me to provide more effective care at the individual level, whether medical or nursing. Moreover, I find that very, very valuable. I think that is what sets us apart as nurse practitioners. (P11)

Providing advanced clinical care with a strong foundation in evidence‐based practice and medicine anchored in patient‐centered care strongly underlined the theme of integrated care providers. Most of the participants highlighted the biopsychosocial model when viewing each patient. One participant explained integrated care as having a ‘helicopter view,’ which they also explained by connecting the four domains in their daily practice: medical, nursing, psychological and social.

It's not just about more time; it's also about the ability to have a helicopter view and think from the four domains. That is really a characteristic of us. (P20)

Several participants expressed that integrated care was a very meaningful and continuous factor in providing effective care. They emphasized that patients could get to know them better as the ANP took the lead in providing integrated care. The stability in the care provided, close proximity and the fact that they did not work in different shifts made them a consistent factor compared to community nurses who worked in different shifts. One participant (P9) mentioned working independently in evening, night and weekend shifts to replace the GP at the GP emergency ward. This setup was made possible by the independent authority endowed on the ANP by Dutch law.

The participants spoke of visiting their patients, not of consulting or paying a visit. According to the participants, this characteristic marked a fundamental difference from the GP, geriatric specialist and community nurse. If a patient was too ill to visit the GP, the GP could arrange a home visit. To request this type of home visit, the patient needed to contact the GP's assistant by telephone. A community nurse would routinely visit several times a week and operate within a strictly defined timeframe to provide fundamental nursing care. The difference was that the ANP acted from a professional, master's, advanced expertise based on the patient's needs in an integrated manner, combining social, medical, nursing and psychological factors in a proactive approach. Their choice to visit the individual at home was based on the patient's context to facilitate integrated care. The participants mentioned that community nurses were responsible for the organizational tasks related to nursing care in the home setting and worked with nursing diagnoses. In the case of dementia, both the GP and ANP would decide to consult a community nurse and arrange a multidisciplinary consultation. In some cases, the GP was not directly involved in the ‘medical part’ as indicated. In this type of situation, the ANP directed the nursing and medical (indicated) parts without any intervention by a GP. One participant described that, in a Parkinson's disease case, the ANP was responsible for providing integrated care across community and hospital settings, including the prescription of medication.

In multidisciplinary consultations, GPs, geriatric specialists, CNs and ANPs reinforced the integrated approach by discussing different patient cases weekly alongside other healthcare professionals. The participants stated that ANPs did not require referrals. Care was allocated according to severity, differential diagnosis and client needs. According to the participants, the multidisciplinary setup varied depending on the needed expertise. The ANP often led this meeting. The participants mentioned that most GPs were satisfied with working with the ANP once they understood the scope of work they were allowed to do. The participants reported observing more scepticism on the side of the medical specialists when a GP or other medical specialist was not familiar with the ANP role.

Innovator

The innovator theme emerged from the participants' innovation‐driven aspects and projects. The participants related different patient cases to their inventive and creative conduct. Table 3 provides examples of the participants' innovative approaches to supportive care by informal caregivers of clients with Parkinson's disease. A few participants mentioned the pioneering role of the ANP. Most participants spoke of a transcendent perspective on the best care, noting that they could independently initiate (acute) care from a broad and in‐depth role, with the authority to make specialized and medical decisions—a unique strength of the ANP. The participants underscored that this characteristic was not only at the individual level, in the direct surroundings of the patient (organizational level), but also at the transactional level. Connected to various network projects, ANPs met with colleagues to establish new projects and plans for building and strengthening the integrated community healthcare environment. They mentioned taking a leading role in formulating strategies for project implementation.

We are trying to outline what else the community needs in terms of healthcare in order to implement integrated community care properly. I think that ANPs have a transcendent view of that. (P4)

Professional

Most participants described their inner drive and compassion to improve individuals' lives. To enhance patients' quality of life, they sought to support and optimize ‘a lot that is going on behind the front door’ (P16). Several other participants supported this quote, stating that ANPs must be flexible in a range of challenging situations. Protocols or guidelines may be unhelpful, and the ANPs need to respond quickly and creatively. ANPs can rely on a foundation of knowledge, which allows them to diverge from standard protocols to provide the best‐suited care:

In our profession, precisely the nursing part that we take into account, we not only work from guidelines and optimize medication but also look for innovation in how you get the quality of my patient's lives better and what is important that can influence the daily lives of my patients, to make that as bearable as possible and to see people much more separately from the disease but as humans. (P14)

Several participants described the importance of being the central figure in providing integrated care. ANPs are equipped with this overview. This aspect was strongly demonstrated in the FGDs. Accessible contact was a fundamental outcome of the FGDs in general. A participant working for a care group organization described this as follows:

We provide reliability, safety, and continuity by serving as a single point of contact. You know the client, and I think that is a very valuable addition to our practice. (P11)

Most participants also described the importance of professionalism in expanding their knowledge of other healthcare professionals in the medical domain, such as general practitioners (GPs) and medical specialists. ANPs transfer knowledge from the different situations and cases they encounter daily. Most of the interviewed ANPs were experienced nurses before becoming ANPs. Their primary motivation for pursuing an educational trajectory toward becoming an ANP was to gain more knowledge and independence in their daily work. P13 illustrated this with an example of bedside teaching to other healthcare professionals:

That aspect of quality improvement that you can take with you to other places where you provide bedside teaching was a factor. I think of pieces of knowledge that I could constantly transfer. (P13)

All the participants expressed pride in their nursing backgrounds. According to one, ‘I do not have to be a doctor; having a nursing background is so valuable’ (P11). Their nursing knowledge enables them to assess risks and consider complications related to case complexity. Participants in the second FGD mentioned that GPs sometimes have a narrow view of a patient's medical condition, resulting in a limited understanding of the patient as a whole person. The participants in this FGD indicated that, in some instances, they feel the need to intervene to ensure a human (biopsychosocial)‐oriented focus rather than an organ‐ or disease‐oriented one.

Networker

The participating ANPs characterized themselves as connecting figures in the community network of individual patients. Noting this network, several participants mentioned that it prevents unnecessary care and hospital admissions. A participant in GP reflected on this type of prevention and on the role of serving as a link among different healthcare professionals. Notably, although they might supervise others, they did not refer to being in a directing role.

I think that is also characteristic of me. I am mainly a networker, lowering thresholds but also preventing hospital admissions. That does not always work, but I am also the link to geriatrics and rehabilitation, for example, in the second line or at the community clinic, so that someone can be temporarily admitted and then return home. I am there on the way to and back. I also supervise other healthcare professionals. (P3)

Hybrid Professional

The participants described the current primary characteristics of the ANP role in community healthcare.

The ‘in‐between’ seems the most suitable term to describe their general position. The participants viewed themselves as working as liaisons between the medical and nursing domains. As one participant stated, ‘Oh, what characterizes me? I think I'm not a doctor, and I'm no longer a community nurse. I'm an ANP, so I'm in between, I think’ (P16).

By analysing the data and extracting themes, our study explored views on the precise nature of the current ANP role as intermediaries directing the functions of care providers within community healthcare settings. From the participants' perspectives, an Advanced Nurse Practitioner (ANP) within a community healthcare setting is a hybrid professional who serves as a liaison between the nursing and medical domains, drawing on aspects of both, resulting in an elevated level of performance that creates a distinct domain of its own. ANPs in community healthcare settings can utilize a so‐called helicopter view when applying integrated community nursing care. As described by the participants, this combination of comprehensive biopsychosocial care and a key coordinating role was not perceived as being provided by either a GP or a (community) nurse. For example, the fact that an ANP from a community healthcare setting can describe medication, evaluate the situation over time, work with the client and other informal caregivers and act from a nursing perspective is unique. Taken together, these dimensions provide the basis for an emerging HP framework that captures the Hybrid Professional role as described by participants. From their perspective, its added value lies not in its comparability with other professional roles, but in the new expertise that emerges from combining these dimensions. Rather than representing a definitive model, the framework offers an initial analytical representation of the Hybrid Professional role identified in this study and a basis for further investigation.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to explore ANPs' perspectives on their current role in community care for home‐dwelling patients in the Netherlands. The insights into their experiences and distillation of the fulfilment of their role characteristics helped distinguish four different roles that are primarily aligned with the CanMEDS roles of professional, leader, health advocate, expert and collaborator. Notably, the participants did not explicitly refer to the CanMEDS scholar role. However, several examples described by participants, such as innovation in care delivery, quality improvement activities and teaching, reflected competencies associated with the scholar role. These activities appeared to be embedded within their daily practice rather than being recognized or articulated as a distinct role. This might be because we asked them directly about their current role within community care, which could have steered their answers toward patient care. The primary role distilled from the data analysis—the Hybrid Professional—reflects the current liaison, or, in their own words, the ‘intermediate’ position of the ANP in community healthcare. The identified themes should therefore not be viewed as mutually exclusive categories, but as complementary dimensions that together characterize the ANP as a hybrid professional operating across clinical, organizational and network boundaries. This is evident in the term ‘job‐crafting,’ which refers to the way employees adapt their jobs by actively modifying their tasks, relationships or approach to their work. The job‐crafting function refers to how employees tailor their work to their own capabilities, knowledge, preferences and goals (Dubbelt et al. 2019).

Our study showed that job‐crafting occurs at the individual ANP level, where they experience the extension of knowledge and autonomy in practice. Van Offenbeek and Leverment (2004) demonstrated similar role‐related outcomes in shaping the professional identity of ANPs: ‘These particular professionals might represent a special brand of people who have particular ways of crafting their job and constructing their professional identity, regardless of the structural context in which they find themselves working’ Thus, ANPs can tailor their job to different contexts and specialized situations, such as working at night in an emergency ward, a GP practice or at a client's home in collaboration with a home care or nursing home organization. Van Offenbeek and Leverment (2004), in comparing the situations in Great Britain and the Netherlands, highlighted that ANPs can shape their work and form their identities through negotiations with proactive, supportive and constructive managers and colleagues. Our research revealed diversity among ANPs in how they exercise their roles, in line with de Leede‐Brunsveld (2023), who advocated for a clear job profile and improved collaboration with other healthcare professionals. Notably, the implementation of the ANP role in community care is in development. A well‐defined job profile is crucial for addressing the lack of clear role definitions, which can lead to role ambiguity and the underutilization of Advanced Nurse Practitioners (ANPs) in older adult care (Lovink et al. 2018). Thus, the healthcare system must have a broader recognition of the role of ANPs in community healthcare and among other healthcare professionals.

The independent authority of the ANP, endowed by Dutch law and their job‐crafting competencies, puts them in a position to direct integrated care (Region, W. H. 2023). Our findings emphasized that ANPs believed they could provide integrated and appropriate care for home‐dwelling patients due to their unique position. They are embedded in the medical, nursing, psychological and social environment of the patients and their families. From the psychosocial aspect, Wallenburg et al. (2015) reported that ANPs in secondary and tertiary settings innovate care by strengthening psychosocial support for patients and their families. Our participants also mentioned the importance of psychosocial aspects in the context of their care provision. The ability to intervene in the patient's social network makes ANPs accessible. This finding is consistent with the findings of multiple studies, in which healthcare provided by ANPs is associated with higher patient satisfaction, lower overall mortality and fewer hospital admissions (Dierick‐van Daele et al. 2009; van Dusseldorp 2018). Simultaneously, an ANP depends on a robust interprofessional network. Their effectiveness is maximized when they function as a pivotal link among various domains. Overall, innovating care, as already mentioned by Bryant‐Lukosius et al. (2004) and shaping the professional ANP identity through job‐crafting are in progress, as distilled from the responses of ANPs in community care who participated in this study.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

A strength of the study is its use of FGDs, which enabled an in‐depth understanding of the current role from the ANPs' perspective. The participants were recruited from various regions and counties in the Netherlands, resulting in a diverse group of professionals with varied backgrounds, tasks and perspectives. They had various years of experience as ANPs in community care. All participants worked in a community healthcare setting, providing care to home‐dwelling patients. Another strength of the study is that it introduced nurse practitioners from across the country through the focus group discussions (FGDs).

Several limitations must be considered. Participants were recruited on a voluntary basis, which may have resulted in a sample of particularly motivated ANPs. The participating ANPs could apply independently, which may suggest that they have a greater affinity for the studied theme than their non‐participating colleagues in the field. As the findings are based on self‐reported experiences, they should be interpreted as perceptions of practice rather than objective evidence of role effectiveness. The duration of the FGDs also posed a challenge, as accommodating all participants may have limited the depth of probing. Another limitation is the risk of social desirability bias in participants' answers. Although participants were invited to provide feedback through member checking, no feedback was received, limiting the opportunity to verify the researchers' interpretations, while credibility was supported through iterative analysis and discussion among multiple researchers. Furthermore, while the findings may be relevant beyond the Dutch context, their transferability should be considered in light of the relatively high level of autonomy and the specific professional positioning of ANPs within the Dutch healthcare system.

Recommendations for Further Research

Based on the findings of our study, future research should focus on the ANP domain in community care as a recent development for the current role of the ANP in general. The specific framework of this domain and the role of ANPs in community care must be investigated further, as they are considered pioneering professionals. They work within their own practice setting. That is, community health care lacks a unified vision of the precise nature of ANPs' tasks, responsibilities and roles. According to the participating ANPs, professional network programs in community healthcare could help elucidate the potential impact of ANPs' role in community care, support their network‐based competencies, empower job crafting and position the role.

Our study provides information to clarify the role and, consequently, strengthen the professional identity of ANPs. ANPs must support one another and work together to develop their profession by forming professional networks. Other healthcare professionals, management and policymakers should proactively support the role ANPs can play in community care. We recommend that further research be conducted on ANPs, who partly shape their work and identities through dialogue and comparison with other healthcare professionals, to increase recognition of the ANP role. A proposed impact study could focus on evaluating the role of ANPs in community care and examining how their involvement contributes to achieving national healthcare goals, such as appropriate care and care at the right place. Furthermore, it is essential to research education and practice, examining how the role can be effectively integrated into curricula.

Conclusion

Our study identified four role‐related characteristics that formed one primary role: the Hybrid Professional. ANPs fulfil various roles in community healthcare, partly in line with the CanMEDS roles. The optimal fulfilment of the ANP role in an interprofessional team contains multiple unexplained issues, such as collaboration and the adoption of a leading or supporting role, depending on the situation. Our study contributed to the body of knowledge on the professional development of ANPs by exploring their current roles in community healthcare for home‐dwelling patients. Since the ANP position in Dutch community healthcare is relatively new, ANPs primarily work within their own practice setting in various roles. The field lacks a unified vision of the ANP's exact role, tasks and responsibilities in community healthcare. Further research is recommended to support the development of a shared vision on the role of the ANP in community healthcare, with particular emphasis on the care of older adults living at home.
