Non-Gaussian states via pump-depleted SPDC Preprint: APS/123-QED
Colin Vendromin, Samuel E. Fontaine, J. E. Sipe

Abstract
We develop a model for non-Gaussian state generation via spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) in InGaP microring resonators. The nonlinear Hamiltonian is written in terms of the asymptotic fields for the system, which includes a phantom channel to handle scattering loss. The full ket for the system is written as a Gaussian unitary acting on a residual non-Gaussian ket, which is vacuum initially and evolves according to a non-Gaussian Hamiltonian. We show that for realistic parameters we can access the pump depletion regime, where the Wigner function for the residual non-Gaussian ket has negativity. But we find that the non-Gaussian features for the full ket could be unobservable due to the large amount of squeezing required to lead to pump depletion. We show that a potential solution in the low-loss regime is to implement an inverse Gaussian unitary on the accessible modes to remove most of the squeezing and reveal the non-Gaussian features. This work provides a foundation for modeling pump-depleted SPDC in integrated lossy microring resonators, opening a path toward a scalable on-chip non-Gaussian source.



I
Introduction

Spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) is a widely used process in which a high-energy photon is converted into a pair of lower-energy photons via the second-order nonlinear susceptibility (
\chi^{(2)}
) of a medium. Heralded single photons
Hong and Mandel 1986
, entangled photon pairs
Kwiat et al. 1995
, and squeezed states of light
Wu et al. 1986
;
Schnabel 2016
can all be generated using SPDC, with applications across many quantum technologies, including quantum communication
Gisin and Thew 2007
;
Halder et al. 2008
, quantum key distribution
Gisin et al. 2002
;
Zhong et al. 2015
, and continuous-variable quantum computing
Menicucci et al. 2006
;
Yokoyama et al. 2013
.

Indium gallium phosphide (InGaP) is a promising material to enhance SPDC efficiency due to its large
\chi^{(2)}
coefficients
Thiel et al. 2024
. For example, InGaP microring resonators have recently demonstrated high conversion efficiencies on the order of
10^{5}\,\%/{\rm W}
with on-chip power on the order of
1\,\mu{\rm W}

Zhao and Fang 2022
;
Akin et al. 2024
. Higher efficiency enables stronger squeezing and the potential for non-Gaussian states generated by pump depletion
Flórez et al. 2020
;
Jankowski et al. 2024
;
Yanagimoto et al. 2024
. Non-Gaussian states are a key resource for continuous-variable quantum computing, but they are typically generated probabilistically
Aghaee Rad et al. 2025
. Achieving a deterministic non-Gaussian source in a scalable, on-chip source would represent a crucial step toward scalable quantum computing architectures.

The combination of low pump power and high efficiency in InGaP microring resonators could allow
access to the pump-depletion regime. Pump depletion from SPDC has already been observed in periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN) waveguide systems
Flórez et al. 2020
. In these systems the generated photon number saturates in the depletion regime, and deviates from the exponential growth expected for purely Gaussian states. These observations suggest the generation of non-Gaussian states, consistent with theoretical modelling

Yanagimoto et al. 2022
. Demonstrations of pump depletion from second harmonic generation have also been demonstrated in a number of lithium niobate platforms, including PPLN
Park et al. 2022
and thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN)
Zhao et al. 2020
waveguides, and in lithium niobate microrings
Du et al. 2024
.

Recent theoretical work has begun to characterize the non-Gaussian states that emerge in the pump-depletion regime with models focused on PPLN waveguides
Yanagimoto et al. 2022
. In these models about
-20\,{\rm dB}
of squeezing is predicted from SPDC with only 200 pump photons, corresponding to roughly 10
\%
pump depletion for a
1.4\,{\rm cm}
crystal length, assuming advanced dispersion engineering. In such cases non-Gaussian features in the Wigner function appear along with the Gaussian ones characterizing
squeezing, but if the squeezing is too large the non-Gaussian features are overwhelmed

Yanagimoto et al. 2024
, which is often the case when generating non-Gaussian states via pump depletion. Compared to microring models, waveguide models seem to achieve pump depletion with lower amounts of squeezing. However, even this lower amount of squeezing (around
-20
dB) obscures the non-Gaussian features in the Wigner function, practically rendering these features inaccessible.
In addition, waveguide sources lack the compactness and scalability of integrated microrings. Theoretical work on the pump-depletion regime in microring resonators remains largely unexplored, particularly when accounting for multiple modes and scattering loss.

Reaching the depletion regime in microring models requires substantially more pump photons and higher squeezing than in the previous PPLN waveguide model
Yanagimoto et al. 2022
. Higher squeezing makes the Gaussian features dominate, and the
non-Gaussianity becomes even harder to detect. Instead of relying on sources with lower squeezing to observe the non-Gaussianity, when scattering losses are sufficiently small a possible strategy would be to employ
a squeezed state interferometer
Caves 2020
.
This would effectively apply an inverse Gaussian unitary on the accessible modes, removing the Gaussian features from the state and isolating its interesting non-Gaussian features.

In this work we develop a multimode model of SPDC in InGaP microring resonators that is capable of describing non-Gaussian state generation in the pump-depletion regime. Our primary focus is on InGaP, but the model we present is readily adaptable for different
\chi^{(2)}
materials. The Hamiltonian is written in terms of the asymptotic fields for the system, which includes a phantom channel to describe
scattering loss
Liscidini et al. 2012
;
Quesada et al. 2022
. The full ket for the system is written as a Gaussian unitary acting on a residual non-Gaussian ket
Yanagimoto et al. 2022
, where we derive an effective Hamiltonian that governs the evolution of the non-Gaussian ket. By writing the effective Hamiltonian in terms of supermodes, we are able to perform efficient numerical simulations for the non-Gaussian ket with only a few dominant supermodes.

Using realistic parameters we calculate the squeezing level of the signal and idler fields, the amount of pump-depletion, and the Wigner function for the non-Gaussian ket. We show that under high levels of squeezing the non-Gaussian features in the Wigner function for the full ket indeed become obscured,
but that if the scattering loss is sufficiently small the non-Gaussian features can be restored by applying an inverse Gaussian unitary on the accessible modes. This unitary effectively
stretches
the full ket to remove the squeezing, isolating the non-Gaussian features.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec
II
we introduce the fields and Hamiltonian for the microring resonator system, including loss. In Sec.
III
we then present
the solution of the Schrödinger equation for the full ket for the system, where we separate its evolution into Gaussian and non-Gaussian contributions. In Sec.
IV
we first present results for the Gaussian part of the ket, including squeezing and the conversion efficiency of signal and idler photons, followed by results for the Wigner function of the non-Gaussian ket. We conclude that section with a discussion of how to isolate the non-Gaussian features of the full ket when there is loss. Finally, in Sec.
V
we finish with
a conclusion and outlook.

(39)

and the second moments are defined as

\displaystyle[\bm{M}_{SI}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi_G(t)}a_{S\mu_{1}}a_{I\mu_{2}}\ket{\psi_G(t)}

\displaystyle=[\bm{W}_{SI}(t)\bm{V}^{\rm T}_{II}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}},

(40)

\displaystyle[\bm{N}_{SS}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi_G(t)}a^{\dagger}_{S\mu_{1}}a_{S\mu_{2}}\ket{\psi_G(t)}

\displaystyle=[\bm{W}^{*}_{SI}(t)\bm{W}^{\rm T}_{SI}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}},

(41)

\displaystyle[\bm{N}_{II}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi_G(t)}a^{\dagger}_{I\mu_{1}}a_{I\mu_{2}}\ket{\psi_G(t)}

\displaystyle=[\bm{W}^{*}_{IS}(t)\bm{W}^{\rm T}_{IS}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}.

(42)

These moments can be partitioned into into
2\times 2
block matrices involving the four different combinations of output channels

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{SI}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{M}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{SI}(t)&\bm{M}^{\rm ac\,ph}_{SI}(t)\\
\bm{M}^{\rm ph\,ac}_{SI}(t)&\bm{M}^{\rm ph\,ph}_{SI}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(43)

\displaystyle\bm{N}_{JJ}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{N}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{JJ}(t)&\bm{N}^{\rm ac\,ph}_{JJ}(t)\\
\bm{N}^{\rm ph\,ac}_{JJ}(t)&\bm{N}^{\rm ph\,ph}_{JJ}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(44)

for
J\in\{S,I\}
. The block matrices labeled by
(\rm ac\,ac)
and
(\rm ph\,ph)
account for cases where both signal and idler are in the actual output and phantom output channels, respectively, while those labeled by
(\rm ac\,ph)
and
(\rm ph\,ac)
account for cases where the signal and idler pairs are split across the two output channels.

Given
\bm{x}(t)
,
\bm{M}_{SI}(t)
,
\bm{N}_{SS}(t)
, and
\bm{N}_{II}(t)
, we obtain a complete description of the Gaussian dynamics. We emphasize that this solution fully describes non-degenerate squeezing of the signal and idler fields for arbitrary pump energies, including pump depletion and scattering loss.

III.2
Non-Gaussian evolution

We now turn our attention to the non-Gaussian dynamics. Given that the Gaussian parameters
\bm{x}(t)
,
\bm{V}_{SS}(t)
,
\bm{V}_{II}(t)
,
\bm{W}_{SI}(t)
, and
\bm{W}_{IS}(t)
are solutions to the differential Eqs. (
31
)–(
33
) this guarantees that all the Gaussian terms in Eq. (
27
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
cancel, leaving only the non-Gaussian terms (see Appendix
B
)

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime}}\hbar[\Lambda^{*}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle\times\big([\bm{V}_{SS}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{V}_{II}(t)]_{\mu_{2}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a_{I\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a_{S\mu_{1}^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle+[\bm{W}_{SI}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{V}_{II}(t)]_{\mu_{2}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{I\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a_{I\mu_{2}^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle+[\bm{V}_{SS}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{W}_{IS}(t)]_{\mu_{2}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{S\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a_{S\mu_{1}^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle+[\bm{W}_{SI}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{W}_{IS}(t)]_{\mu_{2}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{I\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{S\mu_{2}^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}\big)

\displaystyle+{\rm H.c.}.

(45)

Given that Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
only contains non-Gaussian terms, Eq. (
26
) for the residual Schrödinger equation yields a
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
that describes only the non-Gaussian features of the light.

Before obtaining a numerical solution for the non-Gaussian ket
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
using Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
we seek a perturbative Dyson series solution under the assumption that the effect of
H_{\rm eff}(t)
acting on the vacuum is small. Only keeping up to the first-order term in the Dyson series, the ket can be approximately written as

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle\approx\ket{{\rm vac}}+|\tilde{\psi}_{1}(t)\rangle,

(46)

where we have defined the first-order correction to the ket as

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}_{1}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{\mu^{\prime}_{1},\mu^{\prime}_{2},\mu_{3}}[\mathcal{O}(t)]_{\mu^{\prime}_{1}\mu^{\prime}_{2}\mu_{3}}a^{\dagger}_{I\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{S\mu_{2}^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}\ket{{\rm vac}},

(47)

where

\displaystyle[\mathcal{O}(t)]_{\mu^{\prime}_{1}\mu^{\prime}_{2}\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}[\Lambda^{*}(t^{\prime})]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle\times[\bm{W}_{SI}(t^{\prime})]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{W}_{IS}(t^{\prime})]_{\mu_{2}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}.

(48)

The perturbative solution in Eq. (
46
) is valid as long as the norm
of the correction is much less than unity for all
t
,

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{1}(t)|\tilde{\psi}_{1}(t)\rangle\ll 1\,\,\,\rightarrow\,\,\,\sum_{\mu^{\prime}_{1},\mu^{\prime}_{2},\mu_{3}}\left|[\mathcal{O}(t)]_{\mu^{\prime}_{1}\mu^{\prime}_{2}\mu_{3}}\right|^{2}\ll 1.

(49)

The Gaussian approximation would be exact were the norm
of the correction
zero; a nonzero norm
indicates
a deviation from the Gaussian regime. As the value of the norm becomes significant,
the perturbative non-Gaussian solution (
46
) should not be trusted either. This occurs when the generation of signal and idler photons is very efficient, as we
show in Sec.
IV
.

When

\langle\tilde{\psi}_{1}(t)|\tilde{\psi}_{1}(t)\rangle

becomes non-negligible, we must move beyond the perturbative solution (
46
) and look for a numerical solution of Eq. (
26
) for
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
. However, it is numerically impractical to obtain
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
using Eq. (
III.2
), where

H_{\rm eff}(t)
is written in terms of asymptotic field operators. A more practical method
is to express
H_{\rm eff}(t)
in a basis of supermodes that can be truncated to a few that are dominant.
This strategy has been investigated in previous work
Yanagimoto et al. 2022
in the context of degenerate squeezing in PPLN; in what follows we adapt this strategy to identify the dominant supermodes in our microring system.

We identify the dominant supermodes based on the heuristic assumption that non-Gaussian features are concentrated in supermodes with substantial squeezing. The information about the squeezed state is contained in the matrices
\bm{V}_{SS}(t)
,
\bm{V}_{II}(t)
,
\bm{W}_{SI}(t)
, and
\bm{W}_{IS}(t)
. To extract the modes that are most squeezed we perform the following joint singular value decomposition (SVD) of the matrices
Houde et al. 2024

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{SS}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{S}(t)\cosh[\bm{r}(t)]\bm{G}_{S}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{II}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{F}^{*}_{I}(t)\cosh[\bm{r}(t)]\bm{G}^{*}_{I}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{SI}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{S}(t)\sinh[\bm{r}(t)]\bm{G}_{I}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{IS}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{F}^{*}_{I}(t)\sinh[\bm{r}(t)]\bm{G}^{*}_{S}(t),

(50)

where
\bm{F}_{S}(t)
,
\bm{F}_{I}(t)
,
\bm{G}_{S}(t)
, and
\bm{G}_{I}(t)
are unitary matrices,
\bm{r}(t)={\rm diag}(r_{1}(t),r_{2}(t),\ldots)
is a diagonal matrix, where
r_{u}(t)\geq 0
is the squeezing amplitude for supermode
u
. Here we can write
\cosh[\bm{r}(t)]={\rm diag}(\cosh[r_{1}(t)],\cosh[r_{2}(t)],\ldots)
and
\sinh[\bm{r}(t)]={\rm diag}(\sinh[r_{1}(t)],\sinh[r_{2}(t)],\ldots)
. Putting Eq. (
III.2
) for the joint SVD into Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
we obtain

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}}\,\sum_{\mu_{3},\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime}}\hbar[\bm{L}(t)]_{\mu_{3}uu^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}\Big(\cosh[r_{u}(t)]\cosh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)][\bm{G}^{*}_{I}(t)]_{u^{\prime}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a_{I\mu_{2}^{\prime}}[\bm{G}_{S}(t)]_{u\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a_{S\mu_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\sinh[r_{u}(t)]\cosh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)][\bm{G}_{I}(t)]_{u^{\prime}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{I\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{G}^{*}_{I}(t)]_{u\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a_{I\mu_{2}^{\prime}}+\cosh[r_{u}(t)]\sinh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)][\bm{G}^{*}_{S}(t)]_{u^{\prime}\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{S\mu_{2}^{\prime}}[\bm{G}_{S}(t)]_{u\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a_{S\mu_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\sinh[r_{u}(t)]\sinh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)][\bm{G}_{I}(t)]_{u^{\prime}\mu_{1}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{I\mu_{1}^{\prime}}[\bm{G}^{*}_{S}(t)]_{u\mu_{2}^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{S\mu_{2}^{\prime}}\Big)+{\rm H.c.},

(51)

where we have introduced the tensor

\displaystyle[\bm{L}(t)]_{\mu_{3}uu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}}[\Lambda^{*}(t)]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}}[\bm{F}_{S}(t)]_{\mu_{1}u}[\bm{F}^{*}_{I}(t)]_{\mu_{2}u^{\prime}}.

(52)

With
Eq. (
III.2
) in hand it is natural to define the supermode annihilation operators for the signal and idler as

\displaystyle A_{Su}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}_{1}}[\bm{G}_{S}(t)]_{u\mu^{\prime}_{1}}a_{S\mu^{\prime}_{1}},

(53)

\displaystyle A_{Iu}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}_{2}}[\bm{G}^{*}_{I}(t)]_{u\mu^{\prime}_{2}}a_{I\mu^{\prime}_{2}},

(54)

where
\bm{G}_{S}(t)
and
\bm{G}_{I}(t)
are the unitary matrices from the joint SVD (
III.2
). We can write Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
in terms of the signal and idler supermode operators as

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,u^{\prime},\mu_{3}}\hbar[\bm{L}(t)]_{\mu_{3}uu^{\prime}}b^{\dagger}_{P\mu_{3}}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\cosh[r_{u}(t)]\cosh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A_{Su}(t)

\displaystyle+\sinh[r_{u}(t)]\cosh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A^{\dagger}_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A_{Iu}(t)

\displaystyle+\cosh[r_{u}(t)]\sinh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A^{\dagger}_{Su^{\prime}}(t)A_{Su}(t)

\displaystyle+\sinh[r_{u}(t)]\sinh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A^{\dagger}_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A^{\dagger}_{Su}(t)\Big)+{\rm H.c.}.

(55)

Considering Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
, the tensor element
[\bm{L}(t)]_{\mu_{3}uu^{\prime}}
describes the coupling between the pump supermode labeled by
\mu_{3}
and the pair of signal and idler supermodes labeled by
(u,u^{\prime})
. In general every element of
\bm{L}(t)
is nonzero, which implies that each pump mode is coupled with some strength to every signal-idler pair. But for efficient simulation we aim to identify a single pump supermode that couples predominantly to a subset of signal and idler supermodes. To try to isolate the pump from the signal and idler, we perform an SVD of
\bm{L}(t)
, reorganizing
Eq. (
52
) for the tensor
\bm{L}(t)
into a matrix by grouping together the signal and idler supermode indices as
(u,u^{\prime})
. We perform the following SVD on the reshaped
\bm{L}(t)
matrix

\displaystyle[\bm{L}(t)]_{\mu_{3}(u,u^{\prime})}=\sum_{\lambda}[\bm{X}(t)]_{\mu_{3}\lambda}[\bm{D}(t)]_{\lambda\lambda}[\bm{Q}^{\dagger}(t)]_{\lambda(u,u^{\prime})},

(56)

where
\bm{X}(t)
is the square unitary matrix that results,
\bm{Q}(t)
is a non-square semi-unitary matrix, with
\bm{Q}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{Q}(t)=\bm{I}
but
\bm{Q}(t)\bm{Q}^{\dagger}(t)\neq\bm{I}
, and
\bm{D}(t)={\rm diag}(D_{1}(t),D_{2}(t),\ldots)
is a diagonal matrix with
D_{\lambda}(t)\geq 0
. Putting Eq. (
56
) for
\bm{L}(t)
into Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
we obtain

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,u^{\prime},\lambda}\hbar D_{\lambda}(t)[\bm{Q}^{\dagger}(t)]_{\lambda(u,u^{\prime})}B^{\dagger}_{P\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\cosh[r_{u}(t)]\cosh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A_{Su}(t)

\displaystyle+\sinh[r_{u}(t)]\cosh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A^{\dagger}_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A_{Iu}(t)

\displaystyle+\cosh[r_{u}(t)]\sinh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A^{\dagger}_{Su^{\prime}}(t)A_{Su}(t)

\displaystyle+\sinh[r_{u}(t)]\sinh[r_{u^{\prime}}(t)]A^{\dagger}_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A^{\dagger}_{Su}(t)\Big)+{\rm H.c.},

(57)

where we have introduced the pump supermode annihilation operators as

\displaystyle B_{P\lambda}(t)=\sum_{\mu_{3}}[\bm{X}^{*}(t)]_{\mu_{3}\lambda}b_{P\mu_{3}},

(58)

where
\bm{X}(t)
is the square unitary matrix from the SVD (
56
).

Eq. (
III.2
) is our
final result for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
, written in terms of the supermodes. Each pump supermode labeled by
\lambda
is coupled with every pair of signal and idler supermodes labeled by
(u,u^{\prime})
, with the strength of each connection weighted by
D_{\lambda}(t)[\bm{Q}^{\dagger}(t)]_{\lambda(u,u^{\prime})}
. The benefit of having the form (
III.2
) is that the pump singular values
D_{\lambda}
are organized in decreasing magnitude, so we obtain an ordering of the coupling strength between a pump mode and each signal-idler pair that we can exploit. For example, using realistic parameters we demonstrate in Sec.
IV

that all
D_{\lambda}(t)
are virtually zero except for one.
So effectively
a single pump supermode
couples to every signal-idler pair. Additionally, we find that keeping only a few signal and idler supermodes in
H_{\rm eff}(t)
is sufficient to capture the total number of photons in the non-Gaussian ket. These observations justify truncating Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
to a small number of supermodes. In the truncated supermode basis we can perform efficient numerical simulations to obtain the non-Gaussian ket.

We stress that Eqs. (
53
) and (
54
) for the signal and idler supermode operators annihilate photons in supermodes that have components in both the actual and the phantom channels.
So Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)
describes the evolution of the non-Gaussian ket in the entire system.

With Eq. (
III.2
) for
H_{\rm eff}(t)

we are now in a position to solve Eq. (
26
) for the Schrödinger equation for the non-Gaussian ket
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
. To benefit from the supermode basis we represent
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
in the basis of time-dependent supermode Fock states given by

\displaystyle\ket{n_{Su}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{Su}!}}[A^{\dagger}_{Su}(t)]^{n_{Su}}\ket{0},

(59)

\displaystyle\ket{n_{Iu}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{Iu}!}}[A^{\dagger}_{Iu}(t)]^{n_{Iu}}\ket{0},

(60)

\displaystyle\ket{n_{Pu}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{Pu}!}}[B^{\dagger}_{Pu}(t)]^{n_{Pu}}\ket{0},

(61)

where at each time
n_{Su}
,
n_{Iu}
, and
n_{Pu}
are the number of photons in the signal, idler, and pump supermode
u
respectively. We consider a truncated basis consisting of
M
signal kets,
M
idler kets, and
L
pump kets, where each ket contains at most
N
photons. We choose the same number of signal and idler kets, but this can be readily generalized. The non-Gaussian ket can be written in this truncated basis as

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{S1},\ldots,n_{SM}=0}^{N}\,\,\,\sum_{n_{I1},\ldots,n_{IM}=0}^{N}\,\,\,\sum_{n_{P1},\ldots,n_{PL}=0}^{N}

\displaystyle\times c_{n_{S1}\ldots n_{SM}n_{I1}\ldots n_{IM}n_{P1}\ldots n_{PL}}(t)

\displaystyle\times\ket{n_{S1}\ldots n_{SM}}_{t}\ket{n_{I1}\ldots n_{IM}}_{t}\ket{n_{P1}\ldots n_{PL}}_{t},

(62)

where
c_{n_{S1}\ldots n_{SM}n_{I1}\ldots n_{IM}n_{P1}\ldots n_{PL}}(t)
are the coefficients that we determine. Supermode basis kets outside the truncated basis are assumed to be in the vacuum state, and are not included in the expansion (
III.2
). Taking the time derivative of Eq. (
III.2
) for
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
we obtain a linear Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\rm L}(t)
given below that captures the time dependence of the supermode basis. Putting Eq. (
III.2
) into Eq. (
26
) for
i\hbar\,d|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle/dt
, we show in Appendix
C
that we can write

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle}{\partial t}=\left(H_{\rm eff}(t)+\tilde{H}_{\rm L}(t)\right)|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle,

(63)

where
H_{\rm eff}(t)
is given by Eq. (
III.2
) and we have defined

\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{S1},\ldots,n_{SM}=0}^{N}\sum_{n_{I1},\ldots,n_{IM}=0}^{N}\sum_{n_{P1},\ldots,n_{PL}=0}^{N}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[c_{n_{S1}\ldots n_{SM}n_{I1}\ldots n_{IM}n_{P1}\ldots n_{PL}}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\times\ket{n_{S1}\ldots n_{SM}}_{t}\ket{n_{I1}\ldots n_{IM}}_{t}\ket{n_{P1}\ldots n_{PL}}_{t}.

(64)

The linear Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\rm L}(t)
, which arises because the supermodes themselves are time dependent, is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{L}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\hbar\left[\frac{d\bm{G}^{*}_{S}(t)}{dt}\bm{G}^{\rm T}_{S}(t)\right]_{uu^{\prime}}A^{\dagger}_{Su^{\prime}}(t)A_{Su}(t)

\displaystyle-i\hbar\left[\frac{d\bm{G}_{I}(t)}{dt}\bm{G}^{\dagger}_{I}(t)\right]_{uu^{\prime}}A^{\dagger}_{Iu^{\prime}}(t)A_{Iu}(t)

\displaystyle-i\hbar\left[\frac{d\bm{X}^{\rm T}(t)}{dt}\bm{X}^{*}(t)\right]_{uu^{\prime}}B^{\dagger}_{Pu^{\prime}}(t)B_{Pu}(t),

(65)

where
\bm{G}_{S}(t)
and
\bm{G}_{I}(t)
, and
\bm{X}(t)
are the unitary matrices from the SVDs in Eqs. (
III.2
), and (
56
) respectively.

We numerically solve Eq. (
63
) for the coefficients of
|\tilde{\psi}(t)\rangle
by evolving the state over short time steps
\Delta t
, during which the time dependence of the Hamiltonian can be neglected. This approach allows us to efficiently obtain the non-Gaussian ket for a number of supermodes on the order of 1 (
M\approx L\approx 1
) and a Fock space cutoff for the non-Gaussian ket on the order of 10 (
N\approx 10
).

IV.3
Stretching the full ket in the actual channel

In the previous subsection we calculated the Wigner function for the non-Gaussian ket. In practice one does not have access to the non-Gaussian ket itself. To model a realistic measurement we calculate the expectation value of observables involving the actual channel operators with respect to the full ket. We show that its non-Gaussian features are obscured by the large squeezing amplitude, but they can be revealed by applying a unitary
to the state
in the actual channel.

For microring resonator systems pumped with a coherent state, strong squeezing is required to achieve sufficient pump depletion to observe non-Gaussianity. If one calculates the Wigner function of the hybrid mode
[A_{S1}(t_{1})+A_{I1}(t_{1})]/\sqrt{2}
using the full ket, the phase-space portrait in Fig.
8
(b) for the reduced Wigner function in the actual channel is squeezed along the
q
-axis by a factor
\exp(-r_1)\approx 0.025
, where the squeezing parameter is
r_{1}=3.7
. This extreme squeezing obscures the non-Gaussian features.

In principle, the Gaussian features in the Wigner function of the full ket can be removed by applying the inverse Gaussian transformations to the light after the ring. Prior to any measurement, one could imagine applying a Gaussian unitary that would stretch the Wigner function
along the
q
-axis by a factor of
\exp(r_1)
, cancelling
the initial squeezing. An appropriate rotation and displacement of the ket could
also be applied
if needed. These operations would be
equivalent to applying the Hermitian conjugate of the Gaussian unitary
U^{\dagger}
to the full ket after the ring, leading to a final full ket equal to the non-Gaussian ket,

U^{\dagger}\ket{\psi}=|\tilde{\psi}\rangle
. The Wigner function of the final full ket would then be equal to that of the non-Gaussian ket.

We employ a strategy in which the Gaussian unitary is initially calculated under the artificial condition of no scattering loss,

\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}=U^{\dagger}_{\rm no\,loss}

\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\approx U^{\dagger}_{\rm no\,loss}

V
Conclusion

We have developed a framework for modeling non-Gaussian state generation via SPDC
that incorporates scattering loss with a phantom channel.
The full ket for the system is written as a Gaussian unitary acting on a residual non-Gaussian ket, separating the evolution into Gaussian and non-Gaussian parts. In this framework we are able to use the Gaussian solution to find supermodes that enable the efficient simulation of the non-Gaussian ket.

Using this approach, in calculations using realistic parameters for InGaP microrings we found
a Wigner negativity volume of approximately
0.18
for the residual non-Gaussian ket. While
the non-Gaussian features are largely obscured in the Wigner function of the full ket by the squeezing, they could
be revealed by applying an appropriate inverse Gaussian unitary that undid the squeezing by “stretching.”
Although the intrinsic quality factors needed for this approach are
roughly an order of magnitude higher than those currently being reported in the
InGaP literature, with future design and fabrication improvements this should be a viable approach.

In the InGaP microrings considered, the onset of non-Gaussianity required high escape efficiencies and pump energies on the order of
0.1\,{\rm pJ}
, yielding a Wigner function that was squeezed along one axis
by a factor of
\sim 0.025
. In contrast, PPLN waveguide models require less pump energy, typically achieving non-Gaussianity with a comparatively smaller squeezing factor (e.g.,
\sim 0.1
)
Yanagimoto et al. 2022
. Although waveguide models achieve non-Gaussianity with comparatively less squeezing,
both microring and waveguide systems share the challenge that the
ability to reveal non-Gaussian features depends on suppressing the dominant Gaussian features in the Wigner function. In addition, we showed that the suppression of Gaussian features critically depends on the accurate inclusion of scattering loss in the model. Ultimately, the advantage of microring resonators are their compactness, and with effective handling of scattering loss, they open a path for the deterministic generation of non-Gaussian states in a compact and scalable architecture.

Appendix D
Treatment of squeezing in the Gaussian approximation using asymptotic fields

In this section we model a homodyne measurement of the generated squeezed state within the Gaussian approximation in the actual output channel.
We describe the Lagrange multiplier method that we use to obtain the optimal local oscillator that minimizes the quadrature noise, and we derive an analytical formula that approximates the minimum noise.

Consider a general quadrature operator written in terms of the asymptotic-out operators for the actual output channel

\displaystyle X_{\rm ac}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k}\left([\bm{f}_{S}]_{k}a_{{\rm ac}Sk}+[\bm{f}_{I}]_{k}a_{{\rm ac}Ik}\right)+{\rm H.c.},

(111)

where
k
is a wavenumber in the actual waveguide, and the vectors
\bm{f}_{S}
and
\bm{f}_{I}
are the local oscillator profiles for the signal and idler, respectively. The local oscillator profiles for the signal and idler are normalized such that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{S}\bm{f}_{S}+\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{I}\bm{f}_{I}}{2},

(112)

ensuring that the commutator is satisfied

\displaystyle[X_{\rm ac},X^{\dagger}_{\rm ac}]=1.

(113)

The orthogonal quadrature operator

is given by

\displaystyle Y_{\rm ac}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k}\left([\bm{f}_{S}]_{k}a_{{\rm ac}Sk}+[\bm{f}_{I}]_{k}a_{{\rm ac}Ik}\right)+{\rm H.c.},

(114)

where

\displaystyle[X_{\rm ac},Y_{\rm ac}]=i.

(115)

At a very late time

when the pump pulse has left the ring, and the generated signal and idler light is far from the ring propagating in the actual and phantom output channels, we consider a homodyne measurement in the actual output channel. The noise in the
X_{\rm ac}
quadrature within the Gaussian approximation is given by

\displaystyle\bra{\psi(t_1)}X^{2}_{\rm ac}\ket{\psi(t_1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{S}\bm{N}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{SS}(t_{1})\bm{f}_{S}+\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{I}\bm{N}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{II}(t_{1})\bm{f}_{I}

\displaystyle+\left(\bm{f}^{\rm T}_{S}\bm{M}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{SI}(t_{1})\bm{f}_{I}+{\rm c.c}\right)+1,

(116)

where
\bm{M}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{SI}(t_{1})
and
\bm{N}^{\rm ac\,ac}_{JJ}(t_{1})
are given by Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
), respectively. The commutation relation (
115

X_{\rm ac}
and
Y_{\rm ac}

\displaystyle\bra{\psi(t_1)}X^{2}_{\rm ac}\ket{\psi(t_1)}\bra{\psi(t_1)}Y^{2}_{\rm ac}\ket{\psi(t_1)}\geq 1,

\displaystyle\bra{\psi(t_1)}X^{2}_{\rm ac}\ket{\psi(t_1)}<1.

Consider Eq. (

\displaystyle=\bm{f}^{*\rm T}\bm{N}\bm{f}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{f}^{\rm T}\bm{M}\bm{f}+{\rm c.c}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\bm{f}^{\rm T}\bm{f}^{*},

.

Having Eqs. (

139

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=(\bm{f}^{\rm T}_{S}\bm{U}^{*}_{SS}\overline{\bm{\Sigma}}_{SI}\bm{U}^{\dagger}_{II}\bm{f}_{I}+{\rm c.c})

\displaystyle+\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{S}\bm{U}_{SS}\bm{\Sigma}_{SS}\bm{U}^{\dagger}_{SS}\bm{f}_{S}+\bm{f}^{\dagger}_{I}\bm{U}_{II}\bm{\Sigma}_{II}\bm{U}^{\dagger}_{II}\bm{f}_{I}+1,

(140)

where we have introduced the
n_{k}\times n_{k}
matrix

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\Sigma}}_{SI}=\bm{U}^{\rm T}_{SS}\bm{U}_{SI}\bm{\Sigma}_{SI}\bm{O}_{SI}\bm{U}_{II}.

(141)

We make the ansatz that the minimum quadrature noise is closely achieved when the signal local oscillator
\bm{f}_{S}
is the first column vector of
\bm{U}_{SS}
and the idler local oscillator
\bm{f}_{I}
is the first column vector of
\bm{U}_{II}

\displaystyle[\bm{f}_{S}]_{k}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{i\theta}[\bm{U}_{SS}]_{k1},

(142)

\displaystyle[\bm{f}_{I}]_{k}

\displaystyle=[\bm{U}_{II}]_{k1},

(143)

where
\theta
is a global phase.
These vectors correspond to the largest singular values in
\bm{\Sigma}_{SS}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{II}
.

Putting Eqs. (
142
) and (
143
) for the optimal local oscillators into Eq. (
D
) for the noise we obtain

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta_{S}\eta_{I}}\big({\rm e}^{i\theta}\left[\sinh(\bm r_{[1,1]})\cosh(\bm r_{[1,1]})\right]_{uu}P_{u}+{\rm c.c}\big)

\displaystyle+(\eta_{S}+\eta_{I})\sinh^{2}(r_{1})+1,

(144)

where summation over
u

\displaystyle P_{u}=[\bm{U}^{\rm T}_{SI}\bm{U}_{SS}]_{u1}[\bm{O}_{SI}\bm{U}_{II}]_{u1}.

(145)

Here
\sinh^{2}(r_{1})

r_{1}

We choose the phase
\theta

\exp(i\theta)P_{1}=-1

D
) we obtain

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-2r_1)}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{S}+\eta_{I}}{2}+\sqrt{\eta_{S}\eta_{I}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\exp(2r_1)}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{S}+\eta_{I}}{2}-\sqrt{\eta_{S}\eta_{I}}\right)

\displaystyle+1-\frac{\eta_{S}+\eta_{I}}{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\eta_{S}\eta_{I}}}{2}\sum_{u=2}^{n_{k}}\sinh(2r_u)\big(P^{*}_{1}P_{u}+{\rm c.c}\big).

(146)

We find numerically that
P_{u}\approx 0
for

\displaystyle\langle X^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\exp(-2r_1)}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{S}+\eta_{I}}{2}+\sqrt{\eta_{S}\eta_{I}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\exp(2r_1)}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{S}+\eta_{I}}{2}-\sqrt{\eta_{S}\eta_{I}}\right)

\displaystyle+1-\frac{\eta_{S}+\eta_{I}}{2}.

(147)

For
\eta_{S}=\eta_{I}=\eta
and
\exp(-2r_1)\rightarrow 0
the noise is simply limited by
1-\eta

Appendix E
Wigner function in the actual channel for the non-Gaussian ket

In this section we show how to obtain the Wigner function of the non-Gaussian ket in the actual channel.

Consider the operators
\differential_{S}
and
\differential_{I}
that are linear combinations of the signal and idler operators for the asymptotic field for the actual output channel

\displaystyle\differential_{S}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}_{S}\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}S},

(148)

\displaystyle\differential_{I}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}_{I}\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}I},

(149)

where
\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}S}
and
\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}I}
are
n_{k}\times 1
column vectors of operators for the
n_{k}
modes in the actual channel for the signal and idler and
\bm{\beta}_{S}
and
\bm{\beta}_{I}
are
n_{k}\times 1
column vector of the expansion coefficients that we will determine. We normalize
\bm{\beta}_{S}
and
\bm{\beta}_{I}
so that the commutators are satisfied

\displaystyle[\differential_{S},\differential_{S}^{\dagger}]=[\differential_{I},\differential_{I}^{\dagger}]=1.

(150)

It will be convenient to write Eqs. (
148
) and (
149
) as

\displaystyle\differential_{S}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}_{S}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{n_{k}}&\bm{0}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{S},

(151)

\displaystyle\differential_{I}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}_{I}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{n_{k}}&\bm{0}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{I},

(152)

where
\bm{I}_{n_{k}}
and
\bm{0}_{n_{k}}
are the
n_{k}\times n_{k}
identity matrix and zeros matrix respectively, and we have introduced the
2n_{k}\times 1
column vectors
\bm{a}_{S}=[\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}S}^{\rm T}\,\,\,\bm{a}_{{\rm ph}S}^{\rm T}]^{\rm T}
and
\bm{a}_{I}=[\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}I}^{\rm T}\,\,\,\bm{a}_{{\rm ph}I}^{\rm T}]^{\rm T}
. Here
[\bm{I}_{n_{k}}\,\,\,\bm{0}_{n_{k}}]

is an
n_{k}\times 2n_{k}
matrix that projects onto the part of the operator associated with the actual channel.

We will calculate the Wigner function for the non-Gaussian ket for the actual channel in terms of
\differential_{S}
and
\differential_{I}
. However, Eq. (
III.2
) for the non-Gaussian ket is written in terms of Eqs. (
53
) and (
54
) for the non-Gaussian supermode operators at the final time
\bm{A}_{S}(t_{1})
and
\bm{A}_{I}(t_{1})
. It can be easily shown that Eqs. (
151
) and (
152
) for
\differential_{S}
and
\differential_{I}
can be written in terms of
\bm{A}_{S}(t_{1})
and
\bm{A}_{I}(t_{1})
as

\displaystyle\differential_{S}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}^{T}_{S}\mathcal{G}_{S}\bm{A}_{S}(t_{1}),

(153)

\displaystyle\differential_{I}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}^{T}_{I}\mathcal{G}_{I}\bm{A}_{I}(t_{1}),

(154)

where we have introduced the
n_{k}\times 2n_{k}
reduced matrices
\mathcal{G}_{S}
and
\mathcal{G}_{I}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{n_{k}}&\bm{0}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{G}^{\dagger}_{S}(t_{1}),

(155)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{I}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{n_{k}}&\bm{0}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{G}^{\rm T}_{I}(t_{1}),

(156)

which are formed by removing the rows associated with the phantom channel from the original
2n_{k}\times 2n_{k}
matrices
\bm{G}^{\dagger}_{S}(t_{1})
and
\bm{G}^{\rm T}_{I}(t_{1})
. Eqs. (
153
) and (
154
) for
\differential_{S}
and
\differential_{I}
represent a weighted sum of the supermode operators for the signal and idler, where the weights are given by the
1\times 2n_{k}
row vectors
\bm{\beta}^{T}_{S}\mathcal{G}_{S}
and
\bm{\beta}^{T}_{I}\mathcal{G}_{I}
respectively.

We will now show that these weights contribute to a Gaussian noise term in the Wigner characteristic function.
Consider the hybrid mode
(\differential_{S}+\differential_{I})/\sqrt{2}
in the actual channel. Using Eqs. (
153
) and (
154
) we can write the Wigner function for
|\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})\rangle
in terms of this hybrid mode as

\displaystyle W^{\rm NG}_{\rm ac}(q,p)=\int\frac{dxdy}{\pi^{2}}\langle\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})|\exp(\frac{x+iy}{\sqrt{2}}[\bm\beta^\dagger_S\mathcal{G}^*_S \bm A^\dagger_S(t_1)+ \bm\beta^\dagger_I\mathcal{G}^*_I \bm A^\dagger_I(t_1)] - {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})\rangle{\rm e}^{2i(px-qy)}.

(157)

Assuming that the non-Gaussian ket
|\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})\rangle
is vacuum for all modes expect the first signal and idler modes we can write Eq. (
157
) as

\displaystyle W^{\rm NG}_{\rm ac}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dxdy}{\pi^{2}}\langle\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})|\exp(\frac{x+iy}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{[\bm\beta^\dagger_S\mathcal{G}^*_S]_1 A^\dagger_{S1}(t_1)+ [\bm\beta^\dagger_I\mathcal{G}^*_I]_1 A^\dagger_{I1}(t_1)\right\}- {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle\times\exp(-\frac{x^2+y^2}{4}\sum_{u=2}^{2n_k}\left\{ \left| [\bm\beta^\dagger_S\mathcal{G}^*_S]_u\right|^2 + \left|[\bm\beta^\dagger_I\mathcal{G}^*_I]_u \right|^2\right\}){\rm e}^{2i(px-qy)},

(158)

where
[\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}_{S}\mathcal{G}^{*}_{S}]_{u}
and
[\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}_{I}\mathcal{G}^{*}_{I}]_{u}
are the weights associated with the signal and idler supermode
u
.
So the supermodes
u\geq 2
that are not included in the ket contribute to the vacuum noise term in Eq. (
E
). The noise term vanishes for the ideal weights satisfying
\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}_{S}\mathcal{G}^{*}_{S}=\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}_{I}\mathcal{G}^{*}_{I}=[1\,\,\,0\,\,\,\ldots\,\,\,0]
, where there are
2n_{k}-1
zeros and we put the first element to 1 but any non-zero complex number would also make the noise term vanish. In the presence of loss the noise term will never vanish. The matrices
\mathcal{G}_{S}
and
\mathcal{G}_{I}
are not invertible, so it is not possible to obtain coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{S}
and
\bm{\beta}_{I}
that yield the ideal weights. The best one can do is minimize the vacuum noise term in Eq. (
E
) by choosing the optimal coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{S}
and
\bm{\beta}_{I}
according to

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}_{S}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}_{S}^{+},

(159)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}_{I}^{+},

(160)

where
\mathcal{G}_{S}^{+}
and
\mathcal{G}_{I}^{+}
denote the pseudo-inverses of
\mathcal{G}_{S}
and
\mathcal{G}_{I}
respectively.

Putting Eqs. (
159
) and (
160
) for the optimal coefficients into Eq. (
E
) for the reduced Wigner function and for
\eta_{S}=\eta_{I}\equiv\eta
we numerically find that the reduced Wigner function is well approximated by

\displaystyle W^{\rm NG}_{\rm ac}(q,p)

\displaystyle\approx\int\frac{dxdy}{\pi^{2}}\langle\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})|\exp(\sqrt{\eta}\,\frac{x+iy}{\sqrt{2}}\left\{ A^\dagger_{S1}(t_1)+ A^\dagger_{I1}(t_1)\right\}- {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}(t_{1})\rangle\exp(-(1-\eta)\,\frac{x^2+y^2}{2}){\rm e}^{2i(px-qy)}.

(161)

So in the approximation (
161
) clearly the Wigner function approaches the vacuum result when
\eta\rightarrow 0
and the noise term vanishes as
\eta\rightarrow 1
. We demonstrate in Fig.
8
(d) that for
\eta=0.5
the non-Gaussian features in the Wigner function are erased and remain visible for
\eta=0.95
.

Appendix F
Measuring the non-Gaussian features of the full ket.

In this section we show how a prospective measurement of the portion of light in the actual output channel could reveal the non-Gaussian features in the Wigner function for the full ket.

We consider the setup in Fig.
9
, where the unitary
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
, represented by the red-lined box, acts only on the actual output channel. Before the box the state is
\ket{\psi}=U|\tilde{\psi}\rangle
and after the box it becomes
\ket{\psi'}=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}U|\tilde{\psi}\rangle
. Here
U
(Eq. (
70
)) is the Gaussian unitary already determined, and
|\tilde{\psi}\rangle
is the residual non-Gaussian ket. By adjusting
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
we aim to remove the Gaussian unitary so that
\ket{\psi'}\approx|\tilde{\psi}\rangle
.

The Wigner function for a mode in the actual channel
\differential
using the full ket
\ket{\psi'}
is given by

\displaystyle W^{\rm full}_{\rm ac}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dxdy}{\pi^{2}}\bra{\psi'}\exp(\gamma\dd^\dagger- {\rm H.c.})\ket{\psi'}{\rm e}^{2i(px-qy)}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dxdy}{\pi^{2}}\langle\tilde{\psi}|\exp(\gamma\left[U^\dagger\mathcal{U} \dd\mathcal{U^\dagger} U\right]^\dagger- {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle\times{\rm e}^{2i(px-qy)}

(162)

where we put
\gamma=x+iy
. Similar to what was done in Appendix
E
, we write
\differential
as a superposition of the actual channel operators for the signal and idler

\displaystyle\differential=\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}\bm{a}_{\rm ac},

(163)

where
\bm{\beta}
is the local oscillator shape and
\bm{a}_{\rm ac}
is the column vector of signal and idler operators

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{\rm ac}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}S}\\
\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}I}\end{bmatrix},

(164)

where
\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}S}
and
\bm{a}_{{\rm ac}I}
are introduced in Eqs. (
148
) and (
149
) respectively.

To proceed with Eq. (
F
) for the Wigner function we need to calculate the operation
U^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}U
on the actual channel operators.
We assume that unitary
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
does the following transformation on the actual channel operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\bar{\bm{V}}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}+\bar{\bm{W}}\bm{a}^{\dagger}_{\rm ac},

(165)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{a}^{\dagger}_{\rm ac}

(166)

where
\bar{\bm{V}}
and
\bar{\bm{W}}

\mathcal{U}

U

\bar{\bm{V}}
and
\bar{\bm{W}}

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{V}}_{SS}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bar{\bm{V}}_{II}\end{bmatrix},

(167)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{W}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bar{\bm{W}}_{SI}\\
\bar{\bm{W}}_{IS}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(168)

The commutation relations have to be preserved, yielding the following constraints

\displaystyle\bm{I}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger},\mathcal{U}\bm{a}^{\dagger\rm T}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}]\rightarrow\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bar{\bm{V}}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bar{\bm{W}}^{*}=\bm{I},

(169)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger},\mathcal{U}\bm{a}^{\rm T}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}]\rightarrow\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bar{\bm{V}}^{*}-\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bar{\bm{W}}=\bm{0}.

(170)

Applying the full unitary
U
to Eq. (
166
) for
\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
and using Eq. (
B
) for
U^{\dagger}\bm{a}U

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}U

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{V}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{W}^{*}_{\rm ac}\right)\bm{a}

\displaystyle+\left(\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{W}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{V}^{*}_{\rm ac}\right)\bm{a}^{\dagger}.

(171)

where
\bm{a}=[\bm{a}^{\rm T}_{S}\,\,\,\bm{a}^{\rm T}_{I}]^{\rm T}
is a column vector of signal and idler operators for the full system, and
\bm{V}_{\rm ac}
and
\bm{W}_{\rm ac}
are the reduced
\bm{V}
and
\bm{W}
matrices only keeping the elements that involve the actual channel. They are given by

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{\rm ac}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[\bm{V}_{SS}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ac}&\bm{[}\bm{V}_{SS}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ph}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&[\bm{V}_{II}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ac}&[\bm{V}_{II}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ph}\end{bmatrix},

(172)

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{\rm ac}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{0}&[\bm{W}_{SI}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ac}&\bm{[}\bm{W}_{SI}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ph}\\
[\bm{W}_{IS}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ac}&[\bm{W}_{IS}]_{\rm ac\,\rm ph}&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},

(173)

where the frequency bin partitions
\bm{V}_{SS}
,
\bm{V}_{II}
,
\bm{W}_{SI}
, and
\bm{W}_{IS}
encode the squeezing and rotation in the full unitary
U
and are determined. So we can see that when the matrices
\bar{\bm{V}}
and
\bar{\bm{W}}
associated with the Gaussian unitary
\mathcal{U}

and
\bm{W}_{\rm ac}

\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{V}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{W}^{*}_{\rm ac}\approx\bm{R}_{0}

\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{W}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{V}^{*}_{\rm ac}\approx\bm{0}

F

U^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}U\approx\bm{R}_{0}\bm{a}

\bar{\bm{V}}
and
\bar{\bm{W}}

To calculate expectation values using the residual non-Gaussian ket, we need to relate the operators
\bm{a}

F

53

54

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}\bm{a}_{\rm ac}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}U

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{V}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{W}^{*}_{\rm ac}\right)\bm{G}\bm{A}

where
\bm{A}=[\bm{A}^{\rm T}_{S}\,\,\,\bm{A}^{\rm T}_{I}]^{\rm T}
, and for convenience we have introduced the matrix
\bm{G}

\displaystyle\bm{G}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{G}^{\dagger}_{S}&\bm{0}\\

(175)

where the determined matrices
\bm{G}_{S}
and
\bm{G}_{I}

\bm{V}
and
\bm{W}

53

Putting Eq. (
F
) for

\displaystyle W^{\rm full}_{\rm ac}(q,p)=\int\frac{dxdy}{\pi^{2}}\chi(x,y){\rm e}^{2i(px-qy)}.

(176)

where we have introduced the Wigner characteristic function as

\displaystyle\chi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\psi}|\exp(\gamma[\bm C \bm A + \bm D \bm A^\dagger]^\dagger- {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}\rangle,

and we have defined the vectors

\displaystyle\bm{C}

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}\left(\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{V}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{W}^{*}_{\rm ac}\right)\bm{G},

(178)

\displaystyle\bm{D}

[\bm{A}]_{1}

\displaystyle\chi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\psi}|\exp(\gamma\{[\bm C]_1 [\bm A]_1 + [\bm D]_1 [\bm A^\dagger]_1\}^\dagger- {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle\times\prod_{l=2}^{M}\bra{{\rm vac}}\exp(\gamma\{[\bm C]_l [\bm A]_l + [\bm D]_l [\bm A^\dagger]_l\}^\dagger- {\rm H.c.})\ket{\rm vac}

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\psi}|\exp(\gamma\{[\bm C]_1 [\bm A]_1 + [\bm D]_1 [\bm A^\dagger]_1\}^\dagger- {\rm H.c.})|\tilde{\psi}\rangle

\sum_{l=2}^{M}[\gamma\bm{C}^{\dagger}-\gamma^{*}\bm{D}^{\rm T}]_{l}[\gamma^{*}\bm{C}-\gamma\bm{D}^{*}]_{l}\approx 0

\bm{D}

\bm{C}
and
\bm{D}

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\rm target}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix},

(181)

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{\rm target}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix}.

(182)

We attempt to satisfy Eq. (
182

\bm{D}_{\rm target}
by choosing
\bar{\bm{W}}
and
\bar{\bm{V}}

\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{W}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{V}^{*}_{\rm ac}\approx\bm{0}

\bm{C}_{\rm target}

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{\rm T}=\bm{C}_{\rm target}\left[\left(\bar{\bm{V}}^{\dagger}\bm{V}_{\rm ac}-\bar{\bm{W}}^{\rm T}\bm{W}^{*}_{\rm ac}\right)\bm{G}\right]^{+},
