A Judgmental Construction of Directed Type Theory
Jacob Neumann

Abstract
We reformulate recent advances in directed type theory—a type theory where the types have the structure of synthetic (higher) categories—as a logical calculus with multiple context ‘zones’, following the example of Pfenning and Davies. This allows us to have two kinds of variables—‘neutral’ and ‘polar’—with different functoriality requirements. We focus on the lowest-dimension version of this theory (where types are synthetic preorders ) and apply the logical language to articulate concepts from the theory of rewriting. We also take the occasion to develop the categorical semantics of dual-context systems, proposing a notion of dual CwF to serve as a common structural base for the model theories of such logics.



Keywords:
Directed type theory, Semantics of type theory, Category theory, Modal logic, Synthetic Rewriting

1
Introduction

1.1
Directed type theory

A central notion in modern type theories, particularly dependent type theories in the tradition of Martin-Löf
[
ML75
]
, is that of
identity types
. We can understand the purpose of identity types as
internalizing metatheoretic equality
, that is, taking the statement that two terms
s
and
t
(of the same type) are
equal
—which is traditionally a
judgment
, an assertion made
about
the objects of the language but not
in
the language itself—and making it a construct within the language, the type
\mathsf{Id}(s,t)
. In a language equipped with this feature, it can be proved
within
the language that
s
is equal to
t
, by constructing a piece of data, a term of type
\mathsf{Id}(s,t)
.
1
1

1

This is particularly relevant, for instance, in the field of
interactive theorem proving
, where the goal is to represent mathematical proofs within a programming language. Identity types are a natural addition to such a language, as they represent the equality of two things; proving that things are
equal
to each other is arguably the fundamental kind of proof mathematicians perform.
The nuances, pitfalls, and possibilities of including identity types in a language have occupied the attention of type theorists for decades, and will no doubt continue to be a topic of intense investigation.

However, type theorists have only recently begun to grapple with
directed identity types
(also known as
hom-types
), the analogous internalization of
reduction/rewriting
. The informal idea of the directed identity type
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
is that it’s supposed to be
like an identity type, but with a direction
. More exactly, we desire hom-types to be reflexive (the type
\mathsf{Hom}(t,t)
ought always to be inhabited) and transitive (we ought to be able to combine an element of
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
with an element of
\mathsf{Hom}(t,u)
to obtain an element of
\mathsf{Hom}(s,u)
) but
not symmetric
: unlike identity types (which usually
are
provably symmetric), we don’t want a term of
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
to give us a term of
\mathsf{Hom}(t,s)
in general. This reflects the fact that metatheoretic reduction generally goes one way:
s
might reduce to
t
but not vice-versa. Soundly designing a type theoretic language that behaves this way—a
directed type theory
—has proved to be a surprisingly challenging task, for a variety of technical reasons.

1.2
Judgmental directed type theory

Numerous approaches to directed type theory have been put forward in the literature. Were one to arrange these theories into some kind of taxonomy, the top-level division would surely be the
manner of introducing the directed identity types
. The prevailing directed type theory of the past several years has been the
simplicial type theory
originally proposed by Riehl and Shulman
[
RS17
]
, further expanded by Weinberger
[
Wei22
]
, and recently refined by Gratzer et al.
[
GWB24
,
GWB25
]
. The simplicial approach to directed type theory is to first axiomatize a directed interval type
\mathbb{I}
and
define
directed identity in terms of it:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Hom}_{A}(s,t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{f\colon\mathbb{I}\to A}\mathsf{Id}(f\;0,s)\times\mathsf{Id}(f\;1,t)

[
GWB24
, Definition 2.2]
.

This approach comes with numerous benefits, such as superior computational properties.
2
2

2

Simplicial type theory defines hom-types in a manner reminiscent of how
cubical type theory

[
BCH14
,
CCHM18
]
defines identity types, and thereby enjoys many of the computational advantages of the latter.
However, the semantics of simplicial theory are rather complex—they must be at least as complex as homotopy type theory, of which simplicial type theory is an extension.

Also, if we wish to explore directed identity as a reflection of metatheoretic reduction, simplicial type theory is much more than we need. Simplicial type theory is intended as a synthetic language for
\infty
-categories: we can understand the hom-types in a directed type theory as endowing a type with a
synthetic category structure
: the terms of a type
A
represent the objects of the category, the terms of type
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
represent morphisms from
s
to
t
, reflexivity corresponds to identity morphisms, etc. However, since
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
is
also
a type, we can iterate this process, considering hom-terms
between
hom-terms, and hom-terms between those hom-terms, and so on forever. Simplicial type theory is designed to allow its users to manipulate this
\infty
-categorical structure effectively, and the research into it is geared towards that end. However, rewriting systems are not
\infty
-categories, they are preorders, i.e. 0-categories. Therefore, we can get away with much simpler systems with more straightforward semantics.

The standard approach in
un
directed type theory—dating back to Martin-Löf
[
ML75
]
—is to introduce identity types as a primitive type-former, governed by several inference rules. In particular, the elimination principle for identity types, the
J-rule
, is asserted along with a
\beta
-law which holds up to
judgmental equality
.
3
3

3

As opposed to, say, cubical type theory, where there J-rule is merely a theorem, and its
\beta
-law holds only
propositionally
.
We shall use the term
judgmental directed type theory
for a theory which does likewise for directed identity: articulate hom-types as a primitive type-former, along with a directed J-rule whose
\beta
-law holds judgmentally.
4
4

4

This is not to suggest that other directed type theories do not observe Martin-Löf’s distinction between judgment and proposition, but simply that they do not articulate their directed identity types judgmentally.
Our purpose in the present work is to articulate a judgmental directed type theory.

Our approach to directed type theory will reconcile the type theories of North
[
Nor19
]
and Neumann-Altenkirch
[
NA25
,
Neu25
]
.
One benefit of these theories is that their semantics are very straightforward: they are interpreted in 1-categories (namely the
category model
, a directed analogue of Hofmann and Streicher’s
groupoid model

[
HS95
]
). This is in contrast to simplicial type theory (and similar theories, e.g.
[
WL20
]
), as well as the
virtual equipment type theory (VETT)
of New and Licata
[
NL23
]
(which is a ‘judgmental directed type theory’ in the sense given above); the semantics of these theories involve extraordinarily complex mathematical constructions, which are relatively removed from ordinary 1-categories.

One reason we care about semantic complexity is that a straightforward semantics makes it easier to perform metatheoretic arguments, e.g. independence arguments—demonstrating what
cannot
be constructed within the theory. This is of vital importance to directed type theory: if we are to verify that a putative directed type theory is indeed directed, we must be able to argue that symmetry is not provable in general, that
p\colon\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
cannot always be turned around to
p^{-1}\colon\mathsf{Hom}(t,s)
. This concern is especially acute for judgmental directed type theories: in
un
directed type theory, the same J-rule is used to prove both the symmetry and transitivity of equality; why should a directed J-rule be able to prove the latter but not the former? Unfortunately, New and Licata neglect this question, and it’s not obvious why their
\mathrm{ind}_{\to}
term-former cannot be used to prove that all the categories in VETT’s formal category theory aren’t automatically groupoids. In the Neumann-Altenkirch theory, on the other hand, it’s easy to conduct an independence proof
[
Neu25
, Theorem 3.1.20]
modeled on the Hofmann-Streicher proof
[
HS95
, Theorem 5.1]
of the independence of the
uniqueness of identity proofs
principle (UIP) from the rules of Martin-Löf type theory; this proof will work equally well in our system.

Where North and Neumann-Altenkirch differ is the precise way they restrict the J-rule to prevent symmetry. Both theories follow Licata and Harper
[
LH11
]
in imposing a
polarity calculus
: a modal typing discipline which annotates terms as either
co-
or
contra-variant
(“positive” or “negative”, respectively). By requiring the domain term
s
to be annotated negative and the codomain term
t
annotated positive in order for
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
to be well-formed, the proof of symmetry can be prevented but the proof of transitivity undisturbed. The issue comes with reflexivity: how is the judgment
\mathsf{refl}_{s}\colon\mathsf{Hom}(s,s)
supposed to be well-formed under this polarity calculus, if
s
must appear both positively and negatively? North restricts to polarity-invariant
types
, requiring
s
to be invariant in order for
\mathsf{refl}
(and consequently, J) to be given; Neumann-Altenkirch, on the other hand, restrict to polarity-invariant
contexts
, wherein any term can be freely coerced between ‘polarities’. We present the “least upper bound” of these two theories, generalizing both approaches into a common J-rule (which still cannot prove symmetry!).

1.3
Dual-context theories

Logicians have developed several logical calculi that employ multiple
context zones
to contain different kinds of hypotheses, i.e. contexts of the form
\Gamma\mid\Delta
, where
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are the ‘zones’, with different rules for their variables. For instance, Girard
[
Gir93
]
employed zoned contexts to encompass classical and intuitionistic sequents in the same logic; Pfenning and Davies
[
PD01
]
expounded a logic with a context zone for hypothesizing that a proposition is
valid
and one for hypothesizing a proposition is
true
; and, more recently, Shulman
[
Shu18
]
gave a type theory for synthetic topological constructions with “crisp” and “cohesive” context zones, such that a construction depending on several variables is continuous with respect to the synthetic topology of the cohesive variables, but possibly discontinuous with respect to the crisp variables. See e.g.
[
Kav20
, Introduction]
for a more thorough overview of this area of research.

We employ this technology towards judgmental directed type theory. Our types have the structure of synthetic categories, and any construction made within the theory is automatically
functorial
with respect to the category structure of the context. The issue that North and Neumann-Altenkirch confronted is that identity morphisms (
\mathsf{refl}
) cannot be functorial with respect to both endpoints, as they have opposite variances. This is solved by
neutralizing
the polarity, i.e. only requiring
\mathsf{refl}
to be functorial with respect to a groupoid context. This is the function of dual-zoned contexts for directed type theory: for the context
\Gamma\mid\Delta
, the zone
\Gamma
will be structured as a synthetic
groupoid
, so the difference between co- and contra-variance with respect to variables from
\Gamma
disappears. So, we’ll find, terms in context
\Gamma\mid\bullet
, i.e. where the “polarized zone”
\Delta
is empty, will be able to overcome the restrictions of variance to the extent we need.

We also take the opportunity to develop the semantics of this theory. In addition to updating North’s semantics for directed type theory—the
category model
—to match the dual-context situation, we consider how to define an abstract notion of ‘model’ for dual-context type theories. To our knowledge, there is no general-purpose model theory for dual-context type theories, analogous to how Dybjer’s
categories with families
(or the many equivalent notions) serve as a common structural basis for type theories. We propose such a notion, upon which (we conjecture) model notions for the various dual-context type theories can be developed.

Semantics

We now turn our attention to semantics. Our first step will be to expound
a
model of our formal theory. Having at least one model is useful for developing a language because (a) it provides a way to check any putative rules to quickly ensure that they won’t introduce disastrous consequences, and (b) it provides a mechanism to reason metatheoretically about the language, e.g. to establish independence or conservativity results. As mentioned in the introduction, these are particularly relevant tasks for judgmental directed type theorists—with the specter of symmetry ever hanging over us, we need tools to reason about what our formal language
can
and
cannot
do.

But we also aspire towards a more general-purpose model theory. That is, we do not just want to make singular
ad-hoc
interpretations of our formal language, but wish to articulate an abstract notion of ‘model’, reason about constructions and structure-preserving maps between models, and so on. To our knowledge, no abstract notion of ‘model’ has been given for crisp type theory (e.g. Licata et al.
[
LOPS18
]
only focus on presheaf models) nor is there a common model notion for dual-context type theories, analogous to how
categories with families
provide a common base which can be extended to model any structural type theory. These considerations are too involved to fully address here, but we include some reflections on model notions relevant to the present theory which, we hope, will serve as a starting-point for deeper investigations.

3.2
Towards an abstract model notion for dual-context theories

We now wish to begin considering a general-purpose model theory for dual-context type theories. The notion of ‘CwF’ serves as a common base for all varieties of structural type theory: to design a notion of ‘model’ for whatever type theory we’re interested in, we can start with CwFs and just attach the relevant features. But, to our knowledge, it is not so for dual-context type theories. While, for instance, crisp type theory is introduced along side a semantics (see e.g.
[
LOPS18

of a given theory and prove its initiality among models, it is necessary to abstractly define what a ‘model’ is.

untouched (this is what the “first projection” condition achieves).

As an example of how this calculus works, suppose we have a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(\Theta,\Gamma)
. Intuitively, this is a substitution which just operates on neutral/crisp variables, implementing the variables of
\Gamma
in terms of the variables of
\Theta
. The contravariance of the family
\mathcal{D}
says that
\sigma
induces an operation on polar/cohesive context zones:

\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}\quad\mapsto\quad\Delta[\sigma]\in\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}.

This substitution can be regarded as a substitution in the broader CwF: observe by
Definition

3.4
that we can pair
\sigma
with
\mathsf{id}_{\Delta[\sigma]}\in\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}(\Delta[\sigma],\Delta[\sigma])
to obtain a morphism in the total category

(\sigma,\mathsf{id})\quad\colon\mathsf{Sub}\;(\Theta\mid\Delta[\sigma])\;(\Gamma\mid\Delta).

Thus, types and terms in
\Gamma\mid\Delta
can be substituted to types and terms in
\Theta\mid\Delta[\sigma]
.

We also include an extension operator for the neutral context zone, but only by types with no polar dependencies, i.e.

\Gamma\in\mathcal{C},\ A\in\mathsf{Ty}(\Gamma\mid\bullet)\mapsto(\Gamma\rhd^{0}A)\in\mathcal{C}.

This is the kind of theory-specific operator which can be added to the basic definition of dual CwF. To define the abstract notion of ‘model of dual-context judgmental directed type theory’, we would need to add numerous constructors (corresponding to the rules listed in
Section

2
, among others), and likewise to define the notion of ‘model of crisp type theory’. But both could use dual CwFs as a starting-point, as it represents the basic structural foundation of dual-context type theory.

That’s all we’ll say on this matter for the present moment. Again, we do not pretend to have exhausted the matter of an abstract model theory for dual-context type theories. But we hope we have provided a starting point for deeper investigations into this topic.

4
Application: Synthetic Rewriting Systems

Finally, we briefly consider an application of this language: a synthetic framework for reasoning about
rewriting systems
. The basic idea is that the hom-types are intended to internalize metatheoretic reduction in the same way the identity types of
un
directed type theory internalize metatheoretic equality. It’s fitting that we employ a directed type theory which validates the uniqueness of homs principle (e.g. the directed type theory modeled by the setoid-preorder model), as we wish to discuss reduction as a
mere relation
—we are not interested in the
\infty
-categorical structure provided by simplicial type theory, or even in the 1-categorical structure of Neumann’s (1,1)-directed type theory.
11
11

11

We leave to future work to investigate possible connections to “higher-dimensional rewriting”.
We suspect that the present system—which is “directed intensional” in that we don’t assert any kind of
directed equality reflection
principle and allow our directed equality relation to be coarser than metatheoretic reduction—to have a similar relationship to a “directed extensional” type theory (which
does
assert such a principle) as undirected intensional type theory has to undirected extensional type theory (see e.g.
[
Hof95
]
). We leave these detailed technical questions, as well as an in-depth study of what can be done with our system, to future work—here we only wish to indicate how the interpretation goes.

In this setting, any type
A
which we can write down (assume for simplicity that
A
doesn’t depend on any polarized variables, i.e.
\Gamma\mid\bullet\vdash A\;\mathbf{type}
) has the structure of a
synthetic rewriting system
. We’ll think of the terms of
A
as
expressions
and the hom-type relation as
reduction
; indeed, we could write

s\rightsquigarrow t

instead of
\mathsf{Hom}(s,t)
to suggest this interpretation. Of course, this
\rightsquigarrow
relation is reflexive:
Hom Intro
asserts that
\Gamma,y::A\mid\bullet\vdash\mathsf{refl}_{y}\colon\langle-\rangle y\rightsquigarrow\langle+\rangle y
, and, in particular, for any
\Gamma\mid\bullet\vdash t\colon A
, we have
\mathsf{refl}_{\langle+\flat\rangle t}\colon-t\rightsquigarrow t
. We also know automatically that the reduction relation is transitive, by the composition of hom-terms operation provable from the rules of directed type theory.

Using the
\mathsf{J}^{+}
rule we can prove a transport law of the form

\frac{\Gamma\mid\bullet\vdash s\colon A^{-}\quad\Gamma\mid z\colon A\vdash M(z)\;\mathbf{type}\quad\Gamma\mid\bullet\vdash m\colon M(-s)}{\Gamma\mid z\colon A,f\colon s\rightsquigarrow z\vdash\mathsf{tr}^{+}_{M}\;f\;m:=\mathsf{J}^{+}\;m\colon M(z)}

Intuitively: any property
M(z)
we can write down in this language is
preserved under reduction
. That is, if
M
holds of
s
and
s\rightsquigarrow z
, then
M
must hold of
z
as well. Using
\mathsf{J}^{-}
, of course, we can prove the same claim running backwards, namely that if
M
holds of
t
and
x\rightsquigarrow t
, then
M
must hold of
x
too. Now, perhaps this is “too nice”, and we might want to write down more syntactic properties (e.g. “is a value”) or relations among terms (e.g. “is a subexpression”) which are not closed under reduction in this way. Perhaps some system with a further context zone, where constructions need not even be functorial in variables from that zone?

Though it is beyond our scope to precisely articulate a theory of
directed quotient inductive types
, some informal use will help illustrate our point. Recall from
un
directed type theory that a higher inductive type (HIT) is a type generated from a set of constructors (in the same way an ordinary inductive type is generated by constructors, e.g.
\mathbb{N}
from
zero
and
succ
), but where some of the constructors instead construct
identities
of the type being constructed. This is used e.g. in homotopy type theory
[
Uni13
, Section 6]
to construct higher-dimensional homotopy spaces. But the same technique is useful even in the set-truncated world: quotient inductive types allow one to build interesting mathematical structures (e.g. the integers or real numbers) as a quotient of an inductive type. The directed version is to introduce a type by constructors, including constructors of the hom-type. For instance, we could introduce the type
\mathbf{Expr}
of
natural number expressions
via the constructors:

•

\vdash 0\colon\mathbf{Expr}

•

a\colon\mathbf{Expr}\vdash\mathsf{succ}(a)\colon\mathbf{Expr}

•

a\;b\colon\mathbf{Expr}\vdash a+b\colon\mathbf{Expr}

•

a\colon\mathbf{Expr}\vdash\mathsf{pz}(a)\colon a+0\rightsquigarrow a

•

a\;b\colon\mathbf{Expr}\vdash\mathsf{ps}(a,b)\colon a+\mathsf{succ}(b)\rightsquigarrow\mathsf{succ}(a+b)

(we omitted the polarity coercions here for clarity). So, combining the
\mathsf{pz}
and
\mathsf{ps}
constructors in the right way (and liberally using the transport operation above to reduce subexpressions), we can construct internal proofs about this simple reduction calculus. This is of course a very simple example, but serves as a proof of concept.

Combine this with our induced identity types
\mathsf{Id}^{\flat}
and
\mathsf{Id}^{\sharp}
. Recall that we had an identity
\mathsf{Id}^{\flat}(s,t)
when we had
s\rightsquigarrow t
and
-t\rightsquigarrow-s
. Interpreting our types as synthetic rewriting systems,
\mathsf{Id}^{\flat}
encodes some kind of syntactic, literal, intensional equality between
s
and
t
: for a “reasonable” notion of rewriting, we would expect that the only way two terms could rewrite to each other is if they’re already literally the same. On the other hand,
\mathsf{Id}^{\sharp}
gives us a notion of extensional equivalence: it is the equivalence relation generated by reduction, so if
s\rightsquigarrow t
, or
-t\rightsquigarrow-s
, or they reduce to some common expression, etc. then they are equal under
\mathsf{Id}^{\sharp}
. Again, if we make some assumptions of “niceness” for our reduction relation (e.g. confluence), then
\mathsf{Id}^{\sharp}(s,t)
if they reduce to a common expression. So, for our example above, we’d have a proof of
7+5\rightsquigarrow 12
and therefore
\mathsf{Id}^{\sharp}(7+5,12)
, but, since we couldn’t construct a term of type
12\rightsquigarrow 7+5
, we wouldn’t get
\mathsf{Id}^{\flat}(7+5,12)
, as we might hope.
