Detection of nuclear STING in cultured human cells and in the normal and cancer tissues
Authors: Nicolas Vo, Eleanor Chen, Julia M Sidorova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryStimulator of interferon genes (STING) mediates responses to pathogen DNA and to breakdown intermediates of intracellular mitochondrial and genomic DNA. STING is located predominantly on the endoplasmic reticulum; however, a fraction of the protein is found in the inner nuclear membrane. Though STING loss affects processes such as DNA repair and replication stress response, the role of nuclear STING is not understood. We established a proximity ligation assay-based approach to measure nuclear STING based on its proximity to the nuclear lamina. STING nuclear distribution was found to be affected by the expression of a mutant form of lamin A, progerin, and did not require STING C-terminal domain. STING was detected in micronuclei where it promoted the accumulation of cGAS in a manner dependent on oligomerization and activation. Nuclear STING was also found in human formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples of normal and cancerous female reproductive and lung tissues, where its levels were overall proportionate to the total cellular levels of STING.Subject areas: Health sciences, Biological sciences, Cell biology, Cancer systems biology

Introduction

Stimulator of interferon genes (STING) is a key component of the cGAS-STING pathway that mediates innate immune responses (IIRs) to pathogen-derived DNA and RNA and to various disturbances in the metabolism of a cell’s own genomic and mitochondrial DNA.1,2,3 STING regulates cell-intrinsic autophagy, apoptosis, senescence, and cell-extrinsic responses such as immune cell recruitment within tissues.4,5,6,7,8,9 STING may also regulate DNA damage and replication stress responses.10,11,12 Both pro- and anti-tumorigenic effects have been reported for STING, and its expression can be either lost or elevated in different cancers.13,14,15 Hyper-activation of STING is being developed as means to augment chemo-, radio-, and immunotherapies.13,14,16,17,18,19,20 STING is dysregulated in autoimmune diseases21,22 and in chronic inflammation.23,24,25,26 In all, the importance of STING in normal physiology and pathology cannot be overstated.27,28,29

STING is a transmembrane protein. Initial reports identified it in the nuclear envelope (NE)30 and other organelles31; however, its canonical IIR function is best studied on the cytoplasm-facing surface of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where STING’s function is described as three major steps: (1) STING binds the cyclic dinucleotide cGAMP produced by the DNA sensor cGAS and undergoes a conformational change that enables oligomerization; (2) STING-decorated membranes participate in vesicular transport from the ER to Golgi and perinuclear microsomes, where (3) TBK1 kinase docks to the oligomerized STING and phosphorylates it, which enables downstream events leading to induction of type 1 interferon genes and pro-inflammatory cytokines.32,33,34,35,36,37,38 With the discoveries that STING can activate IIRs to genomic and mitochondrial self-DNA, STING localization to the ER has been part and parcel of the model in which only the self-DNA that leaked into the cytoplasm can trigger the STING pathway.

While the enrichment of STING in the ER is indisputable, its smaller presence in the nucleus is expected and supported by experimental evidence. Because the ER is contiguous with the inner and outer nuclear membranes of the NE (INM and ONM, respectively), transmembrane proteins can diffuse along these membranes through lateral channels of the nuclear pores. Detailed examinations of the ER vs. NE distributions find nuclear pools even for the bona fide ER proteins such as calnexin.39 Enrichment of transmembrane proteins in the INM may be achieved via affinity for their nuclear interaction partners (the diffusion-retention model of localization).40,41,42,43 Following the identification of STING in the NE,30 Schirmer and Goffin labs independently confirmed INM localization of STING by immunogold-electron microcopy (EM),10,44 a finding also supported by subcellular fractionation and immunofluorescence (IF) in situ.11,45,46 STING was detected next to lamin A by FRET-FLIM and found to co-purify with several nuclear proteins including lamins10,44 and be proximal to emerin by TurboID.47 Most recently, we detected STING on the replicating and post-replicative chromatin by using iPOND,11 while Zhang et al. observed relative enrichment of STING in MEF nuclei in conjunction with the nuclear translocation of the transcription regulator AHR.46

In human tissue, STING expression follows the same pattern of staining as in cultured cells and is highly variable.15 Epithelia of normal bronchi, lung, fallopian tubes, and ovary have routinely been found to express some of the highest levels of STING in organisms, and the biological reasons for this are unclear.15,48,49 In lung and ovarian cancers, STING expression can be lost or gained.48,50 Only one study, to our knowledge, addressed nuclear STING expression in human tissues.10 The authors observed the co-localization of STING and lamin B1 IF signals in the nuclear rim of neoplastic cells in a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) of breast cancer. However, the resolution of the employed method of detection was not sufficient to distinguish the ONM from the INM/lamina localization, urging a different approach. In all, the functions, determinants, and biological implications of the nuclear pool of STING, the modes of control of the STING pathway activation or inhibition in the nucleus, and not the least, the issue of whether nuclear STING can be found in tissues rather than being a peculiarity of cultured cells, remain largely unknown and are important to be elucidated given the significance of STING in health and disease.

Here, we focused on the nuclear STING to begin to unravel its biological significance. We employed the proximity ligation assay (PLA) to monitor the nuclear STING through its proximity to lamins A/C and B1 and identify specific examples where STING can affect or be affected by the state of the NE or the nucleus. These examples are, respectively, STING presence in micronuclei (MN) and STING co-localization with the progerin variant of lamin A. Our data indicate that activation-dependent oligomerization of STING and its trafficking from the ER are not critical for its nuclear localization. Furthermore, STING mutants lacking the C-terminal domain could still be found in the nuclear proximity of the lamina. Lastly, we show evidence for the presence of nuclear STING in normal and cancerous human tissues and obtain the first data confirming that, by and large, a higher level of STING in a tissue correlates with more STING detectable in the tissue’s nuclei, though exceptions from this rule are found as well.

Results

STING is in close proximity to the nuclear lamina

Subcellular fractionations routinely find a subset of cellular STING, including the active STING phosphorylated on serine 366, in nuclear and/or chromatin fractions (refer to Lazarchuk et al.11 and Figure S1A), a feature that may not be an exception, as nuclear presence has also been noted for such ER proteins as EMC1 and calnexin (Figure S1A; also refer to Cheng et al.39). By IF and confocal microscopy, STING was found in the cytoplasm, perinuclear region, and often in the nuclear rim, where it co-localizes with the nuclear lamina (Figures 1A and S1B). Lamina formations that extend toward nuclear center can also co-stain with STING (Figure S1B). However, these co-localizations are insufficient to attribute nuclear localization to STING because STING in the ONM or nuclear-proximal ER can appear nuclear rim localized,51 while the intranuclear staining pattern of STING can be attributed to invaginations of both nuclear membranes into the nucleus.52 Thus, to differentiate between the ONM and the INM STING in situ, previous studies have used high-resolution microscopy and EM.10,44,53 PLA, in which a fluorescent signal is produced when two antigens are within 40 nm of each other,54 offers a way to monitor STING nuclear localization by regular and confocal microscopy. We observed a PLA signal for physical proximity of STING to lamins A/C and B1 within the perimeter of cellular nuclei under normal growth conditions in several cell lines and primary cells (Figures 1B–1D; see below). The PLA signal was specific and quantitative, as seen, e.g., in cases where LMNB1 levels are down, as in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS) cells55,56 (Figure 1E) or during oncogene-induced cell senescence57 (Figure 1F).

NE level of STING is reduced in lamin A/C-deficient cells53; however, the involvement of lamin A/C in STING partitioning to the NE has not been examined at biochemical level, likely due to the challenges of working with the nuclear lamina in vitro. We observed through PLA that calnexin, an ER marker, displays some reactivity with LMNA/C (Figure S1C), which justifies the argument that a signal in PLA with lamins does not necessarily indicate the existence of a stoichiometric complex between a lamin and its PLA partner but rather a close physical proximity of the INM and the lamina. We next asked if the localization of STING may be altered in cells with abnormal lamin A/C distribution, one example of which is found in cells expressing a mutant version of lamin A called progerin, the causal agent behind HGPS.58 We examined isogenic fibroblast lines expressing wild-type (WT) lamin A/C or a Δ50-aa truncation of lamin A, the most common progerin variant in HGPS,59 as well as three unrelated HGPS patient-derived primary fibroblast cultures. All of these cell lines also express endogenous lamin A/C, while the levels of progerin in these cell lines vary dramatically (Figure 2A). In addition, STING levels may be lower in progerin-expressing cells (Figure S2A), which can complicate the interpretation of STING × LMNA/C PLA. We, thus, focused on the spatial distribution of STING in progerin-expressing cells by IF in situ and confocal microscopy and observed that STING within the nuclear perimeter was more prevalent in progerin-high cells with intranuclear bundles of progerin (Figure 2B; nuclei 1 and 2 vs. nucleus 3). This translated into more nuclear relative to perinuclear STING across multiple independent experiments; however, the effect was modest (albeit statistically significant, Figure 2C), perhaps being diluted by low-progerin cells (Figure 2B). To examine this further, we analyzed confocal images to determine what fraction of the intranuclear area occupied by STING co-localized with progerin and plotted it as a function of progerin expression level for each nucleus (see “quantification and data analysis” in STAR Methods for more detail). The extent of STING and progerin spatial co-localization in the nuclei positively correlated with the progerin level (Figure 2D), suggesting that progerin affected STING distribution within the nucleus.

Progerin aggregates may induce NE invaginations of the INM or of both INM and ONM,60 which can be identified by co-staining for progerin and ribosomal proteins (Figure S2B). These invaginations could juxtapose the ONM over progerin bundles, creating an impression that more STING co-localizes with progerin, even if this STING is in the ONM. However, detection of a PLA signal between STING and progerin (Figures 2E and 2F) supports the argument that the co-localization of STING with progerin includes true physical proximity within the nucleus, likely associated with invaginations of the INM.

Canonical activation of STING and its localization

Oligomerization of STING, its trafficking between ER and Golgi membranes, and accumulation in the perinuclear area in the course of its canonical activation by cGAMP61,62 may affect STING’s mobility in the ONM/INM space. Activation of STING was previously shown to shift its distribution from the INM to the ONM, though not to restrict it to any one compartment.44 To induce cGAMP production and activate STING, we used a standard approach of transfecting cells with short dsDNA (e.g., interferon-stimulating DNA [ISD]). We observed a moderate reduction in the STING to lamin B1 PLA signal (STING × LMNB1) that could not be explained by a reduction in total levels of either STING or lamin B1 in the cell (Figure S2C). Likewise, treating cells with the STING agonist SR-717 that mimics its activation by cGAMP63 showed an overall modest reduction in STING × LMNB1 PLA levels compared to those in untreated cells in three out of four experiments summarized in Figure S2D.

To follow up, we generated STING mutants in which oligomerization and trafficking are altered. STING R284S mutant found in STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI),64 exhibits oligomerization and trafficking in the absence of cGAMP.65 STING Q273A/A277Q mutant (hereafter, QAAQ) fails to oligomerize and exit ER upon being stimulated by cGAMP and is functionally inactive.36 The mutants were introduced into U2OS cells as before11 (U2OS cells are STING-negative66), and the cells were flow sorted to select transduced cells with a particular level of STING (Figure 3A). Previously described WT STING-expressing U2OS cells11 were flow sorted to generate cultures with the expression level closest to those of the mutant STING cell lines (Figure 3A, WT-low). Of note, U2OS expressing the original and reduced levels of the WT STING showed correspondingly high and low STING × LMNB1 PLA signals, confirming that this readout detects quantitative changes in the partner proteins (Figure 3B).

STING mutant phenotypes such as activation of the bona fide downstream target IFNB1 gene and, in the case of STING R284S, oligomerization, were verified by RT-qPCR and native PAGE, respectively (Figures 3C and 3D). The R284S mutant was noted to have a less stable expression pattern than the QAAQ mutant, as STING-low to -negative cells increased with the passaging of the cell line, limiting the scope of analyses carried out with this mutant.

Both mutants still localized to the INM, as seen by PLA. Some quantitative, statistically significant differences between PLA signal levels of the WT and mutant STING proteins were observed; however, they could be explained by differences in the cellular levels of the mutants versus the WT STING (Figures 3E–3I). Interestingly, the QAAQ mutant exhibited the upregulation of both the STING × LMNB1 PLA signal and the level of lamin B1 in the nucleus as seen by IF in situ (Figure 3G). This change, however, did not reflect upregulation of the lamin B1 protein in the cell as measured by western blotting (Figure 3H), possibly indicating a change in lamin B1 state or accessibility. Another phenotype noted for the QAAQ mutant was a more irregular nuclear shape (Figure 3J), captured by a metric of nuclear convexity (Figure 3K, see STAR Methods for the definition). It is conceivable that the apparent change in lamin B1 state and the change in nuclear shape are related phenotypes.67

Overall, the data suggest that, while a quantitative change in STING distribution can be observed upon canonical stimulation of STING, STING oligomerization or activation-associated trafficking is neither an absolute barrier nor a requirement for STING retention in the nucleus. To follow up, we used a previously published STING C-terminal deletion mutant lacking the CTT domain responsible for the final steps of canonical activation of STING68 (STING Δ(aa342-STOP), Figure S3A) and constructed two mutants in which most or all of the C-terminal cyto- or nucleoplasm-facing portion of STING, encompassing the ligand-binding and the CTT domains, was deleted and replaced with an HA tag, as these deletions remove epitopes recognized by STING antibodies (Figure S4A). All mutants were transiently transfected into U2OS cells and analyzed by IF and PLA. Though the transfection efficiency was low, expression of the mutants was clearly evident in a fraction of transfected cells by IF (Figures S3B and S4B). Also, robust STING to LMNA/C or to LMNB1 PLA signal was observed in a fraction of cells transfected with STING Δ(aa342-STOP) (Figures S3C–S3E). Likewise, high HA × LMNA/C PLA signal was observed in a subset of cells transfected with STINGΔ(aa178-371)HA or STINGΔ(aa141-371)HA constructs and not in an HA epitope control expressed from an HA-IPpoI fusion construct,69 which showed distribution to both cytoplasm and nuclei (Figures S4C–S4E). Empty vector U2OS or mock-transfected U2OS cells also displayed only background PLA signal, as expected. These results suggest that the C-terminal domain (CTD) of STING is not critical for STING distribution to the INM.

STING is present in micronuclei

MN are nuclear membrane-enclosed fragments of the genome. MN are induced by DNA damage, replication stress, and faulty mitosis.70 MN envelope is not a faithful replica of a regular NE71,72; however, INM localization of STING makes it conceivable that STING is also present in MN. We first looked for MN-localized STING in the ovarian cancer-derived, BRCA1-negative cell line, UWB1.289,73 in which MN were readily induced by PARP inhibitor olaparib treatment.74 After 24 h of olaparib treatment, STING was detected, by PLA, to be proximal to lamin A/C or lamin B1, respectively, in 28% and 24% of MN (Figure 4A). Numbers of PLA-positive regular nuclei (i.e., primary nuclei, PN) in the same cells were 94% and 80%, respectively. This differential is not surprising because the levels of both lamins were lower overall in MN than in PN (Figure 4B). Complete loss of lamin B1 was seen in collapsing MN.75 Thus, the detection of STING in MN via its proximity to lamin B1 may indicate that these MN are intact and contradicts the view that MN collapse or invasion by the ER71 is the sole source of MN STING. Of note, STING was also detected by PLA at about the same rate in the MN of untreated cells; however, low frequencies of MN in these cells discouraged further quantitative analysis.

STING was also observed in the MN of U2OS cells engineered to re-express the protein (see “canonical activation of STING and its localization”). The cells were treated with olaparib for up to three days to enrich for MN. STING was readily detectable in the MN by STING × LMNA/C PLA regardless of the olaparib treatment, and PLA signal intensity was also lower in MN than in PN (Figures 4C and 4D). No PLA signal was observed in the MN or PN of the empty vector control U2OS (Figure 4D). We anticipated and confirmed that STING PLA signal in the MN overall positively correlated with that of the same-cell PN (Figure 4E, see STAR Methods for details on MN-PN pair assignment). We also verified by IF that a majority of olaparib-induced MN were positive for lamin A/C and lamin B1 as late as day 3 of treatment, although there was a clear variation in lamin levels, which fits with the observation that not all MN were PLA-positive. By IF, STING could be seen either enriched at the MN perimeter, suggesting its localization to the MN envelope or presence throughout the MN at a level equal to or higher than STING level in the cytoplasm (Figure S5).

STING affects MN metabolism

MN envelope can rupture, which can lead to MN collapse. Ruptures are associated with the accumulation of cGAS at MN and the activation of the downstream signaling through STING,76,77 albeit this signaling may be modest compared to that triggered by the transfection of naked DNA.78,79 Our finding of STING in MN raises the question of whether STING can affect cGAS recruitment and the fate of MN in general. We investigated this in STING-negative and STING-complemented U2OS cells treated with olaparib. In untreated cells, MN prevalence was comparable between these cell lines, standing at 8%–12% (Table S1). Marked induction of MN was noticeable only by day 2 of olaparib treatment (Table S1). Remarkably, we observed that cells with STING QAAQ displayed almost 2-fold fewer MN on days 2 and 3 of treatment than the WT STING cells or vector control cells at the same time points (Figure 5A and Table S1). Unlike a one-day treatment (Figures S6A and S6B), a three-day treatment with olaparib reduced colony formation and led to the appearance of senescent cells in the treated empty vector and WT STING cells, but, remarkably, STING QAAQ cells were unaffected (Figures 5B and S6C). On the other hand, the R284S mutant of STING was close to the WT STING in terms of its MN percentage and olaparib sensitivity phenotypes (Figures 5B, 5C, and S6D). Western blotting showed that PARP1 (the major target of olaparib) expression was comparable across the cell lines and conditions, and PARP1 or STING QAAQ levels could not account for the olaparib-resistant phenotype of this mutant (Figure S6E).

PLA to lamin A/C demonstrated that STING was retained in 50%–70% of MN in all cell lines throughout the olaparib treatment. We next investigated the STING QAAQ MN phenotype more deeply by comparing the mean intensities of lamin B1, lamin A/C IF, and STING × LMNA/C PLA signals for each MN-PN pair (as MN/PN ratios) in the WT STING and the STING QAAQ cells. As for STING × LMNA/C PLA described above (Figure 4), lamin A/C and lamin B1 mean intensities overall positively correlated for MN-PN pairs, as expected (Figures S7A and S7B), with overall MN levels being a fraction of PN levels (Figure 5D). Interestingly however, STING QAAQ cells showed a moderate but significant increase in lamin B1, lamin A/C, and STING × LMNA/C MN/PN level ratios compared with the WT STING (Figure 5D). Higher levels of retention of lamin A/C80 or lamin B181 in the MN of STING QAAQ cells suggest a potentially higher integrity of MN in these cells.82 STING QAAQ cells also had overall larger MN than the WT STING and vector-only cells (Figure 5E).

We next measured cGAS recruitment to MN, which is considered a marker of collapsing MN.76,77 cGAS IF showed bright staining in a fraction of MN (Figures 6A and S7C). In general, the percentage of cGAS+ MN, which was defined as MN with cGAS mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) above the background, ranged between 20% and 30% across the samples and was moderately increased in olaparib-treated samples (Table S2). Remarkably, STING QAAQ cells consistently scored lower %cGAS+ MN than WT STING and the STING-deficient control on day 3 of treatment but not earlier (Figure 6B). By day 3, there also accrued a significant difference between WT STING cells and mutant STING QAAQ or vector control cells in terms of the MFI of cGAS measured in cGAS+ MN (Figure 6C). cGAS+ MN displayed overall higher cGAS signal levels in the WT STING cells than in the empty vector control and even more so, in the STING QAAQ cells. Interestingly, STING R284S cells were very close to WT STING in terms of their levels of cGAS in cGAS+ MN (Figure S7D).

Together, these data suggest that STING affects the metabolism of MN induced by olaparib, potentially in more than one way. Comparison of STING-expressing and STING-deficient cells revealed that STING positively regulates cGAS accumulation in MN under conditions of prolonged stress of PARP inhibition (PARPi). In addition, a functionally inactive STING QAAQ mutant that is unable to form active oligomers has a more prominent phenotype than STING absence under the same conditions, reducing cGAS accumulation and MN formation and promoting cellular survival. The latter phenotype may reflect the involvement of STING QAAQ in tolerance or mitigation of olaparib-induced damage. One of the prominent effects of olaparib on the genome, mediated by PARP1 inhibition, is the abnormal excess of single-stranded DNA.83,84 Phospho-RPA, i.e., RPA32 phosphorylated on serine 33, is a marker of single-stranded DNA gaps in DNA. We observed marginal induction of RPA S33P in the PN of all cell lines after one day of olaparib treatment; however, by day three, WT-STING PN exhibited a markedly higher RPA S33P than STING-QAAQ PN (Figure 6D). RPA S33P was also detectable in a minority of MN; however, its levels did not differ between the cell lines even at day three (Figure 6E). The data suggest the following: first, the STING QAAQ phenotype is revealed once the genome-wide response to olaparib has developed, as evidenced by the accumulation of the single-stranded DNA marker phospho-RPA in the PN. Second, STING QAAQ may suppress the formation or processing of single-stranded DNA during a prolonged olaparib challenge, which may determine the rate of MN formation and the state of chromatin inside the MN.

STING is detected in the nuclei of normal and cancer tissues

We next asked if we can detect nuclear STING in normal and/or tumor human tissue specimens and focused on two of the tissue types that are known to express some of the highest levels of STING—epithelia of the lung and female reproductive tissue.49 Success of the detection of nuclear STING by PLA depends on the levels of lamins, and while lamin B1 and lamin A/C are expected to be expressed in these epithelia (Uhlen et al.85, https://v22.proteinatlas.org), variation is possible. Immunostaining of formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSOC) and adjacent ovary tissue revealed specific signals for lamin A/C and STING (Figure 7A, images 1–8), with regional heterogeneity (images 6 and 7). Another section of the same area was subjected to STING × LMNA/C PLA, resulting in the expected red puncta signal (Figure 7A, image 9; also see Figure 7B for another example of IF and PLA results in the same HGSOC area). The same HGSOC specimen was found to express high lamin B1 levels and, accordingly, scored positive for STING × LMNB1 PLA (Figure 7C, images 1 and 2). As expected, omitting one (Figure S8) or both (Figure 7C, 3) primary antibodies from the PLA protocol suppressed the signal. Further study found a starkly non-overlapping pattern of the expression of STING and lamin B1 in a normal fallopian tube epithelium (Figure S9, also see “quantitative assessment of nuclear vs. total levels of STING in female reproductive tissue”): high STING cells were lamin B1-low, and vice versa, resulting in STING × LMNB1 PLA negativity.

We next examined a specimen of mucinous adenocarcinoma of the lung and adjacent normal tissue. Normal airway epithelia exhibited high expression of STING, as expected, and medium-to-high expressions of lamin A/C (Figure 8A, images 1–3 and 5–7; Figures S10A and S10B), while alveolar epithelium (Figure S10A, bottom) was predominantly STING-low. Interestingly, not all instances of airway epithelia displayed STING × LMNA/C PLA signal (Figure 8; image 4 vs. 8). Examination of additional regions of the same sample found more instances of STING × LMNB1 PLA-positive and PLA-negative areas (Figure 8B; images 5 and 6). Of note, STING ×2 PLA signal showed both nuclear and cytoplasmic distribution, as anticipated, while STING × lamin PLA was associated only with the nuclei.

The tumor area of the sample was STING-negative (Figure 8C, images 1 [yellow arrows vs. white arrows] and 2; Figure S10), lamin A/C-high, and lamin B1-low (Figure 8C, images 1 [yellow arrows] and 2). PLA results for STING ×2 (images 3 and 4), STING × LMNA/C (image 5), and STING × LMNB1 (image 6) reflected these differences in the levels of STING and its partner proteins. Together, the findings in ovary, fallopian tube, and lung samples demonstrate the existence of INM STING in normal and cancer human tissues and show the responsiveness of the PLA readout to STING and lamin levels in the tissues. The results also favor lamin A/C, due to its more ubiquitous expression, as a PLA partner for the detection of STING and reveal the existence of regional variability of STING and lamin levels, as well as of the nuclear STING signal levels within the tissue, some of which remain unexplained.

Quantitative assessment of nuclear vs. total levels of STING in female reproductive tissue

We next asked if the correlation between total and nuclear levels of STING could be quantified in tissues to assess their interrelationship. The level of nuclear STING may be proportionate to the total STING level; however, it is also possible that, in some cases, the level of LMNA/C or other unknown factors can dictate how much STING is retained in the nucleus.

Copies of a tissue microarray (TMA) of 99 benign, borderline, and malignant ovarian tumors and normal ovary tissues were obtained from TissueArray.com and stained for STING and LMNA/C in the IF or PLA formats. Table S3 lists the TMA samples, the generated values, and reasons for excluding the samples from the analysis, such as a missing sample, a staining defect, etc. We inspected the H&E staining images of the TMA samples made available by the vendor and reclassified several of the samples of low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC) as benign or borderline tumors upon review by a board-certified pathologist (EC). Figure 9A shows examples of STING-negative and STING-high HGSOC as measured by STING IF and STING × LMNA/C PLA. We collected the IF and PLA images focusing primarily on normal or tumor epithelia, which are STING-positive, as opposed to stroma, which is expected to be STING-negative (https://v22.proteinatlas.org). Normal ovary tissue controls of the TMA comprised exclusively stromal tissue and no epithelia and, indeed, were STING-negative. However, 6 out of 15 cases of ovary tissues from uterine tumor patients showed some presence of STING in the ovary stroma and, thus, were included in the final dataset. Nevertheless, it should be noted that as a non-epithelial lineage, stromal tissue is not an ideal normal control for carcinomas.

All STING-positive and a subset of STING-negative samples were quantified (see STAR Methods and the legend for more detail on the derivation of STING, LMNA/C, IF signal values, and PLA values). Briefly, hundreds of per-cell values were generated for each sample and averaged. Figure 9B shows these per-sample averages of STING staining intensity grouped by the tumor or normal tissue type. Of note, the STING positivity rate and range of expression for endometrioid carcinoma (ENOC), HGSOC, and LGSOC were similar to those observed in a previous study48 (Figure 9B, top). The PLA signal averages largely tracked with the total STING level averages, though some deviations were present. In particular, the total STING level in malignant tissues translated to a less prominent nuclear STING signal compared to the relationship between total and nuclear STING levels in benign/borderline tissues (Figure 9B, bottom).

For a more quantitative look at the data, we measured correlations between per-sample STING PLA, STING IF, and LMNA/C IF averages. When computed across all samples, a statistically significant positive correlation (Pearson’s R = 0.397, p = 0.009) between the total and nuclear STING averages was observed. Also, the linear regression analysis performed on all samples revealed positive associations between nuclear STING by PLA and either total STING (p = 0.0001) or LMNA/C (p = 0.03) IF averages. Within groups, the only correlations that remained significant were the positive correlations between STING IF and PLA averages in malignancies in general (p = 0.01, Figure 9C) and in ENOC in particular (p = 0.04, Figure 9D). These data suggest that, while the nuclear STING level tends to be proportionate to its overall level, some variability is evident. Many factors including the inevitable tissue heterogeneity could contribute to the overall dispersion in the data, but LMNA/C level is an obvious potential variable. When we examined the association between per-cell STING and LMNA/C IF values for each sample, we found that, in most samples, these values displayed a significant negative correlation, which persisted even after excluding from the analysis all samples that displayed background levels of STING (Figure 9E). For HGSOCs, a significant negative correlation (Pearson’s R = −0.46, p = 0.04) also emerged when comparing STING and LMNA/C level averages for all cases (Figure 9F). This unexpected relationship between STING and LMNA/C levels can dampen the positive association between STING IF and STING PLA values. Lastly, we compared the total and nuclear STING sample means as a function of tumor grade for HGSOCs, the most represented type in the TMA. A potential tendency for early-stage HGSOCs to display more nuclear STING was observed, but none of the differences were statistically significant (Figure S11), which was not unexpected given that our study is underpowered to establish stage-specific variation.

To minimize between-sample heterogeneity as another source of variability, we turned to a more detailed analysis of individual samples. LGSOC, which is less common than HGSOC, is a slow progressing but ultimately fatal disease in need of more effective therapies.86 LGSOCs frequently express high STING,48,87 possibly because at least some of the LGSOC precursors—ciliated epithelial cells abundant in the fallopian tube epithelia48,88,89—are STING-high48 (Figure S12), unlike the likely precursors of HGSOC—secretory cells,90,91,92 which are typically STING-low or -negative in the fallopian tube (see https://v22.proteinatlas.org). Thus, LGSOCs enable a unique comparison of the “isogenic” normal epithelia and their malignant counterparts. We identified three LGSOC cases in which areas of neoplastic and normal epithelium of the fallopian tube were available within the specimen and expressed various levels of STING (e.g., Figure S12). Quantitative data for STING and LMNA/C in the IF and PLA formats were collected from one or more areas each of normal and cancerous epithelia for each sample. STING levels ranged widely in carcinoma areas and were high in the ciliated cells of the normal epithelia, as expected,48 where the density of these cells vs. STING-low secretory cells likely defined the area-level average of STING expression (Figure S12). Notably, in two out of three cases, at least some areas of normal epithelia displayed more total and nuclear STING than the isogenic tumor tissue. Linear regression analysis showed a positive relationship (p = 0.01) between the total and nuclear STING per-area averages across all samples (Figure 10). Positive correlations between these variables were also observed within normal and tumor groups; however, the data reached statistical significance only for normal areas (p = 0.037). Of note, LMNA/C and STING levels per cell, determined by IF, displayed predominantly positive or no correlation in these samples, and there was no correlation between per-area averages of LMNA/C and nuclear STING (not shown). Taken together, the data definitively show that both normal and tumor epithelial cells carry nuclear STING and that the overall nuclear STING level increases with the increasing total STING level. However, the finer aspects of this relationship may still differ between normal and cancer cells, which more extensive studies may reveal in the future.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrated the utility of PLA-based detection of STING proximity to the nuclear lamina in locating nuclear STING in cultured human cells and in archival patient-derived tissues as well as quantifying its levels as a function of STING mutations, NE integrity, and cell type. We demonstrated the specificity of PLAs with lamin A/C (or lamin B1) as one of the partners (e.g., for laminA/C, Figures 1C, S3D, S3E, S4D, S3E, and S8; for lamin B1, Figures 1D–1F, 7C, 8B, 8C, and S9; also see Lazarchuk et al.93 for lamin B1 PLA control). We show that the PLA can be used as a readout for STING deletion mutant analyses, even in transient transfection assays where transfection efficiency is low. Our results demonstrated that nuclear STING is not a peculiarity of cultured human cells but can be observed in normal epithelia and carcinomas of the human reproductive tissue and lungs.

According to the diffusion-retention model,40,41,42,43 STING can localize to the INM by a combination of the lateral diffusion through contiguous ER and nuclear membranes and the association with nuclear proteins. Our quantitative analysis of the tissues supports a prediction of the diffusion-retention model that STING level in the nucleus is a function of its total cellular level. However, it remains to be seen which proteins, if any, are responsible for actively retaining STING in the nucleus, and if lamins are among these proteins. Prior evidence supporting this role for lamins includes the identification of lamins in STING immunoprecipitates from the NE or nuclear extracts by MS10,44 and the responsiveness of STING INM level to lamin A/C levels.44,53 In our study, progerin was in physical proximity to STING, and progerin expression expanded the nuclear footprint of STING. However, this phenotype can be mediated through an increase in intrusions of the INM into the nucleus rather than through an association between progerin and STING. Further, detection of calnexin-LMNA/C PLA signal suggests that proximity ligation to a lamin may be due to the structural proximity of the lamina to the macromolecular assemblies of the INM and may not always indicate a direct, stoichiometric protein-protein interaction between a lamin and its PLA partner. Our data suggest that the entire C-terminal domain of STING is not critical for STING localization to the INM, but the possibility of protein-protein contacts contributing to nuclear retention of STING cannot yet be ruled out.

Using our PLA approach on FFPE human tissue samples, we found that nuclear STING was not an exclusive feature either of a cancerous cell or a normal cell and that, overall, the tissues with a higher total level of STING also displayed more nuclear STING. Thus, a mere finding of nuclear STING is unlikely to have any prognostic value for malignancy. However, there was enough dispersion in the data to keep open the question of whether the relative ratios of total to nuclear STING may vary as a function of tumor or cell type. Answering this question will determine whether this ratio can be used as a biomarker to correlate with clinical variables, particularly in cases where high expression levels of total STING do not show prognostic or therapeutic significance.49,94

Over 50% of serous carcinomas (HGSOCs and LGSOCs) express moderate-to-high levels of STING15; however, in neither of these carcinomas do the total STING levels correlate with clinical outcomes, suggesting that additional variables are at play. This raises the question of whether the relative enrichment level of STING in the nuclei (the nuclear to total STING ratio), rather than its total level, may correlate with survival in serous carcinomas. In our HGSOC and LGSOC data, correlation between the nuclear and total STING levels was not statistically significant. A substantially larger dataset will be required to establish, with sufficient statistical power, if there exists a subset of cases in which the nuclear to total STING ratio is significantly altered. The known biological variables will need to be accounted for carefully, e.g., lamin levels in a particular cell type or cancer cell of origin. In the normal epithelia of the fallopian tube, the expression of lamin A/C is supposed to be lower in ciliated cells, which are STING-high, than in secretory cells, which are STING-negative or -low (https://v22.proteinatlas.org). However, we also saw an inverse correlation between STING and LMNA/C levels in many, though not all, of the examined carcinomas of the ovary, particularly in HGSOCs, which originates from cells other than the ciliated cells (reviewed by Shih et al. and Beddows et al.95,96). Lamin B1 is an alternative to lamin A/C for the detection of STING in the nucleus; however, at least in normal secretory vs. ciliated cells, the lamin B1 expression profile is markedly opposite to that of STING according to the Protein Atlas and our data (Figure S10). In addition, some ovarian carcinomas, in particular LGSOC, often express low lamin B1 (JS unpub.). In summary, detection of nuclear STING in tissues via PLA invites a prospect of developing it as a biomarker; however, such development will be contingent on collecting more data, including validation of additional nuclear protein partners to pair with STING in PLA.

The biological significance of nuclear STING in human tissues is not yet understood in full. The molecular findings described in this paper are consistent with the notion that integrity of the STING canonical stimulation pathway is not critical to its nuclear distribution. However, more effort, and particularly the identification of STING localization mutants, is needed to fully establish if this is the case or whether these two processes modulate each other at a more subtle level. Even so, what functions or activities can nuclear STING perform that may modify our understanding of how the cGAS-STING pathway regulates cellular homeostasis? The presence of STING in the MN and its effect on MN metabolism may be one such function.

MN are common in cancer cells and are induced by chemo- and radiotherapies and thought to form around lagging chromosomes or chromosome fragments after mitosis, though other modes of MN generation have also been noted.97,98,99,100,101 The envelope of MN can be low on important constituents, including lamin B1, which has led to a notion that it is prone to rupture and collapse.80,99,102,103 High curvature of the MN envelope due to the small size of MN has been shown to disrupt the lamin B1 meshwork, weakening the MN envelope.104,105 Release of chromatin into the cytoplasm due to MN rupture can precipitate its further fragmentation and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, which can feedforward to senescence or cell-extrinsic cell death but also fuel tumor progression and metastasis.98,100,101 These features make MN stability an important, if challenging, therapeutic target.

MN collapse has been shown to be highly regulated,75,100,106 and, while the recruitment of cGAS to MN is indisputable, the extent of the activation of the pathway downstream of cGAS has been questioned.78,79 Our study shows that STING is present in MN regardless of its ability to undergo cGAMP-dependent oligomerization and trafficking, which are prerequisites for its canonical activation. Furthermore, STING promotes the recruitment of cGAS to MN induced by the genotoxic stress of PARPi. Cells expressing the STING Q273A/A277Q (QAAQ) mutant, which is a loss-of-function mutant for STING oligomerization and canonical activation, recruit less cGAS in cGAS-positive MN, contain fewer cGAS-positive MN, and form fewer MN overall than both WT STING cells and STING null cells upon PARPi. STING QAAQ cells also have larger MN with relatively higher density of STING, lamin A/C, and lamin B1 in MN (compared to their adjacent PN), than WT STING cells. Larger size of MN and higher levels of lamins in the MN are associated with higher stability of the MN envelope.80,81,82

To explain our data in the context of the current research, we propose a hypothetical model in which STING participates in destabilizing MN (Figure S13). Because STING was detected in lamin B-positive MN and a much higher percentage of MN were found to be STING-positive (>50%) than being cGAS-positive (20%–30%), we argue that STING can be present in the intact as well as ruptured MN. Small tears in the MN envelope that expose enough DNA for cGAS recruitment and activation, or the activation of the cGAS resident on the chromatin of MN107 can trigger initial activation of cGAS. This may yield a limited release of cGAMP, insufficient for a measurable global STING activation response but sufficient to oligomerize/activate STING local to cGAS in the MN space. We propose that the oligomerization of STING can further destabilize the MN envelope, which will attract more cGAS to the DNA thereby exposed. One hypothetical possibility for such destabilization is an increase in the envelope’s curvature, which would disrupt the lamin B1 network.105 In fact, studies on STING activation in the ER have found that the oligomerization of cGAMP-bound STING promotes curvature of the ER membrane owing to the structural properties of the oligomer108 as well as by indirect mechanisms.109,110 It is possible that the MN envelope can be subject to similar effects. Our observations that STING QAAQ oligomerization- and activation-defective mutant does not support the progressive accumulation of cGAS in PARPi-induced MN and has relatively higher lamin levels in their MNs are consistent with this model. While many aspects of the model are currently speculative, it will be possible to validate them in future studies.

It is possible that the processes postulated above may also occur in the PN; however, their impact on the stability of the envelope of PN may be proportionately smaller due to a much larger size of the PN. Lastly, while we did not expect that the R284S mutation in STING will be epistatic for cGAS accumulation in olaparib-induced MN, this may indicate that the WT STING is already as active as it can be in this compartment.

The fact that STING QAAQ cells are more resistant to olaparib than WT STING cells but there is no difference between the latter and STING-negative cells suggests that the cytotoxic outcome of cGAS accumulation in MN may involve STING-dependent and STING-independent pathways. It is also possible that cytotoxicity is a consequence of PARPi that is not related to cGAS accumulation in the MN or MN fate in general. Indeed, while PARPi can activate STING and the IIR, STING-dependent cytotoxicity of PARPi in BRCA-proficient cells has been linked to cell-extrinsic mechanisms111,112,113,114,115,116 that are absent in our tissue culture model. Cell-intrinsic olaparib resistance displayed by STING QAAQ cells can then be attributed to a phenotype other than suppression of the cGAS-STING-mediated effect on MN. For example, these cells also appeared to generate less PARPi-associated damage in the PN than the WT STING cells. This property of STING QAAQ is intriguing. We have previously implicated STING in response to replication stress associated with replication forks stalled by hydroxyurea,11 and it will be of interest to further study STING QAAQ in the context of replication stress in general and PARPi-induced replication stress in particular. That said, while it is theoretically possible that the reduced PARPi damage in STING QAAQ cells is the sole source of all of its MN phenotypes, it is currently hard to envision how, for example, it could lead to the higher lamin levels in the MN.

The findings of STING in MN are significant for human tissue physiology. STING agonism in combination with PARPi is being developed as a therapeutic strategy against ovarian and other cancers.113,115,116,117,118 The molecular underpinnings of this strategy are thought to be the leakage of DNA into the cytoplasm and STING activation in the tumor, followed by the tumor microenvironment-mediated immune response. Our data allow to propose that the STING load in PARPi-induced MN, which is related to STING load in the PN, may contribute to the magnitude of the eventual STING response in the tumor as well as in the normal ciliated epithelia, with the latter being an undesirable side effect. Understanding the factors that may differentially affect STING load in the PN (and MN) of normal vs. cancer tissues may one day help optimize therapy.

Limitations of the study

The nature of the PLA and the organization of the NE/lamina layer did not allow us to differentiate between a topological physical proximity and a stoichiometric interaction between the lamina and NE proteins. While our study documents the presence of STING in the MN and the effect of STING on MN stability, additional experiments are needed to validate parts of our current hypothesis and, in particular, confirm that an MN can be destabilized by its resident STING, rather than being affected by the cGAS-STING pathway indirectly. Our FFPE study was devised for discovery and hypothesis generation, and it is underpowered to identify subtypes of ovarian tumors in which the correlation between the nuclear and total STING levels is significantly modified or to uncover the correlations between clinical parameters such as tumor stage and levels of nuclear STING. Our quantitative findings in ovarian cancer FFPE sections lay a foundation for future, dedicated clinical studies but are, thus far, preliminary.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cells and cell lines

See key resources table for the list of cell lines and primary human cells used in this study. 82-6pBlox human skin fibroblasts with and without expression of progerin have been described in.120 75-8 is a human primary fibroblast from an HGPS patient (case mm758), described in.123 The immortalized WI38hTERT human fetal lung fibroblast line (F) contains an inducible GFP-RAF1-ER transgene and was originally described in,119 generation of senesced cells of this line is described in.93 Hi3 primary skin fibroblast cells were described in.124 HGADFN122 (5yo F), HGADFN127 (3yo F), HGADFN143 (8yo M) are Hutchinson-Gilford LMNA/C mutant human primary skin fibroblasts. UWB1.289 is a BRCA1-null human ovarian cancer cell line described in.73

Unless specified otherwise, these primary cells and cell lines were grown in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Minimal Essential Medium (DMEM) with L-glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum, FBS (Hyclone) and antibiotics. WI38hTERT GFP-RAF1-ER was grown in Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) with L-glutamine and sodium pyruvate (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, Nonessential Amino Acids, and antibiotics. UWB1.289 were grown in 1:1 mixture of RPMI and MGEM (Lonza) supplemented with Single Quots (Lonza), 3% fetal bovine serum, and antibiotics. All cell lines were kept in a humidified 5% CO2, 37°C incubator. Mycoplasma testing was performed using the UW/FHCC Cancer Consortium Shared Resource Specimen processing service https://sharedresources.fredhutch.org/services/mycoplasma-testing. The cell lines were not STR-profiled for this study. The key features of cell lines, i.e., GFP-RAF1-ER expression for WI38hTERT GFP-RAF1-ER, BRCA1-null status for UWB1.289, and progerin expression for HGPS lines have been verified.

Human tissue samples

With prior approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB #9362) at the University of Washington, formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were retrieved from anatomic pathology archives in the Department of Laboratory Medicine and Pathology through the Northwest Biospecimen. Search criteria include serous carcinoma (high grade and low grade), serous borderline tumor and lung adenocarcinoma. Tumor and normal tissue blocks (1–2 each case) were selected by a board-certified pathologist (EC). Unstained tissue slides with each section at 4-micron thickness were then obtained from the corresponding tissue blocks through the UW histopathology core service. All slides were de-identified and assigned research identification numbers for downstream assays.

Tissue microarray of benign and malignant ovarian tumors and normal controls (OV991) was purchased from TissueArray.com.

Method details

Drugs and other reagents

4-hydroxytamoxifen, 4-HT, stock (Millipore-Sigma) was 40 μM in ethanol and was used at a final concentration of 20 nM for four-five days to induce senescence in WI38hTERT GFP-RAF1-ER as previously described.93 Olaparib (CST) was at 10 mM in DMSO was used at 10 μM throughout. SR-717 (MedChemExpress) stock was at 10 mM in DMSO and was used at 6-10 μM for 6 h. All stocks were stored at −20°C.

Constructs

pTRIP-SFFV-mtagBFP-2A STING, pTRIP-SFFV-mtagBFP-2A STING-342STOP, and the parental empty vector were a gift from Nicolas Manel (respectively, Addgene plasmid # 102586; http://n2t.net/addgene:102586; Addgene plasmid # 102588; http://n2t.net/addgene:102588; and Addgene plasmid # 102585; http://n2t.net/addgene:102585). The R284S mutation was introduced into the STING ORF in pTRIP-SFFV-mtagBFP-2A STING using QuickChange Multi site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent) with a R284S1_fwd primer. The Q273A, A277Q mutations were introduced using Gibson assembly of the BamHI, XhoI-linearized pTRIP-SFFV-mtagBFP-2A STING vector backbone and PCR fragments generated with Q273AA277QF/S358Aseq.R and STINGORF.F/Q273AR primer pairs using Q5 DNA polymerase and pTRIP-SFFV-mtagBFP-2A STING as template (see Resources Table). Virus generation from these constructs and cell transduction were as described.125 Live transduced cells were sorted on the BD FACS Aria III cell sorter based on the level of BFP expression. STINGΔ(aa141-371) and STINGΔ(aa178-371) deletions combined with HA epitope insertion were generated from pTRIP-SFFV-mtagBFP-2A STING as template and primer pairs delCTDV2/V4 and delCTDV2/V3, respectively, using QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent). Mutations were sequence-verified. pBABe-ER-HA-IPpoI (Addgene plasmid # 32565; http://n2t.net/addgene:32565) was a gift from Michael Kastan.69 Transient transfections of these plasmids into U2OS cells were performed with lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), and transfected cells were analyzed at 36–48 h post-transfection.

Western blotting

Proteins were visualized on Western blots by ECL (Thermo Scientific) and quantified using FluorChem Imager (Alpha Inotech). For presentation, images were saved in TIFF format, adjusted for brightness/contrast and cropped in GIMP, then assembled into figures in CorelDraw. Image brightness/contrast adjustments were made across all lanes of each protein measured. In some cases, lane order was changed and extra lanes were deleted.

Preparation of samples for and running of the Blue Native PAGE followed the protocol in65 using NativePAGE Novex Bis-Tris Gel System (Life Technologies) and manufacturer’s protocols. Cells were lysed in 10% glycerol, 25 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH7.0 buffer supplemented with 1% DDM and protease inhibitors, by rotating for 30 min at 4C. Lysates were cleared at 16,000xg for 10 min. Lysates were mixed with 4x native sample buffer, run on NativePAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose for Western blotting.

RNA isolation and RT qPCR

RNAs were isolated using RNeasy Plus RNA isolation kit (Qiagen). 2 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems) per manufacturer’s protocol. cDNAs were diluted 1:10 and 1 μL of diluted cDNA was used per qPCR reaction. Reactions were run in triplicates with iTaq Universal SYBR Green supermix (Bio-Rad) and the following pairs of primers: 5′ACAACTTTGGCATTGAA3′ and 5′GATGCAGGGATGATGTTCTG3′ for GAPDH; and Sigma-Aldrich KiCqStart predesigned pairs H_IFNB1_1. Triplicate Ct values were averaged, normalized to GAPDH, and fold induction of mRNAs was determined according to the 2–ΔΔCt method. Significance was determined in two-tailed t-tests performed on ΔΔCt values.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA) and immunofluorescence (IF) in situ

Protein/protein PLA and IF in situ staining protocols for cultured cells were performed as described previously,11,93,126 except that after 2% formaldehyde fixation for 20 min cells were washed twice in PBS, permeabilized by addition of 4°C 90% methanol in PBS and used after at least 30 min at −20°C or stored at −20°C. For IF and PLA, cells were washed 2 × 5 min in PBS, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS, washed again and blocked with PBS/3% BSA/3% Normal goat serum for 20 min at room temperature then stained with primary antibodies for 1h at RT. Working antibody dilutions are listed in the Resources table. After washing 3 × 5min in 0.3% BSA/0.1% Tween 20/PBS, coverslips were incubated for 1h with secondary antibodies for IF (at RT) or for PLA (at 37°C), diluted in, respectively, PBS/3% BSA/3% Normal goal serum or Duolink antibody dilution buffer. For IF, coverslips were washed as before, dried, and mounted. For PLA, coverslips were washed 2 × 5 min in PLA wash buffer (10 mM Tris HCl pH7.5/150 mM NaCl/0.05% Tween 20) and incubated in PLA ligation mixture for 30 min at 37°C in accordance with our detailed methods paper127 and the manufacturer’s manual. Next, coverslips were washed 2 × 2min in PLA wash buffer and incubated with Duolink red detection reagents per manufacturer’s manual for 90 min at 37°C. Coverslips were then washed 2 × 10 min in 200 mM Tris HCL pH7.5/100 mM NaCl, dried, and mounted in DUOLINK DAPI mounting medium (Millipore-Sigma).

PLA and IF procedures for FFPE tissues were as follows. Slides were baked for 1h at 58°C and immediately deparaffinized in 3 changes of xylene, 2 min each, then rehydrated in 3 changes of 100%, 2 changes of 95% and one change of 70% ethanol series, 2 min each, followed by 3 changes of PBS, 2 min each. Slides were incubated in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 8 min, washed in 3 changes of PBS, 2 min each, and incubated in TE/tween antigen retrieval buffer (10 mM Tris Base, 1 mM EDTA, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 9.0) at <95°C for 8 min. Slides were left to cool in the retrieval buffer for 30 min, washed 3 times 2 min each in PBS, blocked in PBS/3% BSA for 1h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. Subsequently, the staining proceeded for PLA or IF as described above for cultured cells.

Microscopy imaging

Images of cells were collected under Zeiss Axiovert 200M microscope with a 40× magnification objective using Micro Manager software. Confocal images were collected under Leica SP8X with 63× objective or Zeiss LSM 710 with 40× objective using manufacturers’ software. Our general acquisition guidelines are summarized in.127 Briefly, 30–50 images representing the entire slide except the areas of highly confluent cell density (if present) were collected per sample for cultured cells. For tissues, several zones present in multiple sections of the same sample were identified by their distinct appearance and imaged in IF and PLA preparations. Low magnification images of tissue sections (fluorescence or H & E staining) and of crystal violet-stained cell colony outgrowths were collected using Cytation 7 image reader and Gen5 3.15 software (Agilent).

Transfection of interferon-stimulating DNA (ISD)

2 μg of ISD (Invivogen) was transfected into cells using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) per manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were incubated for 6 h and harvested for RNA analyses or fractionation. Mock-transfected controls received lipofectamine/Optimem mixture only.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Image analysis

Cell Profiler software was used for IF and PLA in situ images. Quantitative data were collected for, on average, 600 cells (min∼100, max∼1000) per sample per experiment. Mean fluorescence intensities (MFI) per nuclear, perinuclear, or cytoplasmic areas were measured using standard modules. PLA foci were identified using IdentifyPrimaryObjects module with custom diameter cutoffs and assigned to nuclei using RelateObjects module. Object area and nuclear convexity (i.e., the proportion of pixels in the convex hull that are also in the object) were measured in the same images using MeasureObjectAreaShape module in Cell Profiler. Percent colocalization within nuclei was measured in confocal images as follows: STING and progerin “objects” were identified within each nucleus based on their fluorescence intensity, and their spatial colocalization was determined using MeasureColocalization module. For each nucleus, an integrated area of the overlap between STING and progerin objects was normalized to an integrated area of STING objects, to derive percent colocalization.

Micronuclei detection in Cell Profiler was based on a pipeline by Alain Jeanrenaud available at https://forum.image.sc/. After background corrections, micronuclei and primary nuclei were identified in the DAPI channel using IdentifyPrimaryObjects Cell Profiler modules with customized size cutoffs and thresholding strategies. Micronuclei and nuclei were designated as belonging to the same cell by finding the nucleus closest to each micronucleus using ExpandOrShrinkObjects and RelateObjects modules. The pipelines (for nuclear IF and PLA measurements, cytoplasmic and perinuclear staining for STING, micronuclei measurement, and colocalization) are available upon request. Global background fluorescence value outside the nuclei was collected for each image to correct MFI values (see below).

Data post-processing

These steps were performed in R studio. For each image, nuclear and micronuclear MFI values were corrected by the average global background fluorescence value. For determination of subcellular distribution, log ratios of cytoplasmic to nuclear or perinuclear to nuclear MFI were used without prior background correction. Cytoplasmic MFIs w/o background correction were used to evaluate total STING in tissues. The levels of lamins for cultured cells were background-corrected nuclear MFIs, and for tissues, these values were calculated as nuclear MFIs normalized to cytoplasmic MFI for each cell. PLA foci were measured as MFI and/or as foci counts within each nuclear area. For tissues, PLA foci counts were converted to counts per 1000 pixels of nuclear area, since nuclear sizes varied significantly between cell and tissue types. Per-samples means were derived from all these values to compare across different tissue samples.

Data visualization and statistical analyses

These steps were done in R studio and were always performed without exclusion of any outlier data points. p values for continuous variables (i.e., MFIs) were calculated in Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test, which can detect distributional differences beyond mean and variance and is appropriate for non-parametrically distributed data such as MFIs or nuclear areas. For discrete variables (i.e., PLA foci numbers), p values were determined in Wilcoxon tests. For TMA analysis, sample averages for STING and lamin A/C MFIs were transformed using scale function in R. Two sets of PLA foci per area sample averages, obtained in technical replicas of TMA staining, were each first transformed using min – max scaling approach that preserved zero values (in the scales package), and then for each sample the larger of the two scaled values was used in the final analysis. Using values from one of the technical replicates did not change the conclusion. lm function was used for linear regression analysis, and cor.test function was used to determine Pearson correlation coefficients and the associated p values. Cliff’s delta (cliff.delta function from the effsize package), used to compare pairs of distributions of MFI values in Figure S2D, is recommended for non-parametric distributions. Cliff’s delta of a distribution A vs. distribution B can range from 1 (if all values in A are larger than all values in B) to −1 (if the reverse is true), and 0 value indicates that the distributions are completely overlapping.

All error bars represent standard deviation. Where applicable, and unless stated otherwise, blue bars in dot plots mark distribution medians and red bars – distribution means. Additional information on statistical analyses is provided in Figure Legends and, for the TMA analyses, also in the main text.
