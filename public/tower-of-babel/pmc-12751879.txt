Preliminary identification of the MiR-548ah-5p/THBD axis for heart failure via DirectTarget causal framework and bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Yixin Li, Jiaming Shuang, Na Yuan, Xiaomin Yang, Guisheng Song, Yanwen Wang, Xinyuan Cui, Yanbo Zhang, Jing Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying putative direct regulatory interactions between microRNAs (miRNAs) and messenger RNAs (mRNAs) is crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms of heart failure (HF). This exploratory study aimed to investigate these putative direct interactions within the miRNA–mRNA axis in HF.MethodsWe used the GSE135055 dataset to screen for differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-MiRNAs) and mRNAs (DE-mRNAs). To infer putative direct interactions between them, we applied the DirectTarget framework, utilizing the Interventional Calculus when the DAG is Absent (IDA) method for causal inference, combined with target information predicted from sequence data. We then constructed putative direct regulatory networks for the screened miRNA–mRNAs. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the genes in the putative regulatory network. Finally, the miRNA–mRNAs exhibiting the strongest negative regulatory relationships were experimentally validated in vitro using dual luciferase reporter assays, reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and western blotting. In addition, we further investigated the hub miRNA–mRNA expression and exploratory discriminatory ability.ResultsDifferential expression analysis between HF and control groups identified 31 miRNAs and 163 mRNAs. The resulting regulatory network comprised 348 putative direct miRNA–mRNA axes, involving 27 miRNAs and 116 mRNAs. Notably, the miR-548ah-5p/THBD pair exhibited the strongest negative regulatory relationship, while miR-4422 and TIFA were involved in the most regulatory interactions. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the extracellular matrix (ECM) was the most significantly enriched term. KEGG pathway analysis identified key pathways such as AGE–RAGE, PI3K–Akt, and ECM–receptor interactions. The gene THBD, with its high connectivity in the network was considered a potential key candidate. Our in vitro experiments further confirmed that miR-548ah-5p negatively regulates THBD expression. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that miR-548ah-5p and THBD exhibit favorable exploratory classification ability for HF.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the effectiveness of the DirectTarget framework in identifying putative miRNA target interactions, providing a valuable addition to existing approaches. To our knowledge, this is the first report that the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis is a key regulatory axis in HF, suggesting a potential new avenue for further research.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03549-5.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a severe terminal-stage cardiovascular disease with a high prevalence and poor prognosis [1, 2]. Although drugs targeting different pathological pathways have considerably improved the prognosis of HF in recent years, the trend towards improvement has slowed [3–7]. One reason is the incomplete understanding of HF pathogenesis [8], necessitating the need to explore other pathological mechanisms of HF to improve its treatment and prognosis.

Studies have shown that microRNAs (miRNAs) are involved in almost all the pathological processes in HF, including myocardial hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and abnormal cardiac energy metabolism [9–13]. MiRNAs also play a crucial regulatory role in the progression of HF [14, 15]. Notably, miR-132 effectively improves cardiac function and reduces myocardial fibrosis. The antisense oligonucleotide, CDR132L, has entered the clinical trial phase and exhibits promising therapeutic potential [16, 17]. MiRNAs are virtually involved at the post-transcriptional level and bind to the 3′ untranslated region (3′-UTR) of their target messenger RNA (mRNA) to suppress expression [18]. The miRNA–mRNA regulatory axis is the core of this process [19]. Exploring the miRNA–mRNA regulatory axis will further clarify the pathogenesis of HF and provide new therapeutic targets.

In terms of molecular mechanisms, miRNA–mRNA regulation represents a causal relationship. However, traditional correlation or association analyses could not reveal this relationship [20]. Pearl established do-calculus as a framework for causal inference, which allows for the estimation of causal effects from observed data [21]. However, the directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure, required by this method, is not fulfilled due to the high dimensionality and complex regulatory relationships in genetic data. The Interventional Calculus when the DAG is Absent (IDA) approach estimates the causal effects of miRNA–mRNA from gene expression data in the absence of a DAG [22]. The DirectTarget framework integrates miRNA target information predicted from sequence data with IDA-based causal inference, allowing differentiation between direct and indirect miRNA–mRNA relationships [23].

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to apply the DirectTarget framework to infer putative direct miRNA–mRNA regulatory interactions in HF pathogenesis using data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Further in vitro cellular experiments were performed to validate the miRNA–mRNA axis with the strongest negative regulatory relationship. This study not only confirms the validity of the inference methods, but also suggests a potential molecular mechanism of HF at the gene level, providing guidance for subsequent experimental studies.

Methods

Data preparation and differential expression analysis

The GSE135055 dataset was downloaded from GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). This dataset comprises RNA-seq profiles generated on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (GPL16791) from left ventricular tissue of 21 HF samples and 9 control samples (sample IDs: GSM3984434 to GSM3984493, see Supplementary Table S1 for the complete list). For miRNA analysis, we filtered out miRNAs with zero average expression across all samples. For mRNA analysis, we excluded genes with FPKM < 1 in more than 80% of samples. The filtered data were then normalized and transformed: miRNA data underwent TMM normalization followed by voom transformation, while mRNA data were converted from FPKM to TPM, quantile-normalized, and log2-transformed. Potential batch effects were assessed by principal component analysis (PCA). Differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-MiRNAs) and differentially expressed mRNAs (DE-mRNAs) were obtained using the “limma” package. To control for multiple testing, we applied global false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method across all hypothesis tests (1972 for miRNAs, 9271 for mRNAs). Significance thresholds were set at FDR < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 1. DE-MiRNAs and DE-mRNAs were visualized using the “ggplot2” package for volcano plots and “pheatmap” package for heatmaps.

Putative direct regulatory relationship inference of miRNA–mRNA

This study inferred a putative direct regulatory relationship between miRNAs and mRNAs through a series of steps. First, the IDA framework, incorporating the Peter–Clark Algorithm (PC Algorithm) and do-calculus method, was used to infer putative direct miRNA–mRNA regulatory relationships. A matrix containing 30 samples (21 HF and 9 control) and 194 variables (the first 31 columns were DE-MiRNAs and the remaining 163 columns were DE-mRNAs) was analyzed, and the PC algorithm was run with a significance level of α = 0.01 for conditional independence testing to construct a partially directed acyclic graph (CPDAG). Do-Calculus was then applied to estimate the causal effects of all DAGs in the CPDAG, with the result considered as the smallest absolute value. To mitigate small sample size effects on the analysis results, 100 bootstrap samplings were performed, and the median was used as the final result. The “pcalg” R package was used to construct the causal structure, and the “RBGL” and “WGCNA” R packages were used to assist causal inference. Second, to predict putative direct miRNA–mRNA interactions, we used TargetScanHuman (8.0) and applied a stringent context + + score threshold of < − 0.1 to retain high-confidence predictions. Third, these predictions were intersected with the results of the IDA analysis to identify miRNA–mRNA pairs with both a predicted direct binding mechanism and a significant regulatory relationship. Fourth, to improve accuracy, we applied the following screening criteria: (1) we retained only pairs whose absolute causal effect size exceeded the median of all effects from the IDA analysis. (2) For genes represented by multiple microarray probes, we selected only the miRNA–probe pair with the highest absolute causal effect for each gene, ensuring that each gene was represented only once by its strongest interaction. Putative direct regulatory networks were visualized using Cytoscape [24].

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis

Gene nodes in the direct miRNA–mRNA causal regulatory networks were analyzed using gene ontology (GO) to determine their enrichment in biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). A threshold of FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Metascape platform (https://www.metascape.org) and Cytoscape-ClueGO plugin [25, 26] were used for visualization.

Cell culture and transfection

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC, AC168, Abcells, China) were cultured in human umbilical vein endothelial cell complete culture medium (Abcells, China). Human embryonic kidney 293 T cells (293 T, AC101, Abcells, China) were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C and 5% carbon dioxide. The miR-548ah-5p mimic, mimic negative control (mimic-NC), inhibitor, inhibitor negative control (inhibitor-NC), and dual luciferase reporter plasmid (GeneCopoeia, Inc., Rockville, MD) were transfected using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Reagent (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol under the same conditions.

Dual-luciferase activity assay

The predicted miRNA binding sites were inserted into the 3′UTR region of the luciferase reporter vector to create wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) reporter vectors. 293 T cells were transfected with one of the above vectors along with the miR-548ah-5p mimic or mimic-NC using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 48 h of transfection, the cells were collected and the luciferase activity was detected with a dual luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, USA).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

The collected HUVEC cells were transfected for 48 h. Total RNA was extracted by RNA extraction kit (Seven, China). RNA concentration was determined by Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA). Then, the RNA was reverse-transcribed with the miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China). The expression levels of miR-548ah-5p and THBD were detected by the SYBR fluorescence quantitative assay kit (Vazyme, China). To perform relative quantitative analysis, internal references such as U6 and GAPDH were utilized. The 2−ΔΔCt method was employed for data analysis in this study. Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from HUVEC transfected for 48 h using a membrane protein extraction kit (epizyme, China) and quantified by BCA kit. Proteins were resolved using SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Ireland). Then, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h. Next, they were incubated with primary antibodies (THBD: ab109189, 1:1000, abcam, USA; β-actin: ab115777, 1:1000, abcam, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, they were treated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated second antibody (RS0002, 1:5000, Immunoway, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected via chemiluminescence and the strip gray scale was quantified by ImageJ software.

Validation of hub miRNA and mRNA

The exploratory diagnostic performance of the identified hub genes was assessed by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The combined diagnostic indicators of miRNA–mRNA were constructed through the logistic regression model. To account for potential overoptimism in a single cohort, we performed internal validation using tenfold cross-validation (repeated 100 times) and the bootstrap 0.632 + method (1000 iterations). The optimism-corrected area under the curve (AUC) values from these procedures are reported. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the expression levels of hub miRNA and mRNA between HF and control samples. Visualization of the results was performed using the R package “ggpubr”. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between hub miRNA and mRNA in HF. Furthermore, we utilized the DGIdb database (https://dgidb.org/) to predict target drugs.

Statistical analysis

R 4.3.2 software was used to complete the bioinformatics analysis. Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 software. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). All p-values reported are two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Discussion

MiRNAs play key roles in HF pathogenesis [27–29]. Exploring the miRNA–mRNA regulatory axis could help clarify HF pathogenesis and provide new therapeutic targets [30–32]. In this study, 348 putative direct interaction axes were obtained using the IDA causal inference method combined with sequence data. Among these, the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis exhibited the strongest negative regulatory effect. THBD was further identified as a target gene of miR-548ah-5p in cell experiments. The results of this study demonstrate that the IDA causal inference method, combined with sequence data, can effectively predict potential regulatory relationships between miRNAs and mRNAs. Identification of the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis provides ideas for further exploration of HF pathology.

DirectTarget framework identifies putative direct miRNA–mRNA regulatory relationships

In this study, the DirectTarget framework was used to precisely identify miRNA–mRNA putative direct regulatory relationships in HF. The framework effectively solved the challenges of traditional methods in predicting miRNA targets. On the one hand, gene chips and bioinformatic methods have mostly focused on statistical correlations; however, they fail to reveal the causal regulatory relationships between miRNAs and mRNAs, which are crucial for understanding biological mechanisms [33, 34]. This study used the IDA method, which includes a PC algorithm and do-Calculus. The causal structure was determined from the expression profiles of the miRNAs and mRNAs using the PC algorithm. Then the causal effect value was calculated using the do-Calculus method. This value represents the regulatory strength of the miRNAs on the corresponding mRNAs. On the other hand, database-based approaches for target prediction often have a high false discovery rate, leading to non-specific targets and requiring extensive yet inefficient validation efforts [35]. The causal effect values obtained from the IDA analysis could not specify whether the relationships between miRNAs and mRNAs were direct or indirect; therefore, we further introduced sequence data, including sequence conservation analysis and miRNA binding site prediction. Through our analysis, only pairs of relationships supported by both causal effects and sequence validation were retained, and indirect regulatory relationships and non-specific targets were effectively filtered out. Compared with traditional methods, DirectTarget significantly improves prediction accuracy and reduces non-specific targets. We identified 348 miRNA–mRNA putative direct regulatory axes involving 27 miRNAs and 116 mRNAs. These results provide high-confidence candidate targets for mechanistic studies on HF. In addition, miR-4422 and TIFA were identified as the most connected nodes in the network, suggesting their potential roles as key regulatory factors in HF. Their biological significance warrants further investigation.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis reveals key biological processes and pathways in HF

In this study, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the 116 mRNAs in the regulatory network. GO enrichment analysis showed that ECM was the most significantly enriched term, consistent with previous studies showing that ECM remodeling is a major pathological feature of HF [36]. Abnormal ECM deposition is closely associated with myocardial fibrosis and ventricular remodeling and severely affects the systolic and diastolic heart functions [37]. Notably, the miRNA–mRNA regulatory network analysis obtained in this study suggests that miRNA-targeted mRNAs play a key role in the regulation of ECM remodeling. Further clarification of the role of miRNAs in this process is essential for effective therapeutic development that aims to inhibit cardiac fibrosis. In addition, KEGG pathway analysis identified key signaling pathways strongly associated with HF pathology, including AGE–RAGE, PI3K–Akt, and ECM–receptor interactions. Previous studies have confirmed the importance of these pathways in HF pathogenesis. The AGE–RAGE signaling pathway plays an important role in vascular endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and ECM overaccumulation [38–40]. The PI3K–Akt signaling pathway is closely related to the proliferation, survival, and metabolic regulation of cardiomyocytes. Dysregulation may cause abnormal cardiac tissue remodelling [41, 42]. The ECM–receptor interaction pathway highlights the importance of ECM remodelling in HF fibrosis and signal transduction [36, 37]. The results of the GO and KEGG analyses focused on the core BPs and key pathways in HF, demonstrating the reliability of our approach using IDA with sequence data to identify HF-related causal networks.

Cell experiments confirm the direct regulation of THBD by miR-548ah-5p

This study confirmed the targeted relationship between miR-548ah-5p and THBD using cellular experiments. Because miRNAs usually downregulate target genes, negative causal effect values of the miRNA–mRNA axis are usually defined as direct relationships, whereas positive causal effect values represent indirect relationships [43, 44]. In this study, the strongest negative causal pair suggests that miR-548ah-5p may play a role in the pathology of HF by directly binding to THBD. However, few studies have investigated the role of miR-548ah-5p. Previous studies have confirmed that miR-548ah-5p promotes cancer development and viral infection by affecting PI3K/Akt, TGF-β and other signaling pathways [45, 46]. However, studies on the mechanism of action of miR-548ah-5p in HF are limited. Further exploration of the specific pathways and their related targets is required. In this study, we confirmed that THBD is a direct target of miR-548ah-5p. THBD is a glycoprotein mainly expressed on the surface of vascular endothelial cells. THBD not only aggravates the pathological process of ischamic cardiomyopathy but is also closely related to various HF etiologies, such as dilated cardiomyopathy and pulmonary hypertension [47, 48]. Other studies have shown that THBD can further aggravate HFD-induced kidney injury caused by HF [49]. Studies have identified THBD as an important molecular target for HF. Regulating THBD signaling with drugs can effectively reduce cardiac fibrosis in mice and the treatment of HF with a reduced ejection fraction [50]. In this study, we further confirmed that miR-548ah-5p could directly bind to the 3′-UTR of THBD and negatively regulate THBD expression using cellular experiments. To our knowledge, this is the first report of the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis as a regulatory mechanism in HF, providing a conceptual framework for future exploratory studies.

Exploratory diagnostic potential of the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis

Our study provides exploratory evidence that the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis may represent a candidate biomarker for HF, as supported by the robust optimism-corrected AUC values. It is important to note that these values were derived from direct tissue analysis, which fundamentally differs from the circulating plasma or serum biomarkers (e.g., miR-214, miR-21-5p, sST2, GDF-15) reported in previous studies [51–53]. The impressive diagnostic performance observed here is preliminary and must be validated in larger, independent cohorts. Furthermore, the translational potential of these tissue-based findings to non-invasive diagnostic tests requires dedicated future investigation. The study also found that miR-548ah-5p was significantly upregulated and THBD was downregulated in heart failure samples, and the two were negatively correlated. This result further confirmed the negative causal regulation of this axis inferred by the DirectTarget framework. Bioinformatic screening revealed that the THBD pathway is potentially associated with several drugs previously reported to possess cardioprotective properties [54–60]. This observation is strictly hypothesis-generating and highlights a potential direction for future mechanistic investigations.

Limitations and future research directions

This study has some limitations. First, the bioinformatic analysis was based on a relatively small sample size (n = 30) without strict matching for age, sex, or etiology of heart failure. Additionally, the heart failure cohort was exclusively composed of DCM and myocarditis patients, which introduces significant spectrum bias. Our findings therefore more directly reflect the molecular mechanisms of non-ischemic heart failure, and their generalizability across the entire spectrum of heart failure requires further validation. Second, our validation was conducted in vitro cell experiments; the specific mechanism and role of this regulatory axis in HF have not been further explored. Third, as the analysis utilized end-stage HF tissue samples, our findings may not be directly applicable to early-stage HF diagnosis. Future work will involve validating this regulatory axis through in vivo animal experiments and prospective cohort studies. Finally, the promising diagnostic performance observed in tissue warrants future validation in a large, independent cohort of patient plasma samples.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identified putative direct regulatory relationships between miRNAs and mRNAs in HF using IDA causal inference combined with sequence data. A direct regulatory relationship with the strongest negative causal effect of the miR-548ah-5p/THBD axis was experimentally verified. This study not only provides evidence that the DirectTarget framework can identify biologically relevant miRNA–mRNA interactions in HF and complement existing approaches, but also provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of HF, laying the foundation for future exploratory investigations into its biological significance.
