Effect of Fluoride Varnishes Containing Casein Phosphopeptide-amorphous Calcium Phosphate on Primary Enamel Microhardness: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Pushpasanthy M, Rena Ephraim, MU Mridhul, Ambili Ayilliath, KB Dhanya, Fazila Azhikoden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundFluoride varnishes containing casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP) have been shown to be beneficial in arresting caries lesions in permanent teeth by increasing surface hardness, causing a shift in the demineralization/remineralization equilibrium. The present study aimed to compare the enamel microhardness of primary teeth after the application of MI Varnish and Enamel Pro Varnish.Materials and methodsSixty-three extracted primary teeth were randomly divided into three groups of 21 teeth each: control group, MI Varnish group, and Enamel Pro Varnish group. All specimens were placed in a demineralizing solution for 48 hours. MI Varnish and Enamel Pro Varnish were applied to the teeth of the respective groups, except in the control group. All specimens were placed in artificial saliva for 1 week. The preoperative and postoperative microhardness values of all specimens were measured. The results were analyzed and compared.ResultsIntragroup comparison was found to be highly significant in the control group and Enamel Pro Varnish group and not significant in the MI Varnish group after remineralization when compared to baseline. However, a greater reduction in Vickers hardness number (VHN) was seen in the MI Varnish group when compared to the Enamel Pro Varnish and control groups.ConclusionThe addition of CPP-ACP to fluoride varnish increases the microhardness of surface enamel in primary teeth.Clinical significanceFluorides with the addition of CPP-ACP will be highly beneficial for increasing the surface hardness of primary enamel and making it less susceptible to acid attack.How to cite this articlePushpasanthy M, Rena Ephraim, Mridhul MU et al. Introduction

Dental caries is an easily transmissible, infectious disease of multifactorial origin caused by acids produced at the tooth–plaque interface. Despite being preventable and showing a declining trend in developed nations, it remains a public health concern among children in developing countries.1,2

Topical fluoride applications remain the cornerstone of preventive strategies. Fluoride varnish, in particular, is recommended for children at high risk of caries as an adjunct to fluoridated toothpaste and water.3 Its ease of application, hydrophobic properties, and comparable efficacy to fluoride gels make it especially suitable for preschool children.4 Unlike fluoride gels and rinses, varnishes minimize the risk of ingestion while enhancing fluoride bioavailability.5

Fluoride acts primarily by inhibiting demineralization, reducing plaque adhesion, enhancing remineralization, and suppressing bacterial enzymatic activity.6,7 Through ion exchange, fluoride ions displace hydroxyl groups of hydroxyapatite (HAP), forming fluorapatite (FAP), which is highly resistant to acid dissolution.8 Additionally, calcium fluoride formed on enamel surfaces serves as a fluoride reservoir, releasing ions during acidic challenges and thereby maintaining mineral balance.9

The addition of CPP-ACP enhances calcium and phosphate ion availability in saliva, thereby accelerating remineralization and improving enamel microhardness.6 This is particularly relevant in primary teeth, which possess thinner enamel and a less organized microstructure than permanent teeth, making them more susceptible to demineralization.10–12

While the remineralization potential of permanent teeth has been widely studied, limited evidence exists for primary dentition. To evaluate and compare enamel microhardness of primary teeth following application of fluoride varnishes with CPP-ACP, we decided to carry out this study.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted after getting approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (MINDS/PG-ETHICAL008/2023-34) in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (1975). Consent was obtained from parents prior to collection of samples.

A total of 63 extracted primary teeth, free of developmental defects, restorations, and fluorosis, were included. Teeth extracted due to natural shedding or over-retention were considered. Teeth with cracks, extensive caries, or intrinsic/extrinsic discolorations were excluded. Sample size (n = 63; 21 per group) was determined with a 95% confidence level and 90% power.

Samples were divided into three groups according to the materials used:

Group I (Control): Artificial saliva only.

Group I (Control): Artificial saliva only.

Group II (MI Varnish™; GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Group II (MI Varnish™; GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Group III (Enamel Pro® Varnish; Premier Dental Products, PA, USA).

Group III (Enamel Pro® Varnish; Premier Dental Products, PA, USA).

Sample Preparation

Crowns were separated 3 mm below the cementoenamel junction, embedded in self-cured acrylic resin (Fig. 1), and the buccal surface was exposed by creating a window of 3 × 3 mm. Remaining surfaces were coated with acid-resistant nail varnish. The preoperative surface microhardness was measured using a Vickers hardness testing machine (VHM) [25 g load for 5 s; mean of three indentations recorded as Vickers hardness number (VHN)].

Primary teeth mounted in acrylic resin

Demineralization Protocol

Specimens were placed in a freshly prepared solution for demineralizing the enamel (0.05 M acetic acid, 2.2 mM CaCl₂, 2.2 mM KH₂PO₄, and the pH was adjusted to 4.5 with KOH) and incubated for 2 days. Microhardness was again recorded.

Treatment Protocol

Group I (Control Group)

The specimens in this group were air-dried for 30 seconds. The specimens were then immersed in artificial saliva for 7 days.

Group II (MI VarnishTM Group)

The specimens that were demineralized were air-dried for 30 seconds. Then MI Varnish™ was applied onto the exposed enamel surface, with the help of an applicator tip, and allowed to dry for 4 minutes.

Group III (Enamel Pro® Varnish Group)

The specimens that were demineralized were air-dried for 30 seconds. Then Enamel pro® Varnish was applied onto the exposed enamel surface, with the help of an applicator tip, and allowed to dry for 4 minutes.

All the samples were washed with distilled water and placed in artificial saliva. Artificial saliva was prepared freshly every day. After 1 week, all samples were rinsed, dried, and microhardness was reassessed. The average of three indentations was taken as the VHN for that specimen.12

Results

The data obtained were compared and analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA) version 26 statistical software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Normality of data was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between the three groups were done using one-way ANOVA with post hoc tests. Intragroup comparison was done using repeated measures ANOVA appropriately. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Intragroup pairwise comparison of surface microhardness (VHN) at different time periods using post hoc Bonferroni test (Table 1). Comparison of mean in different time periods in the control group (group I) showed that there was a difference between baseline and demineralization and baseline and remineralization which were statistically significant p < 0.001; however, statistically significant difference was not noted between demineralization and remineralization. Intragroup comparison of the MI Varnish group found a significant difference (p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was noted between baseline and remineralization. A statistically significant difference was found in the intragroup comparison within the Enamel Pro Varnish group (p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present in vitro study examined changes in the surface microhardness (SMH) of primary teeth after application of fluoride varnishes containing CPP-ACP. Varnishes containing fluoride and remineralizing agents have been shown to enhance enamel microhardness in permanent teeth in studies by forming calcium fluoride deposits on the surface, which serve as a reservoir for the gradual release of fluoride ions. These ions facilitate the formation of fluorapatite crystals, thereby increasing enamel resistance to acid dissolution and reducing demineralization.13,14

The incorporation of CPP-ACP in fluoride varnishes provides additional calcium and phosphate ions, further enhancing remineralization. This process reduces porosity, increases resistance to acid attack, and inhibits plaque accumulation.3,15 Studies done previously have shown the ability of CPP-ACP to improve enamel microhardness in both demineralized and bleached teeth.16,17

Primary teeth were chosen in the present study due to the presence of their thin enamel, low mineral content, and less organized structure compared to permanent teeth, which renders them more susceptible to demineralization.18 However, limited studies have evaluated remineralization potential in primary teeth using CPP-ACP–based fluoride varnishes.

Both MI Varnish™ and Enamel Pro® Varnish contain sodium fluoride and CPP-ACP. MI Varnish has the advantage of a thicker consistency, allowing prolonged retention on tooth surfaces, which may explain the higher microhardness values observed compared to Enamel Pro. Enamel Pro was selected for its gluten-free composition, which is clinically relevant for children with gluten sensitivity, although literature on its effect on enamel microhardness is sparse.19

Microhardness was evaluated using the VHM, which provides high accuracy and penetrates enamel to a depth of ~14 μm. This method is widely used for assessing enamel hardness due to its quantitative reliability.13,20 In this study, artificial demineralization created subsurface lesions simulating early caries, and remineralization was evaluated after 7 days of varnish application in artificial saliva.

As expected, demineralization significantly reduced SMH, consistent with previous findings by Lata et al.21 Both experimental groups showed significant improvement in SMH following remineralization compared with the control group. MI Varnish exhibited the highest microhardness (373.17 VHN), followed by Enamel Pro (312.71 VHN), while the control group showed minimal change (257.42 VHN). These findings align with previous studies demonstrating superior remineralizing potential of MI Varnish compared to other fluoride varnishes.22,23

Overall, both varnishes enhanced enamel microhardness of primary teeth, with MI Varnish providing greater benefit. This suggests that CPP-ACP–based fluoride varnishes may play an important role in preventive pediatric dentistry, particularly in high caries-risk children.

Conclusion

Fluoride varnishes with CPP-ACP increased the microhardness of enamel of primary teeth, which can decrease the susceptibility to acid attack. The addition of remineralizing agents may enhance repair of the damaged enamel surface in a shorter time period and increase surface hardness, thereby preventing fracture of these teeth under masticatory forces.

As the present study was carried out under laboratory (in vitro) conditions, the findings may not precisely mirror the biological responses that occur in a natural oral environment. Therefore, further in vivo research is essential to confirm and validate these results.

Clinical Significance

Prevention of dental caries is being given more emphasis in recent years. Though fluoride is effective on the smooth surface caries, the addition of CPP-ACP can also have an effect on pit and fissure caries. Preventive protocol in high-risk caries children should include fluoride varnishes with the incorporation of CPP-ACP to enhance remineralization of the demineralized enamel surfaces.
