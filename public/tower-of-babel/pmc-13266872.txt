Preserve or ligate?—navigating perfusion-safe oncologic precision in gastroesophageal junction cancer surgery
Authors: Evelyn Alexander, Stephanie G Worrell
Published: 2026

Introduction

Robot-assisted esophagectomy for gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) cancers has ushered in a new era of precision surgery. Yet, a fundamental question remains unresolved: how aggressive should we be in our pursuit of oncologic thoroughness when it may come at the expense of perfusion integrity?

The recent randomized trial by Osterkamp et al. offers a critical data point in this debate. By comparing central ligation of the right gastric artery (RGA) to partial preservation during robotic Ivor Lewis esophagectomy, the study explores whether improved access to lymph node (LN) station 3 can be achieved without compromising the conduit or anastomotic perfusion (1).

The answer, it seems, is yes—but with important caveats.

Perfusion metrics and nodal staging

One of the most important findings from this study is that central ligation, despite being more anatomically radical, did not impair perfusion at any of the three measured stages: baseline, post-conduit formation, or post-anastomosis, as determined by quantitative indocyanine green (ICG) angiography (1). Perfusion values, rates of anastomotic leak, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, and 1-year survival were equivalent between the groups.

These findings challenge long-standing assumptions about the critical perfusion role of the RGA. The gastric conduit, when standardized to a 5 cm width, appears to receive sufficient perfusion from the right gastroepiploic artery alone. This corroborates anatomical data from arteriographic studies showing gastroepiploic dominance in gastric tube vascularization (2).

Despite the increase in overall and station 3b LN yield in the central ligation group, the nodal staging (pN) did not significantly differ (1). Thus, more nodes were resected, but with no increase in upstaging. However, greater LN harvest may still have clinical relevance. More extensive nodal evaluation can reduce the risk of understaging and may refine postoperative risk stratification. In multidisciplinary practice, improved staging accuracy may influence surveillance strategies or decisions regarding adjuvant therapy, even when formal stage migration is not observed. In this light, the clinical benefit of central ligation becomes less certain.

LN station 3b: common site, unclear impact

It is well known that station 3 is a common site of nodal metastasis in GEJ cancer (3,4), and the study reaffirms this, although station 3b involvement was still relatively rare (1/33 patients in the central group vs. 5/32 in the standard group, P=0.11) (1). The increased yield from both stations 3 and 7 in the central group supports its value from an anatomic completeness standpoint.

Yet, without a change in staging or early survival, the true benefit remains theoretical, particularly in the absence of long-term recurrence data. The Japanese Esophageal Society guidelines do not routinely recommend station 3b dissection for GEJ tumors unless the gastric component is significant (5). This study’s results lend further support to that conservative approach, at least for now.

Intraoperative perfusion variability

Perhaps the most intriguing insight was not in the group-level comparisons, but in the individual variation in perfusion along the conduit gradient (1). While averages were statistically similar, some patients showed marked drops or peaks in perfusion across the conduit. This raises an important question: should we begin to tailor anastomotic site selection intraoperatively using real-time ICG mapping?

The study’s suggestion to consider pre-anastomotic perfusion mapping is compelling. Even though the current sample size limited correlation between ICG values and leak rates (3/33 in central ligation vs. 0/32, P=0.56), it highlights a future where data-driven decisions may reduce subjective variation in surgical technique.

Prior work by He et al. and Thammineedi et al. has shown that reduced perfusion at the conduit tip correlates with increased anastomotic leak risk (6,7). However, Sozzi et al. noted in a meta-analysis that while ICG reduces leaks in cervical anastomoses, its predictive value in intrathoracic cases is inconsistent (8). The present study lacks statistical power to resolve this question due to an overall low incidence of leak.

While ICG fluorescence angiography provides real-time visualization of conduit perfusion, quantitative thresholds predicting anastomotic failure remain heterogeneous across studies and have not yet been universally validated. As such, ICG measurements currently function best as relative intraoperative decision-support tools rather than definitive predictors of anastomotic leak.

Central ligation: weighing benefits and limitations

The trial decisively shows that central ligation is safe in expert hands, with no additional perioperative risk. For centers prioritizing maximal nodal harvest, particularly in clinical trials or for high-risk patients, this may justify its use.

But for others, particularly where resource constraints limit intraoperative ICG or robotic access, the absence of a staging benefit makes routine use harder to justify. The surgical dogma of “more is better” in nodal dissection continues to be challenged by emerging data, especially when survival or recurrence is not improved (9).

Future directions

To truly evaluate the role of central ligation, long-term outcomes—especially disease-free survival and recurrence patterns are needed. If central ligation reduces recurrence or improves survival in select subgroups, that would represent a true benefit.

Moreover, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted ICG interpretation with robotic platforms could transform this from a passive observation to an active surgical decision-making tool. Finally, multicenter trials with standardized conduit construction, perfusion mapping, and follow-up protocols are needed to validate and generalize these findings.

Conclusions: oncologic precision, perfusion integrity

The study by Osterkamp et al. represents a valuable contribution to our understanding of the interplay between nodal dissection and perfusion safety in GEJ surgery. While central ligation enhances nodal harvest without compromising conduit perfusion, its routine use may not be necessary in all patients. In clinical practice, central ligation may be most appropriate in high-volume centers performing robotic esophagectomy, particularly when maximizing LN retrieval is prioritized or when nodal disease along the lesser curvature is suspected. Until long-term data provide clarity, the decision to ligate or preserve the RGA will remain a nuanced one guided by anatomy, surgical philosophy, and evolving technology.
