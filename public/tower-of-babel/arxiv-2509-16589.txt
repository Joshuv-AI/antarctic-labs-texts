Benchmarking Contextual and Paralinguistic Reasoning in Speech-LLMs: A Case Study with In-the-Wild Data
Qiongqiong Wang , Hardik B. Sailor , Tianchi Liu , Wenyu Zhang , Muhammad Huzaifah , Nattadaporn Lertcheva , Shuo Sun , Nancy F. Chen , Jinyang Wu , AiTi Aw, Institute of Infocomm Research (IR), ASTAR, Singapore wang qiongqiong@i2r.a-star.edu.sg

Abstract
Recent speech-LLMs have shown impressive performance in tasks like transcription and translation, yet they remain limited in understanding the paralinguistic aspects of speech crucial for social and emotional intelligence. We propose CP-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating speech-LLMs on contextual paralinguistic reasoning the integration of verbal content with non-verbal cues like emotion and prosody. The benchmark includes two curated question-answering (QA) datasets requiring both linguistic and empathetic understanding. We evaluate state-of-the-art speech-LLMs from both open- and closed-source models and perform a comprehensive analysis across different question types. The top two models were further analyzed under temperature tuning to understand its effect on this task. Our benchmark reveals a key gap in existing evaluations and offers insights into building more context-aware and emotionally intelligent speech-capable LLMs.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success in a wide range of natural language processing tasks, and recent advances have extended their capabilities into the multimodal domain, including vision, audio, and speech
Huang et al. 2024b
. In particular, the integration of speech encoders with text-based LLMs, referred to as speech-LLMs, has enabled models to process and reason over spoken language input
Peng et al. 2024
. Recent developments in speech-LLMs demonstrate their ability to capture and generate semantically rich textual output from spoken input, achieving state-of-the-art performance in tasks such as automatic speech recognition, speech translation, and spoken question answering.

Despite this success in modeling linguistic content, current speech-LLMs largely overlook the paralinguistic dimensions of human speech, such as emotion, intent, speaker identity, and social dynamics, which are essential for fully understanding and interpreting spoken communication. These paralinguistic cues are crucial for applications involving empathy, social reasoning, and human behavior analysis, yet they remain underrepresented in current evaluation benchmarks and model objectives. Driven by this potential, researchers have begun developing empathetic speech-LLMs that not only transcribe or interpret speech but also understand the speaker’s affective state and contextual nuances. Several studies have attempted to train models to understand emotions in speech and respond empathetically
Lin et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Kang et al. 2024
. Efforts have also extended to data creation; for example, a contextual paralinguistic data generation pipeline has been proposed that leverages LLMs to automatically produce QA pairs
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Wang et al. 2025c
.

Existing benchmarks for evaluating speech-LLMs primarily target isolated contextual tasks like spoken question answering (SQA) or paralinguistic tasks such as emotion recognition and speaker identification
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Gong et al. 2024
;
Sakshi et al. 2025
. While valuable, these benchmarks fall short in assessing contextual paralinguistic reasoning, which is the integration of speech content, emotional nuance, and speaker-specific traits, which necessitates a more complete understanding of the input.

This gap motivates the need for a new benchmark that moves beyond isolated capabilities and evaluates the holistic reasoning ability of speech-LLMs in multimodal contexts. We introduce the task of contextual paralinguistic question answering (CPQA), which requires reasoning over both linguistic content and paralinguistic cues such as prosody, emotion, and speaker intent. For example, consider the utterance
“There are so many people here. I used to come with my sister every summer.”
Without paralinguistic cues, a question like “Why was the speaker happy?” is ambiguous. The first phrase might signal excitement or frustration depending on tone, while the latter could convey nostalgia and either sadness or joy. Resolving such ambiguity requires interpreting not only the transcript but also the speaker’s tone and affective state. This highlights the necessity of prosody-dependent cues for capturing nuances beyond text. Such questions are also distinct from direct queries like “What is the speaker’s emotion?” as they demand deeper contextual reasoning about causes and states. In short, the evaluations demand a nuanced understanding of both what is said and how it is said. To this end, we propose
CP-Bench
, a novel benchmark specifically designed to assess these capabilities in speech-LLMs. Our benchmark comprises two curated QA datasets derived from long and short in-the-wild speech segments.

In addition to constructing and evaluating several state-of-the-art speech-LLMs on this proposed benchmark, we analyzed the impact of the temperature hyperparameter. Temperature controls the randomness of the generated response and significantly influences reasoning quality.
While higher temperatures can lead to diverse but potentially inconsistent responses, lower temperatures generally produce more factual and stable outputs. This stability is particularly crucial for tasks involving emotion or gender inference
Renze 2024
. To better understand this trade-off, we systematically analyzed how temperature settings affect model performance across different categories of contextual and paralinguistic question answering. Our goal is to identify optimal temperature ranges that balance output diversity with reasoning reliability, thereby enhancing the interpretability and robustness of speech-LLM responses in multimodal evaluation scenarios.

To our knowledge, this work presents the first comprehensive benchmark explicitly targeting reasoning over both context and paralinguistic cues in speech. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose and construct CP-Bench, a benchmark designed to evaluate contextual-paralinguistic reasoning capabilities in speech-LLM models, using two question-answering (QA) sets derived from long and short speech segments. The benchmarking dataset will be shared with the wider community to spur more holistic benchmarking efforts

Our findings reveal critical limitations in current models and offer insights for developing more intelligent, empathetic, and human-aligned speech-LLMs. CP-Bench sets a new direction for multimodal reasoning research at the intersection of speech and language understanding.

3
The Proposed CP-Bench

We propose a contextual-paralinguistic question answering benchmark (CP-Bench) to evaluate contextual-paralinguistic reasoning capabilities in speech-LLM models, using two QA sets derived from long and short speech segments.

3.1
Question Categories and Types

We categorize question types into three primary categories based on their informational focus: content-only (C), direct paralinguistic (DP), and contextual paralinguistic (CP):

•

C
questions assess understanding of linguistic content alone, without relying on any paralinguistic cues.

•

DP
questions directly query paralinguistic attributes—such as emotion, speaker gender, or speaker count – based solely on the auditory signal, without requiring contextual interpretation.

•

CP
questions require the integration of both linguistic content and paralinguistic information to arrive at an appropriate answer.

While speech-LLMs have demonstrated strong performance on C-type questions, most prior work addressing paralinguistics has primarily focused on the DP category. In contrast, the CP question category remains largely underexplored. Our work specifically targets this gap by evaluating models on CP-category questions.

To enable a more fine-grained analysis of paralinguistic understanding in speech QA, we
further decompose the DP and CP categories into subtypes, which capture the specific
attribute under inquiry: emotion (DE/CE), gender (DG/CG), and speaker count (DS/CS).

3.2
Evaluation Dataset Creation

To ensure coverage of all targeted question types, we prioritized collecting emotionally rich speech, as such content is underrepresented in existing resources. Emotionally salient clips were identified using data condensation methods
Wang et al. 2025b
. Accordingly, two sets of 480 speech samples each were initially selected to form a long set (30-60 seconds) and short set (10-30 seconds) in order to evaluate model behavior across differing durations.

Emotion and gender labels were derived through a two-step process: (1) automatic estimation, followed by (2) human correction and verification. For automatic estimation, we employed the Emotion2Vec speech emotion recognition (SER) tool

3.3
Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate model performance across all question types, we assess answer correctness using an LLM-based judge. A specialized prompt, tailored for contextual-paralinguistic QA, guides this evaluation. The prompt incorporates both linguistic content and paralinguistic cues (e.g., emotion, gender) and is carefully designed to avoid penalizing brief but accurate responses. Details are provided in Figure
5
.

To evaluate overall performance across multiple sub-sets (e.g., question types), we adopt an Unweighted Average Score (UAS)

\text{UAS}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}S_{k,n}\right)

(1)

where
K
is the number of subsets (e.g., question types),
N_{k}
is the number of QA pairs in sub-set
k
.
S_{k,n}
is the score for the
n
-th QA pair in subset
k
. This metric ensures a fair comparison between sets, particularly because the number of QA pairs varies across question types in both the long and short sets. UAS treats each subset equally, regardless of its size, thereby preventing any single type from disproportionately influencing the overall metric. This approach is analogous to unweighted accuracy in classification tasks, where each class is given equal importance regardless of its frequency in the dataset.

4.2.2
Investigation across Models on Fine-Grained Question Types

We conduct a more detailed analysis of individual question types within DP and CP categories to gain deeper insights, as illustrated in Figure
1
. There is significant performance divergence across question types in the DP category, showing varying strengths and weaknesses in detecting emotion, gender, and speaker count. Notably, performance disparities among models tend to reduce when contextual information is incorporated, as observed in the CE, CG, and CS question types.

In the CP question category, GPT consistently achieves superior performance on both CE and CG question types across the long and short sets, reflecting its robust capability to reason about emotion and gender within speech contexts. However, MERaLiON notably surpasses GPT on CS-type questions in the short set, underscoring its particular strength in handling speaker-related contextual reasoning with shorter speech samples. Both Gemini and MERaLiON demonstrate competitive, mid-range performance across CP-category questions, often closely following GPT and occasionally surpassing other models. In contrast, Qwen consistently underperforms across all question types in the CP category. CS-type questions exhibit the highest variability, particularly in the short set, with large performance fluctuations between models. This variability is likely due to the small sample size, which limits statistical reliability. Nevertheless, we include the results for completeness and analysis. Notably, Cascade 2 achieves a performance peak on CS-type questions in the short set. The small number of instances also contributes to an inflated CP score for Cascade 2 in Table
3
. In the long set, MERaLiON and Gemini perform comparably. Cascade 2’s surprisingly strong performance on CE-type questions indicates the limitations of current speech-LLM models in fully leveraging audio cues, suggesting potential for improvement through enhanced multimodal integration.

The breakdown within DP-category questions reveals significant performance variation across question types. MERaLiON consistently outperforms Gemini on DE and DG-type questions and even surpasses GPT on DE, showing strong and stable direct paralinguistic recognition. Qwen performs notably well on DG-type questions in the long set but sharply declines on short speech inputs, indicating a sensitivity to input length. Both cascaded models perform moderately well on DE-type questions, achieving the highest score for DE in the short set. This suggests that emotional cues are partly retrievable from transcripts alone. Nevertheless, they markedly underperform on DG and DS questions due to their lack of direct access to audio-based speaker information. In fact, all models struggle with DS-type questions, though MERaLiON leads in the short, and Gemini performs the best in the long set.

Across all question types in the CP category, as well as DE and DS types, all models consistently perform better in the short set compared to the long set, with the exception of DG-type questions, where this trend does not hold. This suggests that shorter speech inputs are generally easier for models to handle, possibly due to constraints in attention span, context window limitations, or a reduced need for long-range reasoning.

4.2.3
Temperature Investigations

Figure
2
presents a detailed analysis of how LLM decoding temperature impacts performance using GPT and MERaLiON, representing closed- and open-source models, respectively. GPT was selected over Gemini as the closed-source model due to its stronger performance on CP question categories, which are central to this study. The results show that temperature tuning significantly influences model accuracy in certain categories, revealing distinct patterns within each model and question type.

For C-type questions, GPT performs better at lower temperatures when processing long-form content, whereas MERaLiON benefits more from higher temperatures in the evaluation set with shorter speech. Temperature has minimal impact on CE-type questions for both models, suggesting that reasoning involving emotion and content is less sensitive to response diversity. GPT’s performance on CG-type questions is more temperature-dependent, peaking at mid-range values, while MERaLiON remains consistently favors lower settings.

MERaLiON consistently outperforms GPT on DE-type questions, with both models showing moderate improvement at higher temperatures. In DG-type questions, GPT struggles significantly across all temperatures due to content filtering restrictions, whereas MERaLiON maintains top performance with only slight degradation at elevated temperatures. For CS-type questions, MERaLiON experiences the largest performance gains with increasing temperature on the short set. GPT, however, displays a different behavior, with a peak at temperature 0.4 followed by a sharp decline. Our analysis reveals distinct optimal temperature ranges for MERaLiON and GPT depending on input length and question type.

5
Summary and Conclusion

We propose CP-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating speech-LLMs on contextual paralinguistic reasoning—the integration of verbal content with non-verbal cues like emotion and prosody. The benchmark includes two curated QA datasets that require both linguistic and empathetic understanding. CP-Bench challenges speech-LLMs to demonstrate linguistic comprehension, speaker information, and empathetic insight. Our comprehensive evaluation of leading speech-LLMs, including an analysis of temperature tuning effects, reveals critical strengths and limitations. This work fills an important gap in current benchmarks and provides valuable guidance towards developing more contextually aware and emotionally perceptive speech-based language models.

For future work, we plan to expand CP-Bench by adding more diverse evaluation sets, including different languages, and by increasing the density of QA pairs for each question type, especially within the multi-speaker subset, to strengthen the statistical reliability of results. We will also explore improved evaluation techniques for such multimodal reasoning, including methods to enhance the robustness and interpretability of LLM-based judgments. Finally, to further ensure reliability, we plan to incorporate human reviews to calibrate and validate LLM-based evaluations.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, most speech samples contain a single type of emotion, which limits the evaluation of models’ ability to handle mixed-emotion inputs. Second, our human annotators generated QA pairs independently resulting in no overlap. This may introduce a degree of bias related to how emotion labels were annotated. However, this lack of overlap does not affect most other QA pairs, which benefit from greater diversity in question types. Third, the number of CS-type questions in the short evaluation set is relatively small, making conclusions drawn for this category less reliable. Finally, our analysis relies on a single LLM-based judge, which may introduce uncertainty due to potential biases or variability in model outputs. Incorporating multiple LLM judges or human evaluators in future work could enhance the robustness and reliability of the evaluation.

A.1.3
Reliance on Content Cues in DG Question Reasoning

Cascaded model 2 showed good performance on DG in the long set (see Figure
1
. To better understand this result, we conducted an error analysis of the LLM judge’s reasoning on a subset of 33 high-scoring DG pairs produced by Cascade Model 2.

Our analysis indicates the LLM primarily relies on explicit textual cues: (1) gender-specific pronouns, titles, nouns, and names, and (2) stereotypical associations and phrasing styles. Among the 33 answers, 6 were based on cue type (1), 7 on cue type (2), and 5 on both. In 15 cases, the model failed to infer gender; 5 of these were polar questions answered correctly by coincidence (e.g., “no” when asked if a specific gender was present).

These findings suggest that the cascaded model’s success on DG questions largely reflects shallow content cues rather than genuine speaker inference. Such cues may yield correct answers at times, but gender is independent of mentioned genders, and stereotypes are unreliable. Hence, content-based evidence alone is not a robust basis for gender identification.

A.3.2
Judge

We adapted the judging prompt from AudioBench
Wang et al. 2025a
to better suit contextual-paralinguistic question answering, with tailored modifications to the scoring criteria. In particular, we refined the definitions to emphasize paralinguistic reasoning and reward responses that are both accurate and concise. The revised scoring rubric is presented in Figure
5
.

Rate the model’s answer based on its alignment with the reference answer, considering both speech content and paralinguistic information, including emotion, speaker identity, speaker transitions, number of speakers, gender cues, tone, intent, and overall feeling. Focus on both what is said and how it is conveyed. Be critical of details, and if the model response is vague or says something like "cannot decide," please rate it as 0.
Evaluation Criteria:Assess whether the model’s response mirrors the reference in terms of content accuracy, paralinguistic relevance, and alignment with speech-specific nuances such as emotion, intent, and speaker characteristics.

•

Score 0: The answer refuses to provide concrete results, saying “cannot decide” or similar.

•

Score 0: The answer is completely misaligned, providing incorrect, misleading, or irrelevant information regarding the speech content or paralinguistic elements.

•

Score 1: The answer minimally aligns with the reference, often misunderstanding the speech or missing important paralinguistic cues (e.g., misidentifying speaker emotion or gender).

•

Score 2: The answer recognizes the general topic but diverges significantly from the reference in accuracy, relevance, or paralinguistic understanding.

•

Score 3: The answer is generally aligned with the reference but lacks detail or precise accuracy in either speech content or paralinguistic aspects or both together.

•

Score 4: The answer is mostly accurate and relevant, closely following the reference but could be clearer or more detailed in either content or paralinguistic interpretation.

•

Score 5: The answer is highly accurate, detailed, and matches the reference answer perfectly, capturing either content meaning or paralinguistic essence (emotion, tone, speaker identity, number of speaker, etc.) or both together.

Correct the pre-assigned emotion and gender labels.

Listen to the corresponding segments in the speech samples and validate or correct the provided labels.

•

You may use the transcript for additional content understanding.

•

Ensure consistency and accuracy when correcting labels.

•

Consider the context of the conversation or narrative to avoid mislabeling emotions. For example, a person may raise their voice due to excitement (happy) rather than anger.

•

•

If a label cannot be confidently corrected, make a note of the ambiguity for review.

You can use any tools of your choice as you will need to select durations based on JSON file to correct/check the emotion and gender labels. Open-source tools like Audacity, WaveSurfer can also work well.

JSON files with corrected emotion and gender labels.
