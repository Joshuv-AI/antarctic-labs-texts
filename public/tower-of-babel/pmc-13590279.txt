Development and Validation of an Interpretable Machine Learning Model for Predicting Early Pulmonary Metastasis Risk in Osteosarcoma
Authors: Jianhua Mu, Yitian Wang, Han Liu, Xuanhong He, Zhuangzhuang Li, Yi Luo, Yong Zhou, Minxun Lu, Fan Tang, Li Min, Chongqi Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma remains a leading cause of treatment failure and mortality. Introduction

Osteosarcoma is a highly malignant tumor originating from mesenchymal tissue, commonly affecting the metaphysis of long bones in children and adolescents [1, 2]. Despite advances in neoadjuvant chemotherapy and limb‐salvage surgery, which have significantly improved the prognosis of patients with localized disease, metastasis, particularly pulmonary metastasis, remains the primary cause of clinical treatment failure and patient mortality [2]. Current monitoring for pulmonary metastasis primarily relies on periodic imaging exams, but these methods usually detect metastases only after macroscopic lesions have formed, potentially missing the optimal window for early intervention [3].

Current knowledge of the risk factors for pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma largely derives from traditional epidemiological studies and statistical analyses based on clinical pathological variables. Many studies, primarily based on the The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and limited demographic data, focus on prediction [4, 5, 6]. These data may contain untapped predictive value that has not been fully exploited. Traditional statistical models struggle to efficiently integrate and mine such high‐dimensional, heterogeneous data. However, with the widespread use of electronic medical records (EMR), large volumes of multi‐dimensional data are routinely generated in clinical practice, including demographic information, detailed laboratory results, radiological findings, surgical details, and tumor biological markers. These factors have currently been used to predict metastasis in various solid malignancies, including lung cancer, [7, 8] prostate cancer, [8, 9] thyroid cancer, [10, 11] colorectal cancer, [12] and breast cancer [13]. The predictive value of EMR data for early lung metastasis of high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma is unclear.

Machine learning (ML) techniques, particularly advanced ensemble algorithms, including random forests, gradient boosting machines, and support vector machines, have demonstrated strong capabilities in handling complex, high‐dimensional datasets. They can automatically learn complex patterns within the data, including nonlinear relationships and feature interactions, and have proven highly accurate in predicting outcomes in other cancers.

This study aims to develop and validate an interpretable ML model based on EMR data to predict the early risk of pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma. By combining the strengths of ML algorithms for handling complex datasets with the decision transparency provided by interpretability techniques, this model offers clear, intuitive risk factor insights for clinicians, thereby enhancing clinical utility. This model could serve as a powerful tool for individualized risk management and precision medicine for high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients.

Methods

Study Population

This was a retrospective study utilizing data from the EMR system at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, to identify osteosarcoma patients. Data were collected from patients hospitalized between January 1, 2010, and January 1, 2025. This study included patients with pathologically confirmed, primary high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma possessing complete baseline clinical and laboratory data, and chest CT imaging for synchronous metastasis screening. All diagnoses were confirmed based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for primary high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma [14]. Two pathologists independently re‐evaluated the histological features. All subjects adhered to a standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) protocol and received definitive surgery. Following the initial diagnosis confirmed by biopsy, patients underwent a period of neoadjuvant chemotherapy [15, 16, 17]. Specifically, the MAP regimen was employed: high‐dose methotrexate (12 g/m2 IV over 4 h on day 1 with leucovorin rescue), doxorubicin (75 mg/m2 continuous IV infusion over 48 h starting day 8), and cisplatin (120 mg/m2 IV over 4 h on day 8 with aggressive hydration) were administered in 28‐day cycles. Patients received two preoperative cycles of this regimen. This was followed by definitive surgical resection (limb‐salvage or amputation) with clear margins. Postoperatively, adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to complete the treatment protocol. The total duration from diagnostic biopsy to the cessation of chemotherapy generally spanned 6–9 months, depending on the individual's recovery and hematological tolerance [18]. To reduce clinical heterogeneity, exclusion criteria were applied to patients with non‐standardized treatment, debilitated performance status, or multi‐organ failure. Additionally, we excluded conventional osteosarcoma variants (Telangiectatic, Small cell, Low‐grade central, Parosteal, and Periosteal osteosarcomas), multiple primary cancers, and radiological images with significant artifacts that prevented precise evaluation. Early pulmonary metastasis was defined as the development of secondary pulmonary nodules, confirmed by high‐resolution CT, within 2 years of the definitive surgery. Patients experiencing metastatic progression within this timeframe were categorized into the early‐metastasis group for subsequent multivariable predictive analysis. Given the extremely low epidemiological incidence of high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma as a rare malignancy, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not conducted. Consistent with the methodology of previously published predictive models for this disease, [19, 20, 21, 22] we consecutively included all eligible patients within the specified study period to maximize the dataset volume and statistical power.

Data Collection and Processing

Demographic characteristics, vital signs, and preoperative laboratory data were used to identify features and construct the predictive model. All data were sourced from the EMR system. Maximum tumor diameter was consistently defined as the largest uni‐dimensional measurement of the primary lesion extracted from pre‐treatment radiological reports derived from MRI. To address multicollinearity, features with high correlation (Spearman's correlation coefficient > 0.6) were removed from the dataset if their correlation with the outcome was low [22].

Outliers in continuous variables were identified using boxplots. Outliers were defined as values exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range above the upper quartile or below the lower quartile. Outliers were replaced with the nearest boundary value to align with the distribution of the main dataset. All laboratory variables remained continuous and were not categorized. Categorical predictors were pre‐processed using one‐hot encoding [23].

Variable Selection

The Boruta algorithm was used to identify the most important features in the dataset. This algorithm compares the Z‐value of each feature with that of its corresponding “shadow” feature, which is a shuffled copy of the real feature. If the Z‐value of the real feature is significantly higher than the highest Z‐value of the shadow features in multiple independent tests, the feature is considered important and retained. Unimportant features, which do not show predictive power in feature selection, are excluded from the final model [24].

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used for variable selection. Variables that were either statistically significant or clinically relevant were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The multivariate model used stepwise regression with a likelihood ratio test (α = 0.05) for final variable selection. To prevent data leakage and ensure an unbiased evaluation of model performance, feature selection was performed strictly on the training set following the initial data splitting. The identified feature subset was subsequently applied to the testing set for independent validation.

Synthetic Minority Over‐Sampling Technique (SMOTE)

The SMOTE is an effective oversampling method used in medical applications to address class imbalance [25]. SMOTE works by generating synthetic data points for the minority class using Euclidean distance from its nearest neighbors. These new instances are similar to the original data, as they are generated based on the original features. However, it is noted that SMOTE may not be optimal for high‐dimensional data due to the introduction of additional noise. In this study, SMOTE was used to generate a new training dataset, with all selected samples randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%). To avoid data leakage, the oversampling process was performed after the training and testing set division. SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training set. This ensured that the class distribution was balanced during the model transition phase without introducing any information from the testing set into the training process, thereby preventing data leakage.

Model Development and Comparison

The dataset comprising the final subset of features identified during the variable selection process was used to develop the predictive models. Eleven ML models were employed for predicting pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine with Radial Kernel (SVM‐Radial), Naive Bayes (NB), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Neural Network (NNET), Elastic Net (GLMNET), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Partial Least Squares (PLS), Bayesian Generalized Linear Model (BayesGLM), Support Vector Machine with Polynomial Kernel (SVM‐Poly), and Support Vector Machine with Linear Kernel (SVM‐Linear). Model performance was evaluated using standard metrics, including area under curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1 score. The DeLong test was used to assess the significance of differences in AUC values between different models.

Feature Selection and Model Explanation

Interpreting ML models can be challenging. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method is a technique used to rank feature importance and interpret prediction outcomes, addressing the “black‐box” problem. SHAP works by calculating the marginal contribution of each feature to the prediction result, providing both local and global explanations, thus improving the transparency and interpretability of the model [26, 27, 28, 29].

In this study, SHAP was applied to analyze individual predictions from the best‐performing gradient boosting model. SHAP values quantify each feature's contribution to the prediction, offering an interpretable framework for understanding model decisions. The SHAP values are computed by considering all possible feature subsets, evaluating how the inclusion of a specific feature alters the prediction, and averaging the marginal contributions across all permutations. This approach ensures local accuracy, meaning that the sum of the SHAP values for all features equals the deviation of the model's prediction from the baseline.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses and graphical outputs were generated with R software, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Specifically, data preprocessing and manipulation were conducted using the dplyr package (v1.1.4), which provides a consistent grammar of data manipulation for structured data frames in R. Missing data were handled through multiple imputation via the mice package (v3.16.0), implementing the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) algorithm, wherein each incomplete variable was imputed by a separate conditional model across five imputation cycles. Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed and results tidied using the broom package (v1.0.5), which provides standardized, tidy data frame outputs from statistical model objects. For model interpretability, SHAP values were computed using the DALEX package (v2.4.3), a model‐agnostic framework that quantifies each feature's marginal contribution to individual predictions through exhaustive permutation of feature subsets; corresponding visualizations, including waterfall plots and summary bar charts, were subsequently rendered using the shapviz package (v0.9.2).

Hyperparameter optimization was performed uniformly across all 11 candidate models. This optimization process was driven by a repeated 10‐fold cross‐validation procedure (10 folds, 5 repetitions; 50 resampling iterations in total) implemented within the “caret” framework in R. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC) was designated as the primary optimization metric, evaluated via the “twoClassSummary” function. For each model, candidate hyperparameter configurations were generated automatically through a grid search with “tuneLength = 5”, whereby five evenly spaced candidate values were constructed per tunable parameter. The optimal configuration was subsequently identified using the “best” selection criterion, retaining the hyperparameter combination that maximized the mean cross‐validated AUC‐ROC across all resampling iterations. To accelerate the training procedure, parallel computation was distributed across 10 CPU cores using the “doParallel” package.

Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t‐tests. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. In addition, the calibration performance of the clinical prediction models was evaluated by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with p > 0.05 usually indicating a good fit between the model the actual data. The performance of two prediction models was evaluated by comparing their AUC values using DeLong's test. Integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) were used to evaluate the prediction performance improvement of a new model over the baseline model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to show the net benefit of using a model at different thresholds to assess the clinical value of the model. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and compared using chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests. Pearson and Spearman correlations were used for correlation analysis. For all statistical tests, a two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise specified. Detailed methods are described in the results and figure legends.

Results

Baseline Clinical Information

As depicted in Figures 1 and 2A, a total of 522 high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients with pathologically confirmed diagnoses were included in this study following post‐imputation preprocessing (Table S1, Figure S1). Of these, 227 were male (43%) and 295 were female (57%), with a mean age of 31.1 ± 18.7 years. Based on the presence or absence of pulmonary metastasis at diagnosis, patients were divided into two groups: the non‐pulmonary metastasis group (N = 488) and the pulmonary metastasis group (N = 34). We systematically collected and compared demographic characteristics, clinical pathological parameters, laboratory test results, and treatment modalities between the two groups.

Study flowchart.

Patient selection process and feature selection for the prediction model. (A) A flowchart of patient enrollment. (B) Boxplot shows the feature importance ranking calculated by Boruta. Venn diagram illustrates the intersection of variables selected by logistic regression and Boruta (n = 522).

In the overall population, the most common primary sites for high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma were the femur (221 cases, 42%), followed by the tibia (73 cases, 14%) and the humerus (58 cases, 11%). The majority of patients (93%) were diagnosed with Enneking stage IIB high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma. Univariate analysis revealed that the tumor's largest diameter was significantly associated with pulmonary metastasis (p < 0.001), consistent with multiple studies showing that larger tumor size is an independent risk factor for pulmonary metastasis [30]. Regarding treatment, the proportion of patients undergoing amputation surgery was significantly higher in the metastasis group than in the non‐metastasis group (25% vs. 4.5%, p < 0.001), which may reflect the need for more aggressive surgical approaches in advanced‐stage patients.

Laboratory test results showed that patients in the pulmonary metastasis group exhibited significantly elevated systemic inflammatory response and poorer nutritional status. Specifically, the percentage of monocytes (8.77% vs. 7.17%, p = 0.007), neutrophils (80.53% vs. 68.83%, p < 0.001), and globulin levels (28.67 vs. 24.69, p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the metastasis group, whereas lymphocyte percentage (16.86% vs. 21.62%, p = 0.008) and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) (6.95 vs. 9.14, p < 0.001) were significantly lower. Coagulation tests revealed that the international normalized ratio (INR) (1.05 vs. 1.00, p = 0.005), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) (32.62 vs. 28.93, p < 0.001), and prothrombin time (PT) (13.26 vs. 11.33, p < 0.001) were significantly prolonged in the pulmonary metastasis group. Furthermore, levels of hydroxylbutyrate dehydrogenase (179.25 vs. 150.00, p = 0.007) and fibrinogen (3.78 vs. 3.18, p = 0.001) were also significantly elevated in the metastasis group. These indicators reflect the critical role of systemic inflammation and coagulation dysfunction in the development of pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma.

Independent Risk Factor Analysis

To identify independent risk factors for early pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients, we performed multi‐stage statistical analysis. Initially, correlation analysis was used to filter variables (Figure S2), followed by univariate logistic regression, which identified 19 potential risk factors related to early pulmonary metastasis (p < 0.05). A multivariate logistic regression analysis further narrowed these down to 12 independent risk factors (Table S2). To validate the importance of these factors, we applied the Boruta feature selection algorithm, intersecting factors with an importance value greater than 0 with the results from multivariate analysis. This process ultimately confirmed 12 key risk factors for inclusion in the predictive model (Figure 2B).

The final model included variables such as prothrombin time, eosinophil percentage, INR, globulin, monocyte percentage, neutrophil percentage, PNI, amputation, tumor size, gender, fibrinogen, and monocyte count. These factors represent various aspects of the body's coagulation function, immune‐inflammatory state, and nutritional metabolism, partially aligning with previous studies [31].

Model Development, Performance Comparison, and Validation

Referring to previous studies, we have employed the SMOTE method to achieve balance in the sample size [32] we employed a 10‐fold cross‐validation strategy for internal validation, constructing and comparing 11 ML models (Figure S3). Results from the training set showed that all models exhibited good predictive performance for early pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma, with AUC values above 0.80 (Figure 3A,D). The GBM performed the best (AUC = 0.891, 95% CI: 0.859–0.923), demonstrating excellent discriminative ability. It was followed by the Naive Bayes model (AUC = 0.860, 95% CI: 0.824–0.896) and the SVM‐Radial model (AUC = 0.848, 95% CI: 0.808–0.889). DCA indicated that the Gradient Boosting model exhibited the best clinical net benefit across the entire threshold probability range, followed by the SVM‐Radial model (Figure 3B). Additionally, the GBM achieved the highest Youden Index, specificity, sensitivity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy (Figure 3C).

In the test set, the predictive performance of the models showed a slight decline compared to the training set, but overall, they still demonstrated good generalizability. The GBM remained the best‐performing model (AUC = 0.742, 95% CI: 0.664–0.819), outperforming the other models (Figure 3E). The Neural Network model (AUC = 0.743, 95% CI: 0.663–0.824) and SVM‐Linear model (AUC = 0.739, 95% CI: 0.658–0.819) performed similarly. Logistic Regression, Elastic Net, and Partial Least Squares models had AUC values around 0.73, with minor differences between models (Figure 3F,H). DCA indicated a slight reduction in clinical net benefit across all models in the test set (Figure 3G, Figure S4). Calibration curves suggested good model fit (Figure 4A,B). Overall, all models exhibited moderate to high predictive performance in the test set, with the GBM demonstrating the best overall predictive performance, comparable to the previously reported AUC of 0.792 for the Support Vector Machine model. Furthermore, DeLong, NRI, and IDI analysis indicated that the GBM significantly outperformed the other models (Table S3).

Model Interpretability Analysis

To enhance clinical applicability and acceptance by physicians, we conducted an interpretability analysis of the GBM, which demonstrated the best predictive performance using SHAP. This method calculates the contribution of each feature to the prediction result, providing both global feature‐level explanations and individual‐level local explanations.

The largest tumor diameter was identified as the most influential predictive feature, indicating that larger tumor size significantly increases the risk of pulmonary metastasis. This finding may reflect the greater biological aggressiveness and propensity for micro‐metastasis of larger tumors. Amputation surgery was also found to be highly important in the model, potentially serving as an indirect marker of disease progression and being associated with extensive local tumor invasion or strong potential for hematogenous spread. Inflammatory and coagulation‐related indicators such as neutrophil percentage and fibrinogen levels also showed high SHAP contributions.

In contrast, coagulation indicators such as prothrombin time and INR had relatively lower SHAP values, indicating their limited contribution to the prediction. (Figure 5A,B).

Discussion

This study identifies a set of risk factors for early pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients, utilizing multi‐source clinical data from a hospital EMR system and employing various ML algorithms to construct a predictive model.

While previous studies have provided valuable insights into predicting pulmonary metastasis, they frequently rely on single, conventional statistical methods. Such linear models often face challenges in processing high‐dimensional, heterogeneous data and may not fully capture complex non‐linear relationships or feature interactions [4, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. To address these limitations, our study employs a comprehensive multi‐model comparison approach, enabling a more robust utilization of the clinical data. The integration of EMR data with complex ML algorithms can enhance the development of clinical prediction models [39, 40, 41]. Previous research has shown that the use of EMR data provides more predictive power than relying on the limited data from the SEER database [23]. This study leverages multimodal data from the EMR system, based on more comprehensive real‐world data, to extract detailed patient information. Twelve features, including tumor size, surgical approach, coagulation function, inflammatory markers, and nutritional status, were incorporated. By combining these complex data with ML models, we were able to significantly improve prediction accuracy, helping clinicians identify high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients at higher risk for pulmonary metastasis and enabling early, targeted interventions.

Several studies have reported that the GBM is highly valuable in medical predictive modeling [42, 43, 44, 45, 46]. In this study, the GBM demonstrated an AUC of 0.891 in the training set and 0.742 in the test set, which outperformed most previous models for predicting early pulmonary metastasis in osteosarcoma patients [33, 34, 35]. This study validates the model's excellent application potential, and its accuracy suggests that it could serve as a valuable decision‐support tool integrated into clinical information systems to help clinicians identify high‐risk patients.

This study identifies several factors as predictors for early pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients, including prothrombin time, eosinophil percentage, INR, globulin, monocyte percentage, neutrophil percentage, PNI, amputation, tumor size, gender, fibrinogen, and monocyte count. The largest tumor diameter, as identified by SHAP, was the most valuable predictor. Numerous studies have reported the predictive effectiveness of tumor size for prognosis and disease outcomes [47, 48]. Studies have shown that as tumor volume increases, tumor cell proliferation accelerates, leading to adverse biological conditions such as hypoxia and acidic environments within the tumor [49]. Hypoxic regions in tumors stimulate the secretion of various pro‐invasive factors, enabling tumor cells to invade surrounding tissues and lay the groundwork for metastasis. Larger tumors are more likely to accumulate mutations, enhancing their aggressiveness and metastatic potential [50]. Larger tumors typically exert greater physical pressure on surrounding tissues, causing microenvironment changes such as compression of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels, promoting tumor cell entry into these systems and increasing the likelihood of metastasis to distant organs [51]. Coagulation function plays a critical role in promoting tumor progression, with cancer‐driving events often resulting in a hypercoagulable state. Numerous studies have reported coagulation as a predictor of cancer prognosis, including for prostate cancer, [52] gastric cancer, [53] and thyroid cancer [54]. The mechanisms include activation of pro‐coagulant factors, impaired fibrinolysis, secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, and adhesion between tumor cells, endothelial cells, and blood cells [55]. Clinical research has shown that pro‐coagulant expression on cancer cells is a predictor of metastasis and poor prognosis. Gender differences in tumors have garnered increasing attention. Large‐scale epidemiological surveys suggest that women are more likely to develop cancer and have poorer prognoses compared to men [56, 57, 58, 59, 60]. Generally, women tend to produce more beneficial inflammatory immune responses, which play an anti‐tumor role [61, 62, 63]. These findings provide a comprehensive list of key indicators that clinicians should focus on when monitoring and intervening in patients, allowing for closer follow‐up and more proactive interventions.

A previous study [64], based on three independent cohorts of metastatic breast cancer, advanced soft tissue sarcoma, and aggressive non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, demonstrated that pre‐treatment peripheral blood lymphopenia is an independent adverse prognostic factor for overall survival. Our results are consistent with this finding and further extend it to the specific tumor type of osteosarcoma. In our cohort of conventional osteosarcoma, the peripheral blood lymphocyte percentage in the group without early lung metastasis (n = 488, 21.615% ± 12.304%) was significantly higher than that in the early lung metastasis group (n = 34, 16.863% ± 12.557%, p = 0.008). This may be because persistent lymphopenia rarely occurs in isolation; it is often accompanied by a relative expansion of immunosuppressive subsets such as regulatory T cells [65] and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells [66], which can directly suppress the cytolytic function of residual cytotoxic lymphocytes through the secretion of immunosuppressive mediators including TGF‐β and arginase. More notably, these bone marrow‐derived myeloid cells belong to the same broader bone marrow‐derived cell lineage as the VEGFR1‐positive hematopoietic progenitor cells first identified by Kaplan et al. [67], which are capable of arriving at distant organs before tumor cells and forming cellular clusters. Previous studies have found that tumor‐derived secreted factors and exosomes can further recruit such cells, induce extracellular matrix remodeling and alter vascular permeability in the target organ, thereby converting the pulmonary microenvironment into a pre‐metastatic state [68]. Thus, a reduced peripheral blood lymphocyte percentage may no longer be considered merely an isolated statistical association. These observations may offer potential insights for future immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at restoring lymphocyte function.

This study has some limitations. The model relies on routine clinical and laboratory parameters. It does not include certain known prognostic factors such as alkaline phosphatase. The exclusion of these variables may affect predictive performance. The model also does not capture tumor biological characteristics or molecular markers relevant to metastasis. Additionally, the imbalance in the outcome variable sample size may affect model stability, and the use of algorithms like SMOTE may influence results. Furthermore, although the model's effectiveness is demonstrated in internal validation, its extrapolative performance needs further validation in prospective, multicenter, large‐scale cohorts. The current model development is based on single‐center data, which may have specificities in terms of population genetic backgrounds, treatment protocols, and other factors, potentially limiting the model's applicability in different regions or ethnic groups. While high‐resolution chest CT serves as the primary imaging modality for detecting pulmonary metastases in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma, [69, 70] it presents inherent diagnostic limitations, particularly regarding its low specificity. Although histopathological evaluation via surgical resection or biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic gold standard, routine invasive procedures for all indeterminate sub‐centimeter nodules are clinically impractical.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we successfully developed an interpretable ML model to predict the risk of early pulmonary metastasis in high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients using easily accessible clinical data from EMR systems. The final GBM demonstrated excellent predictive ability in both the training and testing phases. It has the potential to become a clinical decision‐support tool, helping physicians identify high‐risk patients for early intervention and more personalized treatment strategies. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to determine whether individualized treatment plans and follow‐up strategies based on the final predictive model can effectively improve the prognosis of high‐grade conventional osteosarcoma patients at high risk for early pulmonary metastasis.
