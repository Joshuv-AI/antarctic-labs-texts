A new era beckons in asthma: enhanced models of care, disease remission, artificial intelligence and consumer participation
Authors: John Politis, Kerry Hancock, Jo A Douglass, Paul Leong, Philip Bardin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe landscape of asthma management is undergoing significant transformation. This change is driven by several factors: deeper understanding of asthma pathophysiology, appreciation of the long‐term harm of oral corticosteroids, biological treatments that dramatically improve patient outcomes with asthma remission becoming a realistic goal, and a focus on patient‐centred care combined with the use of virtual health and artificial intelligence strategies. This Viewpoint provides a perspective on current dilemmas and then emphasises recent innovations that can provide solutions. If applied judiciously, these advances will enable healthcare professionals to improve outcomes for people with asthma.

Asthma is a disease of prime importance globally, and in Australia up to 10% of the population is affected. 1 Asthma management is changing, with several powerful trends afoot: inhaled‐corticosteroid (ICS)‐based therapy is now recommended as the starting point for as‐needed asthma treatments, 2 biological therapies dramatically reduce exacerbations and improve outcomes, 3 and there is a strong imperative to minimise oral corticosteroid (OCS) use due to consequent harms. 4 The prospect of clinical remission in asthma is emerging as a realistic goal 5 (Figure 1), with a focus on a substantial reduction or elimination of symptoms, exacerbations and treatment‐related adverse effects, allowing patients to achieve optimal health outcomes without compromising safety. There is also increasing emphasis on patient and consumer engagement. Importantly, the recent advent of artificial intelligence (AI) has potential to revolutionise medical practice. 6 Together, these trends indicate that a new era in asthma care is on the horizon that can help to mitigate or resolve several chokepoints.

Clinical remission criteria in asthma. In the community, zero exacerbations, zero corticosteroid use and symptom control are achievable targets in asthma remission. Monitoring of lung function will depend on availability of testing.

What are the key chokepoints in Australia? Asthma is predominantly managed within primary care, and it ranks as the seventh most managed chronic disease in the community. 7 However, in this setting, provision of care is often challenging facing notable difficulties – including lack of resources, limited time for patient consultation, fragmented disease management and difficulty keeping up to date with asthma guidelines. Evidence suggests that traditional approaches in primary care seldom incorporate aspects that are now central to optimal asthma care, including disease phenotyping, identification of treatable traits 8 and averting disruption of the crucial nexus between hospitals and primary care. 9

In this context, opportunities provided by the advent of biological therapies for severe asthma are a good example. Biologics can induce clinical remission, underscoring a need for change and identifying the type 2 airways disease (eosinophilic) phenotype as critically important (Box 1). It is present in 50%–70% of patients with mild asthma and up to 90% of severe asthma, 10 and blocking the responsible pathways with biologic medicines has reduced exacerbations by at least 50%, improved lung function and enhanced patients’ quality of life and disease control. 3 However, in Australia, effective management depends primarily on primary care practitioners (PCPs) for identification and referral of eligible patients for treatment by general and specialist physicians. This in turn depends on regular use of asthma biomarkers (blood eosinophil count) to identify type 2 inflammatory disease, 2 and here the use of new asthma assessment tools with integrated decision support treatment algorithms can help to identify patients who would benefit from referral to medical specialist care for modern asthma therapies. 11 This implies that a key practical step to improve care would be to implement simple questionnaires within decision‐support tools in existing practice‐based electronic health software.

Other innovations that are likely to be highly effective also link profoundly to general and specialist medical practice. First, principles formulated by the recommendations for primary care by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) and the recently updated Australian Asthma Handbook are essential and require application in practice. 2 , 9 In particular, high‐quality spirometry is a cornerstone of asthma diagnosis (and to exclude unnecessary treatment for people who do not have asthma), but it is hampered by workload pressures, availability, cost and technical barriers. These problems will have to be addressed by policy changes, alternative models for reimbursement and operator training. 12 Second, although curing asthma remains unlikely, advances following the use of biologic therapies have provided tantalising evidence of clinical remission. 5 The concept of clinical remission is useful in primary care (and medical specialist care) because it helps to identify patients who do not respond adequately to treatment, meriting optimisation of inhaler therapy (including non‐adherence and technique), treatment of comorbidities that impact symptom control (e.g. gastroesophageal reflux disease, anxiety/depression and upper airway disease) and implementing risk modification strategies (e.g. weight loss and exercise). When remission is not achieved despite these changes, patients will benefit from referral to medical specialist care or asthma services.

A model of care currently in restricted use merits expansion. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) clinics are recommended by international guidelines for the assessment and management of severe and difficult‐to‐treat asthma and have been shown to improve asthma control, reduce OCS use and improve quality of life in people with asthma. 13 In Australia, contemporary models of care position tertiary MDT clinics as a cardinal pillar for management, but unfortunately, access is often limited, and the composition of clinics varies greatly across Australia. However, MDT clinics can play a key role in supporting PCPs within local health service networks, facilitating patient access to specialised services and enhancing collaboration. A key innovation could be to implement standardised referral criteria, based on biomarkers and other indicators of severe disease (such as failing to achieve remission and OCS use), to assist PCPs in identifying patients likely to benefit from medical specialist assessment. Additionally, facilitation of recruitment and training of asthma clinical nurse consultants and other allied health clinicians who can address the needs of people with asthma are an integral part of any MDT strategy that will need support from state and national healthcare providers. Finally, the development of virtual MDTs, which should include PCPs, will facilitate better asthma care and assist with educational strategies.

Importantly, problems that pertain to the current management of asthma are not limited to primary care. Emergency departments (EDs) are another critical component of asthma care in the Australian community. Evidence indicates that management in ED is often discordant with asthma guidelines, 14 and discharge management is frequently suboptimal, with low rates of ICS prescription and no follow‐up scheduled with the patient's long‐term care providers. 15 Often, patients only come to the attention of their primary treating doctor following an exacerbation, and continuity of care may be compromised since regular review by the same doctor may not occur. 16 Effective referral pathways back to the patient's long‐term care providers will reduce ED visits, OCS use and lower healthcare costs, even among patients at early stages of their illness. 17

Improving processes around ED and hospital admissions potentially provides a unique opportunity to improve the care of asthma in these clinical settings, particularly for patients presenting with acute exacerbations. A simple full blood and eosinophil count can be done to phenotype and identify type 2 airway disease, and ICS‐based therapies can be commenced on discharge. ED physicians are willing to prescribe ICS‐based therapy on discharge, despite initial reservations about initiating long‐term medications in this setting. 18 Moreover, patients are willing to try new asthma inhalers on discharge if continuity of care with their long‐term care providers is assured. 19 ED physicians can also play a role in identifying patients with over‐reliance on SABA inhalers and people with frequent OCS use and type 2 airway disease who will likely benefit from specialist respiratory input and biologic agents. Overall, complex but not insurmountable challenges pertain to contemporary models of asthma care in Australia that can be improved by practical initiatives (Box 2).

A promising role for digital health technologies (DHTs) and AI in the assessment and management of asthma is evolving. 6 Passive monitoring using digital inhalers improves inhaler adherence and technique, improves asthma control and reduces exacerbation risk. 6 Mobile apps that incorporate disease questionnaires and other measures such as digital peak flow can enable patients to monitor their asthma more closely and give clinicians reliable, unbiased data. AI and machine learning techniques have shown promise in predicting risk of exacerbation based on symptom pattern, medication adherence and environmental factors 20 and may prompt proactive interventions, such as medication adjustments or timely evaluation by patients' long‐term care providers. Importantly, limitations associated with DHTs and AI warrant careful consideration, including issues related to access, costs and varying levels of technology literacy, which may exclude participation by patients.

Finally, care in asthma is shifting from a clinician‐directed process to a model that embraces consumer partnership. This trend reflects innovations in healthcare that have prioritised patient‐centred care and that attach high importance to the experience of patients, carers and advocates. Empowering patients to take an active role in their asthma management has achieved more nuanced and personalised care, and shared decision‐making has been shown to improve medication adherence, symptom control and quality of life. 21 Asthma guidelines increasingly emphasise the importance of patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) to assess asthma control since they provide key information that reflects the unique experience of the patient. Clinicians may under‐ or overestimate a patient's asthma control, and the use of PROMs can provide an objective measure of the patient experience to guide treatment escalation or de‐escalation. 22 Consumer participation should extend beyond the consultation room to involvement in research, guideline development and service design, ensuring that care pathways are relevant and responsive to community needs.

In summary, Australia has a reputation for excellence in asthma care, innovation and research. We have ‘dropped the ball’ for several reasons that include the removal of asthma as a national health priority, ageing models of care, fragmented pathways linking ED/hospitals and primary care, patient and clinician complacency about the seriousness of asthma and a failure to adopt some pioneering innovations. Important opportunities remain to modernise models of care, strengthen integration across primary care, hospitals and emergency departments, and fully embed patient‐centred and digitally supported approaches (Box 2). While OCS stewardship is rightly emphasised given the recognised harms of cumulative exposure, reduction in asthma‐related mortality in recent years has occurred alongside wider access to effective systemic therapies as a part of evolving asthma care. Building on these gains, contemporary strategies focussed on remission, multidisciplinary collaboration, digital health technologies and consumer partnership offer abundant potential. We owe it to our patients to take stock, consider options and then refocus to leverage opportunities that will deliver best‐practice care for Australians with asthma. In the future, imagination will be our only constraint.
