Global prevalence of neonatal mortality in preterm infants: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Isabela Cristina Santos Freire de Paula, Caroline Souza dos Santos, Renata Iani Werneck, Cristiano Miranda de Araujo, Ádelin Olívia Lopes Joly Rodrigues, Angela Leite Mendes, Everson Ribeiro de Lima, Michelle de Fatima Tavares Alves, Mario Vianna Vettore, Samuel Jorge Moysés, Juliana Schaia Rocha Orsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe leading cause of neonatal mortality is preterm birth, with the risk of death in the first days of life increasing as the gestational age decreases. The objective was to estimates the global prevalence of neonatal mortality in preterms through a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies.MethodsObservational cohort studies were included irrespective of temporal or linguistic confines. Studies reporting the prevalence of newborns delivered before 37 complete weeks of gestation, with deaths within 28 days postpartum, were included. The bibliographic search and selection were conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed/Medline, SciVerse Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL Information Systems, Scientific Electronic Library Online, EMBASE, Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature and gray literature. Quality assessment was carried out using the Joanna Briggs Institute appraisal tool. Meta-analysis was conducted utilizing RStudio software.ResultsA total of 47 articles were included. Of them, 29 studies analyzed general preterm birth (delivery before 37 complete weeks of gestation), three studies evaluated extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), seven studies assessed very preterm (28 to less than 32 weeks), and five studies examined moderate to late preterm infants (32 to 37 weeks). The pooled prevalence of neonatal mortality was 8%. When considering only the subgroup of general preterm, the mortality rate was 10%. Extremely preterm infants had a mortality rate of 40%, very preterm infants had a rate of 15%, and moderate to late preterm infants had a mortality rate close to 0%. High heterogeneity was found (I² = 100%; Q-test p = 0; wide prediction interval). Meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine the observed heterogeneity. Factors such as Human Development Index and admission to NICUs influenced neonatal mortality. In terms of quality assessment, 89.1% of the studies were considered adequate in representing the target population. Regarding sample size, 30.4% of studies lacked calculations, hindering evaluation.ConclusionThe prevalence of neonatal mortality was 8 per 100 premature births. The prevalence of neonatal mortality varied according to gestational age at birth. These data should be interpreted with caution as it was not possible to identify all sources of heterogeneity in the meta-analysis.

Introduction

It is estimated that approximately 13.4 million babies were born prematurely in 2020 [1, 2]. Preterm births are defined as births occurring before 37 complete weeks of gestation or 256 days of gestation counted from the first day of the last menstrual period [3]. The leading cause of neonatal mortality (NM) is preterm birth, with the risk of death in the first days of life increasing as the gestational age decreases [4–6]. A child’s chances of survival increase as the gestational weeks progress, leading to a reduced length of hospitalization and lower risk of neonatal complications [7].

In Brazil, extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks) with low birth weight may have 200 to 300 times higher chances of death in the first 28 days of life, making this the leading cause of neonatal mortality in the country [8]. It is estimated that in Peru, 25% of neonatal deaths are attributed to preterm birth [9]. The prevalence of neonatal death was 44% was found, although this value may be higher, given that a portion of the population lives in rural areas without access to healthcare services [10]. One in eight babies is born prematurely in the United States of America (USA). In 2013, 67% of all infant deaths in the USA were among premature infants [11, 12].

Policies aiming at improving maternal health and well-being during pregnancy can help reduce the prevalence of preterm births, thereby decreasing infant mortality [13]. However, the prevention and management of preterm birth require a better understanding of risk factors and the involved mechanisms [4, 14]. To enhance the epidemiological understanding of preterm births, it is essential to improve the quality and quantity of data, including standardizing definitions, measurements, and report [15].

Many countries face challenges due to the lack of high-quality registration systems, compromising the availability of essential information for public policies at national and global levels. Preterm birth is a global challenge with an impact on neonatal and infant mortality, recognized in the Sustainable Development Goals [16], that requires improvements in maternal and neonatal care and the strengthening of standardized data. Investing in research and effective measurement tools is essential, especially in resource-limited settings, to reduce mortality rates [4, 15]. Collecting international data on gestational age distribution enables the quantification of preterm births on a global scale and allows for comparisons of preterm birth rates between countries and overtime, considering the level of development, healthcare organization, and existing health promotion strategies [17, 18].

Understanding the prevalence of neonatal mortality in preterm infants is of utmost importance for prevention, risk identification, and proper management of preterm babies, with the aim of reducing early mortality rates [4, 18]. However, a comprehensive global assessment of neonatal mortality prevalence absent. Taking this into consideration, the objective was to estimate the pooled global prevalence of neonatal mortality in preterm infants through a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies.

Methods

Systematic review of cohort studies, based on the recommendations of the Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) [19, 20]. The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (No. CRD42022308030). The following research question was structured in the CoCoPop [21] format: “What is the global prevalence of neonatal mortality among preterm babies?”

Condition (Co): Neonatal mortality.

Context (Co): In the world.

Population (Pop): Preterm babies.

Study Type (S): Cohort Study.

Eligibility criteria

Observational cohort studies were eligible for inclusion. Studies involving babies born before 37 complete weeks of gestation that reported mortality rates occurring within 28 days after birth (neonatal mortality) were included. Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies involving only of mothers with specific health conditions (e.g., hypertensive or diabetic women, or women with previous abortion); (2) studies with a specific age group (e.g., adolescents or women of advanced age); (3) studies including only women with multiple gestations; (4) studies that included stillbirths in the definition of neonatal mortality; (5) studies that included deaths occurring during childbirth; (6) studies without information about gestational age at birth; (7) studies including only low birth weight babies (< 2.500 g); (8) studies focusing on perinatal mortality (0–6 days), with or without stillbirths; (9) review articles or research protocols.

Search strategy

The bibliographic search and selection were conducted across multiple databases, including Pubmed/Medline, SciVerse Scopus, Web of Science, Cinahl Information Systems, Scientific Electronic Library Online (SciELO), EMBASE, and Literatura Latino-Americana e do Caribe em Ciências da Saúde (LILACS). Controlled vocabulary and free terms connected by Boolean operators were utilized, aligning with the guiding question of the study. The vocabulary was constructed following the previously presented acronym. No restrictions applied to publication date or language.

The search terms were derived from the primary articles used to formulate the study question and from relevant systematic reviews on the topic. The search strategy employed in each database is outlined in table format (Table 1).

Gray literature was also searched to identify potential studies not found in the main databases or peer-reviewed journals, including ProQuest Brazilian Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations (BDTD), CAPES Thesis Database, and Google Scholar. Additionally, manual searches were conducted in prominent journals in the field, such as BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth, Maternal and Child Health Journal, and International Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology. Searches were also performed in conference proceedings, including the European Spontaneous Preterm Birth Congress, Clinical Challenges in Labor and Delivery, European Congress on Intrapartum Care Making Birth Safer, and FIGO World Congress of Gynecology & Obstetrics. The reference lists of included articles were also searched to identify any study not identified in the primary search.

All results were imported into the EndNote web reference manager, and selection was carried out using the online platform Rayyan.

Study selection

Initially, articles were selected according to title, followed by abstracts and full texts. Each study from multiple databases was evaluated once. Included studies underwent thorough review to confirm adherence to eligibility criteria. Studies with uncertain eligibility, often due to limited information available in the title or abstract, were retained for full-text evaluation.

Two reviewers (IP and CS) independently conducted the entire article selection process. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Agreement between reviewers was assessed using the Kappa coefficient, with a value exceeding 0.7 [22] deemed acceptable. The Kappa coefficient obtained during study selection after abstract review was 0.724, indicating substantial agreement. Following full-text review, the Kappa coefficient was 0.80, indicating excellent agreement.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was performed using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, independently conducted by two reviewers (IP and CS), with subsequent comparison and discussion of findings. The extraction form was pre-tested, and variables included study identification, study design, study period, city, state, country, nature of data, level of collection, place of collection, origin of data, preterm birth, number of preterm babies, and associated factors.

Quality assessment of articles commenced with evidence level evaluation, utilizing the Joanna Briggs Institute’s “Checklist for Prevalence Studies.” This tool aims to appraise methodological quality and detect biases. The checklist comprises nine items covering sample characteristics, study subject and context, data analysis, disease identification methods, method standardization, statistical analysis, and response rate. Responses options include “yes,” “no,” “inconclusive,” and “not applicable“ [23]. Critical appraisal was conducted by one reviewer (IP) and verified by another (CS), with any discrepancies resolved through discussion. Quality analysis results were graphically represented using Microsoft Word.

Data synthesis

The synthesis of results was carried out qualitatively and quantitatively. The prevalence of neonatal mortality among preterm infants, reported using relative or absolute frequencies, was considered as the outcome. Proportion meta-analyses were conducted using RStudio software (version 2022.12.0, Build 353) to estimate pooled prevalences and 95% confidence intervals using random effects model and the inverse of the variance method. The “meta” package was used to carry out a meta-analysis of prevalence studies [24]. The method used to analyze the data was the inverse of the variance. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore differences in the pooled prevalence estimate [25]. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test [26], I² statistic [27], and Tau².

The following characteristics were considered for heterogeneity analysis: preterm classification, data collection location (neonatal intensive care units (NICU) or other), the Human Development Index (HDI), and year of study (before 2000 and after 2000). Regarding the categorization of preterm birth, the proposal by the World Health Organization (WHO) was considered: extremely preterm (< 28 incomplete weeks of gestation); very preterm (28 to 32 incomplete weeks of gestation), and moderate to late preterm infants (32 to 37 incomplete weeks of gestation). Studies that obtained a sample of newborns with up to 37 incomplete weeks of gestation in general, without categorization, were called “general preterm birth.”

In relation to the study year, a temporal cutoff was made in accordance with the Millennium Development Goals (MDG) [28] and Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) [16]. The Millennium Development Goals (MDG), signed in 2000, committed world leaders to combat poverty, hunger, disease, illiteracy, environmental manipulation, and discrimination against women. In 2015, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were established, seeking to promote prosperity and protect the planet, recognizing that the eradication of poverty must be accompanied by strategies that promote economic growth [16]. It is important to highlight that the study year threshold was made in accordance with MDG [28] and SDG [16], considering that the reduction of preterm birth was a global target during this period.

The Human Development Index (HDI) was used to analyze the social development of the countries where the studies were conducted. HDI is a measure that evaluates human development considering the level of education, income, and longevity [29]. The data were manually retrieved from the official Human Development News website, considering the country and period of each study [29]. Only one study [30] had a change in the HDI during the studied period. In this case, the most recent HDI was considered. When the country did not present HDI in the studied year, the closest year to the study data was selected.

Meta-analysis and Meta-regression with all studies included

Initially, a meta-analysis involving all eligible studies with sufficient data was conducted. A subgroup meta-analysis based on preterm classification was also carried out. Subsequently, a meta-regression was performed to explore the characteristics responsible for heterogeneity, such as preterm classification (general, extremely premature, very premature, moderate to late preterm), Human Development Index (HDI) levels (low, moderate, high, or very high), and exclusive NICU samples (yes or no). Univariate meta-regression initially assessed the variance attributable to each characteristic separately, observing their impact on pooled prevalence. The extent of heterogeneity explained by each predictor was evaluated through the coefficient of determination (R² expressed as a percentage, indicating the proportion of variation in the data explained by the model) and the significance of the regression coefficient (p ≥ 0.05). Significant characteristics were gradually included in a multivariate meta-regression model, prioritizing those with higher R² values.

Individual Meta-analyses according to preterm birth classification

Separate meta-analyses were conducted according to preterm classification to explore differences in the pooled prevalence estimate and sources of heterogeneity [25]. Subgroups were determined when possible, considering the data collection location (Neonatal Intensive Care Units [NICU] or other), the Human Development Index (HDI), and the year of the study (before 2000 and after 2000).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted for all meta-analyses in order to identify potential sources of heterogeneity by excluding studies of low quality and inadequate sample size. To establish the sample size with adequate statistical power in determining prevalence, a sample size calculation was performed using estimates obtained through the global effect when all studies were included. For this calculation, inference was assumed for an infinite population, with a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level.

Publication bias analysis

Funnel plots and Egger’s [31] significance test was employed to assess publication bias.

Considering the definitions of preterm and neonatal mortality according to the WHO, 36 studies [8, 30, 32–36, 38, 41–49, 52, 54–72] adequately presented these concepts, covering both definitions. However, in eight studies [5, 7, 37, 50, 51, 53, 73, 75], only the definition of preterm was provided, while in two other studies [39, 40], neonatal mortality was exclusively addressed.

In total, 29 studies [7, 8, 32–41, 43, 44, 46, 47, 49, 53, 58, 60, 61, 63–70] analyzed general preterm, three studies [30, 54, 73] evaluated extremely preterm infants, six studies [51, 52, 55, 62, 71, 72] assessed very preterm infants, and five studies [5, 48, 56, 57, 59] examined moderate to late preterm infants. Three studies [42, 45, 50] did not provide a breakdown of gestational weeks at birth within the defined categories and were excluded from the meta-analysis.

Neonatal mortality prevalence in the studies was 8% (95% CI: 5% to 13%). In quality assessment, 89.1% of studies adequately represented the target population. Regarding sample size, 30.4% lacked sample size calculations, hindering evaluation. Clear descriptions of neonatal mortality and prematerm occurred in 78.2% of studies. For preterm assessment, 60.8% of studies have used valid methods, with 43.4% using last menstrual period and/or ultrasound, while 15.2% used official population-based data (Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis with all studies, grouped by preterm birth classification

Figure 4 depicts the meta-analysis of 44 grouped studies. For this analysis, three studies [42, 45, 50] were excluded because the gestational age in weeks did not fit into any of the predetermined categories. Considering only the subgroup of general preterm classification, the mortality rate was 11% (95% CI: 7% to 16%). For extremely preterm infants, the mortality rate was 40% (95% CI: 31% to 49%), for very preterm infants the mortality rate was 15% (95% CI: 9% to 26%), and for moderate to late preterm infants the mortality rate was 0% (95% CI: 0% to 1%). High heterogeneity was observed (I² = 100%; Q Test p = 0; T² = 2.93) (Fig. 4).

In the NICU subgroup (n = 29), studies with exclusive NICU samples [32, 33, 35–38, 40, 41, 60, 63, 69] demonstrated a prevalence of neonatal mortality of 19% (95% CI: 11% to 30%). Conversely, in studies where the sample was not restricted to the NICU [7, 8, 34, 39, 43, 44, 46–49, 53, 58, 61, 64–68, 70], the prevalence was 7% (95% CI: 5% to 11%). High heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99%; Q test p = 0; Tau² = 1.23).

Twenty-eight studies were included in the HDI subgroup analyses as HDI value was unavailable for one study [36]. The prevalence of neonatal mortality was lower for countries with high HDI [8, 49, 58, 60, 65, 67, 69] (6%, 95% CI: 2% to 14%) and very high HDI [7, 53, 63] (3%, 95% CI: 1% to 16%) than those with low HDI [32, 33, 35, 37–40, 43, 46, 47] (16%, 95% CI: 10% to 24%) and moderate HDI [41, 44, 61, 64, 66, 68, 70] (12%, 95% CI: 7% to 20%). High heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99%; Tau² = 1.1476; p = 0).

In the sensitivity analysis of both subgroups, the studies with the lowest quality scores in the quality analysis were excluded [36, 39, 53]; however, heterogeneity remained high (I² = 99%; Q test p = 0; Tau² = 1.18). Similarly, heterogeneity persisted at a high level when studies with inadequate sample sizes were removed [39, 53] (I² = 99%; Q Test p = 0; Tau² = 1.23).

Meta-analysis with studies that considered the classification “very preterm” (28 to 32 weeks)

For this section, the NICU subgroups, time of study conduction, and HDI were analyzed (Table 5). Seven studies [51, 52, 55, 62, 71, 72] were included in the subgroup analysis of studies conducted in the NICU. The meta-analysis revealed that those conducted in the NICU [52, 62] presented a prevalence of 30% (95% CI: 23% to 37%), while in studies with an unrestricted sample in the NICU [51, 55, 71, 72], the prevalence was 12% (95% CI: 6% to 20%). Heterogeneity was low for the group admitted to the NICU (I² = 46%; Q Test = 0.17; Tau² = 0.03). It was not possible to detect other sources of heterogeneity in the sensitivity analysis, considering the sample size and methodological quality of the articles.

Regarding the timing of the study, Boland (2015) [71] was not within the established cutoffs periods and then was excluded, leaving six studies. Before the year 2000 [52, 72], the prevalence of neonatal mortality was 29% (95% CI: 21% to 38%), whereas between 2001 and 2015 [51, 55, 62], the prevalence was 12% (95% CI: 5% to 25%), indicating a decrease in preterm birth of more than 50% after 2001. Heterogeneity was smaller for the Before the year 2000 subgroup, but it remained considerable (I² = 75%; Q test p = 0.05; Tau² = 0.02).

Countries with a very high HDI [51, 55, 71] had a prevalence of 9% of neonatal mortality (95% CI: 6% to 15%), while the neonatal mortality was 30% (95% CI: 25% to 35%) for countries with a high HDI [52, 62]. High heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99%; Q test p < 0.01). No other sources of heterogeneity were identified in the sensitivity analysis, considering the study year, methodological quality, and sample size of the studies.

Meta-analysis with studies that considered the “moderate to late preterm” classification (32 to 37 weeks)

Five studies [5, 48, 56, 57, 59] were included in the meta-analysis (Table 5). The prevalence of neonatal mortality of moderate to late preterm infants was close to 0% (95% CI: 0% to 1%). Subgroup analysis was only possible for the HDI groups. It was be observed that heterogeneity remained high, with the prevalence of neonatal mortality among moderate to late preterm infants remaining close to 0%, regardless of the countries’ HDI (I² = 99%; Q test p < 0.01; Tau² = 0.97). The study with the lowest methodological quality score [5] was removed for sensitivity analysis, but heterogeneity remained high (I² = 99%; Q test p < 0.01; Tau² = 0.79).

Meta-analysis with studies that considered the classification “extremely preterm” (below 32 weeks)

Discussion

Preterm birth remains of public health concern and can occur for various reasons. Most cases seem to occur spontaneously and can be caused by infections, multiple pregnancies, chronic diseases, maternal age, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic conditions, alcohol and drug consumption, previous abortion and preterm birth, and unidentified factors, among other factors [3, 11, 76, 77]. Preterm birth contributes to 35% of neonatal mortality, being the leading cause of neonatal mortality worldwide [3, 78]. It is in the interest of the WHO to reduce preterm birth rates and ensure a quality pregnancy and postnatal period, as besides mortality in the early days of life, surviving newborns have a higher likelihood of morbidity and developing chronic diseases [79, 80]. The pooled prevalence of neonatal mortality was 8% when considering the overall classification of preterm; for infants born before 37 complete weeks, this value increased to 10%. Extremely preterm neonates accounted for 40% of neonatal mortality, and very preterm neonates accounted for 25%. Moderate to late preterm infants had a mortality rate of 0%. Factors such as HDI (Human Development Index) and admission to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) also influenced neonatal mortality.

As gestational weeks progress, the prevalence of neonatal mortality decreases. Preterm infants born with low gestational age (GA) are more susceptible to neonatal mortality, as infants with GA less than 28 weeks have 3 to 4 times higher odds of mortality than very preterm infants [7, 62, 71]. This observation was confirmed in this study, as extremely preterm infants exhibited a prevalence of 40%, while moderate to late preterm infants had a prevalence of 0%. Typically, infants with low GA face a greater disadvantage in surviving in non-neonatal hospital settings due to their lower fetal development, increased comorbidities, particularly respiratory distress and limited neurological development, and the absence of specialized care available in neonatal units [41, 71]. Neonates with low GA exhibit a higher risk of mortality associated with preterm-related complications when compared to those born at term [62]. Although mortality prevalence in moderate to late preterm infants is low, studies comparing preterm with term infants reveal significantly higher mortality rates among moderate to late preterm infants. These infants face increased risk of neonatal complications such as respiratory issues, hemorrhage, bacterial sepsis, apnea, hypoglycemia, and jaundice, resulting in elevated morbidity compared to term infants, rendering them a high-risk population [11, 81]. However, using a single classification for gestational age in weeks may obscure prevalence differences, as infants newborns at 34 weeks of gestation are more vulnerable than those who are born at 36 weeks of gestation [7, 48, 59] .

Many of the included studies were conducted in Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICU). The prevalence of neonatal mortality in these studies was more than twice as high than studies with samples from other healthcare services. The survival of newborns admitted to NICU is closely linked to the quality and conditions of care during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period [68]. Birth weight, Apgar score, place of delivery, and gestational age at birth all related to neonatal mortality of these preterm infants [41]. Important NICU newborn sample characteristics include low gestational age and fragile health status. Typically, pregnant individuals at risk of preterm delivery are transferred to neonatal hospitals for intensive care, leading to a higher concentration of vulnerable newborns and increased mortality prevalence. Despite higher neonatal mortality rates in NICUs, it is crucial to note that intensive care for newborns with greater morbidity and lower developmental status improves their chances of survival compared to secondary care hospitals [33, 40, 41, 50]. Preterm newborns represent the majority of cases requiring immediate interventions, such as respiratory support and neonatal resuscitation. These measures are essential to improve survival [82, 83].

Countries with lower Human Development Index (HDI) scores exhibited a higher prevalence of neonatal mortality among preterm infants. One of the indicators within the HDI, falling under the dimension of longevity and healthy life, is life expectancy at birth. However, other indicators, such as per capita income and healthcare investments, also contribute to this higher prevalence [29]. This relationship is evident in countries with less developed healthcare systems, limited infrastructure, and a shortage of qualified healthcare professionals, which face greater challenges in providing specialized hospital care for preterm newborns [80]. Many countries with high neonatal mortality rates have experienced recent conflicts or humanitarian emergencies. Additionally, pregnant women from lower socioeconomic backgrounds have a higher rate of neonatal mortality due to difficulties in accessing quality prenatal care and obtaining adequate perinatal care [8, 50, 68]. Studies conducted in rural areas have also reported high neonatal mortality rates, which can be attributed to limited access to healthcare, lack of awareness, and socioeconomic disparities [32, 34, 44, 49]. This disparity underscores the importance of public policies aimed at reducing mortality in the early days of life [29, 80].

Conducting cohort studies in countries with limited investment in research and education poses significant challenges due to the substantial funding and resources required to monitor populations over time. This difficulty is evident when analyzing article distribution across continents. For example, despite identifying 11 studies in South America and the Caribbean ten studies were conducted in Brazil [8, 61, 62, 64–70]. Conversely, in North America, the United States of America [5, 7, 56, 59, 73], Canada [57], and Mexico [58, 60] each contributed at least one study. Encouraging scientific research should be prioritized as a governmental strategy, as it is crucial for advancing studies related to a country’s population. Such efforts facilitate an understanding of the population’s needs, thereby enabling the development of tailored public health policies to address societal health challenges.

The study conducted in Somalia [36] in 2020 represented an extreme case in terms of prevalence analysis, as it reported a neonatal mortality rate of 48%, focusing on a specific sample of newborns in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). However, the absence of a Human Development Index (HDI) for the country precluded its inclusion in the meta-regression analysis. The authors highlighted several challenges in Somalia, including limited access to laboratory services and healthcare equipment, early discharge from the hospital before completing treatment, and the nomadic lifestyle, all of which contributed to losses in follow-up [36]. Somalia faces dire social and environmental conditions. It is estimated that nearly half of the population requires humanitarian assistance, and the country is grappling with the most severe drought in recent history, resulting in thousands of deaths, famine, and malnutrition [84, 85]. In terms of quality assessment, the study received an overall poor evaluation due to limitations in methodology, including inadequate sample selection, structure, and analysis, a low response rate, and the absence of valid methods for identifying preterm birth.

Analyzing the relationship between study timing and neonatal mortality posed challenges, as most studies with extended follow-ups reported overall neonatal mortality prevalence without categorization by years. This lack of categorization made it difficult to compare with public policy milestones like the Millennium Development Goals (MDG) and Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). Time may influence meta-analysis heterogeneity, particularly since 2000 marked a global effort to reduce neonatal mortality and increase life expectancy [16, 28]. The meta-analysis was restricted to very preterm newborns and studies categorized with a general preterm classification. It unveiled a noteworthy disparity in neonatal mortality prevalence between studies conducted pre-2000 and those from 2001 to 2015, coupled with decreased heterogeneity when accounting for study timing.

In the quality analysis, a significant number of included studies were retrospective and utilized the entire sample of preterm births within the analyzed period, ensuring comprehensive sampling. This approach adequately addressed the target population in 41 studies. However, lack of sample size calculation rendered sample size analysis inconclusive for nearly one-third of the studies. Regarding sample description, most studies accurately specified the gestational weeks analyzed and neonatal mortality days considered. Clear definitions of these concepts are crucial for study standardization, as mortality tends to decrease over gestational weeks and neonatal days, impacting prevalence rates [62, 64]. In terms of valid methods for identifying preterm birth, 43.5% of the studies assessed gestational age at birth using the last menstrual period and ultrasound for obstetric estimation. The Dubowitz test and the Ballard scale were not considered valid methods as they can yield variations in gestational age depending on the professional conducting the assessment [86]. Population-based studies [8, 34, 51, 59, 64, 68, 70] were considered valid methods as they relied on official documents for data extraction.

The present review has limitations to be addressed. Only cohort studies were considered, and other observational studies and government reports were excluded due to the impracticality of analyzing a large volume of documents. Cohort studies, rated at level four in evidence quality, were used, and publication bias assessment suggested symmetry and heavier weight of studied samples [74]. Lack of standardization in defining cohort studies, preterm, and neonatal mortality were limitations. Some authors did not explicitly define the study design, requiring interpretation for inclusion. Accurate neonatal mortality definition is crucial as studies may include stillbirths, perinatal mortality, or early neonatal mortality, impacting prevalence. Proper preterm classification and well-defined criteria were essential for detailed analysis across gestational age, contributing to meta-analysis heterogeneity. The high heterogeneity observed in this study is consistent with prevalence meta-analyses, which often present high I² values, especially when a large number of studies or extreme estimates are involved. It is important to highlight that a high I² does not always reflect true heterogeneity and should be interpreted with caution, supported by sensitivity analyses and prediction intervals [87].

This study enhances understanding the impact of preterm birth on neonatal mortality, elucidating factors that may influence mortality. Such insights are important for the development of public policies and treatment strategies to mitigate risks. Grasping gestational age’s influence on newborn survival is crucial for averting morbidity and mortality. Notably, most of the studies included span from 2000 to 2015, underscoring the necessity for contemporary research to gauge the impact of Human Development Index, developmental goals, and technological advancements in this context.

Conclusion

Global neonatal mortality prevalence stood at 8 per 100 preterm births, varying by gestational age. Studies with a general preterm classification reported a mortality rate of 10 per 100 premature infants, while extremely preterm infants had a rate of 40 per 100. Very preterm infants experienced 15 per 100, and moderate to late preterm infants less than 1 neonatal death per 100 live premature births. Factors such as the HDI, NICU admission, and study timing impacted neonatal mortality. Interpretation of these findings warrants caution due to unidentified sources of heterogeneity in the meta-analysis.
