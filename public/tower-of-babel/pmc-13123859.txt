Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus Nasal Swab Utilization as a Predictor for Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus Skin and Soft Tissue Infections: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Thomas Kalinoski, Michael Kalinoski, Susan Kline, Ann Brearley, Anmol Nigam, James Harmon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections are associated with high morbidity and mortality, though isolation on a culture can often be difficult We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine use of MRSA nasal swabs for the prediction of this organism as a cause of skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI).MethodElectronic databases were systematically searched. Selected studies were combined and data extracted and analyzed to calculate diagnostic performance characteristics.ResultsTwenty-five studies were included. The overall positive predictive value was 70% and negative predictive value 87%. The positive and negative predictive values were 77% and 87% for a mixed composite of SSTIs, 71% and 91% for purulent infections, 63% and 87% for diabetic foot infections, and 48% and 79% for surgical site infections, respectively.ConclusionsNasal screening as a predictor for MRSA SSTIs has a high negative predictive value in areas where MRSA prevalence is low.

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) has long been recognized as one of the leading causes of hospital-acquired infections and has more recently become widespread in the community, with a prevalence of 20 cases per 100 000 individuals [1, 2]. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus is the most commonly identified antibiotic-resistant pathogen in US hospitals and is associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, prolonged length of stay, and increased healthcare costs [3]. It is resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics except 5th generation cephalosporins owing to the presence of PBP2a, a low-affinity penicillin-binding protein encoded by the mecA or mecC genes. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections are often more pathogenic due to a range of virulence factors produced by S. aureus, in addition to antimicrobial resistance, resulting in severe infections. The clinical manifestations of MRSA infections include, but are not limited to, skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs)—including surgical site infections (SSI) and diabetic foot infections (DFIs)—pneumonia, and bacteremia [4].

Diagnostic testing for MRSA varies by infection source but has traditionally relied on definitive culture with susceptibility testing to oxacillin or cefoxitin or, more recently, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification for the mecA gene [5]. Relying on culture data to guide antibiotic therapy typically requires at least 2–3 days of empiric treatment. Moreover, numerous practical barriers to accurate, reliable, and efficient culture collection and interpretation limit the usefulness of these tests in routine practice, and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines recommend against routine culture for nonpurulent SSTIs [6]. As a result, only 23% of all SSTIs are cultured to identify the causative organism [7]. Consequently, in the absence of definitive culture data, treatment for suspected MRSA infections is often empiric and guided by local antibiograms and national, infection-specific guidelines. The IDSA conditionally recommends empiric MRSA coverage, with the choice of oral or IV treatment stratified based on type and severity of infection, for SSTIs exhibiting purulence or based on a number of other risk factors. These factors include penetrating trauma, injection drug use, known past MRSA infection or colonization, neutropenia, or severe and necrotizing infections. For SSI and DFI, further considerations include recent hospitalization or antibiotic use and institutional rates of MRSA [6, 8].

The delay in culture results, or lack of culture altogether, necessitates empiric MRSA therapy when clinical suspicion remains high. In practice, this often results in patients receiving a full course of empiric MRSA therapy, which exposes them to risks of adverse drug effects and contributes to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant organisms while increasing healthcare costs [9]. In addition to the risks of hypersensitivity reactions and Clostridioides difficile infection, other specific antibiotic risks include kidney injury for vancomycin and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX); myelosuppression for linezolid, TMP–SMX, and ceftaroline; and myotoxicity for daptomycin [10].

Interest has recently grown in sampling the anterior nares for MRSA colonization as both a surveillance measure and a potential predictor of subsequent MRSA infection, with initial studies finding an association with an increased risk of invasive MRSA infection, including pneumonia and bacteremia [11–13]. Since then, evidence supporting the use of MRSA nasal screening to guide both diagnostics and antibiotic decision-making has increased [14]. This approach is now widely implemented in hospitals to guide de-escalation of MRSA-targeted antibiotics for pneumonia, largely due to the high negative predictive value (NPV) for these infections described in a review by Parente et al in 2018 [15, 16]. A recent review on the use of MRSA nasal swabs to guide antibiotic stewardship by Saito et al also discussed a possible role for this practice in DFI depending on local MRSA prevalence. They pointed out that the evidence to date for MRSA nasal swab guidance in SSTIs and UTIs was insufficient to support use owing to the extremes of low and high MRSA prevalence in these conditions affecting the NPV [17].

The first systematic review and meta-analysis on the application of MRSA nasal screening to predict MRSA SSTIs in particular was published in 2025 by Bohjanen et al [18], who found an overall NPV of 0.815. These authors included 15 studies of 1970 total adults and children in their analysis without stratification by infection type. Taking advantage of the increasing abundance of studies published on this topic, the primary objective of our meta-analysis was to expand our current knowledge of the adult population with a much larger sample size (25 studies of 14 117 patients). We assessed how this evidence varies by different types of surveillance methods across clinically distinct SSTI subgroups and at different levels of MRSA prevalence. Our subgroup analysis allowed the comparison of test performance among groups considered to have a high prevalence of MRSA (ie, purulent infections [PIs]) for which empiric MRSA therapy is recommended by guidelines, to those at lower or variable prevalence (a mixed composite SSTI group, SSI, and DFI). Furthermore, stratifying by surveillance methods and prevalence allowed us to provide additional insight into the applicability of this study to different practice environments.

The safety of antibiotic de-escalation based on diagnostic results was outside the scope of this study.

METHODS

Literature Review

We conducted a systematic literature review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched for English-language articles involving human subjects published between 1 January 2000 and 1 January 2025. The following search terms were used: (MRSA OR methicillin resistant staph aureus) AND (screening OR PCR OR surveillance culture OR active surveillance OR rapid test) AND (nasal OR nares) AND (skin and soft tissue infections OR SSTI OR cellulitis OR abscess OR diabetic foot infection OR surgical site infection OR wound infection OR impetigo OR ecthyma OR furuncle OR carbuncle). Titles and abstracts were independently screened for relevance by 2 reviewers, followed by full-text review of eligible studies. References of included studies were manually screened to identify additional relevant publications.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included studies that reported MRSA nasal screening using PCR and/or culture and provided rates of culture-confirmed MRSA SSTIs, including DFI and SSI. Exclusion criteria were non-English publications, case reports, case series, abstracts without full-text publications, pediatric populations, and studies addressing wound colonization without infection. In addition, we excluded studies with missing data, those with unequal exposure to decolonization methods, and those in which MRSA swabs were obtained from sites other than the nares.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the diagnostic performance of MRSA nasal screening in predicting MRSA SSTIs, including sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (OR), positive predictive value (PPV), and NPV. Secondary outcomes included factors influencing test performance and variation across studies.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

The following data were extracted: clinical setting, study design, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and rates of MRSA infection confirmed by culture compared with MRSA nasal screening results obtained via PCR or culture.

Methodological quality was independently assessed by 2 reviewers using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [19]. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. For patient selection, unclear risk was assigned when consecutiveness and randomness were not described. Lack of blinding for either the index test or the reference standard was also considered a source of unclear risk. For flow and timing, studies were rated high risk if the interval between the index test and reference standard exceeded 7 days.

Statistical Analysis of Data

We conducted a meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy to pool sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic ORs, PPVs, and NPVs. Univariate analyses were conducted using random-effects logistic regression models to assess sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV, whereas random-effects models for binary data were applied to estimate diagnostic ORs. Results were summarized with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and presented in forest plots, heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, and potential publication bias was evaluated through funnel plots. For bivariate analyses, sensitivity and specificity were modeled jointly using the method of Reitsma et al [20] to account for correlation between outcomes. In addition, prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to infection type and surveillance method. Meta-regression to assess the impact of study prevalence was also performed. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 [21], in RStudio version 2025.05.0.

Subgroups Analysis: Type of Infection

We identified 4 clinically distinct subgroups for analysis: DFIs, SSIs, soft tissue infections not otherwise specified (STI NOS), and purulent infections not otherwise specified (PI NOS).

The DFI subgroup included only DFIs. Lower-extremity wounds in nondiabetic patients were excluded. If surgery was not performed, wound cultures obtained in the emergency department were used; if surgery was performed, surgical wound cultures were used.

The SSI subgroup included only postoperative patients. Two studies involved spine surgery patients and 2 involved abdominal wounds. All cultures were obtained directly from surgical wound sites.

The STI NOS subgroup comprised a mixed composite of heterogeneous studies including cellulitis, PIs, and mixed infections. The location of infection was often variable or unspecified, and many studies did not separate out purulent from nonpurulent infections. Diabetic foot infections and SSIs were excluded, though some lower-extremity wound studies without diabetes status could not be fully excluded.

The PI NOS subgroup included only purulent SSTI. Diabetic foot infections and SSIs were excluded. This subgroup was highlighted because of current guideline recommendations for empiric MRSA coverage in purulent SSTI [6].

Subgroups Analysis: Type of Surveillance Screening

We recognized that surveillance screening tests differ in sensitivity and specificity. The included studies used PCR testing, culture, both methods, or did not specify methodology. For studies with reported methods, additional subgroup analyses were performed to compare diagnostic performance across modalities. Mean sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were calculated within each subgroup.

RESULTS

Study Selection and Quality Assessment

The literature search identified 369 studies in PubMed, 424 in Embase, and 39 in CENTRAL (Figure 1). After removing duplicates, 642 abstracts were reviewed, and irrelevant studies were excluded. Full-text review was performed for 78 studies. Of these, 24 were excluded for missing data, 8 for unequal exposure to decolonization methods, 4 for grouping swabs from multiple sites (such as the axilla and throat) that limited data extraction, 15 for including participants < 18 years of age, and 2 for assessing colonization instead of infection. In total, 25 studies were included and are listed in Supplementary Text 1. Their characteristics were summarized (Table 1) and the studies arranged by infection type (Table 2). The results of the quality assessment are presented in Supplementary Table 1 and Figure 1. Most studies were judged to have an unclear risk of bias for patient selection owing to a lack of information on consecutiveness and randomness. Similarly, the risk of bias for the index test and reference standard was frequently rated unclear due to insufficient reporting of blinding. Of the 25 studies, 10 were rated high risk for flow and timing, most commonly because of the interval between the index test and reference standard. Almost all studies were determined to have low concerns regarding applicability.

Pooled Prevalence and Diagnostic Performance

Meta-analysis of the 25 included studies yielded the following univariate estimates (Table 3; Figure 2): sensitivity 51% (95% CI, 44%–57%), specificity 94% (95% CI, 93%–96%), diagnostic OR 18.8 (95% CI, 13.6–26.0), PPV 70% (95% CI, 61%–77%), and NPV 87% (95% CI, 81%–91%), presented in Supplementary Figures 2–8. The bivariate model produced similar results: sensitivity 53% (95% CI, 47%–58%) and specificity 94% (95% CI, 92%–95%), indicating minimal bias owing to correlation.

Heterogeneity Analysis

Heterogeneity across the 25 studies was statistically significant for all measures. The I2 statistic ranged from 59.1% for sensitivity (P < .0001) to 93.9% for NPV (P < .0001).

Subgroups Analysis: Type of Infection

Prespecified subgroup analyses by infection type included SSI, PI, DFI, and studies reporting multiple infection types (Table 2). The results are presented in Table 3 and Figure 3. Mean sensitivity differed significantly among subgroups (P = .0137), with the lowest sensitivity observed in the SSI group. No significant differences were noted in specificity (P = .8966), which remained high across all subgroups. Positive predictive value did not differ significantly among subgroups (P = .1207), nor did NPV (P = .7649).

Forest plot summary for infection type subgroup estimate of negative predictive value (NPV) of the methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) nasal swab for skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI).

Subgroups Analysis: Type of Surveillance Screening

Subgroup analyses by surveillance method (culture, PCR, both, or unknown) showed no significant differences in mean sensitivity (P = .0981), PPV (P = .0572), or NPV (P = .2594). The NPV results are presented in a forest plot summary in Supplementary Figure 9. The subgroup differences in mean specificity were statistically significant (P = .0013), likely because of specificity being very high in nearly all studies.

Meta-regression Analysis: Prevalence

Meta-regression analysis revealed that NPV decreases significantly with increasing study prevalence (P < .0001) and that PPV increases significantly with increasing study prevalence (P < .0001). The resulting bubble plot for NPV is shown in Figure 4. The meta-regression model predicts that NPV decreases with increasing study prevalence: from NPV = 0.948 at a prevalence of 0.1, to NPV = 0.738 at a prevalence of 0.4, to NPV = 0.306 at a prevalence of 0.7.

Publication Bias

Funnel plots for both sensitivity and specificity were approximately symmetric, indicating no strong evidence of publication bias among the included studies.

DISCUSSION

The most recent IDSA guidelines for SSTIs do not provide recommendations for or against the use of MRSA nasal screening in their diagnostic algorithm [6, 8]. However, obtaining a successful tissue culture for SSTI is often not feasible; therefore, developing an alternative method to help support diagnostic reasoning would be welcomed. Owing to the lack of guideline support and limited comprehensive clinical evidence, MRSA nasal screening for this purpose has not been widely adopted in clinical practice.

This meta-analysis contributes to the growing body of literature assessing the diagnostic utility of MRSA nasal swabs to predict MRSA SSTIs. To our knowledge, it is the first study to comprehensively evaluate purulent infections, DFIs, and SSIs as distinct subgroups. Across infection types, the MRSA nasal swab performed well in terms of diagnostic OR, reflecting the relative likelihood of true positives compared with false positives [22]. The NPV, defined as the proportion of patients with a negative result who truly do not have MRSA, was consistently high across infection types, although lower in studies with high disease prevalence. In contrast, this test displayed poor pooled sensitivity for the detection of MRSA SSTI. Similarly, the PPV, defined as the proportion of patients with a positive result who truly have MRSA, was relatively low [23]. Overall, test performance was similar across subgroups, except for SSIs, where sensitivity was even lower and certainty of evidence reduced by small sample sizes and wide CIs. The high overall NPV observed in this analysis suggests that MRSA nasal screening may help aid clinical decision-making related to MRSA SSTIs. Though in the appropriate clinical context antibiotic selection could be influenced as a result, it should be noted that the safety of antibiotic de-escalation was not specifically studied in this meta-analysis. Therefore, the use of MRSA nasal screening should not be considered definitive for de-escalation, particularly in areas of high MRSA prevalence or in severe or complicated infections. Despite this, early literature on the outcomes of antibiotic de-escalation protocols for SSTI using MRSA nasal swabs do show promise in decreased length of MRSA-directed treatment [24–26]. A 2023 study on this topic in DFI by Harb et al identified a significant decrease in MRSA-targeted antibiotic duration with also similar rates of hospital re-admission, length of stay, and mortality with a trend toward lower rates of acute kidney injury [27]. More research is needed to support these findings before definite conclusions can be drawn. The low sensitivity and PPV across all infection subtypes indicate that this test is not useful for the diagnosis of or to “rule in” MRSA SSTI and should not be used for this purpose. In cases of positive results, confirmatory deep wound cultures, ideally from surgical debridement or incision and drainage, should be considered.

Subgroup findings add nuance to potential applications. For purulent infections, MRSA nasal screening may be particularly useful. Although guidelines recommend empiric MRSA coverage for purulent infections [6], not all purulent SSTIs are due to MRSA; some are caused by methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus or other pathogens. In practice, cultures are not always obtained, and when they are, they often yield no growth [7, 28]. A rapid, simple nasal swab with high NPV could provide a practical initial test in such scenarios. Nonpurulent SSTIs are typically attributed to Streptococcus species, and guidelines do not recommend empiric MRSA coverage [6]. However, clinician tendency to overtreat and the presence of MRSA risk factors in both healthcare and community settings indicate that MRSA nasal screening could still play a role even in nonpurulent cases [29]. For DFIs and SSIs, subgroup analysis is clinically relevant because these infections carry distinct MRSA risks [7]. We noted a high NPV in the DFI subgroup. The SSI subgroup, however, had very low sensitivity and comparatively low NPV, suggesting limited clinical utility for MRSA nasal swab utilization. However, given the small number of available studies and substantial heterogeneity, additional research would be useful for this population.

Several limitations should be noted. In regions with high MRSA prevalence, the NPV of nasal screening decreases, reducing its reliability. As shown in our sensitivity analysis (Table 5), when the prevalence of MRSA SSTI in the population exceeds 20%, the NPV falls below 0.9. Therefore, any interpretation of a MRSA nasal swab result must consider local prevalence. Furthermore, a high degree of heterogenicity evident in the pooled performance values introduces inconsistency in our results—raising the possibility of misleading averages and reducing confidence in our conclusions. Heterogeneity is not unexpected given the diversity in types of SSTIs included and the range of culture methods used (such as superficial swabs or operating room tissue sampling). These factors, along with the difficulty in differentiating between colonization and infection for SSTIs, makes diagnostic testing inherently more complex and prone to variability in performance compared to that of other infections, such as pneumonia or urinary tract infections. Further differences in screening methods, prevalence across populations, timing of sample collection, and healthcare settings between studies also contribute to overall heterogeneity. Most of the included studies enrolled few patients with critical illness, necrotizing soft tissue infection, or complicated infections, such as osteomyelitis or septic arthritis, which limits the generalizability to these high-risk groups. The use of MRSA nasal screening is also likely to be less applicable in patients with recent MRSA infection, prior nasal decolonization, or immunosuppression.

This study has certain limitations. Owing to the heterogeneous nature of the included studies, we could not include a meaningful subgroup involving only nonpurulent SSTIs, creating a mixed composite subgroup (STI NOS) instead. Although still valuable, this will limit the applicability of our findings. Most included studies were retrospective, raising concerns about sampling bias (such as the availability of culture data) and information bias (such as misclassification of the infection type). Each included study also had variable terminology of what was considered an SSTI, raising the possibility of inclusion of cases of colonization without infection (Supplementary Table 2). All included studies were also methodically limited to cases in which a definitive culture was able to be obtained, introducing selection bias. Small study sizes increased variability across subgroups, particularly in the SSI group. Important confounders, such as recent hospitalization, residence in long-term care facilities, immunosuppression, burns or ulcers, and intravenous drug use, were not consistently reported across studies [7].

CONCLUSIONS

This meta-analysis revealed that the application of MRSA nasal screening as a predictor for culture-confirmed MRSA SSTIs has a high NPV only in areas of low MRSA prevalence while displaying low sensitivity and PPV overall and across subgroups. The results were limited by high overall heterogeneity in the pooled values. These findings may inform clinical practice and serve as a foundation for future guideline development.
