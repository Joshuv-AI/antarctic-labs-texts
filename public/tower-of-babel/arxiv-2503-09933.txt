Fast Maxwell Solvers Based on Exact Discrete Eigen-Decompositions I. Two-Dimensional Case
Lixiu Wang, Lueling Jia, Zijian Cao, Huiyuan Li, Zhimin Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we propose fast solvers for Maxwell’s equations in rectangular domains . We first discretize the simplified Maxwell’s eigenvalue problems by employing the lowest-order rectangular Nédélec elements and derive the discrete eigen-solutions explicitly, providing a Hodge-Helmholtz decomposition framework at the discrete level. Based on exact eigen-decompositions, we further design fast solvers for various Maxwell’s source problems, guaranteeing either the divergence-free constraint or the Gauss’s law at the discrete level. With the help of fast sine/cosine transforms, the computational time grows asymptotically as 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log n) with n n being the number of grids in each direction. Our fast Maxwell solvers outperform other existing Maxwell solvers in the literature and fully rival fast scalar Poisson/Helmholtz solvers based on trigonometric transforms in either efficiency, robustness, or storage complexity. It is also utilized to perform an efficient pre-conditioning for solving Maxwell’s source problems with variable coefficients. Finally, numerical experiments are carried out to illustrate the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed fast solver.

Introduction

Electromagnetic phenomena are described by Maxwell’s equations
[
1
]
,
originally presented using first-order curl operators. Many of the Maxwell’s equations can be transformed into the curl-curl formulation.
Consider the model problem of the Maxwell’s curl-curl equations,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{curl}\ \text{rot}\ \bm{u}}+\alpha\bm{u}=\bm{f}\quad{\rm in}\;\Omega,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=0\quad{\rm in}\;\Omega,

(1.1b)

subject to one of the following essential/natural boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bm{u}\times\bm{n}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(1.2a)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{rot}\ }\bm{u}=0\;{\text{and}}\;\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(1.2b)

where
\bm{u}
represents an electric field,
\bm{f}
is the current density,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is a given constant, and
\bm{n}
is the unit outward normal of the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

The curl-curl equations (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) are widely encountered in
time-dependent electromagnetic fields
[
2
]
, time-harmonic fields
[
3
,
4
]
, cavity eigenvalue problems
[
5
]
, magnetohydrodynamic problems
[
6
]
, and so on. Therefore, the development of fast solvers along with appropriate numerical approximation schemes is of particular importance.

Nédélec originally proposed two families of curl-conforming edge elements
[
7
,
8
]
in 1980s, which exactly satisfy the intrinsic requirement of tangential continuity of an electric field. Kikuchi
[
9
]
introduced a mixed formulation by incorporating a Lagrange multiplier that maintains the divergence-free constraint in the weak sense. Their efforts laid a state-of-art foundation for Maxwell’s curl-curl equations, guaranteeing
the well-posedness of discrete problems
[
3
,
10
]

and maintaining the divergence-free constraint at a certain discrete level.
However, the resulting linear algebraic system is larger and of
saddle-point type, making it more difficult to solve than the elliptic problem
. Moreover, it may induce instabilities and spurious solutions, particularly for eigenvalue problems,
as the
degrees of freedom increase.

To solve the saddle-point problems effectively, some methods have been proposed, such as null-space methods
[
11
,
12
]
for transforming an indefinite system into a symmetric positive definite one of smaller dimension, coupled direct solvers
[
12
]
based on triangular factorizations, and Krylov subspace methods
[
13
]
. Besides, preconditioning techniques also have been widely studied, such as block triangular preconditioners
[
14
,
15
]
, inexact constraint preconditioners
[
16
]
, shift-splitting preconditioners
[
17
,
18
]
, primal-based penalty preconditioners
[
19
]
and the references therein.
Moreover, to avoid the saddle-point problem, a
\delta
-regularization method was developed in
[
20
]
to completely ignore the divergence-free constraint.

There are some work on fast solvers for Maxwell’s curl-curl equations. The discrete H(curl) systems could be efficiently solved by geometric multigrids
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, as well as the nodal auxiliary space preconditioner
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, i.e., the popular Hiptmair–Xu preconditioner. Besides, the domain decomposition methods
are frequently employed for solving problems in H(curl), see
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
and the reference therein.
Although a fast algorithm for three-dimensional photonic crystals was found in
[
33
]
,
which combines the null-space free method with fast Fourier transform-based matrix-vector multiplications,
little work has been reported on fast direct solvers for Maxwell’s curl-curl equations up to the present.

The primary purpose of this paper is to design some fast solvers for the Maxwell’s equations (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) with either (
1.2a
) or (
1.2b
) for boundary conditions on a rectangle in two dimensions. Distinct to existing work, the Maxwell’s curl-curl equations are discretized
by using the lowest-order edge elements with a naive variational form instead of the mixed form, and the resulting algebraic linear system
is then solved
in the Hodge-Helmholtz decomposition framework which is constructed through exact discrete eigen-decompositions.
Indeed, as proved in Section
3
and Section
5.1
, the discrete eigen-decompositions of the eigenvalue problem of the Maxwell’s equation,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{curl}\ \text{rot}\ \bm{u}}=\lambda\bm{u}\quad{\rm in}\;\Omega,

(1.3)

with boundary conditions either (
1.2a
) or (
1.2b
), establishes two types of eigenfunctions constituting the entire approximation space:
one is divergence-free
and corresponds to non-zero eigenvalues, while the other satisfies the curl-free constraint and associates with zero eigenvalues.
It is then not astonishing that the numerical solution of the Maxwell’s source problem is simply a linear expansion of all divergence-free discrete eigenfunctions,
whose coefficients can be efficiently evaluated via fast subroutines such as FFTW
[
34
]
for the discrete sine/cosine transforms (DSTs/DCTs) in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log n)
operations. In this way, fast Maxwell solvers are then designed just like fast solvers for scalar Poisson/Helmholtz equations.
Moreover, the divergence-free constraint is naturally guaranteed at the discrete level, and instabilities and spurious solutions are then avoided. The obvious superiority of the fast Maxwell solvers on computational time and storage requirements over other existing direct solvers are demonstrated by various numerical experiments.

We also aim at the fast solvers guaranteeing the Gauss’s law at the discrete level for general Maxwell’s equations,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{curl}\ \text{rot}\ \bm{u}}+\alpha\bm{u}+\nabla p=\bm{f}\quad\text{ in }\;\Omega,

(1.4a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\rho\quad\text{ in }\;\Omega,

(1.4b)

\displaystyle\bm{u}\times\bm{n}=0,\;p=0\quad\text{ on }\;\partial\Omega,

(1.4c)

where
p
represents the multiplier/pseudo-pressure and
\rho
is the charge density. Additionally,
an extended study on our fast solvers as an efficient pre-conditioning for solving the Maxwell’s source problem with variable coefficients
is also carried out
for
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
for some
\beta_{0}>0
.

In summary, the novelty of this work resides in three aspects:

(1)

By exploring the eigenvalue decomposition of the discrete Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem, we find a fast way to compute the specific form of the Hodge-Helmholtz decomposition for electromagnetic fields on rectangular domains at the discrete level.

(2)

We design fast solvers for various Maxwell’s source problems based on exact eigen-decompositions. The computational time grows asymptotically as
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log n)
, making our fast Maxwell solvers as efficient as the fast Poisson/Helmholtz solvers based on the trigonometric transforms.

(3)

Our fast solver, based on the Hodge-Helmholtz decomposition, rigorously adheres to divergence-free constraint at the discrete level, thereby completely avoiding spurious solutions and numerical instability.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we formulate the approximation scheme for the curl-curl equations (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) and (
1.2a
) in matrix form by specifying the basis functions of the lowest-order edge element space and the bilinear element space. Additionally, we provide some notations and preliminaries.
In Section
3
, we analyze the exact discrete eigen-decompositions of the Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem (
1.3
), and
a fast solver for the discrete Maxwell’s source problem is then designed in Section
4
.
The algorithm is further extended to
the Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem and source problems with natural boundary conditions in Section
5
.
Its extension to general Maxwell’s equations (
1.4a
)-(
1.4c
) is discussed in Section
6
.
Illustrative numerical results are reported in Section
7
to confirm the computational efficiency. Finally, a conclusion remark is given in Section
8
.

2.2
Approximation spaces and numerical schemes

For simplicity, we assume that
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
and let

\mathcal{T}_{h}=\left\{K_{i,j}:=[x_{i-1},x_{i}]\times[y_{j-1},y_{j}]:\ 1\leq i,j\leq n\right\}

be a
n\times n

\Omega
with
x_{\nu}=y_{\nu}=\nu h
and
h=\frac{1}{n}
.
Then the lowest-order edge element space is given by

\displaystyle U_{h}=\left\{\bm{w}_{h}\in H(\text{curl};\Omega):\ \bm{w}_{h}|_{K_{i,j}}\in Q_{0,1}\times Q_{1,0},\ \forall K_{i,j}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

(2.1)

where the local Nédélec edge basis functions are explicitly presented in

[
2
]
.
Meanwhile, the bilinear element space is defined by

\displaystyle S_{h}=\left\{{q}_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega):\ q_{h}|_{K_{i,j}}\in Q_{1,1},\;\forall K_{i,j}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

(2.2)

Their subspaces with essential boundary conditions
are given by

\displaystyle U^{0}_{h}=U_{h}\cap H_{0}(\text{curl};\Omega),

\displaystyle S^{0}_{h}=S_{h}\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(2.3)

where
H_{0}(\text{curl};\Omega):=\{\ \bm{u}\in H(\text{curl};\Omega):\ \bm{u}\times\bm{n}=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\}.

Then, the numerical scheme in the
straightforward mixed
variational form for (
1.1a
)-(
1.2a
)
reads: to find
\bm{u}_{h}\in U_{h}^{0}
such that

\displaystyle\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{rot}\ }\bm{u}_{h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{rot}\ }\bm{w}_{h}\right)+\alpha(\bm{u}_{h},\bm{w}_{h})=(\bm{f},\bm{w}_{h}),\quad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{w}_{h}\in U_{h}^{0},

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{u}_{h},\nabla q_{h}\right)=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in S_{h}^{0}.

(2.4b)

Let us write
\bm{u}_{h}
as

\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace\bm{u}_{h}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\widehat{u}_{i,j}\bm{N}^{(1)}_{i,j}(x,y)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{v}_{i,j}\bm{N}^{(2)}_{i,j}(x,y)\in U^{0}_{h},

(2.5)

where
\bm{N}^{(1)}_{i,j}(x,y)
and
\bm{N}^{(2)}_{i,j}(x,y)
are
the global basis functions of
U_{h}^{0}
corresponding to the (normalized) degrees of freedom along the edge
\{x_{i}\}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[}y_{j-1},y_{j}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}]}

and the edge
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[}x_{i-1},x_{i}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}]}\times\{y_{j}\}

[
2
]
, respectively.
The basis functions satisfy the tangent continuity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(\bm{N}_{i,j}^{(1)}(x_{m},y)\cdot\bm{\tau}\right)\Big|_{l_{n}}=\frac{1}{h}\delta_{i,m}\delta_{j,n},\ \text{for}\ l_{n}=[y_{n-1},y_{n}],

\displaystyle\left(\bm{N}_{i,j}^{(2)}(x,y_{n})\cdot\bm{\tau}\right)\Big|_{l_{m}}=\frac{1}{h}\delta_{i,m}\delta_{j,n},\ \text{for}\ l_{m}=[x_{m-1},x_{m}],

where

is the anti-clock unit tangential vector.

Then we derive the equivalent linear algebraic system for the approximation scheme (
2.4a
)-(
2.4b

\displaystyle\begin{cases}UA-B^{\mathsf{T}}VB^{\mathsf{T}}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha UD=h^{2}F,\\[5.0pt]
-BUB+AV+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha DV=h^{2}G,\end{cases}

(2.6a)

\displaystyle BUD+DVB^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{0},

(2.6b)

where

,

,

,
and
G=\big((\bm{f},\bm{N}^{(2)}_{i,j})\big)_{1\leq i\leq n\atop 1\leq j\leq n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}
,

and
A,D\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
are defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&&\\
-1&2&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
&&-1&2\end{pmatrix},\ D=\begin{pmatrix}4&1&&\\
1&4&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
&&1&4\end{pmatrix},\ B=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&&&\\
&-1&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(2.7)

In fact, the matrices

and

correspond to the discrete one-dimensional Laplace operator, divergence operator, and gradient operator, respectively. Moreover, it holds that

2.3
Discrete sine and cosine transforms

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the discrete sine transform (DST) matrix

\displaystyle S=\left(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2},\dots,\bm{s}_{n-1}\right),

(2.8)

where
\bm{s}_{j}=\Big(\sin\frac{j\pi}{n},\sin\frac{2j\pi}{n},\dots,\sin\frac{(n-1)j\pi}{n}\Big)^{\mathsf{T}},\ 0\leq j\leq n-1.

For any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, the matrix-vector multiplication
S\bm{x}
represents the DST of the first type (DST-I) of
\bm{x}
.
One readily checks that
S
is symmetric
and satisfies the following property
[
36
]

\displaystyle S^{-1}=\frac{2}{n}S.

(2.9)

Moreover, the diagonalizations of
A
and
D
defined in (
2.7
)
are

\displaystyle S^{-1}AS=SAS^{-1}=\Lambda^{2},\quad S^{-1}DS=SDS^{-1}=\Sigma,

(2.10)

respectively, where

\displaystyle\Lambda=\diag\left(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\cdots,\tau_{n-1}\right),\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau_{i}=-\sqrt{2\left(1-\cos\frac{i\pi}{n}\right)}=-2\sin\frac{i\pi}{2n}},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\Sigma=\diag\left(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{n-1}\right),\quad\sigma_{i}=2\Big(2+\cos\frac{i\pi}{n}\Big).

(2.12)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the discrete cosine transform (DCT) matrix

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C=\left(\bm{c}_{0},\bm{c}_{1},\cdots,\bm{c}_{n-1}\right),\end{split}

(2.13)

where
\bm{c}_{j}=\nu_{j}^{n}\Big(\cos\frac{j\pi}{2n},\cos\frac{3j\pi}{2n},\cdots,\cos\frac{(2n-1)j\pi}{2n}\Big)^{\mathsf{T}},\ 0\leq j\leq n
and

\displaystyle\nu_{j}^{n}=1\text{ if }1\leq j\leq n-1,\quad\nu_{j}^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\text{ if }j=0\text{ or }j=n.

(2.14)

For any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
, the matrix-vector multiplications
C^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}
and
C\bm{x}
imply
the DCT of the second type (DCT-II) and of the third type (DCT-III) of
\bm{x}
, respectively.
In addition, we have
[
36
]

\displaystyle C^{-1}=\frac{2}{n}C^{\mathsf{T}}.

(2.15)

We emphasize that all DSTs and DCTs can be implemented by fast sine/cosine transforms (FSTs/FCTs) in
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
arithmetic operations
[
36
]
.

Now we conclude the current section with the following lemma on the diagonalization of the matrix
B
defined in (
2.7
).

Lemma 2.1

It holds that

\displaystyle S^{-1}BC=SBC^{-\mathsf{T}}=\Gamma,

(2.16)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}:=}\left[\bm{0},\Lambda\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n},

(2.17)

and
\Lambda
is defined as in (
2.11
).

Proof

Firstly, one readily gets that
B\bm{c}_{0}=\bm{0}
.
Then for
1\leq j\leq n-1,
we have

\displaystyle B\bm{c}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\cos\tfrac{3j\pi}{2n}-\cos\tfrac{j\pi}{2n},\cdots,\cos\tfrac{(2n-1)j\pi}{2n}-\cos\tfrac{(2n-3)j\pi}{2n}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=-2\sin\tfrac{j\pi}{2n}\left(\sin\tfrac{j\pi}{n},\sin\tfrac{2j\pi}{n},\dots,\sin\tfrac{(n-1)j\pi}{n}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}=-2\sin\tfrac{j\pi}{2n}\bm{s}_{j}.

Thus, it is obtained that

\displaystyle BC

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2},\cdots\bm{s}_{n-1}\right)[\bm{0},\Lambda]=S\Gamma.

(2.18)

Besides, according to (
2.9
) and (
2.15
), we also obtain

\displaystyle BC^{-\mathsf{T}}=S^{-1}\Gamma,

which proves (
2.16
).

\square

3
Exact eigen-decompositions of the discrete Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem

Before coming to the fast algorithm for solving (
2.6a
)-(
2.6b
),
we explore the corresponding eigen-decomposition,

\displaystyle UA-B^{\mathsf{T}}VB^{\mathsf{T}}=\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\lambda^{h}UD,

(3.1a)

\displaystyle-BUB+AV=\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\lambda^{h}DV,

(3.1b)

which serves as a discrete version of the Maxwell’s eigenvalue problem (
1.3
) with the boundary condition (
1.2a
).

To this end, we denote

\displaystyle\widehat{U}=C^{\mathsf{T}}US\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-1)},\quad\widehat{V}=SVC\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}.

(3.2)

Then, premultiplying by
C^{\mathsf{T}}
and postmultiplying by
S
in (
3.1a
),
premultiplying by
S^{\mathsf{T}}
and postmultiplying by
C
in (
3.1b
),
we derive by using (
2.9
), (
2.10
), (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) that

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\Lambda^{2}-\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}=\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\lambda^{h}\widehat{U}\Sigma,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle-\Gamma\widehat{U}\Gamma+\Lambda^{2}\widehat{V}=\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\lambda^{h}\Sigma\widehat{V}.

(3.3b)

Further, premultiplying (
3.3a
) by
\Gamma
, postmultiplying (
3.3b
) by
\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}
,
using the fact
\Gamma\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}=\Lambda^{2}
and summing up the resultants, we find that

\displaystyle\lambda^{h}\big(\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma+\Sigma\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\big)=\bm{0}.

To proceed,
let us first assume that
\lambda^{h}\neq 0,
it then yields that

\displaystyle\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma+\Sigma\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{0},

(3.4)

which is exactly the discrete divergence-free constraint (
2.6b
) on the eigen-functions.
In fact, by (
2.10
), (
2.16
) and (
3.2
), we derive

\displaystyle\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma+\Sigma\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}=

\displaystyle\,(SBC^{-\mathsf{T}})(C^{\mathsf{T}}US)(S^{-1}DS)+(SDS^{-1})(SVC)(C^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,S(BUD+DVB^{\mathsf{T}})S,

which states the equivalence of (
3.4
) and the divergence-free constraint (
2.6b
).
Therefore, all eigenfunctions associated with non-zero eigenvalues of (
3.1a
)- (
3.1b
) are exactly the discrete divergence-free solutions.
In the sequel, we combine (
3.3a
)-(
3.3b
) with (
3.4
), then find that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widehat{U}\Lambda^{2}\Sigma^{-1}+\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma\widehat{U}=\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\lambda^{h}\widehat{U},\\[5.0pt]
\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma+\Sigma^{-1}\Lambda^{2}\widehat{V}=\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\lambda^{h}\widehat{V}.\end{cases}

(3.5)

As a consequence, the eigen-solutions of (
3.3a
)-(
3.3b
) with non-zero eigenvalues read,

\displaystyle\Big(\lambda^{h}_{i,j},\widehat{U}_{i,j},\;\widehat{V}_{i,j}\Big)=\Big(\frac{6}{h^{2}}\Big(\frac{\tau_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}}+\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}}{\sigma_{j}}\Big),{\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}}\bm{e}_{i+1}^{n}\otimes\bm{e}_{j}^{n-1}{}^{\mathsf{T}},\;-{\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}}\bm{e}_{i}^{n-1}\otimes\bm{e}_{j+1}^{n}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\Big),

\displaystyle\qquad 0\leq i,j\leq n-1,\ (i,j)\neq(0,0).

Meanwhile, if
\lambda^{h}=0
, (
3.3a
)-(
3.3b
) reduces to

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\Lambda^{2}=\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad\Gamma\widehat{U}\Gamma=\Lambda^{2}\widehat{V}.

(3.6)

Then one readily concludes the corresponding curl-free eigen-solutions as

\displaystyle\Big(\lambda^{h}_{0},\widehat{U}_{i,j}^{0},\;\widehat{V}_{i,j}^{0}\Big)=\Big(0,\;{\tau_{i}}\bm{e}_{i+1}^{n}\otimes\bm{e}_{j}^{n-1}{}^{\mathsf{T}},\;{\tau_{j}}\bm{e}_{i}^{n-1}\otimes\bm{e}_{j+1}^{n}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\Big),\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n-1.

Finally, by (
3.2
), we summarize the eigen-solutions in the following concise form.

Theorem 3.1

The
2n(n-1)
eigen-solutions to (

\widehat{V}=\big[\widehat{V}(i,j)\big]_{1\leq i\leq n+1\atop 1\leq j\leq n}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{U}(i,1)=0,\ \widehat{U}(i,j+1)=\frac{6h^{2}\sigma_{i}\widehat{F}(i,j+1)}{6\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}^{2}+h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+6\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}^{2}}=\dfrac{h^{2}\widehat{F}(i,j+1)}{(\bar{\lambda}_{i,j}^{h}+\alpha)\frac{h^{2}}{6}\sigma_{j}},

(5.19a)

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(1,j)=0,\ \widehat{V}(i+1,j)=\frac{6h^{2}\sigma_{j}\widehat{G}(i+1,j)}{6\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}^{2}+h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+6\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}^{2}}=\dfrac{h^{2}\widehat{G}(i+1,j)}{(\bar{\lambda}_{i,j}^{h}+\alpha)\frac{h^{2}}{6}\sigma_{i}}.

(5.19b)

6:

Compute
U
and
V
by the inverse FSTs/FCTs:

\displaystyle U=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\bar{S}\widehat{U}\bar{C}\bar{M},\quad V=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\bar{M}\bar{C}\widehat{V}\bar{S}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(5.20)

return

U
and
V
.

Remark 5.1

Premultiplying (
5.15a
) by
\bar{\Gamma}^{\mathsf{T}}
and postmultiplying (
5.15b
) by
\bar{\Gamma}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\Gamma}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{F}+\widehat{G}\bar{\Gamma}=\bm{0},

which is acually the compatibility conditions held by the right-hand side terms,

\displaystyle\bar{B}^{\mathsf{T}}F+G\bar{B}=\bm{0}.

(5.21)

Thus, the array
{G}
is actually not required to be stored in Algorithm
2
and
\widehat{V}
can be evaluated by

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(1,j)=0,\quad\widehat{V}(i+1,j)=-\frac{\tau_{i}}{\tau_{j}}\frac{6h^{2}\sigma_{j}\widehat{F}(i,j+1)}{6\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}^{2}+h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+6\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}^{2}},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n.

When
\alpha\neq 0
is a proper parameter, the Maxwell’s equations

are replaced by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widehat{U}(i,j+1)\!=\!\dfrac{6h^{2}\left(6\tau_{i}^{2}\!+\!h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{i}\right)\widehat{F}(i,j+1)\!+\!36h^{2}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\widehat{G}(i+1,j)}{\left(6\tau_{i}^{2}\!+\!h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{i}\right)\left(6\tau_{j}^{2}\!+\!h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{j}\right)\!-\!36\tau_{i}^{2}\tau_{j}^{2}},\;1\!\leq\!i\!\leq\!n,\,0\!\leq\!j\!\leq\!n,\\

then we design

6
Fast solver for general Maxwell’s equations

In this section, the fast solver is extended to the Maxwell’s equation (
1.4a
) under the Gauss’s law (
1.4b
) with the boundary condition (
1.4c
).
A mixed finite element approximation scheme reads: to find
(\bm{u}_{h},p_{h})\in U_{h}^{0}\times S_{h}^{0}
such that

\displaystyle\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{rot}\ }\bm{u}_{h},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{rot}\ }\bm{w}_{h}\right)+\alpha(\bm{u}_{h},\bm{w}_{h})+(\nabla p_{h},\bm{w}_{h})=(\bm{f},\bm{w}_{h}),\quad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{w}_{h}\in U_{h}^{0},

(6.1a)

\displaystyle(\bm{u}_{h},\nabla q_{h})=-(\rho,q_{h}),\quad

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in S_{h}^{0}.

(6.1b)

Similar to the derivation of (
2.6a
)-(
2.6b
), we obtain the linear algebraic system for (
6.1a
)-(
6.1b
): to find
(U,V,P)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-1)}\times\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
such that

\displaystyle UA-B^{\mathsf{T}}VB^{\mathsf{T}}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha UD-\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}B^{\mathsf{T}}PD=h^{2}F,

(6.2a)

\displaystyle-BUB+AV+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha DV-\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}DPB=h^{2}G,

(6.2b)

\displaystyle BUD+DVB^{\mathsf{T}}=6H,

(6.2c)

where

H=\left((\rho,\varphi_{i,j})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n-1}
with

{\varphi}_{i,j}(x,y)
being the global basis of
S^{0}_{h}
at the nodal point
(ih,jh)
.

Next, we shall explore how to solve (
6.2a
)-(
6.2c
) efficiently. Using (
3.2
) and further introducing

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{F}=C^{\mathsf{T}}FS,\quad\widehat{G}=SGC,\quad\widehat{P}=SPS,\quad\widehat{H}=SHS,\end{split}

(6.3)

one readily checks that the following equivalent equations to (
6.2a
)-(
6.2c
),

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\Big(\Lambda^{2}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha\Sigma\Big)-\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}-\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{P}\Sigma=h^{2}\widehat{F},

(6.4a)

\displaystyle-\Gamma\widehat{U}\Gamma+\Big(\Lambda^{2}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha\Sigma\Big)\widehat{V}-\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\Sigma\widehat{P}\Gamma=h^{2}\widehat{G},

(6.4b)

\displaystyle\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma+\Sigma\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}=6\widehat{H}.

(6.4c)

Firstly, we premultiply (
6.4a
) by
\Gamma
, postmultiply (
6.4b
) by
\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}
,
and then find by summing up the resultants that

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}\widehat{P}\Sigma+\Sigma\widehat{P}\Lambda^{2}=6(\alpha\widehat{H}-\Gamma\widehat{F}-\widehat{G}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}),

(6.5)

which gives a diagonal solver for
\widehat{P}
.
Next, combining (
6.4a
) and (
6.4c
) yields

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\Big(\Lambda^{2}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha\Sigma\Big)-\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}(6\Sigma^{-1}\widehat{H}-\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma)-\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{P}\Sigma=h^{2}\widehat{F},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\widehat{U}\Big(\Lambda^{2}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha\Sigma\Big)+\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma=6\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\widehat{H}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{P}\Sigma+h^{2}\widehat{F}.

(6.6)

Analogously, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma\widehat{V}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma+\Big(\Lambda^{2}+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\alpha\Sigma\Big)\widehat{V}=6\widehat{H}\Sigma^{-1}\Gamma+\dfrac{h^{2}}{6}\Sigma\widehat{P}\Gamma+h^{2}\widehat{G}.

(6.7)

A fast solver for (
6.2a
)-(
6.2c
) is then presented
in Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

Solve
U
,
V
and
P
in (
6.2a
)-(
6.2c
).

1:

the arrays

,

,

; the partition size

; the mesh size

.

the solutions

,

and

.

and
\tau_{i}

by

\displaystyle\tau_{i}=-2\sin\frac{i\pi}{2n},\qquad\sigma_{i}=2\Big(2+\cos\frac{i\pi}{n}\Big).

4:

Perform the FSTs/FCTs:

\displaystyle\widehat{F}=C^{\mathsf{T}}FS,\qquad\widehat{G}=SGC,\qquad\widehat{H}=SHS.

5:

Evaluate

,

, and

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{P}(i,j)=\dfrac{6\big(\alpha\widehat{H}(i,j)-\tau_{i}\widehat{F}(i+1,j)-\tau_{j}\widehat{G}(i,j+1)\big)}{\tau_{i}^{2}\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}^{2}},}

(6.8b)

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(i,j+1)=\dfrac{36\tau_{j}\widehat{H}(i,j)+h^{2}\tau_{j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\widehat{P}(i,j)+6h^{2}\sigma_{j}\widehat{G}(i,j+1)}{6\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}^{2}+h^{2}\alpha\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+6\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}^{2}}.

(6.8c)

6:

Compute
U
,

\displaystyle U=\frac{4}{n^{2}}C\widehat{U}S,\quad V=\frac{4}{n^{2}}S\widehat{V}C^{\mathsf{T}},\quad P=\frac{4}{n^{2}}S\widehat{P}S.

return

and

.

Remark 6.1

The fast solver described in Algorithm
3
preserves the Gauss’s law at the discrete level.

Further if the right-hand side term in (
1.4a
) satisfies

\nabla\cdot\bm{f}=\alpha\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\alpha\rho,

then the solution
p
(resp.
P
) in (
1.4a
) (resp. in Algorithm
3
) is identically zero.
As a result, the compatibility condition
\alpha\widehat{H}-\Gamma\widehat{F}-\widehat{G}\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}=0
on right-hand side terms holds.
This indicates that

\frac{\alpha}{6}\big(\Gamma\widehat{U}\Sigma+\Sigma\widehat{V}\Gamma\big)=\Gamma\widehat{F}+\widehat{G}\Gamma\text{ or equivalently }\frac{\alpha}{6}\big(BUD+DVB^{\mathsf{T}}\big)=BF+GB^{\mathsf{T}}.

Then one finds that Algorithm
3
with general
\rho
(resp.
\rho=0
) is degenerated into that in Remark

4.2
(resp. Algorithm
1
) directly.

Finally, we note that a fast solver for (
1.4a
) with natural boundary conditions can be deduced analogously.
However, due to space limitations, we omit the details.

\bm{u}=\big(\sin^{2}\pi x\sin\pi y\cos\pi y,-\sin^{2}\pi y\sin\pi x\cos\pi x\big)^{\mathsf{T}},

while
\beta
and
\alpha
are functions with

\displaystyle\beta=3\pi\cos(\pi x)\cos(\pi y)+10,\quad\alpha=3\pi\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y).

Since the divergence-free condition is guaranteed by the specifically chosen
\bm{f}
when
\alpha>0
in
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
, the Lagrange multiplier is not necessary, resulting in a linear algebraic system that is symmetric and positive definite.
We will initially employ CG method to solve the linear equations and then examine the behaviors of PCG method. The discrete matrix calculated when
\beta=1,\alpha=1
is chosen as the pre-conditioner, enabling the use of FSTs/FCTs in matrix-vector multiplications. The
LU solver
is also produced for comparison with PCG.

Denote the number of iterations by
n_{\rm iter}
.
The exit criterion for the iteration relies on reducing the 2-norm of the relative residual below the prescribed tolerance
\varepsilon=10^{-14}
.
In Table
7.5
, it is demonstrated that errors in the
\bm{L}^{2}
-norm obtained by the three methods (CG, PCG, and
LU solver
) converge at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(h)
, consistent with the theoretical results.
As
n
increases, the number of iterations for the CG method grows faster than the rate of
\mathcal{O}(n)

while that for the PCG method is independent of mesh sizes.
This is explicitly illustrated in Figure
7.6
,
confirming the effectiveness of the preconditioner.
Additionally, the total time of the three methods is recorded in Table
7.5
.
For PCG and the
LU solver
, we also record factorization phase time and the preconditioned phase time, respectively.
In contrast, the computational time of PCG shows orders of magnitude improvement.
Moreover, regarding computational time, we find from Figure
7.6

that for the PCG method, it grows asymptotically as
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log n).
Thus, besides the reduction in the number of iterations, the usage of FSTs/FCTs can also account for the saved computational time.
Memory usage for PCG and the
LU solver
is also recorded in Table
7.6
. When
n=2^{13},
the
LU solver
requires 57.4% of total memory, while the fast solver only needs 9.2%.

The PCG method performs matrix iterations, since the coefficient matrix needs to be stored, which requires additional memory. In contrast, our fast solver is matrix-free, so PCG uses more memory than the fast solver used in previous examples.

Conclusion

In this paper, fast solvers for the Maxwell’s equations are proposed by using the divergence-free constraint / Gauss’s law guaranteed discrete eigen-decompositon and the FSTs/FCTs in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log n)
operations when adopting
the lowest-order Nédélec finite element. Notably, these algorithms strictly fulfill the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition at the discrete level, thus instabilities and spurious solutions can be eliminated.

Moreover, based on ideas from Isogeometric Analysis, we can extend our
fast solver to more general computational domains
\Omega
, which can be easily parameterized by a sufficiently smooth function

G:(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow\Omega
. In this case, the variational problem on
\Omega
could be transferred to
(0,1)^{2}

using substitution and chain rules for integration and differentiation.

Additionally, as part of an ongoing series of works, we are considering the design of fast solvers for Maxwell’s equations in three-dimensions, which offer substantial advantages over existing methods.
