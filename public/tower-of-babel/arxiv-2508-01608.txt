From Pixels to Places: A Systematic Benchmark for Evaluating Image Geolocalization Ability in Large Language Models Conference: ; ;
Lingyao Li, Runlong Yu, Qikai Hu, Bowei Li, Min Deng, Yang Zhou, Xiaowei Jia

Abstract
. Image geolocalization, the task of identifying the geographic location depicted in an image, is important for applications in crisis response, digital forensics, and location-based intelligence. While recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities for visual reasoning, their ability to perform image geolocalization remains underexplored. In this study, we introduce a benchmark called IMAGEO-Bench that systematically evaluates accuracy, distance error, geospatial bias, and reasoning process. Our benchmark includes three diverse datasets covering global street scenes, points of interest (POIs) in the United States, and a private collection of unseen images. Through experiments on 10 state-of-the-art LLMs, including both open- and closed-source models, we reveal clear performance disparities, with closed-source models generally showing stronger reasoning. Importantly, we uncover geospatial biases as LLMs tend to perform better in high-resource regions (e.g., North America, Western Europe, and California) while exhibiting degraded performance in underrepresented areas. Regression diagnostics demonstrate that successful geolocalization is primarily dependent on recognizing urban settings, outdoor environments, street-level imagery, and identifiable landmarks. Overall, IMAGEO-Bench provides a rigorous lens into the spatial reasoning capabilities of LLMs and offers implications for building geolocation-aware AI systems. 1.
Introduction

Image geolocalization, the process of inferring geographic coordinates or addresses from visuals, presents a complex challenge at the intersection of computer vision and geographic information systems (GIS)
(
Lin et al. 2013
;
Workman et al. 2015
;
Jin Kim et al. 2017
;
Zamir and Shah 2014
)
. Image geolocalization requires holistic scene interpretation and reasoning, encompassing both explicit indicators (e.g., landmarks, signage) and contextual cues (e.g., architectural styles, vegetation, spatial layout) to infer a precise location
(
Wu and Huang 2022
;
Lin et al. 2013
)
. The accurate identification of location from images has important implications for real-world scenarios, including digital forensics
(
Kadha et al. 2025
)
, urban analytics
(
Lin et al. 2013
)
and crisis management
(
Li et al. 2025a
;
Yin et al. 2025
)
.

Conventionally, this task has been investigated with computer vision models, using either supervised classification
(
Pramanick et al. 2022
;
Seo et al. 2018
)
or retrieval-based methods
(
Vivanco Cepeda et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Vo and Jacobs 2017
;
Shatwell et al. 2025
)
. These models often leverage metadata embedded in image files
(
Samano et al. 2020
)
or rely on extensive GIS databases
(
Hays and Efros 2014
;
Brejcha and Čadík 2017
)
to recognize landmark or visual cues. However, early methods often fail to generalize to previously unseen environments and perform inadequately in the absence of distinctive landmarks. Their effectiveness is further constrained by data coverage and resolution, especially when the metadata is missing.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs), such as GPT and Gemini, have transformed the landscape of image understanding and spatial reasoning
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025b
;
Tian et al. 2025
)
. These models can describe intricate details, understand spatial relationships between objects, and infer context from subtle visual cues within an image
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
. By leveraging extensive world knowledge and visual-textual alignment, LLMs offer a promising avenue for overcoming the limits of conventional geolocalization, even when confronted with out-of-distribution data
(
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025c
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
. Recent research further demonstrates that targeted strategies, such as supervised fine-tuning
(
Yi and Shan 2025
)
and retrieval-augmented inference
(
Li et al. 2025c
)
, can substantially enhance LLMs’ geolocalization capabilities.

In parallel with LLM advances, there has been rapid progress in developing benchmarks to evaluate their visual and linguistic capabilities
(
Lacoste et al. 2023
;
Hua et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2024
)
. A representative example is GEO-Bench, which evaluates LLMs on classification and segmentation tasks in geoscience
(
Lacoste et al. 2023
)
. For image geolocalization, benchmarks like Img2Loc
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
and LLMGeo
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
have assessed LLMs’ ability to localize images. However, these efforts often rely on established datasets and lag in incorporating the recent model advances. Moreover, key dimensions like LLMs’ spatial reasoning abilities and potential geospatial biases remain underexplored. To address these gaps, we introduce
IMAGEO-Bench
, a benchmark that systematically evaluates LLMs’ image geolocalization abilities. Our key contributions are listed as follows.

•

Comprehensive benchmark datasets
. We compile three datasets with diverse geographic regions, urban/rural scenes, and location indicators, constructed via stratified sampling and augmented with privately collected images to ensure broad coverage.

•

Multi-dimensional evaluation framework
. We propose an evaluation framework that looks beyond standard accuracy to assess spatial precision and computational cost, providing a nuanced understanding of model performance across various dimensions.

•

Geographic bias quantification
. We quantify geographic biases in LLMs by revealing performance disparities across different regions at both global and local scales.

•

Reasoning process analysis
. We employ statistical regression to analyze the reasoning processes behind LLM geolocalization and to identify the most influential visual features.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Image Geolocalization

Image geolocalization in computer vision has focused on two main approaches: classification-based and retrieval-based models
(
Weyand et al. 2016
;
Seo et al. 2018
;
Muller-Budack et al. 2018
;
Pramanick et al. 2022
;
Clark et al. 2023
)
. Classification methods (e.g., PlaNet
(
Weyand et al. 2016
)
) treat geolocation as a discrete prediction task by assigning an image to a predefined spatial grid. These methods can benefit from global coverage and efficient inference but rely on rigid spatial discretization, which can constrain fine-grained accuracy and interpretability
(
Seo et al. 2018
)
. Retrieval-based methods (e.g., IM2GPS
(
Hays and Efros 2008
)
) localize an image by matching it to a geotagged reference set based on visual or semantic similarity. Although enhancements like deep feature learning and probabilistic fusion
(
Vo and Jacobs 2017
)
have improved accuracy, these methods remain reliant on dense reference imagery and struggle in sparsely covered areas. Metric learning models like NetVLAD
(
Arandjelović et al. 2016
)
, SAFA
(
Shi et al. 2020
)
, and VIGOR
(
Zhong et al. 2021
)
further boost retrieval performance using cross-view embeddings and attention mechanisms, particularly effective in visually rich urban settings.

However, these methods often face several limitations: (i) they rely on large labeled datasets and generalize inadequately to unseen or underrepresented regions; (ii) their predictions are often opaque, lacking interpretability or insight into the reasoning process; and (iii) they struggle to incorporate multimodal semantic cues (e.g., textual signage, architectural styles, or cultural markers) that humans intuitively use for geolocation. These limitations call for an approach that can leverages world knowledge and multimodal cues to generalize more effectively. In this regard, LLMs offer a promising solution due to their reasoning abilities, cross-modal integration, and greater interpretability.

2.2.
LLMs for Spatial Reasoning

Recent progress in vision-language pre-training has given rise to multimodal LLMs with strong visual reasoning capabilities. Foundation models (e.g., CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
and ALIGN
(
Jia et al. 2021
)
) have established powerful image-text alignment at web scale, enabling zero-shot classification and retrieval. BLIP-2
(
Li et al. 2023
)
further advances this by connecting frozen language models with visual encoders, supporting open-ended visual description and optical character recognition (OCR). Modern multimodal LLMs (e.g., GPT, Gemini, and Qwen) have been demonstrated effective on tasks such as visual question answering
(
Guo et al. 2023
;
Kuang et al. 2025
)
, document analysis
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, and diagram understanding
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
. However, their spatial reasoning abilities, particularly for fine-grained geolocation, remain underexplored.

A few recent studies have begun to close this gap by evaluating modern foundation models’ abilities to localize images
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. For example, LLMGeo
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
benchmarks GPT-4V and LLaVA on challenging in-the-wild images. It reveals limited accuracy of investigated models and highlights room for improvement. Img2Loc
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
introduces a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline that combines vision-language models with large-scale image search to refine coordinate estimates. A more recent study presents a benchmark built on Google Street View images
(
Jay et al. 2025
)
, showing that VLMs can achieve sub-300km median errors but suffer from notable failure modes and reasoning transparency.

While these studies represent important progress, they are constrained by task-specific designs, limited geographic scope, and the absence of standardized evaluation metrics. These underscore the need for a systematic framework to assess LLMs’ geolocation capabilities across diverse regions and real-world conditions. To address these gaps, we introduce
IMAGEO-Bench
, a benchmark for systematically evaluating LLMs on location accuracy, computational cost, geographic fairness, and reasoning process based on three newly compiled datasets. The framework of this benchmark is illustrated in Figure
1
.

3.
Benchmark Dataset Preparation

We construct three benchmark datasets from distinct sources to enable comprehensive evaluation, with their geographic distribution illustrated in Figure
1
(a). We opt to build new benchmark datasets for two main reasons. First, this helps mitigate data contamination, as widely used datasets like IM2GPS3k and YFCC4k
(
Hays and Efros 2008
)
may have been incorporated in LLM training corpora. Second, our datasets are designed to span diverse visual contexts (e.g., indoor vs. outdoor) and geospatial scales (e.g., global vs. U.S. specific), allowing for a more rigorous assessment of LLM capabilities. Sample images of these benchmark datasets are presented in
Appendix
A
. Please note that the distribution of some visual features, namely environment, scene type, and setting, are extracted from the outputs of gemini-2.5-pro, as our objective is not to evaluate the impact of specific image attributes on LLM geolocalization performance, but rather to summarize the model-identified features.

3.1.
Dataset-GSS: Global Streetscape Set

Dataset-GSS is derived from the NUS Global Streetscapes dataset
(
Hou et al. 2024
)
, a collection of approximately 10 million streetscape images collected from Mapillary and KartaView across 688 cities worldwide. Each image is geo-tagged with precise latitude and longitude coordinates and has city-level labels. Then, the authors of the NUS dataset manually annotate a subset of over 10,000 images with eight contextual attributes: panoramic status, lighting condition, glare, view direction, image quality, reflection, weather, and platform
(
Hou et al. 2024
)
.

We build Dataset-GSS by refining this manually labeled subset through a multi-stage filtering process. First, we filter the images with all eight contextual labels, which results in 8,568 images. Second, we only retain the images with the annotations—
quality: good
,
reflection: no
, and
glare: no
—to ensure image quality, narrowing the dataset to 6,178 images. Last, we manually filter to exclude images with insufficient geographic information (e.g., no signs or clear visual cues). After filtering, we obtain Dataset-GSS, which comprises 6,152 high-quality street-level images from 396 cities across 123 countries. This carefully sampled dataset provides a rich variety of scenes captured under diverse geographies, environmental conditions, architectural styles, and cultural contexts. The data distribution of Dataset-GSS is presented in
Appendix
B
.

3.2.
Dataset-UPC: U.S. POIs Crowdsourced Set

Dataset-UPC is constructed from crowdsourced images from Google Maps Points of Interest (POIs) to capture a diverse range of regions and location types. We first sample POIs from the Google Maps dataset released by UC San Diego
(
Yan et al. 2023
)
, which contains 4,963,111 unique POIs across the United States collected up to September 2021. This dataset includes extensive attributes for each POI, including addresses (street, city, state, ZIP code), geolocation coordinates (latitude and longitude), business descriptions, and categorical labels, which makes it ideal for geolocalization evaluation.

To ensure geographic coverage in Dataset-UPC, we employ a multi-stage stratified sampling strategy. We begin with compiling a list of 17 distinct POI categories, including “restaurant,” “hotel,” “park,” and “scenic spot,” to guide the sampling. For each state, we randomly select three POIs from each category, resulting in a pool of 4,325 POIs. We then refine this pool through a two-step filtering process to ensure data usability and integrity. First, we discard entries with non-functional image URLs. Second, we manually remove advertisement images and those containing identifiable human faces. After this curation, Dataset-UPC comprises 2,929 images spanning all 50 states and the District of Columbia.

3.3.
Dataset-PCW: Privately Collected Wild Set

Dataset-PCW is constructed as a held-out validation set to prevent potential data leakage. Unlike Datasets-GSS or -UPC, it does not draw from publicly available sources. Instead, it consists of 220 original photo–address pairs collected by the authors. The images span a wide variety of scene types (e.g., natural, rural, urban), weather conditions (e.g., sunny, cloudy, snowy), and temporal contexts (e.g., daytime, nighttime). Since none of the images overlap with existing datasets or known LLM training sources, Dataset-PCW is well suited to evaluate LLMs’ geolocation performance on unseen data.

4.
Benchmark Development

IMAGEO-Bench
defines a unified end-to-end task for image geolocation. It includes a structured output format, reasoning template, evaluation metrics, and post-hoc feature-importance analysis tools, allowing for clear and consistent comparisons across LLMs.

4.1.
Task Formalization

Given an input image
\mathbf{x}
, the task requires models to predict a structured geographic output tuple:
G=(C,\;A,\;(\phi,\lambda))
, where
(\phi,\lambda)
are the predicted latitude and longitude in decimal degrees,
A
is a structured textual address, and
C\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
denotes the model’s self-reported confidence score. The objective is to minimize the great-circle distance error
d((\phi,\lambda),(\phi^{\star},\lambda^{\star}))
between the predicted and ground-truth coordinates. Predictions are provided in a structured JSON format for standardized parsing and evaluation. During inference, models are strictly restricted from accessing external APIs or GPS metadata.

4.2.
Output Schema and Reasoning Template

To evaluate LLMs’ geolocalization capabilities, we require models to explicitly reason about the given image by extracting and interpreting salient visual features, as illustrated in Figure
1
(c). These features are collected based on prior research identifying layout, signage, text, architecture, road, infrastructure, and buildings as critical cues for image geolocation
(
Li et al. 2025c
;
Jay et al. 2025
;
Wu and Huang 2022
;
Tian et al. 2017
)
. Such structured output allows us to systematically analyze models’ reasoning process. Since image geolocalization often depends on recognizing features tied to these specific places, prompting models to surface these elements can facilitate a step-by-step reasoning process similar to that of human geographers. Specifically, the structured model output comprises three key components:

•

Image information:
High-level descriptors characterizing the environment (indoor, outdoor), scene type (building, scenery, street, mixed), and setting (urban, suburban, rural, natural).

•

Reasoning:
A detailed justification for location inference, organized into four distinct categories of visual evidence: (i) Landmark recognition: Unique natural or human-made structures serving as distinctive geographic markers. (ii) Text and signage: Textual clues from visible signs, license plates, storefronts, or other textual indicators. (iii) Cultural indicators: Region-specific architectural styles, cultural symbols, or prevalent societal patterns. (iv) Spatial context: Environmental layout, vegetation patterns, road networks, infrastructure types, or building density.

•

Geolocalization
: The final structured geographic prediction tuple
(C,A,(\phi,\lambda))
, as formally defined before.

4.3.
Evaluation Metrics

We establish a set of metrics to measure LLMs’ performance:

•

Identification rate:
It measures the model’s ability to produce a valid output, defined as the percentage of input images for which a complete and correctly formatted JSON is returned. Instances where the model fails to produce a usable output are treated as identification failures.

•

Geographic accuracy:
It evaluates the semantic correctness of the predicted address
A_{pred}
against the ground truth
A_{true}
at three administrative levels, including (i) Country accuracy (
Acc_{\text{country}}
), (ii) State accuracy (
Acc_{\text{state}}
), and (iii) City accuracy (
Acc_{\text{city}}
). This metric quantifies the proportion of predictions that align with the ground-truth labels.

•

Distance error:
It calculates the great-circle distance
d
(kilometers) between the predicted coordinates
(\phi_{\text{p}},\lambda_{\text{p}})
and the true coordinates
(\phi_{\text{t}},\lambda_{\text{t}})
using the Haversine formula:

(1)

d=2r\arcsin(\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\Delta\phi/2)+\cos(\phi_{\text{p}})\cos(\phi_{\text{t}})\sin^{2}(\Delta\lambda/2)})

where
\Delta\phi=\phi_{\text{t}}-\phi_{\text{p}}
,
\Delta\lambda=\lambda_{\text{t}}-\lambda_{\text{p}}
, and
r
represents the Earth’s mean radius (6,371 km).

•

Computational cost:
It assesses the model’s efficiency by tracking the computational costs, including the averaged cost
Avg_{\text{Cost}}
and the tokens per inference
Avg_{\text{Tokens}}
(i.e., the average number of input and output tokens consumed for a single image query).

4.4.
Post-hoc Feature-Importance Diagnostics

IMAGEO-Bench
includes an interpretability module to assess how specific structured output fields correlate with geolocalization performance. This diagnostic suite supports model-agnostic evaluation, identifying which visual attributes are most predictive of successful localization. Specifically, we construct a diagnostic set from each model’s structured JSON outputs. Key categorical predictors—such as environment type, scene category, setting, and landmark presence—are extracted from the predefined schema and one-hot encoded relative to baseline categories. We further define two metrics to represent the geolocation outcome, including (i) a binary indicator representing city-level prediction correctness, and (ii) a continuous log-transformed distance error. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote this encoded feature vector, and let
Y
represent the geolocation outcome (e.g., city-level correctness or log-distance error). To quantify feature importance, we fit the following two regression models.

•

Logistic regression.

For the city-level correctness indicator
Y\in\{0,1\}
, we estimate

(2)

\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b),

where
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
is the sigmoid function.
We report
\exp(w_{j})
as the odds ratio of feature
j
relative to its reference category.

•

Ridge regression.

For the log-distance error, we fit:

(3)

\log(d)=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b+\varepsilon,

where
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
by minimizing the penalized squared error
\sum_{i}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_{2}^{2}
with
\lambda=1.0
.

All predictors are one-hot encoded binary variables, so additional normalization is unnecessary.
Because all features share the same scale, the ridge coefficients
\mathbf{w}
are directly comparable. We aggregate
|w_{j}|
within each semantic field to obtain field-level importance.

The importance of the field level is calculated by aggregating the absolute regression coefficients associated with each predictor group. Coefficients are interpreted relative to their baseline categories, with careful consideration given to potential collinearity effects. These diagnostic results provide valuable information on the semantic and visual cues that most strongly influence model geolocalization
performance.

5.
Experimental Setup

5.1.
Prompt Design

We employ a structured prompting methodology to ensure consistent and parsable outputs. Our approach combines a system prompt, which defines the model’s role as a “geocoding analysis” expert, with a main prompt that directs the model to use a step-by-step, chain-of-thought reasoning process. To further guide the model’s inference, we include a one-shot example within the prompt. We mandate that the response be a valid JSON object partitioned into three sections: (i) Image information for high-level scene classification, (ii) reasoning for a detailed analysis of visual evidence, and (iii) image geolocalization for the final coordinate estimation. This standardized design facilitates a direct and objective comparison of the feature extraction and inference capabilities across all models. Specific prompt design is presented in

5.2.
Model Selection and Experiment Settings

For our benchmark analysis, we select a diverse set of candidate LLMs with vision capabilities from several prominent providers. The models under evaluation include OpenAI’s o3, gpt-4.1, and gpt-4.1-mini; Anthropic’s claude-3.5-haiku; Google’s gemini-1.5-pro, gemini-2.5-flash, and gemini-2.5-pro; and various Llama models, specifically llama-3.2-11b, llama-3.2-90b, and llama-4-maverick-17b. We interface with each model through its designated API.

In our experimental setup, we query each model with a temperature setting of 0.7, which encourages a balance between factual recall and inferential creativity. We log all outputs for each image, including the model’s raw JSON response and error states, to a .jsonl file for subsequent analysis. At the conclusion of each experimental run, we also compute and record a summary of the total operational cost and token consumption. Representative model outputs of image geolocalization are provided in

.

6.
Results and Analysis

6.1.
Benchmark Performance

To benchmark LLMs’ performance, we generate two sets of scatter plots using the introduced metrics. Table
1
presents a quantitative summary, including accuracy at various geospatial scales, median distance error, and computational costs. For visual analysis, Figure
2
displays scatter plots for latitude prediction on (a) Dataset-GSS and (b) Dataset-UPC, while corresponding longitude results are provided in
Appendix
D
. The benchmark performance for Dataset-PCW is included in
Appendix
E
.

Performance across Dataset-GSS and Dataset-UPC shows clear differences, with most LLMs exhibiting higher prediction accuracy on Dataset-GSS. This discrepancy is likely due to the inherent characteristics of the compiled datasets. Dataset-GSS primarily consists of publicly available global street-level images captured in outdoor environments, which provide clear geographic indicators. In contrast, Dataset-UPC comprises crowdsourced images contributed by Google Maps users, many of which are taken indoors, lack explicit geographic cues or distinctive architectural details. These characteristics significantly complicate accurate identification of geolocation. As a result, most LLMs achieve higher accuracy with Dataset-GSS across all evaluated levels, including city, country, and state, and report lower median distance errors.

Closed-source models consistently outperform their open-source counterparts across both datasets. Specifically, gemini-2.5-pro achieves remarkable results in Dataset-GSS, with a median error of 4.2 km and country-level accuracy of 92.4%. Other top performers like gpt-4.1 and o3 maintain high performance with median errors of 10.5 km and 8.2 km, respectively, in Dataset-GSS. Conversely, open-source models such as llama-4-17b and llama-3.2-11b exhibit substantially higher median errors (e.g., 127.0 km for llama-4-17b in Dataset-GSS and 534.3 km in Dataset-UPC). This performance gap likely originates from proprietary advantages of closed-source providers, including larger training datasets, richer geospatial corpora, and more refined model architectures.

We also observe a clear scaling trend, where larger models generally exhibit stronger capabilities. For example, gpt-4.1 surpasses its smaller counterpart, gpt-4.1-mini, with a city-level accuracy of 39.6% versus 34.7% and a much lower median distance error (10.5 km versus 34.8 km). Similarly, llama-3.2-90b improves upon llama-3.2-11b with a city-level accuracy of 26.3% versus 24.0%, and a median distance error of 15.7 km versus 66.6 km. However, performance differences are not solely size-dependent. Smaller models like gemini-2.5-flash (city accuracy 41.7%, median error 6.0 km) outperform many larger models, suggesting that other factors (e.g., model architecture, multimodal fusion techniques, and training quality) can contribute to improved performance. This reinforces that scaling is helpful but not sufficient on its own.

Confidence scores vary significantly across the models, among which gemini-2.5-pro exhibits the highest confidence, while o3 reports the lowest. Even within a single provider, confidence levels differ: for instance, gpt-4.1 is substantially more confident than gpt-4.1-mini, and gemini-2.5-pro scores higher than gemini-1.5-pro. For models like gpt-4.1, o3, and claude-3.5-haiku, higher confidence scores generally align with better prediction accuracy within the same model. However, higher confidence does not always imply greater performance across models. For instance, gemini-2.5-pro shows a higher confidence distribution than o3, yet their performance in Dataset-GSS is comparable (median errors of 4.2 km versus 8.2 km). These findings indicate that confidence alone is not a reliable proxy for model accuracy and should be interpreted in conjunction with performance metrics.

In addition, the computational cost reveal significant variations across models. llama-3.2-11b emerges as the most efficient option, using only
\sim
823 tokens at $0.00015 per request, while o3 is the most expensive model at $0.01460 per request in Dataset-GSS. However, this cost savings of open-sourced models comes with substantially lower performance. Among closed-source models, gemini-2.5-flash offers an optimal balance, achieving 41.7% city accuracy at just $0.00141 per request. Interestingly, model size doesn’t always correlate to resource consumption. For example, gpt-4.1-mini consumes 3,245 tokens despite its “mini” label, nearly 3

6.2.
Geospatial Analysis

We conduct geospatial analyses on Dataset-GSS and Dataset-UPC to evaluate model performance across geographic regions and scales. In Dataset-GSS (Figure
3
), we calculate country-level accuracy for nations with over 20 samples to ensure statistical reliability. Results reveal pronounced geographic bias and model variability. Models such as o3 and gemini-2.5-pro achieve near-perfect accuracy (approaching 1.0) in regions like North America, Russia, and Australia. In contrast, llama-3.2-11b consistently underperforms, with accuracy often below 0.6 and approaching zero in several Latin European countries. Models like llama-3.2-90b and claude-3.5-haiku show intermediate accuracy (0.6–0.9). It is noting that the United States, Australia, and West European Countries maintain high accuracy across all models, suggesting strong model generalization in these well-represented regions.

In Dataset-UPC (Figure
4
), we again observe distinct geographic disparities in model performance across U.S. states. Specifically, gemini-2.5-flash, gemini-2.5-pro, and o3 consistently perform well, particularly in California, Hawaii, and Florida. In contrast, llama-3.2-11b falls below 0.5 accuracy in nearly all states. gpt-4.1 shows moderate performance, with accuracy typically between 0.6 and 0.8 in coastal regions. California and Florida emerge as consistent high-performing states across models, likely due to their dense urban environments and distinctive landmarks that can facilitate image geolocalization.

Together, the results from both datasets illustrate potential geospatial biases in model behavior. High performance in regions like the United States, Australia, and parts of Western Europe points to the models’ strong alignment with densely sampled, visually distinctive, and data-rich environments. In contrast, accuracy declines sharply in countries and states with fewer training representations or more ambiguous visual features. These patterns reflect a potential limitation that LLMs are not yet equipped to generalize robustly across diverse global geographies.

6.3.1.
Feature attribution via reasoning

We apply logistic regression diagnostics to examine the features for the LLM reasoning process. The summarized result for Dataset-GSS is illustrated in Figure
5
. Image elements are categorized into four semantic groups: environment, scene type, setting, and landmark. Across the evaluated LLMs, we observe consistent trends as well as model-specific variations. The main findings are as follows.

First, nearly all models strongly favor urban and outdoor environments while consistently penalizing rural, natural, and indoor scenes. One possible explanation is that dense urban contexts, featuring detailed façades, prominent signage, and infrastructure elements (e.g., roads and street furniture), provide reliable visual cues for accurate geolocalization. Conversely, natural scenes, which often lack distinctive visual anchors, pose significant localization challenges, as reflected by consistently negative model associations.

Second, regarding scene type, street-level imagery tends to provide stronger localization signals than building façades or general scenery. This is likely because street scenes often contain rich contextual cues (e.g., textual signage, road markings, and distinctive urban infrastructure) that are both visually salient and semantically interpretable by LLMs. In contrast, scenic images without clear architectural landmarks can negatively impact prediction accuracy.

Third, the presence of distinctive landmarks consistently improves the geolocation performance across nearly all models. Landmark cues are typically assigned strong positive weights, indicating their critical role in guiding correct predictions. However, the claude-3.5-haiku model is a notable outlier: it assigns marginal or even negative weights to the landmark cue. A post-hoc audit reveals that it labels “landmark = yes” on 99.67% of images, compared to 41.77% for all other models. This over-identification of landmarks suggests that the model frequently misclassifies ordinary structures as distinctive, thereby diluting the predictive value of this feature.

Overall, current LLMs perform best when presented with clear, distinct visual elements, including textual signage, iconic architecture, and densely structured urban layouts. They struggle significantly, however, in contexts that lack easily verbalizable landmarks or signage. 7.
Discussion and Conclusions

Our study introduces
IMAGEO-Bench
, a comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate the image geolocalization capabilities of LLMs across accuracy, computational cost, geographic fairness, and reasoning process. Our key findings are summarized below.

Closed-source models lead in performance but at a higher cost
. Models like gemini-2.5-pro and o3 consistently outperform open-source alternatives in accuracy and distance error. However, this comes with significantly higher computational costs. gemini-2.5-flash offers a compelling balance between cost-efficiency and accuracy. This suggest that model architecture, training data quality, and modality alignment can outweigh parameter count in determining performance.

Geographic performance disparities reflect potential training data and reasoning biases
. Models consistently perform better in high-resource, visually distinctive regions like North America, Western Europe, and Australia, while struggling in underrepresented or ambiguous areas. States like California and Florida see higher accuracy, likely due to richer training data and clearer visual cues. These disparities highlight limitations in global generalization and raise concerns about geographic fairness.

Models rely on visual anchors, not holistic understanding
. LLMs tend to perform better in outdoor urban scenes with clear cues like signage and landmarks, while rural, natural, and indoor images lead to higher errors due to limited identifiable features. Models can infer broad regional context (e.g., country-level), but struggle with fine-grained localization in the absence of strong visual anchors. Moreover, spatial layout or image composition appears to have little impact on prediction performance.

This study opens several avenues for future research. One direction could conduct a more comprehensive study covering diverse geographies and scene types to assess model generalization under real-world variability. Another future work could explore how language-based cues contribute to geolocation predictions, especially in scenes lacking visual anchors. Lastly, future work could explore strategies such as data augmentation or region-adaptive prompting to reduce geographic bias—an essential step toward building more equitable and globally robust geolocation systems.
