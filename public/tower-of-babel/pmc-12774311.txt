Psychotic symptoms of sexual nature as core psychopathology in schizophrenia: Case series
Authors: Anjali Sharma, Manish R. Thakur, Bhavuk Garg, Shiv Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Psychotic symptoms involving sexual content remain underreported in schizophrenia, particularly in Indian settings.Cases:Six patients with schizophrenia presenting with sexually themed delusions or hallucinations as core psychopathology were reviewed. Data were extracted through chart review with informed consent and de-identification. Diagnostic formulation was based on ICD-11 criteria, and insight was assessed on a 5-point clinical scale. The mean age was 32 years; five patients were female. Presentations included somatic passivity, delusional perception, and delusion of control. Three patients met criteria for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and responded to clozapine.Conclusion:Sexually themed psychotic experiences may occur as prominent features of schizophrenia, particularly in chronic or treatment-resistant cases. Awareness of such presentations can facilitate culturally sensitive diagnosis and management.

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a multifaceted, complex, and severe mental disorder characterized by a wide array of symptom domains.[1] Reported estimates have suggested a lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia varying between 0.28% and 0.7%, notwithstanding a vast dissimilitude between countries and ethnicities.[2] The key symptom domains are positive symptoms, negative symptoms, depressive and manic mood symptoms, psychomotor symptoms, and cognitive symptoms. The positive symptom domain includes psychotic symptoms, which include delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking, disorganized behavior, and experiences of passivity and control.[3]

While researchers have made progress in comprehending delusions and hallucinations in schizophrenia-spectrum psychotic disorders using both phenomenological and analytical approaches, psychotic symptoms involving sexual content remain underreported, especially within Indian psychiatric literature.[456] Although there have been publications documenting the occurrence of psychotic symptoms involving sexual content, including among individuals with schizophrenia,[789101112131415] only fewer studies have explored their possible associations with psychopathological factors.[1617] However, among all modalities of hallucinations (i.e., auditory, visual, olfactory, gustatory, and tactile), sexual types have been the least analyzed so far.[17]

In Indian literature, there is a dearth of literature describing sexual symptoms as core psychopathology in schizophrenia and related psychotic spectrum disorders.

CASES DESCRIPTION

This case series was conducted in the in-patient department of a tertiary care psychiatry hospital in Northern India. Six patients fulfilling the ICD-11 criteria for schizophrenia were included. Cases were identified through retrospective chart review, and only those exhibiting psychotic symptoms involving sexual content as the core psychopathology were selected. Clinical information, including socio-demographic details, psychopathology, and treatment response, was obtained from hospital records and verified through patient and caregiver interviews.

Insight was assessed using a five-point clinical scale (1 = no insight, 5 = full insight). Symptom severity was measured using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). All patients and caregivers provided written informed consent for publication. Identifiable details were omitted to ensure confidentiality.

Case 1

A 32-year-old married woman presented with delusions that numerous foreigners were entering her body through her genitalia, engaging in sexual acts, and demanding citizenship. She described sensations of vaginal penetration and pain perceived as externally controlled, leading to distress and social withdrawal. These experiences were accompanied by auditory hallucinations of male voices threatening her. Diagnosed with schizophrenia, continuous course, she responded to oral olanzapine and later maintained stability on long-acting injectable olanzapine.

Case 2

A 29-year-old unmarried woman reported intrusive sensations as if her former male friends were having sexual intercourse with her despite their physical absence. She experienced these as forced and distressing, often crying or isolating herself during such episodes. These phenomena were accompanied by delusions of control and thought broadcasting. Diagnosed with treatment-resistant schizophrenia, she improved on clozapine after inadequate response to risperidone and aripiprazole.

Case 3

A 30-year-old unmarried woman developed fear and avoidance of all men, including family members, due to sensations of unwanted genital touching and the belief that men harbored sexual intentions toward her. These sexually themed passivity experiences were central to her psychosis and severely impaired family relationships. She exhibited persecutory and referential delusions alongside auditory hallucinations. Diagnosed with treatment-resistant schizophrenia, she showed significant improvement on clozapine.

Case 4

A 32-year-old unmarried woman misinterpreted normal vaginal secretions as evidence of sexual intercourse initiated by nearby men. She believed neighbors or strangers engaged in sexual acts with her without consent, leading to family embarrassment and repeated confrontations. These delusional perceptions of sexual experiences occurred without physical contact but caused significant distress. Diagnosed with treatment-resistant schizophrenia, she improved markedly with clozapine.

Case 5

A 35-year-old married woman was convinced that impostors resembling her deceased husband were having sexual intercourse with her. She reported warmth and physical sensations during these episodes and avoided her real husband and children due to fear. The sexual delusions and somatic passivity experiences dominated her presentation and improved substantially with olanzapine long-acting injection.

Case 6

A 34-year-old married man developed a fixed belief that a microchip had been implanted in his brain, allowing external agents to monitor and control him through satellites and television. He also believed this microchip interfered with his sexual functioning, controlling penile erection and preventing intercourse with his wife. These delusions caused marked distress and marital disruption, eventually leading to his wife leaving him. Medical evaluation ruled out organic causes of erectile dysfunction. Diagnosed with schizophrenia, continuous course, he was treated with olanzapine (20 mg), with gradual improvement in his psychotic and sexual control delusions.

DISCUSSION

This case series [Table 1] highlights the presence of psychotic symptoms involving sexual content as central features of schizophrenia. The mean age was 32 years, and five of six patients were female. Three cases demonstrated treatment resistance, requiring clozapine. Physical and laboratory evaluations excluded medical or substance-related causes.

Most presentations reflected Schneiderian first-rank symptoms of passivity (four cases), with others involving delusional perception or control.[18] These findings align with Victor, et al. (2022)[7] and Gerges et al. (2022),[19] who linked sexual psychotic content with greater illness severity. Unlike some previous studies, none of our patients reported sexual trauma histories.

Neurobiologically, such symptoms may reflect aberrant salience in limbic circuits leading to misattributed bodily sensations. This dysfunction is not only associated with the illness itself and the adverse effects of medication, but also with the overall quality of their marital relationships.[20] Sociocultural taboos around sexuality in India may delay disclosure and contribute to distress, particularly among women. Gender differences observed (five females, one male) may reflect both sample bias and gendered vulnerability to misinterpretations of bodily experiences.

Ethical safeguards were ensured through de-identification and informed consent. Medication-induced sexual side effects were considered and ruled out based on chronology and symptom profile.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Sexually themed psychotic symptoms are common among individuals with chronic schizophrenia, indicating a strong correlation with the severity and duration of the illness. They support the importance of early treatment and intervention and the necessity of compliance to prevent the recurrence of symptoms, to avoid such adverse outcomes. This series contributes to the limited Indian literature on the subject. Due to India’s cultural diversity, this study enables clinicians and researchers to make detailed comparisons across various cultural contexts. Additional research at more advanced levels of analysis is necessary to gain a deeper understanding of the biopsychosocial mechanisms that govern these connections.
