Dynamic successive assessment of water environment carrying capacity based on a system dynamics model and an improved variable fuzzy pattern recognition method
Authors: Xinguo Sun, Anbang Peng, Wenxin Xu, Yi Shi, Tianxiang Wang, Wenjia Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water environment carrying capacity (WECC) is both a natural attribute of the water environment and a social attribute that governs regional sustainable development. Jiangsu Province, characterized by a large population and rapid economic growth, faces increasing challenges such as imbalance between water supply and demand and deteriorating water quality. Therefore, assessing WECC in this region is of great importance. Based on an analysis of water resource utilization and water quality conditions, a set of indicators for evaluating WECC was established using the Driving-Pressure-State-Impact-Response (DPSIR) framework. Grey correlation theory was applied to screen these indicators. To account for the different interpretations of index weights with respect to evaluation targets and objects, the variable fuzzy recognition method (VFPR) for water quality evaluation was enhanced by integrating the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and entropy weight method (EWM). This integration ensures that index weights reflect both objective data and expert judgment, thereby improving their rationality. The results indicate that from 2003 to 2021, WECC in Jiangsu Province declined due to rapid economic development and population growth, but gradually stabilized as economic advancement continued, highlighting a reciprocal relationship between WECC and socio-economic development. A dynamic WECC model for Jiangsu Province was developed using Vensim software and applied to scenarios designed to enhance WECC. The predicted development trends under different scenarios allowed the identification of an optimal scenario. A comprehensive evaluation of six scenarios between 2022 and 2030 suggests that maintaining the current high-speed development model would lead to a continued decline in WECC. In contrast, effective water-saving measures and strengthened sewage treatment can greatly improve water resource carrying capacity, maintaining it at a “positive carrying” level while supporting steady socio-economic growth. These findings provide valuable guidance for Jiangsu Province in enhancing its WECC and aligning socio-economic development with environmental sustainability in its top-level planning.

Introduction

Water is an irreplaceable natural resource and a strategic economic resource. As population grows and economies expand, many regions face increasing pressure from both water scarcity and declining water environmental quality1. These challenges are often more pronounced in developing countries, where unequal water-resource distribution can strongly constrain further socio-economic development2. In this context, the concept of carrying capacity has become an important way to understand sustainability limits because it considers societal institutions, development goals, consumption patterns, and technological levels, while also accounting for how human activities affect ecosystems and how ecosystems respond3–5. Research on environmental carrying capacity has expanded to encompass resource, environmental, and ecological dimensions, and has been applied across multiple spatial scales, such as regions, watersheds, and cities, as well as across various carrying factors, including transportation, land, water resources, and tourism areas6–8. More recently, water environment carrying capacity has emerged as a direction that integrates environmental science with carrying capacity research9,10. Many researchers define water environment carrying capacity (WECC) as “the largest population and economic scale that the water environment system can support in a specific basin or region during a period while meeting its own health needs.” Others view WECC more narrowly, focusing only on the processes and capacities of aquatic systems. In general, WECC research typically involves two coupled systems: the human system and the water environment system11.

Quantitative assessment is a core part of applied WECC research12–15. Common approaches include indicator-based evaluation systems, ecological footprint methods, multi-objective analysis, and system dynamics methods. Indicator-system methods typically compute a composite index by comparing each indicator value with a standard value, and they are widely used in WECC studies. However, different indicator-based techniques have clear tradeoffs. For example, the Vector Modulus Method is simple and intuitive but does not fully address conceptual fuzziness, vector directionality, or indicator weighting. Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation can handle both fuzziness and stochastic uncertainty, but its computational process may discard useful information and lead to misjudgment. Principal Component Analysis helps remove interference and identify dominant factors, yet it can be difficult to define standardized criteria for evaluation parameters. The Analytic Hierarchy Process combines qualitative and quantitative reasoning which is relatively easy to apply, but the use of judgment-matrix validation and adjustment can be highly subjective, which limits its methodological robustness16. More broadly, indicator system approaches often reflect the coordination between water environment conditions and human activities rather than WECC in a strict sense. Their outputs are typically dimensionless indices, and they often fail to capture feedback effects between the water environment and socio-economic factors17,18.

System dynamics (SD) offers a complementary perspective because it is designed for dynamic simulation of complex systems with nonlinear relationships, multiple feedback loops, and time-varying behavior, which makes it suitable for evaluating and predicting WECC19,20. At the same time, SD models typically have limited ability to represent spatial heterogeneity within subsystems. Prior studies have therefore coupled SD with other tools (e.g., hierarchical analysis, entropy weighting, and multi-objective linear empowerment function) to enable a more comprehensive quantitative and qualitative evaluation of WECC21–23. Beyond SD, the ecological footprint method is also used in WECC and water ecological carrying capacity research because it is operational and uses standardized representation methods. However, it tends to emphasize regional ecological sustainability while paying less attention to broader sustainable development needs, inclduing economic, social, and technological aspects. Multi-objective comprehensive analysis can integrate multiple objectives (e.g., society, economy, population, and environment) to search for an overall optimal solution, but solving multi-objective nonlinear programming problems can be challenging in practice24.

To enable a dynamic and continuous assessment of Jiangsu Province’s WECC from both objective and subjective perspectives, this study first analyzes local conditions related to water-resource development, utilization, and water quality. Based on this analysis, an initial indicator system for WECC evaluation is constructed using the Driving-Pressure-State-Impact-Response (DPSIR) framework. Grey correlation theory is then used to screen candidate indicators and determine their weights, while variable fuzzy set theory is applied to calculate Jiangsu’s annual WECC. Finally, an SD model of Jiangsu’s WECC is built in Vensim to explore WECC development trends under different improvement scenarios and to identify an optimal management strategy. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section "Study area and data" introduces the study area and datasets; Section "Methodology" presents the SD model and evaluation framework; Section "Results and discussion" provides results and discussion; and Section "Conclusion" concludes the study.

Study area and data

Study area

Jiangsu Province is located on the eastern coast of China, extending from 116°18′ to 121°57′E, and 30°45′ to 35°20′N. The Yangtze River and the Huaihe River cross the province from south to north, while the Beijing–Hangzhou Grand Canal runs through its central area, creating a dense and highly connected river–canal network (Fig. 1). Mean annual precipitation ranges from 800 to 1100 mm, corresponding to an average total precipitation of 102.06 billion m3. Between 2011 and 2024, Jiangsu’s annual water resources averaged 40.38 billion m3, with a per-capita availability of only 494 m3. Both precipitation and surface water runoff increase from north to south, and their distribution is strongly uneven. By the end of 2024, the province’s permanent population reached 85.26 million, which is 7.94% higher than in 2011, and its GDP grew by 181.98%.Fig. 1River network and major lakes in Jiangsu Province.

Since 2021, water environmental quality in Jiangsu has shown steady and notable improvement. The proportion of national monitoring sections meeting or exceeding Class III water quality standards has reached a record high in recent years, and water quality in key river basins has continued to improve. Taihu Lake, in particular, has achieved a historic breakthrough in water quality. These accomplishments demonstrate Jiangsu’s commitment to ecological conservation and green development while maintaining sustained economic growth. Data

Data on water resources and water environment were obtained from the Jiangsu Province Water Resources Bulletin and the Jiangsu Province Environmental Bulletin. Methodology

This study proposes a method for evaluating WECC by integrating a modified VFPR method with a SD model (Fig. 2). First, a comprehensive evaluation system is constructed based on the DPSIR framework, and the grey correlation method is applied to optimize and screen the indicator system. Second, to account for both the subjective importance and objective information of each indicator, the traditional weights in the variable fuzzy model are replaced with integrated weights. The hierarchical analysis (AHP) method is used to determine the subjective importance of each indicator, while the entropy weight method calculates their objective weights. Finally, a system dynamics model is developed for WECC evaluation and applied to Jiangsu Province. The improved model supports dynamic integrated evaluation and scenario simulation of water environment carrying capacity, and the Monte Carlo method is used to assess the impact of random errors on the evaluation results.Fig. 2Research methodology flowchart.

WECC indicator system

The PSR (Pressure State Response) model is a framework for evaluating environmental systems. In this model, “Pressure” refers to the consumption of resources and environmental degradation caused by human activities, which exerts pressure on the environment. “State” represents the condition of the natural environment, including the quality and quantity of various resources. “Response” denotes the actions taken by society to promote sustainable development and mitigate environmental pressures. The PSR model was first proposed by the OECD to assess the global environment status25. Its core idea is that human activities impose pressures on natural resources and the environment, altering the environmental state and resource conditions, and that society responds through environmental, economic, and other policies to reduce these pressures and maintain environmental sustainability26.

The WECC can be considered a complex "human-water-economy" system, which is regulated through continuous interactions and feedbacks, aligning with the principles of the PSR model. The DPSIR (Driving Force-Pressure-State-Impact-Response) model extends both the DSR (Driving Force-State-Response) and PSR frameworks by incorporating the feedback of water environmental conditions on human society. This allows it to capture the interactive relationships between human activities and aquatic ecosystems comprehensively. The DPSIR model has been widely applied in watershed ecological security assessments. In recent years, it has also been increasingly used in studies of WECC. Therefore, selecting an indicator system based on the DPSIR framework enables an objective quantification of the actual WECC.

The DPSIR model provides a comprehensive framework for assessing WECC by examining its subsystems from five perspectives: driving, pressure, state, impact, and response (Fig. 3). By integrating interactions among the regional environment, economic development, resources, and human activities, the model facilitates the construction of a robust and systematic indicator system. This model is particularly useful for exploring system orderliness and causal relationships. The development of a well-structured indicator system is fundamental to WECC research. VFPR model of WECC

Variable fuzzy set (VFS) theory suggests that under co-dimensionality, objective phenomena can exhibit fuzziness during intermediate transitions28. This aligns with the inherent ambiguity in categorizing WECC. Variable fuzzy sets include approaches such as optimization, recognition, and clustering, along with various improved models. Since WECC evaluation is based on grade standards, it can be formulated as an identification of membership degrees. Using variable fuzzy recognition thus reflects the fuzziness of WECC and avoids the singularity of traditional evaluation models. However, traditional models may not accurately capture the impact of sample information when indicators exceed standard limits. This study employs a modified VFPR model combined with an AHP-E model to conduct dynamic successive assessments of WECC. Weighting method of WECC

In traditional variable fuzzy models, indicator weights are typically determined through pairwise comparisons, which are strongly influenced by human judgment and can be interpreted in two layers. The first layer reflects the impact of indicators on the evaluation object, capturing both the magnitude of individual indicators and their interrelationships, and is ideally derived from objective data. The second layer represents the impact of indicators on evaluation objectives, reflecting the relative importance of each indicator, and is usually obtained through subjective judgment. To address these two aspects, the entropy weight method29,30 is used to determine the quantitative weight of indicator values W1i, while the Analytic Hierarchy Process31–33 is used to determine the importance weight W2i. Construction of the SD model

The SD model is well-suited for simulating WECC under multiple scenarios due to its ability to represent nonlinear processes, dynamic accumulation, and feedback mechanisms. The SD model includes three types of variables: state variables, which describe the current system state and accumulated information; rate variables, which directly affect the state variables and represent their rates of change; and auxiliary variables, which provide environmental parameters, process data, or numerical inputs, typically represented using lookup tables34,35. Three scenarios are considered in the model to explore the potential development of the watershed water ecological carrying capacity. These scenarios collectively allow the exploration of system responses under varying management strategies and development pathways. The status quo scenario assumes that the watershed maintains its current development trajectory, with all model parameters remaining unchanged, providing a baseline for comparison with other scenarios. The efficient water conservation scenario emphasizes improving water use efficiency and reducing overall water consumption. Under this scenario, the total water supply in Jiangsu is projected at 56.0 billion m3 in 2015, 58.0 billion m3 in 2020 and 60.0billion m3 in 2030. In line with provincial and national initiatives, Jiangsu has actively promoted the construction of water-saving society pilot projects, with a total of 7 national-level and 11 provincial-level projects completed. Therefore, the water consumption per 10,000 CNY of industrial added value is set at 30m3, and that of agricultural added value at 400m3 in 2030. The strict sewage treatment scenario focuses on enhancing environmental protection, controlling pollution, and improving overall water quality. This scenario assumes an increase in wastewater treatment capacity, maintaining minimum effluent discharge and chemical oxygen demand (COD) coefficients, with treatment capacity increased by 50% in 2025 and 80% in 2030. Furthermore, the scenario incorporates assumptions on socio-economic development, with high and low growth rates for primary, secondary, and tertiary industries set at 9% and 3%, respectively, and population natural growth rates assumed to be 4% and 0.5%.

Results and discussion

Results of the VFPR model

The evaluation results (Fig. 6) show that the WECC of Jiangsu Province remained between Grade 2 and Grade 3 and was consistently close to Grade 3, with a long-term average value of 2.98., This indicates that the WECC was at a middle-lower level. From 2003 to 2008, the WECC was relatively low due to rapid population growth and accelerated economic development. The time series further shows that from 2009 to 2021, as water conservation measures strengthened and efforts to control water pollution increased, the carrying capacity of the water environment continued to improve. Although the total population, GDP, and urbanization level increased during this period, both water consumption and sewage discharge declined, leading to a substantial improvement in surface water quality. As a result, the WECC experienced a notable improvement, shifting from approximately 3.5 before 2009 to around 2.5 in the subsequent years. To verify the reliability of the model, the results were compared with those obtained from the fuzzy comprehensive assessment (FCA) method (Fig. 5). In terms of water resource development and utilization, the province should make full use of its abundant transit water resources, strengthen water conservancy infrastructure, and ensure adequate water supply to support ongoing industrialization and urbanization. In agriculture, water-saving irrigation techniques, including channel seepage prevention, sprinkler irrigation, and micro-irrigation, should be promoted to improve irrigation efficiency and reduce water consumption per unit area. For the industrial sector, it is important to limit high water-intensive industries, upgrade production processes and water-saving technologies, and lower water consumption per unit of industrial output. Establishing industrial water quotas, formulating water-saving plans, increasing water reuse rates, and promoting centralized water supply within industrial parks are also essential measures. In the domestic sector, strengthening the construction of water-saving facilities, enhancing public outreach, and fostering greater awareness of household water conservation are necessary. Strict implementation of domestic sewage and industrial wastewater treatment standards must be ensured. At the management level, institutional innovations should be advanced to develop mechanisms for water conservation, water use regulation, and water pollution control that integrate economic incentives with regulatory measures.

Uncertainty analysis

There are both model and data uncertainties in the prediction of WECC assessments. Many uncertainties exist in the assessment of WECC. In terms of model uncertainty, the uncertainty is analyzed by comparing two models: variable fuzzy identification and fuzzy comprehensive evaluation. Concerning the uncertainty of the weights of the indicator system, the uncertainty was analyzed by comparing different weight calculation methods (Fig. 10 and Table 6). The results show that there is a significant trend difference between subjective weight and objective weight results from 2003 to 2021. The objective weighting method considers that the importance of these indicators in the process of determining the regional WECC is similar, but the Water resources per capita, Surface water environmental quality, and Industrial wastewater discharge rate up to standard directly determines the size of the WECC. Therefore, this paper comprehensively considers the two layers of indicator weights, composite weights are utilized instead of the weights in the traditional variable fuzzy model. That is, the use of AHP method from the perspective of the system, to determine the importance of the indicators of the weight, the use of entropy weight method comprehensive consideration of the correlation between the indicators, to determine the quantitative value of the indicators of the weight. However, with different expectations of the WECC, indicator system weights of the WECC at different stages of development should change dynamically.Fig. 10WECC assessment results under different indicator weights.

Random errors are inevitable in the process of sample collection, calculation, storage, and testing for various evaluation indicators of WECC. It is particularly suitable for stochastic problems. Conclusion

In this study, the DPSIR framework and grey correlation theory were used to construct a comprehensive WECC evaluation indicator system. The modification of VFPR model parameters based on extreme case tests addressed a key limitation of the traditional model, which cannot accurately reflect the impact of sample index changes when the indicators exceed standard ranges. Furthermore, a SD model of WECC was developed to simulate the WECC index of Jiangsu Province under different scenarios. Simulation results indicate that population growth as well as economic and social development place pressure on the carrying capacity while driving improvements in water environment quality. Under the conventional high-growth scenario, the carrying capacity of the water environment is projected to continue declining.. In contrast, scenarios emphasizing water conservation and wastewater treatment exhibit an inverted U-shaped trajectory in carrying capacity, suggesting initial improvement followed by stabilization. The coordinated development scenario, which integrates economic growth, water resource management, and environmental protection, is conducive to the sustainable development of both the ecological environment and socioeconomic systems, achieving a balance among social, economic, and environmental benefits. Future research could focus on exploring the spatial and temporal evolution of WECC from the perspective of underlying physical mechanisms.
