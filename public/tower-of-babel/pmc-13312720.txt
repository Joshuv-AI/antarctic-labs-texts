Abuse and empowerment in the academic field on the academic performance of medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Esteban G Reyes, Matheus Pierini Santos, Edda E Newball-Noriega, Salomón Huancahuire-Vega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic abuse remains a concerning and under-recognized factor affecting medical students’ well-being and academic achievement. Empowerment, on the other hand, may mitigate negative experiences and enhance performance. This study aimed to examine the relationship between academic abuse, empowerment, and academic performance among medical students in a Latin American setting.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 255 medical students at a private university in Lima, Peru. Participants were selected using stratified sampling by year of study. Academic performance was assessed using the RAU Scale, academic abuse through the Teacher Abuse Questionnaire, and empowerment using the Instrument of Perception of Empowerment (IPE). A Poisson regression model with robust variance was used to directly estimate prevalence ratios (PR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The model included academic abuse and its dimensions as the main explanatory variables and was subsequently adjusted for sociodemographic and academic covariates (sex, age, nationality, and having failed a subject).ResultsOf the participants, 72.2% exhibited adequate academic performance, while 76.5% reported low levels of abuse and over 90% demonstrated high empowerment. Multivariate analysis after adjustment showed that high academic abuse (PR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.74–0.96; p = 0.011) and female sex (PR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.69–0.94; p = 0.005) were associated with a lower probability of adequate academic performance, whereas older age (PR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.03; p = 0.013), Peruvian nationality (PR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.02–1.33; p = 0.028), and having failed at least one subject (PR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.05–1.39; p = 0.008), were associated with a higher probability of adequate academic performance. Academic empowerment did not show a statistically significant association with academic performance after adjustment for covariates.ConclusionsAcademic abuse negatively impacts student performance, while empowerment appears to be widespread yet insufficient to fully buffer these effects. Addressing academic mistreatment and fostering a supportive and empowering educational environment should be priorities in medical education reform.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09378-2.Keywords: Students, Medical, Education, Medical, Academic Performance, Abuse, Empowerment, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Medical training is recognized for its high academic load, emotional demands, and constant pressure. Despite its importance in preparing future health professionals, the experience of academic abuse during training is a growing concern [1]. A meta-analysis shows that 59.4% of medical students have experienced at least one type of harassment or discrimination during their training [2]. This mistreatment, which ranges from derogatory comments to abuse of power by teachers, negatively impacts students’ emotional and mental well-being, affecting their academic performance. Victims often face high levels of stress, anxiety, and, in some cases, depression, which decreases their ability to concentrate and actively participate in their education [3].

The medical career, known for its extensive study schedule and rigorous clinical practices, can intensify these negative effects. The demanding academic environment can aggravate emotional exhaustion, creating a vicious cycle where abuse and stress perpetuate a decline in the quality of learning and academic performance [4]. This phenomenon is observed globally. In the United States, a study revealed that 64% of incidents of abuse during medical training were attributed to teachers and 75.5% to residents, with a significant correlation between recurrent abuse and high levels of burnout [5]. In Asia, a study showed that 96.6% of residents perceived abuse during their training, with verbal and academic abuse being the most common. Additionally, 75.9% of residents advised family members not to enter medical school due to the hostile environment [6]. Likewise, in Africa, it was found that almost all medical students had experienced abuse during their training, highlighting shouting, public humiliation, derogatory comments about academic performance, and assignment of tasks as punishment. In addition, 34.2% reported religious or age discrimination, 33.8% sexual harassment, and 26.4% threats of harm, mainly during surgical rotations. These abuses resulted in strained relationships with perpetrators, decreased self-confidence, and depression [7].

In Latin America, mistreatment in medical training also represents a significant problem, with different characteristics in each country. In Mexico, medical residents face a highly competitive and conflictive educational environment, where the perception of intolerance and discriminatory comments mainly affects gender minorities, generating an exclusionary environment [8]. In Chile, a study has shown that abuse is practically universal among medical students, with harmful effects such as demoralization and deregulated demands, which negatively influence their mental health and their relationship with academic staff [9]. In Peru, students report high levels of psychological, academic, and physical abuse, mainly perpetrated by teaching doctors and residents. This mistreatment manifests itself in humiliation, insults, and assignment of punitive tasks, severely affecting his academic performance and emotional well-being [10, 11]. The situation became more critical after the resumption of in-person classes following the pandemic [12].

Faced with this problem, academic empowerment is a promising strategy to improve students’ educational experience, particularly their academic performance. This approach involves not only the acquisition of technical knowledge but also active participation in decision-making within the educational and clinical fields. Various research studies support the effectiveness of empowerment in this context [13]. Curriculum co-creation has increased interest and effectiveness in learning, contributing to an educational environment more tailored to students’ needs and thus improving their performance [14]. In addition, training in critical decision-making has optimized clinical skills and academic performance, encouraging a more proactive and autonomous approach among students [15]. Thus, empowerment improves students’ emotional well-being and is closely related to stronger and more effective academic performance.

However, despite growing concern about academic abuse and its impact on medical students, there is a lack of research that directly explores the relationship between academic abuse, empowerment, and academic performance. Most previous studies have addressed issues such as academic overload, the risk of abandonment and motivation, but have left a gap in understanding how these factors interact with academic performance [15–17].

This study aimed to determine the relationship between maltreatment and academic empowerment with the performance of medical students at a private university in Lima.

Methods

Type of study, population, and sample

This study employed an analytical, cross-sectional design aimed at examining the association between academic abuse and academic empowerment (independent variables) and academic performance (dependent variable) among medical students. The design allowed the identification of statistical associations between the main factors of interest within a defined population at a single point in time, without manipulation of variables. It was carried out on Human Medicine students from a private university in the Lima East region, who totaled 701 enrolled during the 2024-II academic period. A 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error were used to determine the sample size, resulting in a calculated sample of 255 participants. This sample represented 36.4% of the student body (Fig. 1). This study followed the recommendations of the STROBE guide to ensure adequate and complete reporting of cross-sectional studies.

Flow diagram of participants included in the study

The participants were selected using stratified proportional random sampling, with strata were defined according to the students’ years of study (from the first to the seventh year). The number of participants in each stratum was calculated proportionally to its size in the total population of 701 medical students, thus guaranteeing the representativeness of all stages of academic training in the Human Medicine program. This sampling strategy strengthened the representativeness of the study sample and minimized selection bias by reflecting the actual distribution of students within the program. The inclusion criteria considered were: Human Medicine students enrolled in the 2024-II academic period, over 18 years of age, with a full academic load, who attended between the first and seventh year of study, and who agreed to participate voluntarily by signing the informed consent. On the other hand, the exclusion criteria included underage students, those with incomplete academic loads, those who did not provide their informed consent, and students in exceptional situations that could affect their participation, such as extended licenses or interruptions in enrollment.

Data collection instruments and techniques

The survey technique and the following questionnaires were used as instruments for data collection.

Academic performance

The “RAU Scale”, developed by Preciado and collaborators in Mexico in 2019, was used to measure academic performance [18]. This instrument has a reliability greater than 0.5 and was validated in Peru, where it reached a reliability of 0.8 according to Cronbach’s alpha [19]. The Peruvian version of the scale includes 18 items distributed in three dimensions: Contribution to academic activities (items 4, 11, 12, 1, 15, 17, 18, 16, 20), Dedication to study (items 1–3, 5, 19) and Lack of organization of didactic resources (items 6, 8, 9, 10). Participants respond on a Likert-type scale with options ranging from “never” (0) to “always” (6). The total score ranks academic performance as low (0–40 points), medium (41–80 points), and high (81–120 points).

Academic abuse

The “Questionnaire of Abuse from Teachers” was created by Quine and validated by Bastías and collaborators in Chile in 2021, showing a reliability coefficient greater than 0.85 according to Cronbach’s Alpha. It consists of 20 items grouped into three dimensions: demoralization (12 items), deregulated demands (6 items), and physical aggression (2 items). Responses are collected on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “never” (1) to “always” (5). The final score categorizes the perception of abuse into three levels: low (23–54 points), moderate (55–84 points), and high (85–115 points) [20, 21].

Academic empowerment

To measure academic empowerment, the “Instrument of Perception of Empowerment (IPE)” was created by Roller in 1998 and validated by Escobar and collaborators in Mexico in 2021, with a reliability of 0.8 according to Cronbach’s alpha. The instrument consists of 15 items, which are answered on a Likert-type scale of 1 to 5 points. The options are: strongly agree (5), Agree (4), Neutral (3), Disagree (2), and strongly disagree (1). The items are grouped into three dimensions: Autonomy (items 01, 03, 09, 12, 13), Participation (items 02, 04, 06, 11, 14, 15), and Responsibility (items 05, 07, 08, 10). It should be noted that item 06 has a negative direction, so it was inverted for statistical analysis. The total score range is between 15 and 75 points, allowing the average score of each dimension and the overall score to be calculated. High empowerment is considered when the overall score equals or exceeds the average [22].

Although the instruments used in this study had been previously validated in Spanish-speaking populations, their reliability was re-evaluated in the current sample using Cronbach’s alpha to verify internal consistency and applicability. The obtained values were 0.93 for the RAU Scale (academic performance), 0.96 for the Teacher Abuse Questionnaire (academic abuse), and 0.94 for the Instrument of Perception of Empowerment (IPE), indicating excellent reliability in this population.

Data collection occurred between October and November 2024, and each interview lasted approximately 15 min.

Ethical aspects

The work was approved by the University’s Research Ethics Committee (2024.CEB-FCS-UPeU-No.259). The participants signed the informed consent form prior to completing the questionnaires.

Data analysis

The statistical package SPSS v.24 was used for data analysis and processing. Initially, the data was cleaned extensively to ensure its quality and reliability. Subsequently, a descriptive analysis of the study variables was carried out, including simple frequency tables for categorical variables and measures of central tendency and dispersion (mean and standard deviation) for numerical variables.

In the bivariate analysis, associations between abuse, empowerment, and academic performance were examined using χ² (Chi-square) tests of independence in 2×n contingency tables. When more than 20% of the cells had expected frequencies lower than 5, Fisher’s exact test was applied. In addition, Pearson standardized residuals were analyzed to identify cells with observed frequencies greater or smaller than expected. As measures of association strength, the Phi (φ) coefficient was calculated for 2 × 2 tables and Cramér’s V for 2×n tables, following Cohen’s (1988) effect-size criteria: small (≥ 0.10), moderate (≥ 0.30), and large (≥ 0.50).

Academic performance (adequate vs. inadequate) was considered the dependent variable, while academic abuse and academic empowerment were treated as the independent variables. Sociodemographic factors: sex, age, nationality, marital status, employment status, religious belief, and history of course failure, were treated as potential confounders.

Finally, a multivariable analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with academic performance (adequate vs. inadequate). Given the high prevalence of the outcome, a Poisson regression model with robust variance was used to directly estimate prevalence ratios (PR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The model included academic abuse and its dimensions as the main explanatory variables and was subsequently adjusted for sociodemographic and academic covariates (sex, age, nationality, and having failed a subject). Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using deviance and Pearson chi-square statistics, with values close to 1 indicating an adequate fit. The overall contribution of the covariates was evaluated using Wald chi-square tests. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

Two hundred fifty-five medical students from a private university in Lima were included. The distribution by sex was equitable: 50.2% women and 49.8% men, with an average age of 21.72 ± 3.76 years. The majority were of Peruvian nationality (86.7%), were in their second year of studies (42.7%), and were single (92.5%). Regarding employment, 88.6% had no job, and 95.7% believed in God. Regarding academic history, 66.7% reported failing some subjects (Table 1).

The multivariable analysis identified factors independently associated with students’ academic performance. The model showed an adequate goodness of fit, with Pearson χ²/df = 0.29 and deviance/df = 0.45. After adjustment for sociodemographic and academic variables, a high level of academic abuse was significantly associated with a lower probability of adequate academic performance (PR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.74–0.96; p = 0.011), confirming the negative impact of adverse academic environments on student outcomes.

After adjustment, female sex was also associated with a lower probability of adequate academic performance compared with male students (PR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.69–0.94; p = 0.005), representing a reduction of approximately 19%. In contrast, age showed a positive association, such that each additional year of age was associated with a 2% increase in the probability of adequate academic performance (PR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.03; p = 0.013).

Regarding nationality, students of Peruvian nationality exhibited a higher probability of adequate academic performance compared with foreign students (PR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.02–1.33; p = 0.028). Additionally, students who reported having failed at least one subject showed a higher probability of adequate academic performance (PR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.05–1.39; p = 0.008), a finding that should be interpreted considering the academic context and the adjustment variables included in the model.

Finally, academic empowerment did not show a statistically significant association with academic performance after adjustment for potential confounders, including sex, age, nationality, and academic history. Similarly, its dimensions: autonomy (PR = 1.07; 95% CI: 0.53–2.14; p = 0.850) and responsibility (PR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.30–1.21; p = 0.115) were not significantly associated with the outcome (Table 4).

Discussion

This research aimed to determine the relationship between maltreatment and academic empowerment and the performance of medical students at a private university in Lima in 2024. The results indicate that being a woman significantly increases the probability of presenting low academic performance, results that agree with the study by Saxena et al., who found that women reported higher levels of anxiety, which affected their performance in exams and their use of study strategies. Therefore, anxiety could be a contributing factor that increases the probability of presenting lower academic performance in women [23]. However, other research on the subject has found conflicting results. It was observed that female students had a lower number of years of repetition and managed to graduate in less time than their male peers, in addition to obtaining better results in the evaluations, which suggests that in specific cultural and educational contexts, women could have a better academic performance [24]. On the other hand, a study highlights how gender differences in exposure to clinical practices affect student performance, with women performing fewer clinical exams, especially in intimate areas, which could reflect cultural barriers [25]. Although it does not focus on general academic performance, this study suggests that differences in the educational experiences of men and women may be influenced by cultural norms that affect women’s confidence and exposure in certain areas.

Another important finding of this research is that each unit increase in age increases the probability of better academic performance. Results are consistent with some previous studies but have some notable differences. In the analysis by Komasawa et al., the older medical students did not show a significant inferiority in their performance compared to the younger ones, suggesting that age does not always negatively impact academic results. This could be related to the maturity and additional experience that older students can bring, which could compensate for the lack of agility typical of younger students [26]. However, another study found that older students performed better academically than younger ones, coinciding with our findings. This study suggests that older students are more likely to achieve good results, possibly due to greater dedication and previous experience that influences their academic performance [27]. In contrast, a study in Peru on the scores of the National Examination of Medicine (ENAM) revealed that older age (over 25–26 years) is associated with lower scores on the exam, which contrasts with our results. Likewise, this study suggests that factors such as test anxiety and stress management may be more relevant determinants for the performance of older students, which could explain the differences between the studies [28].

Another interesting result of the study is that failing any subject significantly increases the probability of adequate performance. Although this result may appear counterintuitive, it could reflect adaptive responses following academic setbacks. Experiencing failure may increase students’ awareness of academic demands, prompting greater motivation, improved study strategies, and more active engagement with tutoring or institutional support services [27]. In this sense, prior failure might function as a catalyst for behavioral change, fostering academic resilience and recovery rather than directly enhancing performance. Nevertheless, this association should be interpreted cautiously and within the context of the multivariable model, as the observed effect may be influenced by adjustment for confounding factors that modify the relationship between previous failure and current outcomes. Moreover, existing literature presents mixed evidence. The survey by Tadese et al. identified that students with adequate academic performance managed to maintain a regular progression in their studies, which implies the importance of avoiding delays or failing key subjects [27]. This could be related to the cumulative impact of failed subjects on students’ confidence and motivation, and the increase in subsequent academic load. Similarly, irregular academic status, such as failing courses, was shown to be negatively associated with the ENAM results. This result reinforces the idea that unsuccessful subjects not only affect academic performance directly but could also reflect a lack of consistent study habits or difficulties in fundamental skills that impact subsequent evaluations [28]. Conversely, other research found that students who repeated years did not always perform worse on specific skills such as the objective structured clinical examination (OSCE), indicating that the effects of failing subjects may vary depending on the type of assessment and the opportunity for recovery [25, 26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between prior academic failure and subsequent performance is complex and context-dependent, potentially reflecting both risk and resilience processes within students’ academic trajectories.

Finally, the study found that high academic abuse decreases the probability of presenting adequate academic performance. Costa et al. showed that academic abuse, exceptionally verbal and psychological abuse perpetrated by teachers, has a high prevalence, primarily affecting women and students of public institutions; in addition, it was found that more than 50% of the cases reported a deterioration in academic performance, which coincides with the association found in the present analysis [29]. Similarly, a study highlighted that academic abuse seriously affects students’ confidence and emotional stability, generating stress, depression, and demotivation, factors that contribute to low performance and that are often perpetuated due to the low rate of complaints due to fear of reprisals [30]. Likewise, a high prevalence of academic abuse was identified in seven Latin American countries, especially in public universities. It showed that this phenomenon most often affects older students and those in advanced training stages, aggravating the decrease in academic performance [31].

Limitations

The study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between the variables analyzed. Additionally, data collection using self-administered questionnaires may be subject to memory or response bias, which could affect the accuracy of responses. Second, the study focused on a specific population, limiting the generalization of the findings to different contexts or groups. Third, no additional contextual variables, such as family environment or institutional support, that could influence academic performance were evaluated, suggesting the need for future research that integrates these dimensions.

In conclusion, the study’s results show that being a woman, and experiencing high levels of academic abuse significantly decrease the chances of achieving adequate academic performance highlighting the detrimental impact of adverse academic environments on student outcomes. At the same time, age is positively related to better academic performance. The positive association observed among students who had previously failed a subject suggests a complex academic dynamic that warrants further exploration. These findings highlight the need to implement comprehensive strategies that address individual characteristics and contextual factors to ensure an academic environment that favors student development and performance.
