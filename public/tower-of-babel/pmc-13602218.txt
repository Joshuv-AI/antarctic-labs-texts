Pneumococcal meningitis caused by non-vaccine serotype 34 after vaccination with the 20-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine in a child with inner ear malformations and bilateral cochlear implants: A case report
Authors: Yuji Maruo, Hiroki Shimomura, Satoshi Hirakawa, Yoshinori Omori, Atsuo Togashi, Nobuhisa Ishiguro, Kenichi Takano, Satoshi Yakuwa, Bin Chang, Shigeru Suga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough pneumococcal conjugate vaccines have reduced invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in children, infections caused by non-vaccine serotypes remain a significant clinical concern. Children with congenital inner ear malformations and cochlear implants (CIs) face an elevated risk of bacterial meningitis, due to potential abnormal communication between the inner ear and subarachnoid space. We report a case of pneumococcal meningitis caused by the non-vaccine serotype 34 in a child with bilateral CIs, who had completed the recommended pneumococcal vaccine schedule. A 2-year-old boy with bilateral congenital sensorineural hearing loss, secondary to a right common cavity and left cochlear hypoplasia type I, underwent bilateral cochlear implantation. Intraoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage was observed during both implantation procedures. His immunization history was up to date and included both the 13-valent and 20-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV13 and PCV20). He presented with fever, vomiting, and lethargy. CSF examination revealed marked neutrophilic pleocytosis, and a multiplex meningitis/encephalitis polymerase chain reaction assay detected Streptococcus pneumoniae. Blood and CSF cultures confirmed S. pneumoniae serotype 34. Following antimicrobial therapy, he recovered without neurological sequelae. However, recurrent bacterial meningitis two months later necessitated right CI explantation and contralateral device replacement. This case underscores that IPD caused by non-vaccine serotypes can still occur in children with congenital inner ear malformations and CIs who have completed the recommended pneumococcal vaccine schedule. Persistent anatomical susceptibility may drive recurrent meningitis, warranting careful long-term follow-up and the timely consideration of implant removal in cases of recurrent infection.Keywords: Cochlear implant, Inner ear malformation, Non-vaccine serotype, Pneumococcal meningitis, Serotype 34, Streptococcus pneumoniae

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is one of the leading causes of bacterial meningitis in children worldwide and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and vaccination [1,2]. Although the introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines has led to a marked reduction in invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) caused by vaccine serotypes, disease due to non-vaccine serotypes has emerged in the post-vaccine era, a phenomenon known as serotype replacement [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Congenital inner ear malformations have been reported to predispose children to bacterial meningitis, even in the absence of cochlear implantation. This increased risk is thought to result from abnormal communication between the inner ear and the subarachnoid space, which may allow pathogens to spread to the central nervous system [7]. Children with cochlear implantations are also recognized as a high-risk population for bacterial meningitis [8]. This elevated risk has been attributed to several factors, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, a history of otitis media, and the presence of underlying inner ear malformations [7,8]. Here, we report a case of pneumococcal meningitis caused by a non-vaccine serotype 34, which is not included in current pneumococcal conjugate vaccines. In this case, anatomical factors contributed to recurrent infection, ultimately requiring implant removal, highlighting how invasive disease caused by non-vaccine serotypes may emerge following the implementation of conjugate vaccine programs.

Case presentation

A 2-year-old boy presented with a 2-day history of fever and vomiting. His medical history was significant for recurrent otitis media and bilateral congenital sensorineural hearing loss secondary to inner ear malformations (a right common cavity and left cochlear hypoplasia type I), for which he had undergone bilateral cochlear implantation.

At 11 months of age, during left cochlear implantation and external auditory canal closure, a CSF leakage was encountered and sealed intraoperatively, suggesting preexisting communication between the inner ear and subarachnoid space. At 1 year of age, he underwent right cochlear implantation with external auditory canal closure and the removal of a previously placed tympanostomy tube. A small CSF leak was again noted and sealed. His postoperative course was complicated by a right-sided infection requiring mastoidectomy. His immunizations were up to date, without known immunodeficiency; he had received four doses of the Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine, three doses of PCV13, and a single dose of PCV20 eight months prior to admission.

On admission, he was febrile and lethargic, with a positive Brudzinski’s sign but no nuchal rigidity. Laboratory findings indicated marked systemic inflammation: a white blood cell count of 23,900/μL (neutrophil predominance), C-reactive protein of 33.1 mg/dL, and procalcitonin of 28.4 ng/mL, alongside signs of dehydration, with serum immunoglobulin levels being within the age-appropriate reference ranges (Table 1). CSF analysis revealed profound neutrophilic pleocytosis (4798 cells/μL) (Table 2). Gram staining of the CSF demonstrated phagocytosed Gram-positive diplococci, and a multiplex PCR assay (BioFire® FilmArray® Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel, BioFire Diagnostics, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) detected S. pneumoniae. A urinary pneumococcal antigen test was also positive. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the temporal bone showed fluid accumulation in the right mastoid cavity, though this may have represented postoperative changes (Fig. 1). Abdominal CT showed a normally located spleen without apparent structural abnormalities.

A diagnosis of pneumococcal meningitis was established. Blood and CSF cultures confirmed S. pneumoniae. The isolate was fully susceptible to penicillin and cefotaxime (Table 3). Serotyping via the Quellung reaction using pneumococcal antisera (Statens Serum Institut, Copenhagen, Denmark) identified the non-vaccine serotype 34. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) characterized the isolate as ST3116 (10, 8, 6, 1, 9, 1, 279), the predominant lineage among serotype 34 in Japan [9].

Empiric intravenous therapy with meropenem and vancomycin, alongside adjunctive dexamethasone, was initiated. The patient defervesced within two days. Although a cochlear implant (CI)-associated infection was suspected, implant removal was deferred to preserve hearing, as the exact infectious focus remained unlocalized. Following susceptibility results, antibiotics were de-escalated to intravenous ampicillin for a 14-day course. He recovered without neurological sequelae.

Two months post-discharge, he was readmitted with recurrent fever, vomiting, and marked systemic inflammation (white blood cell count, 15,400/μL; C-reactive protein, 1.4 mg/dL; procalcitonin, 10.5 ng/mL) (Table 1). CSF analysis again showed significant neutrophilic pleocytosis (5645 cells/μL) (Table 2), though cultures and PCR were negative. Suspecting recurrent CI-associated pneumococcal meningitis, treatment with dexamethasone, cefotaxime, and vancomycin was initiated, leading to rapid clinical improvement. Given the history of recurrent right-sided postoperative infections, the right CI was deemed the probable infectious nidus and was explanted. Simultaneously, the left CI was replaced due to electrode array extension into the internal auditory canal. Microbiological cultures of all eight specimens obtained during CI-related surgery were all negative for bacterial growth. He had a stable postoperative course, was discharged on postoperative day 3 without neurological sequelae, and is currently undergoing long-term follow-up to monitor for recurrence and delayed complications.

Discussion

S. pneumoniae is the most frequently identified causative pathogen among reported cases of CI-associated meningitis [8]. In the present case, the causative organism was identified as S. pneumoniae serotype 34 (ST3116), a non-vaccine serotype not included in the pneumococcal conjugate vaccines currently in use. Recent nationwide population-based surveillance of pediatric IPD in Japan demonstrated that while disease caused by PCV13 serotypes decreased markedly, the proportion of IPD caused by non-vaccine serotypes has increased, indicating ongoing serotype replacement in the post-PCV era [6,10]. These findings highlight the continued occurrence of IPD caused by non-vaccine serotypes following the widespread implementation of pneumococcal conjugate vaccination. Furthermore, this case provides additional evidence supporting the importance of ongoing surveillance for emerging or previously uncommon non-vaccine serotypes.

Serotype 34 has been detected in pneumococcal carriage studies across different regions and vaccination settings, although its prevalence varies by population and region. In a pre-PCV study of children in Indonesia, serotype 34 accounted for 3.1% of pneumococcal isolates [11]. In a longitudinal study of children in Ethiopia conducted before and after PCV10 introduction, serotype 34 accounted for 4.5% of pneumococcal isolates overall [12]. Notably, in a recent post-PCV study of 353 pneumococcal isolates obtained from children in Hokkaido, Japan, in 2023, serotype 34 accounted for 9.1% of pneumococcal isolates and was the fifth most prevalent non-PCV13/PCV20 serotype [13]. This finding is of particular interest in the present case, which also occurred in a child in Hokkaido. Despite its relatively frequent detection in carriage, serotype 34 has generally not been among the major serotypes causing IPD in Japan [6,14]. Nevertheless, serotype 34 has been reported as a cause of both meningitis and non-meningitis IPD in Japan [6,14], and the present case further demonstrates its potential to cause severe and recurrent pneumococcal meningitis. Continued surveillance of serotype 34 and other non-vaccine serotypes is therefore important in the context of serotype replacement and future pneumococcal vaccine development.

The recurrence of meningitis two months after the initial episode raised concern for an occult implant-associated infection involving the retained CI. Experimental studies have shown that the presence of a CI can lower the threshold for pneumococcal meningitis, supporting a role for the implant as a potential facilitator of infection [15]. In addition, S. pneumoniae can form biofilms on abiotic surfaces, which may promote bacterial persistence [[16], [17], [18]]. Although the contribution of biofilm formation to CI-associated pneumococcal meningitis remains uncertain [15], it may represent a potential mechanism of persistent infection. In our patient, the right CI was subsequently explanted because of recurrent right-sided postoperative infections; however, microbiological cultures of explanted CIs were negative. Because the implant was removed after antimicrobial therapy had been initiated, the negative cultures do not necessarily exclude an implant-associated infection, as prior antimicrobial therapy may have reduced the bacterial burden and the sensitivity of culture. Thus, although neither implant-associated infection nor biofilm formation could be confirmed, both remain possible contributors to the recurrence.

The management of meningitis in children with CIs remains challenging, particularly regarding the indication for implant removal. Initially, implant preservation was selected, to avoid irreversible hearing loss, given the patient’s rapid clinical response to antimicrobial therapy and the expected technical difficulty of reimplantation; however, the recurrence of bacterial meningitis two months later strongly suggested an occult implant-associated infection or a persistent local infectious source. The patient’s history of recurrent otitis media raises the possibility of a persistent nasopharyngeal or local colonization niche that could harbor pneumococci, particularly in the context of an indwelling device. However, this remains speculative in the absence of longitudinal microbiological surveillance. Further studies evaluating serial colonization dynamics in children with CIs and inner ear malformations are required to clarify this potential mechanism. This clinical course demonstrates that determining the optimal timing of implant removal requires carefully balancing the preservation of auditory function against the risk of recurrent meningitis. In our case, repeated postoperative infections supported the subsequent explantation of the right CI.

Regarding the initial antimicrobial therapy, detailed information regarding the patient’s previous medical and surgical history was not yet fully available on initial admission because the cochlear implantation had been performed at another facility. Furthermore, due to the patient's history of recurrent otitis media, we initially could not rule out congenital immunodeficiency. Given the diagnosis of bacterial meningitis and the urgency of treatment, broad-spectrum empirical therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was initiated while gathering additional information [19]. Once further clinical details and underlying condition became clear, and as S. pneumoniae was identified from blood and CSF cultures with its antimicrobial susceptibility, without laboratory findings strongly suggesting congenital immunodeficiency, antimicrobial therapy was de-escalated to ampicillin. However, in retrospect, this presentation could have been managed as community-acquired bacterial meningitis, for which standard empiric treatment with a third-generation cephalosporin, such as ceftriaxone or cefotaxime, combined with vancomycin would have been appropriate [19].

This case highlights several important clinical considerations. First, even when initial medical treatment is successful, persistent anatomical communication or an occult implant-associated infection can predispose patients to recurrence. Second, determining the optimal timing for implant removal remains challenging, as the preservation of auditory function must be balanced carefully against the risk of recurrent meningitis. In our patient, repeated right-sided infections strongly suggested a persistent local nidus secondary to the retained device, ultimately supporting its explantation. Therefore, careful long-term follow-up is essential for children with CIs and inner ear malformations who develop bacterial meningitis. As the pediatric CI population continues to grow [20], awareness of pneumococcal meningitis caused by non-vaccine serotypes is increasingly critical, particularly for those with underlying congenital inner ear malformations.

In conclusion, invasive disease caused by non-vaccine serotypes, such as serotype 34, may occur following the implementation of conjugate vaccination programs. Children with CIs and inner ear malformations remain vulnerable due to anatomical predispositions. Managing this population requires prompt antimicrobial therapy, vigilant long-term follow-up, and ongoing surveillance for non-vaccine serotypes. Furthermore, these findings highlight the need to carefully consider the role of CI-associated anatomical and microbiological factors in recurrent infections. In cases of persistent or recurrent disease, the potential involvement of the indwelling device as a local reservoir or nidus should be thoroughly evaluated, and its timely removal or replacement should be considered when clinically indicated.
