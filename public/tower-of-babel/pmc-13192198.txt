Knowledge, attitudes, and barriers to second-trimester fetal anomaly scan utilization among pregnant women in Jordan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Suhair M.A. Qudsieh, Zainab F. Albikawi, Mohammad Abuadas, Lara Al Barbarawi, Motasem Alzaqh, Sara Muhanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSecond-trimester fetal anomaly scan is an important modality of care in antenatal settings for detecting congenital abnormalities and improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. Despite the importance of second-trimester ultrasound anomaly scan, its utilization remains suboptimal in Jordan, due to the existing knowledge gaps, prevailing attitudes, and various barriers which affect utilization and access to this type of diagnostic procedure.AimTo assess the knowledge, attitudes, and barriers related to the utilization of second-trimester anomaly scans among pregnant Jordanian women and to identify the predictors of women’s knowledge regarding anomaly scan capabilities.MethodsA total of 468 pregnant women attending antenatal clinics in Jordan participated in this cross-sectional study. A valid and self-administered questionnaire was used. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics and multiple linear regressions identified the predictors of knowledge regarding anomaly scan capabilities.ResultsThe findings revealed that 87.0% had good knowledge about anomaly scan capabilities and 13.0% had poor knowledge. The statistically significant predictors of a high level of knowledge were previous anomaly scan history (B = 0.591, p < 0.001), family history of congenital anomalies (B = 0.793, p < 0.001), consanguinity (B = 0.440, p = 0.002), and number of miscarriages (B = 0.221, p = 0.009). The main barriers included: fear of detecting fetal malformations, 17.5%; religious objections to pregnancy termination, 16.6%; and financial barriers, 12.4%. Although most participants (87.8%) expressed an attitude that considered ultrasound to be safe, there were still misconceptions about its potential risks.ConclusionThe study highlights the critical role of personal and familial experiences in shaping women’s knowledge about anomaly scans. Although attitudes toward the safety and usefulness of ultrasound were generally positive, targeted educational interventions are needed to address misconceptions about fetal ultrasound scans and barriers to their utilization. Strengthening healthcare provider training and offering counseling during antenatal visits could improve knowledge and utilization rates. Additionally, this study urges health policymakers to ensure anomaly scan coverage for all pregnant women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09106-0.

Introduction

Obstetric ultrasound is a well-established and essential modality in prenatal care, offering critical insight into fetal development and maternal health. It utilizes high-frequency sound waves to create images of the fetus, allowing healthcare providers to assess fetal anatomy, monitor growth, and detect potential abnormalities [1]. The International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) supports the use of routine obstetric ultrasound scans, particularly during the second trimester, as a standard practice for evaluating fetal health and identifying congenital anomalies [2]. The guidelines recommend that the mid-trimester fetal ultrasound scan be performed between 18 and 22 weeks of gestation, during which a comprehensive assessment of fetal structures is conducted, including the heart, brain, and other vital organs [3]. The significance of obstetric ultrasound extends beyond mere visualization; it plays a pivotal role in guiding clinical decisions and informing expectant parents about their pregnancy. For instance, studies have shown that the detection rates of central nervous system (CNS) anomalies during routine ultrasound examinations can vary significantly, with the second trimester scan being particularly effective for identifying such conditions [4, 5]. A structured approach not only is likely to achieve the maximal chance of diagnosis but also ensures that healthcare providers can offer appropriate counseling and management options to parents based on the findings. Despite the advancements in first-trimester ultrasound technology, which have improved the detection rates of certain anomalies, the second-trimester scan remains indispensable. In fact, although more than 60% of antenatally detected malformations can be diagnosed at the first-trimester scan, it is estimated that the second-trimester scan is of paramount importance in detecting additional anomalies, especially those involving the gastrointestinal and skeletal systems [6, 7].

Knowledge of the purpose and benefits of the second-trimester scan is usually very limited. For example, one study found that only 57% of women felt that they had received sufficient information in preparation for their scan, resulting in incorrect ideas about what the scan could offer [8]. This lack of information can lead to anxiety and unrealistic expectations about the findings of the scan, with many women believing it can confirm fetal health or diagnose chromosomal anomalies [9].

Attitudes to the second-trimester scan can vary significantly between different populations. It has also been demonstrated that women’s perception of the importance of the scan is modulated by cultural belief systems and past experiences associated with health [7]. It is observed that, in some countries, women may insist on having more extensive testing because of beliefs regarding congenital anomalies or other conditions, while in other cultures there is some skepticism about the efficacy underlying such interventions [10].

The barriers to second-trimester scanning are complex and multifactorial. Economic issues, such as the cost of the scan and transportation to a health care facility, may significantly hinder women from receiving this much-needed prenatal service [10]. In addition, practical factors such as transportation and conflicts in schedules further contribute to delayed reaching in time for the scans [8].

Abdo, Ibraheem [11] conducted a study to identify the knowledge, attitude, and practices of Jordanian women about prenatal genetic testing. Though the results showed a good awareness of the availability of genetic testing options, many women had misconceived perceptions about the purpose and implications of such tests. False-positive results could be a reason for avoiding available screenings. Furthermore, Thekrallah, Qatawneh [12] conducted research into pregnant women’s perceptions and expectations of second-trimester ultrasound scans at Jordan University Hospital, who found that 12% of the patients responded that they were referred for the scan to exclude congenital anomalies. Half of the patients were referred to a routine procedure, but they were not aware that the scan would detect malformations. This was because they were not told by their referring doctor, or the facility did not provide information leaflets about the second-trimester morphology scanning.

The literature indicates that while there is a foundational understanding of the importance of ultrasonography in prenatal care among practitioners and women in Jordan, significant gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and barriers persist. Therefore, the current study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and barriers related to the utilization of the second-trimester anomaly scan among pregnant Jordanian women. Additionally, to identify the predictors of women’s knowledge regarding anomaly scan capabilities.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study to assess the knowledge, attitude, and barriers regarding second-trimester fetal anomaly scans among pregnant women in Jordan. The data collection period was six months, from January 2023 to June 2023.

Sample size and sampling

Sample size was calculated by G*Power 3.1.9.7 [13], assuming a medium effect size (0.15), a significance level (0.05), a power (0.80). The required sample size was calculated to be 123 participants. However, this study included 468 participants to increase the diversity and representation of pregnant women in Jordan and to further assure adequate statistical power. This robust sample size assures reliability and enhances the generalizability of the findings. Inclusion criteria required participants to be pregnant at the time of the study, aged 18 years or older, willing to participate, have access to the internet and social media platforms, and have attended public or private antenatal clinics across Jordan.

Data collection

Initial contact with potential participants was made through clinics and online platforms. The questionnaire took approximately 5–7 min to complete. The link to the online survey was shared on various social media platforms. The survey remained open for six months. Before participating, all participants were required to review and confirm a written consent form. Participants were assured that no personal identifiable information would be collected. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered.

The researchers developed an online self-administered questionnaire to collect data (see Appendix A), and three expert obstetricians validated it. The questionnaire was constructed based on a thorough review of existing literature that addressed pregnant women’s knowledge, attitudes, and perceived barriers regarding prenatal anomaly scans and related diagnostic tools [11, 12, 14–17]. These studies informed the structure and content of the questionnaire, ensuring it was grounded in prior empirical evidence and aligned with validated instruments used in similar settings. The internal consistency analysis showed acceptable reliability for the knowledge domain (Cronbach’s α = 0.78) and good reliability for the attitude domain (Cronbach’s α = 0.81). Each correct knowledge item was awarded one point, while incorrect and “don’t know” responses received no points, yielding a total possible score of 12. Knowledge scores were then dichotomized into “good knowledge” (scores 7–12) and “poor knowledge” (scores 1–6), using a majority correct threshold (> 50% of total items correct). This approach is consistent with scoring methods used in similar studies assessing women’s knowledge of prenatal ultrasound and obstetric procedures [14, 18, 19]. The researchers sent a link to 30 randomly selected pregnant women from their clinics. We used their feedback on clarity, language, and the time required to complete the survey to refine the final version. The results did not include data from this testing phase. Initially, the questionnaire was developed in English and later translated into Arabic by bilingual experts to ensure easy accessibility for all participants. It was divided into three sections. The first section elicited information on the respondents’ demographic characteristics, such as age, level of education, employment status, income, residence, and health insurance. The second part measures the knowledge and attitude about fetal anomaly scans. The third section focused on the barriers to obtaining anomaly scans, which encompassed financial, cultural, and logistical barriers.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Yarmouk University (IRB/2023/15).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) [20]. Descriptive statistics in the form of frequencies, percentages, and means were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine predictors of participants’ knowledge of anomaly scan capabilities. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study population was 468 participants, with the median age distribution being within the range of 25–35 years, constituting 51.5%, followed by those over 35 years, 38.0%. The majority reported no history of miscarriages, 62.0%, and 21.8% had a history of consanguinity. Most participants were nulliparous, 52.6%, with 22.2% having three or more pregnancies. Higher educational attainment was common, with 73.1% holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Employment patterns indicated that 53.0% were unemployed, while 12.0% were employed in healthcare services. Monthly family income was mostly between 500 and 999 JD (39.1%), and the majority were from urban areas (76.7%). The majority were from northern Jordan (54.1%), with 44.2% having government insurance, while 20.5% did not have any health insurance. Only 9.2% had a family history of congenital anomalies. Discussion

The current study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitude, and barriers regarding the use of second-trimester anomaly scan in pregnant Jordanian women, and the predictors of women’s knowledge in anomaly scan capabilities. These aspects are important in understanding the factors that influence women’s utilization of anomaly scans. Fear of fetal abnormalities was the most frequently reported barrier (17.5%), consistent with prior studies highlighting emotional distress which suggest that anxiety surrounding potential negative outcomes can deter women from pursuing necessary medical interventions [11, 21]. Prenatal testing is associated with profound psychological effects on women, many of whom struggle with the implications of abnormal findings, including emotional distress associated with having to make difficult decisions about their pregnancies. This is supported by the results from Moncrieff, Finlayson [22] who also reported documented similar apprehensions among women globally, reflecting emotional distress and fear of adverse outcomes. These fears could increase in contexts where support services or interventions after an abnormal finding are not widely available [23]. A related concern, as Oluoch et al. Oluoch, Mwangome [24] found that the threat of diagnosis and possible implications themselves act to deter women from seeking prenatal screening in resource-poor settings. The religious objections to abortion accounted for 16.6% of the responses in this study, reflecting cultural and ethical considerations that play a major role in decision-making in Jordan. Similarly, Arab and Halawani [25] also indicated that religious and cultural beliefs shape attitudes toward prenatal screening among Muslim women. A notable proportion of women (10.8%) reported believing that no antenatal intervention is possible in the event of fetal congenital anomalies. This perception may stem from a lack of awareness regarding available medical options and support services for managing pregnancies with identified anomalies [11]. The economic factors are another critical barrier, as illustrated by 12.4% of the women indicating high costs as a prohibiting factor in accessing anomaly scans. This agrees with general trends in Jordan, whereby financial constraints have been listed as one of the significant burdens to healthcare utilization in the country [26]. Lack of health insurance coverage was also identified by 4.4% of the participants to worsen the situation. These findings highlight the need for policy interventions to improve access to affordable prenatal care services, especially among low-income populations [27].

The findings of this study regarding women’s knowledge of anomaly scan capabilities indicate that most women demonstrated good knowledge about key functions of anomaly scan, such as its ability to determine gestational age (96.2%), estimate fetal weight (94.4%), check for congenital anomalies (91.0%), and define placental location and amniotic fluid volume (91.0%). Despite 87% of participants being categorized as having ‘good knowledge’ on the scoresdistribution used, interpretation should be made with caution. The scoring threshold (≥ 7 out of 12) was chosen pragmatically, representing majority correct responses but not necessarily indicative of complete or clinically relevant knowledge for an anomaly scan. Several misunderstandings were particularly common among respondents, including incorrect beliefs regarding the ability of scanning to detect cerebral palsy or scanned items.

Beyond the basic distinction of whether a participant was considered to have “good knowledge” of the categorical scores, it was also important to evaluate whether those with scores indicating “good knowledge” may still have misconceptions of clinical understanding of the anomaly scan. Such misconceptions of clinical understanding may lead to the development of unrealistic expectations of the anomaly scan procedure, which may be detrimental to the pregnant woman. Thus, the high proportion of women with “good knowledge” should not be equated with complete comprehension of the clinical scope and limitations of the procedure.

These findings support other studies conducted by Abduljabbar, Jabal [14]and Goley, Sakula-Barry [15] who found that pregnant women always appreciate the diagnostic use of ultrasound in monitoring fetal development and detection of congenital malformations. Interestingly, 95.9% of the participants correctly identified that fetal ultrasound can determine fetal sex, a capability culturally emphasized in Jordan and other regions due to its significance in family planning and societal norms as indicated by Arab and Halawani [25] and Sabharwal and Jamwal [16]. The relatively lower but still substantial agreement for diagnosing multifetal gestation (72.4%) suggests the necessity for a more serious consideration of the role of ultrasound in the management of high-risk pregnancies. The studies by Hanprasertpong, Raungrongmorakot [17] and Oluoch, Mwangome [24] have also pointed out the knowledge gaps concerning advanced prenatal diagnostic capabilities, especially in the low- and middle-income settings. On the other hand, lower levels of agreement were exhibited regarding more refined or less clinically emphasized capacities. Only 25% of women thought that prenatal anomaly scan can diagnose autism and 22.6%, determine fetal eye color. These, however, were misconceived ideas of ultrasound technology’s scope. Additionally, 43.4% thought prenatal anomaly scan could identify fetal facial features in detail, and 53.2% believed it could diagnose cerebral palsy. These findings are consistent with a study conducted by Moncrieff, Finlayson [22], who found that unrealistic expectations about the capability of ultrasound usually arise because of a lack of antenatal education and misinformation. Kelly, Leitao [28] also pointed out similar misconceptions in populations with limited access to detailed counseling about the limitations of prenatal screening technologies. As not all congenital anomalies can be diagnosed by anomaly scan.

In terms of attitudes, the pregnant women’s views on prenatal anomaly scan utilization revealed that most participants (56.0% strongly agreed, 31.8% agreed) considered prenatal anomaly scan to be safe, hence reflecting a belief in the safety of this modality during pregnancy, which is a factor of importance in encouraging the uptake of these vital prenatal tests. This further agrees with literature that identifies the importance of maternal perception of safety with regards to medical procedures as one factor that determines acceptance and utilization of prenatal care [29, 30].While a considerable minority expressed uncertainty about the safety of obstetric ultrasound scanning, this finding still calls for continued education to alleviate residual concerns and misapprehensions. In similar findings, Molla, Mengistu [18] had reported that 30.5% of pregnant women exhibited uncertainty or negative attitudes toward obstetric ultrasound. There were mixed reactions to the statement that utilization of anomaly scan may lead to congenital anomalies: 47.0% were uncertain, 16.6% agreed or strongly agreed, while 36.3% disagreed or strongly disagreed. This could be attributed to inadequate counseling regarding the risks and benefits of anomaly scans. Studies have previously shown that misunderstanding prenatal screening as a concept can lead to apprehension and even rejection of testing. Maniragena, Tumuhimbise [19] highlighted similar concerns, noting that while awareness of ultrasound was high, there were significant knowledge gaps and negative perceptions among pregnant women. Almost half of the respondents supported the inclusion of 4D capabilities in ultrasound equipment, even if anomaly scans can, however, be adequately performed using the standard 2D ultrasound, as it already provides the necessary diagnostic information concerning the evaluation of fetal developments and the detection of structural anomalies [31]. Although participants strongly favor 4D ultrasound for its sharper images, it’s crucial to separate those feelings from what’s clinically required. International guidelines such as those from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) recommend standard 2D ultrasound as sufficient for spotting fetal anomalies during routine second-trimester scans [32, 33]. The preference for 4D imaging may reflect a lack of awareness about the clinical capabilities of 2D scans and suggests the need for patient education to align expectations with evidence-based practice. It is important to emphasize that positive attitudes toward anomaly scans do not necessarily translate into adequate functional knowledge or appropriate health behavior. An attitude represents an overall tendency or evaluation of capabilities and limitations. In our study, although most participants were positive towards the safety and usefulness of the anomaly scan, many had misconceptions about the ability of the scan to diagnose certain diseases. For example, some believed that the anomaly scan could diagnose cerebral palsy and autism. This disparity between positive attitudes and insufficient knowledge has also been observed in the context of prenatal care, wherein women who have expressed very positive attitudes toward the procedure still base their decisions on unrealistic expectations or misinformation [18, 19, 22]. In addition, 46.2% strongly agree and 32.7% agree, reflecting a positive attitude toward advanced imaging technologies. The strong support for advanced technology in anomaly scans, particularly the inclusion of 4D capabilities. means that women want improved imaging techniques that can provide them with more detailed assessments of fetal health. This corresponds to trends in obstetric care where the advancement of ultrasound technology has been shown to improve diagnostic accuracy and maternal satisfaction [34]. Most strongly agreed that the practitioners need to be qualified and undergo advanced training, with 75.9% strongly agreeing and 15.2% in agreement. This finding is supported by recommendations from various studies for specialized training among healthcare providers engaged in prenatal diagnostics [35]. This also indicates how much pregnant women consider the expertise of the health workers carrying out ultrasound scans. For instance, Knowledge and preparedness of midwives regarding antenatal screening and genetic testing, identified by a study conducted by Asafa, Ndikom [36], are of essence in care and therefore require well-trained practitioners.

The current study also identified the predictors of women’s knowledge regarding anomaly scan capabilities. The positive association of the history of anomaly scans with the knowledge level, B = 0.591, p < 0.001, indicates that previous exposures to these procedures enhance the women’s awareness and understanding of their capabilities. This agrees with earlier findings that women who have been exposed to prenatal testing are more likely to be knowledgeable about the implications and benefits of such procedures [30]. Similarly, the strong predictor for family history of congenital anomalies (B = 0.793, p < 0.001) points to the role of shared experiences in influencing knowledge. It may be that women who have a family history of congenital anomalies are more motivated to seek information about prenatal screening and its capabilities and therefore have a higher understanding of the importance of anomaly scans [37]. Furthermore, the analysis also revealed that consanguinity (B = 0.440, p = 0.002) is a significant predictor of knowledge, which is particularly relevant in the Jordanian context, where consanguineous marriages are relatively common. Women in such marriages are likely to be more informed about the risks of the pregnancy regarding congenital anomalies thus creating a need to get informed on the options for prenatal screening available [38]. Conversely, factors including age, gravida, parity, educational level, working status, residency and health insurance status were all established to be insignificant in explaining knowledge on anomaly scan. That means the demographic variables never played such a significant role. Personal experiences and familiar conditions tend to be more central influences in shaping women’s knowledge of prenatal diagnostics. In addition, other factors that have been documented to be correlated with knowledge in the previous literature, such as education level and insurance status, were not significant in the present model. This difference may be attributed to Jordan-specific contextual factors, for example: (1) broad accessibility of informal health information and (2) availability of public antenatal education that minimizes the discrepancy between educational levels. It is also likely that unmeasured factors (e.g., quality of antenatal counseling, healthcare provider communication, individual health literacy) may impact female knowledge but were not included in the present analysis. These attributes may need to be explored in future studies to elucidate the predictors for functional knowledge of anomaly scans.

Such findings highlight the need to examine their implications for patient counseling and clinical decision-making. The findings indicate that while general acceptance of anomaly scans is high, residual uncertainty regarding procedural safety and misconceptions about diagnostic capabilities may have important clinical implications. Positive perceptions alone do not necessarily reflect fully informed understanding. Unrealistic expectations about the ability of anomaly scans to detect all congenital anomalies, or uncertainty regarding their safety, may influence decision-making, increase anxiety, or affect responses to abnormal findings. Therefore, antenatal counseling should not only promote utilization but also explicitly address the diagnostic scope, limitations, and safety profile of anomaly scans to support informed and functionally meaningful decision-making.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study that need to be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design captures a single point in time, which is limiting as far as making inferences regarding the causal relationship between predictors and knowledge levels is concerned. Second, self-reported data on knowledge, attitude, and barriers are likely to face recall bias or social desirability bias, hence affecting the accuracy of responses. Third, this study involved a relatively restrictive setting, being limited to health facilities in Jordan. This might further limit how much generalization is possible from the findings of the present study. In addition, the mixed recruitment strategy using both antenatal clinics and online platforms may have introduced selection bias, particularly toward younger, urban, and more educated women, as reflected in the high proportion of university-educated participants. This may have influenced the representativeness of the sample, and future studies should aim for more balanced recruitment methods to capture underrepresented groups. Future longitudinal design involving more heterogeneous samples can become an advantage for further knowledge of whatever factors influence anomaly scan utilization. Finally, the utilization of an online platform for data collection may result in sampling bias or permitting duplicate responses. To mitigate this, the survey was structured to accept a single submission per device, and participants were explicitly instructed to complete the survey only once. Additionally, the portal was accessible for six months to enhance participation and obtain a more representative sample of pregnant women from diverse regions and clinics in Jordan.

Strengths of the study

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It included a large and diverse sample of pregnant women from various regions in Jordan, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The questionnaire was developed based on a thorough review of the literature and validated by experts in obstetrics, ensuring its content relevance and clarity. Additionally, the study provides novel insight into the predictors of anomaly scan knowledge in a Middle Eastern context, which is underrepresented in the existing literature.

Conclusion

This study offers significant insights into the knowledge, attitudes, and barriers about the use of second-trimester anomaly scan among pregnant women in Jordan. Although the majority of participants had good knowledge and positive attitudes, persistent misconceptions and cultural, socioeconomic, and informational obstacles remain. Individual and familial experiences, including previous anomaly scans and a history of congenital anomalies, were significant predictors of knowledge. These findings underscore the necessity for focused educational and policy initiatives to enhance awareness and accessibility of prenatal diagnostic services. Healthcare providers need to incorporate culturally relevant education and counseling regarding anomaly scan into standard antenatal care, especially for women experiencing their first pregnancy or those with minimal experience with prenatal diagnostics. Policymakers are urged to facilitate equal access by subsidizing anomaly scan expenses. Future studies should investigate longitudinal designs to evaluate changes in knowledge and attitudes over time, as well as assess the efficacy of specific educational programs. Furthermore, qualitative studies may offer profound insights into the cultural and emotional barriers that affect decision-making regarding prenatal screening.
