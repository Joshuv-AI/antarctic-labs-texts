Neural network approaches for variance reduction in fluctuation formulas
G.A. Pavliotis a,, R. Spacek b,, G. Stoltz c,, U. Vaes d,

Abstract
We propose a method utilizing physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to solve Poisson equations that serve as control variates in the computation of transport coefficients via fluctuation formulas, such as the Green–Kubo and generalized Einstein-like formulas. By leveraging approximate solutions to the Poisson equation constructed through neural networks, our approach significantly reduces the variance of the estimator at hand. We provide an extensive numerical analysis of the estimators and detail a methodology for training neural networks to solve these Poisson equations. The approximate solutions are then incorporated into Monte Carlo simulations as effective control variates, demonstrating the suitability of the method for moderately high-dimensional problems where fully deterministic solutions are computationally infeasible.



Keywords.

physics-informed neural networks, control variates, Green–Kubo formula

Introduction

In this paper,
we investigate general control variate strategies to reduce the variance of estimators of
transport coefficients
,
understood in a broad sense as
L^{2}
inner products involving the solution to a Poisson equation,
as made precise in (
TC
) below.

Mathematical framework and objective

To precisely describe the mathematical object that the methods we present aim at approximating,
we consider the following general time-homogeneous stochastic differential equation (SDE) on the state space
\mathcal{X}
(where
\mathcal{X}
is typically
d
or
\mathbf{T}^{d}
, with
\mathbf{T}=\real/\mathbf{Z}
the one-dimensional torus):

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}.

(1.1)

The associated Markov semigroup associated to (
1.1
) is given by

\left\lparen\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\varphi\right\rparen(x)=\mathbf{E}\bigl[\varphi(X_{t})\big|X_{0}=x\bigr].

We denote by
\mathcal{L}
its infinitesimal generator on
L^{2}(\mu)
,
which is given on
C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathcal{X})
by

\mathcal{L}=b^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf{T}}\colon\nabla^{2}.

We assume that the dynamics (
1.1
) admits a unique invariant probability measure
\mu
,
and that the equation (
1.1
) is supplemented with stationarity initial conditions
X_{0}\sim\mu
.
Suppose that
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
and
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
. Our focus in this work is to devise and study computational approaches to approximate quantities of the form

\rho=\langle f,G\rangle,

(TC)

where
G
denotes the solution to the Poisson equation (the well-posedness of such equations will be discussed below)

-\mathcal{L}G=g.

(1.2)

For a more convenient presentation of the results to come, we assume throughout this work that the left-hand side of Poisson equations such as (
1.2
) have average 0 with respect to
\mu
.
This is a necessary condition for the well-posedness of the equation (as the left-hand side integrates to 0 with respect to
\mu
) and can be considered without loss of generality, as one can simply recenter functions with nonzero average by replacing
g
by
g-\mathbf{E}_{\mu}(g)
. In (
TC
) and throughout this work, scalar products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
(and associated norms
\lVert\cdot\rVert
) are taken on
L^{2}(\mu)
, unless otherwise specified.

Solving the partial differential equation (
1.2
) with deterministic methods is not computationally feasible in high dimensions,
and so a number of probabilistic methods have been proposed in the literature to approximate quantities of the form (
TC
),
some of which we discuss in the next paragraph.
In this work,
we present a variance reduction approach based on control variates for a class of statistical estimators for
\rho

relying on the celebrated
Green–Kubo formula
: for two functions
f,g\in L^{2}(\mu)
with average 0 with respect to
\mu
,

\rho=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{0})g(X_{t})\bigr]\,dt.

(1.3)

In the rest of this section,
we first present a number of applications where
the calculation of quantities of the form (
TC
) is important.
We then briefly review the control variate approach to variance reduction,
and finally list the contributions of this work.

Applications

Quantities of the form (

•

In molecular dynamics,

[

.
Examples include the mobility, the heat conductivity and the shear viscosity.
See
[

We emphasize that,

A classical example of this is the mobility in the underdamped regime for two-dimensional Langevin dynamics,
even though the state space is only 4-dimensional;

[

.

•

In statistics,
formulas of the form (

) with

),

More precisely, it can be shown that (see
[

]
)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\,dt\xrightarrow[T\to+\infty]{\rm Law}\mathcal{N}(0,\rho).

Thus, the estimation of
\rho
is useful for quantifying the uncertainty associated with the ergodic estimator.
Early works towards this goal are due to Parzen
[
34
]
and Neave
[
31
]
.
See also
[
29
]
for a more modern reference on the subject.

•

In the theory of homogenization for multiscale stochastic differential equations,
quantities of the form (
TC
) appear in the coefficients of the homogenized equation;
see
[
32
,
33
]
,
as well as
[

for a theoretical overview,
and
[

,
14
,
1
]
for numerical works aimed at the efficient calculation of the coefficients of the homogenized equation.
In this context, the functions
f
and

in (

) and (
1.2
)
are usually different,
which motivates the level of generality we consider.

Using control variates for variance reduction

We now motivate and give the flavor of the methods proposed in this work,
without entering into details.
To this end,
assume that an approximation
\psi_{g}
to the exact solution
G
of (
1.2
) is available
and rewrite

\rho=\langle f,\psi_{g}\rangle+\langle f,G-\psi_{g}\rangle.

(1.4)

The first term on the right-hand side is the average of the known function
f\psi_{g}
with respect to
\mu
.
This term can be calculated efficiently,
with classical Monte Carlo methods sampling from
\mu
.
The second term on the right-hand side of (
1.4
), on the other hand,
still involves an inner product between
f
and the solution to a Poisson equation of the form (
1.2
),
but now with the right-hand side
g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}
instead of
g
.
This term can be estimated numerically based on the Green–Kubo formula (
1.3
):

\langle f,G-\psi_{g}\rangle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{0})(g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g})(X_{t})\bigr]\,dt.

(1.5)

A number of statistical estimators can be employed to approximate the integral on the right-hand side,
but we postpone the details of these estimators to
Section

2
.
The point we want to make here is that,
if
\psi_{g}
is a good approximation of
G
in some sense,
then the function
g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}
is “small”,
in which case it is reasonable to expect estimators for (
1.5
) to have a smaller variance than those for (
1.3
).

The method just described is one of the three control variate approaches presented in
Section

3
.
These are based on different rewritings of (
1.4
),
but they all have in common that they require approximate solutions to Poisson equations of the form (
1.2
).

It may seem contradictory,
at this point,
that the control variate approaches we propose are based on approximate solutions to Poisson equation of the form (
1.2
),
given our earlier claim that solving (
1.2
) deterministically was not computationally feasible in high dimension.
The key to resolve this apparent contradiction is to realize that the control variate approaches described in this work can yield substantial variance reduction even when
\psi_{g}
is a rather poor approximation of
G
,
when compared to the usual performance of traditional numerical methods for low-dimensional PDEs.
Therefore, we argue that using control variates of the type presented in
Section

3
is ideal in moderately high dimensions
– for which solving the Poisson equation (
1.2
) precisely using a deterministic approach is impossible,
but a rough solution can still be calculated,
using for example a neural network.
The applicability of the control variate approach depending on the problem dimension is illustrated in
Table

1.1
.
The methods presented in
Section

3
can be thought of as hybrid deterministic-probabilistic approaches for calculating (
TC
),
where a rough deterministic approximation is corrected by a Monte Carlo simulation.

Our contributions

The main contributions of this paper are the following.

•

We review classical estimators for (
TC
) based on the Green–Kubo formula (
1.3
),
and present rigorous results concerning their bias and variance in the longtime limit.
We focus in the mathematical analysis on estimators based on solutions to the continuous-time dynamics (
1.1
) for simplicity.
However, the approach we describe can be employed in combination with discrete-time approximations of (
1.1
) used in practice.

•

We present and analyze three control variate approaches to reduce the variance of the classical estimators.
The first one,
in the same spirit as in
[
42
,
37
]
,
requires an approximate solution to the Poisson equation (
1.2
).
The second one is a new, computationally more efficient
approach based on the approximate solution not of (
1.2
),
but of another Poisson equation involving
f
and the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
.
Finally, the third approach combines the first two,
and yields the largest variance reduction.

•

We use a neural network approach to numerically approximate solutions to the Poisson equations required in the control variates.

•

We illustrate the efficiency of the proposed control variates approaches by means of careful numerical experiments.
Our experiments demonstrate that,
even when the Poisson equations are not solved accurately,
substantial variance reduction can still be achieved.

2.1
Green–Kubo estimator

To numerically estimate the Green–Kubo formula (
1.3
), one typically approximates the expectation as an average over
K
independent realizations of the reference dynamics (
1.1
) started from i.i.d. initial conditions
X_{0}\sim\mu
. Additionally, the time integral is truncated at some finite integration time
T
. This leads to the estimator

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm GK}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}g(X_{t}^{k})f(X_{0}^{k})\,dt.

(GK)

We study in this section the properties of this estimator. We first state a result which makes precise the time truncation bias of the estimator (
GK
).

Proposition 2.2

(Bounds on bias for the standard Green–Kubo estimator)
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
holds, and that
f,g\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
. Then,

\left\lvert\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm GK}\right\rparen-\rho\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{L}{\lambda}\lVert g\rVert\lVert f\rVert\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}.

(2.4)

This result shows that the bias arising from the time truncation of the integral decays exponentially fast in time. It is a standard result in the literature, see for instance
[
26
,
39
]
.
We nonetheless state it, as it will be used to show some error estimates for the improved estimators in
Section

3
.

Proof.

By writing the expectation in terms of the semigroup operator,
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm GK}\right\rparen-\rho\right|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[g(X_{t})f(X_{0})\right]\,dt-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[g(X_{t})f(X_{0})\right]\,dt\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{T}^{+\infty}\left\lvert\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\right\rvert\,dt.

(2.5)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the decay estimate for the semigroup (
Assumption

1
),
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\right\rvert\leqslant\lVert f\rVert\left\lVert\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}g\right\rVert\leqslant L\lVert g\rVert\lVert f\rVert\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}.

Using the above inequality in (
2.5
) yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm GK}\right\rparen-\rho\right\rvert\leqslant L\lVert g\rVert\lVert f\rVert\int_{T}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\frac{L\lVert g\rVert\lVert f\rVert}{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T},

which is the desired result (
2.4
).
∎

We next make precise the scaling, and exact prefactors, of the asymptotic variance of the estimator (
GK
).

Proposition 2.3

(Bounds on variance for the standard Green–Kubo estimator)
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
holds,
and that
X_{0}\sim\mu
.
Consider
f\in L^{4}_{0}(\mu)
and
g\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
such that
G=-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\in L^{4}(\mu)
and
\chi_{G}\in L^{2}(\mu)
. Then,

\lim_{T\to+\infty}T^{-1}\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm GK}\right\rparen=\frac{2\lVert f\rVert^{2}\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle}{K}.

This result shows that the variance scales linearly in time asymptotically as
T\to\infty
.
In regards to the prefactor,
in view of
Corollary

2.1
and
Assumption

1
it holds that

\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle\leqslant L\lambda^{-1}\lVert g\rVert^{2}.

The integrability condition
G\in L^{4}(\mu)
can be shown to hold through hypercontractivity, for which a sufficient condition is
g\in L^{4}(\mu)
when decay estimates from
Assumption

1
hold; see
Appendix

A
, particularly the discussion around
Assumption

2
. Additionally, the condition
\chi_{G}\in L^{2}(\mu)
holds for instance when the diffusion matrix
\sigma
is bounded and
\nabla G\in L^{4}(\mu)
, which can be shown to hold for Langevin dynamics under some conditions on the drift and right-hand side
g
(see
[
46
,
22
]
).

Proof.

Since
\Var(\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm GK})=K^{-1}\Var(\widehat{\rho}^{\rm GK}_{1,T})
,
it suffices to prove the result for
K=1
,
i.e., to prove that

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\var\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}g(X_{t})f(X_{0})\,dt\right]=2\lVert f\rVert^{2}\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle.

(2.6)

Note first that
G
is the unique solution to the Poisson equation (
1.2
), which is indeed well-posed in view of
Corollary

2.1
.
Applying Itô’s formula to
G(X_{t})
, then multiplying the result by
f(X_{0})/\sqrt{T}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}g(X_{t})f(X_{0})\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{f(X_{0})}{\sqrt{T}}\bigl(G(X_{0})-G(X_{T})\bigr)+\frac{f(X_{0})}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}

\displaystyle:=A_{T}+B_{T}.

(2.7)

The first term on the right-hand side of (
2.7
) converges to 0 in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
as
T\to\infty
since
f,G\in L^{4}(\mu)
.
Applying Itô isometry to the second term gives

\displaystyle\var\left[\frac{f(X_{0})}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[f(X_{0})^{2}\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\sigma(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla G(X_{t})\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\lVert f\rVert^{2}\overline{\chi}_{G}+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[f(X_{0})^{2}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{t})\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\lVert f\rVert^{2}\overline{\chi}_{G}+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle f^{2},\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}\right\rangle\,dt.

(2.8)

The second term in (
2.8
) tends to 0 as
T\to\infty
by the decay of the semigroup, since

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\langle f^{2},\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}\rangle\,dt\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{C\lVert f\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert\Pi\chi_{G}\rVert}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,dt\xrightarrow[T\to+\infty]{}0.

To conclude,
we note from the definition of the variance that

\sqrt{\var[B_{T}]}-\sqrt{\var[A_{T}]}\leqslant\sqrt{\var[A_{T}+B_{T}]}\leqslant\sqrt{\var[B_{T}]}+\sqrt{\var[A_{T}]}.

Taking
T\to+\infty
leads to the desired result (
2.6
)
in view of the expression (
2.2
) for
\chi_{G}
.
∎

2.2
Half-Einstein estimator

In this section, we discuss an Einstein-like estimator of the quantity (
TC
). Einstein formulas allow to rewrite time-integrated correlation functions as

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\,dt\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{0})f(X_{t})\bigr]\,dt.

(2.9)

This equality holds since

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t})\,dt\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{s})f(X_{t})\bigr]\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{0})f(X_{t})\bigr]\left\lparen 1-\frac{t}{T}\right\rparen\,dt,

where we used that
\mathbf{E}[f(X_{s})f(X_{t})]=\mathbf{E}[f(X_{0})f(X_{t-s})]
by stationarity for any
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
,
and where we performed a change of variable in the last step (see
Lemma

B.1
).

Evaluating the left-hand side of (
2.9
) in practice involves two key considerations: (i) approximating the expectation as an average over multiple independent replicas of the system;
and (ii) considering (moderately) long integration times
T
.

When the quantity of interest is not given by autocorrelations as above, one can consider generalized Einstein formulas of the form

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})g(X_{t})\,ds\,dt\right]=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{0})g(X_{t})\bigr]\,dt.

(2.10)

Note that the left-hand side of (
2.10
) corresponds to only half of the double integral in the usual Einstein formula, as the quantity of interest is the one-sided correlation
\mathbf{E}[f(X_{0})g(X_{t})]
.
Thus, given
K
independent replicas of the system with i.i.d. initial conditions
X_{0}\sim\mu
, a natural estimator of (
2.10
) is given by

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s}^{k})g(X_{t}^{k})\,ds\,dt.

(2.11)

In fact, a more general estimator for (
2.10
) than (
2.11
) can be formulated in terms of a weight function
w
.
This gives rise to the following estimator of (
2.10
), realized with
K
independent trajectories of the reference dynamics with i.i.d. initial conditions
X_{0}\sim\mu
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s}^{k})g(X_{t}^{k})\,ds\,dt.

(HE)

We next state a result which makes precise the bias associated with the estimator (
HE
).

Proposition 2.4

(Bounds on bias for the general half-Einstein estimator)
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
holds true, and that
X_{0}\sim\mu
. Consider
f,g\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
and
w\in C^{0}[0,1]
. Then,

\left\lvert\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}\right\rparen-\rho\right\rvert\leqslant L\lVert f\rVert\lVert g\rVert\left\lparen T\left\lvert\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(1-w(u)(1-u)\Bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}\,du\right\rvert+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}}{\lambda}\right\rparen.

(2.12)

The result (
2.12
) suggests that there are two distinct contributions to the bias. The integral term represents the bias due to the weight
w
, whereas the term
\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}
corresponds to the standard time-truncation bias. While it is possible that the first term vanishes for finite
T
with an appropriate choice of
w
, the truncation bias, present on any estimator of (
1.3
), only vanishes in the limit
T\to\infty
.

Remark 2.5

(Consistency conditions on
w
)
.

The condition
w(0)=1
ensures that the estimator (
HE
) is asymptotically unbiased,
since the function
u\mapsto\lambda T\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}
tends to the Dirac measure
\delta_{0}
in the sense of distributions as
T\to\infty
, so that

\displaystyle T\left\lvert\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(1-w(u)(1-u)\Bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}\,du\right\rvert\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\lambda^{-1}|1-w(0)|.

This is a standard consistency condition throughout the literature
[
34
,
31
,
3
]
.

Proof of
Proposition

2.4
.

We use the same reasoning as in
[
37
, Lemma 2.7]
. By stationarity and an appropriate change of variables (see
Lemma

B.1
),
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{0})g(X_{t-s})\bigr]\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}w\left\lparen\frac{\theta}{T}\right\rparen\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{\theta\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\left\lparen T-\theta\right\rparen\,d\theta

\displaystyle=T\int_{0}^{1}w(u)\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}g\rangle(1-u)\,du.

It follows that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}\right\rparen-\rho\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert T\int_{0}^{1}w(u)\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\left\lparen 1-u\right\rparen\,du-T\int_{0}^{+\infty}\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\,du\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert T\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(1-w(u)(1-u)\Bigr)\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\,du+T\int_{1}^{+\infty}\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}g\rangle\,du\right\rvert.

(2.13)

By a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and
Assumption

1
,

\displaystyle|\langle f,\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}\rangle|\leqslant\|f\|\|g\|\left\lVert\mathrm{e}^{uT\mathcal{L}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}_{0}(\mu))}\leqslant L\|f\|\|g\|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}.

Applying this inequality in (
2.13
) gives

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbf{E}\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}\right\rparen-\rho\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant L\|f\|\|g\|\left\lparen\left\lvert T\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(1-w(u)(1-u)\Bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}\,du\right\rvert+T\int_{1}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}\,du\right\rparen

\displaystyle=L\|f\|\|g\|\left\lparen\left\lvert T\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(1-w(u)(1-u)\Bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda Tu}\,du\right\rvert+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}}{\lambda}\right\rparen,

which is the desired result (
2.12
).
∎

Before stating the next result,
recall that
\chi_{F}=2\Gamma(F,F)
,
with
\Gamma
the
carré du champ
operator defined in (
2.1
),
and similarly for
\chi_{G}
.
We now state a result making precise the variance of the generalized half-Einstein estimator (
HE
).

Proposition 2.6

(Bounds on variance for the generalized half-Einstein estimator)
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
holds true, and that
X_{0}\sim\mu
.
Assume that
w\in C^{2}[0,1]
and
w(0)=1
.
Consider
f,g\in L^{4}_{0}(\mu)
such that
\mathcal{L}^{-1}f,\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\in L^{4}(\mu)
and
\chi_{F},\chi_{G}\in L^{4}(\mu)
,
where
F,G
denote the solutions in
L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
to the Poisson equations

-\mathcal{L}F=f,\qquad-\mathcal{L}G=g.

(2.14)

Then, there exists
\mathcal{V}_{f,g}\in\real_{+}
such that

\forall T\geqslant 1,\qquad\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}\right\rparen\leqslant\mathcal{V}_{f,g}.

(2.15)

Moreover, the asymptotic variance can be made precise as

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE}\right\rparen=\frac{4}{K}\left\lparen\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)w(v)^{2}\,dv\right\rparen\langle f,\mathcal{-}\mathcal{L}^{-1}f\rangle\langle g,\mathcal{-}\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle.

(2.16)

Note that the right-hand side of (
2.16
) is symmetric in
f,g
.
In contrast with the variance results for Green–Kubo in
Proposition

2.3
, we require stronger integrability conditions on the solution to the Poisson equations, in particular such that
F,G\in L^{4}(\mu)
, as well as
\chi_{F},\chi_{G}\in L^{4}(\mu)
.
These conditions, however, can also be shown to hold with the techniques mentioned in
Proposition

2.3
, namely hypercontractivity arguments discussed in
Appendix

A
and the framework outlined in
[
46
,
22
]
.
Additionally, note that the asymptotic variance is constant for the half-Einstein estimator (whereas it scales linearly in time for Green–Kubo), which suggests that long-time integration is not a numerical hindrance in this case.

Proof.

The well-posedness of the Poisson equations (
2.14
) is ensured by
Corollary

2.1
.
It suffices to prove the result for
K=1
, since
\Var(\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm HE})=K^{-1}\Var(\widehat{\rho}^{\rm HE}_{1,T})
.
In order to more conveniently state the technical results to come, we write the estimator (
HE
) for
K=1
as

\widehat{\rho}_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}I_{t}\,g(X_{t})\,dt,\qquad I_{t}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s})\,ds.

(2.17)

The idea of the proof is to write
\widehat{\rho}_{T}
as the sum of a Brownian martingale and perturbation terms,
followed by showing the
L^{2}(\mu)
convergence to 0 of each of the perturbation terms.
To this end, let
\xi_{t}=G(X_{t})I_{t}
. By Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle dG(X_{t})

\displaystyle=-g(X_{t})\,dt+\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t},

\displaystyle dI_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{w(0)}{T}f(X_{t})+\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s})\,ds\right)dt.

Since
d\xi_{t}=d\bigl(G(X_{t})I_{t}\bigr)=I_{t}dG(X_{t})+G(X_{t})dI_{t}
, it follows that

\displaystyle d\xi_{t}

\displaystyle=-g(X_{t})I_{t}\,dt+\frac{1}{T}G(X_{t})\left\lparen w(0)f(X_{t})+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s})\,ds\right\rparen\,dt

(2.18)

\displaystyle+I_{t}\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}.

Integrating (
2.18
) between
t=0
and
t=T
allows us to write
\widehat{\rho}_{T}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{T}

\displaystyle=-G(X_{T})I_{T}+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left\lparen w(0)f(X_{t})+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s})\,ds\right\rparen\,dt

(2.19)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}I_{t}\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}.

At this stage, we would like to replace
I_{t}
by some Brownian martingale (and boundary terms).
To this end,
applying Itô’s formula to
w((t-s)/T)F(X_{s})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle d\biggl[w\biggl(\frac{t-s}{T}\biggr)F(X_{s})\biggr]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{T}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds-w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s})\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s},

which, after integration in time from
s=0
to
s=t
, allows us to write
I_{t}
as

\displaystyle I_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\left[w\left\lparen\frac{t}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{0})-w(0)F(X_{t})\right]-\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds

(2.20)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}.

Substituting the above equality into (
2.19
) allows us to write
\widehat{\rho}_{T}
as the desired sum of a martingale and remainder terms, namely

\widehat{\rho}_{T}=\frac{A_{T}}{T}+\frac{M_{T}}{T}+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T,1}}{T}+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T,2}}{T^{2}},

where
A_{T}/T
denotes a term which will converge to
\rho
:

A_{T}=w(0)\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})f(X_{t})\,dt,

the martingale
M_{T}/T
is the dominant term among the other terms:

M_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}\right\rparen\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t},

and
\mathcal{R}_{T,1}/T
and
\mathcal{R}_{T,2}/T^{2}
are remainder terms that
we group according to the power of
1/T
that they multiply:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{T,1}&=G(X_{T})\bigl(w(0)F(X_{T})-w(1)F(X_{0})\bigr)\\
&\qquad-G(X_{T})\int_{0}^{T}w\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}\\
&\qquad-\int_{0}^{T}\left[w(0)F(X_{t})-w\left\lparen\frac{t}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{0})\right]\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}\end{split}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(1)}+\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(2)}+\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(3)},

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{T,2}&=G(X_{T})\int_{0}^{T}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\biggl(\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s})\,ds\biggr)\,dt\\
&\qquad-\int_{0}^{T}\biggl(\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\biggr)\nabla G(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{t})\,dW_{t}\end{split}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(1)}+\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(2)}+\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(3)}.

By the triangle inequality,

\begin{split}&\left\lvert\mathbf{E}\left[\Bigl\lvert\widehat{\rho}_{T}-\mathbf{E}[\widehat{\rho}_{T}]\Bigr\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{M_{T}}{T}\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rvert\\
&\qquad\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T,1}}{T}\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T,2}}{T^{2}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{A_{T}}{T}-\mathbf{E}[\widehat{\rho}_{T}]\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

(2.21)

Note that if the right-hand side of (
2.21
) vanishes as
T\to\infty
,
and if the variance of
M_{T}/T
admits a limit as
T\to+\infty
,
then

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert\widehat{\rho}_{T}-\mathbf{E}[\widehat{\rho}_{T}]\bigr\rvert^{2}\Bigr]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\left|\frac{M_{T}}{T}\right|^{2}\right].

The claimed result (
2.16
) follows by showing the
L^{2}
convergence of each term in (
2.21
), namely

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{A_{T}}{T}-\mathbf{E}[\widehat{\rho}_{T}]\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\left|\frac{M_{T}}{T}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=4\left\lparen\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)w(v)^{2}\,dv\right\rparen\langle f,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f\rangle\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle,

(2.22a)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\left|\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T,1}}{T}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\left|\frac{\mathcal{R}_{T,2}}{T^{2}}\right|^{2}\right]=0.

(2.22b)

We successively prove the various limits in the order they appear above.

Convergence of
A_{T}/T

Note first that
\mathbf{E}\left[f(X_{t})G(X_{t})\right]=\rho
.
Since
w(0)=1
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{A_{T}}{T}-\rho\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\Bigl(f(X_{s})G(X_{s})-\rho\Bigr)\Bigl(f(X_{t})G(X_{t})-\rho\Bigr)\right]\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}(T-\theta)\mathbf{E}\left[\Bigl(f(X_{0})G(X_{0})-\rho\Bigr)\Bigl(f(X_{\theta})G(X_{\theta})-\rho\Bigr)\right]\,d\theta.

Using the decay of the semigroup in
Assumption

1
, and noting that
fG-\rho=\Pi(fG)\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
,
we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{A_{T}}{T}-\rho\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}(T-\theta)\Bigl\langle fG-\rho,\mathrm{e}^{\theta\mathcal{L}}(fG-\rho)\Bigr\rangle\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\lVert fG-\rho\rVert^{2}}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}L\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\theta}\,d\theta\xrightarrow[T\to+\infty]{}0.

Convergence of the dominant term
M_{T}/T

We next show the convergence (
2.22a
) of the dominant term
M_{T}/T
. To this end, we introduce

N_{S}:=\int_{0}^{S}w\left\lparen\frac{S-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}.

Recall the definition of
\chi_{G}

By Itô isometry, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\frac{M_{T}}{T}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[N_{t}^{2}\chi_{G}(X_{t})\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\chi}_{G}}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[N_{t}^{2}\right]\,dt+\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[N_{t}^{2}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{t})\right]\,dt.

(2.23)

We also recall that
\overline{\chi}_{G}=2\langle g,\mathcal{-}\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle
from (
2.2
),
and note that
\mathbf{E}[\chi_{G}(X_{t})]=\overline{\chi}_{G}
since by assumption
X_{0}\sim\mu
.
The first term in (
2.23
) is then equal to the right-hand side of (
2.16
) since,
by Itô isometry, stationarity and an appropriate change of variable (see
Corollary

B.2
for details),

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{\chi}_{G}}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[N_{t}^{2}\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\chi}_{G}}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[\chi_{F}]w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen^{2}\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=4\langle f,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f\rangle\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen^{2}\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=4\langle f,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f\rangle\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)w(v)^{2}\,dv.

It remains to show that the second term in (
2.23
) converges to 0 as
T\to+\infty
. Letting
z=t/T
, this term can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{E}\left[N_{zT}^{2}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{zT})\right]\,dz=\int_{0}^{1}z\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{zT}^{2}}{zT}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{zT})\right]\,dz.

In order to show that the integral above vanishes, we first show that its integrand vanishes, i.e.,

\lim_{S\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{S}^{2}}{S}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})\right]=0.

(2.24)

To this end, to make use of the exponential decay of the semigroup, we rewrite

\displaystyle\frac{N_{S}^{2}}{S}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})+\left(\frac{N_{S}^{2}}{S}-\frac{N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S}\right)\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S}).

(2.25)

For the first term, by the Cauchy–Schwarz and Burkholder–Davis–Gundy (BDG) inequalities (with BDG constant
C_{4}
),
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S}\left\lparen\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{S}\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}\right\rparen(X_{S-\sqrt{S}})\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{4}}{S^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{S}\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S-\sqrt{S}})\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{C_{4}}\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle N\right\rangle_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{S}\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S-\sqrt{S}})\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

which vanishes as
S\to\infty
, as the first term is uniformly bounded for
S\geqslant 1
since
\chi_{F}\in L^{2}(\mu)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\left\langle N\right\rangle_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{S-\sqrt{S}}w\left\lparen\frac{S-\sqrt{S}-s}{T}\right\rparen^{2}\chi_{F}(X_{s})\,ds\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S-\sqrt{S}}w\left\lparen\frac{S-\sqrt{S}-s}{T}\right\rparen^{4}\chi_{F}(X_{s})^{2}\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lVert w\rVert_{C^{0}}^{4}}{S}\int_{0}^{S-\sqrt{S}}\mathbf{E}\left[\chi_{F}(X_{s})^{2}\right]\,ds\leqslant\lVert w\rVert_{C^{0}}^{4}\lVert\chi_{F}\rVert^{2};

while the second term tends to 0 by the decay of the semigroup and stationarity
since
\chi_{G}\in L^{2}(\mu)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{S}\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S-\sqrt{S}})\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left\lVert\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{S}\mathcal{L}}\Pi\chi_{G}\right\rVert\leqslant L\lVert\Pi\chi_{G}\rVert\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\sqrt{S}}\xrightarrow[S\to\infty]{}0.

For the second term in (
2.25
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\frac{N_{S}^{2}}{S}-\frac{N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}}{S}\right)\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{S}-N_{S-\sqrt{S}}}{\sqrt{S}}\frac{N_{S}+N_{S-\sqrt{S}}}{\sqrt{S}}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\left\lvert N_{S}-N_{S-\sqrt{S}}\right\rvert^{2}}{S}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\left\lvert(N_{S}+N_{S-\sqrt{S}})\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})\right\rvert^{2}}{S}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.26)

To show that (
2.26
) vanishes, we show that the first term vanishes, while the second one is bounded uniformly in
S
.
We start by considering the first term:

\displaystyle N_{S}-N_{S-\sqrt{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{S}w\left\lparen\frac{S-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}

(2.27)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{S-\sqrt{S}}\left[w\left\lparen\frac{S-s}{T}\right\rparen-w\left\lparen\frac{S-\sqrt{S}-s}{T}\right\rparen\right]\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}.

The first right-hand side term in (
2.27
) is
\mathrm{O}(\sqrt{S})
in
L^{2}
by Itô isometry and the fact that
w
is uniformly bounded, while the second term is
\mathrm{O}(1)
since
w
is Lipschitz (with Lipschitz constant
\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[|N_{S}-N_{S-\sqrt{S}}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{S}w\left\lparen\frac{S-s}{T}\right\rparen^{2}\chi_{F}(X_{s})\,ds\right]+\frac{2S\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}}{T^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S-\sqrt{S}}\chi_{F}(X_{s})\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sqrt{S}\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{C^{0}}\overline{\chi}_{F}+2\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\overline{\chi}_{F}.

Therefore, the first term in (
2.26
) is of order
S^{-\frac{1}{4}}
(and uniformly bounded for
S\geqslant 1
).
We now show that the second term in (
2.26
) is uniformly bounded.
Indeed, by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz and BDG inequalities, and since
\chi_{F},\chi_{G}\in L^{4}(\mu)
and
w
is uniformly bounded,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert(N_{S}+N_{S-\sqrt{S}})\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})\right\rvert^{2}\right]\leqslant 2\mathbf{E}\left[N_{S}^{2}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})^{2}\right]+2\mathbf{E}\left[N_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sqrt{C_{4}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\langle N\right\rangle_{S}^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{s})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\sqrt{C_{4}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\langle N\right\rangle_{S-\sqrt{S}}^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{S})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\sqrt{C_{4}}S\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{C^{0}}\lVert\chi_{F}\rVert\lVert\Pi\chi_{G}\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2},

so we deduce that (
2.26
) is uniformly bounded in
S
and tends to 0 as
S\to+\infty
, and thus (
2.24
) holds. The functions appearing in the integral are uniformly bounded, hence uniformly integrable. Using dominated convergence, we conclude that

\int_{0}^{1}z\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{N_{zT}^{2}}{zT}\Pi\chi_{G}(X_{zT})\right]\,dz\xrightarrow[T\to+\infty]{}0.

This proves (
2.22a
).

Convergence of remainder terms
\mathcal{R}_{T,1}/T
and
\mathcal{R}_{T,2}/T^{2}

In order to conclude the desired result (
2.16
), it remains to prove (
2.22b
), i.e., that the remainder terms
\mathcal{R}_{T,1}/T
and
\mathcal{R}_{T,2}/T^{2}
vanish in squared expectation as
T\to+\infty
.
Let us start with
\mathcal{R}_{T,1}
.
We show that for each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,
the quantity
\mathbf{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(i)}|^{2}]/T
is uniformly bounded in
T
:

•

\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(1)}
:
Since
G,F\in L^{4}(\mu)
and
w
is uniformly bounded, it trivially holds that

\mathbf{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(1)}|^{2}\right]=\mathrm{O}(1).

•

\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(2)}
:
Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz and BDG inequalities (and since
G\in L^{4}(\mu),\chi_{F}\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
w
is uniformly bounded), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(2)}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{T})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}w\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}\right\rvert^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{C_{4}}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}w\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen^{2}\chi_{F}(X_{s})\,ds\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{C_{4}}\sqrt{T}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}w\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen^{4}\chi_{F}(X_{s})^{2}\,ds\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{C_{4}}T\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{C^{0}}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert\chi_{F}\rVert.

•

\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(3)}
: By the Itô isometry and a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, since
F\in L^{4}(\mu),\chi_{G}\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
w
is uniformly bounded, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{T,1}^{(3)}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[F(X_{t})^{2}\chi_{G}(X_{t})\right]\,dt+2\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[w\left\lparen\frac{t}{T}\right\rparen^{2}F(X_{0})^{2}\chi_{G}(X_{t})\right]\,dt

\displaystyle\leqslant 2T\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert+2T\lVert w\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert.

Thus, we conclude that
\mathbf{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{T,1}|^{2}]=\mathrm{O}(T)
as desired.
Let us now consider
\mathcal{R}_{T,2}
.
We have

•

\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(1)}
: By Cauchy–Schwarz and the fact that
w^{\prime}
is uniformly bounded and
F,G\in L^{4}(\mu)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(1)}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{T})^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{T}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{T})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{T-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right\rvert^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant T^{2}\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert^{2}_{C^{0}}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}.

•

\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(2)}
: By the same reasoning we used to write (
2.20
),
we apply Itô’s formula to
w^{\prime}((t-s)/T)F(X_{s})
to rewrite
\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(2)}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left(w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{0})-w^{\prime}(0)F(X_{t})\right)dt

(2.28)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right\rparen\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left(\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}\right)dt.

The expectation of the square of the first two terms in (
2.28
) are easily shown to be
\mathrm{O}(T^{2})
by a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, since
w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}
are uniformly bounded and
F,G\in L^{4}(\mu)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left\lparen w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{0})-w^{\prime}(0)F(X_{t})\right\rparen\,dt\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2T\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\left\lparen\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{2}F(X_{0})^{2}\right]\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{2}F(X_{t})^{2}\right]\,dt\right\rparen

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 4T^{2}\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right\rparen dt\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right\rparen^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{\lVert w^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}}{T}\int_{0}^{T}t^{2}\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt=\frac{T^{2}\lVert w^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}}{3}.

For the third term, since
\chi_{F}\in L^{4}(\mu)
, applying Cauchy–Schwarz then BDG gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}G(X_{t})\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}\right\rparen\,dt\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant T\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\nabla F(X_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}\right\rvert^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant T\sqrt{C_{4}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen^{2}\chi_{F}(X_{s})\,ds\right\rvert^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant T\sqrt{C_{4}}\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert\chi_{F}\rVert\int_{0}^{T}t\mathbf{E}\left[G(X_{t})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt=\frac{T^{3}\sqrt{C_{4}}}{2}\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert\chi_{F}\rVert\lVert G\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2},

so it holds that
\mathbf{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(2)}|^{2}]=\mathrm{O}(T^{3})
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(3)}
: By Itô isometry, a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the fact that
w^{\prime}
is uniformly bounded, a Hölder inequality and
F\in L^{4}(\mu),\chi_{G}\in L^{2}(\mu)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{T,2}^{(3)}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lparen\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right\rparen^{2}\chi_{G}(X_{t})\,dt\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}w^{\prime}\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen F(X_{s})\,ds\right\rvert^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lvert F(X_{s})\right\rvert\,ds\right)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{E}\left[t^{3}\int_{0}^{t}|F(X_{s})|^{4}\,ds\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}t^{2}\,dt=\frac{T^{3}}{3}\lVert\chi_{G}\rVert\lVert w^{\prime}\rVert_{C^{0}}^{2}\lVert F\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}.

Thus, we deduce that
\mathbf{E}[|\mathcal{R}_{T,2}|^{2}]/T^{4}
tends to 0 as
T\to\infty
(which allows to conclude that the right-hand side of (
2.21
) converges to 0),
and in particular that it is uniformly bounded in
T
, which gives (
2.15
).
This concludes the proof.
∎

3.1
Forward control variate

A simple calculation shows that

\rho=\langle f,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle=\langle f,\psi_{g}\rangle+\langle f,G-\psi_{g}\rangle.

(3.3)

The first term on the right-hand side of (
3.3
),
henceforth called the
static
term,
corresponds to an approximation of
\rho
obtained from the approximate solution
\psi_{g}
to the Poisson equation,
and can be computed by resorting to standard Monte Carlo algorithms sampling
\mu
.
The second term is a correction term, which can be approximated using the estimators presented in
Section

2
.
We next discuss estimators for this term and state some technical results which make precise the associated variances.

Static part

In low dimensions,
the average
\langle f,\psi_{g}\rangle
can be estimated via deterministic methods or classical i.i.d. Monte Carlo sampling.
When this is not possible, a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach can be employed:

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm static}(f,\psi_{g})=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{t}^{k})\psi_{g}(X_{t}^{k})\,dt.

In any case, the approximation of
\langle f,\psi_{g}\rangle
is much simpler than expressions involving correlations, and various variance reduction methods can be used; see
[
27
, Section 3.4]
.

Green–Kubo

One possible estimator for the correction term in (
3.3
) is given by the usual Green–Kubo estimator (
GK
),
which gives:

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{g}}^{\rm corr,GK}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}f(X^{k}_{0})\Bigl(g(X^{k}_{t})+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}(X_{t}^{k})\Bigr)\,dt.

(3.4)

We next state a result on the variance of the improved estimator (
3.4
), a straightforward result directly obtained from
Proposition

2.3
by replacing
g
with
g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}
.

Corollary 3.1

(Variance of forward improved GK estimator)
.

Assume that
f,g
satisfy the conditions given in
Proposition

2.3
, and that
\psi_{g}\in L^{4}(\mu)
with
\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
and
\chi_{\psi_{g}}\in L^{2}(\mu)
. Then,

\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{g}}^{\rm corr,GK}\right\rparen=\frac{2}{K}\lVert f\rVert^{2}\Big\langle g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g},-\mathcal{L}^{-1}(g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g})\Big\rangle.

Recall from the discussion after
Proposition

2.3

that bounds on the resolvent are typically of order
\lambda^{-1}
where
\lambda
is the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{L}
. Thus, the
g
term in the expression for the asymptotic variance is of order
\lambda^{-1}\lVert g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}\rVert^{2}
.
Corollary

3.1
thus suggests that the improved estimator is numerically useful when
\lVert g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}\rVert\ll\lVert g\rVert
, namely when the residual associated with the approximate solution of the Poisson equation is small enough.

Half-Einstein

Another possible estimator for the correction term in (
3.3
) is the half-Einstein estimator (
HE
):

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{g}}^{\rm corr,HE}=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen f(X^{k}_{s})\Bigl(g(X^{k}_{t})+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}(X_{t}^{k})\Bigr)\,ds\,dt.

(3.5)

The variance of (
3.5
) is made precise in the following corollary of
Proposition

2.6
.

Corollary 3.2

(Variance of forward improved HE estimator)
.

Assume that
f,g
satisfy the conditions given in
Proposition

2.6
, and that
\psi_{g}\in L^{4}(\mu)
with
\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}\in L^{4}(\mu)
and
\chi_{\psi_{g}}\in L^{4}(\mu)
. Then,

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{g}}^{\rm corr,HE}\right\rparen=\frac{4\zeta_{w}}{K}\langle f,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f\rangle\langle g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g},-\mathcal{L}^{-1}(g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g})\rangle.

Computing either (
3.4
) or (
3.5
) requires evaluating the control variate
g+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}
at every step of the Monte Carlo simulation, which may be an expensive computation.
In the next section,
we present a control variate approach with a lower computational cost,
as it does not require evaluation at all steps.

3.2
Adjoint control variate

It is possible to devise a control variate which need not be evaluated at all steps of the Monte Carlo run.
In particular, we construct a
near-zero-cost
control variate (i.e., zero-cost aside from the computation of the approximate solution to the Poisson equation), as it is only evaluated once at initial time.
To this end, we first write (
3.1
) in terms of the adjoint:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\langle(-\mathcal{L}^{-1})^{*}f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{f}^{*},g\rangle+\langle F^{*}-\psi_{f}^{*},g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{f}^{*},g\rangle+\langle f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*},-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle.

(3.6)

As in the previous section,
the first term on the right-hand side is a static term that can be estimated with standard trajectory averages as

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T}^{\rm static}(g,\psi_{f}^{*})=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}g(X_{t}^{k})\psi_{f}^{*}(X_{t}^{k})\,dt,

(3.7)

while the second term can be approximated using the estimators presented in
Section

2
, as made precise below.

Green–Kubo

The second term in (
3.6
) assumes the following estimator

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{f}}^{\rm corr,GK}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lparen f(X^{k}_{0})+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}(X_{0}^{k})\right\rparen g(X^{k}_{t})\,dt.

(3.8)

Note that this estimator requires the computation of the control variate
f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}
only at the initial time,
eliminating the need to evaluate the control variate at every step of the Monte Carlo simulation.

Corollary 3.3

(Variance of adjoint improved GK estimator)
.

Assume that
f,g
satisfy the conditions given in
Proposition

2.3
, and that
\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\in L^{4}(\mu)
. Then,

\lim_{T\to+\infty}T^{-1}\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{f}}^{\rm corr,GK}\right\rparen=\frac{2}{K}\lVert f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\rVert^{2}\big\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\big\rangle.

Note that
\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}
has average 0 with respect to
\mu
:

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi^{*}_{f}\,d\mu=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi^{*}_{f}(\mathcal{L}\mathbf{1})\,d\mu=0,

so that the condition
\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\in L^{4}_{0}(\mu)
from
Proposition

2.3
reduces to
\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\in L^{4}(\mu)
.

Half-Einstein

The second term in (
3.6
) assumes the following estimator

\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{f}}^{\rm corr,HE}=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left(\frac{t-s}{T}\right)\Bigl(f\bigl(X^{k}_{s}\bigr)+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\bigl(X_{s}^{k}\bigr)\Bigr)g\bigl(X^{k}_{t}\bigr)\,ds\,dt.

(3.9)

Corollary 3.4

(Variance of adjoint improved HE estimator)
.

Assume that
f,g
satisfy the conditions given in
Proposition

2.6
, and that
\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\in L^{4}(\mu)
,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}\in L^{4}(\mu)
and
\chi_{\mathcal{L}^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}}\in L^{4}(\mu)
. Then,

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\Var\left\lparen\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{f}}^{\rm corr,HE}\right\rparen=\frac{4\zeta_{w}}{K}\Big\langle f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*},-\mathcal{L}^{-1}(f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*})\Big\rangle\Big\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\Big\rangle.

3.3
Combining both control variates

By obtaining approximate solutions to both Poisson equations, we can apply both control variates simultaneously.
By writing
-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g=\psi_{g}+(G-\psi_{g})
in (
3.6
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{f}^{*},g\rangle+\langle f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*},G\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{f}^{*},g\rangle+\langle f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*},\psi_{g}\rangle+\langle f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*},G-\psi_{g}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{f}^{*},g\rangle+\langle f,\psi_{g}\rangle+\langle\psi_{f}^{*},\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}\rangle+\langle f+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*},G-\psi_{g}\rangle.

The first three terms can be estimated directly using trajectory averages,
while the last term can be approximated using the estimators in
Section

2
.

The GK improved estimator reads in this context

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{f},\psi_{g}}^{\rm corr,GK}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(f(X^{k}_{0})+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}(X_{0}^{k})\Bigr)\Bigl(g(X^{k}_{t})+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}(X_{t}^{k})\Bigr)\,dt.

(3.10)

Corollary 3.5

(Variance of combined improved GK estimator)
.

Assume that

The HE improved estimator reads

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{K,T,\psi_{f},\psi_{g}}^{\rm corr,HE}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}w\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\Bigl(f(X^{k}_{s})+\mathcal{L}^{*}\psi_{f}^{*}(X_{s}^{k})\Bigr)\Bigl(g(X^{k}_{t})+\mathcal{L}\psi_{g}(X_{t}^{k})\Bigr)\,ds\,dt.

.

4
Applications

The aim of this section is to apply the control variate methods discussed in
Section

3
to different systems in order to assess the effectiveness of the various estimators. We start by discussing in
Subsection

4.1
the general approach for solving Poisson equations using neural networks. We then apply the method to the underdamped Langevin dynamics in
Subsection

4.2
, and to multiscale SDEs in
Subsection

4.3
.

4.1
Approximate solutions to Poisson equations with neural networks

We now discuss the overall strategy for approximating the solution to Poisson equations using PINNs. In particular, we discuss the loss function used and its discretization, and outline the training strategy common to all examples in this section. More specific architectural choices, such as activation functions, featurization strategies and network topology are overviewed in the respective numerical application sections to come.

Loss function

Let us consider the following Poisson equation on
\mathcal{X}
:

-\mathcal{L}\phi=f,

with
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\real
. We denote by
\phi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\theta}(x)
the neural network approximation of
\phi
parametrized by a set of parameters
\theta
. The pointwise loss function we consider is the mean squared error of the residual
r_{\theta}(x)=\mathcal{L}\phi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\theta}(x)+f(x)
. At the continuous level, the loss function is then

\mathscr{L}(\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\mathcal{L}\phi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\theta}(x)+f(x)\right)^{2}\mu(dx).

(4.1)

In practice, the integral in (
4.1
) is approximated with a sum over the data points
\{\mathbf{x}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\in\real^{d}
at which the functions are evaluated. For the situations we consider, the points are directly sampled from
\mu
via rejection sampling, or by drawing random variables according to Gaussian distributions. Additionally, we consider centered finite differences to approximate differential operators in
\mathcal{L}
acting on
\phi
, as automatic differentiation becomes too cumbersome for derivatives of order 3 and higher. Thus, automatic differentiation is only used to take the gradient of the network with respect to the parameters during training.
The finite difference discretizations are implemented with optimal stepsize (see
[
44
]
), namely
\sqrt[3]{\epsilon_{\rm mach}}
for central first derivative approximations, and
\sqrt[4]{\epsilon_{\rm mach}}
for central second derivative approximations, where
\epsilon_{\rm mach}
denotes the machine epsilon.
To this end, let
\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}
denote the discretization of
\mathcal{L}
. Then, our loss function is of the form

\mathscr{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}\phi^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{n})+f(\mathbf{x}^{n})\rvert^{2}.

A similar loss is considered to solve the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}^{*}\phi^{*}=f
.

Training strategy

The same overall strategy was used to train the networks for both examples here presented.
The computational cost associated with sampling the data points is negligible, allowing us to compute a stochastic approximation of the gradient with a batch of
N
new points at every step. Since the usual overfitting risks associated with a finite training set therefore do not apply, we performed a fixed number of training steps and did not implement early stopping. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer
[
20
]
.

The network topologies considered were simple networks with two dense hidden layers, and the same activation function throughout, in particular
\tanh
for both examples. Other activation functions were explored, e.g., regularized ReLU-type functions such as GELU
[
17
]
were observed to perform marginally better than
\tanh
, however at an increased computational cost. The addition of the second hidden layer significantly improves the quality of training, while still retaining a sufficiently low cost. Based on numerical observations from experimental runs, width has a more significant impact on the quality of training than depth for a fixed number of network parameters.

All training parameters are made precise in
Table

4.1
for the Langevin dynamics and multiscale dynamics examples.
Let us emphasize that a perfect training is anyway not necessary, as approximate solutions to the Poisson equation at hand are sufficient to obtain variance reduction.

4.2
Application to the underdamped Langevin dynamics

We apply in this section the methodology to compute the mobility of the two-dimensional underdamped Langevin dynamics on the torus. We start by giving a brief description of the system in
Subsection

4.2.1
, followed by a presentation of the numerical results in
Subsection

4.2.2
.

4.2.1
Description of the system

Let us briefly describe the system under consideration. The two-dimensional Langevin dynamics evolves the positions
q=(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbf{T}^{2}
and momenta
p=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\real^{2}
(so that
\mathcal{X}=\mathbf{T}^{2}\times\real^{2}
) according to the following SDE:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}dq_{t}=M^{-1}p_{t}\,dt,\\
dp_{t}=-\nabla V(q_{t})\,dt-\gamma M^{-1}p_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{\dfrac{2\gamma}{\beta}}\,dW_{t},\end{cases}\end{split}

(4.2)

where
M\in\real^{2\times 2}
denotes the mass matrix,
\gamma>0
the friction coefficient,
\beta>0
the inverse temperature and
V
a smooth periodic potential energy function. The generator of the process (
4.2
) is

\mathcal{L}=p^{\mathsf{T}}M^{-1}\nabla_{q}-\nabla V^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{p}+\gamma(-p^{\mathsf{T}}M^{-1}\nabla_{p}+\beta^{-1}\Delta_{p}).

(4.3)

The dynamics (
4.2
) is ergodic with respect to the Boltzmann–Gibbs probability measure:

\mu(dq\,dp)=\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H(q,p)}\,dq\,dp,\qquad Z=\int_{\mathbf{T}^{2}\times\real^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H(q,p)}dq\,dp.

The transport coefficient of interest is the mobility in the direction
\mathbf{e}
. It is defined as the proportionality constant between the magnitude
\eta
of an exerted constant force proportional to
\mathbf{e}
, and the induced drift velocity
\mathbf{E}(f_{\mathbf{e}})
in this direction, with
f_{\mathbf{e}}=\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}M^{-1}p
. The relationship is linear for
\eta\to 0
, as dictated by linear response theory; see for instance
[
11
, Chapter 8]
and
[
27
, Section 5]
. Through an equilibrium formulation of the linear response based on velocity autocorrelations
[
40
]
, it can in particular be written in terms of the solution to a Poisson equation:

\rho=\beta\langle\phi,f_{\mathbf{e}}\rangle,\qquad-\mathcal{L}\phi(q,p)=f_{\mathbf{e}}(q,p).

Note that the mobility is proportional up to factor
\beta
to the self-diffusion coefficient through Einstein’s relation; see
[
41
,
24
]
.
The points
\{\mathbf{x}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}=(q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2})_{n}\in\real^{4}
are i.i.d. according to the target measure
\mu
, sampled in practice using rejection sampling for the positions, and by sampling a Gaussian distribution for the momenta.

4.2.2
Numerical results

We consider the following nonseperable potential energy function:

V(q_{1},q_{2})=-\frac{\cos(2q_{1})+\cos(q_{2})}{2}-\delta\cos(q_{1})\cos(q_{2}),

with
\delta\in\real
the degree of nonseparability. The numerical results presented here correspond to the value
\delta=0.5
. Additionally, we set the physical parameters
\beta=\gamma=1
and
M=\Id_{2}
.

Structure of the network

Aside from the general training strategy outlined in
Subsection

4.1
, the network considered in this example consists of two dense layers with intermediate dimension 15, with four input nodes and one output. Additionally, we employ a transformation on the inputs in the form of a functional “featurization layer”: the input
(q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2})
is transformed into a 7-node output by the functions

\mathrm{featurization}\left\lparen q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2}\right\rparen=\left\lparen\sin(q_{1}),\cos(q_{1}),\sin(q_{2}),\cos(q_{2}),p_{1},p_{2},\frac{p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}}{2}\right\rparen,

(4.4)

which is then fed to the first trainable layer; see
Appendix

D
for a discussion on the featurization and
Fig.

D.1
for an illustration of the network topology. The periodic nature of the positions is encoded by the sines and cosines in the featurization layer, while the momenta are directly included. Lastly, the kinetic energy term was observed to be a meaningful feature in improving training, and is thus considered.

Monte–Carlo simulations

To numerically integrate (
4.2
), we used the BAOAB splitting scheme
[
25
]
, with an integration time of
T=5
for the Green–Kubo estimator and
T=10
for the half-Einstein estimator, with timestep
\Delta t=0.01
for both. We ran
K=1000
independent realizations of the dynamics in order to empirically estimate the variance. Initial conditions for each realization were also sampled from
\mu
using rejection sampling for the positions.

4.3
Application to multiscale SDEs

The aim of this subsection is to present an application of the control variate approaches studied in
Section

3
,
with slight adjustments,
to fast/slow systems of stochastic differential equations.
We first describe briefly,
in
Subsection

4.3.1
,
the method of homogenization for multiscale SDEs.
Then, in
Subsection

4.3.2
,
we explain how the control variate approaches from
Section

3
can be applied for the calculation of the effective coefficients of the homogenized equation.
Finally, in
Subsection

4.3.3
,
we illustrate the performance of the method on a concrete example.

4.3.1
Homogenization for multiscale SDEs

In this section,
we consider the following multiscale system with state space
\real{}^{d_{x}}\times\real^{d_{y}}
,
for some small parameter
\varepsilon>0
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,dt,\\
dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}g(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,dt+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\alpha(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,dW_{t},\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

where
f\colon\real^{d_{x}}\times\real^{d_{y}}\to\real^{d_{x}}
,
g\colon\real^{d_{y}}\to\real^{d_{y}}

and
\alpha\colon\real^{d_{y}}\to\real^{d_{y}\times d_{w}}

are smooth functions and
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}

is a standard Brownian motion in
\real{}^{d_{w}}
.
This simple setting,
or a variation thereof where the drift and diffusion coefficients of the fast process also include lower order terms allowed to depend on
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
,
is often considered in the literature,
for example in numerical works
[
47
,
14
]
.
The infinitesimal generator associated with the fast process
Y^{\varepsilon}
is given by
\varepsilon^{-2}\mathcal{L}
,
where

\mathcal{L}=g(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(y)\alpha(y)^{\mathsf{T}}:\nabla_{y}^{2}.

By the theory of homogenization for multiscale SDEs,
if the fast process admits a unique ergodic distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\real^{d_{x}})
,
and if the following centering condition is satisfied,

\forall x\in\real^{d_{x}},\qquad\int_{\real{}^{d_{x}}}f(x,y)\,\mu(dy)=0,

then in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the slow process
(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
converges weakly in
C[0,T]
to the solution of the homogenized SDE:

d\overline{X}=F(\overline{X})+A(\overline{X})\,dV_{t}.

(4.6)

Here
V_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\real{}^{d_{x}}
and the so-called effective drift and diffusion coefficients are given by

F(x)=\int_{\real{}^{d_{y}}}\Bigl(f(x,y)\cdot\nabla_{x}\Bigr)\Phi(x,y)\,\mu(dy)\in\real^{d_{x}},\qquad A(x)A(x)^{\mathsf{T}}=A^{0}(x)+A^{0}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\real^{d_{x}\times d_{x}},

where
\Phi
is a solution to

-\mathcal{L}^{x}\Phi(x,y)=f(x,y)

and

A^{0}(x)=\int_{\real{}^{d_{y}}}f(x,y)\otimes\Phi(x,y)\,\mu^{x}(dy).

We refer to
[
38
, Chapter 18]
for a rigorous proof in a simplified setting,
and to
[
32
,
33
]
for extensions and additional results.
Since formally
\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}^{-1}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\nabla_{x}
,
given that
\mathcal{L}
only includes derivatives with respect to
y
,
the coefficients of the homogenized equation (
4.6
) are of the form (
TC
).
More precisely, the components of the coefficients
F
and
A^{0}

can be written as

F_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{x}}\Bigl\langle f_{j}(x,\,\cdot\,),-\mathcal{L}^{-1}\mathfrak{h}_{ij}(x,\,\cdot\,)\Bigr\rangle,\qquad A^{0}_{ij}(x)=\Bigl\langle f_{i}(x,\,\cdot\,),-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f_{j}(x,\,\cdot\,)\Bigr\rangle,\qquad

(4.7)

where
\mathfrak{h}_{ij}(x,\,\cdot\,)=\partial_{x_{j}}f_{i}(x,\,\cdot\,)
,
and with
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
the inner product with respect to
L^{2}(\mu)
,
with
x
viewed as a fixed parameter.
It follows from (
4.7
) that
the homogenized coefficients can be approximated using estimators based on the Green–Kubo formula (
1.3
),
such as those studied in
Section

2
.
This constitutes the basic idea behind the so-called
heterogeneous multiscale method
for multiscale SDEs
[
47
,
14
]
.
The variance reduction method we describe in
Subsection

4.3.2

is based on the specific estimators given in
Section

3
,
but we note that it can easily be adapted to the estimators used within the heterogeneous multiscale method,
such as those given in
[
14
, Section 3.1]
.

4.3.2
Control variate approaches for calculating the effective coefficients

In this section,
we describe how the control variate approaches described in
Section

3
can be employed for the calculation of the coefficients of the homogenized equation (
4.6
).
The main difference with the other examples considered so far is the presence of an
x
-dependence in (
4.7
)
and the associated Poisson equations

-\mathcal{L}\mathscr{H}_{ij}(x,y)=\mathfrak{h}_{ij}(x,y),\qquad-\mathcal{L}\mathscr{F}_{i}(x,y)=f_{i}(x,y),

(4.8)

as well as in the associated adjoint equation

-\mathcal{L}^{*}\mathscr{F}^{*}_{i}(x,y)=f_{i}(x,y).

(4.9)

The control variate methods described in
Section

3
require approximate solutions
to (
4.8
) or (
4.9
) or both.
We next describe two possible approaches for constructing these approximate solutions:

•

A first approach would be to solve the Poisson equations
– (
4.8
) or (
4.9
) or both, depending on which method from
Section

3
is considered –
for each fixed
x
separately as needed.
Combined with a classical time-stepping algorithm for the homogenized equation (
4.6
),
this would lead to a numerical method where a number of Poisson PDEs in dimension
d_{y}
must be solved at each time step,
much like the method studied in
[
1
]
.

•

An alternative approach,
used in
Subsection

4.3.3
,
is to solve the Poisson equations (
4.8
) for all
x\in\real^{d_{x}}

and all vector components simultaneously.
This is not simple to achieve using traditional PDE solvers,
but simple to implement using neural networks.
For example,
in order to numerically solve the second equation in (
4.8
),
we search for an approximate vector-valued solution
\mathscr{F}^{\rm NN}(x,y;\theta)

within a class of functions parametrized by a neural network with parameters
\theta
.
The vector of parameters
\theta
is found by minimizing the following loss function

\mathscr{L}(\theta)=\int_{\real{}^{d_{x}}\times\real^{d_{y}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\Bigl\lvert f_{i}(x,y)+\mathcal{L}\mathscr{F}^{\rm NN}_{i}(x,y;\theta)\Bigr\rvert^{2}\,\nu(dx)\,\mu(dy),

(4.10)

where
\mu
is, as before,
the invariant probability measure associated with the fast processes in (
4.5
),
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\real^{d_{x}})
is a probability measure on
\real{}^{d_{x}}
that
must be fixed a priori as part of the method.
A natural choice, when
\real{}^{d_{x}}
is a compact state space,
is to let
\nu
be the uniform distribution on
\real{}^{d_{x}}
.
When
\real{}^{d_{x}}
is non-compact,
the choice of
\nu
determines the region of
\real{}^{d_{x}}

where we wish
\mathscr{F}^{\rm NN}(x,\,\cdot\,;\mathbf{c})
to be a good approximation of the exact solution
\mathscr{F}(x,\,\cdot\,)
,
and where variance reduction can be expected as a result.
In practice, the loss function (
4.10
) of course needs to be discretized,
for example by estimating the integral via Monte Carlo sampling.

To conclude this subsection,
let us mention that it is not necessary to solve the first equation in (
4.8
) explicitly.
Indeed, the function
\mathscr{H}_{ij}(x,y)
is the
x
-gradient of
\mathscr{F}_{i}(x,y)
,
and so a numerical approximation of
\mathscr{H}_{ij}(x,y)
can be obtained by taking the
x
-gradient of
\mathscr{F}^{\rm NN}_{i}(x,y;\theta)
.

4.3.3
Numerical example

In this section,
we illustrate the performance of the control variate strategies presented in
Section

3
,
when the approximate solutions to Poisson equations are calculated simultaneously for all
x
by a neural network approach.
We consider the following simple example of a multiscale SDE,
taken from
[
38
, Section 11.7.7]
,
to which we refer for additional context:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX_{1}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Y_{1}\,dt\,,\\
dX_{2}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Y_{2}\,dt\,,\\
dX_{3}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(X_{1}Y_{2}-X_{2}Y_{1})\,dt\,,\\

5
Extensions and perspectives

In this paper we demonstrated that neural networks can be an effective tool for constructing control variates via approximating Poisson equations used in the calculation of transport coefficients. The main point of our approach was to construct simple, easy-to-train and inexpensive-to-evaluate networks to be used as control variates, evaluated alongside Monte Carlo simulations. The key observation is that the network must be simple enough so that it is reasonably inexpensive to train and evaluate, while still providing significant variance reduction in order justify the additional computational cost. Clearly, in order for the proposed methodology to be of practical use, the cost (i.e., product of runtime and variance) must be less than that of the standard estimator.

There are several directions for extending the work. While possible, high-order nested automatic differentiation remains a nontrivial challenge, and is generally not an off-the-shelf tool, particularly for high-dimensional systems requiring batched training. Another possible direction is to further optimize the network’s cost-to-variance reduction ratio, in particular by further exploring featurization approaches, which can potentially increase the network’s efficiency without significantly increasing cost. Lastly, another possible extension of this method is for computing the mobility in the underdamped limit
\gamma\to 0
, particularly in the case of nonseparable potentials, which remains a challenge in the literature despite recent efforts
[
37
]
.

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\mathbf{E}\bigl[h_{1}(X_{a})h_{2}(X_{b})h_{3}(X_{c})h_{4}(X_{d})\bigr]\Bigr\rvert

We decompose the estimator as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}w_{\varepsilon}\left\lparen\frac{u}{T}\right\rparen\int_{0}^{T-u}f(X_{s})g(X_{s+u})\,ds\,du

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\omega_{\varepsilon}\left\lparen\frac{u}{T}\right\rparen\int_{0}^{T-u}f(X_{s})g(X_{s+u})\,ds\,du=:\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}+\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}.

The proof of (
A.10
) is based on the following simple inequality:

\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{\rho}_{T}^{2}\bigr]=\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}^{2}\bigr]+2\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}\bigr]\geqslant\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}^{2}\bigr]+2\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}\bigr],

(A.11)

so that

\Var(\widehat{\rho}_{T})\geqslant\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}^{2}\bigr]+2\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}\bigr]-\mathbf{E}[\widehat{\rho}_{T}]^{2}.

(A.12)

It follows from (
A.1
) that

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}^{2}\bigr]=4\langle f,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}f\rangle\langle g,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\rangle\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\lvert w_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\bigr\rvert^{2}(1-\theta)\,d\theta.

This term diverges as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
It remains to analyze the last term on the right-hand side of (
A.11
).
Using Fubini’s theorem and a change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}\bigr]

\displaystyle=T^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}w_{\varepsilon}\left(1-u\right)\omega_{\varepsilon}\left(1-v\right)\int_{0}^{u}\!\!\int_{0}^{\upsilon}E(s,\varsigma,u,\upsilon)\,ds\,d\varsigma\,du\,d\upsilon,

where
E(s,\varsigma,u,\upsilon)=\mathbf{E}\bigl[f(X_{sT})f(X_{\varsigma T})g(X_{sT+(1-u)T})g(X_{\varsigma T+(1-\upsilon)T})\bigr]
.
It follows from (
A.4
), with
t=s+1-u
and
\tau=\varsigma+1-\upsilon
, that

E(s,t,u,v)\leqslant C\max\Bigl\{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T|s-\varsigma|-\lambda T|s-\varsigma+\upsilon-u|},\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T|1-u|-\lambda T|1-\upsilon|}\Bigr\}.

By the triangle inequality,
it holds that
|s-\varsigma|+|s-\varsigma+\upsilon-u|\geqslant|\upsilon-u|

and
|1-u|+|1-\upsilon|\geqslant|u-\upsilon|
,
so we deduce that

\forall(s,\varsigma,u,\upsilon)\in[0,1]^{4},\qquad\forall(s,\varsigma,u,\upsilon)\in[0,1]^{4},\qquad E(s,\varsigma,u,\upsilon)\leqslant C\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|u-v|T}.

Therefore, using this inequality,
the definition of
w_{\varepsilon}
and
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, and (
A.9
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\mathbf{E}\bigl[\widehat{r}_{T,\varepsilon}\widehat{\varrho}_{T,\varepsilon}\bigr]\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant CT^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}w_{\varepsilon}\left(1-u\right)\omega_{\varepsilon}\left(1-v\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|u-v|T}uv\,du\,d\upsilon

\displaystyle=CT^{2}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\!\!\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}w\left(1-u\right)w\left(1-\upsilon\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|u-\upsilon|T}u\upsilon\,du\,d\upsilon

\displaystyle\leqslant CT^{2}\mathscr{W}^{2}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\!\!\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|u-\upsilon|T}du\,d\upsilon\leqslant\frac{C\mathscr{W}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
|u-v|=u-v
(since
u\in[\varepsilon,1]
and
v\in[0,\varepsilon]
), so that

\displaystyle T^{2}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|u-\upsilon|T}du\,d\upsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda T\varepsilon}-1)(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T\varepsilon}-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T})}{\lambda^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}.

The statement (
A.10
) then follows from taking the limits
T\to+\infty

and then
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
A.12
).
∎

Appendix B
Change of variables

Lemma B.1
.

Let
f
be a continuous function on
\mathcal{D}=\{(t,s)\colon 0\leqslant s\leqslant t,\;t\in[0,T]\}
. Then,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}f(t-s)\,ds\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}f(\theta)(T-\theta)\,d\theta.

(B.1)

Proof.

Define the variables
\theta=t-s
and
\tau=t
, and consider the
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism

\Phi(\tau,\theta)=(t(\tau,\theta),s(\tau,\theta))=(\tau,\tau-\theta).

It holds that

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{D}}f(t-s)\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\iint_{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}f\left\lparen\Phi_{1}(\tau,\theta)-\Phi_{2}(\tau,\theta)\right\rparen\left\lvert J_{\Phi}(\tau,\theta)\right\rvert\,d\theta\,d\tau,

(B.2)

with
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}=\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{D})
given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}=\{(\tau,\theta)\colon 0\leqslant\theta\leqslant\tau,\;\tau\in[0,T]\}.

The Jacobian is given by

J_{\Phi}(\tau,\theta)=\det\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial t(\tau,\theta)}{\partial\tau}&\dfrac{\partial t(\tau,\theta)}{\partial\theta}\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{\partial s(\tau,\theta)}{\partial\tau}&\dfrac{\partial s(\tau,\theta)}{\partial\theta}\end{bmatrix}=\det\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}=-1,

so that
|J_{\Phi}(\tau,\theta)|=1
. Thus, applying (
B.2
), using Fubini’s theorem then integrating with respect to
\tau
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}f(t-s)\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(\theta)\,d\theta\,d\tau=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\theta}^{T}f(\theta)\,d\tau\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{T}f(\theta)(T-\theta)\,d\theta,

which is the desired result.
∎

Corollary B.2
.

Under the same setting as
Lemma

B.1
, it holds that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}f\left\lparen\frac{t-s}{T}\right\rparen\,ds\,dt=T^{2}\int_{0}^{1}f(v)(1-v)\,dv.

Proof.

This result follows trivially from
Lemma

B.1
with the additional change of variable
\theta=Tv
in (
B.1
).
∎

In this section,
we illustrate the bias and variance of the estimators (
GK
) and (
HE
),

Specifically, we consider the setting where
X_{t}

and
f,g
are the identity functions.
That is to say,
we consider estimators for the quantity

GK

C.2
Remark on the half-Einstein estimator

Remark C.1
.

The estimator (
HE
) may also be rewritten as

\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T-u}w\left\lparen\frac{u}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s}^{k})g(X_{s+u}^{k})\,ds\,du.

Thus, the estimator could also be interpreted as an approximation of the Green–Kubo formula (
1.3
) where the correlation
\mathbf{E}\left[f(X_{0})g(X_{u})\right]

is approximated by

\mathbf{E}\left[f(X_{0})g(X_{u})\right]\approx\frac{1}{TK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{T-u}w\left\lparen\frac{u}{T}\right\rparen f(X_{s}^{k})g(X_{s+u}^{k})\,ds,\qquad 0\leqslant u\leqslant T.

The right-hand side is an unbiased estimator of the left-hand side for
w(x)=(1-x)^{-1}
.
However, this choice of the weight function leads to the estimator (
2.11
) having an unbounded variance in the limit as
T\to\infty
,
as suggested by
Proposition

2.6
further in this section
and proved rigorously in
Corollary

A.4
.
In practice, other choices of
w
are thus preferred.

Appendix D
Neural network architecture

As discussed in
Subsection

4.2.2
for the Langevin dynamics example, we employ a featurization layer to transform the original four inputs
(q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2})
into a seven-dimensional feature set, ensuring the periodicity of positional terms and augmenting the feature space with additional relevant kinetic terms. This is done via (
4.4
), which we recall for convenience:

\mathrm{featurization}\left\lparen q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2}\right\rparen=\left\lparen\sin(q_{1}),\cos(q_{1}),\sin(q_{2}),\cos(q_{2}),p_{1},p_{2},\frac{p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}}{2}\right\rparen.

Typically, such featurization approaches would directly use the transformed features as the input layer without an additional “functional layer”. However, by retaining the original four inputs before featurization, we avoid the complex chain-rule transformations in the loss function, where the differential operator (
4.3
) acts on the solution with respect to the original variables
(q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2})
.

D.1
for the Langevin and multiscale dynamics examples of
Subsections

4.2
and
4.3
, respectively.
