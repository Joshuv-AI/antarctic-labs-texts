Bacterial stimulation triggers IRF7-mediated IFN-λ production in hepatocytes to attenuate sepsis
Authors: Wei Ma, Junjie Shu, Wanqi Tang, Haoru Liu, Hao Zhang, Xue Yang, Qiangguo Gao, Huaping Liang, Xia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterferon lambda (IFN-λ) is produced early by hematopoietic or epithelial cells and acts as a primary mediator of frontline antiviral defense without inducing inflammatory responses. However, whether hepatocytes, rather than immune cells, can secrete IFN-λs to protect against bacterial infection remains unestablished. Here, we demonstrate that hepatocytes secrete IFN-λs in response to bacterial stimulation both in vivo and in vitro, and that this secreted IFN-λs directly inhibits bacterial proliferation. Mechanistically, during in vitro bacterial infection, the upregulation and activation of the transcription factor IRF7 drive type III interferon (IFN-λ) production in hepatocytes through a cascading TLR4 signaling pathway, rather than via RIG-I or MDA5. Importantly, pretreatment with IFN-λs, through interaction with IFNLR1 in hepatocytes, increases the number of dead bacteria, improves systemic outcomes, and enhances survival in septic mice. Furthermore, sepsis patients show increased expression levels of IRF7, IFN-λ2, and IFN-λ3. These findings reveal that IFN-λs function as active mediators against bacterial infection, eliciting a unique antibacterial response to eliminate bacteria. This work further suggests that enhancing IFN-λs production in hepatocytes could serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06351-6.Keywords: Liver, Interferon lambda, IRF7, Antibacterial capacity, Sepsis

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. This condition remains critical, with persistently high incidence and mortality rates. The pathogens most strongly associated with sepsis-related mortality are Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Streptococcus pneumoniae [2]. Despite updates to the latest international guidelines for sepsis and septic shock management, recommended therapeutic strategies still primarily focus on resuscitation, organ support, early antibiotic administration, and infection source control. Given the severe lack of specific pharmacological treatments for sepsis in clinical practice, numerous researchers are actively developing novel therapeutic approaches.

Macrophages, as specialized phagocytes and the first line of defense against pathogens, engulf microorganisms and trigger microbial killing responses [3]. Other cell types, including endothelial cells [4] and vascular endothelial cells [5], also exhibit antibacterial properties. Accumulating evidence further indicates that hepatocytes contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of viral infections (e.g., SIV [6], HBV [7], HCV [8], HEV [9], HIV [10], SARS-CoV-2 [11], and COVID-19 [12], parasitic infections (e.g., Plasmodium parasites [13], and bacterial infections (e.g., Helicobacter pylori[14]. However, research on the role and mechanisms of hepatocytes in bacterial infections lags far behind that of viral infections, though recent studies are increasingly uncovering their involvement in bacterial immunity.​ During bacterial infections, hepatocytes secrete various immunomodulatory factors, including C-reactive protein (CRP), serum amyloid A (SAA), lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-related signaling molecules, and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [15]. For instance, hepatocytes release lipocalin-2 (LCN2) into the circulation to limit systemic Klebsiella pneumoniae infection [16]; infection with H. hepaticus or overexpression of its CdtB protein induces hepatocellular lipid deposition, highlighting CdtB as a potential therapeutic target [17]; and persistent Listeria monocytogenes infection elicits a robust interferon response, type I in primary mouse hepatocytes (PMH) and type III in HepG2 cells, while Huh7 cells remain unresponsive [18]. Additionally, IL-20 exacerbates concanavalin A-induced acute hepatitis and Klebsiella pneumoniae infection by reducing hepatic IκBζ-dependent antibacterial gene expression [19]. A proinflammatory hepatocyte (PIH) subpopulation, derived primarily from periportal hepatocytes, recruits macrophages via CCL2-CCR2 signaling during endotoxemia progression [20]. Lethal Ehrlichia infection of hepatocytes triggers MyD88 pathway activation, which in turn mediates CASP11 activation, Gasdermin D cleavage, IL-6 synthesis, and the restraint of bacterial proliferation [21]. Hepatocytes also serve as efferocytes, as they engulf and rapidly break down apoptotic and necrotic cells to sustain homeostasis by eliminating proinflammatory debris. Furthermore, crosstalk between hepatocytes and other immune cells occurs during bacterial infections. For example, in Aeromonas hydrophila-infected turtles, hepatic tissues display higher counts of endothelial cells and Kupffer cells, while T cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, and erythroid cells are present in reduced numbers [22]. Critically, hepatocytes directly clear ~ 75% of LPS, surpassing Kupffer cells, demonstrating their ability to phagocytose and eliminate Gram-negative pathogens [23]. Despite these insights, research on hepatocyte-mediated infection responses remains limited compared to the extensive studies on immune cells’ anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory mechanisms. Importantly, no studies have directly demonstrated whether hepatocytes can directly kill bacteria.

Type III interferons function as a first-line defense to restrict local infection at epithelial barriers, with the added benefit of limiting detrimental inflammatory responses [24]. In contrast, type I interferons (IFNIs) are induced later to enhance viral resistance and promote inflammation [25]. While IFN-λ is primarily known to be produced by hematopoietic or epithelial cells, a few studies report hepatocyte-derived IFN-λ during viral infection. The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 and human fetal liver cells (HFLC) secrete IFN-λ1 following HCV infection, and lactate dehydrogenase-elevating virus (LDV) induces IFNL2/3 mRNA expression (without subtype distinction) in the liver [26]. Additionally, IFN-λ produced by hepatocyte cell lines and primary human hepatocytes attenuates Coxsackievirus B3 replication and upregulates interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), including ISG15, 2′−5′-oligoadenylate synthetase 2 (OAS2), dsRNA-regulated protein kinase (PKR), and myxovirus resistance protein 1 (Mx1) [27]. During Listeria monocytogenes infection, primary mouse hepatocytes enhance type I interferon responses, while HepG2 cells upregulate type III interferon-responsive genes. Bacterial infections also alter the expression of cholesterol metabolism-associated genes in HepG2 and Huh7 cells [28]. In preclinical models, IFN-λ reduces Cryptosporidium infection load and prevents severe outcomes (including mortality) in mice, and these protective actions are mediated through STAT1 signaling in enterocytes [29].

However, no research has directly shown that hepatocytes produce IFN-λ to combat bacterial infection, despite their demonstrated potential for anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activity. In the present study, we investigated the role of hepatocytes during bacterial infection and made three key findings. Hepatocytes secrete active mediators that directly inhibit bacterial proliferation. These mediators were identified as IFN-λ2/3, which exert direct bactericidal effects. The transcription factor IRF7 regulates hepatocyte IFN-λs production, with TLR4 activating IRF7 to enhance IFN-λs secretion and bacterial suppression. Furthermore, pretreatment with IFN-λs significantly improved liver function and survival in septic mice. Collectively, these findings reveal that upregulating type III interferons may serve as a novel therapeutic target for sepsis.

Material and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The E. coli, MRSA ATCC43300, and Vibrio vulnificus (V. vulnificus) strains stored in our laboratory were cultured overnight in LB (Luria–Bertani) medium at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 200 rpm, reaching the logarithmic growth phase by the following morning. The growth of E. coli was quantified at OD₆₀₀ (OD₆₀₀ = 1, corresponding to 1 × 10⁹ colony-forming units (CFU)/mL) [30]. The growth of MRSA ATCC43300 was assessed at OD₆₀₀ (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5, corresponding to 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL), in accordance with previous research [31]. A Vibrio vulnificus suspension with a McFarland turbidity of 0.5 (approximately 10⁷ CFU/mL) was prepared using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and set aside for subsequent experiments. Subsequently, the bacterial suspensions were diluted to various concentrations for in vivo animal model and in vitro cell model studies.

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were procured from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities at the Animal Experimental Center of Daping Hospital, Army Medical University (Chongqing, China). Tlr4 knockout mice were kindly provided by Professor Liusheng Peng from the Army Medical University. The mice were maintained under controlled conditions (temperature: 22–25 °C; 12-h light/dark cycle; adequate ventilation) with free access to standard chow and sterile water.

Human liver tissue samples

Healthy human liver tissue samples were obtained from grossly normal residual tissue of patients undergoing resection for benign liver diseases. Infected liver tissue samples were collected from patients diagnosed with acute cholangitis, a condition caused by concurrent bacterial infection and biliary obstruction [32].

Cell culture and model establishment

Mouse liver Kupffer cells (GPCO448) were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC) Cell Bank, while FL83B hepatocytes (CRL-2390) were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Huh7 cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Kupffer cells, FL83B cells, and Huh7 cells were routinely subcultured and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium, Ham’s F-12 K medium, and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), respectively, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. All cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For cell signaling pathway intervention experiments, cells were treated with or without specific agonists and inhibitors. The agonists included Poly(I:C) sodium (10 ng/mL; T12516, TargetMOI) and RS 09 TFA (20 μg/mL; HY-P1439A, MCE), while the inhibitors comprised RIG012 (10 μM; T73486, TargetMOI), TAK-242 (1 μM; TQ0181, TargetMOI), Pepinh-MYD TFA (50 uM; HY-P10719A, MCE) and Pepinh-TRIF TFA (40 μM; T76089, TargetMOI). Additionally, cells were treated with or without recombinant interferon-λ2 (3 pg/mL; HY-P700203AF, MCE) and interferon-λ3 (30 pg/mL; HY-P700204AF, MCE).

Phagocytosis assay in RAW264.7 cells was performed using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, 46945). Cells were incubated with FITC-dextran for 2 h, after which extracellular, non-internalized FITC-dextran was removed. The fluorescence intensity within the cells was then measured.

Isolation of primary hepatocytes and kupffer cells

Primary hepatocytes [33] and Kupffer cells [34] were isolated following established protocols. Briefly, mouse liver tissue was enzymatically digested to generate a hepatocyte suspension, which was then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The filtrate was centrifuged at 50 × g for 2 min at 4 °C to pellet hepatocytes. The resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was retained. After resuspension, the sample was centrifuged again at 50 × g for 2 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was centrifuged once more at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the resulting pellet was resuspended and seeded into culture plates. After a 2-h incubation, non-adherent cells were removed by washing with PBS, yielding purified Kupffer cells.

Construction of bacterial time-kill curves

MRSA, E. coli, and V. vulnificus were separately inoculated into four types of culture systems: LB medium, phenol red-free erythrocyte medium, phenol red-free erythrocyte medium containing Kupffer cells, and phenol red-free erythrocyte medium containing FL83B hepatocytes. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. The OD₆₀₀ values of MRSA, E. coli, and V. vulnificus in each medium were measured at predetermined time points. Bacterial growth curves were plotted with bacterial count (CFU/mL) on the vertical axis and incubation time on the horizontal axis. The time-kill curves of bacteria treated with primary hepatocytes or recombinant proteins (IFN-λ2, IFN-λ3) were generated using the same method.

For the in vitro combination experiments, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of cefixime against E. coli was 125 μg/mL, as previously reported [35]. In the present study, cefixime was used at 1/4 MIC and 1/8 MIC, alone or in combination with either 50 pg/mL IFN-λ2 (HY-P700203AF, MCE) or 500 pg/mL IFN-λ3 (HY-P700204AF, MCE). This concentrations of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were determined based on serum levels in infected mice.

Determination of intracellular reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantified using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (S0033, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular nitric oxide (NO) levels were detected with the DAF-FM DA (NO Fluorescent Probe) Assay Kit (S0019, Beyotime) following the kit protocol. FL83B cells were infected with E. coli at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 2 using a reverse time-course stimulation strategy to ensure uniform termination times for all samples. As adherent cells, the fluorescent probes were loaded in situ, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader.

Assay for cytokines

After infecting cells with E. coli (2 MOI) and adding recombinant proteins, including 3 pg/mL IFN-λ2 (HY-P700203AF, MCE) and 30 pg/mL IFN-λ3 (HY-P700204AF, MCE), cell supernatants were collected at different time points. Separately, serum was isolated from E. coli-infected mice with or without IFN-λs treatment. Inflammatory factors in both the supernatants and serum were then measured according to the kit instructions. Hydrogen peroxide concentrations were quantified using the Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit (S0038, Beyotime). Nitric oxide levels were determined with the Nitric Oxide Assay Kit (S0021, Beyotime). Interferon-beta (IFNβ) levels were measured using the Verikine IFNβ ELISA Kit (PBL), while interferon-lambda 2/3 (IFN-λ2/3) concentrations were assayed with the IFN-λ ELISA Kit (DY1789B-05, R&D). The levels of TNFα (Cat. NO. 88–7324), IL-10 (Cat. NO. 88–7105), IL-1β (Cat. NO. 88-7013A), and IL-6 (Cat. NO. 88–7064) were measured using mouse uncoated ELISA kits from Invitrogen.

Establishment of mouse sepsis and peritonitis models

E. coli was activated and administered to mice via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dose of 5 × 10⁸ CFU/kg (based on body weight). At corresponding time points, mouse serum and organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and thymus) were harvested, immediately homogenized in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. For the lethal E. coli intraperitoneal infection model, mice were injected with E. coli at a dose of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/kg, with concurrent i.p. administration of recombinant IFN-λ2 (250 μg/kg) or IFN-λ3 (250 μg/kg).

Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) was performed by exteriorizing the cecum after laparotomy, packing the fecal material into the distal end, and ligating and puncturing the cecum approximately 1.2 cm from the tip. After extrusion of a small quantity of feces, the cecum was placed back, and the wound was sutured closed.

For the combination therapy, mice received either IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 (250 μg/kg, i.p.) 12 h before infection. After intraperitoneal (i.p.) infection with E. coli, cefixime (14.4 mg/kg) was administered immediately by gavage. Survival was monitored, or mice were sacrificed at 6 h post-infection for further analyses.

Analysis of serum biochemical indicators in model animals

Mouse serum was collected, and the following biochemical parameters were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter). Liver function markers, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TB), direct bilirubin (DBil), and indirect bilirubin (IBil); Kidney function marker, creatinine (Crea).

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript II One Step RT-PCR Kit (RR014BP611, Vazyme). Gene expression was analyzed via quantitative PCR (qPCR) with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q321, Vazyme) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. Following standard experimental protocols, the mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to and compared against those of the control group.

Western blotting analysis

Cells treated with E. coli or left untreated were lysed using RIPA buffer (9806, CST) supplemented with a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (5872, CST). Clarified cell lysates were subjected to conventional immunoblotting to detect endogenous target proteins. Total protein levels, quantified using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad), served as a stain-free loading control for data normalization. The primary and secondary antibodies used in this assay are as follows, p-IRF7(Ser477) antidody (PA5-106184, Invitrogen), p-IRF7(Ser437/438) antidody (24129, CST), p-IRF7(Ser471/472) antidody (bs-3196R, Bioss), IRF7 antibody (ab288440, Abcam), IFN-λ2 antibody (bs-2627R, Bioss), IFN-λ3 antibody (GTX54305, Genetex), β-tubulin antibody (2128, CST), β-actin antibody (4967, CST), and HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (7074, CST).

Histopathological analysis

Mouse liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde following standard procedures and subsequently embedded in paraffin for sectioning. Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate tissue morphological changes. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was conducted using a two-step universal IHC kit (PV-8000, ZSGB-BIO) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The primary antibodies used for IHC included p-IRF7 antibody (PA5-106184, Invitrogen) and IRF7 antibody (A0159, ABclonal). Immunofluorescence staining was carried out following routine protocols using ASGPR antidody (sc-52623, Santa), F4/80 antidody (A18637, Abclonal), Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 647(A21235, Invitrogen) and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 594(A11012, Invitrogen). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime). The percentages of ASGPR-positive and F4/80-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Lentiviral transfection, stable transfectant screening, and validation

To optimize lentiviral transfection efficiency in FL83B cells, the optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) was first determined. The construction of IRF7 overexpression and knockdown lentiviruses was constructed by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. FL83B cells were synchronized to the logarithmic growth phase prior to transfection. One day before viral infection, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a volume of 100 μL per well to achieve 60–70% confluency on the day of infection. Viral dilution solutions were prepared using Ham’s F-12 K medium supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and lentiviruses were diluted to achieve MOI values of 0, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 (with at least two replicate wells per MOI). After aspirating the original culture medium, 100 μL of viral solution was added to each well according to the concentration gradient, and plates were incubated in a 5% CO₂ incubator for an additional 72 h. Fluorescent protein expression and cell viability were observed under a fluorescence microscope to identify the optimal MOI.

Stable transfectants were selected using complete culture medium supplemented with 1 μg/mL puromycin, with medium replacement and cell passaging performed as needed during the selection period. After three consecutive passages, total RNA was extracted from IRF7-overexpressing, IRF7-knockdown, and wild-type FL83B cells to verify transfection efficiency via RT-qPCR. The expression level of IRF7 protein was further validated by Western blotting, and cells with successful stable transfection were used for subsequent experiments.

Bacterial viability staining

Bacterial suspensions were added to solutions containing varying concentrations of recombinant proteins (or protein-free controls) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Bacterial viability was assessed using the LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (L7012, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol, where dead bacteria emit red fluorescence and viable bacteria emit green fluorescence. Fluorescence signals were detected and quantified using either a fluorescence microplate reader or an Olympus super-resolution microscopy system.

Neutralizing antibody and heat inactivation assays

Supernatants were collected from E. coli-infected FL83B cells at 12 h post-infection. The supernatants were centrifuged at 500 × g to remove debris and then filtered through a 0.22 µm filter for sterilization. Heat-inactivated supernatant was obtained by heating at 56 °C for 30 min. For neutralization experiments, an anti-IFN-λ neutralizing antibody (Anti-mIL-28b-mIgG1, cat. no. mil28b-mab9-02, InvivoGen; specific for mouse IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3, with no cross-reactivity to human IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3) was added to the filtered supernatant at a final concentration of 1 µg/mL, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min to allow full binding of the antibody to IFN-λ present in the supernatant. The treated supernatant was then mixed with the bacterial suspension for 2 h. After co-incubation, Live/Dead bacterial staining was performed according to the Bacterial Viability Staining.

Assessment of mitochondrial function and oxidative stress

After FL83B cells were infected with E. coli (2 MOI), the following parameters were measured according to the respective kit manuals. Following cell lysis, the supernatant was collected for determination of ATP levels using an ATP Assay Kit (S0026, Beyotime). Lipid peroxidation was measured using a malondialdehyde (MDA) Assay Kit (S0131, Beyotime). Total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using a Total Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit (WST-8 method, S0101, Beyotime). Mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1 staining) was assessed with a kit (C2003S, Beyotime) by staining the cells, followed by detection with a fluorescence spectrophotometer.

shRNA mediated knockdown of Ifnlr1 in cells

shRNAs targeting mouse Ifnlr1 were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Three target sequences were designed and screened for the most effective knockdown. Transfection of FL83B cells with the shRNA vector was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The final shRNA construct was pGPU6/mCherry/Puro-Ifnlr1-Mus-1218. The sequences were sensese quences, 5′-CACCGCCCAGAAGGAACTGACCATAATT CAAGAGATTATGGTCAGTTCCTTCTGGGTTTTTTG-3′, and antisense sequences, 5′-GATCCAAAAAACCCAGAAGGAACTGACCATAATCTCTTGAATTATGGTCAGTTCCT TCTGGGC-3′.

siRNA mediated knockdown of Ifnlr1 and IRF7 in mice

ISC (in-vivo stability chemistry-modified) siRNAs targeting murine Ifnlr1 and Irf7 were designed and synthesized by GenePharma Technology Co (Shanghai, China). The siRNAs were administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 60 µg per mouse according to the ISC siRNA product manual. At 72 h post-injection, mice were subjected to intraperitoneal (i.p.) infection with E. coli for survival analysis. Mice were sacrificed at 6 h after i.p. E. coli infection to collect samples for subsequent analyses. The final siRNA sequences targeting mouse IRF7 were sense sequence, 5′-AUCUUGCGCCAA GACAAUU-3′, and antisense sequence, 5′-AAUUGUCUUGGCGCAAGAUAA-3′. For Ifnlr1, the sequences were sense sequence, 5′-CAGAAGGAACUGACCAUAA-3′, and antisense sequence, 5′-UUAUGGUCAGUUCCUUCUGGG-3′.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8, Dotmatics) and SPSS Statistics (Version 23, IBM). The normality of all datasets was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which confirmed that the data were normally distributed. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while differences among multiple groups were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P-value 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

Result

Hepatocytes can secrete active mediators to directly inhibit bacterial proliferation

To investigate whether hepatocytes exert a direct antibacterial effect, we first evaluated the proliferation of equal doses of MRSA, Vibrio vulnificus, and E. coli following incubation with either Kupffer cells or FL83B cells (a murine hepatocyte cell line) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere. Bacterial colony-forming units (CFU) were quantified at specified time points (Fig. 1A), and the results showed that CFU counts of all three pathogens were significantly lower in cocultures with Kupffer cells or FL83B cells compared to the control medium (Fig. 1B-D). Notably, FL83B cells displayed inhibitory effects on MRSA and V. vulnificus proliferation, with the most pronounced suppression observed against E. coli (Fig. 1B). Based on this finding, E. coli was selected as the model bacterium for subsequent in-depth analyses. To further confirm the intrinsic antibacterial capacity of hepatocytes, we isolated primary mouse hepatocytes and primary Kupffer cells. Successful separation of hepatocytes (ASGPR+) and primary Kupffer (F4/80+) were confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 1E, F). The results demonstrated that primary mouse hepatocytes directly inhibited E. coli proliferation (Fig. 1G-H), validating that hepatocytes possess inherent antibacterial activity, with the human hepatoma cell line Huh7 included as an additional control.​ Previous studies have reported that bacterial stimuli upregulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and the expression of NOX2, NOX4, and iNOS genes in adipose cells [36]. To explore the potential mechanisms underlying hepatocyte-mediated antibacterial activity, we next examined the production of key antimicrobial mediators. Bacterial infection did not significantly alter intracellular nitric oxide (NO) levels in primary mouse hepatocytes (Fig. 1I). However, intracellular ROS levels were markedly elevated at 24 h post-infection (Fig. 1J). Concomitantly, the secretion of NO and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) into the culture supernatant by primary mouse hepatocytes increased progressively at 6, 12, and 24 h after E. coli stimulation, with peak concentrations detected at 12 h (Fig. 1K-L). In addition, type I IFN (IFNβ; Fig. 1M) and type III IFNs (IFN-λ2/3; Fig. 1N) were significantly upregulated in the hepatocyte culture supernatant at 12 h post-E. coli infection from enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Notably, IFN-λ2/3 expression was induced early and maintained at a stably high level throughout the course of infection. While type I IFNs (IFNα, IFNβ) and type III IFNs (IFN-λ) share several core properties, including induction by viral infection, activation of overlapping signaling pathways, and regulation of similar transcriptional programs [24]. The role of type III IFNs (particularly IFN-λ2/3) in bacterial infection remains largely unexplored. In addition, we further assessed the mitochondrial viability of hepatocytes. A decrease in ATP concentration in hepatocytes at 12 and 24 h post-E. coli infection suggested metabolic impairment of oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 1O). Levels of JC-1 (an indicator of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 1P), MDA (malondialdehyde) (Fig. 1Q), and SOD (superoxide dismutase) (Fig. 1R) increased at 6 h but decreased at 12 and 24 h post-infection. These findings indicate that mitochondria become dysfunctional following E. coli infection. Collectively, these results demonstrate that hepatocytes are activated in response to bacterial challenge and secrete a range of antimicrobial mediators, including ROS, H₂O₂, NO, both type I and type III IFNs, alongside evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction.

Bacterial infection upregulates the expression of type III interferon in hepatocytes in vivo

To further investigate the expression levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 in vivo during bacterial infection, we established an E. coli intraperitoneal infection model using C57BL/6 J mice. Following infection, the levels of liver function markers, including total bilirubin (TBiL), direct bilirubin (DBiL), indirect bilirubin (IBiL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), as well as the kidney function marker urea, were significantly elevated (Fig. 2A). These findings confirmed the successful establishment of the intraperitoneal infection model. Next, we analyzed the mRNA expression patterns of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 across multiple organs at different time points post-infection. The mRNA level of IFN-λ2 was upregulated in the spleen and thymus at 6 h post-infection (hpi) and in the liver at 24 hpi. In contrast, IFN-λ3 mRNA expression was more robustly induced. It was elevated in the spleen, thymus, and kidney, with the most prominent upregulation observed in the liver, and remained high from 6 to 24 hpi (Fig. 2B). To determine whether the upregulation of type III interferons (IFNs) is primarily confined to hepatocytes, we isolated hepatocytes from E. coli-infected mice using gradient centrifugation. We then compared the expression levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 across three distinct liver-derived fractions, whole liver tissue, isolated hepatocytes, and non-hepatocyte cells. Our results demonstrated that the upregulation of IFN-λ2 (Fig. 2C) and IFN-λ3 (Fig. 2D) at the mRNA level was predominantly localized to hepatocytes, with no significant upregulation observed in non-hepatocyte cells (Fig. 2E-F). Consistent with the mRNA data, protein expression analyses confirmed that IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were expressed in whole liver tissue (Fig. 2G-H) and hepatocytes (Fig. 2I-J), but not in non-hepatocyte cells (Fig. 2K-L). To validate these in vivo observations in an in vitro setting, we examined E. coli-infected FL83B cells (a murine hepatocyte cell line) and primary mouse hepatocytes. Consistent with our in vivo findings, E. coli infection significantly elevated the mRNA levels of IFN-λ2 (Fig. 2M, 2Q) and IFN-λ3 (Fig. 2N, 2R) in both FL83B cells and primary mouse hepatocytes. Similarly, the protein levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were significantly increased in E. coli-infected FL83B cells (Fig. 2O-P) and primary mouse hepatocytes (Fig. 2S-T) compared to uninfected controls. To investigate whether IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 are also elevated following infection with V. vulnificus and MRSA, the mRNA and protein levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were measured in FL83B cells. Following V. vulnificus infection for 6 h, both the mRNA and protein expression levels of IFN-λ3 were elevated compared to the uninfected control; however, no significant changes were observed at 12 or 24 h post-infection (Supplement Fig. 1 A, 1B). For IFN-λ2, protein expression was increased after 6 h of V. vulnificus infection, although mRNA levels remained unchanged (Supplement Fig. 1 A, 1B). In response to MRSA infection, the mRNA levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 did not show significant changes (Supplement Fig. 1 C). Nevertheless, the protein levels of both IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were elevated at 6 and 24 h (Supplement Fig. 1D), respectively, compared to the uninfected control cells. Furthermore, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results revealed that primary mouse hepatocytes secreted significantly higher levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 into the culture supernatant following E. coli infection (Fig. 2U), confirming the functional secretion of these type III IFNs by hepatocytes in response to bacterial challenge. Collectively, these results demonstrate that bacterial infection induces the expression of type III interferons, with hepatocytes serving as the primary cellular source of this response.

Bacterial infection induces the upregulation and activation of IRF7 (the transcription factor for type III interferons) in hepatocytes

To validate changes in the expression of type III interferon-associated transcription factors (IRF7) following E. coli infection, we analyzed tissues harvested from the E. coli intraperitoneal infection model described above. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) results showed that IRF7 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in the heart, spleen, lung, kidney, and thymus from 6 to 24 h post-infection (hpi), with the most striking increase observed in the liver (Fig. 3A). To further clarify whether the liver-specific upregulation of IRF7 is primarily driven by hepatocytes, we quantified IRF7 mRNA levels in whole liver tissue, isolated hepatocytes, and non-hepatocyte cells from infected mice. Both mRNA levels (Fig. 3B-C) and protein expression of IRF7, as well as phosphorylated IRF7 (p-IRF7), were increased in the whole liver (Fig. 3D, 3G) and hepatocytes (Fig. 3E, 3H) following E. coli infection, but not in non-hepatocytes (Fig. 3F, 3I). Notably, this upregulation was most pronounced in hepatocytes, confirming that hepatocytes are the primary cellular source of IRF7 induction in the liver. To corroborate these in vivo observations, we conducted in vitro experiments using primary mouse hepatocytes infected with E. coli. Consistent with the in vivo results, IRF7 mRNA expression was significantly elevated (Fig. 3J), along with increased protein levels of total IRF7 and its activated, phosphorylated form (p-IRF7; Fig. 3K–L). In FL83B cells infected with V. vulnificus for 12 and 24 h, IRF7 mRNA expression was also increased (Fig. 3M); however, IRF7 protein levels did not show significant changes under these conditions (Fig. 3N). Furthermore, neither IRF7 mRNA (Fig. 3O) nor protein (Fig. 3P) expression levels were elevated following infection of FL83B cells with MRSA. Based on these findings, E. coli was selected for subsequent experiments. Collectively, these results confirm that E. coli infection directly induces the upregulation and activation of IRF7 in hepatocytes.

IRF7 regulates the production of IFN-λ in hepatocytes

To investigate whether IRF7 regulates IFN-λ production in hepatocytes, we first established two modified FL83B cell lines, an IRF7 knockdown cell line (FL83B-shIRF7) and an IRF7 overexpression cell line (FL83B-Lv-IRF7). The validation of these cell lines to confirm IRF7 knockdown/overexpression efficiency via RT-qPCR (Fig. 4A) and Western blot (Fig. 4B-C) was presented.​ Subsequently, we infected these modified cell lines with E. coli and analyzed the expression and secretion of interferons. In the IRF7-overexpressing cells, the mRNA levels of IRF7, IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4D), along with increased secretion of IFN-λ2/3 (Fig. 4E) and IFNβ (Fig. 4F). Notably, due to commercial unavailability of reagents for detecting IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 individually before, the secreted IFN-λ2/3 levels represent a combined measurement of both cytokines. A key observation was that IRF7 overexpression exerted a more potent regulatory effect on IFN-λ production than on IFNβ production. To further explore whether IRF7 influences bactericidal effectors beyond interferons, we measured the secretion levels of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and nitric oxide (NO) in the modified cell lines. In contrast to its effect on interferons, IRF7 overexpression did not significantly increase the secretion of H₂O₂ (Fig. 4G) or NO (Fig. 4H) compared to control cells. Following E. coli infection for 12 h, all three cell lines exhibited increased production of H₂O₂ and NO. In cells expressing shIRF7, both H₂O₂ and NO levels were reduced relative to the IRF7-overexpressing and control cells (Fig. 4G, H).

We then used ISC siRNAs to generate an IRF7 knockdown mouse model (IRF7KD) to investigate the role of IRF7 in sepsis. RT-qPCR confirmed the knockdown efficiency in the liver (Supplement Fig. 2 A). Compared to the ISC-NC group, the IRF7KD group showed a significantly worse survival rate following intraperitoneal E. coli infection (Fig. 4I). Bacterial loads in major organs were markedly increased in IRF7KD mice (Supplement Fig. 2B, C). Serum levels of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα showed no significant differences between the NC and IRF7KD mice at 6 h post-infection (Fig. 4J). However, serum IL-10 levels were significantly increased (Fig. 4K), while IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 levels were decreased (Fig. 4L) in IRF7KD mice after E. coli infection. Collectively, these results demonstrate that during E. coli infection, IRF7 predominantly modulates IFN-λ production in hepatocytes and also impacts the secretion of H₂O₂ and NO.

Activation of the IRF7–IFN-λ axis enhances hepatocytes suppression of bacterial proliferation

To investigate the role of the IRF7–IFN-λ axis in hepatocyte-mediated antibacterial defense during bacterial infection, we inoculated FL83B-shIRF7 (IRF7 knockdown), FL83B-Lv-IRF7 (IRF7 overexpression), and wild-type FL83B (FL83B WT) cells with equal amounts of E. coli. Bacterial counts were quantified at specified time points to generate growth curves and assess bacterial proliferation. The results showed that FL83B-Lv-IRF7 cells (with IRF7 overexpression) exhibited a significantly stronger inhibitory effect on E. coli proliferation compared to both FL83B-shIRF7 and FL83B WT cells (Fig. 5A), indicating that IRF7 activity enhances hepatocyte antibacterial capacity. Furthermore, WT, shIRF7, and Lv-IRF7 hepatocytes all demonstrated the ability to kill E. coli, MRSA, and V. vulnificus (Supplemental Fig. 2D–F), suggesting that alternative, IRF7-independent pathways also exist in hepatocytes for eliminating these pathogens. To confirm that this enhanced antibacterial activity is mediated by IFN-λs, we supplemented recombinant IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 proteins into FL83B-shIRF7 cell cultures and evaluated their impact on antibacterial efficacy. By quantifying E. coli counts and performing colony-forming unit (CFU) plate assays, we found that the addition of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 reversed the reduced antibacterial capacity of FL83B-shIRF7 cells (Fig. 5B–C). This directly links IRF7-mediated IFN-λs secretion to hepatocyte antibacterial function. As shown in the ELISA results (Fig. 4), hepatocytes upregulate the expression of both type I and type III interferons upon E. coli infection. Research on the antibacterial role of type III interferons remains limited. To clarify whether the antimicrobial effect of type III interferons is direct or indirect, we tested the antibacterial activity of recombinant type III interferon proteins in vitro. Specifically, we cocultured recombinant IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 (at concentrations of 5, 50, 500, and 5,000 pg/mL) with equal amounts of E. coli, MRSA, or V. vulnificus, respectively. IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 were found to suppress the proliferation of E. coli across a broad concentration range of 5–5000 pg/mL (Fig. 5D–E). For MRSA, IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 exerted inhibitory effects starting at 5 pg/mL, with the maximal suppression observed at a concentration of 50 pg/mL (Fig. 5F–G). Additionally, both cytokines inhibited V. vulnificus proliferation at all tested concentrations (Fig. 5H–I). These findings confirm that IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 can directly suppress bacterial proliferation in vitro. To further elucidate the underlying antibacterial mechanism of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3, we performed bacterial membrane permeability assays. The results demonstrated that both IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 partially compromised the integrity of bacterial cell membranes (Fig. 5J–K). We therefore investigated whether this membrane-compromising effect depends on the secretion of IFN-λs. When E. coli was cocultured with the supernatant from FL83B cells that had been infected with E. coli for 12 h, the relative number of dead bacteria increased compared to bacteria cultured in medium alone. However, this increased effect was lost when the supernatant was heat-inactivated. Consistently, pretreatment of the same supernatant with an IFNλ-neutralizing antibody reduced the relative number of dead bacteria compared to bacteria cultured in untreated supernatant (Fig. 5L). Furthermore, we transferred the culture supernatant from E. coli-infected hepatocytes, including IRF7-knockdown (shIRF7), IRF7-overexpression (lv-IRF7), and wild-type cells, to wild-type FL83B hepatocytes for 6 h. Notably, the supernatant from lv-IRF7-cultured cells led to a larger number of dead bacteria (Fig. 5M). These results suggest that bacteriostatic factors secreted from E. coli-infected FL83B cells, primarily including IFN-λs, can partially damage bacterial cell membranes.

The IRF7–IFN-λ is regulated by TLR4 in hepatocytes

To further investigate the regulatory mechanism of the IRF7–IFN-λ axis in hepatocytes and identify key receptors involved in the innate immune response, we focused on well-characterized upstream regulators of IRF7, specifically RIG-I, MDA5, and TLR4, whose roles have been previously defined in immune cells [37]. First, in FL83B hepatocytes, stimulation with E. coli markedly upregulated the protein expression of total IRF7, IFN-λ2, IFN-λ3, p-IRF7 (Ser471/472), and p-IRF7 (Ser477), but not p-IRF7 (Ser437/438) (Fig. 6A-B). Consistent with this trend, bacterial infection also elevated the mRNA expression levels of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) in the same cell line (Fig. 6C). Notably, among these three pattern recognition receptors, TLR4 exhibited the highest fold change in mRNA expression. To identify which of the candidate upstream regulators mediates this activation event, hepatocytes were treated with a panel of specific agonists and inhibitors, namely RS09 (a TLR4 agonist), poly(I:C) (a dual agonist of RIG-I and MDA5), TAK-242 (a TLR4 inhibitor), and RIG012 (a RIG-I inhibitor). Subsequent analysis of molecules associated with the IRF7–IFN-λ pathway revealed that RS09 potently activated this signaling cascade (Fig. 6D–E). Interestingly, exposure of hepatocytes to poly(I:C), the dual agonist of RIG-I and MDA5, directly induced IFN-λ3 expression without causing a significant elevation in p-IRF7 and IRF7 levels. This observation implies the existence of an IRF7 activation-independent pathway responsible for IFN-λ3 production (Fig. 6F–G). In contrast, treatment with TAK-242, a selective TLR4 inhibitor, abrogated E. coli-induced activation of the IRF7–IFN-λ pathway (Fig. 6H–I). Notably, administration of the RIG-I inhibitor RIG012 led to a marked increase in p-IRF7 and IRF7 expression in hepatocytes (Fig. 6J–K). This finding suggests that endogenous RIG-I signaling exerts a suppressive effect on TLR4-mediated upregulation of p-IRF7 and IRF7; thus, blocking RIG-I signaling relieves this inhibitory constraint, leading to elevated p-IRF7 and IRF7 levels. However, despite the increased accumulation of p-IRF7 and IRF7, the expression of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 was significantly reduced. This paradoxical result may be explained by the fact that RIG012 simultaneously blocks the direct inductive effect of RIG-I on IFN-λ production, independent of the IRF7 pathway (Fig. 6J–K).

To investigate whether TLR4 signaling is essential for the IRF7–IFN-λ axis in hepatocytes during bacterial infection, we used TLR4 knockout (KO) mice for in vivo assessment. Wild-type (WT) and TLR4 KO mice were infected with E. coli for 3 h, after which hepatocytes were isolated from each group. Tlr4 mRNA expression was first confirmed in these mice. In hepatocytes from WT mice, Tlr4, irf7, ifnl2, and ifnl3 mRNA levels were elevated at 3 h post-infection compared to uninfected WT mice. In contrast, in hepatocytes from TLR4 KO mice, these genes showed low expression levels and no significant changes after infection relative to uninfected TLR4 KO mice (Fig. 6L). Consistently, protein levels of p-IRF7, IRF7, IFN-λ2, and IFN-λ3 were all increased in hepatocytes from WT mice at 3 h post-infection, whereas no significant changes in these protein levels were observed in hepatocytes from TLR4 KO mice under the same condition (Fig. 6M–N). We next investigated whether downstream components of TLR4 are also involved in the IRF7–IFN-λ response in hepatocytes during bacterial infection. In FL83B cells infected with E. coli for 3 h, the protein levels of p-IRF7, IRF7, IFN-λ2, and IFN-λ3 were abolished by pre-treatment with either a MyD88 inhibitor (Pepinh-MYD TFA) or a TRIF inhibitor (Pepinh-TRIF TFA) for 6 h (Fig. 6O–P). Together, TLR4 signaling is critical for driving the IRF7–IFN-λ response in hepatocytes during E. coli infection.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the IRF7–IFN-λ axis in hepatocytes is specifically governed by TLR4 signaling, rather than by RIG-I or MDA5.

IFN-λ significantly improves survival rates in mice against lethal bacterial infections

To evaluate the potential of IFN-λ as an intervention strategy for sepsis, we investigated the effects of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 on the survival of mice challenged with lethal E. coli infection. As illustrated in Fig. 7A, two distinct administration protocols were designed, pretreatment (administered prior to infection) and concurrent treatment (administered alongside E. coli inoculation). The survival data showed that pretreatment with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 significantly reduced lethality following E. coli infection (Fig. 7B). In contrast, concurrent administration of IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 with E. coli did not confer significant protective effects (Fig. 7C). Consistently, pretreatment with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 also led to a significantly greater survival rate following cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) (Fig. 7D). Subsequently, we collected whole blood, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and thymus from mice in each group at 6 h post-infection. Tissues were homogenized in physiological saline, and homogenates were plated on LB agar plates for bacterial enumeration. Compared to mice with E. coli infection alone, animals pretreated with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 (Fig. 7E, Supplement Fig. 3 A), as well as those receiving concurrent IFN-λ2 treatment (Fig. 7F, Supplement Fig. 3B), exhibited a significant reduction in bacterial load across all tested organs and in the blood. Histopathological analysis of liver tissues (H&E staining) revealed extensive immune cell infiltration (yellow arrows), hepatocyte degeneration, and necrotic foci (green arrows) in mice infected with E. coli alone. In contrast, livers form mice pretreated with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 showed markedly reduced immune cell infiltration, hepatocyte degeneration, and necrotic areas (Fig. 7G), which was accompanied by a significantly lower histological activity index (Fig. 7H). Compared to mice infected with E. coli alone, mice pretreated with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 showed significantly less immune cell infiltration and better structural integrity in the spleen, kidneys, and especially the lungs (Supplement Fig. 3 C). Furthermore, serum cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, IL-10) in pretreated mice were lower than in concurrently treated mice and nearly equivalent to uninfected controls (Fig. 7I). Consistently, CLP mice pretreated with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 decreased the serum AST, ALT, DBil, LDH, and Urea from CLP mice (Supplement Fig. 3D) and also exhibited a significant reduction in bacterial load across all tested organs and in the blood (Supplement Fig. 3E, F).

Then, to distinguish the effects of IFN-λs on hepatocytes from those on RAW264.7 macrophages, we assessed the phagocytosis, bactericidal activity, and cytokine secretion of RAW264.7 cells. Compared to RAW264.7 cells incubated with FITC-labeled dextran alone, RAW264.7 cells pre-treated with the supernatant from hepatocytes infected with E. coli for 12 h showed significantly enhanced phagocytic activity, as indicated by higher fluorescence intensity following a 2-h incubation with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled dextran, while pre-treatment with IFN-λ2, but not with IFN-λ3 or the IFN-λ2–IFN-λ3 mixture, also increased phagocytosis (Supplement Fig. 4 A). Furthermore, RAW264.7 cells incubated with either the supernatant from E. coli-infected hepatocytes, IFN-λ2, IFN-λ3, or the IFN-λ2–IFN-λ3 mixture showed a significant increase in the relative number of dead E. coli compared to RAW264.7 cells infected with E. coli alone (Supplement Fig. 4B). Pretreatment of RAW264.7 cells for 2 h with the same supernatant, IFN-λ2, IFN-λ3, or the mixture did not alter the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, or IL-10 compared to untreated RAW264.7 cells or only the supernatant (Supplement Fig. 4 C). However, pretreatment with the supernatant or IFN-λ2 for 2 h significantly reduced IL-6 secretion following E. coli infection for 6 h; similarly, pretreatment with IFN-λ3 significantly reduced IL-10 secretion under the same conditions (Supplement Fig. 4 C). Collectively, these data indicate that the supernatant from E. coli-infected hepatocytes, as well as IFN-λ2, IFN-λ3, and their mixture, markedly enhanced the bactericidal activity of RAW264.7 cells.

Together, these findings suggest that pretreating hepatocytes with IFN-λs protects against E. coli- and CLP-induced lethality by reducing bacterial dissemination, systemic inflammation, and liver tissue damage.

IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 interact with IFNLR1 to improves survival rates in septic mice

We investigated whether signaling through the IFN-λ receptor mediates the protective effects of IFN-λ in septic mice. First, we examined the mRNA expression levels of IFNLR1 in FL83B (hepatocyte) and RAW264.7 (macrophage) cells following E. coli infection. IFNLR1 mRNA was predominantly expressed in FL83B cells, with lower levels observed in RAW264.7 cells. In FL83B cells, Ifnlr1 mRNA increased after 6 h of E. coli infection (Fig. 8A). Notably, FL83B cells with IFNLR1 knockdown (FL83B-shIFNLR1) exhibited a significantly reduced ability to inhibit E. coli proliferation compared to negative control cells (FL83B-NC) (Fig. 8B), indicating that IFNLR1 contributes to the antibacterial capacity of hepatocytes. To further explore this, we generated IFNLR1 knockdown mice using ISC siRNAs. These mice showed a 73% reduction in ifnlr1 expression (Supplement Fig. 5 A) and had significantly worse survival rates compared to wild-type (WT) mice. As expected, pretreatment with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 did not significantly improve survival in IFNLR1KD mice following E. coli infection, in contrast to WT mice (Fig. 8C). Consistent with the survival data, WT mice pretreated with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 exhibited lower serum levels of IL-10 (Fig. 8D), IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα compared to pretreated IFNLR1KD mice (Fig. 8E).

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we performed RNA-seq on liver samples from mice pretreated with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3, as well as from mice receiving concurrent treatment with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3. A Venn diagram revealed largely distinct sets of differentially expressed genes among the four groups (Fig. 8F). Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis further demonstrated group-specific pathway enrichment (Fig. 8G). In the Biological Process category, concurrent IFN-λ2 treatment enriched pathways related to stress response, protein processing, inflammation, and protein maturation, while concurrent IFN-λ3 enriched similar processes along with lipid metabolism and protein transport regulation. Pretreatment with IFN-λ2 was associated with organ regeneration, hormone regulation, membrane potential control, and steroid biosynthesis, whereas IFN-λ3 pretreatment enriched pathways for cell motility, immune responses, epithelial proliferation, cytokine production, and cell signaling. In the Molecular Function category, concurrent IFN-λ2 enriched inhibitor activity; concurrent IFN-λ3 enriched amino acid transport; IFN-λ2 pretreatment enriched channel, transporter, and transcription factor activities; and IFN-λ3 pretreatment enriched transaminase and cell adhesion molecule binding activities. Together, these findings indicate that IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 interact with IFNLR1 to activate hepatocytes and protect against sepsis by activating different downstream signaling pathways.

Increased expression levels of IRF7, IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 in sepsis patients

To extend these findings to human disease, we investigated whether the expression levels of IRF7 and IFN-λ2/3 correlate with disease severity (assessed by APACHE II score) and survival rates. Data from the GEO public database, including datasets GSE134347, GSE54514, GSE63042, and GSE272769 from patients with sepsis, were included in this analysis. According to transcriptome data from GSE134347, mRNA expression levels of irf7 (Fig. 9A), ifnl2 (Fig. 9B), and ifnl3 (Fig. 9C) were higher in whole-blood samples from sepsis patients than in healthy controls. The expression level of irf7 was higher in non-survivors than in 30-day survivors (Fig. 9D). Notably, ifnl2/3 expression was undetectable in whole-blood transcriptome data from GSE54514. In contrast, in dataset GSE272769, irf7 mRNA expression was lower in non-survivors, while ifnl2 and ifnl3 showed no significant difference between septic survivors and non-survivors (Supplement Fig. 5B). A previous study reported that serum levels of interferon (IFN)-λ2/3 in COVID-19 patients increased rapidly at first but then declined [38]. This decrease in IFN-λ2/3 expression may explain why some whole blood samples were undetectable in sepsis patients, given the prolonged duration of sepsis. Patient characteristics from these GEO datasets are summarized in Fig. 9E. We next analyzed liver samples from patients with acute cholangitis, a bacterial infection-related hepatic disorder. Elevated expression of IRF7, IFNL2, and IFNL3 was observed at both the mRNA (Fig. 9F) and protein levels (Fig. 9G–H). Immunohistological analysis confirmed the protein levels of IRF7 and p-IRF7 (Ser477) in these clinical samples (Fig. 9I–J). To further explore the synergistic effect of IFN-λs with antibiotics, we administered cefixime intragastrically (14.4 mg/kg [39]) in combination with either IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 (250 μg/kg) in vivo. The combination of cefixime with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 significantly improved the survival rate of septic mice (Fig. 9K). However, this combination of low-dose cefixime with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 did not exhibit significant bacteriostatic effects in vitro (Supplement Fig. 5 C). Collectively, these data demonstrate that bacterial infection induces the upregulation and activation of IRF7, an upstream transcription factor for type III interferons, and that this induction is associated with increased IFN-λs expression in hepatocytes in human bacterial liver disease.

Discussion

Hepatocytes, the primary parenchymal cells of the liver, have been well-documented to play pivotal roles in maintaining immune homeostasis during inflammatory responses [40] and mediating host–pathogen interactions with viruses [41]. However, the extent of their direct capacity to combat bacterial infections remains poorly characterized in existing literature. To clarify the cellular heterogeneity of antibacterial immune responses during sepsis, this study established a hepatocyte-centered bacterial infection model to dissect the innate immune mechanisms by which hepatic parenchymal cells eliminate pathogenic bacteria. Notably, we are the first to investigate the regulatory interplay between IRF7 and IFN-λ production in hepatocytes under bacterial infection conditions. Our findings reveal that IRF7 acts as a positive regulator of type III interferon (IFN-λ) expression in hepatocytes during bacterial challenge; activation of the IRF7–IFN-λ signaling enhances hepatocytes’ ability to suppress bacterial proliferation, and exogenous IFN-λ administration significantly improves survival in mice with lethal E. coli infection.

The liver is a key target for immune mediators during bacterial infections. Cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-1, TNFα) secreted by monocytes and macrophages in response to bacterial invasion can stimulate hepatocytes to produce bactericidal proteins and effector molecules. In viral infections, hepatocytes exert intrinsic immune functions by generating antimicrobial effectors (including NO, IFNγ, IFNβ, and ROS) via their innate immune receptors [42]. Despite growing recognition of the liver’s robust immune competence, direct evidence of hepatocytes combating bacterial pathogens remains scarce. In this study, we confirmed that hepatocytes can directly inhibit the proliferation of E. coli, MRSA, and V. vulnificus. Further investigations into the bactericidal mediators secreted by infected hepatocytes revealed elevated levels of NO, IFNβ, and H₂O₂ (a major ROS component), effectors that are also critical for hepatocyte-mediated antiviral defense. Our results show that after 12 h of E. coli infection, all three hepatocyte cell lines, shIRF7, IRF7-overexpressing, and control, exhibited increased production of H₂O₂ and NO. However, in shIRF7-expressing cells, the levels of both H₂O₂ and NO were reduced compared to those in IRF7-overexpressing and control cells. Together, these findings demonstrate that during E. coli infection, IRF7 not only predominantly modulates IFN-λ production in hepatocytes but also influences the secretion of H₂O₂ and NO.

Strikingly, IFN-λ2/3 exhibited higher expression abundance and an earlier induction kinetics compared to IFNβ in bacterially infected hepatocytes. Prior to this work, no reports had linked bacterial infection to IFN-λ production in hepatocytes, and the role of IFN-λs in bacterial immunity remained undefined. We observed robust upregulation of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 in infected hepatocytes, with IFN-λ3 showing the highest expression level in the liver post-infection. By distinguishing between mRNA and protein levels of IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3, and by separating hepatocytes from non-hepatocytic liver cells, we confirmed that hepatocytes are the primary source of type III interferons in the infected liver. This finding was further validated in both hepatocyte cell lines and primary hepatocytes following bacterial challenge.​ The heat-inactivated supernatant from E. coli-infected hepatocytes, or the supernatant pre-incubated with neutralizing antibodies against IFN-λs, decreased the bactericidal activity. This suggests that while the activity is mainly attributable to IFN-λ itself, other active components may also be involved in this process. IFN-λ may exert a weak, possibly indirect or microenvironment-dependent effect on bacterial growth, rather than possessing robust direct antibacterial activity. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear and may involve physicochemical interactions or unknown bacterial targets.

IRF7 is known to regulate not only type I interferons (IFN-I) [43] but also type III IFNs [44]. TLR7/9 signaling pathways activates IRF7 to induce type I IFNs in plasmacytoid dendritic cells [45]. Specifically, the IFN-λ1 gene is co-regulated by virus-activated IRF3 and IRF7 (analogous to IFNβ), while IFN-λ2/3 expression is primarily controlled by IRF7 (similar to IFNα). Previous studies have highlighted the critical role of IRF7-mediated IFN-III (IFN-λ1 and IFN-λ3) in antiviral defense at epithelial barriers [46]. IFN-λ2/λ3 induction is IRF7-dependent rather than IRF3-dependent in viral infection models using A549 cells [47]. IFNL3 promoter contains a functional ISRE that can bind IRF7 and is efficiently activated upon IRF7 overexpression in monocyte-derived DCs. Notably, IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 are highly similar, sharing 96% homology, and the promoter sequences of the IFN-λ2 and IFN-λ3 genes are almost identical [44]. Additionally, IRF7 and p65 physically interact for the activation of the IFNL3 promoter [48]. However, whether IRF7 regulates IFN-λ production in parenchymal cells during bacterial infection remained unknown. Using IRF7-knockdown and IRF7-overexpression cell models, we demonstrated that IRF7 positively regulates IFN-λ expression in hepatocytes and that bacterial infection activates the IRF7–IFN-λ signaling pathways. Comparative analyses of bactericidal mediators across IRF7-manipulated and wild-type cells showed that IRF7 overexpression preferentially enhances IFN-λ2/3 production. Consistent with this, strengthening the IRF7–IFN-λ pathway augmented hepatocyte-mediated suppression of E. coli proliferation. IRF7-overexpressing hepatocytes exhibited significantly stronger antibacterial activity than IRF7-knockdown or wild-type cells, and supplementation with recombinant IFN-λ2/3 restored the impaired antibacterial capacity of IRF7-deficient hepatocytes. In immune cells, IRF7 is activated via multiple signaling pathways, including RIG-I/MDA5–MAVS–IRF7 in colorectal cancer cells, Poly (I:C)–GPATCH3-STING–IRF7-IFN I in CIK cells, RAIDD–IRF7–IFNI, cell-surface TLR4–IRF7–IFN III [49]. The liver predominantly expresses innate immune receptors such as RIG-like receptors (RIG-I, MDA5, LGP2) [50], NOD-like receptors (NLRP1/2/3, NLRC5) [51], and a limited subset of Toll-like receptors (TLR3) [52], TLR4 [53]. Based on this receptor profile, we focused on RIG-I, MDA5, and TLR4 to identify the upstream regulator of the IRF7–IFN-λ pathway in hepatocytes. Using pathway-specific agonists and inhibitors, we confirmed that the IRF7–IFN-λ pathway in hepatocytes is primarily regulated by the TLR4-MyD88/TRIF signaling pathway.

As a key transcription factor, the regulatory role of IRF7 in sepsis is not limited to the induction of IFN-λ in hepatocytes. Recent studies have shown that in a sepsis-induced acute lung injury model, IRF7 can transcriptionally activate the expression of the Srg3 gene, thereby regulating the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and ferroptosis, ultimately promoting M1 macrophage polarization and aggravating lung tissue injury [54]. This finding suggests that IRF7 may have organ-specific regulatory differences in sepsis: in hepatocytes, IRF7 exerts antibacterial and protective effects through the TLR4–IFN-λ axis; while in lung tissue, IRF7 may mediate tissue injury through the Srg3–NF-κB–ferroptosis pathway. This difference may be closely related to the heterogeneity of target organ cell types (hepatocytes vs. lung epithelial cells/macrophages) and the local immune microenvironment. Notably, the transcriptional activation of Srg3 by IRF7 [54] relies on its classical transcription factor function, which is consistent with the mechanism by which IRF7 regulates IFN-λ2/3 expression in this study, further verifying the core regulatory role of IRF7 in sepsis. At the same time, it suggests that intervention strategies targeting IRF7 need to consider its organ-specific effects to avoid side effects caused by single targeting.

To evaluate the translational potential of IFN-λs as a sepsis intervention, we assessed the impact of IFN-λ2/3 on survival in a lethal E. coli or CLP induced model. Pretreatment with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 significantly improved mouse survival rates, preserved liver function, reduced bacterial burden in organs, and inhibited excessive inflammatory activation. In contrast, administration immediately post-infection failed to confer protective effects. The role of IFN-λs in disease progression remains controversial. On one hand, IFN-λ has been shown to exacerbate lung inflammation in Bordetella pertussis infection [55] and IFN-λ derived from lung dendritic cells in response to a synthetic viral RNA triggers barrier damage, a pathological alteration that predisposes the organism to fatal bacterial superinfections [56]. On the other hand, IFN-λ2 alleviates tissue damage and enhances intestinal barrier integrity in ischemia–reperfusion models [57], suppresses numbers of proinflammatory IL-17-producing Th17 and γδ T cells and decreases recruitment of IL-1β-production neutrophils in autoimmune arthritis [58], and relief OVA-induced experimental allergic conjunctivitis reduced clinical symptoms [59]; IFN-λ3 coordinates anti-tumor immunity and boosting macrophage phagocytic function [60]. Integrating existing evidence with our findings, we propose that the immunomodulatory functions of IFN-λs (including λ2 and λ3 subtypes) are highly context-dependent, with their net effects governed by three key factors, timing of administration, local concentration, and the characteristics of the immune microenvironment. Preemptive IFN-λ administration can induce an “immune preconditioning” state, with IFN-λ2 dampening excessive neutrophil-driven inflammation and IFN-λ3 promoting cellular immunity and macrophage-mediated tumor cell phagocytosis [60].

In the clinical treatment of sepsis-induced acute lung injury, glucocorticoids such as methylprednisolone (MP) are commonly used for anti-inflammatory intervention, and its mechanism has been proven to be related to regulating the miR-151-5p/USP38 pathway, inhibiting lung epithelial cell apoptosis and inflammatory responses [61]. However, hormone therapy has limitations such as immunosuppression and risk of secondary infection, and has no direct effect on bacterial clearance. The IFN-λ pretreatment strategy found in this study can significantly reduce the organ bacterial load and alleviate liver tissue injury in septic mice through dual effects of direct bacteriostasis (destroying bacterial cell membranes) and regulating hepatocyte innate immunity, which complements the anti-inflammatory mechanism of MP. In the future, the possibility of combined application of IFN-λ and MP can be explored: IFN-λ is responsible for targeted bacterial clearance and hepatocyte protection, while MP focuses on inhibiting excessive inflammatory responses and lung injury, and their synergy may further optimize the therapeutic effect of sepsis. Clinically, interferons are commonly administered via intravenous or subcutaneous routes for systemic delivery [62]. However, there have been no reports of IFN-λs being used in clinical applications to date. The type III interferon receptor, composed of IFNLR1 and IL10R2 subunits, is preferentially expressed on mucosal epithelial cells, liver cells, and certain myeloid cell populations, indicating that the liver is one of the major responsive target organs for IFN-λs. Nevertheless, systemic administration results in drug dissipation and uptake by non-target epithelial tissues (e.g., intestinal and respiratory epithelia), which reduces effective drug accumulation in the liver and lowers utilization efficiency [63]. In terms of safety, IFN-λs exhibits a distinct tissue-specific receptor distribution compared to conventional type I interferons, thereby conferring fewer systemic adverse reactions and better clinical tolerance [49]. In addition, the combination of antibiotics with IFN-λ2 or IFN-λ3 significantly improved the survival rate of septic mice. Our findings collectively indicate that the protective effects of IFN-λs against sepsis are time-window dependent, which substantially limits its clinical applicability. Therefore, IFN-λs may be more suitable for preventive intervention in high-risk populations, such as patients undergoing elective major surgery, individuals with severe trauma or burns, and those identified as high-risk through biomarker surveillance, rather than as a routine therapeutic agent for patients with established sepsis. This spatiotemporal dependent immune remodeling framework provides a mechanistic basis for reconciling the divergent roles of IFN-λs across disease models and informs the design of precision immune interventions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study elucidated the cellular diversity of antibacterial immunity during sepsis by establishing a hepatocyte-based bacterial infection model and defining the innate immune mechanisms of hepatic parenchymal cell-mediated bacterial clearance. We demonstrated that bacterial infection activates the TLR4–IRF7–IFN-λ pathway in hepatocytes (Fig. 10), and that pathway activation enhances hepatocyte capacity to inhibit bacterial proliferation, thereby identifying novel therapeutic targets for sepsis treatment.
